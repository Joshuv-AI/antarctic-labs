Slot decomposition of continuous Box-Ball Systems
Inés Armendáriz, Pablo Blanc

Abstract
We study a piecewise constant function η : ℝ → { − 1 , 1 } \eta:\mathbb{R}\to\{-1,1\} with a finite number of discontinuities in any interval. We assume that the associated walk ξ : ℝ → ℝ \xi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} satisfying ξ ′ ​ ( x ) = η ​ ( x ) \xi^{\prime}(x)=\eta(x) , pinned by ξ ⁡ ( 0 ) = 0 \xi(0)=0 , has finite length excursions over past minima. This is the continuous generalization of an initial ball configuration in the discrete Box Ball System introduced by Takahashi and Satsuma, where solitons of integer sizes k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 are identified. We extend the slot decomposition developed by Ferrari, Nguyen, Rolla and Wang in the discrete setting to the continuous case. Each soliton of ξ \xi is represented by a point in two dimensional space, one coordinate for position and the other for the soliton height, mapping ξ \xi to a point configuration. We consider a distribution on walks given by a product measure on the decomposition of the path into excursions over past minima. Excursions are distributed as products of their solitons weights, which are determined by the soliton heights. We show that when the weight function is in L 1 L^{1} the slot decomposition of ξ \xi is a Poisson process. This extends to the continuous case an approach of Ferrari and Gabrielli. As an example, we compute the intensity measure of the Poisson process associated to the asymmetric telegraph process introduced by Kac. In a forthcoming paper we discuss the dynamic properties. Keywords : Continuous Box-Ball system, solitons, excursions, Pitman transformation, telegraph process. AMS 2010 Subject Classification : 37B15, 37K40, 60C05

Introduction

The Box-Ball System (BBS) is a prominent cellular automaton introduced by Takahashi and Satsuma
[
30
]
. The model exhibits interesting solitonic behavior while remaining simple enough to be solved and analyzed in detail. Interest in the BBS has been recently revitalized due to growing connections between statistical mechanics and integrable systems. As an ultradiscrete version of the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation, its connections with other models of integrable systems warrant further detailed investigation.

In the discrete setting, Ferrari, Nguyen, Rolla and Wang
[
16
]
introduced a description of the trajectories as a bi-infinite matrix with rows indexed by soliton sizes and columns indexed by the points, or slots, where these solitons are inserted, a construction they labelled the slot decomposition. This representation has enabled the derivation of several features of the model, including hydrodynamic behavior by Croydon and Sasada
[
10
]
, a characterization of invariant measures by Ferrari and Gabrielli
[
14
]
, and the fluctuations of soliton positions by Olla, Sasada and Suda
[
24
]
.

In this paper we consider a continuous version of the BBS. A configuration is a function
(\eta(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
taking values
1
and
-1
, with a finite number of discontinuities in bounded sets. Each
\eta
is associated to a continuous real valued walk
\xi=(\xi(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with derivatives
\xi^{\prime}(t)=\eta(t)
at continuity points of
\eta
. We provide an algorithmic description of the slot decomposition of
\xi
, which yields a point configuration on a half-plane. Generalizing the discrete construction in
[
14
]
, we identify the law of the point process associated with a random
\xi
as a non-homogeneous two-dimensional Poisson process.
In future works we will address the relation between the slot decomposition of
\xi
with its decomposition in terms of real trees, see for instance the lecture notes of Evans
[
12
]
. We will also describe the evolution of the slot decomposition under the continuous version of the BBS dynamics of
\eta
, which is equivalent to Pitman’s transformation of
\xi

[
25
]
, as observed by Croydon and Sasada in
[
9
]
.

There are several equivalent characterizations of solitons, see Ferrari and Gabrielli
[
15
]
for a discussion. We describe a simple algorithm that is particularly well suited to continuous walks. In this representation, every soliton is centered at a local maximum of
\xi
. Solitons are identified independently within each excursion over past minima of the walk. The procedure starts with the shortest solitons and then proceeds recursively to larger ones.

To each soliton we associate a pair
(u,\ell)
, where
\ell
is the height of the soliton and
u
encodes its relative position within the walk.
In this way, a zig-zag walk
\xi
is identified with a point process

This construction defines a bijection between an appropriate space of walks

2
Soliton decomposition and slot representation

The goal of this section is to construct a bijection between walks and point configurations.
After introducing the necessary definitions in Subsection
2.1
, we
identify the solitons in a walk, one per local maximum.
We then construct the point configuration by associating a
2
-dimensional point to each soliton, whose coordinates are determined by the insertion position of the soliton in the walk and its height.
Finally we show how to reconstruct the walk from the point configuration. We do this construction for a single, isolated excursion in a first step, Subsection
2.4
, and then extend it to the full trajectory.

In several stages of the construction we need to identify the points realizing the absolute extrema of a trajectory over a given interval. Where necessary, we define explicit tie-breaking rules. However, in the absence of such instructions, any valid selection may be used. Detailed analyses of specific cases are provided in the Appendix, which serves as a reference for readers seeking further clarification on these subtleties.

2.1
Ball configurations, walks and carrier load

Continuous ball configurations
Let

\displaystyle{\check{\mathcal{X}}}\vcentcolon=\{\eta:\mathbb{R}\to\{-1,1\}:\text{càdlàg with finitely many jumps in bounded intervals}\}.

A function
\eta\in{\check{\mathcal{X}}}
is characterized by the sets of discontinuity points
A[\eta]
and
B[\eta]
, given by

\displaystyle A[\eta]\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\eta(x)-\eta(x-)=2\},\quad B[\eta]\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\eta(x)-\eta(x-)=-2\},

(2.1)

where
\eta(x-)\vcentcolon=\lim_{y\uparrow x}\eta(y)
.
We interpret the interval between an upward jump and the nearest downward jump to its right as a continuous set of balls, in analogy with the discrete BBS.

Walk representation
Given a ball configuration
\eta
denote by
\xi=\xi[\eta]
the associated zig-zag walk, a continuous function pinned at the origin,
\xi(0)\vcentcolon=0
, with derivatives

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(x)\vcentcolon=\eta(x),\quad x\notin A[\eta]\cup B[\eta].

(2.2)

The elements of the sets
A[\eta]
and
B[\eta]
are the local minima and local maxima of
\xi
, respectively. We also refer to them as

Let

be the set of walks given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\vcentcolon=\left\{\xi[\eta]:\liminf_{x\to\mp\infty}\xi[\eta](x)=\pm\infty,\,\eta\in{\check{\mathcal{X}}}\right\},

(2.3)

and let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of ball configurations associated to walks in

, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\vcentcolon=\bigl\{\eta[\xi]:\xi\in\mathcal{W}\bigr\}.

(2.4)

Past minimum
For

by

\displaystyle m(x)\vcentcolon=\min\bigl\{\xi(z):z\leq x\bigr\}.

(2.5)

Records
We say that

, that is,

and

for every
x<r
. The

-record is defined by

\displaystyle r(y)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\inf\xi^{-1}(y).

(2.6)

Notice that
\xi^{-1}(y)
is non-empty and

for
\xi\in\mathcal{W}
.
Denote the set of records by

\displaystyle R[\xi]

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{r(y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(2.7)

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
be the set of walks in
\mathcal{W}
having record

at the origin,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\vcentcolon=\{\xi\in\mathcal{W}:\xi(0)=0<\xi(x),\,x<0\}.

(2.8)

Excursions.
When
\xi\in\mathcal{W}
, the mapping
y\mapsto m^{-1}(y)
associates to each ordinate

a set containing either a single point or the interval
[r(y),r(y-)]
. The restriction of the walk

\displaystyle\big(\xi(x):x\in(r(y),r(y-)]\bigr),

(2.9)

is called the excursion at level

. If

, then

, and the excursion at level

Carrier load process.
The carrier load process
\zeta[\xi]
is defined by

\displaystyle\zeta(x)\vcentcolon=\xi(x)-m(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.10)

Conversely,
the increments of
\xi
are determined by
\zeta

\displaystyle\xi(x)-\xi(x^{\prime})=\zeta(x)-\zeta(x^{\prime})-|\{z\in[x,x^{\prime}]:\zeta(z)=0\}|,\qquad x^{\prime}<x.

(2.11)

The trajectories
\xi
and
\zeta
have the same excursions above the past minimum, and the flat regions of
\zeta
correspond to records of
\xi
.

3
Random distributions of walks

In this section we introduce two families of measures on trajectories. The first family consists of product measures on the decomposition of the walk into excursions, which are separated by iid intervals of records. The distribution of an excursion
\varepsilon
is determined by a vector of weights
(\alpha(\ell_{i}))_{i}
, where
(\ell_{i})_{i}
are the heights of the solitons in
\varepsilon
and the intensity
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies some integrability conditions. We then consider another family of distributions determined by Poisson point processes with configurations in
\mathcal{N}
. We finally show that under some conditions these families of measures are equivalent.

3.1
Random excursions

We start by defining two families of distributions on excursions in
\mathcal{E}_{o}
.

3.1.1
Soliton weights

Recall that
\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)
denotes the set of excursions starting at the origin and having
n
maxima, as defined in (
2.36
).
For
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, define

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)}\alpha(\ell_{1})\dots\alpha(\ell_{n})\,d\underline{a}_{n}\,d\underline{b}_{n},

(3.1)

where
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}\geq\dots\geq\ell_{n}
are the soliton sizes of the excursion
(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)
,
d\underline{a}_{n}\vcentcolon=da_{2}\dots da_{n}
, because
a_{1}\equiv 0
, and
d\underline{b}_{n}\vcentcolon=db_{1}\dots db_{n}
. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bigl\{\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\,:\,Z_{\alpha}<+\infty\bigr\}\,.

(3.2)

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,
define the probability measure
\mu_{\alpha}
on
\mathcal{E}_{o}
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(d\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha}}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt\{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)\}\,\alpha(\ell_{1})\dots\alpha(\ell_{n})\,d\underline{a}_{n}\,d\underline{b}_{n}\,.

(3.3)

The length of
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)
is
{\mathsf{e}}(\varepsilon)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}
, as
{\mathsf{o}}=0
, see (
2.18
). The average length of an excursion distributed as (
3.3
) is
\int{\mathsf{e}}(\varepsilon)\,\mu_{\alpha}(d\varepsilon)
. We call

\mathcal{A}^{+}\vcentcolon=\Bigl\{\alpha\,:\,\int{\mathsf{e}}(\varepsilon)\,\mu_{\alpha}(d\varepsilon)<+\infty\Bigr\}\,

(3.4)

the set of weight functions that determine distributions with finite average excursion length.
It is hard to characterize the families
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{+}
in terms of soliton weights as in (
3.3
,
3.4
), but we will see that they can be easily described using the slot representation of the excursions.

Example 12

(Excursions of a telegraph process)
.

Let
(\eta(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous time Markov jump process on
\{-1,1\}

with rates
r(-1,1)=\lambda_{-}\leq\lambda_{+}=r(1,-1)
. The Kac telegraph process
[
18
]
describes a one dimensional particle travelling with constant speed in the direction given by
\eta
. Let us denote this process by
(\xi(t),\,t\geq 0)
. The typical excursion
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}_{o}
starts with
\eta(0)=1
and runs until the first time
\xi
hits
0
. We get

\displaystyle\xi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s)\,ds,\quad\varepsilon=\bigl(\xi(t)\bigr)_{t\in[0,{\mathsf{e}}]},\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathsf{e}}=\inf\{t>0:\xi(t)=0\}\,.

(3.5)

We can explicitly compute the distribution of the excursions if we use that the stretches where
\xi
has constant direction
\pm 1
are independent exponential variables with parameter
\lambda_{\pm}
. It follows that the distribution of
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}_{o}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}_{+}
, with density
f(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\geq 1}f_{n}(\varepsilon){\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)}(\varepsilon)
, where for
\varepsilon=(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)
we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{+}\,e^{-(b_{i}-a_{i})\lambda_{+}}\Bigr)\,\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{-}\,e^{-(a_{i+1}-b_{i})\lambda_{-}}\Bigr)\,e^{-({\mathsf{e}}-b_{n})\lambda_{-}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{+}\,e^{-\ell_{i}\lambda_{+}}\Bigr)\,\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{-}\,e^{-\ell_{i}\lambda_{-}}\Bigr)\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{-}}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{-}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(\ell_{i}).

(3.8)

We used (
2.19
) to obtain (
3.7
) from the first line. Here
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is given by

\alpha(\ell)=\lambda_{-}\,\lambda_{+}\,e^{-\left(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+}\right)\ell}.

(3.9)

Since
\lambda_{-}\leq\lambda_{+}
the random excursion
\varepsilon
is finite with probability one. If
\lambda_{-}<\lambda_{+}
the process has negative drift, and the excursion has finite mean. In any of these cases we have

\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{o}(n)}f_{n}(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})\,d\underline{a}_{n}d\underline{b}_{n}=P(\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}_{o})=1.

Hence
Z_{\alpha}=\lambda_{-}
,
and the telegraph process has excursions with
law
\mu_{\alpha}
, with
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
as in (
3.9
). If the strict inequality holds,
\lambda_{-}<\lambda_{+}
, then
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

We can construct the telegraph process as seen from a record by considering a sequence of iid excursions
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\geq 1}
with law
\mu_{\alpha}
, inserting an interval of length
{\mathsf{u}}_{k}
between the
k-1
-th and the
k
-th excursion,
k\geq 2
, and adding an interval of length
{\mathsf{u}}_{1}
between the record and the first excursion, see (
2.56
)-(
2.59
). The variables
({\mathsf{u}}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
here are iid exponential random variables with parameter
\lambda_{-}
. This yields the carrier process
\zeta(t)
, in order to obtain the telegraph process
\xi(t)
we use (
2.11
).

3.1.2
First excursion of a Poisson point process

We introduce a second family of distributions on
\mathcal{E}_{o}
, which rests on the representation of an excursion as a slot diagram.

Let

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Big\{q:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ measurable}:\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}q(\ell)d\ell<+\infty\Big\},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}^{+}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Big\{q\in\mathscr{Q}\,:\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}\ell q(\ell)d\ell<+\infty\Big\}\,.

(3.11)

Given
q\in\mathscr{Q}
, let
\mathsf{N}
be the Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity measure
du\,q(\ell)d\ell
.

Denote by
{\mathsf{u}}
is the distance of the leftmost point in
\mathsf{N}_{+}
to the
\ell
axis, if such a leftmost point exists, and
0
otherwise. Since
q\in\mathscr{Q}
, the law of
{\mathsf{u}}
is exponential with rate

\int q\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(y)dy,\qquad\mathbb{P}({\mathsf{u}}>t)=e^{-t\int q},

(3.12)

and
{\mathsf{u}}
is positive with probability
1
. Let
({\mathsf{u}}[\mathsf{N}_{+}],\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{N}_{+}])
be as defined by the process (
2.50
), where

{\mathsf{u}}[\mathsf{N}_{+}]={\mathsf{u}}
and
\mathsf{M}
is the first slot diagram of
\mathsf{N}_{+}
. At this point we cannot exclude the possibility that
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{N}_{+}]
is infinite, but the fact that
q\in\mathscr{Q}
implies that the recursive step in the construction is well defined, and the number of points in
\mathsf{M}\cap(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[a,b])
is a.s. finite for any
0<a<b
. We show in Lemma
14
below that
\mathsf{N}_{+}
belongs to
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{N}_{+}]
is finite with probability
1
.

Note that
{\mathsf{u}}
and
\mathsf{M}
are independent, as
\mathsf{N}
is a Poisson point process with an intensity that is homogeneous in the first coordinate. In particular, the coordinates of the first point
({\mathsf{u}},\ell_{1})
in the leftmost excursion are independent, and
\ell_{1}
is sampled with law
q(\ell)d\ell/\int q
, a fact we isolate for future reference.

Remark 13
.

(Law of the excursion maximum)
The maximum value
\ell_{1}
of the excursion
\mathsf{M}
has distribution

\frac{q(\ell)\,d\ell}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}q(\ell)d\ell}.

(3.13)

:
The regions
{\mathsf{U}}
(red) and
\mathsf{G}
(blue) for the excursion in Fig.

.

In order to identify the leftmost slot diagram
\mathsf{M}
of a configuration
\mathsf{N}\in\mathcal{N}
, the process defined in (
2.50
) explores the regions

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{(z,y):0<z\leq{\mathsf{u}}\}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{(z,y):{\mathsf{u}}\leq z<\mathsf{g}(y)\},

(3.15)

and locates all the points in the diagram, see Fig.
13
. Here
\mathsf{g}=\mathsf{g}[\mathsf{M}]:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined in (
2.41
).

Recall the definitions (
2.38
) and (
2.39
) of the sets
\mathcal{N}_{o}
and
\mathcal{N}_{o}(n)
.
The Poisson process induces a measure
\nu_{q}
on finite slot diagrams
such that for
n\geq 1
and
\mathsf{M}=\{(u_{1},\ell_{1}),\dots,(u_{n},\ell_{n})\}\in\mathcal{N}_{o}(n)

\displaystyle\nu_{q}(d\underline{u}_{n}\,d\underline{\ell}_{n})={\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\mathcal{N}_{o}}\left(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n}\right)\frac{q(\ell_{1})\,d\ell_{1}}{\int q(y)dy}\,\exp\Bigl(-\int_{\mathsf{G}}q(y)\,dxdy\Bigr)\,\prod_{i=2}^{n}q(\ell_{i})\,du_{i}\,d\ell_{i},

(3.16)

where
d\underline{u}_{n}=du_{2}\dots du_{n}
, as
\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{N}_{o}
implies that
u_{1}\equiv 0
, and
d\underline{\ell}_{n}=d\ell_{1}\dots d\ell_{n}
. The indicator function
{\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\mathcal{N}_{o}}\left(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n}\right)
ensures that the coordinates determine a slot diagram. Proving that the first slot diagram determined by the Poisson point process is finite with probability
1
is equivalent to showing that
\nu_{q}
is a probability measure, see Lemma
14
below.

The set
\mathsf{G}
in (
3.15
) is a disjoint union of triangles
(\mathsf{G}_{i})_{i}
, where
\mathsf{G}_{i}
has base
2u_{i}
and height
\ell_{i}
, see Fig.
7
, hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{G}}q(y)\,dxdy=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathsf{G}_{i}}dx\,q(y)\,dy=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\ell_{i}}(\ell_{i}-y)\,q(y)\,dy.

(3.17)

We obtain the following factorization of
\nu_{q}
,

\displaystyle\nu_{q}(d\underline{u}_{n}\,d\underline{\ell}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\mathcal{N}_{o}}\left(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n}\right)}{\int q(y)dy}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-2\int_{0}^{\ell_{i}}(\ell_{i}-y)\,q(y)\,dy\Bigr)\,q(\ell_{i})\,d\underline{u}_{n}\,d\underline{\ell}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\mathcal{N}_{o}}\left(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n}\right)}{\int q(y)dy}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\big(-2\mathcal{Q}(\ell_{i})\big)\,\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(\ell_{i})\,d\underline{u}_{n}\,d\underline{\ell}_{n},

(3.18)

where

\displaystyle Q(y)\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{y}q(z)dz\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Q}(y)\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{y}Q(z)dz.

(3.19)

3.1.3
Finite number of solitons and mean excursion length

The goal of this section is to prove that for
q\in\mathscr{Q}
, the first excursion of the Poisson process with intensity
du\,q(\ell)d\ell
contains a finite number of solitons, while if
q\in\mathscr{Q}^{+}
it has finite mean length, Lemmas
14
and Corollary
16
. To do so we introduce a non homogeneous branching process related to the construction of the slot diagram.

Let
q\in\mathscr{Q}
. For each
\ell>0
consider the distribution on
[0,\ell]
given by

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(y)\vcentcolon=\frac{(\ell-y)q(y){\bf 1}\hskip-1.42262pt_{\,0\leq y\leq\ell}}{\mathcal{Q}(\ell)}.

(3.20)

The process starts with one single node, the root, that has label
\ell>0
. If a node has label
\tilde{\ell}
, it produces a Poisson number of offspring with parameter
(2\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\ell}))
, which are assigned independent labels sampled according to
q_{\tilde{\ell}}
.

Let us call
p_{\ell}
the probability that the process dies out. By conditioning on the number of offspring and their labels we obtain that this probability satisfies the following recursive equation

p_{\ell}=e^{-2\mathcal{Q}(\ell)}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\big(2\mathcal{Q}(\ell)\int_{0}^{\ell}q_{\ell}(y)p_{y}dy\big)^{k}}{k!}=e^{2\mathcal{Q}(\ell)[\int_{0}^{\ell}q_{\ell}(y)p_{y}dy-1]}\,.

(3.21)

Let now
P_{\ell}\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{\ell}(\ell-y)q(y)p_{y}dy
. The integral equation (
3.21
) can be stated as the following differential problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}P^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}=q(\ell)e^{2(P_{\ell}-\mathcal{Q}(\ell))},\\
P_{0}=0,\\
P^{\prime}_{0}=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.22)

This is a Cauchy problem with a unique solution, and by a direct verification it is possible to show that
P_{\ell}=\mathcal{Q}(\ell)
is the unique solution, which leads to
p_{\ell}=1
. The branching process is therefore always subcritical.

The number of nodes in this branching process is distributed as the number of points in the leftmost slot diagram
\mathsf{M}
of the Poisson process with intensity
du\,q(\ell)d\ell
, conditioned to the leftmost point having coordinate
\ell
equal to the label of the initial node in the branching process. Indeed, we may construct the branching process by assigning to each point point
(u_{i},\ell_{i})
in the slot diagram the label
\ell_{i}
(its second coordinate), and identifying its progenie with the points of the process that fall in the triangle
\mathsf{G}_{i}
. We have thus proved the following result.

Lemma 14

(Finite slot diagrams)
.

Let
\mathsf{N}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity measure
du\,q(\ell)d\ell
, and let
\mathsf{M}
be the leftmost slot diagram in
\mathsf{N}_{+}
. Then
\mathsf{M}
contains a finite number of points with probability
1
.

We apply a similar argument to derive conditions on
q
that ensure that the mean length of the leftmost excursion

m(\ell)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{(u_{i},\ell_{i})\in\mathsf{M}}2\ell_{i}\big|\ell_{1}=\ell\big]

(3.23)

is finite, given that the height of leftmost point is
\ell
. By conditioning on the points in the triangle
\mathsf{G}_{1}
with base
2\ell
and height
\ell
determined by this leftmost point, or equivalently, on the first generation of the branching process process, we have that

\displaystyle m(\ell)

\displaystyle=2\ell+\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{(u_{i},\ell_{i})\in\mathsf{G}_{1}}m(\ell_{i})\big|\ell_{1}=\ell\big]

(3.24)

\displaystyle=2\ell+\int_{0}^{\ell}(\ell-y)q(y)m(y)dy,

where the second line follows from Campbell’s formula. We can transform this integral equation into a differential problem by setting
Y(\ell)\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{\ell}(\ell-y)m(y)q(y)dy
. Then (
3.24
) is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Y^{\prime\prime}(\ell)=2q(\ell)\big(\ell+Y(\ell)\big),\\
Y(0)=Y^{\prime}(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.25)

With the exception of certain values of
q
, there is not a general form for the solution of this Cauchy problem. On the other hand, we obtain the following criterion.

Lemma 15
.

Let
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
m
the solution of (
3.24
).
Then
\int_{0}^{+\infty}m(y)q(y)dy<+\infty
if and only if
\int_{0}^{\infty}yq(y)dy<+\infty
.

Proof.

Let us call
X(s)=Y^{\prime}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}m(y)q(y)dy
. The statement of the lemma concerns the behavior at infinity of
X
. In terms of
X
, problem (
3.25
) becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}X^{\prime}(\ell)=2\ell q(\ell)\big(1+\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{\ell}X(y)dy\big),\\
X(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.26)

If
\int_{0}^{+\infty}yq(y)dy=+\infty
then, since all the terms on the right hand side of the equation in (
3.26
) are nonnegative, we get

X(\ell)=\int_{0}^{\ell}X^{\prime}(y)dy\geq 2\int_{0}^{\ell}yq(y)dy\,,

from where it follows that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}m(y)q(y)dy=\lim_{\ell\to+\infty}X(\ell)\geq\int_{0}^{+\infty}yq(y)dy=+\infty\,.

Suppose now that
\int_{0}^{+\infty}yq(y)dy<+\infty
. Since
X
is monotone non decreasing we have from (
3.26
)

\frac{X^{\prime}(\ell)}{1+X(\ell)}\leq 2\ell q(\ell)\,,

that can be integrated and in the limit gives

\int_{0}^{+\infty}m(y)q(y)dy=\lim_{\ell\to+\infty}X(\ell)\leq e^{\int_{0}^{+\infty}yq(y)dy}-1<+\infty\,.\qed

We now return to the Poisson process. By Remark
13
the mean length of the first excursion is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m(\ell_{1})]=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}m(\ell)q(\ell)d\ell}{\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\ell)d\ell},

(3.27)

and Lemma
15
implies the following result.

Corollary 16

(Finite mean excursion length)
.

Let
q\in\mathscr{Q}
. Consider the Poisson point process
\mathsf{N}
in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity
du\,q(\ell)d\ell
, and let
\mathsf{M}
be the leftmost excursion of
\mathsf{N}_{+}
. Then its mean length

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{(u_{i},\ell_{i})\in\mathsf{M}}2\ell_{i}\big]=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}m(\ell)q(\ell)d\ell}{\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\ell)d\ell}<+\infty\iff q\in\mathscr{Q}^{+}.

(3.28)

3.2
Equivalence of measures

In Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
we introduced two probability measures on the space
\mathcal{E}_{o}
of finite excursions. The first one,
\mu_{\alpha}
, is parametrized by a weight function
\alpha
, see (
3.3
), while the second one is associated to a Poisson process with intensity determined by a function
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, see (
3.16
) and (
3.1.2
). We now show that these measures are equivalent by relating the functions
\alpha
and
q
.

Given
q\in\mathscr{Q}
, let
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be given by

\displaystyle\alpha(\ell)\vcentcolon=q(\ell)\,\exp\Bigl(-2\int_{0}^{\ell}(\ell-y)\,q(y)\,dy\Bigr)\,,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,.

(3.29)

With
\alpha
defined as above, and
\mathcal{Q}
given in (
3.19
), we get that
\alpha
and
\mathcal{Q}
satisfy

\displaystyle q(y)=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\alpha(y)e^{2\mathcal{Q}(y)}.

(3.30)

By definition
\mathcal{Q}(0)=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(0)=0
. So to get the inverse map of (
3.29
) we need to solve

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(y)=\alpha(y)e^{2\mathcal{Q}(y)},\\
\mathcal{Q}(0)=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(0)=0,\end{array}\right.

(3.31)

and compute
q(\ell)=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(\ell)
.

For simplicity we prove the following equivalence result assuming the global existence of the differential problem (
3.31
). This assumption can be removed, it follows from the definition of the set
\mathcal{A}
in (
3.2
).

Proposition 17

(Equivalence of measures)
.

Consider the relation between the functions
\alpha
and
q
given by equations (
3.29
) and the Cauchy problem (
3.31
).
This defines a bijection between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathscr{Q}
and between
\mathcal{A}^{+}
and
\mathscr{Q}^{+}
. Moreover if
\alpha
and
q
are in bijection then

\mu_{\alpha}=\nu_{q}
and
Z(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}q(\ell)\,d\ell
.

Proof.

We first show that for each fixed
n
,
the variables
(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n})
and
(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})

are related by a volume preserving linear transformation, i.e. we show that the Jacobian determinant of this transformation is
1
.

We proceed by induction. First of all the Jacobian determinant of the transformation is one when
n=1
since in that case there are just the variables
\ell_{1}
and
b_{1}
with
\ell_{1}=b_{1}
. We assume that the statement is true for
n
and show that it is then true for
n+1
.

Consider an excursion having
n
solitons with lengths
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}\geq\dots\geq\ell_{n}
. Let us call
(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n})
the coordinates in the first representation, and let
(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})
be the corresponding variables in the other coordinate system. By inductive hypothesis we have

(\underline{a}_{n},\underline{b}_{n})=A(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n})
where
A
is a
(2n-1)\times(2n-1)
matrix
having
|A|=\pm 1
. Indeed the structure of the matrix
A
changes for different regions of the parameters but not the value of the determinant.
We call
(A_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2n-1}
the elements of the matrix
A
.

In Fig.
14
we present an excursion with
n=3
red solitons to which we attach a new blue soliton. After attaching this soliton of length
\ell_{n+1}\leq\ell_{n}
, the updated coordinates become

(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n},u_{n+1},\ell_{n+1})
.
The slot diagram of the new excursion will be in correspondence with coordinates
(\tilde{\underline{a}}_{n},\tilde{\underline{b}}_{n},a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, where
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
are the coordinates respectively of the new minimum and maximum created by the insertion of the soliton. Here
(\tilde{\underline{a}}_{n},\tilde{\underline{b}}_{n})
denote the coordinates of the local minima and maxima that were present before the insertion of the soliton, and whose position may have changed due to the new addition.

Figure 14
:
We attached the new smallest blue soliton; the dark part of the excursion is the slot space where it is possible to attach the soliton, the red part where it is not possible.

Suppose we insert the
(n+1)
-th soliton following the first
k
maxima, and let us assume that it is attached in the left part of a soliton, so that the coordinate of the new local maximum
b^{\prime}
is smaller than the coordinate of the new local minimum
a^{\prime}
.
This corresponds to the case shown in Fig.
14
with
k=2
. The other case (
a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}
) can be handled in a similar way.

We have that
\tilde{a}_{i}=a_{i}
and
\tilde{b}_{i}=b_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
so that in particular these coordinates do not depend on the variables
(u_{n+1},\ell_{n+1})
. We have moreover
b^{\prime}=u_{n+1}+(2k+1)\ell_{n+1}
and
a^{\prime}=u_{n+1}+(2k+2)\ell_{n+1}

and finally
\tilde{b}_{i}=b_{i}+2\ell_{n+1}
and
\tilde{a}_{i}=a_{i}+2\ell_{n+1}
for
i=k+1,\dots,n
. Therefore the matrix for the change of variables form
(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{\ell}_{n},u_{n+1},\ell_{n+1})
to
(\tilde{\underline{a}}_{n},\tilde{\underline{b}}_{n},a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
is given as follows

\left(\begin{matrix}A_{11}&\dots&A_{1,2n-1}&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{k-1,1}&\dots&A_{k-1,2n-1}&0&0\\
A_{k,1}&\dots&A_{k,2n-1}&0&2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{n-1,1}&\dots&A_{n-1,2n-1}&0&2\\
A_{n,1}&\dots&A_{n,2n-1}&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{n+k-1,1}&\dots&A_{n+k-1,2n-1}&0&0\\
A_{n+k,1}&\dots&A_{n+k,2n-1}&0&2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{2n-1,1}&\dots&A_{2n-1,2n-1}&0&2\\
0&\dots&0&1&2k+2\\
0&\dots&0&1&2k+1\\
\end{matrix}\right)

If we develop the determinant of the above
(2n+1)\times(2n+1)
matrix along the second to last column we get that, up to a
\pm
sign in front, its determinant is equal to

(2k+2)|A|-(2k+1)|A|=|A|=1\,,

where the last identity follows by the inductive hypothesis.

Consider
q\in\mathscr{Q}
. By Lemma
14
,
\nu_{q}
given in (
3.1.2
) is a probability measure, and relation (
3.29
) and the fact that the Jacobian transformation has determinant
1
imply that
\mu_{\alpha}
in (
3.3
) is a probability measure as well, so that
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Conversely, consider
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
so that
\mu_{\alpha}
is a probability measure, and let
q
be computed by the solution of the Cauchy problem (
3.31
). Once again, it follows from the fact that the determinant of the Jacobian transformation is
1
that
\nu_{q}
is a probability measure. By Remark
13
the maximum value of the excursion has law given by (
3.13
), and hence
q\in\mathscr{Q}
.

The bijection between the sets
\mathcal{A}^{+}
and
\mathscr{Q}^{+}
is similarly obtained. By Corollary
16
the average length of the leftmost excursion
\mathsf{M}
is finite if and only if

q\in\mathscr{Q}^{+}
. On the other hand, from (
3.4
), the excursion with law
\mu_{\alpha}
has finite length only when
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
, so we conclude that
q\in\mathscr{Q}^{+}
if and only if
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

Finally, in view of expressions (
3.3
) and (
3.1.2
) of the measures
\mu_{\alpha}
and
\nu_{q}
, respectively, and relation (
3.29
) that defines
\alpha
in terms of
q
, the normalizing constants
Z(\alpha)
and
\int_{0}^{+\infty}q(y)dy
must be equal. ∎

3.2.1
Poisson intensity for the telegraph process

While it is simple to compute
\alpha
from
q
by formula (
3.29
), there is not a general form of the solution to equation (
3.31
).
We consider here a completely solvable case, the telegraph process of Example
12
.

The weight function
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
is given in (
3.9
),

\alpha(\ell)=\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}e^{-(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\ell}.

(3.32)

Let
A(y)\vcentcolon=\mathcal{Q}(y)-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})y
, where
\mathcal{Q}
is the solution to the Cauchy problem (
3.31
). In terms of
A
, this problem becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}A^{\prime\prime}(y)=\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}e^{2A(y)}\,,\\
A(0)=0\,,\\
A^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-}).\end{array}\right.

(3.33)

This is a one dimensional Newton law and in particular there is a conserved quantity given by the energy

E(A,A^{\prime})\vcentcolon=(A^{\prime})^{2}-\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}e^{2A}\,.

(3.34)

The constant value of the energy is given by

E=E(A(0),A^{\prime}(0))=\frac{1}{4}(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}\geq 0\,.

(3.35)

We consider first the case
E>0
that corresponds to
\lambda_{-}<\lambda_{+}
.

By (
3.34
) we have that

A^{\prime}=\pm\sqrt{E+\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}e^{2A}}\,,

(3.36)

and the correct sign is the negative one to match the initial value (
3.33
). Note that
A^{\prime}<0
at all times. Indeed, if it were to vanish, the conserved energy (
3.34
) would become negative. We have reduced the problem to a first order equation that can be solved by separation of variables:

\int\frac{dA}{\sqrt{E+\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}e^{2A}}}=-\int dy\,.

(3.37)

The primitive on the left-hand side above is given by

-\frac{1}{\sqrt{E}}\coth^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}}{E}e^{2A}}\,\Big),

(3.38)

where
\coth(x)
denotes the hyperbolic cotangent and
\coth^{-1}
its inverse function,

\coth^{-1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{x+1}{x-1}
,
|x|>1
. Then (
3.37
) yields

\coth^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}}{E}e^{2A}}\,\Big)=\sqrt{E}(y+K)\,,

(3.39)

K
is a constant to be determined and from
A(0)=0
. We obtain

K=\frac{1}{\sqrt{E}}\coth^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}}{E}}\,\Big)\,

(3.40)

and

\displaystyle A(y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\Big\{\frac{E}{\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}}\Big[\sinh\Big(\sqrt{E}(y+K)\Big)\Big]^{-2}\Big\}.

(3.41)

Since
q(y)=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(y)=A^{\prime\prime}(y)=\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}e^{2A(y)}
we finally get

q(y)=\frac{E}{\Big[\sinh\Big(\sqrt{E}(y+K)\Big)\Big]^{2}}\,.

(3.42)

As can be easily verified
q\in\mathscr{Q}^{+}
, which we already knew since for
\lambda_{+}>\lambda_{-}
the excursions of the walk have finite average length. Proposition
17
implies that
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

We consider now the case
E=0
that corresponds to equal rates
\lambda_{-}=\lambda_{+}
.
Let
\lambda\vcentcolon=\lambda_{-}=\lambda_{+}>0
. Then (
3.36
) reduces to
A^{\prime}=-\lambda e^{A}
that can be easily integrated. Using the values of the initial conditions in (
3.33
), we obtain

A(y)=\log\frac{1}{\lambda y+1}\quad\text{and}\quad q(y)=A^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda y+1)^{2}}\,.

(3.43)

Clearly in this case
q\notin\mathscr{Q}^{+}
, a fact we already knew, as the telegraph walk with equal rates is symmetric, and the mean length of the excursions is infinite.

3.3
Random walks

In previous sections we introduced two families of distributions on the space of excursions and proved that they are equivalent, Proposition
17
. We now present the extension of these measures to
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
, the space of walks that have a record at the origin (see (
2.8
)), and discuss how these extensions are related.

We consider a walk
\xi\in\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
whose excursions are iid with law
\mu_{\alpha}
as in (
3.3
),
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. The interval between two consecutive excursions is made up of records, that is, times at which
\xi
achieves its value for the fist time, see (
2.6
). The lengths of these record intervals are iid exponential random variables with parameter
Z(\alpha)
, the partition function in (
3.1
).

Recall the notation introduced in Section
2.5
.

Theorem 18
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
q\in\mathscr{Q}
satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha(\ell)=q(\ell)\,e^{-2\int_{0}^{\ell}(\ell-y)q(y)dy},

(3.44)

as in (
3.29
).
Then,

(i) If
\xi\in\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
has excursions and record interval lengths
(\varepsilon_{k},{\mathsf{u}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, satisfying

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\text{ are iid with law $\mu_{\alpha}$}

(3.45)

\displaystyle({\mathsf{u}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\text{ are iid Exponential$(Z(\alpha))$}

(3.46)

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }({\mathsf{u}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\text{ are independent.}

(3.47)

Then the slot decomposition
\mathsf{N}[\xi]
is a Poisson
(q)
process.

(ii) If
\mathsf{N}
is a Poisson
(q)
process, then
\xi[\mathsf{N}]\in\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
and the excursions and record intervals of
\xi[\mathsf{N}]
satisfy (
3.45
), (
3.46
), and (
3.47
).

Proof.

We first show (ii). Recall
\mathsf{N}_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a Poisson process with intensity measure
dx\,q(\ell)d\ell
. In Proposition
17
we have shown that, under (
3.44
), the leftmost excursion
\varepsilon_{0}[\mathsf{N}_{+}]
has distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
and that the law of
{\mathsf{u}}_{0}[\mathsf{N}_{+}]
, the gap between the origin and the leftmost excursion, has law exponential
(Z(\alpha))
. To do that, we have explored the regions
{\mathsf{U}}
and
\mathsf{G}
, whose right boundary
\mathsf{g}
is a “hitting boundary”, in the sense that it has been computed as a function of
\mathsf{N}_{0}\cap({\mathsf{U}}\cup\mathsf{G})
. This implies that, given
\mathsf{M}_{0}=\mathsf{N}_{0}\cap({\mathsf{U}}\cup\mathsf{G})
, the process
\mathsf{N}_{0}\setminus({\mathsf{U}}\cup\mathsf{G})
is a Poisson
(q)
process restricted to
(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\setminus({\mathsf{U}}\cup\mathsf{G})
. The intensity measure of
\mathsf{N}_{+}
is homogeneous in the horizontal coordinates
x
. Then
\mathsf{N}_{1}
, obtained in (
2.52
) by rigidly translating a set of points to the left, is independent of
({\mathsf{u}}_{0},\mathsf{M}_{0})
. This in turn implies that (a) the gap
{\mathsf{u}}_{1}
between excursions
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{1}
has the same law as
{\mathsf{u}}_{0}
, (b) the excursion
\varepsilon_{1}
has the same law as
\varepsilon_{0}
and (c) the pair
({\mathsf{u}}_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
is independent of the pair
({\mathsf{u}}_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
. By iterating this argument we conclude that
({\mathsf{u}}_{k},\varepsilon_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are iid with the same law as
({\mathsf{u}}_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
. Since the record intervals between excursions and the excursions
({\mathsf{u}}_{k},\varepsilon_{k})_{k<0}
have been obtained by reflection over the vertical axis, and
\mathsf{N}_{-}
and
\mathsf{N}_{+}
are independent, we get that
({\mathsf{u}}_{k},\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
are iid with the same law as
({\mathsf{u}}_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
. The variables
({\mathsf{u}}_{k})_{k}
are iid with positive mean and
\sum_{k}{\mathsf{u}}_{k}=+\infty
almost surely, so
\mathsf{N}\in\mathcal{N}
(see Remark
8
). By construction (
2.56
), the carrier process satisfies
\zeta[\mathsf{N}](0)=0
, because
0\in[{\mathsf{a}}_{-1},{\mathsf{a}}_{0}]
, and then
0
is a record for
\xi
and
\xi\in\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
.

Since the map
\mathsf{N}\mapsto({\mathsf{u}}_{k}[\mathsf{N}],\varepsilon_{k}[\mathsf{N}])_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a bijection, item (i) follows from (ii).
∎
