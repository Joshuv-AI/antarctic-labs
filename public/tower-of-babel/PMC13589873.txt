SF1 regulates the transcription of FDX1 to promote cuproptosis in pediatric sepsis-related acute lung injury by interacting with cJUN
Authors: Yanrong Wang, Junhua Sun, Lifen Zhang, Shenghu Zhou, Fang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric sepsis, a major cause of acute lung injury (ALI), involves a dysregulated host response. Cuproptosis is a novel copper-dependent cell death process regulated by Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1). However, its role in pediatric sepsis-associated ALI is unknown. This study investigated the upstream regulation mechanism of FDX1, and its role in pediatric sepsis-related ALI.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected from septic children and healthy controls. All enrolled pediatric subjects were divided into three groups: healthy control group (N = 40), sepsis without ALI group (N = 30), and sepsis with ALI group (N = 30). The clinical significance of FDX1, steroidogenic factor 1 (SF1), and Jun proto-oncogene (cJUN) was assessed by qRT-PCR. A septic ALI cell model was established using LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells. Functional assays (CCK-8, TUNEL, ELISA) assessed viability, apoptosis, and inflammation. An in vivo cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model was employed to confirm the role of FDX1. Molecular mechanisms (transcriptional regulation, protein interaction) were examined via ChIP, dual-luciferase, and Co-IP.ResultsFDX1, SF1, and cJUN levels were significantly elevated in pediatric sepsis patients, especially in those complicated with ALI, and the three genes were positively correlated with each other. In vitro, LPS induced BEAS-2B cell injury, inflammation, and cuproptosis. FDX1 overexpression exacerbated these effects, while its knockdown exerted protective effects. Mechanistically, SF1 directly bound to the FDX1 promoter to activate its transcription. SF1 physically interacted with cJUN, and they synergistically enhanced FDX1 transcription. Knockdown of cJUN abolished SF1-mediated FDX1 upregulation and its pro-cuproptotic effects. In vivo experiments demonstrated that FDX1 knockdown attenuated CLP-induced lung injury, inflammation, and cuproptosis.ConclusionsSF1, cJUN, and FDX1 may be closely involved in pediatric sepsis-related ALI. The physical interaction between SF1 and cJUN potentially modulates FDX1 transcription, which may contribute to cuproptosis and pediatric sepsis-related ALI progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00915-z.

Introduction

Sepsis is a complex disease in which the host’s dysfunctional response to infection leads to acute organ dysfunction [1]. Pediatric sepsis is one of the leading causes of death for children worldwide, which induces an estimated 3.3 million deaths every year [2, 3]. The clinical manifestation of pediatric sepsis is not specific, if not diagnosed and treated in time, it can lead to septic shock and seriously threaten human health [4]. Acute lung injury (ALI) is one of the most common and serious complications in the development of pediatric sepsis [5]. ALI is clinically manifested as progressive and worsening hypoxemia, which can further develop into acute respiratory distress syndrome, which causes irreversible damage to lung tissue [6]. Due to the proposal and use of new antibiotics, the comprehensive treatment plan for sepsis has been further developed, and the case fatality rate has been declining [7]. However, multi-drug-resistant bacterial infections, coagulation disorders and other problems have also made treatment more difficult. Therefore, exploring the pathogenesis of pediatric sepsis has important guiding significance for clinical treatment strategies.

Cuproptosis is a newly reported type of cell death caused by excessive copper [8]. Excess copper induces cuproptosis through copper-dependent abnormal oligomerization of fatty acylated proteins in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), promoting protein toxic stress and reducing iron-sulfur tuftin levels [9]. According to earlier studies, cuproptosis is crucial for the cardiovascular system [10, 11]. Cuproptosis promotes mitochondrial function damage, thus accelerating the process of atherosclerosis [12]. Furthermore, cuproptosis has been shown to promote inflammatory responses [13]. For instance, Zhao et al. reported that cuproptosis contributes to rheumatoid arthritis by inducing protein toxic stress and inflammatory responses [14]. However, the specific regulatory mechanism of cuproptosis in sepsis-related ALI remains to be further studied.

The upstream regulator ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) plays a crucial role in the cuproptosis process [15, 16]. FDX1 mediates the aggregation of mitochondrial lipoyl proteins, such as dihydrolipoic acid transacetylase (DLAT), lipoyl dihydrolipoic acid succinyltransferase (DLST), and the instability of iron-sulfur tufting proteins, as lipoic acid synthase (LIAS), leading to changes in cell metabolic patterns and cuproptosis [10]. Copper ion carriers bind Cu2+ extracellular and transport it to the cytoplasm. Copper ion accumulation causes cuproptosis mainly through mitochondrial protein toxic stress mediated by FDX1 [17]. At present, multiple pan-cancer bioinformatics analyses based on public databases such as TCGA, GTEx and GEO suggest that the levels of FDX1 are altered in most cancer types and affect the prognosis of some cancer patients, such as kidney cancer, glioma, and lung cancer [18–21]. There are few studies on FDX1 in the field of inflammation [22, 23]. Yuan et al. found that FDX1 may promote the progression of Crohn’s disease by inducing immune response and metabolic disorders [23]. The study of Wang et al. showed that FDX1-mediated cuproptosis was associated osteoarthritis development [24]. However, whether FDX1 is involved in pediatric sepsis-related ALI remains unknown.

The study aimed to investigate the role of cuproptosis in pediatric sepsis-related ALI and its mechanism. In the present study, our clinical and experimental data suggest that SF1, cJUN, and FDX1 may participate in the progression of pediatric sepsis-related ALI. We speculate that the physical interaction between SF1 and cJUN may synergistically modulate FDX1 transcription, which is potentially linked to cuproptosis and lung injury in pediatric sepsis. These findings reveal the transcriptional regulation mechanism of FDX1, which is an important protein in cuproptosis, and provide the basis for the development of therapeutic strategies targeting the expression of specific genes in pediatric sepsis.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

Peripheral blood samples were collected from pediatric patients diagnosed with sepsis and healthy children at Ningxia Medical University General Hospital from May 2022 to August 2025. Pediatric sepsis was defined according to the Phoenix Sepsis Score [2]. Patients with a Phoenix Sepsis Score ≥ 2 points with suspected infection were included in the sepsis group. From this sepsis cohort, we strictly divided into two sepsis subgroups based on the presence or absence of ALI: sepsis without ALI group (N = 30) and sepsis with ALI group (N = 30) as defined by the Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC) guidelines [25]. The diagnosis of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS) required: acute onset of respiratory failure within 7 days of a known clinical insult; bilateral chest imaging abnormalities consistent with pulmonary edema; and impaired oxygenation not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. Oxygenation impairment was stratified based on oxygenation index (OI, PaO2/FiO2) or oxygen saturation index (OSI, SpO2/FiO2) under invasive or non-invasive ventilatory support. Age- and gender-matched healthy children who underwent physical examination during the same period were enrolled as the healthy control group (N = 40). Children with chronic inflammatory diseases, autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors, congenital genetic diseases, or other organ dysfunction were excluded from this study. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, PELOD-2 scores, comorbidities, and treatment details, were systematically recorded for all enrolled participants (supplemental Table 1).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from all collected blood samples using standard Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation. Subsequently, the isolated PBMCs were used for RNA and protein extraction. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ningxia Medical University General Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all enrolled pediatric participants.

Cell culture

The human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B was purchased from ATCC (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. BEAS-2B cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h to establish a sepsis-induced ALI cell model referring to a previous study [26].

Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM, MedChemExpress, Princeton, New Jersey, USA), a specific copper chelator and cuproptosis inhibitor, was used to verify the specificity of cuproptosis in LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury. BEAS-2B cells were pretreated with 10 µM TTM for 12 h before LPS (1 µg/mL) stimulation for 12 h.

Cell transfection

To overexpress FDX1 and SF1, the cDNA sequences of human FDX1 and SF1 were amplified and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. To knock down FDX1 and cJUN expression, small interfering RNA (siRNA) against FDX1 (si-FDX1), siRNA against SF1 (si-SF1) and siRNA against cJUN (si-cJUN) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The sequences of siRNAs are listed in Supplemental Table 2. BEAS-2B cells were transfected with corresponding plasmids or siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Ma, USA).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

BEAS-2B cell viability was determined using CCK-8 Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). BEAS-2B cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells/well for 48 h. Then, BEAS-2B cells were incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 reagent for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)

BEAS-2B cell apoptosis was measured using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (MedChemExpress, Princeton, New Jersey, USA). BEAS-2B cells were inoculated in 6-well plates and treated accordingly. BEAS-2B cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min. Then, the cells were washed, and incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 min. Following the washing with PBS, cells were incubated with 10 µL TUNEL solution (MedChemExpress, Princeton, New Jersey, USA) at 37℃ for 1 h away from light. Finally, the cells were sealed with the antifade mounting medium with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The inflammatory cytokines of interleukin-6 (IL-6), monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in cell culture supernatant and mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were detected by ELISA Kits (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Copper assay

Intracellular copper level in cells was detected using the Copper Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). For cell sample preparation, BEAS-2B cells were homogenized after adding 1 mL distilled water. Then, 100 µL samples were added to 35 µL Reagent A. For standard preparation, 20 µL Copper Standard (1.5 mg/dL Cu2+) was diluted to 300 µg/dL Cu2+ and added to 35 µL Reagent A. Then, 100 µL samples and standards were incubated with 150 µL Working Reagent (Reagent B: Reagent C = 1:3) at room temperature for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 359 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Intracellular copper level in serum were measured using the Copper Assay Kit (Elabscience, China). Following the protocol, 20 µL of serum was combined with 300 µL of detection reagent in a 96-well plate for 5 min. The optical density was determined at 580 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Bioinformatic analysis

The binding sites of transcription factors SF1 (NR5A1) and cJUN on the FDX1 gene promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/). The upstream promoter sequence of the FDX1 gene was retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database. A 2000-bp region spanning 1500 bp upstream to 500 bp downstream of the transcription start site was selected as the candidate sequence to cover core regulatory regions. The relative profile score threshold was set to 90% to ensure high prediction confidence.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assay was performed using the ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Ma, USA). BEAS-2B cells were incubated with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and added with glycine for 5 min. The cells were then lyzed by 200 µL Membrane Extraction Buffer and incubated with MNase at 37 ℃ for 5 min. 10 µL ultrasound-treated sample supernatant was used as Input for subsequent testing. 90 µL sample was immunoprecipitated using 1 µg antibody, and IgG antibody as a negative control. The samples were added ChIP Grade Protein A/G Magnetic Beads and incubated at 4℃ for 1 h. DNA crosslinking solution was purified with sodium chloride and protease K. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed according to reaction conditions to verify the binding of FDX1 promoter to SF1 and cJUN.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Human genome FDX1 wild-type and mutant promoter fragments were cloned into the pGL3-Basic-Luciferase vector. The pGL3 vectors of FDX1, and pcDNA3.1 vector of SF1 and cJUN were co-transfected to cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Ma, USA). 48 h after transfection, luciferase assays were performed using the dual luciferase Reporting System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

BEAS-2B cells were cleaved using 750 µL precooled RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and lysed on ice for 10 min. 50 µL supernatant was collected to detect the total protein of input. 700 µL supernatant was added with protein A/G magnetic beads and incubated with the antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Then, the antibody-protein mixture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm/min for 5 min to collect the precipitation. The protein samples were added with 5 × protein loading buffer and heated at 100℃ for 5 min. Western blot assay was performed with the indicated antibodies against SF1 (Cat. 18658-1-AP, 1:1000 dilution, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and cJUN (Cat. ab40766, 1:1000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA).

Animal studies

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade C57BL/6 mice (male, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22–25 °C) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University General Hospital.

Sepsis was induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) as previously described [27]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the cecum, which was ligated with 4 − 0 silk suture at 1 cm from the cecal tip. The ligated cecum was punctured twice with a 22-gauge needle, and a small amount of fecal contents was extruded to ensure patency. The cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was closed in two layers. Mice in the Sham group underwent the same surgical procedure without cecal ligation and puncture. 24 mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): Sham, CLP, CLP + LV-sh-NC, and CLP + LV-sh-FDX1. One week prior to CLP surgery, mice were administered lentiviruses (5 × 107 pfu) via tail vein injection. The lentiviruses (pLKO.1/puro) containing short hairpin RNA against FDX1 (LV-sh-FDX1) and the negative control (LV-sh-NC), were packaged by Clontech (Clontech, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Lung tissues and BALF were collected 24 h after CLP for further analysis.

Lung wet/dry weight ratio

The wet weight of the freshly harvested left lung was measured immediately after collection. The lung was then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h to obtain the dry weight. The lung wet/dry (W/D) weight ratio was calculated to assess pulmonary edema.

Histopathological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Histopathological changes were evaluated by a pathologist blinded to the experimental groups.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Then, the RNA samples were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScipt III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The amplification reaction was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). GAPDH was used as endogenous control. The relative expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Then, protein samples were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4˚C overnight and secondary antibodies for 2 h. The used antibodies were listed as follows: anti-FDX1 (Cat. 12592-1-AP, 1:2000 dilution, ProteinTech, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-LIAS (Cat. ab246917, 1:1000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-DLST (Cat. ab187699, 1:1000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-DLAT (Cat. 13426-1-AP, 1:2000 dilution, ProteinTech, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-GLS (Cat. ab317032, 1:1000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-GAPDH (Cat. 10494-1-AP, 1:5000 dilution, ProteinTech, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-Rabbit IgG (Cat. ab288151, 1:5000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Subsequently, the enhanced chemiluminescence mixture (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Ma, USA) was used to visualize the membranes. The gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used to capture images of the blots.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between two groups were determined using unpaired Student’s t-test. Significant differences among multiple groups were determined using One-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test. Correlations between variables were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. For Pearson correlation analysis, the normality of the data distribution (FDX1, SF1, cJUN expression levels) was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All variables met the assumption of normality (P > 0.05), validating the use of Pearson correlation. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05 groups were considered statistically significant.

The sample size was determined based on a preliminary power analysis. Assuming an expected difference in FDX1 mRNA expression of 1.5-fold between groups with a standard deviation of 0.8, a power of 0.8, and an alpha of 0.05, the calculated minimum sample size was approximately 34 per group. Our final sample sizes (60 septic children and 40 controls) exceeded this requirement to enhance robustness and account for potential subgroup analyses.

Results

Clinical significance of FDX1, SF1, and cJUN in pediatric sepsis

qRT-PCR and western blot were performed to detect the mRNA and protein expression levels of SF1, cJUN, and FDX1 in peripheral blood PBMCs from healthy children and pediatric sepsis patients. All subjects were divided into three groups: healthy control group (N = 40), sepsis without ALI group (N = 30), and sepsis with ALI group (N = 30). The results showed that compared with the healthy control group, the expression levels of SF1, cJUN, and FDX1 were significantly upregulated in pediatric sepsis patients (Fig. 1A and B). Moreover, the expression levels of the three genes were further increased in patients complicated with ALI, showing a gradually increasing trend with the aggravation of sepsis severity (Fig. 1A and B). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between FDX1 and SF1 (Fig. 1C). Besides, FDX1 mRNA levels were positively correlated with cJUN (Fig. 1C). Also, there was a positive association between cJUN and SF1 (Fig. 1C). These clinical expression results preliminarily indicate that the abnormal activation of SF1, cJUN, and FDX1 is closely correlated with the occurrence and severity of pediatric sepsis-associated ALI.

LPS induces bronchial epithelial cell injury and activates cuproptosis

To investigate whether cuproptosis mediates sepsis-related ALI, human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h to establish an in vitro cell model of sepsis-associated ALI. In CCK-8 assay, the viability was reduced in BEAS-2B cells with LPS treatment (Fig. 2A). TUNEL assay showed that LPS treatment significantly increased the BEAS-2B cell apoptosis as compared to the control group (Fig. 2B). ELISA illustrated that LPS induced secretion levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, MCP-1 and TNF-α in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 2C). Importantly, the intracellular copper content in the LPS group was higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2D). At the same time, western blot showed LPS treatment dramatically upregulated cuproptosis-related proteins (FDX1, LIAS, DLST, DLAT, GLS) levels in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that cuproptosis is activated in ALI induced by sepsis.

Pharmacological inhibition of cuproptosis abolishes LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and inflammatory activation

To further verify that LPS-induced BEAS-2B cell injury and inflammatory activation were specifically dependent on cuproptosis, we performed pharmacological rescue experiments using TTM, a specific copper chelator and canonical cuproptosis inhibitor. BEAS-2B cells were divided into four groups: Control group, LPS group, LPS + DMSO group, and LPS + TTM group. CCK-8 and TUNEL assays demonstrated that TTM intervention significantly reversed the LPS-mediated decline in cell viability and robust elevation of apoptotic rate in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 3A and B). ELISA showed that the excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, MCP-1 and TNF-α triggered by LPS was markedly diminished after TTM pretreatment (Fig. 3C). Intracellular copper quantification revealed that LPS-induced copper overload was significantly eliminated by TTM intervention (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, western blot analysis validated that the upregulation of core cuproptosis-related proteins (FDX1, LIAS, DLST, DLAT, GLS) induced by LPS was effectively blocked after TTM pretreatment (Fig. 3E). Collectively, cuproptosis serves as the core mechanism mediating LPS-induced cellular viability impairment, apoptotic injury, and excessive inflammatory response.

FDX1 promotes LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and activates cuproptosis

To determine FDX1 function in sepsis-related ALI in vitro, we transfected BEAS-2B cells with FDX1 overexpression and knockdown vectors for 48 h prior to culturing with LPS. The mRNA and protein expression levels of FDX1 in LPS treatment group were higher than that in control group (Fig. 4A and B). FDX1 overexpression increased FDX1 levels compared to negative control overexpression, and FDX1 knockdown decreased FDX1 levels compared to negative control knockdown in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 4A and B). The overexpression of FDX1 dramatically repressed cell viability, and the knockdown of FDX1 increased cell viability in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, the apoptosis rates of LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells were elevated by FDX1 overexpression, and decreased by FDX1 knockdown (Fig. 4D). ELISA showed that the overexpression of FDX1 notably promoted secretion levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, MCP-1 and TNF-α, and the knockdown of FDX1 suppressed inflammatory cytokine levels in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the results revealed that LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells transfected with FDX1 overexpression had markedly upregulated content of intracellular copper, while transfected with FDX1 knockdown suppressed intracellular copper content (Fig. 4F). In western blot assay, the levels of cuproptosis-related proteins (LIAS, DLST, DLAT, GLS) increased following the transfection with FDX1 overexpression, while the expression levels were downregulated following the silencing of FDX1 in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that FDX1 participates in cuproptosis and ALI in sepsis.

SF1 regulates FDX1 transcription by promoting the activity of its promoter

To clarify the mechanism through which FDX1 is increased during sepsis, we focused on the transcriptional regulation and promoter regions. SF1, also known as NR5A1, is a nuclear receptor, which can bind to the specific binding site on the gene promoter and activate the transcription of gene [28]. qRT-PCR showed that the mRNA levels of SF1 in LPS treatment group were higher than those in control group in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 5A). Considering that FDX1 is positively correlated with SF1 in pediatric sepsis, whether SF1 is involved in the regulation of FDX1 expression is still uncertain. Therefore, we further explored the regulatory relationship between SF1 and FDX1. BEAS-2B cells were transfected with SF1 overexpression vector or SF1-specific siRNA for 48 h, and the transfection efficiency of SF1 was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot (Fig. 5B and C). Meanwhile, FDX1 levels were significantly increased after overexpression of SF1 in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 5D and E). Besides, si-SF1 transfection effectively and significantly downregulated the endogenous SF1 expression level in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 5D and E). The combination of gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments fully verified that SF1 positively modulates FDX1 expression at the transcriptional level.

Subsequently, we explored the potential molecular mechanism. JASPAR database was used to predict the binding sites associated with the promoter sequence of FDX1 and transcription factor SF1, and the potential binding sites were found (Fig. 5F). ChIP confirmed that the relative enrichment levels of FDX1 in SF1 antibody group were significantly higher than those in IgG antibody group (Fig. 5G). The overexpression of SF1 significantly increased the luciferase activity of wild type FDX1 promoter, and did not affect mutant FDX1 promoter (Fig. 5H). These data indicate that the transcription factor SF1 binds and activates the binding site of the promoter region of FDX1, and has a certain positive regulatory effect on FDX1.

SF1 promotes LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and activates cuproptosis via regulating FDX1 transcription

To further verify the influence of SF1 and FDX1 on sepsis-related ALI in vitro, we transfected SF1 overexpression vector or FDX1 knockdown vector in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells, and knocked down FDX1 in SF1-overexpressed BEAS-2B cells for 48 h. qRT-PCR and western blot assays showed that the levels of FDX1 were highly expressed in the LPS+pcDNA3.1-SF1 group compared to the LPS+pcDNA3.1-NC + si-NC group (Fig. 6A and B). Compared to the LPS+pcDNA3.1-SF1 group, the LPS+ pcDNA3.1-SF1 + si-FDX1 group repressed FDX1 levels (Fig. 6A and B). SF1 overexpression observably suppressed BEAS-2B cell viability, which was reversed by FDX1 knockdown (Fig. 6C). The repression of FDX1 abolished the promoting effects of SF1 overexpression on BEAS-2B cell apoptosis (Fig. 6D). Similarly, the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, MCP-1 and TNF-α were markedly increased following the transfection with SF1 overexpression, and partially decreased by the repression of FDX1 in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 6E). The SF1 overexpression-induced upregulated intracellular copper content was abrogated following FDX1 knockdown in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 6F). Consistent with these findings, SF1 overexpression significantly increased cuproptosis-related proteins (LIAS, DLST, DLAT, GLS), and FDX1 knockdown abolished the effects of SF1 overexpression on BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 6G). The transcription factor SF1 upregulates FDX1 levels by influencing the transcriptional regulation of FDX1, thus affecting cuproptosis and ALI in sepsis.

SF1 physically interacts with cJUN and functionally cooperates to activate FDX1 transcription

Studies have shown that SF1 interacts with cJUN to regulate the transcription and expression of related proteins [29], thus we wonder that whether SF1 interacts with cJUN to activate FDX1 transcription. JASPAR database prediction confirmed the presence of independent binding sites for cJUN on the FDX1 core promoter region (Fig. 7A). CHIP assay showed that compared with IgG, cJUN was bound to the promoter of FDX1 when the antibody against cJUN was utilized for immunoprecipitation (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, endogenous Co-IP assay confirmed the physical interaction between SF1 and cJUN in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 7C). Interestingly, overexpression of SF1 and cJUN alone significantly increased the luciferase activity of wild-type FDX1, and overexpression of SF1 and cJUN together had higher luciferase activity (Fig. 7D). The downregulation of cJUN significantly inhibited the ability of SF1 to promote FDX1 expression (Fig. 7E). Taken together, these data suggest that SF1 may physically interact with cJUN, and the two transcription factors may functionally cooperate to modulate FDX1 transcription in bronchial epithelial cells.

SF1 mediates cuproptosis in bronchial epithelial cells by regulating FDX1 via forming a complex with cJUN

To further clarify the core mechanism by which SF1 exacerbates LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and cuproptosis, we focused on the synergistic regulatory function of the SF1-cJUN protein complex on FDX1. A sepsis-related ALI model was constructed by inducing BEAS-2B cells with LPS, and 6 experimental groups were set up: LPS+pcDNA3.1-NC + si-NC group (negative control), LPS+pcDNA3.1-SF1 group (SF1 overexpression), LPS + si-cJUN group (cJUN knockdown), LPS+pcDNA3.1-FDX1 group (FDX1 overexpression), LPS+pcDNA3.1-SF1 + si-cJUN group (SF1 overexpression + cJUN knockdown), and LPS+pcDNA3.1-SF1 + si-cJUN+pcDNA3.1-FDX1 group (SF1 overexpression + cJUN knockdown + FDX1 overexpression). As demonstrated in Fig. 8A and B, SF1 overexpression significantly upregulated FDX1 expression, while cJUN knockdown suppressed endogenous FDX1 levels. Notably, the SF1-mediated upregulation of FDX1 was almost completely abrogated after cJUN silencing, indicating that the physical interaction between SF1 and cJUN is essential for SF1 to activate FDX1 transcription. These results demonstrate that SF1 relies on forming a cooperative protein complex with cJUN to drive FDX1 expression.

Knockdown of FDX1 attenuates sepsis-induced lung injury and cuproptosis in vivo

To validate the regulatory role of FDX1 in sepsis-related lung injury and cuproptosis at the in vivo level, we employed a CLP model in vivo. Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6 per group): Sham group (sham operation without CLP), CLP group (CLP-induced sepsis), CLP + LV-sh-NC group (CLP + injection of negative control lentivirus), and CLP + LV-sh-FDX1 group (CLP + injection of FDX1 knockdown lentivirus). The lung wet/dry (W/D) weight ratio is a classic indicator for assessing lung edema, which reflects the degree of water accumulation in lung tissue. The W/D ratio was significantly increased in the CLP group compared to the Sham group, indicating profound pulmonary edema (Fig. 9A). Notably, this increase was markedly ameliorated in the CLP + LV-sh-FDX1 group, demonstrating that knockdown of FDX1 alleviated sepsis-induced lung edema (Fig. 9A). Histopathological examination of lung tissues via H&E staining revealed intact alveolar structures in the Sham group (Fig. 9B). In contrast, the CLP and CLP + LV-sh-NC groups exhibited characteristic pathological injuries, including alveolar wall thickening, massive inflammatory cell infiltration, and severe alveolar structural destruction, which were reflected in high pathological scores (Fig. 9B). These histopathological changes and scores were significantly attenuated in the CLP + LV-sh-FDX1 group, confirming the protective effect of FDX1 knockdown against sepsis-induced lung injury (Fig. 9B). ELISA was used to detect the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1) in BALF to evaluate the effect of FDX1 knockdown on lung inflammatory response. ELISA showed that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1 were drastically elevated in the CLP group (Fig. 9C). However, FDX1 knockdown significantly suppressed the release of these cytokines, indicating a reduction in the local inflammatory response (Fig. 9C). A copper ion fluorescent probe was used to detect the copper ion concentration in serum. The copper concentration was significantly elevated following CLP, and this was effectively reduced by LV-sh-FDX1 treatment (Fig. 9D). Western blot analysis of lung tissue homogenates demonstrated that compared with the Sham group, the expression levels of FDX1, LIAS, DLST, DLAT, and GLS in the CLP group were significantly upregulated, indicating that CLP-induced sepsis activated the cuproptosis pathway in lung tissue (Fig. 9E). Importantly, the expression level of FDX1 in the CLP + LV-sh-FDX1 group was significantly downregulated compared with the CLP + LV-sh-NC group, confirming the effective knockdown of FDX1 by the lentivirus (Fig. 9E). Meanwhile, the expression levels of LIAS, DLST, DLAT, and GLS in the CLP + LV-sh-FDX1 group were also significantly lower than those in the CLP + LV-sh-NC group (Fig. 9E). These results demonstrate that FDX1 knockdown can inhibit the activation of the cuproptosis pathway in lung tissue induced by CLP, which may be the underlying mechanism by which FDX1 knockdown alleviates sepsis-related lung injury.

Discussion

There are multiple mechanisms of organ dysfunction caused by pediatric sepsis, involving inflammation, immune system, endothelial damage, coagulation dysfunction and other aspects [2]. At present, the theory of unbalanced inflammatory response is the cornerstone of the pathogenesis of organ dysfunction caused by sepsis. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that multiple programmed cell death modalities, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, participate in the progression of sepsis-induced organ injury [30–32]. As a newly discovered copper-dependent metabolic cell death, cuproptosis has been proven to be closely associated with inflammatory diseases and organ damage [13]. Liu et al. found that cuproptosis participated in aseptic inflammation, and its mechanism was closely related to the biological role of HMGB1 protein [33]. Besides, several studies have reported that cuproptosis-associated proteins might interfere with sepsis through immune cell infiltration [34–36]. Evidence showed that sepsis mediated cardiotoxicity by inducing cuproptosis in cardiomyocytes [37]. In the study, LPS was added to BEAS-2B cells to establish a sepsis-related ALI cell model. We found that LPS induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and inflammatory response, which was consistent with the findings of Ling et al. and Wang Y et al. [38, 39]. Furthermore, we found that LPS increased intracellular copper content and cuproptosis-associated protein expression. However, the role and mechanism of cuproptosis in pediatric sepsis-related ALI remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanism and regulatory factors of the key molecule FDX1 in cuproptosis. For the first time, we found that FDX1 was highly expressed in pediatric sepsis samples, and FDX1 promoted cuproptosis and ALI in sepsis. Mechanically, SF1 can individually bind to the FDX1 promoter and activate its transcription, and SF1 may cooperate with cJUN to modulate FDX1 transcriptional activity, suggesting that the three molecules are potentially involved in regulating bronchial epithelial cuproptosis and sepsis-related ALI.

Studies have shown that FDX1 is a key molecule in mitochondrial homeostasis and antioxidant, which is involved in regulating the lipoacylation of proteins and reduces Cu2+ to more toxic Cu+ [16, 17]. Copper can affect the binding of FDX1 to its downstream target molecules DLST, DLAT and LIAS, causing mitochondrial damage and cell death [40]. FDX1 is significantly correlated with tumor metabolic characteristics, mutation load, immune cell infiltration, and immunoassay site expression [41, 42]. Fang et al. found paeoniflorin reduced the concentration of Cu2+ in the serum of rats, repressed the expression of FDX1 and DLAT protein, inhibited oxidative stress response and cuproptosis in the acute myocardial infarction rat model [43]. In this study, we innovatively grouped clinical pediatric sepsis samples based on the presence or absence of ALI complications (healthy control, sepsis without ALI, sepsis with ALI), which is highly consistent with the core research theme of sepsis-related lung injury. Clinical results confirmed that the expression levels of SF1, cJUN and FDX1 were upregulated with the aggravation of septic lung injury, and the three genes were positively correlated with each other, suggesting that the SF1/cJUN/FDX1 axis is closely involved in the progression of pediatric sepsis-associated ALI. We then transfected FDX1 overexpression and knockdown vector in BEAS-2B cells for 48 h prior to culturing with LPS to determine FDX1 function in sepsis-related ALI in vitro. Further research revealed that FDX1 promoted LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and activated cuproptosis. Finally, our in vivo experiments using a CLP-induced sepsis model demonstrated that FDX1 knockdown attenuated sepsis-induced pulmonary edema, histopathological damage, lung inflammation, and suppressed the cuproptosis pathway. These findings provide compelling evidence for FDX1’s crucial role in sepsis-induced ALI and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target. Our results enrich the research on the role of FDX1 in sepsis-related ALI, indicating for the first time the potential therapeutic target of FDX1 in sepsis.

Notably, the present study further clarified the upstream transcriptional mechanism responsible for FDX1 upregulation in septic injury. Abnormal gene overexpression in inflammatory diseases is largely attributed to the dysregulation of transcriptional networks [44, 45]. Transcription factors interact with transcription factor binding sites in promoter/enhancer regions of target genes to achieve transcriptional regulation of target genes [46]. The nuclear receptor SF1, which belongs to the NR5A family, is a key regulator of steroidogenic cell development and many steroidogenic genes [47]. SF1 participates in many processes such as embryonic development, steroid synthesis, neuroendocrine and metabolism [47]. The activity of SF1 is influenced by a variety of factors, including ligands, co-activators, co-repressors, post-translational modifications, and interactions between transcription factors. By binding to the binding site of the target gene, SF1 regulates the transcription of many kinds of solid alcohol synthetases and related proteins, such as FDX1, FDXR, STAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1 [29, 48–50]. SF1 could recognize and bind nuclear receptor reaction elements on the FDX1 promoter, thereby facilitating RNA polymerase II binding and transcriptional initiation in differentiated human mesenchymal stem cells [48]. In the study, we found that SF1 levels in both sepsis without shock and sepsis shock samples were markedly increased, in particular, higher mRNA and protein levels of SF1 were observed in sepsis shock samples. Besides, SF1 levels were highly expressed in the sepsis-related ALI cell model. A positive clinical correlation between SF1 and FDX1 indicated a potential regulatory relationship between the two molecules. Accordingly, we further explored whether SF1 functions as an upstream transcriptional regulator of FDX1 in pediatric sepsis-associated ALI. We found that the overexpression of SF1 significantly increased FDX1 levels. In addition, we also proved that SF1 could bind to FDX1 promoters. These results indicated that the transcription factor SF1 promoted the expression of FDX1 by binding its promoter region. Many studies have found that SF1 plays a key role in regulating the regulation of inflammatory response [51]. One study reported that repression of SF-1 led to oocyte death and increased excessive ovarian inflammation [28]. SF1 was reported to inhibit the inflammatory response caused by a high-fat diet in obesity [51]. However, the role of SF-1 in sepsis remains unclear. In the study, we also explored the mechanism of SF1 and FDX1 affecting sepsis-related ALI. We found that SF1 promoted LPS-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury and activated cuproptosis via regulating FDX1 transcription.

cJUN is encoded by the JUN gene and is the core component of transcription factor activating protein-1 [52]. Previous studies have shown that cJUN is closely related to brain injury, tumorigenesis and inflammatory response [53, 54]. Evidence suggests that SF1 interacts with cJUN to regulate the transcription and expression of related proteins [55]. For instance, SF1 activated the promoter of the mouse Star gene through interaction with cJUN, thus promoting its transcription and expression [29]. In addition, the transcription of CYP11A1 was also regulated by the interaction between SF1 and cJUN [56]. In our study, cJUN expression was elevated in pediatric sepsis samples and positively correlated with both SF1 and FDX1. Co-IP assays confirmed the physical interaction between SF1 and cJUN in BEAS-2B cells. ChIP assays showed that each factor individually binds to the FDX1 promoter, and dual luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that co-overexpression of SF1 and cJUN synergistically activates FDX1 promoter activity. While direct evidence of simultaneous promoter co-occupancy (e.g., from ChIP-reChIP) is not yet available, the combination of physical interaction, individual promoter binding, and functional synergy strongly suggests that SF1 and cJUN cooperate to upregulate FDX1 transcription. Furthermore, cJUN knockdown attenuated SF1-induced FDX1 upregulation, as well as the pro-injury and pro-cuproptotic effects of SF1 overexpression. Based on our findings, we propose a dual positive-feedback regulatory mechanism. On the one hand, sepsis-induced inflammatory microenvironment activates SF1/cJUN transcriptional axis, which directly triggers continuous FDX1 transcription and expression. On the other hand, excessive FDX1 further promotes intracellular copper accumulation and mitochondrial metabolic disorder, which in turn maintains the high expression of cuproptosis-related genes, ultimately amplifying epithelial cell injury and inflammatory response. This self-amplifying loop is the key mechanism driving the continuous progression of pediatric sepsis-related ALI.

In addition, it is necessary to distinguish the unique characteristics of cuproptosis from other cell death modes in septic ALI. Different from caspase-dependent apoptosis and inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, cuproptosis is a metabolic cell death independent of caspase activation. Unlike ferroptosis driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, cuproptosis is specifically triggered by copper overload and relies on FDX1-mediated mitochondrial lipoprotein oligomerization and iron-sulfur protein depletion. In this study, the specific copper chelator TTM almost completely reversed LPS-induced cell damage, inflammation and cuproptosis activation, strongly proving that the phenotypic changes in septic bronchial epithelial injury are specifically dependent on cuproptosis, rather than other cell death programs.

Despite the novel findings of this study, several inevitable limitations should be acknowledged objectively. First, this study adopted peripheral blood PBMCs as surrogate samples for clinical correlation analysis. Clinically, it is ethically and operationally difficult to obtain invasive lung tissue specimens from critically ill pediatric sepsis patients. As a minimally invasive and clinically accessible specimen, PBMCs can sensitively reflect the systemic inflammatory stress and transcriptional disorder induced by sepsis. However, we fully recognize the inherent defect that PBMCs cannot fully represent the pathological characteristics of local lung epithelial tissue. The altered expression of FDX1, SF1, and cJUN in peripheral immune cells does not completely recapitulate the specific changes in bronchial epithelium, resulting in insufficient clinical evidence to fully support the mechanism of pediatric sepsis-associated ALI proposed in this study. Second, all in vitro mechanistic experiments were performed based on the single BEAS-2B bronchial epithelial cell line, which cannot simulate the multicellular interaction microenvironment of alveolar epithelial cells, vascular endothelial cells and immune cells in lung tissue, limiting the extrapolation and clinical applicability of our epithelial-derived mechanistic conclusions. Third, the verification of cuproptosis in this study is relatively inadequate. We confirmed cuproptosis activation through copper content detection and core hallmark protein expression, but lacked classical and specific evidence, including mitochondrial ultrastructural morphological changes and mitochondrial lipoylated protein aggregation detection, which fails to fully and rigorously confirm specific cuproptosis activation. Fourth, the transcriptional regulatory mechanism lacks precise molecular evidence. Although we verified the synergistic regulatory effect of the SF1/cJUN complex on FDX1 transcription, we did not identify the exact binding sites of SF1 and cJUN on the FDX1 promoter, and lacked co-occupancy experimental evidence to confirm the simultaneous binding of the two transcription factors to the FDX1 promoter region, weakening the rigor of the transcriptional synergistic mechanism. Fifth, the design of in vivo animal experiments has obvious limitations. This study adopted adult male mice to construct the CLP sepsis model with only systemic FDX1 knockdown. Adult mice cannot recapitulate the immature immune development and lung pathological characteristics of pediatric patients. Moreover, systemic gene knockdown cannot distinguish the cell-specific function of FDX1 in lung epithelial cells, immune cells and other stromal cells, failing to verify the lung epithelial-specific mechanism of the regulatory axis in septic lung injury. Sixth, there were non-standard problems in the original manuscript including partial statistical description omission, insufficient supplementary material details, non-uniform figure legend format, and individual language and formatting errors, which have been comprehensively revised and standardized in the revised version.

To overcome the above deficiencies, future studies will focus on the following aspects: collecting pediatric lung local samples to verify the clinical value of the SF1/cJUN-FDX1 axis; adopting primary pediatric epithelial cells and multicellular co-culture systems to verify cell-specific mechanisms; supplementing transmission electron microscopy and molecular detection of lipoylated proteins to enrich cuproptosis evidence; and constructing age-matched juvenile mouse models combined with lung-specific gene intervention to complete full in vivo pathway verification. In conclusion, this study reveals that the SF1/cJUN transcription complex synergistically drives FDX1-mediated cuproptosis, thereby promoting the progression of pediatric sepsis-associated ALI, which provides a novel potential therapeutic target for clinical intervention of pediatric sepsis.

Conclusion

In summary, this study has identified for the first time the vital molecules involved in the mechanism of cuproptosis and pediatric sepsis-related ALI. SF1, cJUN, and FDX1 are aberrantly expressed in pediatric sepsis and may be involved in sepsis-induced bronchial epithelial cuproptosis and ALI. SF1 physically interacts with cJUN to form a functional transcription complex, which synergistically upregulates FDX1 transcription, which potentially contributes to the progression of pediatric sepsis-associated ALI. This study provided a potential therapeutic target for clinical pediatric sepsis.
