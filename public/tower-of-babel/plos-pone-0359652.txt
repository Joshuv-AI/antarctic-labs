Revisiting the relation between oral contraceptive use and attentional engagement in everyday life: The role of formulation

Alyssa C Smith, Daniel Smilek

ABSTRACT
Oral contraceptives (OCs) are composed of artificial estrogen (ethinyl-estradiol) and/or progesterone (progestin), which can attach to receptors in the brain and potentially influence cognition. One prior study suggested that OC use was associated with increased mind-wandering – implying that OC use was associated with detriments to attention. In contrast, two subsequent well-powered conceptual replications of this initial finding revealed little evidence that OC use was related to increases in inattentiveness (including mind-wandering). However, these later studies collapsed across different OC formulations, which may obscure effects specific to particular formulations. In the present study, we investigated the possibility that OC formulation might differentially relate to attention-related traits. To do this, we collected trait-level reports of the tendency to experience attention related errors, attention lapses, spontaneous and deliberate mind-wandering, boredom, and flow in undergraduate females using OCs, and we collected the brand of OC used from which it was possible to derive the formulation. OC formulations differed in ethinyl-estradiol dose, progestin dose, dosing schedule, hormonal activity (or potency), and the type (or ‘generation’) of progestin used. We found no evidence that different OC formulations were related to differences in attention-related traits, which suggests that OC users can be treated as a homogenous group when examining trait-level attention. Comparisons of OC users (collapsing across formulation) to non-users again revealed no significant differences in trait-level attention.

Introduction More than 150 million women around the world use oral contraceptives [ 1 ]. Oral contraceptives are a type of hormonal contraceptive composed of ethinyl-estradiol and/or a synthetic form of progesterone known as progestin [ 2 ]. In naturally cycling individuals, estradiol and progesterone typically fluctuate over the course of the menstrual cycle. In contrast, OCs deliver a low consistent dose of synthetic versions of these hormones, suppressing the endogenous (or naturally occurring) estradiol and/or progesterone and replacing them with artificial surrogates [ 2 ]. This suppression of estradiol and/or progesterone (and replacement with artificial surrogates) is important because this process not only occurs in the reproductive system, but also systemically throughout the body. That is, estradiol and progesterone are also synthesized in areas of the brain responsible for higher order cognition (e.g., in the prefrontal cortex), which contain receptors for these hormones [ 3 , 4 ]. Since these molecules can function as neurosteroid hormones, they have the potential to modulate the neural excitability and neural circuits underlying many cognitive functions [ 5 , 6 ]. Accordingly, there has been a growing interest in gaining a better understanding of if and how the artificial hormones in OCs may be related to cognitive performance [ 7 – 9 ]. Reviews of the relations between OC use and cognition (see Warren et al [ 7 ]; Gurvich et al [ 8 ]; Beltz [ 9 ] typically categorize cognitive performance into several cognitive domains (e.g., memory, spatial perception, attention). One domain that had not been well examined is ‘attention’, and particularly processes related to sustained attention, such as mind wandering (i.e., engagement in task-unrelated thoughts). As of this writing, just two studies examined the relation between OC use and mind wandering [ 10 , 11 ]. In the first study addressing this issue, Raymond et al [ 10 ] examined self-reports of mind wandering among small samples of OC users (N = 28), OC non-users (N = 14), and men (N = 29). They claimed that OC users had an increased frequency of mind wandering compared to non-users. These results were in the expected direction given the increased risk of depression among OC users, and they were concerning, suggesting that OC use might be detrimental to attention. If this was in fact the case, women ought to know this risk when making the decision to begin using an OC. In the second study, we [ 11 ] aimed to replicate and extend the work of Raymond et al [ 10 ] with (1) a larger sample of participants, (2) validated measures of attentional engagement in everyday life (including a measure of mind wandering), and (3) a verification of our results across two large independent samples (OC users Sample 1: N = 471; Sample 2: N = 246; those not using any form of hormonal contraceptives including OCs, Sample 1: N = 1330; Sample 2: N = 929). Participants completed self-report measures of their trait-level tendency to experience spontaneous and deliberate mind wandering, attention lapses, and attention-related errors. In Sample 1, we found that compared to non-users, those using OCs reported a significantly lower propensity to experience spontaneous and deliberate mind wandering; there were also no differences between groups with regard to the tendency to experience attention lapses and attention-related errors. However, the effect sizes of the differences in mind wandering between groups were small in size ( d s = 0.17, 0.20). In our second independent sample (Sample 2), we found no significant differences on any of our attention measures between groups. As such, given that the effect sizes in Sample 1 were small, and there were no significant effects observed in Sample 2, we concluded that OC use was not reliably associated with variations in trait-level attention [ 11 ]. While our results showed that the tendency to experience mind wandering, attention lapses, and attention-related errors was not reliably related to OC use, other attention-related traits could be. In a follow-up study we explored whether OC use was related to the tendency to experience boredom and flow [ 12 ]. Boredom is defined as an aversive state that arises when an individual is unable to successfully engage their attention with an internal (e.g., thoughts) or external activity, even though they may want to 24 . On the flip side, flow has been described as a state where individuals report being completely absorbed in a task where the full extent of their skills is used, while still being able to successfully complete that task. Most importantly, a side effect of this flow state is the lack of boredom or worry to the point where individuals can become oblivious to their surroundings [ 13 ]. While boredom and flow are often discussed as momentary states, there are also stable trait-level differences in how frequently individuals experience these states [ 13 – 16 ]. To examine the linke between OC use and these traits, we utilized a similar method to prior work examining attention-related traits. We found OC use was associated with decreased boredom proneness but no significant differences between groups in terms of internal or external flow. In hierarchical regressions predicting boredom, we entered depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms and the semester of data collection in the first step before adding OC use in the second step. This allowed us to examine the unique contribution of OC use by controlling for depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms and the semester of data collection. We found OC use was a unique and significant predictor of boredom proneness. This suggests that OC users and non-users differ in terms of boredom proneness, but that this difference is independent of mental health symptoms and the semester of data collection. Taken together, our prior studies examining attentional engagement in everyday life and boredom and flow [ 11 , 12 ] revealed that OC use does not reliably relate to a variety of attention-related factors (i.e., attention-related errors, attention lapses, spontaneous and deliberate mind wandering, and internal and external flow), with the exception of the trait propensity to experience boredom [ 12 ]. One potentially important limitation of both studies was a lack of information about the formulations of the OCs being used by those in the OC group. OC brands are known to vary in the doses of the synthetic hormones they include, dosing schedule, and estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic activity. It is possible that different OC formulations may act differently on the brain and produce different behaviours as evidenced by different OC formulations being associated with varying risks of developing depression [ 17 ]. By not considering the various formulations of the OCs used, we may have obscured effects that are specific to particular formulations [ 18 – 20 ]. As an example of the possible moderating role of formulation is a study by Beltz et al [ 19 ]. They examined performance on a mental rotation task among women using OCs (with monophasic and triphasic dosing schedules), naturally cycling women, and men. When they collapsed across OC formulation, they found that women using OCs did not significantly differ from naturally cycling women, but when OC formulation was considered, women using monophasic OCs significantly differed from naturally cycling women such that monophasic OC users outperformed naturally women. In a separate study examining mental rotation, researchers again found that mental rotation performance did not significantly differ between OC users and non-users. However, they did find that the relation between self-perceived masculinity and performance on a mental rotation task among naturally cycling women and OC users was moderated by the estrogenic and progestational activity of OCs. This made us wonder if OC users and non-users may similarly differ in terms of attention-related traits once we accounted for the dosing schedule and/or the estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic activity of the OC used. To address this limitation, in the present study we aimed to examine whether collapsing across OC formulation obscured differences in everyday attentional engagement. To do this, we collected data regarding OC brand as well as duration of OC use in order to determine whether there were effects of specific formulations on attentional engagement. Like our prior work, we focused on OC use (opposed to hormonal contraceptives more broadly) as prior work has suggested that the oral delivery of exogenous hormones could be associated with different metabolic and by extension, cognitive effects than other delivery methods [ 21 ]. As in our previous studies, we collected self-report trait-level measures of attention related errors, spontaneous and deliberate mind wandering, attention lapses, boredom proneness, and flow using reliable and validated questionnaires. While self-report measures do have limitations, there is also evidence that self-reports are better predictors of everyday behaviours than some task-based behavioural measures [ 22 ]. We aggregate data across five semesters (Fall 2023, Winter 2024, Spring 2024, Fall 2024, and Winter 2025) to achieve a larger sample. Our main objectives were as follows: 1) replicate the overall null relations between OC use and everyday attentional engagement we reported previously [ 11 , 12 ]; and 2) then examine the role of OC formulation to determine whether collapsing across OC formulations obscured effects of specific formulations. Method Participants This study utilized secondary data. All participants provided informed consent online (indicating their consent via the use of ratio buttons). Participants were recruited between September 5 2023 and April 1 2025. Participants in the secondary dataset were undergraduate students enrolled in at least one psychology course at the University of Waterloo who indicated their sex as female. In exchange for completing the Pre-screen and Mass Testing surveys (see below), participants received partial course credit. Since our goal was to determine whether our prior work [ 11 ] collapsing across OC formulation obscured differences in everyday attentional engagement, we aimed to collect data using the same recruitment techniques. Participants were then screened for the a priori determined exclusion criteria by semester based on the criteria outlined in the Data Cleaning section below. (We note that while a large proportion of participants were excluded, data was not collected for the sole purpose of the present study. Rather, the Prescreen and Mass Testing surveys are constructed for all researchers in the Psychology Department. As such, for the data to be utilized for the present study, inclusion criteria was applied following data collection.) Data Cleaning We utilized a similar data cleaning procedure as in our prior work [ 11 , 12 , 24 ]. Exclusion criteria were determined a priori unless otherwise stated. For all semesters, participants were excluded based on the following criteria: 1) inclusion in a previous semester of data (to ensure no participants were double counted; not applicable for Fall 2023 as the first semester of data collection), 2) use of a hormonal contraceptive other than OC, 3) use of an IUD, 4) failing to disclose whether or not they used a hormonal contraceptive, 5) current pregnancy or breastfeeding (due to a coding error in Winter 2025, the response options for the item asking participants whether they were pregnant or breastfeeding only included “no”. However, participants were not required to respond to all items. Since the inclusion criteria for this item is “no”, we reasoned that those who indicated no were indicating they were not pregnant or breastfeeding, while those who did not respond may have been pregnant or breastfeeding. As such, like the previous samples we only included those who responded “no”.), and 6) failing to disclose whether or not they were pregnant or breastfeeding. To avoid the possible confound of mental health, participants who reported that they were currently receiving treatment for depression or anxiety were excluded (the exogenous hormones in OCs could also interact with psychotropic medications; see Damoiseaux et al [ 25 ]). Participants who did not respond to the items related to the reception of treatment for depression and anxiety were also excluded. Data quality checks were conducted to eliminate poor quality data based on participants’ patterns of responses. Participants were removed prior to analyses if a) the number of clicks they made was fewer than the number of responses per page of a survey, b) their responses were faster than 1-second per scale item on more than 50% of the scales of interest (to respond in good faith, we reasoned participants needed at least 1-second per item to read and respond. If they were faster than this on >50% of the scales we removed them because this may indicate they were responding randomly), and c) participants responded to fewer than half of the items on a scale of interest (as responses on items for a given scale were averaged to calculate scale scores). We analyzed the data with and without exclusions based on age (to target participants of reproductive age; 18–45 years old). This did not change the results in a meaningful way. The below analyses exclude participants who were older than 45 or did not report their year of birth. Materials Spontaneous (MWS) and deliberate (MWD) mind wandering scales The MWS is a self-report measure of spontaneous (or unintentional) mind wandering, while the MWD measures deliberate (or intentional) mind wandering [ 26 ]. These questionnaires are composed of 4 statements each. Example items include: “I allow my thoughts to wander on purpose” (MWD), and “I find my thoughts wandering spontaneously” (MWS). Participants are asked to rate each item on a seven-point scale that ranges from 1 (rarely) to 7 (a lot). Higher scores indicate a greater tendency to engage in mind wandering (deliberate or spontaneous) in everyday life. Mindful attention awareness scale-lapses only (MAASLO) The MAASLO [ 27 ] was adapted from the MAAS [ 28 ] and includes 12 self-report items related to mindlessness or attention lapses in everyday life. Participants rate items such as “I rush through activities without being to really attentive them” on a scale from 1 (almost never) to 6 (almost always). Higher frequencies of attention lapses are indicated by higher scores on the scale. Attention-related cognitive errors scales (ARCES) The ARCES assesses the propensity to experience performance errors as a result of attention lapses [ 27 , 29 ]. Participants respond to items such as “I have to go back to check whether I have done something or not (e.g., turning out lights, locking doors)” on a scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Higher scores indicate a greater frequency of cognitive errors. Short Boredom Proneness Scale (SBPS) SBPS assesses the tendency to experience boredom [ 16 ]. Participants respond to 8 statements such as “I find it hard to entertain myself” and “In most situations, it is hard for me to find something to do or see to keep me interested”. Each item is rated based on how typical the statements are of themselves on a 7-point scale that included anchors from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate a greater tendency to experience boredom. Deep, effortless concentration internal (DECI) and Deep, effortless concentration external (DECE) The DECI measures the tendency to experience flow during internal tasks (such as thinking or remembering) and the DECE assesses the tendency to experience flow during external tasks (such as sports or hobbies) [ 15 ]. Each scale consists of eight items. Participants rate the frequency with which they experience internal and external flow. They respond to statements such as “When thinking/performing an external task, I get completely engaged without having to work at it”. Participants rate each item on a 7-point scale from 1 (never) to 7 (always). Higher scores indicate a greater frequency of flow experiences during internal or external tasks. Depression anxiety stress scale-21 (DASS-21) The DASS-21 is a 21-item measures of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms over the previous week [ 30 ]. Example items include “I felt that I had nothing to look forward to” (depression), “I was worried about situations in which I might panic and make a fool of myself” (anxiety), and “I found it hard to wind down” (stress). Participants rate each item a scale from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much or most of the time). The scale consists of seven items related to each of depression, anxiety, and stress and higher scores on the scale items indicate higher levels of these experiences. OC formulations In order to conduct our analyses by OC formulation, we collected the brand participants were using via the Pre-screen survey. Past work (e.g., [ 20 , 31 ]) has used information reported by Dickey [ 23 ] to link OC brand names with composition, however, Dickey [ 23 ] lists the United States’ Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved OCs, some of which have different names in Canada. To identify the formulation and dosing schedule associated with a brand name, we conducted an internet search (using Google) for each brand name with the word “monograph” to locate each OC’s product monograph (a drug monograph is a factual and scientific document that lists its name, classification, pharmacology, and indication, as well as dosage and administration [ 32 ].This is usually available through the pharmaceutical manufacturers website), which contained each OCs’ formulation and dosing schedule. In most cases, the ethinyl estradiol dose was noted in micrograms (mcg), while the progestin dose varied between milligrams (mg) and mcg. To ensure all doses were on the same scale, we converted progestin doses in mg to mcg (1 mg = 1000 mcg). Once we had the formulation and dosing schedule, we used the information reported by Dickey [ 23 ] in Table 5 to identify the formulation equivalent (by matching the dose, dosing schedule, and type of ethinyl estradiol and/or progestin). Since OCs have undergone four major formulaic iterations (“generations”) in the type of progestin used, we also sought to account for this variance in composition. The generations differ from one another in their molecular structure (the addition or removal of methyl or ethyl groups) which alter the biological activity of the progestin. Since earlier progestins (generations 1–3) were synthesized from testosterone, they have both progestational and androgenic activity (i.e., they can bind to receptors for both progesterone and testosterone), while more modern progestins (generation 4) are synthesized to be anti-androgenic (i.e., made to bind specifically to progesterone receptors while preventing binding to testosterone receptors [ 23 ] (pp 20 –21]). To identify these generations, we referred to Dickey [ 23 ] (pp20–21), which noted the progestin name and corresponding generation. While generations can be helpful to better understand the activity of OCs, the estrogenicity, progestational, and androgenicity of each formulation can be examined on a finer, more continuous scale by taking into account the dose and potency of hormones used in each formulation. These hormonal activities were determined from assays conducted in human and rodent tissue [ 23 ] (estrogenic activity was derived from mouse uterine assays, progestational activity from endometrial responses in humans, and androgenic activity from rat prostate assays [ 23 ]). These activity values can also be ranked as low, intermediate low, intermediate high, or high [ 23 ]. This will allow us to conduct planned comparisons of OC users to non-users in order to test the replicability of our prior findings [ 11 , 12 ]. Second, we will conduct analyses examining the link between OC composition and our trait-level attention measures to determine whether particular formulations are related to differences in attention. Most OCs consist of a package of 28 pills which includes 21–24 days of ‘active’ pills containing ethinyl estradiol/ esterol monohydrate and/or progestin and 4–7 days of inactive or placebo pills (though some brands only contain the active pills and patients are instructed not to take a pill for 4–7 days before starting a new package). The majority of OC users in our sample reported using a monophasic OC (210/ 255; 82%; 206 included in analyses due to Nextstellis exclusions) in which patients receive a consistent dose of ethinyl estradiol and progestin in each their active pills. Fewer participants reported use of other dosing schedules (biphasic, triphasic, or progestin only). Only 1 (1/ 255; 0.39%) participant reported use of a biphasic OC, in which the dose of ethinyl estradiol is consistent but the dose of progestin increases once in the pack. Thirty-three OC users reported use of a triphasic OC (33/ 255; 13%), in which patients receive a consistent dose of ethinyl estradiol across their active pills but an increasing dose of progestin each week (i.e., dose of progestin changes twice in a pack). A small number of OC users also reported using progestin only OCs (11/ 255; 4%; percentages do not sum to 100 due to rounding) which contain no ethinyl estradiol, only a progestin ( Table 4 ). Within these different dosing schedules, OCs can vary further by other aspects of their formulation. While most monophasic and triphasic OCs are composed of both ethinyl estradiol (one monophasic OC formulation (brand name: Nextstellis) has an estradiol component of esterol monohydrate. Esterol is less potent and has a different binding affinity than ethinyl estradiol [ 34 ]. As a result, the dose of esterol monohydrate is much higher than the doses of ethinyl estradiol. Moreover these differences in binding affinity also lead to different effects on different tissues [ 34 ]. Together, this suggests this OC formulation in particular may act differently than the others and the large esterol monohydrate dose makes it difficult to compare with OCs composed of ethinyl estradiol. Progestin only OCs are composed of only progestin and can vary by the type, dose, progestational activity, and androgenic activity of the progestin. As we note earlier, estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic activities range from low to high ( Table 4 ). Four formulations (used by 12 participants) of OCs were not included in Dickey [ 23 ] and as such, we could not determine the activity levels for those OCs. The participants without these activity levels are omitted from analyses by activity level. We also assigned a generation for each progestin type. Most OCs in our sample were synthesized with a third generation progestin (190/ 255; 75%), while only 26 participants reported using an OC with a first generation progestin (10%), no participants reported using an OC with a second generation progestin (0%), and 39 participants using an OC containing a fourth generation progestin (15%; percentages do not sum to 100 due to rounding). We also examined how the estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic activity of OCs related to our attention measures. The benefit of examining this activity (rather than just dose) is that it takes into account both the dose and the potency of the composite hormones. Activity values range on a continuous scale (with higher numbers indicating a greater potency; see Dickey [ 23 ]. These values have also been categorized into 4 levels (ranging from low to high; see Dickey [ 23 ]. Since little work has been conducted using these activity levels (to our knowledge Beltz et al [ 31 ] was the first to conduct such an analysis) we planned to analyze our data both using the continuous activity values in regression analyses predicting each attention measure of interest as well as using the activity level categories (4 levels; low to high; as per Beltz et al [ 31 ] as predictors in separate regression analyses of each attention measure. However, collapsing activity levels into categories created multicollinearity between two levels of activity (intermediate high androgenicity and intermediate low progesticity), which violated the independence assumption of regression. As such, we only include the regression analyses for the continuous activity levels. Discussion In the present investigation, we aimed to address a limitation in our prior work investigating OC use and attention-related traits by considering the role of OC formulation in these relations. Specifically, we explored whether different aspects of OC formulation were related to differences in everyday attentional engagement as well as the propensity to experience boredom and internal and external flow. Our findings indicated that none of the OC formulation aspects (ethinyl-estradiol dose, progestin dose, hormonal activity, dosing schedule, progestin generation) were significantly related to differences in the traits measured. We also compared these same traits in OC users who provided the brand of OC used to OC users who did not provide the brand used and again found no significant differences between groups. This suggested that we could collapse across OC formulation and consider all OC users as a homogenous group in an attempt to replicate our prior work [ 11 , 12 ] compare to OC users and non-users. This comparison largely replicated our previous work showing that OC users and non-users do not significantly differ in the tendency to experience spontaneous and deliberate mind wandering, attention related errors, attention lapses, and internal and external flow [ 11 , 12 ]. In the present study, we found that OC users reported lower boredom proneness than non-users as we did in our prior work [ 12 ], although this difference was only statistically significant before we corrected for multiple comparisons. Importantly, given the present findings we are confident that collapsing across OC formulation in our present (and prior) work is not obscuring differences that emerge when OC formulation is accounted for. We hasten to add that our non-significant differences in attention-related experiences across OC formulation could be the result of a lack of power. Many of our analyses required us to split the OC group into several subgroups. That is, while we have a large group of OC users who reported their brand overall (N = 255), giving us sufficient power to detect medium and small sized effects when looking at aspects of OC formulation on a continuous scale (i.e., ethinyl-estradiol dose, progestin dose, hormonal activity), we lost power when we split this group by dosing schedule and generation. When looking at dosing schedule, the vast majority of our participants used a monophasic OC (other work has also shown that most OC users use a monophasic OC [ 36 ]; These results make sense in the context of prescription guidelines for physicians which recommend prescribing a monophasic OC first. The consistent hormone dose is easier to adapt to, and more is known about the pharmacological action and side effects since monophasic OCs have been around longer than multiphasic formulations. There is also some evidence monophasic OCs are better tolerated in terms of side effects [ 37 – 40 ].. Unfortunately, our sample sizes in the progestin only and biphasic groups were not even powered to detect large sized effects (large sized effects require an N ~ 25 per group [ 41 ]). We were also underpowered to detect medium and small sized effects between the triphasic, progestin only, and biphasic groups (medium effects require N ~ 62 per group, small effects require N > 80–100 [ 41 ]). A similar power issue emerged when examining progestin generation. Again, here most participants used a third generation progestin, so we were underpowered to detect medium or small sized effects when comparing to the first or fourth generation groups (it is difficult to say whether our sample is representative of progestin generation among OC users as to our knowledge there is little to no work on progestin generation prevalence). Though we do not find significant differences in everyday attention by dosing schedule or generation, it possible that in a larger sample with more power differences may emerge. However, we wish to emphasize that the goal of this study was not to determine whether OC formulation was related to differences in attention (for that a much larger sample is required) but to explore whether it was possible that the results of our prior work were obscuring differences in attention that would emerge only when formulation was accounted for. We find that this is not the case. That said, it is also possible that our results do reflect the true state of OC use and everyday attentional engagement – with the exception of boredom proneness – in that, OC use is just not related to differences in attention-related traits. There are several observations that are consistent with this conclusion. First, a broad examination of the literature reveals that sex differences in cognitive performance [ 42 , 43 ] are only found in particular cognitive domains. Only three cognition domains have shown some sex differences: spatial tasks such as mental rotation [ 44 , 45 ], mathematical performance where sex differences tend to be small (or non-existent) but there tend to be more high performing boys than girls [ 46 – 49 ], and verbal abilities where girls tend to outperform boys [ 42 ]. Some researchers have noted that while some small differences in cognition exist between sexes, there is much more overlap between the sexes than there are differences [ 43 ]. These prior sex-related findings are consistent with small to null effects of hormonal variations in OCs on attentional experiences. Second, while some work does show that OCs alter brain structure and function, this work is often inconsistent. For example, some work shows that OC use is associated with increased connectivity in the Default Mode Network (DMN) – the brain network associated with attentional disengagement – while other work shows decreased connectivity or no differences in connectivity [ 50 – 52 ]. The lack of consistent findings with respect to the DMN may suggest there are no true neural differences in the connectivity of this network between OC users and non-users, which would fit with the null effects reported here and in our prior work. Finally, it is important to consider the role of the relation between attention and mood. In this regard, we have previously observed that differences in boredom proneness [ 12 ] between OC uers and non-users become non-significant once we symptoms of depression are accounted for. This suggests that group differences in boredom might be driven by differences in mood – not differences in cognition – emphasizing the importance of controlling for third variables known to vary with both OC use and cognition. This work suggests several future directions. First, this study included a sample of undergraduates who reported their sex as female. Since undergraduates can be a homogenous sample in terms of age, education, and socioeconomic background, it is possible that our sample is not representative of OC users more generally [ 53 , 54 ]. Future work could utilize platforms that recruit a more heterogenous sample (e.g., Prolific) and examine how the results compare to those of the present study. Second, we did not account for several possible confounding factors. While we would have preferred to control for variables that are known to vary with OC use (e.g., depression, duration of OC use), we opted not to as once we split the data by OC formulation many groups were already underpowered and controlling for additional factors would have decreased the power further. Future work could recruit larger samples in order to be able to control for possible confounding factors without compromising power. We provide suggestions for recruiting these large samples in Smith, Smilek, and Fenske (under review) [ 55 ]. Third, as we have noted earlier, the goal of the present study was to determine whether collapsing across OC formulation obscured differences in everyday attention that would emerge once formulation was included. The goal was not to determine whether differences in OC formulation were related to differences in everyday attention. The latter question would require a much larger sample in order adequately power groups once the sample was split by the different aspects of OC formulation (e.g., dosing schedule, progestin generation). Nevertheless, this is a question worthy of investigation and could be examined in future work. It is important to note that there are aspects of studying OCs which make it enharently difficult to elucidate the true effects of OC’s on cognition. The first factor is the survivor effect: prior work has shown that when individuals start using an OC and experience an adverse effect (like depression) they are likely to discontinue use after a short period of time [ 56 – 58 ]. Since these individuals are only on an OC for a short period of time, they are unlikely to have their experience captured in a study that recruits from a community sample (such as this one). The increased risk of depression among OC users tends to emerge in studies that are able to account for participants who use an OC for a short period of time (e.g., Skovlund et al [ 17 ] which utilized Denmark’s National Prescription Register and Psychiatric Central Research Register). It also possible that there are effects of OC use on cognition (e.g., attention-related experiences) shortly after participants first begin to use OCs and that these effects progressively disappear with time as users habituate to the medication. Indeed, it has been argued that some side effects of OCs will decrease or diminish after the second or third cycle of use as the body habituates to the lower endogenous hormone level and consistent exogenous hormone level [ 23 ](p28). It is possible that an individual may notice they struggle to focus during the first two cycles of a new OC, but their assessment of their side effects may change over time. For example, when first beginning an OC an individual might notice they are struggling to focus but this could be re-evaluated after longer use as ‘normal’. To tackle these issues, it may be necessary to conduct more longitudinal studies assessing cognition on a finer time scale following OC users over time (e.g., measuring attention on a daily or bi-daily basis over a 3–6 month period following commencement of an OC regimen).
