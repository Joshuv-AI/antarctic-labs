Abstract
We study the Max-Cut semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxation in the regime where a near-optimal solution admits a low-dimensional realization. While the Goemans–Williamson hyperplane rounding achieves the worst-case optimal approximation ratio α GW ≈ 0.87856 \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\approx 0.87856 , it is natural to ask whether one can beat α GW \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} when the SDP solution lives in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} for a small dimension d d . We answer this in the affirmative for every fixed d d : there is a polynomial-time rounding algorithm that, given a d d -dimensional feasible solution to the standard Max-Cut SDP strengthened with triangle inequalities, produces a cut of expected value at least ( α GW + 2 − O ⁡ ( d ) ) (\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+2^{-O(d)}) times the SDP value. Our improvement is driven by a new geometric anti-concentration lemma for signs of low-dimensional Gaussian projections.

Introduction

Max-Cut is a canonical combinatorial optimization problem of partitioning the vertex set of a
weighted undirected graph
G=(V,E,w)
into two sides so as to maximize the total weight of
edges crossing the cut. One of Karp’s 21 NP-complete problems
[
12
]
, Max-Cut has served as a testing ground for
new ideas in approximation algorithms and hardness of approximation.

From the algorithms side, the Goemans-Williamson (GW) algorithm
[
9
]
is one of the most well-known SDP-based approximation algorithms. It solves the basic semidefinite relaxation

\max\ \ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}\bigl(1-\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\bigr)\qquad\text{s.t. }\|v_{i}\|_{2}=1\text{ for all }i\in V,

(1)

and rounds a feasible solution by sampling a random hyperplane through the origin and cutting
according to the induced signs:
sample
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and output
x_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
.
For an edge
(i,j)
, the cut probability is
\Pr[x_{i}\neq x_{j}]=\arccos(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)/\pi
,
and the worst-case ratio between this probability and the SDP contribution
(1-\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)/2

is the famous Goemans-Williamson constant

\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\ :=\ \min_{\rho\in[-1,1]}\frac{\arccos(\rho)/\pi}{(1-\rho)/2}\ \approx\ 0.87856,

which shows that this algorithms is an
\alpha_{GW}
-approximation.
Moreover, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture
[
14
]
, no polynomial-time algorithm can achieve a

(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+\varepsilon)
-approximation for any constant
\varepsilon>0

[
13
]
. Thus, improving upon
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
in the worst case is believed impossible.

Under what conditions could we improve the
\alpha_{GW}
-approximation by a constant? There have been active studies on this question, mainly relying on structural assumptions on the graph
G
, including when
G
is
dense
(i.e.,
|E|=\Omega(n)
)
[
1
]
or
low-degree
(i.e., the maximum degree is
O(1)
)
[
6
,
10
]
.

Low-dimensional SDP solutions.

In this paper, we study the

low-dimensionality
assumption: the optimal (or near-optimal) SDP solution happens to admit a realization in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
for small
d
.
It departs from previous results in that we pose structural assumptions on the SDP solution rather than the graph.

Avidor and Zwick
[
2
]
initiated the systematic study of this topic.
They showed that in two dimensions, one can achieve the ratio

\frac{32}{25+5\sqrt{5}}\approx 0.884458
(originally obtained by Goemans in unpublished work),
and in three dimensions they designed the “yin–yang” rounding procedure with ratio about
0.8818
.
This resolves in the negative a question of Feige and Schechtman
[
7
]
asking whether one can
have integrality ratios arbitrarily close to
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
while admitting a three-dimensional
optimal embedding.

Avidor and Zwick conjectured that for every fixed
d
there is a
rounding algorithm beating
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
on instances whose optimal SDP solution lies in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The conjecture is stated with respect to the basic SDP (
1
), but they also asked whether the same conclusion can be made with respect to the SDP with the following
triangle inequality constraints
.

\|a_{i}v_{i}-a_{j}v_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\|a_{j}v_{j}-a_{k}v_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\ \geq\ \|a_{i}v_{i}-a_{k}v_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\mbox{ for every }i,j,k\in V\mbox{ and }a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\in\{\pm 1\},

Prior to this work, no such improvement was known beyond
d=2,3
with or without the triangle inequalities.

The lack of progress on this conjecture is somewhat surprising, given that the positive answer has been known for the related
Grothendieck
problems.
In the (bipartite) Grothendieck problem, given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the goal is to compute
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
and
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
to maximize
x^{T}Ay
. In the PSD Grothendieck problem, given positive semidefinite
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the goal is to compute
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
to maximize
x^{T}Ax
. The optimal approximation ratios for these problems, assuming the UGC, are
the Grothendieck constant

K_{G}
and
\pi/2
respectively
[
15
,
5
]
, but for both problems, bounded-dimension SDP solutions can be rounded with a strictly better approximation ratio
[
3
,
4
]
.

Our result.

We give a positive answer to the question of Avidor and Zwick with the triangle inequalities, albeit with an exponentially small improvement.

Theorem 1.1
(Main Theorem)

There exists a randomized polynomial-time algorithm with the following guarantee.
Given a feasible
d
-dimensional solution
\{v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|v_{i}\|_{2}=1\}_{i\in V}
to the
Max-Cut SDP strengthened with triangle inequalities, the algorithm outputs a cut

x\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
whose expected value is at least

\bigl(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+2^{-O(d)}\bigr)\cdot\mathrm{SDP},

where
\mathrm{SDP}
is the objective value of the input solution.

The algorithm is a local-improvement post-processing of GW rounding, following the framework of
Feige–Karpinski–Langberg
[
6
]
and its sharpened analysis by Hsieh–Kothari
[
10
]
for Max-Cut with the bounded (maximum) degree.
Our contribution is a new geometric theorem for low-dimensional Gaussian signs, which lets us replace
the dependence on the (possibly unbounded) graph degree by an explicit dependence on the SDP
dimension.

Finally, we also mention that our proof directly extends to the weaker
local dimension
condition on the SDP solution
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in V}
: for every
i\in V
, the vectors of its neighbors (i.e.,
\{v_{j}:(j,i)\in E\}
) lie in a
d
-dimensional subspace. In particular, our result holds for any graphs with maximum degree
d
as well, though our improvement of
2^{-O(d)}
over
\alpha_{GW}
is much worse than the
1/\widetilde{\Omega}(d^{2})
via the direct analysis
[
10
]
.

1.1
Technical overview

The proof of Theorem
1.1
decomposes into a geometric statement and its algorithmic
application.

1. A geometric anti-concentration theorem.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
.
Sample a random Gaussian vector
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and define

X_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)\in\{\pm 1\}
and
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Our main geometric theorem (proved in Section
2
) shows that

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\ \geq\ n^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}.

In other words, the random hyperplane induced by
g
produces a nontrivial imbalance on the vectors

\{v_{i}\}
.
We present three self-contained proofs, each emphasizing a different viewpoint (tensor moments,
Gaussian/Hermite analysis, and positive-definite kernels on the sphere).

2. From geometry to an improved Max-Cut rounding.

Given the geometric theorem, our algorithm follows the framework of
Feige–Karpinski–Langberg
[
6
]
. After performing the standard hyperplane rounding algorithm (i.e., sampling a random Gaussian
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and setting
x_{i}\leftarrow\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
), the algorithm tries to improve the solution further by performing the local improvement.

More precisely, let
C:=\{i\in V:|\langle g,v_{i}\rangle|\leq\varepsilon\}
for some
\varepsilon>0
be the set of
candidates
. We flip a candidate
i
to the other value (i.e.,
x_{i}\leftarrow-x_{i}
) if the
local gain
, defined as

\sum_{(i,j)\in E:j\notin C\mbox{ and }x_{i}=x_{j}}w(i,j)-\sum_{(i,j)\in E:j\in C\mbox{ or }x_{i}\neq x_{j}}w(i,j),

is strictly positive; it words, we execute the flip of
i
if its gain from its non-candidate neighbors is strictly greater than its loss from its non-candidate neighbors
plus
its total weight to all the candidate neighbors.
This conservative flip strategy ensures that (1) the order of the flips is irrelevant, (2) the Max-Cut value never decreases by any flip, and (3) it suffices to analyze the expected
local gain
at each
i
conditioned on
i
being a candidate.

The Hsieh–Kothari analysis
[
10
]
shows that the expected
local gain
can be expressed in terms of
correlations among the signs of certain Gaussian projections of the neighbors’ SDP vectors, and
proves a quantitative lower bound on this correlation by a factor polynomial in the degree.

In our setting, the SDP vectors already live in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence the relevant Gaussian process has
rank at most
d
regardless of the graph degree.
The geometric theorem above provides a correlation lower bound that only depends on the dimension
d
,
namely
2^{-O(d)}
, which is enough to turn the local improvement into an additive gain of

2^{-O(d)}
times the total edge weight.

2
Key Geometric Theorem

This section proves our key geometric theorem, which can be viewed as an anti-concentration
statement for signs of low-dimensional Gaussian projections.
Throughout,
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
denotes a standard Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For a real number
t
, let
\mathrm{sgn}(t)\in\{\pm 1\}
be the sign of
t
(ties at
0
can be
broken arbitrarily since they occur with probability
0
).

Theorem 2.1
(Geometric theorem)

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be unit vectors satisfying

\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
. Sample
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and define

X_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
and
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\ \geq\ n^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}.

The hidden constant in
O(d)
depends only on the threshold
-0.9
.

The statement is robust: any fixed lower bound
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-1+\Omega(1)
yields

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\geq n^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}
with constants depending only on the gap from
-1
.
We keep
-0.9
for concreteness.
For completeness, we present three proofs for this theorem using different techniques.
Proof I first reduces the theorem to showing that an
\Theta(d)
-th term in the power series expansion of
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
is large (Section
2.1
), which is established via two different routes of an
\varepsilon
-net argument (Proof I-(i), Section
2.1.1
) and matrix algebra (Proof I-(ii), Section
2.1.2
). Proof II uses the theory of Gegenbauer polynomials (Section
2.2
).

2.1
Proof I: Power Series

The first proof analyzes the power series of the
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
and shows that every term is nonnegative and one term is at least
2^{-\Omega(d)}
.
Let us begin with the standard expression for correlations of Gaussian halfspaces.

Lemma 2.2
(Sheppard’s formula)

Let
(Y,Z)
be a centered bivariate normal with
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=1
and

\mathbb{E}[YZ]=\rho\in[-1,1]
. Then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{sgn}(Y)\,\mathrm{sgn}(Z)\bigr]=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\rho).

Applying it with
Y=\langle g,v_{i}\rangle
and
Z=\langle g,v_{j}\rangle
gives

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}]=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\arcsin\bigl(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\bigr).

(2)

We will also repeatedly use the Taylor series

\arcsin(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\,t^{2k+1},\qquad c_{k}\coloneqq\frac{(2k)!}{4^{k}\,(k!)^{2}\,(2k+1)}>0.

(3)

A standard Stirling estimate gives
c_{k}=\Theta(k^{-3/2})
.
In particular, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that

c_{k}\geq\frac{c}{(k+1)^{3/2}}\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 0.

(4)

Let
t_{ij}\coloneqq\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\in[-0.9,1]
.
Using (
3
), we may expand termwise:

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\,t_{ij}^{2k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\underbrace{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}t_{ij}^{2k+1}}_{=:S_{2k+1}}.

Note that for any
k\geq 0
,

S_{2k+1}=\sum_{i,j}\langle v_{i}^{\otimes 2k+1},v_{j}^{\otimes 2k+1}\rangle=\Big\|\sum_{i}v_{i}^{\otimes 2k+1}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0.

Since
a_{k}>0
for any
k\geq 0
, hence for any fixed
k
,

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\ \geq\ a_{k}\,S_{2k+1}.

Therefore, Theorem
2.1
follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3

There exists an odd integer
p=2k+1=\Theta(d)
with
S_{p}\geq n^{2}\cdot 2^{-\Theta(d)}
.

2.1.1
Proof I-(i):
\varepsilon
-Net-Based Proof

One way to prove Lemma
2.3
is a simple net-based argument, showing that there will be a large set of vectors whose pairwise inner products are close to
1
, so that the sum of these inner products raised to the
p
-th power dominates the sum of the
p
-th power of the other inner products, which is at most
(-0.9)^{p}
.

To formalize this idea, fix
\varepsilon\coloneqq 0.1
, and set

\tau\coloneqq 1-2\varepsilon^{2}=0.98,\qquad M\coloneqq(1+2/\varepsilon)^{d}=21^{d}.

A standard
\varepsilon
-net argument guarantees the existence of an
\varepsilon
-net
N\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with
|N|\leq M
so that every
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
has
y\in N
with
\|x-y\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
. By the pigeonhole principle, there exists
y\in N
such that the set

T\coloneqq\bigl\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\|y-v_{i}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}

has size
m\coloneqq|T|\geq n/M
. By triangle inequality, any
i,j\in T
satisfy:

\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon\iff\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq 1-\frac{(2\varepsilon)^{2}}{2}=1-2\varepsilon^{2}=\tau.

Let
p
be an odd integer (to be set shortly). Then

S_{p}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}t_{ij}^{p}\ \geq\ \sum_{i,j\in T}t_{ij}^{p}\ +\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j)\notin T\times T\end{subarray}}t_{ij}^{p}.

For
i,j\in T
,
t_{ij}\geq\tau
, so
t_{ij}^{p}\geq\tau^{p}
, hence

\sum_{i,j\in T}t_{ij}^{p}\geq m^{2}\tau^{p}.

For
(i,j)\notin T\times T
, we only use
t_{ij}\geq-0.9
. Since
p
is odd,

t_{ij}^{p}\geq(-0.9)^{p}=-0.9^{p},

so

\sum_{(i,j)\notin T\times T}t_{ij}^{p}\geq-(n^{2}-m^{2})\,0.9^{p}\geq-n^{2}\,0.9^{p}.

Thus

S_{p}\geq m^{2}\tau^{p}-n^{2}0.9^{p}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{M^{2}}\tau^{p}-n^{2}0.9^{p}=n^{2}\Bigl(\frac{\tau^{p}}{M^{2}}-0.9^{p}\Bigr).

We now choose
p
so that
\tau^{p}/M^{2}\geq 2\cdot 0.9^{p}
, i.e.

\Bigl(\frac{\tau}{0.9}\Bigr)^{p}\geq 2M^{2}=2\cdot 21^{2d}.

Since
\tau/0.9>1
, this is possible with
p=\Theta(d)
. Concretely, define

c\coloneqq\frac{\ln(2\cdot 21^{2})}{\ln(\tau/0.9)}>0,

and set

p\coloneqq 2\lceil cd\rceil+1.

Since this choice of
p
implies
\tau^{p}/M^{2}\geq 2\cdot 0.9^{p}
, the previous bound yields

S_{p}\geq n^{2}(2\cdot 0.9^{p}-0.9^{p})=n^{2}\cdot 0.9^{p},

which proves Lemma
2.3
and Theorem
2.1
.

2.1.2
Proof I-(ii): Matrix-Algebraic Proof

Another way to prove Lemma
2.3
is based on the following linear algebraic lemma.

Lemma 2.4
(A PSD sum lower bound from rank and entry lower bound)

Let
A
be an
n\times n
real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix with

A_{ii}=1
for all
i
,
\mathrm{rank}(A)\leq D
, and
A_{ij}\geq-\delta
for all

i\neq j
, where
\delta\geq 0
.
If
\delta D\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, then

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\ \geq\ \frac{n^{2}}{2(D+1)}.

Let
J
be the all-ones matrix and define
B\coloneqq A+\delta J
.
Since
A\succeq 0
and
J\succeq 0
, we have
B\succeq 0
.
Also
\mathrm{rank}(B)\leq\mathrm{rank}(A)+\mathrm{rank}(J)\leq D+1
.

Entrywise,
B_{ii}=1+\delta
, and for
i\neq j
,

B_{ij}=A_{ij}+\delta\ \geq\ 0.

Moreover, for a PSD matrix with
A_{ii}=1
we have

|A_{ij}|\leq\sqrt{A_{ii}A_{jj}}=1
by Cauchy–Schwarz, hence
A_{ij}\leq 1

and therefore
B_{ij}\leq 1+\delta
.
Thus for every entry
b\coloneqq B_{ij}\in[0,1+\delta]
we have
b^{2}\leq(1+\delta)b
,
and summing gives

\|B\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}^{2}\ \leq\ (1+\delta)\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}.

Let the nonzero eigenvalues of
B
be
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}
,
where
r=\mathrm{rank}(B)\leq D+1
.
Then

\mathrm{tr}(B)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k},\qquad\|B\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}^{2}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

(\mathrm{tr}(B))^{2}=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}\Big)^{2}\leq r\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}^{2}=r\|B\|_{F}^{2}\leq(D+1)\|B\|_{F}^{2}.

Hence

\|B\|_{F}^{2}\geq\frac{(\mathrm{tr}(B))^{2}}{D+1}.

Since
\mathrm{tr}(B)=\sum_{i}(1+\delta)=n(1+\delta)
, combining with the
previous inequality yields

(1+\delta)\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}\ \geq\ \frac{n^{2}(1+\delta)^{2}}{D+1},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}\ \geq\ \frac{n^{2}(1+\delta)}{D+1}.

Finally,
\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}+\delta n^{2}
, so

\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\geq\frac{n^{2}(1+\delta)}{D+1}-\delta n^{2}=n^{2}\cdot\frac{1-\delta D}{D+1}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{2(D+1)},

using
\delta D\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

We also use the following lemma that bounds the rank of Gram matrices of tensor powers of vectors.

Lemma 2.5
(Rank bound for Hadamard powers of Gram matrices)

Let
G
be the Gram matrix
G_{ij}=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
for vectors

v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For any integer
p\geq 1
, the matrix
G^{\circ p}
with entries

(G^{\circ p})_{ij}=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{p}
is positive semidefinite and has
rank at most
\binom{d+p-1}{p}
.

G^{\circ p}
is the Gram matrix of
\{w_{i}\}
defined as
w_{i}\coloneqq v_{i}^{\otimes p}
, hence PSD.
Each
w_{i}
, viewed as an order-
p
tensor, is a symmetric tensor (i.e.,
w_{i}[a_{1},\dots,a_{p}]=w_{i}[\sigma(a_{1}),\dots,\sigma(a_{p})]
for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\in[d]
and any permutation
\sigma:[d]\to[d]
),
whose dimension is
\binom{d+p-1}{p}
.
Therefore the rank of the Gram matrix is at most that dimension.

With these tools, we present the proof. Choose the odd integer

p\coloneqq 100d+1.

Let
A
be the matrix
A_{ij}=t_{ij}^{p}
.
By Lemma
2.5
,
A\succeq 0
and

\mathrm{rank}(A)\ \leq\ D\coloneqq\binom{d+p-1}{p}=\binom{101d}{100d+1}\leq\binom{101d}{100d}.

Also
A_{ii}=1
. For
i\neq j
, since
t_{ij}\geq-0.9
and
p
is odd,

A_{ij}=t_{ij}^{p}\ \geq\ (-0.9)^{p}=-0.9^{p}.

We will apply Lemma
2.4
with
\delta\coloneqq 0.9^{p}
.
To do so, we need
\delta D\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

For upper bounding
D
,
we use the inequality

\binom{N}{K}\leq\frac{N^{N}}{K^{K}(N-K)^{N-K}}\qquad(0\leq K\leq N),

which gives

\binom{101d}{100d}\leq\frac{(101d)^{101d}}{(100d)^{100d}(d)^{d}}=\Big(\frac{101^{101}}{100^{100}}\Big)^{d}=\bigg(101\Big(1+\frac{1}{100}\Big)^{100}\bigg)^{d}\leq(101e)^{d}.

Since
p=100d+1
, we have
\delta=0.9^{p}\leq 0.9^{100d}=(0.9^{100})^{d}
.
Also
(1-x)\leq e^{-x}
gives
0.9^{100}=(1-0.1)^{100}\leq e^{-10}
, hence

\delta D\ \leq\ (e^{-10})^{d}\,(101e)^{d}=(101e^{-9})^{d}.

Since
e>2
, we have
e^{9}>2^{9}=512
and thus
101e^{-9}<101/512<1/2
.

Therefore
\delta D<\tfrac{1}{2}
, and Lemma
2.4
yields

S_{p}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}t_{ij}^{p}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{2(D+1)}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4D}.

Using
D\leq(101e)^{d}<512^{d}=2^{9d}
gives

S_{p}\ \geq\ \frac{n^{2}}{4\cdot 2^{9d}}=\frac{n^{2}}{2^{9d+2}},

proving Lemma
2.3
and Theorem
2.1
.

2.2
Proof II: Using Gegenbauer polynomials

High-level strategy: expand over harmonic basis.

Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
. By linearity of expectation and Sheppard’s formula for the signs of Gaussian random variables, we have:

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{j}\rangle)]=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\arcsin(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)

We are given
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
.

To lower-bound this sum, we first expand
\arcsin(t)
over the harmonic basis, similar to the previous work
[
18
]
.
Intuitively, harmonic basis is to spherical functions as Fourier basis is to functions on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
; every suitably-nice function
F
(say, square-integrable) on the sphere can be decomposed into weighted sums of (spherical) harmonic polynomials
\{H_{k}^{m}\}_{m\in[N_{k}]}
1
1

1

The degree parameter
k
is called the
band index
, and
N_{k}=\smash{{d+k-1\choose k}-{d+k-3\choose k-2}}
is the dimension of the degree-
k
spherical harmonics on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
, where the weight coefficient of
H_{k}^{m}
can be obtained by taking inner product
\langle F,H_{k}^{m}\rangle
in
L_{2}
, the space of square-integrable functions.

Now, our target
\arcsin(t)
is not a function on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, but a
kernel
; more precisely, it can be written as a kernel function
\arcsin(\langle u,v\rangle)
for two points
u
and
v
on the sphere.
By the theory of spherical harmonics (more precisely, the fact that any function
f
continuous on
[-1,1]

corresponds to a
zonal function

F_{v}(\cdot)\coloneqq f(\langle\cdot,v\rangle)
that is invariant under all rotations fixing
v
on the sphere),
f
can be expanded over an orthogonal basis of the zonal functions, known as the
Gegenbauer polynomials
.
Let
P_{k}^{d-1}(t)
be the
Gegenbauer polynomial
of degree
k
for
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, normalized such that
P_{k}^{d-1}(1)=~1
.
2
2

2

Also known as the
ultraspherical polynomial
. Notice that in some of the literature the polynomial is defined for sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
in dimension
d
; in this paper we consistently use
P_{k}(t)
to denote the Gegenbauer polynomial (of degree
k
) for
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
We refer to the notes by Jean Gallier
[
8
]
and the references within for an introduction.

These polynomials can be defined using
Rodrigues’ formula

[
8
, Proposition 1.23]
: let
w\coloneqq\frac{d-3}{2}
.

P_{k}^{d-1}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma((d-1)/2)}{\Gamma(k+(d-1)/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{w}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t^{k}}(1-t^{2})^{k+{w}}.

Throughout the section our sphere is of dimension
d-1
for
d\geq 3
and thus
w\coloneqq\frac{d-3}{2}\geq 0
, and define
\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$P_{k}(t)$}}\coloneqq P_{k}^{d-1}(t)
.
For constant
d
, the Gegenbauer polynomial
P_{k}
is always a polynomial in
t
of degree
k
.

Our goal is to construct another continuous function
Q(t)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}P_{k}(t)
that lower-bounds
\arcsin(t)
.
Intuitively, the reason why working with the Gegenbauer basis gives more information than the polynomial basis (Taylor series) is that the corresponding Gegenbauer coefficients
\{q_{k}\}
are always nonnegative, and every Gegenbauer polynomial
P_{k}
itself is a kernel function
and satisfies the addition formula
[
8
, Proposition 1.18]
. This implies that
P_{k}
as a kernel is positive semi-definite, and the sum over all pairs
(v_{i},v_{j})
is non-negative:

\sum_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)\propto\sum_{i,j}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}H_{k}^{m}(v_{i})H_{k}^{m}(v_{j})=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}\left(\sum_{i}H_{k}^{m}(v_{i})\right)^{2}\geq 0.

Reduction to a lower-bound function.

Suppose we can construct a continuous lower-bound function
\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$Q(t)$}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}P_{k}(t)
satisfying:

(1)

Q(t)\leq\arcsin(t)
for all
t\in[-0.9,1]
.

(2)

q_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\geq 1
.

(3)

q_{0}\geq 2^{-O(d)}
.

For any set of vectors
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$\rho_{ij}$}}\coloneqq\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
.
Then, evaluating the sum yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2n^{2}}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\arcsin(\rho_{ij})

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{\geq}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}Q(\rho_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}P_{k}(\rho_{ij})

\displaystyle=\smash{q_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}P_{k}(\rho_{ij})}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\text{($2$),($\star$)}}}{\geq}q_{0}

\displaystyle\overset{(3)}{\geq}2^{-O(d)}.

Our goal reduces to rigorously constructing a valid
Q(t)
, satisfying (1), (2), and (3).

2.2.1
Construction of the lower-bound function
Q(t)

We define
Q(t)
by subtracting a highly-concentrated polynomial penalty from
\arcsin(t)
.
Let
\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$\Delta(t)$}}\coloneqq(t+0.9)(1-t)^{10d}
; set

\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}Q(t)}}\coloneqq\arcsin(t)-C\cdot\Delta(t),

where
C>0
is a constant to be chosen later.
Both the
\arcsin
function and the polynomial penalty are continuous on
[-1,1]
so
Q(t)
can be expanded over the Gegenbauer basis:
Q(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}P_{k}(t)
.
Since
(t+0.9)\geq 0
for all
t\in[-0.9,1]
and
C>0
, the penalty term is strictly non-negative on the domain of our inner products, guaranteeing
Q(t)\leq\arcsin(t)
on
[-0.9,1]
and thus satisfying (1).

Selecting
C
to guarantee
q_{k}\geq 0
.

Now we show that
Q(t)
satisfies (2).
We expand
Q(t)
over the spherical harmonic basis using Gegenbauer polynomials.
The Gegenbauer coefficients of
\Delta(t)
can be obtained by taking “inner-product” with
P_{k}(t)
:
\displaystyle\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$\delta_{k}$}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\Delta(t)P_{k}(t)\,d\sigma(t)
, where
d\sigma(t)=c_{d}(1-t^{2})^{w}dt
is the normalized spherical measure of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
on
[-1,1]
.
Thus the Gegenbauer coefficients of
Q(t)
are
q_{k}=c_{k}-C\delta_{k}
, where
c_{k}
are the Gegenbauer coefficients of
\arcsin(t)
; because
\arcsin(t)
is a PSD kernel,
c_{k}\geq 0
.

We prove the following useful property on the Gegenbauer coefficients of
\Delta(t)
in Section
2.2.2
.

Lemma 2.6

Let
d\geq 3
.
For every even
k
,
\delta_{k}\leq 0
.
For every odd
k
,
\delta_{k}\geq 0
.
Also, for any nonnegative integer
k\leq 10d+1
, the above inequalities are strict; that is,
\delta_{k}
is a constant (depending on
d
) bounded away from zero. In particular,
|\delta_{0}|\geq C_{0}^{d}
for some
C_{0}>1
.

Now we proceed to show that
q_{k}\geq 0
for every
k\geq 1
using case analysis.

•

Every
k>10d+1
:
\delta_{k}=0
, and thus
q_{k}=c_{k}\geq 0
. This is because
\Delta(t)
is a polynomial of degree
10d+1
;
every
P_{k}(t)
is a degree-
k
polynomial, so switching from standard polynomial basis to Gegenbauer basis shows that every
\delta_{k}
at degree above
10d+1
must vanish.

•

Even
k
between
2
and
10d
:
Because
\arcsin(t)
is an odd function with purely positive Taylor coefficients,
c_{k}=0
for even
k
.
By Lemma
2.6
,
\delta_{k}<0
, which implies
q_{k}=C|\delta_{k}|\geq 0
for any
C>0
.

•

Odd
k
between
1
and
10d+1
:
Again by
\arcsin(t)
being an odd function and Lemma
2.6
,
c_{k}>0
and
\delta_{k}>0
. We require
q_{k}=c_{k}-C\delta_{k}\geq 0
.

We can satisfy all constraints by setting

C\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 10d+1\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{k}}{|\delta_{k}|}.

Because this is a minimum over a finite set of strictly positive values,
C>0
. With this carefully tuned
C
,
q_{k}\geq 0
strictly holds for all
k\geq 1
, which proves that
Q(t)
satisfies (2).

Bounding the constant
q_{0}
.

Lastly, let’s show that
Q(t)
satisfies (3).
Since
c_{0}=0
, By Lemma
2.6
we have
q_{0}=-C\delta_{0}=C|\delta_{0}|
.
We lower bound
C
and
\delta_{0}
in terms of
d
:

•

Bounding
|\delta_{k}|
:

Since
|P_{k}(t)|\leq 1
universally on
[-1,1]
by the normalization, we have

|\delta_{k}|\leq\max_{t\in[-1,1]}|\Delta(t)|\leq 2(2)^{10d}=2^{O(d)}.

•

Bounding

:

Expanding

in the Gegenbauer basis yields
1\cdot t^{k}=\beta\cdot P_{k}(t)+[\text{lower degree terms}]
.
Chebyshev’s extremal polynomial theorem
[
17
,
11
,
16
]
says that any degree-
k
polynomial bounded by
1
on
[-1,1]
has a leading coefficient at most

. If we apply Chebyshev’s theorem on

which is bounded by
1
on
[-1,1]
and compare the coefficients, we get
\beta\geq 2^{1-k}
.
Now

, the
k
-th Gegenbauer coefficient of

, is just
\beta
times the
k
-th Taylor coefficient of
\arcsin(t)
, which is at least
\Theta(k^{-3/2})
.
(see Eq.
3
).
This gives
c_{k}\geq\Theta(2^{-k+1}\cdot k^{-3/2})
.
For

,

.

•

Therefore,
\displaystyle C=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 10d+1\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}(c_{k}/|\delta_{k}|)\geq 2^{-O(d)}/2^{O(d)}=2^{-O(d)}
.

•

Bounding
|\delta_{0}|
:
By Lemma
2.6
,
|\delta_{0}|\geq C_{0}^{d}
for some
C_{0}>1
.

Combining these results,
q_{0}=C|\delta_{0}|\geq 2^{-O(d)}\cdot C_{0}^{d}\geq 2^{-O(d)}
. This established property (3) for

.

2.2.2
Analyzing the Gegenbauer coefficients
\delta_{k}
: Proof of Lemma
2.6

Recall
\delta(t)=(t+0.9)(1-t)^{10d}=1.9(1-t)^{10d}-(1-t)^{10d+1}
.
Define

\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}I_{k}(A)}}\coloneqq\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t)^{A}P_{k}(t)\,d\sigma(t).

We can split the integration by linearity:

\delta_{k}=1.9I_{k}(10d)-I_{k}(10d+1).

Immediately we see the ratio
\frac{I_{k}(10d+1)}{I_{k}(10d)}>1.9
if and only if
\delta_{k}<0
.
Our goal is to show that for every
k\leq A
, (1)
(-1)^{k}I_{k}(A)>0
, and (2)
\frac{|I_{k}(A+1)|}{|I_{k}(A)|}\geq 1+\frac{A}{A+d-1}
.

Exact evaluation of
I_{k}(A)
.

Using Rodrigues’ formula for Gegenbauer polynomials,

P_{k}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}(w+1)^{\overline{k}}}(1-t^{2})^{-w}\cdot\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\Bigl((1-t^{2})^{k+w}\Bigr),

where
\emph{{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}$z^{\overline{k}}$}}\coloneqq z\cdot\ldots\cdot(z+k-1)
is the
k
-th rising power
of
z
, and
w\coloneqq\frac{d-3}{2}
.
The weight function for
S^{d-1}
is proportional to
(1-t^{2})^{w}
.
Plug in
I_{k}(A)
by multiplying
P_{k}(t)
with
(1-t)^{A}d\sigma(t)=c_{d}(1-t)^{A}(1-t^{2})^{w}dt
where
c_{d}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma((d-1)/2)}>0
and
\Gamma(z)
is the Gamma function where
\Gamma(n+1)=n!
for every integer
n
.
The terms
(1-t^{2})^{-w}
and
(1-t^{2})^{w}
cancel out:

I_{k}(A)=c_{d}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}(w+1)^{\overline{k}}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t)^{A}\cdot\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\Bigl((1-t^{2})^{k+w}\Bigr)dt.

Integrating by parts
k
times gives

I_{k}(A)=c_{d}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{k}(w+1)^{\overline{k}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\left(\Big.(1-t)^{A}\right)(1-t^{2})^{k+w}dt.

The boundary terms vanish because
(1-t^{2})^{k+w}
has a zero of order
k+w
at both
t=\pm 1
. (This requires
w\geq 0
and thus
d\geq 3
.)

Two vital properties emerge:

1.

For
0\leq k\leq A
,
since

\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}(1-t)^{A}=(-1)^{k}(A-k+1)^{\overline{k}}(1-t)^{A-k}

remains positive,
so the residual integral is also strictly positive.
Plug in the
k
-th derivative of
(1-t)^{A}
into the formula of
I_{k}(A)
, we have

I_{k}(A)=c_{d}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k}(A-k+1)^{\overline{k}}}{2^{k}(w+1)^{\overline{k}}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t)^{A+w}(1+t)^{k+w}dt.

The sign of
I_{k}(A)
is
(-1)^{k}
.
Therefore,
I_{k}(A)>0
for even
k
, and
I_{k}(A)<0
for odd
k
for every
k\leq A
.
If
k>A
,
\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}(1-t)^{A}=0
, hence
I_{k}(A)=0
.

2.

The ratio of
I_{k}(A+1)
and
I_{k}(A)
has a nice closed form: Again assume
0\leq k\leq A
.
Observe that the integral in
I_{k}(A)
can be written as
2^{A+k+2w}
times a Beta function

B(A+w+1,k+w+1)

where
B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}=\int_{0}^{1}u^{x-1}(1-u)^{y-1}\,du
, by setting
u=\frac{1+t}{2}
.
The Beta function satisfies a nice ratio identity
B(x+1,y)/B(x,y)=x/(x+y)
:

\frac{B(A+w+2,k+w+1)}{B(A+w+1,k+w+1)}=\frac{A+w+1}{A+k+2w+2}.

Therefor we obtain, for
0\leq k\leq A
,

\displaystyle\frac{|I_{k}(A+1)|}{|I_{k}(A)|}=2\left(\frac{A+1}{A+1-k}\right)\left(\frac{A+w+1}{A+k+2w+2}\right).

(5)

For
k\geq 0
, the denominator function
f(k)=(A+1-k)(A+k+d-1)

is strictly decreasing.
Therefore, the ratio is strictly increasing with
k
, achieving its global minimum on
[0,A]
at
k=0
.
This implies

\frac{|I_{k}(A+1)|}{|I_{k}(A)|}\geq 2\left(\frac{A+w+1}{A+2w+2}\right)\geq 1+\frac{A}{A+2w+2}.

Sign Alternation of
\delta_{k}
.

We substitute
A=10d
into the ratio:

\min_{0\leq k\leq A}\frac{|I_{k}(10d+1)|}{|I_{k}(10d)|}\geq 1+\frac{10d}{11d-1}=\frac{21d-1}{11d-1}.

For any dimension
d\geq 2
, this ratio evaluates to at least
\frac{41}{21}\approx 1.952>1.9
.

We can now cleanly determine the signs of
\delta_{k}=1.9I_{k}(10d)-I_{k}(10d+1)
for all
k\leq 10d+1
:

•

For even
k
:
Both
I_{k}(10d)
and
I_{k}(10d+1)
are strictly positive. Since the ratio of their absolute values
\geq 1.952
, we have
I_{k}(10d+1)\geq 1.952I_{k}(10d)>1.9I_{k}(10d)
, forcing
\delta_{k}<0
.

•

For odd
k
:
Both
I_{k}(10d)
and
I_{k}(10d+1)
are strictly negative. Because the ratio of their absolute values
\geq 1.952
, the magnitude of the positive term
-I_{k}(10d+1)
thoroughly dominates, forcing
\delta_{k}>0
.

•

When
k=10d+1
:

I_{k}(10d)=0
and thus the parity of
\delta_{k}
is solely determined by
I_{k}(10d+1)
.

(Note: For
k>10d+1
, the maximum degree of
\Delta(t)
is exceeded, dictating
\delta_{k}=0
exactly).

Lower bounds for
\delta_{0}
.

From the lower bound for the ratio
\frac{|I_{k}(10d+1)|}{|I_{k}(10d)|}
and the exact formula of
|I_{k}(10d)|
using Beta function, by substituting
A=10d
we get

\displaystyle|\delta_{0}|=|1.9I_{0}(10d)-I_{0}(10d)|

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{21d-1}{11d-1}-\frac{19}{10}\right)\cdot I_{k}(10d)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{21d-1}{11d-1}-\frac{19}{10}\right)\cdot 2^{10d+w}\cdot B(10d+w+1,w+1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d+9}{10(11d-1)}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{9d+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{9d+1}

which shows
|\delta_{0}|\geq C_{0}^{d}
for some
C_{0}>1
.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
2.6
.

2.3
Upper Bound on the Variance

The
2^{-O(d)}n^{2}
lower bound on
\mathbb{E}[X]
is asymptotically tight, as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.7

There exist infinitely many integers
d
for which the following holds.
Let

n\coloneqq\left\lfloor e^{d/100}\right\rfloor.

Then there exist unit vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
, so that if
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
X_{i}\coloneqq\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)\in\{\pm 1\}
and

X\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
then
\mathbb{E}_{g}[X^{2}]\leq 2n
.
In particular,

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}[X^{2}]}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{n}\leq 2e^{-d/100}=e^{-\Omega(d)}.

We use the probabilistic method. Sample
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
i.i.d. uniformly at random from the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

1. Ensuring
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
.

Fix
i\neq j
. By symmetry,

\mathbb{P}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle<-0.9)=\mathbb{P}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle>0.9).

We use the following standard claim that shows a tail bound on the inner product of two random
d
-dimensional unit vectors. For completeness, we provide a proof at the of this subsection.

Claim 2.8

For independent uniform
U,V\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and any
a\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}(\langle U,V\rangle\geq a)\leq(1-a^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

(6)

Using the above claim with
a=0.9
we get

\mathbb{P}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle<-0.9)\leq 0.19^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

Let
A
be the event that
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
.
By the union bound over
\binom{n}{2}
pairs,

\mathbb{P}(A^{c})\leq\binom{n}{2}\,0.19^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{2}\,0.19^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

Since
n^{2}\leq e^{d/50}
and
0.19^{(d-1)/2}=\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{d-1}{2}\ln(1/0.19)\big)
,
there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(A^{c})\leq e^{-cd}

for all sufficiently large
d
. In particular, for all sufficiently large
d
,

\mathbb{P}(A)\geq 0.99.

(7)

2. Upper bounding
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
.

For a given realization of
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
define the nonnegative quantity

F(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{g}[X^{2}].

We compute its expectation over the random choice of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
:

\mathbb{E}_{v}[F]=\mathbb{E}_{v,g}[X^{2}].

Expand

X^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}X_{j}.

Since
X_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
, we have
X_{i}^{2}=1
always, hence

\mathbb{E}_{v,g}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\Big]=n.

We claim that for each
i\neq j
,

\mathbb{E}_{v,g}[X_{i}X_{j}]=0.

Indeed, condition on
g
and on all vectors
\{v_{k}\}_{k\neq i}
; under this conditioning,
v_{i}
remains uniform on the sphere and is invariant under
v_{i}\mapsto-v_{i}
. But replacing
v_{i}
by
-v_{i}
flips the sign of
X_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
while leaving
X_{j}
unchanged, hence the product
X_{i}X_{j}
changes sign. Therefore its conditional expectation is
0
, and taking expectations yields
\mathbb{E}_{v,g}[X_{i}X_{j}]=0
.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{v}[F]=\mathbb{E}_{v,g}[X^{2}]=n.

By Markov’s inequality applied to the nonnegative random variable
F
,

\mathbb{P}_{v}(F\geq 2n)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{v}[F]}{2n}=\frac{1}{2},

so if we define
B\coloneqq\{F\leq 2n\}
then

\mathbb{P}(B)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(8)

Step 3: positive probability intersection.

From (
7
) and (
8
),

\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\geq\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)-1\geq 0.99+\frac{1}{2}-1>0.

Hence there exists a deterministic choice of unit vectors
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
such that both
A
and
B
hold, i.e.,

\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9\quad\forall i\neq j,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}_{g}[X^{2}]\leq 2n.

Finally, for this choice,

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}[X^{2}]}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{2n}{n^{2}}=\frac{2}{n}\leq 2e^{-d/100}=e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Now we prove Claim
6
on the tail bound on the inner product between two independent
d
-dimensional unit vectors.

Fix
U
and, by rotational invariance, assume
U=e_{1}
. Let
V
be uniform on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. A standard representation is

V=\frac{G}{\|G\|},\qquad\text{where }G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

Write
G=(Z,W)
where
Z\coloneqq G_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
W\coloneqq(G_{2},\dots,G_{d})
, and let
S\coloneqq\|W\|^{2}=\sum_{k=2}^{d}G_{k}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{d-1}
, independent of
Z
.
Then

\langle U,V\rangle=V_{1}=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{Z^{2}+S}}.

If
V_{1}\geq a
(with
a\in(0,1)
), then
Z\geq 0
and squaring gives

\frac{Z^{2}}{Z^{2}+S}\geq a^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(1-a^{2})Z^{2}\geq a^{2}S\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z^{2}\geq\lambda S,\qquad\lambda\coloneqq\frac{a^{2}}{1-a^{2}}.

Conditioning on
S
, we have

\mathbb{P}(V_{1}\geq a\mid S)\leq\mathbb{P}(Z^{2}\geq\lambda S\mid S)=\mathbb{P}(|Z|\geq\sqrt{\lambda S}\mid S).

Using the standard Gaussian tail bound
\mathbb{P}(|Z|\geq t)\leq e^{-t^{2}/2}
for
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(V_{1}\geq a\mid S)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda S}{2}\right).

Taking expectation over
S
yields

\mathbb{P}(V_{1}\geq a)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda S}{2}\right)\right].

Now
S=\sum_{k=2}^{d}G_{k}^{2}
is a sum of
(d-1)
independent
\chi^{2}_{1}
variables; for
Y\sim\chi^{2}_{1}
one has

\mathbb{E}[e^{-tY}]=(1+2t)^{-1/2}
for
t\geq 0
(a one-dimensional Gaussian integral),
so

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda S/2}\right]=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-(\lambda/2)Y}\right]\right)^{d-1}=(1+\lambda)^{-(d-1)/2}.

Since
1+\lambda=1+\frac{a^{2}}{1-a^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-a^{2}}
, we conclude

\mathbb{P}(V_{1}\geq a)\leq(1-a^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

This is exactly (
6
), completing the proof.

3
Improved Algorithm

In this section we show how Theorem
2.1
yields an improved approximation for Max-Cut. The algorithm and its analysis are almost identical to the local improvement-based framework from
[
6
,
10
]
– the key difference is that we rely on our geometric anti-concentration lemma to show the additional improvement on top of the
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
-factor approximation guarantee for Max-Cut. We describe and analyze the algorithm in detail in this section.

3.1
The SDP Relaxation and Rounding Scheme

As discussed earlier, our first step would be to solve the basic SDP relaxation strengthened with the
\ell^{2}_{2}
-triangle inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathrm{SDP}:=

\displaystyle\max\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}\bigl(1-\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\bigr)

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{s.t. }\|v_{i}\|^{2}_{2}=1

\displaystyle\forall i\in V,

(10)

\displaystyle\|a_{i}v_{i}-a_{j}v_{j}\|^{2}_{2}+\|a_{i}v_{i}-a_{k}v_{k}\|^{2}_{2}\geq\|a_{j}v_{j}-a_{k}v_{k}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\forall a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\in\{\pm 1\},

\displaystyle\forall i,j,k\in V.

(11)

Clearly, the SDP relaxation is feasible, since any integral assignment
\{v_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}\}_{i\in V}
satisfies all the constraints. Hereafter, we will work with a feasible SDP solution
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in V}
where the vectors lie in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or equivalently, the corresponding Gram matrix has rank at most
d
), and use
\mathrm{SDP}
to denote the value of the corresponding solution.

Rounding the SDP solution.

Next, we use the rounding scheme from
[
6
,
10
]
to round the above SDP solution into an integral solution. The rounding algorithm, Algorithm
1
, has two parts. Firstly, it samples a Gaussian vector
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and projects it along the vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
and outputs the corresponding signs
x_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
as a preliminary set of labels. This is then followed by a greedy post-processing step where some of labels of the vertices are flipped to further improve the quality of the solution. For completeness, we describe the rounding scheme in full detail below:

Algorithm 1

GW rounding with local improvement

1.

Input: a
d
-dimensional SDP solution
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

Set
\varepsilon:=2^{-Cd}
for a sufficiently large absolute constant
C>0
.

3.

Sample
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and set
x_{i}\leftarrow\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
for all
i\in V
.

4.

Candidate set:
S\leftarrow\{i\in V:|\langle g,v_{i}\rangle|<\varepsilon\}
.

5.

For each
i\in S
:

(a)

Define
W_{i}:=\sum_{j\in N(i)}w_{ij}
.

(b)

Define
B_{i}:=\{j\in N(i):x_{i}x_{j}=1\ \text{and}\ |\langle g,v_{j}\rangle|\geq\varepsilon\}
.

(c)

If
\sum_{j\in B_{i}}w_{ij}>W_{i}/2
, then flip
x_{i}\leftarrow-x_{i}
.

6.

Output
x
.

Intuitively, one can think of
S
as the subset of vertices that are close to being undecided with respect to the fixed hyperplane, and in particular, for any vertex
i\in V
, the joint distribution over the labels of the neighborhood of
i
remains almost unchanged even after conditioning on the label of
i
; this observation is in crucial in arguing that for the vertices in
S
, the expected gain in the value of the solution after post-processing is at least
e^{-O(d)}
(Lemma
3.3
).

Now, we proceed to analyze the performance of the rounding algorithm. We begin with the following standard fact.

Claim 3.1

For any edge
(i,j)\in E
, we have

\Pr_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[x_{i}\neq x_{j}\right]\geq\alpha_{GW}\cdot\frac{\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|^{2}_{2}}{4}.

Hence, the expected number edges cut by the labeling
(x_{i})^{n}_{i=1}
from Line 3 is at least
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}
.

Next, we can easily verify the following:

Claim 3.2

For any
i\in V
, the
local gain
at flipping
x_{i}\leftarrow-x_{i}
is

\Delta_{i}:=\Bigl(\sum_{j\in B_{i}}w_{ij}-\sum_{j\in N(i)\setminus B_{i}}w_{ij}\Bigr)_{+}=\bigl(2\sum_{j\in B_{i}}w_{ij}-W_{i}\bigr)_{+}.

(12)

Hence, the total increase in the objective due to the post-processing step is at least

\Delta:=\sum_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}
.

In the following section, we will bound the expected total gain
\Delta_{i}
at a given vertex
i
.

3.2
Expected local gain

Next, we give the following lemma (which is an analogue of Lemma 8 from
[
10
]
) which lower bounds the expected gain
\Delta_{i}
during the post-processing phase.

Lemma 3.3
(Expected local gain)

Suppose
\rho_{ij}:=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\in[\rho^{*}\pm 0.01]
for every edge
(i,j)\in E
.
Then, for every vertex
i\in V
,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}\mid i\in S]\ \geq\ \ W_{i}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}.

To prove the above lemma, we will need the following generalization of our geometric anti-concentration theorem (Theorem
2.1
) to the weighted setting:

Corollary 3.4
(Geometric theorem - Weighted Version)

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be unit vectors satisfying

\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq-0.9
for all
i\neq j
. Let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a collection of non-negative weights. Sample
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and define

X_{i}:=\mathrm{sgn}(\langle g,v_{i}\rangle)
and
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\ \geq\ W^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)},

where
W:=\sum^{n}_{i=1}w_{i}
denotes the sum of the weights. Again, the hidden constant in
O(d)
depends only on the threshold
-0.9
.

The weighted version stated above can be derived by a straightforward reduction to the unweighted case, and we give a proof of the corollary in Section
3.4
. Equipped with the above, we can now proceed to prove Lemma
3.3
.

We will proceed by fixing a vertex
i\in V
and condition on
i\in S
, i.e.,
|\langle g,v_{i}\rangle|<\varepsilon
.
By rotational invariance, we may assume
v_{i}=e_{1}
and write
g=(g_{1},g^{\prime})
with

g_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
g^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d-1})
independent. For every
j\in N(i)
, let
\rho_{j}:=\rho_{ij}=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\in[\rho^{*}\pm 0.01]
. Then we can write

v_{j}=\rho_{j}e_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\,u_{j},

where
u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the normalized component of
v_{j}
that is orthogonal to
e_{1}
. Observe that
x_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(g_{1})
, and for each neighbor
j
let us define the Gaussian variable

h_{j}:=\mathrm{sgn}(g_{1})\,\langle g^{\prime},u_{j}\rangle.

(13)

Then conditioned on
g_{1}
, the vector
(h_{j})_{j\in N(i)}
is a Gaussian vector random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with mean
0
and covariance matrix

\Sigma
given by

\Sigma_{jk}:=\mathbb{E}[h_{j}h_{k}]=\langle u_{j},u_{k}\rangle.

(14)

Lower bounding correlations.

We claim that for all distinct neighbors
j,k\in N(i)
,

\Sigma_{jk}\geq-0.2.

(15)

The above can be argued as follows: note that the vectors
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}
satisfy (
11
) and hence,

\|v_{i}+v_{j}\|^{2}_{2}+\|v_{i}+v_{k}\|^{2}_{2}\geq\|v_{j}-v_{k}\|^{2}_{2},

which on rearranging yields
\rho_{jk}\geq-\rho_{ij}-\rho_{ik}-1
. On the other hand, by expanding and substituting the decomposition above yields

\rho_{jk}=\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle=\rho_{j}\rho_{k}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\,\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}\,\langle u_{j},u_{k}\rangle,

and hence

\Sigma_{jk}=\langle u_{j},u_{k}\rangle=\frac{\rho_{jk}-\rho_{j}\rho_{k}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}_{j}}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}_{k}}}\geq\frac{-\rho_{j}-\rho_{k}-1-\rho_{j}\rho_{k}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}_{j}}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}_{k}}},

which along with the fact that
\rho_{j},\rho_{k}\in[\rho^{*}\pm 0.01]
and
\rho^{*}\approx-0.689
implies that
\Sigma_{jk}\geq-0.2
, proving (
15
).

A surrogate random variable.

Now define

Z:=\sum_{j\in N(i)}w_{ij}\,\mathbf{1}[h_{j}\geq 3\varepsilon].

(16)

If
|g_{1}|<\varepsilon
and
h_{j}\geq 3\varepsilon
, then

\mathrm{sgn}(g_{1})\langle g,v_{j}\rangle=\rho_{j}|g_{1}|+\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\,h_{j}\geq\varepsilon

(for a suitable choice of the absolute constant
C
in
\varepsilon=2^{-Cd}
).
In particular, this implies
j\in B_{i}
, so

Z\leq\sum_{j\in B_{i}}w_{ij}.

(17)

Combining (
12
) and (
17
), we observe that

\Delta_{i}\geq(2Z-W_{i})_{+}=2\,(Z-W_{i}/2)_{+},

(18)

and hence it will suffice to focus on lower bounding the random variable
(Z-W_{i}/2)_{+}
.

Second moment computation.

Let
p_{0}:=\Pr[\mathcal{N}(0,1)\geq 3\varepsilon]=\tfrac{1}{2}-\Theta(\varepsilon)
.
A standard Gaussian anti-concentration bound implies that for any correlation
\rho\in[-1,1]
,

\Pr[A\geq 3\varepsilon,B\geq 3\varepsilon]=\Pr[A\geq 0,B\geq 0]\pm O(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\arcsin(\rho)\pm O(\varepsilon),

(19)

where
(A,B)
is a centered bivariate normal with unit variances and correlation
\rho
, and we used
Lemma
2.2
in the second equality.
Using (
19
) and (
14
), a direct expansion gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z-W_{i}/2)^{2}\mid i\in S\bigr]\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j,k\in N(i)}w_{ij}w_{ik}\arcsin(\Sigma_{jk})-O(\varepsilon W_{i}^{2}).

(20)

Invoking the geometric lemma.

The matrix
\Sigma
is a Gram matrix of vectors
u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with diagonal ones and
off-diagonal entries at least
-0.2
by (
15
).
Applying Corollary
3.4
(with a slightly weaker threshold than
-0.9
, which only
improves the constants) yields

\sum_{j,k\in N(i)}w_{ij}w_{ik}\arcsin(\Sigma_{jk})\ \geq\ \ W_{i}^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}.

(21)

Choosing the constant
C
in
\varepsilon=2^{-Cd}
large enough so that

\varepsilon\leq 2^{-\Omega(d)}
is dominated by the right-hand side of (
21
), we
combine (
20
) and (
21
) to obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z-W_{i}/2)^{2}\mid i\in S\bigr]\ \geq\ \ W_{i}^{2}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}.

(22)

From variance to expected gain.

Now we can finally convert the variance bound to a lower bound on the expected gain. Let us define random variables
\widetilde{Z}:=Z-W_{i}/2
,
\widetilde{Z}_{+}:=\max(\widetilde{Z},0)
, and
\widetilde{Z}_{-}:=\max(-\widetilde{Z},0)
. Note that by definition
0\leq Z\leq W_{i}
, and hence it follows that
|\widetilde{Z}|\leq W_{i}/2
. Furthermore, we can also bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{+}]-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{-}]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in N(i)}w_{ij}\,\mathbf{1}[h_{j}\geq 3\varepsilon]\right]-\frac{W_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in N(i)}w_{ij}\mathbb{E}\left[\,\mathbf{1}[h_{j}\geq 3\varepsilon]\right]-\frac{W_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=W_{i}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\Theta(\epsilon)\right)-\frac{W_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=-\Theta(\epsilon)W_{i},

and hence
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{-}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{+}]+O(\epsilon)W_{i}
. Combining the above bounds, we can now observe:

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}^{2}]\leq(W_{i}/2)\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{+}+\widetilde{Z}_{-}]\leq W_{i}\widetilde{Z}_{+}+O(\epsilon W^{2}_{i})

Further, using (
22
) and by setting the parameter
C
large enough in
\epsilon=e^{-Cd}
, we get that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{+}\mid i\in S]\geq W_{i}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}
. Finally, (
18
) shows that:

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}\mid i\in S]=2\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{+}\mid i\in S]\geq W_{i}\cdot 2^{-O(d)},

which completes the proof.

3.3
From local gain to the main theorem

We now complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
W:=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}
be the total edge weight. Let
\mathrm{SDP}
denote the value of the
input solution (
9
).

Case 1: constant fraction of edges are far from worst correlation.

Let
\eta=0.01
. Since the function

F(\rho)=(1-\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\rho))/(1-\rho)
has a unique minimizer
\rho^{*}
and is smooth,
there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

F(\rho)\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+\gamma
for all
|\rho-\rho^{*}|\geq\eta
.
If the total SDP contribution of edges with
|\rho_{ij}-\rho^{*}|\geq\eta
is at least

\delta\cdot\mathrm{SDP}
for
\delta=e^{-C^{\prime}d}
(for
C^{\prime}>0
large enough), then by Claim
3.1
and the definition of

F
, the plain GW rounding already yields expected value at least

(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+\gamma\delta)\cdot\mathrm{SDP}=(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+e^{-O(d)})\cdot\mathrm{SDP}
.

Case 2: the edgewise GW analysis is tight.

Otherwise, after discarding at most
\delta
-fraction of the SDP value, we may assume that every edge
satisfies
\rho_{ij}\in[\rho^{*}\pm\eta]
, which is the hypothesis of Lemma
3.3
.

Now note that
\mathrm{SDP}
is a constant fraction of
W
, because

\mathrm{SDP}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)}w_{ij}(1-\rho_{ij})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho^{*}-\eta)W
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
3.3
and the fact that

\Pr[i\in S]=\Pr[|g_{1}|<\varepsilon]=\Theta(\varepsilon)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta]=\sum_{i\in V}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}[i\in S]\,\Delta_{i}]=\Theta(\varepsilon)\sum_{i\in V}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}\mid i\in S]

\displaystyle\geq\Theta(\varepsilon)\sum_{i\in V}W_{i}\cdot 2^{-O(d)}-O(\delta)\cdot W

\displaystyle\geq W\cdot 2^{-O(d)},

where we used
\sum_{i}W_{i}=2W
and the observation that
\varepsilon=2^{-Cd}
, the factor
\Theta(\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{-O(d)}
is still

2^{-O(d)}
, as claimed. Note that in the above computation, we remove a
O(\delta)W
-weight of edges from the cumulative local gain to account for the edges for which the correlation
\rho\notin[\rho^{*}\pm\eta]
.

Finally, observe that (a) the labeling in Line
3
of Algorithm
1
cuts
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}
weight of edges in expectation (Claim
3.1
), and (b) the expected additive improvement in the number of cut edges due to Lines
4
and
5
is
\mathbb{E}[\Delta]\geq W\cdot 2^{-O(d)}
. Combining these observations we obtain the bound

\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{weight of edges cut}\Big]\geq(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+2^{-O(d)})\cdot\mathrm{SDP},

which concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3
