DateSAT : A Framework for Solving Date and Period Constraints
Leyi Cui, Shrey Tiwari, Rohan Padhye

Abstract
Dates and calendar periods (i.e., days, months, years) appear frequently in tasks involving analysis of software, data, and documents. Prior research has shown that computer logic involving dates and calendrical calculations is error-prone due to tricky rules (e.g., irregularly sized months), ambiguities (e.g., scheduling one month from “Jan 31st”), and edge cases (e.g., leap years). However, existing program analysis and verification tools do not provide native support for dates, making it hard to reason about operations involving calendrical arithmetic symbolically. This paper presents DateSAT , the first framework for expressing and solving satisfiability constraints involving dates and calendar periods. The paper first formalizes an input language and the semantics of date and period arithmetic. The paper then presents five separate strategies for solving DateSAT constraints based on reductions to SMT formulas involving integers, which we have implemented using Z3 as a backend. We curate a dataset of 450 DateSAT constraints synthesized using LLM prompting, grammar-based sampling, and mining legal documents, and then present an empirical evaluation of DateSAT solver performance.

Given a set of constraints in this language, the goal of a

(Decidability)

The

3.3
Bounded Instances

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of subexpressions of
Date
sort (i.e., the
date_expr
non-terminal in Figure
6
) in a given set of
DateSAT
constraints
C
, which together encompass all referenced date values including free variables. Then, we can construct a new constraint set
C^{\prime}
by explicitly adding lower and upper bounds
lb,ub\in\mathcal{D}
to each date such that
C^{\prime}=C\cup\{``lb\leq e\leq ub^{\prime\prime}\mid e\in\mathcal{E}\}
. We call this a bounded instance of the
DateSAT
problem, denoted
\textsc{DateSAT}{}^{[{lb},{ub}]}
.
In our implementation, we use
lb=|1900,3,1|
and
ub=|2100,2,28|
by default. The reason for this is twofold: (i) most real-world software and document analysis tasks we have encountered so far only deal with dates within a few years or decades of the current day, and (ii) these specific bounds span a range of
\sim
200 years every fourth year is a leap year
5
5

5

Outside this bound, the Gregorian calendar specifies an exception that every 100th year which is also not a multiple of 400 is a non-leap year.
, which enables some optimizations when implementing solvers (ref. Section
4.5
). The bounds can be adjusted to any other range relevant to an application, though this is not needed for decidability.

4
Solving
DateSAT
Constraints, Effciently

A simple approach to solve bounded
DateSAT
instances
\textsc{DateSAT}{}^{[{lb},{ub}]}
is to enumerate all possible date assignments within the bounded domain and checks each against the constraint using a reference calendar library such as Python’s
dateutil
. However, this is fundamentally inefficient beyond very small instances. For an instance with
k
free date variables over a bounded range of
N
valid dates, the number of candidate assignments grows as
N^{k}
. Under our default bounds of
|1900,3,1|
to
|2100,2,28|
, even a constraint with just three free date variables yields a search space of over 389 trillion assignments, which is unlikely to be solved quickly.
We therefore solve
DateSAT
constraints by direct translation from the theory of dates (denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{Date}
) to corresponding constraints in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{LIA}}
, the theory of (quantifier-free) linear integer arithmetic extended with divisibility by constants
6
6

6

This allows operators for Euclidean division (
x\mathbin{\mathrm{div}}n
) and non-negative modulo (
x\bmod n
), for constant
n\neq 0
.
, which is known to be decidable
[
30
,
9
]
.
Under this paradigm, solver design becomes a problem of defining an efficient mapping
\mathcal{M}:\Phi_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{Date}}}\to\Phi_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{LIA}}}
, where
\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}
is the domain of constraints in theory
\mathcal{T}
.
However, constructing such a mapping is non-trivial, as calendrical logic is inherently irregular due to varying month lengths and the leap year cycle. Encoding the semantics of dates and periods naively leads to very deeply nested if-then-else (ITE) blocks—we use the term
logical depth
to quantify the nesting—and a large number of integer operations—whose cardinality we refer to as
arithmetic complexity
—thereby necessitating a combinatorial explosion in the solver’s search space.

In this section, we present five encoding strategies that explore the trade-off space between logical depth and arithmetic complexity to optimize solving performance.

Running Example:

Consider the constraint:
a + Period(1, 2, 15) < b
, where both
a
and
b
are free variables of the
Date
sort.
This constraint exercises the salient features of
DateSAT
including symbolic dates, date-period arithmetic, and date comparison.
By applying different encoding strategies to this constraint, we demonstrate how various mappings
\mathcal{M}
transform these high-level operations into
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{LIA}}
.

4.1
Naive Encoding

The most intuitive mapping
\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{Naive}}
is a direct lifting of the semantics defined in Fig.
3
.
Under this encoding, a date
a
is represented as a tuple of three integer variables (
a_{y},a_{m},a_{d}
) representing the year, month, and day respectively. To ensure this represents a valid date according to Def.
1
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{Naive}}
emits integer constraints
1\leq a_{m}\leq 12
and
1\leq a_{d}\leq\mathit{dim}(a_{y},a_{m})
, where
\mathit{dim}(a_{y},a_{m})
is short-hand for
\mathit{ITE}(a_{m}\in\{4,6,9,11\},30,\mathit{ITE}(a_{m}=2,\mathit{ITE}(\mathrm{leap}(a_{y}),29,28),31))
with
\mathit{leap}(a_{y})\Leftrightarrow(a_{y}\bmod 4=0\land(a_{y}\bmod 100\neq 0\lor a_{y}\bmod 400=0))
.

Date-Period Arithmetic.

\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{Naive}}
implements date-period arithmetic (Definition
4
) by directly unrolling the transition rules in Figure
3
into symbolic constraints.
For our running example of
a + Period(1, 2, 15
)—or equivalently
a+\langle 1,2,15\rangle
in the formal semantics—we encode a symbolic result date
a^{\prime}
via a sequence of intermediate symbolic states:

1.

Year/Month Addition.
Following the
Add-Period
rule, we first convert the period’s year and month to total months (
12\times 1+2=14
) and then apply the
Add-Months
rule, resulting in a date represented by integer variables
(y_{0},m_{0},d_{0})
. We emit constraints:

y_{0}=a_{y}+((a_{m}-1+14)\mathbin{\mathrm{div}}12)
and
m_{0}=((a_{m}-1+14)\bmod 12)+1
. The day is potentially rounded down by emitting the constraint
d_{0}=\text{ITE}(a_{d}>\mathit{dim}(y_{0},m_{0}),\mathit{dim}(y_{0},m_{0}),a_{d})
, with
\mathit{dim}()
expanded as before.

2.

Day Addition.
Now to add the 15 days from the period (which is a positive amount) to the symbolic date
(y_{0},m_{0},d_{0})
, we do not know if the result will be in the same month (following the
Add-Days
rule) or overflow into subsequent months (requiring the
Add-Days-Over
rule, potentially more than once). We encode this by iteratively computing a sequence of symbolic states
S_{0},\dots,S_{15}
, where each
S_{i}=|y_{i},m_{i},d_{i}|
using fresh integer variables (state
S_{0}
was computed above). For each step
i\in[0,14]
, the state
S_{i+1}
is determined by first checking if incrementing the day-of-month would cross a month boundary, and if so setting the date to be the 1st of the following month; the latter also requires checking if the month-increment also overflows to January of the following year. Formally,
S_{i+1}=ITE(d_{i}+1>\mathit{dim}(y_{i},m_{i}),ITE(m_{i}=12,|y_{i}+1,1,1|,|y_{i},m_{i}+1,1|),|y_{i},m_{i},d_{i}+1|)
, with
\mathit{dim}()
expanded as before. The final result
a^{\prime}
of date and period addition is the last such state, which is
S_{15}
in our example.

Note that when the number of days is negative, a similar encoding of the
Add-Days-Under-*
rules is performed.

As illustrated, to properly handle overflows, the naive encoding of date/period arithmetic requires a sequence of nested ITE operations proportional to the absolute number of days in the period offset
\lvert n_{d}\rvert
. The logical depth of the resulting formula is therefore
O(\lvert n_{d}\rvert)
.
7
7

7

The
Add-Days-Over
rule in Figure
3
performs addition in larger chunks per recursive step of the transition rules, but the recursion depth has the same complexity.
Such formulas pose challenges for SMT solvers, making this encoding inefficient for date/period arithmetic.

Date Comparison.

Comparisons between date values (e.g.,
a^{\prime}<b
) are mapped to a lexicographical ordering over the integer components, as per Definition
2
, e.g.
(a^{\prime}_{y}<b_{y})\lor(a^{\prime}_{y}=b_{y}\land a^{\prime}_{m}<b_{m})\lor(a^{\prime}_{y}=b_{y}\land a^{\prime}_{m}=b_{m}\land a^{\prime}_{d}<b_{d})
.

4.2
Epoch-based Encoding

The Epoch-based encoding (
\mathcal{M}_{\text{epoch}}
) encodes a date
a
as a single integer variable, representing the number of days elapsed since a fixed epoch
\eta
, similar to the representation of Unix time. But what epoch should we choose? Our objective is to reduce the complexity of the integer arithmetic involved in encoding date/period operations. To that end, we make the key design decision to choose the epoch date
\eta
as “March 1, 2000”, which simplifies calculations significantly. From this epoch, leap days (“February 29”) occur exactly every 1461 days (
365\times 4+1
), and such a 1461-day cycle repeats regularly up to 25 times in both directions until hitting either 1900 or 2100, which are non-leap years.

Date-Period Arithmetic.

How
\mathcal{M}_{\text{epoch}}
handles date and period arithmetic depends on the components of the period
\langle n_{y},n_{m},n_{d}\rangle
:

1.

Only Adding Days.
If a period contains only a day component
n_{d}
, the arithmetic is a trivial integer addition:
a^{\prime}_{\Delta}=a_{\Delta}+n_{d}
. This reduces the
O(\lvert n_{d}\rvert)
logical depth as in the Naive encoding to
O(1)
arithmetic complexity.

2.

Arithmetic Involving Year/Month.

If
n_{y}
or
n_{m}
are non-zero (e.g., adding “6 months”), we cannot adjust
a_{\Delta}
directly without knowing which calendar year and month it represents (e.g., to determine how many days “6 months” means). So, we must temporarily recover the corresponding triple representation
|a_{y},a_{m},a_{d}|=\eta+_{\mathcal{P}}\langle 0,0,a_{\Delta}\rangle
.
Because of our careful choice of epoch
|2000,3,1|
, this can be done efficiently by solving a system of Euclidean divisions and modulos that account for the 1461-day leap cycle.
Once the components are recovered,
n_{y}\times 12+n_{m}
months are added using the same algorithm as the Naive approach. The resulting triple is then re-encoded into an integer
a^{\prime\prime}_{\Delta}
representing days since epoch
\eta
. Finally,
n_{d}
is added via simple integer addition if needed to get the final result, so
a^{\prime}_{\Delta}=n_{d}+a^{\prime\prime}_{\Delta}
.

The main advantage of the Epoch-based encoding is that the addition of the days component does not require deeply nested if-then-else clauses for determining overflows across month boundaries, as in the Naive encoding. However, the trade-off is that arithmetic with months and years requires translation to and from the Naive encoding, shifting the solver’s burden from managing logical depth (ITE branching) to handling lots of integer division and modulo constraints.

Date Comparison.

In this encoding, a date comparison
a^{\prime}<b
is reduced to a single integer comparison
a^{\prime}_{\Delta}<b_{\Delta}
in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{LIA}}
, eliminating the disjunctions required by lexicographical ordering as in the Naive encoding.

4.3
Hybrid Encoding

We hypothesize that, in many real-world
DateSAT
problems, arithmetic is often performed either only with years/months or only with days, but only rarely with a mix of both. The Hybrid encoding (
\mathcal{M}_{\text{hybrid}}
) attempts to minimize both logical depth and arithmetic complexity by maintaining a dual representation of each date value in both the Naive encoding (
a_{y},a_{m},a_{d}
) and the Epoch-based encoding (
a_{\Delta}
). The key idea is to use the Naive encoding’s triple for efficient year/month arithmetic, and to use the Epoch-based offset for efficient day-only arithmetic and comparisons. Instead, this strategy utilizes a lazy mechanism to keep the representations consistent only as-and-when required, with concrete encoder level boolean flags
a_{\mathit{ymd}}
and
a_{\eta}
indicating whether each representation currently holds the up-to-date encoding for a given date value. This avoids introducing unnecessary arithmetic into the formula when a sequence of operations can be handled entirely within one representation. To summarize, a date value
a
is encoded as a 6-tuple
(a_{y},a_{m},a_{d},a_{\Delta},a_{\mathit{ymd}},a_{\eta})
, with the invariant that
a_{\mathit{ymd}}\vee a_{\eta}
is always
\mathrm{true}
, that
a_{\mathit{ymd}}\rightarrow``a\text{ is the date }|a_{y},a_{m},a_{d}|
”, and
a_{\eta}\rightarrow``a\text{ is the date }(\eta+_{\mathcal{P}}\langle 0,0,a_{\Delta}\rangle)^{\prime\prime}
.

Converting between representations.

During constraint generation, whenever
a_{\mathit{ymd}}
or
a_{\eta}
is
\mathrm{false}
and the corresponding representation is needed for an operation, we can “fix” the inconsistency by either converting
a_{\Delta}
to
|a_{y},a_{m},a_{d}|
or vice versa using the same logic as described in Section
4.2
, and setting the corresponding flag to
\mathrm{true}
.

Date-Period Arithmetic.

\mathcal{M}_{\text{hybrid}}
dynamically selects and uses the most efficient representation of a date for the arithmetic based on the period
\langle n_{y},n_{m},n_{d}\rangle
:

1.

Only Adding Days.
If the period contains only a non-zero day component, we use the epoch-based encoding of
a
—this representation is first fixed if
a_{\eta}
is
\mathrm{false}
so that
a_{\Delta}
becomes consistent. Then,
n_{d}
is directly added to
a_{\Delta}
, resulting in
a^{\prime}=(a_{y},a_{m},a_{d},a^{\prime}_{\Delta},\mathrm{false},\mathrm{true})
. Note that the
|y,m,d|
components are now stale, but subsequent comparisons and day-only arithmetic should be efficient.

2.

Arithmetic Involving Year/Month.
If
n_{m}
or
n_{y}
is non-zero, then we need to know the correct component values
|a_{y},a_{m},a_{n}|
, which are derived if
a_{\mathit{ymd}}=\mathrm{false}
. Then,
n_{y}\times 12+n_{m}
months are added using the same method as in Section
4.1
. If
n_{d}=0
, then we are done and the final result will have flag
a_{\mathit{ymd}}
set but flag
a_{\eta}
will be
\mathrm{false}
, indicating that the Epoch-based encoding is inconsistent. Otherwise, we follow the procedure in step 1 to
n_{d}
add days.

Date Comparison.

Date comparisons like
a^{\prime}<b
are encoded depending on which representations of
a^{\prime}
and
b
are consistent. If
a^{\prime}_{\eta}\land b_{\eta}
, then
a^{\prime}_{\Delta}<b^{\prime}_{\Delta}
suffices. If
a^{\prime}_{\mathit{ymd}}\land b_{\mathit{ymd}}
, then we emit the lexicographic comparison as in Def.
2
/ Section
4.1
. Otherwise, we fix whichever operand (
a^{\prime}
or
b
) has an inconsistent Epoch-based encoding and perform the integer comparison.

4.4
Alpha-Beta encoding

From the previous sections, we observe that year/month arithmetic is easy to encode when we have some information about where in the calendar a date is in, and day arithmetic is easy to encode in an epoch-based representation. The Alpha-Beta encoding takes a different spin on a hybrid encoding: keeping track of
both
of these views in one concise representation.
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}
represents a date as a 2-tuple of integer variables
(a_{\alpha\Delta},a_{\beta\Delta})
, where
a_{\alpha\Delta}
represents the number of total months since the epoch
\eta
, and
a_{\beta\Delta}
represents the number of days elapsed since the start of the month, such that
a_{\beta\Delta}=0
corresponds to the first day of a month. In other words, the pair
(a_{\alpha\Delta},a_{\beta\Delta})
represents the
(a_{\beta\Delta}+1)
-th day of the month obtained by advancing
a_{\alpha\Delta}
months from
\eta
. For example, given that our chosen epoch is “March 1, 2000’, the encoding
(2,5)
represents “May 6, 2000”.

Date-Period Arithmetic.

Consider the addition
a^{\prime}=a+_{\mathcal{P}}\langle n_{y},n_{m},n_{d}\rangle
. Since the integer
a_{\alpha\Delta}
indicates exactly which year/month a date belongs to, we define
\mathit{dim}(a_{\alpha\Delta})
as a short-hand similar to Section
4.1
, using ITEs and modulos.

1.

Year/Month Addition.
Following the
Add-Period
rule, we first compute an intermediate date value
a^{\prime\prime}
, for which
a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}=a_{\alpha\Delta}+(12\times n_{y}+n_{m})
. To ensure that the day remains valid in the new month
a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}
, we round-down as needed, so
a^{\prime\prime}_{\beta\Delta}=\min(a_{\beta\Delta},\mathit{dim}(a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}))
.

2.

Day Addition.
Now, to add a day component
n_{d}
to
a^{\prime\prime}
, we first compute another intermediate (though potentially invalid) date value
a^{\prime\prime\prime}=(a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta},a^{\prime\prime}_{\beta\Delta}+n_{d}
). We check if the second component (
a^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\beta\Delta}
) is valid—i.e., in the range
[0,\mathit{dim}(a^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}))
—and if so we are done with the final result
a^{\prime}=a^{\prime\prime\prime}
. If not, then this invalid
a^{\prime\prime\prime}
is converted to a solely Epoch-based encoding (as in Section
4.2
) to get a single integer
a^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\Delta}
, which is then re-converted to the Alpha-Beta encoding to get the final result
a^{\prime}
. Both these steps can be done by emitting
O(1)
division and modulo operations, thus avoiding the iterative process of the Naive encoding.

Date Comparison.

Comparisons
a^{\prime}<b
are performed via a lexicographical ordering over the pair:
(a^{\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}<b_{\alpha\Delta})\lor(a^{\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}=b_{\alpha\Delta}\land a^{\prime}_{\beta\Delta}<b_{\beta\Delta})

4.5
Alpha-Beta-Table Encoding

The Alpha-Beta-Table encoding (
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta\text{-}tab}
) uses the same Alpha-Beta representation
(a_{\alpha\Delta},a_{\beta\Delta})
to represent a date, but employs some extra optimizations to the arithmetic under the following special conditions: (i) this strategy only works on bounded
\textsc{DateSAT}{}^{[lb,ub]}
instances (ref. Section
3.3
) where
|1900,03,01|\leq lb
and
ub\leq|2100,02,28|
, and (ii) this strategy utilizes the theory-of-arrays in addition to linear integer arithmetic, thus encoding
DateSAT
constraints to
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{ALIA}}
, the combination of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathit{LIA}}
with the theory of arrays. The key idea is to optimize away several arithmetic operations required by the standard Alpha-Beta encoding using pre-computed lookup tables. Within the aforementioned bounds, there is a 48-month repeating cycle where we can simplify the logic of determining (i) how many days are in a given month
a_{\alpha\Delta}
, and (ii) how many total days have elapsed since the epoch
\eta
when
a_{\alpha\Delta}
months have elapsed.

Date-Period Arithmetic.

Recall from Section
4.4
that year/month arithmetic requires expanding
\mathit{dim}(a_{\alpha\Delta})
—involving several ITEs and modulos—to correctly round-down the day component
a_{\beta\Delta}
; and for arithmetic involving days, we must convert back and forth between the Alpha-Beta encoding and the single-integer Epoch-based encoding (
a_{\Delta}
). The Alpha-Beta-Table encoding optimizes these operations with two pre-computed lookup tables—
\mathit{DIM}_{48}
(days-in-month) and
\mathit{DBM}_{48}
(days-before-month) for the 48 months in a 4-year leap cycle. For example,
\mathit{DIM}_{48}[2]=31
because “May 2000” has 31 days, and
\mathit{DBM}_{48}[2]=61
because the month starts 61 days after the epoch. Specifically, we re-define
\mathit{dim}(a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta})
as
\mathit{DIM}_{48}[a^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}\bmod 48]
, which is just one modulo operation and one array lookup.
For transforming to Epoch-based encoding, we have:
a_{\Delta}=\mathit{DBM}_{48}[a_{\alpha\Delta}\bmod 48]+a_{\beta\Delta}
, which is only one modulo, one array lookup, and one addition. So, adding a period of
n_{d}
days to a date
a
in Alpha-Beta-Table encoding first evaluates
a^{\prime}_{\Delta}=\mathit{DBM}_{48}[a_{\alpha\Delta}\bmod 48]+a_{\beta\Delta}+n_{d}
. Then, this Epoch-based encoding is converted back to
(a^{\prime}_{\alpha\Delta},a^{\prime}_{\beta\Delta})
by finding the highest concrete value
\alpha\in[0,48)
such that
\mathit{DBM}_{48}[\alpha]\leq a^{\prime}_{\Delta}\bmod 1461
, then setting
a^{\prime}_{\alpha\Delta}=\alpha+48\times(a^{\prime}_{\Delta}\mathbin{\mathrm{div}}1461)
, and
a^{\prime}_{\beta\Delta}=a^{\prime}_{\Delta}\bmod 1461-\mathit{DBM}_{48}[\alpha]
.

4.6
Correctness of Encodings

We have formally verified the correctness of our various encoding strategies using Verus
[
20
]
. We treat the Naive encoding as the ground truth—as it is a direct lifting of the semantics in Fig.
3
(with the exception of the granularity of unrolling the
Add-Days-*
rules). We then show that the Epoch-based, Hybrid, and Alpha-Beta encodings are equivalent to the Naive encoding; that is, well-formed
DateSAT
constraints are equisatisfiable with each encoding.

For this mechanization, we formalize a restricted subset of the
DateSAT
grammar called
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
, to reduce syntactic sugar and simplify the proofs. For example, we only formalize relational operators ‘
<
’ and ‘
=
’, since other comparisons can be re-written in terms of these. More notably: the formalization only allows date literal expressions ‘
Date(y, m, d)
’ when
y
,
m
, and
d
are integer constants. The de-sugaring of general date-literal expressions
Date(e1, e2, e3)
is performed by returning a fresh date variable
x
, and separately adding constraints
x.year() == e1
,
x.month() == e2
, and
x.day() == e3
.

Definition 5
(Well-formedness of
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraints)

We say that a
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraint is well-formed if for every literal expression
Date(y,m,d)
, we have that
valid(|y,m,d|)
holds true as per Def.
1
.

Definition 6
(Closed
DateSAT
constraints)

Let
E
be an environment that maps identifiers to concrete values (integers, booleans, or dates). We say a well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraint is closed with respect to environment
E
if (i) for all
INT_VAR
expressions
x
, the mapping
E(x)
exists and results in an integer value, (ii) for all
BOOl_VAR
expressions
y
, the mapping
E(y)
exists and results in a boolean value, and (iii) for all
DATE_VAR
expressions
z
, the mapping
E(z)
exists and results in a valid date
|z_{y},z_{m},z_{d}|
.

Definition 7
(Evaluation)

Let
\varepsilon
be the name of an encoding strategy (such as “Naive” or “Alpha-Beta”). Then, for any well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraint
C
and environment
E
such that
C
is closed with respect to
E
, we write
eval(C,E,\varepsilon)
to be the boolean result of emitting an
\varepsilon
-encoded constraint
C
to an SMT solver and evaluating it on the concrete assignment
E
.

We prove the following theorems in the accompanying artifact, available at

https://github.com/cmu-pasta/DateSAT/tree/main/proofs
.

(Equivalence of Epoch-based encoding)

Given any well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraint
C
and environment

such that
C
is closed with respect to

, then it must be that
eval(C,E,\text{Epoch-based })=eval(C,E,\text{Naive })
.

Given any well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}

and environment

such that

is closed with respect to

.

Theorem 4.3
(Equivalence of Alpha-Beta encoding)

Given any well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}
constraint
C
and environment

such that

is closed with respect to

, then it must be that

.

(Equisatisfiability of Epoch-based encoding)

Given any well-formed
\textsc{DateSAT}^{\#}

, then

is SAT by the Naive encoding iff

).

(Equisatisfiability of Hybrid encoding)

Given any well-formed

).

Given any well-formed

LLM-Synthesized Constraints.

To validate the correctness of
DateSAT
solvers, we first construct a benchmark of simple constraints that exercise semantic corner cases in calendar arithmetic. To this end, we make use of LLMs provided with context, the
DateSAT
constraint language, and a small number of manually crafted example constraints; we prompt the LLMs to generate new constraints exercising interesting edge cases. This process yields a set of
100 distinct
DateSAT

with only free date variables, focusing specifically on date and period arithmetic. Appendix

provides more details.

Grammar-Sampled Constraints.

To stress-test the performance of
DateSAT
solvers, we make use of randomized grammar-based sampling. Specifically, we use Fandango
[
37
]
, a fuzzer with grammar-based input generation capabilities, to generate thousands of random
DateSAT
constraints. We execute the generated constraints with the Naive encoding strategy and select those that fall into one of the following categories: (1) satisfiable constraints that require a long execution time, (2) unsatisfiable constraints that require a long execution time, and (3) constraints that could not be naively solved within a time limit of 60 seconds. Selecting 50 constraints from each category yields a total of
150 constraints
. Appendix
0.B.2
provides more details.

Legally Grounded Constraints.

To demonstrate the practical utility of
DateSAT
in real-world settings, we additionally construct a suite of constraints derived from the U.S. Internal Revenue Code
[
26

. We use LLMs to translate selected statutes and provisions into our constraint language. Beyond date and period variables, this language supports integer and boolean variables and logical connectives, enabling us to encode common real-world patterns such as threshold conditions (e.g., numeric cutoffs), guarded obligations (e.g.,
if
–
then
applicability conditions), and disjunctive exception structures. This subset consists of
200 unique constraints
, each corresponding to a real-world example, and enables us to evaluate the performance of our tool on practically motivated use cases. Appendix
0.B.3
provides more details.

7
Discussion

Threats to Validity.

To mitigate threats to construct validity arising from implementation errors, we performed differential testing across our various
DateSAT
solvers and validated all SAT solutions using Python’s
dateutil
library as the ground truth. Our internal validity relies on the large size of the benchmark suite (450 constraints). To mitigate threats to the external validity of
DateSATBench
, we diversified the sources from which we drew our constraints; however, we cannot guarantee that the results will remain consistent with a different choice of benchmarks or different distribution of constraints.

Artifact Availability.

We are making the
DateSAT
solver
8
8

8

https://github.com/cmu-pasta/DateSAT
and
DateSATBench
benchmark suite
9
9

9

https://github.com/cmu-pasta/DateSATBench
open-source for reproducibility, tool use, and further research. Our current implementation takes a JSON input of
DateSAT
constraints, and supports a command-line interface, a programmatic Python API, and a Model Context Protocol endpoint for AI tool use. Our implementation uses Z3 as the backend SMT solver, and, for usability purposes, can use Max-SAT with soft constraints on free variables to produce dates closer to the current date.

Potential Future Work.

While our current implementation does not extend an SMT solver directly, it should be possible to add explicit support for dates and periods as a new theory in SMT-LIB
[
4
]
. It should be straightforward to port
DateSATBench
from the current syntax to SMT-LIB format if a theory of dates is supported. Doing so will also enable researching more efficient decision procedures that are built in to an SMT solver directly. We also note the potential for solving bounded
DateSAT
instances faster by encoding into bitvectors. However, a naive translation of the integer constraints into bitvector arithmetic is not sufficient, since bitvector operations may overflow depending on the chosen bitwidth. We therefore do not consider that using bitvectors would make an appropriate baseline without substantial additional guardrails. Moreover, the first four encoding strategies we propose (Sections
4.1
–
4.4
) support unbounded
DateSAT
instances, which neither enumeration nor bitvector-based approaches can support.

8
Related Work

Our work is influenced by past work in developing specialized support for strings in SMT solvers
[
19
,
32
,
39
,
21
,
34
,
6
,
38
,
5
,
24
]
based on domain-specific insights as well as applications to symbolic execution of programs that manipulate strings.

To the best of our knowledge, no explicit support exists for date and calendar logic in SMT solvers today. The main applications of constraint solving to temporal values in the literature is in modeling real-time systems
[
13
]
or optimizing job scheduling across machines
[
3
,
35
]
; these techniques do not deal with absolute date representations.

We build upon prior work on formalizing the semantics of date and period arithmetic
[
23
]
. CUTECat
[
17
]
performs symbolic execution of programs written in a domain-specific language for legal analysis. This tool needs to reason about dates (similar to our example in Section
2
, Figure
2
); however, the paper indicates that due to the complexity of date semantics, they concretize date values at the cost of completeness. Our work directly addresses this gap.

More recently, the research community has begun to curate datasets of date- and time-related bugs in software
[
33
]
, as well as benchmarks aimed at evaluating the temporal reasoning capabilities of LLMs
[
14
,
15
]
. Although these efforts do not directly target constraint solvability or program verification, they underscore the growing prevalence and practical importance of date/time logic in modern software systems. We hope that
DateSATBench
can serve as a foundation for future work on principled reasoning, analysis, and verification of programs involving temporal computations.

9
Conclusion

Reasoning about dates and calendar periods symbolically is very tricky, and so far there has been limited tool support in doing so automatically. We introduced
DateSAT
, the first framework for expressing and solving constraints involving date and period arithmetic. We presented five different strategies for solving date and period constraints, and are making our solver implementation and a dataset of diverse
DateSAT
constraints openly available. We hope that such reasoning capabilities will be integrated into various applications from program verification to Agentic AI-based applications that rely on accurate reasoning of date values.

0.B.1
LLM-Synthesized Constraints

Methodology.
We construct this benchmark using a lightweight LLM-based prompting pipeline based on the Claude Sonnet 4.5 model. The model is tasked with generating synthetic
DateSAT
constraints using a structured system prompt that specifies the generation objective, the constraint language, the set of valid operations, and a rigid output schema. To encourage well-formed outputs, we additionally provide a small set of manually curated examples for few-shot guidance.

To increase diversity, we vary the prompting context across invocations using manually defined coverage specifications. We employ five coverage tags: (1)
Logical Operators
, promoting extensive use of logical connectives; (2)
Month–Day Variation
, targeting subtleties in month- versus day-based period arithmetic; (3)
Year–Day Variation
, exploring interactions between year- and day-based periods; (4)
Symbolic Variables
, encouraging the use of symbolic variables; and (5)
Property Access
, emphasizing constraints that rely heavily on accessing a year, month, or day field of a date.

Finally, the pipeline includes a feedback loop in which parse errors reported by the constraint-language parser are fed back to the model and corrected in subsequent generations, ensuring that all retained constraints conform to the required syntax.

0.B.2
Grammar-Sampled Constraints

Methodology.
We construct this benchmark using a two-step process. First, we use
Fandango
, a grammar-based fuzzer, to generate thousands of random
DateSAT
constraints from a restricted variant of the grammar shown in Figure
6
. This variant supports only date and period operations, excluding integer variables and Boolean logic, allowing us to isolate the core encoding strategies without confounding factors. Redundant canonical forms are removed to improve readability, and Fandango enforces runtime checks to ensure all generated date constructors are within valid bounds. Consequently, all generated constraints are correct by construction.

Second, as described in the main paper, we select constraints that stress the limits of our tool. We evaluate all generated constraints using the naive encoding strategy, rank them by solving difficulty (measured by solving time), and select the 50 hardest constraints from each outcome category: satisfiable, unsatisfiable, and timeout (60,s limit).

0.B.3
Legally Grounded Constraints

Methodology.
We construct this benchmark using a two-stage automated extraction pipeline. In the first stage, we parse all sections of the U.S. Internal Revenue Code (IRC) into structured records and filter them to identify clauses likely to contain calendrical requirements. We retain records that contain at least one of five high-signal temporal pattern classes (e.g., explicit deadline expressions such as “no later than” or calendar dates like “January 1, 2026”) and that match at least five terms from a curated set of 51 keywords covering period expressions, temporal relations, tax-specific anchors, effective-date cues, and date literals. We explicitly exclude clauses that mention time-related information, such as time-of-day or time-zone references.

In the second stage, we randomly sample 200 filtered records and generate corresponding
DateSAT
constraints using an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.5) guided by a strict system prompt that specifies the constraint language and enforces a rigid output schema. All generated constraints are validated by our constraint-language parser prior to inclusion.
