A comparison of the psychological consequences and social support of spontaneous and induced abortion
Authors: Roghaye khodaei, Shirin Shahbazi Sighaldeh, Shima Haghani, Maryam Nekoolaltak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbortion, whether spontaneous or induced, is a challenging experience in a woman’s life. This study aimed to compare the psychological consequences and perceived social support in two groups of women with abortion experiences in East Azerbaijan Province, Iran.MethodsTo enhance the representativeness of the target population, convenience sampling was conducted from both formal centers such as healthcare facilities and informal settings such as women’s parks, mosques, and residential neighborhoods in two cities of Bonab and Maragheh. Data were collected using the validated DASS-21 and MSPSS questionnaires. The post-abortion interval ranged from 3 months to 5 years. Logistic regression analyses adjusted for demographic confounders. ResultsOf the 611 participating women, 321 (52.5%) had experienced a spontaneous abortion and 290 (47.5%) had undergone an induced abortion. Women in the induced abortion group were significantly more likely to be aged 30–39 years and reported significantly lower income levels. A significantly higher proportion of women who were 49 to 60 months post-abortion were in the induced abortion group. Stress scores were significantly higher among women with induced abortions. While perceived social support was generally low in both groups, the patterns differed: support from friends was significantly greater in the induced abortion group, whereas support from significant others was significantly higher in the spontaneous abortion group.ConclusionWomen with history of induced abortions reported higher stress levels than those with spontaneous abortions. Both groups faced insufficient social support and family support during this experience. Implementing family education programs, strengthening various support systems, time-sensitive psychological screening and interventions, is recommended to mitigate adverse post-abortion consequences.Keywords: Spontaneous abortion, Induced abortion, Stress, Psychological, Social support

Introduction

Abortion, defined as the termination of pregnancy before the fetus reaches viability, can occur spontaneously or be induced [1]. Spontaneous abortion has a wide range of causes, including genetic factors such as chromosomal abnormalities, and non-genetic factors such as infections, endocrine disorders, thrombophilia, environmental exposures, and socioeconomic conditions. Despite significant research advancements, the etiology of nearly 50% of spontaneous abortions remains unexplained [2].

In contrast, induced abortion refers to the intentional termination of pregnancy before the fetus becomes viable [1]. Globally, between 2015 and 2019, approximately 121 million unintended pregnancies occurred annually, 61% of which—equivalent to about 73 million per year—ended in abortion [3].

In countries such as Iran, legal, religious, and sociocultural constraints pose significant challenges to data collection, leading to widespread underreporting of abortions [4]. The reasons for induced abortion are complex and multifactorial. Recent studies suggest that the decision to undergo an abortion is typically shaped by a combination of personal and social factors. Major contributing factors include financial constraints, relationship difficulties, the desire to delay childbearing, fear of social stigma (especially among unmarried women), and concerns regarding maternal or fetal health [5]. Research in Iran has indicated that over two-thirds of women describe the decision to undergo an induced abortion as a multidimensional process influenced by various concurrent factors [6], with decisions further shaped by specific cultural and social contexts.

Unsafe abortions, particularly those performed without medical supervision, are associated with serious complications such as hemorrhage, infection, organ failure, psychological distress, and even maternal death [7]. Both spontaneous and induced abortions can result in psychological consequences, including anxiety, depression, guilt, grief, and symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which may persist long-term and affect multiple aspects of a woman’s life [8, 9].

In contexts where abortion is legally or socially stigmatized, access to post-abortion mental health services is often limited, increasing the risk of adverse psychological consequences. A systematic review reported that the prevalence of post-abortion depression in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, including Iran, reaches as high as 43.1%, which is above the global average [8].

Despite the existing body of research, comparative studies on the psychological and social consequences of spontaneous versus induced abortion—particularly in Iran—are scarce. Cultural and legal barriers often hinder open reporting and the conduct of empirical studies in this domain [10–13].

Moreover, recent shifts in Iran’s population policies, coupled with reduced access to contraceptive methods and increased awareness of medical abortion options, may have contributed to a rise in the incidence of induced abortion. Nevertheless, the absence of national data remains a critical barrier to evidence-based policy-making.

The present study aimed to compare the psychological consequences and perceived social support among women with a history of spontaneous and induced abortion in East Azerbaijan Province, Iran. The findings are expected to provide deeper insights into women’s post-abortion mental health needs and inform culturally appropriate reproductive health services and policy development.

Methodology

Study design, participants and inclusion criteria

This research was a descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study conducted in 2023, aimed at examining and comparing the psychological and social consequences of abortion among two groups of women with a history of spontaneous and induced abortion in the cities of Bonab and Maragheh. The study population consisted of women of reproductive age residing in the two cities of Bonab and Maragheh. The samples included women with a history of abortion who met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were married women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years) who were literate, had a history of abortion (induced or spontaneous) in the past three months to five years, had no documented history of mental illness, and consented to participate in the study.

Sample size

To determine the minimum sample size, a confidence level of 95% and an estimation precision of d = 0.04 were used. After substitution into the formula below, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be n = 580 participants.

Data collection procedure

The sampling process began in formal healthcare settings, including hospitals and health centers, in the cities of Bonab and Maragheh in East Azerbaijan Province.

Initially, Medical records of women with a history of abortion within the past five years were reviewed and the eligible individuals were contacted via telephone and invited to participate in the study. However, due to incomplete data in medical records and the national electronic health system (SIB), along with the reluctance of some women to visit healthcare facilities, community-based sampling was concurrently carried out in informal public settings—such as women’s parks, mosques, and residential neighborhoods—to ensure broader and more inclusive population coverage.

Convenience sampling continued from July 25 to October 17, 2023. Ultimately, 197 questionnaires were completed online, and 414 were completed in person, totaling 611 questionnaires. Some online questionnaires were completed with delays, but due to the value of the data, they were not excluded from the study. Thus, the total number of samples increased from 580 to 611.

Post-abortion interval

In alignment with previous studies indicating that psychological symptoms following abortion may persist over extended durations, the present study adopted an inclusion window ranging from 3 months to 5 years post-abortion [18, 19]. Variability in the time elapsed since the most recent abortion was observed among participants, and this factor—along with other sociodemographic variables—was statistically controlled in the analyses to minimize potential confounding effects.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Statistical tests were selected based on the type and level of measurement of variables, as well as the distribution characteristics of the data.

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions) were used to summarize the sample characteristics. For comparisons of mean scores across groups, one-way ANOVA was applied when the assumption of normality was met, assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In cases of non-normal distribution or ordinal-level data, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed as a non-parametric alternative.

To evaluate the impact of abortion type on psychological consequences while controlling for potential confounders such as age, education, and post-abortion interval, logistic regression analysis was performed. Demographic variables were entered into the model as potential confounders or effect modifiers to enhance the accuracy of the findings. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Human ethics and consent to participate

Ethical approval

This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Midwifery and the School of Rehabilitation Sciences, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, under the number IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1402.078.

Institutional access

An official letter of introduction was provided to hospitals and health centers in the cities of Bonab and Maragheh.

Informed consent

All participants were provided with a clear and simplified explanation of the study, including their right to withdraw at any stage. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Confidentiality and anonymity of the collected data were strictly maintained throughout the research process.

Comparison of demographic and individual characteristics between women with a history of spontaneous and induced abortion

Statistical analyses revealed significant demographic differences between the two groups. Women in the induced abortion group were significantly more likely to be in the 30–39 age range (45.5% vs. 34.6%; χ² = 9.086, P = 0.028, h = 0.122, 95% CI:0.061 to 0.209), whereas spontaneous abortions were more frequently reported among women under the age of 20 (8.4% vs. 4.8%).

The mean age of participants’ spouses was also significantly higher in the induced abortion group, with 42.1% of husbands aged ≥ 40 years compared to 26.5% in the spontaneous abortion group (χ² = 31.858, P < 0.001, h = 0.228, 95% CI: 0.150 to 0.302).

In terms of family income, women in the induced abortion group reported significantly lower income levels, with 33.7% earning less than 100 million IRR per month, compared to 20.1% in the spontaneous abortion group (χ² = 15.138, P = 0.002, h = 0.158, 95% CI:0.090 to 0.245).

Participants in the time range of 49 to 60 months post-abortion were significantly higher proportion in the induced abortion group compared to the spontaneous abortion group (29.7% vs. 19.0%; χ² = 13.456, P = 0.019, h = 0.148, 95% CI: 0.097 to 0.241).

However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of marriage duration (P = 0.822, h = 0.050), women’s educational level (P = 0.510, h = 0.047), husbands’ educational level (P = 0.459, h = 0.050), or employment status (P > 0.05, h < 0.07).

The MSPSS yields a total score ranging from 12 to 84. The mean total score was 33.01 in the spontaneous abortion group and 33.33 in the induced abortion group, indicating similarly low levels of perceived social support across both groups.

However, analysis of the MSPSS subscales revealed distinct patterns in the sources of perceived support:

Friend support was significantly higher in the induced abortion group (P < 0.001), with a moderate effect size (d = 0.31; 95% CI:0.15 to 0.47).

Support from significant others (such as partners or close emotional contacts) was reported significantly more in the spontaneous abortion group (P = 0.002), with a negative effect size (d = − 0.25; 95% CI: − 0.40 to − 0.09).

No statistically significant differences were observed between groups in terms of family support (P = 0.585, d = − 0.050; 95% CI: − 0.20 to 0.11) or overall perceived social support (P = 0.720, d = 0.029; 95% CI: − 0.13 to 0.19). The very small effect sizes and confidence intervals crossing zero suggest no clinically meaningful difference in these domains.

These findings indicate that while the overall level of perceived social support was similarly low across both groups, the sources of that support differed significantly: women with induced abortion reported more support from friends, whereas those with spontaneous abortion perceived greater support from significant others.

Discussion

Based on the findings of this study, which aimed to compare the psychological consequences and perceived social support among women with a history of spontaneous and induced abortion in two cities in East Azerbaijan Province, 47.5% of participants had experienced induced abortion, while 52.5% had experienced spontaneous abortion. Absolute abortion rates and the proportion of spontaneous vs. induced abortions may vary depending on each country’s legal context and access to reproductive health services [20].

An analysis of the participants’ demographic characteristics revealed that women with induced abortions were more likely to belong to families with older spouses and lower socioeconomic status. However, due to the inherent limitations of cross-sectional studies, it is not possible to draw definitive conclusions about a causal relationship between poverty and induced abortion. Moreover, national studies have reported conflicting results in this area. For instance, a study by Erfani in Tehran on 2,934 married women aged 15–49 found that the rate of induced abortion was higher among women with higher income levels [21].

One notable finding was that women who had undergone an induced abortion were more frequently represented in the 4–5 years post-abortion timeframe. This pattern could reflect a decrease in abortion rates in recent years; alternatively, it might indicate delayed onset of psychological consequences or women’s reluctance to participate in studies in the early years following abortion. Such a trend highlights the potential long-term psychological impact of induced abortion, which may not be captured in short-term studies. This is consistent with a cohort study by Lundin et al. in Sweden, which showed that some women—especially those with a history of high anxiety and low social support—are at increased risk for persistent PTSD symptoms. These findings underscore the need for longitudinal, time-sensitive research designs [22].

Analysis of scores on the DASS-21 tool showed that stress was the only psychological variable with a statistically significant difference between the two groups, with women who had experienced induced abortion reporting higher levels of stress. This finding aligns with an Iranian study by Alipanahpour and colleagues in Shiraz. Contributing factors may include cultural taboos, inadequate family support, and feelings of guilt or fear of judgment—factors that may discourage women from seeking professional help and exacerbate psychological symptoms [23]. Similar patterns have been observed in other countries. For example, Kukulskienė and Žemaitienė, in a cross-sectional online study conducted in Lithuania, reported that abortion may be associated with persistent psychological symptoms, including PTSD [24].

Likewise, Kulathilaka et al., in a case-control study in Sri Lanka, reported high rates of depression and unresolved grief post-abortion, highlighting the need for psychiatric intervention [25]. In Pakistan, Shakeel et al. found in a cross-sectional study that high levels of post-abortion stress were related to with factors such as age, marital status, and number of children [26].

Collectively, the findings of the present study and previous research demonstrate that abortion—particularly induced abortion—may associated with significant psychological consequences and underscores the importance of timely identification of these issues and provision of appropriate mental health services.

In the present study, although the overall levels of perceived social support were low in both groups, variations were evident in the sources of support.

Women who had experienced a spontaneous abortion reported higher levels of support from significant others—particularly spouses—whereas women with induced abortions were more likely to rely on friends for support. This difference may be explained by the relationship between social stigma surrounding induced abortion and the tendency to rely on non-family support sources.

A particularly concerning finding was the low level of family support reported by women in both groups. In the case of spontaneous abortion, it is possible that the family’s inattention stems from considering it a natural and expected event, while in cases of induced abortion, fear of judgment and social exclusion may lead to secrecy and reduced support. This reveals the inadequacy of traditional family support systems in the face of reproductive crises.

Given the central role of the family in Iranian culture, it is essential to improve psychological literacy among family members, especially in dealing with women’s reproductive health challenges.

A study conducted at the University Clinical Center of Belgrade involving 433 women with induced abortion experience revealed that 50.1% of participants had informed their mothers, 23.1% their fathers, 87.3% their partners, and 42.3% their friends; only 1.6% had not informed anyone. The findings further indicated that older married women with higher education levels and childbearing experience received the greatest support from their spouses. Conversely, younger unmarried women primarily received support from their parents, particularly their mothers [27].

In another descriptive-correlational study conducted in Pakistan on 150 women with a history of spontaneous abortion, 40.7% of the participants reported reduced self-esteem and feelings of inadequacy. This finding highlights the need to establish psychological support services in healthcare centers as part of a comprehensive care system for women [28].

In summary, patterns of social support reception are influenced by factors such as abortion type, age, marital status, family circumstances, and socio-cultural contexts. Crucially, it is essential to establish platforms that provide women with free, non-judgmental access to support resources tailored to their needs and preferences, thereby preventing potential psychological and social consequences.

One strength of current study was the expansion of sampling to informal settings, which broadened and diversified access to the target population. However, the fact that only about one-sixth of the 611 participating women were recruited from formal healthcare centers indicates that the health system has limited capacity to identify and follow up with women with a history of abortion, highlighting the need for comprehensive planning in this area.

Conclusion

Women with abortion experience, whether spontaneous or induced, face insufficient social support, and both groups require attention and tailored care. The family institution has failed to effectively fulfill its supportive role during these critical circumstances. Women with induced abortion histories experience higher stress levels, necessitating systematic screening and appropriate long-term interventions. Establishing specialized pre- and post-abortion counseling services, implementing time-sensitive and sustained psychological care, education and strengthening diverse support systems could mitigate post-abortion consequences.
