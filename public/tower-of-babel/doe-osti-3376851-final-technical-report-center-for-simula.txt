1.0 Introduction
1.1 Background
Magnetically confined nuclear fusion has the potential to provide the world with clean, safe, and
virtually limitless electrical power. The next step towards this goal is ITER, now under
construction in France, that has the objective of demonstrating net energy production. To heat the
plasma to the temperatures required for fusion, ITER will rely on application of high power
radio-frequency (RF) waves in the electron-cyclotron range of frequencies (ECRF) and ion-
cyclotron range of frequencies (ICRF), as well as neutral beam injection (NBI). Thus efficient
application of RF power is paramount to ITER's success. As such, the focus of this proposal was
on developing, validating and utilizing an advanced, leadership-class predictive simulation
capability to discover the controlling physical processes impacting the efficiency and reliability
of “coupling” the applied RF power to the dynamic fusion plasma. Furthermore, at the relevant
megawatt power levels, the application of radio-frequency (RF) fields may lead to the sputtering
of high-Z ions from the plasma-facing material structures; these can
subsequently migrate to the core plasma, possibly leading to radiative
collapse of the plasma.
A poorly confined region of plasma, the "scrape-off-layer" (SOL),
separates antenna structures from the pedestal and core plasma (see Fig. 1).
This region distributes the plasma power and particle exhaust from the
core, as well as shielding it from impurity ions and neutral particles that
can lead to energy losses. However, the SOL plasma can also lead to
parasitic losses, antenna and wall damage, impurity generation, and often
unpredictable performance. Prior to this grant, both the physics underlying
the coupling of RF power, and the functions of the SOL have been
evaluated in isolation. This lack of a predictive capability has led to
machine-specific stop-gap measures, and has limited experimental Figure 1:

the scale of present and planned computing resources.
The physics requirements present significant computational challenges. In addition to
formulating the nonlinear coupling of RF full-wave and SOL turbulence and transport solvers,
which have highly disparate time scales, on leadership class computers, predictive computation
of this system is complicated by sensitivity to geometric details in three-dimension (3D).
Accurate modeling requires solvers with meshes conforming to the geometry of the plasma
facing components, together with unstructured adaptive mesh refinement capabilities to address
highly variable density fluctuation scale lengths, and algorithmic capability to efficiently
simulate hundreds of millions of degrees of freedom in a large domain encompassing much of
the core, SOL, and far-SOL. By incorporating, modernizing, and accelerating existing
computational tools, as well as designing and developing new tools where required to fully
exploit available computing resources and provide the required physics fidelity, we have
delivered an integrated, capability simulation tool for discovery science. Achieving this goal
relied on a coherent, collaborative effort amongst our team of computer science, applied
mathematics, and physics experts, leveraging existing DOE technologies including the Exascale
Computing Project co-design Center for Efficient Exascale Discretizations, the Modular Finite
Element Framework, the Parallel Unstructured Meshing Infrastructure, and frameworks for high
performance computing (HPC) code integration such as the Integrated Plasma Simulator (IPS).

1.2 Original Project Objectives
To address the numerous physics and computational challenges described in the previous
section, we exploited leadership-class computing facilities to develop a predictive understanding
of the physical processes that affect optimal coupling of RF power to fusion plasmas, and to
subsequently design strategies to achieve robust and efficient RF operation. Our approach was to
extend existing, and develop new simulation capabilities, to incorporate the required physics and
to enable full utilization of available computing resources.
The core plasma response to applied RF power has already been well characterized, with mature
computational tools that are ready for integration into larger coupled models. Advanced
simulation tools also existed for the fluid turbulent and transport physics of the SOL plasma. In
understanding the self-consistent interaction between these two areas lies the opportunity for
discovery, for moving towards quantitative prediction and a capability to design robust RF
actuators, and elucidating the impact of RF on the production of impurities (cf. Fig. 2).
Developing such a predictive simulation capability is complicated by sensitivity to hardware
geometry, large global 3D problem sizes, sensitivity to (and possibly driving effects from) a
small scale boundary layer (the RF sheath), sensitivity to the non-local physics of core
absorption, strong anisotropies in transport along field lines, and the importance of both turbulent
and transport timescales, including the possibility of multiple spatial scales appearing within a

 3

non-linear coupling [e.g., short-wavelength (< 1 mm) slow waves appearing after density drops
in front of an antenna].

These challenges made the problem difficult to solve at the required fidelity, but the availability
of computing platforms at the petascale and beyond presented an opportunity for a collaborative
applied math, computer science, and physics effort to take on this challenge. Our scientific
objectives in this multi-disciplinary partnership were as follows:
 1. Develop an integrated simulation of the antenna + sheath + scrape-off-layer + core
 plasma system which fully utilizes leadership class computing resources to move towards
 a quantitative predictive capability for the response to RF power (cf. Fig. 2)
 2. Validate this predictive capability on appropriately diagnosed experiments including
 dedicated RF test stands, linear devices, and existing tokamaks.
 3. Use these tools to inform design of robust, impurity-mitigating RF heating and current
 drive sources for future fusion devices.
The specific applications for this simulation capability were anticipated to be:
 A. Predict the self-consistent interaction of RF power with the scrape-off-layer plasma in the
 fluid approximation, including the effects of ponderomotive density modification and
 fluid turbulence and transport, so as to achieve the most reliable and optimal RF actuator
 designs.

 4

 B. Predict high-Z impurity sputtering and transport induced by large RF-induced sheath
 potentials, and associated localized thermal loads, to guide development of impurity
 mitigation strategies for ITER.
To achieve these objectives we pursued a combination of new developments and leveraged
existing codes to cover the range of physics and geometric fidelities in RF solvers, as well as
fluid transport and turbulence representations of the plasma background at varying geometric
fidelities. These activities were performed under four physics thrusts: (i) The development of an
RF + SOL model hierarchy and components for Whole Device Modeling (WDM); (ii) The study
of RF interactions with plasma turbulence; (iii) The study of RF interactions with equilibrium
transport in the far SOL; and (iv) The study of RF impurity generation.

2.0 Project Accomplishments
2.1 ASCR capabilities and contributions
A key tool in the Center’s ASCR portfolio has been the Modular Finite Element Method (MFEM)
library [MFEM, Anderson 2020], which is designed for parallel scalability and GPU acceleration
and provides state-of-the-art support for high-performance meshing and adaptive mesh refinement,
high-order discretizations, and a range of advanced solver algorithms. MFEM is widely used
across the DOE and has been supported by FASTMath and the Exascale Computing Project (ECP).
MFEM is the foundation for several important software tools that were developed under this
project, which includes the following:
 ● The Physics Equation Translator for MFEM (Petra-M) [Shiraiwa 2017b, Shiraiwa 2023a,
 Shiraiwa 2023b] is a multiphysics FEM framework for rapid model development
 environment that is used for RF wave physics integration.
 ● The Stix code [Migliore 2023a, 2023b] has been developed for modeling full-wave
 electromagnetic (EM) wave propagation. The core solver utilizes MFEM’s advanced
 discretization algorithms to provide support for arbitrary order Nedelec and Raviart-
 Thomas basis functions, arbitrary order geometries, and adaptive mesh refinement on MPI-
 based HPC platforms.
 ● The MFEM Anisotropic Plasma Solver (MAPS) [Lore 2021] utilizes the same MFEM
 library as the Stix and Petra-M to facilitate coupling RF effects into the transport equations.
 In this project we succeeded in simulating diffusion-dominated test cases while
 demonstrating the effectiveness of high order discontinuous Galerkin (DG) finite elements
 and adaptive mesh refinement.
 ● PyMFEM is a Python wrapper for MFEM that allows users without C++ expertise to access
 the MFEM software technology. PyMFEM is distributed as open-source and is now used
 beyond the fusion research community, including in machine learning (ML) projects
     [Gillette 2022].

2.3 Nonlinear RF sheath boundary conditions
The formation of RF-enhanced sheaths at plasma-material interfaces (PMI’s) is one of the primary
mechanisms for impurity production during ICRF heating [Colas 2022; Myra 2021a]. A major
success of this project was the implementation of nonlinear RF sheath boundary conditions (BCs)
in Petra-M [Shiraiwa 2023a], Stix [Migliore 2023a], VSim [Jenkins 2017, Smithe 2022], as well
as detailed studies of the nonlinear behavior of RF sheaths [Poulos 2022]. Electromagnetic field
solvers in the ICRF regime typically treat material boundaries as perfectly conducting, thus
ignoring the effect of the RF sheath. Because the sheath is small relative to the scale of the device,
it can be approximated as a boundary condition with a finite impedance including capacitive and
resistive effects. Our hierarchy of full-wave solvers treats the RF sheath as a nonlinear BC that is
solved simultaneously with Maxwell equations. Furthermore, these implementations were verified
extensively with an earlier 2D simulation model [Kohno 2017].

2.4 RF edge interactions
As RF power propagates through the SOL to the core plasma, several parasitic interactions with
the SOL can occur resulting in modifications in the RF power as well as changes to the SOL itself.
RF power can interact with edge turbulence, enhance sheaths leading to impurity generation at the
plasma-material surface, and directly impact the far SOL through RF-driven ponderomotive forces
or RF-driven convective cells. In the following we summarize progress made in these areas as part
of this project.
Turbulence Effects. The RF waves which propagate through the SOL undergo a degree of
scattering at turbulent/filamentary structures. In this project conditions were identified under
which the interaction between RF waves and turbulence is significant, in ion cyclotron RF [Tierens
2022a, 2022b; Zhang 2022], lower hybrid [Biswas 2020, 2021, 2023], and helicon regimes [Lau
2020, 2021]. Conversely, this scattering process may affect the turbulence, e.g., by exerting
radiation pressure on the filaments [Valvis 2021]. An example of the profound effect that scattering
from turbulent blob-like structures has on LH waves in the Alcator C-Mod tokamak is shown in
Fig. 8. A multiscale simulation approach was used, which allows, for the first time, the inclusion
of full-wave scattering physics in ray-tracing/Fokker–Planck calculations [Biswas 2023]. In this
approach, full-wave scattering probabilities are calculated for a wave interacting with a statistical
ensemble of filaments (left panel in Fig. 8) and probabilities are coupled to ray-tracing equations
using radiative transfer theory. Including the effect of scattering from blobs brings the simulated

 10

counts of hard x-rays into much better agreement with experimental data taken from a discharge
with LH current drive.

RF Impurity Generation. A 1D model of RF sheath impurity generation has been investigated
using a numerical fluid plasma model able to capture the enhanced sputtering yield from the
Faraday screen and the plasma-facing components (PFCs) of an ICRF antenna [Elias 2019]. An
estimate of the impurity fluxes and of the gross-erosion rate has been provided and compared with
experimental data available in the literature. In addition, the kinetic behavior, which plays a crucial
role in RF sheaths, has been investigated with a hybrid particle-in-cell code (hPIC) estimating the
kinetic ion energy-angle distributions impacting the RF antenna and its dependence on various
plasma and RF sheath parameters [Elias 2021]. Furthermore, an evaluation of the sputtering of Al
ions from a helicon antenna and their transport in Proto-MPEX [Beers 2021a, 2021b] has been
carried out by making use of the GITR code [Younkin 2021].
The ion energy angle distribution (IEAD) at a plasma - metallic - interface has been simulated with
the high fidelity hPIC code [Khaziev 2015] discussed earlier. Capturing the ion kinetics is
important for an accurate description of plasma–material interactions, because the ion dynamics
plays a crucial role in RF sheaths. Notably, the hPIC model is able to capture the kinetic behavior
of the ions. Figure 9 shows results from a detailed investigation of the IEAD dependence on RF
frequency and magnetic field alignment that was carried out with hPIC [Elias 2021]. A comparison
of the IEAD distribution predicted by hPIC with the fluid impact energy and angle (black ‘x’
markers) is shown in Fig. 9 for a peak-to-peak RF bias of 200 V.

2.5 RF core components
The core wave physics has reached a degree of maturity significantly greater than the topics
mentioned in the previous subsections. A variety of simulation tools have been developed by the
worldwide fusion community, with different levels of physics fidelity ranging from the asymptotic
method(s) such as the geometrical optics approximation (ray-tracing) to the direct solution of
Maxwell’s equations (generally called a ‘full-wave’ approach). The use of ray-racing and beam-
tracing codes is quite common in the electron cyclotron (EC) [Prater 2008 and references therein]
and lower-hybrid (LH) frequency regimes [Bonoli 2014 and references therein] due to the very
small wavelength, whereas a full-wave approach is generally used for the ion-cyclotron (IC)
frequency regime [Budny 2012 and references therein].
However, a higher level of complexity is required to study the interaction between IC/LH waves
and fast particles. This interaction can play a very important role in tokamak plasmas, as recently
shown in JET experiments where the JET fusion record was achieved with the use of neutral beam
injection (NBI) together with ICRF [Jacquet 2023]. Several numerical approaches to investigating
this interaction with different levels of physics fidelity are available in the literature. The coupling
of ray-tracing/full-wave solvers with Fokker-Planck codes (in 1D or 2D velocity space) or with
Monte-Carlo (MC) codes is generally used to investigate the non-Maxwellian effects.

 14

In this project significant progress was made in the coupling between high-performance
computing-capable RF simulation tools, such as TORIC/TORLH [Brambilla 1999, Wright 2009]
and AORSA [Jaeger 2001], with the continuum Fokker-Planck code CQL3D [Harvey 1993] and
the Monte Carlo (MC) solver MCGO [Harvey 1980], respectively [Frank 2022a, 2022b; Petrov
2020, 2023] in order to take into account the role of fast ions (FI) (and electrons) generated either
by NBI or ICRF (and LH). In addition, the coupling of MCGO (taking into account the FI orbit
width) with TORIC allows us to evaluate fast ion losses in NSTX-U [Petrov 2023].

acceleration of NBI particles by the HHFW power, which could be of concern in terms of potential
damage to the vessel or wall.
We have also utilized the HPC capability of the spectral-based TORLH EM field solver [Wright
2009] and the FEM-based Petra-M field solver [Shiraiwa 2017b] to study lower hybrid wave
propagation and current drive, at unprecedented physics fidelity. It was found that the electron
distributions and LH power absorption from full-wave / Fokker Planck (TORLH/CQL3D) and ray
tracing / Fokker Planck (GENRAY/CQL3D) simulations are quantitatively similar in Alcator C-
Mod LH current drive experiments, thus providing verification for the use of ray tracing in the LH
regime [Frank 2022].

Finally, we show an at-scale simulation of LH wave propagation for the high field side LH

Several of the RF reduced models together with high-fidelity RF tools (HPC-capable) mentioned
above, such as GENRAY (ray tracing), TORIC, TORLH & AORSA (full-wave), and CQL3D
(continuum Fokker Planck), were implemented and maintained as part of this project in
frameworks such as the Integrated Plasma Simulator [Batchelor 2009], IPS-FASTRAN [Park
2018], OMFIT [Meneghini 2015], and TRANSP [Hawryluk 1980].

3.0 Summary, Conclusions, and Future
In summary we have developed a suite of 3D electromagnetic field solvers, both FEM and
FDTD based, that account for the RF antenna and vacuum vessel geometries with unprecedented
accuracy. Workflows were developed that make it possible to translate CAD models for the
antenna and vacuum vessel to physics meshes for RF wave simulation. Nonlinear RF sheath
formation has been incorporated self-consistently as a boundary condition in these solvers. We
have also carried out extensive studies of the impact of RF sheaths on the ion energy angle
distribution at plasma-material interfaces, using high fidelity particle-in-cell codes.
Comprehensive simulation models were developed to assess the impact of blob-like edge

 16

turbulence on RF wave propagation and the impact of the RF ponderomotive force on the plasma
scrape-off layer (SOL). A fluid transport solver for the far-SOL was also developed which
accounts for the high parallel to perpendicular heat anisotropy on an unstructured mesh, thus
making it possible to precisely represent an antenna structure in the presence of edge transport.
Finally we have developed a hierarchy of core wave propagation and absorption models that self-
consistently combine continuum Fokker Planck and Monte Carlo treatments of fast ion evolution
with ICRF full-wave field solvers and continuum Fokker Planck treatments of fast electron
evolution with both full-wave and ray tracing models.
The research discussed in this Final Technical Report was reported in 41 manuscripts published
in refereed journals. All of these manuscripts appear in the Reference Section of the Report.
The simulation capability developed and knowledge acquired under this grant is now being
applied in a new “SciDAC-5” Partnership - the Center for Advanced Simulation of RF-Plasma-
Material Interactions. A comprehensive suite of high-fidelity simulation tools is being developed
that integrate RF-material interactions, realistic antenna structure and first-wall components, and
RF wave physics across different regions. This includes examining relevant phenomena such as
RF-sheath wall erosion, impurity production and transport, and RF wave propagation and power
deposition in the scrape-off layer, plasma edge, and hot core regions. These tools comprise both
high-fidelity and reduced RF and material models with varying levels of physics fidelity,
including machine learning-based models, that can be integrated into a community Whole
Facility Model (WFM).
This research will also extend and generalize a hierarchy of RF tool capabilities to non-tokamak
configurations that are viewed in the community as leading candidates for a Fusion Pilot Plant
(FPP), i.e., the stellarator, and mirror machine. The physics basis of each FPP configuration drives
unique RF actuator requirements. For different magnetic configurations, we will examine the
optimal choice of RF actuator as well as different antenna concepts, and antenna placement. These
will be enabled by the use of a modern framework (the Integrated Plasma Simulator-IPS) for the
coupling of multi-physics packages that are needed in a WFM.
