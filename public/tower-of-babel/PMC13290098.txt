Impact of ASO conjugation and receptor binding affinity on intracellular transport of mono- and bispecific TfR- and CD98-BrainshuttleTM variants
Authors: Tatjana Sela, Tamara Wirth, Annika Sommer, Vincent Larraillet, Joachim Butzer, Sabine Lohmann, Pia Wittmann, Christian Klein, Hendrik Knoetgen, Roberto Villasenor, Annette Indlekofer, Kerstin Hofer, Felix Schumacher, Jens Niewöhner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) represent a promising therapeutic modality for central nervous system (CNS) disorders, offering highly specific modulation of gene expression. However, their clinical utility is severely limited by their inability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), necessitating effective shuttling strategies. While transferrin receptor (TfR1)-mediated shuttling has shown therapeutic promise, the fundamental mechanisms governing the delivery of antibody-ASO conjugates across the BBB remain poorly understood. This study directly addresses this critical knowledge gap by establishing a mechanistic understanding of how the ASO cargo impacts major cellular interactions during the BrainshuttleTM-mediated transport across the BBB. Using a panel of advanced in vitro assays developed specifically for this purpose, including quantitative transcytosis, detailed imaging-based intracellular trafficking, and binding assays with brain endothelial cells (BECs), the shuttling process was systematically investigated. We demonstrate that ASO conjugation profoundly alters the cellular fate of the BrainshuttleTM. Specifically, conjugation increased the binding to BECs of low-affinity TfR1 shuttles via avidity effects while paradoxically reducing the binding strength of high-affinity shuttles. Functional assays confirmed the biological activity of the delivered ASOs; however, transcytosis of high-to-moderate affinity binders across the BBB model was significantly delayed upon ASO conjugation. Building on these mechanistic insights, we engineered TfR1 BrainshuttlesTM with optimized affinity and explored the shuttling potential of an alternative BBB receptor, CD98hc. These efforts culminated in the development of a novel bispecific BrainshuttleTM targeting both CD98hc and TfR1. This dual-targeting strategy exploits distinct and potentially non-competing trafficking pathways to overcome ASO-induced delays and significantly enhance in vitro transcytosis efficiency. The in vitro findings in this study underscore the necessity of mechanism-driven design to overcome ASO-induced limitations in delivery across the BBB. The bispecific CD98/TfR1 approach presented here provides a promising new strategy for maximizing delivery efficiency and enabling more effective therapeutic outcomes for CNS diseases.KEYWORDS: Affinity, antibody engineering, antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates, ASO, bispecific antibodies, Brainshuttle, CD98hc, CNS, delivery, TfR, transcytosis

Introduction

RNA therapeutics, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), represent a promising therapeutic modality due to their high specificity and broad therapeutic scope. By sequence-specific binding of the target mRNA, ASOs can inhibit translation, induce splice switching, or promote mRNA degradation via the recruitment of RNase H1.1,2 ASOs are particularly beneficial in the treatment of neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, where pathology involves the aggregation of amyloid beta and hyperphosphorylated microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT).3–5 With their mechanism of action, ASOs enable targeting of disease-causing proteins at the mRNA level independently of protein isotypes and before aggregation occurs.6,7

However, the clinical use of naked ASOs in the treatment of central nervous system (CNS) disorders is severely restricted by their inability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and their poor pharmacokinetic (PK) profile. The highly specialized BBB, strictly controlling the passage of molecules into the CNS, is characterized by endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, supported by pericytes and astrocytic endfeet.8 Apart from this physical barrier, peripheral administration is not the ideal route of administration for naked ASOs due to their low plasma stability and nonspecific cell binding, both of which contribute to their poor PK profile.9–11 This in combination with their highly charged nature and high molecular weight of 6–9 kDa generally precludes ASOs from crossing the BBB.12

Currently, intrathecal administration is a common route for CNS delivery of ASOs, but the brain distribution of ASOs following intrathecal delivery is often non-homogeneous, limited primarily to regions near cerebrospinal fluid compartments and leaving deeper brain regions less accessible.13–15 Furthermore, this method is highly invasive and requires specialized procedures and trained personnel for lumbar punctures, thereby increasing procedural costs. Therefore, systemic delivery via intravenous or subcutaneous injection would be highly advantageous due to its ease of application, reduced invasiveness, and potential for patient self-administration. To enable this systemic delivery and overcome the limitations of naked ASOs, various delivery platforms, including conjugation of oligonucleotides to full-length antibodies or antigen-binding fragments (Fabs), lipids, and nanoparticles have been developed.16–20

Extensive investigation has established the transferrin receptor (TfR1) as a pivotal target for CNS-delivery strategies.14,21,22 TfR1 is highly expressed at the BBB, regulating CNS iron metabolism by constitutive internalization of transferrin (Tf)-bound iron. In endosomes, low pH triggers the release of iron into endosomes, which will then be exported by divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) and released to the brain parenchyma via iron transporters.23,24 TfR1 is directed from early endosomes to recycling endosomes and then transported back to the basolateral membrane.25,26 This physiological process of receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) is exploited by the BrainshuttleTM technology, which utilizes TfR1-binding antibodies to transport cargos such as conjugated ASOs across the BBB using clathrin-mediated transcytosis 27–29 (Supplemental Figure S1).

Crucially for such AOCs, successful delivery is not defined solely by translocation across the vascular endothelium into the brain parenchyma. Once within the CNS interstitial space, the AOC must undergo secondary internalization into target parenchymal cells, such as neurons or astrocytes, and subsequently escape the endolysosomal pathway to allow the ASO payload to reach its site of action in the cytosol or nucleus.30,31

A persistent challenge for TfR1-mediated CNS delivery is the broad tissue expression of TfR1, which is not exclusive to brain endothelial cells (BECs), leading to rapid drug clearance from the bloodstream and off-target effects.32,33 This broad peripheral TfR1 expression is exemplified by alternative tissue delivery strategies using TfR1 as a targeting receptor, e.g., muscle and cardiac delivery.34,35 A general critical hurdle for antibody-based delivery remains the escape of the targeting antibody-cargo complex from the lysosomal degradation pathway upon receptor binding. Achieving efficient BBB shuttling requires navigating the complex interplay between TfR1 affinity, antibody valency, and epitope selection.32,36–38

Given these intrinsic limitations in current brain delivery systems, the exploration of alternative targeting strategies is warranted. Consequently, we assessed SLC3A2 (CD98hc) as a promising alternative target for brain delivery, in addition to TfR1.39,40 CD98hc is a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in amino acid transport, integrin signaling regulation, and cell fusion mechanisms.41,42 Critically, CD98hc is highly expressed at the BBB and offers a unique opportunity because it is known to follow clathrin-independent endocytosis40,43,44 representing a fundamentally distinct trafficking pathway from that of TfR1. Leveraging a receptor with distinct endocytic trafficking kinetics offers a strategy to circumvent the limitations of TfR1, specifically its susceptibility to lysosomal sorting and degradation.21,37

Building on the insights gained from monospecific TfR1- and CD98-mediated CNS delivery27–29,39,45–47 we explored a dual-targeting strategy to further enhance CNS delivery via bispecific TfR1/CD98 antibodies. While the use of bispecific TfR1/CD98 binders for efficient brain delivery in mice has been reported previously48 a detailed mechanistic understanding of how this dual shuttling approach impacts intracellular trafficking and transcytosis is still missing.

Here, a comprehensive set of in vitro assays is presented to establish a thorough understanding of how antibody affinity, BBB-target identity, and ASO conjugation collectively influence the transcytosis of Brainshuttle™-ASO conjugates. Specifically, we analyzed whether the use of an alternative BBB target like CD98 leads to productive transcytosis and functional ASO shuttling. It is reported that both TfR1 and CD98 show capable transcytosis despite having differential intracellular trafficking patterns25,26,44,49–51 confirming both as suitable targets for brain delivery.27–29,39,45–47 Moreover, the data demonstrated that cellular binding is affected in an affinity-dependent manner and that both intracellular trafficking and transcytosis are delayed for several TfR1 Brainshuttles™ following ASO conjugation. Notably, this impairing effect was less pronounced for CD98-targeting monoclonal antibody (mAb)-ASO conjugates, supporting the potential of CD98 as a promising alternative to TfR1 for oligonucleotide delivery.

Crucially, the data showed that co-engaging TfR1 and CD98 yields a synergistic effect, resulting in significantly increased transcytosis compared to their monospecific counterparts in the in vitro transcytosis assay. This mechanistic data set demonstrates that different affinity combinations of these two targets dictate the resulting trafficking profile of the bispecific Brainshuttle™, with the higher-affinity partner dominating the intracellular trafficking route.

These findings establish clear mechanistic design principles for novel BrainshuttleTM antibodies. By translating these mechanistic insights into optimized mAb-ASO conjugate designs, this work has the potential to significantly increase ASO brain delivery efficiency, enabling lower doses and consequently enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Antibody design

Antibodies were designed based on a human IgG1 backbone, with adapted antigen binding sites. In every antibody, a Q-tag (YRYRQ) with two flanking regions (GGGS) was inserted at the heavy chain position 297 (HC297) (Kabat numbering). Furthermore, LALA-PG (L236A, L237A, P331G) mutations were inserted to reduce effector function and knob-into-hole mutations (knob: T366W, hole: T366S, L368A, and Y407V) were inserted to ensure correct chain assembly.52

The anti-TfR1-binders are in-house designed affinity variants (Table S1). The variable heavy (VH) and variable light (VL) sequences of anti-CD98hc binders were taken from published patent applications (Table 1, Table S2). The patents were selected based on available antibody sequences for human anti-CD98hc mAbs, upon the availability of cell-based analysis data. The binder nomenclature is based on the target description with unique identifiers. The names consist of the target capital letters representing the affinity ranking (Table S1, S2). The bispecific binders were designed as CrossMabs, by exchanging the variable domains VH and VL of one Fab arm, to assure correct chain assembly.53

Antibody production

Monospecific and bispecific antibodies were transiently expressed in HEK cells (A14527, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The antibodies were purified using a two-step chromatography process. An initial affinity chromatography (AFC) step was performed (MabSelect SuRe, 10064394, Cytiva) followed by preparative size-exclusion chromatography (SEC; Superdex 200, 28,989,336, Cytiva) to isolate the monomeric antibody. To ensure high quality, the antibodies were biochemically analyzed using the following methods: hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC), analytical SEC, capillary electrophoresis sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS), mass spectrometry (MS), UV/VIS spectrometry, and endotoxin determination. Purified antibodies were then concentrated and stored at −20∘ C until further use.

Enzymatic conjugation of LNA-ASOs to antibodies

Antibodies containing a Q-tag were conjugated to locked nucleic acid antisense oligonucleotides (LNA-ASOs) via a two-step process.29 First, antibodies were reacted with a K-tag-PEG3-azidolysine linker (Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd.) in the presence of the KTG enzyme (Roche Diagnostics GmbH). The antibody-linker intermediate was purified by SEC using a high-performance liquid chromatography system (Ultimate 3000 HPLC system, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (28-9909-44, GE Healthcare). In the second step, the purified antibody-linker intermediate was conjugated to a bicyclo[6.1.0]nonyne (BCN)-containing LNA-ASO (20 nucleotides, designed as a gapmer, with a central DNA and 4 flanking LNA on both, 3‘and 5’ end; fully phosphorothioated at all linkage positions; Sequence: 5’-[BCN_2]-TTAACTCAAATCAATTCTCA-3’, Roche A/S Diagnostik & Farmaceutika) using click chemistry. This ASO was used for all experiments if not otherwise mentioned and is sometimes referred to as “ASO1” in the supplementary materials. Additional ASO2: 19 nucleotides, gapmer with 4 LNAs at the 5‘end and LNAs at positions 15, 17, 18, and 19 on the 3’ end, interrupted by a DNA nucleotide at position 16; fully phosphorothioated at all linkage positions; Sequence: 5’-[BCN_2]-CTTTAATTTAATCACTCAT-3’). The final antibody-ASO conjugates were purified by SEC to remove unconjugated payload and stored at −80∘C.

Biochemical and quality control analysis

The final mAbs and mAb-ASO conjugates were extensively analyzed to ensure high quality. Analysis of the antibody-ASO conjugates was performed including analytical SEC using TSKgel UP-SW3000 column (0023546, Tosoh Bioscience GmbH), CE-SDS using a Caliper LabChip® GXII TouchTM protein characterization system (Perkin Elmer), hydrophobic-interaction chromatography (HIC) using a TSKgel Butyl-NPR column (0014947, Tosoh Bioscience GmbH), MS (ESI-Q-ToF-MS, Electrospray ionization-Quadrupole-Time of flight-MS; Bruker maXis 4 G, Bruker GmbH), UV/VIS spectrometry using the soloVPE system (CTech, Repligen) and endotoxin measurements (EndogenNexgen MCS, Charles River). Key parameters measured included monomer content (analytical SEC), purity (CE-SDS), drug to antibody ratio (HIC, UV/Vis, and MS), conjugate identity and integrity (MS), and endotoxin levels (kinetic LAL test) (see Supplemental Data, Additional Method Descriptions, Table S3).

Binding assay

Flow cytometry binding assays were performed by incubating human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3, SCC066, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck) and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) (HLECP1, Lonza) for 1 h with varying concentrations of mAbs and their respective ASO conjugates. A commercial human IgG isotype control (1-001-A, bio-techne), and secondary antibody-only treatments (709-116-149, Jackson ImmunoResearch) served as negative controls. Binding of hCMEC/D3 and LSEC cells was quantified by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) at different treatment concentrations (1000 nM, 500 nM, 250 nM, 125 nM, 63 nM, 31 nM, 16 nM, 8 nM, 4 nM, 2 nM, and 1 nM).

Transcytosis assay

A transcytosis assay was performed using an adapted protocol previously described by Sade et al.54 to assess antibody trafficking across the blood–brain barrier model. 8000 hCMEC/D3 cells (SCC066, Sigma-Aldrich) per transwell were seeded on collagen/fibronectin-coated (200 ul fibronectin (50 µg/ml), 33,016-015, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 400 µl collagen (50 µg/ml), 354,236, Corning) 12-well cell culture inserts (9310402, cellQART, SABEU GmbH & Co. KG). Cells were cultured for 12 days to establish a confluent, tight monolayer, with medium changes every 2–3 days. The integrity of the monolayer was confirmed visually and by assessing permeability by determining the permeability coefficient using Lucifer Yellow fluorescent dye (L0259, Sigma Aldrich, Merck).

On the day of the assay, unconjugated mAbs and mAb-ASO conjugates were added at a concentration of 20.1 µg/ml and 2.67 µg/ml, respectively, to the apical chamber and incubated for 1 h to allow for intracellular uptake. A pulse-chase format was then used to eliminate unbound molecules. For this, both apical and basolateral chambers were washed five times. After transferring the inserts to a new plate, a fresh medium was added. Samples were collected from the apical, basolateral, and cell lysate fractions at various time points (0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 5 h, 20 h, or 24 h) to quantify the transcytosed, recycled, and intracellular antibody, respectively. All samples were stored at −80∘ C until analysis.

huIgG ELISA

The concentration of BrainshuttleTM in the transcytosis assay samples was quantified using an in-house sandwich ELISA. 384-well maxisorp plates (460372, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated overnight with 1 µg/ml AffiniPure Goat Anti-Human IgG Fcγ-specific coating antibody (109-005-098, BIOZOL). A standard curve was generated using the respective mAb or mAb-ASO conjugate in a concentration range of 0.0004 to 25 ng/ml. Samples from the apical and basolateral chambers as well as cell lysates were serially diluted up to 1:729, transferred to the previously coated ELISA plate, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature while constant shaking with 450 rpm. Detection was performed using 50 ng/ml biotinylated goat anti-human IgG F(ab’)2 fragment (109-066-097, BIOZOL), followed by a 10 ng/ml streptavidin-poly-HRP40 conjugate (65 R-S104PHRP, Fitzgerald, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After each incubation step, the plate was washed 4–6 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with Tween with a BioTek 405 select microplate washer (Agilent Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove unbound reagents. Luminescence was measured after the addition of SuperSignal™ ELISA Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (37069, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mAb concentration in all samples were determined from the standard curve using a four-parameter sigmoidal dose–response model. For each condition, the average mAb concentration from two replicates was used to calculate the total mAb amount in each compartment.

Conjugate ELISA

To measure the amount of intact antibody-ASO conjugate, a specific sandwich ELISA was developed. Standard curves of each mAb-ASO conjugate were prepared in a concentration ranging from 625 pM to 0.07 pM dilution, and all samples were serially diluted to 1:729 (loading concentration of 2.67 µg/ml). Streptavidin-coated plates (Roche Diagnostics GmbH) were used to capture the conjugate via a biotinylated, sequence-specific probe directed against the ASO (Roche Diagnostics GmbH). Detection was performed using 200 ng/ml of a proprietary, in-house produced pan-human Fc-specific antibody conjugated with digoxigenin (Fc-pan-Dig, Roche Diagnostics GmbH) and 5 mU/ml anti-digoxigenin antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (anti-DIG-POD reporter enzyme, 11,633,716,001, Roche Diagnostics GmbH). After each incubation step, the plate was washed six times with a 2xSSCT buffer with a BioTek 405 select microplate washer (Agilent Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove unbound reagents. The quantification was performed as described for the huIgG ELISA. Negative controls (unconjugated antibody and ASO) were included to confirm assay specificity.

Trafficking

The trafficking assay followed a pulse-chase setup. hCMEC/D3 (SCC066, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck) cells were incubated for 30 min with 30 nM of mAb or mAb-ASO conjugates, followed by washing four times with PBS (P04-36500, PAN Biotech GmbH) to remove all compounds that had not been internalized. Fresh cell culture medium was added, and hCMEC/D3 was fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (28908, Thermo Fisher Scientific) after 0 h, 0.5 h, 2.5 h, or 24 h of intracellular trafficking.

Immunocytochemistry staining

The immunocytochemistry staining was performed as described by Capraso et al.55 The fixed cells were washed with sodium-phosphate buffers with increasing salt concentration (140 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM NaCl and 10 mM sodium phosphate, 500 mM NaCl and 20 mM sodium phosphate), before incubation for 30 min in goat-serum dilution buffer (16.7% goat serum, 0.3% TritonX100, 20 mM sodium phosphate, 450 mM NaCl). Primary antibodies against RAB5 (3547S, Cell Signaling Technologies), CD63 (NBP2-22217, Novus Biologicals, Bio-techne), LAMP2 (NB100-77913, Novus Biologicals, Bio-techne) and RAB11A (71-5300, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with a high-salt sodium phosphate buffer before the fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies against mouse (115-585-164, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and rabbit (111-545-046, Jackson ImmunoResearch) were applied. In addition, the different TfR and CD98 BrainshuttlesTM were detected via staining with anti-human IgG (109-606-170, Jackson ImmunoResearch). After washing with decreasing salt concentration, nuclear and cytoplasmic staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 10,236,276,001, Roche Diagnostics GmbH) and CellMask blue (H32720, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) was performed. Imaging was performed in confocal mode using a spinning disc microscope (Operetta CLS, Revvity) at 40× magnification (NA: 1.1) and a pixel size of 2160 × 2016 px, resulting in a xy-resolution of 330 nm. Harmony software (Revvity) was used to analyze object-based colocalization of the BrainshuttleTM with different endosomal markers. The image analysis pipeline consisted of a flatfield correction, segmentation of nuclei and cytoplasm by DAPI and CellMask staining, respectively, as well as the endosomal compartments using the “find spots” function. The PhenoLOGIC linear classifier was trained on a manually curated set of vesicles to accurately distinguish positive BrainshuttleTM signals from background based on object properties, e.g., size, intensity, morphology, texture. To determine colocalization with endosomal markers, the “Restrict by Mask” function was utilized, creating a new region that defined the overlapping spot between the BrainshuttleTM signal and the respective endosomal marker signal. The ratio of BrainshuttleTM intensity within the distinct endosomal compartment to the overall intracellular BrainshuttleTM intensity was then calculated to quantify the intracellular distribution pattern. Representative images of marker staining are available in the Supplemental Figure S11A.

In vitro potency

To evaluate the functional efficacy of antibody-ASO conjugates in neuronal cells, induced pluripotent stem cell glutamatergic neurons (01279, Fujifilm cellular dynamics) were subjected to a single treatment with different antibody-ASO constructs and unconjugated ASOs in a concentration range of 0.32 nM to 1000 nM. The treatment solution was removed during the first medium change on day 4, followed by further medium changes on day 7 and day 10 after. On day 14 after treatment, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to measure the expression levels of the MAPT gene, which encodes for TAU protein.

Total RNA was diluted 2-fold and heat-denatured (90°C for 40 sec) before plating. qPCR reactions were performed in duplicate in 384-well plates using qScript XLT One Step RT-qPCR ToughMix (95133, Quanta Bioscience, Rockville, MD, USA) on a LightCycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics GmbH). Final reactions were assembled on a 384-well qPCR plate (LightCycler 480, Roche Diagnostics GmbH) in duplicates, containing the master mix, 20x assays for the target gene MAPT (Hs00902193_m1, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the housekeeping gene TATA box binding protein (TBP) (Hs.PT.58 v.39858774, Integrated DNA Technologies).

Cycling conditions were: 15 min at 50°C; 3 min at 95°C (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95°C (5 sec) and 60°C (45 sec). Relative MAPT expression was calculated by the delta–delta Ct method, normalizing to TBP and vehicle control (PBS). Dose–response data were fitted using a four-parameter sigmoidal model (GraphPad Prism).

Results

Engineering and conjugation of TfR- and CD98-targeted ASO BrainshuttlesTM

To evaluate in vitro binding, cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and transcytosis of CD98- and TfR-targeting BrainshuttleTM-ASO conjugates, 16 tool antibodies with varying affinities were generated. The antibodies were engineered as monovalent, one-armed IgG constructs with varying binding affinities to hCMEC/D3 cells ranging from sub-nanomolar to several hundred nanomolar (Figure 1(A), Table S1, S2). The affinity range is based on published TfR binders.36,56–58 All antibodies were designed in a monovalent, one-armed format to promote transcellular transport in BECs.21 In addition, knob-into-hole mutations ensured correct chain pairing52,59 and the Fc region was silenced by the insertion of LALA-PG mutation to enhance the safety profile by minimizing the risk of unwanted immune activation during treatment.60,61

To investigate the suitability of the CD98 and TfR antibodies as ASO shuttles, both were conjugated to a microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT)-targeting ASO using the site-specific Kutzneria albida transglutaminase (KTG) conjugation technology (Figure 1(B)). This method as well as the ASO design has been described in detail in the previous work.29 The conjugation process was performed in two sequential steps (Figure 1(B)). In the first step, the KTG enzyme catalyzes the formation of a stable isopeptide bond between the glutamine of the Q-tag (YRYRQ), inserted at heavy chain position 297 of each mAb, and the lysine in the K-Tag (RYESK) attached to a PEG3 linker. In a second step, the resulting antibody-linker intermediate was connected to the ASO via click chemistry, enabled by an azide group in the linker and the BCN moiety attached to the ASO.29 Based on previous work29 a drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of 1 was selected for optimal PK profiles of the resulting conjugates.

ASO conjugation and mAb affinity determine binding to brain- and liver endothelial cells

Successful delivery of therapeutics to the CNS following systemic administration is dependent on efficient binding to BECs, a crucial initial step for subsequent transcytosis across the BBB. In addition, binding to LSECs can serve as a key indicator of in vivo PK profiles, as the liver is the primary site of systemic clearance for both mAbs and ASOs.9,62–66 To comprehensively characterize the binding properties of the mAb variants and assess the impact of ASO conjugation, binding of a selected set of TfR mAbs and mAb-ASO conjugates to both BECs and LSECs was tested using a flow cytometry-based assay.

As anticipated, a positive correlation was observed between the affinity of TfR-mAbs and their corresponding binding strength to BECs (Figure 2(A)). A similar trend was seen for CD98 mAbs (Supplemental Figure S2). However, this correlation was less pronounced in LSECs, as some lower-affinity binders, such as TfR-G and -H showed strong binding to these cells. Overall, the relative binding (ratio BECs:LSECs) was dependent on the mAb’s affinity. Relative binding to BECs in comparison to LSECs revealed an affinity sweet spot comprising TfR-D, -E, and -F, while both higher and lower TfR-affinities showed generally weaker relative binding defined by a binding ratio <1 (Figure 2(B)). Given that effective brain delivery requires strong BEC binding and limited peripheral engagement, the data strengthen the hypothesis that a medium to high binding affinity is beneficial for achieving targeted delivery to the BBB.36,48–50,67

Next, the effect of ASO conjugation on the cellular binding of mAbs was examined. For all mAbs, ASO conjugation consistently increased the binding to LSECs except for the high-affinity TfR-A (Figure 2). This indicates that ASO conjugation increases LSECs binding strongly for low-affinity TfR-mAbs, whereas the effect is weaker for high-affinity TfR-mAbs. In line with what was seen for LSECs, ASO conjugation increased binding for low- and medium-affinity mAbs to BECs, whereas the binding of high-affinity mAbs (TfR-A, -B, -C, -D) was decreased upon ASO conjugation. Comparing the relative binding of mAb-ASO conjugates revealed an increased binding to LSECs of TfR-ASO conjugates (ratio BECs:LSECs <1) of every affinity range. However, there seems to be an affinity sweet spot with TfR-D-ASO and TfR-E-ASO showing a binding ratio BECs:LSECs of almost 1.

Impact of antibody affinity, target, and ASO conjugation on transcytosis

In addition to cell binding, the internalization, intracellular trafficking, and exocytosis in BECs are important parameters for efficient crossing of the BBB. While the intracellular accumulation of mAb-ASO conjugates in LSECs drives clearance, and thereby reduces the exposure of the mAb-ASO conjugates at the BBB, the intracellular trafficking in BECs directly impacts transcytosis and brain uptake. To address the challenge of predicting brain uptake, a model balancing high-throughput screening capacity with physiological relevance was employed. Evaluation of mAbs and mAb-ASO conjugate transcytosis was performed using a hCMEC/D3 pulse-chase assay format within a transwell transcytosis system, serving as a model for BBB passage.54 To validate the physiological relevance of our transport model, we first confirmed the endogenous protein expression of the two primary targets, TfR1 and CD98hc, in hCMEC/D3 cells and as an additional control in iBECs via Western blot (Figure S5, S6). To ensure that the observed effects were specific to intact ASO conjugates, conjugate integrity during transcytosis was confirmed by a conjugate-specific ELISA alongside the standard IgG ELISA (Supplemental Figures S7, S8). Given the crucial role of antibody affinity for delivery across the BBB, it was first investigated how different TfR affinities affect transcytosis efficiency, recycling, and lysosomal degradation in BECs.

The results demonstrate a clear relationship between TfR antibody affinity and transcytosis. High-affinity TfR binders (TfR-A, -B, -C, -D) exhibited greater absolute transcytosis up to 4,091 pg (TfR-A) compared to medium- (TfR-E, -F, -G, -H) and low-affinity (TfR-I, -J, -K) mAbs (Figure 3(B); results for negative control antibody shown in Figures S8 and S9). The TfR-binders with the lowest affinity, TfR-K and TfR-J, showed minimal absolute (Figure 3(B)) and relative transcytosis (Figure 3(C)), indicating that these low-affinity binders facilitate transport to, but not across, the BBB. Whilst the absolute transcytosis levels for TfR-E (388 pg) were modest, the relative transcytosis in comparison to total cellular uptake was relatively high (34.2%). This indicates that sufficient complex stability is essential for successful navigation of the intracellular trafficking pathway. Furthermore, the data implies that the upper affinity threshold, at which impaired endosomal release theoretically limits transcytosis, was not reached within the affinity range of this antibody panel (Figure 3(B,C)).

Whilst ASO conjugation to TfR antibodies typically reduced transcytosis for high-affinity binders (e.g., TfR-A, -B, -C) after 5 h (TfR-B: 3,115 pg; TfR-B-ASO: 1,207 pg), it surprisingly resulted in a strong increase for all low-affinity binders, e.g., a 3.5-fold increase for TfR-I (TfR-I: 148 pg, TfR-IASO: 527 pg) (Figure 3(B)). This opposing effect highlights how the inherent properties of the ASO cargo, such as its hydrophobicity and negative charge, can either negatively interfere with or positively contribute to the antibody’s cellular interactions, ultimately influencing in vitro transcytosis. The presence of the ASO leads to a higher degree of nonspecific binding,30,68,69 thereby compensating the weak interaction with TfR of low-affinity mAbs. To understand if BBB-crossing is truly reduced upon ASO conjugation or if the observations may be a kinetic effect, we tested extended trafficking times (from 5 h to 24 h). The results revealed that the reduced transcytosis of TfR-ASO-conjugates in comparison to unconjugated mAbs after 5 h represents a delay rather than a true decrease, with transcytosis increasing over time (shown for TfR-B, -I, -K, Figure 3(B)) (a simplified schematic overview of transcytosis shown in Figure S1). For example, while 3,115 pg of TfR-B were transcytosed within 5 h, only one-third (1,207 pg) of the respective mAb-ASO conjugate was transcytosed within 5 h, but after 24 h of transcytosis, 2,995 pg of TfR-B-ASO were transcytosed, thereby equaling the levels of unconjugated mAb.

Prompted by the observation that ASO conjugation kinetically impairs TfR-mediated transport and acknowledging the intrinsic limitations of TfR regarding systemic clearance, the transport potential of the alternative BBB receptor CD98hc was evaluated. Similar to TfR, CD98-mAbs exhibited a clear positive correlation between antibody affinity and absolute transcytosis in hCMEC/D3 cells (Figure 3(D)). However, in distinct contrast to TfR binders, CD98 antibodies maintained their transcytosis levels independent of ASO conjugation (Figure 3(D)). A direct comparison of the relative transcytosis and recycling fractions of the high-affinity benchmarks, TfR-B and CD98-A, revealed that after 24 h of trafficking, 44% of the internalized TfR-B was transcytosed, proving slightly superior to the CD98-A Brainshuttle™, which showed 36% transcytosis (Figure 3(E)). Notably, however, CD98-A demonstrated a sustained transport profile, exceeding 50% relative transcytosis after 72 h of trafficking (Supplemental Figure S10).

To evaluate the impact of binding affinity on transcytosis, we correlated TfR-mAb and ASO-conjugate kinetics (Figure 3(F), S11). While increased affinity is generally associated with enhanced internalization and transcytosis (Figure 3(F), S11A), ASO-conjugation further amplified internalization for most variants, excluding TfR-C. Despite this increased uptake, absolute, and relative transcytosis rates decreased for conjugates, suggesting a shift toward “unproductive” endosomal sequestration. Transcytosis efficiency, defined as the ratio of transcytosed cargo to total systemic protein (Figure S11B), was highest for TfR-E and TfR-A. Notably, while mAb transcytosis efficiency scaled with affinity, this correlation was absent in the conjugates. Among the latter, TfR-I-Tau exhibited superior efficiency due to a favorable ratio of transcytosis relative to its low internalization.

Consistent with results for TfR-targeting mAbs, the ASO conjugates of the low-affinity CD98-binding mAbs (CD98-B and CD98-D) mediated enhanced transcytosis relative to the unconjugated antibodies (Figure 3(D)).

To extend the findings to an additional in vitro BBB model, we conducted experiments on the spheroid transcytosis assay, corroborating the results of the transwell model (Supplemental data, additional method description, and Figure S12).

In summary, higher antibody affinity and extended trafficking times appeared to enhance transcytosis for both TfR and CD98. A clear delay in transcytosis was observed for TfR-antibodies upon ASO conjugation, while for CD98hc no delay was observed. These findings prompted us to investigate the potential role of the endocytic and intracellular trafficking pathways that are engaged by the two BBB targets, and which may exhibit different susceptibilities in the presence of hydrophobic ASOs.

Intracellular trafficking of antibody-ASO conjugates is dependent on target receptor but not ASO conjugation

To further elucidate the mechanistic differences in transcytosis between TfR and CD98 mAbs and mAb-ASO conjugates, confocal microscopy was used to analyze the intracellular trafficking of mAbs and the respective ASO conjugates in hCMEC/D3. Using a pulse-chase experiment, hCMEC/D3 cells were incubated with equimolar concentrations of the mAb or mAb-ASO conjugates (pulse), followed by the removal of unbound molecules and analysis after a chase period in fresh medium. Subcellular distribution was measured by staining of internalized BrainshuttleTM and determination of its colocalization with endolysosomal compartments defined by staining of RAB5 (early endosome), CD63 (late endosome), LAMP2 (lysosome), and RAB11A (recycling endosome). Two high-affinity mAbs, TfR-B, and CD98-A, were selected to enable an affinity-independent comparison of TfR and CD98 trafficking. Although both binders are classified as high-affinity, the binding affinity of TfR-B is marginally higher than that of CD98-A.

As expected, effective internalization of both TfR and CD98-targeting mAbs and their corresponding ASO-conjugates in BECs was confirmed (Figure 4(A)). The high-affinity TfR-B mAb demonstrated stronger internalization into BECs after 30 min, compared to the high-affinity CD98-A mAb. Beyond this, the intracellular localization of the TfR-B mAb was different from that of the CD98-A mAb. Fluorescence images revealed that the TfR-B mAb was distributed in small vesicles throughout the cytoplasm, whereas the CD98-A mAb exhibited an enhanced plasma membrane localization (Figure 4(A)). Quantitative analysis of vesicle colocalization further delineated these distinct trafficking routes (Figure 4C). The distribution to early endosomes and late endosomes revealed a differentiated trafficking pattern, while the colocalization with lysosomes and recycling endosomes was almost equal (Figure 4C). Specifically, 46% of the intracellular TfR-B mAb was found in early endosomes, while the biggest proportion of CD98-A colocalized with late endosomes (33%) (Figure 4(C)). This might be caused by the different internalization pathways. While TfR is internalized via a clathrin-dependent pathway known to direct cargo to early endosomes and facilitating rapid recycling back to plasma membrane and transcytosis,40,54 CD98 is internalized via clathrin-independent endocytosis, potentially explaining its higher prevalence in late endosomes compared to TfR.40,49,70

The intracellular amount of both TfR-B and CD98-A mAbs decreased within the first 2.5 h, indicating their subsequent recycling from the endosomal compartment back to the cell surface and lysosomal degradation. Upon ASO-conjugation, the internalization of both TfR-B and CD98-A was increased (Figure 4(B)), but the immediate intracellular fate of both binders was not altered at the 0 h timepoint (Figure 4(C)). ASO conjugation delayed the recycling of both TfR and CD98 mAbs, suggesting a prolonged intracellular retention of the conjugates (Figure 4(C)). After 2.5 h of chasing, only 36% of the internalized TfR-B and 34% of the internalized CD98-A were found intracellular, while 51% of the TfR-B-ASO conjugate and 48% of the CD98-A-ASO conjugate were still found in endosomal vesicles (Figure 4(C)).

To ensure that these effects were not specific to one single ASO, uptake and trafficking experiments were performed with an alternative ASO conjugated to a TfR-antibody. Similar uptake and endosomal colocalizations were observed, indicating a rather general effect upon ASO conjugation to a TfR-Brainshuttle (Figure S13).

Our findings confirm the impact of ASO conjugation on delaying both intracellular trafficking and transcytosis (Figure 3(B), 4(C)), which might increase the intracellular degradation in BECs, thereby reducing the effective concentration of ASOs that can accumulate in the brain parenchyma. The delayed transcytosis and intracellular trafficking of BrainshuttlesTM upon ASO conjugation highlights the need to specifically adapt BrainshuttleTM systems for optimal ASO delivery to improve transcytosis and reduce intracellular degradation.

Bispecific BrainshuttlesTM: combining clathrin-independent (CD98) and clathrin-dependent (TfR) endocytosis results in a unique transcytosis and trafficking pattern

To mitigate the probably less efficient productive release of ASO-conjugates from BECs into the brain parenchyma – a consequence of delayed transcytosis observed with TfR but not CD98 antibodies – a dual-binder strategy was developed. This bispecific antibody format, designed to engage the two different BBB-targeting receptors, was hypothesized to have one or more of the following effects: improved transcytosis, enhanced BBB specificity, and an optimized PK profile of BrainshuttleTM-ASO conjugates. It can be postulated that while the magnitude of receptor expression is important, specific targeting of BECs is paramount. Therefore, the greater challenge lies in minimizing target binding in peripheral organs, particularly the liver and kidney. Given the strong native affinity of ASOs for these tissues10 a high peripheral expression could lead to dose-limiting toxicities and reduced CNS efficacy. For this purpose, two receptor targets with preferably non-overlapping peripheral expression should be selected to enhance BBB-specific delivery. This strategy promotes the preferential internalization of the bispecific antibody by cells co-expressing both receptors. Based on their well-established roles as receptors, mediating transport across the BBB, TfR, and CD98 were chosen as the primary targets for this investigation.27–29,39,45–47

The antibodies were designed in an IgG format as CrossMabs53 including knob-into-hole mutations52 to drive correct heavy chain heterodimerization and light:heavy chain pairing. To ensure that the binding mechanism is driven solely by paratopic affinity to their respective receptor target and to rigorously exclude avidity effects, all mAbs in this study were engineered as monovalent binders.21 Based on the observed benefit of higher affinity for efficient transport of mAb-ASO conjugates, dual-binder constructs featuring two high-affinity (TfR-C/CD98A) binders or a combination of high- and low-affinity binders were generated. To eliminate the possibility of format-driven artifacts influencing the comparative analysis, the monospecific controls in this section were engineered as two-arm IgG-like antibodies, incorporating a non-binding site (DP47 non-binder) for backbone consistency (Figure 5(A)).

Investigation of in vitro transcytosis revealed a critical dependency on the affinity balance between the two targets (Figure 5(B)). While both combinations facilitated transport across the BECs, the specific pairing of affinities proved decisive. The combination of low-affinity TfR (TfR-I) with high-affinity CD98 (CD98-A) did not merely retain activity but strikingly potentiated it, yielding the highest levels of transcytosis (39,263 pg). Notably, this configuration outperformed the dual high-affinity variant (TfR-C/CD98-A; 13,178 pg) by approximately threefold. This enhancement underscores the mechanism of kinetic optimization often described, for example, for biparatopic ADCs, wherein attenuating the affinity toward sorting receptors (like TfR) prevents lysosomal trapping and facilitates intracellular routing.36,71 Thus, the specific interplay of TfR-low/CD98-high affinity appears critical for unlocking the full transport potential of this bispecific format.

The transcytosis assay showed significant differences in transport profiles across targets and affinity combinations, indicating that antibody transcytosis is governed by the distinct intracellular machineries engaged by each receptor – specifically, clathrin-dependent endocytosis for TfR versus clathrin-independent endocytosis for CD98.25,44

To investigate the fate of bispecific antibodies, their intracellular routing was compared to their monospecific counterparts. This trafficking analysis aimed at determining how combining binders with different endocytic mechanisms into a single bispecific molecule influences transcytosis dynamics.

To define the trafficking profiles of the TfR- and CD98-binding bispecific mAbs, their colocalization with key endosomal markers for early endosome, late endosome, lysosome, and recycling endosome was analyzed. This analysis revealed affinity and target-dependent endocytic pathway patterns for the antibodies. Confocal imaging showed a clear divergence in the intracellular location of the two different target binders. TfR-antibodies are rapidly internalized and localized to discrete perinuclear vesicles, while CD98 antibodies remained closer to the plasma membrane (Figures 4(A) and 6(A)). This differential trafficking aligns with previous reports describing clathrin-independent endocytosis cargoes, such as CD98, exhibiting a prolonged retention time at the cell surface compared to TfR-mediated internalization.49

Trafficking analysis revealed that the cellular behavior of the bispecific antibodies was not a simple composite of their monospecific components but was instead dominated by the higher-affinity binder, creating a unique combination pattern. This was specifically evident for the TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) bispecific antibody, where the trafficking profile resembled very closely the high-affinity TfR profile, while CD98 retention effects were also observed, suggesting that the rapid internalization pathway of TfR is dominant and is increased when both targets are engaged with high affinity (Figure 6(C)). This bispecific BrainshuttleTM design (TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high)) synergistically integrates the efficient cellular uptake characteristics of TfR binders with the prolonged cell surface retention conferred by CD98 binders. This was also observed when investigating the colocalization of the TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) bispecific antibody with key endosomal markers (Figure 6(C)). At the 0 h timepoint, the distribution of early endosome (46%), late endosome (24%), lysosome (15%), and recycling endosome (18%) was similar to that of the monospecific TfR-C variant. Following a 2.5 h chase period, the influence of the CD98-A binder became evident, characterized by pronounced cellular retention of TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) in all endosomal compartments (Figure 6(C)). This in vitro observation nevertheless still warrants validation of the anticipated beneficial in vivo pharmacology, specifically, the sustained brain uptake and retention previously associated with CD98-mediated transport.40,72

The profile of the TfR-C (high)/CD98-B (low) binder combination was very much comparable with that of the monospecific TfR-C (high) antibody, indicating that the low-affinity CD98-B binder had a low impact on overall trafficking kinetics (Figure 6(C) and Figure S12). After 2.5 h of chasing, the TfR-C (high)/CD98-B (low) combination still resulted in a stronger cell retention compared to TfR-C in its monospecific format. This was mainly driven by a slight increase in colocalization with late endosome (14%) in comparison to the monospecific TfR-C (8%) after 2.5 h of chasing (Figure 6(C)). In addition, confocal microscopy showed that both the TfR-C binder and the CD98-B binder contribute to the intracellular trafficking, as this bispecific mAb was found both retained at the plasma membrane and in perinuclear vesicles (Figure 6(A)).

Crucially, a clear synergistic effect for the TfR-I (low)/CD98-A (high) bispecific was observed. While the individual monospecific TfR-I and CD98-A antibodies each showed low cellular uptake, the bispecific variant demonstrated a significantly increased uptake, comparable to that of the high-affinity monospecific TfR antibody (Figure 6(B)). This finding suggests a synergistic mechanism where the high-affinity CD98 binder actively contributes to cellular uptake and retention, thereby enhancing the overall effectiveness of the TfR-I (low) antibody. At the 0 h timepoint, the intracellular distribution to the early endosome (45%), late endosome (21%), lysosome (17%), and recycling endosome (17%) was similar to that of TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) and TfR-C (high)/CD98-B (low), indicating that in every affinity combination, both binders contribute to the initial intracellular distribution of the bispecific antibodies (Figure 6(C)). After 2.5 h of chasing, the cellular retention of TfR-I (low)/CD98-A (high) was lower than that of TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) and higher than that of TfR-C (high)/CD98-B (low), indicating that the variation of affinity combinations strongly impacts the intracellular trafficking and retention after the initial internalization (Figure 6(C)).

The incorporation of a CD98-binder into the bispecific antibody generally enhanced cellular retention compared to both TfR and CD98-monospecific binder variants, evident through the increased amount of intracellular BrainshuttleTM observed after 2.5 h and 24 h of trafficking (Figure 6(B,C)). However, it did not consistently predict superior transcytosis rates across all bispecific antibody combinations. Instead, the specific affinity interplay between the TfR and CD98 moieties dictates the transcytosis efficiency of the bispecific construct.

CD98 co-engagement generally enhanced transcytosis compared to monospecific TfR antibodies. However, the TfR-C (high)/CD98-B (low) bispecific variant exhibited reduced transcytosis compared to both the other bispecific formats and the monospecific TfR-C (high) and CD98-A (high) binders (Figure 5(B)). Furthermore, for this bispecific binder combination, there was only very limited retention effect observable, as its internalization profile remained only marginally improved relative to the monospecific TfR-high binder at later timepoints (Figure 6(B)).

These data suggest that CD98‘s effect on TfR trafficking and cellular retention is affinity-dependent. Unlike the high-affinity monospecific CD98 antibody, which displayed a distinct cellular pattern, the bispecific trafficking profiles indicate that a specific CD98 affinity threshold is necessary to promote both increased cell retention and effective TfR transcytosis.

Neuronal uptake is ASO driven and independent of the mAb binding target

While uptake into BECs and subsequent transcytosis across the BBB are prerequisites for ASO delivery, the ultimate therapeutic success of an AOC depends on its ability to navigate the parenchymal microenvironment. Beyond simple translocation into the CNS interstitial space, the conjugate must achieve secondary internalization into target cell populations, such as neurons or astrocytes, followed by successful endosomal escape to allow the ASO payload to reach its functional site of action in the cytosol or nucleus.30,31,73 Consequently, target engagement and mRNA reduction within these specific CNS cells are supplementary metrics of efficacy. In this study, a MAPT-targeting ASO was used to characterize the critical role of the mAb component in achieving efficient neuronal ASO delivery and subsequent target mRNA knockdown. The knockdown of MAPT-mRNA was evaluated in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived glutamatergic neurons via qPCR.

When assessed 14 days after the initial treatment, the MAPT mRNA levels in iPSC-derived neurons were decreased in a dose-dependent manner for both TfR-ASO and the CD98-ASO conjugates, indicating that neuronal uptake and productive MAPT knockdown can be achieved with both TfR- and CD98-antibody MAPT-ASO conjugates (Figure 7). The knockdown efficacy was increased when using the higher-affinity TfR-A-ASO conjugate compared to the lower-affinity TfR-I-ASO conjugate. This indicates that a higher affinity of the TfR antibody enhances productive uptake of the mAb-ASO conjugate into neurons. The IC50 of TfR-A-ASO decreased compared to TfR-I-ASO, but the maximal MAPT knockdown between both conjugates was comparable, suggesting a maximal effect that could not be surpassed even with higher concentrations or higher-affinity binders. Only the treatment with unconjugated ASO achieved a higher maximal MAPT knockdown (Figure 7). This supports the hypothesis of previously published results indicating that gymnotic uptake of unconjugated ASOs, in comparison to TfR-mediated uptake of mAb-ASO conjugates, leads to enhanced intracellular routing to productive endosomal compartments and a stronger endosomal escape.29

These results show that productive MAPT knockdown with CD98-ASO conjugates using the BrainshuttleTM design is possible in vitro. The productive MAPT knockdown achieved by the DP47-ASO conjugate suggests that a mechanism for neuronal uptake independent of TfR, such as gymnosis, is utilized by ASOs. This finding is further supported by the prominent MAPT knockdown observed with the unconjugated ASO, which relies solely on gymnosis for neuronal delivery. To assess the contribution of receptor-mediated internalization to neuronal uptake, we compared the neuronal internalization of fluorescently labeled naked ASO versus the TfR-BrS-ASO conjugate (Figure S15). Although the naked ASO demonstrated superior knockdown potency (Figure 7), it exhibited a slightly lower neuronal uptake at 0.5 h and 24 h compared to the TfR-BrS-ASO conjugate. However, a 10-fold excess of TfR blocking antibodies did not significantly reduce conjugate internalization, suggesting that ASO-cell surface interactions, rather than TfR binding, drive the majority of neuronal uptake in this model. The discrepancy between high uptake and lower knockdown for the conjugate likely reflects delayed endosomal escape kinetics. In contrast to established in vivo benchmarks where AOC and naked ASO potencies are comparable, 18 the observed in vitro potency gap may reflect the temporal constraints of the iPSC-derived neuronal model. The 14-day viability window may be insufficient to accommodate the requisite lysosomal processing or proteolytic cleavage of the linker, both of which are essential for releasing the ASO payload from the antibody scaffold.

In summary, treatment with both TfR-ASO and CD98-ASO conjugates successfully achieved dose-dependent MAPT mRNA knockdown in iPSC-derived neurons, with higher affinity TfR binders showing increased potency but comparable maximal effect to lower-affinity counterparts.

To determine whether the observed effects were generalizable across different antisense sequences, an additional knockdown assay was performed using two distinct MAPT-targeting ASO identities (ASO1 and ASO2) conjugated to the TfR-BrS (Figure S16). Rather than comparing individual ASO potencies, this study aimed to assess whether the mAb-mediated delivery mechanism, specifically neuronal uptake, and endosomal escape remained consistent across different cargo sequences. Both TfR-BrS-ASO conjugates achieved comparable knockdown efficiencies; notably, the unconjugated BCN-ASO control also demonstrated similar levels of residual MAPT expression (Figure S16). These data suggest that the attachment of an antibody or linker may impair intracellular mechanisms, such as endosomal escape, regardless of the specific ASO sequence utilized.

Discussion

The systemic delivery of ASOs to the CNS is fundamentally limited by the properties of the BBB. While receptor-mediated strategies, such as TfR-mediated Brainshuttles™, have proven useful, efficacy is limited by the broad peripheral expression of TfR1, which drives rapid systemic clearance.32,33 Addressing these challenges necessitates the development of next-generation delivery platforms with improved efficacy and safety profiles. To overcome the current mechanistic constraints of delivering ASOs across the BBB, this study was designed to mechanistically dissect the impact of ASO conjugation on Brainshuttle™ biology, evaluate the potential of the alternative BBB target CD98hc, and investigate a dual BBB-targeting bispecific strategy.

A central observation of this work is that the ASO payload is not inert; rather, its conjugation was found to fundamentally alter the cellular interactions of the antibody. The Brainshuttle™ properties were observed to be systemically modified upon ASO conjugation, with this effect being dependent on both target binding and affinity. ASO conjugation consistently enhanced apparent binding and internalization in BECs for low- to medium-affinity antibodies, likely by increasing their cell-surface residence time. It is hypothesized that this enhanced interaction is driven by avidity effects, in which the ASO moiety acts as a secondary binder. This multivalent interaction with cell surfaces increases the overall binding strength, a nonspecific mechanism known to critically influence the efficacy and safety profile of oligonucleotide therapeutics.30,68

Conversely, for high-affinity mAbs, these same nonspecific interactions generally resulted in reduced receptor engagement. It is hypothesized that the hydrophobicity and polyanionic charge of the ASO payload may favor intramolecular interactions, which would reduce the high on-rate required for high-affinity binding, effectively acting as a hindrance to optimal receptor recognition.74

Despite the enhanced internalization observed for low-to-medium affinity TfR mAbs, ASO conjugation generally resulted in a reduction or delay in transcytosis efficiency across the hCMEC/D3 monolayer. This suggests that the physicochemical properties of the ASO which promote cellular uptake may concurrently increase unspecific intracellular protein interactions74–76 thereby immobilizing the conjugate and impeding its basolateral release.

A prerequisite for effective CNS delivery is the maintenance of conjugate integrity during BBB passage. Selective quantification via a conjugate-specific ELISA confirmed that the transcytosed species was indeed the intact antibody-ASO complex. This demonstrates that the conjugate remains stable throughout the endosomal sorting and transcytosis events within the brain endothelial layer, validating the platform’s capability to deliver the payload to the parenchyma.

According to previous studies, unconjugated ASOs can interact with broadly distributed cell-surface receptors like integrins, receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and Toll-like receptors (TLRs, especially TLR9), which facilitate their endocytosis across the plasma membrane in many tissues and cell types.74–77 This observation seemingly contrasts with reported results where ASO conjugation had minimal impact on uptake.18 However, such discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in ASO sequences and chemical modifications, but also differences in the experimental systems, including the used cell lines (e.g., HEK or SH-SY5Y vs. hCMEC/D3).18

While ASO conjugation did not appear to fundamentally reroute intracellular trafficking, it resulted in an enhanced accumulation of antibody conjugates inside the cell, particularly after longer chasing times, corroborating the delay of transported ASO conjugates.

These mechanistic findings challenge the current paradigms regarding optimal Brainshuttle™ design. The widely held hypothesis that high antibody affinity for TfR1 inevitably leads to enhanced lysosomal degradation and reduced transcytosis was not supported by the data generated in our human cell models.36,37 In hCMEC/D3 cells used in this study, a correlation between high affinity and lysosomal routing was not found, contrary to observations described in foundational murine in vivo studies.36,46,56–58,67

The investigation of alternative targets showed that CD98 binders exhibit a distinct and potentially advantageous profile compared to TfR1 targeting antibodies. CD98-mediated transcytosis was observed to be either unimpaired or even increased by ASO conjugation, suggesting that the clathrin-independent endocytosis pathway utilized by CD98 may be inherently more permissive to the transport of this specific ASO and potentially other large, chemically complex payloads. However, it should be noted that only a limited number of CD98-binders and one tool ASO were used; further confirmation with a larger set of antibodies and payloads is required to validate this hypothesis.

Trafficking analysis indicated that CD98 exhibits an extended residence time at the plasma membrane, a finding consistent with reports that CD98 bypasses transport to late endosomes and lysosomes.49 Specifically, studies in HeLa cells have described that the heavy chain (CD98hc) tends to bypass transport to late endosomes and lysosomes, thus facilitating its prolonged membrane presence.49 While the monospecific CD98 transcytosis efficiency did not surpass the TfR1 benchmark in this model, the profile of slow but sustained transcytosis aligns with in vivo reports of extended brain exposure, marking CD98 a compelling target.39,48 In summary, our results suggest CD98 may be a promising target for enhanced brain delivery, which aligns with findings previously reported in the literature.39,40,46

Building on these insights, a dual-targeting bispecific antibody approach was developed to co-engage TfR1 and CD98hc. The relevant constructs showed a synergistic effect in vitro, with combinations featuring a high-affinity CD98 arm achieving a significant increase in transcytosis that clearly surpassed the levels of monospecific Brainshuttles™. Mechanistic analysis revealed that the cellular interactions of the bispecific antibodies were not a simple composite of the monospecific components but were instead dominated by the higher-affinity binder. For instance, the TfR-C (high)/CD98-A (high) bispecific demonstrated a trafficking profile resembling the efficient uptake of the high-affinity TfR binder while adding the retention properties of CD98. Notably, the synergy observed in the TfR-I (low)/CD98-A (high) bispecific – where transcytosis activity significantly exceeded that of the individual monospecific components – suggests that the high-affinity CD98 binder actively contributes to cellular uptake and retention.

The transcytosis and trafficking results raise critical questions regarding the underlying transport mechanism: specifically, whether both clathrin-dependent (TfR1) and clathrin-independent (CD98) endocytic pathways are utilized simultaneously, or if, in the case of the low-affinity TfR-I, the CD98 pathway becomes dominant, creating a highly efficient transport route. Given the observed tolerance of CD98 to an ASO payload, testing these bispecifics in a conjugated format with different payloads would be a logical next step.

The finding that the CD98 binder actively contributes to cellular uptake and retention suggests a synergistic mechanism, highlighting that determining the optimal affinity combination is critical to leverage this effect for maximizing transcytosis while minimizing degradation. Recent findings by Wells et al. using a similar dual-targeting approach (TfR/CD98hc) corroborate the potential for enhanced brain delivery, but offer a distinct mechanistic perspective driven by in vivo biodistribution.48 While our in vitro data demonstrates a direct increase in transcytosis for the bispecific format, Wells et al. report enhanced brain concentrations in vivo despite faster systemic clearance, primarily through a combination of TfR1-mediated uptake and CD98hc-mediated retention (reduced brain clearance).48 Specifically, it was shown that CD98hc engagement reduces the TfR1-mediated neuronal internalization that typically leads to lysosomal catabolism, thereby shifting the biodistribution toward astrocytic processes and prolonging brain residence.48 This affirms that the “synergy” observed in our hCMEC/D3 transcytosis assay likely captures the efficient crossing facilitated by the dual-targeting but cannot fully model the parenchymal retention that results in the in vivo benefit.

Furthermore, the potential for simultaneous versus mutually exclusive binding warrants consideration in the context of the specific antibody formats employed. Wells et al. utilized a Fc-engineered Transport Vehicle (TV) where the binding sites are embedded within the CH3 domains of the Fc region.48 While their modeling supported simultaneous binding in solution, their cell-based assays suggested that simultaneous engagement on the cell surface was minimal or absent, likely due to steric constraints or a lack of receptor colocalization.48 In contrast, the bispecific antibodies generated in this study position the binding regions on the Fab arms. It is hypothesized that this Fab-based architecture may offer greater geometric flexibility than the Fc-restricted format, thereby increasing the theoretical probability of simultaneous receptor engagement. However, as experimental confirmation of simultaneous cell-surface binding was not performed in this study, it remains to be elucidated whether the observed functional synergy arises from cooperative co-engagement enabled by the Fab-based format or through independent, sequential receptor interactions.

While this dual BBB-targeting strategy introduces significant technical and safety complexity regarding the mitigation of peripheral binding to broadly expressed receptors, the dramatic enhancement in transcytosis achieved in vitro by co-engaging TfR1 and CD98 highlights the promising potential of this approach.

Functionally, successful dose-dependent MAPT mRNA knockdown was achieved by both TfR-ASO, CD98-ASO, and DP47-ASO conjugates in iPSC-derived neurons. This suggests that, following transcytosis across the BBB model, neuronal uptake, and bioactivity in vitro are primarily driven by the ASO payload itself, likely through mechanisms, such as gymnosis, independent of the antibody target. This ASO-driven internalization is a consistent feature across our models. Non-targeting mAb-ASO conjugates demonstrated measurable accumulation in the lysate fractions of BECs (Figure S8, Figure S9), mirroring the baseline uptake and bioactivity seen in neurons (Figure 7, S15). After cellular uptake, however, an important mechanistic difference in conjugate trafficking between BECs and neurons becomes apparent: the majority of targeted conjugates leaves the BEC intact, while successful knock-down in neurons likely requires cleavage or degradation of the antibody component.

A limitation of this study is the restricted scope of oligonucleotide diversity; differences in sequence or chemical modifications may lead to differential effects on binding, trafficking, and knockdown efficiency.78 Furthermore, while the in vitro data provide a strong mechanistic foundation, the translation of these findings to a systemic context requires in vivo validation.

Our findings regarding the impact of ASO conjugation on antibody trafficking raise important questions for the broader field of oligonucleotide-conjugates, particularly double-stranded payloads like small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). Unlike the phosphorothioate (PS)-modified ASOs used in this study, which possess a relatively hydrophobic and polyanionic nature that facilitates gymnotic uptake, siRNAs are generally larger, more hydrophilic, and do not inherently undergo significant intracellular uptake without specialized delivery systems.34,78–81 Consequently, the mechanistic divergence observed here, for example, where the ASO payload partially drives neuronal entry, may be less pronounced for siRNA-conjugates.

Future studies comparing ASO and siRNA trafficking kinetics will be essential to determine if the kinetic delays observed here are a universal feature of oligonucleotide-conjugates or specific to the protein-binding properties of ASO chemistries.

In conclusion, this work establishes that the ASO payload has an impact on the intracellular behavior of the conjugate, necessitating a holistic co-engineering approach. The results support a reconsideration of affinity-selection criteria for TfR-ASO conjugates and demonstrate that leveraging alternative (CD98), or combined (TfR/CD98) receptor-mediated transcytosis pathways represent a promising strategy for developing novel Brainshuttles™ with improved capacity for delivering complex biologics to the brain.
