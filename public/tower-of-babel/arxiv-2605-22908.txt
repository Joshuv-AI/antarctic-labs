Fractionalization, emergent SU( N N ) symmetries, and fragmentation in layered quantum spin-orbital models
Pedro M. Cônsoli ∗, Aayush Vijayvargia ∗, Onur Erten

Abstract
We propose a family of layered quantum spin-orbital models as a platform to study fractionalization, unconventional forms of symmetry-breaking order, and their possible coexistence. The models are built by stacking N N layers of a square-lattice system in which Kitaev-type interactions promote the formation of a ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} quantum spin-orbital liquid and coupling the different layers via Ising spin interactions. Using a parton construction, we show how, at low energies, these Hamiltonians can be mapped to N N -component Fermi Hubbard models on a π \pi -flux square lattice at half filling. We also demonstrate that the models acquire an emergent SU( N N ) symmetry in the limit of equal all-to-all interlayer couplings and argue that, for N > 2 N>2 , the proximity to this limit offers the potential to realize an array of competing phases. To illustrate this point, we compute the zero-temperature phase diagram of the effective N = 3 N=3 Hubbard model within mean-field theory and uncover rich phenomena, including intertwined orders and flavor-selective localization. Mapping back to the original degrees of freedom reveals that the ground states realize distinct forms of magnetic fragmentation, wherein the orbitals remain in a quantum liquid state whereas the spins can present conventional long-range order or nonlocal order characterized by a nontrivial string order parameters. We highlight possible extensions of our construction as well as its potential to provide concrete microscopic models for different fractionalized quantum critical points.

\left\{n_{i},\mathbf{m}_{i}\right\}
. Hence, it is clear that a nonzero
\mathbf{m}_{i}
signals the onset of SU(
N

II.2
.
In the usual
N=2

\mathbf{m}_{i}=e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{i}}\mathbf{m}
lowers the symmetry of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
from the emergent group
G=\mathrm{SU(2)}
to the subgroup
H=\mathrm{U(1)}
formed by spin rotations around the ordering axis
\mathbf{m}
.
However, the situation becomes richer for
N>2
because a FDW
\mathbf{m}_{i}=e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{i}}\mathbf{m}
can have different residual symmetries
H
, and consequently realize different types of order, depending on the
direction
of
\mathbf{m}
.

To illustrate this last point, consider the case
N=3
, for which the eight generators
S_{i}^{a}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}f_{i\ell}^{\dagger}\lambda^{a}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}f_{i\ell^{\prime}}
are specified by the Gell-Mann matrices
\lambda^{a}
.
Given an order parameter
\mathbf{m}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the symmetry
H

of Eq. (
27
) is determined by finding the set of vectors
\mathbf{t}
for which
\commutator{ \vec{t} \cdot\vec{S}_i }{ \vec{m} \cdot\vec{S}_i }=0
. This is equivalent to obtaining the centralizer of the matrix
M=\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\lambda}=\sum_{a}m_{a}\lambda^{a}
in SU(3), i.e., the set of matrices
T=\mathbf{t}\cdot\bm{\lambda}
that commute with
M
.
We can now contrast two configurations of
\mathbf{m}
that couple to the different diagonal Gell-Mann matrices: (i)
M=m\lambda^{3}
and (ii)
M=m\lambda^{8}
, where

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\lambda^{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-2\end{pmatrix}.

(28)

In case (i), the most general matrix that commutes with
M
has the form
T=t_{3}\lambda^{3}+t_{8}\lambda^{8}
. This implies that Eq. (
27
) is only invariant under global SU(3) rotations generated by
S=t_{3}S^{3}+t_{8}S^{8}
, which can be decomposed into the product of transformations generated by
S^{3}
and
S^{8}
separately. Therefore, the residual symmetry is
H_{1}=\mathrm{U(1)}\times\mathrm{U(1)}
.
By contrast, in case (ii),
M
commutes with any matrix
T=\sum_{a=1}^{3}t_{a}\lambda^{a}+t_{8}\lambda^{8}
, so that Eq. (
27
) is invariant under SU(3) rotations generated by
S=\sum_{a=1}^{3}t_{a}S^{a}
,
S^{8}
, or any linear combination thereof. From this we conclude that the system now has a larger invariant subgroup
H_{2}=\mathrm{SU(2)}\times\mathrm{U(1)}
, which leads to a different phase than (i).

By generalizing this analysis (see Appendix
B
), we find that most mean-field configurations
\mathbf{m}
yield the same residual symmetry as case (i). The exceptions all correspond to case (ii) and are identified as follows. Given a mean field
\mathbf{m}
, one can diagonalize the matrix
M=\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\lambda}
via a unitary transformation
U
to obtain
M^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}MU=m_{3}^{\prime}\lambda^{3}+m_{8}^{\prime}\lambda^{8}
. As depicted in Fig.
2
, the invariant subgroup will be
H_{2}
if and only if
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}=(m_{3}^{\prime},m_{8}^{\prime})
is perpendicular to one of the three vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\bm{\alpha}_{2}=(-1,\sqrt{3})/2
, or
\bm{\alpha}_{3}=(1,\sqrt{3})/2
corresponding to the (positive) roots of SU(3)
92
. As shown in Appendix
B
, this condition is equivalent to
M
having two degenerate eigenvalues.

IV
Mean-field results for
N=3

We now focus on the solution of the half-filled
N=3
mean-field theory, which is the simplest case to admit symmetry-broken states with different residual symmetry groups
H
.
As mentioned previously, we will treat
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{K}
in a fixed gauge sector
\{u\}
that implements a
\pi
-flux hopping background. With this choice, the system’s unit cell is doubled from the outset, so that a staggered FDW order is no longer described by an ordering wavevector
\mathbf{Q}=(\pi,\pi)
, as in a zero-flux square lattice, but instead by
\mathbf{Q}=\bm{0}
and sublattice-dependent mean fields. Assuming that this type of FDW is the leading instability out of the Dirac semimetal realized at low
J/K
, we restrict ourselves to states where
n_{i}=n_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{m}_{\mu}
for sites
i
in sublattice
\mu\in\left\{A,B\right\}
.
We further set
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}_{A}=-\mathbf{m}_{B}
, which follows from the invariance of the Hamiltonian under a spatial inversion that exchanges sublattices
A\leftrightarrow B
and a global
\pi
-rotation
U=\exp(-i\pi S^a)
.
Altogether, this leaves us with nine independent mean-field parameters, which can be chosen to be the eight components of
\mathbf{m}
plus
\Delta n_{A}=n_{A}-3/2
, the deviation of the
A
-sublattice occupation from half filling.

The remainder of this section is structured as follows. In Sec.
IV.1
, we use the above assumptions to derive a specialized version of the theory laid out in Sec.
III
. Then, in Sec.
IV.2
, we examine the solutions of the mean-field equations for a specific, but physically motivated, type of anisotropy of the couplings
J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
. After describing the zero-temperature phases in detail, we discuss the resulting phase diagram and potential effects beyond the scope of our mean-field theory.

IV.1
Mean-field equations

By imposing the assumptions outlined at the beginning of this section and performing a Fourier transform for a system with
N_{c}
unit cells, we arrive at the mean-field Hamiltonian

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm MF}=N_{c}e_{0}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{J}&g_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathds{1}_{3}\\
g_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{3}&-\mathcal{J}\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},

(29)

where
g_{\mathbf{k}}=4K(\sin k_{x}+\mathrm{i}\cos k_{y})
is the hopping form factor in the
\pi
-flux sector,
\mathds{1}_{3}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix, and

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\mathbf{k}A1}^{\dagger}&f_{\mathbf{k}A2}^{\dagger}&f_{\mathbf{k}A3}^{\dagger}&f_{\mathbf{k}B1}^{\dagger}&f_{\mathbf{k}B2}^{\dagger}&f_{\mathbf{k}B3}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

(30)

is a vector of operators
f_{\mathbf{k}\mu\ell}^{\dagger}
that create fermions of flavor
\ell
spread over the sites of sublattice
\mu
with a periodicity given by the wavevector
\mathbf{k}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=e_{0}^{\prime}-8\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}>\ell}J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\left[\left(\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}\right)^{2}+(M_{\ell\ell}+M_{\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}})\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=e_{0}^{\prime}-8\sum_{\ell}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}>\ell}J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}\left.+M_{\ell\ell}M_{\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}-\absolutevalue{M_{\ell\ell'}}^{2}\right],

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\right)4J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}M_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\!{}^{\prime}\neq\ell}4J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\!{}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}+M_{\ell^{\prime}\!{}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}\!{}^{\prime}}\right),

(32)

with
e_{0}^{\prime}
being an irrelevant constant and
M=\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\lambda}
. The presence of the cross-terms in Eq. (
31
) hints at a possible intertwinement of FDW and CDW orders, which is indeed observed in the phase diagram of the model (see Sec.
IV.2

The solutions
\{\Delta n_{A},\mathbf{m}\}
of the mean-field theory are obtained by fulfilling the following set of self-consistency equations:

\displaystyle\Delta n_{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\ell}\expectationvalue{ f_{\vec{k}A\ell}^\dagger f_{\vec{k}A\ell} }-\frac{3}{2},

(33)

\displaystyle m_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N_{c}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\expectationvalue{ f_{\vec{k}A\ell}^\dagger\lambda_{\ell\ell'}^a f_{\vec{k}A\ell'} },

(34)

with
a=1,\ldots,8
. Whereas different types of FDWs can be diagnosed by the vector
\mathbf{m}
,
\Delta n_{A}
serves as an order parameter for a CDW, since the occupation of
A
and
B
sites differs whenever
\Delta n_{A}\neq 0
.

We can translate a solution
\{\Delta n_{A},\mathbf{m}\}
of the mean-field equations into flavor-dependent densities

by means of the transformation (see Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}n_{\mu 1}\\
n_{\mu 2}\\
n_{\mu 3}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}\pm\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1&1/\sqrt{3}\\
1/3&-1&1/\sqrt{3}\\
1/3&0&-2/\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta n_{A}\\
m_{3}\\
m_{8}\end{pmatrix},

(35)

where the upper and lower signs refer to sublattices
\mu=A
and
B
, respectively.
In particular, this transformation shows that, if
\mathbf{m}
lies along one of the dashed lines
\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}
in Fig.
2
, then two of the flavor-resolved densities are identical:
n_{\mu\ell}=n_{\mu\ell^{\prime}}
. This is a manifestation of the enhanced SU(2)
\times
U(1) symmetry obtained along those special directions in
\mathbf{m}
-space.

IV.2
Mean-field phases and phase diagram in the presence of a partial layer anisotropy

To proceed, we consider a specific anisotropy of the interlayer couplings:
J_{12}=J_{23}=J
and
J_{13}=J^{\prime}
. This choice is justified if the system has a mirror symmetry with respect to the middle layer,
\ell=2
[see Fig.
1
(b)]. The Hamiltonian
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
then features an emergent

symmetry, where
\mathrm{SU(2)}_{1,3}
refers to the group of global SU(2) transformations that act on the equivalent flavors
\ell=1,3
and are generated by
\{\tilde{S}^{3},S^{4},S^{5}\}
with
\tilde{S}_{3}=(S^{3}+\sqrt{3}S^{8})/2
. The remaining U(1) subgroup in

is generated by

\tilde{S}^{8}=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{3}S^{3}-S^{8})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\ell}f_{i\ell}^{\dagger}\tilde{\lambda}_{8}^{\ell\ell^{\prime}}f_{i\ell^{\prime}},

(36)

with
\tilde{\lambda}_{8}=\diag(1,-2,1)/\sqrt{3}
being an analog of
\lambda_{8}
that treats flavors
\ell=1,3
on equal footing. At the isotropic point
J^{\prime}/J=1
, the symmetry group of the Hamiltonian is enhanced to

.

We solved Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
) numerically at temperature
T=0
for a wide range of parameters
\{K,J,J^{\prime}\}
on square clusters with
N_{c}=L^{2}
unit cells and linear system sizes
L\in\{80,160,320,640,1280\}
. As a result, we found that, although a generic mean-field solution
\{\Delta n_{A},\mathbf{m}\}
can feature more than two nonzero components
m_{a}
, there is
always
an element in
G
that connects it to a state
\{\Delta n_{A},\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\}
with
m^{\prime}_{a}=0
for
a\neq 3,8
3
3

3

For
J^{\prime}/J\neq 1
, this implies that the matrix
M=\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\lambda}
can be written as a linear combination of just
\lambda_{a}
with
a=3,4,5,8
. For
J^{\prime}/J=1
,
M
can receive nonvanishing contributions from all Gell-Mann matrices.
.
Thanks to this property, we can gain analytical insight into the nature of the fermionic spectrum of the different mean-field phases. Indeed, if we set
M=m_{3}\lambda_{3}+m_{8}\lambda_{8}
, Eq. (

) decomposes into

blocks of fixed momentum

and flavor

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm MF}=N_{c}e_{0}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\ell}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{\ell}&g_{\mathbf{k}}\\

(37)

where
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\ell}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}A\ell}&f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}B\ell}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}J_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\Bigl(\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}+M_{\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}\Bigr).

The straightforward diagonalization of Eq. (

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}\ell\pm}=\pm\sqrt{|g_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}+\Delta_{\ell}^{2}}.

Hence, it is clear that a nonzero

\lambda_{3}

IV.2.1
Phases at

After carefully extrapolating the finite-size solutions of Eqs. (

The first phase is a Dirac semimetal, which is characterized by

and has a total of six Dirac cones (two per flavor
\ell
). This is a maximally symmetric phase, since it preserves both the

The second phase corresponds to solutions that develop CDW order while preserving

\Delta n_{A}\neq 0
, but the second only requires
\mathbf{m}=\bm{0}
at the SU(3)-symmetric point

along the

\displaystyle n_{\mu 1}=n_{\mu 3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}m,

\displaystyle n_{\mu 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{\Delta n_{A}}{3}\mp\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}m,

(40)

where, as before, the upper (lower) sign refers to sublattice

(

VI
Summary and outlook

In summary, we constructed a family of layered Hamiltonians where each member, composed of
N
identical layers of a two-dimensional quantum spin-orbital model, realizes an emergent SU(
N
) symmetry in the limit of equal all-to-all interlayer interactions. We showed how, at low energies, these models can be mapped to
N
-component Hubbard models on a
\pi
-flux square lattice. Furthermore, we invoked symmetry-based arguments to predict that the models with
N>2
harbor a rich
T=0
phase diagram in the proximity of their SU(
N
)-symmetric limit, and then supported this claim by presenting an explicit mean-field calculation for
N=3
. Finally, we elucidated the connection between the ground states of the
N=3
system and different forms of magnetic fragmentation.

Interesting directions for future work include the exploration not only of unconventional phases, but also of quantum critical properties that arise in spin-orbital models with different values of
N
.
In view of their correspondence to effective Hubbard models of relativistic fermions (see Sec.
II.1
), our layered Hamiltonians may – at least in their SU(
N
)-symmetric limits – realize a slew of quantum phase transitions in different Gross-Neveu* universality classes
73
;
27
. The latter are fractionalized variants of the Gross-Neveu universality classes, which were first discussed in the context of high-energy physics
58
;
31
, but later identified as relevant to graphene
34
;
32
;
33
;
12
and related moiré systems
51
;
9
;
40
.
In particular, the link to Gross-Neveu* criticality is guaranteed to apply in the
N=2
case, where tuning
J/K
drives a transition between the spin-orbital liquid and a Néel antiferromagnet
85
that falls within the Gross-Neveu-Heisenberg* universality class
41
;
68
.

Finally, we note that one can effectively dope the spin-orbital Hamiltonians considered above with the addition of a chemical potential term

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{i\ell}\Gamma_{i\ell}^{5}=-\mu\sum_{i\ell}\left(f_{i\ell}^{\dagger}f_{i\ell}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

(47)

Since Lieb’s theorem does not apply away from half filling
50
, this may provide a useful connection to the physics of
N
-component Hubbard models in different flux sectors. It may also reproduce different phases discussed in past studies of the large-
N
limit of SU(
N
) Hubbard-Heisenberg models
1
;
54
, including a chiral spin liquid expected to emerge at strong coupling for average filling of one fermion per site
35
.
