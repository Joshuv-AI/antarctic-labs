Molecular characterization and antimicrobial resistance profiles of Shigella flexneri isolates from pediatric clinical cases in Ahvaz, Iran
Authors: Vamis Kamil, Mohammad Yazdanmanesh, Keyvan Tadayon, Saeed Khoshnood, Behrooz Sadeghi kalani, Hossein Kazemian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShigella is a highly invasive pathogen that causes dysentery and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality in children under five years of age. This agent is a major public health problem in developing countries. Multiple-locus variable-number tandem repeat (VNTR) analysis (MLVA) is a reliable, cost-effective typing method with high discriminatory power and reproducible results. The rise of drug resistance in Shigella strains is a growing global health threat. Despite the significance of Shigella in Iran, there is limited knowledge about genetic diversity and drug resistance profiles of local strains. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to characterize the genetic diversity and drug resistance profiles of Shigella strains isolated in Ahvaz, Iran. A total of 49 Shigella flexneri isolates were recovered from 500 stool samples of pediatric patients. Routine biochemical tests were used to identify all isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed, and resistance genes were detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL), carbapenemase, and Metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) production were detected phenotypically using combination disk assays and confirmed by the CLSI-recommended modified Carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) and EDTA-modified carbapenem inactivation method (eCIM). MLVA based on seven VNTR loci was performed to characterize the genetic diversity of the isolates. All 49 isolates were resistant to ceftazidime, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, ampicillin, and ceftriaxone (100% each). High resistance rates were also observed for imipenem 36/49 (73.5%), meropenem 36/49 (73.5%), azithromycin 21/49 (42.9%), and ciprofloxacin 16/49 (32.7%). Furthermore, phenotypic testing revealed ESBL production in 46/49 (93.9%) isolates and carbapenemase activity in 36/49 (73.5%), of which 22/49 (44.9%) were MBL. PCR analysis identified blaCTX−M 38/49 (77.6%) and blaSHV 35/49 (71.4%) as the most prevalent ESBL genes, whereas blaNDM 14/49 (28.6%), and blaOXA−48 14/49 (28.6%) were the most common carbapenemase genes. MLVA typing divided the isolates into 22 different MLVA types, including 10 clusters and 12 singletons, and locus ms21 showed the highest discriminatory power. The isolates exhibited high genetic diversity with a non-clonal distribution of resistance, which indicates dissemination through horizontal gene transfer. Our results demonstrated that mCIM/eCIM and MLVA are viable methods for investigating Shigella species as they are cost-effective, provide quick results, and allow for easy sharing of numerical data between laboratories.Keywords: Shigella flexneri, Antimicrobial susceptibility tests, MLVASubject terms: Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Shigella spp. are gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped, non-motile bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae1,2. The genus Shigella is classified into four species: Shigella dysenteriae (serogroup A), Shigella flexneri (serogroup B), Shigella boydii (serogroup C), and Shigella sonnei (serogroup D)3. S. flexneri is the most prevalent species in developing countries, while S. sonnei predominates in industrialized nations. The spread of Shigella remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low-resource settings4,5. The emergence of antimicrobial resistance in Shigella strains is a serious challenge. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains frequently exhibit resistance to commonly used first-line oral antibiotics, such as ampicillin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and ciprofloxacin. Additionally, resistance mediated by β-lactamase and carbapenemase genes has complicated treatment options. Although carbapenems remain effective against many MDR Shigella strains, the increasing resistance threatens their utility6–8. Compared with other enteric pathogens such as Salmonella and Vibrio, Shigella requires a notably low infectious dose (10–100 organisms) to cause illness1,9. Genotyping techniques are essential tools for epidemiological investigations, allowing differentiation of bacterial isolates within the same species and offering insights into their genetic relatedness10. A variety of genotyping methods have been applied to Shigella species, including pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)11–13, multilocus variable-number tandem-repeat analysis (MLVA)14–16, multilocus sequence typing (MLST)17, plasmid profiling18,19, ribotyping20,21, and repetitive sequence-based PCR17,22. Among these methods, PFGE is considered the gold standard for molecular subtyping and source tracking of foodborne pathogens. However, PFGE is time-consuming, costly, and requires strict standardization, with results that can be difficult to interpret. In contrast, MLVA is a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based technique that is relatively simple, cost-effective, and highly reproducible. This method exploits the variability in short tandem repeat sequences at multiple VNTR loci and generates portable numerical data, which is suitable for comparison across studies and laboratories2,10. Given the high incidence of shigellosis in children and its impact on public health, focusing on this issue is particularly important. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the genetic diversity and antibiotic resistance patterns of S. flexneri strains among pediatric patients.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection and bacterial isolates

In this study, 500 stool specimens from children with diarrhea were collected from December 2022 to November 2023 at Taleghani Hospital in Ahvaz, Iran. All participants were under 15 years of age. Written informed consent for participation was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all pediatric patients. Samples were cultured on MacConkey agar, Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar, and Salmonella–Shigella (SS) agar (Merck, Germany). In order to detect Shigella isolates, conventional biochemical tests, including Gram stain (Baharafshan, Iran), Triple Sugar Iron Agar (TSI) (MirMedia, Iran), Methyl Red (MR) and Voges-Proskauer (VP) Broth (Merck, Germany), Lysine Iron Agar (LIA) (Merck, Germany), Urea broth, Sulfide Indole Motility (SIM) (Merck, Germany), and Citrate utilization (Merck, Germany), were used23.

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

The antibiotic susceptibility test (DST) of the isolates was performed by the combination disk diffusion method following recommendations of Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2020 guidelines24. The tested antibiotics included ceftriaxone (30 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), meropenem (10 µg), and imipenem (10 µg) (Padtan Teb, Iran).

Phenotypic tests for Extended Spectrum β -Lactamase, Carbapenemase, and Metallo β-lactamase

All isolates were screened for the production of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) enzymes using the combination disk method. In brief, an overnight culture of each isolate was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar (Merck, Germany). ESBL production was evaluated using ceftazidime and ceftazidime/clavulanic acid disks according to CLSI 2020 guidelines24. Carbapenemase production was initially assessed using the carba-NP test method, as described previously25. Since Carba-NP test shows variable sensitivity for different carbapenemase classes, Carbapenem Inactivation Method (mCIM) was used as the definitive phenotypic assay for accurate detection of carbapenemase production according to CLSI guidelines, which detects carbapenemase production through inactivation of meropenem24. Furthermore, MBL production was evaluated using the EDTA- modified Carbapenem Inactivation Method (eCIM), which differentiates MBLs based on EDTA-mediated inhibition of zinc-dependent carbapenemases.

DNA extraction and molecular detection of resistance genes

Genomic DNA was extracted from Shigella colonies by the boiling method. For MLVA PCR, genomic DNA was standardized to 30–50 ng per reaction, with an A260/280 purity ratio between 1.8 and 2.0, as measured by NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, United States). Carbapenemase genes (blaNDM, blaOXA−48, blaOXA−11, blaOXA−23, blaIMP, blaVIM, blaKPC) and ESBL genes (blaCTX−M, blaSHV, blaTEM) were detected by PCR using specific primers Table 126–28. All PCR assays for resistance genes were performed in 25 µL reaction volume containing 12.5 µL PCR Master Mix (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), 2.5 µL of the extracted DNA sample (20 ng/µL), 1 µL Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 2 µL of each primer (10pm/µL), and finally 5 µL distilled water suitable for molecular tests. PCR cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing for 45 s at the temperatures specified in Table 1, extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were analyzed using electrophoresis in 1.5% agarose and gel staining (safe stain loading dye; CinnaGen Co., Tehran, Iran).

MLVA assay

The following seven VNTR loci were selected: ms06, ms07 (CVN001), ms09, ms11, ms21, ms23, and ms32, as previously described by Gorgé et al.29,. The list of primers used for PCR amplification of these VNTR loci is shown in Table 2. For each locus, PCR was performed in a total volume of 25 µL including 12.5 µL PCR Master Mix (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), 3 µL of the extracted DNA sample (20 ng/µL), 2 µL DMSO, 2 µL of each primer (10 pm/µL), and finally 3.5 µL distilled water suitable for molecular tests. The amplification conditions consisted of 93 °C for 5 min, followed by 34 cycles of 93 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. The amplified products were run on 1.5% agarose gels, and stained with safe stain loading dye (CinnaGen Co., Tehran, Iran), and visualized using UV transillumination.

Data analysis

PCR fragment sizes for each VNTR locus were determined by comparison with a 50-bp DNA ladder (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), and the number of repeat units at each locus was calculated by subtracting the flanking region size from the total amplicon size and dividing by the length of the repeat unit. The allelic data were then imported into BioNumerics software (version 3.5; Applied Maths, Kortrijk, Belgium) for analysis. Isolate clustering was performed using categorical coefficients, and genetic relationships were evaluated through a minimum spanning tree (MST). Isolates with identical allelic profiles were considered the same MLVA type (MT). Singletons were classified as unique genotypes, and clusters were defined based on MST analysis. Isolates that differed in no more than one VNTR locus (single-locus variant, SLV) were considered as the same cluster. To statistically evaluate the relationship between MLVA clustering and antimicrobial resistance profiles, concordance analysis was performed using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI).

Results

Description of sample population

In this study, out of the 500 children experiencing acute diarrhea, 49 (9.8%) were diagnosed with shigellosis based on Gram staining and standard biochemical tests, including, TSI, MR-VP, LIA, Urea broth, SIM, and Citrate utilization. A total of 33/49 (67.3%) were detected in males, whereas 16/49 (32.7%) were identified in females.

Antimicrobial resistance profiles

According to our results, ampicillin, ceftazidime, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and ceftriaxone resistance was observed in 49/49 (100%) of S. flexneri isolates, followed by imipenem 36/49 (73.5%), meropenem 36/49 (73.5%), azithromycin 21/49 (42.9%), and ciprofloxacin 16/49 (32.7%).

Phenotypic detection of resistance enzymes

Based on the phenotypic assays, ESBL and carbapenemase activities were observed in 46/49 (93.9%), 36/49 (73.5%) isolates, respectively. According to the CLSI-recommended carbapenem inactivation methods, carbapenemase activity was confirmed using mCIM, while the eCIM confirmed MBL activity. These results were consistent with the results of molecular testing.

Molecular detection of resistance genes

Based on the results, 46/49 (93.9%) harbored at least one ESBL gene, with blaCTX−M 38/49 (77.6%) and blaSHV 35/49 (71.4%) being the most common ESBL genes. Among the carbapenemase genes, blaNDM 14/49 (28.6%) and blaOXA−48 14/49 (28.6%) were the most prevalent genes. No isolates carried blaKPC. A total of 36/49 (73.5%) isolates were positive for at least one carbapenemase gene. The frequency and co‑occurrence patterns of ESBL and carbapenemase genes are shown in Table 3. Among the 36 carbapenemase‑positive isolates, eight isolates (8/49, 16.3%) carried other carbapenemase genes including blaIMP (n = 3), blaVIM (n = 3), and blaOXA−23 (n = 2).

The S. flexneri strains were characterized using MLVA based on seven VNTR loci. In general, 49 S. flexneri isolates were grouped into 22 different MTs with 10 clusters and 12 singleton genotypes. The highest number of isolates was observed in cluster W (n = 8, Fig. 1) and cluster R (n = 6, Fig. 1), respectively. Discussion

Shigellosis primarily affects children, especially in developing countries, where poor sanitation, contaminated water supplies, and inferior living conditions facilitate Shigella transmission4,30. Globally, Shigella infections are estimated to cause approximately 163.2 million cases and 1.1 million deaths annually31. Over the past few decades, S. flexneri has been reported as the predominant Shigella species in Iran10. The present study appears to be the first report to characterize both MLVA-based genotypes and antimicrobial resistance profiles of S. flexneri among children in Ahvaz, Iran. Our findings confirm the high rate of S. flexneri among pediatric patients in this region. The increasing persistence of antibiotic resistance in Shigella highlights the importance of identifying genetic determinants involved in the acquisition and dissemination of resistance. In our study S. flexneriexhibited high rates of resistance to ceftazidime (100%), ceftriaxone (100%), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (100%), which was different from the results found in previously published studies conducted by Nouruzi et al.32,, Medeiros et al.33,, and Dallal et al.34,. These differences in resistance levels may be explained by regional variations in antibiotic consumption patterns and prescribing practices, which play an important role in the development and dissemination of resistant Shigella strains. In the current study, the frequency of ESBL and carbapenemase genes among S. flexneri isolates was evaluated. In recent years, the prevalence of ESBL-producing Shigella isolates has been confirmed in different regions, varied from 9.9% to 54.5%30,35–38. Based on our results, 93.8% (n = 46) of the isolates were positive for ESBL genes. Detection of β-lactamase enzymes in Shigella strains is a definite concern, as it limits the choices for antibiotic therapy. Moreover, the dissemination of mobile resistance determinants may pose a significant threat to the management of serious diseases in the future. Some carbapenemases, especially OXA-48–like enzymes, exhibit weak enzymatic activity, which can cause isolates to appear susceptible or intermediate to meropenem or imipenem in routine disk diffusion. Therefore, mCIM/eCIM provide a more reliable method for detecting carbapenemase activity than routine disk diffusion. In our study, applying the CLSI-recommended mCIM in combination with the eCIM provided higher accuracy and reliability compared to older tests such as the Carba-NP or modified Hodge test. Molecular analysis confirmed these results, showing that blaCTX−M and blaSHV were the most common ESBL genes, while, blaNDM, and blaOXA−48 were the predominant carbapenemase genes among the isolates. The coexistence of these resistance genes suggests the involvement of mobile genetic elements, which may facilitate the dissemination of multidrug resistance. The notable presence of blaNDM and blaOXA−48 in S. flexneri isolates in this study is alarming, especially since carbapenems are not used as first-line agents for the treatment of shigellosis. This finding suggests that the acquisition of these carbapenemase genes is driven by horizontal gene transfer from other carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria present in the gut microbiota or the environment such as contaminated water, inadequate sanitation, and hospital effluents. MLVA is an effective molecular typing technique based on counting the number of VNTR repeats and has advantages such as rapidity, ease, and convenience of interpretation2,4. MLVA typing demonstrated high genetic diversity, dividing the 49 isolates into 22 MTS, 10 clusters, 12 singletons. The Hunter–Gaston discriminatory index (HGDI) for the MLVA assay was 0.94, which indicates a high level of genetic diversity among the isolates. In addition, among the analyzed loci, ms21 showed the highest discriminatory power. The low concordance between MLVA genotypes and resistance profiles (ARI = 0.013) indicates that resistant isolates are distributed across multiple MLVA clusters and that resistance traits are not confined to a single clone but are likely disseminating horizontally among different lineages.

In the present study, a higher proportion of S. flexneri isolates was recovered from male children (67.3%) compared with female children (32.7%). Previous studies have reported gender differences in the incidence of shigellosis among pediatric populations1,39–41. However, despite the higher proportion of male cases observed in our study, MLVA analysis revealed no gender-specific clustering. This suggests that transmission of S. flexneri is more likely associated with shared environmental exposure rather than gender-related factors.

In recent years, several MLVA assays have been developed for Shigella species. Overall, most MLVA-based investigations, including the present study, demonstrate moderate to high genetic diversity, reflecting mixed transmission patterns. In addition, antimicrobial resistance was not confined to specific MLVA types, which indicates the important role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of resistance determinants. In a study by Ranjbar et al., 53 Shigella isolates were divided into 36 different genotypes based on seven loci, which indicates a high level of genetic diversity4. In addition, Ranjbar et al., analyzed 47 S. sonnei isolates were discriminated into 21 different subtypes based on seven loci10. In a study conducted by Liang et al., 60 S. flexneri isolates were grouped into nine serotypes based on eight VNTR loci, which indicated a low level of genetic diversity2. In another study by Wang et al., 36 VNTR loci were identified after testing 67 selected tandem repeat loci on nine S. flexneri isolates of various subserotypes42. In a study carried out by Gorgé et al., 15 VNTR loci were discovered by examining five genomic sequences from four Shigella species. The 15 loci were evaluated on 19 S. flexneri isolates of 8 serotypes29. In a study conducted by Qu M et al., in Beijing, China, 180 strains of S. flexneri and S. sonnei were divided into 84 MLVA subtypes based on ten VNTR loci, suggesting a moderate diversity43.

Overall, the presence of several small clusters with multiple singleton MTs suggests a mixed transmission dynamics in Ahvaz. This pattern may indicate limited clonal expansion of certain successful lineages (clusters) coexisting with multiple independent introductions or localized microevolutionary events (singletons).

Standardized MLVA protocols for Shigella have been successfully applied in multicenter and international studies, demonstrating high reproducibility and inter-laboratory comparability15,29,44. These reports are consistent with our results and support the use of MLVA as a portable and exchangeable typing method for epidemiological investigations. Compared with whole-genome sequencing (WGS), PFGE, and MLST, MLVA is cost-effective, time-efficient, and more accessible for routine use45,46. An additional advantage of MLVA is the generation of numerical allelic profiles, which facilitates comparison and data exchange between laboratories. Despite these advantages, MLVA also has limitations, such as potential susceptibility to homoplasy, where unrelated isolates may develop similar VNTR patterns. Consequently, this may occasionally reduce the discriminatory power of MLVA compared with WGS.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insight into the genetic diversity and antimicrobial resistance patterns of S. flexneri isolates from pediatric patients in Ahvaz, Iran. Our MLVA findings indicated moderate to high genetic diversity, which shows mixed transmission patterns. In addition, antimicrobial resistance was generally distributed across different MLVA types, suggesting the role of horizontal gene transfer. These results support the use of MLVA as a practical typing method and may assist in future epidemiological monitoring of Shigella species.
