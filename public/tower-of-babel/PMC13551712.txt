Efficacy of abdominal drainage in perforated appendicitis: a retrospective single-center study using AAST grading and inverse probability treatment weighting analysis
Authors: Chengzhi Wang, Shikuan Li, Hao Lu, Hairui Liu, Zongping Yu, Shengqiang Wang, Dawei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile routine use of abdominal drainage in cases of perforated appendicitis is not generally recommended, there exists a paucity of evidence regarding the detailed stratification of its necessity based on varying degrees of intraoperatively detected abdominal contamination. This study aimed to assess the relationship between abdominal drainage and postoperative outcomes in patients with perforated appendicitis, categorized according to AAST severity grades.MethodsThis study retrospectively included 278 patients with perforated appendicitis who underwent laparoscopic surgery between January 2021 and December 2025. Patients were classified according to the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) grading criteria into AAST grades 3&4 (126 patients) and AAST grade 5 (152 patients). The primary outcome measured was the occurrence of surgical complications within 30 days post-surgery. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to control for confounding variables, and E-value analysis was conducted alongside multifactorial logistic regression to evaluate the relationship between drainage and outcomes.ResultsIn patients with AAST grade 5 appendicitis, abdominal drainage did not significantly reduce the risk of intra-abdominal abscess (aOR = 1.60, 95% CI: 0.54–4.72, P = 0.396) and may be linked to an increased risk of overall surgical complications (aOR = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.88–5.28, P = 0.091). Multifactorial logistic regression analysis indicated that drainage was an independent risk factor for surgical complications (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.02–5.77, P = 0.045). The median length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the drainage group compared to the non-drainage group (5.0 days vs. 3.0 days), as were medical costs (CNY 16,100 vs. CNY 14,100), with both comparisons yielding P < 0.001. Among patients with AAST grades 3&4 appendicitis, no statistically significant differences were observed between the drainage and non-drainage groups regarding overall complications (15.4% vs. 7.0%, P = 0.236) or intra-abdominal abscess (11.5% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.102). Furthermore, in AAST grades 3&4 patients, drainage did not significantly influence the risk of overall complications, abdominal abscesses, or other secondary outcomes (all P > 0.05).ConclusionFor AAST grade 5 perforated appendicitis, routine drainage fails to prevent complications and may increase risks, recovery time, and costs. Likewise, no significant benefit was observed in grades 3&4. Therefore, routine drain use is not recommended, favoring individualized intraoperative decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05025-8.Keywords: Abdominal Drainage, Perforated Appendicitis, AAST grading, Laparoscopic Appendectomy

Background

Perforated appendicitis represents a form of acute appendicitis that requires urgent surgical intervention [1–3]. The intraoperative placement of an abdominal drain has historically been considered a standard practice intended to mitigate the risk of abdominal infection and to monitor postoperative bleeding or bowel leakage [4, 5]. Since Dr. Kurt Semm performed the first laparoscopic appendectomy (LA) in 1980, this technique has gained widespread adoption globally [6]. In comparison to open surgery, LA enables thorough exploration of the abdominal cavity, facilitates an accurate assessment of contamination levels, and ensures a clear operative field. These advantages facilitate meticulous dissection and precise hemostasis, thereby significantly decreasing the incidence of complications, including intestinal leakage and postoperative bleeding [7, 8].

Acute appendicitis is internationally classified into uncomplicated and complicated forms, with perforated appendicitis categorized as complicated [1]. While abdominal drainage is frequently employed in cases of complicated appendicitis to mitigate the risk of postoperative abdominal infections, numerous meta-analyses, retrospective studies, and small prospective studies have raised questions regarding its actual efficacy [9–12]. Furthermore, there remains a paucity of research investigating the impact of drainage on infection rates in relation to the extent of intraoperative abdominal contamination. The grading system for acute appendicitis, proposed by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST), has been extensively validated and utilized [13, 14]. Dr. Yu-Tso Liao has further refined the interpretation of the AAST grading, particularly in distinguishing between grades 3 (confined contamination), 4 (confined abscess or peripheral organ inflammation), and 5 (diffuse peritoneal contamination) in cases of perforated appendicitis, thereby providing a crucial foundation for this study [15].

Given the aforementioned controversies and the absence of refined evidence, coupled with the current practice at our center where surgeons frequently base drainage decisions on the extent of intraoperative contamination, this study aimed to stratify patients with perforated appendicitis according to the AAST grading system. Additionally, it sought to evaluate the postoperative clinical significance of abdominal drainage across varying levels of contamination.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study involved 278 patients diagnosed with acute perforated appendicitis who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy (LA) at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University between January 2021 and December 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) confirmation of perforated appendix via abdominal CT, surgical records, or postoperative pathology. The exclusion criteria included: (1) conversion to open surgery during laparoscopy; (2) postoperative pathological confirmation of appendiceal tumor; (3) pregnancy or perinatal period; (4) immunosuppressive conditions, such as HIV/AIDS co-infection, long-term or preoperative use of immunosuppressants, glucocorticosteroids, anti-tumor chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, or immunotherapeutic drugs. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University(QYFY WZLL 30896). Due to the retrospective design and the use of anonymized clinical data for analysis, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

Grouping basis

Abdominal contamination was assessed using the AAST grading criteria for acute appendicitis. Grading involved confirming appendiceal perforation through intraoperative findings and postoperative pathology. Subsequently, the distribution of peritoneal contamination was evaluated following the allocation algorithm outlined by Liao et al. [15], as shown in Fig. 1. Due to the limited sample size from a single center, AAST grade 3 (limited contamination) and grade 4 (limited abscess/peripheral organ inflammation) were combined, while AAST grade 5 (diffuse abdominal contamination) was analyzed independently in this study.

Drain management

The abdominal drains utilized in this study were 18-Fr latex passive drainage tubes. The standard guideline for drain removal was a drain output of less than 50 mL/day of clear serous fluid, with the ultimate determination of removal timing being at the discretion of the attending surgeon, contingent upon the patient’s clinical status.

Endpoints

Primary endpoint: surgical complications occurring within 30 days post-surgery, evaluated for severity using the Clavien-Dindo grading system (with the most severe complication recorded for each patient) and independent counts of specific complications. Major surgical complications included wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess (IAA), ileus, and appendiceal stump fistula. Major medical complications encompassed pulmonary infection and bloodstream infection. Wound infection was characterized by redness, swelling, and purulent exudate from the incision within 30 days postoperatively. IAA was defined by the presence of fever, elevated infection markers, and signs of abdominal infection confirmed through imaging (CT/Ultrasound) within 30 days postoperatively. Ileus was indicated by abdominal pain, vomiting, and cessation of gas and bowel evacuation within 30 days postoperatively, with CT confirmation of intestinal dilatation accompanied by air-fluid levels. Appendiceal stump fistula was identified by the drainage of fecal fluid via drain or puncture within 30 days postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included unplanned readmissions to the Emergency Department(ED) within 30 days post-surgery, postoperative length of stay (LOS, measured in days from the end of surgery to discharge), and total hospital costs. Notably, readmissions to the ED within 30 days were classified as complications only if they resulted in the diagnosis of a new complication that necessitated corresponding treatment. Patients who were evaluated solely for symptoms and excluded organic disease were included in the analysis of readmission rates only.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were found to be non-normally distributed according to the Shapiro-Wilk test; therefore, they were reported as median (interquartile range). Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To mitigate selection bias and confounding factors in drainage decisions for AAST 5, inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed for analysis [16]. Based on clinical judgment, the following covariates were selected to develop a propensity score model: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, history of previous abdominal surgery, Appendicitis Inflammatory Response (AIR) score, and appendix diameter. Covariate balance was evaluated using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an absolute SMD value of < 0.1 deemed well balanced. To visually assess covariate balance before and after weighting, we created a Love plot of the SMD. The figure illustrates the SMD distribution of covariates before and after weighting, where absolute SMD values below 0.1 indicate a well-balanced distribution between groups. Following the application of weighting, the impact of abdominal drainage on postoperative complications was evaluated using a logistic regression model that incorporated survey weighting (employing stable weights). The primary analysis model included solely the “drainage” variable to compute its adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for total complications. To examine the potential influence of unmeasured confounders on the study results, E-values were determined for the association between “abdominal drainage” and the primary outcomes of “surgical complications” and “IAA"in the inverse probability-weighted logistic regression model [17]. Variables with a weighted SMD exceeding 0.1 were included in the traditional multivariate logistic regression model, along with “drainage,” for the purpose of sensitivity analysis. In a supplementary analysis, IPTW was applied to the entire cohort, incorporating AAST grade (3&4 vs. 5) as an additional covariate in the propensity score model. Subsequently, weighted logistic regression was conducted to assess surgical complications and IAA. To visually summarize the findings from the primary analysis, an aOR forest plot was generated. A post hoc power analysis for surgical complications was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) with Fisher’s exact test (two-sided α = 0.05), utilizing the observed subgroup rates and sample sizes. All tests were two-sided, with P-values less than 0.05 deemed statistically significant. The primary statistical analyses and figures were generated using RStudio (version 4.5.2; RStudio Inc., Boston, MA, USA), with the exception of the AAST grading flowchart.

Results

A total of 278 patients participated in this study, comprising 126 individuals in the AAST 3&4 group and 152 individuals in the AAST 5 group. Among the patients classified with AAST grades 3&4, 26 were in the drainage group and 100 in the non-drainage group. In the AAST grade 5 cohort, 59 patients were in the drainage group, while 93 were in the non-drainage group. The clinical characteristics of the patients upon admission are presented in Table 1. For those with AAST grades 3&4, a borderline significant difference in gender distribution was observed between the drainage and non-drainage groups (females: 53.8% vs. 33.0%, P = 0.050). However, no significant differences were noted in age, BMI, comorbidities, or history of previous abdominal surgeries (P > 0.05). The drainage group exhibited a longer median pain duration (60.0 h vs. 38.0 h, P = 0.016), a higher AIR score (9.0 vs. 7.0, P < 0.001), and a larger appendix diameter (15.0 mm vs. 13.0 mm, P = 0.004). No statistical difference was found in the distribution of ASA grading between the two groups (P = 0.152).In patients classified as AAST grade 5, the two groups exhibited comparable characteristics regarding gender, age, comorbidities, and history of previous abdominal surgery (P > 0.05). However, the drainage group demonstrated a higher body mass index (BMI) of 25.39 compared to 23.63 (P = 0.029), a longer duration of pain at 48.0 h versus 24.0 h (P = 0.043), elevated AIR scores of 9.0 compared to 8.0 (P < 0.001), and a larger appendiceal diameter measuring 15.0 mm versus 12.5 mm (P < 0.001). Additionally, the distribution of ASA grading did not show a significant difference (P = 0.106). Table 2 presents a comparison of postoperative outcomes between the drainage and non-drainage groups across various AAST stratifications. In patients classified with AAST grades 3&4 (N = 126), no significant differences were observed in complication rates between the drainage group (n = 26) and the non-drainage group (n = 100). The distribution of Clavien-Dindo grading was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.148). Additionally, the incidence of surgical complications (15.4% vs. 7.0%, P = 0.236), wound infections (0.0% vs. 4.0%, P = 0.580), IAA (11.5% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.102), ileus (3.8% vs. 1.0%, P = 0.371), appendiceal stump fistula (3.8% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.206), and medical complications (3.8% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.206) did not differ statistically. The rate of re-ED visits within 30 days was also similar (11.5% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.390). However, the drainage group incurred a higher median total hospitalization cost (CNY 16,300 vs. CNY 13,900, P < 0.001) and experienced a longer postoperative LOS (5.0 days vs. 3.0 days, P < 0.001). In patients with AAST grade 5 (N = 152), there was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of Clavien-Dindo grades between the two groups (P = 0.029), and the incidence of surgical complications in the drainage group was significantly higher than that in the non-drainage group (27.1% vs. 12.9%, P = 0.028).Other complications, including wound infection (6.8% vs. 1.1%, P = 0.075), IAA (15.3% vs. 8.6%, P = 0.205), ileus (6.8% vs. 5.4%, P = 0.736), appendiceal stump fistula (1.7% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.388), medical complications (5.1% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.057), and re-ED rate within 30 days (16.9% vs. 10.8%, P = 0.271), did not show statistically significant differences. In accordance with the AAST grades 3&4 results, total hospitalization costs were higher in the drainage group (CNY 16100 vs. CNY 14100, P < 0.001), and the postoperative LOS was extended (5.0 days vs. 3.0 days, P < 0.001). For patients classified as AAST grade 5, we employed IPTW to mitigate confounding factors. The weighted effective sample size was 115.6. Except for “previous abdominal surgery " and “AIR score”, the absolute values of the other SMD were all < 0.1 (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1).The weighted logistic regression analysis indicated that the aOR for drainage compared to surgical complications was 2.16 (95% CI: 0.88–5.28, P = 0.091), while the aOR for IAA was 1.60 (95% CI: 0.54–4.72, P = 0.396), neither of which achieved statistical significance (Table 3). To further address residual confounding, we conducted multifactorial logistic regression using raw data that included drainage, history of previous abdominal surgery, and AIR score (Table 4). The findings revealed that drainage served as an independent risk factor for surgical complications (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.02–5.77, P = 0.045), whereas it did not demonstrate a significant association with IAA (aOR = 1.58, 95% CI: 0.55–4.51, P = 0.396). Additionally, neither the history of previous abdominal surgery nor the AIR score exhibited a significant independent association with complications in the multivariate analysis. The aOR and their 95% CI derived from the aforementioned regression models have been synthesized and illustrated using aOR forest plots (Fig. 3). A supplementary IPTW analysis of the entire cohort, incorporating AAST grade as an additional covariate, produced results that aligned with the primary analysis. For surgical complications, the aOR for drainage was 2.02 (95% CI: 0.97–4.25, P = 0.062), while the aOR for intra-abdominal abscess was 1.45 (95% CI: 0.54–3.92, P = 0.463) (Supplementary Table S3). All covariates demonstrated adequate balance following weighting, with all SMD<0.1 (Supplementary Table S2). A post hoc power analysis for surgical complications yielded an achieved power of 0.549 for the AAST grade 5 subgroup (actual α = 0.038) and 0.260 for the AAST grades 3&4 subgroup (actual α = 0.024).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical efficacy of abdominal drainage in patients with perforated appendicitis by stratifying them according to the extent of abdominal contamination and adjusting for confounding variables using IPTW. Our findings indicated that among patients classified as AAST grade 5, abdominal drainage did not decrease the risk of postoperative complications or IAA. Conversely, in patients with limited contamination, specifically those with AAST grades 3&4, we observed no statistically significant association between drainage and outcomes, which can be attributed to the low incidence of complicating events.

Previous studies have generally indicated that the routine prophylactic placement of drains for perforated appendicitis does not reduce postoperative complications and may extend hospitalization [12, 15]. However, most of these studies have treated perforated appendicitis as a homogeneous condition and have infrequently stratified their analyses based on the degree of abdominal contamination. In this study, we demonstrated that patients undergoing drainage in AAST grade 5 typically exhibited higher AIR scores, prolonged abdominal pain, and larger appendiceal diameters, along with a significantly higher BMI. These characteristics collectively suggest a more severe condition, a heightened systemic inflammatory response, or a more complex surgical procedure. Therefore, drainage is more likely to act as an indicator of “seriousness of illness” in this cohort rather than as an independent cause of complications. This finding aligns with the conclusions of multiple studies indicating that drainage is more frequently employed in instances of significant intraoperative contamination and complexity [9, 10].

This study further substantiates that abdominal drainage may elevate the risk of postoperative complications, extend hospitalization, and increase treatment costs in patients with AAST grade 5. Even in cases of diffuse abdominal contamination, routine abdominal drainage remains inadvisable, aligning with the recommendations of the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) guidelines [18]. Potential mechanisms of harm associated with drainage involve the compromise of skin and peritoneal integrity, which can introduce iatrogenic infections or facilitate the flow of contaminated fluids, thereby heightening the risk of incisional or abdominal infections. Additionally, drainage may compress surrounding tissues, adversely affecting blood flow and function, which can delay the recovery of intestinal peristalsis, increase the risk of intestinal infarction, and prolong hospital discharge [12].

The primary findings of this study indicate that prophylactic abdominal drainage does not fulfill its intended purpose of reducing IAA rates in patients with AAST grade 5 appendicitis and may pose potential harm. The Clavien-Dindo classification I/II complication rate among AAST grade 5 patients who underwent drainage in this study was 25.4%, comparable to the findings of a previous retrospective study, which reported a rate of 28% [15]. In contrast, the IAA rate in this study was 15.3%, exceeding the rate observed in that study (4.41%) but aligning more closely with another study that reported an IAA rate of 11% [19]. Among AAST grade 5 patients, the relationship between drainage and surgical complications varied based on the analytical approach employed. Multifactorial logistic regression analysis revealed that drainage constituted a significant independent risk factor for surgical complications (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.02–5.77, P = 0.045).After adjusting for baseline differences in gender, age, BMI, comorbidities, history of previous abdominal surgery, appendiceal diameter, and AIR score through IPTW, the strength of this association was slightly diminished, resulting in a borderline significant p-value (aOR = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.88–5.28, p = 0.091). A supplementary IPTW analysis of the entire cohort, incorporating AAST grade as a covariate, produced results consistent with the primary analysis. Drainage continued to be associated with an increased risk of surgical complications (aOR = 2.02, P = 0.062), with no observed benefit for the prevention of IAA. Effect estimates were modestly attenuated compared to the AAST grade 5 subgroup, likely reflecting dilution from lower-severity cases and underscoring the importance of severity-stratified assessment. Theoretically, IPTW is more effective in controlling for measured confounding by simulating randomization, which suggests that its results may more accurately reflect the true effect. This indicates that the trend of heightened complication risk related to drainage continues; however, the post hoc power analysis reveals that the AAST 5 subgroup possessed only 54.9% power to identify this association. This limitation may partially account for the borderline significance observed in the weighted model. Furthermore, it implies that factors influencing the decision to drain may also elevate the risk of postoperative complications (e.g., more severe local inflammation or intraoperative conditions) in the unweighted data, thereby exaggerating the “apparent” effect of drainage in the conventional model. The robustness of unmeasured confounding was further evaluated using the E-value. In the IPTW model, the association between drainage and surgical complications (aOR = 2.16) yielded an E-value of 3.74. This indicates that a stronger unmeasured confounding effect (risk ratio ≥ 3.74) would be necessary to fully explain the observed association, thereby suggesting a degree of robustness in the findings. Conversely, the association between drainage and IAA was not significant in either model (aOR = 1.60, P = 0.396) and exhibited a lower E-value of 2.58. This suggests that the negative finding is more fragile and warrants careful interpretation.

Considering both analyses, this study concludes that in patients with AAST grade 5 diffuse contamination, abdominal drainage does not confer a net benefit and may, in fact, elevate the overall risk of surgical complications. This finding challenges the prevailing notion that “severe contamination must be drained.” Furthermore, the increased length of hospitalization and higher medical costs observed in the drainage group underscore the necessity of avoiding unnecessary drainage procedures. In patients with AAST grades 3&4, no significant differences in complications were noted between the drainage and non-drainage groups. Nevertheless, the post hoc power analysis revealed that the AAST 3&4 subgroup had a mere 26.0% power to detect the observed difference, indicating a substantial risk of type II error. Hence, the absence of significance should not be construed as proof of parity between drainage and non-drainage within this subgroup. Clinically, this study advocates against the routine use of abdominal drainage in patients with AAST grade 5 perforated appendicitis, recommending that its application be assessed on an individual basis, taking into account intraoperative specifics such as pus distribution, flushing effects, and the patient’s systemic condition.

Limitations: This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design may introduce unmeasured confounders, including surgeon experience, drain type and duration of retention, and abdominal irrigation methods. Although inverse probability weighting and E-value analysis were employed to mitigate bias, the potential for residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, the limited sample size, particularly within the AAST grades 3&4 drainage subgroup (n = 26), constrained the statistical power of this study and necessitated the combination of grades 3&4 into a single subgroup. While both grades indicate localized contamination, grade 3 pertains solely to confined contamination, whereas grade 4 encompasses a localized abscess; thus, merging these categories may obscure potential differences between them. The low number of events also precluded the application of IPTW to this subgroup, restricting its analysis to the AAST grade 5 group. A post hoc power analysis confirmed the limited statistical power for the AAST 3&4 subgroup (26.0%), in contrast to 54.9% for the AAST grade 5 subgroup. Consequently, non-significant findings, particularly in the 3&4 subgroup, may indicate type II error rather than true equivalence. Therefore, larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and to evaluate grades 3 and 4 separately. Finally, the data were sourced from a single center, which, while ensuring process consistency, may limit the external applicability of the conclusions. Future multicenter, prospective studies are essential to validate the findings of this research. A prospective design would facilitate the harmonization of drainage indications with perioperative management, thereby enabling a more precise assessment of the independent effects of drainage. Furthermore, an increased sample size would permit a more detailed stratification of varying levels of contamination and underlying disease status, allowing for the identification of patient subgroups that may either benefit from or be adversely affected by drainage. Ultimately, this approach would yield a higher level of evidence-based and individualized guidance for decision-making concerning abdominal drainage in cases of perforated appendicitis.

Conclusion

This study confirms that in patients with AAST grade 5 perforated appendicitis, abdominal drainage does not decrease the risk of postoperative abdominal abscesses or overall complications. Instead, it may be linked to an increase in surgical complications, extended hospitalization, and elevated healthcare costs. Moreover, no statistically significant differences in complication rates were observed between the drainage and non-drainage groups among patients with AAST grades 3 &4; however, given the low statistical power for this subgroup, this non-significant finding should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence.

These results indicate that, even in patients with severe abdominal contamination classified as AAST grade 5, routine abdominal drainage does not provide clear benefits and may introduce additional risks. Consequently, the routine placement of intraoperative abdominal drainage is not recommended, even in cases of perforated appendicitis that have progressed to diffuse abdominal contamination. Clinical decision-making should be tailored to the specifics of intraoperative contamination, the effectiveness of irrigation, and the patient’s systemic condition. Future multicenter prospective studies are essential to further validate these findings and to identify specific subgroups that may derive benefits from drainage.
