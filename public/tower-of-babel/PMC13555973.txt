Patronin facilitates neurite remodeling via epithelial-to-neuronal signaling
Authors: Wanyue Xu, Su Wang, Xinting Lv, Keyao Long, Keyi Mo, Menglong Rui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During development, the formation of precise neuronal connections and the refinement of neural circuits involves neurite pruning, a process that eliminates superfluous axons and dendrites without losing their parental cells. In Drosophila, the larval dendrites of class IV dendritic arborization (C4da) sensory neurons are selectively removed during development. Epidermal cells are known to be the primary phagocytes in the clearance of pruned and injured dendrites of Drosophila sensory neurons. However, its potential to act as a microenvironment that transmits signaling to facilitate dendritic pruning prior to performing phagocytosis is ambiguous. Here, we show that epithelium downregulation of Patronin, a conserved microtubule (MT) minus-end-binding protein, leads to dendrite pruning deficiency of C4da neurons. Mechanistically, Patronin compromises Flamingo, an adhesion GPCR, in the epithelium, maintaining normal function of neuronal Flamingo and facilitating dendritic breakage at the proximal region of C4da neuron soma, implying that epithelium may promote dendritic pruning through an intercellular mechanism, earlier than the execution of their phagocytic function. Furthermore, we also find that neuronal Flamingo represses the Drosophila Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3β (GSK3β) homolog Shaggy (Sgg) in dendrite remodeling. Collectively, these results demonstrate a novel epidermal-derived mechanism in promoting dendritic pruning, with the expectation of understanding the microenvironmental role of epithelial cells from a new perspective.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03026-4.

Introduction

Neurons often make excessive branches and connections close to their final targets during the early development of the nervous system. To ensure the establishment of functionally mature neural circuits, it is necessary to remodel the nervous system at later stages of development [1, 2]. Neuronal pruning is a highly regulated self-destruct process that eliminates excessive neuronal branches in the absence of cell death [3]. This mechanism widely occurs in the developing nervous systems of both vertebrates and invertebrates [4, 5]. It is noteworthy that developmental neurite pruning has been found in several regions of the mammalian brain, such as the hippocampus, the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum, and the olfactory bulb over the last few decades [2, 4, 5]. Importantly, it has been discovered that people with autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) have higher dendritic spine densities that might be due to impaired pruning [6, 7], whereas schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s disease are thought to be associated with excessive synaptic pruning, which leads to a significant reduction in synaptic density during specific developmental periods [8,9]. Besides, developmental pruning entails local degeneration and terminal retraction, which have a morphological resemblance to neurodegeneration linked to nerve injury and age-related neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, the pathogenesis of human brain disorders would greatly benefit from a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms of developmental pruning.

During metamorphosis in holometabolous insects, the nervous system undergoes significant remodeling. Stereotyped pruning is performed on many larval-born neurons in the central and peripheral nervous systems of Drosophila to eliminate their larval dendrites and/or axons during metamorphosis [10]. The γ neurons of the mushroom body (MB) in the central nervous system (CNS) eliminate the dorsal and medial axons and all dendrites and then regenerate the medial branches to establish adult circuits [11]. Within the Drosophila peripheral nervous system (PNS), there are several types of sensory neurons that either undergo apoptosis or, in an ecdysone-dependent manner, prune their larval projections. During metamorphosis in Drosophila, the motor neurons that innervate larval muscles at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) are pruned mainly by a retraction mechanism [12–14]. In particular, the class IV dendrite arborization (C4da) neurons keep axon integrity while specifically and completely truncating the long and ramified dendrites during the early pupal stage [3, 15, 16]. Dendritic pruning of C4da neurons occurs in the following steps: at the start of the pupal phase, ecdysone triggers the expression of pruning factors, comprising Sox14 and Mical, in addition to headcase [17, 18]. Subsequently, the proximal dendrites undergo severing, and then the epithelium acts as a phagocyte, recognizing phosphatidylserine (PS), a conserved “eat-me” signal on the surface of degenerating neurites, through the engulfment receptor Draper, and engulfing the fragmented dendrites, eventually completing the clearance (Fig. 1A) [19, 20]. Whereas, phagocytosis of degenerating axons and dendrites is primarily carried out by glial cells in the CNS [21–24], and a recent study has shown that peripheral glial cells can regulate dendritic pruning via TNF/Eiger in the PNS [25]. Regardless, phagocytes seem to engulf and clear the neurites that have already undergone severing and fragmentation in an immune-responsive manner. It is completely unclear whether, prior to phagocytosis, these peripheral phagocytes can act as a “microenvironment” to facilitate the severing process, an early phase of dendritic pruning, through an intercellular mechanism.

The calmodulin-regulated spectrin-associated proteins (CAMSAPs) are a conserved family of microtubule (MT) minus-end-binding proteins that play an essential role in the stabilization and formation of non-centrosomal MTs. They protect the minus end from the depolymerizing effects of the kinesin-13 family depolymerases [26–28]. Three CAMSAP proteins exist in mammals: CAMSAP1, CAMSAP2, and CAMSAP3/Nezha [29, 30]. CAMSAP2 and CAMSAP3 are capable of binding and stabilizing the growing minus ends of noncentrosomal MTs at the adherens junctions, providing a source for MT plus-end outgrowth [29, 31]. Destabilizing MTs and reducing dendrite complexity is a consequence of the compromised CAMSAP2 in cultured hippocampal neurons [32]. Drosophila Patronin, known as short spindle protein 4 (Ssp4), is the most well-known protein in the CAMSAP/Patronin family, which shares the highest homology with CAMSAP3. It was first discovered that Patronin regulates the length of the mitotic spindle in Drosophila S2 cells [33]. C. elegans PTRN-1, orthologous to CAMSAP/Patronin, is necessary for synaptic vesicle positioning, neuronal patterning, and axonal regeneration [34–36]. The amino-terminus region of Patronin/CAMSAP/PTRN-1 contains a calponin homology (CH) domain, the central region holds three predicted coiled-coil domains, and the carboxyl-terminus has one CAMSAP/KIAA1078/KIA1543 (CKK) domain [37]. The C-terminal CKK domain is responsible for detecting the minus-end of MTs, and this sequence is highly conserved across distinct eukaryotes [38, 39]. Previous research in Drosophila has analyzed the functional interactions between the spectraplakin Short stop (Shot) and Patronin in establishing noncentrosomal MT arrays to specify the anterior-posterior axis of embryos [40, 41]. Recently, Patronin has been identified as a factor that facilitates the reactivation and regeneration of quiescent neural stem cells in Drosophila [42]. In Drosophila, Patronin is also involved in the organization of presynaptic MTs and the development of neuromuscular junctions [43]. Moreover, the minus-end-out orientation of dendritic MTs is controlled by Patronin in Drosophila C4da sensory neurons, which is necessary for dendritic pruning [44]. However, it is not yet clear whether Patronin functions in non-neuronal cells and has a non-cell-autonomous mechanism for regulating neural remodeling.

Dendrites from dendritic arborization (da) neurons are extensively surrounded by the epidermal cells in Drosophila larvae [45, 46]. The epidermis encloses dendrites, that is, wraps around a part of the dendritic tree, and it is suggested that the enclosure of dendrites by epidermal cells restricts dendritic branching and allows for the coordinated growth of da sensory neurons that overlap in space [47–49]. In this study, we first demonstrate that Patronin is involved in neural remodeling through a transcellular way in epithelial cells, in addition to its known cell-autonomous role in regulating the pruning of dendrites in C4da sensory neurons. Dendritic severing, as revealed by dendritic breakage at the proximal region of C4da neuron soma, is suppressed by Flamingo in epithelial cells, but it is promoted by Flamingo in C4da sensory neurons. The functional antagonism between epithelial cells and C4da sensory neurons appears to result in opposite regulatory effects on the dendritic pruning of Flamingo. Remarkably, we show that Patronin in epithelial cells facilitates dendritic pruning during development by maintaining the balance of antagonistic effects of Flamingo. Thus, our study elucidates an important and novel role of epidermal-derived mechanism as extrinsic cues in facilitating dendrite pruning.

Results

Patronin in epithelium is required for dendritic pruning of C4da sensory neurons

Epidermal cells are proposed to actively participate in engulfing degenerating dendrites, both during dendritic pruning and following injury [19]. They are therefore a genetic platform for the elucidation of the peripheral regulatory mechanisms of developmental neuronal remodeling. Our previous study demonstrated that Patronin regulates dendritic pruning in C4da neurons cell-autonomously [44]. However, whether Patronin also participates in dendritic pruning through a non-cell-autonomous mechanism in the epithelial cells surrounding C4da neurons remains unclear. To address this question, we used the pan-epithelial driver A58-Gal4 to specifically knocked down Patronin in epithelial cells with two independent RNAi lines, BL36659 (#1) and THU1753 (#2), and then examined the dendritic pruning phenotype. Epithelial-specific downregulation of Patronin resulted in pronounced pruning defects, as evidenced by a fully penetrant phenotype characterized by residual C4da dendrites at 16 h after pupal formation (16 h APF), whereas they normally cleared in wild-type (Fig. 1B-D, J, K). This result implies that epithelial-derived Patronin facilitates dendritic pruning during development. To further validate it, we next want to know whether the dendrite pruning phenotype in the Patronin downregulated epidermis could be ameliorated by restoring Patronin in epidermal cells. The results showed that overexpression of full-length RNAi-resistant Patronin in the background of Patronin knockdown could alleviate the dendritic pruning phenotype caused by it (Fig. 1F, J, K). In contrast, overexpression control could not (Fig. 1E, J, K). Since previous studies have shown that the CKK domain is the main structural region of Patronin that recognizes the minus terminus of MTs for its function, we subsequently found that induction of the Patronin CKK domain alone also ameliorated the pruning-deficient phenotype caused by the patronin RNAi (Fig. 1G, J, K), suggesting that the CKK domain is essential for the function of Patronin in the epidermis. Furthermore, we also constructed a transgenic fly with sgRNA targeting Patronin and attempted to knock out Patronin specifically in the epithelium, using the method as previously described [50, 51]. When Cas9 and Patronin sgRNA were driven simultaneously and specifically in the epithelium, it resulted in an obvious dendritic pruning deficit similar to that of RNAi (Fig. 1H-I, J, K). At the same time, we found that epidermal Patronin is required for proper dendritic severing, as both Patronin knockdown and knockout resulted in neurons retaining attached primary dendrites (Fig. 1C, D, I). R38F11-GAL4 was likewise shown to be an epidermal cell-specific driver [52, 53], and we utilized this GAL4 to drive the expression of patronin RNAi in epithelial cells to observe 16 h APF dendritic pruning, which showed similar dendritic pruning defects (Figure S1A-D), further validating the important role of epithelial Patronin in neural remodeling.

Noteworthily, previous reports have suggested that epithelial cells were in direct contact with the dendrites of C4da neurons and that approximately 22% of the dendrites were encapsulated by epithelial cells at the 3rd instar larval stage [47]. In addition, studies have revealed that epithelial cells play an important role in regulating space-filling and self-avoidance of dendrites in C4da neurons, which are growth-related processes [48, 54, 55]. We, therefore, ask whether the role of epithelial cell-derived Patronin in dendritic pruning could be attributed to secondary effects on dendritic growth. To rule out this possibility, we modulated the knockdown of Patronin in epithelial cells at the desired time using the temperature-sensitive (ts) Gal80 system. The results showed that after the wandering 3rd instar larval stage, a time point at which dendritic growth has taken shape, RNAi interference with Patronin expression similarly causes dendritic severing and fragmentation defects (Figure S2A-D). In summary, our systematic analysis of epithelial Patronin-mediated dendritic pruning illustrates an unknown non-cell-autonomous regulatory mechanism for developmental neuronal remodeling.

Flamingo in epithelium negatively governs dendritic pruning

Subsequently, we want to know what signals or molecules in epithelial cells are involved in Patronin-mediated dendritic pruning during development. Given that Patronin is essential for maintaining the polarity of MTs in dendrites [44, 56], and previous studies showed that the orientation of MTs in cells is critical for cell polarity [57, 58]. We, therefore, wonder whether Patronin in epithelial cells promotes developmental dendritic pruning by maintaining cell polarity. To this end, we knocked down core planar cell polarity (PCP) genes, including frizzled, dishevelled, flamingo, strabismus/Van Gogh, prickle, and diego [59, 60], and examined the dendritic pruning phenotypes (Figure S3A-F, Fig. 2A-C, and Fig. 3A-C). From these candidate genes, we isolated Flamingo, also known as Starry night (Stan), a conserved, seven-pass transmembrane adhesion GPCR. Flamingo in Drosophila is homologous to the mammalian Celsr protein, which functions as a member of the LNB-GPCR (long N-terminal B-type-G protein-coupled receptor) family, with both intercellular adhesion and intercellular signaling [61]. Members of the Flamingo family are PCP proteins and play a divergence of roles in neurodevelopmental processes. Ranging from the establishment of PCP [62, 63], axon pathfinding of photoreceptors [64, 65], preventing axonal and synaptic degeneration and promoting synapse formation in Drosophila [66], to the dendrite growth in Drosophila and mammals [67–69].

Downregulation of Flamingo by two independent RNAi lines, THU2126#1 and THU1679#2, in the epithelium, consistently caused precocious elimination of C4da dendritic branches at 10 h APF (Fig. 2B-C, J), whereas more dendritic branches in the wild-type were observed at this time point (Fig. 2A, J). Moreover, at this stage, the proportion of neurons that retained attached primary dendrites was lower upon Flamingo knockdown (Fig. 2K). This precocious severing and elimination imply a repressive role for the epidermal Flamingo in C4da dendrite pruning, whereas most previous studies have identified mechanisms that promote dendritic pruning in C4da neurons. To demonstrate that this phenotype is indeed due to Flamingo knockdown, we first examined the knockdown efficiency of RNAi in these two different lines, and the results showed that the Flamingo mRNA levels were significantly reduced compared to the control group (Fig. 2T). The efficiency of RNAi-mediated knockdown was further confirmed by immunostaining using an anti-Flamingo antibody (Fig. 2P-R’, S). Next, we also overexpressed Flamingo, containing mutations in the region targeted by shRNA silencing (RNAi-resistant), in the context of Flamingo knockdown to see if it could ameliorate the pruning phenotype, and the results showed that reintroduction of RNAi-resistant Flamingo substantially rescued the precocious pruning of C4da neurons at 10 h APF (Fig. 2D-E, J-K). To further confirm this phenotype, we subsequently established a transgenic fly with short guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting Flamingo. After driving Cas9 and Flamingo sgRNA expression specifically in epithelial cells, tissue-specific flamingo knockout was performed, causing a precocious dendritic pruning phenotype similar to that of RNAi (Fig. 2F-G, L-M). However, in flamingo mutant, we did not observe a phenotype of precocious dendritic pruning, and we speculate that this may be attributable to antagonistic effects brought about by Flamingo deletion in tissues other than epithelial cells, for instance, neurons (Fig. 2H-I, N-O). It should be noted that theoretically, we could achieve Flamingo knockout within a single epithelial cell to observe pruning of C4da neurons using the MARCM technique, but given the high probability that the interaction with C4da neurons is with a cluster of continuously arrayed epithelial cells in the surrounding area, it would be extremely difficult to accomplish this by random individual cell knockout. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Flamingo negatively regulates dendritic pruning in epithelial cells.

To further characterize the role of epithelial Flamingo in neurite pruning, we next examined the phenotype of dendritic pruning after Flamingo knockdown at 16 h APF, and as expected, dendrites were nearly cleared in both control and knockdown groups (Fig. 3A-C, G, H). However, overexpression of the full-length Flamingo without mutations at 16 h APF resulted in significant dendritic pruning defects. Notably, dendritic severing was also disrupted, as indicated by the persistence of attached primary dendrites in C4da neurons (Fig. 3D, G, H). Importantly, this result implies that epithelial cells may regulate dendritic pruning in C4da neurons through an intercellular mechanism, earlier than the execution of their phagocytic function. Besides, to confirm that this phenotype is due to excess Flamingo, we down-regulated the level of Flamingo in this context and found that the pruning-deficient phenotype was reverted at 16 h APF (Fig. 3E-F, G, H). Combined, these results shed light on an important and previously unknown function of the adhesion GPCR Flamingo in epithelium in controlling dendritic pruning.

Patronin facilitates dendritic pruning via attenuating Flamingo in the epithelium

Given that in epithelial cells, Patronin and Flamingo participate in regulating dendritic pruning through contrary mechanisms, we therefore hypothesize that Patronin suppresses the function of Flamingo in epithelial cells, promoting dendritic pruning during development. To determine whether Patronin and Flamingo in epidermal cells act in the same genetic pathway that underlies neuronal remodeling, we first double-knockdown Patronin and Flamingo via the co-expression of RNAi lines in epidermal cells and then compared the phenotype to Patronin single-RNAi. Our experimental result demonstrated that after attenuating Flamingo, the dendrite pruning phenotype was extensively ameliorated in the Patronin-ablated epidermis (Fig. 4A-B, D, F-G, J, K). To provide further validation of the genetic relationship between Patronin and Flamingo, we subsequently overexpressed Flamingo in the context of Patronin knockdown and found that simultaneous Flamingo overexpression and Patronin knockdown caused more severe pruning defects than knockdown of Patronin alone (Fig. 4B-C, E, H-I, J, K). Collectively, these data indicate that Patronin, which is derived from the epidermis, facilitates dendritic elimination during development by compromising the function of epidermal Flamingo.

We thus performed an immunostaining experiment with the antibody against Flamingo without permeabilization (without Triton) to label the membrane Flamingo. In the control group, Flamingo was distributed as a punctate pattern in the cell membrane, especially at the peripheral border of epithelial cells. Interestingly, Patronin deficiency led to a significant increase in membrane Flamingo localization (Fig. 4L-N). Also, we observed the signals of Flamingo co-localized with dendrites and showed that the punctate number representing Flamingo was also increased to some extent after knockdown of epithelial Patronin (Fig. 4L-N). Therefore, it suggests that Patronin is involved in maintaining membranous Flamingo homeostasis during development. Since Flamingo has been previously reported to act not only as a cadherin but also as an LNB-GPCR transmitting intercellular information, we thought that it was likely to serve as a transmembrane structure for the regulation of dendritic pruning. The altered distribution of Flamingo in the absence of epithelial Patronin may interfere with its intercellular transmission of signals. In addition, we also examined the epithelial morphology labelled by DLG, and the result showed that downregulation of Patronin in epithelial cells did not produce an obvious detectable change in epithelial size (Figure S4A-B). Previously, the chimera Nod-β-gal, also known as Khc::Nod::LacZ, was employed as an indicator to identify the regions enriched with the MT-minus ends in several Drosophila cell types [70–72]. Considering that Patronin is a MT-minus binding protein and that altered polarity of MTs in epithelial cells may impact the membrane localization of Flamingo, we next investigated the distribution of Khc::Nod::LacZ in epithelium, and our result showed that epithelial knockdown of Patronin relative to control resulted in local aggregations of MTs minus ends in epithelial cells, implying a possible change in the MT polarity (Fig. 4O-P). Taken together, our results confirm that epithelial Patronin governs the pruning of dendrite through Flamingo in the epithelium. Notably, considering that functional downregulations of the core PCP modulators in epithelium have no significant influences on the process of dendritic pruning (Figure S3A-F), we thus conjecture that the important role of epithelial Patronin in dendritic pruning may be independent of the PCP function of Flamingo. However, the potential relationship between Patronin and epithelial polarity pathways remains an important open question for future investigation.

Neuronal Flamingo promotes dendritic pruning in C4da sensory neurons

Subsequently, we want to know how Patronin in epithelial cells transcellularly facilitates dendritic remodeling in C4da sensory neurons during development. We first observed some known regulators of dendritic pruning, such as EcR-B1, Headcase, and Nrg, in the context of epithelial Patronin knockdown. In the meantime, we also examined Coracle, an adhesion molecule expressed in both dendrites and epidermis that mediates the wrapping of dendrites by the epidermis [47]. Nonetheless, the immunostaining results indicated that there were no significant differences in the expression or localization of EcR-B1, Headcase, Nrg, and Coracle in epidermal-Patronin knockdown compared to the control group (Figure S5A-L). These results imply that epithelial-derived Patronin may not transcellularly regulate dendritic pruning through these classical factors.

Previous studies in mammals have shown that the transmembrane cadherins Celsr2 and Celsr3 modulate neurite growth in an opposite way through homophilic interactions [73]. Interestingly, a report also suggested that an increase in epithelial Flamingo may aid dendrite growth of Drosophila class I dendrite arborization neurons through competitive binding with neuronal Flamingo [74]. These findings provide us with a fascinating clue, and we query whether Flamingo in the epithelium is likewise antagonistic to the neuronal Flamingo during dendritic pruning of C4da neurons. According to this hypothesis, Flamingo should have the opposite effect on dendrite pruning as it does in epithelial cells, facilitating the elimination of dendrite branches during development. To verify it, we next used a single copy of class IV da-specific driver pickpocket (PPK)-Gal4 to express two independent transgenic RNAi to specifically down-regulate Flamingo in C4da neurons, while UAS-Dicer (Dcr)2 was simultaneously driven to enhance knockdown efficiency, and UAS-mCD8::GFP was driven to label C4da neurons. In Control RNAi, almost all dendritic branches were cleared at 16 h APF (Fig. 5A, F, G), however, the specific downregulation of Flamingo via distinct RNAi lines in C4da neurons consistently led to significant dendritic pruning deficiency (Fig. 5B-C, F, G). To further characterize the function of neuronal Flamingo in dendritic pruning, we next elevated the level of Flamingo via overexpressing full-length Flamingo modified to be resistant to RNAi in Flamingo knockdown C4da neurons, which was shown to alleviate dendritic severing and fragmentation defects caused by Flamingo knockdown in comparison to the control (Fig. 5D-E, F, G). Therefore, Flamingo in neurons and epithelial cells acts oppositely in dendritic pruning. This finding is in line with our earlier conjecture, and thus neuronal Flamingo could conflict with negative signals for neural pruning that are normally transduced by epithelial Flamingo.

Epidermal Patronin governs dendritic pruning via neuronal Flamingo

To date, we have characterized the functional relationship between Patronin and Flamingo in epithelial cells and proposed that this epithelial mechanism may contribute to the regulation of dendrite pruning by antagonizing neuronal Flamingo. To further validate this potential transcellular mechanism, we next sought to determine whether epithelial Patronin synergizes with Flamingo in neurons to facilitate developmental neural pruning. To this end, we employed a dual-expression strategy combining the LexA/LexAop and GAL4/UAS systems, enabling cell-type-specific manipulation of gene expression in epithelial cells and neurons. We subsequently generated PPK-LexA, LexAop-Flamingo-RNAi, and LexAop-Flamingo Drosophila strains, respectively. Considering that RNAi knockdown mediated by the LexA/LexAop system may be less efficient than that mediated by the UAS-Gal4 system, we first assessed the efficacy of PPK-LexA-driven LexAop-Flamingo-RNAi. To this end, C4da dendrites were visualized using PPK-Gal4-driven UAS-mCD8::GFP, while Flamingo was simultaneously knocked down through PPK-LexA-driven LexAop-Flamingo-RNAi. At 16 h APF, control neurons had eliminated nearly all larval dendrites, whereas Flamingo knockdown via the LexA/LexAop system resulted in residual dendritic branches, indicating impaired dendrite pruning. Notably, this phenotype was detectable but less severe than that observed upon PPK-Gal4-mediated expression of UAS-Flamingo-RNAi (Figure S6A-D). Besides, knockdown of Patronin in epithelial cells using the UAS-Gal4 system resulted in relatively more severe dendritic pruning defects, although no significant differences (Fig. 6B-C, G, H). Intriguingly, upon simultaneous knockdown of Patronin and Flamingo in epithelial cells and C4da neurons, respectively, we found that relative to the single knockdown of Flamingo or Patronin, it resulted in significantly increased phenotypes (Fig. 6B-D, G, H). Additionally, overexpression of Flamingo in C4da neurons was able to rescue the dendritic pruning defect caused by knockdown of Patronin in epithelial cells (Fig. 6E-F, G, H). To further confirm the epithelial-to-neuronal mechanism, we subsequently examined whether perturbing Patronin or Flamingo in epithelial cells affects Flamingo levels in C4da neurons. Because Flamingo signals along dendrites may derive from epithelial membranes adjacent to dendrites, neuronal dendrites, or epithelial–neuronal homophilic interactions, we quantified Flamingo staining in the C4da soma as a more reliable readout of neuronal Flamingo levels. Compared with controls, epithelial Patronin knockdown and Flamingo overexpression consistently reduced Flamingo level in the C4da soma to some extent (Figure S7A-B). Collectively, these findings, together with the genetic interaction data, provide evidence for an epithelial–neuronal Flamingo-mediated regulatory mechanism during C4da dendrite pruning. We next wondered whether Patronin in neurons is involved in the process of epithelial Patronin-mediated dendritic pruning, and the results showed that there was no significant amelioration of the dendritic pruning phenotype caused by knockdown of Patronin in epithelial cells by overexpression of Patronin in neurons, as compared to the overexpression of UAS-Luciferase control in neurons (Figure S8A-D).

Previous work demonstrated that mechanical tearing driven by morphogenetic movements of epithelial tissue and C4da neurons contributes to dendrite severing during early pupal stages [75]. We therefore ask whether the pruning defects caused by epithelial Patronin knockdown or altered Flamingo expression were associated with abnormal epithelial–neuronal morphogenetic movements or disrupted epithelial wrapping of C4da dendrites. To this end, we examined epithelial and C4da neuronal morphogenetic dynamics under conditions of epithelial Patronin knockdown, epithelial Flamingo overexpression, and neuronal Flamingo knockdown. Compared with controls, all three manipulations attenuated epithelial–neuronal morphogenetic movements to some extent during pupal development (Figure S9A-D), suggesting that the epithelial to neuronal Patronin–Flamingo axis may contribute to the coordination of epithelial/neural morphogenetic dynamics during pruning. We further assessed the spatial relationship between epithelial cells and C4da dendrites [47] and found that C4da dendrites remained wrapped by epithelial cells under these conditions, without a significant disruption of epithelial–dendrite association (Figure S10A-C). These results suggest that the observed pruning defects may be partly associated with weakened epithelial–neuronal morphogenetic movements. During C4da dendrite pruning, MT severing within proximal dendrites precedes dendrite severing and represents an early event in the pruning process [76, 77]. We therefore examined whether the pruning defects caused by epithelial Patronin knockdown and altered Flamingo expression were associated with impaired MT severing or instead reflected later membrane breakdown defects. Compared with wild-type controls, epithelial Patronin knockdown, epithelial Flamingo overexpression, and neuronal Flamingo knockdown each slightly reduced the proportion of proximal dendrites showing MT severing events during the early pupal stage (Figure S11A-D). These results indicate that Patronin and Flamingo promote dendrite pruning, at least in part, by modulating early MT severing.

Overall, our results indicate that Patronin in epithelial cells regulates developmental dendritic pruning through Flamingo in C4da neurons, and that this process may be mediated, at least in part, by epithelial–neuronal morphogenetic movements and MT severing.

Neuronal Sgg acts as an underlying target of Flamingo during dendrite pruning

We are next interested in the downstream mechanisms by which Flamingo specifically governs dendritic pruning in C4da neurons. In mammals, the PCP signaling pathway in neurons is activated by Wnt signaling, and it has been shown that in the noncanonical Wnt signaling pathway, Wnt5a can exert its biological effects through the Frizzled8 (Fz8) and Flamingo co-receptor. Thus, Flamingo aids Fz8-mediated noncanonical Wnt signaling [78], which was also reported in C. elegans [79], suggesting that this pathway is conserved. Interestingly, Wnt5 was recognized to be possibly involved in the C4da dendrite pruning process [80]. Dishevelled (Dsh) is an important effector molecule in Wnt activation, and when Wnt binds to its membrane receptor, it activates the intracellular protein Dsh and inhibits the Drosophila glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) homolog Shaggy (Sgg) activity [81, 82]. Besides, it has been discovered that the morphogen molecule Wingless (Wg) receptor Frizzled (Fz) acts in conjunction with Flamingo and Dsh to transmit Wg signals into ddaE, a Drosophila class I peripheral sensory neuron, to control the directional growth of primary dendrites of ddaE neurons [83]. Noteworthily, a recent study showed that Sgg is necessary for cell-autonomous dendrite pruning of C4da neurons by maintaining MT stabilization in dendrites, overactivated Sgg disrupts dendritic MT orientation, while both deletion and overexpression of Sgg affect MT assembly and lead to pruning defects [84]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the absence of neuronal Flamingo may cause the overactivation of Sgg, which disrupts MT organization in C4da neurons, resulting in defective pruning. Significant dendritic branching residues and a severe severing-deficient phenotype were discovered for both overexpression of the Sgg full-length (SggFL) and the continuously activated form (SggS9A) in comparison to the control (Fig. 7A-C, D, E). Also, we knocked down Sgg expression in C4da neurons using sgg RNAi strains from different sources, slight dendritic pruning defects were observed (data not shown). Interestingly, the knockdown of Sgg in the context of Flamingo RNAi partially rescued the pruning-defective phenotype (Fig. 7F-G, H, I). Previous studies have shown that N-terminal serine phosphorylation (Ser9 in GSK3β/Ser21 in GSK3α) is inhibitory, whereas tyrosine phosphorylation (Tyr216 in GSK3β/Tyr279 in GSK3α) correlates with kinase activation [85]. To assess Sgg activity in C4da neurons, we employed a mammalian anti-phospho-GSK3 antibody directed against the activating phospho-tyrosine residues (p-Tyr216/p-Tyr279). Sequence alignment indicates that the antibody’s antigenic peptide is highly conserved in Drosophila, supporting its cross-reactivity with phosphorylated Sgg (p-Sgg). Immunostaining showed a significant increase in p-Sgg levels in Flamingo-deficient neurons relative to controls (Fig. 7J-K, L-M), consistent with the hypothesis that Flamingo antagonizes Sgg activity.

In summary, our results imply that Flamingo is involved in governing developmental neurite pruning partially through neuronal Sgg. To this point, we raise a potential mechanism as epithelium-derived Patronin facilitates developmental dendritic severing, a key dendritic pruning procedure that precedes engulfment of the epithelium, by suppressing Flamingo in the epithelium and transcellularly maintaining neuronal Flamingo and downstream Sgg signaling. Importantly, this finding will bring new perspectives to understanding the microenvironmental regulatory role of epithelial cells in dendritic remodeling, as well as provide new insights into the etiology of associated neurological diseases.

Discussion

In a similar manner to the apoptotic process, degenerating neuronal projections are cleared by phagocytic cells. In the Drosophila CNS, astrocytes act as phagocytes responsible for removing broken axons [21–24]. Whereas, in the PNS, during dendrite pruning and injury in Drosophila sensory neurons, epidermal cells rather than hemocytes are the primary phagocytes that remove degenerating dendrites [19]. Investigations of both glial and epithelial cell-mediated axonal and dendritic clearance during development highlight an important role for the engulfment receptor Draper (Drpr)-mediated recognition signal [19, 22, 23]. Based on current knowledge, phagocytes remove axons or dendrites that have already undergone severing or fragmentation, suggesting that phagocytic clearance represents a relatively late stage of neuronal pruning. However, the phagocyte-derived signals that promote neurite severing prior to phagocytic engulfment remain poorly understood. Interestingly, we find that downregulation of the MT minus-end-binding protein Patronin in epithelial cells results in defective dendritic severing of C4da neurons during development. Therefore, our findings identify Patronin as a previously unrecognized epithelial cell–intrinsic factor that promotes dendrite severing.

How does epithelial cell–derived Patronin govern dendritic pruning in a transcellular manner? Previous studies have demonstrated that Patronin, as an MT minus-end-binding protein, not only contributes to the maintenance of MT stability but is also essential for establishing MT polarity [86]. Specifically, within C4da neurons, Patronin preserves MT orientations in dendrites by counteracting the function of Klp10A, an MT depolymerizing protein. This ultimately promotes dendritic pruning during development [44]. MTs, as the main cytoskeleton in cells, are involved in the transport and localization of organelles and macromolecules, control cell division, and mechanically support cell shape [87, 88]. In addition to these fundamental characteristics, noncentrosomal MTs contribute to cell polarity through their own orientation, an event that is essential to the physiological function of the cells [57, 58]. Epithelial cells are generally characterized by two major forms of polarity: apical-basal polarity and planar polarity [89]. Does Patronin in epithelial cells modulate neural pruning by maintaining cell polarity? Studies of cell polarity proteins have shown that loss of Flamingo in epithelium, leads to precocious dendritic pruning. Further studies elucidated that Patronin antagonizes the function of Flamingo in epithelial cells, thereby promoting dendritic pruning. However, whether and how Patronin governs the polarity of epithelial cells needs to be further investigated. Since functional impairment of other epithelial core PCP-related genes does not result in obvious dendritic pruning defects (Figure S3A-F), epithelial Patronin may modulate neuronal remodeling through a non-canonical, PCP–independent function of Flamingo.

Although we illustrate that Patronin downregulation in epidermal cells causes aberrant Flamingo distribution, the mechanism by which Patronin regulates Flamingo localization remains unclear. Typically, membrane proteins are transported through a network of MTs. However, research has also shown that apical membrane proteins can be transported independently of MTs. After prolonged disruption of MTs, Golgi fragmentation and intracellular dispersion occurs [90]. Golgi scattering is caused by the collapse of the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), resulting in the appearance of small Golgi stacks at the exit sites of the endoplasmic reticulum, many of which are positioned near the cell surface [91, 92]. This spatial organization may facilitate MT-independent trafficking through the secretory pathway [93]. Thus, transient MT disruption may interfere with the membrane delivery or insertion of membrane proteins, whereas prolonged MT disruption may eventually allow membrane trafficking to proceed in an MT-independent manner, potentially because secretory compartments become interconnected and positioned close to the cell surface. Considering the antagonism of Patronin and Flamingo in epithelial cells, we therefore propose that the transport to the membrane of Flamingo might not be impaired after MT perturbation. In contrast, we suspect that the absence of Patronin may influence the endocytic degradation pathway, such as the inability to target proteins to the lysosome. The internalization of Flamingo has been reported to be partially dependent on Dynamin- and Clathrin-mediated endocytosis [94]. Flamingo, as a PCP protein, requires the removal of mislocalized or unstable components from the membrane to produce and maintain PCP patterns, and endocytosis, endosomal transport, and degradation are essential for this process. Both Rab GTPases Rab5 and Dynamin have been shown to play a role in the endocytosis of unstable PCP signaling components, and compromised endocytosis may lead to abnormal accumulation of Flamingo at the plasma membrane [95]. Notably, previous in vitro experiments showed that the knockdown of CAMSAP3 in epithelial cells impaired apical aggregation of Rab11-positive (also known as Rab11A) endosomes [96]. This may be due to the fact that loss of CAMSAP3 leads to the formation of more centrosome-centered MTs, which affects the organization of the actin cytoskeleton at the top of the cell, resulting in the mislocalization of Rab11A-positive circulating endosomes. Also, the interference of the retrograde dynein-dependent endosomal trafficking by intracellular MT destruction was reported in Drosophila adipose body cells, where it was discovered that the basement membrane proteins are abnormally accumulated at the plasma membrane after adipose body non-centrosomal MT organizing center (ncMTOC) disruption, with overgrowth of the plasma membrane [97]. Nevertheless, further studies are still needed to clarify the precise molecular mechanisms through which Patronin regulates Flamingo in epithelial cells.

How does epithelial Patronin transcellularly promote dendritic severing in C4da neurons? Studies in mammals have revealed that two seven-pass transmembrane cadherins, Celsr2 and Celsr3, perform opposing roles in neurite growth through their homophilic interactions [73]. In Drosophila, Flamingo was found to induce self-avoidance between dendritic terminals via homophilic binding of its cadherin domains at dendritic interfaces [74, 98]. Thus, Flamingo in epithelial cells may regulate the neural remodeling process by antagonizing the function of Flamingo in C4da neurons. It was observed that downregulation of Flamingo in C4da neurons resulted in defective dendritic pruning that is opposite to its function in the epithelium, and further genetic interactions showed that epithelial Patronin facilitates developmental neurite pruning via neuronal Flamingo. Besides, a previous report showed that Flamingo binds homophilically to heterologous or homologous cells to transmit information. This process relies on the N-terminal portion of Flamingo for recognition and the C-terminal portion for signal transduction. In the mechanism of Flamingo suppression of dendritic growth in neurons, neuronal expression of Flamingo variants lacking either the N-terminal or C-terminal domain failed to affect dendritic growth, suggesting that both extracellular activation and intracellular signaling are required for Flamingo to suppress dendritic growth [74]. Nevertheless, we are currently unable to ascertain whether both the intracellular and extracellular portions of Flamingo are important for dendritic pruning, and we can further investigate this by constructing Flamingo with the deletion of the N- or C-terminal parts.

Furthermore, our previous results found that the dendritic pruning phenotype of Flamingo could only be partially rescued by Sgg in C4da neuron, suggesting that there may be other unknown dendritic pruning regulatory mechanisms downstream of Flamingo. Also, it was shown that overactivation of Sgg inhibits dendritic pruning by interfering with MT polarity in dendrites [84], so whether Flamingo maintains MT polarity through Sgg remains unclear, and these concerns will need to be further addressed in the future. In fact, we have also made efforts to further explore other potential downstream mechanisms. Gq, another heterotrimeric G protein implicated in the modulation of dendritic growth, has been reported to couple with Fz in Drosophila [99] and to function downstream Flamingo in controlling dendritic growth [83]. However, our experimental results suggest that Gq may not be involved in dendritic pruning (Figure S12 A-C). By contrast, neuronal loss of Furrowed (Fw) or the transmembrane receptor Golden Goal (Gogo) resulted in phenotypes resembling those caused by Flamingo downregulation (Figure S12 A-C). Both Fw and Gogo have been reported to interact with Flamingo, for example during photoreceptor axon targeting. More recently, Gogo was identified as a molecular chaperone for Flamingo and shown to physically associate with Flamingo [100]. Therefore, it is likely that they work together with Flamingo to regulate dendritic pruning, but further investigation is needed. Moreover, it has been discovered that Flamingo modulates actin filaments during dendritic development and that the tropomyosin II gene (TmII) genetically interacts with Flamingo to control the size of the dendritic field by actin cytoskeleton [101]. Besides, the Flamingo homolog FLAMINGO-1 has been shown to regulate dendritic self-avoidance through F-actin assembly in C. elegans [35], which implies an underlying function of Flamingo in the cytoskeleton. On the other hand, Fz-Flamingo, as a receptor for Wnt5 in neurons, is also closely related to the JNK signaling pathway, and Fz can activate JNK to govern asymmetric cytoskeletal organization [102]. Noteworthily, the JNK signaling has been reported to modulate dendritic pruning [80], thus it should be further verified whether the Fz-Flamingo complex is involved in dendrite remodeling by regulating the cytoskeleton through the JNK signaling pathway.

In summary, we illustrate that epithelial cell-derived Patronin maintains neural Flamingo function during development by antagonizing epidermal Flamingo and promotes dendritic severing partially through Sgg. It is implied that epithelial cells may modulate dendritic severing through an intercellular mechanism before executing their phagocytic function. However, whether Patronin and Flamingo also participate in the epithelial phagocytosis of dendritic fragments during the relatively late stage of dendritic pruning remains unclear. In future studies, the roles of Patronin and Flamingo in regulating epithelial cell-mediated phagocytosis during the fragmentation phase should be further examined. For example, it would be informative to assess whether loss of Patronin or Flamingo affects the expression or localization of the engulfment receptor Draper in epithelial cells, the exposure of the “eat-me” signal phosphatidylserine (PS) in degenerating neurons, or the fluorescence changes of dendrites labeled with membrane-associated pH sensors after endocytosis. These analyses would help further clarify the specific functions of epithelial Patronin and Flamingo in dendrite pruning. Remarkably, in addition to epithelial cells, a recent study has revealed that peripheral glial cells also play an essential role in C4da dendritic pruning [25]. Thus, elucidating the contribution of peripheral glial cells to the local microenvironment during dendritic pruning represents an important direction for future investigation. Importantly, by gaining a comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the mechanisms by which the neural microenvironment regulates neurite remodeling, we can provide new scientific insights into the pathogenesis of related neurodevelopmental disorders.

Generation of transgenes

For the rescue experiment, the full-length RNAi-resistant of UAS-Patronin-HA, UAS- Flamingo, and UAS-Patronin-CKK constructs were made by cloning the full-length with modified to be resistant to RNAi and truncated cDNA into the transformation vector pUAST-attB. Transgenic fly lines were generated by standard germline transformation. cDNAs of Patronin and Flamingo were obtained from reverse transcription of genomic mRNA. Fly head mRNA was extracted using TRIzol (Life Technologies), and reverse transcribed using cDNA Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Applied Biosystems). The Patronin-CKK was generated by PCR using Patronin CDS as a template. Besides, the genome of Drosophila UAS-Flamingo-RNAi #2 (THU1679) was extracted and utilized as a template for dsx-shRNA amplification. The LexAop-Flamingo-RNAi, LexAop-Flamingo, and LexAop-tub-GFP plasmids were constructed by digesting the vector plasmid LexAop-dsxM-RNAi kept in our laboratory and replacing dsxM-RNAi with the amplified flamingo-shRNA, Flamingo-CDS, and tub-GFP-CDS. A double sgRNA construct directed against Flamingo, sgRNAs GAGTTCCCTCAAAGGCCCCTGGG (target 1) and TTCGAAGCGAGTATCCGTGAAGG (target 2), were cloned into pCFD4w+. A double sgRNA construct directed against Patronin, sgRNAs CCGCAGATCATCGTGGAGCTGGG (target 1) and TATGCGAAGGAAATATCATCGGG (target 2) were cloned into pCFD4w+.The transgenic lines were generated by the UniHuaii Inc.

Live imaging analysis

Drosophila at the 3rd instar larval, white prepupal (WP) stages were first washed in PBS buffer briefly for three times and subsequently immersed with 90% glycerol. Pupal cases at 10 h and 16 h APF were carefully removed before mounted with 90% glycerol. Images of C4da neurons were captured with a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry and antibodies

For immunostaining, white prepupae were dissected in pre-cooling PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min. The experimental and control samples were incubated simultaneously in the same tubes. Mounting was performed in the mounting medium of VectaShield, and the samples were directly visualized by a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. The following primary antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry at the denoted dilutions: mouse anti-Nrg (1:50, BP104, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), mouse anti-EcR-B1 (1:20, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), mouse anti-Headcase (1:20, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), mouse anti-Coracle (1:50, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), mouse anti-DLG (1:50, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), rabbit anti-HRP (1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA), mouse anti-Flamingo (1:10, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA), and rabbit anti-p-Sgg (1:100, Abcam, ab75745). The following secondary antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry at the denoted dilution: Alexa Fluor 488- and Alexa Fluor 555- conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA).

Quantification of dendrites

The dendritic morphologies of the C4da neurons at the 3rd instar larvae, WP, 10 h APF, and 16 h APF stages were captured through live confocal imaging. The length of unpruned dendrites and the percentage of pruning defects are utilized to evaluate the neural pruning phenotype. The length of unpruned dendrites was measured in a 275 μm × 275 μm region derived from the dorsal dendritic field of C4da neurons, ranging from the abdominal segments 2–4 [103]. The severing defect is defined by the primary dendritic branches that are still attached to the soma at 16 h APF. Defects in fragmentation refer to those neurons in which more than 200 μm of total dendritic fragment length remains at the 16 h APF stage, whereas wild-type dendrites have almost no dendritic residues at this stage. Both types of defects are collectively referred to as pruning defects.

Statistics

For pairwise comparison, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to determine statistical significance. For multiple-group comparison, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s test was used to determine significance. Error bars in all graphs represent s.e.m. Statistical significance was defined as ns, not significant, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. The number of neurons (n) in each group is shown on the bars.
