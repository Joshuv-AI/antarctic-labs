Gamma‐glutamyltransferase and immunoglobulin G in intestinal maturation: Pilot study in preterm and term infants and the influence of breast milk
Authors: Johanna Jakobs, Teresa Riedl‐Seifert, Franziska Jakobs, Markus Waitz, Andreas Jenke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGamma‐glutamyltransferase (γGT), a key enzyme in glutathione metabolism, is abundant in colostrum and may indicate neonatal gastrointestinal maturation, although normative serum values and associations with breast milk intake remain unclear, particularly in preterm infants. In this prospective study, serum γGT was measured in 84 neonates, including 36 born before 32 weeks' gestation and 48 born at or after 32 weeks, across the first 4 postnatal weeks. Breast milk γGT was analyzed in samples from 107 mothers, and correlations with colostrum intake were assessed in 66 neonates. Serum γGT remained relatively stable (110–130 U/L). Preterm infants receiving colostrum had higher γGT in Week 1 than non‐exposed infants, although not conventionally significant, and lower values by Week 4 (138.7 vs. 91.7 U/L, p = 0.056; Week 4, p = 0.027). Week‐1 breast milk intake correlated with serum γGT across all 4 weeks in preterm infants (p = 0.007, 0.001, 0.003, 0.022).Keywords: cholestasis, gut, nutrition

INTRODUCTION

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract develops from early fetal life and continues maturing after enteral nutrition begins. 1 In preterm infants, GI immaturity involves reduced intestinal length, impaired motility, limited enzymatic capacity, altered microbiota, and an underdeveloped epithelial barrier. 2 , 3 Transplacental transfer of macromolecules, including maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG), starts around gestational Week 13 and increases during the third trimester. 1 , 4 In preterm neonates, delayed intestinal “closure” prolongs macromolecular permeability, resulting in higher serum levels of dietary proteins. 5 , 6

Breast milk supports postnatal GI development. Colostrum, rich in growth factors and immunomodulatory components, is particularly important for intestinal maturation. 7 , 8 Its immunoglobulins can be absorbed by the neonatal gut and are associated with reduced infection rates. 9 , 10

γ‐Glutamyltransferase (γGT), another colostral enzyme, breaks down glutathione to release cysteine. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In animal models, γGT indicates passive immunity acquisition and gut function. 15 , 16 Its role in human neonates remains unclear. Although serum γGT may be higher in preterm than term infants, normative data for early gestational ages (GAs) are limited. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Moreover, neonatal serum γGT is influenced by endogenous production, and the relative contribution of milk‐borne versus endogenous γGT remains unknown. Thus, associations between milk exposure and infant serum γGT require cautious interpretation and cannot be assumed to reflect direct maternal γGT absorption or gut integrity.

This pilot exploratory study aimed to describe the observed distribution of serum and breast‐milk γGT in preterm and term neonates and to explore whether early human‐milk intake is associated with serum γGT concentrations. The findings are intended to generate hypotheses regarding feeding‐related postnatal adaptation, particularly in the context of prematurity, and may contribute to improved interpretation of neonatal serum γGT values in clinical care. They do not establish reference intervals or prove a causal relationship between maternal milk γGT, infant serum γGT, and GI function.

METHODS

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was granted by the Landesärztekammer Hessen Ethics Committee (Vorgangsnummer: 2020‐1807‐evBO).

Study design

This prospective clinical pilot study was conducted in the Department of Neonatology at Klinikum Kassel between July 2021 and February 2022. All admitted neonates were eligible for inclusion with parental written consent. Those receiving palliative care or with known genetic, GI, or hepatic disorders were excluded.

No a priori sample‐size calculation was performed; this exploratory cohort (n = 84 serum; 66 paired serum–milk; 36 < 32 weeks) provides ~80% power to detect moderate correlations (r ≈ 0.4–0.45) within the preterm subgroup at α = 0.05, but is underpowered for smaller effects and definitive reference‐interval estimation.

Data collection

Data were extracted from neonatal and maternal medical charts, including GA, birth weight, sex, age, fluid balance, weight gain, and specifically looking into the route of feeding, type, and volume of milk received.

Blood samples were collected as clinically indicated and additionally analyzed for γGT concentrations, with no extra phlebotomy. Weekly measurements were intended for the first 4 weeks. Breast milk samples (1–3 mL) were obtained from mothers before feeding. Both fresh and frozen, pasteurized and non‐pasteurized breast milk was collected for the study.

Feeding volumes were abstracted from patient charts, expressed as mL/kg. “Colostrum exposure” was defined a priori as receipt of human milk during postnatal Days 1–3, reflecting the earliest feeding period in which colostrum predominates and breast‐milk γGT activity is highest. Because this dichotomization simplifies a biologically continuous exposure, it was used only for exploratory subgroup comparisons; the principal intake analyses were conducted using human‐milk volume as a continuous variable (mL/kg). Weekly human‐milk intake refers to cumulative intake during Weeks 1–4.

Serum and breast milk γGT concentrations were measured using the Alinity Gamma‐Glutamyl Transferase 2 Assay by Abbott Laboratories, Chigago, Illinois. While this assay is approved for serum and cerebrospinal fluid, it was adapted and validated for use with breast milk in our laboratory following established protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted as week‐specific exploratory comparisons rather than as a formal repeated‐measures longitudinal model. This approach was chosen because sample availability was incomplete and unbalanced across time points, with many infants not contributing measurements at each week.

Data management was conducted using Microsoft Access (v2311) and Excel (v16.78). Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (v29.0.0.0), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1).

RESULTS

The serum analysis cohort comprised 84 neonates (49% male, 51% female), 43% born <32 weeks and 57% ≥32 weeks of gestation. The mean GA was 32.6 weeks. Birth weights ranged from 485 to 3870 g (mean: 1887 g) (Table S1). Serum sample availability was highest in Week 1 (n = 79) and lowest in Week 4 (n = 28). Gestational‐age stratification at 32 weeks was chosen a priori for clinical reasons, as infants born <32 weeks represent a more immature subgroup with greater GI vulnerability. These subgroup analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Observed serum γGT distribution in more mature and very preterm infants

Serum γGT concentrations in neonates remained relatively stable during the first 4 weeks of life (110–130 U/L). Descriptive intervals were established using the 25th and 75th percentiles, as summarized in Table S2. In Week 1, γGT IQR levels ranged from 66.0 to 142.5 U/L; in Week 2, from 60.0 to 148.0 U/L; in Week 3, from 59.0 to 174.0 U/L; and in Week 4, from 45.75 to 184.4 U/L.

To assess the influence of GA, the cohort was stratified into two groups: infants born <32 and ≥32 weeks GA. In infants <32 weeks GA, the mean γGT values were 110.2 U/L (Week 1), 116.4 U/L (Week 2), 108.6 U/L (Week 3), and 124.8 U/L (Week 4). Infants born ≥32 weeks exhibited mean γGT concentrations of 144.0, 102.9, 160.6, and 128.2 U/L, respectively. Statistical comparison revealed a significant difference in γGT levels between the two groups in Week 3 only (p = 0.045).

γGT concentrations in colostrum and breast milk and their relationship with infant serum levels

Colostrum exhibited markedly elevated γGT concentrations, which declined over the subsequent weeks of lactation. During Week 1, γGT levels in colostrum ranged from 13.0 to 60,115.0 U/L, with a mean of 13,970.0 U/L and a standard deviation of 15,275.1 U/L. These values decreased to 4141.4 U/L in Week 2 and 2612.6 U/L in Week 3, followed by a slight increase to 3916.8 U/L in Week 4.

A sub‐analysis focused on 66 neonates for whom both serum and corresponding breast milk samples were available. Among these, 31 infants (47%) were born before 32 weeks GA, and 35 infants (53%) were born at or beyond 32 weeks GA. For exploratory descriptive comparisons, colostrum intake was also recorded as a binary variable, defined as receiving >10 mL/kg/day within the first 3 days of life. This threshold was not intended to represent a biological cutoff; because smaller volumes may also be biologically relevant, the continuous intake analyses are considered more informative and are emphasized in the revised manuscript.

As illustrated in Figure 1, infants who received colostrum displayed higher mean γGT serum concentrations in Week 1 (163.1 U/L), with a subsequent decline over the following weeks. Conversely, infants who did not receive colostrum showed lower γGT levels initially (102.9 U/L), followed by a progressive increase, peaking at 156.0 U/L in Week 4. T‐tests revealed statistically significant differences between the groups in Week 1 (p = 0.007), whereas the difference in Week 4 was not statistically significant (p = 0.059).

Differential γGT response to colostrum by GA

Given the potential role of intestinal maturation in γGT metabolism, the relationship between colostrum intake and serum γGT concentration was further analyzed by GA group, as depicted in Figure 2. Because infants born before 32 weeks generally represent a more immature subgroup with greater GI vulnerability and delayed postnatal adaptation, this stratified analysis was performed on biological and clinical grounds; however, it reduces statistical power and should be interpreted cautiously.

Among infants <32 weeks GA, those who received colostrum exhibited the highest γGT levels in Week 1 (138.7 U/L), followed by a gradual decline (132.2; 98.8; 82.5 U/L). In contrast, preterm infants without colostrum exposure had lower initial γGT concentrations (91.7 U/L), which increased and peaked at 167.1 U/L in Week 4. T‐test comparisons showed a numerical difference in Week 1 (p = 0.056) and a statistically significant difference in Week 1 (p = 0.027).

In infants born at or beyond 32 weeks GA, no statistically significant association was observed between colostrum intake and serum γGT concentrations. Given the exploratory subgroup design and limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive evidence of gestational‐age‐dependent modulation of γGT dynamics.

Linear association between γGT serum levels and breast milk intake in preterm infants

Pearson correlation analysis examined associations between breast milk intake, expressed as mL/kg body weight, and serum γGT levels. Week‐1 breast milk intake correlated positively with serum γGT in Weeks 1 (p = 0.028) and 2 (p = 0.023). After stratification by GA, this association was strongest in preterm infants <32 weeks, in whom week‐1 intake correlated with serum γGT across all 4 weeks (p = 0.007, 0.001, 0.003, and 0.022, respectively). No significant correlation was observed between serum γGT and breast‐milk γGT concentration. For infants born at or beyond 32 weeks GA, no relevant significant correlation was observed. Because repeated measurements were analyzed primarily as week‐specific exploratory comparisons rather than as a formal longitudinal model, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating.

DISCUSSION

γGT is widely used in veterinary neonatology as a marker of passive immunity, though its relevance in human neonates remains unclear. This pilot exploratory study examined postnatal serum γGT patterns in hospitalized neonates and explored their association with early human‐milk exposure, particularly in preterm infants. The clinical relevance of this work lies primarily in improving interpretation of serum γGT values in preterm infants and in generating hypotheses about the relationship between early human‐milk exposure and postnatal GI adaptation, rather than in establishing serum γGT as a validated clinical biomarker at present.

Serum γGT values showed wide interindividual variability over the first four postnatal weeks and should be interpreted as descriptive intervals for hospitalized but stable neonates, as infants with significant GI, hepatic, or congenital disease were excluded.

In Week 1, mean γGT was higher in infants born ≥32 weeks than in those <32 weeks, although not significantly. Associations with GA and birth weight in Weeks 3 and 4 suggest developmental regulation. Differences from previous studies may reflect varying gestational‐age thresholds and sampling times, particularly given the approximately 3‐day γGT half‐life. 20 , 21

Breast‐milk γGT peaked in colostrum and declined thereafter, consistent with earlier reports. 12 , 13 , 14 Pasteurization effects could not be assessed adequately because of sample‐size limitations. In preterm infants, early human‐milk volume was moderately associated with serum γGT across weeks, possibly reflecting either colostral absorption or colostrum‐driven endogenous γGT expression.

The absence of a significant correlation between breast‐milk and infant serum γGT does not support a direct‐transfer mechanism. Instead, the association between early human‐milk volume and serum γGT patterns in preterm infants may reflect feeding‐related maturation, including intestinal adaptation and/or regulation of endogenous γGT expression. Serum γGT should therefore be interpreted only as an exploratory correlate of early postnatal adaptation, not as a validated marker of gut integrity or passive transfer.

Limitations include the lack of standardized methods for measuring γGT in breast milk, despite use of a validated protocol adapted from CSF analysis, and possible measurement variability. Smaller samples in Weeks 2 and 4, partly due to early discharge of more mature infants, limited longitudinal assessment. Analyses were primarily week‐specific and exploratory rather than formal repeated‐measures models, meaning within‐infant correlation was not fully addressed. Finally, the design could not distinguish exogenous uptake from endogenous γGT production.

CONCLUSION

This pilot exploratory study describes serum γGT patterns during the first 4 postnatal weeks in hospitalized neonates and suggests that early human‐milk intake may be associated with later serum γGT levels in very preterm infants. Given endogenous γGT production and the non‐causal design, findings require cautious interpretation. Larger longitudinal studies should assess whether serum γGT reflects feeding‐related GI adaptation.
