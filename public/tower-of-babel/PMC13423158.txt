Anakinra for intravenous immunoglobulin-resistant Kawasaki disease: a systematic literature review
Authors: Caterina Matucci-Cerinic, Alessia Alunno, Jan W Schoones, Marco Gattorno, Isabelle Koné-Paut, Perrine Dusser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApproximately 10%–20% of patients with Kawasaki disease (KD) are resistant to intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) and are at increased risk of developing coronary artery aneurysms (CAA). A potential therapeutic role of anakinra (ANK) in refractory KD has been suggested. The aim of this work is to systematically review and critically appraise the available clinical evidence on the use of ANK in the treatment of IVIG-resistant KD, focusing on treatment indications, timing, dosage, efficacy on fever, inflammation, CAA and safety.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare, Academic Search Premier and Google Scholar from inception to 9 October 2025, in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. Eligible studies included all study designs reporting outcomes of patients with KD treated with ANK.ResultsThirty-two publications (31 identified through database search and one by cross-referencing) describing 81 patients were included. Most reports were single cases or small series, with two early-phase clinical trials. Treatment with ANK was associated with resolution of fever and systemic inflammation in 94.9% of patients. Complete CAA resolution was reported in 32% and stabilisation/dimensional reduction in 56.3%. No serious safety concerns were identified. However, these findings derive from heterogeneous and low-quality evidence and should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionsIn IVIG-resistant KD, ANK was effective in controlling systemic inflammation, although more data are needed to assess its efficacy on coronary outcomes. While these findings are encouraging in supporting consideration of ANK for refractory KD, larger randomised clinical studies are warranted to define optimal ANK timing of introduction and dosing, and to evaluate its long-term efficacy on cardiac outcomes.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420252129435.

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute, self-limited, febrile vasculitis of medium-sized vessels, affecting predominantly infants and children under 5 years of age.1 The disease typically presents with high fever associated with mucocutaneous manifestations and cervical lymphadenopathies and, if not promptly treated, it may affect the coronary arteries. The diagnosis is clinical and is based on the 2024 American Heart Association proposed criteria.1 Until now, intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) are the only evidence-based treatment able to prevent the development of coronary artery aneurysms (CAA), reducing their risk of development from 25%–30% to 5%–7%.2

Patients who present a fever recrudescence or persistence within the first 36 hours of IVIG treatment are classified as ‘IVIG-resistant’ and are at increased risk of developing CAA.2

The treatment of IVIG-resistant patients is not codified. The recommendations suggest a second IVIG infusion, alone or in combination with steroids and propose several anti-inflammatory therapies including tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors (infliximab (IFX) and etanercept), interleukin (IL)-1 inhibition (anakinra (ANK)) and cyclosporin.1

ANK is a homologue of human IL-1 receptor antagonist, which acts as a competitive inhibitor of IL-1α and IL-1β, blocking signal transduction of IL-1 type I receptor. The first evidences of a role of IL-1 in KD were provided in the 80s,3 and since then, evidence from laboratory research and experimental KD mice models have highlighted the central role of IL-1 as a key mediator of vascular inflammation and endothelial activation in the pathogenesis of acute KD and in the development of CAA and myocarditis.

Consistent with this biological rationale, an increasing number of patients with refractory KD treated with ANK have been reported, and an open-label phase II trial suggested its potential efficacy in IVIG-resistant patients.4

The aim of this systematic literature review is to summarise and critically appraise the published clinical evidence on the use of ANK in KD, highlighting treatment indications, timing, dosage, safety and outcomes related to fever resolution, systemic inflammation and coronary artery involvement.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic literature search was conducted on PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare, Academic Search Premier and Google Scholar from inception to 9 October 2025 according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO on 17 December 2025 (ID: CRD420252129435). The search strategy was developed by an experienced librarian (JWS) based on the Population/Intervention/Comparator/Outcome framework (online supplemental table S1).

Title and abstract screening were conducted in duplicate independently by two authors (CM-C and PD), with any disagreements resolved by discussion. No language restriction was applied at the initial screening stage; however, for feasibility, only articles in English, French and Spanish were retained for full-text review. Reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Original research articles of any design (randomised controlled clinical trials, non-randomised interventional studies, prospective and retrospective case series, single case reports) enrolling: (P) patients with KD, (I) receiving ANK, (C) compared with other treatments or without a comparator; (O) describing any outcome related to the treatment, were included. Studies not providing sufficient information on patient characteristics, treatment regimen or outcomes were excluded. Moreover, studies describing patients with multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children or with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection were excluded. Review articles and meta-analyses were also excluded, but their reference lists were screened for additional eligible studies (referred to as ‘cross-referencing’ in the search flow chart).

Data analysis

Two reviewers (CM-C and PD) independently extracted data from each included study using a standardised form. Collected variables included patient demographics, timing of ANK initiation, dosage and administration details, prior and subsequent treatments, presence and evolution of CAA, treatment response (fever, inflammation) and adverse events.

Data were synthesised descriptively following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis reporting guidelines.5 Continuous variables were summarised using medians and ranges or IQRs, given the expected non-normal distribution and the high heterogeneity of the data derived from case reports, case series and early-phase clinical trials. Categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. A formal meta-analysis could not be conducted due to the variability in study design, patient characteristics and reported outcomes. Aggregated outcome measures are therefore presented as descriptive summaries of the collected cases and do not represent results of a meta-analysis or quantitative estimates of treatment effect.

Risk of bias and data quality

For non-randomised clinical trials, the risk of bias was assessed with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Intervention (ROBINS-I) across seven domains.6 Each domain was rated using direct quotes and data from the trial report. For case reports and case series, methodological quality was appraised descriptively based on completeness of case description, clarity of outcome reporting and temporal relationship between intervention and outcomes. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) framework,7 which was applied narratively given the predominance of case reports and small case series.

Results

The literature search yielded a total of 629 deduplicated references, 59 of which were eligible for full-text assessment. Fifty-three articles describing 135 patients with KD treated with ANK were initially identified: 21 were excluded because of insufficient data (online supplemental table S2).

The final analysis included 32 publications (31 identified through database search and one via cross-referencing) describing 81 unique patients treated with ANK. Most studies were single case reports (n=25) or small case series (n=5), with two multicentre non-randomised clinical trials.4 8 Publications spanned from 2012 to 2025. Clinical trials

Only two clinical trials on ANK and KD have been completed up to now.

The KAWAKINRA trial was a phase IIA, open-label, 45-day, proof-of-concept, multicentre study with a single treatment arm to assess the efficacy and safety of ANK in IVIG-resistant KD.4 The patients were included within the first 14 days of fever, with a starting dose of 2–4 mg/kg/day depending on patients’ weight. In case of fever persistence, the dose could be escalated every day up to 8 mg/kg/day. ANK was administered for 14 days, and patients were re-evaluated up to the 45th day after ANK initiation. The study included 16 patients, of whom one was excluded because they were assigned a different diagnosis.

The other clinical trial was a phase I/IIa dose-escalation study of ANK in patients with KD and CAA reported by Yang et al that included 22 patients.8 The goal of the trial was to study the safety and tolerability and to generate pharmacokinetic data for ANK. Using a dose-escalation protocol, a minimum of three subjects were enrolled at three dose levels: 2–4 mg/kg, 5–7 mg/kg and 8–11 mg/kg, with a maximum dose of 100 mg/day and a variable treatment length of 2–6 weeks. All the study patients received IFX prior to ANK.

Clinical status prior to anakinra initiation

The median age at disease onset was 12 months (range, 2 months–16 years). Among patients with reported sex, 61 of 79 (77.2%) were male. All patients had failed first-line therapy with aspirin and at least one course of IVIG. A second IVIG dose was administered in 64 patients (81.0%) and a third dose in four patients (5.1%) (in two cases, doses were not specified).9 10 Steroids were used in 40 patients (49.3%), with different regimens ranging from intravenous pulses to oral steroids, with a variable oral tapering. Of the 41 patients not treated with steroids, 20 were part of a phase I/IIa trial8 and 12 were part of the KAWAKINRA trial.4

Prior to ANK initiation, IFX was administered in 29 patients (35.8%)8 11–16 (5 mg/kg as a single infusion, or 10 mg/kg in two cases14 16; in one patient, the dose was not specified12), while two patients received cyclosporin and one received methotrexate17 (figure 2A).

In 67 patients (82.7%), CAA were present before ANK introduction. The median timing of CAA appearance was 12 days after disease onset (range, 3–75 days; IQR 7, 16; n=29; timing not specified in 42 patients). Specifically, 13 patients presented with small-size CAA (Z-score 2.5–4.9), 14 with medium-size CAA (Z-score 5–9.9) and 15 with giant aneurysms (Z-score >10) (in the 22 patients reported by Yang et al and three single patients, Z-score was not reported).8 18–20 Among the 14 patients who did not present CAA, seven had mild coronary dilations (Z-score 2–2.5).

Anakinra timing, dosage and rationale

ANK was introduced after a median of 15 days from disease onset (range minimum–maximum 7–87 days) (9.5 days (range 5–12) in the KAWAKINRA trial4 and 9 days (range 4–22) in the trial by Yang et al8). In seven cases, timing was not specified: in two cases, ANK was administered after the first month of illness,16 21 while in another case, ANK was introduced on day 18 for 7 days and then reintroduced at day 54 due to disease recrudescence and CAA appearance.22

The median starting dose was 4 mg/kg/day (range 1–10 mg/kg/day), with different dose-escalation protocols used in the two clinical trials and several case reports (in two cases, the dose was not specified).10 23 ANK was administered subcutaneously in most patients (n=70), with an initial intravenous course in 11 patients.8 24 25 The median duration of therapy was 4 weeks (range 5 days–24 weeks) (not specified in six patients and still ongoing in one patient).

In the 37 patients included in the clinical trials, ANK was introduced as per the study design. In the remaining patients, the reasons for ANK introduction were fever recrudescence, persistence of clinical symptoms, persistence of systemic inflammation or the appearance/worsening of CAA (see online supplemental table S3 for details of each case), with a combination of these factors present in the majority of patients. In the 10 cases that never developed CAA, ANK was introduced because of persistent fever or inflammation, Kawasaki shock syndrome or macrophage activation syndrome,24–27 and in five cases, as per the inclusion criteria of the KAWAKINRA trial.4

Efficacy on fever/inflammation

In 75 patients (94.9%), ANK was efficacious in resolving fever and systemic inflammation (n=79; one patient was already afebrile when ANK was introduced9 and one patient died due to rupture of a giant CAA17). One patient showed partial improvement and responded after the addition of etanercept.28 Three patients experienced fever recrudescence, requiring a switch to either cyclophosphamide8 or IFX.29

Coronary artery outcomes

Among the published reports, complete CAA resolution was described in 25 patients, while coronary stabilisation or reduction was described in 40 patients. Conversely, worsening of CAA was reported in five patients, while one death occurred due to aneurysm rupture. These overall outcomes are summarised in figure 2B, while specific coronary Z-score data for each patient are provided in online supplemental table S4 and figure 2C. These descriptive observations should not be interpreted as estimates of treatment efficacy because of the heterogeneous study designs, variable follow-up and substantial risk of publication bias. Of the 14 patients who received ANK before CAA development, only 4 subsequently developed aneurysms, 3 of whom later achieved complete resolution. A transient worsening of CAA immediately after ANK introduction was described in 13 cases (median time of worsening, 5 days (range 4–29 days)), with complete resolution in 2 cases,17 19 dimensional reduction in 5 cases17 28 30 31 and progressive dimensional worsening in 6 cases.8 17 17

No significant differences in CAA outcomes were observed according to prior steroid exposure (p=0.32, n=24 steroids vs six non-steroids, patients with previous exposure to other drugs were excluded) or between patients with early (<14 days) and late (>14 days) ANK initiation (p=0.9). Moreover, no statistically significant differences were found according to sex (p=0.43), age at onset (below vs over 1 year of age, p=0.2) or the presence of giant aneurysms at baseline (p=0.08). A comparison between the patients treated with/without IFX prior to ANK was not feasible, for the lack of specific CAA outcomes in IFX-treated patients.

The time of the last follow-up was generally short and highly heterogeneous, ranging from 6 days to 37 months (median 2 months), with only five patients having a follow-up period longer than 1 year, thereby limiting the assessment of long-term outcomes.

Safety outcomes

ANK was generally well tolerated. The most frequently reported adverse events were injection-site reactions (13 patients),4 8 while hepatic cytolysis occurred in five cases,4 8 leading to treatment discontinuation in one patient.25 A single episode of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia was reported after IVIG, with an uncertain causal relationship to ANK.8 No serious infections or treatment-related deaths were described (table 2).

Risk of bias and data quality

The two clinical trials were rated as having a critical overall risk of bias due to their single-arm, non-randomised design (online supplemental table S5). For case reports and case series, the overall certainty of the evidence was judged to be very low, reflecting the high risk of publication bias, study heterogeneity and imprecision due to study designs.

Certainty of evidence (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations)

Given the predominance of uncontrolled studies, the certainty of evidence for primary efficacy and safety outcomes was rated as very low according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) criteria (table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review presents the available clinical evidence on the use of ANK in KD, highlighting its administration regimens, safety profile and reported outcomes on fever, systemic inflammation and CAA. Given the nature of the available literature, which is largely composed of case reports, small case series and early-phase non-randomised trials, the findings should be interpreted with caution and primarily considered as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

The biological rationale for IL-1 blockade in KD is robust. As key mediators of vascular inflammation and endothelial activation,32 33 IL-1α and IL-1β are central to the pathogenesis of KD and the development of CAA and myocarditis.34–38 Preclinical models have confirmed that IL-1 inhibition can prevent myocarditis and coronary lesions,39 thus providing a biologically coherent rationale for targeting IL1 pathway in refractory KD.

The clinical challenge is significant: approximately 10%–20% of patients with KD fail to respond to first-line IVIG therapy and are at increased risk of developing CAA.2 Identifying an effective second-line strategy for this group of patients therefore remains a major unmet need.

Across 81 published cases, ANK was associated with resolution of fever and systemic inflammation in 94.9% of patients with refractory KD, and coronary stabilisation or improvement was reported in a substantial proportion of cases. Nonetheless, the definitive impact of ANK on long-term coronary outcomes remains uncertain: these observations must be interpreted with caution, given the risk of publication bias, the incomplete reporting of coronary Z-scores, heterogeneity in outcome definitions and short and variable follow-up durations. In addition, the natural course of KD and the cumulative effects of the exposure to prior therapies such as IFX, further limit the ability to draw conclusions regarding the specific impact of ANK on coronary outcomes. Interpretation of coronary outcomes is further complicated by the marked heterogeneity of baseline coronary involvement, ranging from mild coronary dilatation to giant aneurysms, which are characterised by different clinical courses and long-term prognoses.

The comparison with IFX is fundamental. While extensive evidence from randomised trials, meta-analyses and retrospective studies confirms that IFX significantly reduces fever and systemic inflammation,40–43 its ability to prevent CAA remains debated.43–48 Specifically, when administered during active KD, IFX did not significantly reduce the rate of IVIG resistance or of CAA rate compared with IVIG alone,46 nor did it demonstrate a statistically significant effect on CAA outcomes when used in refractory KD.43–45 47 48 Nonetheless, some observational data suggest the potential benefit from an early IFX use or with higher doses.49 50 Indeed, in a retrospective multicentre study, the early administration of IFX led to a lower incidence of CAA,49 and a meta-analysis showed a lower risk of CAA with higher regression rates in patients treated with IFX prior to CAA development, even if no statistical significance was reached.47

Indeed, among the 14 patients in the present review who received ANK before CAA appearance, only 4 developed CAA, with subsequent complete aneurysm resolution in 3 of them. These data, for both IFX and ANK, may suggest that the early introduction of targeted biologic therapy in KD could modify the course of coronary vasculitis. However, since these findings are based on small numbers of patients, uncontrolled data and limited statistical power, they should be interpreted with caution, and the question of early introduction of a biologic agent should be specifically addressed in prospective studies.

Nonetheless, the persistence of coronary complications despite optimised use of steroids and anti-TNF therapy suggests a possible role for other inflammatory cascades—particularly IL-1-driven mechanisms—in vascular injury. In this context, ANK could represent a promising alternative for IVIG-refractory KD.

This line of reasoning inevitably raises the question of whether targeted IL-1 blockade should be considered earlier in the treatment course rather than as a rescue therapy, particularly in patients identified as being at high risk of IVIG resistance. However, several critical barriers currently prevent such a recommendation. First, the majority of patients with KD respond to IVIG alone. Second, and most importantly, there is a complete lack of high-quality evidence from randomised controlled trials to support the use of ANK as a first-line agent. Future research should therefore focus on rescue therapies and on identifying biomarkers that could help stratify patients at diagnosis, thus allowing targeted first-line use of biologic therapies in high-risk patients.

This review has several limitations. The primary limitation is the very low quality of the studies present in the literature, which consist of small case reports and case series, along with two non-randomised clinical trials at critical risk of bias. Moreover, publication bias should be taken into account, as negative or inconclusive results are less likely to be reported. Therefore, favourable outcomes may be over-represented, and the reported response rates should be interpreted with caution. The available data are highly heterogeneous in terms of patient characteristics, treatment protocols and outcome assessment, with variable and often short follow-up durations that limit the evaluation of long-term coronary outcomes. Importantly, the frequent prior or concomitant use of therapies such as IVIG, corticosteroids, IFX and other immunomodulators precludes attribution of any observed clinical improvement specifically to ANK, as patient outcomes may also reflect the cumulative effects of previous treatments or the natural course of KD. Given the heterogeneity and descriptive nature of the available studies, the aggregation of these reports, although informative, does not yet allow for quantitative estimation of treatment effects, and subgroup comparisons should be considered exploratory. For these reasons, the findings of this review should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy, but rather as a structured synthesis of currently available observations.

Nevertheless, while a proper meta-analysis could not be conducted, the descriptive comparison of published cases and trials raises the problem of the potential efficacy of ANK in refractory KD. Indeed, the reported observations require confirmation through prospective, randomised controlled studies. A new clinical trial on the efficacy of ANK is currently ongoing (the ANACOMP trial),51 which will likely provide more evidence on the efficacy and safety of IL-1 blockade in KD.

Future studies should address whether earlier IL-1 blockade can modify coronary outcomes, including the prevention of CAA development and the management of established coronary involvement, as well as clarify the role of ANK as rescue therapy versus early treatment intensification in patients at high risk of IVIG resistance.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available literature on the use of ANK in IVIG-resistant KD. The collected data suggest that ANK may be associated with improvement in systemic inflammation and, in some cases, with stabilisation of coronary artery involvement. However, given the very low quality of the available data, the high risk of bias and the presence of significant confounding factors, no definitive conclusions regarding treatment efficacy or coronary outcomes can be drawn. Therefore, caution is warranted in drawing firm conclusions and further prospective trials are needed to establish the optimal patient selection, timing, dosage and duration of ANK therapy in KD.
