Construction of the framework for lifelong learning ability of college nursing teachers based on Ecological Systems Theory
Authors: Wenbin He, Xiaoyan Chen, Ying Zhang, Jian Yang, Fei Fang, Shuting Li, Bilong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo construct a systematic framework for college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability guiding the professional development of college nursing teachers.MethodsTwenty participants (including 12 nursing teachers, 3 educational administrators, and 5 nursing students) were selected through purposive sampling from a university between July and December 2024. Semi-structured in-depth interviews guided by the five nested systems of Ecological Systems Theory (EST) were conducted to collect data, which were then analyzed using NVivo 12 software and grounded theory coding.ResultsGrounded in the five nested systems of EST (individual, micro, meso, exo, macro), the “adaptation, planning, renovation, transformation, shaping (APRTS)” framework for college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability was proposed for the first time, including 5 core dimensions and 15 sub-dimensions. A dynamic ecological cycle mechanism (adaptation→planning→renovation→transformation→shaping) emerged across these dimensions, reflecting the systematic and interactive nature of ability development.ConclusionsGrounded in EST, this framework systematically expounds the internal logic and development laws of college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability, providing a theoretical basis and practical reference for responding to social changes, empowering educational transformation, and optimizing the educational ecosystem. It promotes a paradigm shift in teachers’ career development from passive acceptance to active change.

Introduction

Talent cultivation is the core goal and fundamental mission of education. In an era marked by rapid global technological advancement, deepening cultural integration, and diverse value orientations, education faces unprecedented challenges—including environmental crises, health threats, and the need to nurture innovative, interdisciplinary talent capable of adapting to complex social change. These shifts demand that teachers not only possess solid professional knowledge but also continuously upgrade their abilities through lifelong learning to meet evolving educational needs [1,2]. Education should not only enrich the knowledge reserves and system of the educated, but also shape their values and moral cultivation [3]. To address this challenge, teachers must first acquire the competencies they intend to foster in students, and subsequently, enhance their own capabilities through lifelong learning.

Lifelong learning ability is a comprehensive concept that integrates aspects such as learners’ motivation, emotions, values, and willpower [4]. The UNESCO Global Teacher Report notes that teachers need to shift from “phased learning” to “lifelong learning” to address the challenges posed by accelerated knowledge renewal and diverse educational goals [5]. In 2006, the Council of the European Union issued the Recommendations on Key Competencies for Lifelong Learning, which defined the relevant concepts and core content of lifelong learning competencies [6]. In 2018, the Council of the European Union further revised the content on lifelong learning ability [7]. China’s emphasis on the quality of higher education and the professional development of teachers is a core driver of lifelong learning among university teachers [8]. The National Medium and Long-Term Education Reform and Development Plan Outline clearly set out the strategic goal of “strengthening the construction of the teaching staff,” emphasizing that teacher education needs to transform from “expanding quantity” to “improving quality,” and requires teachers to have a lifelong learning awareness to adapt to the demands of educational modernization [9]. In addition, documents such as the Education Informatization 2.0 Action Plan further emphasize that teachers need to enhance their information technology application capabilities through continuous learning to support the systematic transformation of education [10]. These policies provide institutional guarantees for teachers’ lifelong learning, elevating it from a personal choice to a professional responsibility. Against the backdrop of globalization and the internationalization of higher education, international student enrollment in Chinese universities has expanded significantly in recent years, and international academic exchanges, cross-border joint research projects, and collaborative talent training programs have become increasingly frequent [11]. For nursing disciplines in particular, the global consensus on clinical care standards and the international mobility of nursing talents require Chinese college nursing teachers to continuously improve their international competitiveness, master cutting-edge international nursing knowledge and educational concepts, and adapt to the diversified teaching needs of both domestic and international students [12]. In this context, lifelong learning has become an important pathway for Chinese university teachers to enhance their interdisciplinary integration capabilities, absorb advanced international educational experience, and improve the quality of talent training to meet the development needs of higher education internationalization. This demand is also consistent with the global trend toward the internationalization of nursing education [13].

Nurse educators are required to continuously update their knowledge and adapt to educational reforms to improve the practice competence of nursing graduates [14]. However, existing evidence indicates that nursing teachers face significant competency gaps in knowledge translation, including insufficient preparation for clinicians transitioning to educator roles, a lack of standardized guidance on competency frameworks, and inadequate mentorship support, which hinder the effective transfer of cutting-edge clinical knowledge into teaching practice [14]. At the practical level, existing research has shown that the efficiency of knowledge transformation among nursing teachers in Chinese universities has significant room for improvement. Due to insufficient clinical practice opportunities and heavy teaching loads, effectively implementing knowledge updates for some teachers is difficult in classroom teaching [15]. At the external support level, A regional cross-sectional study found that the top barriers to evidence-based practice include inadequate environmental support (e.g., lack of expert guidance and specialized training opportunities) reported by 42.7 % of institutions [16]. For nursing teachers, these barriers are further exacerbated by heavy teaching and administrative workloads, which limit their time and resources for continuous learning and for transforming knowledge into teaching practice [16]. Despite growing recognition of lifelong learning’s importance for nursing teachers, three gaps persist: 1) few systematic ability frameworks specific to this group have been established; 2) the dynamic mechanism linking the external environment to individual ability development remains unexplored; and 3) existing support strategies are fragmented, lacking multi-level coordination.

The Ecological Systems Theory (EST) was proposed by the developmental psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner [17]. This theory emphasizes that individuals are nested within a series of interrelated environmental systems, and that the dynamic, two-way interaction between these systems and individuals shapes human growth and development. At present, this theory has been increasingly applied in education research, providing a systematic perspective for analyzing the interaction between individual development and multi-level environmental factors [18]. This system provides a framework for explaining how environmental factors at different levels interact and influence an individual’s growth process [19]. The selection of EST as the theoretical foundation is based on three core matching points with this study: EST’s multilevel, nested structure perfectly fits the complex environmental system that affects nursing teachers’ lifelong learning. Existing studies often focus on single dimensions, such as individual ability or institutional policies, while ignoring cross-level interactions [20]. EST’s five nested systems (individual, micro, meso, exo, macro) can systematically integrate the influence of personal traits, peer interactions, inter-institutional collaboration, educational policies, and social trends, thereby avoiding fragmented analytical perspectives. EST’s dynamic two-way interaction perspective aligns with the developmental nature of lifelong learning. Unlike static ability frameworks, EST emphasizes that individuals are both influenced by the environment and actively shape it through practice [21]. This perspective helps reveal the dynamic ecological cycle mechanism of nursing teachers’ ability: teachers adjust their learning behaviors based on environmental feedback, and their practice, in turn, promotes the optimization of the educational ecosystem, consistent with the iterative characteristic of lifelong learning. EST’s problem-solving orientation meets the demand for constructing practical support strategies. EST has been widely applied in education to design multi-level intervention systems [22]. Based on this theory, the framework can not only clarify the connotations of ability dimensions but also propose targeted support strategies at different system levels, thereby providing strong practical guidance for the research results.

Given the global shortage of nursing faculty, improving teacher quality through lifelong learning is critical. Therefore, this study employed grounded theory and in-depth interviews to construct a framework of lifelong learning ability for college nursing teachers, guided by the multilevel perspective of EST.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study design with a grounded theory approach was employed to construct the lifelong learning ability framework. This study was reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (CORE-Q) guidelines.

Participants

Rationale for participant selection

Purposive sampling was used to recruit three groups of participants, which aligned with the multi-level perspective of EST to ensure comprehensive data collection. 1) College nursing teachers: as the core research subjects, they can directly report their lifelong learning practice, needs, and challenges, reflecting the individual and microsystem levels of the ecological framework. 2) Educational administrators: as the designers and implementers of institutional training policies, they can provide insights into mesosystem and exosystem factors that affect teachers’ learning (e.g., resource allocation, policy formulation). 3) Nursing students: as the direct recipients of teaching outcomes, they can provide objective feedback on the practical effects of teachers’ knowledge transformation, verifying the rationality of the ability framework from the demand side.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows. For nursing teachers: full-time teachers working in the nursing college for at least 1 year, who voluntarily participate in this study and sign informed consent. For educational administrators: working in the nursing college or university education management department with at least 2 years of management experience; familiar with the teacher training and professional development system of the university; voluntarily participate in this study and sign informed consent. For nursing students: full-time undergraduate or postgraduate students in the nursing college who have studied for at least 1 semester; able to objectively evaluate the teaching effect of teachers; voluntarily participate in this study, and sign informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: part-time teachers, visiting teachers, and temporary administrative staff; participants who could not complete the interview due to work or study commitments; participants who withdrew from the study after signing the informed consent form. The study was conducted from July to December 2024, and data saturation was reached after three consecutive interviews, during which no new concepts or themes emerged, following recruitment of 20 participants.

Development of the interview outline

The interview outline was developed with EST as the core guiding framework. The overall structure was organized around the five nested systems of EST (individual, micro, meso, exo, macro) to ensure comprehensive coverage of factors influencing nursing teachers’ lifelong learning, from individual traits to macro-environmental support. The question logic followed EST’s core perspective of “individual-environment dynamic interaction,” with each system level addressing both “environmental support for learning” and “learning outcomes feeding back to practice.”

The development process involved three stages. First, the research team drafted an initial version of 28 questions based on a systematic review of 32 relevant studies on teachers’ lifelong learning ability (including 11 in nursing education). Second, the draft was reviewed by 5 experts in teacher education and nursing education (all with >10 years of experience and associate or senior titles), yielding 12 revision suggestions, including adjustments to question wording and the merging of repeated items. Third, 3 pre-interviews (1 nursing teacher, 1 administrator, 1 student) were conducted to refine ambiguous wording and optimize question order. The final version comprised 21 questions classified by EST system level (Appendix A). For different participant groups, questions were tailored to their ecological roles: teachers focused on personal learning practice, administrators on system construction and resource allocation, and students on teaching effectiveness.

Data collection

This study collected data through semi-structured, in-depth interviews and recorded the interview content using audio recordings and notes. All interviews were conducted in a dedicated, quiet conference room in the nursing college office building, with no other unrelated personnel present to ensure privacy and avoid interference. The room was equipped with comfortable seating and stable audio recording equipment, and the ambient temperature and lighting were adjusted to a comfortable level before the interview to help participants relax and express their views truthfully. Interview in accordance with the interview outline; adjusted the question order based on the actual situation; listened carefully to the participant’s statement; and observed and recorded the interviewee’s expressions, tone, and body language. During the interview, interviewers maintained neutral language and avoided leading questions. Each interviewee’s interview lasted 30 to 50 min.

Data analysis

Within 24 h of each interview, one designated researcher transcribed the audio recordings verbatim. After all interviews were completed, another researcher checked the transcripts for accuracy. Data were analyzed using NVivo 12 software and content analysis methods. Two researchers independently read the transcripts to gain an overall understanding, then conducted line-by-line coding to identify recurring concepts and meaningful statements. The cumulative interview time was 566 min.

Quality control

All researchers received 16 h of standardized qualitative research training and passed the assessment; the interview outline was validated through 3 rounds of expert consultation and 3 pre-interviews. During the interview, when interviewees used unclear, ambiguous, or contradictory expressions, the interviewer would immediately use neutral language to follow up for confirmation. Each interview was recorded simultaneously on two devices, and nonverbal cues were captured in detail. Post-interview: Two researchers independently transcribed the audio and cross-checked transcripts to ensure 100 % accuracy; two independent coders coded the transcripts, achieving an inter-coder reliability of 0.85; data saturation was reached when no new themes emerged in three consecutive interviews. Coding differences were resolved through team discussion and expert consultation.

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2023053K). All respondents voluntarily participated in this study and signed the informed consent form.

Results

Basic information of interviewees

A total of 20 participants (1 nursing college dean, 2 department heads, 12 nursing teachers, 5 nursing students) were finally included, numbered N1-N20 to protect privacy. The overall sample was predominantly female (80.0 %), with ages mainly distributed between 31 and 40 years (50.0 %), spanning all career stages from young to senior practitioners. The nursing teachers teaching experience ranged from less than 5 years to more than 20 years, including lecturers (50.0 %), associate professors (33.3 %), and professors (16.7 %), with doctoral degrees accounting for 58.3 %. Their teaching areas covered fundamental nursing, clinical nursing, community nursing, and nursing education. Educational administrators were all middle and senior managers with more than 5 years of management experience, including 2 department heads and 1 nursing college dean, who were fully familiar with the school’s teacher training policy and resource allocation. Nursing students were all undergraduates, including 2 first-year students and 3 juniors, who could provide feedback on the effects of teaching at different stages of learning. The specific demographic data are shown in Appendix B.

Construction of the lifelong learning ability framework for college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability

The adaptation, planning, renovation, transformation, shaping (APRTS) framework (Fig. 1) was developed through grounded theory coding of interview data: 74 initial codes were extracted from the interview texts, then aggregated into 15 subcategories, and finally abstracted into 5 core dimensions, namely adaptation, planning, renovation, transformation, and shaping, forming the “APRTS” acronym. 1) Adaptation: The foundational dimension of the framework, including three driving factors: demand-driven learning motivation, structured training opportunities, and peer & institutional learning support. This dimension reflects teachers’ ability to perceive external environmental changes and actively integrate available resources to lay the foundation for subsequent learning activities. 2) Planning: The directional dimension includes three components (goal-oriented learning design, disciplinary knowledge update goals, and student-responsive learning planning). This dimension guides teachers in translating adaptive needs into clear, actionable learning plans, balancing demands for personal development, disciplinary progress, and student growth. 3) Renovation: The innovation dimension includes three components (digital skill adoption, teaching method innovation, and disciplinary knowledge updating). This dimension reflects the process of teachers systematically updating their knowledge reserves and skill systems in line with planning objectives, which is the core link in ability improvement. 4) Transformation: The practice output dimension includes three application scenarios (learned skill application, curriculum & teaching refinement, and classroom practice optimization). This dimension focuses on the conversion of updated knowledge into practical teaching outcomes, realizing the value release of learning activities. 5) Shaping: The internal driving dimension includes three motivational sources (reflective learning practice, active learning values, and inherent professional motivation). This dimension runs through the entire learning cycle, providing sustained internal impetus for teachers’ long-term lifelong learning and promoting the continuous iteration of the ability system. The framework aligns well with EST: adaptation corresponds to the individual system, planning and renovation dimensions correspond to the microsystem (daily interaction with colleagues/students) and mesosystem (inter-departmental collaboration, academic exchanges) levels, transformation corresponds to the exosystem, and shaping runs through all ecological levels, reflecting the macro-environment’s shaping effect on teachers’ motivation.Fig. 1The APRTS framework of lifelong learning ability for college nursing teachers.

The operational mechanism of the lifelong learning ability framework for college nursing teachers

The operational mechanism of the lifelong learning ability framework is derived through systematic analysis of interview data, forming a closed-loop ecological cycle mechanism of “adaptation→planning→renovation→transformation→shaping” (Fig. 2). The formation of the mechanism is based on three levels of evidence: firstly, the learning experience narratives of most interviewees show a consistent sequence of “perceiving needs→formulating→plans→updating knowledge→applying practice→forming motivation;” secondly, correlation analysis of coding results confirms that there is a significant positive correlation between the five dimensions, reflecting a sequential interaction relationship; thirdly, negative case analysis shows that the blockage of any link in the sequence will lead to the interruption of ability development. This mechanism presents three characteristics: dynamic cyclicality, forming a continuous upward cycle of ability improvement; ecological nesting, nested within the multi-level ecological environment of EST; and differentiated adaptability, with different cycle foci for teachers at different career stages. The factors of each system are determined as follows: individual system (teachers’ personal traits and learning habits); microsystem (daily interactions with students, colleagues, and departmental peers); mesosystem (collaborations between departments or inter-university academic exchanges); exosystem [institutional policies (training programs, research grants) and educational reforms]; macrosystem (global trends and national regulations). Discussion

This study developed the APRTS framework for college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability and revealed its dynamic operational mechanism through grounded theory analysis of interview data and EST.

Rationality of the APRTS lifelong learning ability framework

The APRTS framework proposed in this study fills the gap in targeted ability frameworks for college nursing teachers, and both empirical data and existing research support its rationale. 1) Consistency with existing research consensus: the core dimensions of the framework are highly consistent with the findings of existing studies. Previous studies have confirmed that environmental adaptation ability, learning planning ability, knowledge renewal ability, achievement transformation ability, and internal motivation are the core components of teachers’ lifelong learning ability [[23], [24], [25]]. The innovation of this study lies in the systematic integration of these dimensions for the first time and in the development of a logical hierarchical system specifically for nursing teachers, which addresses the problem of fragmented ability classification in previous studies. 2) Specificity for nursing discipline characteristics: the sub-dimensions of the framework fully reflect the professional characteristics of nursing. For example, the Renovation dimension includes “disciplinary knowledge renewal,” focusing on clinical nursing technology update, and “digital skill adoption,” focusing on intelligent nursing technology application, which is in line with the clinical practicality and rapid knowledge iteration characteristics of the nursing discipline [26], different from the general teacher’s lifelong learning ability framework. The interview data show that 90 % of nursing teachers reported the need to update clinical knowledge in real time, which fully supports the rationale for this dimension’s setting. 3) Theoretical consistency with EST: the dimension design of the framework perfectly matches the nested structure of EST. Adaptation corresponds to the individual system’s response to external stimuli, planning and renovation correspond to the interaction between teachers and the micro/meso systems, Transformation corresponds to the ability output under the influence of the exosystem, and shaping corresponds to the long-term shaping effect of the macro system on individual values [27]. This theoretical embedding makes the framework not only a static classification of abilities but also a dynamic system that can explain interactions between teachers and the environment, offering stronger explanatory power than previous single-dimensional frameworks.

Significance and practical implications of the operational mechanism

The “adaptation→planning→renovation→transformation→shaping” closed-loop operational mechanism revealed in this study clarifies the dynamic development law of college nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability and has important theoretical and practical significance. The mechanism breaks through the previous static understanding of lifelong learning ability and reveals the internal logical relationships among dimensions for the first time. Existing studies mostly focus on describing ability dimensions while ignoring interactions among them [24,25]. This study confirmed that ability development is a continuous cyclical process, thereby deepening understanding of the mechanism underlying the formation of lifelong learning ability. The negative case analysis further shows that blocking any link in the cycle will interrupt the development of ability, providing a new perspective on the obstacles to teachers’ ability development.

The mechanism’s differentiated adaptability suggests that targeted training programs should be designed for teachers at different career stages. For young teachers, the training focus should be on the adaptation and renovation stages, helping them quickly adapt to the teaching environment and master new knowledge and skills; for senior teachers, the focus should be on the Transformation and Shaping stages, supporting them to transform teaching experience into achievements and form a stable internal motivation for lifelong learning. The mechanism’s closed-loop logic provides a self-assessment tool for teachers’ personal development [28,29]. Teachers can diagnose the blocked links in their own ability development cycle and implement targeted ability improvement to enhance the efficiency of lifelong learning. For low- and middle-income countries facing similar nursing teacher shortages and rapid updates in clinical technology, the APRTS framework can provide a low-cost, systematic path for teacher capacity building with potential global applicability.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has two notable strengths: it applies EST to the study of nursing teachers’ lifelong learning ability, constructs a systematic APRTS framework, and reveals its dynamic operational mechanism, thereby filling the research gap in this field and providing a new theoretical perspective for subsequent related research. The framework and mechanism are closely aligned with the practical needs of nursing education, and the proposed practical implications are highly applicable, providing direct guidance for formulating teacher training policies, designing training programs, and supporting teachers’ personal development. This study also has some limitations that should be noted. The participants are all from one university, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Subsequent studies can expand the sample across different regions and university levels to further verify the framework’s applicability. This is a cross-sectional qualitative study that can reveal the structural characteristics of the framework and mechanism, but cannot track the dynamic process of teachers’ ability development over time. Subsequent longitudinal studies can be conducted to verify the operational mechanism’s long-term effectiveness further.

Conclusions

Drawing on EST, this study constructs a lifelong learning competence framework for college nursing teachers—one that facilitates two key transitions aligned with EST’s core tenets: from environmental empowerment to subjective self-awareness, and from knowledge construction to practical transformation. The framework embodies an internal ecological cycle (adaptation→planning→renovation→transformation→shaping), reflecting the dynamic, iterative nature of lifelong learning, and signals a paradigm shift in nursing teachers’ professional development: from standardized training to ecological growth. During implementation, it is necessary to account for differences in teachers’ capabilities across educational stages. While maintaining the stability of the framework structure, reserve space for practical flexibility to accommodate dynamic emotional changes and to achieve coordinated progress in individual teachers’ growth and the optimization of the educational ecosystem.
