Effect of immunonutrition on surgery outcomes in cancer patients: evidence from an umbrella review on interventional and observational meta-analyses
Authors: Helia Mardani, Ulduz Riazi Khameneh, Ali Jafari, Sepideh Dolati, Nima Baziar, Maede Amirpour, Ghazal Zamanian, Omid Zamani, Mohammad Amin Tofighi Zavareh, Mohammad Esmail Akbari, Atieh Akbari, Nasser Malekpour Alamdari, Zeinab Javid, Jamal Rahmani, Seyed Hossein Ardehali, Mahdi Shadnoush

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition is highly prevalent in cancer patients, leading to increased post-surgical complications. Introduction

Cancer is considered one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. Anti-cancer treatments can lead to poor nutritional status in these patients by increasing energy requirements, reducing food intake, and/or impairing nutrient absorption [2]. Protein-energy malnutrition also occurs in 30–90% of patients with cancer [3].

Malnutrition adversely affects immunity through various mechanisms, including lymph node atrophy, decreased lymphocyte count and IgA production, and suppression of cellular immunity. Several studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of nutritional support in malnourished patients [4, 5]. One particular type of nutritional support is Immunonutrition (IMN) [6].

This approach suggests that specific nutrients, including amino acids such as glutamine and arginine, polyunsaturated fatty acids such as omega-3, and ribonucleic acid (RNA), can serve as immune modulators, individually or in combination [7]. Immunonutrition enhances mucosal barrier function, bolsters cellular defense, and decreases local or systemic inflammation [8].

A meta-analysis of 3,692 patients undergoing surgery for gastrointestinal cancer who received enteral immunonutrition (EIN) compared with controls found that the EIN group had a significantly lower incidence of overall complications. The rates of infectious complications, surgical site infections, anastomotic leakage, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) duration, and antibiotic therapy length were significantly reduced in the EIN-treated group [9].

The results of another study evaluating the effect of immunonutrition supplementation 5 days prior to mastectomy on immediate breast reconstruction outcomes in women with breast cancer, compared to controls, indicated that wound complications decreased from 49.06% to 32.20% following the intervention. Specifically, the rate of mastectomy skin flap necrosis dropped from 24.53% to 8.47%, and the rate of wound dehiscence fell from 15.09% to 1.69% in the intervention group [10].

However, not all studies can reach the same conclusion. Some have suggested that EIN does not significantly reduce postoperative complications, mortality, or length of hospital stay [11–13]. While the impact of enteral immunotherapy nutrition on clinical outcomes, immune levels, and nutritional status is compelling, not all clinical trials have demonstrated similar effects, and some have yielded conflicting results [14]. Furthermore, there is controversy about the results of the meta-analyses in this regard. While some meta-analyses have significantly improved immunonutrition’s effect on postoperative outcomes in cancer patients [9, 15], others have reported its ineffectiveness on overall complications [16, 17]. Therefore, this umbrella review study was conducted to investigate the impact of immunonutrition on postoperative complications in cancer patients. Several meta-analyses have been published on this issue recently, but these studies often focus on specific cancer types, intervention timing, or limited outcomes. There are unresolved questions about overall efficacy, optimal timing (preoperative, perioperative, or postoperative), and generalizability across diverse populations. No prior studies have comprehensively evaluated evidence to address these inconsistencies and controversial issues. Therefore, this umbrella review was conducted to investigate the impact of immunonutrition on postoperative complications in cancer patients. While previous umbrella reviews have focused on specific cancers, this review provides the first comprehensive review across all cancer types and immunonutrition intervention timing.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this umbrella review was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024619753). This study adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines [18].

Research objectives

This umbrella review of meta-analyses aimed to assess the impact of immunonutrition on surgical outcomes in cancer patients. Primary outcomes included postoperative overall complications, infectious complications, noninfectious complications, mortality, surgical-site infections, anastomotic leak, and length of hospital stay. No secondary outcomes were evaluated.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search also included a manual review of references from retrieved articles, and a Google Scholar search to identify additional relevant studies. The search covered database inception to Sep 2025, with no time restrictions. Keywords included (“immunonutrition” OR “arginine” OR “omega-3 fatty acids” OR “glutamine” OR “nucleotides”) AND (“cancer” OR “neoplasm” OR “carcinoma”) AND (“surgery” OR “operative”) AND (“meta-analysis” OR “systematic review”). The complete search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion criteria

Meta-analyses investigating the effect of immunonutrition (arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, glutamine, nucleotides, or combinations) on surgical outcomes in adult cancer patients (≥ 18 years) were included. The PICO framework is presented in Table 1. No restrictions were applied to the type, dosage, duration, or route of administration (oral, enteral, or parenteral) of immunonutrition, nor to the timing of intervention (preoperative, perioperative, or postoperative). Only English-language meta-analyses were included, with no publication date restrictions.

Exclusion criteria

Meta-analyses not based on RCTs or observational studies were excluded, as were those lacking data on the specified outcomes or involving pediatric populations (< 18 years). Studies evaluating immunonutrition combined with non-immunonutrient supplements were excluded, except when one or two such studies were inadvertently included. Meta-analyses involving immunonutrition in fortified foods, where nutrient quantities were unclear, were also excluded.

Study screening and inclusion

Two independent reviewers (HM, URK) screened titles and abstracts of retrieved articles. Studies with uncertain eligibility were advanced to full-text review to minimize erroneous exclusions. The same reviewers independently evaluated full texts, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (JR). Excluded studies during full-text screening were documented with the reasons for exclusion.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (HM and URK) extracted data independently using a standardized spreadsheet to minimize errors. In cases of missing data, study authors were contacted. Extracted data included: first author, publication year, number of studies and participants, cancer type, immunonutrient type, administration route (oral, enteral, parenteral), intervention timing (preoperative, perioperative, postoperative), duration, and effect sizes (ES) with confidence intervals (CIs) for postoperative overall complications, infectious complications, noninfectious complications, mortality, surgical-site infections, anastomotic leak, and length of hospital stay.

Quality assessment

One reviewer (MA) assessed the quality of the included meta-analyses using the AMSTAR 2 tool [19], focusing on 16 items, including seven critical domains. The GRADE system was used to evaluate the quality of evidence for pooled outcomes [20].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Pooled effect sizes (relative risk (RR), hazard ratio (HR) (HR used for mortality due to time-to-event reporting in included meta-analyses), or weighted mean difference (WMD)) and CIs were calculated using a random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood method. Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-squared test (P < 0.10) and the I² statistic; I² > 75% indicated high heterogeneity, and I² ≤ 40% indicated low heterogeneity [21]. Subgroup analyses were performed based on intervention timing (preoperative, perioperative, postoperative, or mixed). Sensitivity analyses evaluated the impact of removing individual studies on overall results. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 17.0, with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Assessment of bias

Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots, supplemented by formal statistical tests, including Egger’s regression test. Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with sufficient numbers of studies. When evidence of publication bias was detected, appropriate adjustments were considered in interpreting the results.

Results

PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. A systematic search of the PubMed/MEDLINE, WOS, and SCOPUS databases yielded 459 articles, of which 106 were duplicates. In the title/abstract screening, 269 articles were excluded, and 84 were included in the full-text screening. After the full-text screening process, 50 potentially relevant articles were included in the analysis. A Google Scholar search also added one additional article, three studies were added to the included studies in the update, and ultimately, 54 studies were included in the final analysis, meeting the inclusion criteria [15–17, 22–72].

Results of meta-analysis

Twelve articles investigated the effect of immunonutrition on postoperative overall complications [15–17, 23, 29, 38, 41, 43, 49, 59, 62, 63]. The pooled results were RR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.70–0.88; P < 0.001 for postoperative overall complications by immunonutrition intervention (Fig. 2a). There was no significant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 39.5%).

The effects of immunonutrition

Compared with placebo (standard nutrition), immunonutrition was associated with a decrease in infectious complications in postoperative cancer surgeries (RR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.58–0.65; P < 0.001; I2 = 47.3%) (Fig. 2b).

Noninfectious complications in patients with immunonutrition intervention are lower than in patients with standard nutrition (RR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.82–0.94; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). There was no significant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 0%).

The immunonutrition intervention was associated with a decrease in the hazard of mortality compared with placebo (HR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.74–0.99; P < 0.03; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2d).

Eleven articles investigated the effect of immunonutrition on postoperative surgical-site infection [23, 41, 45, 46, 52, 54, 56, 63, 66, 70, 71]. The pooled results showed a decrease in postoperative surgical-site infections with immunonutrition intervention (RR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.59–0.74; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2e). There was no significant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 6.5%).

Twenty-one studies with 32,919 participants evaluated the effect of immunonutrition on postoperative anastomotic leak, and the combined results showed a significant decrease in the risk of anastomotic leak (RR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.62–0.77; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2f). There was no significant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 0%).

Compared with placebo, the immunonutrition intervention was associated with a significant decrease in the length of hospital stay (WMD = −1.75 days; 95% CI: −2.09, −1.41; P < 0.001; I2 = 90.3%) (Fig. 2g).

Subgroup analysis based on time of immunonutrition intervention

Figure 3 summarises the results of the subgroup analyses of immunonutrition use by intervention time. Overall, complications show a greater decrease with postoperative immunonutrition intervention (RR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.54–0.89) than with preoperative (RR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.61–1.01) or perioperative (RR = 0.99; 95% CI: 0.80–1.22) interventions. Immunonutrition intervention is associated with a significant decrease in infectious complications at each time point (preoperative P < 0.001, postoperative P < 0.001, perioperative P < 0.001, and Across Different times P < 0.001). Noninfectious complications decreased in the postoperative (P < 0.001) and perioperative (P = 0.002) periods with the intervention compared with the preoperative period (P = 0.289). Likewise, the anastomotic leak rate is decreased in the postoperative (RR = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.53–0.97) and perioperative (RR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.42–0.79) interventions compared with the preoperative period (RR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.65–1.23). The largest decrease in hospital length of stay is in the postoperative period (2.26 days, P = 0.001), perioperative period (1.59 days, P = 0.001), and preoperative period (1.05 days, P = 0.001), respectively. Subgroup analyses based on the timing of intervention substantially reduced heterogeneity in certain categories (e.g., I² reduced from 90.3% to 64.3% for postoperative interventions), highlighting the importance of temporal factors in outcome variability.

GRADE system and publication bias

Table 4 provides the quality of pooled outcomes results using the GRADE system. Most GRADE results are of low quality. Egger’s tests show no publication bias for overall complications (p = 0.25), noninfectious complications (p = 0.13), mortality (p = 0.17), surgical-site infection (p = 0.67), and anastomotic leak (p = 0.07). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of immunonutrition on postoperative complications in patients with cancer. The findings indicated that immunonutrition reduces overall, infectious, and noninfectious complications, lowers the risk of mortality, and reduces surgical site infections, anastomotic leakage, and the length of hospitalization.

The effect of the time factor on immunonutrition intervention indicators was significant: overall complications in the postoperative period were significantly reduced compared with preoperative or perioperative interventions, and intervention at any time also led to a notable decrease in complications. Noninfectious complications and anastomotic leakage were reduced more in the postoperative and perioperative phases than in the preoperative phase, and the greatest reduction in hospitalization duration occurred during the postoperative, perioperative, and preoperative phases, respectively. The timing of the immunonutrition intervention did not impact mortality and was not particularly effective.

It has been noted that glutamine levels decline during critical illness, and these reduced levels correlate with adverse clinical outcomes [7]. Glutamine enhances lymphocyte function and bolsters antioxidant defence. It also supports intestinal integrity and minimizes bacterial translocation. Omega-3 fatty acids may benefit critically ill patients by modulating the production of inflammatory eicosanoids and cytokines. Consequently, they enhance various physiological functions, including immune response, cell proliferation, blood clotting, and inflammation [73]. Arginine, a non-essential amino acid that becomes conditionally essential during critical illness, is recognized as a precursor of polyamines and nucleosides that stimulate growth hormone production. This amino acid exhibits anabolic activity and enhances the number of T-helper cells [74]. Nucleotides influence the maturation, activation, and proliferation of lymphocytes; furthermore, nucleotide supplementation aids in the regeneration of intestinal villi [75].

Effect of immunonutrition on overall complications

The results of this study indicated a reduction in overall complications associated with immunonutrition, aligning with findings from several other studies [15, 76–81]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 studies involving 3,692 patients who underwent surgery for gastrointestinal cancers (including gastric, colorectal, oesophageal, and pancreatic cancers) demonstrated that immunonutrition led to a significant decrease in overall complications compared to standard nutritional support [9].

The American Society for Enteral and Parenteral Nutrition (ASPEN) and the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines recommend immunonutrition for patients undergoing abdominal surgery, particularly those with upper gastrointestinal cancer [82, 83]. However, some studies have reported results that contradict our findings. For instance, Howes N et al. (2018) examined 1,099 malnourished patients with head and neck cancer who received immunonutrition preoperatively, perioperatively, and postoperatively. Most studies used arginine-containing nutritional support formulas, but there were variations in the products and the amounts used, ultimately showing no evidence that nutritional support reduced postoperative complications compared with standard nutrition [84].

Moreover, the timing of immunonutrition interventions affects the incidence of overall complications. In this context, our results are consistent with the results of some studies.

For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Cheng Y et al. in 2018, involving 583 patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer who received immunonutrition for 7 days postoperatively, found that overall postoperative complications were significantly reduced in this group compared to the control group [59].

The use of immunonutrition in cancer has received much attention in recent years. However, given the mixed results on overall morbidity, the small sample sizes of current studies, and the inadequate clinical guidelines regarding immunonutrition, including dosage and composition of different formulations [80], further research is needed to fully understand its mechanisms of action, monitor patient response to treatment, assess its impact on clinical outcomes, identify which patients are most likely to benefit, and develop standard protocols for its use in cancer patients.

Effect of immunonutrition on infectious and noninfectious complications

According to this study’s findings, immunonutrition was associated with a decrease in both infectious and noninfectious complications following cancer surgery compared with standard nutrition.

A study assessing the effectiveness of preoperative immunonutrition in patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal cancer surgery—incorporating arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, or glutamine—found that immunonutrition lowered the average rate of postoperative infectious complications compared to standard nutrition [85]. This improvement could be related to the modulation of the inflammatory response in the preoperative period [86].

Immunonutrition reduces both infectious and noninfectious complications by selectively activating immune cells to enhance their function, regulate cytokine production and release, reduce inflammatory processes, and maintain the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier [87].

However, our results contradict the findings of some studies, which show that immune nutrition does not reduce infectious complications in this group of patients.

Even an increased infection rate was observed in patients undergoing intervention compared with those in the standard enteral diet (SEN) group [88, 89].

Infectious complications are characterized by high heterogeneity, very low GRADE, and evidence of publication bias. The lack of reduction in these complications may be attributed to the timing and duration of immunonutrition, as well as the type of cancer. The studies conducted on patients with gastrointestinal cancers followed a 5-day preoperative immunonutrition protocol, which appears insufficient to strengthen the immune system and reduce postoperative complications. Therefore, it is recommended that immunonutrition be initiated before surgery and continued afterwards.

A study of 176 patients with colon cancer who received oral dietary supplementation (400 ml/day) with arginine and ω−3 fatty acids for 7 days prior to surgery did not show a reduction in infectious complications compared with the control group receiving dietary supplementation [90]. This may be attributed to the absence of the amino acid glutamine in the solutions used. In cases of increased catabolism, endogenous glutamine production may be compromised and insufficient. It is essential for differentiating intestinal epithelial cells and is an energy source for lymphocytes and macrophages [91].

A common theme among influential articles on reducing infection may be the inclusion of omega-3 and arginine in nutritional solutions, which is an important point. Arginine supports immune function and wound healing, which are essential for cancer patients whose immune systems are often compromised and who aim for a complete recovery after surgery (ERAS). Omega-3 fatty acids may help reduce inflammation and improve the nutritional status of cancer patients [92].

Effect of immunonutrition on mortality

According to this study’s findings, immunonutrition intervention was associated with a reduced risk of mortality compared with placebo, consistent with the results of a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis by Ricci et al. The results of this study, which included 87 studies and 8,375 patients who underwent major abdominal surgeries due to tumours, indicated that perioperative oral or injectable immunonutrition (including preoperative oral with arginine, glutamine, and polyunsaturated fatty acids) decreased mortality in these patients; however, this reduction was not statistically significant, mirroring the findings of our study [93].

It is important to note that most studies systematically reviewed and meta-analyzed on the effects of immunonutrition and various cancer surgeries on mortality reported results contrary to the current findings of our study [9, 39, 54, 81, 87, 94].

The absence of an effect of immunonutrition on mortality can be attributed to several factors. A reason is the complex relationship between malnutrition and immune function. Nutritional supplements alone may not adequately alter immune responses or improve survival outcomes. Malnutrition alters the immune response, making individuals more vulnerable to infections, yet interventions may not fully reverse these effects. Additionally, multiple nutrient deficiencies in patients may hinder the effectiveness of single-nutrient supplements [95, 96].

The effect of immunonutrition on surgical site infection

Another result of our study was the impact of immunonutrition on reducing postoperative surgical site infections, which aligns with findings from several studies [9, 23, 37, 50]. A commonality among these studies is that most utilized this type of nutrition in patients with various gastrointestinal cancers.

Regardless of the timing of immunonutrition administration, this effect may be attributed to the presence of vital compounds such as glutamine and arginine [97, 98]. However, some studies have reported conflicting results. A 2022 meta-analysis involving 1,056 patients undergoing total gastrectomy found that immunonutrition did not reduce surgical site infections compared to enteral nutrition.

There is disagreement between these studies’ results and ours, which may be due to heterogeneity across studies, including variations in disease type, demographic characteristics, types of parenteral nutrition, nutritional or metabolic status, and infusion timing.

The key point of these studies was the use of a standardized nutritional solution containing three omega-3 compounds: arginine, nucleotides, and soluble fibre. Various results indicated that EIN effectively increased levels of IgA, IgG, and IgM, CD4þ and CD3þ cells, the CD4þ/CD8þ ratio, and the number of NK cells, while also improving nutritional and immunological status and reducing infection risk, ultimately leading to a decreased likelihood of infection [99].

Effect of immunonutrition on anastomosis

The results of this study showed that immunonutrition reduced the risk of postoperative anastomotic leakage, aligning with several studies that utilized this nutrition at various times preoperatively, perioperatively, or postoperatively [22, 37, 100].

However, a prospective cohort study examining the effectiveness of an arginine-enriched immunonutrition solution (237 ml, three times daily) in 3,375 patients for 5 days prior to elective colorectal surgery found that this nutritional solution did not reduce the risk of anastomotic leakage [79].

Anastomotic leak remains a dreaded postoperative complication, especially in gastrointestinal surgery. Surgical stress has been shown to cause acute arginine depletion and wound healing. Therefore, many community guidelines recommend routinely including immunonutrition supplements as part of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) to combat malnutrition and its infectious complications. However, its effect on reducing the rate of anastomotic leakage and the difference in the time of administration – preoperative, perioperative, or postoperative – remains unclear due to the diversity of cancers, nutritional status, metabolic needs and comorbidities, the composition and dosage of nutritional solutions [101], and there is no consensus on the benefits of immunonutrition in reducing this complication.

Immune nutrition on the length of hospital stays

Another finding of this study is that immunonutrition intervention significantly reduced the length of hospital stay compared to standard nutrition. The heterogeneity in LOS warrants caution in generalizing the results. A meta-analysis of six studies involving 379 individuals undergoing head or neck cancer surgery found that enteral nutrition with arginine at 6.25–18.70 g/L shortened hospital stay [66].

Scientific sources suggest that immunonutrition’s effectiveness in reducing the length of hospital stay for surgical and trauma patients may be due to the high protein content of arginine and other immune nutrients [102].

However, results from a retrospective study involving 110 patients with gastric cancer who received immunonutrition preoperatively and postoperatively indicated no difference in the length of hospitalization between the intervention and control groups [103].

The lack of difference in hospitalization length in the mentioned study and similar research may be attributed to patient factors, including the presence or absence of malnutrition, disease stage, cancer type, and the amount and dosage of the immunonutrition solution. Therefore, extensive prospective, multicenter studies focusing on immunonutrition support are necessary to examine the precise relationship between this type of nutrition and hospitalization length in cancer patients.

Considering the time factor, the most significant reduction in hospital stay due to immunonutrition was observed postoperatively (2.26 days, P = 0.001), perioperatively (1.59 days, P = 0.001), and preoperatively (1.05 days, P = 0.001), respectively, with some studies yielding results consistent with ours. For example, Daly et al. investigated the effect of postoperative immunonutrition in 60 adult patients undergoing surgery for upper gastrointestinal cancers, finding that the mean hospital stay was reduced by 6 days [104].

The pronounced effect of postoperative immunonutrition on reducing hospital stay may be attributed to the presence of arginine. Arginine plays a vital role in immune function and wound healing, essential for cancer patients whose immune systems are often compromised and who aim for enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) or radiotherapy [105].

Some studies reported no difference in preoperative and postoperative intake. A prospective, randomized study divided 40 patients with esophageal cancer who were candidates for esophageal surgery into two groups: those receiving an immunonutrition intervention (IMPACT solution) and a standard nutrition control group from 7 days before to 7 days after surgery. This study’s results demonstrated no significant difference in postoperative ICU stay or postoperative hospital LOS between the two groups [106].

Heterogeneity arising from differing compositions and timing of EIN regimen administration, insufficient sample sizes, and the limited number of eligible studies in most sensitive analyses with subgroup analysis may undermine the studies’ power. Consequently, large-scale, well-designed studies are essential to further validate the effects.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of our umbrella review is the comprehensive and rigorous methodological framework employed throughout the study. By adhering to PRISMA guidelines and registering the protocol with PROSPERO, we ensured complete transparency and reproducibility, establishing a gold standard for umbrella reviews in the field of surgical oncology nutrition. Our search strategy contained multiple high-impact databases, manual reference screening, and Google Scholar searches, thereby capturing the most comprehensive body of evidence available on immunonutrition in cancer surgery. The implementation of the AMSTAR-2 tool for quality assessment of included meta-analyses, combined with the GRADE framework evaluation of evidence quality, provides a dual-layered approach to evidence appraisal that significantly enhances the reliability and clinical applicability of our findings.

Despite these methodological strengths, several important limitations must be acknowledged. GRADE assessments provided low-quality ratings, which may be due to heterogeneity in study designs, intervention protocols, and patient populations across the included meta-analyses. This heterogeneity, while addressed through random-effects modelling, introduces uncertainty in the precision of effect estimates and limits the ability to make strong recommendations for specific patient subgroups. The restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias, potentially excluding relevant non-Englishstudies. Furthermore, the reliance on published meta-analyses rather than individual patient data limits our ability to perform more analyses, such as exploring dose-response relationships or identifying optimal patient selection criteria based on individual risk factors. The substantial heterogeneity observed in hospital length-of-stay analyses (I²=90.3%) suggests significant variability in healthcare systems, surgical practices, and discharge criteria across studies, which may limit the generalizability of this finding to specific clinical settings.

Another significant limitation stems from the variability in immunonutrition formulations and dosing regimens across the included studies. While we identified consistent benefits with formulations containing arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, glutamine, and nucleotides, the lack of standardization in dosages, ingredient ratios, and supplementation duration prevents us from providing precise clinical recommendations for optimal protocols. Although we conducted subgroup analyses by intervention timing to address some aspects of clinical heterogeneity, we were unable to perform meta-regression or more detailed subgroup analyses (e.g., by arginine dose, patient BMI, or specific IMN formulas) due to the aggregated nature of the data in the included meta-analyses. Although the long inclusion period (1999–2025) encompasses evolving practices such as Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, neoadjuvant therapies, and minimally invasive techniques, which may influence outcomes, our subgroup analyses by intervention timing help reduce this by identifying postoperative immunonutrition as particularly effective.

Due to the overlap between the original studies in the included meta-analyses and the impact of this overlap on the overall pooled results, caution should be taken in interpreting and applying the results in clinical practice.

Clinical implications of findings

The findings of this umbrella review provide robust, evidence-based guidance for healthcare practitioners managing surgical oncology patients. For oncology surgeons and perioperative care teams, integrating immunonutrition—specifically formulations enriched with arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, glutamine, and nucleotides—offers clinically meaningful benefits across multiple postoperative outcomes.

A 21% reduction in overall complications (RR: 0.79) underscores the potential of immunonutrition as a standard adjunctive therapy in cancer surgery protocols. Notably, the intervention led to a 31% reduction in anastomotic leaks (RR: 0.69) and a 34% decrease in surgical site infections (RR: 0.66), both of which are major contributors to postoperative morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden. Additionally, the 14% reduction in mortality hazard (HR: 0.86) highlights the life-saving potential of this nutritional strategy, elevating its role from supportive care to therapeutic intervention.

From a policy and administrative standpoint, these findings support the integration of immunonutrition into Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols and institutional surgical guidelines. The average reduction of 1.75 days in hospital stay translates into cost savings and improved resource utilization, while decreased complication rates alleviate pressure on intensive care and surgical wards.

Clinicians should particularly consider immunonutrition for high-risk patients undergoing major gastrointestinal surgery, where the absolute risk reduction is greatest. However, given the moderate quality of evidence for most outcomes (as assessed by GRADE), clinicians must still tailor decisions based on individual patient factors, including baseline nutritional status, cancer stage, and comorbidities.

Future research direction

Future research should prioritize the development of standardized immunonutrition protocols through large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that address the current variability in formulation composition, dosing regimens, and administration timing. Given the wide heterogeneity observed in our review—particularly in the combinations and concentrations of arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, glutamine, and nucleotides—systematic dose-response studies are urgently needed to identify optimal ratios tailored to specific cancer types and surgical procedures.

Moreover, long-term follow-up beyond the perioperative period is essential to evaluate the sustained benefits of immunonutrition on oncologic outcomes, including disease-free survival, overall survival, and quality of life.

To address the limitations of aggregated data in current meta-analyses, future research should prioritize individual-patient-data (IPD) meta-analyses. These would enable more granular subgroup analyses (e.g., by patient BMI, specific immunonutrient doses, or IMN formulations), meta-regression to explore sources of clinical heterogeneity, and better control for confounders, ultimately providing stronger evidence for personalized immunonutrition strategies in surgical oncology.

Finally, investigating the synergistic effects of immunonutrition with other perioperative strategies—such as prehabilitation programs, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways, and multimodal analgesia—could help create comprehensive care bundles that improve outcomes while optimizing healthcare resource use.

In parallel, comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses are essential to quantifying the economic value of immunonutrition. Given the observed reductions in hospital length of stay and postoperative complications, such studies can support healthcare policy decisions, insurance coverage, and budget allocation for perioperative nutrition programs, especially in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

This umbrella review resolves key controversies by demonstrating the benefits of immunonutrition. Despite predominantly low-quality evidence per GRADE, evidence from the studies reviewed in this analysis demonstrates reductions in overall complications, infectious complications, non-infectious complications, mortality, surgical-site infections, anastomotic leaks, and length of hospital stay in patients with cancer. The heterogeneity in LOS warrants caution in generalizing the results. Postoperative intervention is more effective than pre- or perioperative intervention.
