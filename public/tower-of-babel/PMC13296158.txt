Efficient sorption of zirconium (IV) from zircon leachate using a carboxymethyl cellulose based sorbent
Authors: Sh. M. Abdo, Zakaria A. Mekawy, G. A. Dakroury, K. M. El-Azony, Reda R. Sheha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a hydrogel carboxymethyl cellulose-polyvinyl alcohol-fumaric acid (CPF hydrogel) composed of Sodium Carboxymethyl Cellulose (CMC) and Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) with the ratio 3:1 and Fumaric Acid was synthesized through the wet chemical method and evaluated for the sorption of Zr (IV) ions from acidic leachate solution. The CPF hydrogel was characterized using FT-IR, SEM and EDX. The results confirmed its porous structure and surface functionality that promoted the effective metal ion sorption. Batch experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of contact time, initial concentration, V/m ratio, temperature, and pH on Zr (IV) extraction. Equilibrium was reached within 90 min at pH 1, V/m = 0.3, and 20 °C. The maximum sorption capacity was 239.7 mg g− 1. The sorption kinetics followed the pseudo-nth-order model, while equilibrium data best fit the Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R) isotherm models, indicating the chemisorption nature of the sorption process. Thermodynamic analysis revealed that the sorption process is spontaneous and endothermic. Desorption studies showed that 0.2 M H2SO4 was an effective eluent to recover about 90% Zr (IV). These findings highlight CPF hydrogel as a promising, cost-effective material for the selective recovery of Zr (IV) from acidic leachates, offering potential applications in metal separation and purification processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01842-w.

Introduction

Zirconium is a transition metal with a high melting point and does not easily react with acids, water, or air. It is durable and resistant to corrosion. Because of these properties, it is useful in harsh applications like nuclear reactors, space missions, and chemical factories [1]. One of the main uses of zirconium is in the nuclear industry, where it is used to make fuel cladding for nuclear reactors. This is because zirconium does not absorb neutrons easily, allowing nuclear reactions to continue smoothly. It is also highly resistant to heat and corrosion, which helps keep nuclear fuel rods safe and long-lasting [2]. Additionally, Zirconium is used in aerospace engineering, where it is valued for being both strong and lightweight. It is also used in medical implants, chemical equipment, and ceramics. Zirconium compounds like zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) are important for making dental crowns, artificial joints, and heat-resistant coatings. Because of its special properties and many uses, the demand for zirconium keeps increasing [3].

Environmentally, Zirconium is mostly found in nature as zircon (ZrSiO₄), a silicate mineral commonly extracted from heavy mineral sands. It can be obtained from baddeleyite (ZrO₂) and zircon-rich ores. However, extracting pure zirconium is a challenge because it is often found with hafnium (Hf), which has similar chemical properties and hence it is difficult to separate [1]. Then, the zirconium purification has essential importance in nuclear applications, where hafnium can absorb neutrons and disrupt the nuclear reaction process [4].

The extraction of zirconium from zircon ore has traditionally been performed using several methods. The most common techniques include alkaline fusion where zircon is treated with sodium hydroxide at elevated temperatures to break down the zircon structure and release zirconium [5]. This process requires a significant amount of energy and generates waste materials. Chlorination is another traditional technique at which zircon is treated with chlorine gas and carbon at elevated temperatures to form zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl₄), which is then purified and reduced to metallic zirconium [6]. Although the effectiveness of this method, it produces harmful chlorine-based byproducts. Further, the most common traditional technique is solvent extraction at which zirconium is separated from hafnium using organic solvents [7]. While it is widely used in industry, it involves the use of hazardous chemicals and includes complex processing steps. These traditional methods have certain disadvantages, such as high energy consumption, environmental pollution, and the need for multiple purification steps [8]. Because of these limitations, researchers are continuously looking for improved alternative methods that are more environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and efficient [5, 9].

Recently, the use of hydrogel-based sorption techniques has gained more attention as an efficient and eco-friendly approach for metal extraction [10–12]. Hydrogels are three-dimensional polymeric networks that can absorb and retain large amounts of water. They have a high ability to sorb different metal ions, as well as high selectivity, biodegradability, reusability, and high sorption capacity [13].

The hydrogel used in this study was prepared from carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and fumaric acid. Each of these components plays a crucial role in the sorption performance of the hydrogel. CMC is a water-soluble and biodegradable polymer made from cellulose [14]. It has carboxyl (-COO⁻) groups that strongly attract metal ions, rendering it as an effective sorbent material [15]. PVA is an artificial polymer known for its flexibility, mechanical strength, and biocompatibility. It forms a stable hydrogel matrix, providing durability and structure to the material. PVA also helps in water retention, which is an essential route for metal ion sorption [16]. Fumaric acid is an organic acid acts as a crosslinker that introduces an additional carboxyl group to the hydrogel, increasing its metal-binding sites [17–20]. By combining these materials, which have admirable features, an excellent hydrogel composite can be obtained. This composite can be considered an efficient, reusable, and environmentally friendly sorbent for zirconium extraction. Hence, the efforts in this work were devoted to upgrade a zirconium extraction using a hydrogel.

The novelty of this work lies in three aspects: (i) the first application of fumaric acid-crosslinked CMC/PVA hydrogel (CPF) for Zr(IV) recovery from real zircon leachate; (ii) exceptional selectivity for Zr(IV) over Hf(IV) and other coexisting ions (Al, Ti, Ce, etc.) under strongly acidic conditions; (iii) validation using an actual industrial leachate rather than synthetic solutions, demonstrating practical applicability.

The aim of this study was focused on synthesis a hydrogel (CPF) using carboxymethylcellulose, polyvinyl alcohol, and fumaric acid. It is then used as a sorbent to extract zirconium from zircon leachate. To improve the extraction process, optimization of the sorption parameters was assessed. The sorption mechanism was explored using different kinetic and isotherm models. Additionally, this research highlights the possibility of using biopolymer-based materials for metal recovery, promoting eco-friendly technologies in metallurgy.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Carboxymethylcellulose was purchased from Food Chem Co., USA and used as received without further purification. Polyvinyl alcohol (Cat No. 7200, degree of polymerization: *1600 and degree of hydrolysation: 97.5–99.5 mol%) and fumaric acid (Cat No. 47910) were supplied via Fluka. The real zirconium solution was collected from zircon leachate that provided from the Nuclear Materials Authority.

Preparation of CPF hydrogel

To prepare CPF hydrogel, 6 g of sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was dissolved in 300 mL distilled H₂O (2% w/v), and separately 2 g of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was dissolved in 100 mL distilled H₂O (2% w/v). Both solutions were stirred at 60 °C until complete dissolution. The two solutions were mixed and magnetically stirred at 500 rpm for 2 h, then left undisturbed for 24 h at room temperature, yielding a clear homogeneous solution. Then 1.5 g (18.75%) of fumaric acid (crosslinker) was added, and the mixture was subjected to ultrasonic treatment (DAIHAN Scientific, 60 Hz) for 30 min, followed by continuous magnetic stirring for 24 h at 50 °C to promote esterification crosslinking. The final mixture was dried in an air oven at 50 °C for 24 h, then ground into a fine powder (particle size < 250 μm) for further use [17]. The crosslinking reaction is proposed to occur through esterification between the carboxylic acid groups (–COOH) of fumaric acid and the hydroxyl groups (–OH) of both CMC and PVA chains, resulting in the formation of covalent ester linkages (–COO–) and the generation of a three-dimensional polymeric network. The stereochemical configuration of the β-D-glucopyranose units in CMC is represented according to the IUPAC recommendations for graphical depiction of stereochemical configuration [21]. The conformational representation of the glucose ring follows the acceptable drawing conventions [22]. This representation ensures accurate visualization of the β-configuration at C1 and the relative spatial orientation of substituents at C2 and C3, in accordance with internationally accepted stereochemical drawing standards. Preparation of zirconium leachate

The zirconium leachate was prepared from a highly pure zircon mineral composed primarily from ZrO2 and HfO2 (66.62 wt%) and SiO2 (32.53 wt%), and minor impurities such as Fe2O3, TiO2, MgO, CaO, A2O3, K2O, Na2O, and P2O5 [23]. Table 1 investigate the XRF of Zircon mineral. Zircon mineral (250 g) was mixed with 312 g of NaOH pellets and subjected to fusion at 650 °C in a stainless-steel crucible. The resulting fused mass consisted of water-soluble sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) and water-insoluble sodium zirconate (Na2ZrO3). After cooling, the fusion product was treated with hot distilled water in a volume four times its weight at 60 °C for 2 h to dissolve Na2SiO3 and partially hydrolyse Na2ZrO3 hydrated zirconium species. The undissolved solid fraction, consisting primarily of Na2ZrO3, was separated by hot filtration and subsequently dissolved in HCl at 90 °C for 2 h to obtain a zirconium-rich leachate. Finally, the solution was filtered to remove the residual impurities, including unreacted zircon, TiO2, Al2O3, and other trace oxides [5]. For further applications, the obtained zirconium solution was diluted to 1000 ppm to use as a stock solution.

Instruments

DAIHAN Scientific ultrasonic cleaner (model WUC-AOIH, 60 Hz, 230 V, Korea) was used to homogenize hydrogel. Samples were treated for 10 min at room temperature in three independent runs to ensure reproducibility. Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using the KBr pellet method on a Perkin-Elmer 1600 FTIR spectrophotometer. The measurement range was 400–4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. Each spectrum represents an average of 32 scans. Data were processed using PerkinElmer Spectrum software, including baseline correction and normalization. For post-sorption analysis, the Zr (IV)-loaded CPF hydrogel was dried and measured under identical conditions. Surface morphology and elemental composition were examined via Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) using an FEI Quanta FEG-250 system. Samples were dried, mounted on aluminium stubs, and sputter-coated with gold. SEM imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV under high vacuum, with a working distance of 10 mm. EDX spectra were obtained from three different areas per sample and analysed using EDAX software. Pore size analysis was performed on SEM images using ImageJ software (average pore diameter ~ 45 nm). The zirconium concentration in solutions was determined spectrophotometrically via the alizarin red method. Measurements were performed in triplicate, over a wavelength range of 400–600 nm, using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, model UV-1800). Data was processed for baseline correction and calibration using UV Probe software. All measurements were repeated at least three times, and mean values ± standard deviation are reported.

Error functions

The best-fitting isotherm model was determined by evaluating the error distribution between the experimental data and the isotherm predicted data that describes the interaction between CPF hydrogel and Zr (IV). The coefficient of determination (R²) and the non-linear Chi-square (χ²) test were used as quantitative error functions in this study, while the residual error was used as qualitative error function estimation. According to the error function definitions, the optimal adsorption isotherm model is identified by maximizing R² while minimizing χ², ensuring the best representation of the adsorption process. Thermodynamic studies

The thermodynamic analysis is essential for understanding the nature of the sorption process and enable determining whether it occurs spontaneously or not. Three key thermodynamic parameters were evaluated using the equations given below (Eqs. 17–20). Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG) indicates the spontaneity of the adsorption reaction. A negative ΔG value suggests a spontaneous process while a positive value implies non-spontaneity. Enthalpy Change (ΔH) represents the heat absorbed or released during the sorption process. A positive ΔH indicates an endothermic reaction while a negative ΔH suggests an exothermic process. Entropy Change (ΔS) reflects the degree of randomness at the solid-liquid interface. Results and discussion

Characterization of CPF Hydrogel

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectrum of the CPF hydrogel in Fig. 2a reveals key functional groups responsible for metal ion binding. A broad absorption band at about 3439 cm− 1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups from water and polymer chains [17]. The band at about 2926 cm− 1 is attributed to aliphatic C–H stretching, while a strong band at about 1631 cm− 1 is assigned to C = O stretching vibrations, characteristic of carboxyl groups. The FT-IR results confirm the involvement of –COOH and –OH groups in binding Zr (IV) ions. After Zr (IV) sorption, notable spectral changes were observed. The intensity of the O–H band decreased, suggesting hydrogen bonding or complexation with Zr (IV). The C = O vibration band at 1631 cm⁻¹ diminished and shifted slightly to 1625 cm⁻¹, indicating direct involvement of carboxyl groups in metal coordination. New bands emerged at 1326 cm⁻¹ and 1152 cm⁻¹, corresponding to C–O stretching vibrations and the formation of metal–carboxylate complexes (C–O–Zr). These assignments are consistent with literature reports [25, 26] for metal binding to carboxylate groups. Overlap with other functional group vibrations is possible, but the consistent shift and intensity change across multiple samples support Zr coordination. These findings are consistent with the proposed adsorption mechanism and further supported by isotherm analysis and EDX mapping.

SEM

SEM images of the CPF hydrogel before and after Zr (IV) sorption are presented in Fig. 2b. Prior to sorption, the hydrogel surface shows high porosity and an irregular morphology, with cracks and interconnected cavities, indicating a large surface area and abundant active sites favourable for metal ion adsorption. After Zr (IV) uptake, the surface becomes smoother and more compact, with visible agglomerates reflecting local accumulation of zirconium species, confirming successful sorption. The reduction in visible porosity is attributed to the occupation of internal pores and active sites by Zr (IV) ions, rather than classical pore blocking. This porous, interconnected structure provides multiple accessible pathways that facilitate ion diffusion into the hydrogel matrix, explaining the high adsorption capacity observed.

Sorption studies

Preliminary test

A comprehensive sorption experiment was conducted to evaluate the efficiency of the prepared CPF hydrogel beads in recovering Zr (IV) ions from real zircon leachate as shown in Table 4. In this test, 0.05 g of hydrogel beads was contacted with 15 mL of leachate at pH 1 and stirred for 24 h to reach equilibrium. Following filtration, both the initial and post-sorption solutions were analysed using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to determine elemental concentrations, sorption capacity (qe), and uptake percentage. The leachate was found to contain a high concentration of Zr (IV) ions (2617 ppm), along with moderate levels of Ti (IV) (138 ppm) and Hf (IV) (59 ppm). Trace amounts of Al (III), P(V), K(I), and Ce (IV) were also detected, ranging from 3 to 13 ppm. After equilibrium, the concentration of Zr (IV) was reduced to 1 ppm, corresponding to an uptake efficiency of approximately 100%, indicating near-complete removal by the CPF hydrogel. In contrast, the hydrogel showed minimal affinity for Al (III), P(V), K(I), and Ce (IV), with qe values of 0.16, 1.23, 9.49, and 0.27 ppm, respectively. These results demonstrate the high selectivity and sorption capacity of CPF hydrogel for Zr (IV) ions, with negligible interference from coexisting ions. The findings support its potential application as an effective and selective sorbent for zirconium recovery from complex leachate matrices. It is important to note that the prepared CPF hydrogel beads have no affinity to sorb Hf (IV) ions. The Hf (IV) ions recorded zero uptake on the surface of used hydrogel. Where it stood in the aqueous leachate solution, it was 59 ppm after equilibrium, indicating a strong affinity of the applied hydrogel for zirconium ions, which are completely removed. Ti (IV) showed that CPF had a moderate affinity. For Al (III), P(V), K(I), and Ce (IV) with no adsorption of Hf (IV). The closer inspection of these results prove that the prepared CPF hydrogel beads can be considered as a promising resin for separation of Zr (IV) ions from their matrix with a negligible depress in its efficiency due to the presence of interfering ions. The findings show that Zr (IV) could be sorbed using CPF hydrogel in situations where many cations do not interfere. However, Ti (IV) and Na(I) caused significant interferences. Ti (IV) could be separated via solvent extraction in succinate media with N-n-octylaniline [27]. Na(I) could be separated via the precipitation method using H2SO4. The proposed separation strategies for Ti (IV) and Na(I) are based on reported chemical behaviour and literature data under similar conditions. These approaches are suggested to highlight the potential applicability of the CPF hydrogel system; however, they were not experimentally evaluated in the present study. Future studies will focus on validating the separation performance of the hydrogel in multi-ion systems. These results indicate that CPF hydrogel could be an effective sorbent for zirconium recovery from acidic leachates.

To upgrade the performance of the prepared CPF hydrogel, a set of experimental investigations were performed to tune and optimize the working conditions of these conditions Solution pH, analyte Concentration, contact time sorbent dose and temperature were explored.

Solution pH.

Solution pH.

Solution pH.

The influence of pH on the sorption of Zr (IV) from Zircon leachate onto CPF hydrogel is depicted in Fig. 4a. The graph demonstrates that Zr (IV) sorption increases with rising pH. At low pH values, sorption capacity is limited due to the high concentration of H+ ions in solution [28] which compete with Zr (IV) ions for active binding sites on the CPF hydrogel. Moreover, under acidic conditions, the functional groups of the hydrogel may undergo protonation, diminishing their ability to effectively coordinate with Zr (IV) ions [29]. As the pH increases, the competition from H+ ions decreases, thereby enhancing Zr (IV) uptake. Figure 4b illustrates the chemical stability of CPF hydrogel in HCl solutions of varying molarities. The data confirm that the hydrogel maintains structural integrity down to pH 1. The increase in solubility with increasing acid concentration indicates partial dissolution of the hydrogel network, suggesting weakening or disruption of crosslinked structures. This structural change may lead to a reduction in available active sites and consequently affect adsorption performance. The species distribution of zirconium, modelled using Hydra/Medusa chemical equilibrium software, is presented in Fig. 4c [30]. The diagram reveals that in strongly acidic conditions (pH < 1), Zr (IV) exists as Zr4+, Zr (OH)3+, and Zr (OH)22+ species that exhibit strong affinity for sorption. As the pH rises above 1.0, hydrolysed species such as Zr3(OH)48+ and Zr3(OH)57+ emerge, which may interact differently with the functional groups of CPF hydrogel. Beyond pH 2.5, Zr (IV) tends to precipitate as Zr (OH)4, and precipitation becomes the dominant removal mechanism (Fig. 4d), thereby reducing the contribution of sorption to overall Zr recovery. To maximize adsorptive removal while minimizing precipitation, experiments were conducted at moderately acidic conditions (pH 1.0–1.4). Within this range, slight pH fluctuations are not expected to significantly affect adsorption performance, as Zr (IV) remains positively charged and available for coordination with functional groups of the hydrogel. However, larger pH changes may influence both metal speciation and hydrogel stability. Based on these findings, pH 1 was selected as the optimal condition for subsequent sorption studies. The strong dependence of adsorption on pH can be attributed to the protonation state of functional groups and the speciation of Zr (IV) ions, which directly influence the adsorption mechanism.

Regeneration studies

The desorption behaviour of Zr (IV) ions from CPF hydrogel was investigated using different eluents (0.25 M HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, and distilled water), as shown in Fig. 6a. Among these, H₂SO₄ exhibited the highest desorption efficiency, reaching 92% at 0.3 M (Fig. 6b). This superior performance can be attributed to the strong stability of Zr (IV)–sulphate complexes, which facilitates effective desorption. In comparison, HCl showed moderate desorption efficiency (78%), while HNO₃ was less effective (65%). Distilled water resulted in negligible desorption (< 5%), confirming that the adsorption process is not reversible by simple washing and is governed by strong interactions. The reusability of the CPF hydrogel was further evaluated over four consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles using 0.3 M H₂SO₄ (Fig. 6c). The adsorption capacity decreased gradually from 239.7 mg g⁻¹ to 186.5 mg g⁻¹ after four cycles, corresponding to approximately 78% retention of the initial capacity. This decline may be attributed to partial loss of active sites or slight structural changes during repeated regeneration. Despite this decrease, the CPF hydrogel maintained a high adsorption efficiency, demonstrating good stability and reusability for potential practical applications. The observed stability further supports the structural integrity of the hydrogel under acidic regeneration conditions.

Mechanism of the sorption reaction

The adsorption of Zr (IV) ions onto CPF hydrogel is governed by multiple mechanisms, including electrostatic attraction, ion exchange, and coordination interactions, while physical adsorption plays a minor role. These routes are explained in detail as:

Electrostatic attraction and coordination mechanism

This step represents the dominant adsorption mechanism, where strong chemical bonding occurs between Zr (IV) ions and the hydrogel matrix.

Broader impact and limitations

The development of CMC/PVA hydrogels crosslinked with fumaric acid presents a sustainable and environmentally benign approach for designing functional polymeric materials. The use of naturally derived CMC and a low-toxicity dicarboxylic acid crosslinker aligns with current trends toward green chemistry and biodegradable hydrogel systems. The covalent ester crosslinking strategy enhances structural integrity and mechanical stability compared to physically crosslinked systems, expanding potential applications in water remediation, controlled release systems, and biomedical fields. From an environmental perspective, the incorporation of fumaric acid as a bifunctional crosslinker provides a safer alternative to conventional toxic crosslinking agents such as glutaraldehyde. Additionally, the tunable network offers flexibility in tailoring swelling behavior and sorption performance, which may be advantageous for heavy metal ion removal and wastewater treatment technologies. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the ester crosslinks formed via thermal treatment may be susceptible to hydrolysis under extreme pH conditions, potentially affecting long-term stability. Second, large-scale fabrication and cost-efficiency were not evaluated in the present study. Future studies should therefore focus on durability assessment, biodegradation behaviour, and pilot-scale validation to fully establish the industrial applicability of the proposed hydrogel system.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel carboxymethyl cellulose-polyvinyl alcohol-fumaric acid (CPF) hydrogel was successfully synthesized via green crosslinking and applied in sorption of zirconium (IV) from zircon leachate. The prepared hydrogel exhibited a high sorption capacity due to abundant functional groups such as –COOH and –OH on its surface which facilitated complexation interactions with Zr (IV) ions. Kinetic modelling revealed that the sorption process followed the pseudo-nth-order model, indicating complex or multistep sorption as the dominant mechanism. Isotherm studies demonstrated that the D-R model provided the best fit with a mean free energy of 8.7 kJ mol− 1, confirming chemisorption and monolayer sorption behaviour on heterogeneous surface. The values of the thermodynamic parameters confirmed the spontaneity and endothermic nature of the process. The desorption experiments indicated that sulfuric acid was the most effective eluent, due to the stronger complexation of sulphate with Zr (IV) ions. These findings suggest that CPF hydrogel is a promising material for the selective extraction and recovery of zirconium from complex mixtures. Future studies could focus on optimizing the hydrogel formulation and extending its application to other metal separations.
