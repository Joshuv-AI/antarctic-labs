Programmable Spatial Demixing in Prebiotic Coacervates
Authors: Zhichen Xiong, Jie Wang, Zhang Liu, Yaxun Fan, Yilin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStratified vertical positioning of protocells offers evolutionary opportunities in paleo-oceans yet remains challenging achieve under prebiotic resource scarcity. Physical encounters among protocell populations are essential for molecular exchange and chemical complexity. Here, we demonstrate minimal structural variations of amphiphiles and small molecules—including isomers, alkyl-chain length, and counterion—dictate coacervate buoyancy, thereby governing vertical migration and stratification in water columns. Systematic parameter tuning produces coacervates residing in either upper or lower phases, while environmental cues like concentration and temperature dynamically modulate droplet positioning. Structural analysis reveals buoyancy arises from a sponge-like architecture composed of hydrated, flexible multilayers, with vertical positioning determined by hydration layer thickness and hydrophobic-to-hydrated layer ratio. Temperature-driven migration further induces collisions and fusion between coacervate populations, promoting molecular exchange and generating new substances. These findings suggest a physicochemical mechanism by which subtle molecular variations can encode protocell-like spatial organization, interaction frequency, and access to external energy in stratified aqueous environments.Subject terms: Origin of life, Origin of life, Self-assembly

Introduction

The origin of life from non-living chemistry and its subsequent dispersal across diverse environments remains one of the most profound questions in science. As the cradle of life, the paleo-ocean provides a primordial soup that may have harbored diverse protocells throughout evolution. A critical step toward biological complexity involves spatial organization1–4 and spontaneous motion5–7 of protocells. Spatial isolation provides opportunity for initially similar protocells to differentiate into diverse groups, and rhythmic movement of organisms provides possibility to obtain energy and resources, or to encounter other species mediate essential matter exchanges and produce new chemical substances8–11. Just as the striking divergence of modern marine ecosystems display: phototrophic communities thriving in sunlit shallow waters differ drastically from chemotrophic life forms inhabiting the dark, serene depths of ocean12–16. However, in a prebiotic milieu composed of simple components with scarce resources, merely achieving differences in spatial distribution among protocells is quite difficult. While such biogeographic patterns are generally attributed to prolonged evolutionary adaptation, elemental questions remain largely unexplored: in the limited repository of simple organic molecules on the primordial Earth, are finely subtle molecular structural differences (such as isomerism) sufficient to drive macroscopic spatial sorting, and what exactly triggers these primitive cells to acquire their initial ability to move?

Coacervates formed through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) have been compelling membraneless protocell models, offering a versatile platform for probing the origin of life17–22. Coacervate droplets spontaneously assemble, compartmentalize biomolecules, and promote internal chemical reactions, thereby mimicking essential features of a prebiotic environment23–27. In order to reproduce the differences in the spatial distribution of droplets and to demonstrate their movement capabilities, current studies have primarily focused on external stimuli, including light28,29, temperature30, electric/magnetic fields31–33, or embedded catalysts34,35, to manipulate droplet motion, but neglecting potential influence of intrinsic delicate structures of molecules. If subtle structural variations in molecular building blocks can indeed dictate distinct phase-separation behaviors and migratory trajectories in coacervates, such findings would offer transformative insights into the spatial distribution of primordial droplets across shallow and deep marine environments. This approach could further illuminate how early evolutionary processes selected specific amphiphiles and small molecules, which are driven by their capacity to enable spatially programmed protocell behaviors.

The present work aims to uncover the intrinsic mechanism governing the spatial positioning of protocells and demonstrate how their fate—whether they settle in sunlit shallow waters or migrate into deep-sea environments—can be predetermined via simple “molecular coding”. Although fatty acids and alkyl phosphates are generally considered prebiotically plausible constituents of early boundary structures, their strong pH dependence can easily confound the mechanistic interpretations of assembly and migration behaviors. Therefore, to isolate the variables of interest, we designed a prebiotically inspired physicochemical model system using simplified analogues. Considering the critical role of amphiphiles in early compartments and supported by prebiotic evidence of alkyl-chain amphiphiles detected in meteorites36–39, we selected alkyltrimethylammonium bromides (CnTAB, n = 10, 12, 14, 16) as building blocks for coacervate assembly. Drawing inspiration from the adaptive strategy of deep-sea organisms that accumulate small organic molecules as osmolytes40–43, we incorporate a series of biologically relevant methyl salicylate (n-MSA) isomers in coacervates. While CnTAB and n-MSA are not authentic prebiotic molecules, their structural simplicity—consisting of basic alkyl chains, benzene rings, and minimal functional groups—aligns well with the expected chemical landscape of early Earth. Crucially, because CₙTAB is insensitive to pH and the ionization state of n-MSA remains stable, they provide a robust and tunable platform to probe how general physicochemical principles collectively regulate phase separation and buoyancy. Through a systematic investigation of the phase behavior in n-MSA/CnTAB isomer mixtures, we observe an aggregation transition from micelles to coacervates driven by the synergistic interplay of electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic interactions, and hydrogen bonding between the two components. Furthermore, the multilayered tubular microstructures with tunable interlayer spacing form within the coacervates directly govern their buoyancy. Our findings reveal that the alkyl-chain length of amphiphiles and small-molecule isomerism can encode phase behavior, microstructure, and migratory capacity of coacervates, enabling fuel-free, buoyancy-driven vertical movement and spatial stratification. Furthermore, by programming spatial distribution, the coacervates located at the bottom of the water column can rise and obtain sufficient light energy by modulating concentration, temperature, and counterions. They can then, either spontaneously or by integrating with other coacervates, complete material exchange and generate new species (Fig. 1). Collectively, our prebiotically-inspired model system demonstrates how the delicate differences in small molecules lead to variations in spatial distribution of coacervates, and how these differences can be easily triggered to undergo active migration movements, ultimately resulting in new substance formation under the input of external energy.

Coacervates droplets are constructed by methylsalicylic acid isomers (n-MSA) and alkyl trimethyl ammonium bromides in different alkyl chain length (CnTAB). By manipulating the molecular regulation of building blocks (isomerization, alkyl-chain length, and counterions) and changes in environmental factors (concentration and temperature), it is possible to artificially control the spatial location of coacervates. Meanwhile, the active migration of spatial positions can enable coacervate droplets to be directly exposed to light, or fusion, achieving serial reactions.

Results

Construction and spatial regulation of n-MSA/CnTAB coacervates

We create model prebiotic environments by forming coacervate droplets from populations of structurally similar small molecules, mirroring the simple and limited organic diversity thought to exist on primordial Earth. Inspired by similar amphiphiles that have been found in meteorites and natural osmolytes are used to stabilize deep-sea organisms, amphiphilic alkyl trimethyl ammonium bromides (CnTAB, n = 10, 12, 14, 16) and isomers of methylsalicylic acid (3-MSA, 4-MSA, and 5-MSA) are selected as key building blocks to form coacervates. Notably, the specific isomer of MSA determines whether the resulting coacervate droplets float or sink in solution. Under identical conditions, coacervates made with 3-MSA sink, while those made with its isomers, 4-MSA and 5-MSA, float (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Spherical droplets in these dispersed coacervate solutions exhibit sponge-like internal structures and distribute from 4~80 μm in diameter (Fig. 2b, c). Meanwhile, the alkyl-chain length of CnTAB affects the locations of the dense coacervate phase (Fig. 2d), moving from bottom to top with elongating chain length. Inorganic chaotropic anions also modulate the location of coacervate phase, broadly following the Hofmeister series, that smaller-sized counterions usually cause coacervate phase to remain in lower layer (OH-, F- and Cl-), while the introduction of larger-sized counterions leads to upper layer (NO3-, Br-, I-, H2PO4-, SCN-, CO32-, SO42-, Fig. 2e). Furthermore, environmental conditions, like concentration and temperature, can trigger dynamic changes. Increasing 3-MSA concentration reverses the position of the coacervate phase from top to bottom (Fig. 2f). More strikingly, as the temperature changes between low and high, the coacervate phase migrates between the bottom and top of the solution. The critical high temperature for this reversible transition is unique for each isomer, approximately 25 oC for 3-MSA, 18 °C for 4-MSA, and 12 °C for 5-MSA (Fig. 2 g and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2).

These findings demonstrate that subtle molecular structural variations and environmental perturbations can synergistically drive macroscopic spatial segregation. Specifically, under multifactorial conditions, buoyancy is governed by a hierarchical regulation mechanism: molecular composition inherently dictates the baseline state and intrinsic density of the coacervate phase, whereas continuous environmental factors act as external modulators to further propel or amplify its vertical migration (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, these coacervates can encapsulate various fluorescent molecules with different hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity (rhodamine 6G, methylene blue, fluorescein, or nile red) as well as biological macromolecules (FITC-dextran, Alexa488-labeled ssDNA and green fluorescent protein, GFP, Fig. 2h). This ability to enrich diverse molecules suggests that these compartments could provide a favorable microenvironment for molecular exchange and new substance generation.

Physicochemical properties of n-MSA/C12TAB coacervates

To investigate the essence by which subtle differences in molecular structure led to distinct spatial distributions in aqueous solution, a systematic characterization of the coacervate properties is conducted. By monitoring the turbidity variations of n-MSA/C12TAB mixtures and identifying the inflection points associated with phase transitions (Supplementary Fig. 3), we determine the phase boundaries for n-MSA/C12TAB mixed solution. All three systems exhibit a broad two-phase region (Fig. 3a). Meanwhile, phase boundaries of CnTAB with varying alkyl chain length (n = 8, 10, 14, and 16) in the presence of n-MSA are also systematically investigated (Supplementary Figs. 4–7). The results reveal that the coacervate region progressively narrows as the alkyl chain length increases; notably, the n-MSA/C8TAB system fails to form a coacervate phase altogether. These results indicate that coacervate formation arises from the combined effects of sufficiently strong hydrophobic interactions and favorable molecular packing. To achieve an optimal balance between assembly capability and aqueous solubility, we selecte the n-MSA/C12TAB system for its relatively broad coacervation window, which is the primary focus of this study. Microdroplets are observed within the coacervation regions of all systems (Supplementary Fig. 8), and these structures remain stable over a wide range of ionic strengths (0-500 mM NaCl, Supplementary Fig. 9) and pH values (pH 5-12, Supplementary Fig. 10). Analysis of droplet size distributions (Cn-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) reveals multimodal characteristics across all n-MSA/C12TAB systems (Fig. 3b). The broad size distribution of coacervate droplets with an average diameter of ~30 μm, indicates the multi-dispersity of the flexible droplets44,45. Zeta potential measurements indicate a weakly negative charge of the droplets, confirming that excessive n-MSA is essential for coacervation. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles show closely matched characteristic peak positions at the scattering vector q for the three systems—1.79 nm−1 for 3-MSA/C12TAB, 1.73 nm−1 for 4-MSA/C12TAB, and 1.72 nm−1 for 5-MSA/C12TAB—suggesting similar internal nanostructures (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 1). The structural similarity is further corroborated by the highly consistent fluorescence recovery kinetics of Rhodamine 6G (R6G)-loaded coacervates (Fig. 3d). The apparent diffusion coefficients (Dapp) calculated from fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments are 6.47 × 10−14 m2·s−1 for 3-MSA/C12TAB (region of interest radius r = 3.48 μm), 6.56 × 10−14 m2·s−1 for 4-MSA/C12TAB (r = 4.00 μm), and 6.78 × 10−14 m2·s−1 for 5-MSA/C12TAB (r = 3.26 μm)46, indicating comparable molecular mobility in coacervate phase (Fig. 3e). Consistently, FRAP measurements further confirm the liquid-like nature of coacervates formed by amphiphiles with different alkyl chain lengths (CnTAB, n = 10, 12, 14, 16) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Notably, even after complete phase separation, the dense coacervate phase retains a substantial amount of free water (approximately 80%), further supporting its non-solid, fluid-like character (Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, the molar ratio of C12TAB to n-MSA determined by ¹H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14) is approximately 1:1.2, indicating that the two components co-assemble efficiently in a near-stoichiometric manner to form the coacervate.

Using a real-time density monitoring system, we synchronously measure the densities of dense and dilute phases throughout the liquid-liquid phase separation process. Regardless of n-MSA isomer, the density of dense phase decreases with increasing temperature, whereas the density of dilute phase shows an increasing trend (Fig. 3f). This opposing behavior leads to a gradual reduction in the density difference between the two phases upon heating, ultimately resulting in density inversion at a specific critical temperature (Tc), which induces an abrupt change in the spatial position of dense phase. Notably, the subtle molecular structural variation among the n-MSA isomers dictates distinct critical temperatures, following the order of Tc (5-MSA) < Tc (4-MSA) < Tc (3-MSA), which thermodynamically explains their differential droplet spatial distributions at a fixed temperature. Similarly, increasing alkyl-chain length of CnTAB (Fig. 3g), decreasing C3-MSA (Fig. 3h), and adding chaotropic anions (Supplementary Fig. 15) induce analogous trends in the phase densities. Exceeding the respective critical values for these parameters causes the bottom coacervates to buoyantly rise to the surface.

The existence of a critical temperature not only serves as a predictive indicator for phase behavior transition but also corresponds, according to Stokes sedimentation theory47 (see Experimental Methods), to maximum stability for the coacervate system. By precisely tuning the environmental temperature and n-MSA concentration, we systematically monitor the stability duration of n-MSA/C12TAB coacervates, and find that there is an optimal temperature or 3-MSA concentration that markedly enhances the coacervate stability. Specifically, the optimal stability temperatures are determined to be 21.5 °C for 3-MSA/C12TAB, 17.5 °C for 4-MSA/C12TAB, and 10.0 °C for 5-MSA/C12TAB coacervates (Fig. 3i), while the critical 3-MSA concentration for 3-MSA/C12TAB system is found to be 62.2 mM (Fig. 3j). Under these optimized conditions, the coacervates remain stable for nearly 24 hours, however, the stability decreases precipitously with deviations as small as 1.0 °C in temperature or 0.1 mM in concentration. It demonstrates that despite minimal molecular structural differences, phase separation can be controlled through precise adjustment of environmental parameters. Such spatially dispersed coacervates may enable primordial biomolecular communities to adapt to diverse environments, facilitating intercompartmental molecular exchange and promoting specific chemical reactions, reminiscent of dispersed biomolecular condensates in modern cells.

Molecular insights into dynamic vertical migration of coacervates

Given the tunable spatial positioning of coacervate dense and dilute phases by multiple stimuli, we aim to elucidate the underlying structural changes driven by these internal and external factors. Based on the hierarchical assembly of surfactant molecules during coacervation, we hypothesize that the droplets possess flexible, compressible, aquifer-like architectures capable of dynamically adapting to changes in molecular isomerization, temperature, alkyl-chain length, small-molecule concentration, and ionic environment. In support of this hypothesis, cryo-TEM imaging reveals detailed microstructural features of the dense phase in 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate intuitively (Fig. 4a). Irregularly shaped fragments (~2.5 μm in length and ~0.8 μm in width) are captured on the copper grid, and porous structures of the sponge-like morphology are clearly visible (Fig. 4a1, a2 and Supplementary Fig. 16). These pores may underpin the high-water content of the dense phase and likely mediate water exchange between the dense and dilute phases. Higher-magnification images reveal a flexible layered architecture, where stacked layers form “tube walls” exhibiting negative curvature and an interlayer spacing of ~3.42 nm (Fig. 4a3, a4). The cryo-SEM images of the dense phase of 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate reveal a more extensive compact network structure similar to those in Fig. 2b, which confirms the consistent structures between the coacervate microdroplets and the fused dense phase of coacervate, while the cross-sectional profile of the network skeleton further verifies the presence of stacked layers (Supplementary Fig. 17). The formation of continuous sponge-like networks with negative curvature can be fundamentally rationalized by surfactant self-assembly principles using the molecular packing parameter, p = v / a · l, where a is the effective headgroup area, and v and l are the volume and length of the hydrophobic tail, respectively. Herein, upon the addition of n-MSA, the electrostatic repulsion between the charged headgroups of C12TAB is effectively screened, reducing the effective headgroup area (a), while the relative volume of the hydrophobic moiety (v) increases. This synergistic shift in molecular geometry thermodynamically drives the formation of bilayers with negative Gaussian curvature, which subsequently interconnect into the observed macroscopic sponge-like microstructures within both the dispersed microdroplets and the bulk dense phase.

In parallel, the SAXS profiles display a single broad peak in the q-range of 0.4-4.0 nm-1, with maximum intensity near qmax ≈ 1.75 nm-1 (Fig. 4b). Across different coacervate compositions, the scattering intensity decays as I(q) ∼ q-2, consistent with the local bilayer organization, a hallmark of lipid sponge phases. As the 3-MSA concentration increases in the coalesced phase, the SAXS peak shifts to higher q values, from 1.746 nm-1 at 30 mM to 1.839 nm-1 at 70 mM (Fig. 4c). Correspondingly, the characteristic length scale (d = 2π/qmax), reflecting the average interlayer spacing within the sponge-like framework, decreases from 3.599 nm to 3.417 nm. This compression indicates a tightening of the spongy skeleton with increasing C3-MSA, which reduces internal water content, raises density, and ultimately leads to sedimentation of dense phase. This structural response is consistently observed under other modulating conditions, including variations among n-MSA isomers and temperature changes from 4 to 25 °C (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 18). Specifically, shifting the methyl group position from meta to ortho on n-MSA or raising temperature enlarges the interlayer spacing, correlating with upward migration of the dense phase. These trends, together with the results from shortening CnTAB alkyl chain (Supplementary Fig. 19) and adding kosmotropic anions (Supplementary Fig. 20), reach a general rule, the factors that reduce the compactness of sponge-like skeleton then promote coacervate to move up, while those enhancing skeleton compression induce coacervate to move down.

The water content within the dense phase is another primary determinant of density; thus, LF-NMR was used to analyze the hydration in the coacervate skeleton (Fig. 4e). Shorter transverse relaxation times (T₂) reflect restricted water mobility due to hydration or confinement. Typically, T₂ values between 1–100 ms indicate bound water, while the values > 100 ms correspond to free, mobile water48–50. The bound-to-free water ratio analysis (Fig. 4f) shows that bound water content first increases sharply and then declines as C3-MSA rises, varying from 13.7% to 2.2%. This trend is attributed to the increased availability of hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on 3-MSA, which provide additional hydration sites. As more 3-MSA incorporates into the coacervate, the dense phase initially retains more bound water. However, further addition of 3-MSA leads to excess molecules in the aqueous phase, forming strong hydrogen bonds with bilayer-incorporated 3-MSA, narrowing the interlayer spacing and expelling bound water. Consequently, the skeleton density increases, raising overall coacervate density and inducing sedimentation. Accompanied by these changes, ATR spectroscopy reveals strengthening hydrogen bonding and reduced tetrahedral order of water in the hydration shells of the coacervate, evidenced by an increasing integrated area and a blue shift (~3450 cm⁻¹ to ~3480 cm⁻¹) of the O–H vibration band at higher C3-MSA (Fig. 4h, i)51–53. The zeta potential decreased from about –2.25 mV to –11.3 mV (Supplementary Fig. 21) further confirms the incorporation of more 3-MSA into coacervates.

Similarly, the increases in interlayer spacing (d-spacing) induced by the changes in n-MSA isomerization or elevated temperature are accompanied by elevated bound water content (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 22), albeit through distinct mechanisms. For n-MSA isomers, the ortho-positioned methyl group relative to the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups promotes the insertion of hydrophobic moieties (methyl group and benzene ring) into the palisade layer, while the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups become more exposed near the headgroups of amphiphiles. This configuration enhances the water-binding capacity via hydrogen bonding. Under experimental pH condition, the carboxyl groups are fully ionized, leaving hydroxyl groups as the primary hydrogen-bonding sites. Consequently, 5-MSA, in which the hydroxyl group is positioned opposite to the methyl group, exhibits the strongest water-binding capacity, thereby reducing coacervate density and promoting upward migration. In contrast, the temperature increase enhances the thermal motion of water molecules. While this enhanced thermal motion may slightly weaken individual hydrogen bonds, the heightened kinetic energy may loosen the headgroup packing of amphiphiles, exposing additional hydration sites and increasing bound water content. Moreover, thermal expansion of the hydration layer may create more space for free water penetration. Thus, the temperature increase lowers the density of the dense phase.

Regarding the ion effect, coacervate vertical migration is primarily governed by anion charge density and ionic radius. Chaotropic anions (e.g., NO3⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, H2PO4⁻, SCN⁻, CO32-, SO42-) competitively absorb at the amphiphile headgroups, partially displacing native counterions. This effect reduces electrostatic screening among the headgroups, expanding interlayer spacing (Supplementary Fig. 20). Additionally, the relatively large ionic radii of these chaotropic anions disrupt the headgroup hydration shell, diminishing interfacial water ordering. This weakens the bonds between free water molecules and sticks out the proportion of bound water packaging in the skeleton (Supplementary Fig. 23). A corresponding regularity also shown in functional group variation in ATR spectrum, exhibiting the strengthening of the O-H stretch-vibration between 3-MSA and water molecules (Supplementary Fig. 24). In summary, the results above indicate multiple factors—including isomer variation, chaotropic anions, temperature increase, and excess 3-MSA—promote coacervate flotation by enhancing the headgroup hydration or enlarging the interlayer spacing via strengthened electrostatic repulsion and hydration layer loosening.

Conversely, the effect of the amphiphile alkyl-chain length on the coacervate migration appears largely independent of hydration. The bound water content in the 3-MSA/CnTAB coacervates remains nearly constant at ~13.8%, varying by less than 1.0% across the four systems (Supplementary Fig. 25). Moreover, the ATR spectra only show subtle changes in the O–H vibration peak position and intensity (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 26). However, the intensity of the C–H stretching vibration near 2900 cm⁻¹ increases with the CnTAB chain length, suggesting that tighter alkyl-chain packing, driven by enhanced hydrophobic interaction and coupled with the incorporation of lower-density long alkyl chains, plays a more dominant role in regulating coacervate density and spatial distribution than hydration effect.

Coarse-grained molecular dynamic simulations of n-MSA/CnTAB coacervates

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the differential behavior of coacervates formed by n-MSA isomers, we perform coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of 3-MSA/C12TAB and 5-MSA/C12TAB systems. These simulations track molecular arrangements over a 1 μs timeframe, with final-state configurations analyzed to characterize interactions and hydration patterns (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 27). The distinct methyl group positions—ortho in 3-MSA and meta in 5-MSA relative to the hydroxyl group—lead to markedly different binding modes with the C12TAB headgroups. In 3-MSA, steric hindrance from the adjacent methyl, hydroxyl, and carboxyl groups prevents deep insertion into the C12TAB palisade layer, leading to a loose association near the amphiphile headgroups. In contrast, 5-MSA exhibits a clear spatial separation between the hydrophilic and hydrophobic moieties, enabling near-vertical insertion into the palisade layer and stronger electrostatic interaction with the C12TAB headgroups.

Spatial distribution analyses along the bilayer normal (x-axis, binned at 1 Å) reveal that the C12TAB headgroups, n-MSA molecules, and counterions (Br⁻ and Na⁺) are confined in ± 15 Å of the bilayer center (Fig. 5c), while water molecules distribute more broadly with increasing steadily up to ~30 Å before plateauing, indicating the accumulation of bound water with distance from the interface. Quantification within the 0–20 Å region shows fewer 5-MSA molecules than 3-MSA (Fig. 5d), consistent with the deeper 5-MSA insertion and tighter packing with C12TAB. This tighter binding reduces the number of 5-MSA molecules near headgroups, increasing the availability of C12TAB positive ammonium charges for the association with Br⁻ counterion. Consequently, the 5-MSA system exhibits higher counterion binding and local water concentration in headgroup region, reducing the density of spongy framework and promoting the coacervate to move up.

On the other hand, when the alkyl chain length of the surfactant increases (e.g., from C10TAB to C12TAB) at a fixed 3-MSA concentration (Supplementary Fig. 28 and Supplementary Table 2), molecular dynamics simulations reveal only a marginal change in the volume of the hydrated phase (12.4 → 12.9). In contrast, the volume of the hydrophobic phase expands markedly (33.5 → 38.6). Consequently, the volume ratio of the hydrophobic to hydrated phase rises significantly (2.70 → 2.99), accompanied by an increase in the free volume within the hydrophobic domains (31.7 → 32.5). These quantitative metrics indicate that longer alkyl chains lead to looser molecular packing within the hydrophobic domains of the coacervates, effectively reducing the overall packing efficiency and mass density. This is consistent with the macroscopic observation that n-MSA/CnTAB coacervates with longer alkyl chains are more prone to buoyantly floating. In other words, buoyancy regulation by alkyl chain length operates largely independent of hydration effects; rather, it is primarily governed by hydrophobically driven loose chain packing and the inherent incorporation of low-density long alkyl segments.

The simulation results align with the experimental SAXS, LF-NMR, and ATR data. Thus, we propose a molecular mechanism for the observed phase behaviors (Fig. 5e): in 3-MSA/C12TAB system, the group steric hindrance leads to loosen molecular packing near the headgroups, disrupt water-hydrogen bonding, reduce bound water content, decrease interlayer spacing, and thus increase the coacervate density; in 5-MSA/C12TAB system, deeper insertion of molecules enhances the headgroup hydration, strengthens water hydrogen-bonding networks, increases interfacial water volume, and thus lowers the coacervate density. As a result, the coacervate droplets move down or move up, respectively.

Fusion event and chemical reaction between different coacervate species

Having established coacervates capable of vertical migration in response to various stimulus, we further explore whether substances originally sequestered in bottom-phase coacervates could relocate upward upon environmental changes, thereby gaining access to ultraviolet (UV) light and undergoing photochemical transformations. In early Earth, temperature gradients and fluctuations were likely widespread, particularly in settings such as hydrothermal vents, geothermal pools, and shallow or tidal waters subject to diurnal cycles. Although the specific temperature range required for triggering migration may not be universally accessible, and magnitude and reversibility of this response depend on system composition, temperature can serve as a convenient and well-controlled model stimulus to probe the underlying mechanism and acts as a proof-of-concept for deep-surface cycling coupled with mass transport. The primitive cells in the primordial ocean may migrate from bottom to top, influenced by the diurnal cycle of sun to carry out a series of biochemical reactions upon light (Fig. 6a). Spiropyran, a photoresponsive molecule, cleaves its C–O bond at the spiro junction under UV irradiation (365 nm), converting into a highly polar, open-ring merocyanine form54,55. When spiropyran is encapsulated (0.2 g/L) in 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervates—initially situated at the bottom and shielded from UV—no photoreaction occurs, and the dense phase remains light yellow (Fig. 6b). However, upon raising temperature above 37 °C, the coacervates migrate upward (Supplementary Movie 3). Once reaching surface and exposed to UV light, spiropyran undergoes ring-opening isomerization, resulting in a distinct color change from light yellow to rose red due to merocyanine formation. Given the intrinsic ability of coacervates to sequester diverse molecules, the coacervate dense phases of different densities may merge and exchange contents under environmental cues, potentially enabling more complex reactions. To test this, we selectively encapsulate Rhodamine 6G (R6G) in an upper-layer coacervate and Methylene Blue (MB) in a bottom-layer coacervate. At 37 °C, the MB-loaded bottom coacervate (blue) deforms and ascends, and then upon contact with the R6G-loaded upper coacervate (red), the coacervate continues to move upward due to reduced density and eventually the two different coacervates fuses completely, yielding a dark blue mixture that confirms the content exchange of the coacervates (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Movie 4).

a A hypothetical model of buoyancy-driven vertical movements and fusion actions in different protocell populations stimulated by the change of external environmental condition and internal composition, facilitating diverse populations of protocells to encounter, fuse with each other and produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to UV harvest. b Photochromism of spiropyran open-ring isomerization conducted by temperature-regulated vertical migration of 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate (C3-MSA = 70 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM). c Optical images of migration and fusion of bottom phase (blue, C3-MSA = 70 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) and upper phase (red, C5-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) over time by heating from 25 oC to 37 oC. d Photocatalysis reaction triggered by migration and fusion of the bottom dense phase and the upper dense phase. Prussian blue (0.1 mM) and P25 (0.2 g/L) are sequestrated in the bottom and upper dense phases, respectively. e CIE chromaticity diagram portrayed by color recording at the time of sampling along the direction of the ROS harvest reaction.

As a proof-of-concept, we test if a photocatalyst encapsulated in one coacervate transfers reactive species (e.g., radicals) to another upon fusion, could a photocatalytic reaction be initiated inside the combined coacervate droplets? Consequently, commercial TiO₂ (P25, 0.2 g/L) is dispersed into an upper-layer 5-MSA/C12TAB coacervate, and Prussian blue—a ROS-sensitive photocatalytic indicator—into a lower-layer 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate56,57. Upon heating to 37 °C, the Prussian blue-containing coacervate droplets rise and reach the surface in 300 s. After encountering and fusing with the TiO₂-bearing upper coacervates, a photocatalytic reaction is initiated under UV light (λ = 365 nm) with high-speed stirring (mimicking oceanic currents). The solution color shifts gradually from sky-blue to dark green over 150 min, indicating Prussian blue is oxidized to Berlin green via TiO₂-generated ROS58–62. This color transition is tracked colorimetrically (Fig. 6d) and quantified via a CIE chromaticity diagram (Fig. 6e), showing that spontaneous coacervate migration and fusion provide a plausible pathway for protocell populations in prebiotic oceans to interact and exchange materials, and evolve through coupled chemical reactions.

Discussion

Here, we have demonstrated that minimal structural variations in molecular building blocks—such as isomerism, alkyl chain length, and counterion identity—can be transduced into macroscopic spatial segregation of coacervate protocells through precise modulation of their buoyancy. By adjusting temperature, these protocell communities can controllably float or sink, offering opportunities to encounter each other and produce new substances under the input of light energy. It establishes a flexible “molecular coding” strategy for programming the vertical positioning and migration of membraneless compartments in water columns. The underlying mechanism hinges on the tunable hydration and compression of an amphiphilic-bilayer-based aqueous sponge-like microstructure, where the factors that increase interlayer spacing and bound water content lower the dense phase density, prompting upward migration. This discovery shed light on a bottom-up physicochemical framework for understanding the diversification of early life, the initial partitioning of life in the nutrient-scarce primordial ocean might not be merely a result of chaotic flow but rather an active selection process driven by physicochemical laws. The density-dependent segregation-motion mechanism likely facilitates the emergence of pre-adapted protocell populations in diverse shallow and deep-sea environments, laying the groundwork for subsequent divergent evolution.

Looking forward, programmable coacervate behavior through molecular design opens several prospects. First, it provides an example for constructing advanced protocell models with life-like motility and environmental responsiveness, moving beyond static compartmentalization. Second, the interplay between molecular structure, microstructure, and buoyancy could inform the design of “smart” soft materials for targeted delivery or as sensors, where spatial positioning is crucial. Finally, integrating different regulatory cues—perhaps even incorporating primitive biochemical feedback loops—lead to systems exhibiting more complex, adaptive behaviors, bridging the gap between passive physicochemical laws and the dynamic homeostasis characteristic of living systems. This work thus not only deepens our understanding of how life might have organized itself spatially at its inception but also provides a versatile toolkit for engineering functional soft matter from the bottom up.

It is important to note that the present study is based on the specific n-MSA/CnTAB system, which is not prebiotically realistic and limits immediate generalization while advantageous for mechanistic elucidation across multiple variables. However, the preliminary results have revealed that the observed phenomena and underlying mechanisms are expected to be broadly applicable to other amphiphilic systems of potential prebiotic relevance, and the range of coacervate chemistries needs to be extended to establish more general and appropriate design principles for systems capable of deep-surface cycling and mass transport in prebiotic environments. Additionally, the inherent tendency of coacervates to undergo fusion constrains their use in high-resolution studies of individual and collective migration. Addressing this limitation needs to involve integrating features of vesicles and oil droplets—for example, through the development of membrane-bound coacervates that combine tunable density and molecular enrichment with enhanced structural stability—thereby enabling more precise tracking and control. Overall, by establishing a mechanism-oriented framework centered on tunable hydration and microstructural properties, this work lays a generalizable foundation for understanding and engineering coacervate migration across diverse molecular systems.

Methods

Phase boundary

The phase boundaries of the condensed phase can be identified by observing the differential turbidity curves of C12TAB (0-100 mM) and 0-50 mM n-MSA (n = 3, 4, 5) mixtures. Turbidity variations were assessed using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-1780, Shimadzu, Japan) at λ = 450 nm, with each sample thoroughly dispersed via vortexing (Vortex Genie2, SI, USA) for 10 seconds at 2000 rpm/min prior to testing. All experiments were conducted at 25 ± 0.1 °C, and each sample underwent a minimum of three repetitions in a quartz cuvette with an optical path length of 10 mm.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The images of 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate droplets (C3-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) were collected by CLSM (FV4000-IX81, Olympus Ltd, Japan). 40 μM R6G were added in the coacervate mixtures as fluorophores (excitation: λ = 559 nm, HeNe laser). The types, dosages and excitation wavelengths of the other dye molecules and biological macromolecular markers added are as follows: fluorescein (40 μM, excitation: λ = 488 nm); nile red (40 μM, excitation: λ = 488 nm); FITC-dextran (50 mg/L, excitation: λ = 488 nm); Alexa488-ssDNA (50 nM, excitation: λ = 488 nm); GFP (0.1 μM, excitation: λ = 528 nm). The brightness profile lines were processed using the cellSens software.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

FRAP experiments were conducted on CLSM (FV3000-IX83, Olympus Ltd, Japan) by exposing the region of interest (ROI) to a 100% power laser beam (λ = 488 nm) while photobleaching. Three photos were captured before bleaching, and a 2 s LSM simulation was applied immediately after pre-bleaching process. Time sequence of fluorescence images was collected under free run mode, which has a 3.22 s interval in the recovery process (the image scanning speed is 200.0 μs/pixel). Each independent FRAP experiment involves droplets of uniform shape and approximately the same size, with a sample quantity of 5 on the same focal plane.

Zeta Potential and droplet size determination

The zeta potential of n-MSA/C12TAB (Cn-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) were measured by a NanoSizer (Nano ZS90, Malvern, U.K.) equipped with a 4 mW He-Ne laser (λ = 633 nm). The sample was introduced into a plastic capillary cuvette (DTS1070) with copper electrodes via a 1 mL injector. The scattering angle was maintained at 13 °, and the temperature was regulated by a thermostat at 25.0 ± 0.1 °C. Coacervate droplet size distribution was determined using Nano Measurer software (Version V1.2), with 200 droplets analyzed as a statistical sample. Initial images were acquired using CLSM in bright field mode.

Cryogenic scanning electron microscopy (Cryo-SEM)

The 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate dispersion (C3-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM, pH 7.5) was placed between two gold planchettes and then rapidly frozen by immersion in a liquid nitrogen slush. The frozen samples were subsequently transferred into a cryo-preparation chamber (Leica EM ACE600, Leica, Germany) under vacuum, where they underwent freeze-fracturing and sublimation at −100 °C for approximately 15 minutes. Following this, the sample surfaces were coated with gold to enhance conductivity in an argon atmosphere (10 mA for 60 seconds). The samples were then transferred to a cryo-stage at −137 °C within the microscope (S-4300, HITACHI Ltd, Japan), and images were captured using a landing energy of 10.0 kV and an electric current of 10 µA.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM)

5 μL of dense phase of 3-MSA/C12TAB coacervate (C3-MSA = 50 mM, CC12TAB = 20 mM) was loaded onto a carbon-coated TEM grid (300 meshes) and blotted with filter paper for 10 seconds. Subsequently, the grid was swiftly immersed into a reservoir of liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen, reaching a temperature of −179 °C. The frozen sample was stored in liquid nitrogen until it was transferred to a cryogenic sample holder and examined using Cryo-TEM (Themis-300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating at 200 kV, at a temperature of approximately −179 °C.

Density measurement of dense and dilute phases

All tested coacervation samples (30 mL) were prepared in 50 mL centrifuge tubes and then incubated in a metal bath (DC10, Ruicheng, China) for 72 h at various temperatures (4, 12, 18, 25 °C). A high-precision balance (XPE2005, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) with an accuracy of 1/100000 was used to measure the weight of the dense or dilute phase, along with the pipette tip. Ambient temperature was controlled to match the desired temperature for each measurement. The pipette tip and beaker were tared and zeroed before each measurement. Additionally, all experiments were repeated at least three times.

Stability of coacervate solutions

The period starting from the formation of coacervate droplets until notable sedimentation (either rising upwards or sinking downwards) appears is defined as their stability duration. Small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

In-situ small-angle X-ray scattering experiments were conducted on the SAXS/WAXS beamline at SAXSess mc2, Anton Paar, Austria, utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1542 nm, Germany). Dense phases of coacervates with various compositions were collected in 500 μL centrifuge tubes and incubated under relative testing temperatures (4, 12, 18, 25 °C). A volume of 20 μL of dense phase was loaded into a stainless-steel sample holder with a quartz capillary slit (internal width: 1.5 mm). The sample chamber was hermetically sealed and inserted into a temperature controller (0 to 200 °C), with each sample equilibrated at the respective test temperature for 300 seconds prior to scanning. Scattering patterns were captured using X-ray energy of 40 keV, with the sample-to-detector distance fixed at 261 mm, covering a q-range of 0.05 to 7 nm-1. The scattering vector q, is defined as q=2π/d, where d represents for the interfacial spacing (d-spacing) of ordered and periodical structure. All statistics were disposed by SAXSquant software.

Low field NMR spin-spin relaxation (T2) measurements (LF-NMR)

The LF-NMR analysis was performed at 4 and 25 °C. T2 relaxation time measurements of dense phases of various coacervates were conducted using Niumag VTMR20-010V-I equipment (0.47 T, 20 MHz, China). The average transverse (spin-spin) relaxation time (T2) of water protons within the samples was acquired through Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) sequence {90 °[-τ-180 °-τ(echo)]n-TR} with a 2.60 µs wide 90 ° pulse, and a 4.64 µs wide 180 ° pulse63. The echo time (τ) is 2.0 ms, and the waiting time is 10000.0 ms. The T2 relaxation curve was fitted to a multi-exponential curve with the MultiExp Inv Analysis software. Typically, water is categorized into three hydration states based on T2 values: bound water (T2 ≈ 0-100 ms) and free water (T2 ≥ 1000 ms)48–50. Each measurement was repeated six times under consistent experimental conditions.

Infrared attenuated total reflection spectrum (ATR)

The ATR spectrum was acquired using a VERTEX 70 v spectrometer (Bruker, U.S.). The sample stage is composed of diamond, serving as the reflective base, and all tests were conducted at room temperature. MIR-DTGS.XPM was selected as the fundamental measurement parameter, and the water background was subtracted prior to measurement. The amount of sample covering the stage was carefully controlled by pipetting 20 μL of each dense phase. All profiles were corrected for the effects of CO2.
