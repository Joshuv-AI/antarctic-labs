Phenotypic variability and floral trait associations in bael [Aegle marmelos (L.) Correa] from the North-Western Himalayan plains
Authors: Prabhdeep Singh, Akash Sharma, Romesh Kumar Salgotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bael is a medicinal fruit tree of considerable economic and therapeutic importance and is predominantly propagated through seeds. The present investigation was undertaken to evaluate and interpret variation in floral characteristics among eighty wild bael genotypes, alongside two widely cultivated varieties, NB-5 and NB-9. Among the genotypes examined, JMU-Bael (Sel-27) consistently recorded the highest measurements for most floral attributes, including flower and bud dimensions, petal size, pedicel traits, as well as reproductive organ characteristics such as filament, anther, ovary, style and stigma size. Multivariate analysis using principal component analysis revealed that the first five principal components together explained 73.90% of the total variability, demonstrating that these components effectively summarized the majority of information contained in the floral traits. Each principal component was associated with a distinct set of correlated floral parameters. Furthermore, cluster analysis grouped the eighty wild genotypes and the two commercial cultivars into two major clusters, each further subdivided into smaller groups based on similarity in floral traits. Overall, the results highlight substantial phenotypic diversity and clear relationships among the evaluated bael genotypes, providing a valuable foundation for future breeding, selection and germplasm improvement programmes. However, as canopy directions of the same tree were used as replications, the observed variability and associated genetic parameter estimates should be interpreted with caution, as environmental and sampling effects may also contribute to the detected variation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58520-z.

Introduction

Bael, an indigenous tree fruit species of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia is taxonomically classified under the family Rutaceae1,2. Bael is known for its adaptability to challenging soil and environmental conditions. It can withstand temperatures ranging from 7 to 48 degrees Celsius3. It thrives in swampy, alkaline, and sandy wastelands, presenting as a medium-sized tree with abundant dimorphic branches. Its leaves are alternately arranged, trifoliate, deep green, and membranous, while its flowers are large, sweet-scented, greenish-white, and bloom from May to July4.

The plant’s composition includes polyphenols, carotenoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, coumarins and fibers, showcasing various health benefits such as anticancer, antidiarrheal, antimicrobial, antiulcerative, antioxidant, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, cardioprotective and gastroprotective effects5. While the fruit is the most valued and consumed part of the tree, Ayurvedic medicine also utilizes its leaves, stems, bark, and roots to address a range of human ailments6. The bael tree is widely recognized for its exceptional therapeutic properties and is regarded as one of the most important medicinal plants of the Indian subcontinent1,2,7. In addition to its medicinal significance, bael possesses notable industrial value owing to the production of mucilage, a gummy substance utilized as an adhesive and as an additive in water-based paints to improve strength and brightness8.

India harbors substantial genetic diversity in bael germplasm, encompassing a wide range of horticultural desirable traits of relevance to end users9. Bael exhibits considerable variability in both vegetative and reproductive phenology, which can be attributed to the fact that most trees in the wild or homesteads are grown from sexual seeds. The adaptability of bael allows it to flourish in diverse environments such as dry forests, hills, semi-arid regions, and plains10. Flowering traits hold immense importance in determining the reproductive success and overall productivity of plant species. Consequently, there has been a growing interest in recent years to investigate the flowering characteristics of bael, aiming to unravel the genetic, physiological, and environmental factors that influence its flowering behavior. A comprehensive evaluation of the flowering traits in bael can provide valuable insights into its reproductive biology, facilitate efficient crop management practices, and support breeding programs for yield improvement. In the absence of suitable cultivars, achieving consistent growth and fruit set after flowering in bael has been challenging. Therefore, it is essential to identify suitable genotypes for promoting productivity, production, and the quality of bael produce in the Jammu region. In order to initiate any crop improvement program, it is crucial to gather information regarding the reproductive behavior, including floral traits of the crop. With this perspective in mind, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the flowering behavior and associated floral traits of bael genotypes under the semi-arid ecosystem of the north-western plains of India.

Results

Floral traits

The variation observed in floral characteristics among the bael selections indicated substantial diversity across the eighty wild genotypes when compared with the two commercial cultivars. The bael genotypes initiated flowering between the 3rd and 4th weeks of May, with forty five genotypes including NB-5 (54.88%) start flowering in the 3rd week and thirty seven genotypes including NB-9 (45.12%) in the 4th week of May. The end of flowering occurred from the 2nd to the 4th week of June, with the majority of sixty one genotypes including NB-5 and NB-9 (74.39%) completing flowering in the 3rd week, followed by twenty genotypes (24.39%) ended in 4th week of June and one genotype (1.22%) in the 2nd week of June. The maximum flower length (18.73 mm) and width (36.55 mm) were recorded in JMU-Bael (Sel-27), whereas the minimum values were observed in JMU-Bael (Sel-72) for length (12.35 mm) and in JMU-Bael (Sel-66) for width (24.16 mm). Similarly, bud length and width reached their highest values in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (13.24 mm and 9.85 mm, respectively), while the lowest bud length (10.18 mm) was found in JMU-Bael (Sel-76) and the lowest width (7.05 mm) in JMU-Bael (Sel-77). Petal length and width were also maximum in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (18.65 mm and 9.80 mm, respectively), whereas the minimum values were noted in JMU-Bael (Sel-66) for length (11.26 mm) and JMU-Bael (Sel-41) for width (7.19 mm). Pedicel length and width showed the highest values in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (9.38 mm and 2.58 mm, respectively), while JMU-Bael (Sel-66) recorded the minimum values (5.18 mm and 2.02 mm, respectively) (Fig. 1) (Supplementary Table 1).Figure 1Pheatmap showing floral traits of bael genotypes on the basis of mean performance.

Filament traits followed a similar trend, with maximum length (5.05 mm) and width (0.81 mm) observed in JMU-Bael (Sel-27), while the lowest length (3.54 mm) was noted in JMU-Bael (Sel-62) and width (0.48 mm) in JMU-Bael (Sel-76). Anther length and width were maximum in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (4.41 mm and 0.83 mm, respectively), whereas the lowest values were recorded in JMU-Bael (Sel-18) for length (3.29 mm) and in JMU-Bael (Sel-8) for width (0.50 mm). Similarly, ovary length and width reached maximum values in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (7.71 mm and 4.87 mm, respectively), while the minimum ovary length (4.25 mm) was observed in JMU-Bael (Sel-4) and width (3.34 mm) in JMU-Bael (Sel-21) (Fig. 1) (Supplementary Table 1).

Style dimensions also exhibited variation, with the maximum length (1.52 mm) and width (2.53 mm) recorded in JMU-Bael (Sel-27), whereas the lowest length (1.02 mm) and width (1.49 mm) were found in JMU-Bael (Sel-74) and JMU-Bael (Sel-58), respectively. Stigma length and width were highest in JMU-Bael (Sel-27) (3.48 mm and 2.93 mm, respectively), while the lowest length (2.25 mm) and width (2.12 mm) were found in JMU-Bael (Sel-8) and JMU-Bael (Sel-76), respectively (Fig. 1) (Supplementary Table 1).

Genetic parameters

Estimates for eighteen floral traits included the Environmental Coefficient of Variation (ECV), Genotypic Coefficient of Variation (GCV), Phenotypic Coefficient of Variation (PCV), Heritability in Broad sense (h2b), Genetic Advance (GA), Genetic Advance in Percentage of Mean (GAM) (Table 1). Flower width and petal length showed the highest h2b values, while GA was highest for flower width and ECV was highest for anther width. Pedicel length exhibited a higher percentage for GCV, PCV, and GAM. Conversely, flower width found the lowest ECV, anther length had the lowest GCV and PCV, filament width showed the lowest GA, and anther length had the lowest GAM. Overall, fifteen floral traits expressed high broad-sense heritability (> 98%), indicating substantial phenotypic consistency among the evaluated genotypes. Considering that measurements were obtained from different canopy directions of the same tree and treated as replications, the estimated broad-sense heritability values should be interpreted as being conceptually closer to repeatability than to classical heritability estimates derived from replicated experimental designs. Correlation matrices

Correlation studies at both genotypic and phenotypic levels are indispensable in breeding programs, aiding in the identification and selection of parental plants possessing advantageous trait combinations. The magnitude and direction of correlations were similar at both genotypic and phenotypic levels, although genotypic correlations were slightly higher than phenotypic correlations, suggesting that genetic factors played a major role in determining the relationships among the floral traits. At the genotypic level, flower length exhibited positive significant correlations with flower width (r = 0.810), bud length (r = 0.838), bud width (r = 0.614), petal length (r = 0.620), petal width (r = 0.538), anther length (r = 0.711), anther width (r = 0.317), ovary length (r = 0.479) and ovary width (r = 0.567), while its negative correlations with pedicel width (r = − 0.013) was relatively not statistically significant. Flower width was positively correlated with several floral traits including bud length (r = 0.729), bud width (r = 0.585), petal length (r = 0.571), petal width (r = 0.564), anther length (r = 0.606), ovary length (r = 0.456) and ovary width (r = 0.518). Petal length showed a positive significant correlation with petal width (r = 0.903), anther length (r = 0.492), ovary length (r = 0.388) and ovary width (r = 0.484). Petal length had negative and non-significant relationships with style length (r = − 0.127) and filament width (r = − 0.115). Pedicel width generally exhibited negative correlations with filament width (r = − 0.001), ovary length (r = − 0.154), ovary width (r = − 0.027), style length (r = − 0.176), style width (r = − 0.147), Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis was performed using eighteen floral traits to evaluate the multivariate variation among the studied bael genotypes was presented in Table 4; Supplementary Table 2 and illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3. A total of twelve principal components were generated, of which the first five components had eigenvalues greater than one and together accounted for 73.90% of the total variation, indicating that these components captured most of the variability present in the dataset. The first principal component (PC1) had the highest eigenvalue (6.22) and explained 34.54% of the total variance. Traits with loading ≥ 0.5 were considered major contributors; ≥ 0.3 moderate. Although several traits showed moderate positive loadings in this component, none exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of loading ≥ 0.5, indicating that PC1 was associated with bud length (0.35), flower length (0.34), anther length (0.33), flower width (0.32) and ovary width (0.30). The second principal component (PC2) accounted for 17.75% of the total variance (eigenvalue = 3.20). Traits related to stigma and style dimensions showed moderate loadings in this component; however, none surpassed the threshold of 0.5, suggesting that PC2 was mainly associated with stigma length (0.47), style width (0.47), style length (0.46) and stigma width (0.34). The third principal component (PC3) contributed 8.90% of the total variance (eigenvalue = 1.60). Traits associated with anther and filament morphology exhibited moderate loadings, including anther width (0.45), filament width (0.43) and ovary length (0.38). The fourth principal component (PC4) explained 6.87% of the total variance (eigenvalue = 1.24) and was primarily influenced by pedicel width (0.62) and pedicel length (0.57), both of which exceeded the threshold of 0.5 and therefore represented the major contributors to this component. These traits reflect variation in pedicel morphology among the studied genotypes. Discussion

The flowering period is important in the execution of successful characterization and also for successful hybridization programme. Flowering behaviour among bael genotypes varied considerably and may have been influenced by prevailing climatic and environmental conditions. Differences in the onset of flowering may be attributed to variations in topography, altitude, and mean temperature of the locations where the bael selections were growing. The flowering attributes recorded in the present study exhibited substantial variation among the evaluated genotypes, thereby confirming the existence of wide variability among the wild bael genotypes in comparison with the two commercial cultivars. Such variation in flowering traits may result from the presence of genetically superior clones as well as the influence of diverse biotic and abiotic factors. Although floral traits are generally considered to be relatively stable and less influenced by environmental conditions, measurable differences were still evident among the genotypes studied. Similar variability in floral characters of bael under different agro-climatic conditions of India10–16.

In the present investigation, substantial variability was observed in floral traits among the eighty wild bael genotypes evaluated alongside two commercial cultivars, NB-5 and NB-9, indicating a broad genetic base within the studied genotypes. Among all the genotypes, JMU-Bael (Sel-27) consistently exhibited superior floral characteristics, outperforming not only the other wild genotypes but also the commercial checks. The consistent performance of JMU-Bael (Sel-27) across multiple floral parameters indicates distinct phenotypic variation among the evaluated genotypes; however, further genetic and reproductive studies are necessary to validate its functional significance. Larger floral structures, including increased flower size, petal dimensions, and well-developed reproductive organs, may potentially influence pollinator attraction and reproductive processes; however, these relationships were not directly evaluated in the present study and require further validation through reproductive biology and fruit set studies. The superior performance of JMU-Bael (Sel-27) is further substantiated by its outstanding pomological and physico-chemical traits, highlighting its potential as an elite genotype for varietal improvement17,18. Moreover, molecular characterization using SSR markers on the same set of eighty wild genotypes and the commercial cultivars, NB-5 and NB-9 confirmed substantial genetic divergence9. From a breeding perspective, the identification of superior genotypes such as JMU-Bael (Sel-27), supported by both phenotypic performance and previously reported molecular divergence, provides a strong foundation for parent selection in hybridization programmes. Furthermore, the floral traits characterized in this study can be effectively integrated with future evaluations of fruit yield, quality parameters and reproductive biology traits such as fruit set percentage, pollen viability and stigma receptivity to develop a more comprehensive selection index.

In crop improvement programmes, the genotypic coefficient of variance (GCV) and heritability are pivotal for understanding the efficacy of selective breeding. High estimates of GCV and phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV) reflect substantial genetic variability, which can be efficiently exploited for the development of superior cultivars, whereas low values indicate limited scope for improvement through selection. In vegetatively propagated crops such as bael, broad-sense heritability estimates may provide preliminary information regarding the consistency of trait expression; however, under observational field surveys these estimates should not be interpreted as definitive measures of genetic control because environmental influences cannot be completely excluded. In this study, the highest heritability (h2b) was observed in flower width and petal length, while the highest genetic advance (GA) was recorded for flower width and the highest environmental coefficient of variance (ECV) for anther width. Pedicel length exhibited the highest percentages of GCV, PCV, and genetic advance as percent of mean (GAM). Conversely, the lowest ECV was noted in flower width, the lowest GCV and PCV in anther length, the lowest GA in filament width, and the lowest GAM in anther length. These results are in agreement with the findings of Debbarma and Hazarika19, who reported comparable patterns of ECV, GCV and PCV for fruit traits among thirty bael accessions. The predominance of high GCV and PCV estimates for several traits in the present study indicates considerable phenotypic variability among the evaluated genotypes. However, because the evaluated trees were sampled across different districts and elevations, environmental effects may have contributed to the observed variation under the present observational design.

Since floral trait measurements were conducted in situ on wild bael genotypes across multiple districts (Jammu at 300 m a.s.l., Samba at 384 m, and Kathua at 393 m), some observed phenotypic variation may be influenced by local environmental conditions, such as potential differences in climate, soil or microhabitats, in addition to genetic factors. Furthermore, the use of canopy directions of the same tree as replications represents an observational sampling approach rather than independent biological replication. Therefore, the estimates derived from the present study should be interpreted cautiously within the limitations of the observational design. Future common-garden evaluations and multi-location validation studies would help to more effectively partition genetic and environmental sources of variation. Similar integrative approaches combining phenotypic characterization with environmental validation have also been emphasized in medicinal and aromatic crops for understanding genotype performance and trait stability across environments20,21.

For breeders, correlation analysis serves as an invaluable tool for selecting appropriate parents possessing favourable combinations of traits. In this investigation, partitioning of the correlation coefficients into genotypic and phenotypic levels using error variance and covariance matrices, significant associations among most traits were revealed. Petal length exhibited a positive significant correlation with petal width, while flower length showed positive significant correlations with flower width, bud length, bud width, petal length, petal width, anther length, anther width, ovary length and ovary width, while its negative correlations with pedicel width was relatively not statistically significant at genotypic and phenotypic levels. Overall, genotypic correlation coefficients were generally higher than phenotypic correlations, suggesting comparatively stronger associations among traits at the genotypic level; however, environmental influences may also contribute to the observed relationships under field conditions. The observed associations among floral traits may reflect coordinated trait expression; however, further controlled studies would be necessary to confirm the underlying genetic or physiological mechanisms. These associations may provide useful preliminary information for future breeding and selection studies; however, their practical application should be validated under controlled experimental conditions. For instance, selection for increased flower size may simultaneously improve ovary and anther development, thereby enhancing reproductive output. The strong positive correlation observed between floral dimensions (flower length/width and reproductive traits (anther/ovary size) suggests a coordinated development strategy. This integration ensures functional symmetry, optimizing the plants efficiency for pollinator attraction and successful fertilization. These observations are consistent with the findings of Debbarma and Hazarika19, who also reported significant genotypic and phenotypic correlations among morphological traits in bael accessions. Previous studies in bael have reported that cultivars with comparatively larger flowers have been reported to produce bigger size fruits, whereas medium-sized flowers were commonly associated with intermediate fruit weight and smaller flowers with lower fruit weight, rare exception has also been observed in bael, which are having small flower size but producing bigger fruits22.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a crucial multivariate tool for reducing dimensionality and summarizing variation in complex datasets like floral traits, while preserving key information through uncorrelated components23,24. In bael, PCA helps identify trait combinations contributing to genetic diversity, likely arising from population heterogeneity, genetic architecture, and developmental factors25. In this study on floral traits of 80 wild genotypes and two cultivars, the first five principal components explained 73.90% of the total variance, effectively capturing most floral variability and enabling selection of superior individuals based on integrated trait scores. PC1 (34.54% variance, eigenvalue 6.22) was driven by moderate loadings from bud length, flower length, anther length, flower width and ovary width, representing overall floral size and reflecting coordinated variation among floral dimensions observed in the evaluated genotypes. PC2 (17.75%) emphasized female reproductive structures (stigma length, style width, style length, stigma width), potentially influencing pollen tube growth and fertilization. PC3 (8.90%) highlighted male structures (anther width, filament width) with ovary length, suggesting coordination or trade-offs in male–female function. PC4 (6.87%) was dominated by pedicel width and length, reflecting floral support and nutrient transport, while PC5 (5.85%) isolated filament width. These patterns align with prior bael studies using PCA on morpho-pomological and fruit traits, where floral/reproductive dimensions often load on early components for diversity assessment17,19,26. For future breeding and diversity studies, high PC1 and PC4 scores may help identify genotypes with comparatively larger floral architecture and robust pedicel traits for further evaluation.

Cluster analysis divided the genotypes into two major clusters, with Cluster II exhibiting significantly higher mean values for most floral traits (flower length 19.7% higher, flower width 18.3% higher, petal length 19.2% higher, ovary length 13.9% higher) compared to Cluster I, indicating superior overall floral size and reproductive organ dimensions. Pedicel and filament traits showed smaller differences (3–4% higher in Cluster II), suggesting Cluster II’s advantage lies mainly in larger flower architecture rather than support structures alone. The elite genotype JMU-Bael (Sel-27), with the highest individual floral measurements, aligns with Cluster II’s profile of enhanced floral vigor. No clear geographical correspondence (e.g., Jammu vs. Samba/Kathua) was evident, implying clusters reflect genetic divergence more than local adaptation. The observed clustering pattern indicates phenotypic divergence among the evaluated genotypes and may provide useful baseline information for future hybridization and diversity studies. Thus, PCA complements cluster analysis by not only confirming genetic divergence but also identifying specific trait combinations that can be targeted in selection and hybridization programmes. Similar investigations have been conducted on other crops, including Bael26; Apricot27; Calamansi28; Camellia oleifera29.

Conclusion

Based on these findings, it can be concluded that there is a considerable variability in the floral traits among the evaluated bael genotypes, reflecting a rich genetic diversity within the germplasm. The genotype JMU-Bael (Sel-27) consistently exhibited superior floral characteristics in terms of size and dimensions compared to the other genotypes and commercial cultivars. These findings provide baseline information on floral phenotypic variability among bael genotypes under natural field conditions and may support future breeding, reproductive biology studies, which can contribute to the improvement of bael cultivation and their productivity. However, as canopy directions of the same tree were used as replications, the observed variability and associated genetic parameter estimates should be interpreted with caution, since environmental and sampling effects may also have contributed to the detected variation. Further evaluation under controlled and multi-location conditions is needed to confirm the stability and genetic basis of these traits.

Materials and methods

Survey coverage and plant material

To evaluate the diversity in floral traits of bael, a comprehensive survey was conducted in the major bael cultivation areas of Jammu province, located in the Jammu and Kashmir Union territory of India. The survey encompassed Jammu (32.73° N, 74.87° E, 300 m above sea level), Samba (32.57° N, 75.12° E, 384 m above sea level) and Kathua (32.37° N, 75.52° E, 393 m above sea level) districts (Fig. 5). Eighty wild bael genotypes were selected from these locations, along with two commercially important cultivars, NB-5 and NB-9, noted for their divergent characteristics (Supplementary Table 3). Plant material was collected following all pertinent institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. The collected bael genotypes were identified and authenticated by Prabhdeep Singh under the supervision of Dr. Akash Sharma for their utilization in breeding programmes aimed at cultivar development. Voucher specimens were conserved in the Field Genebank of ICAR–Central Horticultural Experiment Station, Vejalpur, Godhra, Gujarat and assigned the accession number IC657953.Figure 5Geographical distribution of collected bael genotypes in the districts of Jammu and Kashmir of India. The map was generated using ArcGIS software to illustrate the sampling locations of the collected genotypes.

Geographical distribution of collected bael genotypes in the districts of Jammu and Kashmir of India. The map was generated using ArcGIS software to illustrate the sampling locations of the collected genotypes.

Floral traits

The floral traits were recorded in accordance with the Guidelines for the Conduct of Test for Distinctiveness, Uniformity and Stability of bael (Aegle marmelos Correa) Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers Right’s Authority (PPV&FRA) Government of India30, as well as the bael descriptors developed by the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources31. To collect thorough morphometric data on floral organs, twenty one shoots were tagged on three different canopy directions of the bael plant and each direction was considered as one replication. From each direction of seven tagged shoots, seven fully opened flowers were randomly sampled in one replication. A total of sixty-three flowers per genotype were collected for floral trait evaluation. The recorded observations for each floral trait were expressed as the mean of three observational replications. Since observations were recorded from naturally growing trees across different geographical locations, the study design should be considered observational in nature and environmental effects could not be completely separated from genotypic effects. The date of start of flowering was recorded when 5–10 per cent of the flowers had opened, while the date of end of flowering was noted when approximately 90 per cent of the flowers had shed and only about 10 per cent flower buds remained unopened. A digital vernier caliper was used to measure various characteristics such as flower size, bud size, petal size, pedicel size, filament size, anther size, ovary size, style size, and stigma size. These observations were carried out during the month of May–June, facilitating a comparative analysis of the gathered information.

Statistical analysis

The study integrates descriptive evaluation of phenotypic traits with analytical statistical methods to assess variability and significance among genotypes. Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and treatment means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at the 5% level of significance (p < 0.05) using OriginPro 9.1 software.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The relationship among bael genotypes was evaluated through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using OriginPro 9.1 software. Additionally, a scatter plot depicting the first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) was generated using OriginPro 9.1 software. Interpreting extensive datasets in research can prove challenging. However, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) alleviates these challenges by enhancing data comprehensiveness while mitigating information loss. This is achieved by generating fresh, uncorrelated variables that progressively maximize variance. The process involves identifying these novel variables and principal components, specific to the dataset. It ultimately involves solving an eigenvalue/eigenvector problem through PCA, as exemplified in the study by Jolliffe and Cadima23.
