Preoperative TAPSE/sPAP ratio predicts early residual pulmonary hypertension following pulmonary endarterectomy in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Junyu Ma, Dezhi Ren, Yanan Zhen, Aili Li, Jun Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResidual pulmonary hypertension (RPH) is a major postoperative complication after pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) in patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) and is associated with worse recovery and prognosis. This study evaluated the predictive value of the preoperative tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to systolic pulmonary artery pressure (TAPSE/sPAP) ratio, a non-invasive surrogate of right ventricular-pulmonary arterial (RV-PA) coupling, for early RPH.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 183 consecutive patients with CTEPH who underwent PEA at a high-volume national center between December 2017 and September 2025. Early RPH was defined as a postoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 30 mmHg. Independent predictors were identified using multivariable logistic regression. Predictive performance and model improvement were evaluated in the complete-case cohort using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), the likelihood ratio test, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and continuous net reclassification index (NRI). Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples.ResultsEarly RPH occurred in 48 patients (26.23%). Compared with the non-RPH group, patients with RPH had a lower preoperative TAPSE/sPAP ratio (0.21 ± 0.10 vs. 0.29 ± 0.15, P = 0.001), higher preoperative PVR (14.45 ± 5.39 vs. 10.16 ± 5.63 WU, P < 0.001), and a higher RV/LV ratio (P < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, preoperative PVR (OR 1.0010, P = 0.045) and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio (per 0.01-unit increase, OR 0.93, P = 0.046) were independently associated with early RPH. In the complete-case cohort (n = 151), the combined model including PVR and TAPSE/sPAP had an AUC of 0.753 (95% CI: 0.666–0.844), compared with 0.751 (95% CI: 0.664–0.830) for the PVR-only model. Although the increase in AUC was minimal (ΔAUC = +0.002, DeLong P = 0.940), the likelihood ratio test showed a significant improvement in model fit (P = 0.012). Reclassification analysis showed a positive integrated discrimination improvement (IDI = 0.034, P = 0.005), whereas the continuous net reclassification index showed only a numerical improvement (NRI = 0.297, P = 0.117). Internal validation showed a bias-corrected AUC of 0.724 and good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, P = 0.676; calibration slope = 0.92).ConclusionPreoperative PVR and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio were independently associated with early RPH after PEA. Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a debilitating cardiovascular condition characterized by elevated pulmonary artery pressure and increased pulmonary vascular resistance [1–4]. Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) remains the gold-standard treatment, as it directly removes organized thrombi to significantly improve hemodynamics [5–7]. Common complications affecting short-term and long-term prognosis after PEA include residual pulmonary hypertension (RPH) and reperfusion pulmonary edema [8, 9]. Despite surgical success, RPH remains a major complication, affecting approximately 25% of patients and serving as a leading cause of early postoperative morbidity and in-hospital mortality [10–12]. The development of RPH is attributed to the combined effects of distal mechanical obstruction and irreversible secondary microvasculopathy, which sustain high vascular resistance even after proximal clearance [11].

Currently, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is the most established predictor for RPH; however, its assessment requires invasive right heart catheterization [13]. There is an urgent clinical need for reliable, non-invasive preoperative indicators that can be easily obtained at the bedside. Such tools would allow for repetitive monitoring and early identification of high-risk individuals, providing critical guidance for tailoring surgical strategies and intensifying perioperative management to improve patient outcomes.

Central to this risk assessment is the concept of right ventricular-pulmonary arterial (RV-PA) coupling, which reflects the functional coordination between right ventricular (RV) contractility and its afterload [14]. In CTEPH, the RV initially adapts to chronic pressure overload through hypertrophy, but persistent stress eventually leads to fibrosis and “uncoupling,” signaling the onset of RV decompensation [1]. This preoperative decoupling represents an exhausted functional reserve; a heart in this state may lack the compensatory capacity to adapt to the acute hemodynamic shifts and reperfusion stress that occur immediately after PEA. Consequently, even if the primary mechanical obstruction is removed, this lack of reserve predisposes the patient to persistent hemodynamic derangement and RPH.

The TAPSE/sPAP ratio has emerged as a simple, non-invasive surrogate for RV-PA coupling [15–18]. Its prognostic value has been validated in various forms of pulmonary hypertension and heart failure [19–21]. However, its specific utility in predicting early RPH after PEA remains to be fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the value of the preoperative TAPSE/sPAP ratio in predicting the occurrence of early RPH in CTEPH patients following PEA.

Methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital, involving 183 consecutive patients diagnosed with CTEPH who underwent PEA between December 2017 and September 2025. This was a retrospective analysis of consecutive patients who had already undergone PEA during the study period. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–80 years; (2) a definitive diagnosis of CTEPH according to international guidelines; and (3) completion of PEA surgery. Patients were excluded if they underwent PEA for conditions other than CTEPH (e.g., pulmonary artery sarcoma or acute embolism) or lacked essential preoperative right heart catheterization (RHC) or echocardiographic data.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Approval No. 2023-KY-060) on March 24, 2023. Given the retrospective nature of the data review, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Surgical procedure and perioperative management

All PEA procedures were performed by a single experienced multidisciplinary team following a standardized protocol. Surgery was conducted under general anesthesia and extracorporeal circulation. Sequential endarterectomies were performed from the main pulmonary arteries down to the subsegmental levels during periods of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. Postoperatively, patients were managed in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) by a dedicated team. Standardized management included hemodynamic monitoring via Swan-Ganz catheters, early weaning from mechanical ventilation, and initiation of anticoagulant and diuretic therapies within 24 h if clinically stable.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed by a single specialized echocardiographer prior to surgery to ensure internal consistency. The TAPSE/sPAP ratio [22] was calculated as follows: Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (TAPSE) was measured in the apical four-chamber view using M-mode, representing the maximum displacement of the tricuspid annulus from end-diastole to end-systole. Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) was estimated using the modified Bernoulli equation based on the peak velocity of tricuspid regurgitation (TRV) and right atrial pressure (RAP) estimated via the inferior vena cava diameter and collapse. Additionally, the right ventricular to left ventricular end-diastolic dimension ratio (RV/LV ratio) was calculated to assess cardiac chamber remodeling. The determination of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) severity was based on the consensus statements of the European Association of Echocardiography [23].

Hemodynamic measurements and grouping

Preoperative hemodynamics, including cardiac index (CI), mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), were obtained via RHC. Postoperative parameters were recorded immediately after completion of the procedure in the operating room using a Swan-Ganz catheter. In our practice, postoperative mPAP was measured at the end of surgery, after hemodynamic stabilization and before transfer from the operating room to the ICU. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence of early postoperative Residual Pulmonary Hypertension (RPH), defined as a postoperative mPAP > 30 mmHg [8, 12]. This time point was chosen because in some patients the Swan-Ganz catheter was removed within 24 h after surgery, making later pulmonary artery pressure monitoring unavailable. In addition, pulmonary artery pressure may change substantially during the first postoperative day because of sedation withdrawal, ventilator weaning, and other ICU interventions. Therefore, immediate postoperative mPAP was used to provide a consistent early hemodynamic assessment after PEA.

The diagnostic definition of PH/CTEPH followed contemporaneous international guidelines during the study period, whereas the study endpoint was early postoperative residual PH, for which no universally accepted definition currently exists. We therefore used a predefined postoperative threshold based on prior PEA literature rather than the updated diagnostic threshold for PH.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normal data were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate.

To identify independent predictors of RPH, univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. Variables for the multivariable analysis were selected based on clinical relevance and the results of the univariable analysis. Variables with significant or borderline associations, together with clinically important factors, were considered. Because the TAPSE/sPAP ratio includes TAPSE in its calculation, these two variables were not entered into the same model. Given the limited number of RPH events, the final model was kept simple to reduce the risk of overfitting. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed by forcing Jamieson classification into the multivariable model to assess the robustness of the association between TAPSE/sPAP and postoperative RPH.

The incremental predictive value of adding the TAPSE/sPAP ratio to the baseline PVR model was assessed using the likelihood ratio test. Furthermore, we quantified the improvement in model performance through the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) and the continuous Net Reclassification Index (NRI). Due to the small numerical values of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio (typically < 1.0), the ratio was multiplied by 100 prior to regression analysis to facilitate interpretation. Consequently, the reported odds ratios (ORs) reflect the risk change per 0.01-unit increase in the ratio. The predictive performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). All ROC-based analyses in section "Predictive value of echocardiographic parameters and the multivariable regression model", including the echocardiographic ROC comparison, the PVR-only versus combined model comparison, and the reclassification analyses, were performed using the same complete-case dataset with available data for RPH, TAPSE, sPAP, and PVR (n = 151). The optimal cut-off value of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio was determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated.

To assess the stability of the multivariable model and account for potential overfitting, internal validation was performed using a bootstrapping procedure with 1,000 resamples. This approach was used to calculate the bias-corrected AUC and the calibration slope. The agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was evaluated via calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Statistical analyses and visualization were performed using Python (version 3.x), utilizing the scikit-learn, statsmodels, and matplotlib libraries. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 183 patients with CTEPH who underwent PEA were enrolled in this study, among whom 135 (73.77%) had no RPH after surgery and 48 (26.23%) developed postoperative RPH. As shown in Table 1, there were no statistically significant differences in age, BMI, smoking index, sex distribution, Jamieson classification, and Smoking History between the non-RPH group and the RPH group (all P > 0.05). Medical history duration (P = 0.096) and cardiac index (CI, P = 0.079) showed borderline differences between the two groups without statistical significance.

Although 6MWD and TAPSE were significant in the univariable analysis, they were not included in the final multivariable model. This was because the number of RPH events was limited, and TAPSE overlaps with the TAPSE/sPAP ratio. After adjustment for potential confounders, multivariable logistic regression showed that PVR (OR = 1.0010, 95% CI: 1.0001–1.0020, P = 0.045) and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio (per 0.01 increase; OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99, P = 0.046) remained independently associated with postoperative RPH in patients with CTEPH undergoing PEA.

As a sensitivity analysis, Jamieson classification was forced into the multivariable model. In this model, the association of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio with postoperative RPH remained significant, whereas Jamieson classification itself was not significantly associated with RPH.

Predictive value of echocardiographic parameters and the multivariable regression model

Given that both TAPSE and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio were significantly associated with postoperative RPH in the univariable analysis, ROC analyses were performed to compare their predictive performance. Using the same complete-case dataset (n = 151), TAPSE alone showed modest discrimination for postoperative RPH, with an AUC of 0.628 (95% CI: 0.523–0.732). The TAPSE/sPAP ratio showed numerically better discrimination, with an AUC of 0.702 (95% CI: 0.599–0.797) (Table 4), although formal comparison between the two did not show a statistically significant difference. Using the Youden index, the optimal cut-off value of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio for predicting postoperative RPH was 0.194, with a sensitivity of 64.9%, specificity of 71.9%, positive predictive value of 42.9%, and negative predictive value of 86.3%. Among the 183 patients in the full cohort, 151 had complete data for RPH, TAPSE, sPAP, and PVR and were therefore included in the complete-case dataset used for all ROC-based analyses in this section. Baseline characteristics of patients included in the complete-case analysis and those excluded because of missing data are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Model validation and calibration

Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples supported the stability of the combined model, yielding a bias-corrected AUC of 0.724. The model showed good calibration, with a Hosmer-Lemeshow test P-value of 0.676 and a calibration slope of 0.92, indicating good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed RPH events (Fig. 3).

Calibration curve of the combined model. The plot illustrates the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of residual pulmonary hypertension, validated through 1,000 bootstrap resamples

Although the predicted probabilities were primarily concentrated within the 0 to 0.35 range—reflecting the relatively low incidence of RPH in the cohort—the calibration curve remained closely aligned with the ideal diagonal line throughout the clinically relevant spectrum. Visual inspection of the reclassification scatter plot and density curves also suggested better separation between RPH and non-RPH cases in the enhanced model than in the baseline model (Fig. 2). In Fig. 2, the enhanced model tended to assign higher predicted probabilities to patients with RPH and lower predicted probabilities to those without RPH, indicating improved separation between the two groups. This pattern is consistent with the positive continuous NRI and the improvement in IDI observed after adding the TAPSE/sPAP ratio to the baseline PVR model.

Discussion

RPH after PEA remains a significant challenge, directly impacting the short-term recovery and long-term survival of patients with CTEPH [8–12]. In our cohort of 183 patients, the incidence of RPH was 26.23%, which is highly consistent with the approximately 25% reported in international meta-analyses [5]. Our results demonstrate that preoperative PVR and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio were independently associated with early RPH. By integrating these two markers, the combined model had an AUC of 0.753 and may provide additional value for non-invasive preoperative risk stratification.

The clinical significance of RPH extends far beyond hemodynamic measurements; it is the primary driver of adverse clinical outcomes. Our use of the mPAP ≥ 30 mmHg cutoff is supported by long-term data. A multicenter Scandinavian study found that early postoperative mPAP ≥ 30 mmHg carries a hazard ratio of 2.49 for mortality, resulting in a 32% lower survival rate over 20 years [8]. Immediate postoperative mPAP was also considered clinically meaningful, as it may help the ICU team identify patients at higher risk and prepare early management strategies for postoperative pulmonary hypertension. We found that the RPH group had significantly longer mechanical ventilation and ICU stays. More critically, early postoperative RPH is recognized as the most common cause of in-hospital mortality following PEA [11]. This is further illuminated when comparing our results with the landmark study by Dartevelle et al. [24], which reported that patients with markedly high preoperative PVR (> 1200 dyn·s/cm⁵, approximately 15 WU) faced a mortality rate of 20%, compared to only 4% in those with lower resistance.

By identifying PVR as an independent predictor of RPH, our study provides a possible mechanistic link to Dartevelle’s findings. High preoperative PVR may reflect extensive and irreversible distal microvasculopathy that persists after surgery, leading to RPH and increasing perioperative risk. Furthermore, although our study focused on early outcomes, existing literature emphasizes that early RPH is a decisive factor for long-term prognosis, as persistent hemodynamic derangement often leads to chronic right heart failure.

In addition, TAPSE alone was also significantly associated with postoperative RPH in the univariable analysis, suggesting that impaired RV longitudinal systolic function itself is related to postoperative hemodynamic burden. However, ROC analysis showed that the TAPSE/sPAP ratio had numerically better discriminative ability than TAPSE alone (AUC 0.702 vs. 0.628), although formal comparison did not show a statistically significant difference. This finding suggests that incorporating afterload information through sPAP may improve risk stratification beyond TAPSE alone. At the same time, the incremental value of the ratio should be interpreted with caution in a cohort of this size.

The predictive value of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio lies in its role as a preoperative surrogate for right ventricular-pulmonary arterial (RV-PA) coupling. Our findings align with the research by Guazzi et al. 20, who demonstrated that the TAPSE/sPAP ratio is a valuable index for assessing RV contractile function and prognosis in heart failure patients. While Guazzi’s work established this in heart failure, our study suggests that it may also be useful in the CTEPH surgical population. Physiologically, a low preoperative TAPSE/sPAP ratio represents an exhausted functional reserve. While PEA removes the proximal mechanical obstruction, the RV must still adapt to acute hemodynamic shifts, increased pulmonary blood flow, and reperfusion stress post-surgery. An “uncoupled” RV—one already struggling to adapt to its afterload before surgery—lacks the compensatory capacity to handle these stresses, leading to persistent hypertension even after a technically successful clearance.

From a statistical perspective, we addressed the small numerical values of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio by multiplying it by 100 prior to regression analysis. This adjustment ensures that the reported OR reflect the risk change per 0.01-unit increase in the ratio, facilitating a more intuitive clinical interpretation. In our multivariable model, although the OR was numerically small, it remained significantly associated with lower odds of RPH (P = 0.046).

Although RHC is mandatory for the diagnosis and operability assessment of CTEPH, it is not suitable for frequent reassessment during the waiting period before surgery or during bridging PH-targeted therapy. In contrast, TAPSE/sPAP can be repeatedly obtained at the bedside and may provide dynamic information on RV-PA coupling and functional reserve between invasive evaluations. By combining PVR (reflecting vascular anatomy and resistance) with TAPSE/sPAP (reflecting the heart’s functional adaptation), clinicians obtain a more holistic preoperative view. From a practical perspective, this combined assessment may be most helpful in patients whose preoperative hemodynamic risk is not clearly low or high based on PVR alone. In such patients, a markedly reduced TAPSE/sPAP ratio may suggest limited RV functional reserve and may support closer perioperative monitoring and earlier preparation for postoperative pulmonary hypertension management. Although our study was not designed to test specific treatment strategies, these findings suggest that TAPSE/sPAP may help refine risk stratification when considered together with PVR. This allows for the early identification of high-risk individuals, enabling the optimization of surgical strategies and the intensification of perioperative support to mitigate the risks of postoperative death and long-term failure. This complementary value may help explain why TAPSE/sPAP remains clinically relevant even when PVR is already available. PVR mainly reflects pulmonary vascular resistance and afterload, whereas the TAPSE/sPAP ratio may provide additional information on how well the right ventricle adapts to that afterload. In this sense, TAPSE/sPAP is not intended to replace PVR, but to add a bedside echocardiographic measure of RV functional adaptation. This may be particularly useful when repeated bedside assessment is needed during the perioperative period, as echocardiography can be more easily repeated than invasive hemodynamic measurements. In addition, the clinical utility of the identified cut-off appears to lie more in its rule-out value than in confirmation of risk. Given its relatively high negative predictive value and lower positive predictive value, a TAPSE/sPAP ratio above 0.194 may suggest a relatively lower likelihood of early postoperative RPH and may therefore serve as a practical safety window during preoperative risk assessment.

One notable finding is the discrepancy between the AUC change and the reclassification metrics. Although the increase in AUC was not statistically significant by the DeLong test (P = 0.940), the likelihood ratio test (P = 0.012) and IDI (0.034, P = 0.005) suggest that the TAPSE/sPAP ratio may still provide complementary predictive information. In contrast, the continuous NRI showed only a numerical improvement (0.297, P = 0.117).

Our study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design. While the 1000-bootstrap internal validation supports the model’s stability and calibration, future prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate their impact on long-term survival.

In conclusion, preoperative TAPSE/sPAP and PVR are independent predictors of early RPH after PEA. Given the substantial impact of RPH on both early recovery and long-term prognosis, these markers may provide complementary information for preoperative risk stratification and identification of patients at higher perioperative risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, long-term follow-up data were not available, so we were unable to assess the relationship between early RPH and long-term survival. Third, a fixed definition of RPH was used in this study, which may limit comparisons with studies using other criteria. In addition, interobserver variability of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio was not assessed. Also, sPAP was estimated by Doppler echocardiography rather than invasive measurement, and the difference in tricuspid regurgitation severity between the two groups may have affected the accuracy and comparability of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to further examine the role of RV-PA coupling in the long-term course of CTEPH. At the same time, we acknowledge that immediate postoperative hemodynamics may still have been influenced by perioperative factors such as anesthesia depth, mechanical ventilation settings, vasoactive support, fluid status, and early ICU management, and this should be considered when interpreting the endpoint. Missing values were mainly related to incomplete availability of echocardiographic or hemodynamic measurements in the retrospective dataset, and no formal assumption of missing completely at random was made.
