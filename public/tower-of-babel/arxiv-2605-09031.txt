Spherical Boltzmann machines: a solvable theory of learning and generation in energy-based models
Thomas Tulinski, Simona Cocco, Rémi Monasson, Jorge Fernandez-de-Cossio-Diaz

Abstract
Energy-based models (EBMs) are flexible generative architectures inspired by statistical physics, but their learning and generative properties remain poorly understood. Here, we analyze a solvable EBM in the high-dimensional limit: the spherical Boltzmann machine (SBM). Combining tools from random matrix theory and dynamical mean-field theory, we: solve exact equations describing the training dynamics of the SBM; compute the Bayesian evidence, which acts as a partition function in parameter space and encodes global properties of the trained model; and uncover cascades of phase transitions that occur both during training and as a function of hyperparameters, related to successive alignment and condensation of the top modes of the coupling matrix to the data. We connect these transitions to sampling-time generative phenomena in a teacher-student scenario, including: sampling temperature tuning, double descent as a function of regularization strength, tempered posterior effects, and out-of-equilibrium effects during training that induce biases in the trained model. We provide numerical evidence demonstrating that all these phenomena appear in standard generative architectures, beyond the SBM. 1 1 1 Code: https://github.com/anonymous-avatar/spherical-bm-2026 .

Introduction

Energy-based models (EBMs) define complex probability distributions over high-dimensional spaces, placing mass on regions where realistic data lie while suppressing it elsewhere. The main ingredients are an energy function
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x};W)
, defined over data points
\mathbf{x}
and depending on parameters
W
, which is used to define a probability distribution through a Boltzmann law
P(\mathbf{x}|W)=e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x};W)}/Z_{\beta}(W)
, where
\beta
is an inverse sampling temperature and
Z_{\beta}(W)=\int e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x};W)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
a normalization constant known as the partition function in statistical physics. EBMs are competitive generative models in diverse applications, including images
(
21
)
, biological sequences
(
25
;
57
)
, and others. But the computation of
Z
requires a typically intractable integration over the high-dimensional point
\mathbf{x}
. As a consequence, training and sampling of EBMs often relies on approximate Monte Carlo (MC) techniques. Beyond computational cost, the intractable
Z
also blocks theoretical progress on the analysis of the trained model.

Contributions.

We study
spherical Boltzmann machines
(SBMs) trained on data in the high-dimensional limit. SBMs are solvable EBMs defined on the
N
-sphere. Combining recent results on finite-rank spherical-integral asymptotics
(
30
)
which encode the alignment of
W
’s eigenvectors with the data principal components, with a Coulomb-gas analysis of
W
’s eigenvalues as repelling charges in an external potential set by the data and the prior, we obtain a closed-form large-
N
theory of this model. We derive the equilibrium phase diagram of the trained SBM, which has qualitatively different properties in different hyperparameter settings; solve the high-dimensional Langevin dynamics of training via dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
(
15
)
uncovering a non-equilibrium phase transition in the training dynamics; and compute closed-form metrics of generative performance in a teacher-student setting.

Our solvable model predicts four effects, that we fully characterize:
sampling temperature tuning
, where tuning
\beta\neq 1
post-training can improve sample fidelity;
double descent
of generative metrics as a function of regularization;
tempered posterior
effects, where the performance of an ensemble of models weighted by the Bayes posterior improves if the posterior is annealed at a posterior temperature
\eta\neq 1
; and
out-of-equilibrium
training dynamics leading to eventual deterioration of the model during training.

We provide numerical evidence that all these phenomena arise in standard generative architectures, in qualitative agreement with the predictions from our solvable SBM theory. These results demonstrate that the phenomenology and mechanisms uncovered in this work extend beyond the theoretical setting of the SBM.

2
Spherical Boltzmann machines: Equilibrium and training dynamics

The SBM is defined on the
N
-sphere,
\mathcal{S}_{N}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=N\}
, with the energy function

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x};W)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}W\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{N},\quad W\in\mathrm{Sym}_{N},

(1)

The SBM is a classical model from the statistical physics of disordered systems
(
6
;
36
)
, arising as a continuous relaxation of Ising spin systems, in which the per-site constraint
x_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
is replaced by the global constraint
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=N
. Despite the quadratic energy,
P(\mathbf{x}|W)
is not Gaussian: it is the Bingham distribution of directional statistics
(
7
;
34
)
. The phenomenology described below depends critically on the spherical constraint. Whereas
W
is purely random in prior works, here the SBM is treated as an EBM trained on data, which imprints statistical structure in
W
that we characterize both at equilibrium and along the training dynamics.

Without loss of generality, one can assume that
\beta=1
during training, but
\beta\neq 1
is often tuned post-training with the hope of improving the quality of generated samples. Given a training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N}
, the likelihood assigned by the SBM depends on
\mathcal{D}
only through the empirical covariance matrix
C=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\mathbf{x}^{k}\mathbf{x}^{k\top}
. The model is then trained by ascent of the log-posterior. In the continuous-time limit, the training dynamics can be described by a Langevin equation,

\partial_{t}W(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(C-\frac{K}{N}\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\rangle_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{W(t)}}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{2}W(t)+\frac{\Omega(t)}{\sqrt{\eta N}},

(2)

where
\gamma>0
controls the strength of weight decay,
\Omega(t)
is a symmetric matrix-valued white noise satisfying
\left\langle\Omega_{ij}(t)\Omega_{kl}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)\delta(t-t^{\prime})
arising
e.g.
from minibatch sampling in stochastic gradient ascent, and
\eta>0
an inverse
learning temperature
controlling the amplitude of this noise.
It is important to distinguish the two inverse temperatures from each other:
\eta
controls the dispersion in the space of model parameters
W
, while
\beta
acts in the sample-space,
\mathbf{x}
.

The moment average of
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}
, also called the negative phase in the EBM literature, must be computed by drawing equilibrated samples from
P(\mathbf{x}|W(t))
, a daunting task in high-dimensional settings. In practice, this term is approximated by MC sampling. A popular variant of the Boltzmann machine learning algorithm consists of running persistent Markov chains, which are frequently updated before each gradient update of the weights
(
71
;
64
;
56
)
. In this case, the training dynamics can be modeled by the coupled Langevin equations
(
20
;
14
)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(C-\frac{K}{N}\mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)^{\top}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{2}W(t)+\frac{\Omega(t)}{\sqrt{\eta N}},

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\nu W(t)\mathbf{x}(t)-\kappa(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+\sqrt{2\nu}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(t),

(4)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)
is the persistent MC chain, with
\kappa(t)
a Lagrange multiplier enforcing
\|\mathbf{x}(t)\|^{2}=N
, the independent white noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)
satisfying
\left\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and a rate
\nu
controlling how many sampling steps are performed on the persistent MC chain per gradient update of the weights. In practice, increasing
\nu
requires spending more compute cycles updating the MC chains during training.

In the following paragraphs we present the solution of this model both at equilibrium and its training dynamics. Although we now focus on an undersampled regime, where the number of training samples
K
stays finite as
N\to\infty
, we show in App.
C
that the same theory applies to large datasets (
e.g.
scaling with
N
) provided they are effectively low-dimensional,
i.e.
if they lie in a thin
K^{\prime}
-dimensional slab: a finite number
K^{\prime}
of eigenvalues of
C
scale linearly with
K
while the remaining
K-K^{\prime}
stay sublinear.

2.1
Equilibrium phase diagram

In the limit
\nu\to\infty
,
\mathbf{x}(t)
approaches an equilibrium sample from
P(\mathbf{x}|W)
in a quasi-stationary
W
background. In this case, the trained
W(t\to\infty)
converges to the stationary distribution:

P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})=\frac{e^{\mathcal{L}(W;C)}}{\mathcal{Y}(C)},\quad\text{with }\quad\mathcal{L}(W;C)=\frac{N}{2}\eta\Trace CW-\frac{N}{4}\gamma\eta\Trace W^{2}-K\eta\ln Z(W),

(5)

a tempered posterior at temperature
1/\eta
, that interpolates between the standard Bayes posterior at
\eta=1
and the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) limit
\eta\to\infty
. The tempered evidence,
\mathcal{Y}(C)=\int_{W\in\mathrm{Sym}_{N}}e^{\mathcal{L}(W;C)}\,\mathrm{d}W
, plays the role of a partition function over the weights.

Changing variables to the eigendecomposition
W=\tfrac{1}{N}V\Lambda V^{\top}
, we can rewrite
\mathcal{L}(W;C)
as a Coulomb gas over eigenvalues
\Lambda
:
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{N}

(
40
)
,

\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}(\Lambda;C)=-\frac{N}{4}\gamma\eta\Trace\Lambda^{2}-K\eta\ln Z(\Lambda)+\ln\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{HCIZ}}(\Lambda;C)+\sum_{i<j}\ln|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|,

(6)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{HCIZ}}
is the real analogue of the Harish–Chandra–Itzykson–Zuber (HCIZ) integral
(
30
)
over the eigenvectors
V=(\mathbf{v}_{j})
, normalized to
\mathbf{v}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{j}=N\delta_{ij}
.

The position of the eigenvalues in the large
N
limit follows from optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
. A dominant
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
contribution comes from the terms
-\tfrac{N}{4}\gamma\eta\Trace\Lambda^{2}+\sum_{i<j}\ln|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|
, which do not involve the training data. These terms describe a standard Coulomb gas, with half-width parameter
\sigma=1/\sqrt{\gamma\eta}
. The bottom
N-K
eigenvalues then form a Wigner semicircle bulk,

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N-K}\sum_{i=K+1}^{N}\delta(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)\underset{N\to\infty}{\to}\frac{\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{|\lambda|\leq 2\sigma}.

(7)

C=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}c_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition of
C

\mathbf{c}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{c}_{j}=N\delta_{ij}

\mathrm{O}(N)
-rotation invariance of (
1

C
only through its eigenvalues,
c_{1}>\dots>c_{K}>0
. Stationarity of (
6

\mathcal{O}(N)

\lambda_{k}

\text{HCIZ pull away from bulk: }\frac{N}{2}\eta c_{k}u_{k}^{2}\qquad\text{partition push toward bulk: }-\frac{NK}{2}\eta h^{2}

(8)

where
u_{k}^{2}=\langle(\mathbf{c}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{k}/N)^{2}\rangle
is the overlap of the
k
-th eigenvector of

2.2
Training dynamics

We next analyze how the Langevin dynamics (
3
)–(
4
) reach the equilibrium
P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})
described in Sec.
2.1
. Equilibration is guaranteed only in the fast sampling limit
\nu\to\infty
; at finite
\nu
training can settle into a non-equilibrium stationary state qualitatively distinct from the equilibrium phase diagram. We initialize
W(0)
at random from the prior and
\mathbf{x}(0)
with a small seed overlap with the top data mode
\mathbf{c}_{1}
. Under these assumptions, Eq. (
3
) can be rewritten in the equivalent integrated form:

W(t)=W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}C-\frac{K}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}\frac{\mathbf{x}(u)\mathbf{x}(u)^{\top}}{N}\mathrm{d}u

(9)

where
W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)
is a standard Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) matrix process,

\partial_{t}W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)+\frac{\Omega(t)}{\sqrt{\eta N}},\quad W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(0)=W(0),

(10)

The first term in (
9
) is a growing GOE noise, the second a deterministic spike in the data directions, and the third a negative-phase feedback from the generated samples. Their combination produces a cascade of eigenvalue detachments from the bulk. At every time,
W(t)
consists of a Wigner bulk plus at most
K
upper outliers (proof in App.
B.1
).

Effective DMFT.

The Martin–Siggia–Rose path integral
(
44
)
, after the Gaussian weight and noise integrations, collapses (
3
)–(
4
) in the large-
N
limit onto a closed DMFT. The data overlap
s_{k}(t)=\mathbf{c}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)/N
, the correlator
Q(t,t^{\prime})=\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t^{\prime})/N
, and the causal response
R(t,t^{\prime})
satisfy the closed equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=-\kappa(t)s_{k}(t)+\frac{\nu}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})c_{k}s_{k}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}M(t,u)s_{k}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\kappa(t)R(t,t^{\prime})+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}M(t,u)R(u,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}u,

(12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}s_{k}(t)s_{k}(t^{\prime})-\kappa(t)Q(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}M(t,u)Q(u,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}D(t,u)R(t^{\prime},u)\mathrm{d}u,

(13)

subject to
Q(t,t^{\prime})=Q(t^{\prime},t)
,
R(t,t^{\prime})=0
if

,

, with
\kappa(t)
at all times fixed by the spherical constraint

. See App.
B

M(t,u)
and the noise correlator

.

3
Generative metrics in a teacher–student scenario

We now evaluate the generative performance of an SBM student trained on
K
samples drawn from a teacher model
\mathbf{x}^{k}\sim P^{\ast}

(
60
;
22
)
. We consider two student models. A
typical
student commits to a single weight matrix
W\sim P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})
drawn from the posterior, and then generates data from:
\mathbf{x}\sim P_{W}(\mathbf{x})=P(\mathbf{x}|W)
. The
posterior predictive
student instead marginalizes over the weights before generation, effectively drawing samples from an ensemble of trained models weighted by their posterior probabilities:
\mathbf{x}\sim P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})=\int P(\mathbf{x}|W)P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})\,\mathrm{d}W
.

The quality of a student generated sample
\mathbf{x}
is measured by its surprise under the teacher,
-\ln P^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})
. Averaging over
\mathbf{x}\sim P_{W}
yields the cross-entropy
\mathcal{H}[P_{W};P^{\ast}]=-\int P_{W}(\mathbf{x})\ln P^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. Minimizing this quantity alone, however, selects a singular student placing all its probability mass at the maximizer of
P^{\ast}
. Diversity collapse is undesirable for a generative model, so we are naturally led to the reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})=\mathcal{H}[P_{W};P^{\ast}]-\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]
, where the entropy
\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]=-\int P_{W}(\mathbf{x})\ln P_{W}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
penalizes students with poor diversity. Reversing the arguments defines the forward-KL,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{W})
. Whereas the reverse-KL is mode-seeking, concentrating student mass where
P^{\ast}
is large, the forward-KL is mode-covering, penalizing regions of
P^{\ast}
the student misses
(
8
)
. Both are widely used to train and evaluate generative models in practice.In the large
N
-limit we consider here, they are self-averaging: for any typical realization
W\sim P_{\eta}
, both KLs stay close to their average values,
\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}
and
\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{W})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}
, respectively, so we may omit the average brackets to lighten the notation.

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\mathrm{pp}})\leq\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{W})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}

In what follows we consider a simple SBM teacher model with rank-one weights

The forward and reverse KL divergences, for typical and posterior predictive students, are all computable in closed form in this setting. Each of the phenomena introduced above surfaces most naturally in a different KL, motivating our examination of all four KL variants. Detailed calculations are deferred to App.

Sampling temperature tuning.

After training, one can re-introduce a sampling temperature
1/\beta\neq 1
. What is the effect of tuning
\beta
at generation-time? The forward

is a convex function of
\beta

or

according to whether the entropy
\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]
is larger or smaller than the cross-entropy
\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast};P_{W}]
. Intuitively,
\beta>1
(
<1
) compensates for an excess (lack) of entropy in the trained model.

The MAP limit

4
Validation of phenomenology in standard generative architectures

To demonstrate that the qualitative phenomenology predicted by our theoretical model extends beyond the setting of the SBM, we conducted numerical experiments in standard generative architectures.

Sampling temperature tuning.
We trained Potts Boltzmann machines on protein sequences from Pfam
(
67
;
43
;
32
)
, at various hyperparameter settings. In sufficiently regularized models, the learned coupling matrix contain modes aligned to the top covariance modes of the data, that stay approximately silent in the generated data, reminiscent of the
h=0,u_{1}\neq 0
phase in the SBM. Tuning
\beta>1
up to an optimal value, rescues the silent modes in generated data, in a manner compatible with the mechanism depicted in Fig.
3
. See App.
F.1
.

Double descent.
We confirmed double descent as a function of regularization in various generative architectures: a normalizing flow on
\mathcal{S}_{N}

(
55
)
targeting the rank-one SBM teacher of Sec.
3
, a Gaussian-Bernoulli restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) trained on a financial dataset
(
24
;
38
)
, and an autoregressive belief-network trained on samples from the 2D Ising and a Curie-Weiss model, respectively. See App.
F.2
.

Tempered-posterior effects.
To the best of our knowledge, warm/cold posterior effects have not been studied in generative models. We trained Bayesian GANs
58
on synthetic datasets, and demonstrate that the optimal posterior temperature can be
<1
or
>1
, depending on the regularization, as in the SBM. See App.
F.3
.

Out-of-equilibrium training dynamics.
We trained Potts Boltzmann machines on protein sequence alignments at varying sampling rates
\nu
during training, observing overshooting of the top weight modes, with data overlaps that converge to sub-optimal values, reproducing the qualitative behavior of the SBM. See App.
F.4
.

5
Conclusion, limitations, and broader impact

Solvable models provide guidance to navigate complex phenomena in machine learning
(
61
)
. We introduced the SBM as a solvable EBM, derived its equilibrium phase diagram, solved its Langevin training dynamics via DMFT, and obtained teacher–student generative metrics in the high-dimensional limit.
Standard generative architectures reproduce qualitatively the predicted temperature tuning, double-descent as a function of regularization strength, tempered-posterior effects, and out-of-equilibrium training behavior. These effects depend critically on the spherical constraint of the SBM, which drives the condensation transition, and would not occur in a multivariate Gaussian model (see App.
E
for further discussion).

Limitations.

The SBM is a minimal solvable EBM rather than a realistic generative architecture.
Our analysis assumes effectively low-dimensional data (App.
C
).
Our derivations are heuristic in the statistical-physics sense (saddle-point evaluation of the Coulomb gas, MSR/DMFT closure of the Langevin dynamics) rather than rigorous proofs; results are expected to be asymptotically exact in the
N\to\infty
limit, and we verify them against finite-
N
simulations throughout.

Broader impact.

This is a theoretical paper on the statistics of generative models; it does not propose new models or release datasets, and we do not see a direct avenue for societal harm.

-\frac{\eta\Trace C}{2}h^{2}\epsilon_{*}+\sum_{k\leq d}\left\{-\frac{\gamma\eta}{2g_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\eta c_{k}u_{k}^{2}+\frac{g_{1}}{2}\right\}\epsilon_{k}+\sum_{k>d}\left\{-\frac{\gamma\eta}{2g_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\eta c_{k}u_{k}^{2}+\frac{g_{k}}{2}\right\}\epsilon_{k}\,.

The
\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\gamma\eta}},

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma\eta},

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta c_{k}}+\frac{c_{k}}{\gamma},

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c_{k}},

\displaystyle u_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta c_{k}\geq\sqrt{\gamma\eta}.

A.3
Computation of sample and weight statistics from the evidence integral

In this Appendix we establish the following two formulas:

\left\langle W\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=\frac{1}{N\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\eta\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right)\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}-I\right).

(48)

and

\frac{K}{N}\left\langle\left\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{W}}\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=C-\frac{\gamma}{N\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\eta\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right)\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}-I\right)

(49)

A.3.1
Perturbation theory of the evidence

Let
U
be a symmetric matrix of finite rank, with
\Trace U=0
. Calling
c_{k}^{\prime}
the eigenvalues of
C^{\prime}=C+zU
, first-order perturbation theory gives

c_{k}^{\prime}=c_{k}+\frac{z}{N}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}U\mathbf{c}_{k}+\mathcal{O}(z^{2})

(50)

for
k\leq K
(the non-zero eigenvalues of
C
are assumed non-degenerate, with eigenvectors
\mathbf{c}_{k}
normalized to
\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{c}_{k}=N
). Let
U^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname*{ker}(C)}U\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname*{ker}(C)}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname*{ker}(C)}=I-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}
. Then
C^{\prime}
gains
R=\Rank U^{\prime}
additional non-zero eigenvalues,

c_{j}^{\prime}=z\alpha_{j}+\mathcal{O}(z^{2}),\quad(j>K)

where
\alpha_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
U^{\prime}
.

The only explicit dependence on the eigenvalues of
C
comes from the HCIZ integral:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\mathcal{Y}(C+zU)\right|_{z=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left.\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{HCIZ}}(\Lambda;C+zU)}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0}

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\eta\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right)\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}U\mathbf{c}_{k}

where we used
\frac{1}{\eta c_{k}^{\prime}}G(\chi_{k})=1-u_{k}^{2}
and
(\lambda_{k}-\chi_{k})u_{k}^{2}=0
. For
j>K
,
c_{j}^{\prime}\to 0
gives
\chi_{j}=G^{-1}(\eta c_{j}^{\prime})\sim(\eta c_{j}^{\prime})^{-1}
, so
(\eta\chi_{j}-1/c_{j}^{\prime})(\partial c_{j}^{\prime}/\partial z)=\mathcal{O}(c_{j}^{\prime})\to 0
; the newborn modes

drop out.

A.3.2
Weight statistics

Directly differentiating
\ln\mathcal{Y}(C+zU)
in its integral representation gives

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\mathcal{Y}(C+zU)\right|_{z=0}=\frac{N\eta}{2}\left\langle\Trace UW\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}.

(52)

Combined with (
51
),

\left\langle\Trace UW\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=\frac{1}{N\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\eta\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right)\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}U\mathbf{c}_{k}

(53)

for any
U
with
\Trace U=0
. Since
\Trace\langle W\rangle_{P_{\eta}}=0
, this pins down the mean to Eq. (
48
).

A.3.3
Sample statistics

The same generating trick on the other side of the
\ln Z(W)
factor yields sample moments under the posterior predictive. Shifting
W\to W-zU
inside the posterior-weighted integral gives

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\int\exp\left\{-K\eta\ln Z(W-zU)-\frac{N\gamma\eta}{4}\Trace W^{2}+\frac{N\eta}{2}\Trace CW\right\}\mathrm{d}W\right|_{z=0}\\
=\frac{K\eta}{2}\left\langle\left\langle\mathbf{x}^{\top}U\mathbf{x}\right\rangle_{P_{W}}\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}.

(54)

Completing the square, the log of the integral equals
\ln\mathcal{Y}(C-z\gamma U)-\tfrac{N}{4}z^{2}\gamma\eta\Trace U^{2}+\tfrac{N\eta}{2}z\Trace CU
. Differentiating and using (
51
),

\displaystyle\frac{K\eta}{2}\left\langle\left\langle\mathbf{x}^{\top}U\mathbf{x}\right\rangle_{P_{W}}\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\mathcal{Y}(C-z\gamma U)\right|_{z=0}+\frac{N\eta}{2}\Trace CU

(55)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\eta\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right)\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\top}U\mathbf{c}_{k}+\frac{N\eta}{2}\Trace CU.

Since
\Trace\langle\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\rangle_{P_{W}}\rangle_{P_{\eta}}=N
, the arbitrariness of

then pins down the full covariance to Eq. (
49

A.4
Replica approach to equilibrium properties of trained SBM ensembles

The equilibrium properties of SBM ensembles learned from data were recently derived in an independent manner using the replica method from the statistical physics of disordered systems
[
66
]
. We summarize here how their approach relates to ours.

Effective theory.

The derivation is based on the rewriting

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(C)=\lim_{n\to-K\eta}\langle Z(W)^{n}\rangle_{W\sim\exp(-\frac{N\gamma\eta}{4}\operatorname{tr}W^{2}+\frac{N\eta}{2}\text{tr}(CW))},

(56)

where
n
is the replica number. Pretending that
n
is a positive integer,
Z(W)^{n}
is the partition function of a system made of
n
“replicas” of the SBM. The samples
\|\mathbf{x}^{a}\|^{2}=N
generated by replicas
a=1,\dots,n
are mutually independent conditionally on
W
. Integrating over
W
creates effective interactions of replicas with each other,
Q_{ab}=\mathbf{x}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{b}/N
, and with the data,
M_{ak}=\mathbf{x}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}_{k}/N
, both of which are introduced as auxiliary integration variables, leading to the expression

\displaystyle\langle Z^{n}\rangle\propto\int\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{d}Q\,\exp\left(N\Phi_{n}^{\text{rep}}(M,Q)\right)

(57)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{rep}}(M,Q)=\frac{\eta\mathrm{tr}(C^{2})}{4\gamma}+\frac{\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})}{4\gamma\eta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\mathrm{tr}(M\text{diag}(c_{k})M^{\top})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\ln(Q-MM^{\top})+\frac{n}{2}.

(58)

The spherical constraint
\Trace Q=n
is enforced by a Lagrange multiplier
\Phi_{\mathrm{rep}}\mapsto\Phi_{\mathrm{rep}}-\frac{\mu}{2}(\mathrm{tr}(Q)-n)
.

Saddle-point analysis.

It was shown in
[
66
]
that, to leading order in
N
, the integral
\langle Z^{n}\rangle
is invariant not only under permutations of replicas
a=1,\dots,n
, but also under orthogonal transformations in
n
-dimensional replica space. As a result, the effective action
\Phi_{\text{rep}}
depends on
M
and
Q
only through the squared norm of the
d\leq K
nonzero columns
\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{d}
of the former and eigenvalues of the latter, with
d
determined
a posteriori
from the optimizing of
\Phi_{\mathrm{rep}}
.

Integrating over
Q
and
M
by saddle point reveals that

(i)

columns of the optimal
M
are eigenvectors of the optimal
Q
, with eigenvalues
q_{1},\dots,q_{d}
;

(ii)

the optimal
Q
is isotropic in subspace orthogonal to their span, with eigenvalue
\widetilde{q}
;

(iii)

these
\text{O}(n)
-invariants are related via

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{k}}{\eta\gamma}+\frac{c_{k}}{\gamma}=\frac{q_{k}}{\eta\gamma}+\frac{1}{q_{k}-m_{k}^{2}}=\frac{\widetilde{q}}{\eta\gamma}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{q}},

(59)

where
m_{k}^{2}=\|\mathbf{m}_{k}\|^{2}/n
;

(iv)

it is sufficient for the spherical constraints
\|\mathbf{x}^{a}\|^{2}=N
to hold on average,

\displaystyle\Trace Q=n,

(60)

by
\text{O}(n)
-invariance to leading order in
N
.

The replica order parameter
m_{k}
tracks the overlap
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{c}_{k}/N
of generated samples with the data eigenvector
\mathbf{c}_{k}
, whereas the condensation parameter
h_{k}
of §
2.1
tracks the overlap
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{k}/N
with the
k
-th eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{k}
of
W
. At saddle the two are related by
m_{k}=h_{k}u_{k}
with
u_{k}=\mathbf{v}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{c}_{k}/N
the
W
-to-data alignment, so
m_{k}=0
whenever
h_{k}=0
or
u_{k}=0
. The phase labels below are written in the main-text
(h,u)
convention of §
2.1
for direct cross-referencing.

Eqs. (
58
)-(
60
) are valid provided
n>n_{c}
where

\displaystyle n_{c}=-K\eta\,\frac{\sum_{k\leq d}\frac{c_{k}}{K}(1-\frac{1}{c_{k}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\eta}})}{1-\sqrt{\eta\gamma}}.

(61)

The boundary of the
h,u_{1}\neq 0
edge phase solves the equation
n(\eta)=n_{c}(\gamma,\eta,c_{1},\dots,c_{d})
, which is equivalent to Eq. (
45
). For
n<n_{c}
, the solution
\{m_{k}(n),\widetilde{q}(n)\}
obtained for

is frozen, and the analytical continuation of
\Phi_{\text{rep}}(n)
viewed as a function of
n
is
\Phi_{\text{rep}}(n)=\Phi_{\text{rep}}(n_{c})+(n-n_{c})\Phi_{\text{rep}}^{\prime}(n_{c})
, see
[
52

66
]
for an interpretation of the freezing.

Eq. (
60
) is quadratic in
\widetilde{q}
. The solution,
\widetilde{q}(d)
, depends on
d
and equals the coalesced

, Eq. (
41
). The conditions determining

can be derived from the local stability of the

[
66
]
, and are
\gamma/c_{d}<\widetilde{q}(d)<\min\{1,\sqrt{\eta\gamma},\gamma/c_{d+1}\}
for

From Eqs. (

has

\displaystyle\frac{\eta c_{k}^{2}}{4\gamma}+\frac{q_{k}^{2}}{4\gamma\eta}+\frac{c_{k}m_{k}^{2}}{2\gamma}=\begin{cases}\frac{g_{1}^{2}}{4\gamma\eta}+\frac{\gamma\eta}{4g_{1}^{2}},&k\leq d,\\

(62)

Substituting into

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{rep}}(-K\eta)=\frac{\eta}{4\gamma}\sum_{k>d}c_{k}^{2}+\frac{d\gamma\eta}{4g_{1}^{2}}-\frac{Kg_{1}^{2}}{4\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\ln\frac{\gamma}{c_{k}}-\frac{K\eta+d}{2}\ln g_{1}-\frac{K\eta}{2}.

\displaystyle\frac{K}{2}\ln(\gamma\eta)-\frac{K-d}{2}\ln(\gamma\eta)-\frac{K\eta}{2}\ln g_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\leq d}\ln(g_{1}\eta\,c_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\leq d}\ln\frac{\gamma}{c_{k}}-\frac{K\eta+d}{2}\ln g_{1},

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{rep}}=\frac{\eta}{4\gamma}\sum_{k>d}c_{k}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{\gamma}}\Big(\sum_{k\leq d}c_{k}-K\Big)+\frac{K\eta}{4}-\frac{3d}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\leq d}\ln\frac{\gamma}{c_{k}}-\frac{K\eta+d}{4}\ln(\gamma\eta),

\langle W_{\mathrm{GOE},ij}(t)W_{\mathrm{GOE},kl}(t^{\prime})\rangle=(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})e^{-\gamma|t-u|/2}/(N\gamma\eta)
, and

W(t)=W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}\frac{\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^{\top}}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}\frac{\mathbf{x}(u)\mathbf{x}(u)^{\top}}{N}\,\mathrm{d}u.

(68)

Representing the Langevin equations as delta functionals with response fields
\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)
and
\hat{W}(t)
, averaging over the noises, and integrating out
W,\hat{W}
using (

(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}})
alone,

\mathcal{Z}\propto\int\mathcal{D}[\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}]\,e^{-N\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{x}}[\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}]},

(69)

with action

N\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{x}}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\left\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{x}}+\kappa(t)\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\nu\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}-\frac{\nu}{\gamma N}(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\}\\
+\frac{1}{N}\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u\,\Theta(t-u)\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}\,\biggl\{\tfrac{\nu}{2}(\mathbf{x}(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}(u))(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}(u))\\
-\tfrac{\nu^{2}}{\eta\gamma}(\mathbf{x}(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}(u))(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u))-\tfrac{\nu^{2}}{\eta\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}(u))(\mathbf{x}(t)\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u))\biggr\},

(70)

in which the Heaviside
\Theta(t-u)
is inherited from the causal structure of (

only through the rotation-invariant combinations
\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(u)
,
\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u)
, and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u)
. Introducing the order parameters

of (

s
through Hubbard–Stratonovich identities, and by

(response fields generate no equal-time variance), the saddle reduces to a sum of decoupled one-site contributions at fixed
c
,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{x}}[\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathfrak{a}[x_{i},\hat{x}_{i}|c_{i}],

(71)

from which the effective single-site Gaussian process reads

\dot{x}(t)=-\kappa(t)x(t)+\frac{\nu}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})s(t)\,c+\int_{0}^{t}M(t,u)\,x(u)\,\mathrm{d}u+\zeta(t),

(72)

with the kernels

and

of (

\langle\zeta(t)\zeta(u)\rangle=D(t,u)

\langle x(t)|c\rangle=c\,h(t)
for a scalar

(using

) closes the signal equation. The two-time order parameters then satisfy the ODEs (
11
)–(
13
) of Sec.
2.2
, supplemented by
R(t,u)=0
for

\lim_{t\to u^{+}}R(t,u)=1

\kappa(t)

Stationary regime.
For long times, the system reaches a stationary, time-translation invariant, regime, for which:
Q(t,u)\to Q_{\mathrm{st}}(t-u)
,
R(t,u)\to R_{\mathrm{st}}(t-u)
,
s(t)\to s_{\mathrm{st}}
,
\kappa(t)\to\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}
, with
Q_{\mathrm{st}}(-\tau)=Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)
. The stationary kernels collapse to

M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)=e^{-\gamma\tau/2}\left\{-\frac{K\nu}{2}Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\eta\gamma}R_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\right\},\quad D_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)=2\nu\delta(\tau)+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\eta\gamma}e^{-\gamma|\tau|/2}Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau).

(73)

The stationary equations become,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(-\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}+\frac{\nu c_{a}}{\gamma}+\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right)s_{a,\mathrm{st}},\qquad a=1,\dots,K,

(74)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}R_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)+\int_{0}^{\tau}M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\sigma)R_{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma,

(75)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{\gamma}\sum_{a=1}^{K}c_{a}s_{a,\mathrm{st}}^{2}-\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)+\int_{0}^{\tau}M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\sigma)Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau+\sigma)Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{0}^{\infty}D_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau+\sigma)R_{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma

(76)

These equations always admit a solution with
s_{\mathrm{st},k}=0
. The corresponding solution of
R_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau),Q_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau),\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}
with
s_{\mathrm{st}}=0
will be denoted by
R_{P}(\tau),Q_{P}(\tau),\kappa_{P}
.

A condensed solution may also exist, satisfying
\kappa_{\mathrm{st}}=\nu c_{a}/\gamma+\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau
. To locate its onset, we linearize the stationary signal equation around the uncondensed bath
(R_{P},Q_{P},\kappa_{P})
and set
s(t)=s_{0}e^{zt}
. The memory integral evaluates as
\int_{0}^{t}M_{P}(t-u)e^{zu}\mathrm{d}u\to e^{zt}\check{M}_{P}(z)
at large
t
, with
\check{M}_{P}(z)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z\tau}M_{P}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
, so the signal equation reduces to

z+\kappa_{P}-\check{M}_{P}(z)=\frac{\nu c_{a}}{\gamma}.

(77)

The Laplace transform of the uncondensed-bath response equation
\partial_{\tau}R_{P}(\tau)=-\kappa_{P}R_{P}(\tau)+\int_{0}^{\tau}M_{P}(\tau-\sigma)R_{P}(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
with
R_{P}(0)=1
gives

\check{R}_{P}(z)=\frac{1}{z+\kappa_{P}-\check{M}_{P}(z)}.

(78)

Substituting (
78
) into (
77
) yields the spectral equation

1=\frac{\nu c_{a}}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z\tau}R_{P}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(79)

whose marginal solution
z=0
isolates the static condensation threshold of mode
a
,
\nu_{c,a}=\gamma/(c_{a}\chi_{P})
, with
\chi_{P}=\int_{0}^{\infty}R_{P}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
. Since
c_{1}>\dots>c_{K}
, mode
1
destabilizes first, fixing the dynamical phase boundary at

.

Numerically, the two-time system (

)–(

B.1
Upper bound on the outlier count

The decomposition (
9
) implies that
W(t)
carries at most
K
upper outliers at every fixed time. The negative-phase integral

\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}\frac{\mathbf{x}(u)\mathbf{x}(u)^{\top}}{N}\mathrm{d}u\;\succeq\;0

(80)

is positive semidefinite, so

(81)

Monotonicity of ordered eigenvalues under the Loewner order then gives
\lambda_{j}(W(t))\leq\lambda_{j}(W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)+\tfrac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}C)
for every
j\geq 1
, where
\lambda_{j}(\,\cdot\,)
labels eigenvalues in decreasing order. Since
C
has rank
K
and is positive semidefinite, the min-max principle yields

\lambda_{K+j}\left(W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}C\right)\leq\lambda_{j}\left(W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)\right),\qquad(j\geq 1),

(82)

and in particular
\lambda_{K+1}(W(t))\leq\lambda_{1}(W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t))
. With the stationary GOE initialization
W(0)\sim e^{-N\gamma\eta\Trace W^{2}/4}

W_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t)
remains GOE at every time, with semicircle edge
2/\sqrt{\gamma\eta}
; hence for every
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability at large
N
,

\lambda_{K+1}(W(t))\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\gamma\eta}}+\varepsilon.

(83)

Therefore, no more than
K
eigenvalues of
W(t)
are detached above the bulk edge at any given time.

Appendix C
Nearly finite-dimensional data

This appendix shows that the static and dynamical solutions obtained here are valid descriptions of SBM training on
K\to\infty
data living in a
K^{\prime}
-dimensional slab embedded in
N
-dimensional space,
N\to\infty
. The condition is that
C
has
K^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(1)
eigenvalues which are
\mathcal{O}(K)
while the remaining
\min\{N,K\}-K^{\prime}
stay
o(K)
. The relative scaling of
N
and
K
, it turns out, is irrelevant. Depending on the scaling of
t
and
\gamma,\eta,\nu
with
K
, different regimes arise, some of which we characterize below.

C.1
Statics

C.1.1
Bare large-
K
regime

We consider the regime
\gamma,\eta=\mathcal{O}(1)
. This regime was first characterized through a replica analysis in
[
66
]
. We summarize their main findings in the following paragraph.

A typical
W\sim P_{\eta}(\cdot|\mathcal{D})
after training is found to exhibit the following properties. Its eigenvectors accurately recover the manifold directions,
\mathbf{v}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}=\mathbf{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}
, provided extensive modes are non-degenerate, and
h_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}^{2}=\widetilde{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}
where
\widetilde{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}=c_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}/K
. Generated data
\mathbf{x}\sim P_{W}
have vanishing projections along the other directions as long as

\gamma>\gamma_{\text{bulk}}\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma_{\text{bulk}}=c_{K^{\prime}+1}\left(\delta_{K}+\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}c_{K^{\prime}+1}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad\delta_{K}=1-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K^{\prime}}c_{\ell}\,.

(85)

Here
\delta_{K}
is the fraction of variance unexplained by the
K^{\prime}
projections of the generated data along
\mathbf{v}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}
. When
\gamma>\gamma_{\mathrm{bulk}}
, the
\eta_{c}(\gamma,c_{1,\dots,K})
delimiting the boundary of the edge phases with the others, scales like
K^{\prime}/K
for thin slabs,
\delta_{K}\ll K^{-1/2}
, and like
\gamma/\delta_{K}^{2}
for thick slabs,
\delta_{K}\gg K^{-1/2}
.

C.2
Dynamics

C.2.1
Bare large-
K
regime

We consider the regime

\displaystyle t,\gamma,\eta,\nu=\mathcal{O}(1).

(86)

Large-
K
limit of the DMFT

We take
K\to\infty
in the DMFT derived sending
N\to\infty
first while holding
K
fixed. For large
K
, the noise correlator stays
\mathcal{O}(1)
. The memory kernel behaves as

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(t,u)\equiv\frac{M(t,u)}{K}=-\frac{\nu}{2}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}(t-u)}Q(t,u)+\mathcal{O}(K^{-1}),\qquad t>u,

(87)

dominated by the negative phase.

Using that
\kappa(t)
satisfies
\partial_{t}Q(t,t)=0
, we get that
\kappa(t)
also scales linearly with
K
, as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\kappa}(t)\equiv\frac{\kappa(t)}{K\nu}=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K^{\prime}}\widetilde{c}_{\ell}s_{\ell}(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(K^{-1}).

(88)

On
\mathcal{O}(1)
timescales, the signals
s_{\ell}(t),s_{k}(t)
(
\ell\leq K^{\prime}<k
) are quasistatic, with

\displaystyle\!\!\!\begin{cases}0=-\widetilde{\kappa}(t)s_{\ell}(t)+(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\frac{\widetilde{c}_{\ell}}{\gamma}s_{\ell}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)s_{\ell}(u)+o(1),\\
0=-\widetilde{\kappa}(t)s_{k}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)s_{k}(u)+o(1).\end{cases}

(89)

The correlation function also behaves quasistatically on finite timescales,

\displaystyle\!\!0

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\kappa}(t)Q(t,t^{\prime})+(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\sum_{\ell}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{\ell}}{\gamma}s_{\ell}(t)s_{\ell}(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)Q(u,t^{\prime}),

(90)

while the response function obeys

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime})=-K\nu\widetilde{\kappa}(t)R(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{K\nu}{2}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)R(u,t^{\prime})+O(1),\quad t>t^{\prime},

(91)

together with
R(t,t)=1
. Since the restoring force is linear in
K\nu
, we expect small perturbations at a time
t^{\prime}<t
to be suppressed on fast time scales
t-t^{\prime}\sim 1/K\nu
. Inspecting
R(t,t^{\prime})
close to the diagonal,
t=t^{\prime}+\theta/K\nu
, we see that the integrand of the memory term is
\mathcal{O}(1)
, so the integral is
\sim 1/K\nu
, and
R(t,t^{\prime})\sim e^{-K\nu\widetilde{\kappa}(t^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})}\downarrow 0
(
t>t^{\prime}
).

Large
K
non-equilibrium stationary state

Now take
t,t^{\prime}\to\infty
, with
\tau=t-t^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and write
Q(t,t^{\prime}),R(t,t^{\prime})\to Q_{\text{st}}(\tau),R_{\text{st}}(\tau)
,
s_{\ell}(t),s_{k}(t),\kappa(t)\to s_{\ell,\text{st}},s_{k,\text{st}},\kappa_{\text{st}}
.

The
\mathcal{O}(1)
bulk modes stay uncondensed,
s_{k}=0
(
k>D)
, meanwhile extensive modes satisfy

\displaystyle 0=\left[\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\gamma\tau/2}Q_{\text{st}}(\tau)-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{\ell}}{\gamma}\right]s_{\ell,\text{st}},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,K^{\prime}.

(92)

Hence, only modes with the same maximum rescaled eigenvalue
\widetilde{c}_{\max}
can condense.

The stationary response function satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{\text{st}}(\tau)=-K\nu\left[\widetilde{\kappa}_{\mathrm{st}}R_{\mathrm{st}}(\tau)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-\gamma\sigma/2}Q_{\text{st}}(\sigma)R_{\text{st}}(\tau-\sigma)\right]

(93)

together with
R_{\mathrm{st}}(0^{+})=1
. Taking a one-sided Laplace transform
\check{R}(p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\tau p}R(\tau)
yields

\displaystyle\check{R}_{\text{st}}(p)=\frac{1}{p+K\nu(\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-(p+\gamma/2)\tau}Q_{\text{st}}(\tau))}.

(94)

In particular,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,R_{\text{st}}(\tau)=\check{R}(0)=\frac{\gamma}{K\nu\widetilde{c}_{\max}}\downarrow 0
.

The equation for the stationary correlation function is

\displaystyle 0=-\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}Q_{\text{st}}(\tau)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{\ell}\widetilde{c}_{\ell}s_{\ell,{\text{st}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-\gamma\sigma/2}Q_{\text{st}}(\sigma)Q_{\text{st}}(\tau-\sigma).

(95)

Writing
\mathbf{x}(t)=\langle\mathbf{x}(t)\rangle+\delta\mathbf{x}(t)
with
\langle\delta\mathbf{x}(t)\rangle=0
, one has
Q_{\text{st}}(\tau)=q+C(\tau)
,
q=\sum_{\ell\leq K^{\prime}}\delta_{c_{\ell},c_{\max}}s_{\ell}^{2}
,
C(\tau)=\langle\delta\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\delta\mathbf{x}(t-\tau)\rangle/N
. Combining the multiplier and signal equations,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\gamma\tau/2}C(\tau)^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma}(1-q)(q-\widetilde{c})+\frac{1-2q}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\gamma\tau/2}C(\tau).

(96)

From Eq. (
72
), fluctuations obey the linear equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta\mathbf{x}=-K\nu\left[\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}\delta\mathbf{x}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-\gamma\sigma/2}Q_{\text{st}}(\sigma)\delta\mathbf{x}(t-\sigma)\right]+\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t).

(97)

When
\delta\mathbf{x}(t)
varies slowly,
\delta\mathbf{x}(t-\sigma)\approx\delta\mathbf{x}(t)
over the memory window
\sigma\sim 2\gamma^{-1}
and then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta\mathbf{x}\approx-K\nu[\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-\gamma\sigma/2}Q_{\text{st}}(\sigma)]\delta\mathbf{x}(t)
. Stationarity implies that the bracket equals
\widetilde{c}_{\max}/\gamma>0
, hence slow fluctuations are suppressed, and the overlap of
C(\tau)
with kernels of a fixed
\mathcal{O}(1)
width vanishes. In particular,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\gamma\tau/2}C(\tau)=0
. Using
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\gamma\tau/2}C(\tau)=0
, Eq. (
96
) gives
q\geq\widetilde{c}_{\max}
since
q\leq 1
and LHS
>0
, while Eq. (
92
) gives
\gamma\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}=\widetilde{c}_{\max}-q
, whence
q\leq\widetilde{c}_{\max}
. As a result,
q=\widetilde{c}_{\max}
and
\Delta=\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}=0
. We conclude that
Q_{\text{st}}(\tau)=q
with
\tau>0
for
K\to\infty
. Contrast this with the finite-
K
case, where we expect
Q_{\text{st}}(\tau)
to plateau only when
\tau\to\infty
.

The non-equilibrium stationary state is thus characterized by

\displaystyle q=\sum_{\ell}\delta_{c_{\ell},c_{\max}}s_{\ell}^{2}=\min\{1,\widetilde{c}_{\max}\},\qquad\widetilde{\kappa}_{\text{st}}=\frac{(\widetilde{c}_{\max}-1)_{+}}{\gamma},

(98)

independently of
\eta
and
\nu
. To leading order in
K
, then, the negative phase cancels the contribution to
W
of the top extensive mode in the positive phase, see Eq. (
9
). As a result, at long times, the top mode of
W
is not the sampled direction, but the largest extensive unsampled direction.

Validity of the bare large-
K
regime

The solution above was derived by sending
K\to\infty
after having first taken
N\to\infty
holding
K
fixed. Inspection of Eq. (
70
) shows that the relative scaling of
K
and
N
is irrelevant, as long
N,K\to\infty
. After power counting,
N\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{x}}=NK\mathcal{A}_{\text{eff}}+o(NK)
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{eff}}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\widetilde{\kappa}(t)\frac{\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)}{N}-\int\mathrm{d}t\,(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\sum_{\ell\leq K^{\prime}}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{\ell}}{\gamma}\frac{\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}{N}\frac{\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}{N}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t>u}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)\frac{\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(u)}{N}.

(99)

The correction consists of the kinetic term, the spin noise, the weight noise, and the bulk modes

\displaystyle o(NK)=\begin{cases}\int\mathrm{d}t\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t),\\
-\nu\int\mathrm{d}t\,\kappa(t)\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t),\\
\frac{K\nu}{2N}\int_{t>u}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}\left[(\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(u))(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u))+(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(u))(\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}(u))\right],\\
-\nu\int\mathrm{d}t\,(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})\sum_{k>D}\frac{c_{k}}{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{k})(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{k})).\end{cases}

The leading action
\mathcal{A}_{\text{eff}}
reproduces the large-
K
equations already analyzed.

C.2.2
Invariant large-
K
regime

We now show that the DMFT (
11
)-(
67
) is left invariant under the rescaling

\displaystyle\overline{t}=\frac{K}{K^{\prime}}t,\quad\gamma=\frac{K}{K^{\prime}}\overline{\gamma},\quad\eta=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}\overline{\eta},\quad\nu=\frac{K}{K^{\prime}}\overline{\nu},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\overline{t},\overline{\gamma},\overline{\eta},\overline{\nu}=\mathcal{O}(1),

(100)

and describes the large-
K
Langevin training dynamics Eq. (
3
)-(
4
) on nearly finite-
K^{\prime}
training data.

We introduce

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}=K^{\prime}\widetilde{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}\Big(\!\!=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}c_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}\Big),\qquad\overline{C}=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}C,

(101)

together with the scaled-time dynamical variables

\displaystyle\overline{W}(\overline{t})=W(t),\qquad\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})=\mathbf{x}(t),\qquad\overline{\kappa}(\overline{t})=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}\kappa(t).

(102)

The scaled covariance
\overline{C}
has eigenvalues
\overline{c}_{1,\dots,K}=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}c_{1,\dots,K}
, for which
\overline{c}_{1,\dots,K^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
while
\overline{c}_{K^{\prime}+1,\dots,K}=o(1).
Under these scalings,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{W}}{\mathrm{d}\overline{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{C}-\frac{K^{\prime}}{N}\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})^{\top}\right)-\frac{\overline{\gamma}}{2}\overline{W}(\overline{t})+\frac{\overline{\Omega}(\overline{t})}{\sqrt{N\overline{\eta}}},

(103)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\overline{t}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nu}\overline{W}(\overline{t})\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})-\overline{\kappa}(t)\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})+\sqrt{2\overline{\nu}}\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\overline{t}),

(104)

where
\overline{\Omega}(\overline{t}),\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\overline{t})
, denote white noises in the scaled time
\overline{t}
. Following the same steps as in App.
B
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{s}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\overline{t}}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\mu}(\overline{t})\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})+\frac{\overline{\nu}}{\overline{\gamma}}\left(1-e^{-\overline{\gamma}\,\overline{t}/2}\right)\overline{c}_{k}\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})+\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{u})\,\mathrm{d}\overline{u},

(105)

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{t}}\overline{R}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\overline{\mu}(\overline{t})\,\overline{R}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})+\int_{\overline{t}^{\prime}}^{\overline{t}}\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\,\overline{R}(\overline{u},\overline{t}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\overline{u},

(106)

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{t}}\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\nu}}{\overline{\gamma}}\left(1-e^{-\overline{\gamma}\,\overline{t}/2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{c}_{k}\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t}^{\prime})-\overline{\mu}(\overline{t})\,\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})

(107)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\,\overline{Q}(\overline{u},\overline{t}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\overline{u}+\int_{0}^{\overline{t}^{\prime}}\overline{D}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\,\overline{R}(\overline{t}^{\prime},\overline{u})\,\mathrm{d}\overline{u}.

(108)

where

\displaystyle\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{K^{\prime 2}}{K^{2}}M(t,t^{\prime})=e^{-\overline{\gamma}(\overline{t}-\overline{u})/2}\left\{\frac{\overline{\nu}^{2}}{\overline{\eta}\,\overline{\gamma}}\,\overline{R}(\overline{t},\overline{u})-\frac{K^{\prime}\overline{\nu}}{2}\,\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\right\},

(109)

\displaystyle\overline{D}(\overline{t},\overline{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{K^{\prime 2}}{K^{2}}D(t,t^{\prime})=2\overline{\nu}\,\delta(\overline{t}-\overline{u})+\frac{\overline{\nu}^{2}}{\overline{\eta}\,\overline{\gamma}}\,e^{-\overline{\gamma}|\overline{t}-\overline{u}|/2}\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{u}),

(110)

and we introduced the scaled-time dynamical order parameters

\displaystyle\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})=s_{k}(t),\qquad\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=Q(t,t^{\prime}),\qquad\overline{R}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=R(t,t^{\prime}).

(111)

To complete the reduction, we show that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{c}_{k}\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K^{\prime}}\overline{c}_{k}\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t})\,\overline{s}_{k}(\overline{t}^{\prime}).

(112)

Let

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})^{\top}\overline{B}\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t}^{\prime}),\qquad\text{with}\qquad\overline{B}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a=K^{\prime}+1}^{K}\overline{c}_{a}\,\mathbf{c}_{a}\mathbf{c}_{a}^{\top},

(113)

be the remainder of the above approximation. By the spherical constraint:

\displaystyle|\Delta(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})|\leq\|\overline{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{\frac{\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t})\|^{2}}{N}\frac{\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\|^{2}}{N}}=\|\overline{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\overline{c}_{K^{\prime}+1}=o(K).

(114)

Thus
\Delta(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})\to 0
as
K
grows, uniformly in
\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime}
by the spherical constraint, and the claim follows. Fig.
11
validates this scaled DMFT against finite-
N
Langevin simulations on nearly finite-dimensional training data, sampled from the two-dimensional Ising model below the critical temperature (
K^{\prime}=1
).

C.2.3
Recovering the bare from the invariant large-
K
regime

We now check that the bare large-
K
regime of Sec.
C.2.1
is recovered as a boundary limit of the invariant scaling of Sec.
C.2.2
.

Let

\varepsilon=\frac{K^{\prime}}{K}\to 0.

If the bare variables
t,\gamma,\nu,\eta
are kept
O(1)
, then in the invariant variables one has

\overline{\gamma}=\varepsilon\gamma\to 0,\qquad\overline{\nu}=\varepsilon\nu\to 0,\qquad\overline{\eta}=\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\to\infty,\qquad\overline{t}=\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\to\infty.

This is the boundary of the invariant scaling corresponding to the bare regime.

Since the scaled covariance is
\overline{C}=\varepsilon C
, its leading eigenvalues translate across the two regimes as

\overline{c}_{\ell}=\varepsilon c_{\ell}=K^{\prime}\widetilde{c}_{\ell},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,K^{\prime}.

We use

\overline{s}_{\ell}(\overline{t})=s_{\ell}(t),\qquad\overline{Q}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=Q(t,t^{\prime}),\qquad\overline{R}(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=R(t,t^{\prime}),\qquad\overline{\kappa}(\overline{t})=\varepsilon\kappa(t).

Injecting

\overline{\gamma}(\overline{t}-\overline{u})=\gamma(t-u),\qquad\frac{\overline{\nu}^{2}}{\overline{\eta}\,\overline{\gamma}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\nu^{2}}{\eta\gamma}=O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad K^{\prime}\overline{\nu}=K^{\prime}\varepsilon\nu,

in the scaled memory kernel (
109
) gives

\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})=-\frac{K^{\prime}\varepsilon\nu}{2}e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Since
\mathrm{d}\overline{u}=\mathrm{d}u/\varepsilon
, the memory integral has a finite limit

\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\mathrm{d}\overline{u}\,\overline{M}(\overline{t},\overline{u})\overline{s}_{\ell}(\overline{u})=-\frac{K^{\prime}\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}Q(t,u)s_{\ell}(u)+o(1).

Using
\partial_{\overline{t}}=\varepsilon\partial_{t}
and
\varepsilon\to 0
in Eq. (
105
) gives the bare large-
K
quasistatic signal equation, Eq. (
89
).

The same reduction applies to the correlation equation. The smooth part of

\overline{D}
is
O(\varepsilon^{2})
, and the white-noise term does not contribute
to the off-diagonal equation
t>t^{\prime}
. From there, using
\partial_{\overline{t}}=\varepsilon\partial_{t}
and
\varepsilon\to 0
once again gives back the bare large-
K
quasistatic correlation equation, Eq. (
90
).

Applying similar manipulations turns Eq. (
106
) into Eq. (
91
), thereby concluding the reduction.

Teacher-generated training data

The teacher is the rank-one SBM

of Sec.

\mathbf{w}^{\ast}

, we have almost surely that

has the two eigenvalues:

c_{1}=2-\frac{1}{\omega^{\ast}},\qquad c_{2}=\frac{1}{\omega^{\ast}}.

(115)

The corresponding eigenvectors
\mathbf{c}_{1,2}
satisfy:

\frac{(\mathbf{c}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{\ast})^{2}}{N}=1-\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1},\quad\frac{(\mathbf{c}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\ast})^{2}}{N}=0

(116)

to leading order in

\frac{1}{N}\ln Z(W^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e)+\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1-\ln\omega^{\ast}}{2},\quad\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast}]=\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\omega^{\ast}

(117)

where we used
\ln Z(0)=\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi e)
.

D.2
Posterior predictive as a ratio of evidence integrals

We compute:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|C)=\langle P(\mathbf{x}|W)\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=\int P(\mathbf{x}|W)P_{\eta}(W|C)\mathrm{d}W

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Y}(C)}\int\exp\left\{\frac{N}{2}\Trace\left[\left(\eta C+\frac{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}}{N}\right)W\right]-(K\eta+1)\ln Z(W)-\frac{N}{4}\gamma\eta\Trace W^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}W

Defining
C_{\mathbf{x}}=C+\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}}{N}
, so that
\eta\Trace C_{\mathbf{x}}=K\eta+1
, the integrand is
e^{\mathcal{L}(W;C_{\mathbf{x}})}
. Then

P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|C)=\frac{\mathcal{Y}(C_{\mathbf{x}})}{\mathcal{Y}(C)},\qquad C_{\mathbf{x}}=C+\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}}{N}.

(119)

This identity, noticed in
[
66
]
, reduces the posterior predictive to evaluating the evidence at a low-rank perturbation of
C
, so we can reuse the evidence formulas derived above rather than computing a separate
W
integral.

D.3
MAP and flat limits of the posterior predictive

P_{\mathrm{pp},\eta}(\mathbf{x}|C)
interpolates between the flat measure on
\mathcal{S}_{N}
for
\eta\downarrow 0
and the MAP likelihood for
\eta\uparrow\infty
.

Flat posterior predictive

In the
\eta\to 0^{+}
limit, the posterior predictive is uniform on the sphere,

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{N}\log P_{\mathrm{pp},\eta}(\mathbf{x}|C)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e)+o_{N}(1).

(120)

P_{\mathrm{pp},\eta}(\mathbf{x}|C)
depends on
\eta
through
P_{\eta}(W|C)\propto e^{-\frac{N\gamma\eta}{4}\Trace W^{2}+\frac{N\eta}{2}\Trace CW-K\eta\ln Z(W)}
.

We argue that, under the rescaling
W\mapsto\eta^{-1/2}\widetilde{W}
, and assuming that
\eta^{1/2}\Trace C\to 0^{+}
, the dominant contribution to the exponent is
-\frac{N\gamma}{4}\Trace\widetilde{W}^{2}+o(1)
. To start, since
|\Trace C\widetilde{W}|\leq\Trace C\,\|\widetilde{W}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, we have

\displaystyle\eta\Trace CW=\eta^{1/2}\Trace\widetilde{W}C=o(1)

(121)

for a typical
\widetilde{W}
with
\|\widetilde{W}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. To bound
\eta\ln Z(\eta^{-1/2}\widetilde{W})
, use

\displaystyle N\lambda_{\min}(W)\leq\mathbf{x}^{\top}W\mathbf{x}\leq N\lambda_{\max}(W),

(122)

which holds pointwise on
\mathcal{S}_{N}
. Exponentiating and integrating over
\mathcal{S}_{N}
gives

\displaystyle 0<Z(0)e^{\frac{N}{2}\lambda_{\min}}\leq Z(W)\leq Z(0)e^{\frac{N}{2}\lambda_{\max}}.

(123)

Taking logarithms and using
|\lambda_{\min}|,|\lambda_{\max}|\leq\|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\ln Z(W)-\ln Z(0)|\leq\frac{N}{2}\|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(124)

Injecting
W=\eta^{-1/2}\widetilde{W}
, multiplying by
\eta
and using
\eta\ln Z(0)=\mathcal{O}(\eta)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{N}\log Z(\eta^{-1/2}\widetilde{W})=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{1/2}).

(125)

Then
P_{0^{+}}(\widetilde{W}|C)\propto e^{-\frac{N\gamma}{4}\Trace\widetilde{W}^{2}+o(1)}
. The Jacobian from
W\mapsto\widetilde{W}
is independent of
C
and
\mathbf{x}
, and absorbed into the normalization of the posterior over
\widetilde{W}
. Using
P_{0^{+}}(O^{\top}\widetilde{W}O|C)=P_{0^{+}}(\widetilde{W}|C)
and
P_{W}(O\mathbf{x})=P_{O^{\top}WO}(\mathbf{x})
for any orthogonal matrix
O
, we have
P_{\mathrm{pp},0^{+}}(O\mathbf{x})=P_{\mathrm{pp},0^{+}}(\mathbf{x})
. Any normalized rotationally invariant density on
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is constant, whence the claimed

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{pp},0^{+}}(\mathbf{x}|C)=\frac{1}{Z(0)}.

(126)

MAP posterior predictive

In the
\eta\to+\infty
limit,
P_{\eta}(W|C)\Longrightarrow\delta(W-W_{\mathrm{MAP}})
, where
W_{\mathrm{MAP}}
is the unique maximizer of
P_{\eta}(W|C)
. Hence

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to+\infty}P_{\mathrm{pp},\eta}(\mathbf{x}|C)=P_{W_{\mathrm{MAP}}}(\mathbf{x}).

(127)

Stationarity of the log-posterior in
W
shows that
W_{\mathrm{MAP}}
solves

\displaystyle\gamma W_{\mathrm{MAP}}=C-\frac{K}{N}\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\rangle_{P_{W_{\mathrm{MAP}}}}.

(128)

D.4
Posterior-predictive entropy

Applying (
119
) to the posterior-predictive entropy gives

\mathcal{H}[P_{\mathrm{pp}}]=\ln\mathcal{Y}(C)-\left\langle\ln\mathcal{Y}(C_{\mathbf{x}})\right\rangle_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{\mathrm{pp}}}.

(129)

Under
\mathbf{x}\sim P_{\mathrm{pp}}

m_{k}^{2}=\frac{(\mathbf{c}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{2}}{N^{2}}=\frac{c_{k}}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(\chi_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta c_{k}}\right),\quad k=1,2,

(130)

The three nonzero eigenvalues

D.5

Since the cross-entropy is linear in its first argument, the reverse cross-entropies of a typical

where

D.6
Forward cross-entropies

Posterior-average forward cross-entropy.

Posterior-predictive forward cross-entropy.

From (

\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast};P_{\mathrm{pp}}]=\ln\mathcal{Y}(C)-\left\langle\ln\mathcal{Y}(C_{\mathbf{x}})\right\rangle_{\mathbf{x}\sim P^{\ast}}.

The eigenvalues of

D.8
Posterior-predictive gap identities

We show that:

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{pp}}\|P^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MI}(W;\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})\geq 0,

(144)

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{W})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\mathrm{pp}})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})\|P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D},\mathbf{x}))\bigr\rangle_{\mathbf{x}\sim P^{\ast}}\geq 0,

(145)

proving the inequalities stated in Sec.
3
of the main text. These gaps vanish in the MAP limit.

Reverse KL gap.
Using (
133
):

\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\right\rangle-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{pp}}\|P^{\ast})=\mathcal{H}[P_{\mathrm{pp}}]-\left\langle\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}

(146)

The right-hand side equals the mutual information
\mathrm{MI}(W;\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})
between the posterior weights
W
and student-generated data
\mathbf{x}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(W;\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\int P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})P(\mathbf{x}|W)\ln\frac{P(\mathbf{x}|W)}{P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})}\mathrm{d}W\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(147)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}[P_{\mathrm{pp}}]-\left\langle\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}\geq 0

establishing (
144
).

Forward KL gap.
We compute:

\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{W})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\mathrm{pp}})=\int\mathrm{d}W\,P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,P^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})\ln\frac{P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})}{P(\mathbf{x}|W)}

(148)

Using the Bayes update
P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D},\mathbf{x})=P(\mathbf{x}|W)P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})/P_{\mathrm{pp}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D})
, the inner integral over
W
becomes
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D})\,\middle\|\,P_{\eta}(W|\mathcal{D},\mathbf{x})\right)
, yielding (
145
).

Discussion.
Both identities are classical Bayesian facts from two largely disjoint literatures. The reverse gap is the mutual information
\mathrm{MI}(W;\mathbf{x})
, equivalent to Lindley’s expected-information gain
[
39
]
and to the epistemic/aleatoric decomposition of predictive entropy used in Bayesian deep learning
[
33
,
19
,
37
]
. The forward gap goes back to
[
1
]
, who showed that
P_{\mathrm{pp}}
dominates typical
P_{W}
under KL loss. The distinction between posterior predictive and a typical posterior sample is mainstream in feed-forward Bayesian models but, to our knowledge, has not been emphasized for generative models.

D.9
Sampling temperature tuning

We focus on the effect of sampling temperature tuning on the forward KL. We have:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\beta W})=\beta\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast};P_{W}]-\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast}]+\ln Z(\beta W)-\beta\ln Z(W)

(149)

This is a convex function of
\beta
, which therefore has a single minimum:

\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\beta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\beta W})

(150)

To determine whether the optimal
\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}
is
>1
or
<1
, it suffices to compute the sign of:

\left.\frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\beta W})}{\partial\beta}\right|_{\beta=1}=\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast};P_{W}]-\mathcal{H}[P_{W}]

(151)

\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}
is
>1
or
<1
according to whether (
151
) is positive or negative, respectively. Note that equations (
149
) and (
151
) are general identities, valid for any temperature-tuned energy-based model.

Now we specialize to the teacher-student scenario introduced in the main text, with
K=2
and the rank-one teacher. Averaging over the posterior
W\sim P_{\eta}
and substituting the forward average energy (
137
) and the model average energy (
26
) gives, to leading order in
N
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left.\frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\ast}\|P_{\beta W})}{\partial\beta}\right|_{\beta=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu-1}{2}-\left(\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)^{2}\left(c_{1}\chi_{1}-\frac{1}{\eta}\right)

(152)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\gamma\eta}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1}{\omega^{\ast}}\right)^{2}&h=0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma\eta}}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1}{\omega^{\ast}}\right)^{2}&h\neq 0,u_{1}=0\\
\frac{\lambda_{1}-1}{2}-\left(\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)^{2}\left(c_{1}\lambda_{1}-\frac{1}{\eta}\right)&h,u_{1}\neq 0\end{cases}

(153)

after substituting the saddle-point values for
\mu
and
\chi_{1}
in each phase.

After temperature tuning, the model can be transported from one phase to another.

•

If
h=u_{1}=0
, then
\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}
is
<1
or
>1
according to whether
2\eta[(\omega^{\ast}-1)/\omega^{\ast}]^{2}
is
<1
or
>1
. In this case, the temperature-tuned model remains in the initial phase (with
h=u_{1}=0
).

•

If
h\neq 0
and
u_{1}=0
, then
\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}<1
is such that the temperature-tuned model lands in a phase with
h=u_{1}=0
, killing the condensed random direction.

•

If
h=0
and
u_{1}\neq 0
,
\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}>1
or
<1
according to whether
\frac{\omega^{\ast 2}}{2(\omega^{\ast}-1)^{2}}
is
<\eta
or
>\eta
. If
\frac{(\omega^{\ast}-1)^{2}(2\omega^{\ast}-1)}{\omega^{\ast}}<\frac{\gamma}{2\eta}
the model stays in the same phase (
h=0,u_{1}\neq 0
), but otherwise it activates the dormant aligned mode, landing in
h,u_{1}\neq 0
, which is beneficial because this mode was already aligned to the teacher.

•

If
h,u_{1}\neq 0
, both
\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}>1
or
<1
are possible, and the model can stay in the same phase or move to
h=0,u_{1}\neq 0
.

Generally, since
\beta
only rescales the spectrum of
W
without deforming it, temperature tuning cannot create new data-aligned outliers or merge two distinct outliers. Temperature tuning can only activate or deactivate an already present aligned mode.

D.10
Double descent

We derive the threshold
\eta_{\mathrm{DD}}(\omega^{\ast})
above which the typical reverse KL of Fig.
4
acquires a local minimum inside the data-aligned condensed phase
h\neq 0,u_{1}\neq 0
, creating the first descent of the double-descent profile. The post-condensation
h=0
branch is governed by

\tfrac{1}{N}\left\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\right\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1-\ln\omega^{\ast}}{2}+\frac{1}{4\gamma\eta},

(154)

with
\gamma
-derivative
-1/(4\eta\gamma^{2})<0
strictly negative for any finite
\eta
; the second descent therefore exists trivially, and the analysis reduces to the condensed branch. We work in the
K=2
rank-one teacher of App.
D.1
with eigenvalues
c_{1}=2-1/\omega^{\ast}
,
c_{2}=1/\omega^{\ast}
from (
115
).

On the condensed branch the top-outlier Stieltjes transform
g_{1}=G(\lambda_{1})
provides a smooth parametrization,

\gamma(g_{1})=2g_{1}^{2}-\frac{g_{1}}{\omega^{\ast}},\qquad\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{g_{1}}+\frac{g_{1}}{\gamma\eta},\qquad\frac{1}{\omega^{\ast}}<g_{1}<1,

(155)

with the outlier-detachment condition
g_{1}<\sqrt{\gamma\eta}
equivalent to
\eta>\eta_{F}(g_{1}):=\omega^{\ast}g_{1}/(2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1)
, and the branch terminating at the condensation threshold
g_{1}=1
,
\gamma=c_{1}
. Substituting (
155
) into (
140
) and using
G(\mu)=1-h^{2}=g_{1}
on this branch yields the intensive reverse KL as a function of
g_{1}
,

\tfrac{1}{N}\bigl\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\bigr\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}=\\
\frac{\omega^{\ast}-1-\ln(\omega^{\ast}g_{1})}{2}-\frac{\omega^{\ast}(\omega^{\ast}-1)(1-g_{1})}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\!\left[1-\frac{\omega^{\ast}g_{1}}{\eta(2\omega^{\ast}-1)}\right]+\frac{\omega^{\ast}g_{1}}{4\eta(2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1)},

(156)

which matches (
154
) continuously at
g_{1}=1
.

Since
\mathrm{d}\gamma/\mathrm{d}g_{1}=4g_{1}-1/\omega^{\ast}>0
on this branch, extrema in
\gamma
and in
g_{1}
coincide. With

g_{\mathrm{MAP}}:=\frac{2\omega^{\ast}-1}{2\omega^{\ast}(\omega^{\ast}-1)},

(157)

the
g_{1}
-derivative writes,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}g_{1}}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{N}\bigl\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{W}\|P^{\ast})\bigr\rangle_{W\sim P_{\eta}}\right]=\frac{\omega^{\ast}(\omega^{\ast}-1)(g_{1}-g_{\mathrm{MAP}})}{g_{1}(2\omega^{\ast}-1)}\\
+\frac{1}{\eta}\!\left[\frac{\omega^{\ast 2}(\omega^{\ast}-1)(1-2g_{1})}{(2\omega^{\ast}-1)^{2}}-\frac{\omega^{\ast}}{4(2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1)^{2}}\right].

(158)

In the MAP limit only the first term survives, and is positive on
(g_{\mathrm{MAP}},1)
when
g_{\mathrm{MAP}}<1
, equivalently
2\omega^{\ast 2}-4\omega^{\ast}+1>0
, equivalently

\omega^{\ast}>\omega_{\mathrm{DD}}:=1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

(159)

Below
\omega_{\mathrm{DD}}
the condensed branch is monotone for every
\eta
and no first minimum exists.

When (
159
) holds, an interior local minimum appears at
g_{1}\in(g_{\mathrm{MAP}},1)
as soon as the slope (
158
) is positive and the outlier-detachment condition
\eta>\eta_{F}(g_{1})
holds. The sharp threshold is the one-dimensional variational formula

\eta_{\mathrm{DD}}(\omega^{\ast})=\inf_{g_{\mathrm{MAP}}<g_{1}<1}\,\frac{\omega^{\ast}g_{1}}{2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1}\times\\
\max\!\left\{1,\;\frac{2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1}{(2\omega^{\ast}-1)(g_{1}-g_{\mathrm{MAP}})}\!\left[(2g_{1}-1)+\frac{(2\omega^{\ast}-1)^{2}}{4\omega^{\ast}(\omega^{\ast}-1)(2\omega^{\ast}g_{1}-1)^{2}}\right]\right\},

(160)

with
\eta_{\mathrm{DD}}(\omega^{\ast})=\infty
for
\omega^{\ast}\leq\omega_{\mathrm{DD}}
. For
\eta_{\mathrm{DD}}<\eta<\infty
the reverse KL has the double-descent profile of Fig.
4
; the MAP limit collapses the post-transition branch to a flat line, so the second descent ceases to be strict while the first minimum survives.

D.11
Tempered-posterior effects

The optimal posterior temperature

Optimal tempered-posterior according to the forward KL

Smooth branch.

(170)

(172)

\eta_{\pm}=\frac{4\gamma}{\Bigl[\gamma+c_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}(c_{1}-c_{2})^{2}\,\mp\,\sqrt{\bigl[\gamma+c_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}(c_{1}-c_{2})^{2}\bigr]^{2}-4\gamma c_{1}}\Bigr]^{2}}.

(174)

above which the warm flat regime no longer exists. For

Phase classification.
The minimisation has four exclusive outcomes (Fig.

the warm flat interval

is the optimum-set, with smooth-branch representative
\eta_{0}<1

traces a non-convex W shape with the two troughs at the same value to leading order in

Student average energy and entropy

Let

D.12.2
Forward cross entropy

We first compute

(178)

(179)

and where we used

\frac{\langle\mathbf{x}^{\top}W(t)\mathbf{x}\rangle_{P^{\ast}}}{N}=\frac{\langle(\mathbf{w}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{2}\rangle_{P^{\ast}}}{N}\mathbf{w}^{\ast}\!{}^{\top}W(t)\mathbf{w}^{\ast}
and

\Theta(t)
has at most

\theta_{k}(t)

\mathbf{t}_{k}

\mathbf{w}^{\ast}

\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{K}

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\ast}\!{}^{\top}\Theta(t)\mathbf{w}^{\ast}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)\left(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t/2}}{\gamma}c_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\gamma(t-u)/2}s_{1}(u)^{2}\right),

(180)

where we used Eq. (
116

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{H}[P^{\ast};P_{t}]=\frac{\ln 2\pi+\mu(t)-F(\mu(t))}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\omega^{\ast}}\right)\mathbf{w}^{\ast}\!{}^{\top}\Theta(t)\mathbf{w}^{\ast}.

(181)

\Theta(t)

Properties of finite-rank deformed Wigner matrices then imply

\displaystyle g_{t}=\begin{cases}\min\{1,\sqrt{\gamma\eta}\}&\theta_{1}(t)\leq\theta_{c},\\

D.12.3
Reverse cross entropy

Injecting

upon using

\Theta(t)

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbf{v}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{t}_{1}(t))^{2}}{N^{2}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma\,\eta\,\theta_{1}(t)^{2}}\right)_{+}+o(1).

(184)

The component of

orthogonal to
\mathbf{t}_{1}(t)
is asymptotically isotropic in the
(N-1)

\mathbf{t}_{1}(t)
and is therefore asymptotically orthogonal to

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbf{v}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{w}^{\ast})^{2}}{N}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma\,\eta\,\theta_{1}(t)^{2}}\right)_{+}\frac{(\mathbf{t}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{w}^{\ast})^{2}}{N}+o(1).

To evaluate

(186)

D.12.5
Reverse KL

Using Eqs. (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t}\,\|\,P^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}(\omega^{\ast}-1-\ln\omega^{\ast})-\frac{1}{2}\ln g_{t}+\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{4\gamma\eta}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{\ast}h_{t}^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma\eta\,\theta_{1}(t)^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)\frac{\left(\mathbf{t}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{1}\right)^{2}}{N^{2}}.

(188)

At very early times,

, until the student condenses along an outlier direction, the reverse KL stays flat, with plateau height

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t}\,\|\,P^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}(\omega^{\ast}-1-\ln\omega^{\ast})-\frac{1}{2}\ln g_{0}+\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{4\gamma\eta},\qquad

\displaystyle g_{0}=\begin{cases}1,&\gamma\eta\geq 1\\
\sqrt{\gamma\eta},&\gamma\eta<1.\end{cases}

(189)

Optimal stopping time.

For

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\omega^{\ast}h_{t}^{2}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma\eta\,\theta_{1}(t)^{2}}\Big)\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\Big)\tfrac{\left(\mathbf{t}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{1}\right)^{2}}{N^{2}}\Big)\Big|_{t=t^{*}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\ln\theta_{1}(t)+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma\eta\theta_{1}(t)^{2}}\right)\Big|_{t=t^{*}}.

When

, setting

gives a quadratic in

, whose positive root is

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\omega^{\ast}\left(1-\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)+\sqrt{\omega^{\ast 2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}-1}\right)^{2}+\frac{4}{\gamma\eta}}\right].

(190)

The early-training approximation,
\theta_{1}(t)\simeq\frac{c_{1}}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\gamma t/2})
, leads to the
\nu
-independent estimate

\displaystyle t^{*}\simeq-\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\left(1-\frac{\gamma\,\theta^{*}}{c_{1}}\right).

(191)

Injecting the parameter values of Fig.
6
yields

, in excellent agreement with the numerical value.

\mathcal{O}(1)

K\eta/(2\lambda_{k})

\lambda_{k}=(\eta c_{k})^{-1}+c_{k}/\gamma
(Fig.
13
). The condensed phases (
h\neq 0
) have no Gaussian analog, and the double-descent spike at the
h\neq 0
to
h=0
boundary (Fig.
4
) together with the warm-posterior effect
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}\neq 1
(Fig.
5
B) trace to the non-conjugate, phase-dependent
\ln Z
: under the Gaussian with its conjugate prior, Bayesian inference is exact at
\eta=1
, no warm-posterior effect arises, and the KL divergences are smooth in
\gamma
.

Computational experiments in generative models

F.1
Sampling temperature tuning in Potts BMs trained on protein sequences

A prediction of the SBM phase diagram is that
L_{2}
regularization shrinks the coupling eigenvalues and pushes the trained model toward the
h=0
phase, where generated samples are near-isotropic noise. Raising the sampling inverse temperature
\beta>1
amplifies signal modes and can trigger an
h=0
to
h\neq 0
condensation that rescues these modes, giving a principled mechanism for the widely-used practice of sampling energy-based models at temperatures below the training value
[
26
]
. We now show that this picture quantitatively describes pairwise Potts Boltzmann machines trained on real protein sequence data.

Expanding the sampling covariance of a model trained at
\beta=1
with regularization
\gamma
around the training point and choosing
\beta
to optimize the fit of the model covariance to the empirical data covariance, to leading order in
\gamma
, yields the closed-form optimum

\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}^{\mathrm{cov}}=1+\gamma\,\frac{\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}\,c_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}(\lambda_{k}-\mu^{\prime})\,c_{k}^{4}},

(192)

where
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
are, respectively, the coupling eigenvalues of the trained model and the data covariance eigenvalues in a common eigenbasis, and
\mu^{\prime}
encodes the on-site restoring force set by the normalization constraint:
\mu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{SBM}}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{2}/\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}
for the spherical constraint, and
\mu^{\prime}=0
for the per-site softmax of the Potts model. We use covariance matching rather than minimizing a KL divergence because any KL between the model and the data distribution requires the Potts partition function
Z(\beta J)
and is intractable in this regime, while the model and data covariances are directly accessible from samples.

Derivation.

Work in the joint eigenbasis of the trained coupling
J
and the data covariance
C
, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
, and let
\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta)
denote the sampling-covariance eigenvalue at inverse temperature
\beta
. The
L_{2}
-regularized stationarity condition
\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\rangle_{J}=C-\gamma J
at
\beta=1
reads in this basis

\sigma_{k}^{2}(1)=c_{k}-\gamma\lambda_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2}).

(193)

Sampling at
\beta
rescales
J\mapsto\beta J
. For the spherical model
P_{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\propto e^{(\beta/2)\mathbf{x}^{\top}J\mathbf{x}}\,\delta(\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-N)
the Lagrange multiplier
\mu_{\beta}
gives
\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta)=(\mu_{\beta}-\beta\lambda_{k})^{-1}
, hence

\partial_{\beta}\sigma_{k}^{2}\big|_{\beta=1}=(\lambda_{k}-\mu^{\prime})\,\sigma_{k}^{2}(1)^{2}=(\lambda_{k}-\mu^{\prime})\,c_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma),

(194)

with
\mu^{\prime}\equiv\partial_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}|_{\beta=1}
. Differentiating the trace constraint
\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta)=N
at
\beta=1
fixes

\mu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{SBM}}=\frac{\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma);

(195)

the Potts softmax enforces normalization site-wise, leaving
\mu^{\prime}=0
. Expanding
\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta)
to first order around
\beta=1
via (
193
)–(
194
) gives the residual

\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta)-c_{k}=-\gamma\lambda_{k}+(\beta-1)(\lambda_{k}-\mu^{\prime})\,c_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2}).

(196)

A scalar
\beta
cannot zero this mode-by-mode; we project onto the coupling direction weighted by the data covariance,

\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\,c_{k}^{2}\bigl(\sigma_{k}^{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}^{\mathrm{cov}})-c_{k}\bigr)=0.

(197)

Substituting (
196
) into (
197
) yields

-\gamma\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}c_{k}^{2}+(\beta_{\mathrm{TT}}^{\mathrm{cov}}-1)\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}(\lambda_{k}-\mu^{\prime})c_{k}^{4}=0,

(198)

which solves to Eq. (
192
).

F.2.3
Double descent in an RBM trained on financial data

The double-descent phenomenon predicted by the theory is not limited to the SBM. A qualitatively similar behavior as a function of regularization strength also appears in a restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) trained on financial data, where the peak in test loss coincides with a spectral transition in the learned weight matrix, the same mechanism that drives the SBM double descent at the
h\neq 0
to
h=0
boundary.

Data.

We use the Kenneth R. French 49 industry portfolio daily value-weighted returns
[
24
,
38
]

\pm 5\sigma

\lambda_{2}\approx 1.6

\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\approx 15

Model and training.

We train a Gaussian-visible, Bernoulli-hidden RBM with
N=49
visible units (one per industry) and
M=20
hidden units, using persistent contrastive divergence (PCD)
[
64
]
with
L_{2}
weight decay of strength
\gamma

. The visible biases and precisions are unregularized. Training consists of 6 000 Adam
[
35
]
burn-in iterations followed by 2 000 SGD iterations at constant learning rate (the latter phase used for SWAG snapshot collection
[
42
]
, though posterior averaging turns out to have a negligible effect here). The log-partition function is estimated by annealed importance sampling
[
59
]
with 10 000 inverse-temperature steps and 15 chains. We sweep
\gamma
from
10^{-6}
to
10

\pm 1

Results.

F.4
Out-of-equilibrium training dynamics in a Potts Boltzmann machine

The training dynamics of Sec.
2.2
predicts that the stationary sample–data overlap
s_{\mathrm{st}}
undergoes a phase transition at the critical sampling rate
\nu_{c}=\gamma/(c_{1}\chi_{P})
[Fig.
2

\nu_{c}
the persistent chain aligns with the data spike, while below
\nu_{c}
the chain remains uncondensed (
s=0
) even though the weight matrix has already acquired the data signal. This out-of-equilibrium (OOE) phenomenon mirrors the empirical findings of
[
16
]

We test whether this OOE mechanism is observable in a discrete Potts Boltzmann machine trained on real sequence data. Specifically, we train a pairwise Potts model on sequences sampled from a lattice-protein teacher at
\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}=1000
(
L=27
sites,

amino acids), using PCD in the MAP limit (
\eta\to\infty

L_{2}
regularization
\gamma=0.01

We monitor the chain variances

. Here

are the empirical one-site frequencies and
\mathbf{c}_{a}
are the eigenvectors of the centered one-hot data covariance. The top coupling eigenvalue integrates the OOE bias over the full training trajectory: when the negative phase is biased (
\nu<\nu_{c}
), the data term in (
3

direction

\sum_{a}\|\mathrm{Proj}_{J}\mathbf{c}_{a}\|^{2}

14
