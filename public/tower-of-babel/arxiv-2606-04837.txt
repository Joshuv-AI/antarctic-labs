Maximal Minimal Spacing for Random Points
Fabio Deelan Cunden, Noemi Cuppone, Giovanni Gramegna, Pierpaolo Vivo

Abstract
. From N + 1 N+1 random points on a line we wish to select M + 1 M+1 points so as to maximize the minimal spacing between them. We consider an initial configuration with independent and identically distributed spacings. The problem is equivalent to optimally grouping consecutive gaps into M M blocks and maximizing the smallest block sum. For general gap distributions, and for all M ≤ N M\leq N , we derive exact distributional identities for the optimal spacing and obtain its asymptotic behavior. The problem admits a reformulation in terms of a threshold-resetting random walk. The walk advances by successive random increments and is reset to the origin upon exceeding a fixed threshold. The probability that the optimal spacing exceeds a given value coincides with the probability that the walk completes at least M M reset cycles within N N steps. This yields an exact representation in terms of first-passage functionals of the walk. The same mapping suggests a numerical scheme for the max-min spacing problem in the regime of large N N and M M , whose accuracy is tested against the exact results obtained here.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The max-min spacing problem

Take a collection of
N+1
points on the real line, listed in increasing order:
P_{0}<P_{1}<\cdots<P_{N}
.
We would like to choose a subset of
M+1
points such that the minimal spacing between any two consecutive points is as large as possible.

If
M=1
, the problem is trivial: we keep only the two extreme points.
If
M=N
, there is no choice to make: the smallest gap between the original points provides the solution to our problem.
The interesting regime lies between those extreme cases, in which we must discard
N-M
points.
Any optimal choice must include the extreme points
P_{0}
and
P_{N}
: removing an endpoint can only decrease the total range without decreasing the smallest spacing.

The problem can be formalised as follows. Given the initial
N+1
points, choosing
M+1
of them is equivalent to choosing an index subset
i:=(i_{0},\dots,i_{M})
, such that
i_{0}<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{M-1}<i_{M}
. In our case,
i_{0}:=0
and
i_{M}:=N
, as we are always choosing the extremal points. Let

(1.1)

I_{M,N}:=\Bigl\{i=(i_{0},\dots,i_{M}):0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{M-1}<i_{M}=N\Bigr\}

denote the collection of admissible index subsets. There are

(1.2)

|I_{M,N}|=\binom{N-1}{M-1}

of such selections.
For
i\in I_{M,N}
, the
minimal spacing
among the selected points is

(1.3)

S^{(i)}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq M}\bigl(P_{i_{j}}-P_{i_{j-1}}\bigr)\ .

We define the
max-min spacing
, as the maximum of the minimal spacing over all possible selections

(1.4)

S^{*}:=\max_{i\in I_{M,N}}S^{(i)}\ .

The optimal selection may not be unique (see Fig.
1
).

In order to obtain a measure of diversity that is applicable to general samples, we normalize
S^{*}
by the total range and thus define the
relative max-min spacing
as follows

(1.5)

\widetilde{S}:=\frac{S^{*}}{P_{N}-P_{0}}\ .

We are interested in the case of
random
initial configuration of
N+1
points
\{P_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
. Our goal is to characterize the distribution of the max-min spacing (
1.4
). How does the distribution of
S^{*}
depend on
M
and
N
? What is the typical size of
S^{*}
? How to characterize typical and atypical fluctuations away from the mean?

1.2.
Motivations

The max–min spacing
S^{*}
sits at the intersection of several classical themes in probability, statistical physics, and combinatorial optimization.
The classical theory of spacings usually studies the gaps that are already
present in a random configuration
[
16
,
23
,
24
,
10
,
1
]
. The largest spacing is by now well understood
[
2
,
11
,
22
,
8
]
. Here the situation is
different: the gaps are not observed but created by selection. We ask how
large the minimal spacing can be made after selecting
M+1
points from an
initial collection of
N+1
. Equivalently, one seeks the largest gap that
can be enforced simultaneously across
M
consecutive blocks.

The resulting
optimization ranges over strongly correlated configurations and leads to a new
exactly solvable model in the broader setting of extreme-value statistics for
correlated random systems
[
19
,
27
,
18
]
.

Problems involving unusually large gaps appear throughout probability and
mathematical physics. In random matrix theory, for example, one studies large
gaps between neighboring eigenvalues or eigenangles
[
3
,
14
,
12
]
. Our setting differs in one
essential respect: the large gaps are produced by a coarse-graining of the
configuration rather than by the original process itself.

There are several equivalent ways to think about the quantity
S^{*}
.
In statistical physics,
S^{*}
is the largest hard-core exclusion radius
compatible with keeping
M+1
particles from a configuration of
N+1
,
connecting the problem to Rényi’s classical parking model
[
26
]
.
In ecology, if
N+1
individuals compete for territory and exactly
M+1

survive,
S^{*}
is the largest minimum territory each survivor can claim.
In operations research, the problem is a one-dimensional instance of the

p
-dispersion problem
[
17
,
13
,
25
]
—select

M+1
facilities from
N+1
candidate sites to maximize the minimum pairwise
distance—which is NP-hard in general metric spaces but exactly solvable on
the line; our distributional results provide analytical benchmarks for random
instances, complementing the heuristic literature reviewed in
[
20
]
.

The present work is also related to the companion paper
[
6
]
, where
the objective is not the minimal spacing but the total dispersion (sum of distances). Both belong
to the broad family of maximum diversity/dispersion problems

[
15
,
21
,
20
]
, although the probabilistic structure
turns out to be rather different.

2.
Definition of the model

The problem is translation invariant; there is no loss of generality in fixing the leftmost point
P_{0}=0
. We consider a random configuration of points on the line whose successive spacings are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

Let
(T_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be i.i.d. positive random variables. Let,

(2.1)

P_{0}:=0,\quad P_{1}:=T_{1},\quad P_{2}:=T_{1}+T_{2},\quad\ldots\quad P_{N}:=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{N},\quad\ldots

Thus
(P_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a
discrete-time random walk
with positive i.i.d. increments
T_{j}
’s. The points
(P_{n})_{0\leq n\leq N}
form our initial random configuration.

In this setting, for each choice of indices
i\in I
, the minimal spacing defined in (
1.3
) is

(2.2)

S^{(i)}=\min_{1\leq j\leq M}(T_{i_{j-1}}+\cdots+T_{i_{j}})\ .

The max-min spacing

(2.3)

S^{*}=\max_{i\in I_{M,N}}\min_{1\leq j\leq M}(T_{i_{j-1}}+\cdots+T_{i_{j}})

is therefore the maximum of
|I_{M,N}|
random variables correlated through the overlap induced by the selection of indices. Our main result shows that the seemingly complicated optimization problem admits a remarkably simple generating function representation.

2.1.
Threshold-resetting framework

The max-min spacing problem can be mapped exactly to a
threshold-resetting problem

[
5
,
4
]
: a discrete-time random walk on the positive half-line, which starts from zero and resets to zero every time it crosses a threshold
s>0
. This mapping allows for a representation of the tail distribution
{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)
of the max-min spacing in terms of the generating function of the first passage time of this auxiliary random walk.

The

first-passage time
of
(P_{n})_{n\geq 0}
at level
s>0
is

(2.4)

\tau_{1}{(s)}:=\min_{n>0}\left\{n\colon P_{n}\geq s\right\}\ .

In order to define the associated reset-to-zero process, we define the subsequent
crossing times
through the threshold
s>0

(2.5)

\tau_{k}{(s)}:=\min_{n>\tau_{k-1}(s)}\left\{n\colon P_{n}-P_{\tau_{k-1}(s)}\geq s\right\},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,

with
\tau_{0}(s):=0
.
So,
\tau_{1}(s)
is the first-passage time of the random walk at level
s
,
\tau_{2}(s)
is the first time that, starting from
P_{\tau_{1}(s)}
, the random walk has another excursion larger or equal than
s
, and so on. The sequence of stopping times
(\tau_{k}(s))_{k\geq 0}
forms a renewal process.
Now we define the
reset-to-zero process

\left(X_{i}^{s}\right)_{i\geq 0}
as

(2.6)

X_{i}^{s}:=P_{i}-P_{\Gamma_{i}^{s}},\quad\text{where $\Gamma_{i}^{s}:=\max_{k\geq 0}\{\tau_{k}(s)\colon\tau_{k}(s)\leq i\}$}\ .

Therefore,
X_{i}^{s}
tracks the excursion since the most recent reset to the origin, and it is set to zero again as soon as it crosses
s
(see Figure
2
). Alternatively, we can write

(2.7)

X_{0}^{s}:=0\ ,\quad X_{i}^{s}:=\begin{cases}X_{i-1}^{s}+T_{i}&\text{if $X_{i-1}^{s}+T_{i}<s$}\\
0&\text{if $X_{i-1}^{s}+T_{i}\geq s$}\end{cases}\ ,\quad i\geq 1\ .

The reset-to-zero process
\left(X_{i}^{s}\right)_{i\geq 0}
is a Markov chain on
[0,s]
.

Whenever the random walk
X_{i}^{s}
resets to the origin, we say that it completed a cycle. Each cycle has length

(2.8)

L_{k}(s):=\tau_{k}(s)-\tau_{k-1}(s),\quad\text{for $k\geq 1$}\ .

Cycle lengths
\{L_{k}(s)\}_{k\geq 1}
are i.i.d. discrete positive random variables and each cycle is independent of the others. Therefore,
L_{k}(s)
is the first passage time through the threshold
s
of the
k
-th independent random walk starting from the origin. See Figure
2
.

We define the
number of complete cycles
of the reset-to-zero process
\left(X_{i}^{s}\right)_{i\geq 0}
up to time
N

(2.9)

K_{N}(s)=\max_{k\geq 0}\left\{k\colon\tau_{k}(s)\leq N\right\}.

The key identity is: the max-min spacing (
2.3
) is at least
s
, if and only if the number of complete excursions of the reset-to-zero process (
2.9
) with threshold
s
up to time
N
is at least
M
.

Lemma 2.1
.

S^{*}\geq s\iff K_{N}(s)\geq M\iff\tau_{M}(s)\leq N
.

Note that the above Lemma is an equality between events
\{S^{*}\geq s\}=\{K_{N}(s)\geq M\}=\{\tau_{M}(s)\leq N\}
(the probability distribution of the
T_{j}
’s plays no role).

.

complete cycles of length
L_{1}(s)=4
,

,

in

3.
Main results

3.1.
Distribution-free exact formula

Our main result is an exact formula for the tail distribution of
S^{*}
, valid for all distributions of the i.i.d. gaps
T_{j}
’s.
A central role is played by first-passage time at level
s>0
, that we rewrite as

(3.1)

\tau_{1}(s)=\min\left\{k\geq 1\colon T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}\geq s\right\}.

Its probability generating function (PGF) is

(3.2)

p_{s}(z)={\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{1}(s)}]=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{1}(s)=k\right)z^{k}.

For a power series
a(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+a_{2}z^{2}+\cdots
, we write
[z^{n}]a(z):=a_{n}
for the coefficient of
z^{n}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Distribution-free formula for the max-min spacing)
.

For all
1\leq M\leq N
:

(3.3)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=[z^{N}]\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{1-z}.

For a numerical illustration, see Fig.
3
.

Lemma 3.2

(Telescopic formula)
.

The distribution of the first-passage time
\tau_{1}(s)
is

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)=\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s)-\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)\ .

In particular,

(3.5)

\displaystyle p_{s}(z)

\displaystyle=z-(1-z)\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k}\ .

Moreover, if
{\mathbb{P}}(T_{1}=0)=0
, then
p_{s}(z)
is analytic in the whole complex plane
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let us spell out the resulting procedure. The model is defined by the distribution of the i.i.d. gaps
T_{j}
’s. The distribution of the partial sums

P_{k}=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}
is the
k
-fold convolution of the distribution of the
T_{j}
’s. Evaluating
the distribution functions of these partial sums at the threshold
s
gives,
through the identity (
3.4
), the law of the first-passage
time
\tau_{1}(s)
. Summing these probabilities with weights
z^{k}
gives the
generating function
p_{s}(z)
in (
3.5
). Finally, inserting

p_{s}(z)
into the coefficient formula (
3.3
) yields the tail
probability
{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)
of the max–min spacing. Schematically:

{\mathbb{P}}(T\geq t)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{convolution}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\,{\mathbb{P}}(P_{k}\geq s)={\mathbb{P}}(T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}\geq s)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:PL}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\,\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:pgf-telescopic}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\,p_{s}(z)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:main}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\,{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s).

3.2.
Saddle-point asymptotics

Formula (
3.3
) is amenable to a saddle-point calculation in the limit of large
N
and
M
with fixed ratio
M/N=\alpha
. Since
p_{s}(z)^{M}/(1-z)
is analytic in the unit disk
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon|z|<1\}
, Cauchy’s integral formula gives

(3.6)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{(1-z)\,z^{N+1}}\,dz=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\,dz\ ,

where
\gamma
is any circle centred at
z=0
and contained in the unit disk, and
g(z)=\alpha\log p_{s}(z)-\log z
.

For
z>0
, define the tilted first-passage time
\tau_{1}(s,z)
by

(3.7)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}(s,z)=k)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)z^{k}}{p_{s}(z)}\ .

Its generating function is

(3.8)

{\mathbb{E}}[w^{\tau_{1}(s,z)}]=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{1}(s,z)=k\right)w^{k}=p_{s}(wz)/p_{s}(z)\ .

When
z=1
, one recovers the original law:
\tau_{1}(s,1)=\tau_{1}(s)
. Values
z>1
bias the distribution toward larger passage times, while
z<1
favor smaller ones.

The mean and the variance are:

(3.9)

\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]=\frac{z\;p_{s}^{\prime}(z)}{p_{s}(z)},\qquad\operatorname{Var}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]=z\frac{d}{dz}\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]\ .

The saddle point equation (see Sec.
6
) becomes

(3.10)

\alpha\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]=1\ .

Define the ‘typical value’
s^{*}
of
S^{*}
by the equation

(3.11)

\alpha\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}({s^{*}},1)]=1\ .

We can now state the large deviation theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(Large deviations)
.

Let
(T_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be i.i.d. strictly positive random variables, and
let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Assume that, for each value of
s
,
the saddle-point equation

(3.12)

\alpha{\mathbb{E}}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]=1

admits a positive solution
z(s)>0
, and that

(3.13)

\operatorname{Var}(\tau_{1}(s,z(s)))>0\ .

Let
s^{*}
be defined by (
3.11
). Suppose that
N\to\infty
with
M=\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor
. Then, for
s>s^{*}
,

(3.14)

{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)=\frac{e^{-N\psi(s)}}{(1{-}z(s))\,\sqrt{2\pi\alpha N\sigma^{2}(s)}}\left[1+o(1)\right]\ ,

while for
s<s^{*}
,

(3.15)

{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}<s)=\frac{e^{-N\psi(s)}}{(z(s){-}1)\,\sqrt{2\pi\alpha N\sigma^{2}(s)}}\left[1+o(1)\right]\ .

Here,

(3.16)

\psi(s)=\ln z(s)-\alpha\log p_{s}(z(s)),\qquad\sigma^{2}(s)=\operatorname{Var}[\tau_{1}(s,z(s))]\ .

Equation (
3.10
) selects the tilted law under which the typical value of
\tau_{1}(s,z)
matches the constraint
M/N=\alpha
. The theorem identifies not only the rate function
\psi(s)
governing the probability of atypical fluctuations of
S^{*}
away from its typical value
[
28
]
, but also the subleading prefactors arising from Gaussian fluctuations around the saddle point
[
9
]
.
For an illustration of the large deviation law above in the exponential case, see Fig.
5
.

Assumptions (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) are satisfied whenever the law of the increments
T_{j}
’s has strictly positive density on
(0,+\infty)
. This covers a broad and natural class of models.

4.
Exactly solvable cases

The two models below can be analyzed explicitly because the first-passage
generating function
p_{s}(z)
can be computed in closed form.
In both cases this
ultimately comes from a memoryless structure of the increments. Exponential gaps lead to Poisson counting on the line, while geometric gaps produce the corresponding discrete
binomial picture (see Remark
4.5
).

4.1.
Notation

We use the symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
to denote
identity in distribution
, and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}
for
convergence in distribution
.
A
Gamma
random variable of shape
n>0
and rate
\lambda>0
, denoted
\operatorname{Gamma}(n,\lambda)
, is a random variable with density
f(x)=\frac{\lambda}{\Gamma(n)}(\lambda x)^{n-1}e^{-\lambda x}
, for
x>0
. When
n=1
, this reduces to an
exponential
random variable with rate
\lambda
, denoted
\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)
.
A
Beta
random variable with parameters
a,b>0
, denoted
\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)
, is a random variable with density
f(x)=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}
, for
0<x<1
.
Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
a
Gaussian
random variable with mean
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
.

4.2.
Exponential gaps

If
T_{i}
is exponential with rate
1
, then each block sum

T_{i_{j-1}+1}+\cdots+T_{i_{j}}

is Gamma distributed with shape
n_{j}=i_{j}-i_{j-1}
(
j=1,\ldots,M
) and unit rate, and the
M
block sums are independent.
Hence
S^{(i)}
in Eq. (
2.2
) is the minimum of
M
independent Gamma variables with shapes
n_{j}
and rate
1
. The survival function is

(4.1)

\mathbb{P}(S^{(i)}\geq s)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}(T_{i_{j-1}+1}+\cdots+T_{i_{j}}\geq s)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\overline{F}_{\Gamma(n_{j},1)}(s)\ .

The corresponding density is obtained by differentiation

(4.2)

f_{S^{(i)}}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}f_{\Gamma(n_{j},1)}(s)\prod_{\ell\neq j}\overline{F}_{\Gamma(n_{\ell},1)}(s),\qquad s>0\ ,

where

f_{\Gamma(n,1)}(s)=\frac{s^{n-1}e^{-s}}{(n-1)!},\qquad\overline{F}_{\Gamma(n,1)}(s)=e^{-s}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}\ .

The distribution of
S^{*}
turns out to be considerably simpler than the individual densities (
4.2
).

Theorem 4.1

(Max-min spacing for exponential gaps)
.

Let
0=P_{0}<P_{1}<\cdots<P_{N}
as above with gaps
(T_{j})_{j\geq 1}
i.i.d. exponential with rate
1
. Then, for any integer
M=1,\ldots,N
,

(4.3)

S^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{{Gamma}}\left(N-M+1,M\right),

and for all
M=2,\ldots,N
,

(4.4)

M\widetilde{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{Beta}(N-M+1,M-1)\ .

Equivalently,
S^{*}
has density

(4.5)

f_{S^{*}}(s)=\frac{M}{(N-M)!}(Ms)^{N-M}e^{-Ms},\quad s\geq 0,

while the relative max-min spacing
\widetilde{S}
has density

(4.6)

f_{\widetilde{S}}(s)=\binom{N-1}{M-1}M(M-1)(Ms)^{N-M}\left(1-Ms\right)^{M-2},\quad 0\leq s\leq\frac{1}{M}\ .

For a numerical illustration, see Fig.
4
.
The mean and variance are:

(4.7)

\displaystyle ES^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-M+1}{M},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}S^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-M+1}{M^{2}}\ ,

(4.8)

\displaystyle E\widetilde{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-M+1}{MN},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\widetilde{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{(M-1)(N-M+1)}{M^{2}N^{2}(N+1)}.

Note that if
(T_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are i.i.d.
\operatorname{Exp}(1)
variables, then

P_{j}=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{j}
,
j\geq 1
are the arrival times of a
unit–rate Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. The identity in distribution (
4.3
) is equivalent to the equality

(4.9)

{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-M}\frac{(Ms)^{k}}{k!}e^{-Ms},

or, equivalently,

(4.10)

{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)={\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\mathrm{Poisson}(Ms)\leq N-M\right),

where
\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)
denotes a
Poisson
random variable with parameter
\lambda>0
, with distribution
p(k)=\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}e^{-\lambda}
, for
k=0,1,\ldots
.

Remark 4.2
.

The relative max-min spacing
\widetilde{S}
for the model above coincides with the max-min spacing for
N-1

points on the unit interval.
More precisely, let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{N-1}
be i.i.d. random variables uniformly distributed in the interval
[0,1]
, and denote the corresponding order statistics by
0=:U_{0:N-1}<U_{1:N-1}<U_{2:N-1}<\cdots<U_{N-1:N-1}<U_{N:N-1}:=1
. Set
P_{i}:=U_{i:N-1}
, for

. Then, for all
2\leq M\leq N
, the max-min spacing between these points has density (
4.6
).

Remark 4.3
.

The cases
M=1
and
M=N

of Theorem
4.1

reduce to classical identities:

(1)

When
M=1
, the only possible selection of indices is
i=(i_{0},i_{M})=(0,N)
, and the max-min spacing
S^{*}
is simply the range of the sample,
P_{N}-P_{0}
. This is the sum
T_{1}+\cdots+T_{N}
of
N
i.i.d. exponential random variables with unit rate, that is a variable
\operatorname{Gamma}(N,1)
.

(2)

For
M=N
, the only selection of indices is
i=(0,1,\ldots,N)
, and the max-min spacing
S^{*}
is nothing but the minimal spacing in the original sample. This is the minimum
\min\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\}
of
N
i.i.d. exponential random variables with unit rate, namely
\operatorname{Gamma}(1,N)
.

Remark 4.4
.

It is natural to compare the distribution of
S^{*}
with that of the minimal spacing
S_{\mathrm{rand}}

obtained when the
M+1
selected points are chosen at random. Let
S_{\rm rand}
denote the minimal spacing obtained by choosing the
index set
i\in I_{M,N}
uniformly at random, independently of the gaps.
For exponential gaps,
S_{\rm rand}
is not in general exponentially
distributed. Conditional on the block lengths

n_{j}=i_{j}-i_{j-1}
(
j=1,\ldots,M
),
the
M
retained spacings are independent Gamma random variables with
respective shapes
n_{1},\ldots,n_{M}
and unit rate. Hence

(4.11)

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{\rm rand}\geq s\mid n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})=e^{-Ms}\prod_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{r=0}^{n_{j}-1}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}\ .

Since choosing
i
uniformly from
I_{M,N}
is equivalent to choosing uniformly
a composition
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{M}=N
into
M
positive parts, we obtain

(4.12)

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{\rm rand}\geq s)=\frac{e^{-Ms}}{\binom{N-1}{M-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{M}\geq 1\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{M}=N\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{r=0}^{n_{j}-1}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}\ .

Equivalently, using the generating function identity

(4.13)

\sum_{n\geq 1}x^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}=\frac{xe^{sx}}{1-x}\ ,

the tail probability can be written as

(4.14)

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{\rm rand}\geq s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-M}\frac{\binom{N-k-1}{M-1}}{\binom{N-1}{M-1}}\frac{(Ms)^{k}}{k!}e^{-Ms}.

This should be compared with the optimal tail in (
4.9
).
The two coincide in the two trivial cases
M=1
and
M=N
; in general,
S^{*}\geq S_{\rm rand}
and indeed, from
\binom{N-k-1}{M-1}\leq\binom{N-1}{M-1}
, we have a quantitative control on the inequality

(4.15)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(S^{*}\geq s\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(S_{\rm rand}\geq s\right).

From the explicit formulae (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), the large-
N
behavior follows by routine
analysis. We separate the typical fluctuations from the
large–deviation regime.

4.3.
Geometric gaps

We now consider a discrete case where the random points
P_{0}<P_{1}<\cdots<P_{N}
are located on the integers, so that the max-min spacing
S^{*}
is integer-valued.

Suppose that the
T_{j}
’s are independent geometric random variables with parameter
0<p<1
:

\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}\,p,\qquad k=1,2,3,\ldots

We can compute the PGF of the first-passage time
\tau_{1}(s)
using (
3.5
):

p_{s}(z)={\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{1}(s)}]=z-(1-z)\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}{\mathbb{P}}\left(P_{k}<s\right)z^{k},

where we used
{\mathbb{P}}\left(P_{k}<s\right)=0
for all
k=s,s+1,\ldots
. The distribution of
P_{k}
(a sum of i.i.d. geometric random variables) is a negative binomial distribution, and this gives (
q=1-p
):

(4.25)

\displaystyle p_{s}(z)={\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{1}(s)}]=z\,(q+pz)^{s-1},\quad\text{for all $s=1,2,\ldots$}.

Applying formula (
3.3
), for integer thresholds
s
, we get

(4.26)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-M}\binom{M(s-1)}{k}\,p^{k}\,q^{M(s-1)-k}\ ,\quad s=1,2,\ldots.

Equivalently,

(4.27)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{Bin}(M(s-1),p)\leq N{-}M\right),

where
\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
denotes a
binomial
random variable with distribution
p(k)=\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}
, for
k=0,\ldots,n
.

Formula (
4.27
) is the geometric (discrete) analogue of (
4.10
) for exponential (continuous) spacings.

Remark 4.5
.

The memoryless property of exponential and geometric gaps is reflected in the distribution of the
first-passage time:

(4.28)

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(s)-1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{Poisson}(s),\quad\text{for exponential gaps},

(4.29)

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(s)-1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{Bin}(s-1,p),\quad\text{for geometric gaps}.

The reproductive property of the Poisson and Binomial distribution leads to the closed formulae (
4.10
) and (
4.27
), respectively.

5.
Iterative Interval Refinement Method

For large values of
N
and
M
, an exhaustive search over the

|I_{M,N}|=\binom{N-1}{M-1}
possible selections of points is computationally
infeasible. The correspondence to the threshold-resetting (Lemma
2.1
) suggests an iterative
procedure to compute the max–min spacing, together with an optimal selection
of
M+1
points. The method
can be viewed as a variant of the bisection method for root finding.

Let
P_{0}<P_{1}<\cdots<P_{N}

be the ordered initial point configuration, not necessarily random.
Observe that, for all
M\leq N
, the max–min spacing
s^{*}
satisfies

(5.1)

0\leq s^{*}\leq\frac{P_{N}-P_{0}}{M}\ .

We therefore initialise the search interval as

(5.2)

[a,b]=\left[0,\frac{P_{N}-P_{0}}{M}\right].

At each iteration, we bisect the current interval and set

(5.3)

s=\frac{a+b}{2}.

We then test whether the threshold
s
is feasible by the greedy construction
suggested by Lemma
2.1
. Starting from the left endpoint, set

(5.4)

j_{0}:=0\ ,

and define recursively

(5.5)

j_{r}:=\min\{\ell>j_{r-1}:P_{\ell}-P_{j_{r-1}}\geq s\},\qquad r=1,\ldots,M,

whenever the set on the right-hand side is non-empty. If at some step the set
is empty, the construction stops and the threshold
s
is declared infeasible.

By Lemma
2.1
, the threshold
s

is feasible if and only if the greedy construction completes
M
steps no
later than the final point, namely

(5.6)

s^{*}\geq s\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j_{M}\leq N.

Equivalently, if (
5.5
) produces indices

0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots<j_{M}\leq N
, then the points

(5.7)

P_{j_{0}},P_{j_{1}},\ldots,P_{j_{M-1}},P_{N}

form an admissible selection whose consecutive spacings are all at least
s
.

Accordingly, the interval is refined as follows. If the threshold
s
is
feasible, we continue the search in the right subinterval by setting

(5.8)

a:=s.

If the threshold
s
is infeasible, we continue the search in the left
subinterval by setting

(5.9)

b:=s.

After a prescribed number of iterations, or once the desired precision is
reached, the midpoint of the final interval is returned as an approximation of

s^{*}
.
The same procedure also returns an approximately optimal selection of points:
one runs the greedy construction (
5.5
) at the final feasible
threshold and retains the corresponding points, together with the right
endpoint
P_{N}
.

We used Theorem
4.1
as a benchmark for validating the algorithm.
For values of
N
and
M
for which exhaustive search is computationally
infeasible, the numerically computed max–min spacing can be compared with the
exact distribution given in (
4.5
). Fig.
6

presents the results of numerical experiments conducted on several realisations
of random points
P_{0}<\cdots<P_{N}
with i.i.d. exponential spacings.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
(\tau_{k}(s))_{k\geq 0}
be the renewal times defined in (
2.5
) with
\tau_{0}(s):=0
. The second equivalence is immediate:
K_{N}(s)\geq M
(the reset-to-zero process completes at least
M
excursions in
N
steps), if and only if
\tau_{M}(s)\leq N
(the reset-to-zero process reaches the threshold
s
for the
M
-th time no later than the
N
-th step).

Now, suppose
\tau_{M}(s)\leq N
. Consider the selection of indices defined by the first
M-1
reset-to-zero times

(6.1)

i^{s}=(i_{0}^{s},i_{1}^{s},\ldots,i^{s}_{M-1},i_{M}^{s})=\left(0,\tau_{1}(s),\ldots,\tau_{M-1}(s),N\right).

This is a valid grouping
i^{s}\in I_{M,N}
: the blocks are consecutive, they cover
all
N
gaps, and there are exactly
M
of them.
By definition of the
\tau_{k}(s)
’s,

\displaystyle P_{i^{s}_{1}}-P_{i^{s}_{0}}=P_{\tau_{1}(s)}-P_{0}

\displaystyle\geq s,

\displaystyle P_{i^{s}_{2}}-P_{i^{s}_{1}}=P_{\tau_{2}(s)}-P_{\tau_{1}(s)}

\displaystyle\geq s,

\displaystyle P_{i^{s}_{M-1}}-P_{i^{s}_{M-2}}=P_{\tau_{M-1}(s)}-P_{\tau_{M-2}(s)}

\displaystyle\geq s

\displaystyle P_{i^{s}_{M}}-P_{i^{s}_{M-1}}=P_{N}-P_{\tau_{M-1}(s)}

\displaystyle\geq P_{\tau_{M}(s)}-P_{\tau_{M-1}(s)}\geq s.

All spacings arising by this selection of indices in
I_{M,N}
are at least
s
. Therefore,

S^{*}\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq M}(P_{i^{s}_{j}}-P_{i^{s}_{j-1}})\geq s.

Conversely, assume that
S^{*}\geq s
. Then, there exists a selection of indices

0=i^{*}_{0}<i^{*}_{1}<\cdots<i^{*}_{M-1}<i^{*}_{M}=N

such that for all
1\leq j\leq M
,

P_{i^{*}_{j}}-P_{i^{*}_{j-1}}\geq s.

Since the
\tau_{k}
’s are first-passage times,

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(s)=i^{s}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq i^{*}_{1},

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(s)=i^{s}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq i^{*}_{2},

\displaystyle\tau_{M-1}(s)=i^{s}_{M-1}

\displaystyle\leq i^{*}_{M-1}

\displaystyle\tau_{M}(s)

\displaystyle\leq i^{*}_{M}=N.

Hence
\tau_{M}(s)\leq N
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

As already remarked, the sequence
(\tau_{k}(s))_{k\geq 0}
forms a renewal process: the cycle lengths
L_{1}:=\tau_{1}(s)-\tau_{0}(s)
,
L_{2}:=\tau_{2}(s)-\tau_{1}(s)
,…, are i.i.d. copies of the first-passage time
\tau_{1}(s)
.

By Lemma
2.1
,
S^{*}\geq s
if and only if
\tau_{M}(s)=L_{1}+\cdots+L_{M}\leq N
.
Therefore,

(6.2)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(S^{*}\geq s\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{M}(s)\leq N\right)=\sum_{n=M}^{N}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{M}(s)=n\right)\ .

Consider the PGF of
\tau_{M}(s)
:

(6.3)

{\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{M}(s)}]=\sum_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{M}(s)=n\right)z^{n}\ .

Then,

(6.4)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(S^{*}\geq s\right)=\sum_{n=M}^{N}[z^{n}]{\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{M}(s)}]\ .

Since
L_{1},\ldots,L_{M}
are independent, and distributed as
\tau_{1}(s)
,

(6.5)

{\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{M}(s)}]={\mathbb{E}}[z^{L_{1}+\cdots+L_{M}}]={\mathbb{E}}[z^{L_{1}}]\cdots{\mathbb{E}}[z^{L_{M}}]={\mathbb{E}}[z^{\tau_{1}(s)}]^{M}=p_{s}(z)^{M}\ .

We conclude that

(6.6)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(S^{*}\geq s\right)=\sum_{n=M}^{N}[z^{n}]p_{s}(z)^{M}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}[z^{n}]p_{s}(z)^{M}=[z^{N}]\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{1-z}\ .

In the last steps we used the fact that the power series
p_{s}(z)^{M}
has no term lower than
z^{M}
, and we then multiplied by a geometric series to obtain the cumulative sum of the first
N
coefficients of the power series.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

The distribution of the first-passage time
\tau_{1}(s)
is determined by the
law of gaps
T_{j}
’s. Indeed,

(6.7)

\tau_{1}(s)=\min\left\{k>0:P_{k}\geq s\right\}=\min\left\{k>0:T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}\geq s\right\}.

For
k\geq 1
,

(6.8)

\tau_{1}(s)=k\iff P_{k-1}<s\,\text{ and }\,P_{k}\geq s,

with the convention
P_{0}=0
. Since the increments
T_{j}
’s are strictly positive, the sequence
(P_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is increasing, and therefore

P_{k}<s\Rightarrow P_{k-1}<s
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s,\ P_{k}\geq s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s)-\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s,\ P_{k}<s)

(6.9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s)-\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s).

Thus the distribution of
\tau_{1}(s)
can be written as a
telescopic difference of the distribution functions of the partial sums

P_{k-1}
and
P_{k}
:

(6.10)

\displaystyle p_{s}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\left[\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s)-\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)\right]z^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k-1}<s)z^{k}-\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k+1}-\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k}

\displaystyle=z-(1-z)\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k},

where in the last line we used
{\mathbb{P}}(P_{0}<s)=1
for all
s>0
.

We now prove that, if the increments
T_{j}
’s are strictly positive, then
p_{s}(z)
is entire in
z
. From (
6.10
), it is enough to show that the series

(6.11)

\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)z^{k}

is convergent for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
By the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem, the radius of convergence of (
6.11
) is

(6.12)

R=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)^{1/k}}.

We shall prove that, for all
s>0
,

(6.13)

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)^{1/k}=0.

Let
0<a<s
, and write

(6.14)

q(a)=\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\leq a).

Since
\mathbb{P}(T_{1}=0)=0
,
q(a)\downarrow 0
, as
a\downarrow 0
.
Let

m=\left\lfloor\frac{s}{a}\right\rfloor.

If
P_{k}<s
, then at most
m
of the
k
jumps
T_{i}
can exceed
a
. Indeed, if

m+1
jumps were larger than
a
, then
P_{k}>(m+1)a>s
.
Therefore, for all
0\leq m\leq k
,

\mathbb{P}(P_{k}\leq s)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\#\{1\leq i\leq k:T_{i}>a\}\leq m\right).

Since the variables are independent, the number of jumps exceeding
a
is a
binomial random variable with parameters
k
and
1-q(a)
. Hence

(6.15)

\mathbb{P}(P_{k}\leq s)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{k}{j}(1-q(a))^{j}q(a)^{k-j}.

We estimate this upper bound as

\mathbb{P}(P_{k}\leq s)\leq q(a)^{k-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{k}{j}\leq q(a)^{k-m}2^{k}.

Consequently,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}<s)^{1/k}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P_{k}\leq s)^{1/k}\leq 2q(a),

for all

, and hence the claim (
6.13
).
∎

Proof of Theorem (
3.3
).

Therefore, by Theorem

(6.16)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=[z^{N}]\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{(1-z)}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{(1-z)\,z^{N+1}}\,dz=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\,dz\ ,

where

is a positively oriented simple contour entirely contained in the unit disk and enclosing

By Lemma

, the integrand in (

) is meromorphic with poles at

and

, only.
Choose
\gamma
to be a circle

of radius

Since

is a power series with positive coefficients,

, i.e.

, where

The meaning of this saddle-point equation is the following. The event

\{S^{*}\geq s\}
is equivalent, by Lemma

, to completing
M

. Hence, if

\mu(z,s)={\mathbb{E}}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]\ .

Thus the saddle-point condition

\mu(z,s)=\frac{1}{\alpha}

selects the value of the tilt for which a typical renewal cycle has the length

z<1
biases the renewal process

By assumption, for each
s>0

\mu(z,s)=\frac{1}{\alpha},\qquad\mu(z,s):=z\frac{p_{s}^{\prime}(z)}{p_{s}(z)}={\mathbb{E}}[\tau_{1}(s,z)]\ .

Moreover, from (

Hence, whenever

g^{\prime\prime}(z(s))=\alpha\frac{\operatorname{Var}[\tau_{1}(s,z(s))]}{z(s)^{2}}>0\ .

Thus

(Since

is real.)
Finally, the steepest descent direction for

at

. We are now prepared to apply the steepest descent method.

.
Contours of integration appearing in the calculation of
\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)

By definition,
s^{*}
is the solution of
\mu(1,s^{*})=1/\alpha
. Hence
z(s^{*})=1
and, by monotonicity,

(6.26)

\begin{cases}&s<s^{*}\Rightarrow z(s)>1\\
&s=s^{*}\Rightarrow z(s)=1\\
&s>s^{*}\Rightarrow z(s)<1\ .\end{cases}

Therefore, if
s>s^{*}
, the contour
C_{z(s)}
is contained in the unit disk where the integrand
\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1-z)z}
has a single pole at
z=0
(see Figure
7
). Therefore,

(6.27)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}\geq s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\exp\{Ng(z(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\}}{1-z(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-N\psi(s)}}{(1-z(s))\sqrt{2\pi\alpha N\sigma^{2}(s)}}\left[1+o(1)\right],

which yields (
3.14
).

For
s<s^{*}
, the contour deformation from
\gamma
to
C_{z(s)}
crosses the pole at
z=1
. Therefore,

(6.28)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\,dz=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{C_{z(s)}}\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\,dz-\operatorname{Res}_{z=1}\left(\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\right)\ .

The residue is:

(6.29)

\operatorname{Res}_{z=1}\left(\frac{e^{Ng(z)}}{(1{-}z)\,z}\right)=\lim_{z\to 1}(z-1)\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{(1-z)z^{N+1}}=-p_{s}(1)^{M}=-1\ .

Therefore,

(6.30)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(S^{*}<s)=1-\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\exp\{Ng(z(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\}}{1-z(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-N\psi(s)}}{(z(s)-1)\sqrt{2\pi\alpha N\sigma^{2}(s)}}\left[1+o(1)\right],

which matches (
3.15
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We prove the identity in distribution (
4.3
) by computing the tail.
It suffices to show (
4.9
) for all
s\geq 0
.
(Differentiation then yields the density (
4.5
).)
The identity is a specialisation of Theorem
3.1
. Recall that
P_{k}=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{Gamma}(k,1)
, for all
k\geq 1
. Then, by (
3.4
),

(6.31)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(P_{k-1}<s)-{\mathbb{P}}(P_{k}<s)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(P_{k}\geq s)-{\mathbb{P}}(P_{k-1}\geq s)

\displaystyle=e^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{s^{j}}{j!}-e^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\frac{s^{j}}{j!}=e^{-s}\frac{s^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\ .

The PGF is therefore

(6.32)

p_{s}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{1}(s)=k)\;z^{k}=z\,e^{s(z-1)}\ .

The coefficient of
z^{n}
of
p_{s}(z)^{M}
for
n\geq M
is

[z^{n}]\,z^{M}\,e^{Ms(z-1)}=[z^{n-M}]\,e^{Ms(z-1)}=e^{-Ms}\cdot\frac{(Ms)^{n-M}}{(n{-}M)!}\ .

Therefore, by Theorem
3.1
:

(6.33)

\mathbb{P}(S^{*}\geq s)=[z^{N}]\frac{p_{s}(z)^{M}}{1-z}=\sum_{n=M}^{N}[z^{n}]p_{s}(z)^{M}=e^{-Ms}\sum_{k=0}^{N-M}\frac{(Ms)^{k}}{k!}

as claimed.

We now prove the identity (
4.4
).
Let

(6.34)

W:=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{N}=P_{N}-P_{0}

and introduce the normalised points

(6.35)

Y_{j}:=\frac{P_{j}}{W}=\frac{T_{1}+\cdots+T_{j}}{T_{1}+\cdots+T_{N}},\qquad\text{for $j=1,\ldots,N-1$},

and
Y_{0}:=0
and
Y_{N}:=1
.
A classical calculation, shows that
W
and
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N-1})
are independent, with

(6.36)

W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{Gamma}(N,1),\quad(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(U_{1:N-1},\ldots,U_{N-1:N-1}),

where
U_{1:N-1}<\cdots<U_{N-1:N-1}
are the order statistics of

N-1
independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
.

Indeed, the change of variables

(6.37)

\begin{cases}t_{1}=wy_{1},\\
t_{j}=w(y_{j}-y_{j-1}),\qquad j=2,\ldots,N-1,\\
t_{N}=w(1-y_{N-1})\ ,\end{cases}

maps the region
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}>0
onto

(6.38)

w>0,\qquad 0<y_{1}<\cdots<y_{N-1}<1\ .

Its Jacobian is
w^{N-1}
(see, e.g.
[
7
, Appendix B.1]
). Since the
T_{j}
’s are i.i.d.
\operatorname{Exp}(1)
, their joint density is
e^{-(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{N})}=e^{-w}
.
Therefore,

(6.39)

\displaystyle f_{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N-1},W}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N-1},w)

\displaystyle=w^{N-1}e^{-w}\bm{1}_{\{w>0\}}\bm{1}_{\{0<y_{1}<\cdots<y_{N-1}<1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{N-1}e^{-w}}{\Gamma(N)}\bm{1}_{\{w>0\}}\cdot(N-1)!\,\bm{1}_{\{0<y_{1}<\cdots<y_{N-1}<1\}}.

This proves the claim.

We now use the homogeneity of the max–min spacing: multiplying all
points by a positive constant multiplies all spacings, and hence also the
max–min spacing, by the same constant. Hence,

(6.40)

S^{*}=W\,\widetilde{S},

where
\widetilde{S}
is the max–min spacing of the normalised configuration

(6.41)

0=Y_{0}<Y_{1}<\cdots<Y_{N-1}<Y_{N}=1.

By the independence just proved,
\widetilde{S}
is independent of
W
, and
the normalised configuration has the same law as

(6.42)

0=U_{0:N-1}<U_{1:N-1}<\cdots<U_{N-1:N-1}<U_{N:N-1}=1.

Thus the relative max–min spacing for exponential gaps coincides in law with
the max–min spacing of
N-1
independent uniform points in the unit
interval, together with the two endpoints.

It remains to identify its density. Since
S^{*}=W\widetilde{S}
, with

W\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(N,1)
independent of
\widetilde{S}
, the density
of
S^{*}
is the Mellin convolution

(6.43)

f_{S^{*}}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\widetilde{S}}\!\left(\frac{s}{w}\right)f_{W}(w)\,\frac{dw}{w}\ .

Substituting the claimed density (
4.6
)
and

(6.44)

f_{W}(w)=\frac{w^{N-1}e^{-w}}{\Gamma(N)},\qquad w>0,

we get

(6.45)

\displaystyle f_{S^{*}}(s)=\binom{N-1}{M-1}M(M-1)(Ms)^{N-M}\frac{1}{\Gamma(N)}\int_{Ms}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{Ms}{w}\right)^{M-2}w^{M-1}e^{-w}\,\frac{dw}{w}.

By the change of
variables
r=w-Ms
, the integral evaluates to

\Gamma(M-1)e^{-Ms}
, and hence we get exactly the density in (
4.5
). This proves the claimed
density (
4.6
) of
\widetilde{S}
, and therefore the distributional identity (
4.4
).

∎

7.
Conclusions and Outlook

We considered the following selection problem on the line. Starting from

N+1
points with i.i.d. positive spacings, retain
M+1
of them so
that the minimal spacing between consecutive retained points is as large as
possible. Although the optimization ranges over

\binom{N-1}{M-1}
strongly correlated admissible selections, the resulting
random variable admits a simple renewal description.

The key observation is the equivalence, stated in Lemma
2.1
,
between the event
\{S^{*}\geq s\}
and the completion of at least
M

threshold-crossing cycles by a random walk with positive increments, reset to
the origin whenever it crosses the level
s
. This mapping reduces the original
combinatorial optimization problem to the study of a
first-passage generating function. The resulting distribution-free formula,
given in Theorem
3.1
, provides the tail distribution of
S^{*}
for arbitrary i.i.d. spacings. As a byproduct, it gives the probability that a threshold-resetting random walk with arbitrary jump distribution completes at least
M
resetting cycles in
N
steps.

This representation is particularly effective to analyze the asymptotic regime

M,N\to\infty
with
M/N=\alpha
. The saddle-point equation
(
3.10
) acquires a probabilistic meaning: the saddle selects the
exponentially tilted law for which the typical cycle length matches the
constraint imposed by the ratio
M/N
. This leads to the large-deviation estimates of
Theorem
3.3
, with upper and lower tail asymptotics given in
(
3.14
) and (
3.15
). The explicitly solvable cases
illustrate the general theory. For exponential spacings, the max–min spacing
has the Gamma distribution in (
4.3
), while the relative max–min
spacing has the Beta distribution in (
4.4
). For geometric spacings,
the corresponding discrete formula is expressed in terms of binomial tails, as
shown in (
4.26
) and (
4.27
).

Several directions are worth exploring. The renewal structure used
throughout the paper relies on the independence of the spacings. Correlated
increments, conditioned point processes, or interacting particle systems would
require different ideas and may produce a different limiting behavior.

A second direction concerns other scaling regimes. In this paper we mainly
focused on the proportional regime
M/N=\alpha
, together with some boundary
regimes in the exponential case. The cases in which
M
is fixed,
N-M
is
fixed, or
M
grows sublinearly with
N
should exhibit different asymptotic
scales and may connect more directly with classical extreme-spacing theory.
Similarly, a refined analysis of the critical window around the typical
value defined by (
3.11
) would clarify the crossover between typical
fluctuations and the large-deviation tails described here.

From the computational point of view, the renewal characterization suggests
efficient decision procedures for a prescribed threshold
s
, and hence
bisection-type algorithms for approximating
S^{*}
in large deterministic
instances. The exact formulae obtained here, especially in the exponential
case, provide benchmarks for such algorithms far beyond the range where
exhaustive search over all admissible selections is possible. A systematic
analysis of the algorithmic complexity, numerical stability, and possible
improvements of this procedure would be a useful complement to the present
probabilistic results.

Finally, the model studied here is a one-dimensional bottleneck counterpart of
maximum-diversity and maximum-dispersion problems. Extending the present exact
approach to higher-dimensional random point configurations is a challenging open problem. Understanding whether an
analogue of the threshold-crossing representation survives in special
geometries, or whether different probabilistic structures replace it, would
help connect the exact solvability found here with broader questions in random
combinatorial optimisation and spatial selection problems.
