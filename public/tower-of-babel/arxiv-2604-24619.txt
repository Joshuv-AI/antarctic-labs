CaTherine Wheels
Danny Calegari, Ino Loukidou

Abstract
. A CaTherine wheel is a surjective continuous map f : S 1 → S 2 f:S^{1}\to S^{2} such that for every closed interval I ⊂ S 1 I\subset S^{1} the image f ⁡ ( I ) f(I) is homeomorphic to a disk, and f ⁡ ( ∂ I ) f(\partial I) is contained in the boundary of this disk. CaTherine wheels arise in many areas of low-dimensional geometry and topology, including conformal dynamics (expanding Thurston maps, expanding origamis), probability theory (whole plane SLE κ \textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa} for κ ≥ 8 \kappa\geq 8 , LQG metric trees) and elsewhere. We develop their theory in generality, and explain how CaTherine wheels and their associated structures can serve as a dictionary between these various fields. Our most substantial applications are to the theory of hyperbolic 3-manifolds. If M M is a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold and G = π 1 ​ ( M ) G=\pi_{1}(M) , we show that there is a canonical bijection between four kinds of structures associated to M M : (1) orbit-equivalence classes of pseudo-Anosov flows on M M without perfect fits; (2) G G -equivariant CaTherine wheels up to conjugacy; (3) minimal G G -zippers; and (4) connected components of the space of uniform quasimorphisms on G G . This generalizes and amplifies the theory of fiberings of hyperbolic 3-manifolds over the circle and the Thurston norm.

We briefly recall the notion of a
quasimorphism

\phi:G\to\mathbb{R}

on a group. This is a function for which there is some least non-negative real

|\phi(gh)-\phi(g)-\phi(h)|\leq D(\phi)

Any (absolutely) bounded function on a group is a quasimorphism; the set of quasimorphisms
modulo bounded functions on

An unbounded quasimorphism on a finitely generated group is Lipschitz

[

[

It is a consequence of this theorem that for

If

Here is how to think about what it means for a quasimorphism to be uniform.
If
G
is a finitely generated group, a surjective
homomorphism

is uniform (as a quasimorphism) if and only if the kernel is finitely generated, and
more generally, a non-zero homomorphism

is
uniform if and only if its projective class is contained in the BNS-invariant of
G

[
15
]
. For

has finitely generated kernel if and only if it arises from

. Such homomorphisms are precisely the primitive elements of

(or, by Poincaré duality,

) that projectively intersect the interiors of the

Thus our theorem may be seen as generalizing the classical correspondence,
between orbit equivalence classes of

suspension
pseudo-Anosov flows on
M
, and cones on (open) fibered faces of
the Thurston norm ball.

One important remark to make is that the existence of a uniform quasimorphism on a group
is a structure that exists purely at the level of coarse geometry in the sense of Gromov.
In particular, we are able to give a characterization of a 3-manifold
with a pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits purely in terms of group theory. The main
advantage of our perspective is that we work directly with objects that exist on
the Gromov boundary of
G
; as an application, we are able to show
(Theorem

) that if

hyperbolic group with

, and if there is a

G

is
virtually a 3-manifold group; i.e. Cannon’s Conjecture is true for
G
. It is not
clear to what extent this represents a viable approach to the full conjecture.

Point set topology

One of the most attractive and useful aspects of the theory of CaTherine wheels is that
so much of their structure may be derived directly from the axioms without any
assumption of symmetry or analytic regularity whatsoever.

A surjective map
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
induces an equivalence relation on
S^{1}
, whose
equivalence classes are the point preimages. If
f
is a CaTherine wheel, it turns out
that the set of nontrivial equivalence classes
\mathcal{L}
admits a
canonical

partition into two subsets
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{+}\sqcup\mathcal{L}^{-}

in such a way that each of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
is
laminar
:
i.e. the equivalence relation on
S^{1}
induced by each
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
separately is closed,
and distinct equivalence classes in
\mathcal{L}^{+}
are unlinked in
S^{1}
(and similarly for

\mathcal{L}^{-}
).

The laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
let us build a canonical 2-sphere
S^{2}_{f}
with
S^{1}
as the
equator, and a surjective map
F:S^{2}_{f}\to S^{2}
whose restriction to
S^{1}
agrees with
f
.
The images
Z^{\pm}
of the upper and lower (open) hemispheres of
S^{2}_{f}
under
F
are
a zipper: they are disjoint, dense, path-connected subsets of
S^{2}
, each of which
is a countable increasing union of finite trees, and in which every point is a cut point.

Part
I
describes the relationship between
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}

and
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
and
Z^{\pm}
, and gives necessary and sufficient conditions for
a pair of laminar relations or a zipper to arise from a CaTherine wheel.

A pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
is said to have
no perfect fits
if the
equivalence classes in
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and in
\mathcal{L}^{-}
are disjoint. A laminar relation
\mathcal{L}

gives rise to an associated
boundary lamination

\Lambda:=\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})
(see
Definition
2.2
). The laminar relation is said
to have
no isolated sides
if each leaf
\lambda
of
\Lambda
is either a
nontrivial limit of leaves of
\Lambda
on either side, or it is a nontrivial limit
of leaves on one side, and bounds a nontrivial polyhedron
associated to an equivalence class
\mu
of
\mathcal{L}
with
|\mu|\geq 3
on the other side.

A zipper
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}
has the
strong landing property
if, roughly speaking,
every proper ray in
Z^{\pm}
extends continuously to a unique endpoint in
S^{2}
, and
every point in
S^{2}
arises as an endpoint of some ray in
Z^{+}
and in
Z^{-}
;
see Definition
3.9
for details.
A zipper is
hairy
if every embedded arc in
Z^{+}
has arcs branching off it on either side
and similarly for
Z^{-}
(see Definition
3.10
).

The following amalgamates Theorem
1.20
,
Theorem
1.23
, Theorem
2.13
,
and Theorem
3.14
:

Equivalence Theorem
3.17
.

There is a canonical bijection between isomorphism classes of the following objects:

(1)

CaTherine wheels
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
;

(2)

pairs of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
on
S^{1}
with no perfect fits and no isolated sides; and

(3)

hairy zippers
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}
with the strong landing property.

Thus each of the three
f,\mathcal{L}^{\pm},Z^{\pm}
are interchangable and faithful avatars
of the same abstract mathematical object. The laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
live on
S^{1}
,
the zipper
Z^{\pm}
lives on
S^{2}
, and
f
relates the two.

Here is an application. Let
\mathfrak{E}
denote the space of embeddings
S^{1}\to S^{2}
, thought
of as a subspace of the space of all maps from
S^{1}
to
S^{2}
in the compact-open topology.
Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be a nowhere locally
constant map which is not an embedding, but admits a
pseudo-isotopy
; i.e.
there is a 1-parameter family of maps
f_{t}:S^{1}\to S^{2}
for
t\in[0,1]
which are
embeddings for
t<1
and for which
f=f_{1}
. Let
\mathfrak{P}(f)
denote the space of
pseudo-isotopies for
f
. Theorem
4.10
says
that for
f
a CaTherine wheel,
\mathfrak{P}(f)
is path-connected. The
alternative to this is a
self-bumping point
in the frontier of

\mathfrak{E}
: an
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
that admits two pseudo-isotopies that are not homotopic
through a family of pseudo-isotopies. Such self-bumping points occur at a dense
subset of points in the frontier of
\mathfrak{E}
; their analysis
and classification is almost entirely unknown.

Sullivan’s dictionary

One advantage of developing the theory of CaTherine wheels in generality is that once
we identify the intrinsic ‘meaning’ of a CaTherine wheel in a specialized
context, we may transport this meaning to different contexts where it will
motivate new definitions and constructions, and give rise to interesting questions.
Let us give three examples.

1:

G
-zippers were defined and investigated in
[
30
]
for
G

the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold. Yan He and Daniel Meyer
realized that such objects and suitable generalizations should exist for
G
the
semigroup generated by an
expanding Thurston map

H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
. In the case
that there is a
G
-CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
, the zippers
Z^{\pm}
are the
full inverse images under iterates of
H^{-1}
of the

trimmed Hubbard trees

T^{\pm}_{0}
— ordinary Hubbard trees with their
1-valent vertices cut off; see Proposition
7.9
.
For general expanding Thurston maps the definition and construction of
Z^{\pm}

is more subtle; see
[
4
]
.

2:
For
G
the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
, a

G
-CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
determines a canonical properly embedded
3-manifold
\tilde{N}\subset UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
which is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is
foliated by flowlines of the geodesic flow, and which admits a free properly
discontinuous action of
G
. Such a manifold
\tilde{N}
can be constructed for

any
CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. CaTherine wheels
f
for which the
target 2-sphere admits a natural conformal structure include
f
coming from
(holomorphic) expanding Thurston maps, and whole plane
Schramm–Loewner evolution (i.e.
SLE
; see §
8
)
from
\infty
to
\infty
with driving parameter
\kappa
for
\kappa\geq 8
.
Can one define the dynamics of

on
\tilde{N}
for
H
an expanding Thurston map?
What does the geometry of a random
\tilde{N}
look like? What can one say about the
(countably many) singular orbits — how close do they come to each other?

3:
For
G
the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
,
there is an equivalence between
G
-CaTherine wheels and pseudo-Anosov flows without
perfect fits on
M
. What objects correspond to general pseudo-Anosov flows?
Many (most?) pseudo-Anosov flows are quasigeodesic, and conversely
Frankel–Landry
[

showed that every quasigeodesic flow
on a closed hyperbolic 3 manifold is homotopic to a (quasigeodesic) pseudo-Anosov flow,

For such quasigeodesic flows, the associated laminar relations

are an
example of what Frankel–Landry call an

(see
[
49
]

Definition 3.1); but not every

.
Suppose we try to define a P-CaTherine wheel
to be a surjective map

satisfying certain desirable properties,
so that in the
G

.
How can one characterize such P-CaTherine wheels intrinsically? What are the corresponding
P-zippers? Can such

be characterized in terms of topological properties of
a pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
? A tentative investigation of such matters
is begun in §
5
.

pertain to expanding Thurston maps, or to

Part I

1.
CaTherine wheels

1.1.
Definition

In what follows let
S^{1}
and
S^{2}
denote the standard circle and the standard 2-sphere
respectively. These are closed orientable manifolds, and we fix for all time a choice
of orientation on each of them.

A
closed interval

I\subset S^{1}
is a closed subset
homeomorphic to
[0,1]
. We can and do think of such an

as a (codimension 0) submanifold
of
S^{1}
; the boundary
\partial I
is both the boundary as a submanifold, and the frontier
as a closed subset of a topological space. Such an interval inherits an orientation
from
S^{1}
. If we give
[0,1]
the standard orientation, then we may choose an
orientation-preserving homeomorphism
[0,1]\to I
, and denote the images of
0
and
1

under such a homeomorphism by
I^{-}
and
I^{+}
respectively. Thus
\partial I
is the
disjoint union of
I^{-}
and
I^{+}
.

Definition 1.1

(CaTherine wheel)
.

A
CaTherine wheel
is a continuous map
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
so that

(1)

f
is surjective; and

(2)

for any closed interval
I\subset S^{1}

(a)

the image
f(I)
is homeomorphic to the closed unit disk
D^{2}
; and

(b)

the image
f(\partial I)
is contained in the boundary circle
\partial f(I)
.

Remark 1.2
.

By the Jordan Curve Theorem every topological embedding of
D^{2}
in
S^{2}
is standard,
i.e. there is a self-homeomorphism of

taking the boundary to a great circle and
the interior to an (open) half-space in the usual round metric.

Here is a suggestive image to keep in mind. If we fix
f
and ‘grow’
the interval
I
inside
S^{1}
then the image
f(I)
also grows, like an accumulating blob
of foam extruded from a nozzle with its tip always at
f(\partial I)
, which
lies in the boundary of what has been extruded so far.

Lemma 1.3

(Basic properties)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be a CaTherine wheel. Then:

(1)

for any nonempty open subset
U\subset S^{2}
there is a closed interval
I\subset S^{1}

with
f(I)\subset U
;

(2)

f
is nowhere locally constant; and

(3)

if
I
and
J
are closed intervals in
S^{1}
with disjoint interiors then

f(I)
and
f(J)
have disjoint interiors.

Proof.

Property (1) is true for any surjective continuous map, whereas property (2) follows
immediately from the fact that for
f
a CaTherine wheel,
the image of any nontrivial interval in
S^{1}
has nonempty interior.

To prove (3), choose an arbitrary point
p\in J
for which
f(p)
is in the interior
of
f(J)
. By the definition of a CaTherine wheel,
p
is in the interior of
J
and is
therefore in the complement of
I
. Let
K
denote the closed interval with
K^{-}=p
and

K^{+}=I^{+}
; thus in particular
I\subset K
and therefore
f(I)\subset f(K)
.
But
p\in\partial K
so
f(p)\in\partial f(K)
and is therefore not in the interior of
f(I)
.
Interchanging the roles of
I
and
J
finishes the proof.
∎

Two closed intervals
I,J\subset S^{1}
are
adjacent
if they have disjoint interiors and
exactly one (end)point in common. They are
complementary
if they have disjoint interiors
and both endpoints in common. The unique complementary interval to

is denoted
I^{c}
; note
that
I^{c}
is not the set-theoretic complement of
I
, rather it is the set-theoretic complement
of the interior of
I
, or equivalently it is the closure of the set-theoretic complement of
I
.
Evidently
I^{cc}=I
.

Lemma 1.4

(Adjacent intervals)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be a CaTherine wheel, and let

be two closed intervals
with disjoint interiors.

(1)

if
I
and

are adjacent, then

is a closed interval which is contained in
\partial f(I)\cap\partial f(J)
; and

(2)

if

Proof.

We have already seen by Lemma

and
f(J)
have disjoint

f(I)\cap f(J)=\partial f(I)\cap\partial f(J)
.
On the other hand,

or all of

if

. Since

the intersection

and therefore consists of a point, a closed interval, or a circle. The first case cannot occur

are adjacent and complementary respectively.

Definition 1.5

(Degenerate intervals)

Lemma 1.6

(Boundary continuous)
.

The map that sends

to

to the

Proof.

Fix an interval

to
\partial f(I)

1.2.
Monotonicity

For any closed interval
I\subset S^{1}
the subset
\partial f(I)-f(\partial I)
consists
of either one or two open intervals, depending on whether
I
is degenerate for
f
or not.
In either case, let
Y
be a component of
\partial f(I)-f(\partial I)
and let

C:=f^{-1}(Y)\subset I
. Since
f
is a CaTherine wheel and the
image of
C
has no interior,
C
is totally
disconnected. The orientation on
I
determines a total order on
C
; likewise, any choice
of orientation on the interval
Y
determines a total order on
Y
(as a set). The next
proposition relates these orders:

Proposition 1.7

(Monotonicity)
.

For any closed interval
I\subset S^{1}
and any component
Y
of
\partial f(I)-f(\partial I)

with preimage
C:=f^{-1}(Y)\subset I
there is a canonical orientation on
Y
for
which the map

is monotone increasing with respect to the associated orders.

If
I
is nondegenerate for
f
, the canonical orientation on
Y
is the one for which the
associated orientation on the closure
\overline{Y}
runs from
f(I^{-})
to
f(I^{+})
.

Proof.

We first prove this proposition for nondegenerate intervals.
Thus, let
I
be nondegenerate for
f
so that
f(I^{-})
and
f(I^{+})
are
distinct points in
\partial f(I)
. Let
Y
be one of the open complementary intervals
of
\partial f(I)-f(\partial I)
, and orient the closed interval

\overline{Y}
so that the orientation runs from
f(I^{-})
to
f(I^{+})
.

Suppose there are points
x
and
y
in
C
so that
I^{-}<x<y<I^{+}
in

, but
f(I^{-})<f(y)<f(x)<f(I^{+})
in the orientation on
\overline{Y}
.
The image of the interval

is a closed disk contained entirely in the disk

, and
therefore the interior of

contains an arc
\alpha
properly embedded in the

f(I)
and running from
f(I^{-})
to

. Likewise, the interior of

contains an arc
\beta
properly embedded in the interior of
f(I)
and running from

f(y)
to
f(I^{+})
. The pairs
\{f(I^{-}),f(x)\}
and
\{f(y),f(I^{+})\}

are linked in

by hypothesis, so

must intersect. But
this means that the disjoint intervals

and

in

, have images
whose interiors intersect, contrary to Lemma
1.3

contradiction proves the proposition for nondegenerate intervals.

Now let’s suppose

is degenerate for

. If we choose two points

in

with

for which

in

.
Now suppose there is

1.3.
Image Relations

A CaTherine wheel
f
induces an equivalence relation on
S^{1}
where
p\sim q
if and only if

f(p)=f(q)
; we refer to this as the
image relation
. Any symmetric reflexive
relation on a set is a subset of the set of unordered pairs on that set containing the
diagonal, and is therefore determined by its restriction to the set of unordered distinct pairs.
We may therefore encode the image relation by a (closed) subset of the space
\mathcal{M}
of
unordered pairs of distinct points in
S^{1}
; evidently
this subset is precisely the set
\mathcal{L}(f)
introduced in §
1.5
.

A subset
K
of
\mathcal{M}
corresponds in this way to an equivalence relation on
S^{1}
if and only
if it is transitive — i.e. if
\{p,q\}
and
\{q,r\}
are in
K

where
p,q,r
are distinct, then
\{p,r\}
is in
K
.

We shall see that
\mathcal{L}(f)
has a canonical decomposition into a
disjoint union
\mathcal{L}(f)=\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)\sqcup\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)

where each of
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
separately is closed, and comes from an
equivalence relation on
S^{1}
in the sense above.
It will turn out that an unordered pair

\{p,q\}
is in
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
resp.
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
if and only if
[p,q]
is
positively resp. negatively degenerate for
f
. Note that because
\mathcal{L}(f)
is a set of

unordered
pairs it will follow that
[p,q]
is positively resp. negatively
degenerate for
f
if and only if
[q,p]=[p,q]^{c}
is positively resp. negatively
degenerate for
f
; this is a special case of
Lemma
1.11
to be proved in the sequel.

The crux of the matter is the fact that positively (resp. negatively) degenerate pairs mapping
to distinct points in
S^{2}
are unlinked in
S^{1}
:

Proposition 1.10

(Classes unlinked)
.

Suppose
[p,q]
and
[r,s]
are both positively degenerate, or both
negatively degenerate for
f
, and
f(p)=f(q)\neq f(r)=f(s)
. Then
the pairs
\{p,q\}
and
\{r,s\}
are unlinked in
S^{1}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the proposition under the assumption that
[p,q]
and
[r,s]
are
both positively degenerate for
f
.

Suppose not, so that without loss of generality
[p,s]
is a closed oriented interval
in which
p<r<q<s
. By hypothesis
[p,s]
is nondegenerate for
f
. The
interval
[p,s]
is the union of two adjacent intervals
[p,q]
and
[q,s]
, the
first positively degenerate for
f
and the second nondegenerate. Let
x=f(p)=f(q)

and
y=f(r)=f(s)
; these are both in
\partial f([p,s])
and the complement

\partial f([p,s])-(x\cup y)=Z^{+}([p,s])\sqcup Z^{-}([p,s])
, each of which is nonempty
and is canonically oriented to run from
x
to
y
. By Lemma
1.4

the intersection
\alpha:=f([p,q])\cap f([q,s])
is a proper interval in the interior of

f([p,q])
which contains
x
and
y
but can’t contain them in the interior, and therefore

\partial\alpha=x\cup y
.

Figure 6.
The arc
\alpha
is the common boundary of
f([p,q])\cap f([q,s])
and
is proper in
f([p,s])
.

By construction each of the (nonempty!) intervals
Z^{+}([p,s])
and
Z^{-}([p,s])
is entirely contained
in
\partial f([p,q])
or
\partial f([q,s])
, and since the parameterization of

\partial f([p,q])
by its preimage in
[p,q]
is monotone non-increasing, it follows that

Z^{-}([p,s])\subset\partial f([p,q])
and
Z^{+}([p,s])\subset\partial f([q,s])
.

But
f([r,q])
is entirely contained in
f([p,q])
and therefore
Z^{+}([p,s])
is contained
in
\partial f([r,s])
. Since
[r,s]
by hypothesis is positively degenerate,
Z^{+}([r,s])

is empty; it follows that the parameterization of
Z^{+}([p,s])\subset Z^{-}([r,s])
by
its preimage in
[p,s]
is monotone positive, but by its preimage in
[r,s]
is monotone negative
which is a contradiction. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 1.11

(Complementary orientations)
.

Let
I\subset S^{1}
be a closed interval. Then
Z^{+}(I)=Z^{+}(I^{c})
and
Z^{-}(I)=Z^{-}(I^{c})
;
equivalently these intervals are equal as subsets, and their canonical orientations
disagree.

Proof.

We have already seen by Lemma
1.4
that

\partial f(I^{c})=\partial f(I)
and it is also true that
f(\partial I^{c})=f(\partial I)
.
Let
Y
be a component of
\partial f(I)-f(\partial I)
.
It suffices to show that the canonical orientations on
Y
(coming from
I
and from
I^{c}
)
disagree.

The interval
Y
is monotonely parameterized by subsets
C
and
C^{\prime}
of
I
and
I^{c}
respectively.
For each point
x
in
Y
there are
p(x)\in C
and
p^{\prime}(x)\in C^{\prime}
in the preimage, and therefore
each interval
[p(x),p^{\prime}(x)]
is either positively or negatively degenerate. In particular, we
may find at least two distinct points
x,y\in Y
for which
[p(x),p^{\prime}(x)]
and
[p(y),p^{\prime}(y)]
are
both positively degenerate or both negatively degenerate, and therefore by
Proposition
1.10
the pairs
\{p(x),p^{\prime}(x)\}
and

\{p(y),p^{\prime}(y)\}
are unlinked in
S^{1}
. But this implies that the canonical orientations
on
Y
disagree and the lemma is proved.
∎

As a special case, if
[p,q]
is positively degenerate for
f
, then
[q,p]=[p,q]^{c}
is
also positively degenerate for
f
. We may therefore unambiguously make the following
definition:

Definition 1.12

(Positive and negative relations)
.

An unordered pair
\{p,q\}
is in
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
resp.
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
if and only if

[p,q]
is positively resp. negatively degenerate for
f
.

Observe that
\mathcal{L}(f)=\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)\sqcup\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
.

Lemma 1.13

(Closed subsets)
.

Both
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
are closed as subsets of
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
1.9
.
∎

By abuse of notation, and in anticipation of things to come, we refer to an unordered pair

\{p,q\}\in\mathcal{L}(f)
as a
leaf
.

Proposition 1.14

(Relations transitive)
.

With notation as above,

(1)

each of the subsets
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
are transitive;

(2)

no leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
can have a point in common with a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
;

(3)

two adjacent degenerate intervals in
S^{1}
are either both positively
degenerate or both negatively degenerate.

In particular, each of the relations
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
is separately an
equivalence relation, and the equivalence classes are closed as subsets of
S^{1}
.

Proof.

First observe that all three conditions are equivalent: since
\mathcal{L}(f)
is transitive,
and
\mathcal{L}(f)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
it follows that each
of these subsets is individually transitive unless they have a pair of leaves with
a point in common; thus (1) and (2) are equivalent. To see that (2) is equivalent to (3),
replace an interval by its complement if necessary and apply
Lemma
1.11
.

Thus it suffices to show that if adjacent intervals
[p,q]
and
[q,r]
are both
degenerate for
f
then they are either both positively degenerate or both negatively
degenerate. If
[p,q]
and
[q,r]
are degenerate then
[p,r]
is also degenerate for
f
;
let’s suppose for simplicity that it is positively degenerate, so that
Z^{+}([p,r])
is
empty. Then
Z^{-}([p,r])
contains open subintervals both of
\partial f([p,q])-f(\partial[p,q])

and of
\partial f([q,r])-f(\partial[q,r])
and therefore
Z^{-}([p,q])
and

Z^{-}([q,r])
are nonempty. But these intervals are both degenerate, so they are both positively
degenerate. The case that
[p,r]
is negatively degenerate is similar.

Since equivalence classes of
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
or
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
are
equivalence classes of the equivalence relation
\mathcal{L}(f)
,
they are point preimages under
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
and are therefore closed as subsets of
S^{1}
.
∎

Complementary to Proposition
1.10
we have the following:

(Linking endpoints)
.

Let
\{p,q\}
be an arbitrary unordered pair of distinct points in

.
Then either
\{p,q\}
is a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
,
or there is a disjoint leaf
\{r,s\}
of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
so that

\{p,q\}
and

link in

,
and similarly with
\mathcal{L}^{+}
replaced by
\mathcal{L}^{-}
.
In particular, a pair

that does not belong to

is linked by leaves of both
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}
.

If
\{p,q\}
is a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
there is nothing to prove, so suppose not.
Let
I=[p,q]
and
I^{c}=[q,p]
. Then by hypothesis

are nonempty subsets of

\partial f(I)=\partial f(I^{c})
. Choose

and

with the
same image

. Then
[r,s]

Let
J=[p,s]
and

. Let

be the open subinterval

. By monotonicity

, and therefore

. But

1.4.
Laminar decomposition

Let us summarize where we are. We have demonstrated that the image relation

\mathcal{L}(f)
has a canonical decomposition into disjoint subsets

\mathcal{L}(f)=\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)\sqcup\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)

which are themselves associated to closed (Lemma
1.13
)
unlinked (Lemma
1.10
) equivalence relations
on
S^{1}
(Proposition
1.14
). Furthermore,
since each equivalence class in
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}(f)
is an equivalence class in
\mathcal{L}(f)
,
it is closed as a subset of
S^{1}
.

Since the concept will arise again in the sequel we formalize these properties as
follows:

Definition 1.16

(Laminar equivalence relation)
.

An equivalence relation on
S^{1}
is
laminar
if the equivalence classes are closed
as subsets of
S^{1}
, if the relation is closed in the space
\mathcal{M}
of unordered distinct pairs,
and if distinct equivalence classes are unlinked.

In particular,
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}
are (associated to) laminar equivalence relations.
We refer to these equivalence relations as the
positive image relation
and the

negative image relation
respectively.

We now use these equivalence relations to build a decomposition of a 2-sphere.
Let’s take two copies
P^{\pm}
of the hyperbolic plane, each thought of as an open
unit disk in the Poincaré model, and identify the ideal boundary of each copy with
S^{1}
.
Under this identification the union
S^{1}\cup P^{\pm}
may be naturally topologized as a sphere
that we denote
S^{2}_{f}
to distinguish it from ‘the’
S^{2}
.

For each equivalence class
\nu
in the image relation we define a closed subset
C(\nu)

of
S^{2}_{f}
as follows:

(1)

if
\nu
consists of a single point
p\in S^{1}
then
C(\nu)=p
;

(2)

if
\nu
is a nontrivial element of the positive image relation,
then
C(\nu)=\nu\cup H^{+}(\nu)
where
H^{+}(\nu)
is the convex hull of
\nu

in
P^{+}
; and

(3)

if
\nu
is a nontrivial element of the negative image relation,
then
C(\nu)=\nu\cup H^{-}(\nu)
where
H^{-}(\nu)
is the convex hull of
\nu

in
P^{-}
.

Note that each
C(\nu)
is closed as a subset of
S^{2}_{f}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the
collection of subsets
C(\nu)
as
\nu
ranges over the equivalence classes of the image
relation.

Each convex hull
H^{+}(\nu)
is bounded by a countable family of bi-infinite geodesics in
P^{+}

limiting to pairs of points of
\nu
that bound a maximal open interval in
S^{1}
in the
complement of
\nu
. We refer to a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
as a
boundary leaf
if it corresponds
to such a boundary component of
H^{+}(\nu)
(and similarly for
\mathcal{L}^{-}
). Note that we
include the case that
\nu
consists of only two points, in which case
H^{\pm}(\nu)
consists
of a single bi-infinite geodesic whose endpoints should be considered a boundary leaf.
It is immediate from Lemma
1.13
that the set of boundary leaves is closed.

Figure 7.
Nontrivial subsets
C(\nu)
in
S^{2}_{f}
.

Lemma 1.17

(Boundary leaves accumulate)
.

Let
\ell
be a boundary leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
corresponding to some boundary component of

H^{+}(\nu)
. Then
\ell
is a limit of a sequence of boundary leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{+}

on each side (in
P^{+}
) that does not intersect
H^{+}(\nu)
.

Likewise, if
p\in S^{1}
is an arbitrary point not in
a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
then
p
is a limit of nested boundary leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
, i.e.
leaves whose endpoints approach
p
from both sides in
S^{1}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell=\{p,q\}
in such a way that the oriented interval
[p,q]\subset S^{1}

does not intersect
\nu
in its interior. Then by Lemma
1.15
,
for every
r\in(p,q)
there is a positive
leaf
\{s,s^{\prime}\}
that links
\{p,r\}
. The endpoints
s,s^{\prime}
are themselves
contained in a positive equivalence class
\mu
that is entirely contained in
(p,q)

by Proposition
1.10
and therefore without loss of generality we may
assume
\mu
is disjoint from the intervals
(p,s)
and
(s^{\prime},q)
and therefore that

\{s,s^{\prime}\}
is a boundary leaf of
H^{+}(\mu)
separating
H^{+}(\nu)
from

H^{+}(\mu)
.

We may repeat the argument with
s
in place of
r
and obtain a new boundary leaf

\{t,t^{\prime}\}
with
t\in(p,s)
and
t^{\prime}\in(s,q)
. Actually, by
Proposition
1.10
we must have
t^{\prime}\in(s^{\prime},q)
. Thus we
obtain a sequence of boundary leaves whose endpoints move monotonely towards
p
and

. But

The second assertion of the lemma is proved almost exactly the same as the first
assertion, with

∎

Definition 1.19

(Upper semi-continuous decomposition)

Let

(2)

(4)

(upper semi-continuity): for every element

of
\mathcal{C}
and every open neighborhood

of

of

with

such that

is a union of elements of
\mathcal{C}
.

If

is an upper semi-continuous decomposition of

we may form the quotient space

in which the points of
X_{\mathcal{C}}
correspond to the elements of
\mathcal{C}
, and
give

the quotient topology.

In our context where
X
is a compact Hausdorff space, Proposition 2.9 in
[
56
]
says
that when

is an upper semi-continuous decomposition of

, the quotient space

is also a compact Hausdorff space.

Theorem 1.20

(Laminar decomposition)

Theorem 1.23

(Zippers)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be a CaTherine wheel. Define
Z^{+}:=\cup_{I}Z^{+}(I)
and

Z^{-}:=\cup_{I}Z^{-}(I)
as subsets of
S^{2}
. Then
Z^{\pm}=F(P^{\pm})
and furthermore,

(1)

Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
are disjoint, dense, consist entirely of cut points,
and each of them is a countable increasing union of finite trees
(in particular, they are path connected, and in the path topology
they are each homeomorphic to a
\sigma
-compact topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree);

(2)

each of
Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
satisfy the strong landing property for proper rays;
in other words: every embedded half-open interval
r:[0,1)\to Z^{+}
extends
to an embedded closed interval
\bar{r}:[0,1]\to S^{2}
in one of three ways:

(a)

(type 0):
\bar{r}(1)\in Z^{+}
;

(b)

(type 1):
\bar{r}(1)\in S^{2}-(Z^{+}\cup Z^{-})
and there is another embedded
half-open interval
s:[0,1)\to Z^{-}
extending to
\bar{s}:[0,1]\to S^{2}
with
r(1)=s(1)
; or

(c)

(type 2):
\bar{r}(1)\in Z^{-}
;

and conversely every point in
S^{2}
is obtained as
\bar{r}(1)
for some ray

r
; and similarly for
Z^{-}
; and

(3)

each of
Z^{\pm}
is hairy: every embedded interval in
Z^{+}
has arcs
branching off it on either side, and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

In fact, every
r:[0,1)\to Z^{+}
actually has image contained in
Z_{e}^{+}(I)
for some
closed interval
I\subset S^{1}
and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

Proof.

We have already seen by Lemma
1.22
that
Z_{e}^{+}(I)=F(\gamma^{+})

where
\gamma^{+}\subset P^{+}
is the positively oriented geodesic running from
I^{-}
to

I^{+}
. Thus
F(P^{+})=\cup_{I}Z_{e}^{+}(I)
. To prove the stronger statement that

it suffices to find, for each

,
a geodesic
\gamma^{+}
containing

whose endpoints are not in
\mu
, so that

.
Since

is closed and totally disconnected, this is easy to arrange. This proves

and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

Since

is the map to the quotient space defined by
\mathcal{C}
, and since the elements
of
\mathcal{C}
that intersect

are disjoint from the elements that intersect

, it

and
Z^{-}
are disjoint. Since the closure of each of

in

has at least two points but is nowhere dense, the

is a cut point (and similarly for

).

Since

are path-connected, so are

. It follows, because

are disjoint, dense and path-connected, that every two

are joined by a unique embedded path in

(and likewise
for

) or else an innermost pair of distinct embedded paths between two points of

would form a Jordan curve which would necessarily intersect

.

Here is an explicit way to construct these unique paths. If

are any two distinct points,

,

that intersect

in nonempty convex hulls

,

and

and let

be the (oriented) geodesic segment

is an embedded path in

running from

to

. To see this, extend

to a bi-infinite oriented geodesic
\gamma^{+}

running between the endpoints

of a closed interval

.
By Lemma
1.22
the map

is a monotone surjection,
and therefore its restriction to
\delta
is an embedded subpath of
Z_{e}^{+}(I)
running
from

to

. Notice by the way that this path is also a subpath of

for every interval

that intersects both

and

.

To see that each of

whose

. Any equivalence class

that intersects

must intersect its

.
But

is a finite union of embedded closed paths, each contained in

. Since there is a unique path in

joining any two points,

Now let’s prove the strong landing property. Let

be an

we may choose a point

with

and let
\gamma_{n}
be the oriented geodesic in

from

to

. Then

. There is some subsequence of

that converges to some

, and we let
\gamma^{+}
be the bi-infinite geodesic in

whose closure contains

and

. Let

be the closed interval

.

and

if necessary with image in

.

Finally we show that

are hairy. Suppose not, so that without loss of generality

in

(say) which has no branching on the right hand side. Let
\gamma
be
an oriented geodesic in
P^{+}
containing a segment

mapping to

Consider the intersection of
\gamma^{\prime}
with decomposition elements

, and
for each of these consider the intersection of

with the interval of

on the right of

∎

The objects

2.
Laminar Relations

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
2.13

which is a converse to
Theorem
1.20
. Namely, we shall characterize exactly
which pairs of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{+}
,
\mathcal{L}^{-}
can arise from CaTherine wheels.
This will be useful in the sequel for constructing examples with desirable properties.

2.1.
Definitions

Although it is not strictly necessary, it is convenient to express things in the
language of laminations, since this makes it easier to connect the contents of this paper
to the literature. See for example Baik–Kim
[
7
]
for an introduction to the theory
and significance of laminations in low-dimensional topology, or
Thurston
[
93
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Lamination)
.

A
lamination
is a closed subset of the space
\mathcal{M}
of unordered pairs of points
in
S^{1}
such that no two elements of this subset are linked in
S^{1}
. The elements
of a lamination are called
leaves
.

It is convenient to alternate between thinking of a leaf of a lamination as an unordered
pair of points in
S^{1}
, or as a geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
; likewise, we will think of a nontrivial
equivalence class
\nu
of a laminar relation either as a closed subset of
S^{1}
, or
as its convex hull
H(\nu)
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Thinking of leaves as geodesics in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, it makes sense to think of each leaf as having
two ‘sides’. If
\Lambda
is a lamination, a leaf
\lambda
of
\Lambda
may be
a limit of leaves of
\Lambda
on both sides, on one side, or on neither side.
A leaf which is a limit of leaves of
\Lambda
on neither side is an
isolated leaf
.

There is a close relationship between laminations and laminar relations, although this
relationship has some subtlety. This is explained carefully in
[
93
]
.
We do not pursue this matter in the greatest possible generality, restricting ourselves
to the laminations and laminar relations that are important for CaTherine wheels.

First of all, a laminar relation determines a lamination as follows:

Definition 2.2

(Boundary lamination)
.

A laminar relation
\mathcal{L}
determines a lamination
\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})
whose elements
are the boundary leaves of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e. the boundaries of the convex hulls of the
equivalence classes of
\mathcal{L}
.

To be precise, if
\nu
is a nontrivial equivalence class of
\mathcal{L}
, we may take the
convex hull
H(\nu)
. If
|\nu|=2
then
H(\nu)
consists of a single geodesic,
and this geodesic is a leaf of
\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})
. If
|\nu|>2
then
H(\nu)
is a convex
polygon, and the sides of
H(\nu)
are leaves of
\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})
.

A lamination that arises this way is called
a
boundary lamination
.

A lamination
\Lambda
generates a laminar relation
\mathcal{L}
in several different ways,
which we now distinguish:

Definition 2.3

(Two relations)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a lamination. The
little relation
, denoted
\textnormal{rel}(\Lambda)
,
is the smallest closed equivalence relation on unordered pairs that contains
\Lambda
.

The
big relation
, denoted
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
is the smallest closed equivalence
relation on unordered pairs that contains
\Lambda
, and contains
every unordered pair that crosses only countably many leaves of
\Lambda
.

It is straightforward to check that both
\textnormal{rel}(\Lambda)
and
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
are
laminar relations. Notice that every two distinct elements of
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)

are separated from each other in the disk
by uncountably many distinct elements of
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
; this is an essential
property if we want the quotient of the disk by this relation to be Hausdorff
and path-connected.

Remark 2.4
.

Thurston defines only the little relation in
[
93
]
, and denotes
it
Rel
, which is an unfortunate clash of notation. For the relations that arise
from CaTherine wheels it is the big relation that is important, as we shall see,
and it seems to make sense for us to denote it by
Rel
.

In general, neither
rel
nor
Rel
are inverse to
Lam
. However for the laminar
relations that arise from CaTherine wheels the situation is cleaner.

Proposition 2.5

(Back and forth)
.

Suppose
\Lambda
is a boundary lamination. Then no point
p\in S^{1}
is contained in
more than two leaves of
\Lambda
.

Conversely, suppose
\Lambda
is a lamination such that

(1)

no point
p\in S^{1}
is contained in more than two leaves of
\Lambda
; and

(2)

\Lambda
has no isolated leaves.

Let
\mathcal{L}:=\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
. Then
\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})=\Lambda
; in particular,

\Lambda
is a boundary lamination.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is a laminar relation, and
\Lambda=\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})
. Let
p\in S^{1}

and let
\nu\subset S^{1}
be the equivalence class of
\mathcal{L}
containing
p
.
Then
\{p,q\}
is a leaf of
\Lambda
if and only if
(p,q)\subset S^{1}

or
(q,p)\subset S^{1}
is a maximal open interval in the complement of
\nu
;
in particular, at most two leaves of
\Lambda
may contain
p
.

Conversely, suppose
\Lambda
is a lamination satisfying the two properties above.
Let’s realize
\Lambda
as a geodesic lamination in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Since
\Lambda
has no
isolated leaves, each leaf

is a limit of leaves
\lambda_{i}
from at least
one side; and furthermore, the approximating leaves
\lambda_{i}
can have no endpoints
in common with
\lambda
or we would violate bullet (1). A countable closed set
of leaves must have some isolated leaves, by considering Cantor–Bendixson rank;
thus the nontrivial equivalence classes of
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
are of exactly two kinds:
the endpoints of leaves
\lambda
of
\Lambda
that are not isolated on either side,
and the closures in
S^{1}
of components

of
\mathbb{H}^{2}-\Lambda
. From this it
follows that
\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L})=\Lambda
as claimed.
∎

Before we state the main theorem of this section,
we must state some elements of decomposition theory,
and a theorem of R. L. Moore
[
78
]
about
upper semi-continuous decompositions of 2-manifolds.
We state the theorem only in the case that the 2-manifold
in question is either a sphere or a disk, since these are the only cases we need to use,
but the reader should know that Moore’s theorem holds more generally. An elegant modern
proof is given by Timorin
[
94
]
.

First a couple of definitions.

Definition 2.9

(Cellular)
.

A closed subset
K
of a manifold is
cellular
if it is the intersection of
a nested sequence of closed balls.

Definition 2.10

(Shrinkable decomposition)
.

An upper semi-continuous decomposition
\mathcal{C}
of
X
is
shrinkable
if for every
open cover
\mathcal{V}
and every
\mathcal{C}
-saturated open cover
\mathcal{U}
(i.e. one
for which each
U\in\mathcal{U}
is a union of elements of
\mathcal{C}
) there is a
homeomorphism
h:X\to X
such that for every
K\in\mathcal{C}
the set
h(K)
is contained
in
V
for some
V\in\mathcal{V}
, and for every
x\in X
there is
U\in\mathcal{U}

so that
x,h(x)\in U
.

The following theorem is essentially due to Bing
[
16
]
; for a proof see e.g.
Theorem 4.4 from
[
56
]
:

Theorem 2.11

(Shrinkable is approximable)
.

If
X
is a complete metric space and
\mathcal{C}
is a shrinkable upper semi-continuous
decomposition, then the map
f:X\to X_{\mathcal{C}}
from
X
to its quotient space is a
near-homeomorphism; i.e. for every open covering
\mathcal{U}
of
X_{\mathcal{C}}
there is a homeomorphism

h:X\to X_{\mathcal{C}}
such that for every
x\in X
the points
f(x)
and
h(x)
are in some

U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Notice in particular that if
\mathcal{C}
is shrinkable,
X_{\mathcal{C}}
is homeomorphic to
X
.
Finally we state Moore’s theorem:

Theorem 2.12

(Moore; decompositions of 2-manifolds)
.

Let
X
be one of
S^{2}
or
D^{2}
and let
\mathcal{C}
be an upper semi-continuous decomposition
of
X
. Suppose every element of
\mathcal{C}
is cellular, and if
X
is
D^{2}
suppose further
that every element is non-separating. Then
\mathcal{C}
is shrinkable.

Together with Theorem
2.11
this implies that
X_{\mathcal{C}}
is
homeomorphic to
X
, and the map
X\to X_{\mathcal{C}}
is approximable by homeomorphisms.

We are now ready to state and prove the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 2.13

(CaTherine wheels from laminations)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
be a pair of laminar relations. Then
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
arise from a CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
if and only if

(1)

(no isolated sides): the laminar relations have no isolated sides; and

(2)

(no perfect fits): no pair of leaves of the laminar relations
\lambda^{\pm}\in\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

have an endpoint in common.

Proof.

First we show that the conditions are necessary. The fact that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no
isolated sides follows from Lemma
1.17
, and
the fact that no pair of leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have an endpoint in common follows from
Proposition
1.14
.

Now we show that the conditions are sufficient.
We repeat the constructions of an
upper semi-continuous decomposition
\mathcal{C}
of a 2-sphere as in
§
1.4
as follows. First, build a 2-sphere

S^{2}_{\Lambda}
from the union of
S^{1}
with two hyperbolic planes
P^{\pm}
.
Associated to
\mathcal{L}^{+}
we form a decomposition
\mathcal{C}
of
S^{2}_{\Lambda}
whose elements
are single points
p\in S^{1}
not contained in either of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
, and
convex hulls
C(\nu):=\nu\cup H^{+}(\nu)
for
\nu
a nontrivial equivalence class
associated to
\mathcal{L}^{+}
, and likewise for
\mathcal{L}^{-}
.

We claim that
\mathcal{C}
forms an upper semi-continuous decomposition of
S^{2}_{\Lambda}
. The
proof very closely mirrors the proofs of Lemma
1.17
and
Theorem
1.20
and we are consequently brief.
First we show that an arbitrary
p\in S^{1}
not in a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
resp.
\mathcal{L}^{-}
has a
positive resp. negative rainbow.

Lemma 2.14

(Rainbow)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
has no isolated sides, and no perfect fits. Then
every
p\in S^{1}
not contained in a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
resp.
\mathcal{L}^{-}
has a positive
resp. negative rainbow.

Proof.

Define
\Lambda^{\pm}:=\textnormal{Lam}(\mathcal{L}^{\pm})
. We define a partial order on

\Lambda^{+}
by
\mu>\lambda
if the leaf
\mu
separates
p
from
\lambda
. Choose some maximal
ascending chain. Any such chain must have a
cofinal countable increasing subchain; call it
\{s_{i},s_{i}^{\prime}\}
where

s_{i}
and
s_{i}^{\prime}
approach
p
monotonely from either side. None of
s_{i}
or
s_{i}^{\prime}
can
be equal to
p
since by hypothesis
p
is not contained in a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
. Furthermore
we may assume that no two succesive
s_{i}
or
s_{i}^{\prime}
are equal, since otherwise

\{s_{i},s_{i}^{\prime}\}
and
\{s_{i+1},s_{i+1}^{\prime}\}
are both boundary leaves
of the same element of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and we may choose the greater one and discard the other.
We claim this sequence forms a rainbow. For if not, we may extract a limit

\{s_{\infty},s_{\infty}^{\prime}\}
which is a leaf of
\Lambda^{+}
because
\Lambda^{+}

is closed. But
\Lambda^{+}
is a boundary lamination, and therefore either

\{s_{\infty},s_{\infty}^{\prime}\}
is a limit of an infinite
decreasing

sequence of leaves from above, or it is the boundary of
H(\mu)
for some nontrivial equivalence
class
\mu
of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
, and some other leaf of
\partial H(\mu)
separates
p
from

\{s_{\infty},s_{\infty}^{\prime}\}
; in particular, the original sequence was not cofinal after
all, contrary to hypothesis. Thus this sequence forms a rainbow as claimed.
∎

We are now in a position to prove that
\mathcal{C}
is an upper semi-continuous decomposition.
The classes of
\mathcal{C}
are closed and disjoint by construction. The argument in
Theorem
1.20
that the union of
\mathcal{C}
is all of
S^{2}_{f}

only used the fact that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
are closed, and that they have no isolated sides.
Likewise, the proof that the decomposition is upper semi-continuous only used the
fact that the
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no isolated side, and the existence of rainbows as
proved in Lemma
1.17
. Thus we may repeat the proof,
using Lemma
2.14
and the hypothesis that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no isolated sides.
In conclusion,
\mathcal{C}
is an upper semi-continuous decomposition of
S^{2}_{\Lambda}
.

At this point we appeal to Moore’s Theorem
2.12
and Bing’s
Theorem
2.11
. Let
F
be the map from
S^{2}_{\Lambda}
to its quotient
space, and let
f
be the restriction to
S^{1}
. The decomposition
\mathcal{C}
is evidently
cellular, so the quotient space is homeomorphic to a sphere, and we may choose an
identification of it with
S^{2}
so that
F:S^{2}_{\Lambda}\to S^{2}
and
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
.
To see that
f
is a CaTherine wheel, choose a closed interval
I
. This bounds closed
half spaces
H^{\pm}(I)
in
P^{\pm}
, and the union
D(I):=H^{\pm}(I)\cup I
is a disk. The
restriction of the elements of
\mathcal{C}
to this disk is an upper semi-continuous
decomposition, and the elements are still cellular and non-separating, since each
decomposition element is either entirely contained in
H^{+}(I)\cup I
or
H^{-}(I)\cup I
.
Thus
F(D(I))
is a disk. Since each decomposition element of
\mathcal{C}|D(I)
meets
I
,
we also have that
f(I)
is a disk. Since
\partial I\in\partial D(I)
it follows
that
f(\partial I)\subset\partial f(I)
and the theorem is proved.

Remark 2.15

If each equivalence class of

Remark 2.16
.

A pair of laminar relations

with no perfect fits and no isolated sides are

in

.

By the way, Moore’s Theorem

and Bing’s Theorem

together prove that every CaTherine wheel can be approximated by embeddings. In fact

,

.

.

We may simply repeat the proof of the first part of

and the fact that

3.
Zippers

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.14

which is a converse to Theorem
1.23
. Namely, we shall
characterize exactly which zippers
Z^{\pm}
can arise from CaTherine wheels.

3.1.
Definitions

In this subsection we give the definition of a zipper, and recall some basic properties
and constructions from
[
30
]
. We are concerned with
the path connectivity of certain subsets of
S^{2}
; thus throughout this section ‘component’
means path component, and ‘cut point’ means path cut point (i.e. a point in a
path connected set whose removal creates at least two distinct path components).

Definition 3.1

(Zipper)
.

A pair of nonempty subsets
Z^{\pm}
of
S^{2}
are a
zipper
if they satisfy the following
conditions:

(1)

Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
are disjoint;

(2)

each of
Z^{\pm}
is dense in
S^{2}
;

(3)

every point of
Z^{\pm}
is a cut point; and

(4)

each of
Z^{\pm}
is a countable increasing union of finite trees.

Remark 3.2
.

In fact this definition diverges very slightly from the definition of zippers
in
[
30
]
. The context of that paper is
that one has a cocompact Kleinian group
G
acting on
S^{2}_{\infty}
,
and in that context, what is called a zipper is a
pair of nonempty
G
-invariant
subsets
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}_{\infty}
that are disjoint and path-connected.
Since
G
acts minimally on
S^{2}_{\infty}
, both of
Z^{\pm}
are necessarily dense.
Furthermore, it is shown (Proposition 2.10 in that paper) that
any zipper in this sense contains a minimal subzipper, for which every point of

Z^{\pm}
is a cut point, and each of
Z^{\pm}
is
necessarily a countable increasing union of finite trees. In particular, a minimal zipper
in the sense of
[
30
]
is a zipper in the sense of
Definition
3.1
, and conversely a zipper in the sense of
Definition
3.1
invariant under a cocompact Kleinian group
G

contains a minimal subzipper in the sense of
[
30
]
.
In this paper by abuse of notation we will reserve the word zipper for a zipper
in the sense of Definition
3.1
, and will refer to a minimal
zipper in the sense of
[
30
]
as a
G
-zipper.

In the sequel we will appeal to certain constructions and facts proved about

G
-zippers in
[
30
]
that do not use
G
-invariance, and
therefore hold also for zippers.

Lemma 3.3

(Unique path)
.

Let
Z^{\pm}
be a zipper. Then any two distinct points in
Z^{+}
may be joined by a
unique embedded path in
Z^{+}
, and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

Proof.

Bullet (4) from the definition implies that each of
Z^{\pm}
is path-connected. If

p,q\in Z^{+}
are joined by distinct embedded paths
\alpha,\beta
then by restricting
to smaller subpaths if necessary, we may assume
\alpha\cup\beta
is a Jordan curve
in
Z^{+}
, so that by disjointness,
Z^{-}
is forced to lie in one of the complementary
components. But this violates density.
∎

The set of
ends
of a zipper
Z^{\pm}
, denoted
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
, is the set of topological ends
in the sense of Freudenthal (although we will give them a different topology);
by bullet (4) of Definition
3.1

we can take
\mathcal{E}^{+}=\varprojlim\pi_{0}(Z^{+}-T)
where the
limit is taken over all (compact) finite subtrees
T\subset Z^{+}
, and similarly for

Z^{-}
. Thus one may easily see that the ends of
Z^{\pm}

are in bijection with equivalence classes of (unparameterized) proper rays,
where
r\sim r^{\prime}
if they are equal away from some pair of compact initial segments.

It is convenient to define the convex hull of a subset
S\subset Z^{+}\sqcup\mathcal{E}^{+}
.
This is exactly what the name suggests: it is the union of all embedded paths (finite
or infinite) ‘between’ pairs of distinct elements of
S
(with some exceptional
cases when
|S|<2
) but we spell it out:

Definition 3.4

(Convex hull)
.

Let
S
be a subset of
Z^{+}\sqcup\mathcal{E}^{+}
. If
S
is empty or consists of a single point of

\mathcal{E}^{+}
, the convex hull of
S
is empty. Otherwise the convex hull of
S
is the smallest
path-connected subset of
Z^{+}
containing
S\cap Z^{+}
and containing rays representing
every element of
S\cap\mathcal{E}^{+}
.

The key property of zippers that we shall use is that the sets
\mathcal{E}^{+}
have a natural

circular order
, which can be completed by adding certain objects called
ideal
ends
.

Lemma 3.5

(Circular order)
.

The end spaces
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
each admit a canonical circular order.

This is proved in
[
30
]
, Lemma 2.13. Since we need to
understand the relationship of this order to the topology of
Z^{\pm}
and the way they
are embedded in
S^{2}
we describe the circular order explicitly. To give a circular
order on a set is to give the assignment clockwise or anticlockwise to each distinct
ordered triple of elements in a set, in a way which is invariant under cyclic permutation,
and is compatible on distinct 4-tuples of elements.
If
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
are three distinct ends, we may find disjoint representative
rays
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
that begin on some compact subtree
K
. We may then find a closed
disk
D
in
S^{2}
that contains
K
in the interior but does not contain all of any
of the
r_{i}
. Let
p_{i}
be the first point of intersection of
r_{i}
with
\partial D
.
The Jordan curve
\partial D
inherits an orientation from
D
which in turn inherits it from

S^{2}
, and
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
thereby inherit a circular order from
\partial D
. This is
easily seen to be independent of choices, and defines the circular order on
\mathcal{E}^{+}

and likewise on
\mathcal{E}^{-}
.

Each of the subsets
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
gets a natural
order topology
from its circular
order (this is quite different from the usual Freudenthal topology on the end space).
Any circularly ordered set is Hausdorff in its order topology. The spaces
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
furthermore are
separable, by bullet (4) of Definition
3.1
.

A set with a separable (circular) order topology has a natural separable order completion. We
denote these completed spaces by
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
. The points of
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}-\mathcal{E}^{\pm}

are represented by geometric objects called
ideal gaps
that we now define,
following
[
30
]
, § 2.5.

If
e^{L},e^{R}\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
, we define
[e^{L},e^{R}]\subset\mathcal{E}^{+}
to be the set

[e^{L},e^{R}]:=\{e^{L},e^{R}\}\cup\{e\in\mathcal{E}^{+}\text{ such that }e^{L},e,e^{R}\text{ is anticlockwise}\}

Lemma 3.6

(Nonempty intersection)
.

Suppose there is an infinite nested sequence

\cdots[e_{i+1}^{L},e_{i+1}^{R}]\subset[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]\subset\cdots

whose intersection is empty. For each
i
let
\gamma_{i}\subset Z^{+}
denote the convex hull of

e_{i}^{L}
and
e_{i}^{R}
(this is a properly embedded line), oriented to run from
e_{i}^{L}
to
e_{i}^{R}
.
Then there is some
i
so that the intersection
X:=\cap_{j\geq i}\gamma_{j}
is either
a point, or a compact, nonempty interval.

This is parallel to
[
30
]
, Lemma 2.15 and the proof is identical.
Note that we do not assume the
e_{i}^{L}
or the
e_{i}^{R}
are all distinct, though of course
the hypothesis of the lemma implies that at least one of the sequences may be chosen to
be so.

We are now in a position to define ideal gaps.

Definition 3.7

(End nested; ideal gap)
.

A sequence of proper lines
\gamma_{i}
in
Z^{\pm}
which are the convex hulls of a
sequence of nested intervals
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
in
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
is said to be
end nested
.

A point
p\in Z^{\pm}
together with an end nested sequence of proper lines

\gamma_{i}
associated to a nested sequence of closed intervals in
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
with empty
intersection for which
p\in\cap_{i}\gamma_{i}
(as in the statement of
Lemma
3.6
) is called an
ideal gap
. Two ideal
gaps are equivalent if they contain cofinal end nested subsequences of some end
nested sequence.

Figure 12.
Branch points in
Z^{+}
are in the support of
>2
ideal gaps.

Informally, an ideal gap is an equivalence class of pairs consisting of a
point
p\in Z^{+}
together with
a ‘direction of approaching’
p
in the complement
S^{2}-Z^{+}
(and similarly for
Z^{-}
).
A point
p
as above is said to be contained in the
support
of the ideal gap (and by abuse of notation,
the support of an equivalence class). From the proof of Lemma
3.6

(see
[
30
]
, Lemma 2.15) one may see that each equivalence
class of ideal gap has support of the form
\cup_{i}\cap_{j\geq i}\gamma_{j}
for some
end-nested sequence, and is therefore either a single point or a closed subset of
Z^{\pm}
homeomorphic
to an interval. If an interval, this interval is necessarily closed, since any
ray it contains would determine an end that lies in the intersection of all the
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]

contrary to hypothesis. For a zipper that is
hairy
(see Definition
3.10
)
the support of an equivalence class of ideal gap necessarily consists of a single point
(see Lemma
3.11
).

Lemma 3.8

(Order completion)
.

The union of
\mathcal{E}^{+}
together with the equivalence classes of ideal gaps of
Z^{+}

admit a natural circular ordering which is equal to the order completion
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{+}
,
and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

This is immediate from the definition of ideal gaps, and from
Lemma
3.6
.

3.2.
Proof of the main theorem

The zippers that arise from CaTherine wheels are not arbitrary, but satisfy two
important properties. First, they satisfy the strong landing property:

Definition 3.9

(Strong landing property)
.

Let
Z^{\pm}
be a zipper. Then
Z^{\pm}
have the
strong landing property
if
every ray in
Z^{+}
, i.e. every embedded half-open interval
r:[0,1)\to Z^{+}
extends
to an embedded closed interval
\bar{r}:[0,1]\to S^{2}
in one of three ways:

(1)

(type 0):
\bar{r}(1)\in Z^{+}
;

(2)

(type 1):
\bar{r}(1)\in S^{2}-(Z^{+}\cup Z^{-})
and there is another embedded
half-open interval
s:[0,1)\to Z^{-}
extending to
\bar{s}:[0,1]\to S^{2}
with

\bar{r}(1)=\bar{s}(1)
; or

(3)

(type 2):
\bar{r}(1)\in Z^{-}
;

and conversely every point in
S^{2}
is obtained as
\bar{r}(1)
for some ray

r
; and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

Second, they are hairy:

Definition 3.10

(Hairy)
.

A zipper
Z^{\pm}
is
hairy
if for every closed oriented interval
I
embedded in
Z^{+}

there are nontrivial rays
r,l
in
Z^{+}
that begin on
I
and intersect

I
only at these initial points, and which lie respectively to the right and to the left
of the oriented interval
I
in
S^{2}
, and similarly for
Z^{-}
.

We refer to
r
and
l
as above as ‘right and left hairs’ on
I
.
Let us now state and prove some important consequences of hairiness.

Lemma 3.11

(Ideal gaps of hairy zippers)
.

Suppose
Z^{\pm}
is a hairy zipper. Then the support of each equivalence class of ideal gap
is a single point.

Proof.

The support of an equivalence class is the intersection
J:=\cup_{i}\cap_{j\geq i}\gamma_{j}

of end nested proper lines associated to a nested sequence of closed
intervals
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
in
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
with empty intersection.
If
J
contains more than one point, it cannot be hairy, since otherwise there would
be a ray
r
starting at an interior point of
J
on a side that forces the end
e

associated to
r
to lie in each of the intervals
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
, contrary to the
hypothesis that they have an empty intersection. Thus
J
consists of a single point.
∎

Lemma 3.12

(Order completions are circles)
.

Suppose
Z^{\pm}
is a hairy zipper. Then the order completions
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
are
homeomorphic to circles
S^{1}_{\pm}
.

Proof.

We have already seen that
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
, and therefore also
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
, are separable.
Furthermore, we claim
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
have no
gaps
, i.e. nontrivial
closed intervals
[e^{L},e^{R}]
in
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
with no interior points. To see this,
suppose to the contrary that
[e^{L},e^{R}]
is an interval with no interior points. Then
each of
e^{L}
and
e^{R}
is either an ordinary end or an ideal gap, and the convex hull
of these two elements or supports as appropriate is a nontrivial interval

J\subset Z^{\pm}
which is closed as a subspace of
Z^{\pm}
. But exactly the same
argument as the proof of Lemma
3.11
shows that
J

must fail to be hairy on at least one side, or we would be able to find a point
in the interior of
[e^{L},e^{R}]
. This contradiction proves the claim.

A separable, complete circularly ordered set with no gaps is homeomorphic to a
circle; see e.g.
[
47
]
, Construction 5.7.
∎

Lemma 3.13

(Laminar relations from hairy zippers)
.

Suppose
Z^{\pm}
is a hairy zipper.
Define equivalence relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
on
S^{1}_{\pm}
whose trivial equivalence classes
are precisely the points of
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
and whose nontrivial classes
are generated by pairs of ideal gaps of
Z^{\pm}
with the same support. Then
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
are
laminar relations with no isolated sides.

Proof.

It is evident that these really are equivalence classes, since the set of ideal gaps
with support
p
is disjoint from the set of ideal gaps with support
q
, by the
definition of support.

The first thing to see is that the equivalence classes of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
are unlinked.
This is because if
p,q\in Z^{+}
are distinct points, and
\gamma_{i}
is an end nested
sequence associated to
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
with
p:=\cup_{i}\cap_{j\geq i}\gamma_{j}
then
the
\gamma_{j}
are eventually contained in the component of
Z^{+}-q
containing
p
, and
conversely with the roles of
p
and
q
interchanged.
It follows that any pair of ideal gaps supported by
p
or
q
are approximated by
pairs of ideal points, so that the convex hull of the pair converging to
p
is disjoint
from the convex hull of the pair converging to
q
. The claim follows.

The second thing to see is that the equivalence classes of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
are closed.
This is immediate for the trivial equivalence classes, so let
p\in Z^{+}
be a point
which is the support of a set
\nu\in S^{1}_{+}
of ideal gaps. We shall show, for every

x\in S^{1}_{+}-\nu
that
x
is contained in a nontrivial interval in
S^{1}_{+}
disjoint
from
\nu
. If
x
corresponds to a ray
r
then we may truncate
r
so that it lies
in a component of
Z^{+}-p
and then by hairiness, find rays on either side of
r
separating
it from every ideal gap of
p
. If
x
corresponds to an ideal point with support

q\neq p
then an argument as in the previous paragraph, together with hairiness, gives
the desired separation. Thus
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
are laminar relations.

What are the boundary leaves? They all arise in the following way: for each

p\in Z^{+}
and each component
K
of
Z^{+}-p
the set of rays of
Z^{+}
contained in
K

correspond to ends in
S^{1}_{+}
that are all contained in a minimal closed interval,
and the endpoints of that closed interval are (by construction) ideal points in the class
of
p
. Conversely, suppose
\{x,y\}\subset S^{+}_{1}
is a boundary leaf of
the class with support
p
. Pick a point in
S^{1}_{+}
which is represented by a ray
r
.
This ray can be truncated to a ray contained in some component of
Z^{+}-p
and therefore
this boundary leaf is of the form above.

Notice by the way that if
p\in Z^{+}
is 2-valent (i.e.
Z^{+}-p
has exactly 2 components)
then the equivalence class with support
p
has exactly 2 points in
S^{1}_{+}
(and likewise
for points of any finite valence
k
). Since
Z^{+}
is a countable union of finite trees,
it has only countably many points of valence
>2
. Now, if
p
is the support of an
ideal class
\nu
, and
\{x,y\}
is a boundary leaf of
\nu
, it corresponds to
a component
K
of
Z^{+}-p
. Take a sequence of 2-valent points
p_{i}
in this component
that converge to
p
and let
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
be the (boundary) leaves
associated to the
p_{i}
. Then
x_{i}\to x
and
y_{i}\to y
, both nontrivially. This
proves that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no isolated sides, and we are done.

Figure 13.
A sequence of 2-valent points
p_{i}
converging to
p
gives a sequence of
leaves converging to a boundary leaf of
\nu
.

∎

With these definitions, we are ready to state the main theorem:

Theorem 3.14

(CaTherine wheels from zippers)
.

Let
Z^{\pm}
be a zipper. Then
Z^{\pm}
arises from a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}

if and only if
Z^{\pm}
is hairy, and has the strong landing property.

Proof.

A CaTherine wheel gives rise to a hairy zipper with the strong landing property, by
Theorem
1.23
. Thus we must prove the converse.

Since
Z^{\pm}
is hairy Lemma
3.12
lets us obtain circles

S^{1}_{\pm}
homeomorphic to the order completions
\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}
.
Lemma
3.13
says that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no isolated sides.
We must now show that there is a canonical identification of
S^{1}_{+}
and

S^{1}_{-}
for which
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no perfect fits. This is the next proposition:

Proposition 3.15

(Canonical identification)
.

Suppose that
Z^{\pm}
is a hairy zipper with the strong landing property. Then there is a
canonical (orientation reversing) identification of
S^{1}_{+}
and
S^{1}_{-}
for which
the associated laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}
have no perfect fits.

Proof.

The correspondence between
S^{1}_{+}
and
S^{1}_{-}
is defined and constructed
in
[
30
]
, § 2.7, and in
fact only depends on the existence of a dense set of landing rays in either zipper.
We summarize the construction.

Let
r
be a proper ray in
Z^{+}
. If it is type 1 it extends to a landing ray
\bar{r}

that lands at
\bar{r}(1)\in S^{2}-(Z^{+}\cup Z^{-})
, and there is another proper ray

s
in
Z^{-}
that also lands at
\bar{r}(1)
. The equivalence classes of rays in
Z^{+}

or
Z^{-}
landing at any point
p\in(Z^{+}\cup Z^{-})
are unique, or else we could find
a pair of proper rays
r,r^{\prime}
in
Z^{+}
both starting at the same point, both landing at

p
, and both disjoint except for their initial point. But then
p\cup r\cup r^{\prime}
would
be a Jordan curve disjoint from
Z^{-}
, contradicting the fact that
Z^{-}
is connected
and dense in
S^{2}
. Thus, type 1 landing rays determine a correspondence between certain
pairs of points in
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}
. One may check that this correspondence is
circular order-reversing (see e.g.
[
30
]
, Lemma 2.25)
and evidently injective where defined.

If
r
is type 2 then it lands at a point
p\in Z^{-}
. It evidently determines a cut
in the circular order of the components of
Z^{-}-p
and therefore determines an
ideal gap supported at
p
. Again, one may check that this correspondence is circular
order-reversing and compatible with the type 1 correspondences.
It is injective where defined, since if
r,r^{\prime}
are two type 2 rays that
land at
p\in Z^{-}
then after modifying
r,r^{\prime}
in an initial segment so they start at
the same point and are disjoint except for their initial point, the Jordan curve

p\cup r\cup r^{\prime}
intersects
Z^{-}
only at
p
, and there is one side of it that is
disjoint from
Z^{-}
or else there would be a component of
Z^{-}-p
witnessing that

r
and
r^{\prime}
determine different cuts, and therefore different ideal points of
p
.

Conversely, if
p\in Z^{-}
and
x\in S^{1}_{-}
is an ideal gap with support equal to
p
,
we shall show there is a type 2 ray in
Z^{+}
that lands at
p
and represents
x
.
This argument is rather involved, and uses the full force of the strong landing property.

Let
\gamma_{i}\subset Z^{-}

be an end nested sequence with
\cup_{i}\cap_{j\geq i}\gamma_{j}
associated to a nested
sequence of intervals
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
converging to
x
.
For each
i
we may find a path
\delta_{i}

in
Z^{+}
whose endpoints are either type 1 landing points for rays
representing
e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}
or are ordinary points of
Z^{+}
representing type 2
landing points for these rays. The unions
\beta_{i}
obtained from

\gamma_{i}\cup\delta_{i}
by adding landing points (if any) form a sequence of Jordan
curves bounding nested closed disks
D_{i}\subset S^{2}
whose intersection
K

contains
p
(because every
\gamma_{i}
does).

We build a ray
r
in
Z^{+}
that lands at
p

and determines the ideal gap
x
. The start of this argument parallels the proof of

[
30
]
, Lemma 2.15 very closely.

Choose a point
q_{0}\in\delta_{0}
and for each
i
let
J_{i}
be the shortest embedded
interval in
Z^{+}
from
q_{0}
to some unique nearest
q_{i}\in\delta_{i}

(it is possible that
q_{i}=q_{0}
and
J_{i}
is degenerate). Then because the intervals

[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
are nested, we have
J_{0}\subset J_{1}\subset\cdots
and we may
therefore form the union
J=\cup J_{i}
. If this is a proper ray then it lands
at some point
q
in
K
. Otherwise it is not proper, and may be completed by adding
an endpoint
q
in
Z^{+}\cap K
. But
K
is connected, and therefore either
K=p=q

and we are done, or
K
contains a sequence of points converging nontrivially to
p
.
By the strong landing property, these points are landing points of rays in
Z^{+}
,
and by construction the corresponding points in
S^{1}_{-}
are all contained in every
interval
[e_{i}^{L},e_{i}^{R}]
. But the only point contained in all of these intervals is

x
, and we have already seen that different type 2 rays must land at different
ideal gaps. It follows that
K
is a point after all, and
J
is the desired proper
(type 2) ray, landing at
p
and corresponding to
x
.

Constructing a ray in
Z^{+}
that lands at
p
.

Thus, by considering the correspondences of type 1 and 2 we obtain an order-reversing
bijection between
S^{1}_{+}
and
S^{1}_{-}
. Since the topology agrees with the (circular)
order topology, this extends uniquely to a homeomorphism.

It remains to show that in this correspondence the laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have
no perfect fits. But this is obvious: we have just shown that the correspondence either
pairs trivial equivalence classes of one of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
with trivial equivalence classes
of the other (type 1) or trivial equivalence classes with elements of non-trivial
equivalence classes (type 2). Another way to see this is the observation
that every ideal gap
x\in S^{1}_{+}
corresponds to some landing ray
r\in Z^{-}
, and
by taking a sequence of 2-valent points on
r
converging to the support of
x
we
obtain a rainbow in
\mathcal{L}^{-}
for the point corresponding to
r
.
∎

But now the proof of Theorem
3.14
is immediate:
Proposition
3.15
constructs a circle with
a pair of laminar relations with no perfect fits whose associated boundary laminations
have no isolated leaves, and then Theorem
2.13

constructs the desired CaTherine wheel. One can check that this construction is the
inverse of the procedure in Theorem
1.23
that gives
a zipper from a CaTherine wheel.
∎

3.2.1.
Half-zippers

Suppose you have a zipper
Z^{\pm}
and you lose half of it. When can you recover
Z^{-}

from
Z^{+}
alone?

The following definition is made in
[
28
]
:

Definition 3.18

(Half-zipper)
.

A
half-zipper
is a subset
Z\subset S^{2}
such that

(1)

Z
is dense in
S^{2}
;

(2)

there is a unique embedded path in
Z
connecting any two distinct points; and

(3)

every point of
Z
is a (path) cut point.

A half-zipper is said to have
short hair
if, furthermore, for every

\epsilon>0
there is a compact subset
T\subset Z
homeomorphic to a finite
(simplicial) tree and so that every (path) component of
Z-T
has diameter
<\epsilon

in the spherical metric.

[
28
]
Theorem 1.3 says that every half-zipper with short hair is
actually
Z^{+}
for some unique zipper
Z^{\pm}
associated to a unique CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
, and conversely
[
28
]
Proposition 1.4 says that if

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
is any CaTherine wheel with zipper
Z^{\pm}
, then each of

Z^{\pm}
has short hair. The proof builds on the material in this section.

The short hair property is rather strong, and it is hard to come up with natural
examples of half-zippers (or even zippers) that have this property without already knowing
that they come from CaTherine wheels. However, some such examples do
occur in the theory of probability, as Liouville quantum gravity geodesic trees. See
Example
8.3
.

4.
Embeddings

We have already seen that a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
is approximable by
embeddings. A stronger statement is that it admits a pseudo-isotopy, i.e. that
there is a path of maps
f_{t}:S^{1}\to S^{2}
for
t\in[0,1]
such that

f_{t}
is an embedding for
t<1
, and
f=f_{1}
.
This is a standard result in decomposition theory, given
Theorem
2.13
and a strengthening of Bing’s
Theorem
2.11
due to Daverman
[
37
]
says
that any shrinkable decomposition of a manifold can be realized by pseudo-isotopies.

Kerbs
[
58
]
defines an invariant of a pseudo-isotopy of a
map
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
, and it turns out (Proposition
4.7
)
that it is a complete invariant of the homotopy
class of the pseudo-isotopy.

Using Kerbs theorem, the main result of this section
(Theorem
4.10
) is the statement
that a CaTherine wheel admits a
unique
homotopy class of
pseudo-isotopy.

Theorem 4.10

(Uniqueness of pseudo-isotopies)
.

Let

Proof.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}

\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
. Any CaTherine wheel admits a pseudo-isotopy with associated Kerbs

\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

\mathcal{K}^{\pm}
is an entire equivalence class of one of

\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

\mathcal{K}^{+}
and also (say) in
\mathcal{L}^{+}

5.
P-CaTherine wheels

The definition of CaTherine wheel is simple enough to be useful, and flexible enough
to cover a great many important examples that arise in practice, as we shall see
(pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits; some expanding Thurston maps;
whole plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
for
\kappa\geq 8
). But there are important exceptions:
Cannon–Thurston maps associated to hyperbolic punctured surface bundles; general
pseudo-Anosov flows or expanding Thurson maps; Brownian webs and space-filling

\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
for
\kappa\in(4,8)
and so on; see e.g.
[
53
]

Figure 5.

It is challenging to give a useful definition of a surjective map
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
that covers
all and only the examples we want. Let us give a name to this class of maps, in the
hope that this will bring us closer to a definition: P-CaTherine wheels, where the P
stands of Peano, parabolic, puncture, pseudo-Anosov, pseudo-isotopy, provisional,
or whatever the reader likes.

One might try to define P-CaTherine wheels in terms of a pair of laminar relations

\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
. We have already seen that a pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
of
S^{1}
arises from
a CaTherine wheel if and only if it is an especial pair (in the sense of
Frankel–Landry) without perfect fits. An arbitrary especial pair gives rise to two
decompositions of
S^{1}
, but the nontrivial elements in these two decompositions
can overlap nontrivially, and the maximal common quotient is typically not homeomorphic to
a sphere, for instance,
if the especial pair contains a finite cycle of leaves alternating between
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and

\mathcal{L}^{-}
for which each leaf shares exactly one endpoint with the next one in the cycle.
More generally, an especial pair might not contain any finite alternating cycles, but
it might contain finite cycles of infinite alternating chains whose closures share an
endpoint in common with the closure of their successor, and so on.

At a minimum, a P-CaTherine wheel should have the property that for every closed
interval
J\subset S^{1}
the image
f(\partial J)
is contained in the frontier

\partial f(J)
. This property alone implies the analog of bullet (3) from
Lemma
1.3
, and with essentially the same proof:

Lemma 5.1

(Interiors disjoint)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be any map so that for all closed intervals
J\subset S^{1}
,
the image
f(\partial J)
is contained in the frontier
\partial f(J)
. The for
disjoint closed intervals
I,J\subset S^{1}
the interiors of
f(I)
and
f(J)
are disjoint.

Proof.

If
p\in J
is arbitrary with
f(p)
in the interior of
f(J)
then
p
is in the interior
of
J
by hypothesis and therefore in the complement of
I
. Let
K
be the closed
interval
[p,I^{+}]
. Then
I\subset K
and therefore the interior of
f(I)
is contained in
the interior of
f(K)
. But
f(p)
is contained in the frontier of
f(K)
by hypothesis and
therefore the interior of
f(I)
does not contain
f(p)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Before giving a precise definition,
Let’s discuss a couple of examples to get more clarity on the properties that a
P-CaTherine wheel should have.

flow with perfect fits.

A typical configuration is a

, a pair of leaves
\mu^{+},\nu^{+}
in
\mathcal{L}^{+}

and another pair
\mu^{-},\nu^{-}
in
\mathcal{L}^{-}
sharing endpoints as in

be the interval indicated. Then

has two local cut points.

f(J)
has two local cut points. One (in black) is genuine; the

The local cut points in Example

are different in kind from each other. Roughly speaking, one of the local cut points
locally separates the track of
f
(for now, call such a cut point
genuine
) whereas the
other one does not (call such a cut point
fake
). For example,
the local cut point in Example
5.2
is fake.

Taking complements reverses this: the interior of
f(J^{c})
is the complement of

f(J)
and vice versa, and the fake local cut point in
\partial f(J)
is a
genuine local cut point in
\partial f(J^{c})
and vice versa. This is essential if we
want

to admit a pseudo-isotopy: if

and

are closed disjoint intervals
with a common point

which is a genuine local cut point in both, and
if the local tracks of

and

cross essentially at

, then

cannot
be perturbed to an embedding. With this in mind we give the following provisional
definition:

Provisional Definition 5.4

(P-CaTherine wheel)
.

A
P-CaTherine wheel
is a map
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
which is surjective, and such
that for every closed interval
J
in
S^{1}
,

(1)

the interior of
f(J)
is dense;

(2)

every component of the interior of
f(J)
is an open disk;

(3)

f(\partial J)
is contained in the frontier
\partial f(J)
; and

(4)

(no essential crossing):
if
I
and
J
are disjoint and their
images
f(I)
and
f(J)
contain a common point
x
which is a local cut point in both,
then
f|I
and
f|J
do not cross essentially at
x
.

Given
f(J)
we would like to
resolve
the fake local cut points. In our (toy)
examples, this means to remove each fake local cut point and add a new point on either
side so that the resolution is still compact and path-connected. If we perform this
resolution, then we may embed the resolutions of
f(J)
and
f(J^{c})
disjointly from
each other as path-connected and contractible subsets of
S^{2}
separated by an
abstract ‘circle’
S^{1}(J)=S^{1}(J^{c})
.

In general we must deal with the fact that a local cut point may have a high
(even infinite) valence.

Definition 5.5

(Local resolution)
.

The
resolution

f_{R}(J)
of
f(J)
is a compact, connected, locally
connected subset of
S^{2}
together with a monotone decomposition
S^{2}\to S^{2}

taking
f_{R}(J)
to
f(J)
so that
f_{R}(J)\to f(J)
is
1
–
1

on the complement of the local cut points of
f(J)
, so that
f|J
factorizes as

J\to f_{R}(J)\to f(J)
, and so that
f_{R}(J)
is maximal with respect to these properties.

Here is how to construct
f_{R}(J)
. We explain the construction first in the case of
a single local cut point. If
x\in f(J)
is a local cut point we give
f(J)-x

the path topology, and define
\mathcal{E}_{x}
to be the set of ends of
f(J)-x
in this
topology. Give
\mathcal{E}_{x}
the obvious (circular) order topology, and let
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{x}

be the order completion, and embed
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
in
S^{1}
in an order-reversing
way. Now choose a homeomorphism
S^{2}-x
into
S^{2}-D_{x}
for
D_{x}
a closed unit disk
in such a way that the homeomorphism extends to the given embedding of

\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
into
S^{1}_{x}=\partial D_{x}
. The embedding
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{x}\to S^{1}_{x}

should be order reversing, because
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
lives on the ‘outside’ of
D_{x}

whereas
S^{1}_{x}
inherits its orientation from the inside, i.e. from
D_{x}
itself.

For some pairs of ends
e,e^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}_{x}
there is a germ of an arc
I\subset J
so that

f|I
nontrivially crosses from
e
to
e^{\prime}
; this collection of pairs determines a
relation on
\mathcal{E}_{x}
, and we let
\mathcal{L}_{x}
denote the closed equivalence

it generates.

Lemma 5.6

(Cut point relation laminar)
.

With notation as above, the relation
\mathcal{L}_{x}
is laminar.

Proof.

For, if not, we could find disjoint embedded intervals
I,I^{\prime}
in
J
for which the local
tracks
f|I
and
f|I^{\prime}
cross essentially over
x
between pairs of local components
that are linked in
\mathcal{E}_{x}
, violating the no essential crossing axiom.
∎

Define a decomposition of
D_{x}
whose elements are the convex hulls of classes in

\mathcal{L}_{x}
, and extend it trivially to
S^{2}-D_{x}
. The quotient of
S^{2}

by this decomposition is
S^{2}
, and
f_{R}(J)
sits inside it. Further quotienting
all of
D_{x}
to a point gives the desired map
f_{R}(J)\to f(J)
, and by construction

f
factors to
J\to f_{R}(J)\to f(J)
.

When there are more local cut points we perform this operation simultaneously on all
of them. This is a little subtle, since
a priori
there might be uncountably many local cut
points. However, there are only countably many local cut points with valence at least

3
(this is for essentially the same reason that one can only draw countably many
disjoint copies of the letter T in the plane); and of the valence
2
local cut
points, only countably many fake ones. Since this discussion is largely informal,
details are left to the reader.

Proposition 5.7

(Canonical cactus resolution)
.

For every closed interval
J
in
S^{1}
, the canonical resolution
f_{R}(J)
is a cactus:
it is a nonempty, compact, locally connected, simply connected planar set with dense interior.

Proof.

The resolution
f_{R}(J)
is compact, locally connected and has dense interior
because it is the surjective image of
J

under the factorization
J\to f_{R}(J)\to f(J)
. We show it is simply-connected.
Suppose not, so that there are at least two disjoint complementary regions
U
and
V

to
f(J)
contained in disjoint complementary regions of
f_{R}(J)
.
Pick points
x\in U
and
y\in V
with preimages
p,q\in S^{1}
. Up to exchanging the
order, the interval
[p,q]
is disjoint from
J
and therefore the interior of
f([p,q])

is disjoint from the interior of
f(J)
. It follows that there must be some genuine local
cut point of
f(J)
that
f|[p,q]
crosses essentially, violating the no essential crossing
axiom.
∎

For any interval
J
, the boundary of the (planar) cactus
f_{R}(J)

has a canonical parameterization by an abstract circle
S^{1}(J)
that may be identified
with the space of prime ends of the complement.

Proposition 5.8

(Monotonicity)
.

With notation as above, if
C(J)\subset J
is the preimage of
\partial f(J)
, there is a canonical
factorization
C(J)\to S^{1}(J)\to\partial f(J)
, and the
map from
C(J)
to
S^{1}(J)
is monotone in the complement of the image of
\partial J
,
in the sense of Proposition
1.7
.

Proof.

By continuity of
f
it suffices to prove this in the generic case that
f(J^{-})
and
f(J^{+})
are
distinct, and are not local cut points of
f(J)
. Let
C^{o}(J)\subset J
denote the preimage
of the set of points on
\partial f(J)
that are not local cut points. Note that

C^{o}(J)
is dense in
C(J)
, and by definition, there is a factorization

C^{o}(J)\to S^{1}(J)\to\partial f(J)
. As in the proof of
Proposition
1.7
we suppose that the proposition is false, and that there
are
J^{-}<x<y<J^{+}
with
x,y\in C^{o}(J)
and such that
f(J^{-})<f(y)<f(x)<f(J^{+})
in
the orientation on

. Consider the images

and

in the resolution

. Because the extreme points link in

, the images must intersect. The intersection
cannot be in the interior of either, and therefore it must be a local cut point in both.
But this violates the no essential crossing axiom.

Monotonicity is the key to the structure theory of (ordinary) CaTherine wheels
developed in §

. Our
confidence in Provisional Definition

.

Part II
Dynamics and Conformal Geometry

6.
Automorphisms

Some CaTherine wheels enjoy a large family of symmetries. These are in many ways
among the most interesting examples, and it is in this section that we start to
connect up our technology to low-dimensional geometry, especially to the theory of
hyperbolic 3-manifolds.

6.1.
Classification of automorphisms

Definition 6.1

(Automorphism)
.

An
automorphism
of a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
is a pair of

orientation preserving
homeomorphisms
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
and
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
such
that
fh=Hf
.

Remark 6.2
.

There is no a priori reason why we can’t consider orientation-reversing homeomorphisms
but in this section we restrict to the orientation-preserving kind for simplicity. Note that in
case one map is orientation-reversing on one of
S^{1}
and
S^{2}
and the other map is
orientation-preserving on the other, it will interchange
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
. Otherwise
it preserves each of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
.

Theorem 6.3

(Automorphisms)
.

Let
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
,
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
be an automorphism of a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
.
Then

(1)

some finite power
h^{n}
of
h
is either the identity, or has a finite even
positive number of fixed points on
S^{1}
with alternating source-sink dynamics;

(2)

up to replacing
h^{n}
by
h^{-n}
if necessary, the attracting/repelling fixed
points are joined by leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}
respectively;

(3)

if there are at least 4 fixed points for
h^{n}
, the image in
S^{2}
of
the attracting/repelling fixed points of
h^{n}
in
S^{1}
are the unique fixed points
of
H
and are attracting/repelling for
H
; and

(4)

if there are exactly 2 fixed points for
h^{n}
, the image in
S^{2}
of
these fixed points are the unique fixed points of
H
and either

(a)

they are distinct and are attracting/repelling for
H
; or

(b)

(exotic): they are equal in
S^{2}
,
and the restriction of
H
to the complement of this
unique fixed point is conjugate to a translation of the plane.

Proof.

Let
h,H
be an automorphism of
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
. The first step is to show that no
point of
\textnormal{fix}(h)
is isolated in
\textnormal{fix}(h)
on exactly one side, and every isolated
point of
\textnormal{fix}(h)
is either a source or a sink. Equivalently, after possibly replacing
h
with
its inverse, we must show that if
p\in\textnormal{fix}(h)
is attracting on one side
(without loss of generality, the positive side) then it must also be attracting on the
other side. Suppose not, so that there is
p\in\textnormal{fix}(h)
attracting on the positive side
and there exist
p_{i}\to p
on the negative side which are either fixed or repelled by
h
.
Without loss of generality
p
has a positive rainbow. In particular, there are positive
boundary leaves
\ell:=\{x,y\}
and
\ell^{\prime}:=\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}
with

p_{i-1}<x<p_{i}<x^{\prime}<p<y^{\prime}<y

and so that
y^{\prime},y
are in the attracting basin of
p
on the right. But then there
is a power
h^{n}
for which
h^{n}(x)<x^{\prime}<p<h^{n}(y)<y^{\prime}
so that
h^{n}(\ell)
crosses

\ell^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction.

It follows that every nontrivial
h
with a fixed point has an isolated fixed point
p
,
without loss of generality a source. Again without loss of generality there is a positive
rainbow for
p
, and we choose a positive boundary leaf
\ell
in this rainbow with
endpoints in the repelling basin of
p
. The
iterates
h^{n}(\ell)
must limit somewhere. Either they limit to a single sink point
q\in\textnormal{fix}(h)

or
h^{n}(\ell)
limits to a leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
whose endpoints
p_{1},p_{-1}
are both in
\textnormal{fix}(h)
. By
construction, these endpoints are both isolated in
\textnormal{fix}(h)
on at least one side, and
therefore they are isolated sinks in
\textnormal{fix}(h)
. The points
p_{\pm 1}
have
negative rainbows, so we may repeat this construction and obtain a countable
chain of consecutive isolated fixed points
p_{i}\in\textnormal{fix}(h)
indexed by
i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

for some
n\geq 0
, that alternate between sources and sinks; and providing
n>2
,
all the sources resp. sinks are in a single equivalence class of
\mathcal{L}^{-}
resp.
\mathcal{L}^{+}
.
But this implies
n>0
, for if
n=0
the
p_{i}
have a limit point in
\textnormal{fix}(h)
, and
this limit point is in a nontrivial equivalence class of both
\mathcal{L}^{-}
and
\mathcal{L}^{+}
,
contradicting no perfect fits.

If no power of
h
has a fixed point, it has irrational rotation number.
By the Lefschetz fixed point theorem, the map
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
must have some fixed point,
and the preimage of this (call it
C
in
S^{1}
) must be a nontrivial equivalence
class (without loss of generality in
\mathcal{L}^{-}
) permuted by
h
. Note that
C

can’t be countable, or else by Sierpinski induction it would contain an invariant
finite subset, which would show that the rotation number is rational after all.
Thus after passing to an invariant subset of
C
if necessary, we may assume
C
is
a Cantor set, and
f
is transitive on the complementary intervals
J_{i}

(i.e. they are indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
so that
h(J_{i})=J_{i+1}
).
Let
J_{0}
be any such complementary interval. Without loss of generality the
endpoint
J_{0}^{+}
has a positive
rainbow, and therefore there is a boundary leaf
\mu
of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
with endpoints in
J_{0}
and
in some other
J_{i}
. But complementary intervals are dense and share no endpoints,
so there must be some complementary interval
J_{j}
between
J_{0}
and
J_{i}
, and
then
h^{j}(\mu)
must cross
\mu
transversely, giving a contradiction.

We are thus reduced to proving the third and fourth bullet. Without loss of generality,

h
has a finite even number of fixed points that alternate between attracting and
repelling; if there are at least
4
points, the attracting points are all contained
in a single equivalence class of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
(say) and the repelling points are all
contained in a single equivalence class of
\mathcal{L}^{-}
;
in particular, these have different images in
S^{2}
which are the unique
attracting/repelling points of
H
and we are done.

On the other hand, if there are exactly 2 points it is a priori possible that these two
points are joined by a leaf
\mu
of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
(say) and that
H
has a unique fixed point
x

in
S^{2}
. Notice that
\mu
cannot be a boundary leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
since otherwise it would be
accumulated on at least one side by leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{+}
in distinct equivalence classes, which would
intersect themselves under powers of
h
. In any case, for every compact subset

K\subset S^{2}-x
the preimage of
K
in
S^{1}
is compact and disjoint from the
endpoints of
\mu
, and therefore under positive/negative powers of
H
the image of

K
must converge to
x
. This shows that the action of
\langle H\rangle
on

S^{2}-x
is a covering space action with Hausdorff quotient. Since the quotient space
is orienable and has fundamental group
\mathbb{Z}
it is an annulus,
so
H
is conjugate to a translation.
∎

We call this last kind of automorphism
exotic
. Evidently it cannot
arise as an element of the group
G
of automorphisms of
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
when
G

is a cocompact Kleinian group.

Example 6.4

(Exotic automorphism)
.

We describe a construction in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. First, start with any CaTherine wheel.
Pick a leaf
\lambda
in
\mathcal{L}^{+}
that is a limit leaf on both sides, and a pair

\mu,\nu
in
\mathcal{L}^{-}
that are limit leaves on both sides, and whose geodesic

intersect the geodesic representative of

in two

. Now form the infinite dihedral cover of

with order 2

The preimage of (the geodesics associated to)

(

If

together with an

6.2.
Hyperbolic 3-manifolds that fiber over the circle

Let
M
be a closed 3-manifold. If there is a fibration over the
circle
F\to M\to S^{1}
then there is an associated short exact sequence of fundamental
groups
\pi_{1}(F)\to\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathbb{Z}
where
\phi:\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathbb{Z}
is the surjective homomorphism
induced by
M\to S^{1}
.

The kernel of
\phi
is
\pi_{1}(F)
which is finitely generated (in fact, finitely
presented). Stallings
[
85
]
showed the converse: if

\phi:\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathbb{Z}
is a surjective homomorphism, and
\ker(\phi)
is finitely
generated then
M
fibers over the circle, and the fibering induces
\phi

(in fact Stallings needed to assume further that
M
is irreducible, but that hypothesis
is superfluous now that the Poincaré Conjecture has been proved).

Thurston
[
89
]
showed that
M
as above is hyperbolic if and only
if the fiber
F
has
\chi(F)<0
and the monodromy is pseudo-Anosov (see

[
88
]
), and in this case one obtains a pseudo-Anosov suspension flow
X
.
This means in particular that the monodromy preserves a
pair of projectively invariant transversely measured singular foliations
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{F}
on

F
that lift to singular foliations
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\pm}
of the universal cover
\tilde{F}

invariant under the natural action of
\pi_{1}(M)
. The data of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\pm}
is
encoded by a pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
without perfect fits
on the ideal boundary
S^{1}_{\infty}
of
\tilde{F}
.

The leaf spaces of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\pm}
are topological
\mathbb{R}
-trees, and the endpoint
maps of the lifted suspension pseudo-Anosov flow
\tilde{X}
on
\tilde{M}=\mathbb{H}^{3}
have
disjoint images
Z^{\pm}
that are homeomorphic to these leaf spaces.

Finally, Cannon–Thurston
[
35
]
used this structure to build a
map
f:S^{1}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
equivariant under the natural action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on

S^{1}_{\infty}
coming from the fibration and the geometric action of
\pi_{1}(M)

on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
coming from the hyperbolic structure.

Setting
G=\pi_{1}(M)
, the four structures
X,f,Z^{\pm},\phi
are respectively
examples of a pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits, a
G
-CaTherine wheel,
a
G
-zipper, and a uniform quasimorphism on
G
. Looking ahead
to §
6.6
we
shall see that there is an equivalence of these four different
structures in general.

6.3.
Kleinian groups and quasicircles

Definition 6.6

(Quasicircle)
.

A Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a
K
-quasicircle
if it is the image of a
round circle under a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. It is a

quasicircle
if it is a
K
-quasicircle for some
K
.

For an introduction to the theory of quasiconformal maps and quasicircles, see
Ahlfors
[
3
]
or Hubbard
[
54
]
.

Proposition 6.7

(Quasicircle)
.

A Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a quasicircle if and only if
every Hausdorff limit of translates of
\Gamma
by conformal maps is
either a Jordan curve or a single point.

This is essentially equivalent to Ahlfors’ turning criterion for quasicircles:
pairs of points in
\Gamma
which are
close in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
are joined by an arc of
\Gamma
of small diameter. According to
Curt McMullen, the characterization of quasicircles in Proposition
6.7

is “well known in Finland”.

Definition 6.8

(
K
-CaTherine wheel)
.

For
K\geq 1
a
K
-CaTherine wheel
is a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}

for which
\partial f(I)
is a
K
-quasicircle for every closed interval

.

As an easy application of Proposition
6.7

one has the following elegant observation by McMullen
[

]
:

Proposition 6.9

(McMullen, uniform
K
)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be any CaTherine wheel invariant under a Kleinian group
G

which is either

(1)

cocompact; or

(2)

a doubly degenerate closed surface group
with the injectivity radius of
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
bounded below.

Then

is a

-CaTherine wheel for some

. Furthermore in the second case,

depends only on the genus of the surface and the injectivity radius of
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
.

Proof.

The first case follows immediately from Proposition

.
The set of (pointed) manifolds
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
arising as in the second case is closed
under geometric limits and hence compact, and invariant under the obvious conjugation
action of the Möbius group. Again, apply Proposition

.
∎

Example 6.10

(Surface bundles over a graph)
.

This example was kindly explained to us by Chris Leininger.

Let
S
be a closed oriented surface of genus at least
2
, and let
S\to Y\to\Gamma

be a surface bundle over a finite graph
\Gamma
(for concreteness, we can let
\Gamma
be
a wedge of
k>1
circles) for which
\pi_{1}(Y)
is word-hyperbolic.
Many such examples are known from the seminal work of Farb–Mosher

[
43
]
; they are associated to so-called ‘convex co-compact’ free subgroups
of the mapping class group of
S
. Let
\tilde{\Gamma}
denote the universal cover of
\Gamma
;
this is a regular
2k
-valent tree. For every proper embedded line
\ell
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
the
total space of the pulled back
S
bundle over
\ell
is uniformly quasi-isometric
to
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
where
G
is a doubly degenerate surface group isomorphic to
\pi_{1}(S)
,
and furthermore the injectivity radius in this quotient is uniformly bounded
below over all such
\ell
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the space of (Freudenthal) ends of
\tilde{\Gamma}
; this is a Cantor set.
It follows that we obtain a parameterized family of CaTherine wheels

S^{1}\times(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}-\Delta)\to S^{2}\times(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}-\Delta)\to\partial_{\infty}\pi_{1}(Y)

whose image
S^{2}
s each surject in a complicated way to
\partial_{\infty}\pi_{1}(Y)
; see
Mj
[
77
]
for details.

Each one of these
S^{2}
s has a natural conformal structure for which the associated
CaTherine wheel is a
K
-CaTherine wheel, with
K
uniform over the entire family, by
Proposition
6.9
.
Can one use the images of these
K
-quasicircles in
\partial_{\infty}\pi_{1}(Y)
to
estimate (e.g. by the method of Gromov as developed
by Bourdon
[
21
,
51
]
) the conformal dimension of
\partial_{\infty}\pi_{1}(Y)
?

Because of Proposition
6.9
we are emboldened to make a conjecture:

Conjecture 6.11

(Injectivity radius)
.

For any
R>r>0
there is a finite
K(r,R)
such that
any CaTherine wheel invariant under a Kleinian group
G

for which
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
has injectivity radius in the interval
[r,R]
everywhere
is a
K(r,R)
-CaTherine wheel.

We shall see some considerable evidence for Conjecture
6.11

in §
6.4.1
after we relate
G
-CaTherine
wheels to pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits.

If
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a
K
-CaTherine wheel, then so is
f\circ g:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}

for any
g\in\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})
. Up to this flexibility (i.e. choice of ‘gauge’
g
),
and without any assumptions on dynamics, we have the following:

Proposition 6.12

(
K
is compact)
.

Let
f_{n}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be any sequence of
K
-CaTherine wheels. Then after passing
to a subsequence, and precomposing with a sequence
g_{n}\in\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})
if
necessary,
f_{n}\to f_{\infty}
in the compact-open topology, and

f_{\infty}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a
K
-CaTherine wheel.

Proof.

Fix a round metric on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
in the correct conformal class. After making this
(arbitrary) choice, for each
K
-CaTherine wheel
f_{i}
and
each interval
I\subset S^{1}
, the image
f_{i}(I)
has positive Lesbesgue measure.
Since the boundary of
f_{i}(I)
is a
K
-quasicircle, it has
Hausdorff dimension at most
2K/(K+1)<2
and therefore Lesbesgue measure
0

(see e.g.
[
6
]
, Corollary 1.3).
Thus in particular, and because the images of disjoint intervals have disjoint
interiors, this assignment of Lesbesgue measure to intervals determines a
measure of full support on
S^{1}
with total mass
4\pi
and no atoms. We can therefore find
a gauge
g_{i}
(that we call the
Lebesgue gauge
)
so that
\textnormal{area}(f_{i}\circ g_{i}(I))=2\cdot\textnormal{length}(I)
for all
I
, and for the usual
metric on
S^{1}
.

Now it is a fact that for any
K
-quasidisk
D
in the Euclidean plane (or the
round sphere) there is a constant
C(K)
so that

\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{\textnormal{area}(D)}\leq\text{diameter}(D)\leq C(K)\cdot\sqrt{\textnormal{area}(D)}

The first inequality is just the usual isodiametric inequality for the plane, and has nothing
to do with quasidisks per se. The second inequality may be proved as follows.
Pick a pair of points
p,q\in\partial D
with
d(p,q)=\text{diameter}(D)
. If

\text{diameter}(D)\gg\sqrt{\textnormal{area}(D)}
then by the coarea formula, we can find a
line
\ell
perpendicular to the segment
pq
and far from both
p
and
q
so that
every component of
\ell\cap D
is very short. But this means we can find a pair
of points on
\ell\cap\partial D
that are very close, but can’t be joined by
a path of small diameter in
\partial D
, contrary to Ahlfors’ turning criterion
for quasicircles. We remark that the dependence of
C(K)
on
K
is of order
e^{cK}
,
although we do not use this fact.

From this inequality it follows that
in the Lebesgue gauge, a
K
-CaTherine wheel is Hölder continuous with exponent
1/2
,
and has a uniform modulus of continuity depending only on
K
.

Thus after passing to a subsequence, we may assume
f_{n}
(in the Lebesgue
gauge) converges in the compact-open topology to some (necessarily surjective) map

f_{\infty}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. Evidently
f_{\infty}
is not locally constant, since for any
fixed interval
I
the images
f_{i}(I)
have diameter bounded below; conversely it is not
all of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
since the same argument applies to
f_{i}(I^{c})
. But
f_{i}(I)

is a sequence of
K
-quasidisks converging to
f_{\infty}(I)
and therefore by
Proposition
6.7
the limit is a
K
-quasidisk.
Evidently the limit of
\partial f_{n}(I)
is

\partial f_{\infty}(I)
and therefore by continuity,
f_{\infty}(\partial I)\subset\partial f_{\infty}(I)
and we are done.
∎

In other words, up to reparameterization (‘gauge’), the space of
K
-CaTherine wheels
is compact. By the way, the existence of Lebesgue gauge only depends on the
fact that the area of
\partial f(I)
is zero for all closed intervals
I\subset S^{1}
.

In the Lebesgue gauge, the action of
g\in G
near its periodic points in
S^{1}

is especially nice.

Lemma 6.13

(Bilipschitz)
.

Suppose
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a
G
-CaTherine wheel for some Kleinian group
G

that admits a Lebesgue gauge (i.e. the Lebesgue area of
\partial f(I)
is zero for all
closed intervals
I\subset S^{1}
). Let
g\in G
be nontrivial, and suppose that

|g_{*}d\textnormal{area}/d\textnormal{area}|\in[C^{-1},C]
on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. Then

g
acts on
S^{1}
by a
C
-bilipschitz homeomorphism of
S^{1}
in the
Lebesgue gauge.

Furthermore, if
p\in S^{1}
is an attracting resp. repelling periodic orbit for
g
,
and
\tau(g)
is the translation length of
g
on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,
then for every
\epsilon
there is an open neighborhood
I_{p}
of
p
so that
the derivative of
g
restricted to
I_{p}
is within
\epsilon
of
e^{-2\tau(g)}

resp.
e^{2\tau(g)}
.

Proof.

The first statement is immediate from the definition of the Lebesgue gauge, and the
way in which the action of
g
transforms area on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

Any nontrivial
g\in G
has only finitely many periodic
points in
S^{1}
, that alternate between attracting and repelling, by
Theorem
6.3
. If
I_{p}
is a sufficiently small neighborhood
of one such point,
f(I_{p})
is contained in a small neighborhood of a
fixed point of
g
on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
where the corresponding statement is obvious.
∎

The hypothesis of Lemma
6.13
is satisfied whenever

f
is a
K
-CaTherine wheel for any
K
. It follows that if
f_{i}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}

are a sequence of
G
-CaTherine wheels which are also
K
-CaTherine wheels
(an unfortunate clash of terminology) the action of each
g\in G
on
S^{1}

in the Lebesgue gauge is uniformly bilipschitz independent of
i
,
and the limit
f_{\infty}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}

is also a
G
-CaTherine wheel, and with the same bilipschitz
constants for the
G
action on
S^{1}
.

In fact for
G
a fixed cocompact Kleinian group, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 6.14

(Actions are precompact)
.

Let
G
be a cocompact Kleinian group, and let
f_{i}:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be a sequence
of
G
-CaTherine wheels. Then in the Lesbesgue
gauge, the sequence of associated
G
actions
\rho_{i}:G\to\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})

has a convergent subsequence.

Proof.

For
G
cocompact, any
G
-CaTherine wheel is a
K
-CaTherine wheel for some
K
.
Let
\rho_{i}:G\to\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})
be the action of
G
on
S^{1}
corresponding to
f_{i}

in the Lesbesgue gauge (the representation
\rho_{i}
is only well-defined up to conjugacy
by a rigid rotation of
S^{1}
). By Lemma
6.13
for each

g\in G
the element
\rho_{i}(g)
is
C
-bilipschitz for some
C(g)
independent of
i
.
Thus each
\rho_{i}(g)
has a convergent subsequence, and since
G
is finitely generated,
there is a limit
\rho_{\infty}:G\to\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})
for which each
\rho_{\infty}(g)

is still
C(g)
-bilipschitz with the same constant.
∎

If one could show that the limiting action
\rho_{\infty}:G\to\Homeo^{+}(S^{1})

extended to a
G
-CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, then a posteriori there would be
a uniform
K
for all
G
-CaTherine wheels, and (looking ahead) this would give a new
proof of Theorem
6.22
.

We remark that if
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}_{i}
are the
\rho_{i}(G)
-invariant laminar relations associated
to
f_{i}
, then some subsequence converges to
\rho_{\infty}(G)
-invariant laminar relations

\mathcal{L}^{\pm}_{\infty}
. These limit laminar relations have no isolated sides, but a priori
there is no reason why they should have no perfect fits.

We end this subsection with an inequality relating the analytic quality of
a
G
-CaTherine wheel to the dynamics of
G
on
S^{1}
:

Proposition 6.15

(
K
bounds valence)
.

Let
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be a
K
-CaTherine wheel. Then there is a bound
n(K)
on
the number of
f
preimages of any point in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

In particular, if
f
is also a
G
-CaTherine wheel for some subgroup
G

of Möbius transformations of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, then with the same bound
n(K)
as above,
every nontrivial
g\in G
has at most
2n(K)
periodic points in
S^{1}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is a
K
-CaTherine wheel, it has a Lebesgue gauge.
Suppose there is a point
p\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with
n
preimages
q_{1},\cdots,q_{n}\in S^{1}
.
In the Lebesgue gauge, we can put disjoint intervals
J_{1}\cdots J_{n}\subset S^{1}

centered at the
q_{i}
each of length some small positive number
A
.
The images
f(J_{1})\cdots f(J_{n})
have disjoint interiors,
but have a point in common (the common fixed point of
g
in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
).
But then the union
B:=\cup f(J_{i})
has area
nA
but diameter at most

2C(K)\cdot\sqrt{A}
by the diameter–area estimate for
K
-quasidisks. On the other hand,
this violates the elementary diameter–area inequality in round
S^{2}
for
fixed
C(K)
if
n
is big enough.

Now suppose
f
is a
G
-CaTherine wheel for some Möbius group
G
.
By Theorem
6.3
any nontrivial
g\in G

has only finitely many periodic orbits on
S^{1}

that alternate between attracting and repelling, and the images of these in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
are
(respectively) the unique attracting and repelling fixed points for
g
.
∎

Bounds on the cardinality of point preimages of certain Cannon–Thurston maps are obtained via

\delta
-hyperbolic (as distinct from analytic) methods in
[
14
]
.

Corollary 6.16

(No parabolics)
.

Let
G
be a Kleinian group containing a parabolic element. Then no
G
-CaTherine wheel
is a
K
-CaTherine wheel.

Proof.

Let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
be a
G
-CaTherine wheel.
Let
g\in G
be parabolic and let

be its unique fixed point.
Then by Theorem
6.3
the element

g
fixes exactly two points
p,q
in
S^{1}
in the preimage of

with source-sink dynamics. Furthermore,
the leaf
\{p,q\}
is contained in a nontrivial equivalence class
\mu

in
\mathcal{L}^{+}
(say), but it is not a boundary leaf. In particular,
\mu
contains points

, and since

is

-invariant it

. Thus by

,

is not a

-CaTherine wheel for

.
∎

.

6.4.
Pseudo-Anosov flows

Suppose
M
is a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
with fundamental group
G
acting on

\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. In this subsection we show that a
G
-CaTherine wheel gives rise to a
pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
without perfect fits. This is the converse of a deep theorem of
Fenley
[
46
]
, and together with Fenley’s theorem
this gives an equivalence between such flows up to orbit equivalence,
and
G
-CaTherine wheels up to conjugacy.

There is a vast literature on the theory of pseudo-Anosov flows, and there are
many equivalent ways of representing such structures (bifoliated planes, branched surfaces,
veering triangulations etc). See e.g.

[
79
,
2
,
62
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
44
,
45
,
11
,
12
,
50
]
.
There is a considerable overlap of ideas and methods among these papers, and some of
the same ideas go into the proof of our theorem, and the foundations that it rests
on that were developed in Part
I
.

Theorem 6.19

(Pseudo-Anosov flows and CaTherine wheels)
.

Let
M
be a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold with fundamental group
G
.
Then there is a bijection between orbit equivalence classes of
pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits
X
on
M
and
conjugacy classes of
G
-invariant CaTherine wheels
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
.

Proof.

One direction is the Main Theorem of Fenley from
[
46
]
that
we summarize as follows: the lifted flow
\tilde{X}
has an orbit space which is
homeomorphic to a plane that admits a natural compactification by a circle,
and the stable/unstable foliations of the flow determine a pair of laminar relations
of this circle with no perfect fits and no isolated sides. Thus there is a CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
(Fenley constructs this map directly). Fenley shows that the
action of
G
on
S^{2}
is a convergence action, and therefore by Bowditch the action is
conjugate to the action of
G
on
S^{2}_{\infty}
. This proves one direction, which
is Fenley’s theorem restated in our language.

Conversely, let
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
be a CaTherine wheel invariant under
G
.
Then there is a
G
-zipper
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}_{\infty}
for which
Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
are
homeomorphic to quotient spaces induced by decompositions of planes
P^{\pm}

coming from a pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
of
S^{1}
without perfect fits.
Identify
S^{1}
with the ideal boundary
S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
of

\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and thereby identify
P^{\pm}
with
\mathbb{H}^{2}
in such a way that the boundary leaves
are honest geodesics.

Now let’s intersect decomposition elements associated to
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}
. Identify
P^{+}

and
P^{-}
with a single copy of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and form a new
decomposition of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
whose elements are pairwise intersections of hulls
H^{+}(\mu)
and

H^{-}(\nu)
for positive resp. negative laminar equivalence classes
\mu
and
\nu
.
The topology (though not the geometry) of this decomposition does not depend on any choices,
and is preserved by
G
.
The decomposition elements are closed and convex, because they are the intersection of
closed convex subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. They are compact, because otherwise
H^{+}(\mu)
and

H^{-}(\nu)
would have an ideal point in common, contradicting the no perfect fits property.
Frontier points are contained in boundary leaves, thus these decomposition elements are all
compact finite sided polyhedra (including the degenerate case of a closed geodesic interval
or a single point). Since boundary leaves are not isolated, it follows easily that this
decomposition is upper semi-continuous; since the elements are non-separating, the quotient
space is homeomorphic to a plane
P
.

The plane may be identified with a subset
P\subset Z^{+}\times Z^{-}
where the image in
each factor is the projection to the further quotient space of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by the decomposition
associated to the positive and negative laminar relations respectively.

Now, the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
admits a foliation by lifts (from
\mathbb{H}^{3}
) of
bi-infinite geodesics, giving it the structure of a product

UT\mathbb{H}^{3}=(S^{2}\times S^{2}-\Delta)\times\mathbb{R}
and inside this space we take

\tilde{N}:=P\times\mathbb{R}
, using the given embedding of
P
in

Z^{+}\times Z^{-}\subset S^{2}\times S^{2}-\Delta
. Ignoring its subspace topology for now,

\tilde{N}
is certainly homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since the map from the plane to

S^{2}\times S^{2}-\Delta
is continuous and injective, its restriction to every
compact subset is a homeomorphism onto its image, and therefore compact subsets
of
\tilde{N}
with its
\mathbb{R}^{3}
topology are also compact in
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
. But

G
acts on
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
freely and properly discontinuously, in the strong sense that
for any compact subset
K
of
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
the intersections
K\cap gK
are empty for
all but finitely many
g
. This is true in particular for
K
which are compact
subsets of
\tilde{N}
, and therefore the action of
G
on
\tilde{N}
is also free
and properly discontinuous in the strong sense. This implies that the quotient
N:=\tilde{N}/G

is Hausdorff (see e.g.
[
57
]
, Lemma 9 and Theorem 11),
and the quotient map
\tilde{N}\to N
is a covering space, so that
N
is a
(Hausdorff!) 3-manifold with fundamental group
G
.
Furthermore,
N
comes equipped with a tautological flow
X
coming from the
\mathbb{R}
factors in

UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
, which are Möbius invariant. Hence in particular
N
is a closed 3-manifold
which by Mostow rigidity is homeomorphic to
M
, and a posteriori
\tilde{N}

is properly embedded in
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
so that its subspace topology agrees with
its
\mathbb{R}^{3}
topology.

It is easy to see from the structure of the decomposition space
that the tautological flow
X
on
N
is already a (topological)
pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits, since it has singular stable/unstable
foliations coming from the separate decompositions associated to
\mathcal{L}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}

respectively.

Alternately, by considering the intersection of
\tilde{N}
with translates
of a compact fundamental domain for
G
on
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
that march along a flowline
\ell

of
\tilde{X}
we can see that
\ell
is quasigeodesic in
UT\mathbb{H}^{3}
and therefore
also in
\tilde{N}
, and then the main theorem of Frankel–Landry
[
49
]
,
Theorem 1.1 implies that this quasigeodesic flow may
be straightened to a pseudo–Anosov flow (unique up to orbit equivalence),
with the same action on the circle at infinity
(which is the
S^{1}
from the CaTherine wheel).

In any case we obtain a canonical route from
G
-CaTherine wheels to pseudo–Anosov
flow without perfect fits, precisely inverse to Fenley’s construction.
∎

Remark 6.20
.

In fact, the major part of Frankel–Landry’s argument, starting with a geodesic flow,
is precisely to construct the data of a (universal) circle with a pair of especial
laminar relations. When the relations have no perfect fits, they exactly satisfy the
sufficient conditions to arise from a CaTherine wheel; Frankel–Landry’s argument at
this point is parallel to ours, though more roundabout since they are not able to work
directly with the zipper
Z^{\pm}
in
S^{2}
. Thus logically the appeal to Frankel–Landry
is superfluous.

In fact, we may construct a pseudo-Anosov flow from a CaTherine wheel even without
assuming the CaTherine wheel comes from a 3-manifold. Recall that Cannon conjectured
(this conjecture is implicit in
[
32
]
, § 8)
that if
G
is a word hyperbolic group with Gromov boundary

\partial_{\infty}G
homeomorphic to
S^{2}
, then
G
is virtually the fundamental group
of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold. We shall now show that this is true whenever
there is a
G
-invariant CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\partial_{\infty}G
.

Theorem 6.21

(CaTherine wheels and Cannon’s Conjecture)
.

Suppose
G
is a word hyperbolic group with Gromov boundary
\partial_{\infty}G

homeomorphic to
S^{2}
, and suppose that there is a
G
-invariant CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to\partial_{\infty}G
. Then
G
is virtually the fundamental group of a closed
hyperbolic 3-manifold; i.e. the Cannon Conjecture is true for
G
.

Proof.

For brevity, let’s denote
\partial_{\infty}G\times\partial_{\infty}G-\Delta
by

\partial^{2}_{\infty}G
.
As in the proof of Theorem
6.19
we obtain a
G
-invariant
continuous injective map from a plane to
P\subset Z^{+}\times Z^{-}
.
Mineyev’s flow space (see
[
76
]
, Theorem 60) is a metric space
\mathcal{F}(G)
quasi-isometric
to
G
with a cocompact isometric
G
-action, and is homeomorphic to

\partial^{2}_{\infty}G\times\mathbb{R}
in such a way that the
G
action preserves the foliation
by
\mathbb{R}
factors and descends to the usual
G
action on
\partial^{2}_{\infty}G
.
(Technically what Mineyev denotes by
\mathcal{F}(G)
is the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
quotient of this space
by a free involution that interchanges the two
\partial_{\infty}G
factors, and reverses
the orientation of each
\mathbb{R}
leaf; this is the difference between the unit tangent bundle
and the projective unit tangent bundle).

We may therefore form, just as above, the product
\tilde{N}:=P\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathcal{F}(G)
and observe
that it is
G
-invariant and homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with a
G
-invariant
product-covered foliation by lines. Now, we cannot assume a priori that
G

is virtually torsion free. But it does act properly discontinuously on
\tilde{N}
.
We claim the quotient space
N:=\tilde{N}/G
has the structure of a smooth 3-dimensional
orbifold. Although finite
groups can act on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with wild fixed point sets, the action of
G
on
\tilde{N}

descends to an action on
P
. As is well-known, a theorem of Kérékjartò
[
59
]

easily implies that any action of a finite
group on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is topologically conjugate to a finite group of isometries of the Euclidean
plane, and therefore
N
has the structure of a smooth 3-dimensional orbifold. Thus
a posteriori
G
is virtually torsion free, and without loss of generality
N
is a
3-manifold with fundamental group commensurable to
G
, and incidentally also admits
a pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits, by Theorem
6.19
.

In particular, Cannon’s Conjecture is true for
G
.
∎

6.4.1.
Finiteness and compactness

If Conjecture
6.11
were true, it would imply
(together with Proposition
6.12
) that
the space of
G
-CaTherine wheels for any fixed cocompact Kleinian group
G
is compact.
By Theorem
6.19

G
-CaTherine wheels (up to gauge) are
in bijection with pseudo-Anosov flows on
M:=\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
without perfect fits. Since
pseudo-Anosov flows on a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold are structurally stable, a
compact family of
G
-CaTherine wheels is necessarily finite. Thus finiteness of
orbit-equivalence classes of pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits on
M
is

equivalent
to a fixed
K(M)
so that every
G
-CaTherine wheel is a
K(M)
-CaTherine
wheel.

But in fact this finiteness is a very recent
theorem
of
Barthelmé–Tsang–Zung
[
13
]
, building on work of Li
[
65
]
.
Explicitly:

Theorem 6.22

(Barthelmé–Tsang–Zung
[
13
]
finiteness)
.

Let
M
be a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold. Then there are finitely many orbit equivalent
pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits
X
on
M
.

In turn, this theorem depends crucially on the following finiteness theorem of Li:

Theorem 6.23

(Li
[
65
]
finiteness)
.

Let
M
be a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold. Then there are only finitely many
isotopy classes of closed curves
\gamma
in
M
that are singular orbits of
pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits
X
on
M
.

Of course, this trivially implies that for any
0<r
and
v<\infty
there is a

K(r,v)
so that any
G
-CaTherine wheel invariant under a cocompact Kleinian group

G
for which
\mathbb{H}^{3}/G
has injectivity radius
\geq r
and volume
\leq v
is a

K(r,v)
-CaTherine wheel, since there are only finitely many such
G
up to conjugacy,
and each one is compatible with only finitely many CaTherine wheels.

6.5.
Zippers

Suppose
G
is the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
,
thought of as a Kleinian group acting on
S^{2}_{\infty}
.
How can we construct
G
-CaTherine wheels without assuming that
M
admits
a pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits and invoking Fenley
[
46
]
?

One way to do it is via the theory of
G
-zippers. Every
G
-CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
gives rise to a unique (necessarily minimal)
G
-zipper.
In this subsection we prove the
converse: every minimal
G
-zipper gives rise to a
G
-CaTherine wheel, unique up
to conjugacy; and this correspondence is a bijection. We prove this in two steps;
first we show (Theorem
6.24
) that a hairy
G
-zipper
always comes from a (unique up to conjugacy)
G
-CaTherine wheel, and then we show
(Theorem
6.26
) that every minimal
G
-zipper is hairy.

Theorem 6.24

(Hairy
G
-zipper to
G
-CaTherine wheel)
.

Let
G
be the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
.
Suppose that
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}_{\infty}
is a minimal
G
-zipper
and suppose further that
Z^{\pm}
is hairy. Then the laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

associated to
Z^{\pm}
have no perfect fits, and there is a
G
-CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}

for the given
G
action (unique up to conjugacy) and with associated zippers
Z^{\pm}
.

Proof.

By hairiness,
S^{1}_{\pm}
may be identified with the union of the ends and ideal gaps
of
Z^{\pm}
. For a minimal
G
-zipper, the action of
G
on
S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
is minimal, and
therefore
S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
may be identified canonically with each of
S^{1}_{\pm}
.
By Lemma
3.13
we obtain a pair of laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

each with no isolated sides.

If we can show
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no perfect fits, we will obtain a CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}\to S^{2}
for
some

G
-action on
S^{2}
. A more careful analysis will show
both that there are no perfect fits, and that the range of
f
may be taken to be

S^{2}_{\infty}
with the given
G
-action. In fact, the same argument will prove both
claims.

We would like to define the map
f:S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
as follows: a point

e\in S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
corresponds to a pair of points
e^{\pm}\in S^{1}_{\pm}
. Each of the
points
e^{\pm}
either corresponds to an equivalence class of proper ray, or to an
ideal gap.

The argument may be shortened by appealing to
Kim
[
60
]
, Theorem B which says that for any minimal
G
-zipper,
landing rays of type 2 exist, and by minimality they are dense.
A type 2 landing ray determines a pair of points
x^{\pm}\in S^{1}_{\pm}

corresponding to a single point
x\in S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}

for which one of the
x^{\pm}
is an end and the other is an ideal gap (where a proper ray
representing the end lands).
A pair of type 2 landing rays determines two pairs of points
x^{\pm},y^{\pm}

and we may form a Jordan curve
\gamma
in
Z^{+}\cup Z^{-}
which is the union of the
convex hull of
x^{+},y^{+}
in
Z^{+}
and the convex hull of
x^{-},y^{-}
in
Z^{-}
.
Associated to
e\in S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
we may choose any sequence of points
e_{i}\in S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}

that converge to
e
from alternating sides, and for each consecutive
pair
e_{2i}^{\pm},e_{2i+1}^{\pm}\subset S^{1}_{\pm}
we form a Jordan curve

\gamma_{i}\subset Z^{+}\cup Z^{-}
as above.

The
\gamma_{i}
bound a nested decreasing sequence of disks
D_{i}
, and the intersection
of this family of disks is a compact, connected, cellular set
K_{e}
associated
canonically to
e
. Notice by the way that the construction
of
K_{e}
is functorial, so that
gK_{e}=K_{ge}
for every
g\in G
.
A similar subset
K
was constructed in
Proposition
3.15
; the key in that proposition was
to show that
K
consisted of a single point, and that is the key here too.

We explain how the theorem follows if we can show that every
K_{e}
consists of a
single point. First of all,
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
can have no
perfect fits, since otherwise for some
e
the points
e^{\pm}
would both correspond
to ideal gaps with support
p^{\pm}\in Z^{\pm}
. By construction,
p^{\pm}\subset D_{i}
for
every
D_{i}
as above, and therefore
p^{\pm}\in K_{e}
. But if
K_{e}
is a single point
then
p^{+}=p^{-}
and
Z^{\pm}
intersect, contrary to the definition of zippers.
Second of all, if every
K_{e}
is a single point, then we can define
f:S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

by
f(e)=K_{e}
. The shrinking families of disks
D_{i}
associated to each
K_{e}
certify
that this map is continuous; by construction the point preimages are precisely
the decomposition elements associated to
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
(the use of shrinking point neighborhoods
associated to leaves of the stable/unstable lamination to prove continuity is
the heart of the classical Cannon–Thurston argument for hyperbolic surface
bundles; see
[
35
]
). This completes the proof, modulo the
fact that every
K_{e}
consists of a single point.

The argument to show that each
K_{e}
consists of a single point is surprisingly delicate;
it would be much easier if we could assume a rather plausible general conjecture
about the action of
G
on
S^{2}_{\infty}
that we call ‘no wandering continua’
(Conjecture
6.29
) but for now this general statement
seems out of reach. Let us therefore assume that some
K_{e}
consists of more than one
point, and derive a contradiction.

Let us first establish some basic properties of
K_{e}
.
The first property is that
K_{e}
can intersect
Z^{\pm}
only in the support of

e^{\pm}
(if either or both of these are ideal gaps). For if
x\in K_{e}\cap Z^{+}
(for instance)
we can construct the convex hull
\delta
in
Z^{+}
of
x
with the support/end of
e^{+}
and
observe that this entire (nontrivial!) segment
\delta
is contained in all of
K_{e}
. But by
hairiness, there are many hairs on
\delta
on either side separating
x
from
K_{e}

after all. This establishes the first property.
Let’s refer to these points of
K_{e}\cap Z^{\pm}
(if any) as the ‘endpoints’ of
K_{e}
.

It follows that for the shrinking disks
D_{i}
in the definition of
K_{e}
that the
intersection of the
interiors
of the
D_{i}
contains all of
K_{e}
except possibly
for the endpoints. For, by construction, each
\partial D_{i}
is
entirely contained in
Z^{+}\cup Z^{-}
, and now apply the previous observation.

The second property is that for all
g\in G
if
gK_{e}\cap\textnormal{int}(D_{i})
is nonempty,
then
gK_{e}\subset D_{i}
. To see this, note that if the interior of

gD_{j}
intersects
\partial D_{i}
, this intersection divides
gD_{j}
into two subdisks,
and (by the definition of the
D_{i}
and of
K_{e}
) the set

gK_{e}
must be contained in one of them.
A related fact, true for essentially the same reason, is that the intersection

K_{e}\cap gK_{e}
is either all of
K_{e}
, which can happen if and only if
ge=e
;
or it is contained in the common endpoints of both sets, which can happen if and only
if these endpoints are distinct ideal points in either
Z^{+}
or
Z^{-}
with the same support.

Notice by the way, since points in
S^{1}_{\textnormal{univ}}
have at most cyclic stabilizer, that
if
gK_{e}=K_{e}
then
g
acts on
K_{e}
properly discontinuously away from the fixed
points of
g
in
S^{2}_{\infty}
. It follows that if
K_{e}
consists of more than one point,
we may take the intersection of
K_{e}

with a fundamental domain for
g
and obtain a possibly smaller compact connected
subset
K^{\prime}\subset S^{2}_{\infty}
, nontrivial if
K_{e}
is nontrivial, and so that
K^{\prime}
is

disjoint
from all its nontrivial
G
translates. We strongly suspect that this
already gives a contradiction (see Conjecture
6.29
)
but this seems very subtle if true, and we do not pursue it further here in this generality.

The third property is that the set
K_{e}
is
porous
. Because
G
is cocompact, this
may be stated as the property that there is no sequence of translates
g_{i}K_{e}
whose
Hausdorff limit is all of
S^{2}_{\infty}
. For, if this were true, some translate

g_{i}K_{e}
would intersect some
\textnormal{int}(D_{j})
without being contained in
D_{j}
, contrary to
the second property.

We now need an elementary geometric lemma:

Lemma 6.25

(Porous multiple components)
.

Suppose
L\subset\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a nontrivial connected porous closed set. Then
there are a sequence of Möbius transformations
g_{i}
so that the Hausdorff
limit
L_{\infty}:=\lim g_{i}L
exists, and for which the
complement
\mathbb{CP}^{1}-L_{\infty}
has at least two distinct (open) components.

Proof.

To prove this lemma, we may assume first of all that
U:=\mathbb{CP}^{1}-L
is connected,
or else there is nothing to prove, and since
L
is connected,

U
is homeomorphic to an open disk. After a conformal change of coordinates,
let’s suppose
L\subset\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and that the intersection of
L

with the real axis is contained in the interval
[-1,1]
and contains the points

\pm 1
. Let
\delta
be the arc
\mathbb{RP}^{1}-[-1,1]
which is properly embedded in
U
,
and divides it into two open subdisks, which we denote
U^{\pm}

respectively.

The property that
L
is porous is equivalent to the fact that there is a
positive constant
C_{L}>0
so that every round disk in
\mathbb{C}
of radius
r
contains a
round disk of radius
C_{L}\cdot r
in
\mathbb{C}-L
. For every round disk
D
of radius
r
let

C^{\pm}(D)\in[0,1]
be the biggest numbers such that
D
contains an open round disk
of radius
C^{+}(D)\cdot r
in
D\cap U^{+}
and an open round disk of radius

C^{-}(D)\cdot r
in
D\cap U^{-}
. The pair of numbers
C^{\pm}(D)
are continuous functions of
the disk
D
, and
\max(C^{+}(D),C^{-}(D))\geq C_{L}
for all
D
. For
D
entirely contained
in
U^{+}
we have
C^{+}(D)=1
and
C^{-}(D)=0
, and likewise for
D
entirely contained in
U^{-}
.
It follows by the intermediate value theorem that we may find disks
D_{r}
of any small radius
r

whose center is far from
\delta
, and such that
C^{+}(D_{r})=C^{-}(D_{r})\geq C_{L}
.
Now take a sequence of such disks with
r_{i}\to 0
and rescale
L
centered at these disks
so that the rescaled disks have center
0
and radius
1
. A Hausdorff limit of any subsequence
of these blowups necessarily has at least two complementary components, proving the lemma.
∎

Finally, let’s apply Lemma
6.25
to
K_{e}
and let

\delta
be as in the proof of the lemma.
Since
K_{e}
is porous, we can find a sequence of elements
g_{i}\in G
so that
the Hausdorff limit
K_{\infty}:=g_{i}K_{e}

has at least two distinct complementary components
U,V
. We may further choose
D_{r}

as in the lemma whose center is far from the endpoints of
K_{e}
(if any). It follows
that we may find a sequence of closed disks
E_{i}
of diameter at most
1/i
that
contain both
g_{i}\delta
and the endpoints of
g_{i}K_{e}
(if any).

Choose points
a^{\pm}\in Z^{\pm}\cap U
and
b^{\pm}\in Z^{\pm}\cap V

and let
\alpha^{\pm}\subset Z^{\pm}
be the unique embedded arcs joining
a^{\pm}
to
b^{\pm}
.
Each
\alpha^{\pm}
is disjoint from
g_{i}K_{e}
except possibly at its endpoints. On the
other hand,
E_{i}
separates
a^{+}
from
b^{+}
and
a^{-}
from
b^{-}
in
S^{2}_{\infty}-g_{i}K_{e}
, when
i
is large. It follows that
\alpha^{\pm}
both intersect
E_{i}
.

After passing to a subsequence, we may assume
E_{i}
converges to a single point

c\in K_{\infty}
. Since
\alpha^{\pm}
are both closed arcs and hence compact,

\alpha^{\pm}
must both contain
c
. But this means that
Z^{+}
and
Z^{-}
intersect,
which is a contradiction after all.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Theorem 6.26

(Minimal
G
-zippers are hairy)
.

Let
G
be the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
.
Suppose that
Z^{\pm}\subset S^{2}_{\infty}
is a minimal

-zipper. Then
Z^{\pm}
is hairy.

Proof.

We suppose not, so that there is some open interval
J
in
Z^{+}
which is not hairy
on at least one side. Choose a proper closed interval
K\subset J
.

Lemma 6.27

(Porous)
.

The arc
K
is porous.

Proof.

We first construct a Jordan curve
X
in
S^{2}

which is the union of an open arc
\alpha\subset Z^{+}
, an open arc
\beta\subset Z^{-}
,
and two points

neither of which are in
Z^{+}
(they might be in
Z^{-}
or not).
The construction of

depends on some known facts about the dynamics of

on a zipper,
proved in
[
30
]
and
[

]
.

If there is some element
g\in G
that acts on

without fixed points, it has an

Z^{+}
that lands at its endpoints

by

[

Lemma 2.23. Either one of these

Z^{-}
, or there is an axis in

that also lands at

.
In either case we can find a closed embedded interval
\gamma:[0,1]\to S^{2}

and

while

\gamma(1/2)\in S^{2}-Z^{+}
. If

can find

as a subset of the convex hulls of
\gamma(I)\cup h\gamma(I)
.

Kim
[

]
, Theorem A says that no nontrivial element

can have both

or both in

. So let’s suppose every nontrivial element

. Then by
[

]

there is some nontrivial

and leaving invariant some

that lands on the other fixed point

.
Either

is in

, or

has an axis in

which has an end that lands at

.
In either case we get a closed embedded interval

as above, and obtain

.

Now we show that
K
is porous. Suppose not. The complement

consists of two

and

. If

is not porous, there is some translate

which
intersects both

, and therefore

must intersect the

, necessarily in a closed connected segment. But then the complementary

are hairs on either side of

∎

The end of the argument closely follows the end of the proof of

6.24
. First of all, by Lemma

we may choose a Jordan arc

,

By the choice of

Choose points

.
Note that

There are paths

to

. Evidently

. For otherwise, if

but not

∎

(Properties of

The first bullet is Theorem

6.6.
The coarse viewpoint

At this point we have estabished an equivalence between pseudo-Anosov flows without
perfect fits on
M
up to orbit equivalence,
G
-CaTherine wheels up to conjugacy,
and minimal
G
-zippers. In fact, we can add a fourth element to this correspondence.

Definition 6.31

(Uniform quasimorphism)
.

Let
G
be a group. A
quasimorphism
is a function
\phi:G\to\mathbb{R}
for which
there is some least non-negative real number
D(\phi)
(called the
defect
)
so that for all
g,h\in G
there is an inequality

|\phi(gh)-\phi(g)-\phi(h)|\leq D(\phi)

If
G
is finitely generated, and we fix any word metric
d_{G}
, a quasimorphism on
G

is
uniform
if it is unbounded, and if the coarse level sets are coarsely connected.
This means there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
so that if
g,h\in G
are any two elements
with
\phi(g),\phi(h)\in[-C_{1},C_{1}]
then there is a sequence of elements

g=g_{0},g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}=h
so that
\phi(g_{i})\in[-C_{1},C_{1}]
for all
i
,
and
d_{G}(g_{i},g_{i+1})\leq C_{2}
for all
i
.

Any bounded function on a group is a quasimorphism; quasimorphisms on
G
modulo bounded
functions form a vector space
Q(G)
which admits a natural norm with respect to which
it is a (typically non-separated) Banach space.
For an introduction to the theory of quasimorphisms, see
[
26
]
.

Uniform quasimorphisms were introduced by Calegari–Loukidou
[
30
]

(although the underlying concept has been familiar to geometric group theorists for some time)
and it is shown there (
[
30
]
Theorem 4.10)
that if a hyperbolic group
G
admits a uniform quasimorphism then it admits a
G
-zipper
(in this paper we are only concerned with the case that the ideal
boundary of
G
is homeomorphic to
S^{2}
, but a more general definition makes sense
for any hyperbolic group).

Conversely, (Calegari–Zung
[
31
]
Theorem 2.10 part 1)
show that if
X
is any pseudo-Anosov
flow on
M
without perfect fits, one can construct uniform quasimorphisms
\phi:G\to\mathbb{R}

that are
adapted to
X
. This means that the values of
\phi
grow linearly
along any quasigeodesic in
G
that tracks (i.e. stays a bounded distance from) a
flowline of
\tilde{X}
, the lift of
X
to the universal cover.

Associated to
X
, let
Q_{X}(G)
denote the space of uniform quasimorphisms on
G
adapted
to
X
. Here we think of
Q_{X}(G)
as a subset of
Q(G)
. It turns out
(Calegari–Zung
[
31
]
Theorem 2.10 part 2) that
Q_{X}(G)
is
an open convex cone in
Q(G)
. On the other hand, by combining

[
30
]
Theorem 4.10 with Theorem
6.26
,
Theorem
6.24
and Theorem
6.19

every uniform quasimorphism
\phi
on
G
gives rise to a pseudo-Anosov flow
X
without
perfect fits, and it is immediate from the construction that
\phi
is adapted to
X
.

We therefore obtain the second main result of this paper:

Theorem 6.32

(
G
-Equivalence Theorem)
.

For
M
a fixed closed hyperbolic 3-manifold with fundamental group
G
acting on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}

there are canonical bijections between the following structures:

(1)

pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits on
M
up to orbit equivalence;

(2)

G
-CaTherine wheels up to conjugacy;

(3)

(minimal)
G
-zippers; and

(4)

connected components in
Q(G)
of the space of uniform quasimorphisms
\phi:G\to\mathbb{R}
.

In particular, the set of uniform quasimorphisms on
G
is the union of finitely many
open convex cones; this is a coarse analog of part of Thurston’s theory of fibrations

[
87
]
, that may be paraphrased as saying that the subset of
H^{1}(M;\mathbb{R})

represented by closed nowhere zero 1-forms is the union of finitely many open convex cones
(which correspond precisely to the open fibered faces of the Thurston norm ball
on
H_{2}(M;\mathbb{R})=H^{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
).

Let us end this subsection with a conjecture.
Uniform quasimorphisms make sense on any hyperbolic group, and in fact

[
30
]
Theorem 4.10 constructs a
G
-zipper

Z^{\pm}\subset\partial_{\infty}G
for any
hyperbolic group
G
from a uniform quasimorphism
\phi
on
G
.

(Zippers to flows)
.

Let

homeomorphic to

, and

.

If this conjecture is true for some

7.
Endomorphisms

This section is mostly expository, and is concerned almost entirely with
examples. An
endomorphism
of a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
is a pair of
endomorphisms
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
and
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
for which
Hf=fh
. There is
probably no hope for a full classification, and even if it were possible,
such a classification would leave
out some important and closely related examples (semigroups or pseudogroups of partially
defined endomorphisms of
S^{1}
and
S^{2}
respectively).

An important class of examples arise from
expanding Thurston maps
, whose
theory is developed in great detail by Bonk–Meyer
[
19
]
. For these examples,

h
is (conjugate to) the covering map
z\to z^{d}
on
S^{1}
for some
d>1

and
H
is a degree
d
branched covering of
S^{2}
(for the same
d
),
which in many cases can be taken to be a degree
d
rational map on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. We
emphasize that it is only
some
of these examples that give rise to
CaTherine wheels; many (the majority) give rise to Peano curves that should be
P-CaTherine wheels in the sense of Definition
5.4
.
The difference has to do with the absence of perfect fits; in the language of matings,
the ray equivalence classes should have diameter zero.

A closely related, though hitherto unexplored (as far as we know) class of examples
are those that arise from
expanding origamis
. For these examples the map
h

is piecewise linear on
S^{1}
with slopes of constant absolute
value
\lambda>1
, whereas the map
H
is piecewise conformal (alternately holomorphic
and anti-holomorphic) on a subdivision of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
into finitely many
tiles. This is a subject that deserves a more thorough treatment, that we plan
to develop in future projects (e.g.
[
29
]
).

Further afield, there are many examples of CaTherine wheels and related objects
that admit nontrivial families of partially defined symmetries — Bowen–Series Markov maps

[
22
]
, Dicks’ lightning paths
[
33
,
34
,
40
]
,
Arnoux’ IET suspensions
[
5
]
— even Hilbert’s square-filling curve is of
this kind (
[
25
]
Example 10.14). This subject is too big to
survey here.

7.1.
Expanding Thurston maps

In this subsection we discuss CaTherine wheels
f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
admitting endomorphisms

h,H
for which
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
is conjugate to
z\to z^{d}
and
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
is a
degree
d
branched cover.

Bonk–Meyer’s approach is to start with the branched cover
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
; our
account of their theory starts with the map
z\to z^{d}
on
S^{1}
and directly construct
invariant laminations and laminar relations. These laminations are well-studied, and
their analysis is rather subtle; see e.g.
[
92
,
61
,
17
,
18
,
93
]
.

When is a degree
d
covering map
S^{1}\to S^{1}
conjugate to
z\to z^{d}
? When it is
locally eventually onto:

Lemma 7.1

(LEO)
.

A degree
d>1
covering map
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
is conjugate to
z\to z^{d}
for some
d>1

if and only if it is LEO (locally eventually onto): for any open interval
U\subset S^{1}

there is an
n
such that
f^{n}(U)=S^{1}
.

Proof.

This condition is evidently necessary. Conversely, it we start with a fixed point
p
and
form the increasing union of preimages of
p
, this sequence of subsets converges to
all of
S^{1}
and defines the coordinates of the obvious conjugacy.
∎

7.1.1.
Invariant laminations

Let’s fix an integer
d
and discuss laminations. It is convenient to think of the
circle
S^{1}
as
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and the map
h
as
x\to d\cdot x\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Definition 7.2

(Major)
.

Fix
d
. A (degree
d
)
critical leaf of multiplicity
n
is an unordered

(n+1)
-tuple of distinct points
\{x_{0},\cdots,x_{n}\}
in
S^{1}

for which
d(x_{i}-x_{j})=0\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
for all
i,j
.

A (degree
d
)
major
is a collection
C
of disjoint (degree
d
)
critical leaves which are pairwise unlinked and have total multiplicity
d-1
.

A critical leaf is simple if it has multiplicity
1
, and a major is simple if
all its critical leaves are simple.

A non-simple critical leaf of multiplicity
n
may be thought of as a union of

n
(ordinary) leaves, and a major may be thought of in this way as a finite lamination.
Equivalently, a major may be thought of as the nontrivial elements in an equivalence
relation on
S^{1}
.

Now let’s discuss dynamics. If
\lambda:=\{x,y\}
is a non-critical leaf, we
define
h\lambda
to be the leaf
\{d\cdot x,d\cdot y\}
, and otherwise
we define
h\lambda
to be empty. The
grand orbit
of a leaf
\lambda

is the set of leaves
\mu
such that either
h^{n}(\lambda)=\mu
for some
n
, or

h^{n}(\mu)=\lambda
for some
n
.

Definition 7.3

(Invariant lamination)
.

A lamination
\Lambda
of
S^{1}
is said to be
invariant
if

(1)

for every non-critical leaf
\lambda
, the leaf
h\lambda
is a leaf of
\Lambda
; and

(2)

for every leaf
\lambda
there are exactly
d
leaves
\mu
of
\Lambda
with

h\mu=\lambda
, and these leaves are all disjoint.

The condition that preimages are disjoint implies that if
\Lambda
is an invariant
lamination and
\lambda:=\{x,y\}
is a leaf of
\Lambda
then for every

x^{\prime}\in S^{1}
with
hx^{\prime}=x
there is a unique
y^{\prime}\in S^{1}
with
hy^{\prime}=y
for which

\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}
is a leaf of
\Lambda
.

Proposition 7.4

(Invariant lamination)
.

For any
d>1
and a degree
d
major
C
there is an invariant lamination

\Lambda
containing
C
.

Furthermore, suppose there are no points
x,y\in S^{1}
(not necessarily distinct)
contained in leaves of
C
and an
n>0
so that
h^{n}(x)=y
. Then
\Lambda
is
unique.

Proof.

For a proof see e.g.
[
93
]
Theorem 4.3 or

[
27
]
, Proposition 5.3 (in the very slightly different context of
invariant elaminations). For the sake of the reader we give a sketch of the construction.

The idea is very simple: the result of quotienting

S^{1}
by the equivalence relation
\sim
associated to the major
C
is a cactus, i.e. a
tree of
d
smaller circles
C_{i}
, for
i=0,\cdots,d-1
, each of perimeter
1/d
.
The map
h
factors through
\sim
to give a map
h:C/\sim\;\to C
which takes each

C_{i}
to
C
homothetically, scaling lengths by
d
. Define
\Lambda_{0}:=C
and
inductively define
\Lambda_{i}
by pulling back
\Lambda_{i-1}
by
h
to each
C_{i}
,
and then taking the union of the preimages in
C
. Then take
\Lambda
to be the closure of
the increasing union
\cup_{i}\Lambda_{i}
.

The step that takes a collection of laminations on the
C_{i}
and forms the
preimage in
C
is ambiguous when one of the laminations has endpoints in
C_{i}
at the
image of leaves of
C
(which become points in
C/\sim
); one may resolve this
ambiguity by always making a leftmost choice of preimage at every stage.
By construction, this ambiguity arises when, and only when, there are points

x,y\in S^{1}
contained in leaves of
C
(not necessarily distinct) and an
n>0

so that
h^{n}(x)=y
.
∎

It should be emphasized that Proposition
7.4
is
entirely constructive, and invariant laminations (or: finite approximations to them)
can be computed efficiently. When endpoints of the majors are rational, the
leaves of the invariant lamination (expressed as unordered pairs of right-infinite
strings in their base
d
expansion) are parameterized by infinite walks in an
explicit finite digraph, which may be thought of as the analog of the
(projectively weighted) invariant stable traintrack for a pseudo-Anosov automorphism of
a closed surface.

If
C
is a degree
d
major with invariant lamination
\Lambda
, then
we can form the big invariant relation
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
. It is possible for an invariant
lamination
\Lambda
to contain only countably many leaves, so that
\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda)
is
the complete equivalence class and
\textnormal{Lam}(\textnormal{Rel}(\Lambda))
is empty!

Set

7.1.2.
Mating

Mating of (connected polynomial) Julia sets was first described by Douady and worked
out in detail by Tan Lei, Rees, Shishikura, Chéritat
and others; see
[
86
,
82
,
84
,
36
]
.
Milnor
[
75
]
worked out an explicit example of the mating of
two (degree 2) dendrite Julia sets both associated to
z\to z^{2}+c
for

c\approx-0.228+1.115i
, the unique root of
4c^{3}+4c^{2}+1
with a positive
imaginary part, and one can hardly do better than read his paper.

Let
H^{\pm}:\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be degree
d
polynomials with connected filled Julia
sets
K^{\pm}
. Assuming that
\partial K^{\pm}
are locally connected,
there are surjective maps
f^{\pm}:S^{1}\to\partial K^{\pm}

conjugating
h:z\to z^{d}
on
S^{1}
to
H^{\pm}
on
\partial K^{\pm}
.
A
topological mating
is obtained as a quotient

K:=K^{+}\sqcup K^{-}/\sim
where
p\in\partial K^{+}
is identified with
q\in\partial K^{-}

if there is
x\in S^{1}
with
f^{+}(x)=p
and
f^{-}(-x)=q
.
Evidently the maps
f^{\pm}
paste together (after conjugating
f^{-}
by the antipodal
map, which commutes with
z\to z^{d}
) to form
f:S^{1}\to K
, and there is a
degree
d
endomorphism
H:K\to K
agreeing with
H^{\pm}
on
K^{\pm}

respectively so that
fh=Hf
as maps from
S^{1}
to
K
.

When
K^{\pm}
are dendrites, and
f^{\pm}
are surjective and arise by quotienting
the circle by laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
, then providing
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
have no perfect
fits, the quotient
K
is a sphere and
f:S^{1}\to K
is a CaTherine wheel.
More generally, whenever
K^{\pm}
are dendrites and
K
is a sphere, then
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

form an especial pair and we conjecture that
f:S^{1}\to K
is a P-CaTherine wheel
in the sense of §
5
. In the terminology of mating, leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

are called
rays
, and chains of leaves alternating between
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
each with
one endpoint in common with its adjacent leaves define an equivalence relation on rays
called
ray equivalence
. The
diameter
of a ray equivalence class is one
less than the number of leaves in such a maximal chain. Much is known and
much is unknown about ray equivalence in general; see
[
55
]
or
[
23
]
, § 3.
For instance, Epstein showed that whenever postcritically finite quadratic maps can be
geometrically mated, the diameter is always finite; see

[
84
]
or
[
81
]
Proposition 4.12.

If
K
is a sphere one says
H:K\to K
is obtained
by
topological mating
. When
H
is conjugate to a (degree
d
)
rational map on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
one says it is a
geometric mating
. One should beware
that the holomorphic conjugacy class of this rational map is not necessarily unique!

When
H^{\pm}
are strictly postcritically quadratic polynomials (associated to
Misiurewicz points), Shishikura
[
84
]
showed there
is a geometric mating if and only if

H^{+}
and
H^{-}
are in nonconjugate limbs of the Mandebrot set
\mathcal{M}
. This means the
following. For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
there is a unique
c(\lambda)
for which

H_{c(\lambda)}:z\to z^{2}+c(\lambda)
has a fixed point
z
with

H^{\prime}_{c(\lambda)}(z)=\lambda
, namely

c(\lambda)=\lambda/2-(\lambda/2)^{2}

For each nonzero rational number
p/q
and each root of unity

Zippers

If
\mathcal{L}
is an invariant laminar relation for a postcritically finite major

Let’s suppose

to a relation

to

(see
[

, § IV),

If
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}

the interiors of the Hubbard trees

.

Definition 7.8

(Trimmed Hubbard trees)
.

The

are obtained from the Hubbard trees

by removing the 1-valent vertices.

Proposition 7.9

(Zipper is full preimage)
.

Suppose a topological mating of dendritic Julia sets admits a CaTherine wheel

f:S^{1}\to S^{2}
. Then the unions of the iterated preimages of the trimmed Hubbard trees

Z^{\pm}:=\cup_{n}H^{-n}T^{\pm}_{o}
are the zipper for

.

Proof.

Evidently
\cup_{n}H^{-n}T^{\pm}_{o}
is contained in the zipper for

, so it suffices to
prove the converse. These unions are path-connected, since each successive preimage intersects
the preimages at the previous stage, and they are dense in
Z^{\pm}
because the preimages
of the major (whose image is in
T^{\pm}_{o}
) are dense in
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
by definition.
Equality follows.
∎

7.1.4.
Expanding Thurston maps

We are now in a position to make contact with Bonk–Meyer.

Definition 7.11
.

An orientation-preserving branched cover
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
is an
expanding Thurston map

(1)

if it is post-critically finite; i.e. if the forward orbit of the set of critical
points is finite; and

(2)

if there is a Jordan curve
\Gamma
containing the postcritical set so that the
mesh size of the graphs
\Gamma_{n}:=H^{-n}{\Gamma}
goes to zero.

With this definition, one has the following theorem of Meyer
[
70
]
:

Theorem 7.12

(Meyer; Power is mating)
.

Let
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
be an expanding Thurston map without periodic critical points.
Then every sufficiently high iterate of
H
is topologically conjugate to the
topological mating of two polynomials.

We explain the idea of the proof in a simple case. Let
d
be the degree of
H
,
denote the critical points by
C
and the strictly forward orbit of
C
by
P
.
Adjust
\Gamma
by an isotopy if necessary so that the germ of
\Gamma
through

P
is invariant. We need one further combinatorial condition: there should
be an
H
-invariant Jordan curve
\tilde{\Gamma}
(also containing the
postcritical set) and such that
\tilde{\Gamma}
is isotopic to
\Gamma
rel.

P
. To achieve this one might need to pass to a power of
H
; that it can be
done at all is
[
19
]
Theorem 15.1.

Let’s suppose for simplicity the critical points are all simple (in particular
there are
2d-2
of them) so that
\Gamma_{1}
is a 4-valent graph with
vertices at
C
. In the sequel we refer to a
2-coloring of a graph
as a choice
of two colors (black and white) for the complementary regions, so that different
colors meet along every edge; and by abuse of notation we refer to a
2-colored graph

as a graph with such a choice of 2-coloring.

Any planar graph with even valence vertices admits a 2-coloring. Since
\Gamma
is
a Jordan curve it (trivially) admits a 2-coloring; choose one. This 2-coloring pulls
back to a 2-coloring on each of the graphs
\Gamma_{n}
. There are
d
complementary white and
d

complementary black disks to
\Gamma_{1}
, so if we resolve the 4-valent
vertices in such a way as to create
d
new white isthmuses and
d
new
black isthmuses, the result is a Jordan curve
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
bounding a single
black and a single white disk.

Now we discuss how to resolve
\Gamma_{2}
. At a vertex
p
of
\Gamma_{2}
which is
the preimage of a vertex
q
of
\Gamma_{1}
, we pull back the resolution of
\Gamma_{1}

at
q
to a resolution of
\Gamma_{2}
at
p
. This resolves all vertices of
\Gamma_{2}

except for the
2d-2
vertices at
C
.
Since the germ of
\Gamma
through
P
is invariant,
the germ of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
near the critical set
C
agrees, so it makes
sense to do ‘the same’ resolution of
\Gamma_{2}
at
C
that we did at
\Gamma_{1}
.
This produces
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
. The net result is to create as many black isthmuses and
white isthmuses, so that
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
is a Jordan curve bounding a single
black and a single white disk. Iterate the process.

If we think of each resolution
\Gamma_{n}^{\prime}\to\Gamma_{n}
as a parameterization by a
circle, we get an inverse sequence of degree
d
maps

\cdots\to\Gamma^{\prime}_{n+1}\to\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}\to\Gamma^{\prime}_{n-1}\to\cdots

parameterizing the inverse sequence

\cdots\to\Gamma_{n+1}\to\Gamma_{n}\to\Gamma_{n-1}\to\cdots

The set of white vertices in
\Gamma_{n}
(those that resolve to a white isthmus) pulls back
to a set of unordered pairs of points
\Lambda^{+}_{n}
in
\Gamma_{n}
, whereas the black
points pull back to
\Lambda^{-}_{n}
, and each of these is a (finite) lamination.
The circular orders on the
\Gamma_{n}
let us identify each
\Lambda^{\pm}_{n}
as a
sublamination of
\Lambda^{\pm}_{n+1}

and in the limit we find a pair of laminations
\Lambda^{\pm}
of
S^{1}
invariant
under a degree
d
map
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
which is conjugate to
z\to z^{d}
by
Lemma
7.1
(implicitly this depends on a choice of isotopy rel.
P
of
each
\Gamma_{n}
to
\Gamma_{n-1}
; this is where
\tilde{\Gamma}
plays a role,
though we do not explain it here and refer the reader to
[
19
]
).
Each of the laminations
\Lambda^{\pm}
arises from
a dendrite Julia set of a postcritically finite polynomial, and by construction

H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
is obtained by (topological) mating.

Notice that this construction is
not unique
: it depends on a choice of
resolution of the vertices of
\Gamma_{1}
. Different resolutions give representations of

H
as a topological mating of different pairs of polynomials.

For most expanding Thurston maps, the laminations
\Lambda^{\pm}
will have perfect fits.
Some examples where they do not (and therefore give rise to CaTherine wheels) include
a mating that gives rise to the map
H:z\to 1-(3z+1)^{3}/(9z-1)^{2}
. This
map is a Lattés example, derived from a Euclidean
crystallographic group of type
(236)
(see
[
19
]
, Example 3.25). It arises
as a mating in more than one way; some of these matings have perfect fits and do

not
give rise to CaTherine wheels.

7.2.
Expanding Origamis

The construction of
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
and
Z^{\pm}
from a CaTherine wheel

depends
crucially on a fixed choice of orientation on both
S^{1}
and
S^{2}
. Endomorphisms that
do not (locally) preserve this orientation destroy this structure
unless
the
endomorphisms

and
H:S^{2}\to S^{2}
simultaneously reverse the orientation
on both
S^{1}
and
S^{2}
on subsets related by

. Rather surprisingly, one can
find many such examples which respect the linear resp. conformal structure on
S^{1}
resp.
S^{2}
.
In particular, many interesting examples exist for which the maps
h
and
H
are

expanding origamis
, to be defined in the sequel.

7.2.1.
Invariant laminations

An expanding origami
h:S^{1}\to S^{1}

is a piecwise linear map with slope
\pm\lambda
for some fixed
\lambda>1
.
We shall always assume that
h
achieves both positive and negative slopes (or else

h
would be a covering map).
LEO covering maps of
S^{1}
are classified up to conjugacy by their degree. By contrast,
there are many different (topological) conjugacy classes of expanding origamis

h:S^{1}\to S^{1}
of fixed degree and slope.

We say that a lamination
\Lambda
is invariant for
h
if

(1)

for every leaf
\lambda
either the points of

\lambda
are identified by
h
, or
h\lambda
is a leaf of
\Lambda
; and

(2)

for every leaf
\lambda
there is some leaf
\mu
with

h\mu=\lambda
.

For typical
h
different points in
S^{1}
will have different numbers of preimages.
If it happens that every point in
S^{1}
has
d
preimages then we say
h
has

geometric degree

d
.

Suppose
h
has geometric degree
d
and
\{x,y\}
is a leaf of
\Lambda
.
There are
d
preimages
x_{0},\cdots,x_{d-1}
and
y_{0},\cdots,y_{d-1}
of each
of
x
and
y
. We say that
\Lambda
is
degree
d
invariant

if for every leaf
\{x,y\}
the
2d
preimages of
x
and
y
are
partitioned into
d
pairs which are all leaves of
\Lambda
.

By abuse of notation we refer to these as
preimage leaves
. A preimage
leaf of the form
\{x_{i},y_{j}\}
is
ordinary
; otherwise it is

folded
. Note that folded leaves, strictly speaking, have empty forward
image under

. We must therefore distinguish between those leaves that have
no forward image and are preimages, and those that have no forward image and are
not preimages. In place of the term ‘major’ we introduce a new term:

Definition 7.13

(Seed)
.

Let
h
have geometric degree
d
, and let
\Lambda
be degree
d
invariant.
A finite subset
C\subset\Lambda

if every leaf of
\Lambda

7.2.2.

Expanding origamis

are defined as follows:

Definition 7.16

(Expanding origami)
.

A map
H:\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}

if there is a 2-colored
graph
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
so that

(1)

the sequence of graphs on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
obtained as iterated preimages

has mesh size going to 0; and

(2)

the restriction of
H
to each white tile is holomorphic, and the

H

points of these restrictions to each tile are either in the interior, or at a vertex.

An expanding origami is
special
if there is a subdivision
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
\Gamma
with respect
to which
H
is cellular.

Under many conditions, expanding origamis give rise to CaTherine wheels with nontrivial
endomorphisms.

8.
Probability

Conformally natural examples of CaTherine wheels,
and much of the associated structure (laminar relations, zippers)
are extremely well-studied by probabilists, and arise naturally as
whole plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
for
\kappa\geq 8
.
We are indebted to Ewain Gwynne and Greg Lawler for explaining some of this
theory to us, and guiding us to some key references. This is a vast subject, and the
purpose of this section is simply to state some of the relevant facts of the subject
in the language of the rest of this paper, and to refer the reader to the
relevant literature.

Let
\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the open unit disk.
Suppose
\eta:[0,\infty]\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is a continuous simple curve in the
unit disk with
\eta(0)\in\partial\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
\eta(\infty)=0
and

\eta(0,\infty]\subset\mathbb{D}
. For each
t\geq 0
set
K_{t}:=\eta[0,t]
and

U_{t}:=\mathbb{D}-K_{t}
and
let
g_{t}:U_{t}\to\mathbb{D}
be the unique conformal homeomorphism with
g_{t}(0)=0
and

g_{t}^{\prime}(0)
a positive real number. After a suitable reparameterization of
\eta

one may furthermore assume
g_{t}^{\prime}(0)=e^{t}
(one says
\eta
is parameterized

by capacity
from
0
).

For each
t\in[0,\infty)
the limit

W(t):=\lim_{z\to\eta(t)}g_{t}(z)

exists, and
W:[0,\infty)\to\partial\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is continuous.
Furthermore, if we assume
\eta
is paramterized by capacity,
g_{t}
satisfies
Loewner’s differential equation
[
67
]

\partial_{t}g_{t}(z)=-g_{t}(z)\frac{g_{t}(z)+W(t)}{g_{t}(z)-W(t)}

In fact, given
W
one may use this differential equation to recover
K_{t}

(which now might not be simple, or even a curve). Schramm
[
83
]
defined
radial
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
to be
K_{t}
when
W(t)=e^{iB(\kappa t)}
where

B:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is Brownian motion.

Whole-plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
from
\infty
to
\infty
for
\kappa\geq 8

is a variant of
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
first studied in
[
71
]
.
It can be built from chordal
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
and whole plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{16/\kappa}

[
41
]
, footnote 4. The result is a continuous proper map
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}

that extends to
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

The following theorem concatenates results from
[
71
]
and

[
74
]
Remark 5.3:

Theorem 8.1

(SLE;
[
71
,
74
]
)
.

Whole-plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}
from
\infty
to
\infty
for
\kappa\geq 8
gives
rise almost surely to a CaTherine wheel
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. Furthermore, for such an
f

almost surely every point has at most
3
preimages; in detail

(1)

a full measure set of points has only 1 preimage;

(2)

a measure 0 uncountable set has exactly 2 preimages; and

(3)

a countable set has exactly 3 preimages.

The laminar relations
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
associated to
f
for whole-plane
\textnormal{SLE}_{\kappa}

are described in terms of a pair of Brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}
in

[
41
]
Theorem 1.9. These Brownian motions are independent for
\kappa=8

and negatively correlated for
\kappa>8
. This result has many applications
to SLE and Liouville quantum gravity (hereafter LQG); see e.g.
[

Example 8.3

(Half-zippers from Liouville quantum gravity)
.

This example is taken from Calegari–Gwynne
[

, which was written
simultaneously with this paper.

For a parameter
\gamma\in(0,2)
,
Liouville quantum gravity
(hereafter

D

\mathbb{CP}^{1}
heuristically associated to the
random ‘metric tensor’

\Phi
is the
Gaussian free field (GFF). The GFF is not exactly a random function, rather it
is a random distribution, and is characterized by the property that
for every smooth, compactly supported (real-valued) function
f
on
\mathbb{C}
,
the
L^{2}
inner product
\langle\nabla\Phi,\nabla f\rangle
is Gaussian with
mean 0 and variance
\|\nabla f\|^{2}
. In any case, although
\Phi
itself
does not make sense as an honest function, the
\gamma
-LQG metric
D
on

\mathbb{C}
exists as an honest metric almost surely.

It turns out in this metric that geodesics exist, and for almost every
p\in\mathbb{C}

there is a
D
-geodesic from
p
to
\infty
. The
D
-geodesic tree rooted
at
\infty
, denoted
Z_{\infty}
is defined to be the union of all
D
-geodesics
to
\infty
, minus their starting points. In other words,

Z_{\infty}:=\{\sigma(t)\text{ such that }\sigma\text{ is a $D$-geodesic from
a point of $\mathbb{C}$ to $\infty$ and }0<t<\infty\}

See Figure
34
.
