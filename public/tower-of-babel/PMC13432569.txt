Early outpatient follow‐up, hospital‐free survival, and health‐related quality of life in older adults after hospitalization for acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Alexandra Hajduk, Lisa M Kern, Jason R Falvey, Jingjing Shang, Jeph Herrin, Sui Tsang, Allison Aviles, Sarwat I Chaudhry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent evidence regarding the benefits of early outpatient follow‐up for older adults after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is conflicting, and prior studies may have been impacted by confounding by indication by not accounting for geriatric vulnerabilities. We used data from a unique nationwide cohort of older adults hospitalized with AMI, linked to Medicare data, that captured rich data on geriatric vulnerabilities and other potentially influential covariates. We used inverse‐probability of treatment‐weighted analyses to evaluate associations of early outpatient follow‐up with hospital‐free survival and physical and health‐related quality of life 180 days post‐discharge. More than two‐fifths of the 1539 participants had a follow‐up visit within 7 days, and one quarter had a follow‐up visit within 8–14 days. Geriatric vulnerabilities were not more prevalent among participants receiving early outpatient follow‐up, and early outpatient follow‐up was not associated with differences in hospital‐free survival or risk of decline in health‐related quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Outpatient follow‐up within a week after hospitalization for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is endorsed by national guidelines (e.g., American College of Cardiology's “See You in 7” Initiative 1 ). Timely post‐discharge follow‐up is promoted as a means to facilitate successful hospital‐to‐home transition 2 and prevent avoidable readmissions among older adults. 3 However, evidence of benefit for early outpatient follow‐up after AMI is mixed, 4 , 5 and much of the evidence supporting the See You in 7 Initiative is based on studies showing benefits among patients with heart failure, 6 not AMI. Additionally, prior studies have not accounted for geriatric vulnerabilities, such as functional impairment, which may strongly impact older AMI patients’ propensity to receive early outpatient follow‐up as well as their risk for poor outcomes, 7 introducing confounding by indication. Furthermore, little is known about how early outpatient follow‐up, which may help with symptom management, medication optimization, and improve patient satisfaction, impacts patient‐centered outcomes that are most important to many older adults, for example, health‐related quality of life.

This study evaluated the association of early outpatient follow‐up with two increasingly recognized important clinical and patient‐centered outcomes, that is, hospital‐free survival 8 and health‐related quality of life (HR‐QoL), among older adults hospitalized with AMI, while accounting for geriatric vulnerabilities and other covariates that are known to impact care and outcomes older adults. 7 We hypothesized that, after accounting for confounding by indication, early outpatient follow‐up would be associated with improved outcomes at 180 days post‐discharge.

METHODS

Study design and sample

This retrospective cohort study used data from the SILVER‐AMI study 9 linked to data from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). Briefly, SILVER‐AMI was a prospective study of 3,041 adults age ≥75 years hospitalized with AMI at 94 academic and community hospitals across the United States from 2013 to 2016. Participants underwent a structured interview and physical assessment during AMI hospitalization to collect information on geriatric vulnerabilities, demographics, and psychosocial factors. Clinical characteristics were abstracted from the medical record. Patient‐reported outcomes were collected via survey at 180 days post‐discharge. CMS data were linked to each SILVER‐AMI participant through standard CMS identifiers. Participants who died prior to discharge (n = 35), were not discharged to the community (n = 445), were not linked to CMS data (n = 694), or were covered by Medicare Advantage prior to 2015 (due to less standardized data reporting, n = 308) were excluded. Study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Yale and all participating sites.

Study measures

Independent variable

Outpatient follow‐up was defined as the first ambulatory visit after hospital discharge. We identified ambulatory visits in outpatient and carrier files using a list of Current Procedural Terminology codes endorsed by the National Committee for Quality Assurance reflecting in‐person, office‐based visits for adults involving evaluation and management (99201‐99245, 99324‐99350, 99387, 99397). Participants were categorized as having a follow‐up visit within 7 days, within 8–14 days (to evaluate whether a more flexible follow‐up window may be associated with differences in outcomes), or no visit within 14 days post‐discharge.

Outcomes

Hospital‐free survival at 180 days post‐discharge was defined as the number of days following discharge from the index AMI admission that participants remained free from death, readmission, or emergency department visit. Date of death was derived from the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File. Date of first readmission or emergency department visit was derived from MedPAR, inpatient claims, and encounter files (readmissions), or outpatient claims and encounter files (emergency department visits).

HR‐QoL was assessed using the SF‐12 10 administered during AMI hospitalization (asking participants to report pre‐AMI HR‐QoL) and at 6 months post‐discharge, yielding physical and mental component scores ranging from 0 to 100 (higher indicates better HR‐QoL). We calculated change in physical HR‐QoL and mental HR‐QoL scores from hospitalization to 6 months post‐discharge by subtracting each participant's score at baseline from their score at 6 months. We defined clinically significant decline in physical or mental HR‐QoL as the minimally important clinical difference of ≥5 points. 11

Covariates

Demographics and health behaviors (age, sex, race, ethnicity, marital status, education, smoking status) were collected via self‐report or medical record review. Medicare type (traditional vs. Medicare Advantage) at the time of index admission was ascertained from the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File. Medical history and hospitalization characteristics, including treatments, were extracted from participants’ medical records. Geriatric vulnerabilities (mobility impairment, cognitive impairment, upper extremity weakness, activities of daily living, grip strength, vision impairment, hearing impairment) were evaluated with brief geriatric assessments or self‐report, and depressive symptoms and social support were captured via validated questionnaires as previously described. 9 A 180‐day mortality risk score, previously developed in the SILVER‐AMI study, 12 was used to account for heterogeneity in survival probability.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics with standardized mean differences (SMD) were used to examine differences in characteristics among participants with follow‐up visits within 1–7 days, within 8–14 days, and no early follow‐up visit. We considered SMD values ≥ 0.2 to indicate meaningful differences among groups. We used Cox proportional hazards regression to assess the association between early outpatient follow‐up and 180‐day hospital‐free survival and logistic regression to assess the association with risk of decline in physical and mental HR‐QoL. To address potential confounding by indication, we estimated propensity scores for the probability of early outpatient follow‐up using LASSO, 13 , 14 including all variables (including geriatric vulnerabilities) in Table 1. We applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), 15 constructed from propensity scores, to all models. We used multiple imputation to account for missing data. We performed all analyses in Stata 19.5 (StataCorp).

RESULTS

Among the 1559 SILVER‐AMI participants who met the inclusion criteria, 20 patients who died between hospital discharge and 14 days post‐discharge were excluded, leaving an analytic cohort of 1539 participants. Of these, 681 (44.2%) had a follow‐up visit within 7 days, 396 (25.7%) had a follow‐up visit within 8–14 days, and 462 (30.0%) had no follow‐up visit within 14 days. Differences in demographic, clinical, functional, and psychosocial characteristics among early outpatient follow‐up groups are shown in Table 1. The groups demonstrated standardized mean differences >0.2 for only two characteristics. Participants with no early outpatient follow‐up were slightly more likely to have been hospitalized in earlier study years (2013–2014; 43.5%) than participants with outpatient follow‐up within 7 days (34.8%) or 8–14 days (39.3%; SMD = 0.21). AMI treatment also differed meaningfully (SMD = 0.25): participants with no early follow‐up were less likely to undergo percutaneous intervention (56.3%) than participants with outpatient follow‐up within 7 days (65.6%) or 8–14 days (63.1%), but were more likely to undergo coronary artery bypass grafting (10.0% vs 4.7% and 7.1%, respectively). The prevalence of geriatric vulnerabilities did not differ substantially between groups. All covariates demonstrated SMDs < 0.2 after applying IPTW (Table 1).

At 180 days post‐discharge, 50.8% of participants with follow‐up within 7 days, 48.0% with follow‐up within 8–14 days, and 51.5% with no early outpatient follow‐up remained free from readmission, ED visit, or death. In IPTW models, hospital‐free survival did not differ among participants with outpatient follow‐up within 7 days (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.86–1.22) or 8–14 days (HR = 1.17, 95% CI = 0.96–1.44) compared to participants with no early outpatient follow‐up (Figure 1).

Clinically significant declines in physical HR‐QoL and mental HF‐QoL were experienced by 33.8% and 33.7% of participants with follow‐up within 7 days, 33.8% and 33.5% of participants with follow‐up within 8–14 days, and 27.6% and 35.5% of participants with no early outpatient follow‐up, respectively. In IPTW models, early outpatient follow‐up was not associated with risk of decline in physical HR‐QoL [odds ratio (OR) (95% CI) = 1.23 (0.92–1.65) for participants with follow‐up within 7 days, OR = 1.23 (0.88–1.71) for participants with follow‐up within 8–14 days], nor risk of decline in mental HR‐QoL [(OR (95% CI) = 1.03 (0.74–1.44) for participants with follow‐up within 7 days, 0.87 (0.59–1.28); Figure 1].

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide cohort study, we found that approximately half (48.5%) of older adults hospitalized with AMI completed early outpatient follow‐up within 7 days, as recommended by national guidelines. Contrary to our hypotheses, geriatric vulnerabilities were not more prevalent among older AMI patients with early outpatient follow‐up. Furthermore, early outpatient follow‐up was not significantly associated with hospital‐free survival nor HR‐QoL at 6 months post‐discharge.

This study is the first, to our knowledge, to account for geriatric vulnerabilities in examining early outpatient follow‐up and outcomes among older adults with AMI. This study has several limitations, including observational study design (precluding causal inference and carrying the potential for residual confounding), lack of detailed information about follow‐up visit (e.g., provider type, specialty, relationship with patient), and an event horizon of 6 months, which does not capture potential longer term impacts of early outpatient follow‐up. Nevertheless, this study contributes to the growing evidence base that early outpatient follow‐up, defined strictly by the number of days after hospital discharge, irrespective of other attributes, may not be the correct (or sufficiently precise) target for improving post‐AMI outcomes in older adults. Contextual factors, such as provider type or whether the patient is known to the physician from the hospitalization, 5 in addition to patient factors, such as patient complexity, may influence success of early outpatient follow‐up in improving outcomes. In the future, it may be important for national guidelines to consider the context, not just the timing, of early outpatient follow‐up when making recommendations.
