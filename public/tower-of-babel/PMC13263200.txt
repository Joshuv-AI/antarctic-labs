Early-onset rice body synovitis of the knee in children under three years: a case series and review of the literature
Authors: LunQing Zhu, Cong Liu, Yao Liu, BingQing Mao, YouHui Huang, Jun Wen, YiCong Liu, Tao Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRice body synovitis reports have been rarely reported in children under three years of age. We aimed to explore the clinical characteristics and treatment experience of rice body-like synovitis in children’s knees.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical and imaging data of 4 cases (5 knees) of rice body-like synovitis in children treated at our hospital from January 2014 to December 2021. Among the patients, 1 was male and 3 were female, with an average age of 22.5 months. The average length of symptoms before admission was 1.86 months. Clinical presentations included swelling and limping, with no fever or rash.ResultsThree patients with unilateral onset and one patient with bilateral onset underwent unilateral arthroscopic excision of synovial lesions. All 4 patients had pathological findings consistent with chronic inflammation, leading to a postoperative diagnosis of juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). Postoperatively, all patients received standardized pharmacological treatment and were followed for 24 to 48 months in collaboration with the rheumatology department. None of the 4 knees undergoing surgery experienced recurrence, and the symptoms of the knee treated conservatively resolved spontaneously. In the last follow-up session, MRI and ultrasound showed only mild synovial thickening, with effusion and loose bodies disappearing.ConclusionRice body-like synovitis may be a specific manifestation of JIA. It can be observed in younger children, presenting with insidious symptoms. Arthroscopic debridement combined with post-operative standard medical treatment can offer satisfactory therapeutic outcomes.

Introduction

Rice body-like synovitis, known for its macroscopic rice-like appearance in the joint synovium, is a rare and nonspecific form of arthritis [1]. The exact cause and pathological mechanisms remain unclear. Due to its extremely low incidence, most of the previous studies were case reports, primarily affecting the knee and shoulder joints [2, 3]. Although adults with rice body-like synovitis are often treated with arthroscopic or open surgery [4, 5], there is no consensus on the management of pediatric cases, particularly for very young children less than three years old [3, 10]. This study retrospectively analyzed four pediatric patients (five affected knees) with rice body-like synovitis treated at our hospital. Three knees underwent arthroscopic debridement, while the one bilaterally affected patient only underwent left side surgery, but the contralateral knee was managed conservatively. Histopathological examination of the operated knees was consistent with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). After at least two years of multidisciplinary follow-up with rheumatology and orthopedic specialists, the outcomes were satisfactory. Clinical diagnostic features and treatment experience are summarized in the following sections.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

We retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 4 cases (5 knees) of pediatric knee rice body-like synovitis treated at our hospital from January 2014 to December 2021. The patients included 1 male and 3 females, with an average age of 22.5 months. The affected sides were the left knee in 1 case, the right knee in 2 cases, and the bilateral knees in 1 case. The average duration of symptoms was 1.86 months. The clinical manifestations in all cases were knee swelling and limping. Two cases suffered from mild limitation of passive flexion, but there were no significant symptoms, such as fever and rash. Two cases had elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), 2 cases had increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), 2 cases were positive for antinuclear antibodies (ANA), and none had positive rheumatoid factor (RF) (Table 1).

Surgical methods and postoperative management

All 4 knees underwent arthroscopic debridement, conducted by the same surgeon. Joint fluid aspiration for culture was conducted preoperatively. A 2.5 mm arthroscope, a 2.7 mm disposable shaver, and radiofrequency ablation were employed. The anteromedial, anterolateral, and suprapatellar portals were used to explore and clean the suprapatellar bursa and the posterior joint capsule. No damage to articular cartilage, menisci, or cruciate ligaments was observed during surgery. The synovium showed partial proliferation and edema. Rice bodies were white, rice-like bodies, softer than synovial chondromatosis (Fig. 2a, b). The rice bodies and the proliferated synovium were cleared using the shaver and sent for pathological examination and tissue culture.

One child with bilateral involvement underwent left knee surgery due to mild swelling and no limitation of motion on the right side, which was treated conservatively with medications and monitored.

Joint fluid and tissue cultures were negative. All patients were subjected to 2 weeks of soft plaster immobilization and were instructed to avoid weight-bearing activities.

Postoperative follow-up was conducted by the rheumatology and orthopedic specialists. Based on pathological results and clinical diagnostic criteria [6], all 4 cases were diagnosed with early-onset JIA.

Postoperative medications: All patients were treated with oral ibuprofen (2–3 times daily) combined with methotrexate (once a week). Regular follow-up included blood tests for complete blood count, CRP, and ESR every month, and a knee ultrasound every 3–4 months. MRI of the knee was repeated annually. Ibuprofen was gradually tapered once the clinical symptoms, such as swelling and limited range of motion, improved. Methotrexate was gradually tapered after 1–2 years, once clinical symptoms improved and MRI became normal.

Pathological results

The rice bodies consisted of abundant fibrinoid necrosis, fibrous tissue, small blood vessels, and infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells into the synovium (Fig. 2c).

Postoperative follow-up and evaluation

Follow-up was conducted at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months after discharge. Due to the young age of the children, joint function assessment was challenging; thus, we used swelling and joint range of motion for functional assessment. Joint effusion and rice body resolution were evaluated based on joint ultrasound or MRI. Inflammatory markers, including ESR, CRP, and ANA, were also monitored. The Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score 27 (JADAS 27) was employed to evaluate disease activity. It included active joint count (0–27), physician’s global assessment (using a 10 cm visual analog scale), and parent/child global assessment (using the same 10 cm scale to assess health status), along with laboratory inflammatory markers (usually ESR) [7].

According to the JADAS 27 scoring system, disease activity was classified as follows:

Results

During follow-up, which ranged from 24 to 48 months (mean length: 34.5 months), none of the surgically treated knees showed symptom recurrence. The symptoms resolved in the conservatively treated knee (Fig. 3). Follow-up MRI and ultrasound exhibited significant improvement, including the resolution of joint effusion and rice body-like masses. However, one patient who was ANA-positive showed mild limitations in knee flexion, with a 10° limitation in the range of motion.

Discussion

JIA is a common rheumatic disease among pediatrics; however, in some cases, the absence of typical symptoms and imaging findings makes diagnosis challenging [8]. For the first time, Riese et al. described rice body synovitis in a patient with tuberculous knee arthritis, and it was named for its appearance, which resembled rice grains [9]. The formation mechanism of rice bodies remains unclear. Most reported cases of rice body synovitis suffered from rheumatoid arthritis, but it was also observed in infectious arthritis (non-tuberculous and fungal infections), JIA, and systemic lupus erythematosus [10].

Early-onset JIA, defined as arthritis occurring before three years of age, represents a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by insidious onset, oligoarticular involvement, and higher rates of ANA positivity [11, 21]. However, rice body synovitis occurring in this age group has been rarely reported, and its clinical course and optimal management remain unclear.

This study reports 4 cases of pediatric rice body synovitis, all diagnosed with JIA, thereby offering clinical and therapeutic insights.

Clinical features

Our case series comprised four children under three years of age (female: male ratio 3:1), showing disease onset intervals ranging from 0.5 to 5 months. These cases exhibited characteristically indolent clinical courses with subtle manifestations, predominantly presenting with knee joint effusions and gait disturbances, while notably lacking significant tenderness, cutaneous changes, or febrile responses. The early onset of disease in all cases (< 3 years) is noteworthy, as this age group exhibits the earliest clinical spectrum of JIA. The symptoms are often vague, resulting in delayed recognition and frequent misdiagnosis [11, 21].

These clinical features show remarkable concordance with established phenotypes in the literature. The largest case series of rice body arthritis (n = 24; mean age 6.1 ± 2.4 years; female predominance 17/24) reported analogous presentation patterns, with the knee joint affected in 75% of cases and articular swelling detected in 95.8% of patients [10]. This demographic and clinical profile was further corroborated by an aggregate analysis of five additional case reports (mean age 3.0 ± 1.2 years; female ratio 10/13).

The presentation observed in our series, including monoarticular knee effusion, slow progression, and minimal inflammatory response, reflects the early-onset phenotype typically observed in toddlers and young children [11]. Similarly, data from the Canadian multicenter ReACCh-Out cohort showed 4.3 months (IQR 2.4–7.3 months) of diagnostic delay in early-onset oligoarticular JIA (representing 50% of cases; median onset age 4.6 years), with frequent initial misclassification regarded as benign growth-related pain or post-traumatic arthropathy due to the indolent nature of clinical presentations [12].

In the laboratory, RF was negative in all patients. The 2 patients with positive ANA showed some degree of increase in ESR and CRP, while the 2 ANA-negative patients had normal ESR and CRP levels. This suggests that rice bodies may not be directly associated with ANA, ESR, or CRP levels. Despite the absence of significant laboratory changes, two patients were diagnosed with JIA based on pathological results and clinical diagnostic criteria. These findings are consistent with previous findings showing that early-onset JIA often exhibits minimal systemic inflammation despite active synovial disease [11]. A single-center study of 202 children with JIA found that the positive rates of RF and ANA were relatively low, with only 10.2% of patients being positive for RF and 10.3% being positive for ANA. Notably, RF-negative and ANA-negative patients were observed in both oligoarthritis (single joint involvement) and polyarthritis (multiple joint involvement) subtypes, suggesting that negative RF or ANA results do not rule out JIA [13]. Ma et al. analyzed 120 cases of oligoarticular JIA stratified by the ANA status and found no significant differences in inflammatory marker levels between groups. The mean ESR (25.67 vs. 23.23 mm/h, p = 0.626) and median CRP levels (6 vs. 3 mg/L, p = 0.196) were comparable between ANA-positive and ANA-negative patients [14].

Treatment outcomes

Surgical indications for JIA mainly include joint deformities, limb length discrepancies, or joint replacement in patients with end-stage arthritis [15]. The goal of synovectomy is to reduce the volume of articular inflamed tissues, alleviate swelling, and reduce joint damage. However, the optimal timing and efficacy of this surgical procedure remain controversial [16]. Historically, synovectomy is mostly conducted as an open surgery. Some studies have shown that open synovectomy can significantly alleviate joint pain and swelling and improve joint function during follow-up [15], whereas others reported only partial symptomatic relief without long-term improvement in mobility, and even potential harm in some cases [17, 18].

Arthroscopy provides significant diagnostic value in differentiating other pathologies in pediatric patients with knee synovitis. Compared to traditional open surgery, it is less invasive, allows broader visualization and debridement, and brings a lower risk of postoperative adhesion [19]. In this study, all four patients underwent arthroscopic debridement to remove rice bodies for pathological confirmation and conduct partial synovial debridement to alleviate inflammation. Intraoperatively, rice bodies were observed as white, partially free-floating structures that were morphologically and chromatically distinct from normal synovial tissues. Accordingly, the arthroscopic technique used in this study differed from complete synovectomy, emphasizing selective debridement of rice bodies and visibly inflamed synovium while preserving healthy synovium to maintain joint lubrication and nutrition. This approach reduced the risk of postoperative adhesion and functional limitation. One patient with unilateral involvement exhibited mild flexion limitation (≈ 10°). Possible contributing factors included: (1) very early disease onset (21 months), as early-onset JIA has been linked to less favorable outcomes [20]; (2) the ANA titer was as high as 1:640, indicating strong positivity. Furthermore, elevated levels of ESR and CRP suggested an active disease. Studies have shown that ANA-positive patients often suffer from more severe limitations in joint mobility [11]; (3) the small but recognized risk of postoperative stiffness even with minimally invasive procedures [15]. Therefore, careful selection of surgical indications remains essential for pediatric synovitis.

All four patients were followed by rheumatology and orthopedic specialists and received non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and immunosuppressive agents. Currently, pharmacologic therapy, including NSAIDs, corticosteroids, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biologic agents, remains the cornerstone of JIA management [21]. While arthroscopic synovectomy is increasingly explored for children with oligoarticular JIA, postoperative standardized pharmacological treatment remains an indispensable part of treatment [15, 21, 22, 23].

Conclusion

Rice body-like synovitis in children may represent a specific manifestation of JIA. It typically has a younger age of onset, insidious symptoms, and non-specific laboratory findings, including changes in ANA, ESR, CRP, and RF levels. Arthroscopic technology is mainly used for pathological biopsy and differential diagnosis. Arthroscopic debridement can achieve satisfactory therapeutic results, but further treatment with rheumatology is necessary postoperatively.
