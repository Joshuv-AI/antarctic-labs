Distinctive mortality patterns and the survival paradox in intracerebral haemorrhage subtypes: a 15-year longitudinal study
Authors: Jiann-Der Lee, Chi-Hung Liu, Ting-Yu Chang, Kuo-Lun Huang, Yi-Chun Chou, Tsong-Hai Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe investigated long-term outcomes, survival trajectories and cause-specific mortality in intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) subtypes stratified by aetiology, location and recurrence status.Patients and methodsThis retrospective cohort study analysed 15,186 nontraumatic ICH patients enrolled in the multi-centre Stroke Registry of the Chang-Gung Healthcare System (SRICHS) in Taiwan (2007-2018). Registry data were linked to the Chang Gung Research Databank (clinical data, 2007-2018), the National Health Insurance Research Databank (longitudinal comorbidities, 2007-2022) and the Taiwan Death Registry (cause of death, 2007-2022). Patients were stratified into 4 subtypes: hypertensive (deep) ICH (first-ever = HICH-F and recurrent = HICH-R) and non-hypertensive including lobar ICH (first-ever = NHICH-F and recurrent = NHICH-R).ResultsHypertensive ICH patients had a higher burden of metabolic risk factors compared to NHICH. A distinct “survival paradox” was observed in the NHICH-F group: despite having the highest initial stroke severity and 30-day mortality (22.9%, lobar = 19.5%), survivors demonstrated the best long-term survival probability among all subtypes. Conversely, HICH-F patients had the highest rate of subsequent ICH recurrence (50.5%). Recurrent ICH groups (HICH-R/NHICH-R) had the highest overall mortality (~66%), driven significantly by pneumonia and diabetes. While stroke was the most common primary cause of death across all groups, cancer was the second most common primary cause in first-ever ICH, whereas infectious and metabolic causes drove mortality in recurrent cases.ConclusionLong-term ICH prognosis is highly heterogeneous. First-ever non-hypertensive ICH carries high acute lethality but favourable long-term survival for 30-day survivors. In contrast, hypertensive and recurrent subtypes face a continuous risk of vascular events and comorbidity-related mortality.

Introduction

Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) accounts for a disproportionately high burden of stroke in Asian populations (22%–30%) compared to Western populations (8%–15%).1–4 Despite improvements in acute management, long-term outcomes remain poor, with high rates of mortality and permanent disability.5

Intracerebral haemorrhage is etiologically heterogeneous. While hypertension is the dominant risk factor, non-hypertensive mechanisms—including vascular anomalies and cerebral amyloid angiopathy—represent a distinct pathophysiological group.6 Most epidemiological studies aggregate these subtypes, potentially masking divergent survival trajectories and causes of death. Furthermore, while short-term case fatality is well-documented, data regarding cause-specific mortality spanning over a decade are scarce. Understanding whether a patient is likely to die from recurrent stroke, cancer or infection is crucial for tailoring long-term surveillance and prevention strategies.

This study leveraged a large multi-centre registry linked to national administrative and death registries to characterise the clinical profiles, 15-year survival probabilities and specific causes of death in 15,186 patients. We specifically aimed to compare these outcomes across 4 subtypes defined by hypertensive aetiology, haematoma location and recurrence status to identify distinctive epidemiological patterns.

Patients and methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data linkage across 3 major databases: (1) Stroke Registry of the Chang-Gung Healthcare System (SRICHS): a prospective registry from the largest healthcare system in Taiwan, covering approximately one-third of the population7; (2) Chang Gung Research Databank (CGRD): provides granular inpatient data including laboratory values, vital signs and admission severity scores,8 and was reviewed retrospectively to correct the missing data in SRICHS; (3) National Health Insurance Research Databank (NHIRD) & Taiwan Death Registry (TDR): the NHIRD provided longitudinal comorbidity data, while the TDR provided the date and specific cause of death (ICD-9/10 coded) through 2022.9 The present study was approved by the institutional review board of the National Health Research Institutes (EC1060704-E) and the Ethics Institutional Review Board of Linkou Chang Gung memorial hospital (No. 202101079B0, 2501060072).

Study population

We identified patients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with nontraumatic ICH (ICD-9-CM 431) discharged between 2007 and 2018. Medical records and neuroimaging data of all patients with ICH were retrospectively reviewed by T.Y.C. and K.L.H. for the classification of ICH subtypes. Patients who experienced an aneurysm rupture or vascular anomaly with subarachnoid haemorrhage that resulted in a concurrent intracerebral haemorrhage (coded as 431) were included and classified under the NHICH category. Exclusion criteria included trauma, coagulopathy, neoplasms and haemorrhagic transformation of ischaemic stroke. Patients who died in the hospital after admission to the ward were recorded as mortality at discharge. Those who died in the emergency department before admission to the ward were not included in the study.

Subtype classification

Patients were stratified into hypertensive (HICH) and non-hypertensive (NHICH) aetiologies based on criteria by Qureshi et al.6: (1) HICH: haemorrhage in deep brain regions (basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, cerebellum), which is known to be strongly associated with high blood pressure10,11; (2) NHICH: lobar haemorrhages and ICHs attributed to vascular anomalies (eg, AVM, aneurysm, cavernoma), which may be located in supratentorial or infratentorial compartments, involving either deep or lobar brain regions. It has been reported that in a Chinese cohort, 49% of lobar ICH patients had positive angiographic findings of vascular anomaly,12 and pure intraventricular haemorrhage had high association with vascular anomalies.13 Therefore, both aetiologies were included in the NHICH group.

Patients were further stratified by recurrence status (first-ever vs recurrent). Recurrence was defined as any prior admission for ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke. This resulted in 4 cohorts: hypertensive ICH-first-ever (HICH-F), hypertensive ICH-recurrence (HICH-R), non-hypertensive ICH-first-ever (NHICH-F) and non-hypertensive ICH-recurrence (NHICH-R).

Covariates and severity measures

Baseline characteristics including demographics, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, atrial fibrillation) and lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, betel nut use) were collected from the SRICHS and CGRD. Stroke severity was assessed using the Stroke Severity Index, estimated NIHSS (eNIHSS) according to a previous study14 and Glasgow Coma Scale at admission and mRS at discharge derived from the SRICHS and CGRD. Comorbidity burden was quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI).15

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was recurrence of ICH. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, cause-specific mortality (cardiovascular, cancer, infection, other) and major vascular events (haemorrhagic or occlusive). All-cause mortality and major comorbidities were defined according to our previous NHIRD study (Table S1A).9 Cause of death was determined via the TDR. Deaths were categorised temporally: ≤ 30 days, 31–365 days, 366–1,825, 1,826–3,650 and > 3,650 days.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests and categorical variables using chi-square tests. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Mortality rates were calculated using life table analysis. Multivariable adjustments for survival analysis were performed controlling for age, sex, year of enrolment and relevant comorbidities (CCI, eNIHSS). Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0.

The final cohort comprised 15,186 patients (Figure 1). Hypertensive ICH-first-ever was the largest subgroup (n = 7,043), followed by NHICH-F (n = 5,689). Hypertensive ICH patients were predominantly male and had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia compared to NHICH patients (Table 1).

A gradient in age and lifestyle factors was observed: First-ever ICH patients were younger and had higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption compared to recurrent patients. Notably, the NHICH-F group contained the highest proportion of young adults (≤40 years: 12.4%, Table S1) yet presented with the highest admission stroke severity (mean eNIHSS 14.84) and worst discharge functional status (mean mRS 4.43).

Acute clinical profile

Hypertensive ICH patients presented with significantly higher systolic blood pressure (mean 171 mmHg in HICH-F). Laboratory analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles; HICH patients had higher lipid panels (total cholesterol, LDL, triglyceride) and uric acid levels. In contrast, NHICH-F patients presented with higher inflammatory markers (WBC count) (Table S1).

Mortality and the survival paradox

During the follow-up (2007-2022), 52.1% of the cohort died. We observed a “survival paradox” regarding aetiology (Figure 2; Table 2). In the acute phase, NHICH-F patients had the highest in-hospital mortality rate (22.9%) and 30-day mortality (22.9%), driven by high initial stroke severity. The first-ever lobar ICH (lobar-F) group had a high in-hospital mortality rate (19.0%) and 30-day mortality (19.5%) but low initial stroke severity. These acute-phase mortality rates of lobar-F fall between those of HICH-F (18.23%, 18.65%) and NHICH-F (22.94%, 22.94%). However, lobar-F has higher overall mortality rate (57.34%) than HICH-F (51.31%) and NHICH (47.07%). In the chronic phase, NHICH-F survivors demonstrated the best long-term survival probability. In contrast, HICH-R patients had the lowest 30-day mortality (17.0%) but the highest late mortality (>1 year: 38.4%).

Causes of death and recurrence

Stroke remained the leading cause of death across all subtypes and time periods. However, the second most common primary cause of death diverged significantly (Table 2). In first-ever ICH, cancer was the second most common primary cause of death. In recurrent ICH, pneumonia and diabetes-related complications surpassed cancer as the second most common primary cause. Hypertensive ICH-first-ever patients had the highest rate of subsequent ICH recurrence (50.5%), nearly double that of NHICH-F patients (25.8%) (Table S1).

Comparison with literature

We compared our findings with 31 prior epidemiological studies (Table 3). The pooled data reflected that Asian ICH patients had a higher proportion of males (62.3%) but lower prevalence of hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia compared to non-Asian cohorts. The cumulative 1-month, 1-year and 5-year case fatality rates are detailed in Table 3. Notably, the 1-month and 1-year case fatality rates in Asian cohorts were generally lower than those reported in Western cohorts. Additionally, the 1-month and 1-year fatality rates for lobar ICH in our cohort were much lower than those reported in US studies.

Discussion

This study utilised linked registry and national administrative data to provide a comprehensive 15-year longitudinal analysis of ICH. The stratification by aetiology, location and recurrence reveals distinct epidemiological trajectories that are obscured in aggregate analyses.

The “survival paradox”

The most striking finding is the survival pattern of NHICH-F. These patients face the highest acute-phase mortality, likely due to larger haematoma volumes or intraventricular extension associated with vascular anomalies.6 However, those who survive the acute phase have the best long-term prognosis. This paradox suggests that mortality in this group is driven by the acute lesion; once the lesion is stabilised or treated, these patients—who are generally younger and have less systemic “vascular rot” (lower rates of diabetes and renal disease)—have a life expectancy approaching the general population. This supports aggressive acute care for NHICH patients especially for those with vascular anomalies, as the long-term payoff for survivors is substantial.

The burden of recurrence and comorbidity

Conversely, HICH patients face a relentless trajectory of recurrence. The 50.5% recurrence rate in HICH-F confirms that hypertensive small-vessel disease is a progressive, diffuse pathology. For patients with recurrent ICH (HICH-R/NHICH-R), the cause of death shifts significantly towards pneumonia and metabolic failure. This indicates that long-term survival in recurrent stroke is threatened as much by immobility-related complications (aspiration pneumonia) and frailty as by the cerebrovascular event itself. While previous literature16,17 suggests a higher recurrence risk in lobar ICH due to underlying cerebral amyloid angiopathy, our cohort demonstrated higher recurrence in the hypertensive group. This discrepancy may be attributed to the younger demographic of our non-hypertensive cohort and the high prevalence of secondary vascular aetiologies rather than pure cerebral amyloid angiopathy which typically carries a higher recurrence burden.

Epidemiological context

Our data reinforce distinct Asian epidemiological patterns, specifically the younger age of onset but lower prevalence of vascular risk factors compared to Western cohorts (Table 3). The high rates of smoking (31.2%) and alcohol use (23.7%) in Asian ICH cohort (Table 3) highlight a critical, modifiable target for primary prevention in this population.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the large sample size and the robustness of the data linkage, which minimised loss to follow-up for mortality outcomes. Limitations include first, the reliance on ICD codes for comorbidity and aetiology classification without universal MRI or angiography confirmation for cerebral amyloid angiopathy. However, the location-based stratification is a validated proxy in epidemiological research. Secondly, as a hospital-based registry, referral bias is possible, though the large catchment area mitigates this. Thirdly, the SRICHS registry only captures stroke patients who survive to hospital admission, allowing for thorough evaluation of ICH aetiology and risk factors in the ICU or ward. Consequently, patients who died in the emergency department were not registered. Excluding these early-mortality patients likely underestimates the true case fatality rate of ICH in our cohort. Fourth, SRICHS followed the registry guidelines of “Get With The Guidelines-Stroke in Taiwan,”18 and data regarding exact dosages, duration and specific categories of prior antithrombotic use before ICH onset were unavailable. Previous studies in Japan19 and Denmark20 reported antithrombotic use in deep ICH patients could increase in-hospital and 90-day mortality, respectively. Our study showed HICH-F had a higher rate of atrial fibrillation/flutter and HICH is a hypertensive small vessel disease, so were presumably more likely to be on antithrombotics before ICH onset. However, NHICH-F still exhibited higher in-hospital and 30-day mortality. This suggests that prior antithrombotic use, while prognostic, may not completely explain the mortality differences observed among ICH subtypes.

Conclusion

Nontraumatic ICH is not a monolithic disease entity. Classifying patients by aetiology, location and recurrence reveals divergent prognoses. The “survival paradox” dictates that acute care for NHICH patients should be aggressive, as long-term survival is superior. Conversely, the care of hypertensive and recurrent patients requires a shift towards holistic chronic disease management to prevent recurrence and non-vascular deaths like pneumonia.
