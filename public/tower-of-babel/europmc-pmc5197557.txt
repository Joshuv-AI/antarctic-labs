A NOTE ON THE PARASITE OF " DER/
MAL LEISHMANOID." j
By Assistant Surgeon B. M. DAS GUPTA, ^
Assistant Professor of Protozoology, Calcutta School
of Tropical Medicine.
The condition known as " dermal leishma-
noid," first described by Brahmachari (1922),
has now become a commonplace at the Cal-
cutta School of Tropical Medicine. Every
month some three or four such cases are seen
in Major Acton's skin outpatient clinic at the
School and are sent to the Protozoology
Department for examination.
The history of t'hese patients is a typical
one. In the great majority of cases they
have suffered from kala-azar for which they
have been successfully treated with antimony
injections, with complete recovery. Some
months?or more usually years?later tiny
leucodermic spots appear on the skin, especi-
ally of the face, forearms, trunk, and in the
case of the male of the scrotum. This is
followed by the development of soft non-
ulcerative granulomatous lesions. The final
appearance of the patient is often suggestive
of nodular leprosy, whilst one recent case?
illustrated in the Annual Report of the Calcutta
School of Tropical Medicine for 1925?'simu-
lated xanthoma tuberosum multiplex, and the
condition had lasted for sixteen years.
In a few instances no history of previous
kala-azar can be obtained, but all the patients
come from kala-azar endemic areas. Apart
from the skin lesions their general health is
normal, and the laboratory finding's are al-
ways t'he same. Films from the lesions show
leishmania, sometimes in very considerable
numbers, sometimes in only scanty numbers,
whilst cultures from the lesions give a rich
growth of the herpetomonad form of Leish-
mania. Cultures from the patient's peri-
pheral blood for leishmania are invariably
negative, and on those occasions when an
enlarged spleen permitted spleen puncture,
films and cultures of spleen juice have in-
variably given a negative result. The condi-
tion thus appears to be one of purely cuta-
neous leishmaniasis, associated with no vis-
ceralised infection.
Is the parasite which causes these lesions
Leishmania donovani or Leishmania tropical
The whole trend of the clinical evidence
goes to show that it is Leishmania donovani.
The association of the disease with kala-azar.
and the distribution of the lesions on the body
are evidence for this. The lesions themselves
are of a proliferative granulomatous character
with no tendency to atrophy of the skin or
to ulceration?(except in some instances in
the scrotum where irritation has occurred
'/ from the clothing, with secondary sepsis).
The following experiments in connection
with one of these patients shew that t'he
leishmania parasite concerned in this infection
is capable of causing a generalised, visceral
infection.
The patient, a Hindu male, aged 27 years
of age, was well nourished and had no enlarge-
ment of the spleen or liver. He had kala-
azar three years ago, was treated with anti-
mony injections, and made a complete
recovery.
On examination, small leucodermic spots
were present on t'he forehead and chin, and
a few small nodules on the left ear. The
duration of the condition was about two
months.
One of the nodules was painted with tinc-
ture of iodine and then with alcohol, and a
tiny piece of the growth snipped off with fine
scissors and placed in a sterile Petri dish.
This was next cut into three bits. Two
were sown direct into the water of condensa-
tion of NNN medium, whilst films were prepared
from the third piece. Scanty leishmania were
found in the films.
The NNN cultures were inciihated at
22?C., and shewed no growth on the fourth
day. On the twelfth day, however, both cul-
tures shewed positive results. Films were

prepared, fixed over osmic-acetic acid vapour
and stained with dilute Giemsa's stain (Shortt's
technique, 1923). Many flagellates were found,
both free and in rosettes, together with some
rounded forms shewing badly staining nuclei and
probably degenerating.
Next day the water of condensation of one
of the tubes was diluted with a little citrated
saline, aspirated into a hypodermic syringe,
and the material injected intraperitoneally
into two (English) white mice.
One of the mice was killed 94 days later.
Its spleen was found to be enormously
enlarged, t'he other organs being apparently
normal. A culture was taken from its spleen
by the following technique :?
The body of the animal is placed on its
back on a tray of paraffin wax, the surface
of which has been previously washed with
lysol, and the legs fastened down wit'h pins.
The fur of the chest and abdomen is cut
short and the skin thoroughly painted with tinc-
ture of iodine. The abdomen is opened with
fine scissors and an assistant holds aside the
abdominal walls with sterile forceps. With
fresh fine sterile scissors and forceps tiny
pieces of the spleen and liver are snipped off
and immediately dropped into the water of
condensation of tubes of NNN medium.
Prior to using this technique I have twice
tried the method recommended by Christo-
phers, Shortt and Barraud (1926) of grinding
and emulsifying the tissue in saline and
inoculating the NNN tubes with the emulsion,
but on both occasions all the cultures taken became
contaminated. With my present technique con-
tamination has never occurred.
T'he cultures from the spleen and liver of
this mouse showed scanty flagellates present
on the 18th and 19t'h days, together with a
large number of aflagellate forms. In sub-
culture there was a fairly rich growth, and
the strain has been maintained by sub-culture.
Smears were also taken from the heart
blood, spleen, liver, and bone marrow, but
showed no parasites. The films of the heart
blood showed marked basophilia, but t'his is
not an abnormal finding in the white mouse.
The second mouse died at night, 95 days
after inoculation, and was not examined, as
its organs were decomposed the next morn-
ing.
The fact that the Leishmania parasite con-
cerned in dermal leishmanoid is thus capable
of producing a visceralised infection in a
susceptible animal goes to show that it is
L. donovani, and not L. tropica.
