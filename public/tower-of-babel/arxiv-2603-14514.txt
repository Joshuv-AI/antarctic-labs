High-Probability Bounds for SGD under the Polyak-Łojasiewicz Condition with Markovian Noise Thanks: Corresponding Authors:
Avik Kar, Siddharth Chandak, Rahul Singh, Eric Moulines, Shalabh Bhatnagar, Nicholas Bambos

Abstract
We present the first uniform-in-time high-probability bound for SGD under the PL condition, where the gradient noise contains both Markovian and martingale difference components. This significantly broadens the scope of finite-time guarantees, as the PŁ condition arises in many machine learning and deep learning models while Markovian noise naturally arises in decentralized optimization and online system identification problems. We further allow the magnitude of noise to grow with the function value, enabling the analysis of many practical sampling strategies. In addition to the high-probability guarantee, we establish a matching 1 / k 1/k decay rate for the expected suboptimality. Our proof technique relies on the Poisson equation to handle the Markovian noise and a probabilistic induction argument to address the lack of almost-sure bounds on the objective. Finally, we demonstrate the applicability of our framework by analyzing three practical optimization problems: token-based decentralized linear regression, supervised learning with subsampling for privacy amplification, and online system identification.

Introduction

We consider the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm for solving the unconstrained optimization problem
\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
. Given a stepsize (learning rate) sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, the SGD iteration is as follows.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}G_{k},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,

where
G_{k}
is a noisy estimate of
\nabla f(x_{k})
. In this work, we analyze the finite-time behaviour of the iterates, in particular the suboptimality gap of the iterates,
f(x_{k})-f^{\star}
, where
f^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f(x)}
. SGD is widely used to solve optimization problems in machine learning due to its simplicity and scalability in high-dimensional settings. Over the past two decades, it has played a central role in numerous successful machine learning applications. Much of the practical success of modern machine learning can be attributed to SGD and its variants. Although most theoretical analyses of SGD are conducted under the assumption of convexity of the objective, most optimization tasks in machine learning, including neural network training, are non-convex.

Many relevant non-convex optimization problems in machine learning satisfy the PŁ condition
[
28
,
15
]
. The objective function
f
is said to satisfy the PŁ condition with constant
\mu>0
(or “
f
is
\mu
-PŁ”) if

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\geq 2\mu\left(f(x)-f^{\star}\right),\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1)

The PŁ condition guarantees global linear convergence of gradient descent
[
15
]
. Several important problem classes satisfy the PŁ condition. For example, the class of “strongly convex composed with linear” functions, where the objective takes the form
f(x)=g(Ax)
for a strongly convex function
g
and matrix
A
satisfies the PŁ condition
[
15
]
. An important problem instance in this category is the least-squares problem (i.e.,
f(x)=\|Ax-b\|^{2}
). The PŁ condition also holds for logistic regression when the gradient descent iterates remain in a compact set, even though the loss function is strictly convex rather than strongly convex
[
15
]
. It also arises in control and reinforcement learning problems; for instance, the objective function associated with the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem satisfies the PŁ condition
[
10
]
. The PŁ condition has also been established in several modern machine learning models.
[
20
]
showed that wide neural networks satisfy PŁ condition on a set of radius
R
, and
R
is proportional to the width of the neural network. Similar results hold for wide convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and residual neural networks (ResNet)
[
20
]
.

A significant limitation of much existing finite-time analysis of SGD is the assumption that the noise process forms a martingale difference sequence. In many applications, however, samples are generated along a Markov process, leading to temporally correlated noise. Such settings arise naturally in decentralized optimization, privacy-preserving learning, reinforcement learning, and stochastic control.

For example, in token-based algorithms for decentralized optimization
[
9
]
, a token carrying the current iterate performs a random walk over a network of agents, and the sequence of nodes visited forms a Markov chain. In differential privacy, certain subsampling mechanisms used for privacy amplification generate minibatches whose evolution can be modeled as a Markov chain
[
7
,
3
,
4
]
. Markovian data also appears in online system identification, where observations are generated by a linear dynamical system
[
18
]
. Additionally, Markov chain sampling methods are commonly used when direct sampling from a complex target distribution is computationally expensive, and the resulting gradient estimates inherit the temporal dependence of the chain
[
31
]
.

1.1
Our Contributions

To address these limitations, we obtain both high-probability and expected bounds on the suboptimality gap of SGD iterates when the objective satisfies the PŁ condition and the gradient noise contains both Markovian and martingale difference components. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We establish the first uniform-in-time high-probability bound on the suboptimality gap of SGD iterates under the PŁ condition with Markovian gradient noise. In particular, we show that with probability at least
1-\delta
, the suboptimality gap decays as
\mathcal{O}\big(t_{\text{mix}}^{2}\log\left(k/\delta\right)/k\big)
for all
k\geq 0
, where
t_{mix}
denotes the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain.

•

By working under the general ABC condition
[
16
]
, we allow the gradient noise to grow with both the value of the loss function and the true gradient, enabling the analysis of practical subsampling strategies.

•

Under this general setup, no almost-sure bound exists for the loss function values, which prevents the direct application of standard concentration inequalities such as the Azuma-Hoeffding bound
[
32
]
. To address this challenge and obtain uniform-in-time guarantees, we introduce a novel technique based on defining a
good event

E_{k}
and inductively establishing high-probability bounds on the loss function. This approach is simple and intuitive, and may be useful in other optimization and stochastic approximation settings.

•

We also show that the expected suboptimality gap is
\mathcal{O}\!\left(t_{mix}/k\right)
for all
k\geq 0
.

•

In
Section

5
, we study three optimization problems that fit within our framework: token-based decentralized linear regression, supervised learning with subsampling for privacy amplification, and online system identification.

1.2
Related Works

SGD was first introduced in
[
17
]
as a stochastic approximation
[
30
]
scheme. Since then, it has become a central tool in optimization and machine learning. Due to its wide applicability, numerous works have studied its convergence guarantees. The almost sure convergence of SGD iterates to the set of stationary points was first established in
[
22
]
. Over the past two decades, significant effort has been devoted to deriving finite-time guarantees for SGD; see, for example,
[
26
,
12
,
33
,
34
]
.

The PŁ condition first appeared in
[
28
]
, while the term
Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) condition
was popularized by
[
15
]
, which also presented a systematic study of optimization algorithms under this condition. Most works studying SGD under the PŁ condition derive bounds on the expected suboptimality of the iterates. While
[
15
]
assumes bounded variance of the gradient noise,
[
19
,
16
]
allow the noise variance to grow with the function value and derive an
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate. Beyond expected bounds,
[
23
]
obtains a high-probability bound when the noise sequence is sub-Weibull with bounded variance, and
[
14
]
derives an asymptotic almost sure convergence rate.

In all the works discussed above, the gradient estimate is assumed to be unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}\mid x_{\ell},\ell\leq k\right]=\nabla f(x_{k})
. To the best of our knowledge, existing works studying SGD under Markov noise derive bounds only on the expected suboptimality; see
[
31
,
5
,
6
,
9
]
. Among these, SGD under the PŁ condition is considered in
[
6
,
9
]
.

In contrast to the above works, we derive both high-probability and in-expectation bounds on the suboptimality of SGD iterates when the objective satisfies the
\mu
-PŁ condition and the noise contains both Markovian and martingale difference components.

2
Problem Formulation

In this section, we set up the notation and state the assumptions for the problem setting. Consider the iteration

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}G_{k},

(2)

for
k=0,1,\ldots
. Here
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the iterate, and
G_{k}
a noisy estimate of the gradient
\nabla f(x_{k})
at instant
k
, where the objective function
f(\cdot)
satisfies the PŁ condition (
1
). The noise in the gradient estimate
G_{k}
has two components: a Markovian component and a martingale difference component. We now state additional assumptions on the objective function and formalize the class of admissible noise sequences.

Our first assumption is that the objective function is
L
-smooth, meaning its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz. This is a standard assumption in the optimization literature
[
26
,
29
]
and is essential for establishing convergence guarantees on SGD.

Assumption 1
.

The function
f
is
L
-smooth, i.e., there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|,\penalty\ \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3)

A result of this assumption is that
\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\leq 2L\left(f(x)-f^{\star}\right)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see Lemma
41
). This is often referred to as the descent lemma for SGD iterations with constant stepsizes
[
27
]
. Hence, if
f
is both
\mu
-PŁ (
1
) and
L
-smooth (
3
), then
\mu\leq L
.

The noisy gradient
G_{k}
is defined as

\displaystyle G_{k}\coloneqq g(x_{k},Z_{k})+M_{k+1},\penalty\ \forall k\in\{0,1,\ldots\},

(4)

where
g(x_{k},Z_{k})
depends on the current iterate
x_{k}
and the state of a Markov chain
\{Z_{k}\}
.
\{M_{k}\}
forms a martingale difference sequence and each
M_{k+1}
is allowed to depend on the current iterate
x_{k}
. Our next assumption formalizes this.

Assumption 2
.

Define the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}
where
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\sigma\left(x_{0},M_{\ell},Z_{\ell},\ell\leq k\right)
,
k\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
. Then
\{M_{k}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[|M_{k}|]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
.

We next assume the well-known
ABC
condition on noisy gradient samples
[
16
]
.

Assumption 3
.

There exist constants
A,B,C\geq 0
such that for all
k
,

\displaystyle\left\|G_{k}\right\|^{2}\leq A\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|^{2}+B\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)+C,\quad a.s.

(5)

To derive bounds on the expected suboptimality, it is sufficient to assume a bound only on the conditional second moment of the noisy gradient; see, for example,
[
12
,
16
,
19
]
. This motivates the relaxed assumption used in our result on expected suboptimality (Assumption
8
). However, such a conditional expectation bound is generally insufficient for establishing high-probability guarantees. A standard assumption for high-probability analysis is the almost-sure bound stated above. For instance,
[
21
]
assumes a bound of this form to derive asymptotic convergence rates. We emphasize that the function value or its gradient may be unbounded, and consequently, the noise may also be unbounded. The above assumption can be relaxed by instead assuming that
G_{k}
is sub-Gaussian, with variance controlled by the same
ABC
structure. Our analysis extends to this relaxed setting without any significant modification of the proof technique. Since our primary focus is on handling Markovian noise, we omit these technical extensions. We formalize the Markov noise in the next assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The process
\{Z_{k}\}
is a time-homogeneous Markov chain on a compact measurable space
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}})
with initial distribution
\pi_{0}
and transition kernel
p
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}\in B)=\pi_{0}(B)
, and for every
k\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{k+1}\in B\mid Z_{\ell},\ell\leq k)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{k+1}\in B\mid Z_{k})=p(B\mid Z_{k}),\penalty\ \forall B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}}.

The Markov chain has a unique invariant distribution
\pi
. Moreover, it is geometrically ergodic with mixing time
t_{\text{mix}}\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, i.e., for every
k\geq 0

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}{\|p^{\left(k\right)}(\cdot\mid z)-\pi\|_{TV}}\leq 2^{-\left\lfloor k/t_{\text{mix}}\right\rfloor},

(6)

where
p^{\left(k\right)}
denotes the
k
-step transition kernel induced by
p
. Finally, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\int{g(x,z)\penalty\ \pi(dz)}.

Throughout the paper, we use the following definition of total variation norm
[
11
]
. Let
\xi
be a signed measure on
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}})
with Jordan decomposition
\xi=\xi_{+}-\xi_{-}
. Then,
\left\|\xi\right\|_{TV}:=\left|\xi\right|(\mathcal{Z})
. The above assumption is stated in a general form to accommodate both finite-state and continuous-state Markov chains. In the finite-state case, irreducibility and aperiodicity imply geometric mixing. In the continuous-state case,
\mathcal{Z}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where geometric mixing can hold under standard regularity conditions. By directly assuming uniform geometric ergodicity in total variation, we avoid imposing separate structural assumptions for discrete and continuous models. The last part of the assumption states that the true gradient is the stationary expectation of the gradient samples generated by the Markov chain.

The following assumption imposes Lipschitz and boundedness conditions on the gradient samples corrupted by Markovian noise.

Assumption 5
.

The map
g(\cdot,z)
is
L_{g}
-Lipschitz for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\|g(x,z)-g(y,z)\right\|\leq L_{g}\left\|x-y\right\|,\penalty\ \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(7)

Additionally,

\displaystyle\left\|g(x,z)\right\|^{2}\leq A\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}+B(f(x)-f^{\star})+C,\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},z\in\mathcal{Z}.

(8)

We use the same
A,B,
and
C
here as in Assumption
3
for simplicity.

3
Results

In this section, we present the key high-probability and expectation bounds on the suboptimality of the SGD iterates. For convenience, we denote the suboptimality by
\Delta_{k}
, i.e.,
\Delta_{k}=f(x_{k})-f^{\star}
. We consider the stepsize

\alpha_{k}=\frac{a}{k+K_{0}},

where
a\geq 2/\mu
and
K_{0}>0
. This choice yields the optimal convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
and is standard in the analysis of stochastic approximation and SGD. Our analysis can also be extended to stepsizes of the form
\Theta(1/k^{b})
for
b\in(0.5,1]
, which yields a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{b})
. We now present our main result: a high-probability bound on the suboptimality of iterates generated by the SGD iteration (
2
).

Theorem 6
.

Let the objective function
f(\cdot)
be
\mu
-PŁ (
1
) and
L
-smooth (Assumption
1
). Suppose the noisy gradient samples satisfy Assumptions
2
–
5
. Then for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
\mathfrak{c}_{1}=\Omega\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
such that for
K_{0}\geq\mathfrak{c}_{1}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{2}\log{\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}}{\mu^{2}k}\right)\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

An outline of the proof for
Theorem

6
is given in
Section

4
. The complete proof, including explicit constants and a fully specified (non-orderwise) version of the result, is presented in Appendix
A
. The above bound matches the optimal
1/k
decay rate and the
\delta
dependence obtained for unbiased noise with bounded second moment
[
23
]
. The quadratic dependence on
t_{mix}
appears because of the decomposition of Markov noise into a martingale difference component, followed by an application of the Azuma-Hoeffding bound. In contrast, the expected suboptimality depends only linearly on
t_{mix}
, leaving open the question of whether the same linear dependence can be achieved in the high-probability bound.

High-Probability guarantees in absence of Markov noise:
For completeness, we also present a special case where the Markovian noise is absent, i.e., the noisy gradient samples are given by
G_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})+M_{k+1}
. This corresponds to the standard martingale-difference noise model widely studied in stochastic optimization. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work establishing high-probability guarantees for SGD under the PŁ condition with the
ABC
noise structure. The closest related work is
[
23
]
; however, unlike
[
23
]
, we allow the noise magnitude to scale with the gradient, and our proof technique is substantially different.

Theorem 7
.

Let the objective function
f(\cdot)
be
\mu
-PŁ (
1
) and
L
-smooth (Assumption
1
). Suppose the noisy gradient samples are free of Markov noise, i.e.,
G_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})+M_{k+1}
for all
k=0,1,\ldots
, and satisfy Assumptions
2
and
3
. Then for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
\mathfrak{c}_{2}=\Omega\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
such that for
K_{0}\geq\mathfrak{c}_{2}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log{\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}}{\mu^{2}k}\right)\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

The proof follows the same outline as Theorem
6
and is presented in Appendix
D
.

Bound in expectation:
We now present a bound on the expected suboptimality of the SGD iterates. As discussed after Assumption
2
, we do not need an almost sure bound on the noise to obtain bounds in expectation. Instead, it suffices to impose the following relaxed condition on the conditional second moment of the noisy gradients.

Assumption 8
.

There exist constants
A,B,C\geq 0
such that for all
k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|G_{k}\right\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq A\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|^{2}+B\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)+C,\mbox{ almost surely.}

(9)

We have the following result on the expected suboptimality of iterates.

Theorem 9
.

Let the objective function
f(\cdot)
be
\mu
-PŁ (
1
) and
L
-smooth (Assumption
1
). Suppose the noisy gradient samples satisfy Assumptions
2
,
8
,
4
,
5
. Then there exists constant
\mathfrak{c}_{3}
such that for
K_{0}\geq\mathfrak{c}_{3}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}}{\mu^{2}k}\right)\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

The proof of this theorem is presented in Appendix

4
Proof Outline

In this section, we outline the proofs for Theorems
6
and
9
. The complete proofs are provided in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
, respectively. In addition to standard arguments used in analyzing SGD under the PŁ condition, the proof relies on solutions to the Poisson equation to handle the Markovian noise
[
25
]
. We also introduce a
probabilistic induction
argument, which enables the application of concentration inequalities despite the absence of an almost-sure bound on the iterates.

The noisy gradient sample observed at every time
k
can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle G_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})+(g(x_{k},Z_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k}))+M_{k+1}.

Here
\nabla f(x_{k})
is the true noiseless gradient, and
M_{k+1}
is the martingale difference noise. The term
(g(x_{k},Z_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k}))
represents the Markovian noise. To handle this error term, we define the following solutions of the Poisson equation.

\displaystyle V(x,z)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{(g(x,Z_{\ell})-\nabla f(x))}\mid Z_{0}=z\right].

(10)

The following lemma states that
V(x,z)
is a solution of the required Poisson equation.

Lemma 10
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
V(x,z)
satisfies the following.

\displaystyle g(x,z)-\nabla f(x)=V(x,z)-\int{V(x,z^{\prime})p(dz^{\prime}\mid z)}.

(11)

The above lemma allows us to decompose the Markovian noise as follows:

\displaystyle g(x_{k},Z_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})

\displaystyle=V(x_{k},Z_{k})-\int{V(x_{k},z)p(dz\mid Z_{k})}=\widetilde{M}_{k+1}-d_{k},

where,

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq V(x_{k},Z_{k+1})-\int{V(x_{k},z)p(dz\mid Z_{k})},

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq V(x_{k},Z_{k+1})-V(x_{k},Z_{k}).

Note that
\{\widetilde{M}_{k}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
,
\forall k
.
The term
\widetilde{M}_{k+1}
can be combined with
M_{k+1}
and handled together. Hence, the SGD iteration (
2
) can be rewritten as,

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}(\nabla f(x_{k})+M_{k+1}+\widetilde{M}_{k+1}-d_{k}),\penalty\ k=0,1,\ldots.

Thus, the Poisson equation cleanly separates the Markovian noise into a martingale difference component and an additional correction term that can be shown to have a negligible effect on the rate.

For ease of notation, we define

\displaystyle\zeta_{m,n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{j=m}^{n}{\left(1-\mu\alpha_{j}\right)},\mbox{ for natural numbers }m\leq n.

(12)

The following lemma presents an intermediate bound on
\Delta_{k}
.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
6
holds. Then for all
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}\zeta_{0,k-1}+\frac{CL}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}{\alpha_{\ell}^{2}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}{\alpha_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{\ell}),\,M_{\ell+1}+\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{\ell}),\,d_{\ell}\right\rangle.

(13)

The term
\Delta_{0}\zeta_{0,k-1}+\frac{CL}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell}^{2}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}
can be bounded using standard properties of the stepsize sequence. The last term above can also be bounded using the properties of the solution of the Poisson equation (
10
) and using straightforward algebraic manipulations. It therefore remains to analyze the martingale term

\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\langle\nabla f(x_{\ell}),M_{\ell+1}+\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}\rangle.
To obtain a bound on the expected suboptimality gap, we take conditional expectations with respect to the sigma-fields
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
. Using the martingale difference property of
M_{\ell+1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}
, each term in this summation has zero conditional expectation, which yields the result in Theorem
9
.

To obtain high-probability bounds, one would typically apply concentration inequalities such as the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality to this term. However, in our setting, we do not have a deterministic almost-sure bound on the martingale, which prevents a direct application of such inequalities. To address this issue, we use a
probabilistic induction
approach.

We first explain the argument informally to build intuition. Suppose that up to time
\ell
the iterates behave well, meaning that the suboptimality gap has been decaying at the desired rate; we denote this event by
E_{\ell}
. On this event, the martingale increments
M_{\ell+1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}
can be shown to be bounded. Consequently, the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality can be applied to control the stochastic term in the recursion, which implies that the desired bound on the suboptimality gap continues to hold at time
\ell+1
. Thus, if
E_{\ell}
holds, the event
E_{\ell+1}
also holds with high probability. The formal argument below implements this idea by defining the good events
E_{k}
and controlling the associated martingale terms.

Define the following
good
event for
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle E_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{K_{0}\Delta_{0}+\mathbbm{1}\left(k\neq 0\right)\left(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}\log{\left(\frac{2K_{0}}{\delta}\right)}+\Gamma_{2}\log{\left(\frac{2k}{\delta}\right)}\right)}{k+K_{0}}\right\}.

(14)

Here
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are constants defined in (
26
) and (
27
), respectively. We also define the sequence
\{\overline{M}_{\ell}\}
as follows:

\overline{M}_{\ell+1}\coloneqq-\alpha_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\langle\nabla f(x_{\ell}),M_{\ell+1}+\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}\rangle\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{\ell}\right).

(15)

Note that
\{\overline{M}_{\ell}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\}
. The only difference in the definition of
\overline{M}_{\ell+1}
and
-\alpha_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\langle\nabla f(x_{\ell}),\,M_{\ell+1}+\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}\rangle
appearing in Lemma
11
is the multiplication with the indicator
\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{\ell}\right)
. On the event
E_{\ell}
, the suboptimality gap is bounded, which implies that function value and the gradient are bounded. Consequently, under the ABC condition, the noise terms are also bounded. Hence,
\overline{M}_{\ell+1},l\geq 0
, has an almost sure bound, which allows us to apply the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality on the martingale
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\overline{M}_{\ell+1},k\geq 1
.

It remains to relate the resulting high-probability bound on
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\overline{M}_{\ell+1}
to the event that the suboptimality gap
\Delta_{k}
decays at the desired rate. The constants
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are chosen precisely so that this connection holds. Observe that our desired result is equivalent to obtaining an upper bound on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}\bar{E}_{k}\right)
, where
\bar{E}_{k}
denotes the complement of
E_{k}
. The following lemma provides the key relation needed.

Lemma 12
.

If the setting of Theorem
6
holds, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}{\bar{E}_{k}}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\overline{M}_{\ell+1}>\frac{\Gamma_{2}\log{\left(\frac{2k}{\delta}\right)}}{k+K_{0}}\right\}\right)}.

The above lemma, together with an application of the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality, completes the proof of Theorem
6
.

The proof of Theorem
7
follows the same steps as that of Theorem
6
, except that the terms
\widetilde{M}_{\ell+1}
and
d_{\ell}
are absent in this case, which simplifies the analysis.

In this section, we discuss three applications of the SGD algorithm when the objective function is
\mu
-PŁ and the gradient is corrupted by Markov noise. We provide high-probability and in-expectation bounds on the values of the corresponding objective functions of the SGD iterates. The proofs of the propositions in this section are deferred to Appendix

5.1
Token Algorithm for Decentralized Linear Regression

Consider a network consisting of
M
agents (nodes). Each node
i
for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
locally stores dataset
(\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)},\mathbf{b}^{\left(i\right)})
, where
\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(i\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
denote the design matrix and the corresponding target vector, respectively. Here,
N_{i}
is the number of samples available at node
i
, and
d
is the feature dimension. Let
\theta
denote the parameter vector to be learned. The local objective function at the
i
th node is defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta;i):=\frac{1}{2N_{i}}\left\|\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)}\theta-\mathbf{b}^{\left(i\right)}\right\|^{2}.

The global objective is to minimize

\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2N}\left\|\mathbf{A}\theta-\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are the global design matrix and target, respectively, obtained by stacking all
\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)}
’s and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(i\right)}
’s, and
N:=N_{1}+N_{2}+\ldots+N_{M}
. By expanding the squared norm, one can show that
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{q_{i}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta;i)}
, where
q_{i}=N_{i}/N
. Note that
\mathcal{L}
is not strongly convex if
\mathbf{A}\theta=\mathbf{b}
has infinitely many solutions, as is commonly encountered in fields where obtaining samples is expensive and features are high-dimensional, for example, statistical genomics
[
24
]
.

Decentralized algorithms are often preferred in settings where data is privacy-sensitive, such as individuals’ health or financial records. A commonly used approach in such scenarios is the
token algorithm

[
13
]
. In this framework, a token traverses the network of nodes according to a Markov chain
\{Z_{k}\}
defined over the set of nodes, while carrying the current iterate. The movement is governed by a transition kernel
p
, which is designed in such a way that its unique invariant distribution is
\pi=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{M})
. At time
k
, the node visited by the token,
Z_{k}
, receives the global iterate
\theta_{k}
and performs one local gradient descent update. The decentralized SGD iteration is as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k};Z_{k}),

(16)

where
\alpha_{k}
is the stepsize. We can rewrite the SGD update equation as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k};Z_{k})\right),

where
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k};Z_{k})
constitutes the Markov noise.

Remark 13
.

For simplicity, we have assumed that the gradient step at node
Z_{k}
is computed using the entire local dataset. In practice, agents can also perform minibatch SGD, where a subset of datapoints is sampled uniformly and independently from
\{1,\ldots,N_{Z_{k}}\}
. In this case, the update includes an additional martingale difference noise term arising from the gradient estimate. This noise depends on the current iterate
\theta_{k}
. Our analysis can accommodate this setting, since the assumptions on the gradient noise allow it to scale with the gradient.

The following proposition states some important properties of the loss function and the token algorithm.

Proposition 14
.

1.

\mathcal{L}
is
(\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})/N)
-PŁ, where
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
is the smallest non-zero singular value of
\mathbf{A}
.

2.

\mathcal{L}
is
(\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\mathbf{A})/N)
-smooth, where
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
is the largest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
.

3.

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot;i)
is
(\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)})/N_{i})
-smooth for every
i
.

4.

For every
i
and
\theta
,
\|\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta;i)\|^{2}\leq 2N\frac{\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{\left(i\right)})}{N_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
.

Note that the problem setup satisfies all required assumptions stated in Section
2
, and thus Theorem
6
and Theorem
9
directly hold for this problem.

Corollary 15
.

Consider the decentralized SGD algorithm (
16
) for linear regression. Let
t_{\text{mix}}
denote the mixing time of the Markov chain
\{Z_{k}\}
and let
\alpha_{k}=\frac{a}{k+K_{0}}
, where
a\geq 2N/\sigma_{\min(\mathbf{A})}
. Then there exists
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\Omega(\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta}))
and a constant
\mathfrak{a}_{2}
, such that

1.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{1}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k})-\inf_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}(\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{2}N^{2}\log{\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

2.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k})-\inf_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}N^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

5.2
Supervised Learning with Subsampling for Privacy Amplification

We consider the supervised learning task where, given a dataset
D=\{\mathbf{A}(i),\mathbf{b}(i)\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
\mathbf{A}(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{b}(i)\in\mathbb{R}
, and a parameterized class of functions
\phi(w;\mathbf{A}(i))
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the learner has to learn a parameter
w^{\star}
such that
\phi(w^{\star};\mathbf{A}(i))\approx\mathbf{b}(i)
for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
denotes the collection of feature maps where the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
is
\mathbf{A}(i)^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{b}
denotes the target vector. We wish to minimize the squared loss,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(\phi(w;\mathbf{A}(i))-\mathbf{b}(i))^{2}}.

Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent relies on randomized subsampling for privacy amplification
[
1
]
. Popular subsampling schemes with correlated noise include cyclic Poisson subsampling
[
3
]
, in which the dataset is partitioned into subsets, and the datapoints are sampled from these subsets in a round-robin fashion, and balls-in-bins subsampling
[
4
]
, which mimics shuffling by assigning each example to a random batch index within an epoch. Recently,
[
7
]
introduced
b
-min-sep subsampling, a generalization of both Poisson and balls-in-bins subsampling.

In
b
-min-sep subsampling, the minibatch at time
k
,
B_{k}
, is constructed as follows. First, a subset
D_{k}
of the dataset
D
is created by excluding all samples that appeared in the previous
b-1
minibatches, i.e.,
D_{k}=D\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{b-1}{B_{k-i}}
. Then, each datapoint in
D_{k}
is independently included in the current minibatch
B_{k}
with a probability of
\rho
. This mechanism ensures that no example participates more than once in any window of
b
steps, preserving the necessary structure for an efficient privacy analysis.

This subsampling scheme can be equivalently described through a Markov chain governing the availability of each datapoint. Specifically, each datapoint occupies one of
b
states labeled
0,1\ldots,b-1
, where state
0
indicates that the datapoint is available for sampling in the next timestep. If a datapoint is selected in the minibatch, it transitions to state
b-1
. Thereafter, the state deterministically decreases by one at each step until it reaches
0
, where it again becomes eligible for sampling. Let
\zeta_{k}
denote the
N
-dimensional random vector such that the
i
-th coordinate of
\zeta_{k}
,
\zeta_{k}(i)
, is the state of the
i
-th datapoint at time
k
. Define the loss function at time
k
,

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w;\zeta_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}\left(\zeta_{k}(j)=b-1\right)(\phi(w;\mathbf{A}(j))-\mathbf{b}(j))^{2}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}\left(\zeta_{k}(i)=b-1\right)}},

with the convention that
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w;\zeta_{k})=0
if
\mathbbm{1}\left(\zeta_{k}(i)=b-1\right)=0
for all
i
. The
k
-th iteration of the SGD is then as follows:

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w_{k};\zeta_{k}),\penalty\ k=0,1,\ldots.

(17)

Let
\pi
denote the stationary distribution of the Markov chain
\{\zeta_{k}\}
. Although the exact form of the distribution
\pi
is non-trivial, the following lemma shows that the stationary expectation of
\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w;\zeta_{k})
is the loss function we aim to minimize.

Lemma 16
.

For all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathcal{L}(w)=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\pi}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w;\zeta)\right]
.

The SGD iteration can then be written as

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\mathcal{L}(w_{k})+\alpha_{k}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(w_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w_{k};\zeta_{k})),\penalty\ k=0,1,\ldots,

where
\nabla\mathcal{L}(w_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w_{k};\zeta_{k})
constitutes the Markov noise. We need the following two technical assumptions on the model.

Assumption 17
(Uniform conditioning).

Let
\nabla\phi(w;\mathbf{A})
be the differential of the map
\phi
at
w
. The tangent kernel of
\phi
is defined as an
N\times N
matrix
K(w)=\nabla\phi(w;\mathbf{A})\nabla\phi(w;\mathbf{A})^{\top}
. The smallest eigenvalue of the tangent kernel
K(w)
is bounded below by
\mu
for all
w
.

Assumption 18
(Smoothness).

The function
\tilde{\ell}_{i}(w):=\frac{1}{2}(\phi(w;\mathbf{A}(j))-\mathbf{b}(j))^{2}
is
L
-smooth for every
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

Assumption
17
implies that
\mathcal{L}(w)
is
\mu
-PŁ
[
20
]
. The same work showed that wide neural networks satisfy Assumption
17
on a set of radius
R
, and
R
is proportional to the width of the neural network. Assumption
18
implies
L
-smoothness of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\zeta)
for every
\zeta\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{N}
. Moreover, Assumption
18
implies the ABC condition. We show these results in the following proposition.

Proposition 19
.

1.

Under Assumption
17
,
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is
(\mu/N)
-PŁ.

2.

Under Assumption
18
,
\mathcal{L}(\cdot;\zeta)
is
L
-smooth for every
\zeta\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{N}
. Further,
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is
L
-smooth.

3.

Under Assumption
18
, for every
\zeta\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(w;\zeta)\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2LN}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}\left(\zeta(i)=b-1\right)}}\mathcal{L}(w).

Having shown that the loss function and the noisy gradient samples satisfy all assumptions from Section
2
, we can now state the following convergence results.

Corollary 20
.

Consider the SGD algorithm (
17
) for the differentially private supervised learning setup described above that satisfies Assumptions
17
and
18
. Let
t_{\text{mix}}
denote the mixing time of the Markov chain
\{\zeta_{k}\}
and let
\alpha_{k}=\frac{a}{k+K_{0}}
, where
a\geq 2N/\mu
. Then there exists
\mathfrak{a}_{3}=\Omega(\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta}))
and a constant
\mathfrak{a}_{4}
, such that

1.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{3}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(w_{k})-\inf_{w}{\mathcal{L}(w)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{2}N^{2}\log{\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}}{\mu^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

2.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{4}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(w_{k})-\inf_{w}{\mathcal{L}(w)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}N^{2}}{\mu^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

5.3
Online System Identification

Let the process
\{Z_{k}\}
evolve as
Z_{k+1}=A_{\star}Z_{k}+w_{k}
, where
A_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and
w_{k},k\geq 0
, is i.i.d. noise with zero mean and bounded variance. The objective of the online system identification problem is to estimate
A_{\star}

[
9
,
18
]
. Consider that an optimizer is trying to estimate the system parameter
A_{\star}
by minimizing the squared distance loss,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A):=\frac{1}{2}\int{\left\|Az-A_{\star}z\right\|^{2}d\pi(z)}.

Here,
\pi
denotes the stationary distribution of the Markov process
\{Z_{k}\}
. The following assumption guarantees its existence and uniqueness.

Assumption 21
.

The eigenvalues of
A_{\star}
are strictly inside the unit disc, i.e.,
\lambda_{\max}:=\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,d}{\left|\lambda_{i}\right|}<1
, where
\lambda_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues of
A_{\star}
.

Proposition 22
.

Let
A_{\star}
satisfy Assumption
21
. Then there exists a unique stationary distribution
\pi
for the Markov chain
\{Z_{k}\}
. Moreover, for any initial condition
Z_{0}
, the distribution of
Z_{k}
converges to
\pi
at a geometric rate.

The loss observed at time
k
is denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(A,Z_{k})
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(A,Z_{k}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|AZ_{k}-A_{\star}Z_{k}\right\|^{2}.

We consider the SGD update,

\displaystyle A_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k}-\alpha_{k}(A_{k}Z_{k}-Z_{k+1})Z_{k}^{\top}.

(18)

Replacing
Z_{k+1}
with
A^{\star}Z_{k}+w_{k}
, we rewrite the update as follows:

\displaystyle A_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k}-\alpha_{k}(A_{k}Z_{k}-A_{\star}Z_{k})Z_{k}^{\top}+\alpha_{k}w_{k}Z_{k}^{\top}

\displaystyle=A_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\mathcal{L}(A_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}(A_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(A_{k},Z_{k})\right)+\alpha_{k}w_{k}Z_{k}^{\top},

where
\nabla\mathcal{L}(A_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(A_{k},Z_{k})
is the Markovian noise and
w_{k}Z_{k}^{\top}
is the martingale difference noise component. The following assumption is required for the Markov chain to lie in a compact state space, and for the PŁ condition to hold.

Assumption 23
.

(i) There exists a constant
B>0
such that
\left\|w_{k}\right\|\leq B
almost surely for every
k
.
(ii)
\Sigma:=\int{zz^{\top}d\pi(z)}
is strictly positive definite with minimum eigenvalue
\mu_{\min}>0
.

Proposition 24
.

Under Assumption
21
and Assumption
23
,

1.

\left\|Z_{k}\right\|\leq\max{\{\left\|Z_{0}\right\|,B/(1-\lambda_{\max})\}}
almost surely for every
k
.

2.

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is
\mu_{\min}
-PŁ.

3.

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is
\mu_{\max}
-smooth, where
\mu_{\max}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
.

4.

\left\|\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(A_{k},Z_{k})\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2B^{4}}{(1-\lambda_{\max})^{4}\mu_{\min}}\mathcal{L}(A)
almost surely for every
k
.

5.

\left\|w_{k}Z_{k}^{\top}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{B^{4}}{(1-\lambda_{\max})^{2}}
almost surely for every
k
.

Propositions
22
and
24
together show that all assumptions in Section
2
are satisfied, and hence we have the following bound on the rate of convergence.

Corollary 25
.

Consider the SGD algorithm (
18
) for the system identification problem described above that satisfies Assumption
23
. Let
t_{\text{mix}}
denote the mixing time of the Markov chain
\{Z_{k}\}
and let
\alpha_{k}=\frac{a}{k+K_{0}}
, where
a\geq 2/\mu_{\min}
. Then there exists
\mathfrak{a}_{5}=\Omega(\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta}))
and a constant
\mathfrak{a}_{6}
, such that

1.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{5}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{2}\log{\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}}{\mu_{\min}^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

2.

If
K_{0}>\mathfrak{a}_{6}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(A_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}}{\mu_{\min}^{2}k}\right),\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we establish the first uniform-in-time concentration bound on the suboptimality of SGD iterates under the PŁ condition with Markovian noise. Our analysis relies on the Poisson equation to handle the Markov noise, and a novel
probabilistic induction
argument to deal with the lack of almost sure bounds on the loss function. We also demonstrate the practical relevance of our framework by analyzing three representative applications. Future directions include extending these results to broader classes of objective functions, such as those satisfying the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz condition and establishing bounds for SGD with asynchronous updates, which would enable a more realistic treatment of decentralized optimization.
