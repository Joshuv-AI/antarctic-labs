National prevalence of human immunodeficiency virus among long-distance truck drivers in Mali: cross-sectional study
Authors: Nouhoum Telly, Abou Sogodogo, Sory Traoré, Madina Konaté, Mamadou Diop, Mahamane Maiga, Shahul Ebrahim, Boubacar M Dembélé, Mariam Berthé, Mariame T Traoré, Ibrahim T Diallo, Birama A Ly, Oumar Sangho, Hamadoun Sangho, Seydou Doumbia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLongue-distance trucks drivers are a population at increased risk of HIV infection compared with the general population. This study aimed to estimate the national prevalence of HIV and identify factors associated with infection among longue-distance trucks drivers in Mali. We conducted a cross-sectional bio-behavioral survey using Time-Location Sampling (TLS) between July 2019 and February 2020. Eligible participants were truck drivers aged ≥ 18 years who regularly undertook long-distance trips (at least 200 km one way). Following pre-test counseling, HIV testing was performed according to national testing algorithm, using Determine as the screening test and SD Bioline for confirmation. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 1,100 participants were included in the study. Most participants were aged ≥ 20 years (91.5%), 63.5% had received formal education, and 57.2% were married. Risk behaviors were common: 37.7% reported having multiple sexual partners, 23.5% reported drug use before sexual intercourse, 44.6% reported condom use during their last sexual intercourse, and 51.8% reported spending more than one month away from home. Although 78.8% recognized condoms use a protective measure against HIV infection, preventive practices remained inconsistent. The national HIV prevalence was 1.9%. Higher prevalence estimates were observed in the regions of Ségou (2.7%), Sikasso (2.6%), and Koulikoro region (2%). In bivariate analyses, HIV infection was significantly associated with older age (median 38 vs. 31 years; p = 0.002), marital status (p = 0.002), education level (p = 0.015), and longer work experience (p = 0.001). In multivariate analysis, no factors remained statistical significance. However, longer work experience (> 5 years) was associated with higher odds of HIV infection (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 3.68; 95% CI: 0.93–21.27), whereas formal education appeared to have a protective effect (aOR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.21–1.29). HIV prevalence among long-distance truck drivers in Mali remains higher than reported in the general population, alongside persistent high-risk behaviors and regional disparities. Targeted, context-specific preventive measures are urgently needed to reduce new infections in this highly mobile and vulnerable population.Keywords: Prevalence, HIV, Long-distance truck drivers, Mali

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 40.8 million people living with HIV worldwide [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa continuous to bear a disproportionate burden of the epidemic, accounting for approximately 65% of all people living with HIV globally [1]. In Mali, the HIV epidemic is considered concentrated, with a relatively low prevalence in the general population compared with key populations. The national HIV prevalence was estimated at 1.0% in 2021 [2]. However, HIV prevalence remains disproportionately high among key populations. In 2020, prevalence estimates reached 8.7% among female sex workers, 12.6 among men who have sex with other men, and 5.1% among people who inject drugs [2, 3].

Despite significant progress in HIV prevention and treatment, key populations continue to play an important role in HIV transmission dynamics [3, 4]. Long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs) represent one suck population. Due to the nature of their occupation, which is characterized by prolonged mobility, extended periods away from home, and frequent stops along major transport corridors, LDTDs are particularly vulnerable to HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [5]. A global estimate reported an HIV prevalence of 3.86% among LDTD, with the highest prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa (14.34%), followed by Asia-Pacific region ( 2.2%) [6].

LDTDs are exposed to multiple overlapping risk factors that increase their vulnerability to HIV infection. Studies have reported a high levels of risky sexual behaviors among this group, including multiple sexual partnerships and inconsistent condom use [7, 8]. In addition, alcohol consumption and illicit drug use before or during sexual encounters may further increase the likelihood of engaging in unsafe sexual practices [9]. The high mobility of LDTDs facilitates the formation of extensive sexual networks across geographic regions, thereby contributing to the transmission and spread of HIV along major transport corridors [10, 11]. Stopover locations are often associated with commercial sex activities involving female sex workers, further amplifying the risk of HIV transmission [8]. Collectively, these factors contribute to increasing the burden of HIV in this group.

Evidence from the Fourth National Integrated Bio-Behavioral Survey conducted in 2009 reported an HIV prevalence of 2.7% among LDTD in Mali [12]. In response to the heightened vulnerability of this population, HIV prevention strategies have focused on behavior change communication, promotion of condom use, and raising awareness of the risks associated with alcohol and substance use [3, 13]. However, the effectiveness of these interventions depends on the availability of up-to-date and representative epidemiological evidence.

To date, there is a lack of recent national-level data on HIV prevalence among LDLDs in Mali. Most available evidence are outdated or geographically limited. Given the potential role of LDTDs as a bridge population linking high-risk and general populations, it is essential to better understand the current burden of HIV infection within this group. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the national prevalence of HIV and identify associated factors with HIV infection among long-distance lorry drivers in Mali.

Methods

Study settings

The survey was conducted on seven selected sites in Mali: Bamako, Kayes, Koulikoro, Sikasso, Ségou, Mopti, and Gao. These regions include major administrative capitals, highly populated urban centers, and key economic hubs of the country. They are also characterized by high population mobility and substantial flows of long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs), making them suitable settings for assessing HIV prevalence and associated factors in this population.

Study design and period

We conducted a cross-sectional bio-behavioral survey was conducted among long-distance truck drivers in Mali. Participants were recruited using a Time-Location Sampling (TLS) approach, which involved sampling individuals from predefined locations and time periods where LDTDs commonly congregate [14]. Data collection was conducted between July 2019 to February 2020.

Study participants

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they me all of the following criteria: (i) Male; (ii) aged 18 years or older; (iii) employed as a long-distance truck driver, defined as driving at least 200 km one way and/or operating cross-border routes to neighboring countries; (iv) having spent at least one night at a study site during the month preceding the survey; (v) residing, working, or making regular stopovers at one of the selected study sites; (vi) able to communicate in French, English or Bambara, and (vii) willing and able to provide written informed consent.

Individuals were excluded if they were under the influence of alcohol or drugs at the time of interview or if they had previously participated in the study.

Sample size and sampling procedures

The minimum required sample size was estimated at 1,009 participants using the single-population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, and expected HIV prevalence of 2.7% [12], and am margin of error of 1%. After accounting for a 5% non-response rate, the required sample size increased to 1,065 participants. To ensure adequate statistical power the final sample size was rounded and up to 1,100 participants.

A formative assessment was conducted prior to data collection to guide the implementation of the Time-Location Sampling (TLS) strategy. The assessment aimed to identify and map locations where long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs) commonly congregate, including truck stops, parking areas, bother transit points, and rest sites. It also helped to determine the days and times periods during which LDTDs were most likely to be present at each site, thereby maximizing recruitment opportunities.

In addition, the formative assessment evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of this study procedure among the target population and relevant local stakeholders. Key informant interviews and field observations were conducted to better understand drivers’ movement patterns, peak transit periods, and the accessibility of the study sites. The findings informed the selection of time-location clusters used for participant recruitment during the main survey.

Eligible LDTDs were approached for recruitment by trained study staff during the predefined days-time periods corresponding to each selected time-location cluster. Using a standardized screening form, study staff introduced the survey, explained its objectives, and assessed participants’ eligibility. The voluntary nature of participation, as well as confidentiality and privacy protections, were clearly explained.

Participants were informed that the survey required a single study visit consisting of a face-to-face interview, HIV and syphilis testing using finger-prick blood samples, and urine testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea. Appropriate counseling and referrals to relevant health services were provided when indicated, according to national guidelines and community referral mechanisms.

Data collection

Eligible long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs) were provided with detailed information about the study and invited to participate. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers using electronic tablets. Interviews were administrated in French, English or Bambara, according to participants’ preferences, to ensure optimal comprehension and accuracy of response.

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics, occupational and mobility patterns, sexual behaviors, number and types of sexual partners, condoms use, self-reported symptoms of STIs, knowledge of HIV and its prevention, and exposure to HIV prevention interventions.

Laboratory methods, quality assurance (QA) / quality control (QC) procedure

Participants who consented to HIV testing received pre-test counseling followed by rapid HIV testing in accordance with the national HIV testing algorithm of Mali. The Determine HIV-1/2 was used as initial screening assay. Reactive samples were subsequently tested using the Sd Bioline HIV-1/2 rapid test for confirmation. In case of discordant results between the two tests, the First Response HIV 1–2.O Card Test was used as a tie-breaker.

Dried blood spots (DBS) samples were collected for quality control purposes. Blood samples were also used for the syphilis test. In addition, urine specimens were collected from all participants for the detection of Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis infections.

Quality control of laboratory results was conducted at the laboratory of the National Institute of Public Health (INSP). All HIV-positive samples identified in the field, as well as a random sample comprising 20% of HIV-negative specimens, were retested. Negative sample were selected using systematic sampling procedure.

For quality assurance, two enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) tests were performed in parallel (EnzygnostR HIV Integral 4, MurexR HIV Ag / Ab combination). The Western Blot HIV 2.2 from MP Diagnostics was used as a confirmatory test for samples with discrepant ELISA results and for samples that tested positive on both ELISA assays. Concordance between field and reference results was assessed by calculating the proportions of matching test results among all samples subjected to quality control testing.

Compensation and referral for treatment

Participants received a compensation of 3,000 CFA francs (approximately US $5.35) for the time spent participating in the study. They were also provided with condoms and information on HIV prevention and treatment. Following confirmation of test results, trained HIV counselors delivered post-test counseling to all participants. Those diagnosed with HIV or another sexually transmitted infection (STIs) were referred to appropriate care and treatment facilities based on service availability, accessibility, and patient’ preference.

Study variables

This study assessed sociodemographic, behavioral, and HIV-related knowledge variables. The primary variables included: region (geographic location where the survey was conducted), age of participants (collected as a continuous and categorized into two age groups (adolescent < 20, and adult ≥ 20), marital status (categorized as married or single/widowed/divorced), number of sexual partners (categorized as one or more), occupational experience (duration of driving experience, categorized as: ≤ 5 years and > 5 years), knowledge and belief about HIV prevention assessed using binary (Yes/No) responses for the following items (fidelity, abstinence, condom use, avoiding contaminated objects, belief that a person can have HIV while appearing healthy), risk behaviors evaluating using (Yes/No) for (alcohol consumption, drug use, drug injection, condom use during the last sexual activity), and prior HIV testing: whether the participant have ever been tested for HIV (Yes/No).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics, occupational and behavioral factors, and the prevalence of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among participants. Cross-tabulations were performed to examine the distribution of HIV prevalence across key variables. Associations between independent variables and HIV infection were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’ exact test, as appropriate. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with HIV infection. In multivariable analysis, due to small number of HIV-positive cases and evidence of sparse data bias, penalized logistic regression (Firth method) was considered. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. P-value of < 0.05 was considered statically significant.

The national HIV prevalence among truck drivers in Mali was 1.9% (21/1100). The prevalence of HIV was 1.8% (6/334) in Bamako, 1.9% (5/270) in Kayes, 2% (1/49) in Koulikoro, 2.6% (4/152) in Sikasso, 2.7% (5/192) in Ségou, 0% (0/55) in Mopti and Goa.

Knowledge of HIV and attitudes among LDTDs in Mali

Overall, exposure to HIV education among LDTDs was low, with only 25.3% (278/1100) reporting having received HIV-related education. However, a large proportion (74.8%; 823/1100) perceived their knowledge about HIV as adequate. Knowledge of HIV prevention methods varied considerably. Condom use was the most common (78.8%; 866/1100).

A high proportion of participants (82.3%; 905/1100) correctly recognized that a person living with HIV can appear healthy. However, personal exposure to HIV appeared limited, as only 14.5% (159/1100) reported knowing someone living with HIV and 14.7% (162/1100) knowing someone who had died to HIV. Belief in the effectiveness of HIV treatment was divided, with 49% (445/908) acknowledging effective treatment. Discussion

HIV prevalence among long-distance truck drivers

The national HIV prevalence among truck drivers in Mali was 1.9%. The highest prevalence rates were observed in the regions of Ségou (2.7%), Sikasso (2.6%), and Koulikoro (2%). This prevalence is nearly twice that reported in the general population of Mali in 2021 (1.0%) [15]. The elevated prevalence observed among long-distance truck drivers may be attributable to the nature of their occupation, which is characterized by prolonged mobility, extended periods away from home, and engagement in behaviors that increase the risk of HIV acquisition and transmission.

A recent systematic review estimated the HIV prevalence among long-distance truck drivers in Sub-Saharan Africa at 14.3% [6]. Higher prevalence estimates have been reported in several African settings. For example, a study conducted in South Africa found that 26% of truck drivers were living with HIV in 2014 [16], while a study from southwestern Nigeria reported an HIV prevalence of 2.4% among long-distance truck drivers in 2010 [5]. In contrast, our findings are higher than those reported by Karimi et al. [17], who found an HIV prevalence of 0.33% among truck and bus drivers in the Kurdistan province of Iraq in 2021. Similarly, a study conducted in New Mexico, United States, reported an HIV prevalence of 0.2% among long-distance drivers was in 2006 [18]. Even lower prevalence estimates have been reported in Bangladesh, Iran, and China, were studies conducted in 2001, 2010, and 2006, respectively, found no HIV-positive cases among surveyed drivers [19–21]. These variations in HIV prevalence across countries may reflect differences in the underlying epidemiology of HIV, access to prevention and testing services, patterns of sexual risk behaviors, and the socioeconomic and cultural contexts in which drivers operate.

Extended periods away from home create conditions that may facilitate engagement in risky sexual behaviors. LDTDs often establish multiple sexual partnerships across different destinations, engage in extramarital sexual relationships, and use condoms inconsistently, all of which increase their vulnerability to HIV infection. In addition, truck drivers frequently interact with female sex workers who are readily accessible at rest stops along major transport corridors, where drivers commonly stop to rest, eat, and spent the night [8]. These rest stops often function, as commercial hubs that bring together truck drivers, migrant workers, local residents, and other travelers, thereby creating environments that may facilitate HIV transmission. A study conducted in South African, in 2006 reported that 37% of long-distance drivers always stopped for sexual encounters during their journeys [22].

The relatively high burden of HIV among long-distance truck drivers in Africa could be attributed to a combination of factors, including limited access to HIV prevention and healthcare services, inadequate risk perception, and persistent engagement in high-risk sexual behaviors [16, 23, 24]. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions addressing the specific vulnerabilities of this population. Comprehensive awareness campaigns should be strengthened to promote consistent condom use, improve HIV-related knowledge, and encourage safer sexual behaviors. Efforts are also needed to expand access to sexual health education, HIV testing, prevention services, and linkage to care along major transport routes. Furthermore, continued research and surveillance among LDTDs are essential to monitor trends in HIV infection and to inform the development of effective, evidence-based interventions tailored to the needs of this population.

Sociodemographic Factors of HIV among LDTDs in Mali

The median age of participants was 32 years, indicating a predominantly mature and economically active population. This age structure is consistent with the occupational demands of long-distance trucking and aligns with findings from previous studies. For instance, Yaya et al. [25] reported a median age of 29 years among LDTDs in Togo in 2016, while Pandher et al. [26] reported a mean of 33.7 years in India in 2021.

In the present study, HIV-positive participants were significantly older than HIV-negative participants in the univariable analysis (median: 38 vs. 31; p = 0.002). However, this association was not retained in multivariable model. This finding suggests that the observed effect of age may be explained by other correlated factors, such as occupational experience and cumulative exposure to HIV risk.

Marital status and education attainment were also associated with HIV infection in univariable analysis but did not remain significant after adjustment. Previous studies have reported that marital status may influence sexual behavior and condom-use practices among LDTDs rather than providing direct protection against HIV-infection [25, 27]. Likewise, higher education attainment has been associated with improved HIV knowledge and greater awareness of preventive measures [25, 28–30].

Longer duration in the trucking profession may increase cumulative exposure to HIV high-risk environments. Although occupational experience was not significantly associated with HIV infection in the adjusted analysis, drivers with longer work experience showed higher odds of HIV infection. Previous studies conducted along major transport corridors in Africa have shown that prolonged mobility and extended periods on the road may increase exposure to circumstances associated with HIV transmission [16, 31]. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening HIV prevention, and testing services along major transport corridors. Expanding mobile and roadside health services along transport corridors could improve access to HIV testing, counselling, and treatment.

Risk behaviors of HIV among LDTDs in Mali

A substantial proportion of drivers reported multiple sexual partnerships and inconsistent condom use, indicating persistence gaps in prevention practices. These findings are consistent with studies conducted among LDTDs in sub-Saharan Africa, were high levels of partner concurrency and low condom use have been widely documented [32]. A study conducted in Burkina Faso in 2015, found that more than three-quarters of truck drivers reported having sexual partners other than their wives, and 34% reported engaging with female sex workers, among whom 76% reported unprotected sexual intercourse [33]. Similarly, Adeoti et al. [34] reported that longer duration in the trucking profession and patronage of female sex workers were associated with having multiple sexual partners in Nigeria, in 2019.

These findings suggest that sexual risk behaviors remain common among LDTDs and may contribute to their increased vulnerability to HIV infection. Strengthening peer-leading education programs, promoting consistent condom use, and improving access to prevention services at truck stops and border crossings could help reduce risk in this population.

Less than one-quart of drivers reported alcohol consumption. Although alcohol use was not significantly associated with HIV infection in this study, previous research has shown that alcohol and other psychoactive substances may increase engagement in high-risk sexual behaviors. In India, alcohol consumption before sexual intercourse is significantly associated with higher likelihood of unprotected sex (OR = 6.46 [2.63–15.89]) [26]. Similarly, a study conducted in Kenya in 2020 identified alcohol consumption (OR = 10.9) and khat use (OR = 7.05) as important predictors of risky sexual behavior and LDTDs [9]. These findings underscore the importance of integrating substance-use prevention and risk-reduction counseling into HIV prevention programs targeted this population.

Long-distance truck drivers’ knowledge of HIV

This study revealed important gap in HIV-related knowledge of HIV among long-distance truck drivers. Although many participants reported being aware of HIV prevention methods, exposure to formal HIV education was relatively limited. Insufficient knowledge may contribute to the adoption of behaviors that increase vulnerability to HIV infection and hinder the uptake of prevention services. Similar knowledge gaps have been reported elsewhere. In a study found only 4% had accurate knowledge of HIV/AIDS [35].

In this study, condom use was the most cited prevention method (78.7%), whereas abstinence and fidelity were less commonly mentioned. This findings is consistent with results from Singh et al. [36], who reported that 81.8% of truck drivers were aware that HIV can be transmitted through unprotected sexual intercourse. However, a study conducted in Niono, Mali, found that more than 80% of participants identified both abstinence and condom use as effective HIV prevention strategies [37]. The prominence of condom-related knowledge in our study may reflect the emphasis placed on condom promotion in HIV prevention programs and communication campaigns.

However, awareness alone is insufficient to prevent HIV transmission. The effectiveness of condom depends on their consistent and correct use, as well as their availability and accessibility.

Given the mobility of LDTDs and the challenges associated with accessing health services along transport routes, strengthening comprehensive HIV education, and ensuring continuous access to prevention tools remain essential. This intervention should address not only awareness but also practice skills, risk perception, and the adoption of safer sexual behaviors.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is the first in Mali to include enough long-distance truck drivers, providing valuable and context-specific insights into HIV-related behaviors, knowledge, and attitudes within this key population. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution. The time-location sampling may have introduced selection bias, as only truck drivers present at selected venues during specific time periods were included. The reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive behaviors such as sexual practices and substance use. In addition, cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between identified factors and HIV risk.

Conclusion

This study highlights a high burden of HIV among long-distance truck drivers in Mali, accompanied by important gaps in HIV knowledge and widespread engagement in high-risk behaviors such as psychoactive substance use (alcohol and drugs) and multiple sexual partners. These findings underscore the vulnerability of this mobile population and the limitations of current prevention efforts. There is a need to implement targeted, comprehensive HIV prevention strategies tailored to LDTDs, including strengthened health education, improved access to testing and prevention services, and interventions addressing substance use and mobility-related risks along transport corridors.
