Barriers to patient engagement with community-based resources following social determinants of health (SDOH) screening: a mixed-methods study across hospitals in the Chicago metropolitan area
Authors: Dania Jaamour, Shravan Asthana, Yarlenny Zuniga, Mita Goel, Cynthia Barnard, Lori Ann Post, Diana Xu, Teresa Pollack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuality and accreditation standards from the centres for medicare and medicaid services have prompted acute care hospitals to integrate social determinants of health (SDOH) screening into clinical workflows. However, the design of SDOH screening and response programmes is not standardised, and actual engagement of patients to use referrals, and barriers to community resources and engagement, remain understudied.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study across three hospitals within the Northwestern Medicine health system in metropolitan Chicago and surrounding suburbs. Surveys and interviews were administered to discharged inpatients who screened high risk for social needs and to community-based organisations (CBOs) that received hospital referrals. Quantitative and qualitative analyses assessed barriers to patient engagement.Results30 patients and 6 CBO sites were included. The patient survey demonstrated overall low engagement with resources, with most patients not using the provided resources despite agreeing that they matched their social needs and comfort level. Key barriers included limited English proficiency, perceived lack of benefit and difficulty accessing CBO services. CBO responses highlighted operational challenges such as underfunding, understaffing and an inability to track referred patients from hospitals.ConclusionIn this Chicago-based multi-hospital study, substantial gaps were identified between discharge referrals and actual resource utilisation. Strengthening follow-up, bidirectional hospital–CBO communication and community capacity are essential to improve the effectiveness of SDOH screening programmes.Keywords: Community Health, Preventive Medicine, Secondary Prevention, Social Planning

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) refer to the community-level, non-medical conditions in which people are born, live, learn and age, and which substantially influence quality of life and long-term health outcomes. These determinants or influences have more impact on preventable chronic conditions’ prevalence, morbidity and mortality than direct healthcare access and quality.1 Health outcomes are influenced 20% by clinical care, while 80% of the variation in outcomes is driven by social determinants.2 Thus, strategies to address SDOH must be included in designing quality improvement (QI) efforts to improve health outcomes and reduce disparities. Addressing areas such as housing, food and nutrition, transportation and access to community resources appears to be associated with improvement in patients’ healthcare outcomes, total healthcare costs and patient financial exposure.3

While SDOH have been a target of research and public health for years, the centres for medicare and medicaid services (CMS) have recently integrated screening for SDOH in their Health People 2030 objectives.4 CMS established an SDOH screening measure for acute care hospitals as part of its inpatient quality reporting programme, which reflects a national standard of measurement and transparency of hospital quality. Hospitals must submit two new measures: (1) SDOH-1: social drivers of health, total number and proportion of inpatients who were screened for five specific domains (food insecurity, housing insecurity, transportation needs, utility difficulties and interpersonal safety) and (2) SDOH-2: the proportion of adult patients who screened positive in at least one of the five social domains.3 While CMS labels these measures as SDOH-1 and SDOH-2, reflecting the reporting framework of this protocol, the five domains themselves align with the health-related social needs (HRSN) domains from the accountable health communities Model, which assess the individual factors that directly impact a person’s health. It is essential to recognise the distinction between these two terms, as individual-level needs may differ significantly from the broader needs of the community. Beyond CMS metrics, accreditation standards for healthcare organisations currently include HRSN performance requirements, including assessing patients for HRSN needs and developing plans to meet such needs, though this may change.1

As a result of these mandates and emerging evidence of need and efficacy, health systems have responded to improve their screening, reporting and intervention to mitigate SDOH impact on health outcomes. These facilities use screening tools to identify patient risk, which can support and help facilitate more informed care during admissions and clinical visits, and tailored referral resources.5 6 Such screening and interventions have been found not only to increase the identification of patients who were at higher risk of having unmet HRSN, but also to improve patient satisfaction and patients’ access to HRSN-related resources. However, when further attention is paid to follow-up for positive screening, a trusting relationship is built between patient and provider, and clear communication is used to explain the purpose of screening and how to use referred resource.7,9 However, there is a dearth of evidence to ascertain the sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of such tools in identifying social risk factors and effectuating resource engagement to improve overall health outcomes.10

Despite the recent robust expansion of SDOH screening and response systems throughout the US, evidence is lacking to characterise how patients interact with screening-triggered resources provided at discharge. Also understudied are barriers to such engagement, and patient perspectives on the screening and referral process. Although many healthcare systems have adopted universal SDOH screening tools within electronic medical records (EMRs), implementation remains highly variable across institutions. Challenges include limited interoperability between health and social service data systems, lack of standardised referral workflows, insufficient staffing for follow-up and fragmented coordination with community-based organisations (CBOs).11,13 Many hospitals also face resource constraints and competing priorities, which can limit the integration of social care within routine clinical practice. These systemic challenges often result in incomplete referrals, low patient engagement and difficulty tracking outcomes across the continuum of care. In this study, we aimed to identify barriers to utilisation of community resources that were provided in response to patients’ HRSN, by interviewing both patients and CBOs. The team categorised the barriers based on the source of the constraint and the various factors, such as social, economic and cultural components that are involved in successful HRSN referral utilisation.

Methods

Study sites

The Northwestern medicine (NM) health system includes eleven hospitals in metropolitan Chicago and the region. In order to study a diverse, representative sample of patients in our health system, three acute care hospitals were selected in this study: Northwestern Memorial Hospital (NMH), a large academic, urban, quaternary care hospital in Chicago and two smaller, suburban community hospitals, Northwestern Lake Forest Hospital (LFH) and Northwestern Central DuPage Hospital (CDH). LFH is located in Lake County in the northern suburbs of Chicago; CDH is in DuPage County in the western suburbs.

At NM, screening occurs on admission using pre-established questions from the CMS-validated HRSN questionnaire, which is integrated into the EMR to assess patients’ social needs (11). The EMR used at all three sites was the EPIC platform. If the patient endorses needs, the “high risk” screen displays a figure in the patient’s EMR highlighting them (figure 1). The most common referral service types included free or low-cost transportation to medical appointments, food pantry, soup kitchen or free meals, SNAP application assistance, mental health support group, mental health education, medical expense assistance and individual counselling. Once needs are identified, referral guides are provided as a physical copy to patients by the provider team prior to discharge (online supplemental file 1). Patients may also request to have instructions to contact referrals uploaded to their electronic patient portal for future access via request to their provider.

Example of a health-related social needs patient screening tool in a patient’s electronic medical record.

Eligibility criteria

Patients data was obtained from one inpatient general medicine floor, a non-intensive care unit hospital unit where patients receive multidisciplinary medical management and monitoring for acute or complex conditions, at each facility. Patients who were discharged after 12 January 2023 were eligible for inclusion in the study. Patients were eligible for participating if they met the following criteria: screened positive for at least one HRSN during their most recent hospitalisation, received an after-visit summary (AVS), and if they had received one or more community referrals targeted to their HRSN at the time of discharge. Patients were also required to have received a HealtheRx, which is a community resource prescription programme based in Chicago, providing patients with printouts of free or low-cost resources that are tailored to patient needs. This ensures patients with needs from each HRSN domain are selected with consideration of race, gender, language spoken and age makeup. After approval by a QI committee that runs research and programme developments that focus on health system optimisation to improve patient outcomes, NMH eligible patients were identified through the EMR. 12 patients were eligible from CDH, 18 patients were eligible from NMH and 32 patients were eligible from LFH.

As for the selection of CBOs, Community Affairs at NMH selected CBOs based on their existing relationship with the hospital, value return in building the relationship with the CBO in the future and the likelihood of a timely response, with Community Affairs identifying a single representative from each CBO that would be appropriate for questionnaire administration. CBO selection and subsequent interviews were not conducted at LFH due to limited engagement among CBO entities following outreach. After reviewing the CBOs with the most referrals from NMH, LFH and CDH, 10 CBOs were selected for interview from NMH referrals and 10 were selected from CDH referrals based on their frequency of referral during the study recruitment period, thus a total of 20 CBOs were recruited. These CBOs were verified to be included at least once in the AVS of each eligible patient at NMH and CDH respectively.

Survey administration and procedure

To gather information about SDOH resource referral engagement after patient discharge, interview guides were created to assess both patient and CBO-reported barriers. Surveying patients and community organisations simultaneously helps to assess the workflow from the point of patient discharge to contact or engagement with the community organisations. A multidisciplinary team consisting of social workers, community nurse coordinators and quality leaders collaborated to construct the interview guides. These guides evaluated patient understanding, comfort, usage and convenience of referrals as well as access, quality, usage and effectiveness of the referred resource. In the CBO interview, we evaluated barriers to assistance, NM referral awareness, match between CBO-provided services and referral platform information, service provision and service load. The interview guides were modelled to include open-ended, yes/no and Likert scale questions (Interview Administration Protocol included in online supplemental file 2).

The interview guides were implemented, and a team of employees administered the surveys to a representative patient and community organisation sample that was selected based on EMR chart review. Administering the survey included calling patients individually while CBOs were first approached in thirty-minute semi-structured interviews with follow-up digital surveys.

Survey analysis

Patient and CBO responses were reported with frequency distributions for Likert-scaled questions. Open-ended questions were analysed narratively based on thematic evaluation of responses. Key themes and words were to be chosen from each response and categorised into codes that fall under specific themes that they discuss, such as transportation, language, distance, etc. However, due to the qualitative responses being minimal, no coding was necessary and themes were assessed in each individual quote in its entirety. (15) To enhance trustworthiness, findings were informed by triangulation of patient and CBO perspectives, multidisciplinary review of results and inclusion of representative participant quotations.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was not feasible in the production and development of this paper, as this paper primarily was targeted to assess the healthcare system’s impact on these populations through interviews, thus patient and CBO opinions were obtained through the methods outlined above.

Patient survey results

Most patients (n=17, 56.7%) reported that they contacted none of the resources on their AVS, followed by 8 (26.7%) who called “some”. Most patients did not use any of the referred resources for assistance (n=20, 66.7%), and six patients (20%) used some of them. Despite this low engagement and utilisation, most patients either agreed or strongly agreed (n=23, 76.7%) that they were comfortable contacting the resources that were referred. Furthermore, the vast majority (n=25, 83.3%) of patients did not use the internet for information on their resources, even for resources with online applications or service lines. Most patients agreed or strongly agreed that the referred resources matched their needs (n=22, 73.3%), but ease of access was variable: half of the patients denied that the resources were hard to reach in person (n=14, 46.7%), a quarter were neutral, and a quarter agreed that they were difficult to access. Finally, the majority of patients (n=18, 60%) agreed or strongly agreed that the resources in the discharge paperwork helped them, even though, as noted above, most of them did not actually use them (table 3).

CBO outreach

We interviewed three CBO entities across six different sites from the 20 entities eligible by study criteria, which included a county health department, local senior centre and a county community services office. On interviewing, CBOs indicated they had no tracking system for how and from where clients were referred to them, thus there were no established records to indicate which clients were patients within our hospital system. This limited exploration of NM referral coordination of care awareness as well as match between patient-specific needs and service offered. Each of these organisations was also unable to describe the volume of their referrals from these three hospitals. Nevertheless, when generally inquiring about service load, the CBOs did not report any perceived increase in their service demand or caseload since the implementation of the screening and referral programme. Regarding general barriers to assisting clients referred to their organisations, CBO locations reported common obstacles of being underfunded, understaffed, overworked and inability to meet demand. Furthermore, the range in organisational capacity was quite heterogenous, ranging across the three CBOs from support for 12 clients a month to 12 000+.

Discussion

This study identified several barriers to HRSN interventions and emphasises that optimising the post-discharge follow-up process is a key priority. Although most patients expressed comfort in their ability to contact referred resources and felt these resources met their needs, engagement after discharge remained low. Despite adequate awareness and direct provision of resource tools, utilisation was modest, suggesting that behavioural and structural barriers extend beyond access alone. These findings align with prior studies showing that engagement rates in social care programmes are comparable to adherence patterns in traditional clinical interventions. For example, medication adherence for chronic diseases often ranges between 26% and 50%, yet such treatments remain standard of care.13 Social interventions, such as resource guide utilisation, should therefore be evaluated through the same pragmatic lens, recognising that imperfect uptake does not indicate programme failure but reflects the inherent behavioural and structural barriers patients face after discharge. Limiting benchmarks for success may undervalue their broader population-level impact and sustainability within complex care systems.

Patient self-efficacy and trust emerged as crucial yet under-recognised determinants of referral success. Patients who perceive low personal agency, limited digital literacy or uncertainty about benefits may not engage with even well-matched resources. Identifying which patients can independently navigate resource guides versus those requiring higher-touch support, such as community health worker (CHW) follow-up or active navigation, warrants further study. Hospitals employ a spectrum of intervention models to address identified social needs, ranging from low-touch strategies, such as providing printed resource guides or automated referrals through the EMR, to high-touch interventions involving social workers or patient navigators who directly assist patients in accessing services. While low-touch approaches may expand reach and efficiency, high-touch models often yield stronger engagement and follow-up, particularly for patients with limited self-efficacy or complex needs. Future work should also examine provides resource guides to patients (eg, nurses, physicians or social workers), provider explanation quality and provider training in having motivational discussions to endorse self-efficacy.

At the system level, communication between hospitals and CBOs remains limited. Establishing direct hospital–CBO communication platforms, closed-loop referral repositories and strategies that match referrals to real community capacity can reduce delays and improve patients’ ability to follow through after discharge. Such system-level interventions are critical in helping to close the gap between screening, referral and engagement.

Screening is the first step to increase identification of HRSN within the clinical setting, healthcare accessibility and engagement with HRSN resources. Studies have assessed the impact of HRSN screening on healthcare systems and the subsequent impact on the patients those systems serve. Screening advances patient care by identifying upstream factors that affect a person’s overall health; however, screening alone cannot suffice to improve health outcomes, as patients may not connect with the referral. Indeed, identifying patient need without support solutions may well exacerbate frustration and moral distress within the healthcare team.14 Providing patients with the resources to bridge the existing gap in their social needs is a vital step, though the optimal approach remains unclear; whether it be a process fully automated through the EMR; requiring patient assessment and counselling with social workers, CHWs or patient navigators; or increasing partnerships with local organisations.15,17 Hospitals have employed a spectrum of intervention models to address identified social needs. Strategies range significantly and may include low-touch interventions, such as providing printed resource guides or automated referrals through the EMR as well as temporary housing vouchers or access to food banks.18 While other facilities may provide more high-touch interventions involving social workers or CHWs who directly assist patients in accessing services. While low-touch approaches may expand reach, high-touch models may yield stronger engagement and follow-up, particularly for patients with limited self-efficacy or complex needs. However, evidence to evaluate and compare the efficacy of these approaches remains scarce across both the inpatient and ambulatory settings.19 Programmes are highly tailored to local contexts, and there are no current national standards to drive alignment of programme structure, process or evaluation of outcomes. Each community possesses unique local resources to respond to identified needs, and queues for services vary widely.17

Yan et al conducted a systematic review assessing the effectiveness of SDOH screening measures in clinical settings across the US. Across 28 studies, there were mixed findings on the impact of HRSN screening on health outcome measures; some studies found improved health measures, such as blood pressure and cholesterol levels, while other studies found no improvement in such metrics after screening was implemented in healthcare facilities.14 This can possibly be attributed to the variability in the nature of the screening, local resource variation and different intensity of the response and interventions among patients who screened positive for social needs.

Those interventions that featured additional navigation or in-person assistance to access the HRSN referred services reported more improvement in health outcomes after the intervention compared with control groups.20 21 Furthermore, only 39% (n=11) and 32% (n=9) of the studies summarised by Yan reported health outcomes and impact on healthcare cost and utilisation, respectively, underscoring the limited breadth of evidence on HRSN screening impact on patients’ health.14 Despite mixed and limited evidence, however, it is evident that screening is a necessary (although not sufficient) step in building a bridge between identifying the patient’s needs and providing the needed assistance. The nature of such intervention may vary across medical systems. By screening universally, the focus can go beyond identifying patients’ HRSN to curate actionable plans to successfully connect them with the necessary services to improve their health.22

As another key impeding factor to referral success remains personal patient factors that act to hinder a successful connection with a CBO, more intensive involvement in tailoring resources to patients’ needs while addressing other personal factors that may serve as obstacles in referral utilisation. Patients may benefit from increased pre-discharge planning with physicians, social workers or nurses, so that a follow-up plan may be set up and patients understand the referrals. Furthermore, in order to increase patient outreach to such resources, as was a significant problem identified in this study, social services may be provided at points of care, such as food banks at clinics or medical-legal partnerships, thus creating a network of touchpoints for patients from hospital admission to community resources to minimise fallout in patients reaching out to and successfully finding transport to referred resources.18

CHW teams can also serve a role in improving referral success. Establishing better networks with CBOs and hospital CHWs may allow closer monitoring of referral utilisation as well as consistent updates of the referral system to ensure resources are most appropriate, accessible and functional for specific needs. Patients may find it easier to trust a CBO with a strong and well-established referral system with a CHW in their hospital or clinic, which may also help CBOs which are already overloaded. However, this has been presented as an implementation challenge among referral platforms, with limited community-based social services organisations using platforms to link them with healthcare systems. This suggests an increased need for increased engagement with social service partners via integrated systems that can optimise communication between CBOs and health facilities.23

Finally, CBOs struggle with staffing, funding and capacity. It is possible that closer relationships with hospital systems may help to ensure that referrals are targeted and appropriate and potentially to align patient caseload with CBO capacity. This can be further optimised by CBO network hubs, where stakeholders across communities can endorse collaborative technology to scale referrals across CBO networks to better align supply and demand of HRSN resources.7 24 By implementing such changes to encourage a more efficiently linked healthcare and social care network, patients may more effectively engage with needed resources that can help them beyond their hospital stay. This can help keep patients engaged with their healthcare team and motivated to maintain their health. Over the long run, this may help to optimise the link between clinical care delivery and social care so that healthcare utilisation and patient outcomes may improve.

Limitations

This study has potential limitations, including a small sample size of patients from an inpatient setting over a short period of time. Results may not generalise to patients engaging with the healthcare system via other means. However, as the study’s aim is to identify barriers to utilisation of community resources using a mixed-methods approach, a large dataset with subsequent quantitative analysis is not required as in other studies which aim to establish statistical associations, measure prevalence or evaluate the effectiveness of interventions. Therefore, we find that the sample is sufficient to provide both limited quantitative and contextual qualitative information to address the research question and provide rich actionable insights for health systems and the readership to incorporate. Additionally, this study faced limitations methodologically inherent in employing surveys, as patient-reported responses may be more subject to response bias. Furthermore, the discrepancy observed between quantitatively low use of resources and high perceived benefit from resources may be due to desirability bias. Despite heterogeneity among the study group, patients were only assessed across three hospitals within urban and suburban communities, thus for further generalisability, future studies must cast a wider net across various patient populations. We did not measure financial assistance status or insurance coverage as potential driver variables. We were not able to measure objectively the patients’ engagement with the follow-up CBO resource, so we could not evaluate health and healthcare outcomes from the intervention. These are common limitations among the studies of this topic to date.25

Future research avenues

This study is part of increasing efforts to address the many gaps in mitigating SDOH needs for patients, such as successfully supporting patient engagement and utilisation of resources provided by CBOs. As most research has focused on the qualitative patient experience at healthcare visits when they undergo SDOH screening and the overall increased identification of patient SDOH needs, research remains mixed and limited regarding the association between healthcare interventions for patient SDOH needs and patient health outcomes, as prior studies lack exclusion of confounding variables impacting a patient’s health and well-being.17 26 27

Conclusion

Patients with significant social needs may feel that discharge referrals to support them are helpful, but they face barriers in successfully accessing and using these resources. Indeed, low engagement with resources could be due to a disconnect between eliciting a patient’s social needs in the hospital and the patient contacting those resources. Possible barriers may include lack of awareness of resources, low self-efficacy in contacting resources, varying perception about the utility of the resources, distance to the CBO and/or limited CBO capacity. For an effective programme to support patients in mitigating the health impact of social needs, robust social risk screening and discharge follow-up practices will be needed to increase patient success in referral usage. In addition, more attention to creating closed-loop feedback systems with CBO agencies may be beneficial.
