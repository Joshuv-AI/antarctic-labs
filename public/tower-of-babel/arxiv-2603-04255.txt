Learning Read-Once Determinants and the Principal Minor Assignment Problem
Abhiram Aravind, Abhranil Chatterjee, Sumanta Ghosh, Rohit Gurjar, Roshan Raj, Chandan Saha

Abstract
A symbolic determinant under rank-one restriction computes a polynomial of the form det ( A 0 + A 1 ​ y 1 + … + A n ​ y n ) \det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n}) , where A 0 , A 1 , … , A n A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n} are square matrices over a field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} and rank ( A i ) = 1 \rank(A_{i})=1 for each i ∈ [ n ] i\in[n] . This class of polynomials has been studied extensively, since the work of Edmonds (1967), in the context of linear matroids, matching, matrix completion and polynomial identity testing. We study the following learning problem for this class: Given black-box access to an n n -variate polynomial f = det ( A 0 + A 1 ​ y 1 + … + A n ​ y n ) f=\det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n}) , where A 0 , A 1 , … , A n A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n} are unknown square matrices over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} and rank ( A i ) = 1 \rank(A_{i})=1 for each i ∈ [ n ] i\in[n] , find a square matrix B 0 B_{0} and rank-one square matrices B 1 , … , B n B_{1},\ldots,B_{n} over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} such that f = det ( B 0 + B 1 ​ y 1 + … + B n ​ y n ) f=\det(B_{0}+B_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+B_{n}y_{n}) . In this work, we give a randomized poly ( n ) \pol(n) time algorithm to solve this problem; the algorithm can be derandomized in quasi-polynomial time. To our knowledge, this is the first efficient learning algorithm for this class. As the above-mentioned class is known to be equivalent to the class of read-once determinants (RODs), we will refer to the problem as learning RODs . An ROD computes the determinant of a matrix whose entries are field constants or variables and every variable appears at most once in the matrix. Thus, the class of RODs is a rare example of a well-studied class of polynomials that admits efficient proper learning. The algorithm for learning RODs is obtained by connecting with a well-known open problem in linear algebra, namely the Principal Minor Assignment Problem (PMAP), which asks to find (if possible) a matrix having prescribed principal minors. PMAP has also been studied in machine learning to learn the kernel matrix of a determinantal point process. Here, we study a natural black-box version of PMAP: Given black-box access to an n n -variate polynomial f = det ( A + Y ) f=\det(A+Y) , where A ∈ 𝔽 n × n A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} is unknown and Y = diag ( y 1 , … , y n ) Y=\diag(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}) , find a B ∈ 𝔽 n × n B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} such that f = det ( B + Y ) f=\det(B+Y) . We show that black-box PMAP can be solved in randomized poly ( n ) \pol(n) time, and further, it is randomized polynomial-time equivalent to learning RODs. The algorithm and the reduction between the two problems can be derandomized in quasi-polynomial time. To our knowledge, no efficient algorithm to solve this black-box version of PMAP was known before. We resolve black-box PMAP by investigating a crucial property of dense matrices that we call the rank-one extension property. Understanding “cuts” of matrices with this property and designing a black-box cut-finding algorithm to solve PMAP for such matrices (using only principal minors of order 4 4 or less) constitute the technical core of this work. The insights developed along the way also help us give the first NC algorithm for the Principal Minor Equivalence problem, which asks to check if two given matrices have equal corresponding principal minors.

Introduction

Learning arithmetic circuits is a fundamental problem in algebraic complexity theory. An arithmetic circuit, which is an arithmetic analogue of a Boolean circuit, consists of addition and multiplication operations and computes a multivariate polynomial in the input variables. The
learning problem
(a.k.a.
reconstruction problem
) for a circuit class
\mathcal{C}
asks to find a circuit
C
from black-box access to a polynomial
f
that is computable by a circuit in class
\mathcal{C}
such that
C
computes
f
. Black-box access to
f
allows us to obtain the value of
f
at any chosen point in one time step. If the output circuit
C
belongs to class
\mathcal{C}
, then we say that the learning is
proper
.

Despite research spanning over two decades, efficient proper learning algorithms are known for only a handful of circuit classes. This is not surprising, as proper learning is considered a computationally hard problem for most circuit classes. Some of the important classes for which proper learning is known are sparse polynomials (that is, depth two circuits)
[
BT88
,
KS01
]
, read-once formulas (ROFs)
[
HH91
,
BHH95
,
SV14
,
MV18
]
, and read-once oblivious algebraic branching programs (ROABPs) with known variable ordering
[
BBB+00
,
KS06
]
. Algebraic branching programs (ABPs), which can be viewed as skew circuits, form a powerful model (class) of arithmetic computation. One of the most important polynomials in algebraic complexity, namely the
determinant
polynomial, can be computed by an ABP of polynomial size
[
Ber84
,
MV97
]
. Due to a known reduction from ABPs to symbolic determinants
[
Val79
]
, it can be assumed that an ABP computes a polynomial of the form
\det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n})
, where
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
are variables and
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
are
r\times r
matrices over an underlying field
\mathbb{F}
.

A subclass of ABPs that has received a lot of attention in the past is
symbolic determinants under rank-one restriction
. A polynomial
f
is computable by this class if
f
can be expressed as
\det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n})
, where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
are rank-one matrices; there is no restriction on
A_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
. This class has been studied extensively over the past several decades in the context of matroid problems, matrix completion, and polynomial identity testing
[
Edm67
,
Edm68
,
Edm79
,
Lov89
,
Mur93
,
Gee99
,
HKM05
,
IKS10
,
IKQS15
,
GT20
]
. The border complexity of this class has also been recently investigated
[
CGGR23
]
. In this work, we study the learning problem for this class.

Problem 1.1
.

Given black-box access to an
n
-variate polynomial
f=\det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n})
, where
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
are unknown square matrices over
\mathbb{F}
and
\rank(A_{i})=1
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, find a square matrix
B_{0}
and rank-one square matrices
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}
such that
f=\det(B_{0}+B_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+B_{n}y_{n})
.

It is known that a polynomial computable by a symbolic determinant under rank-one restriction is also computable by a
read-once determinant
(ROD) of small size (see Lemma 4.3 in
[
GT20
]
).

Read-once determinants.
An ROD is the determinant of a matrix
M
whose entries are variables or field constants such that every variable appears at most once in
M
. The dimension of
M
defines the
size
of the ROD. In the converse direction, it is easy to see that an ROD is (by definition) a symbolic determinant under rank-one restriction. As the two classes, RODs and symbolic determinants under rank-one restriction, are essentially the same, we will refer to Problem
1.1
as
Learning RODs
. Both ROABPs and RODs are more expressive classes than ROFs
[
Val79
]
. But, unlike ROABPs, RODs are
not universal
in the sense that not every polynomial can be expressed as an ROD. For example, the elementary symmetric polynomial and the permanent polynomial cannot be expressed as ROD
[
AJ15
]
. On the other hand, the determinant and the iterated matrix multiplication polynomials (both of which define complete polynomial families for the important algebraic complexity class
\mathsf{VBP}
) admit small RODs
[
Val79
]
. It is worth noting that any ROABP computing the determinant has exponential size
[
Nis91
]
, and so, learning ROABPs is not a viable way to learn RODs, even improperly. Thus, Problem
1.1
is interesting in its own right in algebraic complexity theory. Besides, there is another strong motivation to study this problem which comes from its connection with the
Principal Minor Assignment Problem
(PMAP).

Principal Minor Assignment Problem.
PMAP is a well-known open problem in linear algebra
[
HS02
,
GT06b
]
. Given a list
\calP=(p_{S})_{\varnothing\neq S\in 2^{[n]}}
of
2^{n}-1
field elements, PMAP asks to find (if possible) a matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that the principal minor of
A
defined by the rows and columns indexed by
S
is
p_{S}
for all nonempty
S\in 2^{[n]}
. If
\calP
is promised to be the list of principal minors of some unknown matrix
A
, then it is typically assumed that
\calP
is given as an oracle that returns
p_{S}
when queried with
S
; the goal is to minimize the number of queries to the oracle
\calP
. Efficient algorithms for PMAP that ask only
\pol(n)
queries to the oracle are known if the matrix
A
to be reconstructed is special, such as a symmetric or magnitude-symmetric matrix
[
RKT15
,
Bru18
,
BU24
]
or a certain kind of dense matrix
[
GT06b
]
. But the problem of learning an arbitrary
A
in
\pol(n)
time using oracle access to the list
\calP
remains open. Although PMAP is interesting in its own right in linear algebra, another motivation to study it comes from learning
determinantal point processes
(DPPs).

A DPP associated with an
n\times n
kernel matrix
K
is a probability distribution over subsets of a set of
n
items, where the probability of getting any subset
T\subseteq[n]
is given by the principal minor of
K
indexed by
T
(see
[
KT12
]
).
DPPs are useful in settings where one needs to sample a diverse set of objects. The learning question for DPP asks to learn the associated kernel matrix, given samples from the distribution. Efficient PMAP algorithms for symmetric matrices
[
Oed11
,
RKT15
]
played a crucial role in making significant progress on learning symmetric DPPs
[
UBMR17
]
as the principal minors of the kernel can be well estimated from the observed samples using a method of moments. A similar progress via PMAP is also made for learning signed DPPs in which the underlying kernel is magnitude-symmetric
[
Bru18
,
BU24
]
.

Now, observe that the coefficients of a polynomial
f=\det(A+Y)
, where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
, are the principal minors of
A
. This naturally motivates us to study the following
black-box version
of the Principal Minor Assignment Problem.

Problem 1.2
.

Given black-box access to an
n
-variate polynomial
f=\det(A+Y)
, where
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
is unknown and
Y=\diag(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, find a
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
f=\det(B+Y)
.

As the black-box allows us to recover a principal minor of
A
in
\pol(n)
time using interpolation (assuming
|\mathbb{F}|>n
), we will refer to Problem
1.2
as
Black-box PMAP
. Note that
\det(A+Y)
is an ROD, and so, Problem
1.2
is clearly related to Problem
1.1
. Also, both are proper learning problems.

Observe that the output
B
in Problem
1.2
has the same corresponding principal minors as
A
. Two matrices are said to be
principal minor equivalent
(PME) if they have equal corresponding principal minors of all orders. The decision problem of testing if two given matrices are PME is naturally related to PMAP. A characterization of PME was known for some special cases like symmetric matrices
[
ES80
]
, matrices with no cuts
[
HL84
,
Loe86
]
, and few other classes related to skew-symmetry
[
BC15
,
BC16
,
BCCL21
]
.
Recently, a characterization of PME was proven for the general case
[
CGGR25
]
,
where a polynomial-time deterministic algorithm was also given to test if two given matrices are PME.
Informally speaking, their characterization proves that two matrices are PME if and only if they are related by a sequence of simple operations – called
cut-transpose
(see Section
2.5
).
The algorithm starts from one of the matrices, keeps applying appropriate cut-transpose operations and reaches the other matrix in at most
2n
steps or says that they are not PME.
The algorithm seems to be inherently sequential, raising an interesting question:

Problem 1.3
.

Is there an efficient parallel (NC) algorithm for testing PME?

The class NC contains problems for which we have parallel algorithms running in poly-logarithmic time using only a polynomial number of processors. Interestingly, if we allow randomization then there is a simple parallel algorithm to test PME via a reduction to polynomial identity testing (PIT). As indicated before, two matrices
A
and
B
are PME if and only if the polynomial identity
\det(A+Y)=\det(B+Y)
holds.
A simple randomized algorithm to check a polynomial identity is to plug in random numbers for variables and check equality
[
Sch80
,
DL78
,
Zip79
]
, which succeeds with high probability.
Note that the determinant can be computed efficiently in parallel (NC) (see, e.g.,
[
Csa76
,
Ber84
]
), and thus, the above algorithm is in RNC.
While derandomization of PIT is a challenging open question, we can ask whether we can derandomize the specific instance arising from PME and get an NC algorithm.

In this work, we give randomized polynomial-time algorithms to solve Problem
1.1
(Learning RODs) and Problem
1.2
(Black-box PMAP) (see Theorem
1.1
). In fact, we show a randomized polynomial-time equivalence between the two problems (in Section
3
), and then solve the latter in randomized polynomial time (in Sections
4
-
7
). The steps involving randomization (in the equivalence and the algorithm) can be derandomized in quasi-polynomial time. Thus, the class defined by RODs is one of the rare classes of polynomials for which efficient proper learning is possible. Also, to our knowledge, no efficient algorithm for any natural black-box version of PMAP was known prior to this work. Further, we give an NC algorithm for testing PME, thereby solving Problem
1.3
(see Theorem
1.2
and Section
8
). These algorithms are obtained by studying the principal minors of order at most
4
of dense matrices satisfying a key property that we call the “
rank-one extension
” property (see Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
).

1.1
Our results

We now state our results formally. The results hold under a mild condition on the size of the underlying field
\mathbb{F}
. We also assume that a field operation takes unit time, and square root computation over
\mathbb{F}
can be done in polynomial time

Over
\mathbb{Q}

\mathbb{F}_{q}

Theorem 1.1

(Main theorem)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
|\mathbb{F}|>n^{6}
.

1.

(Learning RODs)
There is a
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
time randomized algorithm that takes input black-box access to an
n
-variate polynomial
f=\det(A_{0}+A_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}y_{n})
, where
A_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
is unknown and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
are unknown rank-one matrices and
r\in\mathbb{N}
is also unknown, and outputs (with high probability) a matrix
B_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
and rank-one matrices
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
f=\det(B_{0}+B_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+B_{n}y_{n})
. The algorithm can be derandomized in quasi-polynomial time.

2.

(Black-box PMAP)
There is a
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
time randomized algorithm that takes input black-box access to an
n
-variate polynomial
f=\det(A+Y)
, where
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
is unknown and
Y=\diag(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, and outputs (with high probability) a matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
f=\det(B+Y)
. The algorithm can be derandomized in quasi-polynomial time.

An algorithm for PMAP.
The algorithm for Black-box PMAP immediately implies a
2^{n}\cdot\pol(n)
time randomized algorithm for PMAP as the input list
\calP=(p_{S})_{\varnothing\neq S\in 2^{[n]}}
can be used to implement a query to the black-box in
2^{n}\cdot\pol(n)
time. Note that the complexity is almost linear in the size of the input
\calP
which is the best one can hope for if it is not promised that
\cal{P}
is consistent with the principal minors of some matrix. In the absence of such a promise, one needs to check if
\cal{P}
is consistent with the principal minors of the reconstructed matrix. As the size of
\cal{P}
is
2^{n}-1
and principal minors of a matrix can be computed in
O(2^{n})
time
[
GT06a
]
, any algorithm for PMAP takes
\Omega(2^{n})
time. In this sense, we give a
near optimal
time algorithm for PMAP. To our knowledge, no
\tilde{O}(2^{n})
time algorithm was known for PMAP before this work. A naive reduction to polynomial solvability incurs a cost of
n^{O(n^{4})}
over
\mathbb{C}

[
Ier89
]
, as there are
n^{2}
unknown entries of a matrix.

If it is promised that
\cal{P}
is the list of principal minors of some matrix, then it is conceivable that far fewer queries to the oracle
\cal{P}
suffice. For instance, if
\cal{P}
is the list of principal minors of a symmetric matrix, then
O(n^{2})
queries are necessary and sufficient
[
RKT15
]
. The algorithm is based on
[
Oed11
]
that characterizes the variety of principal minors of symmetric matrices. Unfortunately, the ideal of polynomial relations satisfied by the principal minors of a generic
n\times n
matrix is not well-understood (except for
n=4

[
LS09
]
). Nevertheless, we show that PMAP for matrices satisfying a certain genericity condition can be solved using
\pol(n)
queries (see Theorem
1.4
).

Theorem 1.2

(PME is in NC)
.

Testing principal minor equivalence of two given matrices is in NC.

Definition 1.1

(Rank-one extension and property
\mathcal{R}
)
.

A matrix is
dense
if all its off-diagonal entries are nonzero. We say that a matrix
A
satisfies the
rank-one extension
property if the following condition holds: if
\rank(A[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}])=1
for any four distinct indices
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
, then
there exists a subset
S\subseteq[n]
with
i,j\in S
and
k,\ell\in\overline{S}
such that
\rank(A[S,\overline{S}])=1
. We say that a matrix satisfies
property
\mathcal{R}
if it is dense and satisfies the rank-one extension property.

Understanding and exploiting the rank-one extension property is at the heart of the technical contributions of this work. The following theorems demonstrate the usefulness of this property.

Theorem 1.3

(Sufficiency of PME up to order
4
)
.

Let
A
be a matrix satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
.
Let
B
be another matrix of the same size such that
A
and
B
have equal corresponding principal minors of order at most 4.
Then
A
and
B
have equal corresponding principal minors of all orders.

What makes property
\mathcal{R}
so effective is that Black-box PMAP for arbitrary matrices randomly reduces to that for matrices satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
(see Section
4
). This fact, coupled with the next theorem (whose proof sketch appears in Section
1.2.1
), gives us a proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.4

(PMAP for matrices with property
\mathcal{R}
)
.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that, given oracle access to all the principal minors of order at most
4
of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
, reconstructs a matrix
B
such that the corresponding principal minors of
A
and
B
are equal.

Remarks.

1.

The above theorem solves PMAP for matrices satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
in
\pol(n)
time as we need oracle access to only the principal minors of order at most
4
. In comparison, we use black-box access to
\det(A+Y)
(in Theorem
1.1
) to solve
Black-box PMAP
for an
arbitrary

A
. It turns out that the proof of Theorem
1.1
requires only oracle access to the principal minors of
A
and that of
A+D
for a random diagonal matrix
D
.

2.

A random matrix satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
as it does not have a
2\times 2
submatrix of rank one with high probability. So, property
\mathcal{R}
defines a genericity condition. In
[
GT06b
]
, a
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
time PMAP algorithm was given for matrices satisfying another genericity condition, namely
off-diagonal full (ODF)
. Property
\mathcal{R}
and ODF seem incomparable (see Section
1.3
). However, Black-box PMAP for aribitrary matrices reduces to PMAP for matrices with property
\mathcal{R}
(see Section
4
). It is unclear if the same is true for matrices satisfying ODF.

3.

A
random kernel
of a DPP can be defined as follows: Let
n>1
,
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
and
\mu\in[0,\lambda/(n-1))
. Let
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a diagonal matrix with
D[i,i]\in[\lambda,1-\lambda]
for all
i\in[n]
, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
whose entries are randomly and independently chosen from
[-1,1]
. As there is a DPP with kernel
K=D+\mu A
(see Section 2.2 in
[
Bru18
]
), we can say that
K
is a random kernel. Note that
K
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
as
A
is random. In this sense, a random kernel can be learnt from its principal minor oracle in
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
time using Theorem
1.4
.

1.2
Proof ideas

1.2.1
Proof sketch for Theorem

and Theorem

As an initial first step towards proving Theorem
1.1
, we establish a randomised polynomial-time reduction from Learning RODs to Black-box PMAP (in fact, we show an equivalence between the two problems in Section

). Then, we use the three broad steps outlined in the algorithm below to solve Black-box PMAP. The algorithm uses the notion of

of a matrix: A set

is a cut of a matrix

if the ranks of the submatrices

and

are at most one. The
principal minor oracle
of a matrix

takes input a nonempty set

and outputs the principal minor of

defined by the rows and columns indexed by
S
.

, where

A matrix

such that

.

1:

Reduce the problem to learning
M\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}

, where

(see Algorithm

in Section

).

Use the

) to reduce the problem further to learning

from the principal minor oracle of

, where

.)

The usefulness of the sequence

(1)

1.2.2
Proof sketch for Theorem

Consider two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
that have equal corresponding principal minors of order at most 4. Suppose further that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Our goal is to show that
A
and
B
have equal corresponding principal minors of all orders up to
n
. We begin with the case where
A
has no cut, which constitutes the main technical component of the proof.

1.

Suppose
A
has no cut. We prove the principal minor equivalence of
A
and
B
by induction on
n
. The base case
n=4
is immediate.

For any
k\in[n]
, let
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
denote the principal submatrices of
A
and
B
, respectively, obtained by deleting the
k
-th row and column.
Let
I\subseteq[n]
be the set of indices for which
A_{i}
is diagonally similar to
B_{i}
or
B_{i}^{T}
.
Following Lemma
2.2

[
HL84
, Lemma 1]
, it suffices to prove
|I|\geq 5
as it would imply that
A
is diagonally similar to
B
or
B^{T}
. Consequently,
A
and
B
are principal minor equivalent
[
Loe86
, Theorem 1]
(also see Lemma
2.3
).

For
n\geq 5
, we show that there exist at least three indices

such that each
A_{i_{j}}
has no cut. We crucially use property
\mathcal{R}
to show the existence of these indices (see
Corollary

5.1
). By the induction hypothesis,
A_{i_{j}}
is principal minor equivalent to
B_{i_{j}}
. Since
A_{i_{j}}
has no cut, it again follows from
[
Loe86
, Theorem 1]
(also see
Lemma

2.3
) that
A_{i_{j}}
is diagonally similar to
B_{i_{j}}
or its transpose.

We have
|I|\geq 3
as
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in I
. We then use the principal minor equivalence of
A
and
B
up to order 4 and property
\mathcal{R}
of
A
to prove that if
|I|
were
3
or
4
, then
A
would necessarily have a cut (see
Lemma

5.5
, and
Lemma

5.6
). It contradicts our assumption that
A
has no cut. A similar result appears in
[
Loe86
, Theorem 2 and Theorem 4]
with the assumption that
A
and
B
are dense and principal minor equivalent of all orders. On the other hand, we show the result still holds even if we assume the matrices to be principal minor equivalent up to only order
4
and additionally
A
satisfies rank-one extension property.

2.

If
A
has a cut, we consider a minimal cut
S
of
A
. We first show that either
S
is also a cut of
B
or there is another matrix
\widetilde{B}
principal minor equivalent to
B
such that
S
is a cut of
\widetilde{B}
(see
Lemma

2.7
,
Lemma

5.8
). Some weaker versions of
Lemma

2.7
and
Lemma

5.8
appear in
[
CGGR25
, Lemma 3.5 and Lemma 3.6]
. We observe that almost the same proofs even work for our case. The argument then relies on the decomposition lemma (see
Lemma

5.7
) that asserts the equivalence of the following two statements:

(a)

A
is principal minor equivalent to
B
.

(b)

For some common cut

of
A
and
B
,

is principal minor equivalent to
B[S+t]
and

is principal minor equivalent to

for any

.

We use ideas from the proof of

to prove the decomposition lemma. With the above setup, the inductive proof proceeds where the base case is either

1.2.3
Proof sketch for Theorem
1.2

To design an NC algorithm for principal minor equivalence, we first consider the special case where one of the matrices satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. In this case,
Theorem

1.3
immediately yields an NC algorithm, since we can, in parallel, verify whether
\det(A[S])=\det(B[S])
for all
S\subseteq[n]
of size at most
4
to determine the principal minor equivalence of
A
and
B
. To solve the general case, we provide an NC reduction to the above special case through the following steps.

1.

The matrices
A
and
B
are principal minor equivalent if and only if their maximal irreducible submatrices induce the same partition of the index set
[n]
, and the corresponding principal submatrices are themselves principal minor equivalent (see
Lemma

2.1
). The maximal irreducible submatrices correspond to the strongly connected components of a graph associated with the input matrix (see
2.1
), which can be computed in NC using standard parallel algorithms for finding strongly connected components
[
GM88
,
CV89
]
.

2.

Let
M^{\mathrm{adj}}
denote the
adjugate
of a matrix
M
and
Y
be an
n\times n
diagonal matrix with distinct indeterminates on its diagonal. Then,
A
is principal minor equivalent to
B
if and only if
(A+Y)^{\ad}
and
(B+Y)^{\ad}
are principal minor equivalent (
[
HL84
, Lemma 4]
). Furthermore, we show that
(A+Y)^{\ad}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
(see
4.1
).

Our goal is now to compute, in NC, a diagonal matrix
D
such that
(A+D)^{\ad}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Consequently, it reduces the principal minor equivalence testing of two irreducible matrices
A
and
B
to testing principal minor equivalence of
(A+D)^{\ad}
and
(B+D)^{\ad}
. However, we do not know of an NC procedure to compute such
D
directly. Instead, we first compute, in NC, a diagonal matrix
D_{1}
such that all entries of
A_{1}=(A+D_{1})^{\ad}
are nonzero (
Lemma

8.1
). Next we compute another diagonal matrix
D_{2}
in NC such that
A_{2}=(A_{1}+D_{2})^{\ad}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
(
Lemma

8.2
). Since the determinant of a matrix can be computed in NC
[
Csa76
,
BCP83
]
, all these matrices can be computed in NC.

1.3
Comparison with previous works

In this section, we provide comparisons with known results that are directly relevant to our work.

Randomized polynomial-time learning algorithms for ROFs were given in
[
HH91
,
BHH95
]
. These algorithms were derandomized in polynomial-time in
[
SV14
,
MV18
]
using hitting-sets for ROFs. Similarly, the randomized polynomial-time learning algorithm for ROABPs (with known variable ordering) in
[
BBB+00
,
KS06
]
was derandomized in quasi-polynomial time in
[
FS13
]
using hitting-sets for ROABPs. If the variable ordering in unknown, then ROABP learning is hard
[
BDGT24
]
. The class of RODs is more powerful than ROFs (by a reduction in
[
Val79
]
). It is easy to show that the degree-
3
determinant polynomial, which has an ROD of size
3
, cannot be computed by an ROF. But, RODs do not capture ROABPs – the elementary symmetric polynomial, which has an ROABP of polynomial size, cannot be expressed as an ROD
[
AJ15
]
. On the other hand, any ROABP that computes the degree-
n
determinant polynomial has size
2^{\Omega(n)}

[
Nis91
]
. So, the learning algorithms for ROFs and ROABPs do not imply that RODs are efficiently learnable. Furthermore, the partial derivatives and evaluation dimension based techniques used for learning ROFs and ROABPs do not seem effective for learning RODs. However, as is the case for ROF and ROABP learning, the hitting-set for RODs in
[
GT17
]
can be used to derandomize our ROD learning algorithm in quasi-polynomial time (see Section
4.5
).

The PMAP problem was known to have a polynomial-time solution for the following classes of matrices: symmetric matrices
[
Oed11
,
RKT15
]
, magnitude-symmetric matrices (
|A(i,j)|=|A(j,i)|
)
[
UBMR17
,
BU24
]
, and ODF (off-diagonal full) matrices
[
GT06b
]
.
The property of symmetry or magnitude-symmetry is crucially used in the first two algorithms, and thus, their approaches do not seem directly applicable for the non-symmetric case.
The ODF property, introduced in
[
GT06b
]
, involves a technical condition which states that at every step of their algorithm, the solution should be unique.
Though this condition is true generically, it does not seem to correspond to any structural property of the given matrix that will gurantee an efficient solution for PMAP.
On the other hand, property
\mathcal{R}
, for which we solve PMAP (
Theorem

1.4
) is a generic structural condition on the matrix.
Also note that by definition, ODF matrices cannot have a cut, while property
\mathcal{R}
does not have such a restriction.
Moreover, we show that blackbox PMAP for arbitrary matrices reduces to PMAP for matrices with property
\mathcal{R}
.
It is not clear if there is such a reduction for ODF matrices.

As mentioned in Section
1.1
, identifying property
\mathcal{R}
and exploiting it for PMAP is one of our key technical ideas.
Though property
\mathcal{R}
does not appear explicitly in the literature, its definition is inspired by the proofs
found in the works of Hartfiel-Loewy
[
HL84
, Theorem 2]
and
Loewy
[
Loe86
, Lemma 6]
.
The same is true for the result that for any irreducible matrix
A
,
(A+D)^{-1}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
for a random diagonal matrix
D
(
Lemma

4.2
).

Our solution for PMAP builds on the fact that for matrices with property
\mathcal{R}
, PME up to order 4 implies PME (Theorem
1.3
).
Similar results were known for other classes of matrices, namely, skew-symmetric matrices with some other conditions
[
BC16
,
BC15
]
, generalized tournament matrices
[
BCCL21
]
, and magnitude symmetric matrices
[
BU24
]
.
In contrast to our algebraic approach, most of these results rely fundamentally on graph-theoretic techniques.

These results also imply efficient parallel algorithms for testing PME for these classes: in parallel, one can test equality of all corresponding principal minors up to order 3 or 4.
For the general case, no such parallel algorithms were known.
The only known efficient algorithm for PME testing
[
CGGR25
]
involves finding a sequence of cut-transpose operations, which does not seem parallelizable.
Here we make use of property
\mathcal{R}
and Theorem
1.3
to get a parallel algorithm for PME testing for two arbitrary matrices (
Theorem

1.2
).

1.4
Other related works

Proper learning is known for a few other classes of polynomials. These classes are defined by restricted depth three and depth four arithmetic circuits having bounded top fan-in
[
Shp09
,
KS09
,
GKL12
,
Sin16
,
BSV20
,
BSV21
]
. Like RODs, these models are not universal. The learning algorithm for ROABPs implies improper learning algorithms for two important subclasses of depth three circuits, namely depth three powering circuits (symmetric tensors) and set-multilinear depth three circuits (tensors). However, learning general depth three or depth four circuits (even improperly) in the worst case is presumably hard (see the discussions about hardness of learning in
[
KS19
,
GKS20
]
). Consequently, the complexity of learning has been investigated for various circuit models, including depth three and depth four circuits, in the average-case
[
GKL11
,
GKQ14
,
KNS19
,
KS19
,
GKS20
,
BGKS22
]
. Note that a natural way to define an average-case version of the PMAP problem is to let the input matrix be chosen randomly. Since a random matrix satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
with high probability (see the second remark after Theorem
1.4
), we have a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for this average-case version of PMAP (by Theorem
1.4
).

2.1
Reducible and Irreducible matrix

Definition 2.1

(Reducible and Irreducible matrix)
.

A matrix is called
reducible
if it can be written as a block upper triangular matrix after permuting the rows and the corresponding columns. A matrix that is not reducible is called
irreducible
.

Equivalently, if we replace the nonzero off-diagonal entries with one and the diagonal entries with zero, then a reducible matrix corresponds to the adjacency matrix of a directed graph having more than one strongly connected component.

From the above definition, it is easy to see that any matrix
A
with all nonzero off-diagonal entries is an irreducible matrix. The above definition directly gives us the following observation.

Observation 2.1
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
such that the row and columns of
A
are indexed by
[n]
. Let
G_{A}
be a directed graph defined as follows: the vertex set in
[n]
, and a tuple
(i,j)
is an edge of
G_{A}
if and only if
i\neq j
and
A[i,j]\neq 0
. Let
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{s}
be the strongly connected components of
A
. Then, after permuting the rows and the corresponding columns, the matrix
A
can be made a block upper triangular matrix, and the diagonal blocks
A[I_{1}],A[I_{2}],\ldots,A[I_{s}]
are irreducible matrices.

For two reducible matrices
A
and
B
, the next lemma helps to reduce the testing of whether
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
to multiple instances of testing whether two irreducible matrices have the same corresponding principal minors. The following lemma is a direct consequence of
[
Ahm23
, Corollary 5.4]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Suppose that after permuting the rows and the corresponding columns,
A
can be written as a block upper triangular matrix with
s
diagonal blocks
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{s}
where each
A_{i}
is irreducible and the rows and columns of
A_{i}
are indexed by set
T_{i}\subseteq[n]
. Then,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
if and only if the following holds:

1.

After permuting some rows and the corresponding columns,
B
can be written as a block upper triangular matrix with
s
diagonal blocks
B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{s}
such that each
B_{i}
is irreducible and the rows and columns of
B_{i}
are indexed by the same sets
T_{i}
.

2.

For each
i\in[s]
,
A_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B_{i}
.

2.2
Diagonal similarity

Suppose that
A
and
B
are
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
. We say that
A
is
diagonally similar
to
B
, denoted by
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B
, if there exists an invertible diagonal matrix
D
such that
B=D^{-1}AD
. If no such matrix exists, we use
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{\neq}}B
to denote that
A
is
not
diagonally similar to
B
. We say that
A
is
diagonally equivalent
to
B
, denoted by
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
, if
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B
or
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B^{T}
. For a matrix with nonzero off-diagonal entries, we make the following observation.

Observation 2.2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
with nonzero off-diagonal entries. Then, for every
i\in[n]
, there exists a unique
n\times n
matrix
M_{i}
such that

M_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}A\text{ and }M_{i}[i,j]=1\ \ \forall\,j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}.

For a fixed
i\in[n]
, the matrix
M_{i}
in the above observation can be identified as a
canonical representative
of all matrices that are diagonally similar to
A
. We next define two subsets
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
of
[n]
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)

\displaystyle:=\{i\in[n]\,\mid\,A[[n]\setminus\{i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[[n]\setminus\{i\}]\}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)

\displaystyle:=\{i\in[n]\,\mid\,A[[n]\setminus\{i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[[n]\setminus\{i\}]^{T}\}

(3)

From these two subsets, we have the following result.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
HL84
]
, Lemma 1)
.

Let
n\geq 4
be an integer. Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Suppose that all the off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero. Then,

1.

if
|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)|\geq 3
then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B
.

2.

if
|\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|\geq 3
then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B^{T}
.

3.

if
|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|\geq 5
then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B

2.3
Principal minor equivalence

Suppose that
A
and
B
are two
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
. Then, the matrices
A
and
B
are said to be
principal minor equivalent
if all the corresponding principal minors of
A
and
B
are equal, i.e., for all
S\subseteq[n]
,
\det A[S]=\det B[S]
. We use
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
to denote that
A
is principal minor equivalent to
B
, and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,k)
to denote that all the corresponding principal minors of
A
and
B
of order
at most

k
are equal, that is, for all
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leq k
,
\det A[S]=\det B[S]
. By
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,\geq k)
, we denote that all the corresponding principal minors of
A
and
B
of order
at least

k
are equal, that is, for all
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\geq k
,
\det A[S]=\det B[S]
.

We now discuss the relationship between diagonal equivalence and principal minor equivalence. To begin, we state the following observation.

Observation 2.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be an
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
such that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
. Then,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
.

We next consider the converse of the above observation. Hartfiel and Loewy
[
HL84
, Theorem 3]
showed that if
A
and
B
are irreducible matrices of size
n=2
or
3
, then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
implies that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
. Subsequently, Loewy
[
Loe86
, Theorem 1]
proved that for irreducible matrices
A
and
B
with
A
having no cut,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
implies that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
. Combining these results, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
such that
A
is irreducible and
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
. Then,

1.

if
n=2
or
3
, then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B

2.

if
n\geq 4
and
A
has no cut, then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
.

2.4
Cut of a matrix

Definition 2.2

(Cut of a matrix)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
with
n\geq 4
. A subset
X
of
[n]
is called a
cut
of the matrix
A
, if
2\leq|X|\leq n-2
, and the rank of the submatrices
A[X,\overline{X}]
and
A[\overline{X},X]
are at most one.

In particular, if
A
is an irreducible matrix, then
\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=\rank A[\overline{X},X]=1
.

The following lemma demonstrates how to derive new cuts from an existing collection of cuts.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
with
n\geq 2
and all the off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two subsets of
[n]
such that both
X_{1}\cap X_{2}
and
\overline{X}_{1}\cap\overline{X}_{2}
are nonempty. Let
\rank A[X_{1},\overline{X}_{1}]=\rank A[X_{2},\overline{X}_{2}]=1
. Then,

\rank A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},\overline{X}_{1}\cup\overline{X}_{2}]=\rank A[X_{1}\cup X_{2},\overline{X}_{1}\cap\overline{X}_{2}]=1.

Proof.

From the lemma hypothesis, we have

\rank A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},\overline{X}_{1}]=\rank A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},\overline{X}_{2}]=1

Let
k\in\overline{X}_{1}\cap\overline{X}_{2}
. Then, the above condition implies that for all
j\in\overline{X}_{1}\cup\overline{X}_{2}
, the column
A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},j]
is a scalar multiple of
A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},k]
; that is,

A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},j]=cA[X_{1}\cap X_{2},k]\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{F}.

Hence, it follows that
\rank A[X_{1}\cap X_{2},\overline{X}_{1}\cup\overline{X}_{2}]=1
. A similar argument shows that
\rank A[X_{1}\cup X_{2},\overline{X}_{1}\cap\overline{X}_{2}]=1.

∎

Suppose that
A
is an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
. Then, a family
\mathcal{A}(A)
of ordered pair of disjoint subsets of
[n]
is defined as follows

\mathcal{A}(A):=\left\{(S,\overline{S})\,\mid\,S\subseteq[n],\ 2\leq|S|\leq n-2,\ \rank A[S,\overline{S}]=1\right\}

(4)

We next consider the following two subsets of
[n]
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(A)

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in[n]\,\mid\,\exists\,X\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}\text{ s.t. }(X+i,\overline{X})\in\mathcal{A}(A)\text{ and }(\overline{X}+i,X)\in\mathcal{A}(A)\right\}

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}(A)

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in[n]\,\mid\,\exists\,X\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}\text{ s.t. }(X,\overline{X}+i)\in\mathcal{A}(A)\text{ and }(\overline{X},X+i)\in\mathcal{A}(A)\right\}

(6)

Note that in the above equations,
\overline{X}=[n]\setminus(X+i)
. From the above definitions, Loewy
[
Loe86
]
showed the following lemma:

Lemma 2.5

(
[
Loe86
]
, Theorem A.1)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
such that
A
is irreducible and has no cut. Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}(A)
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}(A)
be defined as
Eq.

5
and
Eq.

6
, respectively. Then,

|\mathcal{B}_{1}(A)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(A)|\leq n-3.

2.5
Cut-transpose operation

Motivated by
[
Ahm23
, Lemma 4.5]
,
[
CGGR25
]
introduced the concept of the
cut-transpose operation
and provided a characterization of principal minor equivalence using this operation. Below, we describe the definition and some results that will be useful in our setting. For further details, see
[
CGGR25
, Section 2.5]
.

Definition 2.3

(
[
CGGR25
]
, Definition 2.10)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
irreducible matrix, and
X\subseteq[n]
be a cut of
A
. Let
q,u\in\mathbb{F}^{|\overline{X}|}
such that
q^{T}
is the first nonzero row of
A[X,\overline{X}]
, and
u
is the first nonzero column of
A[\overline{X},X]
. Let
p,v\in\mathbb{F}^{|X|}
such that
A[X,\overline{X}]=p\cdot q^{T}
and
A[\overline{X},X]=u\cdot v^{T}
. That is,

A=\begin{pmatrix}A[X]&p\cdot q^{T}\\
&\\
u\cdot v^{T}&A[\overline{X}]\end{pmatrix}

Then, the
cut-transpose operation on
A
with respect to
X
, denoted by
\tw(A,X)
, transforms
A
to the following matrix:

\tw(A,X)=\begin{pmatrix}A[X]&p\cdot u^{T}\\
&\\
q\cdot v^{T}&A[\overline{X}]^{T}\end{pmatrix}

Like diagonal equivalence (see
Section

2.2
), the principal minors of a matrix preserved under the cut-transpose operation.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
irreducible matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Let
X\subseteq[n]
be a cut of the matrix
A
. Then,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}\tw(A,X)
.

For a proof see
[
CGGR24
, Appendix A]
(a full version of
[
CGGR25
]
). The next lemma describes a relationship between the cuts of two matrices whose corresponding principal minors up to order
4
are equal.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices with nonzero off-diagonal entries such that
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
and
S=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
is a cut of
A
. Then either
S
is a cut of
B
, or for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, the set
X_{i}
, defined as

X_{i}=\{t\in\overline{S}\,\mid\,A[S+t]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[S+t]\}\cup\{s_{i}\},

is a cut of
B
and
S
is a cut in
\tw(B,X_{i})
.

A similar result was shown in
[
CGGR25
, Lemma 3.5]
, with the key difference that their assumption is stronger: they require
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
, whereas the lemma above only assumes
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Nonetheless, the proof remains essentially the same. A close reading of their argument reveals that only the equality of principal minors of order at most 4 is used. For full details, refer to the proof of
[
CGGR25
, Lemma 3.5]
.

2.6
Black-box evaluation with inverted inputs

In
Theorem

3.1
and
Lemma

4.3
, we have black-box access to polynomials such that some of the inputs to the black-box are inverted. As an example, given black-box access to a degree
d
polynomial
f(y)
, consider the black-box for the polynomial
g(y,z)=z^{d}f(\frac{y}{z})
constructed using the black-box for
f
. The inverse of
z
is an input to this black-box for
g
. Since
g
is a polynomial that can be evaluated at all points, we need to be able to evaluate the black-box for
g
at points where
z
is zero.

To generalise, suppose we have a degree
d
polynomial
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m},z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
such that the inverses of
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
are inputs to a black-box. Suppose further that we are trying to evaluate
f
at a point where
k\leq n
of the
z_{i}
variables are set to zero. Assume without loss of generality the point is
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n-k},0,0,\dots,0)
. We can set the variables
z_{n-k+1},z_{n-k+2},\dots,z_{n}
to a new variable
t
to get

f(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n-k},t,t,\dots,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}c_{i}t^{i}\text{.}

Let
g(t)=f(a_{1},\dots,a_{m},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-k},t,\dots,t)
. Note that
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{m},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-k},0,\dots,0)=g(0)=c_{0}
is the value we need to compute. If
|\mathbb{F}|>d+1
, we can find
d+1
distinct non-zero values
\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{d}
and use interpolation to compute
c_{0}
:

\begin{pmatrix}g(\beta_{0})\\
g(\beta_{1})\\
\vdots\\
g(\beta_{d})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta_{0}&\beta_{0}^{2}&\cdots&\beta_{0}^{d}\\
1&\beta_{1}&\beta_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\beta_{1}^{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\beta_{d}&\beta_{d}^{2}&\cdots&\beta_{d}^{d}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}\\
c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{d}\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

On the right is a Vandermonde matrix. We can then express
c_{0}
as a linear combination
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}g(\beta_{i})
for
\alpha_{i}
’s computable from
\beta_{i}
’s. This requires
d+1
queries to the black-box for
f
.

Observation 2.4
.

Suppose we have black-box access to a degree
d
polynomial
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
where some of the inputs to the black-box are inverted variables. Then if
|\mathbb{F}|>d+1
, we can evaluate the polynomial at any point using
d+1
queries to the black-box.

Remarks.

1.

For multilinear polynomials,
d\leq n
and we would require at most
n+1
queries.

2.

The values
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{d}
only depend on
\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{d}
and thus can be computed in advance.

3
Equivalence between
Learning RODs
and
Black-box PMAP

In this section, we show a randomised polynomial-time equivalence between learning read-once determinants and the black-box principal minor assignment problem. We first show show a randomised polynomial-time reduction from learning polynomials of the form
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det(A_{0}+\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{i}y_{i})
where
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
are rank-one matrices to learning a polynomial of the form
h(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det(A+Y)
where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. Observe that for the polynomial
f
, if
A_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}^{T}
for some vectors
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
, then
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det(UYV^{T}+A_{0})
where
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
are the columns of
U
and
V
respectively. In addition to using these two representations interchangeably, we shall also use the following lemma to represent
f
as a read-once determinant:

Lemma 3.1

(
[
GT20
, Lemma 4.3]
)
.

For matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
,
A_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
and
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
,

\det(UYV^{T}+A_{0})=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&Y&0_{n,r}\\
0_{n,n}&I_{n}&V^{T}\\
U&0_{r,n}&A_{0}\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

We show that for
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det(A_{0}+\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{i}y_{i})
, we can assume the matrices
A_{i}
are no larger than
n\times n
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a non-zero polynomial
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det(UYV^{T}+A_{0})
with
U,V\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
and
A_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
r\leq n
and
U
is full-rank.

Proof.

Suppose
k\leq n
is the rank of the matrix
U
. By
Lemma

3.1
,

f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&Y&0_{n,r}\\
0_{n,n}&I_{n}&V^{T}\\
U&0_{r,n}&A_{0}\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

Performing row transformations on
U
and absorbing resulting scalars into the last row,

f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&Y&0_{n,r}\\
0_{n,n}&I_{n}&V^{T}\\
\hat{U}&0_{k,n}&C_{1}\\
0_{r-k,n}&0_{r-k,n}&C_{2}\end{pmatrix}\text{,}

where
\hat{U}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times n}
,
C_{1}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times r}
,
C_{2}\in\mathbb{F}^{(r-k)\times r}
and
\hat{U}
is full-rank. For
f
to be non-zero,
C_{2}
must be full rank. Performing row and column transformations on
C_{2}
and absorbing scalars into a column of
\hat{A_{0}}
, we have

\displaystyle f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&Y&0_{n,k}&0_{n,r-k}\\
0_{n,n}&I_{n}&\hat{V}^{T}&\hat{V}_{1}^{T}\\
\hat{U}&0_{k,n}&\hat{A_{0}}&C_{3}\\
0_{r-k,n}&0_{r-k,n}&0_{r-k,k}&I_{r-k}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&Y&0_{n,k}\\
0_{n,n}&I_{n}&\hat{V}^{T}\\
\hat{U}&0_{k,n}&\hat{A_{0}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det(\hat{U}Y\hat{V}^{T}+\hat{A_{0}})\text{,}

where
\hat{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times n}
and
\hat{A_{0}}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times k}
.
∎

The following lemma shall be helpful in converting a read-once determinant back into a symbolic determinant under rank 1 restriction.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
A\in\mathbb{F}^{(n-r)\times r},B\in\mathbb{F}^{(n-r)\times(n-r)}
and
C\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times(n-r)}
, then

\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
I_{r}&C\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{r(n-r)}\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-C\\
I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}\right)\text{.}

Proof.

Moving
\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&C\end{pmatrix}
to the top (each of the
r
rows being swapped
n-r
times, totalling
r(n-r)
swaps) and using block Gaussian elimination,

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
I_{r}&C\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r(n-r)}\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&C\\
A&B\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r(n-r)}\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&0_{r,n-r}\\
A&B-AC\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r(n-r)}\det(B-AC)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r(n-r)}\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-C\\
I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}\right)\text{.}

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})

\displaystyle=\det(T)\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&T^{-1}Y&0_{n,r}\\

\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{n+r}&H\end{array}\\

.

•

is the

•

is the scalar resulting from the transformations.

Observation 3.1

\displaystyle=\det\left(Z+\bar{Z}A^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}Z&\bar{Z}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}\\

\displaystyle=\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Z&0_{n,n}\\

D
is a ‘random’ diagonal matrix.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\geq n^{6}
. Consider a matrix
D\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
D=\diag(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
and each
d_{i}
is chosen independently and uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}
. Then, for any irreducible matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
,
(A+D)^{\ad}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
with high probability.

Proof.

It suffices to show that a randomly chosen diagonal matrix
D
satisfies the properties stated in
4.2
. Consider the following small collection of polynomials, where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
:

1.

\det(A+Y)
.

2.

For each
1\leq i,j\leq n
,
\det((A+Y)[\overline{i},\overline{j}])
.

3.

For each distinct
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
,
\det\left((A+Y)^{\ad}[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}]\right)
.

It follows from the Polynomial Identity Lemma
[
Sch80
,
Zip79
,
DL78
]
that, upon substituting each
y_{i}
with a randomly chosen
d_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
, each nonzero polynomial in the collection remains nonzero with high probability. Consequently, for the diagonal matrix
D=\diag(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
, all the properties mentioned in
4.2
hold. Hence,
(A+D)^{\ad}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The proof of
Lemma

4.2
continues to hold even if, for every distinct
i,j,k,\ell
, we replace
\det\left((A+Y)^{\ad}[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}]\right)
in the collection with the polynomial
\det((A+Y)[\overline{\{k,\ell\}},\overline{\{i,j\}}])
. This follows from Jacobi’s identity
[

, Page 21]
, which implies that
\det\left((A+Y)^{\ad}[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}]\right)\neq 0
if and only if
\det((A+Y)[\overline{\{k,\ell\}},\overline{\{i,j\}}])\neq 0
. Furthermore, each polynomial in the collection is now computable by a read-once determinant (ROD). Therefore, we can use the quasi-polynomial size hitting set for RODs
[
GT20
]
to compute a quasi-polynomially large collection of
n\times n
diagonal matrices such that there exists a diagonal matrix

in the collection for which

satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Consider a matrix
D\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
D=\diag(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
and each
d_{i}
is chosen independently and uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}
. Then, for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
, each irreducible block of
(A+D)^{\ad}
(and as a result,

) satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
with high probability.

4.2
Black-box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
from that to
\det(A+Y)

We are given black-box access to
\det(A+Y)
for some matrix
A
and

. We demonstrate that we can obtain black-box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
as follows:

Lemma 4.3
.

If
|\mathbb{F}|>n+1
, from black box access to
\det(A+Y)
with

, we can obtain black box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
where

is a diagonal matrix such that
(A+D)
is invertible.

Proof.

By translating
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
, we get black-box access to
\det((A+D)+Y)
. Since we know the value of

and that it is non-zero, we see

\det(A+D)^{-1}\det((A+D)+Y)=\det(I_{n}+(A+D)^{-1}Y)\text{.}

By
2.4
, we can invert the variables and multiply by

to get black-box access to

\det((A+D)^{-1}Y^{-1}+I_{n})\det(Y)=\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)\text{.}

∎

Let
D
be a diagonal matrix of randomly chosen field elements. Since
\det(A+Y)
is invertible,
\det(A+D)
is invertible with high probability. Thus, we have black-box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
, and by
Corollary

4.1
, the irreducible blocks of
(A+D)^{-1}
satisfy property
\mathcal{R}
with high probability.

4.3
Learning the index sets of the irreducible blocks of
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)

Suppose we have an index set
T\subseteq[n]
and we wish to get black-box access to
\det(A[T]+Y[T])
as well as the principal minor
\det(A[T])
from black-box access to
\det(A+Y)
where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. We can do so using polynomial interpolation:

Claim 4.3
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|>n
. Suppose we have black-box access to
\det(A+Y)
where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}

T\subseteq[n]

\det(A[T])

n-|T|+1

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume

From the nature of the polynomial

If

By the previous subsection, we have black-box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. Let

. Suppose

2.1
, there exists a permutation matrix

To learn the irreducible blocks of

Claim 4.4
.

Let

If

If

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For

By

Claim 4.5
.

Let

Proof.

\displaystyle\det(B[\{i,j\}]+Y[\{i,j\}])

\displaystyle=(y_{i}+B[i,i])(y_{j}+B[j,j])-B[i,j]B[j,i]

\displaystyle=y_{i}y_{j}+y_{i}B[j,j]+B[i,i]y_{j}+B[i,i]B[j,j]-B[i,j]B[j,i]\text{.}

B[i,i]B[j,j]-B[i,j]B[j,i]=B[i,i]B[j,j]
if and only if

∎

4.4
Recovering a matrix that is principal minor equivalent to
A

Let
T_{1}\sqcup T_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{s}=[n]
be the indices corresponding to the irreducible blocks of
(A+D)^{-1}
where
D
is the diagonal matrix with randomly chosen entries. Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{s}
be matrices such that
C_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}(A+D)^{-1}[T_{i}]
. Since
Algorithm

4
gave us the index sets
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{s}
, we can find a permutation matrix
P
such that
C=P^{T}\diag(C_{1},\dots,C_{s})P
is principal minor equivalent to
(A+D)^{-1}
(by
Lemma

2.1
). We now show how to recover a matrix
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
from

.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}(A+D)^{-1}
where
D
is a diagonal matrix such that
A+D
is invertible. Then,
C^{-1}-D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
.

Proof.

Since
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}(A+D)^{-1}
,
\det(C+Y)=\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
where
Y=\diag(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. As
\det(C)=\det((A+D)^{-1})\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\det(C^{-1})\det(C+Y)

\displaystyle=\det(A+D)\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)

\displaystyle\implies\det(I_{n}+C^{-1}Y)

\displaystyle=\det(I_{n}+(A+D)Y)\text{.}

Inverting the variables and multiplying by
y_{1}\dots y_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\det(C^{-1}Y^{-1}+I_{n})\det(Y)

\displaystyle=\det((A+D)Y^{-1}+I_{n})\det(Y)

\displaystyle\implies\det(C^{-1}+Y)

\displaystyle=\det(A+D+Y)\text{.}

Translating
Y
by
-D
, we see that
\det(C^{-1}-D+Y)=\det(A+Y)
. Thus,
C^{-1}-D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
.
∎

4.5
Derandomising the reduction

The randomised reduction from
Black-box PMAP
to PMAP for matrices with property
\mathcal{R}
, shown in the preceding sections, can be derandomised in quasi-polynomial time using the hitting-set construction from
[
GT20
]
. We provide a sketch of this deterministic reduction below:

1.

Using
Remark

4.2
, we can find the index sets corresponding to the irreducible blocks of
A
.

2.

By
Remark

4.1
, which uses the
[
GT20
]
hitting-set, we have a list of quasi-polynomially many diagonal matrices
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}
that contains at least one matrix
D
such that the irreducible blocks of
(A+D)^{-1}
satisfy property
\mathcal{R}
. We say that such a
D
is correct.

3.

How do we identify a correct
D
in the above list? If we proceed with an incorrect
D
, then it is quite possible that the reconstructed matrix
B
is not principal minor equivalent to
A
. This issue can be avoided if we succeed in checking at the end that the reconstructed
B
is indeed principal minor equivalent to
A
. Next, we argue how this is done.

4.

To verify if
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
, given
B

explicitly
and black-box access to
\det(A+Y)
, we first check if their irreducible blocks have the same index sets (using
Remark

4.2
). Then, we verify PME of the irreducible blocks separately. For the rest of the argument, assume
A
and
B
are irreducible. By
Remark

4.1
, we have quasi-polynomially many diagonal matrices
D_{1},\dots,D_{r}
that contain at least one matrix
D
such that
(B+D)^{-1}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Since we know
B
explicitly, we can find the correct
D
by checking if it satisfies the conditions listed in
4.2
; the third condition can be verified by checking if
\det\left((B+D)^{\ad}[\{i,j\},\{k,l\}]\right)=0\Longrightarrow\forall m\in[r],\ \det\left((B+D_{m})^{\ad}[\{i,j\},\{k,l\}]\right)=0
. By
Lemma

4.3
, we have black-box access to
\det((A+D)^{-1}+Y)
and its principal minors. We can now verify if the corresponding principal minors of orders at most
4
of
(A+D)^{-1}
and
(B+D)^{-1}
are equal. If they are, then by
Theorem

1.3
,
(B+D)^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}(A+D)^{-1}
, and by
Lemma

4.4
,
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
.

5
Sufficiency of PME up to Order
4
: Proof of Theorem
1.3

A key technical contribution of this paper is the identification of a property, denoted by
\mathcal{R}
, such that for any two square matrices
A
and
B
with nonzero off-diagonal entries, if
A
satisfies
\mathcal{R}
, then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
if and only if
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. In this section, we first define property
\mathcal{R}
and then prove
Theorem

1.3
. Finally, we construct a counterexample showing that if
A
does not satisfy
\mathcal{R}
, it is possible for
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
to hold while
A
and
B
are not principal minor equivalent.

Definition 5.1

(Property
\mathcal{R}
)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
over a field
\mathbb{F}
. We say that
A
has the
property
\mathcal{R}
if it satisfies the following:

1.

all the off-diagonal entries are nonzero.

2.

if
\rank A[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}]=1
for any four distinct elements
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
, then there exists a subset
X
of
[n]
such that
i,j\in X
,
k,\ell\in\overline{X}
, and
\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=1
.

The following lemma shows that if a matrix
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, then every principal submatrix of
A
also satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
with
n\geq 5
. Suppose that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Let
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\geq 5
. Then,
A[S]
also satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Note that off-diagonal entries of
A[S]
are also off-diagonal entries of
A
. Hence, they are nonzero. Let
i,j,k,\ell
be four distinct elements from the set
S
such that
\rank A[\{i,j\},\{k,\ell\}]=1
. Since
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, there exists a subset
X
of
[n]
such that
i,j\in X
,
k,\ell\in\overline{X}
and
\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=1
. Let
X_{S}=X\cap S
and
\overline{X}_{S}=\overline{X}\cap S
. Then, the submatrix
A[X_{S},\overline{X}_{S}]
is a submatrix of
A[X,\overline{X}]
. Therefore,
\rank A[X_{S},\overline{X}_{S}]=1
.
∎

Our next lemma is inspired by
[
Loe86
, Lemma 6]
. Suppose
A
is an
n\times n
matrix. The lemma provides a way to derive rank-one constraints on certain submatrices of
A
from a cut in one of its
(n-1)\times(n-1)
principal submatrices. Assume that
A
has no cut. Then, combined with
Lemma

2.5
, this result implies that
A
must have at least three
(n-1)\times(n-1)
principal submatrices with no cut. This will be useful for the inductive step in our proof.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 5
be a positive integer. Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
. Let
X\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
be a cut of the matrix
A[[n]\setminus\{i\}]
and
\overline{X}=[n]\setminus(X\cup\{i\})
. Then,

\rank A[X+i,\overline{X}]=1\text{ or }\rank A[X,\overline{X}+i]=1,

and

\rank A[\overline{X}+i,X]=1\text{ or }\rank A[\overline{X},X+i]=1.

Proof.

From the definition of the cut of a matrix, we know that the cardinality of both
X
and
\overline{X}
is at least
2
. Let
s\in X
and
t\in\overline{X}
. Let
X^{\prime}=X-s
and
\overline{X}^{\prime}=\overline{X}-t
. Since
\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=1
, for all

and

,

\rank A[\{s,u\},\{t,v\}]=1.

Property
\mathcal{R}
of
A
implies that, for all
u\in X^{\prime}
and
v\in\overline{X}^{\prime}
, we have a subset

of
[n]

Now, for every
u\in X^{\prime}
, consider the following subsets of
[n]
.

X_{u}=\bigcap_{v\in\overline{X}^{\prime}}X_{uv}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \overline{X}_{u}=[n]\setminus X_{u}=\bigcup_{v\in\overline{X}^{\prime}}\overline{X}_{uv}.

Then,
Lemma

2.4
implies that for all
u\in X^{\prime}
,
\rank A[X_{u},\overline{X}_{u}]=1
. Also, observe that for all
u\in X^{\prime}
,
\overline{X}
is a subset of
\overline{X}_{u}
and
\{s,u\}\subseteq X_{u}
. We now consider the following subsets of
[n]
.

T=\bigcup_{u\in X^{\prime}}X_{u}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \overline{T}=[n]\setminus T=\bigcap_{u\in X^{\prime}}\overline{X}_{u}.

Note that
X\subseteq T
and
\overline{X}\subseteq\overline{T}
. Furthermore, from
Lemma

2.4
, we have
\rank A[T,\overline{T}]=1
. Since
X\subseteq T
,
\overline{X}\subseteq\overline{T}
, and
(X,\overline{X})
is a partition of
[n]\setminus\{i\}
,

\text{either }T=X+i\text{ and }\overline{T}=\overline{X},\ \text{ or }\ T=X\text{ and }\overline{T}=\overline{X}+i,

and

\rank A[X+i,\overline{X}]=1,\ \ \text{ or }\ \ \rank A[X,\overline{X}+i]=1

(8)

Similarly, using
\rank A[\overline{X},X]=1
, we can show that

\rank A[\overline{X}+i,X]=1,\ \ \text{ or }\ \ \rank A[\overline{X},X+i]=1

(9)

Thus,
Eq.

8
and
Eq.

9
, taken together, complete the proof of the lemma.
∎

The combination of
Lemma

2.5
and
Lemma

5.2
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 5
be a positive integer. Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
. Suppose that
A
has no cut. Then, there exist at least three elements
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[n]

Proof.

Note that
A
has no cut. Therefore, for every
i\in[n]
with
A[[n]\setminus\{i\}]
has a cut,
Lemma

5.2
, when applied to
A
, yields a constraint of type
Eq.

5
or
Eq.

6
. Consequently, by applying

2.5
, the corollary follows.
∎

Suppose that
n\geq 5
is a positive integer. Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices such that
A
has no cut and
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Additionally, assume that
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,n-1)
. Then, from
Corollary

5.1
and
Lemma

2.3
, we know that

|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|\geq 3.

On the other hand, applying
Lemma

2.2
, if

|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|\geq 5,

then
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B
, and therefore using
2.3
,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
. We also show that assumption

|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=3\text{ or }4.

(10)

leads to a contradiction. Therefore,

|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|\neq 3\text{ or }4.

This, in turn, allows us to prove that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
if and only if
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. First, we show that if
Eq.

10
holds, then
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
cannot have a nonempty intersection.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 5
be a positive integer. Suppose that
A
and
B
are two
n\times n
matrices with all off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero, and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,3)
. Let

|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=3\text{ or }4.

Then, the intersection of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
is empty.

Remark 5.1
.

If
|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=4
, we do not require the assumption
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,3)
in our proof. Moreover, when
|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=3
, it suffices to assume
A[S]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[S]
where
S
is the union of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
has nonempty intersection. We now divide our proof into the following two cases.

Case I (
|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=4
).

The nonempty intersection between
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
implies that either
|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)|=3
or
|\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|=3
. Therefore, from
Lemma

2.2
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
have an empty intersection.

Case II (
|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|=3
).

If the cardinality of the intersection of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
is at least two, then either
|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)|=3
or
|\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|=3
. Therefore, like
Case I
,
Lemma

2.2
implies that
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cap\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|=1
. Without loss of generality, assume that

\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=\{1,2\}\text{ and }\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=\{2,3\}.

For all
k\in[n]
, let
N_{k}=[k]
. Note that

\{1,2\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[N_{4}],B[N_{4}])\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \{2,3\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[N_{4}],B[N_{4}]).

From the hypothesis of the lemma, we know that
A[N_{3}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[N_{3}]
. Therefore, applying
Lemma

2.3
,
4\in\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[N_{4}],B[N_{4}])
or
4\in\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[N_{4}],B[N_{4}])
. Based on that, using
Lemma

2.2
, we obtain that

A[N_{4}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[N_{4}]\ \ \text{ or }\ \ A[N_{4}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[N_{4}]^{T}.

Thus, by repeated application of
Lemma

2.2
, we obtain that
A[N_{k}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B[N_{k}]
for all
k\in\{5,6,\ldots,n\}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

The above lemma implies that in
Eq.

10
, we can assume that the intersection of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
is empty. Next we show that
|\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A,B\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A,B\right)|\neq 4
. To that end, the following lemma establishes a key property of
4\times 4
matrices having nonzero off diagonal entries.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
4\times 4
matrices such that all off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero, and
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
. Let
X\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}
with
|X|=2
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=X
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=\overline{X}
. Then,
X
is a cut of
A
.

The proof closely follows the proof of
[
HL84
, Theorem 4]
. For details see
Section

A.1
. The following lemma addresses the case when the union of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
contains four elements (see
Eq.

10
). A lemma of similar flavour appears in
[
Loe86
, Theorem 2]
with the following differences. In their hypothesis, they assume that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
, which is stronger than our assumption
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. On the other hand, we assume that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, but they only require that the off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero, that is, the first condition of property
\mathcal{R}
. Nevertheless, the conclusion remains same in both the cases. Additionally, the proof of our lemma is significantly simpler compared to the proof of
[
Loe86
, Theorem 2]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
n\geq 4
. Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
such that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in[n]
be four distinct positive integers such that
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
. Then, there exists an
X\subseteq[n]
such that
i_{1},i_{2}\in X
,
i_{3},i_{4}\in\overline{X}
, and
X
is a cut of
A
.

Remark 5.2
.

In the lemma above, it is sufficient to use
A[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\}]
in place of
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
to establish the same conclusion.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}=\{1,2\}
and
\{i_{3},i_{4}\}=\{3,4\}
.

Observe that there exists a matrix
A^{\prime}
such that it is diagonally similar to
A
and
A^{\prime}[[n]\setminus\{j\}]=B[[n]\setminus\{j\}]
for
j=1,2
. Also, instead of
A
, we can work with any matrix diagonally similar to
A
. Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume that
A[[n]\setminus\{j\}]=B[[n]\setminus\{j\}]
for
j=1,2
. This implies that

A[i,j]=B[i,j]\text{ for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,2),(2,1)\}.

(11)

Let
C
be an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
diagonal matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
[n]\setminus\{3\}
and for
i\in[n]\setminus\{3\}
,
C[i,i]=c_{i}
with
c_{1}=1
. Let
D
be another
(n-1)\times(n-1)
diagonal matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
[n]\setminus\{4\}
and for
i\in[n]\setminus\{4\}
,
D[i,i]=d_{i}
with
d_{1}=1
. Furthermore, assume that

B[[n]\setminus\{3\}]^{T}=C^{-1}A[[n]\setminus\{3\}]C\ \ \text{ and }\ \ B[[n]\setminus\{4\}]^{T}=D^{-1}A[[n]\setminus\{4\}]D.

(12)

The above equation implies that

c_{2}=d_{2}=\frac{B[2,1]}{A[1,2]},\text{ and for all }5\leq i\leq n,c_{i}=d_{i}=\frac{A[i,1]}{A[1,i]}

(13)

Additionally, from
Eq.

11
,
Eq.

12
and
Eq.

13
, we have the following:

1.

B[1,2]=A[2,1]c_{1}c_{2}^{-1}
and
B[2,1]=A[1,2]c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}

2.

A[j,4]=A[4,j]c_{j}c_{4}^{-1}
for all
j\in\{1,2,5,6,\ldots,n\}

3.

A[j,3]=A[3,j]c_{j}d_{3}^{-1}
for all
j\in\{1,2,5,6,\ldots,n\}

4.

A[j,i]=A[i,j]c_{j}c_{i}^{-1}
for all
j\in\{1,2,5,6,\ldots,n\}
and
i\in\{5,6,\ldots,n\}

Let
e_{1}=1
,
e_{2}=c_{2}
,
e_{3}=d_{3}
,
e_{4}=c_{4}
and for all
5\leq k\leq n
,
e_{k}=c_{k}
. Then, from the above discussion, we obtain that

A[j,i]=A[i,j]\cdot\frac{e_{j}}{e_{i}}\ \ \text{ for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,2),(2,1),(3,4),(4,3)\}.

(14)

Let
E
be an
n\times n
diagonal matrix such that for all
i\in[n]
,
E[i,i]=e_{i}
. Thus, applying
Eq.

14
, for any
S\subseteq[n]
with
\{1,2\}\subseteq S
and
\{3,4\}\subseteq\overline{S}
,

A[\overline{S},S]=E[\overline{S}]\cdot A[S,\overline{S}]^{T}\cdot E[S]^{-1}.

(15)

From the hypthesis of the lemma, we know that

\{1,2\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)\text{ and }\{3,4\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right).

Next, we argue that

\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)=\{1,2\}\text{ and }\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)=\{3,4\}.

For the sake of contradiction, first, assume that either
3
or
4
is in
\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)
. Then, from
Lemma

2.2
,
A[\{1,2,3,4\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3,4\}]
. Applying
Eq.

11
, we obtain that
A=B
. This implies
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. Now, assume that either
1
or
2
is in
\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)
. Then, again from
Lemma

2.2
,
A[\{1,2,3,4\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3,4\}]^{T}
. This combined with
Eq.

12
implies that
B^{T}=E^{-1}AE
. Therefore,
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. Thus,

\mathcal{D}_{1}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)=\{1,2\}\text{ and }\mathcal{D}_{2}\left(A[\{1,2,3,4\}],B[\{1,2,3,4\}]\right)=\{3,4\}.

(16)

Eq.

16
and
Lemma

5.4
combined imply that
\{1,2\}
is a cut of the matrix
A[\{1,2,3,4\}]
. Since
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, there exists a subset
X\subseteq[n]
with
1,2\in X
,
3,4\in\overline{X}
and
\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=1
. Now, applying
Eq.

15
,
\rank A[\overline{X},X]=1
. Therefore,
X
is our desired cut for the matrix
A
.
∎

Our next lemma deals with the case when the union of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
contains three elements (see
Eq.

10
). As with the previous lemma, a result of similar flavour appears in
[
Loe86
, Theorem 4]
. Furthermore, the differences in the hypotheses between our lemma and
[
Loe86
, Theorem 4]
are the same as before: they assume
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
, whereas we assume only
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
; and we assume that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, while they require only that the off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero. Nevertheless, the conclusions in both cases remain the same.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
n\geq 4
. Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
such that
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[n]
be three distinct positive integers, and
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=\{i_{3}\}
. Let

T_{1}:=\left\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\}\,\mid\,B[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},j\}]^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}A[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},j\}]\right\}

and

T_{2}:=\left\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\}\,\mid\,B[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},j\}]^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{\neq}}A[\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},j\}]\right\}.

Then, there exists a subset
X
of
[n]
such that
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\subseteq X
,
T_{2}+i_{3}\subseteq\overline{X}
and
X
is a cut of the matrix
A
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}=\{1,2\}
and
i_{3}=3
. We divide our proof into two cases.

Case I (
n=4
).

We show that for
n=4
the hypothesis of the lemma is false. Since
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]
, from
Lemma

2.3
,
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}A[\{1,2,3\}]
. Therefore,
4\in\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)
, which is a contradiction. Hence, for
n=4
, the lemma holds vacuously.

Case II (
n\geq 5
).

We first show that
T_{2}
is nonempty. For the sake of contradiction, assume that
T_{2}=\emptyset
. For all
i,j\in\{4,5,\ldots,n\}
, let
S_{i,j}=\{1,2,3,i,j\}
. Observe that

T_{2}=\emptyset\Longrightarrow\{3,i,j\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[S_{i,j}],B[S_{i,j}]).

Therefore, from
Lemma

2.2
,
A[S_{i,j}]^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[S_{i,j}]
. Thus, repeatedly applying
Lemma

2.2
, we obtain that
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)=[n]
which is a contradiction. Therefore,
T_{2}
is a nonempty set.

Since
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
,
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]
. Therefore, from
Lemma

2.3
,
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]
, or
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]^{T}
. Suppose that
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]
. Then, for all
i\in\{4,5,6,\ldots,n\}

\{1,2,i\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}]).

Therefore, applying
Lemma

2.2
,
A[\{1,2,3,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3,i\}]
for all
i\in\{4,5,6,\ldots,n\}
. Thus, repeatedly applying
Lemma

2.2
, we obtain
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B
. Therefore,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)=[n]
, which is a contradiction. Thus,

A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]^{T},\text{ but }A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{\neq}}B[\{1,2,3\}]

(17)

Suppose that
i\in T_{2}
. Then, from the definition of
T_{2}
,
A[\{1,2,3,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{\neq}}B[\{1,2,3,i\}]^{T}
. From the hypothesis of the lemma and
Eq.

17
, we have that

\{1,2\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}])\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \{3,i\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}]).

Moreover, applying
Eq.

17
, we obtain that
i\notin\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}])
. Since
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
, from
Lemma

2.3
,
A[\{1,2,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,i\}]
or
A[\{1,2,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,i\}]^{T}
. If
A[\{1,2,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,i\}]
, then applying
Lemma

2.2
,
A[\{1,2,3,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3,i\}]
. This implies
A[\{1,2,3\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3\}]
, which is a contradiction due to
Eq.

17
. Therefore,
A[\{1,2,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,i\}]^{T}
but
A[\{1,2,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{\neq}}B[\{1,2,i\}]
. Thus,

\{1,2\}=\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}])\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \{3,i\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}]).

Furthermore, if
1
or
2
is in
\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}])
, then again by
Eq.

17
and
Lemma

2.2
,
A[\{1,2,3,i\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DS}}}}}{{=}}B[\{1,2,3,i\}]^{T}
. This contradicts that
i\in T_{2}
. Therefore, for all
i\in T_{2}
,

\{1,2\}=\mathcal{D}_{1}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}])\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \{3,i\}=\mathcal{D}_{2}(A[\{1,2,3,i\}],B[\{1,2,3,i\}]).

(18)

Lemma

5.4
and
Eq.

18
imply that for all
i\in T_{2}
,
\{1,2\}
is a cut of the matrix
A[\{1,2,3,i\}]
. Therefore,
\rank A[\{1,2\},\{3,i\}]=1
. Since
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, for all
i\in T_{2}
, there exists
X_{i}\subset[n]
such that
1,2\in X_{i}
,
3,i\in\overline{X}_{i}
and
\rank A[X_{i},\overline{X}_{i}]=1
. Consider the following sets

X=\bigcap_{i\in T_{2}}X_{i}\>\>\text{ and }\>\>\overline{X}=\bigcup_{i\in T_{2}}\overline{X}_{i}.

From

\rank A[X,\overline{X}]=1\>\>\text{ with }\>\>1,2\in X,\>\>\text{ and }\>\>T_{2}+3\subseteq\overline{X}.

(19)

Observe that there exists a matrix

such that it is diagonally similar to

for

. Additionally, instead of

. This implies that

A[i,j]=B[i,j]\text{ for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,2),(2,1)\}.

Let

be an

diagonal matrix such that for all

with

, and the following hold:

2.

B[\{1,2,3\}]^{T}=C[\{1,2,3\}]^{-1}\cdot A[\{1,2,3\}]\cdot C[\{1,2,3\}]

3.

for all

,
B[\{1,2,3,i\}]^{T}=C[\{1,2,3,i\}]^{-1}\cdot A[\{1,2,3,i\}]\cdot C[\{1,2,3,i\}]

Observe that the existence of such an

follows from

, and

. Thus,

1.

B[1,2]=A[2,1]c_{1}c_{2}^{-1}
and

2.

A[j,i]=A[i,j]c_{j}c_{i}^{-1}
for all

and

.

A[3,i]=A[i,3]c_{3}c_{i}^{-1}

.

This implies that for any

with

and

,

A[\overline{S},S]=C[\overline{S}]\cdot A[S,\overline{S}]^{T}\cdot C[S]^{-1}.

The above equation combined with

imply that
X
is a cut of
A
with
1,2\in X
and
T_{2}+3\subseteq\overline{X}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we prove

1.3
for no-cut case. Specifically, we show that for any two square matrices

and

with

has no cut and satisfies property

, the corresponding principal minors of

and

are equal if and only if the corresponding principal minors of them of order up to

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A
and

be two
n\times n
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Suppose that

satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
and has no cut. Then

if and only if
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
n
.
This is trivially true for
n=4
.

Assume that
n\geq 5
and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Following
Lemma

2.2
, to prove
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
, it suffices to show

|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|\geq 5.

From our assumption,
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
and
A
does not have a cut. Applying
Corollary

5.1
, there exist at least three distinct indices
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[n]
such that the submatrices
A[[n]\setminus\{i_{j}\}]
for each

do not have a cut.
Furthermore, each
A[[n]\setminus\{i_{j}\}]
continues to satisfy property
\mathcal{R}
following
Lemma

5.1
. Additionally,
\mathrm{PME}(A[[n]\setminus\{j\}],B[[n]\setminus\{j\}],4)
. Therefore, from the induction hypothesis,
A[[n]\setminus\{j\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[[n]\setminus\{j\}]
for each
j\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Since
A[[n]\setminus\{j\}]
has no cut and all the off-diagonal entries are nonzero, from
Lemma

2.3
,
A[[n]\setminus\{j\}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.5}{\mbox{DE}}}}}{{=}}B[[n]\setminus\{j\}]
.
Equivalently,

|\mathcal{D}_{1}(A,B)\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}(A,B)|\geq 3.

However,

5.6
and

5.5
combined with

rule out the possibility of

being equal to 3 and 4 respectively, as that would imply the matrix

to have a cut which leads to a contradiction.
∎

We now focus on proving the main technical result of this paper: for any two square matrices
A
and
B
with nonzero off-diagonal entries, if
A
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, then

and

are principal minor equivalent if and only if their corresponding principal minors of order up to

are equal. Our proof proceeds by induction on the size of the matrices. To facilitate this, the following lemma provides a decomposition of larger matrices into smaller ones, allowing us to apply the induction hypothesis to the smaller matrices. The core idea of this lemma already appears in
[

, although not in the precise form stated here.

Lemma 5.7

(Decomposition lemma)
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
with nonzero off-diagonal entries. Let

be a cut of both
A
and
B
. Consider any two indices

such that

and

if and only if

and
A[\overline{S}+s]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[\overline{S}+s]
. Furthermore, if

is a minimal cut of

, then

has no cut.

For a proof see
Section

A.2
. We also require the following result, which establishes a relationship between the cuts of two matrices whose corresponding principal minors of order up to 4 are equal.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
n\times n
matrices with nonzero off-diagonal entries and
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Let

be a minimal cut of

is also a cut of

.

A closely related result appears in
[
CGGR25
, Lemma 3.6]
, with the key difference being the strength of the hypothesis: while
[

]
assumes

, the above lemma only requires the weaker assumption

. Nonetheless, the proof of the above lemma follows the same general outline as that of
[
CGGR25
, Lemma 3.6]
, with an additional reliance on

5.1
. For a complete proof, refer to
Section

A.3
. With all necessary tools now in place, we are ready to formally prove

1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Property
\mathcal{R}
ensures that all off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonzero. Next, we show that all off-diagonal entries of
B
are also nonzero. For the sake of contradiction, assume that
B[i,j]=0
for some
i\neq j\in[n]
. Then,
\det(A[\{i,j\}])\neq\det(B[\{i,j\}])
, contradicting
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
. Therefore, all the off-diagonal entries of

are nonzero.

We prove the theorem by induction on
n
.
This trivially holds for
n=4
. If
A
does not have a cut, the statement follows from
Theorem

5.1
.

For the inductive step, suppose that
\mathrm{PME}(A,B,4)
and
A
has a cut and let
S\subseteq[n]
be a minimal cut of
A
. Now we divide our proof into the following two cases.

Case I (
S
is a cut of
B
).

Following
Lemma

5.7
, it suffices to prove
A[S+t]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[S+t]
and
A[\overline{S}+s]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B[\overline{S}+s]
for some

and

. Notice that both
A[S+t]
and

continue to satisfy property
\mathcal{R}
following

5.1
. Additionally,
\mathrm{PME}(A[S+t],B[S+t],4)
and
\mathrm{PME}(A[\overline{S}+s],B[\overline{S}+s],4)
. The proof now follows from our induction hypothesis.

Case II (
S
is not a cut of
B
).

From
Lemma

5.8
, the cardinality of
S
must be two. Then, applying
Lemma

2.7
, there exists a cut

of
B
such that

is a cut of
\widetilde{B}=\tw(B,X)
. Since the principal minors are preserved under the cut-transpose operation (see

),
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}\widetilde{B}
and
\mathrm{PME}(A,\widetilde{B},4)
. Additionally,

is now also a cut of
\widetilde{B}
. Therefore, invoking

, we obtain
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}\widetilde{B}
. Thus,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
.
∎

6.1
Cut Finding Algorithm

Suppose that
A
is an
n\times n
matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
. Our goal is to determine whether
A
has a cut in a
black-box manner
; that is, instead of direct access to the entries of
A
, we are allowed to query its principal minors. Furthermore, if
A
has a cut, the algorithm also outputs a subset
S\subseteq[n]
with
2\leq|S|\leq n-2
such that
S
is a cut in some matrix
C\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
satisfying
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
.

We emphasize that, we later (in
Section

7.1
) provide an algorithm which, for any
I\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=4
, given access to the principal minors of
A[I]
, computes the family
\mathcal{S}_{A[I]}
. Consequently, we can obtain the family
\mathcal{F}_{A}
. Access to this family is sufficient for our purpose. Moreover, this algorithm will be used as a subroutine in a later reconstruction algorithm, where
\mathcal{F}_{A}
is computed explicitly from the principal minors of
A
. Therefore, in the description of the cut-finding algorithm, we assume that
\mathcal{F}_{A}
is given as input, rather than access to the principal minors.

We now define a property for the
4\times 4
principal submatrices of an
n\times n
matrix
A
. It will be crucial in designing our algorithm.

Definition 6.1

(Property
\calP
)
.

Given a matrix
A
, a set of two pairs
t=\{\{i,j\},\{k,l\}\}
with distinct
i,j,k,l
is said to satisfy property
\calP
if there exists a
4\times 4
matrix
C_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A[\{i,j,k,l\}]
such that
\{i,j\}
(equivalently
\{k,l\}
) is a cut of
C_{t}
.

The following observation establishes that verifying whether a pair satisfies property
\calP
can be done efficiently.

Observation 6.1
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
, given the family
\mathcal{F}_{A}
and a pair
t=\{\{i,j\},\{k,l\}\}
, we can check whether
t
satisfies property
\calP
in constant time.

Intuitively, the above definition captures a certain kind of
local
property that is applicable to only the
4\times 4
principal submatrices of a large
n\times n
matrix
A
. Next, using this local property, we define a global object for
A
. It will be helpful to characterize whether a matrix
A
satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
has cut.

Definition 6.2

(Plausible set)
.

Given an
n\times n
matrix
A
, a subset
S\subseteq[n]
of size at least two and at most
n-2
is called a
plausible set
, if for each
\{i,j\}\subset S,\{k,l\}\subset,\overline{S}
,
\{\{i,j\},\{k,l\}\}
satisfies property
\calP
.

Next, we describe two lemmas that are backbone in designing our algorithm. We defer the proof of these lemmas to the end of the section. Suppose that
A
is a matrix that satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Then, the following lemma establishes a transitivity relation on the set of all pairs
\{\{i,j\},\{k,l\}\}
satisfying property
\calP
. Intuitively, given two such pairs that share common indices, one can derive a new pair that also satisfies
\calP
.

Lemma 6.1

(Transitivity of property
\calP
)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix that satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Let
i,j,k,l_{1},l_{2}\in[n]
be distinct elements such that both
\{\{i,j\},\{k,l_{1}\}\}
and
\{\{i,j\},\{k,l_{2}\}\}
satisfy property
\calP
, then
\{\{i,j\},\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\}
also satisfies property
\calP
.

For proof of the above lemma, see
Section

6.1.2
. The following observation presents an analogous result for cuts instead of plausible sets and is straightforward to verify.

Observation 6.2
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix with non-zero off diagonal entries. Let
i,j,k,l_{1},l_{2}\in[n]
be distinct elements such that both
A[\{i,j,k,l_{1}\}]
and
A[\{i,j,k,l_{2}\}]
have the cut
\{i,j\}
. Then,
\{i,j\}
is also a cut of
A[\{i,j,l_{1},l_{2}\}]
. In other words,
\{i,j\}
is a cut of
A[\{i,j,k,l_{1},l_{2}\}]
.

Suppose that
A
is a matrix satisfying property
\mathcal{R}
. Suppose that instead of the entries of
A
, we have access to the principal minors of
A
. Then,
A
has a cut if and only if any matrix principal minor equivalent to
A
has a cut. It follows from
[
CGGR25
, Theorem 1.1]
. Thus, the existence of cut in
A
is a global property over all the matrices principal minor equivalent to
A
. However, that is not true for the question of whether a particular subset
S
is cut in
A
. It may happen that
S
is not a cut in
A
, but is a cut in some other matrix
C
such that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}C
. It follows from
[
CGGR25
, Theorem 1.1 and Lemma 3.2]
. Thus, given access to only the principal minors of
A
, it is expected that we would not be able to output a cut. Fortunately, for our purpose, it suffices to find a subset
S
such that
S
is cut in some matrix
C
with
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
, and plausible sets exactly characterize such sets. We first note the following immediate observation.

Observation 6.3
.

For an
n\times n
matrix
A
with
S\subset[n]
as a cut,
S
is also a plausible set for
A
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix that satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
. Then, a subset
S\subset[n]
is plausible if and only if it is a cut of some matrix
C
such that
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}A
.

For proof of the above lemma, see
Section

6.1.3
.

Remark 6.1
.

We make the following two remarks about

1.

Given an explicit irreducible matrix

A plausible set

Since

and

∎

Now, we come back to the proof for a general plausible set

In this section, we prove

, in time

Definition 7.1

(Principal minor oracle)
.

Let

be an
n\times n

denotes the principal minor oracle for the matrix

The following lemma describes that given access to the oracle

, some tasks that can be performed very efficiently.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}
. Then, the following holds.

1.

For any
i\in[n]
,
A[i,i]
can be computed using one query to the oracle
\textsc{PM}_{A}
.

2.

For any
\{i,j\}\subseteq[n]
,
A[i,j]A[j,i]
can be computed using three queries to the oracle
\textsc{PM}_{A}
.

3.

For any
\{i,j,k\}\subseteq[n]
,

can be computed using seven queries to
\textsc{PM}_{A}
.

For all

, we obtain

by querying
\textsc{PM}_{A}
on input

. For every

,

Therefore,

can be computed by querying

on inputs

,
\{j\}
and
\{i,j\}
. For every

\displaystyle A[i,j]A[j,k]A[k,i]+A[i,k]A[k,j]A[j,i]

\displaystyle=\det(A[\{i,j,k\}])-\det(A[\{i,j\}])A[k,k]

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\det(A[\{j,k\}])A[i,i]-\det(A[\{i,k\}])A[j,j]

\displaystyle\ \ \ +2A[i,i]A[j,j]A[k,k]

Hence, we can compute

.
∎

8
An NC Algorithm for PME: Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we present an NC algorithm to test principal minor equivalence of two matrices and prove
Theorem

1.2
. The high level idea is as follows: given two input matrices
A
and
B
of size
n\times n
, we design an NC reduction that transforms the problem of testing whether
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B
into testing principal minor equivalence for polynomially many pairs
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
of matrices with
A^{\prime}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
.

From
[
Csa76
,
BCP83
]
, we know that the determinant of a matrix can be computed in NC. Combined with
Theorem

1.3
, this implies that principal minor equivalence of matrices
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, where
A^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathcal{R}
, can also be tested in NC. Thus, our reduction yields an NC algorithm for testing principal minor equivalence of general matrices
A
and
B
.

The NC reduction proceeds via the following steps.

Step I:

We perform an NC reduction that reduces the problem of testing principal minor equivalence of two arbitrary matrices to testing principal minor equivalence for at most
n
instances where the input pairs are irreducible matrices. By
Lemma

2.1
, matrices
A
and
B
are principal minor equivalent if and only if their maximal irreducible submatrices define the same partition of the index set, and the corresponding principal submatrices are themselves principal minor equivalent.

In a directed graph, we can find strongly connected components in NC
[
GM88
,
CV89
]
. Hence, using
2.1
, we can find the maximal irreducible submatrices of the input matrices in NC. If the index sets of the irreducible components of two matrices do not match, we output that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{\neq}}B
. Otherwise, we test principal minor equivalence for each corresponding pair of maximal irreducible submatrices, in parallel.

Step II:

In this step, we transform, in NC, an input pair
(A,B)
of
irreducible
matrices into another pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
such that all off-diagonal entries of
A^{\prime}
are
nonzero
, and

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B\Longleftrightarrow A^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B^{\prime}.

For details, see
Section

8.1
.

Step III:

Given a pair
(A,B)
with all off-diagonal entries of
A
are
nonzero
, we further transform it, in NC, to another pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
where
A^{\prime}
satisfies property
\mathcal{R}
, such that

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B\Longleftrightarrow A^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}B^{\prime}.

For details, see
Section

8.2

Currently, we do not know of an NC procedure to construct such a pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
directly from irreducible matrices
(A,B)
. If such a procedure were available, we could bypass
Step II
entirely.

Next, we provide a detailed description of
Step II
and
Step III
.

8.1
Ensuring nonzero off-diagonal entries

The following lemma ensures that the
Step II
of the above-mentioned procedure can be done.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
M
and
N
be two
n\times n
irreducible matrices over
\mathbb{F}
and
Y=\diag(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})
. Then,

1.

M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}N
if and only if
(M+Y)^{\ad}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{.4}{\mbox{PME}}}}}{{=}}(N+Y)^{\ad}
.

2.

Moreover, if

A^{\prime}=(A+Y)[\overline{T},\overline{S}]=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,3}&a_{1,4}&a_{1,5}&\cdots&a_{1,n}\\

\vdots&&&\ddots&\\

We now perform some column operations on
