 Abstract

Asset valuation by electric utilities is becoming increasingly difficult in the rapidly changing electric
sector. Rapid deployment of variable generation and inverter-based storage systems along with uncertain
demand growth, policies, and other factors create a challenging environment for understanding the value
proposition of a new potential asset. This report describes an effort between the Tennessee Valley
Authority and three U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) laboratories (Argonne National Laboratory,
National Laboratory of the Rockies, and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory) to perform a detailed
review of utility modeling and analysis practices for asset valuation and to identify challenges and
opportunities for advancing its methods into the future. It focuses on a case study of new potential
pumped storage hydropower (PSH) because of growing interest in new PSH capacity to provide energy
balancing, firm capacity, and a range of ancillary services. Staff from the DOE labs conducted systematic
interviews about current practices in capacity expansion modeling, production cost modeling,
hydrological modeling, and transmission reliability modeling while also discussing how scenario analysis
is conducted and how models and data are integrated. The effort resulted in a set of strategic actions in the
areas of models, model integrations, and scenarios that could be valuable to utilities, system operators,
and other stakeholders conducting integrated grid analysis. Individual model actions suggest exploring
computational trade-offs with detail and resolution across spatiotemporal structure, supply- and demand-
side details, transmission overlays, market interactions, and ancillary services. Automated processes to
pass data between models and conduct larger scenario suites could also enhance valuation practices.
Doing so could enable a more consistent study of asset value across a broader range of uncertain future
grid conditions, where PSH could be particularly valuable. Utilities and other industry stakeholders can
learn from and adapt applied research-grade methods developed by DOE laboratories and other research
institutions to improve decision-making and accelerate progress toward a reliable, secure, and affordable
energy system.

 1.0 Motivation and Context
Electric utility investments in generation, storage, transmission, and other assets must firmly establish the
value proposition for those assets relative to the costs. Doing so is especially important for high capital
cost assets (i.e., billions of dollars in cost), particularly when the project development can take up to a
decade and the asset may operate continuously over 50 to 100 years. Utilities have many valuation tools
at their disposal, including both internally developed and commercial tools ranging from spreadsheet cost-
benefit calculators to sophisticated optimization-based or other modeling and analysis techniques (EPRI
2024). All tools, models, and techniques have limitations. Although this simple reality is unavoidable, an
obvious goal is to mitigate limitations that substantially affect the question being asked—in this case,
what is the value of the asset, relative to its cost?

In the rapidly changing landscape of the U.S. electric sector, valuation techniques need to adapt to
emerging industry trends. These include accelerated deployment of variable renewable generation (VG);
growth in shares of inverter-based resources (IBRs), including solar photovoltaics (PV) and battery
storage; demand electrification; rapidly changing regulations; and uncertain future weather conditions
(Gagnon et al. 2024; Craig et al. 2018). It can be difficult to quickly modify valuation tools used by
industry practitioners to accommodate these trends for various reasons. Proprietary software might lack
customizability, exclude specific technology representations, and have a lag in necessary updates. Internal
tools are readily modifiable but could still include legacy structural or other challenges (e.g., inflexible
spatial and temporal resolution, limited operational detail) to capture emerging trends. Practitioner tools
are also often designed to examine an outlook no further than 20–30 years in the future, and often a
shorter time frame than that, depending on the decision and asset type (Cooke 2022; Wilson and Biewald
2013). They are often grounded firmly in current technologies, infrastructure, markets, and institutions
and implicitly assume these core system characteristics do not change.

On the other hand, research-grade tools are often developed specifically to examine emerging long-term
trends that might be beyond typical utility decision horizons. These trends could impact near-term
decisions if they accelerate faster than median expectations or demonstrate a risk of stranded assets
(Chernyakhovskiy et al. 2021). Uncertainty around the ultimate impacts of new grid technologies,
policies, and environmental conditions is a key concern driving grid modeling and analysis research that
is fundamental to understanding future asset value (Feng et al. 2023; Scott et al. 2021; Hu et al. 2024).
Research-grade tools developed by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) national laboratories aim to
capture these effects in an applied setting that is actionable for electric sector practitioners and decision
makers (NREL 2021; Ho et al. forthcoming; Koritarov et al. 2021; Stephen 2021; Brinkman et al. 2021).
These tools might not capture all of the technical and institutional constraints on a given asset, system, or
market, so they might not be suitable for final investment and other decisions (e.g., for integrated resource
plans). However, they can complement practitioner decisions via expanded and informative scenario
analysis.

Considering this alternative focus of DOE laboratory models, researchers at Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL), National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
engaged with the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) in a review exercise to characterize electric utility
industry practices and offer perspective on future potential practices for asset valuation throughout the
industry.

Valuation improvements might include model parameter or formulation adjustments, tighter model
coupling, expanded scenario suites, or changes to financial modeling methods. Valuation of a pumped
storage hydropower (PSH) plant is used as a case study based on the particular interest and support of the
project. With the potential growth of VG, new nuclear, and carbon capture and storage, flexible

 1.1

technologies could become increasingly valuable to complement these less-flexible assets (Gagnon et al.
2024; Larson et al. 2021; Steinberg et al. 2023). Valuing utility-scale storage such as PSH is thus an acute
interest area for power system planners.

Lessons learned through this PSH valuation exercise could apply to broader storage and asset valuation
and be considered among utilities in both market-based and vertically integrated structures. The
overarching question this work addresses is: What challenges and opportunities exist in utility power
sector asset valuation practices in light of the changing electric sector landscape?

 2.0 Methods
The primary method of gathering information for this report was consultation with TVA experts.
Laboratory staff worked with TVA to identify key individuals with deep knowledge and experience in the
following model classes: (1) capacity expansion modeling (CEM), (2) production cost modeling (PCM),
(3) river operations, and (4) transmission reliability modeling. CEM primarily analyzes investment
decisions over years-to-decades timescales with limited operational detail. PCM represents detailed
operations down to minute-to-hour timescales for 1 or more years to understand bulk supply and demand
of energy and ancillary services. Transmission reliability modeling examines subminute behaviors to
analyze grid strength along with static and dynamic stability, often including nonlinear behaviors of the
power system. River operations encompass hydrological modeling, environmental modeling, river
routing, and reservoir operations. Resource adequacy assessment was also discussed briefly and is
discussed here in its relationship to other model classes.

design, financial analysis, and transmission analysis, but these are outside the scope of this discussion.

series of questions to better understand the technical and institutional characteristics of existing practices.

These discussions helped to achieve consensus on limitations and potential areas of improvement. These
questions were as follows:
1. What are key data inputs, assumptions, projections, and limitations?
2. What are limitations imposed by model structure?
3. What are limitations imposed by workflow processes?
4. What is the model logic underpinning these approaches?
5. How do evolving grid conditions and needs inform model design and logic?
6. What scenarios are implemented and why? What are the limitations and constraints on scenario

challenges in greater detail. The discussions on hydrological modeling practices examined water
management changes and operational considerations with a potential new PSH facility.

 3.0 Model Review Discussion
Asset valuation involves executing multiple model classes, and valuation practices can be influenced by
how models are run individually as well as model interdependencies (Koritarov et al. 2021). These
interdependencies can range from one-way data transfer to manual iteration between models to automated
model coupling with iteration and convergence criteria. Because there are unique considerations between
independent and interdependent model execution, we discuss individual model attributes before
addressing model integration practices. We then provide a holistic discussion of scenario exploration
strategies across all model types.

3.1 Individual Model Attributes
Models are often characterized by several basic attributes—for example, spatial and temporal scope and
resolution, solution method, core assumptions, and formulations of domain-specific aspects such as
technology availability and operating constraints. This section examines each of the four model classes
included in this study, highlighting challenges and opportunities for several key model attributes related
to asset valuation.

3.1.1 Capacity Expansion Modeling

CEM tools are designed to inform investment decisions and typically operate on multidecadal timescales
to align with economic lifetimes, that is, the desired time frame to recoup investment costs (Gacitua et al.
2018). These tools typically optimize a least-cost set of investment (and sometimes operating) decisions
across a range of possible generation, storage, transmission, and/or other investment classes.

3.1.1.1 Temporal Scope and Resolution

Key model structural decisions must be made around temporal scope and resolution to balance accuracy
with computational tractability (Mallapragada et al. 2018; Brown et al. 2024; Levin et al. 2024). For
example, one might optimize the CEM over a full 20-year study period representative of an economic
lifetime. This full intertemporal optimization ensures a true optimal solution under the given model setup.
However, the need to incorporate 20 years of data and constraints could limit model resolution elsewhere
or the restrict the ability to include additional features or constraints, such as a more detailed
representation of energy storage systems that better demonstrate the value of PSH (Bistline et al. 2020).

Intra-annual time resolution is also important to represent coincidence between load, renewable energy,
and operations; one area of increased resolution could be to increase the number of representative days in
the model (Brown et al. 2024; Levin et al. 2024). Depending on the approach for defining representative
days, it is possible the chosen set of representative days does not accurately capture the distribution of
possible operating conditions that would drive the value proposition for alternative technologies,
including PSH. Further, critical operating conditions may change over time as variable renewable energy
deployment alters net load profiles, making it increasingly difficult to properly value PSH and other
technologies across all modeled years (Kaur et al. 2016; Craig and Brancucci 2021). Intra-annual time
resolution is also critical for capturing storage energy arbitrage value (Bistline et al. 2020; Sánchez-Pérez
et al. 2022; Haas et al. 2017; Frazier et al. 2020). Representative days can capture diurnal arbitrage value
that is likely the highest-value near-term opportunity for all storage systems, including PSH. However,
energy arbitrage value across multiple days, weeks, or months is not captured, and this longer-term value
is one potential advantage PSH has over other storage technologies (Denholm and Mai 2019). The value-

 3.5

versus-duration relationship is particularly important when making investment decisions about storage
assets. Periodic reevaluation of alternative representations of intra-annual time resolution could be
important as the storage value proposition and broader power sector evolve.

A limited interannual planning model horizon can also ignore any value of assets beyond the horizon.
This limitation is especially disadvantageous to PSH, which, along with other hydropower assets, tends to
have the longest physical life among all electricity generation and storage assets (National Renewable
Energy Laboratory [NREL] 2023). For example, comparing PSH and batteries over a 20-year life is very
different from comparing PSH and batteries over 100 years in which the original PSH asset remains but
batteries must be replaced multiple times. The relatively long life of PSH relative to other technologies
could be considered by extending the planning model horizon or adjusting its cost of capital based on the
weighted average cost of capital for the true physical life (e.g., 100 years) relative to the default 20-year
economic life, as was done in the 2016 DOE Hydropower Vision (DOE 2016).

3.1.1.2     Load Shapes

Related to the challenges associated with time resolution is the representation of load shape, including the
degree of load elasticity and flexibility in response to price or other signals. CEM studies might have
difficulty capturing key drivers of PSH value if they are unable to effectively represent demand-side
flexibility that competes with PSH flexibility and services. This might entail alternative scenarios and
operational constraints governing building and transportation electrification technologies or other
distributed resources (Murphy and Mai 2021; Marañón-Ledesma and Tomasgard 2019; Fitiwi and
Bertsch 2018; Pfeifer et al. 2018). Demand-side flexibility, such as with large industrial demands or
aggregated residential and commercial demand response programs, could compete with supply-side
flexibility if it existed in significant quantities, creating more uncertainty in PSH value. Truly addressing
this limitation could require rigorous bottom-up load modeling that includes emerging technologies and
trends. This issue is discussed more in Section 3.3.2 about uncertainty characterization but is introduced
here because CEM is an important starting point to consider alternative and flexible load scenarios.

3.1.1.3 Capacity and Ancillary Service Value

The CEM representation of capacity and ancillary service value, or the lack thereof, is critical to its ability
to value flexible technologies such as PSH (Emmanuel et al. 2020; IRENA 2019; Stephen 2021; Frew et
al. 2017; Cole et al. 2017). It is first important to consider the changing contributions, typically declining,
of VG and storage to resource adequacy requirements as more of these assets are deployed. To represent
resource adequacy requirements, utilities or system operators might use a summer and winter reserve
margin requirement to capture PSH and other capacity value for resource adequacy. This requirement
appropriately estimates changes to variable generation and storage capacity credit, but the requirement
remains fixed, based on characteristics of the current system. However, although it is common practice to
use a fixed reserve margin requirement for one or more seasons, doing so could either overvalue or
undervalue capacity for resource adequacy by not explicitly representing periods of capacity stress within
an investment planning framework that spans 20 years or more (Stephen 2021). This is particularly
important for future years in which nondispatchable resources are likely to make up a much larger portion
of the asset base (Cole et al. 2017). The model integration discussion in Section 3.2 addresses greater
integration with resource adequacy models. In the specific context of CEM models, it is important to
consider going beyond a reserve margin representation of resource adequacy requirements when
considering accelerated variable renewable and storage deployments.

Although the true relationship between variable renewables and ancillary services requirements is
uncertain, techniques exist to approximate it within CEMs (Lew et al. 2013). New constraints could also
be considered to better capture PSH value for maintaining grid strength and stability. For example, IBR
deployment (including battery storage) could drive a requirement for technologies that provide inertia and
ride-through capabilities. Ancillary services representation in CEM is an ongoing research topic but
remains important for consideration by practitioners responsible for investment decisions involving
multidecadal assets (Denholm et al. 2024).

Another aspect of capacity and ancillary services value is the relationship between transmission and other
generation/storage/demand resources for service provision. This relationship and its effect on investment
decisions could be best understood by representing transmission endogenously within the CEM. A
transmission overlay within the CEM would enable it to capture potential value of PSH for alleviating

transmission congestion and reducing transmission investment needs (Brown and Botterud 2021; Tröndle
et al. 2020; ESIG 2022). It would also incorporate costs and constraints consistent with realistic
transmission expansion timelines and restrictions. Then an analyst could use CEM to explicitly

concentrations of variable renewables or a rise in distributed energy resources (DERs). A CEM
transmission representation would enable an endogenous understanding of the trade-offs between
generation, storage, and transmission technology choice and location. There is a trade-off between
transmission detail and computational tractability, and this trade-off could be explored with various levels
of transmission aggregation.

3.1.1.4 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider in capacity expansion modeling:
1. Compare valuation results under alternative PSH economic lifetimes closer to the true physical life of
 the asset.
2. Perform sensitivity analysis considering alternative temporal scope and resolution to better
 understand accuracy-tractability trade-offs and how they affect solutions, including PSH value.
3. Consider alternative choices for representative days that better capture changes to net load profiles
 over time and enable representation of storage arbitrage value beyond diurnal scales.
4. Develop CEM capabilities to represent flexible loads that might compete with PSH for providing grid
 services.
5. Account for changes in ancillary services requirements with variable renewable deployment, and
 value all services appropriately.
6. Study the impact of incorporating transmission constraints within the CEM, starting from a coarse
 zonal aggregation.

3.1.2 Production Cost Modeling

Production cost models (PCMs) are designed to simulate detailed operations for a fixed set of system
resources without considering potential resource additions or retirements (Stoll et al. 2016; Diakov et al.
2015). They are typically operated at time intervals of 5 minutes to 1 hour and capture the operating
behavior of individual units or power plants. Typically, these models minimize the cost of system
operations over a specified time horizon, often 1 year, while considering detailed plant-level constraints
on energy, capacity, ramping, and other operational characteristics. In addition to modeling unit dispatch,
PCMs may also optimize unit commitment decisions for individual resources, or they alternatively may
rely on commitment heuristics or assumptions to limit computational burden. Transmission systems may
be represented through any of three primary approaches, with increasing computational requirements: (1)
an unconstrained copper-plate representation that does not consider transmission constraints, (2) a “pipe-
and-bubble” representation that determines transmission flows on individual lines to balance supply and
demand in each load zone, and (3) a full direct current optimal power flow representation that uses
physical laws to calculate transmission flows based on zonal or nodal generation and load (U.S.
Department of Energy 2024). Key model inputs include hourly demand projections, fuel prices, and unit
cost and performance characteristics.

 3.8

3.1.2.1 Temporal Scope and Resolution

Hourly simulation over the full model horizon is one approach, given sufficient computational resources.
Although this configuration is effective at producing intertemporal least-cost solutions for a given
simulation, it has similar implications on PSH valuation as with CEM models. Optimizing over the full
horizon could strain computational capacity and prevent modeling teams from considering greater detail
elsewhere, such as in the operational detail of PSH and other storage systems or spatial disaggregation of
generation and transmission assets (Stoll et al. 2016; Akdemir et al. 2024). For example, certain
technology types such as wind, solar, and hydropower might be aggregated, which limits the accounting
of variability of renewable resources in evaluating the PSH operation. Depending on the model horizon, it
might also preclude any consideration of longer time scopes (30 years or longer) that span a realistic
physical lifetime of PSH facilities.

Depending on the length of current model runtimes, it might be useful to consider alternative temporal
scope and resolution that explores trade-offs between optimizing across long time horizons and expanding
scope or detail in different areas (Stoll et al. 2016; Akdemir et al. 2024). For instance, several sequential
5- or 10-year simulations could allow better technology or other representations with an acceptable
deviation from a 20-year optimum. Alternatively, or in addition, a large set of individual single-year
production cost runs might be considered to explore a broader range of scenarios—for example, to
explore the impacts of variations in weather profiles without necessarily capturing multiyear sequential
progression. Deviations could be tested with equivalent model configurations and different temporal
scopes.

3.1.2.2 Transmission Representation

Another important consideration for future PSH valuation and PCM in general is the representation of
power flows within the service area and between external service areas. Historically, transmission system
capacity planning has often taken place downstream from generation planning, which often assumes
sufficient transmission capacity to justify ignoring transmission constraints in PCM simulations.
However, as the system continues to evolve, and new variable renewable generation resources are
introduced further from load centers, transmission usage patterns may change substantially (Medina et al.
2022). In particular, transmission congestion may become increasingly impactful and therefore not
prudent to ignore in PCM.

Including transmission in PCM could better demonstrate the value of PSH and other assets for alleviating
future potential transmission congestion or better management of resources. One example of improved
resource management could be reduced variable renewable curtailment, which could exhibit more value
when representing realistic transmission constraints given the potential concentration of variable
renewable generator deployments to specific areas with high resource quality (Jorgenson et al. 2022). In
general, congestion relief can facilitate more efficient use of low-cost generation resources, and PSH
could thus play a role in most cost-effective generation and transmission operation.

3.1.2.3 Imports and Exports

Power flows between a given service territory and other service territories might use an aggregated
representation because of legal and/or jurisdictional boundaries. For example, the PCM might represent
only one or a few external resources that can be called up to help serve demand with prices and available
quantities that vary hourly. These prices are determined through an external electricity market price
forecasting process, which does not include extensive sensitivity analysis. This formulation can be
effective at representing present-day contracts and constraints, but it does not consider how external

 3.9

markets and providers might change over time and how that would affect broader power exchanges
throughout an interconnect.

One possible enhancement would be to separate the current single external market representation into
multiple customers, each representing the physical or legal constraints on a different customer or region,
then allowing for the possibility of expanded trade with external service providers. Another option would
be to consider additional external price scenarios, either for the current single external market framework
or in conjunction with expanded external representation. These enhancements could help to reveal
scenarios in which PSH holds more, or less, value than when only a single external market is represented
(Anwar et al. 2022).

3.1.2.4 Distributed Energy Resource Representation

A separate but related challenge is the representation of power flows and trade with owners and
aggregators of DERs, which could include solar PV, batteries, or electric vehicles. A utility might not
model these resources explicitly because of their historical low market share and the lack of any direct
authority or control, but increased adoption could impact the bulk power system (Yip et al. 2023). The
quantity, location, and flexibility of DERs can affect the value proposition of PSH from the perspective of
energy balancing, reserve capacity needs, and managing grid stability (Ahmadi et al. 2021; Kang et al.
2017; Quint et al. 2019). The challenge of accounting for growing DER adoption also relates to spatial
resolution and transmission representations, where additional detail could become more important as
DER adoption increases.

3.1.2.5 Ancillary Service Value

PCM tools typically are better able to represent ancillary services markets and value than CEMs can.
However, they still must simplify these into model formulations that do not represent the nuances of real-
time grid operation and short-term stability and dynamics (Brinkman et al. 2021; Energy Exemplar
2024a). Fixed ancillary service requirements are typical. Often these constraints are nonbinding and
relatively easy to satisfy with traditional thermal resources. Thus, they do not produce a shadow price or
explicit market value. Therefore, there has been minimal need to consider providing them with PSH
resources. However, emerging trends suggest increasing deployment of variable renewables and thermal
resource retirements without equivalent replacement (e.g., coal) could increase the need for and value of
ancillary services that PSH is well suited to provide. Specifically, fixed-speed PSH, currently used
exclusively in the United States, cannot provide regulation reserves while operating in pumping mode.
Traditionally, this has not been a limiting factor because of the abundance of other resources providing
this service. As this dynamic changes and variable-speed pumping becomes a more attractive value
proposition, it may be prudent to revisit these constraints through informed modeling (Viola et al. 2024;
Ela et al. 2019). A key consideration for future modeling efforts is therefore capturing how system reserve
requirements may change as the generation portfolio evolves (Lew et al. 2013).

To address these emerging challenges, utilities might consider developing a new tool to determine
ancillary services requirements as a function of variable renewable energy deployments. For example, the
ReEDS CEM uses literature-derived relationships between variable renewable deployments and ancillary
services requirements, and these relationships are passed along to PCM models downstream (Lew et al.
2013). Similarly, the PCM used for the North American Renewable Integration Study calculates ancillary
services requirements considering wind and solar resources uncertainty with regional heterogeneity
(Brinkman et al. 2021).

 3.10

Utilities might also consider representing additional reserve or ancillary services products within the PCM
or in a complementary analysis. Competitive electricity markets in the United States include a wide range
of services for response times ranging from 30 seconds to 30 minutes, and some, like the California
Independent System Operator and Southwest Power Pool, also have ramping products. Although
vertically integrated utilities would not operate markets for these services, it might consider how their
inclusion in its operating procedures and modeling might affect economic and reliable operations. This
could be explored in PCM by testing new constraints, which could show the potential value of including
such constraints in operations and of the flexible technologies like PSH that can effectively satisfy them
(Ela et al. 2019; Viola et al. 2024). Utilities could also conduct analyses to assess how changing the
operating constraints that limit PSH ancillary service provision could impact system costs in different
future generation portfolios (Cohen and Mowers 2022; Stark et al. 2023; Koritarov et al. 2014). A simpler
approach could be to supplement PCM-derived value with a presumed value of additional services using
prices or compensation charges from other systems (Viola et al. 2024; Gilmore et al. 2024; Almetwally et
al. 2024). This approach could be more appropriate for services such as voltage support or black start
capability.

3.1.2.6 Platform Challenges

All modeling platforms have limitations associated with core model structures. One challenge with
commercial software is these structures might not be obvious, observable, and modifiable. For example,
software might automatically relax physical constraints such as ramp limits or minimum run times using
penalized slack variables. This practice can improve solution feasibility in the model but also produces
unrealistic operating conditions. Specifically, if the model allows greater operational flexibility in other
resources than is physically possible, it could underestimate the value of PSH for providing grid
flexibility.

One option would be to work with the software developers to increase certain slack variable cost penalties
to prioritize enforcement of certain operational constraints. This practice could introduce scaling issues
and increase run times, but the computation cost may be worth the improved accuracy (Chen et al. 2010;
Stark et al. 2023). Incorporating postprocessing procedures that enforce physical feasibility and adjust the
model dispatch outputs accordingly might also be considered, but these can be hard to implement, and it
could be difficult to anticipate what manual feasibility checks are necessary before errors occur (Stark et
al. 2023).

PCMs might also be unable to distinguish between fixed- and variable-speed pumping PSH plants. This
capability is important for capturing PSH value; however, specifying constraints that require fixed-speed
pumps to pump at maximum capacity or allow variable-speed pumps to supply regulation reserves while
pumping has been shown to create computational difficulties while still failing to represent realistic PSH
operations (Cohen and Mowers 2022; Koritarov et al. 2014; Stark et al. 2023). This challenge alone
reflects strong structural uncertainty around the model’s estimation of PSH value, particularly if ancillary
services market value grows with variable renewable deployment. Improving the distinction between
fixed-speed PSH, variable-speed PSH, and other flexible storage and generator technologies will be
important for accurately estimating the value of new PSH resources.

3.1.2.7 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider in production cost modeling:
1. Perform internal analysis to study the implications of alternative temporal scopes, then study
 computational trade-offs between temporal scopes and other model configuration choices.
2. Test the impacts of explicitly representing a transmission system at zonal or nodal resolution along
 with additional options for capturing interactions with neighboring entities.
3. Consider disaggregating traded electricity to consider multiple specific customers, including DERs.
4. Work with PCM commercial vendors to develop methods to prioritize individual operating
 constraints if they are not inherently strictly enforced by the tool.
5. Improve ancillary services representations to consider growing reserve requirements with variable
 renewable deployments and additional or alternative service requirements.
6. Study methods to better differentiate fixed-speed versus variable-speed PSH operations in PCM
 models.

3.1.3 Hydrological Systems Impacts Modeling

New PSH can have several types of site configurations, with one distinction being open-loop versus
closed-loop. Open- and closed-loop PSH have different impacts on hydrological systems that require
differences in the focus of their impact assessment. Open-loop PSH is continuously connected to a
flowing body of water, and impact assessments primarily address water quality, water quantity, and
aquatic resource impacts on surface water systems. Hydrological system impact assessments from add-on
PSH, that is, PSH created through adding an upper reservoir to an existing lower reservoir, are also
focused primarily on surface water quantity, water quality, and aquatic biodiversity, although water
quality impacts of constructing the new reservoir are also important. Unless otherwise noted, open-loop
PSH is hereafter inclusive of add-on PSH.

Closed-loop PSH, which relies on reservoirs that are disconnected from flowing rivers, has assessments
with greater focus on groundwater than open-loop PSH. However, closed-loop PSH that uses an existing
river or stream for initial reservoir fill and evaporation make-up water will also require assessment of
surface water impacts (Saulsbury 2020; Pracheil et al. 2025).

Although surface and groundwater quantity and quality and aquatic resource impacts are important to
assess for construction and operation of all types of PSH, additional impact categories will eventually be
assessed as part of National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) requirements that must be met as part of
the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licensing or federal hydropower development
process. Additional categories assessed as part of NEPA include cultural resources, geology and soils,
terrestrial resources, recreation and land use, threatened and endangered species, socioeconomics, and air
quality and noise (Saulsbury 2020; Pracheil et al. 2025). State and federal impact assessments beyond
those required by NEPA may also be required as part of water quality, historic property, and other
regulations.

Because of their integration with flowing rivers, open-loop PSH operations may have water-level
fluctuation impacts in downstream reaches and reservoirs from timescales ranging from minutes to
multiple days. Those interactions can propagate into impacts on hydropower production and scheduling,
water temperature and dissolved gas concentration, recreation, and infrastructure such as municipal
drinking water and cooling water intakes (Saulsbury 2020). From an aquatic ecology perspective, fish and
other aquatic biodiversity can have diurnal to seasonal changes in movements, habitat use, and life stage

 3.12

distribution that may be disrupted by short-term fluctuations in downstream reservoir elevation as well as
local changes in stream temperature and dissolved gas from PSH water releases. For example, fish that
move over the course of the day from deeper to shallower water or from cove habitats to the main body of
the reservoir may become stranded as the downstream reservoir elevation changes during pumping
(Bozeman et al. 2025) or entrained into the upper reservoir (Mathur et al. 2018). Changes in
reservoir/river elevation where the pumping and releases occur may also mean that recreational anglers
and boaters will navigate a changing water level throughout the course of a day as some underwater
hazards may become visible, safe to pass over, or submerged and a navigation hazard.

Published reports provide a comprehensive overview for investors exploring whether a closed-loop
(Pracheil et al. 2025) or open-loop (Saulsbury 2020) project might be more cost- and time-effective for
getting the state and federal permits (e.g., FERC preliminary permit), certifications (e.g., § 401 Clean
Water Act certification), and licenses (FERC license) required for PSH construction and operation
(Levine et al. 2021). However, integrated power system, hydrological system, and natural resource impact
assessments that consider a range of water availability and power demand scenarios are not currently part
of these requirements yet are essential for understanding the viability of developing a PSH site. Moreover,
because there is no single tool available that can address all the needs of an integrated hydrological
system impact assessment, a team of experts will likely be required for its completion. Below, we provide
strategic actions for performing such an integrated PSH-hydrological system operation impact
assessment. These actions are designed with open-loop PSH in mind because they have the greatest
interaction with the rest of the hydrological system, although they are largely applicable to closed-loop
PSH as well.

Assessing impacts of a PSH system can be performed in three stages. In Stage 1, the impact on the
surface water system can be evaluated with the PSH intended operations (typically price following or
peak load shaving), without any change to existing hydrological system operations. This assessment
provides a benchmark for the cost of PSH operations on aquatic resources and river services, including
hydropower operations. It also provides information needed for designing appropriate mitigations,
particularly flow requirements such as reservoir elevation requirements or PSH pumping or generation
restrictions.

The second and third stages of the assessment are performed as needed, depending on the outcome of
Stage 1. Stage 2 considers assessing hydrological system impacts of the proposed PSH operations on the
aquatic resources and river services under one or more potential flow mitigation scenarios. In this way,
Stage 2 of the assessment provides a benchmark for evaluating the minimum cost of the intended PSH
operations.

If costs of PSH operations determined in Stage 2 are not acceptable, Stage 3 includes a reoperation of
both PSH and the hydrological system, which can be performed as a co-optimization or iterative process.
Proceeding from Stage 1 directly to Stage 3 might appear reasonable, with the caveat the computational
cost of co-optimization and iterations typically does not support a thorough evaluation of all aquatic
resources and river services in the process. The Stage 2 benchmark is essential to assess the influence of
the mitigation revenue and power system outcomes performed by the optimal co-optimized reoperation of
the hydrological system.

For the Stage 1 evaluation, an operational river routing and reservoir operation model from the institution
operating the river can be leveraged. Typically, the models do not represent all aquatic resources and river
services such as water quality, recreation, and biodiversity, and additional analysis is necessary. For Stage
2 evaluation, a reoperation of the hydrological system under mitigation scenarios can be an intensive
process depending on the capabilities of the initial model. Stage 3 is the most computationally intensive

 3.13

and interdisciplinary effort, prone to results that depend on the representation of hydropower operations
and scheduling (Stark et al. 2023).

3.1.3.1 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider in hydrological systems impacts modeling:
1. Focus PSH impact assessments on the integration among hydrological systems, aquatic resources, and
 river services, by first identifying permitting requirements in terms of metrics and analytical resources
 needed for their evaluation.
2. Use a three-stage process that provides best practices in assessing, designing, and reporting on the
 impacts of PSH operations under multiple water availability and power system scenarios.

3.1.4 Transmission Reliability Modeling

Several factors are driving increased concerns over weakening grid stability and strength in certain
regions. These factors include large and potentially concentrated IBR deployments, retirements of large
fossil fuel plants such as coal, and large loads including PSH in pumping mode. Given broad connectivity
across the Eastern Interconnection, frequency stability is not an urgent problem in the eastern United
States (NERC 2023). However, in the eastern load center, fossil fuel plants are planned to retire soon,
whereas renewable energy development will be located mainly in the western region. This uneven
geographic distribution between the load and new generation limits the ability of reactive power
generated in the west to support the eastern grid, with voltage sag and stability being the primary issue
(TVA 2023). Meanwhile, in existing dynamic model libraries, there is no robust variable-speed PSH
model, hindering the understanding of this advanced technology’s role in supporting voltage stability.
These issues could be exacerbated in other interconnects with smaller systems or more geographically
sparse generation assets and loads.

PSH operations in general might undergo significant changes. Traditionally, PSH systems have been used
for load leveling—pumping water during off-peak hours and generating electricity during peak demand to
help stabilize the grid. However, with the continued expansion of renewable energy, particularly solar,
PSH operations will need to adapt to new net load patterns. For example, PSH may need to shift to
pumping mode during peak solar generation to absorb excess low-cost energy midday, partially because
of power purchase agreement terms that can reduce the financial incentive to curtail excess solar
generation or otherwise limit operational flexibility (Denholm et al. 2010; Ela et al. 2013; Koritarov et al.
2014). Because of this shift in PSH operations, stability scenario design will become more complex,
requiring the implementation of not just peak load scenarios but also high IBR scenarios, such as solar,
wind, and batteries (MISO 2021). During high IBR scenarios, net load decreases, and more traditional
generators that provide voltage support may be offline. This highlights the need to explore and value how
variable-speed PSH can be used to provide voltage support under these conditions.

As the grid’s stability margin declines with the transition to renewable energy, a more integrated approach
is becoming increasingly necessary. Conducting stability scenario design and analysis only during the
dynamic simulation stage is insufficient because the available resources for stability support are limited
by the type and location of online generators. Incorporating new transmission reliability constraints into
the planning and operation stage can help improve system strength and stability (Chu and Teng 2023;
Gao et al. 2023; Wogrin et al. 2020; Ullmark et al. 2024). Variable-speed PSH with the advanced PSH
technology can play a crucial role in meeting these reliability requirements effectively, making it
increasingly important to represent the operational differences between fixed- and variable-speed PSH in
all model classes. This would include transient stability analysis of contingency events to understand

 3.14

system needs and study active and reactive control strategies for variable-speed PSH. Examples include
doubly fed induction generator technologies that provide fast power control using a power converter
while the turbine governor controls rotational speed with the gate position. Following contingency events
such as a generation loss, this fast power control capability can offer superior transient performance
compared to a conventional synchronous machine unit.

3.1.4.1 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider in transmission reliability modeling:
1. Analyze grid stability in scenarios that include peak load, high IBR deployment, and alternative PSH
 operation modes.
2. Compare the contribution of fixed-speed versus variable-speed PSH to grid frequency and voltage
 response under a range of contingency scenarios.
3. Conduct transient stability analysis across contingency scenarios to identify frequency and voltage
 support needs that can be used to validate device-specific dynamic stability performance and develop
 effective control strategies.
4. Incorporate transmission reliability considerations endogenously into CEM planning and PCM
 operation model stages—for example, by adding requirements for frequency or voltage services, or
 developing heuristics that require reliability-oriented operational constraints (e.g., must-run) at certain
 load or other grid conditions.

3.2 Model Integration Practices
Grid modeling tools of varying scales and scopes are often used together to provide a more
comprehensive integrated picture of current and future grid planning and operations (Brinkman et al.
2021; DOE 2024; Cochran 2021). This practice allows for the study of a greater set of analysis questions
so that decisions are better informed and there is greater confidence in the accuracy and robustness of
results. Figure 1 is a model process flow diagram showing several possible ways model components
discussed in this report could be linked. A major challenge with model integration, however, is achieving
consistency across different models of varying scope, resolution, and structure (Iyer et al. 2019). Different
models use different data in different formats and represent physical systems in different ways, depending
on their structure, features, and computational tractability. Achieving consistency across models thus
entails manual or automated data transfer processes that can proceed in one direction or establish
feedback loops (Iyer et al. 2019; Vorosmarty et al. 2017; Mongird and Rice 2024).

The next several subsections discuss various ways in which CEM, PCM, river operations, and
transmission reliability models are integrated into cohesive workflows and how these workflows might
evolve to support PSH valuation and any other integrated grid analysis. We begin with discussing water-
energy connections in grid modeling because of the unique characteristics of hydropower and water-
energy interactions. Then we more generally discuss one-way workflows and model feedback, in which
the latter could involve one or more iterations.

3.2.1 One-Way Workflows

One-way workflows in integrated grid planning typically involve CEM simulations that inform future
infrastructures to simulate in PCM (Cowiestoll and Frazier 2022). Transmission reliability modeling
could also be conducted downstream from CEM and PCM modeling using future infrastructure
information from the CEM (DOE 2024). Other tools such as load forecasting, resource adequacy, or
resource potential models can also exist upstream or downstream from CEM-PCM workflows, depending
on whether they are being used to inform CEM or PCM data or constraints or evaluate CEM or PCM
outcomes (Stenclik 2024; Maclaurin et al. 2021; Mai et al. 2018).

3.2.1.1 Capacity Expansion Modeling to Production Cost Modeling

Typically, infrastructure results from a CEM can be used to create PCM model inputs, but this is often a
largely manual process. In practice, this process can be time-consuming to maintain fully consistent
datasets and assumptions for both models and transfer data from CEM to PCM with any necessary data
expansions. Although effective at creating consistency in the generation fleet between CEM and PCM
models, the required time prevents resources from being used in other areas, such as expanded scenario
analysis or improving base model formulations. It also inherently limits the degree to which integrated
modeling studies can iterate as study needs evolve.

Increased automation and model choice are two strategies to reduce CEM-to-PCM conversion time.
Although automation is initially a high-effort (and thus costly) endeavor, it could lead to substantial
improvements in workflow efficiency that enable orders of magnitude of more scenarios to be explored
(Cowiestoll and Frazier 2022). Although considering more scenarios means more data to process, it can
lead to more robust decision-making under uncertainty, particularly in a dynamic system like the
electricity and broader energy system. A downside to automation is the need to maintain any automation
tools so that they stay compatible with both upstream and downstream models. However, automated
translation could be developed incrementally (e.g., start only with generating unit data). Effectively
developing and maintaining CEM-to-PCM translation tools could allow utilities to perform internal
analysis with scopes on par with the NREL Standard Scenarios and Cambium datasets (Gagnon et al.
2024), which in 2023 included 70 CEM scenarios and 8 PCM scenarios in 5-year intervals between 2025

 3.16

and 2050. Expanded scenarios could be important to better identify the relationship between key system
drivers and PSH value.
Simply choosing models that combine CEM-PCM capabilities or have built-in CEM-to-PCM conversions
could also greatly expedite workflows but at significant upfront cost (Bonaldo et al. 2024; Energy
Exemplar 2024b). Many commercial tools and open-source research-grade tools have sufficient flexibility
to define a solution space that spans both planning and operational horizons (Energy Exemplar 2020;
Bonaldo et al. 2024; Energy Exemplar 2024a; NREL 2024). This level of flexibility and integration can
become increasingly important as emerging technologies such as variable renewables, distributed
resources, electrified transportation, and storage create greater uncertainty in the future of the grid.
Changing tools and processes creates substantial challenges from a staff training and capability
perspective, but these challenges can be weighed against the benefits.

3.2.1.2 Informing Transmission Models

Transmission models designed to evaluate grid deliverability, reliability, strength, and stability must use
nodal generation and transmission information, and upstream CEM-PCM models might not have such
resolution. Downscaling a future generation and transmission infrastructure for use in a nodal
transmission reliability model is a challenging process that involves substantive engineering judgment
around asset siting while also identifying critical reliability periods (DOE 2024; Kuna et al. 2024).
Without nodal resolution in CEM and PCM models, any detailed transmission system expansions must be
considered downstream from CEM and PCM work and thus cannot influence least-cost planning and
operations solutions. This practice increases uncertainty around the cost-optimal way to site new
transmission assets and limits the understanding of how transmission constraints and costs can influence
the value proposition for new PSH and other assets. It also prevents insights into how transmission
constraints and reliability needs might influence siting of PSH and other facilities (Wiedemann and
Adjiashvili 2022; Jayadev et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2024). As grid stability becomes a greater concern
with retiring thermal generation and increased deployment of variable renewables and inverter-based
battery storage, it becomes increasingly difficult to confidently plan and study new transmission needs
after decisions are made upstream on generation investments.

If CEM and PCM tools include transmission constraints, even at an aggregate level, these can help inform
scenarios for future nodal transmission systems to study in grid stability and other high-resolution
transmission models (DOE 2024; Jayadev et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2024). In the context of PSH, this
could substantially help establish PSH value for providing grid reliability services, or it could identify
siting trade-offs by showing where the PSH might improve or degrade grid stability under alternative
scenarios (Koritarov et al. 2021; Prasasti et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2022). Even if engineering judgment and
heuristics remain necessary to convert from an aggregate to a nodal transmission representation, the
improved one-way workflow from CEM/PCM to transmission models can facilitate more robust analysis.

3.2.1.3 Other Model Connections

Grid models are often informed by a wide range of other specialized models and data tools to populate the
breadth of required input data. Here we focus on two: long-term load forecasting and resource adequacy
assessment.

Load forecasting is necessary to project the quantity and profiles of future electricity demand. The shape
of the load curve over various timescales (diurnal, interday, interweek) is critical to estimating the value
of energy storage assets, especially large-scale assets such as PSH (Sánchez-Pérez et al. 2022; Sepulveda
et al. 2021; Denholm et al. 2021). Load forecasting is also becoming increasingly difficult because of
uncertainties in data center load growth, transportation and building electrification, and DERs. Each of

 3.17

these technologies changes the load shapes observed at the substation level by the utility (Sigrin et al.
2016; Mai et al. 2018; Hale et al. 2018; Willems et al. 2022). In addition, some electrified loads provide
opportunities for aggregation and centralized demand response management (Obi et al. 2020; Riaz and
Mancarella 2022; Eid et al. 2016). It is unclear how flexible loads will interact with PSH and other
flexible assets, but there is potential to influence the PSH value proposition. Expanded scenarios for
future load growth and load profiles could lead to new insights into PSH value and other planning
decisions.

Resource adequacy modeling is used to test the ability of a grid infrastructure to serve load under a large
sample of outage conditions and other uncertain grid conditions (Stephen 2021; Alberta Electric System
Operator 2017). These models can be used downstream from CEM tools using a projected future
infrastructure. Alternatively, they could be used upstream with a given infrastructure to help determine
the capacity reserve margin a CEM should use to ensure it plans a system that meets resource adequacy
requirements (Kuna et al. 2024; Stephen and Kirschen 2024). Utilities might use resource adequacy
models to inform the reserve margin requirement in their CEM. Although this one-way process may have
been effective to date, the growing deployment of variable renewables and storage complicates resource
adequacy estimation that must now consider forecast uncertainty of renewables and storage energy limits
on top of load uncertainty and asset availability. There is thus a risk the reserve margin used in CEM
misrepresents true stress periods for meeting load. This is important for PSH valuation because capacity
value for resource adequacy can be a major driver of storage value (Sodano et al. 2021; Frew et al. 2018).
It is thus important to consider the uncertainty around an appropriate reserve margin requirement or if the
current approach to specify a reserve margin in CEM is appropriate for the expected future grid
composition. The next section on model feedback elaborates further on how CEM and resource adequacy
models could interact.

3.2.1.4 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider when exploring one-way workflows:
1. Conduct cost-benefit analysis to decide if resources should be committed to automating data
 translation processes between models or using models that are more integrated or flexible.
2. Consider methods for representing endogenous transmission expansion within CEM and PCM tools
 to create more robust grid topologies to study PSH value for providing transmission reliability
 services.
3. Identify key data workflows upstream from grid models (e.g., load, resource adequacy) and use them
 to develop scenarios for alternative data and assumptions to use across models.
4. Expand resource adequacy modeling to consider a broader range of possible grid configurations and
 events that might occur over the operating lifetime of the PSH facility.

3.2.2 Model Feedback Loops

Feedback over one or more iterations enables increased consistency across models while allowing the
broader modeling suite to better optimize its objective, given the full breadth of features available across
all models. However, this often comes at substantial computational expense and upfront cost to design
data transfer and other procedures for iterating through one or more model linkages (EIA 2023; Caron et
al. 2018; Mongird and Rice 2024; Reed et al. 2022). In practice, feedback often occurs informally through
lessons learned from one model that helps inform future use of another. Here we discuss ways feedback
might be considered solely from a technical perspective; this report does not discuss the potential for
organizational strategies to facilitate cross-model feedback.

 3.18

3.2.2.1 Transmission Reliability To Inform Resource Planning

A key challenge to the field of integrated grid modeling is incorporating transmission reliability and grid
stability considerations into long-term planning and operations modeling. Doing so is becoming
increasingly important as the grid evolves toward variable renewables and storage while retiring thermal
generation and dealing with challenging constraints on transmission asset expansion (DOE 2024; Lew et
al. 2021; Lin et al. 2022). In practice, transmission management has a substantial effect on grid dispatch
and operations, particularly PSH that provides many reliability services, such as voltage support and
frequency regulation, which are not explicitly represented in CEM or PCM tools (Koritarov et al. 2021).
If they were, it would enable a more complete picture of the range of services that PSH provides and
enable a more balanced examination of optimal asset investment strategies.

Although it could be extremely difficult to tightly couple transmission reliability models with CEM or
PCM, it could be beneficial to incorporate transmission reliability analysis earlier in the grid modeling
workflow and consider how infrastructure plans may be modified to account for stability results. For
example, cost-optimal CEM might favor large-scale battery deployment, but this outcome might create
stability concerns with unexplained battery trips during disturbance events (Grid-India 2023; Xie and
Shair 2024). The CEM does not represent these events or grid behaviors, but it could consider a cost or
performance penalty (or benefit) by technology to reflect their impact on grid stability (Ullmark et al.
2024; Wogrin et al. 2020; Chu and Teng 2023; Gao et al. 2023). Including a transmission overlay in CEM
or PCM could enable other options for using transmission reliability models to inform transmission
constraints, particularly if any transmission expansions are then included in transmission reliability
models to assess the combined impact of transmission and other assets such as PSH. In a high-variable
renewable, high IBR future, transmission reliability analysis has a key role to play in investment
decisions.

3.2.2.2 Capacity Expansion Modeling–Production Cost Modeling Coupling

If efficient one-way workflows are established from CEM to PCM modeling, PCM outcomes could be
used to better parameterize the CEM (Mongird and Rice 2024; Mansouri et al. 2022; Iyer et al. 2019).
CEMs typically have a coarser representation of operational parameters and constraints than PCMs,
meaning there could be discrepancies between intra-annual dispatch results that are critical for
establishing energy, capacity, and reserve value for PSH and other resources. If operational outcomes
diverge enough between PCM and CEM, particularly at the new PSH facility being evaluated, it could be
important to consider how the CEM configuration might be adjusted to better match the more detailed
PCM operating results. Iteration between the two could then achieve an acceptable level of convergence
to foster confidence that the investment decisions in the CEM are fully consistent with the detailed
operating decisions in the PCM (Mansouri et al. 2022).

3.2.2.3 Dynamic Resource Adequacy Requirements

The previous section on one-way feedback discussed how stochastic resource adequacy models can be
used to inform CEM capacity reserve margin requirements. Resource adequacy models, however, use an
exogenously defined infrastructure configuration that could be updated with results from a CEM. There is
thus an opportunity for feedback between the two model types, where the resource adequacy model could
inform any resource adequacy (e.g., reserve margin) constraints in the CEM with an initialized
infrastructure, then performance of the resulting CEM infrastructure could be checked in the resource
adequacy model (Kuna et al. 2024; Stephen and Kirschen 2024). If unserved energy or other metrics
violate standards, the resource adequacy model could then be used to update CEM parameters or

 3.19

constraints to facilitate improved performance. This process could repeat until the CEM infrastructure
meets resource adequacy standards.

This iterative process could proceed in multiple ways. The resource adequacy model could simply update
the reserve margin requirement in the CEM. Alternatively, the resource adequacy model could be used to
identify which time periods across a multiyear stochastic dataset should be considered “stress periods,” in
which resource adequacy is difficult to maintain (Kuna et al. 2024). These stress periods could then be
included explicitly in the CEM along with any necessary reserve margin to ensure resource adequacy in
those periods. Stress periods can vary over time and location with the deployment of variable renewables,
storage, and other grid assets, so any coupling framework must allow for this flexibility. Enabling
dynamic resource adequacy requirements in a CEM would require substantial time and cost to develop
efficient data transfers between the two models. However, it would ultimately allow a better
understanding of the value of PSH in satisfying resource adequacy requirements and result in a more
reliable system.

3.2.2.4 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider when exploring model feedback loops:
1. Identify ways to incentivize or disincentivize technology investments and operation in CEM and
 PCM to reflect their impacts on grid strength stability. This may involve model coupling or modified
 workflows that include transmission reliability analysis at an earlier stage.
2. Compare model operational outcomes between PCM and CEM tools to determine if they are
 sufficiently consistent across temporal and spatial scales, or if feedback should be considered to
 facilitate greater agreement.
3. Consider ways to dynamically update resource adequacy requirements in the CEM by coupling or
 otherwise iterating with a probabilistic resource adequacy model.

3.3 Scenario Exploration Strategies
Given the number of key drivers of electric sector outcomes and the uncertainty around those outcomes, it
is not practical to simulate all possible outcomes or even a statistically representative sample that
represents the distributions of all key drivers. Therefore, it is common practice to define a set of
representative scenarios that identify key drivers of sectoral outcomes and examine the sensitivity of
outcomes to variations in those drivers. These scenarios can be chosen based on known trends, or there
can be a quantitative sampling procedure that defines some scenario inputs. Determining a suitable
scenario suite is a complex process constrained by computational and staff resources, and it is made more
complex when considering consistency across multiple model types that offer different scenario options
because of structure and data requirements (Li et al. 2022; Witt et al. 2020; Fodstad et al. 2022). This
section discusses some of those challenges in detail and how future scenario modeling might achieve
greater cross-model consistency while covering a broader range of electric sector uncertainty.

3.3.1 Consistency Across Models

Some aspects of cross-model consistency, such as unit characteristics or load projections, are readily
maintained by one-way model workflows and data development processes. With other more nuanced and
model-specific assumptions, such as capacity credit for variable renewables and storage, it is more
difficult to achieve consistency. If spatial and intra-annual temporal resolution is not the same across a
CEM, PCM, and transmission reliability analysis workflow, the expected production from variable

 3.20

renewables might imply different abilities to serve load, even if aggregated availability is consistent.
Resource adequacy modeling is also able to consider variable renewable capacity availability under a
broader set of conditions that more explicitly represent periods in which capacity credit is more valuable;
this value is not as well represented in the PCM and CEM tools with more direct economic outcomes
(Stephen and Kirschen 2024; Frew et al. 2017). Difficulty consistently capturing capacity value is
particularly important for PSH valuation, given its known strength in this area. Increased integration of
resource adequacy and CEM models, as discussed earlier, is one way to improve this consistency.

Resource adequacy modeling also highlights the inability to represent certain scenario classes effectively
across all models, which further complicates a value assessment for PSH and other technologies. For
example, extreme weather and other hazard or contingency events can technically be represented in CEM
or PCM, but doing so overstates their expected frequency because they are not represented across a
stochastic or probability-weighted sample space (Bennett et al. 2021; Moreno et al. 2020; Perera et al.
2020; Brockway and Dunn 2020). PSH has known anecdotal value for responding to hazards and grid
events, but this value is not well represented in common least-cost modeling frameworks. Creating or
expanding upon existing stochastic sampling methods for PCM and CEM model inputs could resolve this
inconsistency but at significant cost and time requirements. Recognizing this limitation in overall value
assessment is a first step, followed by continual reevaluation of available methods for incorporating
extreme events into long-term integrated grid planning decisions (Bennett et al. 2021; Moreno et al. 2020;
Perera et al. 2020; Brockway and Dunn 2020).

Model scenario inconsistencies can also be created simply by updating data or assumptions in
downstream models without restarting the full workflow. This can be exacerbated if model teams are not
closely coordinated and can be improved by increasing automation of data transfers between models and
teams. It is reasonable to expect data and assumptions to change over the course of a study, so it is
important to develop tools that facilitate rapidly achieving consistency and determine how to most
efficiently use these tools given study needs (Cowiestoll and Frazier 2022; Iyer et al. 2019; Mongird and
Rice 2024; Hardy et al. 2024). Doing so can improve both the quality and possible scope of a valuation
study.

3.3.1.1 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider when exploring consistency across models:
1. Consider advanced methods in scientific literature for incorporating extreme events into integrated
 grid modeling scenario suites to consider the value of PSH for responding to such events.
2. Create tools and processes that achieve greater and more automatic consistency across models and
2. iterations of analytical workflows.

3.3.2 Characterizing Uncertainty

The key components of characterizing uncertainty are first deciding on the critical uncertain elements
driving outcomes, then determining how to represent the uncertainty in those elements (Li et al. 2022;
Roald et al. 2023; Singh et al. 2022). The first step is discussed here as the choice of scenario types, or
scenario levers. The second is discussed as scenario sampling, or scenario settings. Together, these define
a scenario suite for one or more models. Valuing a specific asset such as PSH can also be facilitated by
simulating any scenario with and without the new asset so that its impacts can be isolated. Valuation of
new potential assets could be much improved simply by expanding the scenarios used in valuation
procedures in ways such as those discussed in the next section.

 3.21

3.3.2.1 Scenario Types

Scenario types are typically chosen based on which data or assumptions are thought to have the biggest or
most uncertain impact on model outcomes, but they are also driven by modeling capabilities and other
interests. Here we discuss elements both inside and outside utility control as part of a broad scenario
definition because controlled elements such as generation technology investment choices are still often
driven by uncontrolled elements such as technology costs and supply chain constraints. Common scenario
types include: load growth, fuel prices, policy/regulations, and technology choice (Child and Farrell
2024). Technology choice reflects how investments are directed to emphasize certain grid characteristics
such as resiliency, distributed generation, renewable energy, or environmental outcomes. These are
typically implemented first in the CEM, then infrastructures are passed to the PCM to maintain as much
consistency as possible.

It is important to continually reevaluate scenario types to remain responsive to evolving electric sector
trends and cover a meaningful range of plausible outcomes. For example, fuel price scenarios could
become less important as fossil generation, particularly coal, largely retires. On the other hand, there is
growing uncertainty around transportation and building electrification and how these and other demand-
side generation and storage systems will interact with the bulk power system (Murphy and Mai 2021).
Data center growth is another key demand driver (Shehabi et al. 2024). Although demand-side resources
are not in the direct control of a vertically integrated utility, their increasing impact on net load and grid
management needs cannot be ignored, and including alternative demand-side and distributed resource
scenarios could inform utility policy and practices (Prasanna et al. 2021; McCabe et al. 2022; Fitiwi and
Bertsch 2018; Marañón-Ledesma and Tomasgard 2019). These might vary the effect on demand quantity,
load shape, and load flexibility, all of which will interact strongly with a proposed PSH facility.

Generation, storage, and transmission technology uncertainty is also an important consideration,
particularly in a multidecadal planning period. Technology costs and performance for storage
technologies competing with PSH are changing rapidly (National Renewable Energy Laboratory 2023).
Future solutions such as hydrogen-based systems, carbon dioxide capture, and advanced nuclear could
also play a role that either competes with or complements flexible assets like PSH (Gagnon et al. 2024;
Larson et al. 2021; Iyer et al. 2017). Although some technologies might be perceived as low-probability
investments in the near future, they could be more important to consider for a 100-year asset such as PSH.
Scenarios involving transmission technologies and investment options, including line upgrades, new
corridors, or reliability assets such as synchronous condensing, could also affect the value proposition of
PSH or other assets (DOE 2024). A transmission representation in CEM and PCM models would enable
exploration of this sensitivity.

Another dynamic area worth considering under a scenario suite is the interactions with other service
territories. Broadening the spatial scope of the models to include other service territories might not be
practical, but it could be useful to consider how developments in other nearby service territories such as
new investments, load growth, or new policies, could alter assumptions around imports, exports, and
multi-market participation. External markets could enhance or reduce opportunities for PSH depending on
how they change over time, making such sensitivities a useful exercise. These types of cross-market
interactions have been studied at an interconnect scale in NREL’s Interconnections Seams study (Bloom
et al. 2020).

Extreme weather and other hazards also constitute a large array of possibilities that impact the grid over
many spatial and temporal scales (Perera et al. 2020; Brockway and Dunn 2020; Craig et al. 2018).
Common practices use static profiles that reflect normalized weather conditions. However, long-term
trends in temperature-driven load and technology performance as well as precipitation-driven hydropower
and cooling water availability create uncertain future grid conditions (Auffhammer et al. 2017; Craig et

 3.22

al. 2018; Miara et al. 2019). All of these effects can influence long-term planning decisions. Short-term
weather events could change in frequency and intensity and could offer another scenario consideration
relevant to flexible technologies like PSH (Perera et al. 2020; Bennett et al. 2021). These can be
considered in PCM or transmission reliability modeling. Such extreme operating conditions may be
significant drivers of PSH value and are therefore important to capture and assess.

Transmission reliability analysis typically has its own set of scenarios focused on identifying worst-case
situations for grid strength and stability, such as peak gross load with solar PV oversupply that prompts
PSH to operate in pump mode. An expanded scenario approach could also consider changes to
transmission infrastructure or changes to infrastructure in other service territories. Other scenarios could
represent extreme events that go beyond single or double contingency events (Guo et al. 2021; Mujjuni et
al. 2023; Jufri et al. 2019). These can be informed by CEM and PCM scenarios even if they are not fully
consistent. Expanding transmission reliability scenarios in creative directions is important to understand
the reliability contributions of flexible assets such as PSH.

3.3.2.2 Scenario Sampling

Once scenario types are chosen, the range and distribution of inputs and assumptions for each scenario
type define the full scenario set. Ideally, one would identify a probability distribution for each key input
then randomly sample across all key inputs. However, there are computational and staff resource limits on
the number of distributions that can be created and samples that can be run, preventing modelers from
creating a complete output distribution. Thus, care must be taken to understand any potential biases based
on the characteristics of the chosen samples and to appropriately frame the likelihood of each outcome.

Stochastic CEM can be very computationally intensive, so practitioners must identify a coherent set of
scenario settings for its CEM modeling (Diaz et al. 2020; Valencia Zuluaga et al. 2024; Mansouri et al.
2020; da Costa et al. 2021). Given the large and compounding uncertainty over long-term planning
timescales, scenario settings must be carefully chosen to capture the range and distribution of possible
outcomes of interest. Doing so could require iteration and scenario discovery when unforeseen outcomes
and interactions occur (Hadjimichael et al. 2024; Bojórquez-Tapia et al. 2022). An overly restrictive set of
scenario types and settings can unintentionally overstate the likelihood of the outcomes in those scenarios
and have negative impacts on decision support. One way to expand the scenario range for a given study
could be an initial screening step that uses a simplified model setup to quickly explore a wider range of
scenarios and identify key drivers and interactions for a final scenario set. The value of a specific asset
such as PSH could be very sensitive to many scenario settings; it is important to establish and explore
which ones.

Extreme event scenarios are inherently difficult to characterize because of the sheer number of possible
hazards and outcomes. Although there is insufficient scope in this document to fully characterize these
options, it deserves mention in the context of PSH valuation because PSH holds value for responding to
extreme events of many types (Koritarov et al. 2021; Ela et al. 2013). Identifying a few critical hazards
and how they could manifest on the system could be an important way to evaluate new asset plans in
PCM and transmission reliability models. Although these events might be unlikely, the high value of
mitigating them should not be ignored in the overall value proposition of PSH or other assets.

Transmission reliability modeling offers a range of assumptions not necessarily available to other model
types because of the highly detailed transmission system representation. Static power flow models
typically have more detail than is included in PCM and CEM, and dynamic stability models go even
further. For a given asset plan, it could be worth considering multiple assumptions around the locations
and characteristics of future renewable energy, batteries, or loads connecting to the grid. Changes to
interactions with external service territories could occur at varying locations and consist of many

 3.23

alternative asset changes. Sampling larger contingency events that align with extreme event scenarios
could include varying degrees of grouped versus distributed outages of different asset types. When so
many options are considered, it is often helpful to focus first on bounding conditions but do so across a
full range of possibilities. Expanded transmission reliability analysis could produce new insights into PSH
value while also identifying future transmission needs and challenges.

3.3.2.3 Strategic Actions

The following are strategic actions to consider in characterizing uncertainty:
1. Identify extreme events scenarios of interest to PSH valuation and broader grid analysis and design
 implementations in the relevant model types.
2. Consider broader ranges of scenario samples for key scenario drivers that better identify grid
 conditions where PSH is most valuable.
3. Expand scenarios around cross-market interactions to acknowledge the rapidly evolving nature of
 surrounding service territories and beyond.
4. Use automated workflows to more readily expand scenario suites, iterate between models, and
 conduct custom or rapid-response studies as needs arise.

 4.0 Summary of Strategic Actions
The full set of strategic actions included throughout this report are listed in Table 3, according to the
corresponding category by model type, model integration method, or scenario exploration strategy.

Many of the individual model actions suggest renewed exploration of the trade-offs between
computational difficulty and various aspects of model resolution and detail. These exercises are common
among research enterprises pushing the boundaries of what is possible in grid and river operations
modeling. Furthermore, the rapid changes occurring in the power sector suggest they could become a key
part of utility practices. Particularly while assessing the value of flexible assets like PSH, there could be
core model characteristics such as temporal scope and resolution, flexible demand- and supply-side
technology representations, hydrological and ecological interactions, and transmission representations
that are more critical to value assessment than in the past. There might also be new ways to incorporate
and value ancillary services, examine changes to external market interactions, and account for long PSH
lifetimes when valuing new potential PSH assets. Expanding and improving studies of transmission
reliability can also better inform the value proposition for PSH and how its contributions to reliability can
be considered in upstream PSH and CEM modeling.

Automated model integration requires significant effort at the outset but could vastly improve cross-
model consistency and enable expanded scenario exploration of uncertainties. Doing so in a one-way
fashion could reveal important areas where feedback or further integration is warranted between CEM,
PCM, transmission reliability, resource adequacy, and other tools. Expanded automated processes can
enable a more comprehensive and robust study of PSH value that considers extreme events, alternative
technology assumptions, new trade and market considerations, demand-side developments, and
distributed technologies. Evolving power sector trends are creating new forms of uncertainty and
expanding the ranges and probabilities of possible grid conditions. It is important to consider these in
valuation studies because PSH could be a leading technology to address many of these uncertainties and
to maintain a reliable and resilient system.

Although this set of actions were derived in partnership and collaboration with TVA, they offer a range of
considerations that could be applicable to all vertically integrated utilities, investor-owned utilities,
system operators, or other entities conducting integrated grid modeling and analysis. As the grid of the
future comes into focus, industry stakeholders can learn from and adapt applied research-grade methods
developed by DOE laboratories and other research institutions. Doing so could improve decision-making
and accelerate progress toward a more reliable, secure, and affordable energy system.
