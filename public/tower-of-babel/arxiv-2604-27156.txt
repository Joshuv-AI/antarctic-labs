Interval Orders, Biorders and Credibility-limited Belief Revision
Richard Booth, Ivan Varzinczak

Abstract
Rational belief revision is commonly viewed as being based on a preference order between possible worlds, with the resulting new belief set being those sentences true in all the most preferred models of the incoming new information. Usually, such a preference order is taken to be a total preorder. Nevertheless, there are other, more general classes of ordering that can also be employed. In this paper, we explore two such classes that have been studied within the theory of rational choice but have seen limited or no application in belief revision. We begin with interval orders , introduced by Fishburn in the ’80s, which associate with each possible world a nonnegative ‘interval’ of plausibility. We then move on to biorders , studied by Aleskerov, Bouyssou, and Monjardet, which generalise interval orders by allowing the intervals to have negative lengths, a feature that can be used to capture a notion of dissonance or instability. We provide axiomatic characterisations of these two resulting families of belief revision operators, as well as of two further families of interest that lie between interval orders and biorders. We show that while biorder-based revisions satisfy the Success postulate, they do not always yield consistent outputs. By modifying their definition to discard inputs that lead to inconsistency as ‘incredible’, we derive new families of so-called non-prioritised revision that satisfy the Consistency postulate, but not the Success one. These families are linked to credibility-limited revision operators of Hansson et al ., but for which the set of credible sentences does not satisfy the single-sentence closure condition. We argue that the biorder-based approach is well-suited for scenarios where an agent might initially reject new information, but may accept it when presented with additional explanation.



1
Introduction and Motivation

Belief revision is concerned with the incorporation of any new item of information into an agent’s belief set in a principled way. The so-called AGM-approach to belief revision
[
1
]
does so by formulating a formal account of rationality in this setting, usually characterised by a set of postulates capturing the expected behaviour of revision. Traditionally, two pillars of the AGM approach are the postulates called Success and Consistency, which ensure that the new incoming information is always accepted and the result is a consistent belief set (whenever the incoming information is consistent).

As it turns out, the AGM approach assumes an idealised setting, which is not suitable for modelling the behaviour of realistic rational agents. As a result, the past decades have seen many attempts to bring more realism into it. This is one of the motivations underlying the present paper.

Here, we argue that the requirement for a consistent revision result could be dropped. Realistic agents may face new information that is
destabilising
, represented as sentences that just do not cohere with the agent’s current beliefs. As an example, adapted from Fermé (
?
), upon learning “My five-year-old nephew had lunch with King Charles today”, the agent’s beliefs get destabilised and switch to ‘maintenance mode’, where some form of ‘repair’, which can take the form of further clarification, is needed.

Alternatively, if we prize consistency, we may drop the blind acceptance of new incoming information, specifically of problematic sentences. This amounts to giving up the Success postulate and is the stance in non-prioritised revision (NPR). In the example above, the sentence is just ignored or ‘put on hold’ until further clarification or explanation, if any, might make it become acceptable. For instance, by learning that “King Charles visited a local school today”, the agent is now keen to accept the first claim above.

In the present paper, we tackle both issues above and their interplay, investigating the consequences but mainly the fruitfulness of dropping either Success or Consistency. Our main tools are a family of orderings on possible worlds, more general than the total preorders usually assumed in the AGM approach. More specifically, we look at
interval orders

[
4
]
, which have already been utilised in the context of belief change, namely for belief contraction
[
10
]
. But there is a more general type of ordering that has been looked at in economics, and which can result in an empty choice set, namely
biorders
, as studied most extensively by Aleskerov
et al
. (
?
). We bring these into the landscape of belief change, which is part of the novelty of the present work.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows:
In Section
2
, we give the formal background for the upcoming sections. In Section
3
, we present our first contribution by completing the picture initiated by Rott (
?
), namely shifting the focus from total preorders in the definition of belief change operators to interval orders. We then generalise our constructions to the broader class of biorders, showing in particular that intervals of negative length can be used to capture an alternative form of impossibility, which we refer to as dissonance or instability (Section
4
). One of the consequences of this generalisation is the loss of the Consistency postulate. Instead of throwing in the towel at this stage, we try to ‘remedy’ the situation by exploring two subclasses of biorders satisfying a form of transitivity and that lie between interval orders and biorders. These strengthen the constructions and results for the general biorder case, but do not bring back Consistency. This serves as a motivation for an investigation of non-prioritised revision (NPR) grounded on the operators previously defined (Section
5
), in which inputs leading to inconsistency are discarded as ‘incredible’. Given that, in Section
6
, we show how our two subclasses of NPR can be formulated as credibility-limited revision operators. Before concluding the paper, we discuss some relevant related work (Section
7
).
Proofs of all stated results are given in an Appendix.

2.2
Belief Revision

Belief change is concerned with the problem of modifying an agent’s beliefs in a principled way. The AGM paradigm for belief change
[
1
]
has become the gold standard in the area. It assumes an underlying logic with a propositional language, as
\mathcal{L}
, and the above consequence operator
\text{\it Cn}(\cdot)
.

A
belief set

\mathcal{KB}
is a
deductively closed
set of sentences,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{KB}=\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB})
.
The
revision
of
\mathcal{KB}
with
\alpha
corresponds to a consistent resulting belief set
\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha
from which
\alpha
follows. In the AGM paradigm, the expected behaviour of the revision operator is characterised in terms of the postulates below (we assume

that the input
\mathcal{KB}
is

):

P0

\mathcal{KB}\ast\top=\mathcal{KB}
(Tautology)

P1

\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha=\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha)
(Closure)

P2

\alpha\in\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha
(Success)

P3

\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha\subseteq\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB}\cup\{\alpha\})
(Inclusion)

P4

If
\lnot\alpha\notin\mathcal{KB}
, then
\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB}\cup\{\alpha\})\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha
(Vacuity)

P5

If
\alpha\equiv\beta
, then
\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha=\mathcal{KB}\ast\beta
(Extensionality)

P6

If
\alpha\not\models\bot
, then
\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha\not\models\bot
(Consistency)

P7

\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha\cap\mathcal{KB}\ast\beta\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\ast(\alpha\lor\beta)
(Disjunctive Overlap)

P8

If
\lnot\alpha\notin\mathcal{KB}\ast\beta
, then
\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB}\ast\beta\cup\{\alpha\})\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\ast(\alpha\land\beta)
(Subexpansion)

Let
\ast
be a standard AGM revision operator, which can be defined in terms of a total preorder (i.e., a transitive and complete relation) on
\mathcal{U}
as follows. To a belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, we associate a total preorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{tpo}}_{\mathcal{KB}}

s.t.

\preceq^{\mathsf{tpo}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
is anchored on
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket
(see paragraph following (
1
) above), and the models of the new belief set

are selected from

by choosing the optimal ones

to
\preceq^{\mathsf{tpo}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
,

,

\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha\rrbracket\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\mathrm{opt}(\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket,\preceq^{\mathsf{tpo}}_{\mathcal{KB}})

(2)

where the function
\mathrm{opt}(\cdot)
is defined as in (
1

.

The representation result below

]

Theorem 1
.

The revision operator
\ast
satisfies Postulates P1-P8 iff for each

there is a total preorder

,

, and their respective impact on the behaviour of the corresponding belief revision operators. Regarding the notation for these, we adopt the following conventions: the operator

is used in contexts in which it is assumed both Success and Consistency hold,
\bullet
in contexts in which Success is assumed but Consistency is not,

in Section
4
, and

in contexts in which Consistency is assumed but not necessarily Success,

Interval Order-based Revision

Interval orders, which, roughly, allow for the comparison of intervals, form a well-known subclass of ordering relations generalising total preorders
[
4
,
9
]
. They have proven particularly fruitful in the recent literature on non-monotonic reasoning, with Rott’s interval-based belief contraction
[
10
]
and Booth and Varzinczak’s weakening of rational closure for defeasible inference
[
3
]
. In this section, we complete the picture initiated by Rott by exploring the role of interval orders in defining a belief

3.1
Interval Orders

An

over

is a binary relation
\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}
satisfying reflexivity, i.e.,
v\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}v
for all
v\in\mathcal{U}

v,u,v^{\prime},u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}

1

\textrm{If }v\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}u\ \textrm{and }v^{\prime}\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}u^{\prime},\ \textrm{then }v\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}u^{\prime}\ \textrm{or }v^{\prime}\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}u

(3)

There are alternative ways to characterise interval orders. A particularly useful one is in terms of interval-based interpretations, as done by Booth and Varzinczak (

(Interval-Based Interpretation)
.

An

3.2
IOB Revision

An interval order yields a (successful) belief revision operator
\ast
in the same way as it is traditionally done for total preorders. To each belief set
\mathcal{KB}

\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\ast\alpha\rrbracket\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\mathrm{opt}(\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket,\preceq^{\mathsf{int}}_{\mathcal{KB}})

(Interval Order-Based Revision)

4.1
Biorders

A

Definition 3

(Dissonant valuation)

Given a biorder

\mathcal{U}

z\not\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}z

We will usually reserve

There are various ways to characterise biorders
[
2
]

v

\mathscr{B}
, and
\mathscr{U}(v)
is the

v

in

\mathscr{B}

v

in

\mathscr{B}

Let

.

Definition 5

(Compressed Biorder-Based Interpretation)
.

A biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle
is
compressed
if, (a) for all
1\leq i<j
,
\mathscr{L}^{-1}(j)\neq\emptyset

\mathscr{L}^{-1}(i)\neq\emptyset
, (b) for all
0\leq i<j
,
\mathscr{U}^{-1}(j)\neq\emptyset
implies
\mathscr{U}^{-1}(i)\neq\emptyset
, and (c)
0\leq\max\mathscr{L}-\max\mathscr{U}\leq 1
, where
\max\mathscr{L}=\max\{i\mid\mathscr{L}^{-1}(i)\neq\emptyset\}
(and similarly for
\max\mathscr{U}
).

In words,
\mathscr{B}
is compressed if there are no ‘gaps’ in either the lower or the upper rankings (except, possibly,
\mathscr{L}^{-1}(0)
, which will be empty exactly when
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
has no optimal elements overall), and the largest lower rank is either equal to the largest upper rank or is bigger by one. For example Figure

shows a compressed biorder-based interpretation that gives the same biorder as the one in Figure

.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&0&1&2\\

\hline\cr\mathscr{U}&\bar{p}q,p\bar{q}&pq&\bar{p}\bar{q}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Figure 3:
A compressed biorder-based interpretation.

For convenience, we assume that every biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle
is
normal
in that there is at least one valuation
v
such that
\mathscr{L}(v)=0
. This ensures that the set of worlds that are optimal overall in
\mathcal{U}
is non-empty.

4.2
BOB Revision

As we have done for interval orders, we can define a belief revision operator yielded by a biorder. To each (consistent) belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, we therefore associate a biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
such that the optimal models overall are the ones in
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket
. The models of the resulting new belief set
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
are selected from
\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket
by choosing the optimal ones according to
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
:

\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\rrbracket\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\mathrm{opt}(\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket,\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}})

(7)

Definition 6

(Biorder-Based Revision)
.

\bullet
is a biorder-based (BOB) revision operator if, to each
\mathcal{KB}
we can associate a biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
anchored on
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket

s.t.
, for each
\alpha
,
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
is determined from
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
as in (
7
) above.

Just as for IOB revision (
cf.
end of Section
3.2
), we can define
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\rrbracket
directly in terms of
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{U}
as follows:

\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\rrbracket\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\{v\in\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket\mid\mathscr{L}(v)\leq\mathscr{U}(\alpha)\}

(8)

where
\mathscr{L}(\gamma)=\min\{\mathscr{L}(v)\mid v\in\llbracket\gamma\rrbracket\}
and
\mathscr{U}(\gamma)=\min\{\mathscr{U}(v)\mid v\in\llbracket\gamma\rrbracket\}
.
Thus, we can see that
\bullet
will not satisfy Consistency in general. Indeed, if
\mathscr{U}(\alpha)<\mathscr{L}(\alpha)
, then
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\rrbracket
will be empty, even if
\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket
is non-empty. For an example of such a situation, let
\mathcal{KB}=\text{\it Cn}(\{\lnot p\land\lnot q\})
, let
\alpha=p
, and let
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}=\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
, where
\mathscr{B}
is as in Example
3
.

Definition 7

(Destabilising Sentences)
.

Given a BOB revision operator
\bullet
and a belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, we say that
\alpha
is
destabilising
for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
if
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
is inconsistent.

A rational agent may have sentences that are destabilising for it.
Intuitively, a destabilising sentence is an incoming piece of information that would make an agent’s beliefs ‘collapse’ if accepted. It turns out that one can have
\alpha
as destabilising, while
\alpha\land\beta
, for some
\beta
, is not. For instance, for
\mathcal{KB}
as above, we have that
p
is destabilising while
p\land q
is not. This corresponds to situations in which some extra information, or context, as called by Schwind (
?
) (see also Section
7
), makes the acceptance of the sentence compatible with the agent’s current beliefs. We shall give a special name to those destabilising sentences that cannot be rendered stable even with the addition of more information.

Definition 8

(Irreconcilable Sentences)
.

Given a BOB revision operator
\bullet
and a belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, we say that
\alpha
is
irreconcilable
for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
if
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\beta
is inconsistent for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\beta\models\alpha
.

For an example of an irreconcilable sentence, let
\mathcal{KB}=\text{\it Cn}(\{\lnot p\land\lnot q\})
and let
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}=\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
, where
\mathscr{B}
is as in Example
3
. Then
\neg p\wedge q
is irreconcilable.

We have the following representation result for BOB revision operators, which shows that BOB revision is characterised by the same list of postulates as IOB revision, just without Consistency:

Theorem 3
.

An operator
\bullet
is a BOB revision operator iff it satisfies Tautology, Closure, Success, Inclusion, Extensionality, Disjunctive Overlap, and Disjunctive Rationality.

For the completeness part of the proof of this result, we construct,
from a given operator
\bullet
satisfying the stated postulates, and for each
\mathcal{KB},
a biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB},\bullet}
via a compressed biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}=\langle\mathscr{L}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}},\mathscr{U}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}\rangle
as follows. We inductively build two sequences of subsets of
\mathcal{U}
, an increasing and a decreasing one:

U_{0}\subseteq U_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq U_{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq V_{n}

Initially
U_{-1}=\emptyset
and
V_{-1}=\mathcal{U}
. And then, for each
i\geq-1
:
2
2

2

Here whenever a valuation appears within the scope of a propositional connective, we are taking it to stand for any sentence which has that valuation as its only model.

U_{i+1}\ \>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\ U_{i}\cup\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\bigvee V_{i}\rrbracket

V_{i+1}\ \>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\ \bigcup\{Y\subseteq\mathcal{U}\mid\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\bigvee Y\rrbracket\nsubseteq U_{i+1}\}

Let
n
be minimal
s.t.

n\geq 1
and
U_{n+1}=U_{n}
. (Note
n
is guaranteed to exist since
\mathcal{U}
is finite.)
If, after the procedure terminates, we have
U_{n}\neq\mathcal{U}
, then we also add
\mathcal{U}
to the end of the list to make sure every member of
\mathcal{U}
appears in at least one
U_{i}
and thus that
\mathscr{L}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}
defined below assigns a value to each valuation. Finally, from these two sequences we read off
\mathscr{L}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}
and
\mathscr{U}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}
:

\mathscr{L}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}(u)\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\min\{i\mid u\in U_{i}\},\ \mathscr{U}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}(u)\>\raisebox{4.30554pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathrm{def}$}}\hskip-7.10411pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\>\min\{i\mid u\not\in V_{i}\}

It can then be shown that
\mathscr{U}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}
also assigns a value to each valuation, that
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB},\bullet}
is anchored on
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket
and that
\mathrm{opt}(\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket,\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB},\bullet})=\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\rrbracket
for each
\alpha
.

Definition 9

(Canonical Biorder-based Interpretation)
.

Given a BOB revision operator
\bullet
and belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, the biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{KB}}
defined above is the
canonical biorder-based interpretation associated to
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
.

We observe that two different biorders may yield the same revision behaviour. In particular, for every dissonant valuation
z
, the value of
\mathscr{L}(z)
is irrelevant,
i.e.
, we can increase
\mathscr{L}(z)
without changing the resulting revision behaviour. This has essentially been pointed out by Aleskerov
et al
. (
?
) as well. To put it another way, if, for a given biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}
, we have
z\not\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}z
, then, for all
v\in\mathcal{U}
, the relation given by
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}\setminus\{(z,v)\}
, which is a biorder, will yield the same revision behaviour. As a result of this, every BOB revision operator
\bullet
can be generated by a function that assigns to each
\mathcal{KB}
a biorder satisfying the following additional property (which does not play any role in the soundness proof of Theorem
3
above):

\text{If }v\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}v^{\prime},\text{then }v\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}v

(9)

That is, no dissonant valuation is as good as any other.
This fact will be useful in proving some results in later sections. (We note that
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
constructed in the completeness proof for Theorem
3
satisfies Property (
9
), as the elements in
U_{n}
are precisely those that are not dissonant in
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
.)

Even though we do not have Consistency in general, given the assumption that
\mathcal{KB}
is consistent, we do get the following restricted version:

P6’

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{KB}
, then
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\not\models\bot
(Relative Consistency)

We will return to the consistency issue later. We also have:

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{KB}
, then
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\subseteq\mathcal{KB}
(Endogenous Inclusion)

Nevertheless, BOB revision does not satisfy the following property, in general:

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{KB}
, then
\mathcal{KB}\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
(Positive Vacuity)

That is, it is possible that, upon being presented with a sentence that is already a consequence of the agent’s beliefs, the agent may in fact remove some beliefs. This can be seen in Example
3
, where we have
\mathcal{KB}=\text{\it Cn}(\{\neg p\wedge\neg q\})
and hence
p\leftrightarrow q\in\mathcal{KB}
, but
\mathcal{KB}\bullet(p\leftrightarrow q)=\text{\it Cn}(\{p\leftrightarrow q\})
, i.e., we lose belief in
\neg p\wedge\neg q
.

It
is
possible to capture Positive Vacuity by enforcing a simple condition on biorder-based interpretations, namely
\mathscr{L}^{-1}(0)\cap\mathscr{U}^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
. However, doing that would still not necessarily give us the following generalisation of it, which corresponds to the rule of Cautious Monotony from conditional reasoning:

If
\beta\in\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
, then
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\bullet(\alpha\wedge\beta)

That is, acceptance of
\alpha
may still leave us open to some kind of instability. We have the following counterexample.

Example 4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{KB}=Cn(\{p\leftrightarrow q\})
and
\bullet
associates the biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
to
\mathcal{KB}
, where
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle
is a compressed interpretation specified by:
\mathscr{L}(pq)=\mathscr{L}(\bar{p}\bar{q})=0
,
\mathscr{L}(p\bar{q})=1
and
\mathscr{L}(\bar{p}q)=2
;
\mathscr{U}(\bar{p}\bar{q})=\mathscr{U}(\bar{p}q)=0
,
\mathscr{U}(p\bar{q})=1
, and
\mathscr{U}(pq)=2
. Then
p\in\mathcal{KB}\bullet(p\vee q)
but
q\in\mathcal{KB}\bullet(p\vee q)\setminus\mathcal{KB}\bullet((p\vee q)\wedge p)
.

In the above example,
p
is an example of what we call a
precarious
input:

Definition 10

(Precarious Sentences)
.

Given a BOB revision operator
\bullet
and a belief set
\mathcal{KB}
, we say that
\alpha
is
precarious
for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
if there is
\beta\in\mathcal{L}

s.t.

\beta\in\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
and
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\nsubseteq\mathcal{KB}\bullet(\alpha\lor\beta)

We have the following easy result concerning the relationship between destabilising, irreconcilable and precarious sentences:

Proposition 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
. If
\alpha
is destabilising for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
, then it is either irreconcilable or precarious for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
.

4.3
ZTBOB Revision

We now turn our attention to a special subclass of biorders, namely those also satisfying some forms of transitivity.

We start by noting that the converse to Proposition
2
does not hold, in general, as can easily be seen in Example
4
, where
p
is precarious but not destabilising for
\bullet
and
\mathcal{KB}
. Since clearly irreconcilability implies destabilising, this boils down to requiring that precarious implies destabilising, which can be expressed as the following Consistent Cautious Monotony (CCM) postulate on the
\bullet
operator:

P10

If
\beta\in\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
and
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\not\models\bot
, then
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\bullet(\alpha\land\beta)
(Consistent Cautious Monotony)

We can satisfy this postulate by forcing a further condition on
\mathscr{B}
and
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
(remember
z
denotes a dissonant valuation):

BZT

If
\mathscr{L}(u)\leq\mathscr{U}(z)<\mathscr{U}(u)
, then
\mathscr{L}(z)\leq\mathscr{U}(z)

In words, there is no dissonant valuation
z
such that
\mathscr{L}(u)\leq\mathscr{U}(z)<\mathscr{U}(u)
. This property fails for the
\mathscr{B}
in Example
4
, as can be seen by taking
u=pq
and
z=\bar{p}q
. If
\mathscr{B}
satisfies BZT, then
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
also satisfies the following weakening of transitivity, which we call
z
-transitivity
:

\textrm{If }u\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}v,\ v\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}z\textrm{ and }z\not\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}z,\textrm{ then }u\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}z

(10)

We call any biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
satisfying Property (
10
) a
z
-transitive
biorder.

Proposition 3
.

(i)
Let
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle
be a biorder-based interpretation satisfying BZT. Then
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
is
z
-transitive.
(ii)
Let
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
be a
z
-transitive biorder. Then every
compressed
biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle

s.t.

\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}=\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
satisfies BZT.

The ordering
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
in Example
4
is not
z
-transitive, as witnessed by the fact that
p\bar{q}\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}pq
,
pq\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}\bar{p}q
,
\bar{p}q\not\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}\bar{p}q
but
p\bar{q}\not\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}\bar{p}q
.

We thus arrive at our first subclass of BOB revision, and its associated axiomatic characterisation.

Definition 11

(ZTBOB Revision)
.

\bullet
is a
z
-transitive biorder-based (ZTBOB) revision operator if, to each
\mathcal{KB}
we can associate a
z
-transitive biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
anchored on
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket

s.t.
, for each
\alpha
,
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
is determined from
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
as in (
7
).

Theorem 4
.

An operator
\bullet
is a ZTBOB revision operator iff it satisfies all the postulates from Theorem
3
,
i.e.
, Tautology, Closure, Success, Inclusion, Extensionality, Disjunctive Overlap and Disjunctive Rationality,
plus
CCM.

4.4
TBOB Revision

As discussed in the previous section,
z
-transitivity is a weakened form of transitivity. A natural question to ask is “what if we enforce full transitivity?” In terms of compressed biorder-based interpretations (Definition
5
), this corresponds to a particularly simple property, namely:

BT

\mathscr{U}(v)\leq\mathscr{L}(v)

Notice that we still allow for valuations to be assigned negative intervals here, but we disallow intervals of non-zero positive length. As a result, transitive biorders are still not necessarily reflexive.

Proposition 4
.

(i)
For every biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}
satisfying BT,
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
is transitive.
(ii)
Let
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
be a transitive biorder; then every
compressed
biorder-based interpretation
\mathscr{B}=\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle

s.t.

\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}=\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}
satisfies BT.

One can see that in the biorder-based interpretation from Example
3
, the condition BT does not hold as
\mathscr{U}(\bar{p}\bar{q})>\mathscr{L}(\bar{p}\bar{q})
. Furthermore, the associated biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
is not transitive as
\{(p\bar{q},\bar{p}\bar{q}),(\bar{p}\bar{q},p\bar{q})\}\subseteq\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
, but
(p\bar{q},p\bar{q})\notin\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathscr{B}}
.

Definition 12

(TBOB Revision)
.

\bullet
is a transitive biorder-based (TBOB) revision operator if, to each
\mathcal{KB}
we can associate a transitive biorder
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
anchored on
\llbracket\mathcal{KB}\rrbracket

s.t.
, for each
\alpha
,
\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
is determined from
\preceq^{\mathsf{bi}}_{\mathcal{KB}}
as in (
7
) above.

As it turns out, TBOB revision brings us a step closer to AGM revision, in that this family of operators captures the second AGM supplementary postulate, Subexpansion. We have the following representation result:

Theorem 5
.

An operator
\bullet
is a TBOB revision operator iff it satisfies all postulates from Theorem
3
,
i.e.
, Tautology, Closure, Success, Inclusion, Extensionality, Disjunctive Overlap and Disjunctive Rationality,
plus
Subexpansion.

In other words, transitive biorders characterise the revision operators that are characterised exactly by the AGM postulates
minus
Consistency
plus
Disjunctive Rationality. (Note that Vacuity is recaptured since it follows from Subexpansion, Tautology and Extensionality). In the literature on rational choice theory, the family of choice functions corresponding to a restricted version of transitive biorders has been characterised by Gerasimou (
?
).

We note that Subexpansion is equivalent to the following Disjunctive Monotony (DM) postulate, given the other postulates for TBOB revision:

If
\lnot\beta\notin\mathcal{KB}\bullet\alpha
, then
\mathcal{KB}\bullet(\alpha\lor\beta)\subseteq\mathcal{KB}\bullet\beta
(DM)

This postulate is essentially the belief revision version of the (
\gamma^{+}
) postulate from rational choice of Salant and Rubinstein (
?
), which has been considered by Gerasimou (
?
), and also by Grossi
et al
. (
?
), from where we have taken the name Disjunctive Monotony. We could substitute it for Subexpansion in Theorem
5
above, but would still need Disjunctive Rationality as it does not follow from the other postulates mentioned in Theorem
5
. As we will see, Disjunctive Monotony comes in handy for the representation results we state in the next section.

The property
\mathscr{U}(u)\leq\mathscr{L}(u)
for all
u
, combined with the property that
\mathscr{L}(u)
is irrelevant for all those
u
such that
\mathscr{U}(u)<\mathscr{L}(u)
roughly means that we only need to keep
\mathscr{U}
. Furthermore any dissonant valuations
z

s.t.

\mathscr{U}(z)=i
can be safely placed in a newly created upper rank in between
i
and
i+1
without changing the revision behaviour. All this means that we can specify an interpretation
\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}\rangle
using a single ranking
\mathscr{R}

plus
a specification of which, if any, ranks are “impossible”. See Figure
4

To sum it up, while both BOB and TBOB revision operators satisfy many important AGM postulates, in particular the Success one, their significance for standard belief revision is, at least at first sight, diminished by the fact that they do not always yield consistent outputs. Indeed, the Consistency postulate is non-negotiable in ‘orthodox’ belief revision. We could, in principle, circumvent this by enforcing consistency. Note, however, that this would lead us back to interval orders or even total preorders, which is essentially where we started from. In the next section, we step back and look at the possibly incoherent revision inputs as still modelling something useful about an agent’s belief state.

5
Non-prioritised Revision

We shall now look at our previously defined operators from a different angle, namely that of non-prioritised belief revision. BOB NPR as Credibility-limited Revision

In this section, we show how the two subclasses of BOB non-prioritised revision from the previous section can be formulated as credibility-limited revision operators.

Credibility-limited revision has been put forward by Hansson
et al

Related Work

As already mentioned, Rott (
?
) was the first to have considered (strict) interval orders in belief change to define belief
contraction
operators. Booth and Varzinczak (
?
;
?
) have utilised such orderings as the semantic foundation for defeasible conditionals, leading to the identification of a relevant notion of non-monotonic consequence relation lying between preferential closure and rational closure
[
8
]
.

An investigation of the interdefinability of Rott’s contraction operators and our IOB revision via the Levi and Harper identities is an exercise we shall leave for future work.

In addition to the work of Rott, there have been several other studies in belief change that deploy kinds of ordering that are different from total preorders, starting with Katsuno and Mendelzon (
?
), who considered using a slightly restricted form of strict partial order. Their work was generalised by Benferhat
et al
. (
?
), who used unrestricted strict partial orders. Semiorders, which are a specific kind of (strict) interval order which correspond to interval-based interpretations in which all non-zero length intervals have the same length, have been considered for belief contraction by Rott (
?
) and for both revision and contraction by Peppas and Williams (
?
). The latter considered a slightly restricted family of semiorders designed to ensure the Vacuity postulate held. This restriction was lifted by León and Pino Pérez (
?
). Finally, Gaigne
et al
. (
?
) recently looked at a novel class of orderings called partitioned linear orders. All of the above papers offer representation results for the resulting classes of revision operators.

With regard to credibility-limited revision, to the best of our knowledge, Schwind (
?
) was the first to have challenged the single-sentence closure condition in this setting. Building on Booth
et al
.’s (
?
) work, he motivates and introduces a family of
context-based
(CB) revision operators. These capture the intuition that a sentence may be deemed credible on the grounds that it strengthens (or provides extra context for) one of its non-credible consequences.

In terms of postulates, Schwind’s proposal consists of replacing Success Monotonicity in Booth
et al
.’s original proposal, which formalises the single-sentence closure condition, with a set of weaker postulates. Semantically, the belief revision operators are defined in terms of total preorders, as in the standard AGM approach to belief change, with the difference that the orderings are informed by a further refinement of the set of possible valuations.

Besides the fact that here we consider alternatives to total preorders in belief revision, our approach to credibility-limited revision differs from Schwind’s in other key aspects. First, the CB revision with an input
\alpha
that is consistent with the agent’s initial beliefs systematically reduces to belief
expansion
,
i.e.
, the result of revision is
\text{\it Cn}(\mathcal{KB}\cup\{\alpha\})
. This corresponds to the Vacuity (P4) postulate, which, as we have seen in Example
2
, does not hold in general in our approach, but which can be enforced if desired, via the condition in (
5
). In that sense, our construction offers the user a choice.

Second, one of the core postulates in Schwind’s proposal is the Contextual Monotonicity (CM) principle, which states that if strengthening a credible sentence results in a non-credible one, then every further strengthening thereof remains incredible. We believe this is a rigid stricture to impose on the behaviour of the revision operator, as the following example, extending the one by Schwind (
?
), shows.

Example 6
.

Let
p
be “Bob had lunch with the prime minister yesterday” (initially non-credible), let
q
be “the prime minister visited Bob’s institute” (and then
p\land q
becomes credible), and let
r
be “Bob had dinner with the prime minister yesterday” (so that
p\land q\land r
is non-credible). In Schwind’s approach, every
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}

s.t.

\alpha\models p\land q\land r
remains non-credible. However, let
s
be “they’ve discussed dinner plans over lunch”, so that
p\land q\land r\land s
ought to be credible.

It can be shown that the principles underlying Schwind’s Contextual Monotonicity do not hold in our framework, in general, and that our CL Structures from Definition
15
can handle cases such as the one in Example
6
correctly, as already suggested by Figure
4
.

8
Concluding Remarks

Recapitulating the main contributions of the present paper, they can be summarised as follows: (
i
) the definition of a family of (successful) belief revision operators, each one informed by a special type of
biorder
, something that had not been sufficiently explored in the belief-change literature before, (
ii
) the provision of axiomatic characterisations
à la
AGM for the respective operators, along with an analysis of the postulates characterising each of them, in particular of the conditions sufficient for enforcing those that do not hold in each of the new settings, (
iii
) the deployment of the biorder-based revision operators in non-prioritised revision for recovering consistency and a provision of the respective representation results, and (
iv
) the formulation of biorder-based non-prioritised revision operators as credibility-limited revision with an accompanying interdefinability result, delivering an intuitive and versatile relaxation of the Single Sentence Closure principle.

Among the consequences of the above contributions to knowledge representation and reasoning is the fact that potential users now have a larger set of (justifiable) choices for belief revision at their disposal. Furthermore, the utilisation of biorders in belief revision opens up a broad avenue for exploration in closely-related areas,
e.g.
in reasoning with defeasible conditionals and in normative reasoning.

Immediate topics for further investigation stemming from the results in this paper include: (
i
) the identification of a meaningful strengthening as well as of an interesting weakening of the postulates on the credible set
\mathcal{C}
, (
ii
) a formulation of
general
BOB revision, besides the ZTBOB and TBOB ones, into the framework of credibility-limited revision, and (
iii
) the definition and study of BOB, ZTBOB and TBOB
contraction
and of how these stand in comparison to Rott’s contraction operators.

A further research question relates to the use of the full expressivity of biorders. As we have seen, different biorders can yield the same revision behaviour. We conjecture the answer to where this extra structure comes from may emerge from an investigation of the iterated revision case.
