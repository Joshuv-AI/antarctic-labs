A machine learning-derived and functionally validated circadian rhythm signature predicts clinical outcomes and in silico drug sensitivity in colorectal cancer
Authors: Huan Jin, Qingqing Xu, Shanshan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Machine Learning Framework: A 14-gene circadian signature (CRS) predicts clinical outcomes in colorectal cancer.•Single-Cell Insights: Dendritic cells act as the main hub of circadian activity in the tumor microenvironment.•Immune Microenvironment: High CRS links to immunosuppression with fewer CD8+ T cells and more M2 macrophages.•Genomic Alterations: CRS correlates with higher TP53 mutation rates and altered WNT and RTK-RAS pathways.•Experimental Validation: BHLHE40 is overexpressed in colorectal cancer, driving cell proliferation and migration.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a formidable global health challenge, characterized by high morbidity and mortality [1]. Its profound heterogeneity, manifesting in diverse molecular subtypes and tumor microenvironments (TME), underlies highly variable patient prognoses and treatment responses [1]. While advancements have been made in therapeutic strategies, including surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [[2], [3], [4]], the five-year survival rate for advanced-stage disease remains disappointingly low, starkly contrasting with the high survival rate of early-stage patients [5,6]. This poor prognosis is primarily due to metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and the fact that current treatments like targeted therapy and ICIs only benefit a subset of patients, contingent upon specific genetic mutations and microsatellite instability status [5,7]. This clinical predicament is particularly evident in cases of postoperative recurrence, where conventional therapies often prove ineffective [8]. Consequently, there is an urgent and unmet clinical need for robust prognostic biomarkers to accurately stratify patients, predict disease progression, and highlight potential therapeutic vulnerabilities for CRC.

In recent years, the intricate interplay between the circadian rhythm and cancer pathogenesis has garnered increasing attention [9,10]. The circadian rhythm, an evolutionarily conserved, intrinsic 24-hour biological clock, governs a wide array of physiological processes, including cell cycle progression, metabolism, and immune function [11]. Mounting evidence suggests that disruption of circadian rhythms, often caused by modern lifestyle factors like shift work, is associated with an elevated risk of developing several cancers, including CRC [[12], [13], [14]]. At the molecular level, core clock genes and their downstream targets function as critical tumor suppressors or promoters by regulating key oncogenic pathways such as apoptosis, DNA damage repair, and cell proliferation [15,16]. For instance, dysregulation of circadian genes has been implicated in CRC tumorigenesis, influencing cancer cell stemness and metastasis [[17], [18], [19]]. Furthermore, the emerging clinical paradigm of "chronotherapy"—which involves optimizing the timing of anti-cancer drug administration according to intrinsic circadian rhythms—has shown promising potential to maximize therapeutic efficacy and minimize toxicity in gastrointestinal oncology [[20], [21], [22]]. However, most existing studies have focused on individual genes, and a systematic investigation into the collective prognostic value of circadian rhythm-related genes (CRGs) and their role in potentially influencing the CRC tumor immune landscape is still lacking.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), a complex ecosystem comprising immune cells, stromal cells, and non-cellular components, is a decisive determinant of tumor behavior and therapy response [23]. Notably, the circadian rhythm is known to modulate various immune functions, such as leukocyte recruitment and cytokine production [24]. This suggests that circadian dysregulation in cancer cells or within the TME itself could create an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates tumor immune escape. While the efficacy of immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, has been established in a subset of CRC patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), the majority of patients with microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors remain refractory to such treatments [25]. This highlights the necessity to identify novel biomarkers that can unravel the immunosuppressive mechanisms and pinpoint potential sensitizers to immunotherapy.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing and sophisticated computational biology provides an unprecedented opportunity to address these challenges. Machine learning algorithms, capable of integrating multi-omics data and identifying complex, non-linear patterns, have emerged as powerful tools for developing prognostic models and uncovering novel biological insights [26]. By applying these approaches to circadian biology in CRC, we can move beyond single-gene analyses to construct a holistic, gene signature-based prognostic tool.

In this study, we hypothesized that a signature derived from CRGs could robustly predict the prognosis of CRC patients and reflect the status of the tumor immune microenvironment. We integrated bulk transcriptomic data from multiple cohorts and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to comprehensively analyze the expression and function of CRGs in CRC. We employed a machine learning framework to construct and validate a Circadian Rhythm Signature (CRS). Furthermore, we systematically correlated the CRS with the TME cell infiltration, genomic alteration patterns, and drug sensitivity profiles. Our findings establish the CRS as a novel and reliable biomarker for prognosis and immunotherapy guidance in CRC, paving the way for more precise and effective patient management.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Transcriptomic data and comprehensive clinicopathological data (including overall survival (OS) status, survival time, and TNM stage) for colorectal cancer (CRC) patients were systematically retrieved from public databases. The training cohort was sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) COAD/READ project. Two independent validation cohorts, GSE39582 and GSE17536, were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, the dataset GSE178318, which contains 6 primary CRC tumor samples, was downloaded from GEO. All datasets were normalized and batch-corrected when necessary using the sva R package to ensure data integrity and comparability. Furthermore, a comprehensive set of 300 circadian rhythm-related genes (CRGs) was curated from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/).

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high and low circadian rhythm score groups were detected with the limma R package. The significance thresholds were set at an absolute log2-fold change (|LogFC|) > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of the identified DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis covered biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). Pathway enrichment was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Terms with an FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data analysis

The scRNA-seq data (GSE178318) were processed using the Seurat R package (v4.0). Quality control was rigorously applied: cells with a percentage of mitochondrial genes > 20%, unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) < 500, or the number of detected genes < 250 were filtered out. After normalization and scaling, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the highly variable genes. Cell clusters were identified using a graph-based clustering algorithm and visualized in two dimensions using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). The activity of the circadian rhythm gene set within each individual cell was quantified using five complementary computational methods: AUCell, UCell, singscore, ssGSEA, and AddModuleScore. This multi-algorithm approach ensured the robustness of the circadian rhythm activity scores assigned to each cell type.

Cellular trajectory and communication analysis

To investigate the developmental dynamics of dendritic cells (DCs), pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using the Monocle2 R package. Cell-cell communication networks were deciphered using the CellChat package, which systematically infers biologically significant ligand-receptor interactions between different cell populations. DCs were subdivided into “High-Circadian” and “Low-Circadian” groups based on their CRS for a focused analysis of their interaction patterns.

Machine learning-based construction of the circadian rhythm signature (CRS)

Univariate Cox regression (p < 0.05) was applied to initially identify prognostic genes. An integrative machine learning framework was then applied, combining 10 different algorithms (including Random Survival Forest (RSF), Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net, and SVM) through 10-fold cross-validation. This comprehensive screening was executed utilizing the standard pipeline of the Shengxin Douyacai online platform (https://sxdyc.ezygene.com/prognosticmodel). To ensure standardized reproducibility, the framework employed the platform’s built-in automated hyperparameter optimization and default configuration settings for all 101 model combinations, rather than custom manual tuning. The model with the highest average concordance index (C-index) across training and validation sets was selected as the final prognostic signature (e.g., the Lasso+RSF model for the CRC circadian rhythm signature, CRS). Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the optimal cut-off value of the prognostic score, determined by the “surv_cutpoint” function from the survminer R package. The prognostic performance of the model was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC).

Development and validation of the nomogram for CRC patients

The TCGA database provided the relevant clinical data of individuals diagnosed with CRC. To validate the clinical relevance of the circadian rhythm score (CRS), we first assessed its correlations with key clinicopathological factors (e.g., age, gender, TNM stage) in the training cohort. A subsequent multivariate Cox analysis, incorporating both the CRS and standard clinical variables, was conducted to determine its role as an independent prognostic factor.

Nomographic charts were plotted to predict possible 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in CRC patients. Enhanced regression nomographic charts on CRS score and clinical traits in CRC patients were plotted with the “regplot” R package. The calibration curve and ROC curve were plotted to test the prediction performance of the nomogram.

Analysis of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME)

The abundances of tumor-infiltrating immune cells were estimated using four deconvolution algorithms: CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCP-counter, and TIMER. Tumor purity, immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores were derived using the ESTIMATE algorithm. Immune-related functions and cell populations were further quantified using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). The expression patterns of immunomodulatory molecules (immune checkpoints) were also systematically compared between the risk groups.

Genomic alteration analysis

The maftools R package was used to analyze and visualize the somatic mutation landscape. The mutation landscape of frequently mutated genes was depicted as oncoprints. The fraction of samples with alterations in key oncogenic signaling pathways (e.g., RTK-RAS, WNT, TP53, NOTCH) was calculated and compared between risk groups. CNV profiles were visualized using GISTIC2.0, and recurrent amplifications/deletions were annotated.

Drug sensitivity analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Drug sensitivity data were obtained from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) and PRISM databases. The correlation between the prognostic risk score or key gene expression and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of compounds was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. A negative correlation indicated that high-risk patients or those with high gene expression were more sensitive to the drug. GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler package to identify enriched Hallmark gene sets and KEGG pathways.

Cell lines and culture

Human colorectal cancer cell lines (SW480, SW620, HCT116) and the normal colon epithelial cell line (NCM460) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Pricella) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Opcell). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAfast200 extraction kit (Feijie, Shanghai). 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Rever Tra Ace qPCR RT Kit (TOYOBO). qPCR was performed with SYBR High-Sensitivity qPCR Supermix (Novoprotein) on an ABI 7900HT system. GAPDH served as the internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-△△ Ct method.

BHLHE40 primers: F: 5’-CAGTGGCTATGGAGGAGAATCG-3’, R: 5’-GCGTCCGTGGTCACTTTTG-3’. GAPDH primers: F: 5’-GGTGGTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACA-3’, R: 5’-GTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTCGTTGT-3′.

Cell proliferation and viability assays

CCK-8 Assay: SW480 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Following 24 hours of treatment, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution (Solarbio) was added to each well. After incubation for 1-2 hours, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

EdU Assay: Proliferation was assessed using the EdU-594 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime). Cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates and incubated with 10 μM EdU working solution. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, cells underwent the Click reaction and were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was analyzed via fluorescence microscopy.

Wound healing assay

Approximately 5 × 10^5 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. Upon reaching confluence, a linear scratch was made using a sterile pipette tip. Cells were washed three times with PBS to remove debris and then cultured in serum-free or low-serum medium. Scratch closure was recorded at 0 and 24 hours using an inverted microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). Survival differences were compared with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified for multiple testing corrections.

Machine learning developed a prognostic CRS

This involved 10 different machine learning algorithms applied through a 10-fold cross-validation process to develop prognostic models for CRS. Based on the best cut-off value, CRC patients were stratified into high and low CRS score groups. Development and validation of a nomogram

The relationship between the CRS score and clinical characteristics was evaluated. Notable differences were observed between the low-risk and high-risk subgroups concerning TNM staging, tumor condition, and recurrence (Fig. 3F). Specifically, the high-risk subgroup exhibited a notably higher percentage of patients presenting with stage III and IV compared to the low-risk subgroup (Fig. 3F). Likewise, the proportion of patients with tumor status was evidently higher in the high-risk subgroup (Fig. 3F). Univariate and multivariate analyses confirmed that the CRS score was independent of other clinicopathological factors (Fig. 3G-H). In multivariate regression analysis (Fig. 3H), the age, CRS score, and TNM stage were identified as separate prognostic factors. Subsequently, a nomogram was constructed using the CRS score, age, and TNM stage from the CRC samples in the training cohort to produce individualized predictions for CRC patients. Using the established nomogram, every patient received a score that could forecast the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 3I). The nomogram revealed an AUC of 0.986 for 1-year survival, 0.989 for 3-year survival, and 0.991 for 5-year survival (Fig. 3J). Fig. 3K displayed a calibration curve at 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years, demonstrating the precise prediction of the nomogram on the OS of patients with CRC. While these exceptionally high AUC values indicate excellent model fitting within the training cohort, we acknowledge that evaluating the nomogram on its own training set inherently leads to an over-optimistic performance evaluation. Therefore, to ascertain the true generalizability of our model without the confounding effects of overfitting, the exact same nomogram formula was applied to two independent external validation cohorts. In the highly heterogeneous, real-world microarray datasets, the nomogram maintained a biologically plausible and reliable prognostic value. Specifically, it yielded a C-index of 0.588 in the GSE39582 cohort (1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.675, 0.606, and 0.606, respectively) and a C-index of 0.603 in the GSE17536 cohort (1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.636, 0.595, and 0.635, respectively) (Supplementary Figure S1). These results indicated that the nomogram is a dependable tool for forecasting the prognosis of patients with CRC across distinct clinical cohorts.

Comparison of the potential functions and clinical characteristics of different risk groups

To investigate the molecular basis of divergent clinical outcomes and immune profiles among the two CRS groups, we performed comparative transcriptome analysis and identified 62 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (FDR < 0.05, |fold change| > 1.5). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly involved in immune-related biological processes, particularly antigen presentation pathways, including peptide antigen assembly with MHC class II protein complex, MHC class II protein complex assembly, and antigen processing and presentation of exogenous peptide antigen via MHC class II (Fig. 4A). Concurrently, KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in key immune pathways, notably Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, intestinal immune network for IgA production, and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 4B). These enrichment patterns collectively indicate that the prognostic stratification in our CRC model is closely associated with dysregulation of adaptive immune responses. The pronounced enrichment in MHC class II-related pathways suggests compromised antigen presentation capacity in the high-risk group, potentially leading to impaired T-cell activation. Furthermore, the involvement of intestinal-specific immune networks underscores the critical role of mucosal immunity in determining CRC progression and patient outcomes. Additionally, an annotated heatmap integrating clinical and molecular characteristics (Fig. 4C) provides a comprehensive overview of multidimensional information, including overall survival, age, gender, TNM stage. Correlation between the CRS and the tumor immunity

To further investigate the role of the CRS score on the immune microenvironment, a heatmap was used to systematically display the expression patterns of various immune checkpoint molecules across different risk groups (Fig. 5A). The molecules were categorized according to their immunomodulatory functions: inhibitory, stimulatory, or not applicable (N/A). Next, a comprehensive immune infiltration analysis was performed using four independent algorithms (Fig. 5B). The findings revealed notable variations in the composition of the tumor immune microenvironment between the two risk categories. Notably, multiple algorithms consistently revealed substantial enrichment of CD8+ T cells and NK cells in the low-risk group (p < 0.001), suggesting enhanced anti-tumor cytotoxic immunity. Conversely, the high-risk group demonstrated a notable presence of immunosuppressive cell populations, including M2 macrophages (p < 0.001). This pattern indicates a more immunosuppressive microenvironment in high-risk patients. Furthermore, the results of ESTIMATE algorithm showed that the immune score, stromal score, and estimate score of the low-risk group were significantly higher than those of the high-risk group, and lower tumor purity was observed in the low-risk group (Fig. 5C-F). Based on the ssGSEA results comparing immune characteristics among the two risk groups, our analysis reveals distinct immune landscape patterns that potentially underlie their differential clinical outcomes (Fig. 5G, H). The low-risk group demonstrates significantly enhanced scores across multiple critical immune cell populations and functional pathways. Particularly noteworthy is the pronounced enrichment of CD8+ T-cells, Th1-cells, and TIL (tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes) (Fig. 5G), suggesting robust anti-tumor immunity mediated by cytotoxic T-cells and T-helper type 1 responses. Concurrently, the low-risk group exhibits elevated scores in key immune functions, including T-cell co-stimulation, MHC class I presentation, and Type II IFN Response (Fig. 5H), indicating effective antigen presentation and T-cell activation mechanisms. The enhanced parainflammation score further supports an active immune microenvironment in these patients. Conversely, the high-risk group shows a comparatively suppressed immune profile, characterized by reduced scores in both adaptive immune cells and critical immune functions (Fig. 5G, H). This pattern suggests impaired immune recognition and effector responses, which may contribute to tumor immune evasion and subsequent poor prognosis. Genomic alteration profiles and mutation landscape in CRC

Based on the comprehensive analysis of copy number variations (CNV) and mutation profiles between the high-risk and low-risk groups in our TCGA cohort, we identified distinct genomic alteration patterns that potentially contribute to their differential clinical outcomes. The CNV landscape (Fig. 6A, B) reveals significant disparities in genomic instability, with the high-risk group demonstrating more extensive and frequent chromosomal alterations. Particularly noteworthy are the recurrent amplifications on chromosomes 8, 13, and 20, and deletions on chromosomes 1, 4, 5, and 18, which encompass multiple oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes critical for CRC pathogenesis. These chromosomal imbalances in the high-risk group likely drive tumor progression through the dysregulation of key signaling pathways, including WNT, TGF-β, and p53 pathways. The convergence of specific CNV patterns with our previously identified immune microenvironment characteristics suggests a potential link between genomic instability and immune suppression in high-risk patients, which may underlie their poor response to conventional therapies and unfavorable prognosis. Based on our comprehensive genomic characterization, distinct mutation profiles and pathway alterations were detected among the two risk groups (Fig. 6C-F). The mutation landscape revealed that APC (78% vs 68%), TP53 (66% vs 49%), and TTN (46% vs 48%) represented the most frequently mutated genes in both groups, with notably higher mutation frequencies in the high-risk cohort (Fig. 6C, E). Pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated substantial disparities in core oncogenic signaling pathways between the groups. Specifically, the high-risk group exhibited significantly greater pathway alterations in RTK-RAS (78/85 vs 83/85 genes affected) and WNT (58/68 vs 66/68 genes affected) pathways, along with prominent involvement of NOTCH, Hippo, and PI3K signaling networks (Fig. 6D, F). These genetic alterations converged to affect critical cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, TGF-β signaling, and TP53 pathway dysfunction. Comprehensive analysis of signature gene correlations with pathway activities and immune infiltration landscape

Based on the comprehensive correlation analysis between the 14 signature genes and both pathway activities and immune cell infiltration, several key patterns emerge that provide mechanistic insights into the prognostic model’s biological basis. SERPINE1 demonstrates strong positive correlations with aggressive cancer pathways, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, KRAS signaling, and hypoxia, positioning it as a key driver of tumor progression. Conversely, DRD4 shows significant negative correlations with cell proliferation pathways such as E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and MYC targets, suggesting potential tumor-suppressive functions. The circadian rhythm gene CRY2 exhibits interesting dual roles, showing positive associations with metabolic pathways (fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation) while negatively correlating with inflammatory responses. To validate the biological function of the core genes in our prognostic model, we first assessed the expression levels of BHLHE40 across various cell lines using qPCR. The results demonstrated that BHLHE40 was significantly upregulated in CRC cell lines (HCT116 and SW480) compared to the normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460, with the highest expression observed in SW480 cells (Fig. 9A). Consequently, the SW480 cell line was selected for subsequent functional assays. To further investigate its role, we designed three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting BHLHE40 (si-BHLHE40-1, -2, and -3). qPCR verification confirmed that si-BHLHE40-1 achieved the most significant knockdown efficiency and was used for all further experiments (Fig. 9B). The CCK-8 assay results indicated that, compared to the control group (si-NC+SW480), SW480 cell viability was significantly reduced following BHLHE40 knockdown (Fig. 9C). Furthermore, EdU proliferation assays confirmed that the loss of BHLHE40 markedly inhibited DNA synthesis and the proliferation rate of SW480 cells (Fig. 9D). Additionally, wound healing assays revealed that BHLHE40 silencing significantly decreased the migration area compared to the control group, indicating impaired migratory capacity (Fig. 9E). Discussion

In this study, we systematically investigated the prognostic significance of circadian rhythm in CRC through an integrated multi-omics approach and machine learning framework. Our findings demonstrate that the developed CRS not only serves as a robust prognostic biomarker but also provides novel insights into how circadian disruption influences tumor progression through modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Crucially, by combining computational predictions with in vitro experimental validation, we have identified and confirmed BHLHE40 as a key functional driver within this signature.

The single-cell analysis revealed that dendritic cells (DCs) exhibit the highest enrichment of circadian rhythm-related gene signatures among all cell types in the TME. This observation carries significant biological implications, as DCs play a pivotal role in initiating and regulating anti-tumor immune responses [27,28]. The enhanced cell-cell communication through signaling axes such as LGALS9-CD44/45 and MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) in circadian-active DCs suggests that the circadian rhythm may influence overall immune landscape homeostasis by modulating DC functionality. While previous studies have established that circadian rhythms regulate immune cell differentiation and function [29,30], our study provides the first evidence at single-cell resolution identifying DCs as a crucial target of circadian regulation in CRC.

While our single-cell analysis highlighted pronounced circadian activity specifically within DCs, the 14-gene CRS was primarily derived from bulk tumor RNA-sequencing, which predominantly captures the transcriptomic profile of epithelial tumor cells. This biological distinction underscores a critical mechanism of tumor-stroma crosstalk mediated by circadian regulators. We postulate that the expression of core CRS genes in tumor cells, such as BHLHE40 and SERPINE1, indirectly orchestrates the functional and circadian states of tumor-infiltrating DCs through the secretion of immunomodulatory factors or extracellular matrix remodeling. Indeed, our cell-cell communication analysis provides robust support for this hypothesis, revealing that epithelial cells actively interact with DCs via specific signaling networks, most notably the MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) ligand-receptor axis. Consequently, circadian dysregulation within the epithelial tumor compartment likely disrupts this intercellular communication, ultimately suppressing DC-mediated antigen presentation and fostering the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of high-CRS patients.

The machine learning framework employed in developing the prognostic signature represents a significant advancement over conventional statistical methods. By evaluating 101 algorithm combinations, we optimized the predictive accuracy of our model through its ability to capture complex non-linear relationships among genes. The final 14-gene CRS includes several genes with established roles in cancer biology, particularly SERPINE1, BHLHE40, and CRY2. SERPINE1, a plasminogen activator inhibitor, has been previously implicated in extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor metastasis [31]. Our findings further substantiate its importance, demonstrating its overexpression in high-CRS patients and its strong association with pro-tumorigenic pathways, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis. While our nomogram demonstrated exceptionally high AUC values (>0.98) in the training cohort, we acknowledge that this may partially reflect model overfitting. Nevertheless, the successful risk stratification of patients across two independent external validation cohorts (GSE39582 and GSE17536) supports the biological relevance and generalizability of the CRS.

The characterization of the tumor immune microenvironment yielded particularly insightful findings. High-CRS tumors exhibited a profoundly immunosuppressive phenotype, characterized by diminished infiltration of critical effector cells such as CD8+ T cells and NK cells, coupled with significant enrichment of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. More importantly, we observed significant downregulation of antigen processing and presentation pathways, particularly those involving MHC class II molecules. This impairment in antigen presentation capacity may explain the poor prognosis of high-CRS patients, as the dysregulation of these circadian-related genes potentially enables immune evasion by compromising tumor cell recognition [32,33]. These computational inferences provide a novel perspective on how circadian rhythms potentially modulate tumor progression.

At the genomic level, high-CRS tumors demonstrated increased genomic instability, with higher TP53 mutation frequency and more extensive alterations in RTK-RAS and WNT signaling pathways. These features align with established molecular classifications of CRC [34], yet our study represents the first systematic linkage of these genomic characteristics with circadian rhythm patterns. Notably, TP53, a crucial tumor suppressor, not only promotes tumorigenesis when mutated but may also interact with the circadian system through cell cycle checkpoint regulation [16,35].

From a clinical translation perspective, our in silico drug sensitivity analysis identified potential therapeutic vulnerabilities for high-CRS patients. The negative correlation between Bexarotene sensitivity and CRS scores suggests that this RXR agonist may exert its effects through modulation of circadian rhythm-related pathways. Similarly, the increased sensitivity of high-CRS tumors to the deubiquitinase inhibitor SMER-3 offers promising avenues for novel therapeutic development. These findings resonate with the emerging concept of “chronotherapy,” which advocates for treatment optimization based on biological rhythm characteristics [[20], [21], [22]].

Our study has several limitations. First, although we validated our findings across multiple independent cohorts, all data were derived from public databases. Future prospective clinical studies are necessary to further validate the clinical utility of the CRS. Second, while our single-cell analysis highlighted DCs as the primary responders to circadian regulation within the TME, our in vitro experimental validation deliberately focused on the tumor-intrinsic oncogenic roles of BHLHE40 (e.g., proliferation and migration). This step-wise biological rationale was designed to first establish the foundational driver role of core CRS genes within the epithelial tumor compartment. Based on our proposed tumor-stroma crosstalk hypothesis, we postulate that the circadian-immune axis operates sequentially: BHLHE40 primarily drives epithelial tumor progression, which subsequently sculpts the immunosuppressive DC phenotype via altered cytokine secretion or surface interactions. We acknowledge that the lack of direct functional assays—such as DC co-cultures to evaluate antigen presentation or cytokine profiling—creates a narrative gap between our TME bioinformatics and in vitro data. Fully elucidating this “Circadian-Immune” axis and the precise molecular pathways through which BHLHE40 regulates DC function through comprehensive in vitro immune assays and in vivo immune-competent models remains a critical imperative for our future studies. Third, the functional validation of BHLHE40 was conducted exclusively in a single CRC cell line (SW480) using fundamental phenotypic assays. Given the pronounced heterogeneity of CRC, relying on a single in vitro model represents a limitation. Future studies should aim to validate these biological findings across a broader panel of CRC cell lines (such as HCT116), particularly those encompassing different microsatellite instability (MSI) and microsatellite stable (MSS) statuses. Additionally, incorporating more comprehensive mechanistic evaluations—such as Transwell invasion assays to robustly assess cell motility—and direct pharmacological assays to explore the causal relationship between BHLHE40 expression and in vitro sensitivity to predicted candidates (e.g., bexarotene or SMER-3) are imperative for our future in vivo and in vitro investigations. Fourth, the single-cell RNA sequencing analysis was based on a dataset (GSE178318) comprising only six primary CRC samples. While this dataset provided valuable high-resolution insights into the tumor microenvironment, this relatively small sample size may limit the capture of broader inter-tumoral heterogeneity. Future studies incorporating larger scRNA-seq cohorts are necessary to validate the circadian rhythm activity observed in specific immune cell subsets, such as dendritic cells. Fifth, a fundamental conceptual limitation of our study is the reliance on static transcriptomic data (TCGA and GEO cohorts). Because these datasets lack time-of-day or Zeitgeber time information, our analyses strictly profile the static expression patterns of a predefined set of circadian-related genes, rather than measuring true biological oscillation or rhythmic activity. Consequently, we cannot confirm temporal circadian disruption via standard chronobiological methods such as cosinor analysis. Future studies incorporating time-series sampling are required to validate the dynamic circadian dysregulation in the CRC tumor microenvironment. Sixth, the characterization of the immunosuppressive landscape, including the differential infiltration of CD8+ T cells and M2 macrophages, relies entirely on transcriptomic deconvolution algorithms. Given that these computational tools often share overlapping gene signature matrices, these findings must be interpreted strictly as computational inferences rather than definitive biological proof. Future studies employing ex vivo or spatial validation, such as immunohistochemistry (IHC) or flow cytometry in local clinical cohorts, are essential to physically confirm these TIME infiltration differences.

Conclusions

Our study establishes a reliable and functionally validated CRS that effectively stratifies CRC patients into distinct prognostic subgroups. The identification of BHLHE40 as a critical promoter of tumor progression, combined with its role in the circadian clock, provides a novel mechanistic link between chronobiology and colorectal oncology, paving the way for more precise and effective patient management.
