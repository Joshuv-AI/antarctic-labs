Adaptive Transform Coding for Semantic Compression
Andriy Enttsel, Vincent Corlay

Abstract
Visual data compression is shifting from human-centered reconstruction to machine-oriented representation coding. In this setting, an image is often mapped to a compact semantic embedding, which is then compressed and transmitted for downstream inference. We propose an adaptive transform-coding method for semantic-feature compression motivated by the conditional rate–distortion function of a Gaussian mixture model. The scheme uses mode-dependent transforms and quantizers selected according to the inferred source component, enabling more efficient coding of heterogeneous feature distributions. Evaluations on features from widely used vision backbones and foundation models show that the proposed method outperforms or is competitive with state-of-the-art neural compression methods while preserving flexibility and interpretability.



I
Introduction

Edge devices, mobile phones, and autonomous vehicles generate massive amounts of visual data. While such data have traditionally been compressed and transmitted to the cloud for storage and processing, vision models are increasingly being deployed at the edge, where they extract semantically meaningful representations locally. In many practical settings, these representations must still be transmitted to a server for aggregation, coordination, or fusion with information from other agents

II-A

Conditional Rate–Distortion Function

Consider an
N
-dimensional source
X
. Under the MSE distortion measure, its rate–distortion function is
[
10
, Chapter 10]

R_{X}(D)=\inf_{p(\hat{X}|X)}\mathcal{I}\big(X;\hat{X}\big)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|X-\hat{X}\big\|^{2}\Big]\leq D,

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X})
is the rate,
\mathbb{E}[\|X-\hat{X}\|^{2}]
is the mean squared error (MSE) distortion, and
p(\hat{X}|X)
is the channel.

Let
C
be a latent variable such that
C\rightarrow X\rightarrow\hat{X}
. By the chain rule for mutual information,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(X,C;\hat{X})-\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X}\mid X)

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X})+\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X}\mid C)-\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X}\mid X)

(3)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X})+\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X}\mid C),

(4)

where (
4
) follows from the Markov condition, which implies
\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X}\mid X)=0
. Therefore (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle R_{X}(D)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p(\hat{X}\mid X)}\big[\mathcal{I}(C;\hat{X})+\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X}\mid C)\big]

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|X-\hat{X}\big\|^{2}\Big]\leq D.

Under the assumption that
C
is revealed to the encoder by a genie, this reformulation does not reduce the rate of the original problem, but suggests a coding scheme in which
C
is first transmitted losslessly to the decoder and
X
is then encoded conditionally on
C
.

Accordingly, the conditional rate–distortion function is
[
14
]

R_{X\mid C}(D)=\inf_{p(\hat{X}|X,C)}\mathcal{I}(X;\hat{X}\mid C)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}[\|X-\hat{X}\|^{2}]\leq D.

(6)

This yields the sandwich bound

R_{X\mid C}(D)\leq R_{X}(D)\leq R_{X\mid C}(D)+\mathcal{H}(C),

(7)

where the genie-aided upper bound corresponds to lossless transmission of
C
followed by conditional coding of
X
given
C
.

If
C\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, then (
6
) decomposes as
[
14
, Theorem 5]

R_{X\mid C}(D)=\inf_{\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}R_{X\mid C=c}(D_{c})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}D_{c}\leq D,

(8)

with
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{K}\}
. Thus, the problem reduces to allocating distortion across components. At the optimum,

R_{X\mid C}(D)=\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}R_{X\mid C=c}(D_{c}),

(9)

where the distortions satisfy the distortion budget
D=\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}D_{c}
and the equal slope conditions

R^{\prime}_{X\mid C=1}(D_{1})=\cdots=R^{\prime}_{X\mid C=K}(D_{K}).

(10)

II-B

Gaussian Mixture Model

Assume now that
X
follows a
K
-component GMM

X\sim\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{c},\Sigma_{c})

(11)

with eigendecomposition
\Sigma_{c}=U_{c}\Lambda_{c}U_{c}^{\top}
and
\Lambda_{c}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{c,1},\dots,\lambda_{c,N})
. Conditioned on component
C=c
, the source is Gaussian, and its rate–distortion function is
[
10
, Theorem 10.3.3]

R_{X\mid C=c}(D_{c})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log_{2}\frac{\lambda_{c,n}}{D_{c,n}},

(12)

where
D_{c,n}=\min\{\lambda_{c,n},\theta_{c}\}
and
\theta_{c}
is the reverse water-filling parameter chosen such that
D_{c}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}D_{c,n}
.
Moreover, it is straightforward to show that
R^{\prime}_{c}(D_{c})=-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\theta_{c}\ln{2}}}
.
Hence, by the equal-slope condition in (
10
), all active dimensions share the same reverse-water-filling parameter
\theta_{1}=\cdots=\theta_{K}=\theta
.

Therefore, (
8
) becomes

R_{X\mid C}(D)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log_{2}\frac{\lambda_{c,n}}{\min\{\lambda_{c,n},\theta\}},

(13)

where
\theta
controlling both inter- and intra-component distortion allocation is chosen such that

D=\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\min\{\lambda_{c,n},\theta\}.

(14)

Finally, if
C
is transmitted losslessly, the total rate of the factorized scheme is given by the right-hand side of (
7
)

\tilde{R}_{X}(D)=R_{X\mid C}(D)-\sum_{c=1}^{K}\pi_{c}\log_{2}\pi_{c}.

(15)

III
Semantic Adaptive Transform Coding

We now describe the practical coding scheme motivated by the analysis above.

III-A

Offline design stage

Given an image
I
, we first extract the embedding
X=f(I)
using a frozen semantic encoder.
We then fit
2
2

2

In the main approach, the GMM is fitted in an unsupervised manner. Possible supervised variants are discussed in the Appendix.
a GMM with
K
components to the embeddings obtained on a dataset representative of
I
.

For each component
c
, after eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{c}
, the transform
U_{c}^{\top}
is the component-specific KLT
[
13
]
. After whitening, the corresponding coefficients become

Z_{c}=\Lambda_{c}^{-1/2}U_{c}^{\top}(X-\mu_{c}).

(16)

For coefficient
Z_{c,n}
, given a global quality parameter
\theta
, the target distortion in the whitened domain is set by the reverse-water-filling rule
d_{c,n}=\min\{1,\theta/\lambda_{c,n}\}
.

To implement this allocation, we use scalar Lloyd–Max quantizers
[
20
]
. First, we define a set of admissible numbers of quantization levels
\mathcal{L}=\{L^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{B}
with the corresponding MSE distortion

d_{Q}(L)=\mathbb{E}\left[(Z-Q_{L}(Z))^{2}\right],\qquad L\in\mathcal{L}

(17)

where
Q_{L}
denotes the
L
-level Lloyd–Max quantizer
for a standard Gaussian source
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then, for each
(c,n)
, we choose the coarsest admissible quantizer as

L_{c,n}^{\star}=\min\left\{L\in\mathcal{L}\;:\;d_{Q}\left(L\right)\leq d_{c,n}\right\}.

(18)

III-B

Online coding stage

At encoding time, given an image realization
i
, we compute its embedding
x=f(i)
and
then for each cluster
c
we compute the whitened vector

z_{c}=\Lambda_{c}^{-1/2}U_{c}^{\top}(x-\mu_{c}).

(19)

Since the true component is unknown, we estimate it as the maximum-a-posteriori GMM component, or equivalently:

\hat{c}=\arg\min_{c}\left\{\|z_{c}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\lambda_{c,n}-2\log\pi_{c}\right\}.

(20)

Conditioned on
\hat{c}
, each scalar coefficient is quantized using the preselected Lloyd–Max quantizer with
L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}
levels:

\hat{z}_{\hat{c},n}=q_{j_{\hat{c},n}}=Q_{L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}}\left(z_{\hat{c},n}\right),

(21)

where
q_{j_{\hat{c},n}}
is one of the
L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}
reproduction centroids. Under the Gaussian model, the probability of output index
j
is

\mathbb{P}\left(J_{\hat{c},n}=j\mid C=\hat{c};L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}\right)=\Phi\left(b_{j+1})-\Phi(b_{j}\right),

(22)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function and
\{b_{j}\mid j=1,\dots,{L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}+1}\}
are the quantizer decision boundaries, with
b_{1}=-\infty
and
b_{L_{\hat{c},n}^{\star}+1}=+\infty
. These probabilities are used for arithmetic coding of the quantizer indices
[
26
,
32
]
.

The mode index
\hat{c}
is encoded separately using an arithmetic code based on the mixture weight
\pi_{\hat{c}}
. At the decoder,
\hat{c}
and
\hat{z}_{\hat{c}}
are recovered, and the feature vector is reconstructed as

\hat{x}=U_{\hat{c}}\Lambda_{\hat{c}}^{1/2}\hat{z}_{\hat{c}}+\mu_{\hat{c}}.

(23)

III-C

Discussion

In classical image compression, transform coding is typically applied to small, assumed to be Gaussian patches, and adaptive extensions often provide limited gains relative to their complexity
[
3
]
. Larger gains can be achieved by nonlinear transform coding schemes
[
6
]
, such as those underlying JPEG AI
[
5
,
1
]
. They rely on a side-information signal, called the
hyperprior
, conditioned on which the residual signal can be modeled by a factorized Gaussian distribution
[
23
]
.

Semantic embeddings are better suited to a mixture-based treatment. Unlike full images in the pixel domain, they are lower-dimensional and more structured, which makes a Gaussian-mixture model more plausible
[
18
]
. In this sense, the inferred component in our scheme plays a role analogous to that of the hyperprior.

The dominant encoder-side computational and storage costs of the scheme scale as
O(N^{2}K)
. These costs can be reduced to
O(NM+M^{2}K)
by first applying a global PCA-based projection that maps
X
from
N
to
M
dimensions, and then fitting the GMM in the reduced
M
-dimensional space. A complete description and analysis of this variant are provided in the Appendix.

Finally, the method readily adapts to different rate–distortion trade-offs, since changing the quality level only requires updating the quantization map.

V
Conclusion

We proposed a task-agnostic semantic feature compression method based on adaptive transform coding, guided by a rate–distortion analysis of Gaussian mixture models. By selecting transforms and quantizers according to the inferred feature mode, the method outperforms both non-adaptive coding and a state-of-the-art semantic codec in preserving semantically relevant information, offering a simple, interpretable, and effective non-neural alternative to standard PCA-based solutions.

Appendix

V-A

Supervised Gaussian Mixture Model

The mixture components can be defined either in an unsupervised or in a supervised manner. In the unsupervised case, the component index is obtained directly by GMM clustering in the feature space. In the supervised case, the component index can be derived from available semantic information, with the component statistics defined as follows

\displaystyle\Sigma_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X_{c}-\mu_{c})(X_{c}-\mu_{c})^{\top}\bigr]=U_{c}\Lambda_{c}U_{c}^{\top},

(25)

\displaystyle\mu_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{c}],\qquad\pi_{c}=\mathbb{P}(C=c).

(26)

For ImageNet, the semantics can be defined through a predefined grouping of fine-grained classes into a smaller number of coarse semantic categories, i.e.,
superclasses
. To estimate the quantities in (
25
), it is sufficient to define the component index as
C=s(\bar{C})
, where
\bar{C}
is the fine-grained label associated with
X
and
s(\cdot)
denotes the mapping from a fine-grained class to its corresponding superclass.

For a standard vision backbone trained for classification, let
h(f(i))
denote the vector of logits for image
i
, where
f(\cdot)
is the feature extractor and
h(\cdot)
is the classification head. At the online coding stage, the component index is given by

\hat{c}=s\!\left(\arg\max_{k}\,h_{k}(f(i))\right).

(27)

For CLIP-based models, let
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{K}\}
be a set of candidate text prompts generated from labels
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{K}\}
, and let
g(t_{c})
denote the corresponding precomputed text embedding. In the online phase, the component index is defined as the prompt whose text embedding is most similar to the image embedding
f(i)
, namely

\hat{c}=\arg\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\frac{f(i)^{\top}g(t_{k})}{\|f(i)\|_{2}\,\|g(t_{k})\|_{2}}.

(28)

That is,
\hat{c}
is the index of the text prompt with maximum cosine similarity to the image embedding.

V-B

Complexity-aware scheme

The definition of
\Sigma_{c}
is quite general. In the main approach, it is obtained by fitting a GMM directly to the feature vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. A more complexity-aware alternative is to first perform a global PCA on
X
. Let

\Sigma=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^{\top}\bigr]=U\Lambda U^{\top},\;\;\;\mu=\mathbb{E}[X].

(29)

Retaining the first
M
principal directions, define

V=[u_{1},\dots,u_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\;\;\bar{X}=V^{\top}(X-\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

(30)

A GMM is then fitted to
\bar{X}
. For component
c
, let
\bar{\Sigma}_{c}=\bar{U}_{c}\bar{\Lambda}_{c}\bar{U}_{c}^{\top}

be the eigendecomposition of the component covariance in the reduced space. If
\bar{\mu}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the mean of component
c
in the reduced space, the corresponding per-mode whitening transform is
Z_{c}=\bar{\Lambda}_{c}^{-1/2}\bar{U}_{c}^{\top}(\bar{X}-\bar{\mu}_{c})
.
Substituting
\bar{X}
defined in (
30
) gives

Z_{c}=\bar{\Lambda}_{c}^{-1/2}\bar{U}_{c}^{\top}\bigl[V^{\top}(X-\mu)-\bar{\mu}_{c}\bigr]=\bar{\Lambda}_{c}^{-1/2}\tilde{U}_{c}^{\top}(X-\tilde{\mu}_{c}),

(31)

with
\tilde{\mu}_{c}=\mu+V\bar{\mu}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\tilde{U}_{c}=V\bar{U}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
,
and where we used the fact that
V^{\top}V=I_{M}
.

This has the same whitening structure as in the original formulation. However, its implementation requires storing one global matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, the global mean vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and, for each component, the matrix
\bar{\Lambda}_{c}^{-1/2}\bar{U}_{c}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
together with the vector
\bar{\Lambda}_{c}^{-1/2}\bar{U}_{c}^{\top}\bar{\mu}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

To select
M
, we use the explained-variance criterion with threshold
\gamma
:

M=\min\left\{m\in\{1,\dots,N\}:\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lambda_{n}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}}\geq\gamma\right\}

(32)

Optimization

All models were trained for up to 1000 epochs using a batch size of 128.
The main optimizer learning rate was initialized to
10^{-3}
and reduced on plateau using a patience of 20 epochs and a reduction factor of 5.
At most five learning-rate reductions were allowed during training.
For the auxiliary parameters of the entropy model, the learning rate was fixed to
10^{-3}
.
Early stopping was applied with a patience of 40 epochs.
The rate–distortion trade-off was controlled by the Lagrange multiplier weighting the MSE term, for which we considered the set
\{1,2,5\}\cdot 10^{\{0,1,2,3,4\}}\cup\{10^{5},10^{6}\}
.
For the architecture parameters, we varied the hyperprior shrink factor as
h\in\{4,8,16\}
, the hidden dimension as
H\in\{N,2N\}
, and the bottleneck dimension as
M\in\{N/2,N\}
.
The results reported in Fig.
3
correspond to the models forming the MSE–rate Pareto frontier among all trained configurations.
