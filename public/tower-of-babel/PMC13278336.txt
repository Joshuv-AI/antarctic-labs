Temporoparietalis Muscle: A Cadaveric Study of 60 Hemisides
Authors: Kyu-Ho Yi, Isaac Kai Jie Wong, Hyun Jin Park, Isabella Rosellini, Mariana César Corrêa, Arthur Swift
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The temporoparietalis muscle (TPM) is a thin, fan-shaped muscle continuous with the superficial musculoaponeurotic system and galea aponeurotica. Despite frequent surgical use of the temporoparietal fascia, TPM anatomy and its variability remain incompletely characterized.Methods:Thirty embalmed cadavers (60 hemisides) underwent a standardized, stepwise layered dissection of the lateral scalp to expose the temporoparietal fascia and the plane deep to it. Using an a priori operational definition and prespecified visual criteria, each hemiside was categorized as (1) visibly thick TPM, (2) present but indistinct TPM, or (3) absent TPM. Proportions are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs).Results:TPM was identified on 37 of 60 sides (61.7%; 95% CI, 49.0–72.9). Visibly thick TPM was present on 10 of 60 sides (16.7%; 95% CI, 9.3–28.0), and present but indistinct TPM was identified on 27 of 60 sides (45.0%; 95% CI, 33.1–57.5). TPM was absent on 23 of 60 sides (38.3%; 95% CI, 27.1–51.0).Conclusions:TPM is present in approximately three-fifths of hemisides but is conspicuously robust in only one-sixth. Anticipating this variability may help surgeons interpret temporal dissection planes and may refine operative planning in procedures involving the temporoparietal region.

Takeaways

Question: How consistently is the temporoparietalis muscle (TPM) present, and how variable is its thickness?

Findings: In 60 hemisides, TPM was present in 61.7%, but only 16.7% showed a visibly thick layer, whereas 45.0% were indistinct and 38.3% were absent.

Meaning: TPM is not a constant structure; recognizing its variability may improve surgical planning and interpretation of temporal dissection planes.

INTRODUCTION

The temporoparietalis muscle (TPM) is one of the lesser-known components of the craniofacial musculature. Located superficial to the auricular region and extending from the temporal to parietal scalp, it lies in close continuity with the superficial musculoaponeurotic system (SMAS) and the galea aponeurotica, thus linking the scalp and upper facial structures. Although the temporoparietal fascia (TPF) is well documented and frequently used as a versatile flap for auricular and orbital reconstruction, the muscle beneath it has received comparatively little attention.

Embryologically, the TPM arises from the second branchial arch mesoderm and therefore shares a developmental lineage with the muscles of facial expression.1,2 It is innervated by branches of the facial nerve—primarily the frontal and posterior auricular branches—and receives vascular supply from the superficial temporal artery and its branches.2,3 Functionally, the TPM may assist in subtle scalp movements and tension modulation through its continuity with the galea, potentially contributing to expressions that involve brow and temporal skin motion.2–4 Given the limited anatomical detail available in the literature and the frequent manipulation of this region in both reconstructive and aesthetic procedures, we undertook a cadaveric study to document the frequency and qualitative thickness of the TPM and to contextualize its clinical relevance (Fig. 1).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Sample

This was a descriptive cadaveric study conducted on 30 embalmed adult cadavers (sex and age not uniformly available), yielding 60 hemisides (left and right) for analysis. Cadavers with obvious prior temporal surgery, major scalp trauma, or extensive pathology in the temporal region were excluded when such information was available.

Ethical Compliance

All cadavers were obtained through an institutional donor program. The study complied with institutional guidelines for research on human remains and adhered to the principles of donor dignity and confidentiality. This work is an exploratory descriptive cadaveric anatomical study (evidence level: V, anatomical observational).

Dissection Protocol

Dissections were performed bilaterally under loupe magnification using a prespecified, stepwise protocol. (1) A preauricular vertical incision was extended superiorly within the hair-bearing scalp. (2) Skin and subcutaneous tissue were elevated in the subdermal plane to expose the TPF. (3) The TPF was identified as the superficial fascial layer continuous with the SMAS/galea and was then reflected to expose the superficial temporal fat/areolar plane deep to it. (4) The plane immediately deep to the reflected TPF was inspected systematically for striated fibers meeting the a priori TPM definition (see Classification), and any candidate fibers were traced cranially toward the galea and caudally toward the auricular musculature. (5) When visible, the superficial temporal vessels and identifiable facial nerve branches were preserved to avoid iatrogenic disruption of thin tissue planes. Standardized photographs were taken to document representative appearances across categories (Fig. 2).

Classification and Observer Workflow

A Priori Operational Definition

Before initiating dissections, we prespecified an operational definition of the TPM as striated muscle fibers within the superficial temporoparietal fascial plane, encountered deep to the reflected TPF and superficial to the loose areolar tissue/deep temporal fascia, showing continuity cranially toward the galea and caudally toward the auricular musculature when present. This definition was applied prospectively to all specimens.

Standardized Identification Criteria

To reduce interpretative variability, the observers used prespecified minimum visual criteria and reference examples: (1) fiber-like striations distinguishable from fascia, (2) continuity of fibers across a minimum field (rather than an isolated small tuft), and (3) persistence of the appearance when the tissue was gently tensioned and viewed under loupe magnification. Borderline appearances were documented and resolved by consensus (see the following section).

Grading Process

Two observers first performed independent grading of each hemiside (visibly thick/present but indistinct/absent) while blinded to the other observer’s rating. Discrepancies were then reviewed jointly at the specimen to reach a final consensus classification. Interobserver agreement was not formally quantified in this study and is acknowledged as a limitation.

No caliper-based thickness measurements, tissue weights, or histological confirmation were undertaken. This pragmatic qualitative grading was selected to reflect typical intraoperative impressions during temporal dissection and flap harvest. We recognize that objective measurements (even in a subset) would improve robustness and plan this in future work.

Data Handling and Statistics

Counts and proportions were calculated for each category across the 60 hemisides. Because this was an exploratory descriptive study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Proportions are presented with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Laterality analyses were not performed because laterality was not retained consistently in the available records; therefore, we do not draw conclusions regarding side-to-side differences.

RESULTS

Frequency of the TPM

The TPM was identified on 37 of 60 sides (61.7%). A visibly thick TPM was present on 10 sides (16.7%), and a present but indistinct TPM on 27 sides (45.0%). The TPM was absent on 23 sides (38.3%) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this cadaveric series of 30 donors (60 hemisides), the TPM was present in 61.7% of sides (37 of 60), but only 16.7% (10 of 60) demonstrated a visibly thick layer; 45.0% (27 of 60) were present but indistinct, and 38.3% (23 of 60) lacked a discrete TPM. These findings indicate that the TPM is not a constant structure and is robustly developed in only a minority of cases. This variability likely contributes to the inconsistent descriptions of the muscle in both classical and contemporary anatomy and may explain intraoperative confusion when the TPF is elevated and a true muscle layer is not clearly encountered.

A key methodological point is how the TPM was defined and distinguished from adjacent layers. Before initiating dissections, we prespecified an operational definition of the TPM as striated fibers within the superficial temporoparietal fascial plane, deep to the reflected TPF and superficial to the loose areolar tissue/deep temporal fascia, with continuity toward the auricular musculature and galea when present. This a priori definition and prespecified minimum visual criteria were applied prospectively across all specimens to improve reproducibility.

We elected a pragmatic qualitative grading scheme (visibly thick/present but indistinct/absent) to mirror how surgeons perceive tissue planes during temporal dissection and flap elevation. Objective measurements (eg, caliper thickness, tissue weight, or histological confirmation), even in a subset, would strengthen construct validity; however, embalmed tissue handling and variable preservation quality can introduce measurement artifacts and were beyond the scope of this descriptive exploratory study. Future work should incorporate quantitative thickness metrics and histological or imaging correlation to validate and refine these categories.

Anatomically, the TPM occupies the superficial temporal plane in continuity with the SMAS and the galea aponeurotica, aligning with its second branchial arch origin and facial nerve innervation. In practical terms, this position means that even a thin or patchy TPM can influence lateral scalp tension and subtle upper-face dynamics. For reconstructive surgeons, awareness of TPM variability is relevant when raising TPF flaps, which are widely used in craniofacial reconstruction and auricular procedures. A thicker-than-expected TPM may alter flap thickness, bleeding, and contour, whereas an absent or indistinct TPM may present a “simpler” plane but reduce expected tissue bulk. Additionally, meticulous respect for the course of the frontotemporal branches of the facial nerve remains paramount during temporal dissection.

From a clinical perspective, the temporoparietal region is frequently manipulated in aesthetic and reconstructive surgery. Variability in TPM presence and development may therefore contribute to patient-to-patient differences in perceived temporal soft-tissue behavior and surgical dissection feel, particularly when operating within or adjacent to the temporoparietal fascial plane. Prior reconstructive literature also supports the relevance of temporoparietal tissues in auricular reconstruction and flap-based procedures.

This study has limitations. Thickness grading was qualitative and not supported by caliper measurements or histology; demographic analyses were limited by de-identified records; and although 2 observers graded each specimen independently before consensus, interobserver agreement was not formally quantified. The use of embalmed specimens is also an important limitation: fixation can reduce tissue compliance and diminish visual contrast between fascia and fine striated fibers. This process may preferentially obscure very thin/patchy TPM, increasing the risk that “present but indistinct” TPM could be misclassified as “absent,” thereby biasing frequency estimates toward underrecognition of TPM presence. Future studies should include fresh/fresh-frozen specimens when feasible, quantitative thickness measurements (even in a subset), and formal reliability metrics (eg, kappa) to improve reproducibility and allow more granular comparisons. Given its predominantly observational and qualitative design, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory anatomical evidence intended to guide surgical understanding and to motivate future quantitative validation.

In summary, the TPM is present in approximately three-fifths of hemisides but is conspicuously thick in only one-sixth. Recognizing this variability can improve interpretation of temporal anatomy during flap harvest and aesthetic manipulation of the lateral brow–temple complex, and it offers a plausible anatomical substrate for patient-to-patient differences in upper-face dynamics and treatment outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

In a series of 30 cadavers/60 hemisides, the TPM was present in 61.7%, visibly thick in 16.7%, indistinct in 45.0%, and absent in 38.3%. Given this variability, surgeons should anticipate differences in tissue planes during temporal dissection and consider the TPM’s potential contribution to scalp dynamics and aesthetic outcomes.
