  VALIDATING GREATER SAGE-GROUSE INDIVIDUAL-BASED MODEL (IBM) TOOL:
 FINAL REPORT

 Yuki Hamada,1 Katherine Szoldatits,1 Paul Tarpey,1 Kyle Ebenhoch,2 and Chad LeBeau 2
 1 Argonne National Laboratory, 2 Western EcoSystems Technology, Inc.

 SUMMARY

The project focused on validating the previously developed Greater Sage-Grouse Individual-
based Model (GrSG IBM; LaGory et al. 2012, 2021). The objective was to transform this
predictive, spatially and temporally explicit model into a portable resource to assist
siting/resource managers in proactively assessing the cumulative impacts of wind energy
development on the greater sage-grouse. Utilizing a bottom-up, individual-based approach, the
GrSG IBM accounts for landscape context and species behavior, aiming to reduce uncertainty in
estimating development impacts and support ecologically mindful land-based wind energy
development. The validation effort covered approximately 6,540 km² near the Seven Mile Hill
Wind Project in Wyoming. The GrSG IBM tool, built on the NetLogo platform (Tisue and
Wilensky 2004), was executed over a 50-year period, with the analysis focusing on years
following a 10-year initialization phase.

Key results demonstrated the tool’s biological soundness across five key biological metrics: non-
chick age class distribution (older than 10 weeks), adult sex ratio, life expectancy, population
size, and overall population growth. For instance, the tool estimated that 58.6% of the non-chick
population was reproductively immature, while the reference ranges from 51.4% to 57.8%
(Patterson 1952, Rogers 1964). Experts confirmed the tool’s estimate was within a reasonable
range for the species. The tool estimated average life expectancy of 1.43 years, while the
reference ranges from 0.9 years to 1.1 years (Ammann 1957, Hamerstrom 1949). Experts also
supported the model’s life-expectancy estimate as ecologically sound for the species in the study
area. In terms of population change, the model estimated an annual shift between a 0.6% decline
and a 1.0% increase over 50 years. While the reference suggests 2.9% annual decline in range-
wide populations (Cortes et al. 2023), that includes many at-risk populations in South Dakota
and Washington, for example. Our study area—in the northeastern part of Carbon County and
western-edge of Albany County, Wyoming—is one of the remaining greater sage-grouse habitats
supporting some of the most stable populations. Experts confirmed that the range of the annual
population change spanning from a 0.6% decline to a 1.0% increase estimated by the tool was
reasonable for our study area for this reason and confirmed that aligned with population
estimates from existing studies on the greater sage-grouse and wind energy development in the
study area (LeBeau et al. 2017a, Smith et al. 2024). Furthermore, the project showed that
temporally explicit biological metrics generated by the GrSG IBM tool can complement the
USGS’ Prioritizing Restoration of Sagebrush Ecosystems Tool (PReSET; Duchardt et al. 2021)
by incorporating habitat restoration strategies into seasonal habitat suitability models to visualize
population responses over time.

Western EcoSystems Technology, Inc (WEST). Key personnel listed include Yuki Hamada (PI),

 2. METHODS

The Greater Sage-Grouse Individual-based Model (GrSG IBM) tool (LaGory et al. 2012, 2021)
was built using the NetLogo modeling platform, which is a multi-agent, individual-based
programming language and environment developed at Northwestern University (Tisue and
Wilensky 2004). This platform allows for spatially explicit modeling and provides a portable
resource that users can run on their own computer devices. The validation effort covered an area
of approximately 6,540 km² near the Seven Mile Hill Wind Project in Wyoming (FIGURE 1),
which was consistent with the studies conducted by LeBeau et al. (2014, 2017a, 2027b). The
project involved four steps: reconstructing seasonal habitat suitability, calculating initial
biological parameters, executing the GrSG IBM, and validating the model output.

2.1. MAPPING ENVIRONMENTS AND CALCULATING INITIAL PARAMETERS

The team prepared input data for the study area across the 2006–2014 study period (TABLE 1).
This involved mapping seasonal habitat suitability using geospatial data such as fractional cover,
vegetation presence, vegetation height (e.g., Multi-Resolution Land Characteristics Consortium
[MRLC] and Landscape Fire and Resource Management Planning Tools [LANDFIRE]), and
topography (National Elevation Dataset [NED]) (TABLE 2). It also involved mapping
infrastructure effects (presence, density, and survivorship impacts) using vector data layers from
U.S. Wind Turbine Database (USWTDB), Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data
(HIFLD), National Transportation Atlas Database (NTAD), and Interactive Oil and Gas Map of
Wyoming (TABLE 3).

Initial parameters—specifically an initial adult population of 7,394 individuals and 132 leks—
were estimated using information from sources such as the Federal Register (USFWS 2010),
breeding density dataset (Doherty et al. 2010a) and published studies on male lek attendance
(Doherty et al. 2010b) and sex ratio (Atamian 2007 in Connelly et al. 2011). All geospatial maps
were converted into a bitmap (.bmp) format at a 240-m resolution for use in the tool.

2.2. EXECUTION AND EVALUATION

The GrSG IBM tool was executed over a 50-year period. The first 10 years served as an
initialization phase. The reconstructed seasonal habitat suitability and infrastructure effects maps
were applied from Year 11 through Year 18. After execution, biological metrics (including
distribution of non-chick age class [older than 10 weeks], sex ratio, life expectancy, population
size, and overall population growth) were extracted for validation. These metric values were
compared against field observations, expert knowledge, and published data.

2.3. ANALYSIS OF PRESET

In addition to validation, the project also included determining how the GrSG IBM tool could
complement the USGS’ Prioritizing Restoration of Sagebrush Ecosystems Tool (PReSET;
Duchardt et al. 2021).

 3. CHALLENGES ENCOUNTERED

We faced challenges in directly utilizing field observations for tool validation because of the vast
difference in spatial scales between the GrSG IBM tool and field observations. We successfully
overcame this challenge because the project team included the researchers who conducted the
studies and collected the field observations. They leveraged their knowledge and experience in
the study area to evaluate the tool's biological metrics.

 4

 4. KEY FINDINGS

4.1. OVERALL ECOLOGICAL SOUNDNESS OF THE TOOL

The GrSG IBM tool demonstrated biological soundness across the five key biological metrics
assessed— non-chick age class distribution (older than 10 weeks), adult sex ratio, life
expectancy, population size, and overall population growth. The tool outputs showed a close
alignment with published data and expert opinions, confirming the tool reliably simulates greater
sage-grouse population dynamics within the study area constraints. The expert feedback
confirmed that the tool generated reliable results for non-chick age class distribution, adult sex
ratios, life expectancy, and especially overall population growth, and highlighted the IBM
approach as a valuable alternative for predicting population outcomes and disturbance estimates.

4.2. SPECIFIC VALIDATION METRICS

For age class distribution, the tool estimated that 58.6% of the non-chick population was
reproductively immature (FIGURE 2). Although this estimate was slightly above the reference
range (51.4–57.8%; Patterson 1952, Rogers 1964), experts confirmed it was within a reasonable
range for the species in the local study area.

 over 50 years, excluding chicks (0–10 weeks).

The estimated average life expectancy for non-chick individuals (older than 10 weeks) was 1.43
years (FIGURE 3). Experts supported this result as reasonable for the species in the study area,
even though it was slightly longer than reference values for related species (0.9–1.1 years;
Ammann 1957, Hamerstrom 1949). The tool also demonstrated a gradual, sustained increase in
non-chick life expectancy throughout the simulation period.

 years, excluding chicks (0–10 weeks). The first 10 years (shaded) are considered
 an initialization phase.

The GrSG IBM tool estimated the annual population change over the 50-year period ranged
between a 0.6% decline (based on initial population size to final population size comparison) and
a 1.0% annual increase (based on the 365-day moving-average comparison post-initialization;
FIGURE 4). An approximately 2.9% population decline has been reported range-wide (Cortes et
al. 2023), which includes many at-risk populations such as in South Dakota and Washington, for
example. Our study area, in eastern Carbon County and on the western-edge of Albany County,
Wyoming, is one of the remaining greater sage-grouse habitats supporting some of the most
stable populations. More specifically, the eastern portion of our study area overlaps with large
portions of sage-grouse Core Areas, which often contain more stable populations (Gamo and
Beck 2017, Spence et al. 2017). This reasoning, combined with the similarities of estimates to
other proximal studies (LeBeau et al. 2017, Smith et al. 2024), led experts to consider the range
of population change estimated by the tool to be reasonable for the study area.

4.3. TOOL UTILITY AND INSIGHTS

The tool has the capability to provide information difficult or impossible to obtain in the real
world, such as time-specific data on lek activity and attendance, nesting activity, and age-sex
class population trajectories. These additional metrics offer valuable insights into the
mechanisms of population changes.

The study demonstrated that the GrSG IBM tool can work in concert with the USGS’ Prioritizing
Restoration of Sagebrush Ecosystems Tool (PReSET). The GrSG IBM tool can use restoration
areas identified by PReSET to modify habitat suitability models and then visualize population
responses to these restoration efforts over time.

 5. FINAL PRODUCT

The project deliverables include a validated GrSG IBM tool and a slide deck outlining the tool.

 6. LESSONS LEARNED

Validation of the tool’s performance using field observations is an important process for
quantifying the reliability of the underlying model and resulting information. Spatial and
temporal scales of field observations are often much smaller and shorter than scales the tool
simulates. This vast scale discrepancy makes direct comparison between real-world observations
and simulation outputs nonviable. Leveraging expert knowledge is the key to overcoming this
challenge. Skepticism toward the model/tool exists among prospective users. Part of their
skepticism stems from unfamiliarity with the model/tool. Inconsistency between their knowledge
of the species and respective elements of the tool also plays a role. Coordinated studies with
experts, regulators, and wind energy industry members, such as case studies, are essential for
building a user base for the GrSG IBM tool.
