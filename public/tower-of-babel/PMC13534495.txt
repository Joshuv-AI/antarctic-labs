Phytochemical profiling and integrated in vitro and in silico assessment of the antioxidant, thrombolytic, and anti-inflammatory properties of Urena lobata L. leaf extracts
Authors: Afruz Dilshad Arney, Nur Mohammad Rasel, Md Aziz Abdur Rahman, Jaytirmoy Barmon, Prattay Karmaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammation and fibrinolytic imbalance, which are frequently caused by oxidative stress and increased pro-inflammatory mediators, are major factors in the onset and progression of chronic illnesses. The study evaluated the phytochemical profile of Urena lobata L. leaf extracts using qualitative chemical tests and spectroscopic analysis, including FT-IR and MC-MS profiling. The pharmacological assessments were performed by antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and thrombolytic capabilities of extracts using various in vitro assay methods along with in silico approaches, including molecular docking and ADMET-based pharmacokinetic assessments. The methanolic extract of Urena lobata L. (ULM) was fractionated using n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and water to obtain the n-hexane (ULH), chloroform (ULC), ethyl acetate (ULE), and aqueous (ULW) fractions. Phytochemical analysis exhibited the presence of flavonoids, steroids, terpenoids, alkaloids, glycosides, and phenols in various fractions. FTIR analysis revealed the presence of a variety of groups, including aliphatic, aromatic, amide, nitrate, methylene, alcohols, phenols, etc. GC–MS study revealed significant bioactive chemicals, including β-amyrenonol acetate derivative, 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide, 9,12-octadecadienoic acid, and N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide. In in vitro antioxidant assay, ULE had the highest antioxidant activity among the investment fractions, with IC50 values of 1.17 µg/mL (DPPH) and 2.60 µg/mL (hydroxyl radical), followed by ULM, ULC, and ULH fractions. In vitro anti-inflammatory studies indicated that the ULE efficiently stabilized human red blood cell membranes (~ 91.57%), comparable to that of the standard medication diclofenac. Additionally, thrombolytic assays revealed considerable clot lysis for ULE (28.40%) and ULM (28.26%) fractions. Molecular docking revealed that β-amyrenonol acetate derivative had a stronger binding affinity to COX-2 (-10.1 kcal/mol) than conventional diclofenac and plasminogen (-9.8 kcal/mol), whereas other elements showed synergistic interactions. ADMET calculation validated favorable pharmacokinetic features and low toxicity risk. Notably, this study presents the first in silico investigation of U. lobata L. phytochemicals as potential thrombolytic agents. Overall, the ethyl acetate fraction of U. lobata L. has substantial antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and thrombolytic properties, highlighting its promise as a multifunctional therapeutic agent and justifying additional bioassay-guided isolation, mechanistic research, and clinical testing.Keywords: U. lobata L., Antioxidant activity, Anti-inflammatory activity, Thrombolytic activity, Molecular docking, ADMET analysis, GC–MSSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Herbal treatments have been used to relieve symptoms of illness for centuries1. The prevalence of traditional herbal supplements, coupled with recent findings that add scientific legitimacy to the use of some medicinal herbs, prompts a question about the origins of herbal medicine in animals and early humans. Both people and animals utilize medicinal plants with the apparent preventive benefit of lessening the chance or severity of future sickness caused by infections and parasites2. Many different phytochemicals, including alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, glycosides, and tannins, are responsible for the pharmacological properties of plants3. One of the potential sources of contemporary medication is medicinal plants and their derived products. Medicinal plants or their parts have long been used, and this use is increasing day by day 4.

U. lobata L., also called Caesarweed and known locally as “Jongli Ghagra” in Bangladesh, is a shrub that is a member of the Malvaceae family. Because of its great resilience and flexibility, it thrives in disturbed soils and floodplains and is extensively spread in tropical and subtropical parts of Asia, Africa, and South America. This plant has been used as a traditional medicinal plant in India and China and is also evidently used traditionally as an antibacterial and amoebicidal, in nephritis, pneumonia, gingivitis, menorrhagia, bronchitis, diarrhoea, dysentery, edema, gastritis, cough, and emmenagogue. Moreover, it has emollient and diuretic effects. The root of U. lobata consists of the following components: carbohydrates 33%, protein 1.9%, fat 1.8%, fiber 51.7%, moisture 6.6%, and ash 5%. The extract of U. lobata L. leaves contains various phytochemicals, including alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, cyanogenic glycosides, steroids, tannins, and terpenoids, and for these compounds, this plant shows many pharmacological activities, like anti-inflammatory5, antidiabetic6, antinociceptive7, cytotoxic and antioxidant8.

Although U. lobata has demonstrated preliminary antioxidant, anxiolytic, and antidiabetic effects in several in vitro and in vivo reports, the majority of published work is still exploratory (phytochemical screening or single-assay studies) with little compound-level characterization or mechanistic insight. Medicinal plants are significant sources of multi-target bioactive molecules. Purnomo et al. showed, for instance, that U. lobata had incretin-related (GLP-1/DPP-4) effects in mouse models9. According to Pieme et al., the extract of U. lobata showed strong antioxidant and antiproliferative effects in MB-MDA535 cell lines10. Mannan et al. demonstrated that a plant extract from the Urena genus has thrombolytic activity in an in vitro assay, and Purnomo et al. reported that leaf extract from U. lobata exhibited anti-inflammatory and analgesic activities. However, the precise chemical component that generated this effect was not identified11,12. Recent GC–MS/HPLC surveys have started to catalogue leaf phytochemicals, although they have not yet connected individual elements to particular thrombolytic or inflammatory pathways.

The biological processes of inflammation and thrombosis are intimately related and are important in the formation of many chronic illnesses, such as cardiovascular and neurological conditions13. Despite the therapeutic efficacy of traditional anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic drugs like diclofenac, aspirin, and streptokinase, side effects like gastrointestinal distress, hypersensitivity reactions, and haemorrhagic complications frequently restrict their clinical use14,15. As a result, there is increasing interest in finding safer, naturally occurring substances that have both thrombolytic and anti-inflammatory properties.

U. lobata L. was selected for further research in consideration of its many applications, phytochemical reports, and accessibility in Bangladesh. The goal of the current investigation is to assess the in vitro anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic activities of the methanolic leaf extract and its fractions of U. lobata L. In addition, some in silico analysis will be performed to correlate the in vitro test results.

Results

Phytochemical screening

The U. lobata L. leaf extract was subjected to a fractionation process using solvents of increasing polarity, which is a widely accepted approach for separating complex plant constituents into chemically distinct groups that help to evaluate the contribution of compounds in extracts with different polarities to the biological activity. The fractionation of whole methanolic extract (ULM) was performed with n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and water to get n-hexane (ULH), chloroform (ULC), ethyl acetate (ULE), and aqueous (ULW) fractions16.

Phytochemical analysis exhibited the presence of flavonoids, steroids, terpenoids, carbohydrates, saponins, alkaloids, glycosides, and phenols in the methanolic extract of various fractions of U. lobata L., as reported in Table 1, where flavonoids, saponins, alkaloids, and phenols were present in all fractions of U. lobata L. (ULM, ULC, ULE, ULW, ULH). Steroids were present in ULE, ULM & ULC. Instead of ULH, glycosides & carbohydrates were present in all other fractions. Terpenoids were absent in U. lobata L. leaf extract.

Antioxidant Activity

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The DPPH scavenging activity of the fractions of U. lobata, expressed as IC50 values, is presented in Table 3. In this experiment, the ethyl acetate fraction (ULE) had the best radical scavenging activity with the lowest IC50 value (1.17 µg/mL), closely followed by the methanolic crude extract (ULM, 1.36 µg/mL). The aqueous (ULW) and chloroform (ULC) fractions had moderate activity, with IC50 values of 4.10 and 4.74 µg/mL, respectively, while the hexane fraction (ULH) had the lowest activity (IC50 = 11.18 µg/mL).

Hydroxyl radical scavenging assay

Table 3 shows the hydroxyl radical scavenging of the U. lobata fractions reported as IC50 values. In vitro anti-inflammatory activity

Heat-induced and hypotonicity-induced membrane stabilization techniques were used to assess the anti-inflammatory effect. Because the membranes of red blood cells and lysosomes are similar, the HRBC membrane stabilization test can be used to assess the in vitro anti-inflammatory properties of potential substances.

The methyl extract (ULM) and its fractions from U. lobata L. showed concentration-dependent anti-inflammatory activity. At all tested concentrations, ULC and ULE demonstrated significantly greater activity than ULM and ULW in the heat-induced hemolysis assay. While ULE showed 91.57 ± 0.32% inhibition at 400 µg/ml, ULC showed 93.15 ± 0.15% inhibition, which was statistically comparable to diclofenac (94.27 ± 0.22%). Significant differences between the treatment groups were found by Tukey’s post hoc analysis, as shown by various superscript letters (a-e) within the same concentration level (Table 4, Fig. 2a).

In vitro thrombolytic activity

The thrombolytic activity of U. lobata L. methanol extract and its fractions was assessed at various doses, with findings given as clot lysis percentages (Fig. 3). In the investigation, ULM, ULC, ULE, ULW, and ULH demonstrated 19.20 ± 1.30, 15.31 ± 0.85, 18.78 ± 0.61, 14.02 ± 1.06, 12.24 ± 0.71, and 28.26 ± 0.63, 26.43 ± 0.81, 28.40 ± 1.05, 23.23 ± 0.39, 18.95 ± 0.73% clot lysis at concentrations of 25 and 200 µg/ml, respectively (Fig. 3).

Percentage (%) of thrombolysis of different fractions of U. lobata L., streptokinase (standard) and distilled water (blank). The statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test; different superscript letters (a-c) denote significant differences between groups at the same concentration level (p < 0.05).

Compared to the other fractions in the analyzed samples, ULE and ULM showed noticeably greater thrombolytic activity. As a positive control, streptokinase (30,000 I.U.) exhibits 62.52 ± 0.67% clot lysis, while distilled water, used as a negative control, indicates a negligible 2.58 ± 0.47% clot lysis. For all samples, the experiment was performed in triplicate (n = 3), and significant differences between groups were verified by statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc test (p < 0.05).

Interaction of ligands with cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)

The molecular docking analysis revealed the binding interactions of various ligands with the cyclooxygenase-2 enzyme, as presented in Table 8 and Fig. 5. Among the compounds under investigation, N-(5-Methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide and 14-Deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide, 3Ac demonstrated the best ADMET profiles, with balanced lipophilicity, high absorption, and low projected toxicity, making them promising therapeutic options. Discussion

The current work conducts a full pharmacological evaluation of U. lobata, including phytochemical characterization, antioxidant capacity, anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic activity, molecular docking, and in silico pharmacokinetic evaluations. Phytochemical screening verified the presence of phenols and flavonoids across all fractions. FTIR analysis revealed typical O–H stretching vibrations (~ 3340–3397 cm-1). The functional groups are recognized for their capacity to donate hydrogen, which serves as the biochemical basis for antioxidant activity17.

This was reflected in in vitro antioxidant assays; the ethyl acetate fraction (ULE) had the lowest IC50 values in DPPH (1.17 µg/mL) and hydroxyl radical scavenging assays (2.60 µg/mL), indicating the highest antioxidant capacity among all fractions and comparable to standard catechin activity. The methanolic extract (ULM) had high DPPH scavenging activity (IC50: 1.36 µg/mL), whereas chloroform (ULC) and aqueous (ULW) fractions had moderate effects, and n-hexane (ULH) was the least active.

U. lobata fraction’s antioxidant potential adds directly to its anti-inflammatory benefits, as oxidative stress causes inflammation by destabilizing cellular membranes and encouraging the release of pro-inflammatory mediators. ULE stabilized biological membrane by scavenging reactive oxygen species, resulting in high membrane stabilization activity (~ 91.57%) equivalent to diclofenac (94.27%). Surprisingly, ULC had slightly stronger anti-inflammatory efficacy (3.15%), implying that both moderately polar and non-polar chemicals work synergistically in membrane protection and inflammation management18.

Thrombolytic activity adds to this pharmacological profile, with ULE (28.40%) and ULM (28.26%) having the highest clot lysis among the fractions. Although lower than streptokinase, these findings indicate the presence of chemicals that can modulate fibrinolytic pathways. Compared to other medicinal plants examined under similar in vitro circumstances, U. lobata appears to have promising anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic properties. The methanol extract of Drynaria quercifolia rhizomes had a membrane stabilization activity of ~ 70.9%, lower than ULE’s ~ 91.6%. The aqueous fraction showed moderate clot lysis (~ 33.30%), slightly higher but in the same range as ULE’s (28.40%) and ULM’s (28.26%). Similarly, Clerodendrum viscosum exhibited around 28.60% clot lysis. Comparative data show that bioactive fractions of U. lobata have anti-inflammatory and fibrinolytic effects comparable to or superior to those of other ethnomedicinal plants, indicating its potential as a source of bioactive therapeutic agents19–21.

GC–MS study of ULE revealed several phytochemicals, including several important molecules that appear to induce biological activity. The most prevalent ingredients were Oleic Acid (27.92%), 9-Octadecenamide, (Z)- (24.12%), 9,12-Octadecadienoic acid (Z,Z)- (6.18%), and the pharmacologically important β-amyrenonol, Ac derivative (2.36%). These results were further corroborated in silico PASS prediction, which revealed high probabilities of activity (Pa > 0.5) for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and thrombolytic effects for β-amyrenonol, Ac derivative, 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide (3Ac), 9,12-Octadecadienoic acid, and N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide.

Molecular docking identified β-amyrenonol, Ac derivative, as the most notable bioactive molecule, exhibiting the strongest anticipated binding to COX-2 (-10.1 kcal/mol), surpassing diclofenac (-8 kcal/mol). This interaction was supported by hydrogen bonds with THR168, TRP337, and HIS338 as well as hydrophobic interactions, demonstrating a strong and specific affinity for the anti-inflammatory target. β-amyrenonol, Ac derivative, had the highest binding affinity to plasminogen (-9.8 kacl/mol), forming hydrogen bonds with TYR628 and ASN626, indicating its possible dual activity in inflammation modulation and clot dissociation. For comparison, phytochemicals from Magnolia champaca show binding energies of -7.7 to -7.1 kcal/mol against tissue plasminogen activator, while many plant-derived flavonoids, including rhamnetin, kaempferol, quercetin, and luteolin, interact with COX-2 with binding energies ranging from -7.5 to -9.6 kcal/mol. By contrast, β-amyrenonol, Ac derivative exhibits greater binding to both COX-2 and plasminogen, indicating its potential as a highly efficient natural ligand with multi-functional thrombolytic and anti-inflammatory activity22–26.

Other Chemicals made important contributions to the observed pharmacology. 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide (3Ac) binds strongly to COX-2 (-7.7 kcal/mol) and plasminogen (-7 kcal/mol), while N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide showed moderate COX-2 binding (-6.8 kcal/mol), indicating a potential role in anti-inflammatory action. These findings suggest that the pharmacological effects result from the synergistic action of many chemicals rather than a single dominant element27.

Pharmacokinetic and ADME analysis found that 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide (3Ac), N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide, and 3-hydroxy-β-damascone have favorable drug-like properties such as moderate molecular weight, balanced lipophilicity (CLogP ~ 2.5), appropriate hydrogen bonding, high gastrointestinal absorption, and good solubility, supporting oral bioavailability. Despite superior docking scores, ADMET analysis found that β-amyrenonol acetate derivative has poor GI absorption due to high lipophilicity (CLogP 7.56), which may limit oral bioavailability and systemic exposure. Although this is a pharmacokinetic limitation, highly lipophilic drugs may still exhibit significant membrane permeability and interaction with lipid-rich biological targets28. Despite its unfavorable pharmacokinetic profile, β-amyrenonol acetate derivative may still have pharmacological value if administered via non-oral routes or optimized formulation techniques. To improve solubility and drug-likeness, future research could look into nanoformulations, lipid based systems, solid dispersions, or structural modifications. All of the drugs that were identified had low potential for CYP450-mediated drug interactions, non-mutagenicity, non-hepatotoxicity, and lack of hERG inhibition, according to ADMET projections29–32.

Overall, it was observed that there were four main bioactive chemicals which played major roles in the pharmacological activities of U. lobata: β-amyrenonol acetate derivative, 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide (3Ac), N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide, and 3-hydroxy-β-damascone. Previous research has found that andrographolide-related diterpenoids and fatty acid derivatives possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which provide support for the biological significance of substances like 14-deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide and similar analogues33,34. In addition, it has been reported that derivatives of thiazole and damascone have shown their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities through modulation of inflammatory mediators and free radical scavenging mechanisms. This suggests that N-(5-methyl-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)-2-phenylacetamide, and 3-hydroxy-β-damascone may be biologically relevant to the pharmacological effects35,36. The enrichment of these compounds may collectively contribute to antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and thrombolytic effects of the ethyl acetate fraction (ULE) through complementary and interconnected mechanisms. These outcomes are further supported by in vitro tests, molecular docking, PASS prediction, and ADMET analyses. These results highlight U. lobata plant’s potential as a source of therapeutically relevant multifunctional bioactive compounds.

Conclusion

The present study confirms that U. lobata L. methanolic extract and its solvent fractions possess significant anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic activities, supported by both in vitro and in silico analyses. Phytochemical screening revealed diverse bioactive compounds, including phenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, and glycosides, which contribute to these pharmacological effects. Among the compounds identified by GC–MS, the β-amyrenonol acetate derivative showed the strongest molecular docking affinity with COX-2 and plasminogen targets, indicating potent dual anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic potential. Additionally, compounds such as 14-Deoxy-12-hydroxyandrographolide and 3-hydroxy-beta-damascone exhibited promising binding affinities and favorable ADME profiles, highlighting their suitability as orally bioavailable drug candidates. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to use integrated experimental and computational methods to systematically assess the thrombolytic activity of U. lobata L. phytoconstituents. These findings validate the traditional use of U. lobata L. and underscore its potential as a natural source for developing novel anti-inflammatory and thrombolytic agents.

Although the FT-IR analyses and GC–MS findings do not represent a complete phytochemical profile of Urena lobata L. extracts, they help the preliminary identification of the major classes of phytoconstituents and characteristic functional groups. Furthermore, molecular docking studies and pharmacokinetic/ADMET evaluations of the tentatively identified compounds through GC–MS analysis explore their possible biological interactions, binding affinities, drug-likeness, and safety-related properties that were intended to provide supportive mechanistic insights rather than definitive confirmation of bioactive constituents. Future studies should focus on the isolation and characterization of active compounds, in vivo validation, molecular dynamics simulations, and formulation optimization to enhance bioavailability, especially for compounds with low gastrointestinal absorption, to fully explore their therapeutic potential.

Methods

Selection, collection, and identification of the chosen plant

With prior consent from the landowner, the full fresh leaves were collected from unrestricted open spaces outside of Jatiya Kabi Kazi Nazrul Islam University, Trishal, Mymensingh, in November 2023. The botanical identification of U. lobata L. was confirmed by an experienced Plant Taxonomist, Professor A.H.M. Mahbubur Rahman, Department of Botany, University of Rajshahi, Bangladesh and assigned an accession number (PH-61), which was subsequently preserved in the herbarium of the Botany Department at the University of Rajshahi.

Preparation of plant materials for extraction

Freshly collected leaves were thoroughly rinsed with clean tap water to eliminate adhering dust and other impurities. The cleaned samples were subsequently shade-dried for several days in a well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight to protect heat-sensitive phytoconstituents, with occasional brief exposure to sunlight when necessary. The partially dried leaves were then further dried in an oven at approximately 30° C to facilitate grinding. Following oven drying, the leaves were ground into a coarse powder using an electric grinder. Finally, the powdered material was stored in a tightly closed amber glass container at 4° C until further use. The total amount of the dried U. lobata L. leaves was 1500 g.

Extraction and solvent–solvent partitioning of crude extract

The crude extract of U. lobata L. was obtained by cold extraction, in which coarse powder was steeped in methanol in an airtight amber bottle 5–7 days with intermittent shaking. The mixture was filtered through markin cloth and cotton, and filtrate was concentrated with a rotary evaporator at 40º C under reduced pressure, yielding methanolic extract (ULM). The extract was then suspended in water and partitioned with n-hexane, chloroform, and ethyl acetate to get four distinct fractions. The fractions were concentrated to produce n-hexane (ULH), chloroform (ULC), ethyl acetate (ULE), and aqueous (ULW) fractions.

Phytochemical screening of the crude extract and its fractions

Phytochemical screening of crude extract of U. lobata L. leaf and its fractions was performed using standard qualitative methods with minor modifications37–39.

Phenols have been identified by adding a 5% ferric chloride solution, which produced a bluish-black coloration. The emergence of an orange color following sulphuric acid treatment proved the presence of flavonoids. Carbohydrates were determined using Fehling’s and 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine assays. Saponins were identified by shaking vigorously with distilled water, resulting in a prolonged foam. Adding chloroform, acetic acid, and concentrated H2SO4 caused a color change from violet to green, indicating the presence of glycosides. Alkaloids were confirmed by treating the extract with 1% HCl followed by Mayer’s and Wagner’s reagents, which resulted in turbidity precipitatin.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of the plant extracts

One method for simultaneously and quantitatively identifying the functional groups of bioactive compounds is FTIR. The fingerprints of a particular molecular structure and chemical bonding are represented by the FTIR peaks and spectra, which help identify the molecular structure present in plant extracts. Attenuated total reflectance–Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) instrument (Brand: PerkinElmer, Model: L1600300, Version 10.6.2), this instrument has two spectrums having S/N: 115,061, that is made in Llantrisant, UK, was used to analyze the extracts. The crude methanolic extract, ethyl acetate fraction, and chloroform fraction of U. lobata L. were all subjected to FTIR analysis in this investigation, and spectra were recorded between 4000 cm-1 and 400 cm-1 in wavenumber, with a resolution of 5 cm-1.

In vitro anti-inflammatory assay

Both heat-induced and hypotonicity-induced hemolysis techniques were used to evaluate the integrity of the human red blood cell (HRBC) membrane. With a few minor adjustments, blood samples were prepared in accordance with the Shindhe et al. (1999)42 techniques. A healthy human volunteer, who had stopped using NSAIDS for at least two weeks before the trial, was given a venous blood sample. After the sample was put in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged for 5 min at 2500 rpm, the supernatant was disposed of. After being cleaned with sterile saline (0.9% w/v NaCl), the erythrocytes were centrifuged once more for 5 min at 2500 rpm. Until the supernatant was clear and colorless, this washing process was carried out three times.

In vitro thrombolytic activity test

Sample preparation

Sample concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/ml were obtained by suspending 5 mg of each fraction in 1 ml of distilled water. After vigorous shaking, the mixer was left at room temperature for the entire time to allow the insoluble components to settle and the water-soluble components to fully dissolve. The clear aqueous supernatant was then separated from the mixture through a paper filter (Whatman No. 1). Then in vitro thrombolytic activity test was conducted using the filtrate.

In vitro thrombolytic assay for extract

5 ml of whole blood was drawn under aseptic conditions from human volunteers who had never taken an oral contraceptive or an anticoagulant. To enable clot formation, 500 µl of blood was put into Eppendorf tubes that had already been weighed. In particular, each pre-weighed, labelled, sterile Eppendorf tube received 0.5 ml of freshly drawn blood, which was then incubated at 37ºC for 45 min. The serum was carefully removed after the clot formed so as not to disrupt it. To find the clot weight, each tube holding the clot was weighed once again.

Each Eppendorf tube with pre-weighed clot was filled with 100 µl of different U. lobata were fractions. Separate control tubes were filled with 100 µl of distilled water for the negative control. After 90 min of incubation at 370C, each tube was checked for clot lysis. The amount of clot disruption was assessed by carefully removing the released supernatant after incubation and reweighing each tube. The proportion of clot lysis was computed from the weight change before and after therapy.

The experiment was repeated several times with the blood samples of different volunteers45 and streptokinase was used as a reference standard.

Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) profiling

The plant’s bioactive ingredients of the ethyl acetate fraction of U. lobata L. (ULE) were identified using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS). The analysis was performed at Bangladesh Reference Institute for Chemical Measurements (BRiCM) with a SHIMADZU (Model: GCMS-TQ8040) instrument and a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 µm film thickness).

Helium was employed as the carrier gas, with a constant flow rate of 1.69 mL/min. The injector temperature remained at 250º C, but the first oven temperature was set to 50º C. Electron ionization was performed at 70 eV and mass spectra were collected across a scanning range of 40–700 m/z.

To identify phytochemical ingredients, the resulting mass spectra were compared to standard reference spectra from existing libraries.

In silico PASS prediction study

Prediction of activity spectra for substances (PASS) has been utilized as a powerful computational tool to predict the biological activity spectra of synthetic compounds, facilitating the identification of novel drug candidates46. The web-based PASS program, accessible at https://www.way2drug.com/passonline/predict.php, was used to forecast the compound’s biological activity spectrum. Results from the PASS analysis are shown as probability of activity (Pa) and probability of inactivity (Pi), with values between 0.000 and 1.000. Notably, the sum of Pa and Pi does not always equal 1 because they are computed independently. Only predicted activities when Pa exceeds Pi are considered potentially relevant for pharmacological activity for a specific drug molecule47. The PASS prediction findings were interpreted using the following probability thresholds: (i) When Pa < 0.5, the likelihood of experimental validation is low, but the prediction may still reflect potential structural relevance to the activity; (ii) when 0.5 < Pa < 0.7, the possibility of experimental confirmation is moderate and the compound may differ structurally from known pharmacological agents; and (iii) when Pa > 0.7, the predicted activity is highly likely to be confirmed experimentally. Thus, the projected activity spectrum is regarded as an intrinsic characteristic of the compound48.

In silico molecular docking

Pharmaceutics can be developed and tested using molecular docking cost-effectively and efficiently49. An atomic-level analysis of phytochemicals (ligand molecules) and proteins (receptors or target molecules) is crucial for determining the binding potential of ligands at target binding sites. Plant-derived phytochemicals obtained by GC–MS analysis were chosen as ligands for the docking study. After obtaining 3D chemical structures from PubChem, the compounds were converted into PDB format using Open Babel. The 3D structures of target proteins were obtained from the RCSB data bank. Protein preparation was done by using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2020, water molecules and unnecessary heteroatoms were eliminated, and polar hydrogen atoms were added to proteins. Following preprocessing, the protein and ligand files were converted into PDBQT format and were ready for PyRx docking simulations.

Preparation of the selected ligands

Phytocompounds found in the extracts GC–MS analysis were chosen as ligands, and their three-dimensional Conformers were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) database in SDF format50. The structures were energy-minimized to improve their shape, and then they were converted to PDBQT format using PyRx version 0.8 platform, which incorporates Open Babel for file processing51,52.

Protein preparation

The corresponding crystal structures of target proteins, human plasminogen (PDB ID: 4DUR) and human cyclooxygenase-2 (PDB ID: 5F1A), were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/)53. To prepare the protein, we used BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2020 to remove heteroatoms and water molecules to eliminate unwanted interactions and ensure proper accessibility of the active binding site, and then saved the cleaned structure in PDB format54. We then used Swiss-PDB viewer to minimize the energy of the process protein to improve structural stability55. Finally, we imported the optimized structure into PyRx, where we converted the macromolecular PDB format into PDBQT format for further computational analysis.

Molecular docking and visualization of protein–ligand interactions

To assess the protein’s binding affinity and interaction potential with the chosen ligands, molecular docking was carried out using Auto Dock Vina. To ensure complete coverage of the active site during docking, a grid box was used with appropriate dimensions and centered at specific X, Y, and Z coordinates. Using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2020, profiles of protein–ligand interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, were further analyzed56,57.

In silico pharmacokinetics and drug-like parameters prediction

The pharmacokinetics and drug-like parameters of the selected compounds were predicted using the SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch)58 server. To enhance the accuracy of drug efficacy predictions, several key pharmacokinetic parameters were assessed, including molecular weight, number of rotatable bonds, number of hydrogen-bond acceptors, number of hydrogen-bond donors, ClogP, TPSA, LogS, and GI Absorption59. Lipinski’s Rule of Five, which is frequently used to predict oral bioavailability and drug-likeness of small compounds based on their physicochemical features, was one of the first filters used to evaluate drug-likeness60. The SMILES representations of each test compound were uploaded to the SwissADME platform.

In silico ADMET analysis

In silico techniques provide an effective way to analyze important pharmacokinetic characteristics such as Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME), aiding early-stage screening and optimization of lead compounds48. The pkCSM (http://structure.bioc.cam.ac.uk/ pkcsm)30 and Deep-PK (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/deeppk/)61 web servers were used to predict the ADMET characteristics of the chosen phytochemicals. The canonical SMILES for each compound was obtained from the PubChem database and used as input. Human intestinal absorption, Caco-2 permeability, BBB permeability, CYP450 inhibition, total clearance, AMES toxicity, hepatotoxicity, hERG inhibition and maximum tolerated dose were among the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity parameters that were estimated using pkCSM. Important pharmacokinetic parameters as oral bioavailability, plasma protein binding, and clearance, were further validated using Deep-PK. The pharmacokinetic behavior and safety of the conventional medication diclofenac were evaluated by comparing the expected values.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were examined with basic statistical methods in Microsoft Excel (Version 13). All tests were done in triplicate, and resulted data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). IBM SPSS Statistics was used to conduct statistical analysis. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hos test was used for statistical analysis among groups at the same concentration level to determine the significant differences. A statistically significant value was defined as p < 0.05. This approach ensures a trustworthy evaluation of data consistency and experimental precision.
