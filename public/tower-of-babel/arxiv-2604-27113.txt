Modeling the impact of host diversity on the evolution of vector feeding preferences and implications for disease control
Shravani Shetgaonkar, Anupama Sharma

Abstract
Vector-borne diseases often infect multiple host species, increasing the likelihood of disease persistence due to the presence of multiple reservoirs. Vector biting patterns and feeding preferences can shift in response to selective pressures introduced by disease control interventions, altering the dynamics of transmission. In this paper, we develop a mathematical model that couples host diversity and adaptive vector behavior with vector-borne disease transmission dynamics, focusing on a system with two hosts and a vector population exhibiting preference for one host. We derive the basic reproduction number, R 0 R_{0} , a threshold that determines the existence of two equilibria in our model, and obtain conditions that can lead to the long-term persistence of the disease. Our analysis suggests that shortening the infectious period of the vector’s preferred host is an effective control strategy. We also identified a threshold that determines whether shifting vector preference toward a non-preferred host amplifies or reduces the disease burden on the primary preferred host. Our results show that protective measures for the preferred host can trigger adaptive shifts in vector preferences, reducing disease prevalence in that host. However, this shift may lead to an increase in overall host prevalence.



1
Introduction

Vector feeding preference refers to the tendency of vector species to feed more frequently on a particular host compared to others. Various genetic and physiological host traits, such as odor, body heat, and body mass, shape the vector feeding preferences
23
. For instance,
Aedes aegypti,
which are vectors for diseases like chikungunya, dengue fever, yellow fever, and Zika, exhibit a strong preference for feeding on humans over other animals
33
. Similar patterens have been documented for vectors of Lyme disease
20
, West Nile virus (WNV)
11
,
8
, and Chagas disease
12
. These feeding preferences not only influence the distribution and intensity of disease transmission, but can also impact the effectiveness of control measures. Furthermore, pathogens can exhibit antigenic variation as an adaptive response to the diverse immune defenses of multiple host species
32
. For instance, African trypanosomiasis, transmitted by the tsetse fly and caused by the protozoan parasite
Trypanosoma brucei,
is characterized by the parasite’s ability to switch between different antigenic variants. This switch allows the parasite to evade the host’s immune system, contributing to the chronic nature of the infection
10
. Such antigenic variations can also lead to the development of drug resistance in pathogens
36
,
26
.

When multiple host species participate in the pathogen infection cycle, one species may act as the primary reservoir, sustaining the pathogen and serving as a continuous source of infection to other hosts. However, most mathematical models for vector-borne disease (VBD) have primarily focused on single-host single-vector frameworks
31
,
21
,
7
,
5
,
30
,
1
. A comprehensive understanding of vector-host interactions is essential for designing targeted prevention and control measures
29
,
3
, as the population composition, i.e., abundance of different hosts, affects the vectorial capacity and alter the course of disease spread. For example, increasing the proportion of incompetent hosts – the host unable to transmit the disease even when bitten by the vector – has been shown to reduce the prevalence of Chagas disease
40
. Ignoring vector feeding preferences in models with diverse host populations can lead to incomplete understanding of transmission dynamics and result in sub-optimal control measures.

Establishing a unifying principle of how host diversity influences VBD transmission is challenging, as its effect on disease dynamics is largely shaped by the ecological specifics of each system
39
,
34
,
28
,
4
,
6
,
38
. For example, whether host diversity amplifies, dilutes, or has no effect on disease prevalence depends on the interplay between vector preference and host transmission abilities, with amplification occurring when both host species are present leading to maximal disease risk
24
. Moreover, vectors may adapt their feeding preferences in response to host specific interventions, creating significant bottlenecks for the control and elimination of VBD. When vectors initially bite only competent hosts, time-dependent feeding preferences can increase disease prevalence and shift the timing of epidemic peaks
22
. Hence, understanding the evolutionary dynamics of vector preference and its effect on disease transmission is crucial for disease control.

In this study, we examine the long-term implications of vector preference on VBD transmission and study the relationship between host diversity and the evolution of vector feeding preferences using adaptive dynamics
14
. Specifically, we analyze how changes in host community composition affect the evolutionary trait of vectors to feed on a non-preferred host. We sought to quantify the relationship between adaptive responses driven by the trade-off between a preference for a specific host and the need to evade the defences of alternative hosts. We found that vector preferences can shift in response to protective measures employed by the preferred host, which
may
lead to an overall increase in disease prevalence amongst the host species. Our approach offers a framework to evaluate the effectiveness of implementing interventions such as ITN and vector repellents for abating prevalence among h1 in the presence of h2.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
we formulate a compartmental model for VBD with two hosts, one vector, and vector preference. Section
3
covers the computation of
R_{0},
and the analysis of equilibria and their stability. Section
4
provides a sensitivity analysis of
R_{0}
. Section
5
explores the evolutionary dynamics of vector feeding preferences using adaptive dynamics, and Section
7
concludes with a discussion of the results.

5
Evolutionary dynamics of vector biting pattern and host-seeking behavior

In a single-host system, protecting the host can effectively reduce disease prevalence. However, this principle does not extend to multi-host systems such as ours, where community composition becomes a critical determinant of disease risk. In a scenario where one host becomes “functionally unavailable" to the pathogen – for example, one host population using protection against vector – an evolutionary question arises: whether selection will favor convergence toward a generalist phenotype capable of exploiting multiple host types, or specialization and host preference will be maintained? Moreover, how would such evolutionary shifts will affect the long-term dynamics of disease transmission?

To answer these questions, we investigate the adaptive dynamics of vector populations for the evolution of their feeding preferences. Furthermore, to verify whether the disease risk among
h1
reduces under such conditions, we investigate the impact of the evolution of a generalist strategy on the dynamics of VBD. We start by assuming that the dynamics of vector population depend on their innate feeding preferences and the strength of the trade-off related to mastering defence based on host type. Denote
\sigma_{h1}
as the probability that the vector attacks
h1
if encountered. We then assume that
\sigma_{h1}=1
as the vectors have an inclination to feed on
h1
. The probability of attacking
h2
for feeding if encountered is indicated by
\sigma_{h2}
and is considered an evolving trait among vectors. Let
\mathcal{E}_{h1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{h2}
represent the encounter rates of
h1
and
h2
respectively. The probability that the vector survives till it detects a host is obtained by the following relation

p_{1}=\frac{\digamma p_{2}}{1-p_{2}(1-\digamma)},

Here
p_{2}
and
\digamma
are the probabilities of surviving and locating a host during a single host seeking period, in which
\digamma=1-e^{-(\sigma_{h1}\mathcal{E}_{h1}+\sigma_{h2}\mathcal{E}_{h2})}

35
.

Also, while attempting to feed, some vectors may die fending off the host defence. Moreover, the vector’s likelihood of evading the defensive behavior of a host enhances if it always attacks that specific host type. However, this could undermine its defence against the other host species. Let the mortality caused due to host defense be at maximum intensity of
\nu
,
0<\nu<1
. And the inverse of the trade-off strength be indicated by
\chi
. According to
27
, such a trade-off can be described to obtain the probability of the vector surviving a feed on
h1
and
h2
:

\displaystyle p_{h1}

\displaystyle=1-\nu\varphi^{\chi},

\displaystyle p_{h2}

\displaystyle=1-\nu(1-\varphi)^{\chi}.

Here
\varphi=\frac{\sigma_{h2}\mathcal{E}_{h2}}{\sigma_{h1}\mathcal{E}_{h1}+\sigma_{h2}\mathcal{E}_{h2}}
is the conditional probability that
h2
is fed upon after being located, and (
1-\varphi
) is the conditional probability of feeding on
h1
. Since
q_{1}=p_{1}(\varphi p_{h2}+(1-\varphi)p_{h1})
accounts for the vectors survival probability of foraging
35
,
p_{rc}=q_{1}q_{2}
is the probability of surviving the whole cycle, where
q_{2}
is the probability that the vector survives the phase after feeding on a host successfully. During this stage, it rests and reproduces before consuming another meal. Let
\tau_{2}
be the duration of this second phase. To associate the daily survival rates of the vector population with their feeding intervals, we consider a similar approach as
18
.
Assume that a normal foraging bout lasts for
\tau_{f}
time. We can find the duration of one complete feeding cycle by accounting for the number of trials carried out to complete the cycle,

\tau_{1}=\frac{\tau_{f}}{1-p_{2}(1-\digamma)}.

The vector biting rate can be expressed as,
c=(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})^{-1}.
Moreover, the death rate of vector can be determined using
d_{3}=-\log(p_3)
where
p_{3}=p_{rc}^{c}
is the probability of surviving every day. Also, if
f_{rc}
denotes the mean number of offspring produced per reproductive cycle, then the daily birth rate is
b_{3}=f_{rc}c
.

To probe the influence of host encounter rate on evolution of trait among vectors to attack
h2
(
\sigma_{h2}
), we employ the adaptive dynamics approach. Consider the equilibrium value of the resident population and evaluate the invasion fitness of the mutant by finding its initial growth rate as

\displaystyle\psi_{\sigma_{h2(r)}}(\sigma_{h2(m)})

\displaystyle=b_{3,\sigma_{h2(m)}}-d_{3,\sigma_{h2(m)}}-b_{31}M^{\star},\text{ here }M^{\star}=\frac{b_{3,\sigma_{h2(r)}}-d_{3,\sigma_{h2(r)}}}{b_{31}}.

The invading genotype will be evolutionarily advantageous if
\psi_{\sigma_{h2(r)}}(\sigma_{h2(m)})>0
. Here
\sigma_{h2(r)}
and
\sigma_{h2(m)}
denote the strategy used by the resident and mutant populations. At the evolutionary singular strategies (ESS),
\sigma_{h2}^{*}
, we have

\left[\dfrac{\partial\psi_{\sigma_{h2(r)}}(\sigma_{h2(m)})}{\partial\sigma_{h2(m)}}\right]_{\sigma_{h2(r)}=\sigma_{h2(m)}^{*},\atop\sigma_{h2(m)}=\sigma_{h2(m)}^{*}}=0.

Using the expression of invasion fitness and its derivative at the ESS, we can study possible evolutionary outcomes in the system. To understand the conditions on host community composition that lead the vector population to evolve no feeding preference or to adopt an opportunistic feeding strategy, we constructed pairwise invasibility plots (PIPs). The occurrence of evolutionary unstable singular points demonstrates the existence of such a generalist strategy. Figure

In the preceding sections, we have characterized the epidemiological dynamics of VBD across host populations and explored the adaptive evolution of vector biting behavior in response to host defenses. In this section, we investigate the feedback loop whereby vector evolution affects epidemiological outcomes, and these outcomes then influence evolutionary trajectories of vector bitting behavior.

Discussion

The diversity - stability debate in ecology is a classic and ongoing discussion about how biodiversity affects the stability of ecosystems. Similarly, epidemiologists continue to examine whether the increased host diversity can amplify or dilute the prevalence of a VBD. Understanding this complex dynamics is crucial from policy making viewpoint as interventions designed with the focus only on a single host species are known to shift vector preference and impact the broader disease ecology
13
,
15
. Our work is a step towards providing a unified framework that integrates host-vector interactions with vector preference adaptation, which are measurable characteristics of host and vector populations. Our model analysis teases out conditions where transmission intensifies when vectors with feeding preferences spread the disease between two hosts. We have also illustrated that
R_{0}
only reaches its minimum value
R_{0c}
when the vector preference equals to measure of host competence of
h2
relative to
h1
. Additionally, we found that the effect of host population size on
R_{0}
is not straightforward, which complements the findings by
24
. We demonstrated that an increase in host population size reduces the spread of VBD when the recovery rate for that host exceeds a certain threshold, which explains the previous results showing that an abundance of the low competent host population can reduce VBD transmission
15
. Our results suggest that a strong vector preference for a particular host population (
\alpha_{v}\gg 1
for
h1
and
\alpha_{v}\ll 1
for
h2
) can hinder disease eradication. However, effective treatment that shortens the infectious period of the preferred host can increase the recovery rate above the threshold, reducing VBD prevalence. Importantly, we have illustrated that by monitoring vector feeding patterns and observing reservoir host populations near human communities, this threshold can be identified, which will enabling timely interventions to facilitate VBD eradication.

A key result of our analysis is the derivation of a critical threshold,
R_{0c}
, that characterizes the impact of vector preference on the equilibrium prevalence of
h1
. This threshold marks a transition in the qualitative behavior of the system and provides a mechanistic basis for anticipating shifts in disease dynamics.
R_{0c}
may serve as a valuable indicator for informing surveillance efforts and optimizing control strategies, particularly in systems where vector behavior is variable or subject to intervention. We found that when
h_{1}
is a highly competent host (owing to high values of
\beta_{hv}
and
\beta_{vh}
, along with a low recovery rate
\mu_{1}
), the prevalence in
h_{1}
increases steadily as vector preference
\alpha_{v}
shifts toward it. Interestingly, in this setting, the presence of a second host,
h_{2}
, with lower competence, can help reduce the infection level in
h_{1}
, particularly if some proportion of vector bites are redirected toward
h_{2}
. The effect is more noticeable when
h_{2}
contributes little to vector infection, acting more as a biological buffer than a source. Diverting vectors from humans to animals as a strategy to mitigate vector-borne disease (VBD) transmission has significant empirical support
17
. The concept of zooprophylaxis - leveraging alternative host species to reduce human-vector interactions - has emerged as a promising approach in VBD management. Moreover, integrating zooprophylaxis with targeted insecticide application to protect these additional host species offers a dual benefit: reducing vector populations while simultaneously disrupting disease transmission cycles
2
,
16
.

Recent studies have found functions of vector preference and transmission ratio to predict amplification and dilution effects in the presence of multiple hosts
24
,
6
. Moreover, these conditions are established on the basis of whether species richness increases or decreases
R_{0}
and fail to address its effects on prevalence among a particular species. Our findings indicate that depending on the value
R_{0c}
, increasing vector preference towards
h2
can trigger a resurgence of VBD prevalence in
h1
,
y_{1}^{*}
. Such behavior is observed from Figure
3
(a)-(b). That is when
R_{0c}
is near 1 and
\alpha_{v}
is decreasing below
\alpha_{vc}
then
y_{1}^{*}
increases and attains a maximum at some point in
(0,\alpha_{vc})
. This suggests that protecting humans (
h1
) can concentrate bites on surrounding unprotected hosts and result in increased infections. Our result agrees with the fact that when
R_{0c}
is low the abundance of
h2
can cause amplification of infected cases
19
. Moreover, our results also indicate that the introduction of secondary hosts like livestock in the presence of vectors that show moderate preference can increase the transmission of VBD. Such a outcome has been witnessed for Malaria,
25
. Hence, conducting a complete evaluation of factors, such as host recovery rate, transmission efficiency, and vector feeding behavior is advised before implementing the intervention strategy to divert vector preference for its effectiveness in controlling VBD. Our model simulations showed a decline in prevalence among
h_{1}
, while prevalence in
h_{2}
and the vector increased, as shown in Figure
7
(f). Hence our approach also offers a framework to evaluate the effectiveness of implementing interventions such as ITN and vector repellents for abating prevalence among
h1
in the presence of
h2
.

Studies incorporating vector preference into their transmission terms
28
,
40
,
22
,
34
have overlooked how changes in the underlying factors that influence vector preference can lead to adverse outcomes. Our work fills this knowledge gap by identifying the conditions under which vector preference may shift from
h_{1}
to
h_{2}
, while explicitly linking vector demographics to their feeding behaviour. Our results reveal that vector populations tend to evolve toward one of two extremes: a
specialist strategy
where vectors feed exclusively on
h_{1}
, or a
generalist strategy
in which vectors consistently bite
h_{2}
whenever possible. The direction of evolution depends on the initial trait distribution which separates the basins of attraction for these two strategies on the Pairwise Invasibility Plot (PIP). By examining how the singular strategy varies with trade-off strength, we found that the probability of a host being targeted by vectors depends not only on its abundance but also on the “cost’ of specialization and the host’s defensive capabilities. In particular, we find that a high feeding preference for
h_{1}
can be redirected toward
h_{2}
when the encounter rate with
h_{2}
equals or exceeds that of
h_{1}
. To explore this further, we consider the case where both hosts have equal encounter rates and identify conditions under which the evolutionary singular point
(\sigma_{h_{2}}^{*})
shifts, making the generalist strategy more stable as the trade-off strength
\chi
increases. We found that the likelihood of vectors attacking
h_{2}
decreases when the trade-off between host specialization and feeding success weakens. Interventions such as insecticide-treated nets or repellents can reduce encounters between vectors and
h_{1}
, thereby strengthening the trade-off and effectively increasing the relative encounter rate with
h_{2}
. This shift lowers the evolutionary preference
\alpha_{v}
for
h_{1}
, and ultimately helps reduce infection prevalence in that host.

Our work raises a number of open questions for future work. From an eco-evolutionary perspective, more detailed models may provide further insight on the effect of insecticide resistance in vectors or seasonal shifts in their populations on disease prevalence, which are not captured by our model. Incorporating real-world data on host and vector populations, vector feeding patterns, and intervention outcomes will allow more robust validation and refinement of model predictions. Our framework highlights the potential for adaptive vector behavior and host heterogeneity to influence disease dynamics, suggesting that interventions targeting a single host or ignoring vector adaptation may have unintended consequences. With further development and integration of evolutionary and adaptive analyses, our modeling approach can help identify optimal strategies for vector control, guide public health decision-making, and improve the overall understanding of VBD dynamics across diverse ecological contexts.

The Next Generation Matrix (NGM) method is employed to determine the basic reproduction number,

T
and
\Sigma

T
represents the transmission terms, capturing the production of new infections, while
\Sigma
accounts for the transitions within compartments, such as recovery, mortality, or immunity acquisition. This decomposition facilitates the derivation of the NGM and the subsequent calculation of
R_{0}
.

Let
c_{1}=\dfrac{\beta_{hv}cM}{(\alpha_{v}N_{h1}+N_{h2})}
and
c_{2}=\dfrac{\beta_{vh}c}{(\alpha_{v}N_{h1}+N_{h2})}
, then for system (
2
) we get the following,

\displaystyle T=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\alpha_{v}c_{1}\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&c_{1}\\
c_{2}\alpha_{v}N_{h1}&0&c_{2}N_{h2}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}-(\mu_{1}+d_{1})&0&0&0\\
\mu_{1}&-(d_{1}+\delta_{1})&0&0\\
0&0&-(\delta_{2}+d_{2})&0\\
0&0&0&-d_{3}\end{bmatrix},

Following the decomposition, the next step involves multiplying the transmission matrix,
T
, by the inverse of the transition matrix,
\Sigma^{-1}.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=-T\Sigma^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&-\alpha_{v}c_{1}\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-c_{1}\\
-c_{2}\alpha_{v}N_{h1}&0&-c_{2}N_{h2}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{-1}{(\mu_{1}+d_{1})}&0&0&0\\
