On non-uniqueness of mild solutions and stationary singular solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations
Alexey Cheskidov, Hedong Hou

Abstract
. We prove that the unconditional uniqueness of mild solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations fails in all the Besov spaces with negative regularity index, by constructing non-trivial stationary singular solutions via convex integration. We also establish uniqueness of stationary weak solutions in an endpoint critical space. Similar results are proved for the fractional Navier-Stokes equations with arbitrarily large power of the Laplacian in both Lebesgue and Besov spaces.

35A02, 35K55, 35Q30, 42B37, 76D05.

1.
Introduction

Consider the Navier-Stokes equations

(NSE)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\Div(u\otimes u)-\Delta u+\nabla p=0\\
\Div u=0\\
u(0)=u_{\initial},\quad\Div u_{\initial}=0,\end{cases}

on the
d
-dimensional torus
{\mathds{T}}^{d}={\mathds{R}}^{d}/{\mathds{Z}}^{d}
. Let
0<T\leq\infty
. For a divergence-free distribution
u_{\initial}\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
, we say
u:[0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}^{d}
is a
mild
solution to (
NSE
) with initial data
u_{\initial}
if it satisfies the integral equation

u(t)=e^{t\Delta}u_{\initial}-B(u,u)(t)

in the sense of distributions
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
, where the bilinear operator
B
is formally given by the integral

B(u,u)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}{\mathds{P}}\Div(u\otimes u)(s)ds,\quad t\in(0,T).

Here,
{\mathds{P}}
denotes the Leray projection on divergence-free vector fields, and the tensor product
u\otimes u
is defined as a paraproduct by the Littlewood-Paley series, see Section
1.3
for detailed discussion
1
1

1

We do not impose
u\in L^{2}_{\loc}
, so
u\otimes u
may not belong to
L^{1}_{\loc}
. One may also call such mild solutions as
singular mild solutions
to distinguish with the classical mild solutions.
.

Based on the scaling property (when posed in
{\mathds{R}}^{d}
)

u(t,x)\mapsto u_{\lambda}(t,x):=\lambda u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x),

there is a chain of scale-invariant (or called

{\dot{H}}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\hookrightarrow L^{d}\hookrightarrow{\dot{B}}^{-1+\frac{d}{q}}_{q,r}\hookrightarrow\bmo^{-1}\hookrightarrow{\dot{B}}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty},\quad 2\leq d<q<\infty.

The pioneering work of Fujita and Kato
[

(together with a complement of Chemin

]
) established local well-posedness of (
NSE
) in
{\dot{H}}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}
. Such results are also valid in
L^{d}
due to Kato
[
22
]
and in
{\dot{B}}^{-1+\frac{d}{q}}_{q,r}
due to Cannone
[
7
]
and Planchon
[
33
]
. In endpoint critical spaces, Koch and Tataru
[
24
]
first proved local well-posedness in
\vmo^{-1}
, the closure of Schwartz functions in
\bmo^{-1}
. On the other hand, Bourgain and Pavlović
[
4

proved ill-posedness of (
NSE
) in
{\dot{B}}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
. See also the work of the first author and Shvydkoy
[
13
]
for ill-posedness in
B^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
, as well as the works of Yoneda
[
40
]
and Wang
[
38
]
in

. The reader can also refer to the works of Coiculescu and Palasek
[

, and the first author, Dai and Palasek
[

on non-uniqueness of mild solutions with initial data in

.

Meanwhile, Fabes, Jones and Rivière
[
18

proved

of mild solutions in subcritical Lebesgue spaces

for
d<p<\infty
. Namely, given the initial data in

, there exists at most one mild solution in the solution class
C([0,T];L^{p})
. Furioli, Lemarié-Rieusset and Terraneo
[

extended it to the critical Lebesgue space

for

(see also
[

Recently, Buckmaster and Vicol

\beta

In this paper, we show that unconditional uniqueness of the Navier-Stokes equations (
NSE

fails
for
all
Besov spaces with negative regularity index, particularly including those in the (sub-)critical regime. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first non-uniqueness result in subcritical solution classes.

1.1.
Failure of unconditional uniqueness

Let us briefly recall the definition of Besov spaces. The reader can refer to
[
37
,
2
,
35
]
for more details of the function spaces to be used below.

For any distribution
f\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
, denote by
{\widehat{f}}
the Fourier transform of
f
in
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathds{Z}}^{d})
, given by the formula

{\widehat{f}}(m)=\langle f,e^{-2\pi im\cdot x}\rangle,\quad m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the dual pairing between distributions and smooth functions on
{\mathds{T}}^{d}
. Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathsf{c}}({\mathds{R}}^{d})
be a radial bump function that is supported in
B(0,1)
and equals 1 on
B(0,9/10)
. For any dyadic integer
N\geq 1
, define the
Littlewood-Paley projection
P_{\leq N}
by

P_{\leq N}f(x):=\sum_{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}}\chi(m/N){\widehat{f}}(m)e^{2\pi im\cdot x},

and the companion projections

P_{>N}:=\id-P_{\leq N},\quad P_{1}:=P_{\leq 1},\quad P_{N}:=P_{\leq N}-P_{\leq N/2}\penalty\ \text{for }N\geq 2.

Let
s\in{\mathds{R}}
and
q,r\in[1,\infty]
. The
Besov space

B^{s}_{q,r}
consists of distributions
f\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
for which

\|f\|_{B^{s}_{q,r}}:=\left(\sum_{N}N^{sr}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{q}}^{r}\right)^{1/r}<\infty,

with the canonical modification when
r=\infty
.

Our first theorem asserts that unconditional uniqueness of (
NSE
) fails in
B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
for any
\theta>0
and
d\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.1

(Failure of unconditional uniqueness of (
NSE
))
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\theta>0
, and
q,r\in[1,\infty]
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists initial data
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
with

\|u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}<\epsilon,

such that there exist
T>0
and two mild solutions

u,v\in C([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,r})

to the Navier-Stokes equations (
NSE
) with

u(0)=v(0)=u_{\initial},\text{ but }u(t)\neq v(t)\quad\text{ for all }t\in(0,T].

Combining this result with
[
7
,
33
]
and the work of Sawada
[
34
]
on local well-posedness in subcritical Besov spaces, we conclude that the Cauchy problem of the Navier-Stokes equations (
NSE
) is locally well-posed but not unconditionally well-posed in (sub-)critical Besov spaces with negative regularity index.

1.2.
Fractional Navier-Stokes equations

Similar results also hold for the fractional Navier-Stokes equations

(NSE
α
)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\Div(u\otimes u)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u+\nabla p=0\\
\Div u=0\\
u(0)=u_{\initial},\quad\Div u_{\initial}=0,\end{cases}

where
(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
denotes the fractional Laplacian operator defined on distributions by the formula

(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u(x):=\sum_{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}}(2\pi|m|)^{2\alpha}{\widehat{u}}(m)e^{2\pi im\cdot x},\quad u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d}).

The series converges in the sense of distributions. For a divergence-free distribution
u_{\initial}\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
, we say
u:[0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}^{d}
is a
mild
solution to (
NSEα
) with initial data
u_{\initial}
if it satisfies the integral equation

u(t)=e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{\initial}-B_{\alpha}(u,u)(t)

in
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
. The bilinear operator
B_{\alpha}
is formally given by the integral

(1.1)

B_{\alpha}(u,u)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}{\mathds{P}}\Div(u\otimes u)(s)ds,\quad t\in(0,T).

The scaling property of (
NSEα
) (posed in
{\mathds{R}}^{d}
) reads as

u(t,x)\mapsto u_{\lambda}(t,x):=\lambda^{2\alpha-1}u(\lambda^{2\alpha}t,\lambda x).

The (homogeneous) Besov space
{\dot{B}}^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{R}}^{d})
is critical if

\theta=2\alpha-1-\frac{d}{q}.

When
\alpha>1/2
, there are (sub-)critical Besov spaces with negative regularity index. Our next theorem shows that unconditional uniqueness of (
NSEα
) fails in such spaces.

Theorem 1.2

(Failure of unconditional uniqueness of (
NSEα
))
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\alpha>1/2
. Let
\theta>0
and
q,r\in[1,\infty]
.

(i)

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists initial data
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
with

\|u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}<\epsilon,

such that there exist
T>0
and two mild solutions

u,v\in C([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,r})

to the fractional Navier-Stokes equations (
NSEα
) with

u(0)=v(0)=u_{\initial},\text{ but }u(t)\neq v(t)\quad\text{ for all }t\in(0,T].

(ii)

Moreover, if
\alpha>(d+2)/4
, then the same statements also hold in
L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
for any
1\leq p<2
.

By scaling, when
\alpha>(d+2)/4
,
L^{2}({\mathds{R}}^{d})
becomes subcritical. In this case, Lions
[
26
]
proved global regularity of (
NSEα
), see also
[
23
]
.

However, there are two conflicting scenarios for local well-posedness of (
NSEα
) in Besov spaces with negative regularity index. When
1/2<\alpha\leq 1
, Wu
[
39
]
proved local well-posedness in the critical Besov spaces
{\dot{B}}^{1-2\alpha+\frac{d}{q}}_{q,r}
for
2<q<\infty
. But for
\alpha\geq 1
, (
NSEα
) is
ill-posed
in the
subcritical
Besov spaces
{\dot{B}}^{-\alpha}_{\infty,r}
, as proved by Bourgain and Pavlović
[
4
]
for
\alpha=1
and
r=\infty
, and later extended by the first author and Dai
[
11
]
to all
\alpha\geq 1
and
2<r\leq\infty
. This suggests that

(1.2)

\theta_{\alpha,\infty}:=\sup\{\theta>0:\eqref{e:NSEa}\text{ is locally well-posed in }B^{-\theta}_{\infty,\infty}\}\leq\alpha.

In Proposition
2.1
, we present a result on local well-posedness for all
\alpha>1/2
. In particular, it provides a lower bound on
\theta_{\alpha,\infty}
as

\theta_{\alpha,\infty}\geq\alpha-1/2,

which indicates sharpness of (
1.2
) with respect to the order of
\alpha
as
\alpha\to\infty
.

1.3.
Stationary singular solutions

Our main strategy to prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
is to construct non-trivial
singular
solutions to the stationary fractional Navier-Stokes equations

(NS
α
)

\begin{cases}\Div(u\otimes u)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u+\nabla p=0,\quad x\in{\mathds{T}}^{d}\\
\Div u=0\\
{\widehat{u}}(0)=0.\end{cases}

By singular solutions, we mean solutions that are not
L^{2}
-integrable, for which the tensor product
u\otimes u
does not belong to
L^{1}
. Instead, we define it as a paraproduct in the space of
distributions modulo constants

{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}/{\mathds{C}}
, which identifies with the dual of the space of smooth functions of mean zero.

More precisely, we define the tensor product
u\otimes u
in homogeneous Sobolev spaces with negative regularity index. Recall that for
s\in{\mathds{R}}
, the
homogeneous Sobolev space

{\dot{H}}^{s}
consists of
f\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}/{\mathds{C}}
for which

\|f\|_{{\dot{H}}^{s}}:=\left(\sum_{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}|m|^{2s}|{\widehat{f}}(m)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Let
u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and
s>0
. Then
u\otimes u
is said to be
well-defined as a paraproduct in
{\dot{H}}^{-s}
, if

(1.3)

\sum_{N,M}\left\|P_{N}u\otimes P_{M}u\right\|_{{\dot{H}}^{-s}}<\infty,

where
N
and
M
range over all dyadic integers. Define the paraproduct

(1.4)

u\otimes u:=\sum_{N,M}P_{N}u\otimes P_{M}u\quad\text{ in }{\dot{H}}^{-s}.

Since the series in (
1.4
) converges absolutely in
{\dot{H}}^{-s}
, we have

(1.5)

u\otimes u=\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\leq N}u\otimes P_{\leq N}u\quad\text{ in }{\dot{H}}^{-s}.

In fact, under the condition (
1.3
), one may also use Bony’s paraproduct decomposition to define
u\otimes u
, see, e.g.,
[
3
,
15
,
2
]
.

We also identify
u\otimes u
with a distribution in the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
H^{-s}
, via the canonical embedding
\iota
given by the formula

\iota(f):=\sum_{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}{\widehat{f}}(m)e^{2\pi im\cdot x},\quad f\in{\dot{H}}^{-s}.

Recall that the
inhomogeneous Sobolev space

H^{-s}
consists of distributions
f\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
for which

\|f\|_{H^{-s}}:=\left(\sum_{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}}\langle m\rangle^{-2s}|{\widehat{f}}(m)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty,

where
\langle m\rangle
denotes the Japanese bracket
\langle m\rangle:=(1+|m|^{2})^{1/2}
. When
u\in L^{2}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
, one has

\iota(u\otimes u)=u\otimes u-\fint u\otimes u.

For convenience, we also abbreviate
\iota(u\otimes u)
as
u\otimes u
in
H^{-s}
.

Definition 1.3

(Singular solutions)
.

We say
u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
is a
singular solution to (
NSα
)
if
{\widehat{u}}(0)=0
;
u
is weakly divergence free; there exists
s>0
so that
u\otimes u
is well-defined as a paraproduct in
{\dot{H}}^{-s}
; and for any divergence-free
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
, it holds that

(1.6)

-\left\langle u\otimes u,\nabla\varphi\right\rangle+\langle u,(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\varphi\rangle=0,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the dual pairing between
distributions and smooth functions on
{\mathds{T}}^{d}
.

Note that any stationary weak solution
u\in L^{2}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
to (
NSα
) is indeed a singular solution, and vice versa. It is known that the existence of non-trivial stationary weak solutions to (
NSα
) indicates non-uniqueness of weak solutions to (
NSEα
). For
\alpha=1
, Luo
[
28
]
proved existence of non-trivial stationary weak solutions to (
NSα
) for
d\geq 4
.

The notion of singular solutions, introduced by Lemarié-Rieusset
[
25
]
, allows us to study stationary solutions with infinite energy. The following proposition shows that they correspond to mild solutions to (
NSEα
).

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
be a singular solution to (
NSα
). Then
U:[0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}^{d}
defined by

U(t):=u,\quad t\geq 0,

is a mild solution to (
NSEα
) with initial data
U(0)=u
.

The loss of unconditional uniqueness of mild solutions to the (fractional) Navier-Stokes equations, cf. Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, hence follows from existence of non-trivial stationary singular solutions.

Theorem 1.5

(Existence of non-trivial stationary singular solutions)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\alpha>0
.

(i)

There exist infinitely many singular solutions
u
to the stationary Navier-Stokes equations (
NSα
) so that

u\in\bigcap_{1\leq p<2}L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})\cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta>0\\
q,r\in[1,\infty]\end{subarray}}B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d}).

(ii)

Moreover, if
0<\alpha<(d+1)/4
, then the above statement also holds in
L^{2}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
.

For
\alpha=1
, Lemarié-Rieusset
[
25
]
first proved existence of non-trivial singular solutions to (
NSα
) in
H^{-1}\cap\bmo^{-1}({\mathds{T}}^{2})
. Later, Ashkarian, Bhargava, Gismondi and Novack
[
1
]
extended it to
\bigcap_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}L^{2-\epsilon}\cap{\dot{H}}^{-\epsilon}({\mathds{T}}^{2})
. We demonstrate that convex integration can be used to construct non-trivial solutions in every Besov space with negative regularity index, and, more importantly, for an arbitrarily large power of the Laplacian. It also generalizes the result in
[
28
]
for
\alpha=1
and
d\geq 4
and yields the following non-uniqueness result.

Corollary 1.6

(Non-uniqueness of weak solutions)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
0<\alpha<(d+1)/4
. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists initial data
u_{\initial}\in L^{2}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
with
\|u_{\initial}\|_{L^{2}}<\epsilon
such that there exist two weak solutions

u,v\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);L^{2})\cap C_{\mathsf{w}}([0,\infty);L^{2})

to the fractional Navier-Stokes equations (
NSEα
) with

u(0)=v(0)=u_{\initial},\text{ but }u(t)\neq v(t)\quad\text{ for all }t>0.

We also show that there does not exist non-trivial stationary
weak
solutions to (
NSE
) in an endpoint critical space
B^{-1}_{\infty,1}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
. This improves a result by Lemarié-Rieusset
[
25
]
for
d=2
in
L^{2,1}({\mathds{T}}^{2})
, since
L^{2,1}({\mathds{T}}^{2})
embeds into
L^{2}\cap B^{-1}_{\infty,1}({\mathds{T}}^{2})
.

Theorem 1.7

(Uniqueness of stationary solutions)
.

There does not exist a non-trivial stationary solution
u
to (
NSα
) satisfying

\begin{cases}u\in L^{2}\cap B^{1-2\alpha}_{\infty,1}({\mathds{T}}^{d})&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq 1\\
u\in L^{2}\cap B^{-1}_{q,1}({\mathds{T}}^{d})&\text{ if }1\leq\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}\\
u\in L^{2}({\mathds{T}}^{d})&\text{ if }\alpha>\frac{d+2}{4}\end{cases},

where
q=\frac{d}{2\alpha-2}
.

We emphasize that the Besov spaces imposed in Theorem
1.7
always lie on the critical line.

After the completion of the present paper, we became aware of recent work by Fujii
[
19
]
who constructed nontrivial singular stationary solutions in
critical
Besov spaces with negative smoothness. This also implies non-uniqueness of mild solutions in those spaces. We emphasize that our singular stationary solutions belong to all the Besov spaces with negative smoothness, not only critical ones.

1.4.
Notation

Throughout the paper, without special mention,
N
and
M
are dyadic integers, by which we mean integers that are powers of 2.

We say
X\lesssim Y
(or
X\lesssim_{A}Y
, resp.) if
X\leq CY

(or depending on
A
, resp.).

For any vectors

. For any matrix-valued function

The pair

denotes the dual pairing between distributions and test functions on the prescribed set, which is clear from the context.

2.
Local well-posedness of the fractional Navier-Stokes equations

Let us start with local well-posedness of (
NSEα
) in subcritical spaces.

Proposition 2.1

(Local well-posedness of (
NSEα
))
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\alpha>1/2
. Let
\max\left\{\frac{d}{2\alpha-1},1\right\}<p<\infty
,
\max\left\{\frac{d}{2\alpha-1},1\right\}<q\leq\infty
,
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}(2\alpha-1-\frac{d}{q})
, and
1\leq r<\infty
.

(i)

For any divergence-free
u_{\initial}\in L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and
\max\{p,p^{\prime}\}\leq\rho\leq\infty
, there exist a time
T>0
and a unique mild solution
u
to (
NSEα
) with initial data
u_{\initial}
in the solution class

X_{T}:=\left\{u\in C([0,T];L^{p}):t^{\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\rho})}u(t)\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\rho})\right\}.

The solution map
u_{\initial}\mapsto u
is continuous from
L^{p}
to
C([0,T];L^{p})
.

(ii)

For any divergence-free
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and
\theta+\frac{d}{2q}<\beta<\frac{1}{2}\left(2\alpha-1\right)
, there exist a time
T>0
and a unique mild solution
u
to (
NSEα
) with initial data
u_{\initial}
in the solution class

Y_{T}:=\left\{u\in C([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,r}):t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}u(t)\in L^{\infty}((0,T);B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r})\right\}.

The solution map
u_{\initial}\mapsto u
is continuous from
B^{-\theta}_{q,r}
to
C([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,r})
.

As a consequence, (
NSEα
) is locally well-posed in
L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and
B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
. Moreover, in both cases,
u\in C^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
.

Remark 2.2

(Local well-posedness in endpoint spaces)
.

The proof below also indicates the following results in endpoint spaces.

(1)

For
p=\infty
, (
NSEα
) is locally well-posed in
L^{\infty}
, equipped with the weak*-topology induced by
L^{1}
. The solution class is
C((0,T];L^{\infty})\cap C_{\mathsf{w}}([0,T];L^{\infty})
.

(2)

For
r=\infty
, (
NSEα
) is locally well-posed in
B^{-\theta}_{q,\infty}
, equipped with the weak*-topology induced by
B^{\theta}_{q^{\prime},1}
. The solution class is
\{u\in C_{\mathsf{w}}([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,\infty}):t^{\beta/\alpha}u(t)\in L^{\infty}((0,T);B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,\infty}\}
.

Indeed, the heat semigroup is not necessarily strongly continuous but merely uniformly bounded and weak*-continuous in these endpoint spaces.

Remark 2.3
.

The same statements also hold in
L^{p}({\mathds{R}}^{d})
and
B^{-\theta}_{q,r}({\mathds{R}}^{d})
.

The proof is based on the classical smoothing effects of the heat kernel and the Oseen kernel.

Lemma 2.4

(Smoothing effects)
.

Let
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
and
\beta\geq 0
. Let
f\in L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
. Then for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}f\|_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}\|f\|_{L^{p}},

\displaystyle\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}{\mathds{P}}\Div f\|_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

The reader can refer to
[
29
]
for the proof.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

In what follows, assume
0\leq t\leq T\leq 1
.

First, consider
(i)
. The norm of the solution class
X_{T}
is given by

\|u\|_{X_{T}}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u(t)\|_{L^{p}}+\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\rho})}\|u(t)\|_{L^{\rho}}.

The estimates for the linear part follow from Lemma
2.4
as

\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{\initial}\|_{X_{T}}\lesssim\|u_{\initial}\|_{L^{p}}.

We show that the bilinear operator
B_{\alpha}
defined by (
1.1
) satisfies

(2.1)

\|B_{\alpha}(u,v)\|_{X_{T}}\lesssim T^{1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha p}}\|u\|_{X_{T}}\|v\|_{X_{T}}.

Indeed, as
\rho\geq p^{\prime}
, one has
(u\otimes v)(t)\in L^{\varrho}
for
\frac{1}{\varrho}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{\rho}\leq 1
. Then we apply Lemma
2.4
to get

\displaystyle\|B_{\alpha}(u,v)(t)\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{\varrho}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u\otimes v(s)\|_{L^{\varrho}}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha\rho}}s^{-\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\rho})}\|u(s)\|_{L^{p}}\,s^{\frac{d}{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\rho})}\|v(s)\|_{L^{\rho}}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha p}}\|u\|_{X_{T}}\|v\|_{X_{T}}.

The estimate for the other term in (
2.1
) follows similarly. As
p>\frac{d}{2\alpha-1}
, one has
1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{d}{2\alpha p}>0
, so the desired properties directly follow from the classical Banach fixed-point argument. This proves
(i)
.

Next, we prove
(ii)
. The norm of
Y_{T}
is given by

\|u\|_{Y_{T}}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u(t)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}+\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\|u(t)\|_{B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}}.

The estimates for the linear part also follow from Lemma
2.4
as

\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}+t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\|u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}.

We show that the bilinear operator
B_{\alpha}
satisfies the estimate

(2.2)

\|B_{\alpha}(u,v)\|_{Y_{T}}\lesssim T^{1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}\|v\|_{Y_{T}}.

The desired properties also follow from the fact that
1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}>0
.

To prove (
2.2
), we first apply Lemma
2.4
to get

\|B_{\alpha}(u,v)(t)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\|u\otimes v(s)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}ds.

As
-\theta+2\beta-\frac{d}{q}>\theta
, we infer from Sobolev’s embedding and the paraproduct estimates (see e.g.,
[
35
, Theorem 4.37]
) that

\|u\otimes v(s)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\|u(s)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}\|v(s)\|_{B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}}\lesssim s^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}\|v\|_{Y_{T}}.

As
\alpha>1/2
and
1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}>0
, gathering these estimates gives that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|B_{\alpha}(u,v)(t)\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,r}}\lesssim T^{1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}\|v\|_{Y_{T}}.

The estimate for the other term in (
2.2
) follows similarly. This proves
(ii)
.

Finally, we show that
u\in C^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
. By Sobolev’s embedding, we may only consider the case
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{q,r}
. For any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
and
t\geq 2\epsilon
, we infer from the identity

u(t)=e^{-(t-\epsilon)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u(\epsilon)-\int_{\epsilon}^{t}e^{-(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}{\mathds{P}}\Div(u\otimes u)(s)ds

and Lemma
2.4
that

\|u(t)\|_{B^{-\theta+4\beta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\epsilon^{-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);B^{-\theta}_{q,r})}+\int_{\epsilon}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\|u\otimes u(s)\|_{B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}}ds.

Since
-\theta+2\beta>\frac{d}{q}
,
B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}
is a multiplication algebra (see e.g.,
[
37
, Theorem 2.8.3]
), so one has

\|u\otimes u(s)\|_{B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\|u(s)\|_{B^{-\theta+2\beta}_{q,r}}^{2}\lesssim s^{-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}^{2}.

As
\beta<(2\alpha-1)/2
and
t\leq 1
, gathering these estimates gives that

\|u(t)\|_{B^{-\theta+4\beta}_{q,r}}\lesssim\epsilon^{-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}+\epsilon^{-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha}}\|u\|_{Y_{T}}^{2}.

So
u\in L^{\infty}((2\epsilon,T);B^{-\theta+4\beta}_{q,r})
. By iteration and arbitrariness of
\epsilon
, we know that
u(t)
is smooth for any
0<t<T
, and hence
u\in C^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
by iteratively taking time derivatives on both sides of the equation
\partial_{t}u=-\Div(u\otimes u)-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u-\nabla p
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.5

(Unconditional uniqueness)
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\alpha>1/2
, and

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{2\alpha-1}<p\leq\infty&\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}\\
2\leq p\leq\infty&\text{ if }\alpha>\frac{d+2}{4}\end{cases}.

Then for every divergence-free
u_{\initial}\in L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and every
T>0
, there exists at most one mild solution to (
NSEα
) in
C([0,T];L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d}))
.

Proof.

The condition on
p
ensures that
p\geq p^{\prime}
and
p>\frac{d}{2\alpha-1}
. When
p<\infty
, the statement hence follows from Proposition
2.1

(i)
by taking
\rho=p
.
When
p=\infty
, the conclusion holds by embedding of
L^{\infty}
into
L^{\tilde{p}}
for
\tilde{p}<\infty
.
∎

3.
Stationary singular solutions and mild solutions

In this section, we prove Proposition
1.4
. As an application, we show uniqueness of solutions to (
NSα
) in subcritical Lebesgue spaces.

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

Let
u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
be a singular solution to (
NSα
), for which
u\otimes u
is well-defined as a paraproduct in
{\dot{H}}^{-s}
for some
s>0
. It is clear that
U\in C([0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\cap C^{\infty}((0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
with
U(0)=u
. Using the identity
U(t,x)=\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(t)\otimes u(x)
as a tensor product of distributions on
(0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}
, we have

\partial_{t}U+{\mathds{P}}\Div(U\otimes U)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}U=0\quad\text{ in }{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}).

Indeed, for any divergence-free
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathsf{c}}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
, one has

-\langle U,\partial_{t}\phi\rangle-\langle U\otimes U,\nabla\phi\rangle+\langle U,(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\phi\rangle=-\langle u\otimes u,\nabla\overline{\phi}\rangle+\langle u,(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\overline{\phi}\rangle=0,

where
\overline{\phi}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t,x)dt\in C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
.

Moreover, as
{\mathds{P}}\Div(U\otimes U)\in C([0,\infty);H^{-s-1})
, we know that

v(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}{\mathds{P}}\Div(U\otimes U)(s)ds,\quad t>0

satisfies the equation

\partial_{t}v+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}v={\mathds{P}}\Div(U\otimes U)\quad\text{ in }{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d}).

By Fourier transform, one gets
v\in C([0,\infty);H^{-s-1+2\alpha})\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);H^{-s-1})
with
v(0)=0
.

Write
w=U+v
. It satisfies
\partial_{t}w+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w=0
in
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
, and
w\in C([0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
with
w(0)=u
. Hence, we infer from uniqueness of heat solutions in the class
C([0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
that
w(t)=e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u
, and thus,

U(t)=w(t)-v(t)=e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}{\mathds{P}}\Div(U\otimes U)(s)ds

in
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
as desired. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.1

(Uniqueness of stationary solutions)
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\alpha>1/2
, and

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{2\alpha-1}<p\leq\infty&\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}\\
2\leq p\leq\infty&\text{ if }\alpha>\frac{d+2}{4}\end{cases}.

If
u\in L^{p}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
is a singular solution to (
NSα
), then
u
is zero.

Proof.

Define
U(t):=u
for
t\geq 0
. Proposition
1.4
yields that
U\in C([0,\infty);L^{p})
is a mild solution to (
NSEα
) with
U(0)=u
. Moreover, we infer from unconditional uniqueness (cf. Corollary
2.5
) and Proposition
2.1
that
U\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})
. So
u\in C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
. Therefore, testing the stationary equation (
NSα
) against
u
itself gives

\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u\cdot u=\int(u\otimes u):\nabla u=0.

Since
u
is of mean zero, we conclude that
u=0
.
∎

4.
Iterative procedure

The proofs of the main theorems are based on the following iterative procedure. Consider the stationary fractional Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system:

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\Div(u\otimes u)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u+\nabla p=\Div R\\
\Div u=0\\
{\widehat{u}}(0)=0\end{cases}

where
R
is defined on
{\mathds{T}}^{d}
with values in the space of symmetric matrices, called the
Reynolds stress
. Here, we do not require that
R
is trace-free. Indeed, if
(u,R,p)
is a solution to (
4.1
), then by taking

{\mathring{R}}:=R-\frac{1}{d}\tr(R){\mathds{I}},\quad{\mathring{p}}:=p-\frac{1}{d}\tr(R),

one gets another solution
(u,{\mathring{R}},{\mathring{p}})
to (
4.1
) so that
{\mathring{R}}
is trace-free. Moreover, the normalized pressure
p
is uniquely determined by the equation

\Delta p=\Div\Div(R-u\otimes u),\quad\fint p=0.

For simplicity, we abbreviate a solution triple
(u,R,p)
to (
4.1
) as
(u,R)
.

Proposition 4.1

(Main iteration)
.

There exists
s>0
depending only on
d
and
\alpha
such that the following holds.

Fix any
1<p<2
,
0<\theta<1
,
\delta>0
, and a dyadic integer
N
. Given a smooth solution
(u,R)
to (
4.1
) with
\supp{\widehat{u}}\subset B(0,N)
, there exists another smooth solution
({\overline{u}},{\overline{R}})
such that

(4.2)

\|{\overline{R}}\|_{H^{-s}}<\delta,

and moreover, the velocity perturbation
w:={\overline{u}}-u
satisfies

(i)

The Fourier support of
w
is away from that of
u
, in the sense that there exists a dyadic number
D>50N
so that

\supp{\widehat{w}}\subset\left\{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}:\frac{1}{2}DN<|m|<\frac{9}{10}DN\right\};

(ii)

The
L^{p}
-estimate and the
B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}
-estimate

\|w\|_{L^{p}}<\delta,\quad\|w\|_{B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}}<\delta;

(iii)

The paraproduct estimate

\|w\otimes w\|_{{\dot{H}}^{-s}}+\sum_{M\leq 2N}\left(\|P_{M}u\otimes w\|_{{\dot{H}}^{-s}}+\|w\otimes P_{M}u\|_{{\dot{H}}^{-s}}\right)<\delta+\|R\|_{H^{-s}}.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
is deferred to Sections
6
,
7
, and
8
. The next proposition is concerned with the iteration in the
L^{2}
-case.

Proposition 4.2

(
L^{2}
-iteration)
.

Let
0<\alpha<\frac{d+1}{4}
. Then there exists a constant
A\geq 1
, depending only on
d
and
\alpha
, such that the following holds.

For any
\delta>0
, given a smooth solution
(u,R)
to (
4.1
), there exists another smooth solution
({\overline{u}},{\overline{R}})
such that

(4.3)

\|{\overline{R}}\|_{L^{1}}<\delta^{2},

and moreover, the velocity perturbation
w:={\overline{u}}-u
satisfies

(4.4)

\|w\|_{L^{2}}<A\|R\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}+\delta.

The proof of Proposition
4.2
is presented in Section
9
.

and

, let us first prove Theorem

.

5.1.1.
Proof of

Pick an arbitrary non-zero smooth divergence-free vector field

with

.

Denote by
{\mathcal{R}}
the anti-divergence operator (introduced in
[
17

) from smooth vector fields

to the space of symmetric trace-free

matrices, given by the formula

({\mathcal{R}}f)_{i,j}:={\mathcal{R}}_{i,j,k}f_{k},\quad f\in C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d};{\mathds{R}}^{d}),

where

{\mathcal{R}}_{i,j,k}:=\frac{2-d}{d-1}\Delta^{-2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}-\frac{1}{d-1}\Delta^{-1}\partial_{k}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{i}\delta_{j,k}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{j}\delta_{i,k}.

Here,
\delta_{i,j}

Note that

is the unique solution

to the equation

\Delta w=f-\fint f,\quad\fint w=0.

Direct computation shows that

\Div{\mathcal{R}}f=f-\fint f.

Define

\|R_{n-1}\|_{L^{1}}<\delta_{n-1}^{2}
, one has

\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}}<2^{-(n-1)}+A^{-1}2^{-n}<2^{-n+2},

so
(u_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}
. Denote by
u
the limit of
(u_{n})
. Then we have

u_{n}\to u\text{ in }L^{2},\quad u_{n}\otimes u_{n}\to u\otimes u\text{ in }L^{1},\quad R_{n}\to 0\text{ in }L^{1}.

We thus conclude that
u\in L^{2}
is a singular (hence weak) solution to (
NSα
). One can mimic the above proof to construct infinitely many solutions in
L^{2}
by taking a sequence of
u_{0}^{(i)}
. This completes the proof.

5.2.
Proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
admitting Proposition
4.1

We prove Theorem
1.2
. Theorem
1.1
corresponds to the case
\alpha=1
. Let us start with
(i)
. Note that the Besov space
B^{-\theta}_{q,1}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
embeds into
B^{-\theta_{1}}_{q_{1},r_{1}}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
when
-\theta_{1}\leq-\theta
,
-\theta_{1}-\frac{d}{q_{1}}\leq-\theta-\frac{d}{q}
, and
1\leq r_{1}\leq\infty
. Therefore, it suffices to prove the statements for

\max\left\{\frac{d}{2\alpha-1},2\right\}<q<\infty,\quad 0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}\left(2\alpha-1-\frac{d}{q}\right),\quad r=1.

We first construct the initial data
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{q,1}
. Let
\epsilon>0
. Pick a non-zero smooth divergence-free vector field
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}
with
\supp{\widehat{u}}_{0}\subset{\mathds{S}}^{d-1}\cap{\mathds{Z}}^{d}
and

\|u_{0}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,1}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\epsilon/4.

A verbatim adaptation of the iteration procedure in the proof of Theorem
1.5
with the new parameters

\delta_{n}:=2^{-n-2}\epsilon,\quad p_{n}=3/2,\quad\theta_{n}=\theta,\quad\text{ for }n\geq 1

gives a sequence of smooth solutions
(u_{n},R_{n})
to the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system (
4.1
). Moreover,
(u_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}
and converges to a singular solution
u_{\initial}\in B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}
to the stationary Navier-Stokes equations (

) with

\|u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{q,1}}\leq\|u_{\initial}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{B^{-\theta}_{\infty,1}}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\delta_{n}<\epsilon.

By Fourier support separation in Proposition
4.1
, each perturbation
w_{n}
only contains high frequencies, so

{\widehat{u}}_{\initial}(m)={\widehat{u}}_{0}(m)
for
|m|=1
. Hence
u_{\initial}\neq 0
.

Then we construct two different mild solutions
u
and
v
to (
NSEα
) with the same initial data
u_{\initial}
. Indeed, Proposition
1.4
yields that

u(t):=u_{\initial},\quad t\geq 0

is a mild solution to (
NSEα
), and particularly,
u\in C([0,\infty);B^{-\theta}_{q,1})
. Meanwhile, Proposition
2.1
says that there exist
T>0
and a mild solution

v\in C([0,T];B^{-\theta}_{q,1})\cap C^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathds{T}}^{d})

to (
NSEα
) with
v(0)=u_{\initial}
.

Observe that

. Otherwise, Corollary
3.1
implies that

u(t)=u_{\initial}\neq v(t)
for any

(i)
.

Statement

follows from a verbatim adaptation of the above argument with a slightly larger exponent.

Apply the iteration with parameters

Then we get a singular solution

Since

.

for any

.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

For
\alpha>\frac{d+2}{4}
, this is Corollary
3.1
. In what follows, we only consider
\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}
. The proof is based on estimates of the energy flux from high to low modes, introduced by
[
9
]
. For any dyadic integer
N\geq 2
, we decompose
u
as

u=u_{\leq N}+u_{>N},\quad\text{ where }\penalty\ u_{\leq N}:=P_{\leq N}u\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ u_{>N}:=P_{>N}u.

Testing the stationary equation against
P_{\leq N}^{2}u
(equivalently, applying
P_{\leq N}
to (
NSE
) and then testing against
u_{\leq N}
), and using that
P_{\leq N}
commutes with
\nabla
and preserves divergence-free vector fields, we obtain

(5.8)

\Pi_{\leq N}:=\int|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u_{\leq N}|^{2}=\int P_{\leq N}(u\otimes u):\nabla u_{\leq N}.

We now exploit the skew-symmetry of the transport term. Introduce the commutator

\displaystyle r_{\leq N}(u,u)(x)

\displaystyle:=P_{\leq N}(u\otimes u)(x)-u_{\leq N}\otimes u_{\leq N}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\check{\chi}_{N}(y)\,\bigl(u(x-y)-u_{\leq N}(x)\bigr)\otimes\bigl(u(x-y)-u_{\leq N}(x)\bigr)\,dy,

where
\check{\chi}_{N}
denotes the kernel of
P_{\leq N}
(i.e., the inverse Fourier transform of
m\mapsto\chi(m/N)
).
Since
\Div u_{\leq N}=0
, we have the exact cancellation

\int(u_{\leq N}\otimes u_{\leq N}):\nabla u_{\leq N}=0,

and hence, from (
5.8
),

(5.9)

\Pi_{\leq N}=\int r_{\leq N}(u,u):\nabla u_{\leq N}

Then the proof is divided into two cases:
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
1<\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}
.

Case 1:
0<\alpha\leq 1

Bernstein’s inequality yields that

\|\nabla u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\sum_{M\leq N}M\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{\infty}}=N^{2\alpha}\sum_{M\leq N}\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)^{2\alpha}M^{1-2\alpha}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{\infty}}=:N^{2\alpha}b_{N}.

Next, we show that

(5.10)

\|r_{\leq N}(u,u)\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim N^{-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}+\sum_{M>N}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Indeed, by Minkowski’s inequality, one has

\|r_{\leq N}(u,u)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\int|\check{\chi}_{N}(y)|\,\|u(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy.

Using the decomposition
u=u_{\leq N}+u_{>N}
, we have
u(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}=\bigl(u_{\leq N}(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}\bigr)+u_{>N}(\cdot-y)
, and therefore,

\|u(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|u_{\leq N}(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u_{>N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Using the identity
u_{\leq N}=\sum_{M\leq N}P_{M}u
and the mean value estimate

\|P_{M}u(\cdot-y)-P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim|y|\,\|\nabla P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim|y|\,M\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}},

we infer that

\|u_{\leq N}(\cdot-y)-u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim|y|^{2}\sum_{M\leq N}M^{2}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim|y|^{2}N^{2-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}.

Thus, (
5.10
) follows from the kernel bounds
\int|\check{\chi}_{N}(y)|\,|y|^{2}\,dy\lesssim N^{-2}
and
\int|\check{\chi}_{N}(y)|\,dy\lesssim 1
.
Applying Hölder’s inequality to (
5.9
) and gathering the above estimates gives that

(5.11)

N^{-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}\leq Cb_{N}\,N^{-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}+Cb_{N}\sum_{M>N}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where the constant
C>0
only depends on
d
.

Let us exploit the smallness of
b_{N}
. For a reference dyadic number
N_{0}\geq 2
, Fubini’s theorem yields that

\displaystyle\sum_{N\geq N_{0}}b_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M}\sum_{N\geq\max\{M,N_{0}\}}\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)^{2\alpha}M^{1-2\alpha}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{M}\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{M}{N_{0}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right\}M^{1-2\alpha}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{\infty}}=:B_{N_{0}}.

Since
\sum_{M}M^{1-2\alpha}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|u\|_{B^{1-2\alpha}_{\infty,1}}<\infty
, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem yields that

(5.12)

\lim_{N_{0}\to\infty}B_{N_{0}}=0.

Pick
N_{0}
large enough so that
CB_{N_{0}}\leq 1/2
. In particular, for any
N\geq N_{0}
, as
Cb_{N}\leq CB_{N_{0}}\leq 1/2
, we hence get from (
5.11
) that

(5.13)

N^{-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}\lesssim b_{N}\sum_{M>N}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then we show that the energy concentrated on high modes is null. Indeed, using (
5.13
), we get that for
N_{1}\geq N_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{N>N_{1}}\|P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{N>N_{1}}N^{-2\alpha}\Pi_{\leq N}\lesssim\sum_{N>N_{1}}b_{N}\sum_{M>N}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{M>N_{1}}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\sum_{N>N_{1}}b_{N}\lesssim B_{N_{1}}\sum_{N>N_{1}}\|P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where the implicit constant also depends only on
d
. Thanks to (
5.12
), for large enough
N_{1}
, we conclude that

\sum_{N>N_{1}}\|P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0,\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad u_{>N_{1}}=0.

Therefore,
u
is smooth. Testing (
NSα
) against
u
and using
\Div u=0
, we get

\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int(u\otimes u):\nabla u=0.

Hence
u
is constant, and the mean-zero condition implies
u=0
. This concludes the case
\alpha\leq 1
.

Case 2:
1<\alpha\leq\frac{d+2}{4}

The proof is a verbatim adaptation of that of Case 1. We just list the main modifications and details are left to the reader. Denote by
p
the Hölder conjugate of
q=\frac{d}{2\alpha-2}
. Applying Hölder’s inequality to (
5.9
) gives that

\Pi_{\leq N}\leq\|\nabla u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{q}}\|r_{\leq N}(u,u)\|_{L^{p}}.

Using Bernstein’s inequality again, one gets that

\|\nabla u_{\leq N}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim N^{2}\sum_{M\leq N}\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)^{2}M^{-1}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{q}}=:N^{2}b_{N}.

Also, write
B_{N_{0}}:=\sum_{N\geq N_{0}}b_{N}
. Using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem again, we get from the condition
\|u\|_{B^{-1}_{q,1}}<\infty
that

\lim_{N_{0}\to\infty}B_{N_{0}}=0.

Next, using the same reasoning as that of (
5.10
), one gets that

\displaystyle\|r_{\leq N}(u,u)\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-2}\sum_{M\leq N}M^{2}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2p}}^{2}+\sum_{M>N}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2p}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-2}\Pi_{\leq N}+\sum_{M>N}M^{2\alpha-2}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we use the embedding
B^{0}_{2p,2}\hookrightarrow L^{2p}
as
p\geq 1
.

Combining these estimates, we infer that for large enough
N
,

N^{-2}\Pi_{\leq N}\lesssim b_{N}\sum_{M>N}M^{2\alpha-2}\|P_{M}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Repeating the above arguments, we get that for large enough
N_{0}
,

\sum_{N>N_{0}}N^{2\alpha-2}\|P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim B_{N_{0}}\sum_{N>N_{0}}N^{2\alpha-2}\|P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
B_{N_{0}}
vanishes as
N_{0}\to\infty
, we obtain that
u_{>N_{0}}=0
, and hence
u
is smooth. Corollary
3.1
thus implies that
u=0
.

This completes the proof.

6.
Convex integration

In this section, we construct the velocity perturbation
w
in Proposition
4.1
via convex integration. We use Mikado flows introduced by Daneri and Székelyhidi in
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
as building blocks of our convex integration scheme, see also
[
5
,
12
,
1
]
. In what follows, we fix

\boxed{\theta\in(0,1),\quad e:=2\left\lceil\theta^{-1}\right\rceil>2}.

6.1.
Mikado flows

Let us start with a geometric lemma by De Lellis and Székelyhidi
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
, which originated from Nash
[
32
]
.

Denote by
B_{1/2}({\mathds{I}})
the ball of positive definite symmetric
d\times d
matrices (with respect to the matrix norm), centered at the identity matrix
{\mathds{I}}
, of radius
1/2
. There exist a finite set
\Lambda\subset{\mathds{Z}}^{d}
and smooth functions
\Gamma_{k}:B_{1/2}({\mathds{I}})\to{\mathds{R}}
for
k\in\Lambda
such that

R=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\Gamma_{k}^{2}(R)k\otimes k,\quad\forall R\in B_{1/2}({\mathds{I}}).

For
k\in\Lambda
, pick
p_{k}\in(0,1)^{d}
and denote by
\ell_{k}
the line passing through
p_{k}
in the direction
k
in
{\mathds{T}}^{d}
, i.e.,
\ell_{k}=\{p_{k}+tk\in{\mathds{T}}^{d}:t\in{\mathds{R}}\}
. One may adjust
p_{k}
so that
p_{k}\notin\ell_{-k}
when both
k
and
-k
lie in
\Lambda
.

Let
\mu\geq 1
be a concentration parameter. Let
\psi:[1/2,1]\to{\mathds{R}}
be a non-zero smooth function such that
\psi_{k}:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}
, defined by

\psi_{k}(x):=c_{k}\mu^{\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}\psi(\mu\dist(x,\ell_{k})),

with
c_{k}>0
a normalization constant, satisfies that

\fint\psi_{k}=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\fint\psi_{k}^{2}=1.

Define the
Mikado flows

W_{k}:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}^{d}
for
k\in\Lambda
by

W_{k}(x):=\psi_{k}(x)k.

Notice that
W_{k}
is of mean zero, divergence-free, and satisfies

\fint W_{k}\otimes W_{k}=k\otimes k.

Also,
W_{k}
is a solution to the pressureless stationary Euler equation, namely,
\Div(W_{k}\otimes W_{k})=0
. Moreover, define the anti-symmetric tensor
\Omega_{k}:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to\mat_{d}({\mathds{R}})
by

\Omega_{k}:=k\otimes\nabla\Delta^{-1}\psi_{k}-\nabla\Delta^{-1}\psi_{k}\otimes k.

Using
(k\cdot\nabla)\psi_{k}=0
and
\fint\psi_{k}=0
, we get

\Div\Omega_{k}=\Div(\nabla\Delta^{-1}\psi_{k})k-(k\cdot\nabla)\nabla\Delta^{-1}\psi_{k}=\psi_{k}k-0=W_{k}.

We collect estimates of the Mikado flows in the following lemma. The reader can refer to
[
12
, Theorem 4.3]
for the proof.

Lemma 6.1

(Estimates on the Mikado flows)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Then for any
m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}W_{k}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{m}\mu^{m}\mu^{(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})},

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}\Omega_{k}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{m}\mu^{m}\mu^{-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

Moreover, for
l\in\Lambda
but
l\neq k
, it holds that

\|W_{k}\otimes W_{l}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\mu^{d-1-\frac{d}{p}}.

6.2.
Velocity perturbation

The main point of our construction is to localize the Fourier transform of the velocity perturbation, while also keeping intermittency. To this end, we define the principal part of the perturbation
w^{(p)}
by

w^{(p)}(x):=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}(x)W_{k}(\gamma x)\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x),

where
\gamma,\sigma_{k}\geq 1
are oscillation parameters, and
k^{\perp}\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
is a lattice point that is perpendicular to
k
with
|k^{\perp}|\leq|k|
. The amplitude function
a_{k}:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}
is given by

a_{k}(x):=(8d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}})^{1/2}\,\Gamma_{k}\left({\mathds{I}}-\frac{R(x)}{4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right),

in case
R(x)
is not identically zero. If
R\equiv 0
, set
a_{k}\equiv 0
for all
k\in\Lambda
. Note that
{\mathds{I}}-\frac{R(x)}{4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}}
lies in
B_{1/2}({\mathds{I}})
. The role of
\sigma_{k}
is to ensure that all
\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}
lie in the same dyadic shell that is sufficiently far away from the origin, particularly when
k^{\perp}
are not of the same length, as we shall see in Lemma
6.2
.

6.2.1.
Localization corrector

We define the frequency localization corrector
w^{(l)}
with respect to a dyadic integer
\lambda\geq 1
by

\displaystyle w^{(l)}(x):=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}(x)P_{>\lambda}(W_{k}(\gamma\cdot))(x)\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\in\Lambda}P_{>\lambda}a_{k}(x)P_{\leq\lambda}(W_{k}(\gamma\cdot))(x)\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x).

Thus,

(6.1)

w^{(p)}+w^{(l)}=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}P_{\leq\lambda}(W_{k}(\gamma\cdot))\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x).

Given a dyadic integer
\lambda\geq 1
, the following arithmetic lemma determines
\sigma_{k}
so that all
\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}
lie in the same dyadic shell.

Lemma 6.2

(Arithmetic lemma)
.

For any dyadic integer
\lambda\geq 1
, there exist integers
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\Lambda}
and a dyadic number
\sigma>50\lambda^{e}
so that

\bigcup_{k\in\Lambda}\big(B(\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},2\lambda)\cup B(-\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},2\lambda)\big)\subset\left\{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}:\frac{1}{2}\sigma<|m|<\frac{9}{10}\sigma\right\}.

Proof.

Write
c_{\Lambda}:=\prod_{k\in\Lambda}|k^{\perp}|
. We claim that it suffices to verify that there exist an integer
b\geq 45\lambda^{e}
and a dyadic number
\sigma
such that

(6.2)

\frac{11}{9}bc_{\Lambda}<\sigma<\frac{9}{5}bc_{\Lambda}.

Indeed, suppose (
6.2
) holds. Then pick
\sigma_{k}:=\left\lceil\nicefrac{{bc_{\Lambda}}}{{|k^{\perp}|}}\right\rceil
. As
b\geq 45\lambda^{e}>40\lambda
, we have
2\lambda<bc_{\Lambda}/20
, and moreover,

\left||\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}|-bc_{\Lambda}\right|\leq|k^{\perp}|\leq c_{\Lambda}<bc_{\Lambda}/20
. Hence, we have

\frac{1}{2}\sigma<\frac{9}{10}bc_{\Lambda}<|\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}|-2\lambda<|\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}|+2\lambda<\frac{11}{10}bc_{\Lambda}<\frac{9}{10}\sigma.

As
b\geq 45\lambda^{e}
, we have
\sigma>55c_{\Lambda}\lambda^{e}>50\lambda^{e}
as desired.

Let us finish by proving (
6.2
). Consider the translation map
T
on
{\mathds{T}}\simeq{\mathds{R}}/{\mathds{Z}}
defined by

T(x):=x+\log_{2}(3\lambda^{e})\mod 1.

Since
\lambda
is dyadic,
\log_{2}(3\lambda^{e})=\log_{2}3+m
for some integer
m
, hence it is irrational. The (positive) orbit of any
x\in{\mathds{T}}
,
\{T^{n}x:n\geq 0\}
, is dense in
{\mathds{T}}
. In particular, there exists
l\geq 1
such that
T^{l}(\log_{2}(55c_{\Lambda}))\in\left(1-\log_{2}\left(81/55\right),1\right)
. Equivalently, there exists an integer
j\geq 1
with

\log_{2}(55c_{\Lambda})+l\log_{2}(3\lambda^{e})<j<\log_{2}(81c_{\Lambda})+l\log_{2}(3\lambda^{e}).

Then (
6.2
) follows by taking
\sigma=2^{j}
and
b=45(3\lambda^{e})^{l}
.
∎

6.2.2.
Divergence-free corrector

Since
w^{(p)}+w^{(l)}
is not divergence-free, we need to introduce the divergence-free corrector
w^{(d)}
. Write

\Psi_{k}:=P_{\leq\lambda}(\psi_{k}(\gamma\cdot))\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x),

and

{\mathbf{W}}_{k}:=\Psi_{k}k=P_{\leq\lambda}(W_{k}(\gamma\cdot))\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x).

Define the anti-symmetric tensor
\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
by

\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}:=k\otimes\nabla\Delta^{-1}\Psi_{k}-\nabla\Delta^{-1}\Psi_{k}\otimes k,

and the divergence-free corrector
w^{(d)}
by

w^{(d)}:=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\nabla P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}:\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}.

Lemma 6.3
.

Given
\lambda,\mu,\gamma\geq 1
and
\sigma_{k}\geq 1
as in Lemma
6.2
, the velocity perturbation

(6.3)

w:=w^{(p)}+w^{(l)}+w^{(d)}

is divergence-free, and there exists a dyadic integer
\sigma>50\lambda^{e}
so that

(6.4)

\supp{\widehat{w}}\subset\left\{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}:\frac{1}{2}\sigma<|m|<\frac{9}{10}\sigma\right\}.

Proof.

We first verify that
w
is divergence-free. Using
(k\cdot\nabla)\psi_{k}=0
and
k\cdot k^{\perp}=0
, we get
(k\cdot\nabla)\Psi_{k}=0
and hence,

\Div\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}=\Div(\nabla\Delta^{-1}\Psi_{k})k-(k\cdot\nabla)\nabla\Delta^{-1}\Psi_{k}=\Psi_{k}k-0={\mathbf{W}}_{k}.

Here, we use the fact that
\Psi_{k}
is of mean zero, as
\supp\widehat{\Psi}_{k}\subset B(\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},\lambda)\cup B(-\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},\lambda)
is away from 0. Thus, we get

w=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}{\mathbf{W}}_{k}+\nabla P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}:\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}=\Div\sum_{k\in\Lambda}P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}.

Since
P_{\leq\lambda}a_{k}\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
is anti-symmetric, we obtain
\Div w=0
.

Next, to prove (
6.4
), we notice that

\supp{\widehat{w}}\subset\bigcup_{k\in\Lambda}\Big(B(\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},2\lambda)\cup B(-\sigma_{k}k^{\perp},2\lambda)\Big)=:{\mathfrak{S}}.

Indeed, we get from (
6.1
) that the Fourier transform of
w^{(p)}+w^{(l)}
is supported in
{\mathfrak{S}}
. Moreover,
\widehat{\Psi}_{k}
is supported in

, and so is
\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}
. Thus, the Fourier transform of

is supported in
{\mathfrak{S}}
, and so is that of

. Then (
6.4
) follows from Lemma

as

\supp{\widehat{w}}\subset{\mathfrak{S}}\subset\left\{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}:\frac{1}{2}\sigma<|m|<\frac{9}{10}\sigma\right\}.

This completes the proof.

6.3.
Reynolds stress

To make
({\overline{u}},{\overline{R}})
a solution to (
4.1
), the corrector
w={\overline{u}}-u
and the new Reynolds stress
{\overline{R}}
should satisfy the equation

(6.5)

\Div{\overline{R}}=\Div(R+w\otimes w)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u)+\nabla P

for certain pressure
P\in C^{\infty}
.

Given
w
defined by (
6.3
), let us construct
{\overline{R}}
. First, consider the non-linear term. Denote by
w^{(c)}
the full corrector

w^{(c)}:=w^{(l)}+w^{(d)}.

So
w=w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}
and

R+w\otimes w=R+w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(c)}+w^{(c)}\otimes w.

Define the correction error
R_{\mathrm{cor}}
by

R_{\mathrm{cor}}:=w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(c)}+w^{(c)}\otimes w.

Then we decompose the tensor
w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}
to the diagonal part and the off-diagonal part as

\displaystyle R+w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}

\displaystyle\quad=R+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}(1+\cos(4\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x))W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in\Lambda\\
k\neq l\end{subarray}}a_{k}a_{l}\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x)\cos(2\pi\sigma_{l}l^{\perp}\cdot x)W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{l}(\gamma x)

Define the off-diagonal error
R_{\mathrm{off}}
by

R_{\mathrm{off}}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in\Lambda\\
k\neq l\end{subarray}}a_{k}a_{l}\cos(2\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x)\cos(2\pi\sigma_{l}l^{\perp}\cdot x)W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{l}(\gamma x),

and the distortion error
R_{\mathrm{dis}}
by

R_{\mathrm{dis}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}\cos(4\pi\sigma_{k}k^{\perp}\cdot x)W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x).

We further decompose the tensor
W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x)
to the average part and the oscillation part. Since
\fint W_{k}\otimes W_{k}=k\otimes k
, we obtain

\displaystyle R+w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}

\displaystyle=R+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}k\otimes k

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}\left(W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x)-\fint W_{k}\otimes W_{k}\right)+R_{\mathrm{dis}}+R_{\mathrm{off}}.

Define the oscillation error
R_{\mathrm{osc}}
by

R_{\mathrm{osc}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}\left(W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x)-\fint W_{k}\otimes W_{k}\right).

Moreover, one has

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}k\otimes k=4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\Gamma_{k}^{2}\left({\mathds{I}}-\frac{R}{4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)k\otimes k=4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}{\mathds{I}}-R.

Therefore, we obtain

(6.6)

R+w\otimes w=4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}{\mathds{I}}+R_{\mathrm{osc}}+R_{\mathrm{dis}}+R_{\mathrm{off}}+R_{\mathrm{cor}}.

Finally, we define the linear error
R_{\mathrm{lin}}
by

R_{\mathrm{lin}}:={\mathcal{R}}((-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u)),

and the new Reynolds stress
{\overline{R}}
by

(6.7)

{\overline{R}}:=R_{\mathrm{osc}}+R_{\mathrm{dis}}+R_{\mathrm{off}}+R_{\mathrm{cor}}+R_{\mathrm{lin}}.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(u,R)
be a smooth solution to (
4.1
) with the pressure
p
. Then for any
\lambda,\mu,\gamma\geq 1
,
({\overline{u}},{\overline{R}})
is another smooth solution to (
4.1
) with the same pressure
p
, where the velocity perturbation
w:={\overline{u}}-u
and
{\overline{R}}
are defined by (
6.3
) and (
6.7
).

Proof.

First, we prove that
{\overline{R}}
is symmetric. Indeed, (
6.6
) implies that
{\overline{R}}-R_{\mathrm{lin}}=R+w\otimes w-4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}{\mathds{I}}
is symmetric. By definition of the anti-divergence operator
{\mathcal{R}}
,
R_{\mathrm{lin}}
is also symmetric, and so is
{\overline{R}}
.

Next, we show that
{\overline{R}}
satisfies (
6.5
) with
P=0
. By (
6.6
), we get

\displaystyle\Div{\overline{R}}

\displaystyle=\Div(R+w\otimes w-4d^{2}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}{\mathds{I}})+\Div R_{\mathrm{lin}}

\displaystyle=\Div(R+w\otimes w)+\Div{\mathcal{R}}((-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u))

\displaystyle=\Div(R+w\otimes w)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u).

The last equality holds thanks to (

(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u)
is of mean zero.
∎

Let us start with a lemma on boundedness of the Littlewood-Paley projections on

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. For any
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d})
and any dyadic
N
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|P_{\leq N}f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}},

\displaystyle\|P_{>N}f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}}.

The first inequality is a direct consequence of Bernstein’s inequality (see e.g.,

\displaystyle\|P_{>N}f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{M\geq 2N}\|P_{M}f\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\sum_{M\geq 2N}M^{-1}\|\nabla P_{M}f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{M\geq 2N}M^{-1}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim N^{-1}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}}.\qed

Proposition 7.2

(Estimates on
w
)
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\theta\in{\mathds{R}}
. There exists a constant

depending only on
(u,R)

(7.1)

(7.2)

\displaystyle\|w^{(l)}\|_{L^{\infty}}

(7.3)

First, (

For (

∎

Proposition 7.3

(Estimates on

)
.

∎

9.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

The proof resembles the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
We introduce a cut-off function
\zeta:\mat_{d}({\mathds{R}})\to[0,\infty)
which is increasing with respect to the matrix norm
|\cdot|
and satisfies

\zeta(X):=\begin{cases}4\|R\|_{L^{1}}&\text{ if }|X|\leq\|R\|_{L^{1}}\\
4|X|&\text{ if }|X|\geq 2\|R\|_{L^{1}}.\end{cases}

Apply it to the Reynolds stress and define
\rho:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
by

\rho(x):=\zeta(R(x)).

It is clear that
{\mathds{I}}-\frac{R}{\rho}
lies in
B_{1/2}({\mathds{I}})
.

Define the new principal part
w^{(p)}
by

w^{(p)}(x):=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}(x)W_{k}(\gamma x),

where the amplitude function
a_{k}:{\mathds{T}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}
is given by

a_{k}:=\rho^{1/2}\Gamma_{k}\left({\mathds{I}}-\frac{R}{\rho}\right).

We do not need the frequency localization corrector in this case. Namely, the new corrector
w^{(c)}
is given by

w^{(c)}(x):=\gamma^{-1}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\nabla a_{k}(x):\Omega_{k}(\gamma x).

The velocity perturbation
w={\overline{u}}-u
is hence given by

w:=w^{(p)}+w^{(c)},

which is divergence-free.

Define the linear error
R_{\mathrm{lin}}
by

R_{\mathrm{lin}}:={\mathcal{R}}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+u\otimes w+w\otimes u,

the correction error
R_{\mathrm{cor}}
by

R_{\mathrm{cor}}:=w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(c)}+w^{(c)}\otimes w,

and the off-diagonal error
R_{\mathrm{off}}
by

R_{\mathrm{off}}:=\sum_{k\neq l}a_{k}a_{l}W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{l}(\gamma x).

To define the oscillation error, we need to introduce a bilinear anti-divergence operator
{\mathcal{B}}
from
C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d};{\mathds{R}}^{d})\times C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d};\mat_{d}({\mathds{R}}))
to
C^{\infty}({\mathds{T}}^{d};\mat_{d}({\mathds{R}}))
defined by

({\mathcal{B}}(f,X))_{i,j}=f_{l}{\mathcal{R}}_{i,j,k}X_{l,k}-{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{i}f_{l}{\mathcal{R}}_{i,j,k}X_{l,k}).

It is clear that
{\mathcal{B}}(f,X)
is a traceless symmetric matrix. The reader can refer to
[
12
, Appendix B.3]
for more detailed properties of
{\mathcal{B}}
. Define the oscillation error
R_{\mathrm{osc}}
by

R_{\mathrm{osc}}:=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla(a_{k}^{2}),T_{k}),

where
T_{k}
is the oscillation part of
W_{k}(\gamma x)\otimes W_{k}(\gamma x)
as defined in (
7.10
). The new Reynolds stress
{\overline{R}}
is hence defined by

{\overline{R}}:=R_{\mathrm{osc}}+R_{\mathrm{off}}+R_{\mathrm{cor}}+R_{\mathrm{lin}}.

Observe that
({\overline{u}},{\overline{R}})
is a smooth solution to (
4.1
). Indeed, direct computation shows that

\Div{\overline{R}}=\Div(R+w\otimes w)+(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w+\Div(u\otimes w+w\otimes u)-\nabla\rho,

which agrees with (
6.5
) with
P=-\rho
.

Now, we summarize the estimates needed.

Lemma 9.1

(Estimates on
w
)
.

There exists
A>0
depending only on
d
and
C_{1}>0
depending on
(u,R)
such that for any
\beta\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, it holds that

(9.1)

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq A\|R\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}+C_{1}\gamma^{-1/2},

(9.2)

\displaystyle\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\gamma^{-1}\mu^{-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})},

(9.3)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}w\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\gamma^{2\beta}\mu^{2\beta+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

Proof.

To prove (
9.1
), we use the improved Hölder’s inequality (see
[
30
, Lemma 2.1]
and
[
6
, Lemma 3.7]
) to get that

\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\left(\|a_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\|W_{k}\|_{L^{2}}+\gamma^{-1/2}\|a_{k}\|_{C^{1}}\|W_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\right).

Since
\Lambda
and
\Gamma_{k}
only depend on
d
, we have

\|a_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{d}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}\lesssim\|R\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}.

Gathering these estimates gives (
9.1
).

Next, (
9.2
) follows from Lemma
6.1
as

\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\gamma^{-1}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\|\nabla a_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Omega_{k}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim C_{u}\gamma^{-1}\mu^{-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

For (
9.3
), since
W_{k}
is of mean zero, we infer from Lemma
6.1
that

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}w^{(p)}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\|a_{k}\|_{C^{2\lceil\beta\rceil+1}}\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}(W_{k}(\gamma x))\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{u}\gamma^{2\beta}\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}W_{k}\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{u}\gamma^{2\beta}\mu^{2\beta+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

Similar computation shows that

\|(-\Delta)^{\beta}w^{(c)}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\gamma^{2\beta-1}\mu^{2\beta-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

As
\gamma\geq 1
and
\mu\geq 1
, combining these estimates gives (
9.3
).
∎

Lemma 9.2

(Estimates on
{\overline{R}}
)
.

Let
1<r<\infty
. There exists
C_{2}>0
depending on
(u,R)
and
r
with the following estimates

(9.4)

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathrm{osc}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\gamma^{-1},

(9.5)

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathrm{off}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\mu^{-1},

(9.6)

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathrm{cor}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\gamma^{-1}\mu^{-1},

(9.7)

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathrm{lin}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\left(\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}+\gamma^{2\alpha-1}\mu^{2\alpha-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r})}\right).

Proof.

First consider (
9.4
). Notice that
{\widehat{T}}_{k}
is supported in
\{m\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}:|m|\geq\gamma\}
. So (
9.4
) follows from the bilinear estimates of
{\mathcal{B}}
(see
[
12
, Theorem B.4]
) and Bernstein’s inequality as

\|R_{\mathrm{osc}}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\|\nabla(a_{k}^{2})\|_{C^{1}}\|{\mathcal{R}}T_{k}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\|a_{k}\|_{C^{2}}^{2}\gamma^{-1}\|T_{k}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C_{u}\gamma^{-1}.

The proof of (
9.5
) is the same as that of (
7.7
) using Lemma
6.1
.

The estimate (
9.6
) follows from Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
9.1
.

To prove (
9.7
), we use Lemma
9.1
to get

\|u\otimes w+w\otimes u\|_{L^{1}}\leq C_{u}\|w\|_{L^{1}}\leq C_{u}\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}.

For
\alpha<1/2
, we infer from Bernstein’s inequality and Lemma
9.1
that

\|{\mathcal{R}}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}.

For
\alpha\geq 1/2
, since
{\mathcal{R}}(-\Delta)^{1/2}
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator, we get

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}w\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mathcal{R}}(-\Delta)^{1/2}(-\Delta)^{\alpha-1/2}w\|_{L^{r}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha-1/2}w\|_{L^{r}}\leq C_{u}\gamma^{2\alpha-1}\mu^{2\alpha-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r})}.

Gathering these estimates gives (
9.7
) as desired.
∎

Now, let us prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
\gamma\geq 1
be a dyadic number to be determined. We first determine
\mu
with respect to
\gamma
. As
\alpha<(d+1)/4
, one has

\epsilon:=\frac{1}{3}\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{2}(d-1)-(2\alpha-1)\right\}>0.

Define

\mu:=\gamma^{2\lceil\alpha/\epsilon\rceil}\geq\gamma^{2}.

By perturbation, pick
r\in(1,2)
sufficiently close to 1 such that

(d-1)\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-(2\alpha-1)\geq 2\epsilon.

Then one has

\gamma^{2\alpha-1}\mu^{2\alpha-1+(d-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r})}\leq\gamma^{2\alpha-1}\mu^{-2\epsilon}\leq\gamma^{-1-2\alpha}\leq\gamma^{-1},

and

\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\leq\gamma^{-1}.

Therefore, Lemmas
9.1
and
9.2
yield that there exists
A>0
depending only on
d
and
C>0
depending on
(u,R)
, such that the following estimates hold:

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq A\|R\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}+C\gamma^{-1/2},

\displaystyle\|{\overline{R}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma^{-1}.

Then Proposition
4.2
follows from a particular choice of
\gamma
as a dyadic number with

\gamma>C^{2}\delta^{-2},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad C\gamma^{-1/2}<\delta.

This completes the proof.
∎
