Individualized antibiotic therapy in patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia in the intensive care unit
Authors: Serhat Atalar, Gürdal Yılmaz, Ahmet Eroğlu, İftihar Köksal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal duration of antibiotic therapy for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) remains controversial. Although current guidelines recommend 7–8 days of treatment, prolonged antibiotic use is still common in clinical practice. However, objective criteria to individualize and safely shorten treatment duration remain poorly defined. The aim of this study was to identify early clinical and laboratory response parameters associated with eligibility for short-term antibiotic therapy and to evaluate the predictive performance of composite criteria assessed on day 5 of treatment.MethodsThis retrospective study included intensive care unit (ICU) patients diagnosed with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria between January 1, 2015 and June 30, 2018. A total of 147 VAP episodes in 111 patients with complete clinical and laboratory follow-up data were categorized according to treatment duration into the short-term treatment group (STTG; 7–10 days) and long-term treatment group (LTTG; > 10 days). Clinical and laboratory response parameters were assessed on treatment days 3, 5, and 7.ResultsAmong 147 VAP episodes, 35 received 7–10 days and 112 received > 10 days of antibiotic therapy. Baseline demographic characteristics, APACHE II scores, CPIS values, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios, and laboratory parameters at diagnosis were largely comparable between groups.On day 5 of treatment, the STTG had significantly lower rates of fever or hypothermia (8.6% vs. 33%; p = 0.004), lower CPIS scores (3.8 ± 1.2 vs. 5.0 ± 2.1; p = 0.003), lower CRP levels (10.7 ± 8.5 vs. 14.2 ± 8.5 mg/dL; p = 0.030), lower procalcitonin levels (2.0 ± 3.6 vs. 6.5 ± 12.5 μg/L; p = 0.009), and higher PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios (335.6 ± 63 vs. 274.9 ± 95.4 mmHg; p < 0.001). Appropriate empirical therapy was more frequent in the STTG (91.4% vs. 71.4%; p = 0.021), whereas VAP-associated bacteremia was less common (8.6% vs. 33.9%; p = 0.003).Seven criteria associated with eligibility for short-term therapy were identified: absence of fever on day 5, procalcitonin ≤ 0.5 μg/L, CRP < 15 mg/dL, PaO₂/FiO₂ > 300 mmHg, CPIS ≤ 4 on day 5, absence of bacteremia, and appropriate empirical therapy. The presence of ≥ 4 criteria yielded a sensitivity of 91.4% and specificity of 50.9%. When PaO₂/FiO₂ > 300 mmHg was included among these criteria, specificity increased to 75%. The presence of ≥ 5 criteria was associated with the highest predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.839, sensitivity of 82.9%, and specificity of 74.1%.ConclusionsIn patients with VAP receiving appropriate empirical therapy and without bacteremia, early clinical and laboratory response parameters assessed on day 5 —including fever status, procalcitonin, CRP, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, and CPIS— may help identify patients potentially suitable for shorter antibiotic therapy. The proposed criteria should be considered exploratory and require prospective validation before routine clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04039-9.

Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a common healthcare-associated infection occurring in approximately 5–40% of intensive care unit (ICU) patients [1–3]. VAP prolongs the duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) and hospital stay, increases morbidity and mortality, and raises healthcare costs [2–4]. Since early diagnosis and effective treatment reduce mortality in VAP, appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy should be initiated promptly after obtaining microbiological samples [1, 5–7].

Patients diagnosed with VAP frequently receive antimicrobial therapy for up to 14 days, and prolonged treatment durations remain common in clinical practice [8–10]. Extended antimicrobial exposure increases the risk of antimicrobial resistance, drug toxicity, drug-drug interactions, healthcare costs, and prolonged hospitalization [1–3, 11, 12].

Critically ill ICU patients are particularly vulnerable to infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens because of prolonged mechanical ventilation, invasive procedures, severe underlying illness, and broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure. Recent studies have demonstrated that delayed initiation of active antimicrobial therapy and inappropriate empirical treatment remain major challenges in ICU-acquired infections, particularly those caused by carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [13, 14]. In addition, prolonged antibiotic exposure may further increase the risk of MDR co-/superinfections and contribute to antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, individualized strategies that safely shorten antibiotic duration while maintaining clinical efficacy are increasingly important for antimicrobial stewardship in VAP management [13, 14].

In recent years, short-course antimicrobial therapy has gained increasing attention in the management of infectious diseases, including VAP [15, 16]. Although previous studies have suggested that shorter antibiotic courses may be safe in selected VAP patients, objective and standardized criteria to individualize treatment duration in routine clinical practice remain lacking. Consequently, decisions regarding treatment duration are often based on subjective clinical judgment, resulting in substantial variability in patient management.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare patients receiving 7–10 days of therapy with those receiving longer treatment courses (> 10 days) in terms of clinical, microbiological, and outcome parameters, and to identify early clinical and laboratory response criteria associated with eligibility for short-term antibiotic therapy.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Karadeniz Technical University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Protocol No: 2018/142). This retrospective study included ICU patients diagnosed with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) between January 1, 2015 and June 30, 2018.

Patients older than 18 years were included if they met the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria for VAP. VAP was defined as pneumonia developing ≥ 48 h after initiation of invasive mechanical ventilation together with compatible clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings according to CDC definitions [17].

Clinical and laboratory data were recorded at the time of VAP diagnosis and on treatment days 3, 5, and 7. These included temperature, Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, leukocyte count, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), renal function parameters, microbiological findings, and treatment characteristics.

Exclusion criteria included community-acquired pneumonia; active or suspected tuberculosis; cystic fibrosis; HIV infection with CD4 < 200 cells/mm3; invasive pulmonary fungal infection; empyema or lung abscess; pneumonia present at intubation; concurrent infection during treatment or within 7 days of VAP diagnosis; and death within 7 days. Only patients with complete clinical and laboratory follow-up data on treatment days 3, 5, and 7 were included in the final analysis.

Demographic data, vital signs, treatment details, mechanical ventilation parameters, radiological findings, laboratory results, and infection control records were retrospectively reviewed using standardized data collection forms.

The duration of antibiotic treatment and decisions to continue, modify, or discontinue therapy were individualized by intensive care and infectious disease clinicians. These decisions were guided by bedside clinical judgment, taking into account the patient's clinical response, microbiological findings, inflammatory marker trends, and oxygenation status.

Patients were divided into two groups according to treatment duration:• Short-term treatment group (STTG): 7–10 days of therapy.• Long-term treatment group (LTTG): > 10 days of therapy.

Comparisons were performed for baseline characteristics; laboratory parameters at diagnosis; serial temperature measurements; changes in acute-phase reactants; renal function; PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio; GCS and CPIS scores; microbiological profile; treatment characteristics; antibiotic-free days; hospital and ICU length of stay; VAP recurrence; VAP-free days; microbiological eradication; and 28-day mortality.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the identification of clinical and laboratory response parameters associated with short-term antibiotic therapy.

Secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality, end-of-treatment mortality, VAP recurrence, VAP-free days, antibiotic-free days, microbiological eradication, ICU length of stay, and hospital length of stay.

Candidate variables were selected based on established indicators of treatment response in VAP, including fever resolution, improvement in oxygenation, reduction in inflammatory biomarkers, and CPIS trends. Variables demonstrating statistically significant associations with short-term therapy were incorporated into the final composite criteria.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, while categorical variables were expressed as number (n) and percentage (%). Normality of distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of statistically significant parameters.

Due to the retrospective design and relatively limited sample size, multivariate analysis was not performed.

Clinical and laboratory response during follow-up

Fever prevalence did not differ 48 h before diagnosis, 24 h before diagnosis, or at the time of diagnosis (p > 0.05). During follow-up, however, fever persisted more frequently in the LTTG on treatment days 3 and 5 (p = 0.020 and p = 0.004, respectively). Time to defervescence was significantly shorter in the STTG (3.2 ± 1.6 vs. 5.3 ± 2.9 days; p = 0.004).

Leukocyte count and erythrocyte sedimentation rate did not differ significantly between groups during follow-up. CRP levels were comparable at baseline and on day 3, but were significantly lower in the STTG on day 5 (10.7 ± 8.5 vs. 14.2 ± 8.5 mg/dL; p = 0.030). Similarly, PCT levels on day 5 were lower in the STTG (2.0 ± 3.6 vs. 6.5 ± 12.5 μg/L; p = 0.009), and the proportion of patients with PCT > 0.5 μg/L was significantly lower (41.9% vs. 67.6%; OR = 0.35; 95% CI = 0.14–0.85; p = 0.010).

PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios were similar at diagnosis and on day 3 (p > 0.05). By day 5, however, PaO₂/FiO₂ values were significantly higher in the STTG (335.6 ± 63.0 vs. 274.9 ± 95.4 mmHg; p < 0.001). Although baseline CPIS values were comparable, CPIS scores on day 5 were significantly lower in the STTG (3.8 ± 1.2 vs. 5.0 ± 2.1; p = 0.003).

Treatment characteristics

Initial empirical treatment regimens did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.08). However, colistin-containing regimens were less frequent in the STTG (25.7% vs. 47.3%; OR = 0.39; 95% CI = 0.15–0.96; p = 0.039).

Treatment modification after initiation was required in 22.9% of episodes in the STTG and 56.3% in the LTTG (OR = 0.23; 95% CI = 0.09–0.59; p < 0.001). Among episodes requiring modification, the time to treatment modification was significantly shorter in the STTG (2.3 ± 1.3 vs. 5.7 ± 3.9 days; p = 0.007). Appropriate empirical therapy was more frequent in the STTG (91.4% vs. 71.4%; OR = 4.27; 95% CI = 1.14–18.85; p = 0.021). Susceptibility of isolated pathogens to the initial empirical regimen was also higher in the STTG (91.2% vs. 71.6%; OR = 4.11; 95% CI = 1.08–18.31; p = 0.035).

Clinical outcomes

VAP-associated secondary bacteremia occurred less frequently in the STTG (8.6% vs. 33.9%; OR = 0.18; 95% CI = 0.04–0.68; p = 0.003). Post-treatment hospital and ICU stay durations were shorter in the STTG, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Following treatment completion, a second episode of VAP developed in 16.7% of STTG and 38.3% of LTTG (OR = 0.41; 95% CI = 0.17–0.97; p = 0.043). The interval between two VAP episodes was 31.7 ± 37.8 days in STTG and 25.1 ± 42.2 days in LTTG (p = 0.207). However, the number of VAP-free days was significantly higher in STTG (17.6 ± 10.3 vs. 12.1 ± 10.7 days; p = 0.004).

Microbiological eradication rates were comparable between groups (p = 0.583).

Treatment duration was significantly shorter in the STTG (8.7 ± 1.5 vs. 14.5 ± 4.2 days; p < 0.001), whereas antibiotic-free days after treatment were significantly longer (15.9 ± 22.7 vs. 8.2 ± 10.6 days; p = 0.004).

At 28-day follow-up, mortality was 5.7% (n = 2) in the STTG and 25.9% (n = 29) in the LTTG (OR = 0.17; 95% CI = 0.03–0.81; p = 0.020). Survival at hospital discharge was higher in the STTG (62.9% vs. 35.7%; OR = 3.05; 95% CI = 1.34–6.96; p = 0.008). ICU and post-treatment hospital stay durations were numerically shorter in the STTG, although the differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Although international guidelines consistently recommend 7–8 days of antimicrobial therapy for most cases of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) [1–4], treatment courses of 14 days or longer remain widely practiced. This discrepancy likely reflects persistent clinical concern regarding relapse, particularly in infections caused by non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli (NF-GNB) [8–10, 18]. However, prolonged therapy carries substantial consequences, including increased antimicrobial resistance, drug-related toxicity, higher healthcare costs, and greater risk of hospitalization-associated complications [1–3]. Accordingly, the critical issue is not whether shorter therapy is feasible, but rather under which objective, reproducible, and clinically defensible conditions it can be implemented safely.

The pivotal study by Chastre et al. demonstrated no significant differences between 8 and 15 days of therapy in terms of mortality, length of intensive care unit stay, ventilator-free days, or overall recurrence; however, recurrence rates were higher in NF-GNB infections treated with shorter courses [8]. Subsequent meta-analyses confirmed that short-term therapy does not increase mortality or overall relapse rates, although recurrence risk may be higher in NF-GNB infections [9, 10, 18, 19]. However, subsequent studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding recurrence according to treatment duration [16–19]. In our cohort, short-term therapy (7–10 days) was associated with lower mortality, longer antibiotic-free intervals, and comparable microbiological eradication rates. These findings suggest that treatment duration should be individualized based on clinical response rather than determined solely by microbiological classification.

Clinical response remains the cornerstone of treatment discontinuation. Resolution of fever, improvement in oxygenation, normalization of leukocyte count, and radiographic stabilization are widely accepted markers of recovery [1–4]. Biomarker-guided strategies, particularly using procalcitonin (PCT), have demonstrated reductions in antibiotic exposure without adverse effects on mortality or ICU stay [20–23]. While current guidelines caution against using PCT in isolation, they support its adjunctive role in discontinuation decisions [1–3]. This aligns with our findings: biomarkers alone are insufficient, but when integrated into a composite framework, they enhance decision accuracy.

Oxygenation dynamics, particularly the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, are among the most consistently validated prognostic markers in VAP [24, 25]. Early divergence in PaO₂/FiO₂ trajectories between survivors and non-survivors supports its role as a marker of treatment response rather than merely disease severity [26–28]. In our study, a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio > 300 mmHg on day 5 emerged as the strongest individual parameter associated with eligibility for short-term therapy. Physiologically, improvement in oxygenation likely reflects resolution of alveolar inflammation, reduction in intrapulmonary shunting, restoration of ventilation-perfusion matching, and recovery of lung function following effective infection control [29]. Notably, although improvement in PaO₂/FiO₂ became apparent by day 3, the most significant intergroup difference was observed on day 5, supporting the relevance of reassessment at this time point. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. Dennesen et al. demonstrated progressive improvement in oxygenation during successful treatment, while Luna et al. showed that PaO₂/FiO₂ trajectories diverged early between survivors and non-survivors, with differences becoming more pronounced by day 5 [26, 27]. Likewise, Luyt et al. identified day-7 PaO₂/FiO₂ as an important determinant of clinical outcome in VAP [30]. Collectively, these findings support the value of PaO₂/FiO₂ as an objective and physiologically meaningful parameter in treatment response assessment and antibiotic discontinuation strategies.

Similarly, fever kinetics, CPIS trends, and CRP evolution provide dynamic insight into treatment response. Defervescence by day 5 demonstrated high sensitivity for safe discontinuation [26, 27]. Declining CPIS values correlate with survival [31–34]. Serial CRP measurement reflects treatment response more reliably than single measurements [35–37].

Secondary bacteremia remains a well-established predictor of worse outcomes in VAP [38, 39]. In our study, the absence of bacteremia demonstrated a strong discriminant performance for short-term treatment.

Appropriate empirical therapy fundamentally influences outcomes. Delayed or inappropriate empirical treatment significantly increases mortality and prolongs hospitalization [1–4]. Meta-analyses demonstrate a substantial increase in mortality when initial therapy is inappropriate [40]. In our study, appropriate empirical initiation was significantly associated with short-term treatment. Recent ICU evidence has emphasized that delayed active therapy and inadequate empirical antimicrobial coverage are associated with worse clinical outcomes and prolonged infection management, particularly in MDR Gram-negative infections [14]. Appropriate empirical therapy was less frequent in the LTTG, where treatment modifications were not only more common but also significantly delayed compared to the STTG.

In our cohort, treatment modification occurred more frequently and later in the long-term treatment group, which may reflect delayed optimization of antimicrobial therapy and a more complicated clinical course. This variable may therefore act as a confounder influencing both treatment duration and outcomes. Due to the retrospective design and sample size limitations, treatment modification was not incorporated into the predictive model.

The predominance of non-fermenting Gram-negative pathogens in our cohort is consistent with recent ICU epidemiology studies demonstrating high rates of Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections, frequently associated with carbapenem resistance and limited therapeutic options [13, 14].

Collectively, our findings do not advocate indiscriminate short-course therapy. Rather, they support a structured, response-based strategy in selected patients with favorable early treatment response. The presence of ≥ 4 predefined criteria on day 5 provided high sensitivity (91.4%) and strong negative predictive value (95.0%), suggesting that early responders may be identified with reasonable reliability.

While clinical improvement is intuitively used to guide treatment decisions, antibiotic discontinuation in VAP is often based on subjective assessment and may vary considerably among clinicians. The value of our approach lies in providing a structured and reproducible framework that integrates objective clinical and laboratory parameters into decision-making. In addition, our findings suggest that timely optimization of antimicrobial therapy and appropriate empirical coverage may substantially influence both clinical response and treatment duration.

From an antimicrobial stewardship perspective, unnecessary prolongation of antibiotic therapy may contribute to antimicrobial resistance, MDR superinfections, drug-related toxicity, and increased healthcare utilization. Therefore, response-based strategies that help identify patients potentially suitable for shorter therapy may support more rational antibiotic use in ICU practice [13, 14].

From a practical perspective, the proposed algorithm may be applied as a structured reassessment on day 5 of treatment. Clinicians may evaluate the seven predefined criteria and use the number of fulfilled criteria to support decisions regarding treatment duration. Patients meeting ≥ 4 criteria may represent potential candidates for shorter therapy, whereas those not meeting these thresholds may require continued treatment and further evaluation.

Overall, our findings suggest that objective clinical and laboratory parameters assessed on day 5 may help identify patients potentially suitable for shorter antibiotic therapy. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The proposed criteria should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and prospective multicenter validation is required before routine clinical implementation.

These criteria may serve as a structured framework for future clinical decision algorithms evaluating antimicrobial discontinuation. Such a response-based strategy has the potential to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, limit antimicrobial resistance, decrease toxicity and healthcare costs, and improve stewardship potentially without adversely affecting patient safety or clinical outcomes. The REGARD-VAP trial demonstrated that individualized discontinuation based on clinical response is non-inferior to fixed-duration long-course therapy while significantly reducing antibiotic exposure and adverse events [15]. Similarly, contemporary analyses emphasize that failure to respond by day 4–5 should prompt reassessment of diagnosis, pathogen coverage, and host factors rather than automatic prolongation of therapy [1–4, 15, 34]. This paradigm shift highlights that decisions regarding treatment duration must be dynamic and data-driven. Notably, baseline clinical and laboratory parameters at the time of VAP diagnosis did not differ substantially between groups, suggesting that treatment duration may be better guided by dynamic response assessment during follow-up rather than by initial severity markers alone. This finding further supports the limited utility of diagnosis-day variables alone in determining individualized treatment duration.

Our composite day-5 model aligns with this evolving framework by operationalizing objective criteria into a reproducible decision structure. Rather than relying solely on isolated biomarkers or microbiological classification, our approach integrates oxygenation recovery, inflammatory marker kinetics, clinical improvement, and appropriateness of therapy, thereby integrating antimicrobial stewardship principles into clinical decision-making. Importantly, the high negative predictive value observed in our model suggests that early responders may be identified with reasonable reliability, potentially minimizing unnecessary antimicrobial exposure without increasing recurrence risk.

Study limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design may limit generalizability and introduces the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Treatment duration was not protocolized or randomized but determined by treating clinicians according to the patient’s clinical course. In particular, patients with a more severe clinical course may have been more likely to receive prolonged antibiotic therapy, resulting in potential confounding by indication and affecting comparability between groups. Although baseline characteristics were largely similar, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Decisions regarding prolonged therapy may also have been influenced by early clinical response, microbiological findings, or treatment modifications during follow-up.

The microbiological profile of our cohort was dominated by non-fermenting Gram-negative pathogens such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Because resistance patterns and ICU epidemiology may differ across institutions and geographic regions, the applicability of the proposed criteria should be validated in diverse clinical settings.

Another limitation is that multiple VAP episodes from the same patient were included in the analysis. Although each episode was treated as a separate clinical event, this approach may introduce intra-patient correlation and affect statistical independence.

In addition, the study period (2015–2018) may limit the contemporary applicability of the findings because antimicrobial resistance patterns, ICU practices, and treatment strategies have evolved over time. Validation in more recent cohorts is therefore warranted.

Finally, due to the retrospective design and relatively limited sample size, neither multivariate analysis nor incorporation of treatment modification into the predictive model was performed, which may limit the ability to fully account for confounding factors. Therefore, the predictive performance of this response-based model should be interpreted cautiously. The proposed criteria should be considered hypothesis-generating and require external validation in prospective multicenter cohorts before routine clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, objective clinical and laboratory response parameters assessed on day 5 may help identify patients with VAP who are potentially suitable for shorter antibiotic therapy. A composite response-based approach integrating clinical improvement, oxygenation status, biomarker trends, absence of bacteremia, and appropriateness of empirical therapy may support individualized treatment decisions. However, these findings should be considered exploratory and require prospective multicenter validation before routine clinical implementation.
