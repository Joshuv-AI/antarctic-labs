Multidrug-resistant avian pathogenic Escherichia coli ST162 among trafficked psittacine birds
Authors: Victória Galdino Pavlenco Rocha, Fernanda Borges Barbosa, Gabriel Gandolfi, Thais Martins-Gonçalves, Daniel Trugillo Martins Fontes, Herrison Fontana, Bruna Fuga, Nilton Lincopan, Terezinha Knöbl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe acquisition of virulence determinants has led to the emergence of certain lineages of extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC), with implications for both human and veterinary medicine. Avian Pathogenic E. coli (APEC) is a specific subpathotype of the broader ExPEC category. This study documents the occurrence of ExPEC/APEC ST162 among seven psittacine birds confiscated from the illegal wildlife trade and housed in a rehabilitation center, and describes temporal phylogenetic patterns compatible with possible within-center circulation, through a descriptive genomic surveillance approach. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed to characterize the virulome and resistome of all isolates and to comparative phylogenomic analysis of avian ST162 genomes with publicly available ST162 genomes from humans, including uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC). The first ST162 isolate was detected in a single bird early during the rehabilitation period. All isolates display similar virulence profiles. Strikingly, three out of seven ExPEC/APEC ST162 were classified as multidrug-resistant, carrying genes and/or mutations conferring resistance to clinically relevant antibiotics. Phylogenomic analysis clustered five psittacine isolates within a clade (52-1753 SNP differences) predominantly composed of human ExPEC ST162, underscoring their One Health significance at the human-animal interface. These results underscore the importance of animal-based genomic surveillance in wildlife rehabilitation centers and highlight the illegal wildlife trade as a potential pathway for the dissemination of ExPEC lineages of epidemiologic and public health relevance.Keywords: ExPEC, ST162, Illegal wildlife trade, Rehabilitation centers

Introduction

Certain Escherichia coli (E. coli) lineages has adapted to colonize extraintestinal sites, giving rise to the extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli group (ExPEC) (Kaper et al., 2004; Riley, 2020). The ExPEC group, which causes extraintestinal infections in humans, is best represented by the well-known pathotypes uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), septicaemic E. coli (SEPEC), and neonatal meningitis E. coli (NMEC) (Kaper et al., 2004; Riley, 2020). Another ExPEC lineage of major relevance in avian medicine is avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC) (Ewers et al., 2003; Mehat et al., 2021). APEC is associated with the systemic disease known as avian colibacillosis and shows tropism for the air sacs, liver, and heart, leading to the most frequent lesions: aerosacculitis, pericarditis, and perihepatitis (Christensen et al., 2021; Knöbl et al., 2008; Saidenberg & Knöbl, 2005). The severity of the condition depends not only on the host’s immune status but also on the presence and expression of chromosomal and plasmid virulence genes (Alber et al., 2021; Kathayat et al., 2021).

ExPEC strains possess an extensive virulence repertoire, reflecting their remarkable ability to acquire genes through horizontal gene transfer (HGT), a key adaptive mechanism enabling colonization of extraintestinal niches (Riley, 2020). From a One Health perspective, this genetic plasticity raises significant concern, as some APEC isolates have shown high genomic similarity to human strains, particularly UPEC, when considering virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes (Johnson et al., 2025; Saidenberg et al., 2022).

Ovi et al. (2023) further argue that differentiating APEC from other ExPEC lineages can be even more difficult than separating APEC from commensal E. coli. The dissemination of ExPEC lineages poses an important risk (Riley, 2020). Such spread may occur under various circumstances, but is intensified in settings with poor sanitary conditions, such as wildlife rehabilitation centres (Vanstreels et al., 2010).

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) assigns sequence types (STs) by comparing allele sequences at seven housekeeping gene loci, providing a widely used framework for characterization of bacterial populations (Maiden et al., 1998, 2013). Riley (2014) describes pandemic ExPEC lineages implicated in human bloodstream infections and urinary tract infections, notably ST131, ST393, ST69, ST95, and ST73. In birds, the APEC population includes some of these same STs (e.g., ST131, ST95, and ST73), while also comprising additional clonal backgrounds frequently reported in avian colibacillosis, such as ST23, ST117, and ST140 (Johnson et al., 2022; Mehat et al., 2021). Moreover, some STs may be emerging or potentially relevant in both human and animal disease; for example, Johnson et al. (2025) reported ST162 as increasingly detected and showing high genomic similarity between human and animal strains.

Although ExPEC ST162 has not been frequently reported in epidemiological studies, it has recently gained attention in antimicrobial resistance research, given its frequent association with MDR strains and clinically relevant determinants such as mcr-1 and blaNDM-1 (Sellera et al., 2022; Szmolka et al., 2023). Studies reporting antibiotic-resistant ST162 isolates have highlighted not only its concerning resistance-associated genetic repertoire, but also its frequent recovery from chicken meat, drawing attention to the potential role of foodborne transmission (Kubelová et al., 2021; Szmolka et al., 2023). Zhuge et al. (2019) demonstrated the high pathogenic potential of animal-derived ST162 strains using multiple mammalian models of human extraintestinal infection.

In Brazil, epidemiological data on ST162 remain exceptionally limited. Sellera et al. (2022) isolated a highly virulent NDM-1-producing ST162 from a stranded pygmy sperm whale on the southern coast of Brazil, representing the only occurrence of this lineage reported in wildlife to date. Notably, ST162 has not been reported among the predominant ExPEC STs identified in Brazilian human infections or in avian colibacillosis studies conducted in the country. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant given Brazil's status as the world's largest exporter of chicken meat and the magnitude of its illegal wildlife trade.

In Brazil, the rehabilitation centres frequently operate beyond their capacity and often lack the infrastructure required for proper animal management. Consequently, birds rescued from the illegal wildlife trade are not consistently subjected to adequate quarantine procedures, frequently sharing enclosures with individuals of other species, thereby increasing the probability of cross-species bacterial and transmission of pathogenic E. coli (Knöbl et al., 2008; Rocha et al., 2022, 2025; Sanches et al., 2017).

Beyond resource constraints and suboptimal biosecurity protocols in rehabilitation centres, birds rescued from the illegal trade often experience prolonged stays. This extended residence amplifies chronic stress and increases cumulative exposure to circulating microorganisms, elevating the risk of pathogen acquisition and onward transmission (Fischer & Romero, 2019).

In our study, we monitored 19 psittacine birds over a 40-day rehabilitation period at a wildlife management centre in São Paulo, Brazil. This descriptive genomic surveillance study aimed to investigate pathogenic E. coli strains recovered from trafficked psittacine birds and to discuss their broader implications for wildlife rehabilitation, biosecurity, and potential risks. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed to characterize the virulence gene repertoire of all isolates and to compare avian ST162 genomes with publicly available ST162 genomes from wildlife and humans, including uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), using phylogenomic analysis, to investigate the One Health significance at the human-animal interface.

Material and methods

Samples and processing

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science at the University of São Paulo (CEUA, FMVZ-USP) under protocol CEUA: 8870200519. Between November and December 2019, a total of 19 psittacine birds (Amazona aestiva and Ara ararauna) seized from the illegal wildlife trade and housed at a triage center for rehabilitation were investigated. Psittacine birds were sampled at 5-day intervals over a 40-day rehabilitation period (Table 1). All the birds arrived at the CETAS on the same day and were housed in the same cage. The birds were juveniles, although their exact ages could not be determined. In addition, all animals appeared clinically healthy and asymptomatic throughout the study period. For sampling, the birds were individually placed on autoclaved paper, and fresh fecal samples were collected immediately after defecation using sterile swabs, as fresh fecal samples provide a broader representation of the enteric microbiota than cloacal swabs.

Swabs were inoculated into Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Difco, BD, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of broth culture was plated onto MacConkey agar (Difco, BD, USA). Colonies showing morphology consistent with E. coli were isolated and confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) using the MALDI Biotyper CA System 3.0 (Bruker Daltonik, Germany).

Screening for virulence markers

Genomic DNA from all E. coli isolates was extracted following the protocol described by Boom et al. (1991) and subsequently subjected to PCR assays targeting virulence markers associated with the APEC pathotype. Primers specific to ompT, iss, hlyF, iutA, and iroN were used following the protocol described by Johnson et al. (2008). PCR amplicons were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis (1.5%), and APEC isolates were then subjected to whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic resistance profiles were determined by the disk diffusion method following standards and quality control parameters from CLSI guideline, using the E. coli ATCC 25922 as reference strain. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in sterile saline (0.9%) to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard and inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates using a sterile swab. Antibiotic disks were applied to the plates, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimetres and interpreted as resistant (R), intermediate (I), or susceptible (S) according to Brazilian Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (BrCAST) breakpoints (BrCAST, 2026 and CLSI, 2024). The antibiotics tested were amoxicillin–clavulanate (AMC, 20/10 µg), ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg), ceftiofur (CTF, 30 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), doxycycline (DOX, 30 µg), gentamicin (GEN, 10 µg), and sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim (SXT, 23.75/1.25 µg). MDR, multidrug resistance defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories (i.e., aminopenicillin, aminopenicillin/inhibitor of beta-lactamase combination, third-generation cephalosporin, fluoroquinolone, tetracyclines, aminoglycoside, sulfonamide/antifolate combination) (Magiorakos et al., 2012).

Genome sequencing and genomic analyses

DNA from the isolates was extracted using the Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) and subsequently purified with the DNA/RNA Purification Column Kit (Kylt, Muehlenstr, Germany). These strains were subjected to WGS by NextSeq (Illumina, San Diego, USA). FASTQ reads were generated and quality-controlled using Read Quality Reports (v. 0.74). Genomes were assembled with Unicycler (v. 0.5.1) and annotated using Prokka (v. 1.14.6), all within the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.eu/).

Serotyping was performed using Serotype Finder (v. 2.0.1) (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/SerotypeFinder/), plasmid replicons were identified using PlasmidFinder 3.03 (https://genepi.dk/plasmidfinder), resistome was predicted using ResFinder 4.7.2 (https://genepi.food.dtu.dk/resfinder), and virulence factors were predicted using VirulenceFinder 2.0 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/). A minimum threshold of 90% was used to determine the nucleotide identity and gene coverage. To visualizes co-occurrence patterns among virulence genes, we constructed a two-mode sample-gene network in Gephi (v0.9.7). Multilocus sequence typing was determined using MLST (v. 2.22.0). The phylogroup was investigated by JBrowse (v. 1.16.11) according to previously defined parameters (Clermont et al., 2013).

To assess clonal relationships among isolates, phylogenetic analysis was performed using the CGE Phylogeny pipeline v1.0 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/CSIPhylogeny/) based on core-genome SNP analysis. The following parameters were applied: minimum depth at SNP positions: 10x; minimum relative depth at SNP positions: 10%; minimum distance between SNPs: 10 bp; minimum SNP quality: 30; minimum Z-score: 1.96. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL (v. 7.2.2).

A comparative phylogenetic analysis was performed using the same core genome SNP-based methodology, incorporating the seven ST162 APEC isolates from this study and 62 publicly available ST162 ExPEC genomes of human and animal origin retrieved from EnteroBase (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/species/ecoli/allele_st_search) (Table S1).

Results

WGS, plasmids, serotype and MLST analysis

Between November and December 2019, 19 psittacine birds (Amazona aestiva and Ara ararauna) confiscated from the illegal wildlife trade were investigated at a rehabilitation triage center. Psittacine birds were sampled at 5-day intervals over a 40-day rehabilitation period. PCR screening identified 10 APEC isolates recovered from the birds during the 40-day sampling period. For clonal relatedness and transmission analyses, we restricted the dataset to isolates belonging to the same ST. Of the 10 isolates, 7 were ST162: five were isolated from five different Amazona aestiva individuals (2901, 2906, 2909, 2911 and 2912) and two from two Ara ararauna individuals (2904 and 2905). The remaining 3, whose STs could not be determine, were excluded from further analysis. One ST162 isolate was obtained on day 5 following admission to the CETAS (2905), whereas six were collected between days 30 and 35 of captivity (Fig. 1).

In silico WGS typing identified all ST162 isolates as belonging to the O8/O160:H40 serotype. The strains were positive for the discriminatory markers of groups F and G (Clermont et al., 2019). However, negativity for all four initial markers (arpA, chuA, yjaA, TspE4.C2) precludes assignment to any recognized phylogroup, leaving these isolates phylogenetically unclassified under the Clermont et al. (2019) scheme.

Plasmid analysis showed that all isolates carried IncFIB_1 and IncFIC (FII)_1 replicons, a combination that is characteristic of IncF (ColV-like) virulence plasmids in ExPEC, particularly APEC harbouring extensive repertoires of virulence-associated loci (Mehat et al., 2021). In addition, isolates 2909 and 2912 also possessed a Col_1 replicon, whose co-occurrence with IncFIB_1 and IncFIC (FII)_1 has been associated with the carriage of colicin-type bacteriocin genetic determinants.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Strikingly, three (strains 2904, 2906 and 2911) out of seven ExPEC/APEC ST162 displaying resistance to clinically relevant aminopenicillins, aminoglycosides, sulphonamides, trimethoprim, tetracyclines, and quinolones (Table 1), being classified as MDR (Magiorakos et al., 2012). Strains 2901, 2905, 2909 and 2912 remained susceptible to ampicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ceftiofur, ciprofloxacin, doxycycline, gentamicin, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim.

Viruloma and resistome analysis

The virulence-factor analysis indicated that the isolates harbour a well-defined repertoire of ExPEC associated genes. Overall, the most frequent genes were those involved in iron acquisition, such as the iucABCD operon, iutA, iroN, and the sitABCD operon, and genes related to adhesion and invasion, such as papC, csgA, fdeC, aatA, and the yeaABCDJ operon, which together were detected in 100% of isolates (Fig. 2).

Genes typically implicated in urinary tract infection (UTI) pathogenesis were also identified, including type 1 fimbriae (fimH) and P fimbriae (papC). Moreover, we detected the hlyCABD operon encoding α-hemolysin, an exotoxin associated with uroepithelial exfoliation and enhanced tissue dissemination. Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that all seven isolates recovered from psittacine birds were distributed within the broader diversity of ST162 ExPEC and APEC genomes of human and animal origin included in the comparative dataset (Fig. 3; Table S1). Overall, the tree did not show strict separation according to isolate source, continent or collection period, as human and animal derived genomes were interspersed across ST162 phylogeny.

The APEC isolates formed three main phylogenetic groupings. Isolates 2904, 2905 and 2911 clustered together (78-548 SNP differences) and were positioned near ST162 ExPEC genomes of both animal and human origin (870-1653 SNP differences) (Table S1; Table S2). Isolates 2901 and 2906 formed a second closely related cluster (52 SNP differences), positioned near human-derived ST162 genomes (1425-2849 SNP differences) from South America, including Brazil and Ecuador, as well as a human-derived genome from United States (Table S1; Table S2). Finally, isolates 2909 and 2912 formed a distinct sublineage (127 SNP differences) composed only of isolates from this study, with no public genome clustering within this small subcluster.

Together, combined phylogenetic analysis indicates that the psittacine ST162 APEC isolates are not restricted to a single phylogenetic background within the ST162 lineage. Instead, they were distributed across distinct sublineages within a broader ST162 ExPEC/APEC population that includes genomes from human and animal sources, different continents, and multiple collection periods (Table S1).

When the first two phylogenetic groupings are considered within their broader clade, isolates 2904, 2905, 2911, 2901, and 2906 were embedded in a region of the tree in which the publicly available genomes were predominantly human-derived. In this broader clade, Eco 08 represented the only animal-derived genome. This pattern indicates that most psittacine ST162 isolates were positioned within a phylogenetic background mainly represented by human-derived ST162 genomes in the available comparative dataset, although this should be interpreted as phylogenetic contextualization.

Discussion

The spread of virulent strains within wildlife rehabilitation centres represents a potential threat not only to the infected species but also to other animals housed in the same facilities. Failures in biosecurity protocols can greatly facilitate the dissemination of pathogenic bacteria, particularly when many birds are kept together in the same aviary, which increases the likelihood of fecal-oral transmission (Salles & Moraes, 2009). The growing number of animals seized from illegal wildlife trade each year has led to overcrowding in rehabilitation centres, increasing sanitary challenges. There is also significant underreporting in official channels, making it difficult to accurately determine how many animals are seized annually in Brazil. Nevertheless, official records show a consistent upward trend: according to the Brazilian Institute of the Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (IBAMA), approximately 50 thousand animals were seized from illegal trade in 2021, and this number increased to 60 thousand in 2023, with birds representing >65% of all individuals confiscated in both years (IBAMA, 2024, 2025).

Xenoulis et al. (2010) conducted a comparative study between captive and free-living psittacines and reported that Enterobacteriaceae were isolated exclusively from captive individuals, with E. coli being the most prevalent species. In the context of the wildlife trade, birds are exposed from the outset to chronic stress and nutritional deficiencies, conditions that, when combined, can significantly compromise immune function (Fischer, 2019).

Our study documented the presence and putative intra-facility dissemination of virulent APEC ST162 strains, with a temporal detection pattern of ST162 consistent with possible ongoing circulation throughout the rehabilitation period, persisting up to the point of anticipated release. The first bird to be colonized by an APEC ST162 strain was an Ara ararauna, detected on Day 5 (Fig. 1). While this temporal pattern is consistent with intra-facility acquisition, alternative sources of introduction cannot be exclude, as no environmental sampling of enclosure surfaces, water, or feed was not conducted. Following the first isolation, six additional ST162-positive isolates were recovered from other birds between days 30 and 35 of sampling (Fig. 1). Future investigations incorporating comprehensive environmental surveillance would provide more definitive insights into transmission dynamics in wildlife rehabilitation settings.

This outcome might have been mitigated through the implementation of appropriate quarantine measures, as recommended by IBAMA in the normative instruction regulating wildlife rehabilitation and triage centres in Brazil. These guidelines emphasize the initial isolation of newly admitted animals as a preventive strategy to reduce pathogen and transmission risks within these facilities (IBAMA, 2021). Our findings underscore the practical importance of strengthening biosecurity protocols and implementing routine microbiological screening in wildlife rehabilitation centres, particularly those receiving animals from the illegal trade. Early pathogen detection, combined with systematic quarantine of newly admitted animals, could significantly reduce environmental contamination and minimize risks to staff and co-housed species. Comparative studies evaluating the effectiveness of different biosecurity interventions in these settings are warranted. The clustering patterns observed in this study placed the seven APEC isolates within the broader diversity of ExPEC genomes of human and animal origin (Fig. 3). The formation of distinct clusters among isolates may reflect differential acquisition of mobile genetic elements carrying accessory virulence genes, which were detected exclusively in a subset of strains but absent in others, contributing to genomic diversification whitin the ST162 lineages.

The placement of five psittacine isolates within a broader clade predominantly composed of human-derived ST162 ExPEC genomes should be taken into consideration. This pattern suggests that strains from this study are not confined to an avian- or wildlife-specific genomic background but instead fall within a portion of the ST162 diversity that also includes human ExPEC genomes. This finding is consistent with the recognized difficulty of sharply separating APEC from other ExPEC lineages based solely on host origin, particularly when virulence-associated repertoires overlap across pathotypes. The predominance of human-derived genomes in the clade containing most psittacine isolates may partly reflect sampling and deposition biases rather than the true ecological distribution of ST162. Nevertheless, the absence of a clear separation between human- and animal-derived genomes in the tree reinforces the need for broader genomic surveillance across hosts, environments, and rehabilitation facilities to clarify the epidemiological significance of this lineage.

Beyond the concern of pathogen dissemination within rehabilitation centres, the potential spread of opportunistic and pathogenic bacteria into free-living populations is an equally critical issue. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), one of the major risks associated with releasing rehabilitated wildlife without prior microbiological evaluation is the introduction of novel pathogens into native populations, posing a substantial threat to wildlife species with no prior immunological exposure to these microorganisms (IUCN, 2013).

Several studies have focused on isolating pathogenic and multidrug-resistant bacteria, particularly Enterobacteriaceae, from wildlife rehabilitation centres. Based on the frequency of isolates carrying the minimal predictive APEC virulence markers, multiple authors consistently identify ST117, ST95, ST131, and ST23 as the sequence types of greatest relevance in avian medicine, in both wild birds and poultry (Johnson et al., 2022; Rocha et al., 2025; Steffen et al., 2025; Saidenberg et al., 2024). In contrast, our study recovered ExPEC isolates belonging to ST162, a sequence type that is less frequently described in the literature as emerging or potentially relevant. However, the detection of ST162 isolates carrying ExPEC- associated virulence genes, together with their placement within a broader phylogenetic background that includes human- and animal-derived ST162 genomes, suggests that this lineage may warrant closer attention within the ExPEC epidemiological landscape (Fig. 2).

The plasmid repertoire included IncF replicons in all isolates; strains 2909 and 2912 additionally carried a Col replicon. The presence of these plasmid replicons is noteworthy, given their established association with mobile genetic elements in Enterobacteriaceae. While the coexistence of multiple plasmid replicons in the captivity and rehabilitation settings suggests conditions that could potentially favor the maintenance and circulation of mobile genetic elements, caution is warranted in directly inferring implications for transmission. Nevertheless, this observation reinforces the relevance of genomic surveillance and biosecurity procedures as precautionary measures.

Our virulome profiling revealed a high co-occurrence of genes commonly associated with ExPEC (Fig. 2). Adhesion-related determinants were the most frequent category (detected in 31% of the strains), followed by antimicrobial resistance–associated genes (≈20%) and toxin-related genes (≈13%). The most frequent profiles comprised operons and individual genes that are often reported in APEC (Fig. 2). Notably, some determinants detected in only a subset of strains are classically linked to UPEC virulence, including fimH, papC and the hlyCABD operon, underscoring the overlap in virulence repertoires across ExPEC pathotypes (Hojati et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020). Some of these determinants, which in UPEC are primarily involved in adhesion and invasion of host tissues, have been closely linked to recurrent UTI in patients (Whelan et al., 2023). Chakraborty et al. (2020), in a comparative study, reported that the occurrence of papC, hlyA, and cnf1 was significantly higher among isolates from patients with recurrent infections than among those from patients with single-episode infections. The overlap in virulence gene repertoires across ExPEC pathotypes has been widely investigated. As such, phenotypic studies of APEC isolate in vivo mammalian models are warranted to clarify their true pathogenic potential in non-avian hosts.

Continued monitoring of ExPEC strains in captive birds within rehabilitation centres is essential, particularly for opportunistic pathogens that may not produce overt clinical signs in their hosts. Equally important is the surveillance of these bacteria in free-living populations, as this provides a critical measure for assessing the risk of human-mediated microbial dissemination into natural ecosystems.

Because this study included a limited number of birds and isolates, and did not include environmental sampling, it should be interpreted as a descriptive pilot genomic surveillance investigation. Thus, although the detection of APEC-associated virulence genes and the phylogenetic relatedness to human- and wildlife-derived ST162 genomes support the potential relevance of these isolates, our data do not allow confirmation of their origin, transmission route, or pathogenicity in psittacine birds or humans. The findings of this study carry direct practical implications for wildlife management policy in Brazil and beyond. In Brazil, where the illegal wildlife trade is among the largest in the world, rehabilitation centres serve as critical bottlenecks between captive and free-living animal populations. Without standardized microbiological protocols at this interface, clinically relevant E. coli lineages may be inadvertently introduced into wild populations, potentially bridging the gap between captive and free-living animals. Our results support the implementation of evidence-based biosecurity guidelines in wildlife triage and rehabilitation settings. Moreover, urgent governmental action is needed to improve the infrastructure of wildlife triage centres in Brazil.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the small sample size restricts these findings to a pilot genomic observation, which cannot be generalized to broader trafficked psittacine or wildlife populations. Second, the absence of environmental and source-control sampling (e.g., cages, water, handlers) prevents us from determining whether ST162 was introduced via the illegal trade chain or acquired within the rehabilitation center. Furthermore, while the phylogenomic clustering with human-derived genomes is informative, it does not provide evidence of human origin, host directionality, or zoonotic transmission. Finally, the lack of pathogenicity testing means that despite the detection of ExPEC/APEC virulence genes, the definitive disease-causing ability of these isolates in psittacines or humans remains to be demonstrated. Conversely, a major strength of this study is the investigation of a rarely explored interface related to the illegally trafficked psittacines entering a rehabilitation center, tracked through a temporal sampling design. Based on WGS, we provided a comprehensive resolution of the isolates, including ST, serotype, virulence factors, plasmid replicons, and resistance profiles, integrated into a unified comparative phylogenetic analysis with public human and animal ST162 genomes. Along with phenotypic susceptibility data, our finding that three out of seven isolates MDR offers valuable hypothesis-generating surveillance data. Ultimately, this work raises a balanced, plausible One Health concern, highlighting a potential risk interface and supporting the implementation of precautionary microbiological screening and biosecurity protocols before wildlife release.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that occurrence of avian pathogenic Escherichia coli belonging to ST162 among trafficked psittacine birds. Importantly, the phylogenomic clustering of psittacine isolates within a clade predominantly composed of human ExPEC ST162 genomes provides strong evidence of pathogen sharing. Using WGS, this study underscores a critical One Health link at the human-animal interface, highlighting the urgent need for continuous genomic surveillance of antimicrobial resistance in wildlife populations.
