Implementation science and the clinical trial workforce: A national needs assessment of training and practice
Authors: Ingrid J. Hickman, Jenna Graffini, Mark Liu, Nicola Straiton, Thobe Mthethwa-Pitt, H. Yeo, Tegwen Howell, Kaye Hewson, Emma Tavender, Natalie Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranslating clinical trials into routine healthcare remains fractured and inconsistent, reflecting an enduring challenge in bridging research and practice. Implementation science can be applied to the clinical trial context, to better understand and improve how evidence is adopted and adapted in practice. Building the clinical trial workforce capability in implementation science for clinical trial design, conduct, and dissemination has the potential to address challenges of evidence translation at a much earlier stage of the research pipeline.AimTo determine, across the Australian and New Zealand clinical trials workforce (including consumers), familiarity with and understanding of implementation science, interest to upskill in methodologies, and barriers to and preferences for, accessing support.MethodOnline survey developed with Likert scale and open-ended responses. Snowball recruitment from clinical trial networks and centres, and QR code promotion.Results353 responses received across diverse geographies, professions, and clinical areas. Over 40% were unsure how to define their understanding of implementation science in the context of clinical trials. Most participants (56.7%) were familiar with some methodologies but had limited experience of using them. Training interests were moderate to high, particularly on embedding implementation science in trial design, recruitment strategies, sustainment, and scalability. Over 80% believed competency in implementation science will become essential to the clinical trial workforce in the future.ConclusionThis needs assessment of a clinically, professionally and geographically diverse clinical trial workforce reflects that implementation science is an emerging field of value in this context and there is an appetite for training and support.

Background

There are more than 515,000 randomised controlled trials (RCT) registered globally [1,2]. The time lag from RCT completion to uptake of new evidence in clinical practice is a critical issue [3]. Despite significant global investment [4], the translation and integration of RCT-derived evidence into healthcare practice remains fractured and delayed [5]. Effective translation may be hindered due to a range of limitations in the design and conduct of clinical trials. Overly restrictive trial inclusion criteria, recruitment delays and incomplete outcome measures are common and lack of integration of trials with healthcare systems impedes relevance of findings to practice [6].

Implementation science offers theories, models and frameworks that can be applied to the complexity of the clinical trial context, to better support, understand and improve how evidence is adopted, adapted and sustained in real-world settings. It supports the design of trials that are both scientifically rigorous and contextually relevant and provides mechanisms to close the long-standing effectiveness-practice gap [7,8]. Research professionals may use methods drawn from implementation science to support trial design and delivery (e.g. trial implementation blueprints, monitoring implementation outcomes) and servicing the translation journey (eg informing subsequent implementation trials, policy and scale up).

Accelerating the translation of scientific discoveries into tangible improvements in patient care has become a priority for many health systems [5,9,10] with a recognised need to support knowledge translation capability in the healthcare workforce [11]. Building the clinical trial workforce capability to use implementation science methodologies, outcomes and strategies may address some of the challenges of research translation at a much earlier stage of the research pipeline. The implementation science knowledge and skill capability across the clinical trial workforce are unknown.

This study aims to determine the clinical trial workforce familiarity with, understanding of, and interest in, implementation science research and practice in clinical trial design and conduct. It explores preferences for workforce training and barriers to accessing support.

Methods

Context

A national online cross-sectional survey anonymously sought the Australian and New Zealand clinical trial workforce views on the use of implementation science in trials and their training needs. Participants were eligible if they were involved with designing and/or conducting clinical trials in Australia and or New Zealand within the last year.

Survey development and piloting

A working group of trialists with clinical, implementation science and consumer expertise was convened from the Australian Clinical Trial Alliance (ACTA) to develop the scope and structure of the survey informed by the tailored design method [12]. Three iterations of the survey were reviewed across three monthly meetings. The final iteration of the survey consisted of six sections; 1) Demographics (categorical multi-choice options), 2) Implementation Science in clinical trial design (multiple choice options and free text), 3) Implementation practice and activities related to clinical trials (multiple choice options and free text), 4) Interest in implementation science research and practice training (4 pt Likert scale), 5) Preferences for how to receive training and support (multiple choice options), 6) Additional consideration for the needs of the clinical trial workforce (multiple choice options and free text). The survey was first piloted with experienced trialists, trial managers, implementation researchers and consumers to test face validity (n = 7) and indicated good readability and understanding of the questions and a time burden of approximately 10 – 15 min. The final survey (Supplementary File 1) consisted of 12 mandatory demographic questions followed by logic branching to reduce time burden and exposure to irrelevant questions for those less familiar with implementation science (<10 min, maximum 21 questions), whilst those with experience were offered opportunity for greater description of their utilisation of implementation science in trials if desired (up to 15 min, maximum 34 questions). Respondents could choose ‘prefer not to say’ for any mandatory demographic or skip a response to any question in sections 2-6.

Recruitment

Convenience sampling and passive snowballing sampling technique was used for distribution of the survey by email to key clinical trial sector groups who have established networks with ACTA and recipients were encouraged to forward the email invitation broadly to others in their own workplace or network. Reminder emails were sent two weeks after initial invitation. There was no initial screening of target group prior to distribution. The survey link was also promoted via social media platforms of the key clinical trial sector groups and to all attendees of the ACTA national conference (December 2024). The survey link was open for 8 weeks from November 2024 to January 2025.

Sample size

The size and location of the clinical trial workforce in Australia and New Zealand is unknown and so a recruitment target was prospectively set at minimum of 100 responses with representation from all states and territories.

Data collection

The survey questions were distributed and administered using the Qualtrics XM online platform, using the native anonymous link and QR code generation features.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were exported from Qualtrics XM to Microsoft Excel for an initial cleaning, prior to being imported to SPSS (IBM) for data analysis. Data was analysed descriptively with frequencies and percentages calculated across categories of interest. Entries with at least one datapoint were included in the study. For questions which were optional, no imputation was performed for non-response.

The free text short answer responses related to the respondents' experiences with a range of implementation methodologies used in trial design and/or trial conduct were extracted from Qualtrics and imported into excel for content analysis. The initial data clean involved removing incomplete or irrelevant responses (e.g., n/a). Types of implementation science theories, models and frameworks (TMFs) used in trial design were tallied across the data set and described as a frequency. Respondents’ experience of using each type of implementation methodology in trial conduct followed a systematic approach comprising several key stages. First, an inductive manual coding process was employed to each methodology to identify initial codes emerging directly from the data (content codes) and the frequency of codes across the responses for each methodology were tallied [13]. Once the initial coding was complete, in some instances, codes were grouped into broader categories if they were conceptually similar [14,15]. There was no hierarchy given to codes other than frequency of mentions by respondents. The response to whether they thought the use of implementation methodology had impacted the translatability of the trial was categorised into three categories: yes, no, unsure/too soon to tell.

Inter-rater reliability of content analysis was addressed by having a second independent researcher review the analysis of a sample of the data and any refinement occurred through iterative discussions and consensus. As the survey was completed anonymously, member checking of preliminary interpretation of analysis was not available.

Two questions which captured free text short responses were analysed with Leximancer [16], a text analytics tool appropriate for knowledge discovery tasks. Leximancer is a text analytics tool which identifies the prevalence of a concept and concept correlation within the free text answers to formulate a co-occurrence matrix (a mathematical calculation that allows the prevalence of co-occurrence of all concepts against all others to be explicitly stored). The information is displayed by means of a conceptual map that provides a bird's eye view of the material, representing the main concepts contained within the text and information about how they are related (full method description supplementary material 1).

Ethics

Ethics approval (Approval: 2024/HE001682) was sought from the University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee and respondents provided tick box consent prior to commencement. There was no incentive to participate and no disadvantage by not participating. Data is reported according to the checklist for reporting of survey studies (CROSS) [17].

Participants

There were respondents from New Zealand and each state and territory of Australia representing the expected density of clinical trial activity in each state, predominantly occurring in metropolitan areas [18]. Majority of respondents (58.9%) had a role in both clinical trial design and conduct. Most performed their primary trial activity within Health Services (41.1%) or universities (38%) with the most common clinical backgrounds described as allied health (24%), nursing/midwifery (22%) and medicine (13%) from a diverse range of clinical areas. A broad range of clinical trial roles were captured including investigators, trial managers and consumers. There was an even representation of trial experience ranging from less than two to more than 20 years.

Understanding of implementation science in trials

The participants were not provided with an accepted definition of implementation science. The first question of the survey (after eligibility questions) was to put into their own words their understanding of implementation science in trials. 43.5% (n = 154) did not answer, did not know or were unsure of how to answer the question. The remaining 54% of respondents defined in free text a range of understandings related to linking ‘trial’ and ‘research’ with ‘practice’ and translation into ‘care’ outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S1). There was also an indication that implementation science identifies ‘barriers’ to the intervention and implementation of the trial and provides strategies, processes, and designs to enhance trials.

Familiarity with implementation science in trials

The majority of participants (56.7%) were ‘somewhat familiar’ with implementation science in trials but 28.6% were not at all familiar and only 14.7% were very familiar. Familiarity with implementation science varied depending on years of experience. In early career trialists (<2 years' experience) lack of familiarity was high at 40%. This rate decreased with increasing years of experience but remained consistently above 25% of the workforce even in those with >20 years' experience in trials. Those identifying as chief investigators are most likely to be familiar with implementation science. The roles least familiar were research nurses (56%), trial coordinators (56%), trial sponsors (55%) research officers/assistants (44%), Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) officers (43%). For participants undertaking their research in health services, 34.5% were not at all familiar with implementation science and only 10.3% stating they were very familiar.

Experience with implementation science in trial design

For those who identified with a role in trial design and were somewhat or very familiar with implementation science in trials (n = 216), they were asked to more specifically indicate their experience with a range of implementation methodologies related to trial design. Whilst awareness of methods such as hybrid implementation effectiveness trials, implementation theories, models and frameworks and implementation outcomes was apparent in approximately half the respondents, direct experience was low (14.5 – 26.1%) and many (25 – 34%) were not at all familiar with these methods (Fig. 1). For the minority of participants with direct experience with using implementation theories/models/frameworks or outcomes, the most commonly used were determinant and evaluation frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework, followed by implementation theories and process models (Supplementary Table 1).Fig. 1Familiarity and experience with implementation science methodologies in trial design and conduct.

Interest in training

Interest in training across different topics was dependent on the degree of established familiarity with implementation science and is shown in Fig. 3. Those with none to some familiarity in implementation science had similar moderate to high interest in training topics with the highest preference related to implementation trial designs, research translation, planning for impact, sustaining the intervention after trial completion and supporting trial recruitment and retention. When this group's interests were further compared between those from metropolitan versus regional/rural settings, the types of topics of interest reported were similar however the degree of interest was consistently lower in those from regional/rural settings (Mean interest score metropolitan vs regional/rural: Trial design 3.45 vs 3.23; Recruitment strategies 3.35 vs 3.13; Sustainment 3.38 vs 3.03; Scalability 3.29 vs 2.80). Previous training in implementation science for trials

Of the 300 participants who answered questions about previous training, the majority (73.3%) had not received any previous implementation science learning or professional development. Of the 80 respondents who had been exposed to previous training, 70% had used up to 3 different modes of training including in-person workshop (63%) and “on the job” training (56%) and self-guided online or hard copy textbooks (48%). Over 90% found the training they had received useful in their current role in clinical trials.

Preferences and barriers for implementation science training

Online (61.6%) or hybrid (54.7%) approaches were most preferred with only one third preferring all face to face. Short workshops were the most popular preferred training format (55.4%), followed by beginner level one-day workshop (47.4%), and recorded conference presentations (45.7%) (Table 3). Three quarters (75%) of participants indicated two or more barriers to accessing implementation training if it were made available. Perceived impact of implementation science training on the clinical trial workforce

Over 80% of participants perceived competency in implementation science research and practice will become essential to the broader clinical trial workforce in the future. When asked what the potential biggest impact(s) of upskilling more people in the clinical trial workforce in the use of implementation science for clinical trials would be, the responses were overwhelmingly positive with descriptive words such as ‘better’ and ‘improved’ trials frequently used and common references to increased speed such as trials becoming ‘faster’ or more ‘efficient’ or ‘outcomes realised’ sooner. The Leximancer concept map (Supplementary Fig. S2) revealed greater realisation of evidence-based practice changes, better quality trial design, translation, execution, an improvement of care and health outcomes and research impact as well as reducing research waste and being more likely to attract future research funding. However, perceived benefits of workforce training in implementation science were not universal with 10 (7%) responses indicating that it may not be a priority in time and resource limited environments, that it is a specialised field that may not be appropriate for broad workforce upskill, or that it is costly without proven impact.

Discussion

This needs assessment of a clinically, professionally and geographically diverse clinical trial workforce and consumer partners reflects that implementation science is still an emerging field in this context and there is an appetite for training and support. Over 40% of respondents were unsure how to define their understanding of implementation science in the context of clinical trials. Most participants were familiar with some methodologies but had limited direct experience of their own.

Knowledge translation research most commonly targets clinician uptake of evidence in practice and rarely considers the role of trialists in the implementation effort at earlier stages of evidence generation [19,20]. The concept of ‘shared responsibility’ for the implementation of trial evidence [19] requires the enablement of all stakeholders across the research pipeline to have the skills and confidence to design and conduct activities that target improved translation. In a recent survey of trialists perceptions of implementation of trial findings after stroke rehabilitation, there was limited pre-planning of translation strategies in trial protocols and reliance mostly on anecdotal reports of uptake and adoption of findings in practice [21]. Trialists and trial teams need skills and confidence to expertly engage with methodologies embedded into trial protocols to pre-emptively design for translation. Implementation science offers many theories, models and frameworks for the design of interventions and implementation strategies with various levers targeted at an individual, organisational and systems level [20]. This study illustrates the significant variation in trial workforce uptake and utility of implementation methodologies that support trial conduct. It remains unclear how research teams make decisions about what theories, models or frameworks to use and how they integrate these into trial protocols.

In addition to practical support, infrastructure that supports stronger implementation science integration with clinical trials is critical [22]. We know that clinical trial registration enhances the quality of design, conduct and reporting [23]. However, there is no mandated requirement within clinical trial registration forms to describe implementation methods, outcomes and strategies. In addition, RCT (and RCT protocol) reporting guidelines can play a role in elevating the visibility and rigor of implementation science methods. For example, the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) [24,25] provides a structured approach that encourages the clear distinguishment between effectiveness and implementation components of the trial. The consideration of such guidelines in RCT protocol development can help ensure that implementation science methods are embedded from the outset and reported throughout the trial lifecycle [26]. This in turn has potential to lift the much-needed standardisation and consolidation of implementation data to support identification of the patterns associated with implementation success [27,28].

Reviews that scope the reporting of implementation strategies and outcomes in RCTs suggest limited uptake to date [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. There appears to be limited evidence of robust testing of relationships between implementation strategies and trial outcomes [29] creating uncertainty in translatability of results. However, a surge of popularity in innovative hybrid implementation-effectiveness trials [30] indicates that embedding implementation characteristics in trial design is being embraced by trialists. In our study, the perception that using the methods had positively impacted the translatability was clear and is likely to further motivate ongoing use by those teams with experience in implementation methodologies.

There was moderate to high interest for training and support across a broad range of topics, particularly relating to trial design, recruitment strategies, sustainment, translation and scalability all of which are well aligned to translatability of trial results. Most respondents had never received any previous training in implementation science. Barriers to receiving training were not unexpected and included cost, travel and time, hence preferred formats were short online/hybrid workshops and recordings of expert presentations. There was a reflected need for general foundational training that raises awareness of implementation science in trial contexts and introductory skills rather than high level masterclasses. Trial workforce challenges in accessing training are complex particularly in regional and rural areas with motivation for clinical trial capability training dependent on the amount of established or anticipated local trial activity [34]. This study identified that the clinical trial workforce in regional and rural areas is interested in the same types of implementation topics as those in metropolitan areas, however the strength of interest was generally lower. This could reflect competing priorities for training when sites are at earlier stages of trial maturity and capacity [34]. Around a third of the respondents were open to working with a mentor. Mentoring [35] and implementation journey coaching [36] has been shown as an effective strategy for engaging clinical trial teams at different stages of trial team maturity and for increasing the impact of knowledge translation projects in the health service [11,37].

There was a small but notable cohort of the workforce who were very familiar and experienced with implementation science. A more formalised role for implementation scientists in trial teams is emerging which may support trial conduct as well as the planning for translation, scalability and impact [38]. Whilst these roles may already act as expert advisors to trial teams, they still reported having training needs themselves related, most commonly, to high level statistical analysis of implementation trials.

The clinical trial workforce is a critical cog in the wheels of evidence generation, implementation and translation and advocacy for accredited recognition for a clinical trialist profession is gaining momentum [39]. Currently, Australia lacks an education pathway to provide the specialist skills necessary to conduct clinical trials that embed implementation science into intervention design and outcomes [39]. There is a need for tailored multi-modal training to meet the variation in needs of the sites, workforce and changing maturity of trial teams [35]. Including core competencies in implementation science and practice aligned with research design, feasibility and trial conduct may act as a lever to bring implementation science to the research pipeline through capacity and capability building [35,40,41].

Our study sought the perspectives of trialists in Australia and New Zealand, therefore may not represent views globally, which warrants further exploration. The survey was widely distributed and gathered representation from all states nationally but with an unknown number of people working in clinical trials, a response rate was unable to be determined. The reliance on free text responses to describe experiences with methodologies may have introduced bias given there was a significant proportion of non-responses.

Conclusion

Across the clinical trial workforce there is an appetite for capacity and capability building in implementation science and practice that will target greater translatability of clinical trials. There is potential for advancing the field of implementation science across the sector through innovative integration of implementation methodologies into trial design.
