Disinfection Byproducts in Desalinated Water: Identifying Toxicity Drivers through Nontarget Analysis
Authors: Yufei Chen, Yan Zhang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

Driven by climate change and population growth, severe freshwater shortages have made seawater desalination an increasingly important source of drinking water worldwide. Currently, the global desalination capacity exceeds 115 million cubic meters per day, providing drinking water for more than 300 million people, and this scale continues to expand. However, the growing reliance on desalinated water presents specific water-quality concerns. While advanced desalination processes, such as reverse osmosis (RO) and multistage flash (MSF) distillation, efficiently remove most natural organic matter (NOM), they cannot completely eliminate trace levels of halide ions, particularly bromide (Br–) and iodide (I–). During mandatory postdesalination disinfection steps with chlorine, chloramine, or ozone, these residual halides are oxidized by disinfectants to form reactive halogen species (e.g., HOBr, HOI), which subsequently react with trace organic precursors to generate brominated and iodinated DBPs. , Toxicological studies consistently show that these emerging halocarbon and nitrogenous DBPs are much more cytotoxic and genotoxic than their common chlorinated counterparts. , Therefore, it is essential to systematically characterize the formation and occurrence of these toxic DBPs in desalinated water to ensure public health safety.

Historically, most DBP research has focused on conventional surface water and groundwater sources. Consequently, systematic data on DBP formation in desalinated water remains scarce, particularly regarding how different desalination processessuch as RO, MSF, and MEDinteract with various disinfectants, including chlorine, chloramine, ozone, and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. Because the precursor pool in desalinated water differs significantly from that of surface water, it is difficult to predict which specific DBPs will form. Addressing this knowledge gap requires advanced analytical approaches, such as gas chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (GC-HR-MS), to perform nontarget screening and identify previously uncharacterized DBPs.

In this issue of Environment & Health, El-Shorbagy et al. address this critical gap by presenting a comprehensive nontarget and toxicological assessment of DBPs in desalinated water. The authors collected real water samples from three major desalination plants in the United Arab Emirates and conducted controlled laboratory disinfection experiments to simulate different treatment scenarios. Using GC-HR-MS, they identified 55 DBPs, including several compounds reported for the first time in desalinated water, such as a novel 3,4-dibromo-1-methylpyrazole. Furthermore, by integration of these chemical findings with mammalian cell cytotoxicity assays, the study systematically compared the toxicity profiles of waters generated by different desalination and disinfection combinations. This dual chemical and toxicological approach provides a crucial data set for understanding the health risks associated with desalinated drinking water.

The toxicological analyses revealed that brominated phenols, particularly di- and tribromophenols, induced significantly higher cytotoxicity than did the currently regulated di- and trihaloacetic acids. Among the desalination technologies compared, RO-produced water exhibited greater overall cytotoxicity than that from thermal distillation processes (MSF and MED). Within the RO treatments, the combination of ozone and chlorine resulted in the highest level of toxicity. This elevated toxicity was consistent with the increased formation of toxic nitrogenous DBPs, notably bromonitromethanes as well as dibromoacetic acid.

Notably, when compared to disinfected surface waters from the United States and Europe, the total DBP concentrations and whole-water cytotoxicity of treated desalinated samples were consistently lower. The quantitative data demonstrated that the total DBP levels and the whole-water cytotoxicity of the treated desalinated samples were lower than those typically found in disinfected surface water from the United States and Europe. This objective assessment indicates that, while desalination alters the precursor pool and shifts DBP speciation toward brominated compounds, it ultimately provides a drinking water source with a relatively low overall health risk.

These findings offer practical guidance for process optimization at desalination facilities. For RO plants, operators should carefully evaluate and optimize their disinfection combinationsfor example, by reconsidering the use of ozone followed by chlorine, to limit the formation of potent nitrogenous and brominated DBPs. Furthermore, future water quality monitoring frameworks would benefit from incorporating emerging brominated DBPs, such as the newly identified halopyrazoles. Developing more sensitive targeted analytical methods and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models will be important for accurately identifying the specific drivers of toxicity in desalinated water and continually safeguarding public health.
