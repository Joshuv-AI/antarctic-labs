Toward a fuller statistical picture: a complementary analysis of HFNC versus NIV
Authors: Aurio Fajardo-Campoverdi, Adrián Gallardo, Ângelo Roncalli Miranda Rocha
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the physiological crossover trial by Vieira et al. comparing high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and non-invasive ventilation (NIV) in stabilized hypercapnic exacerbation [1]. The authors report that HFNC at 50 L/min (HFNC50) was non-inferior to NIV in reducing diaphragmatic effort, and interpret an NIV-associated increase in end-expiratory lung volume (EELV) as an “undesired effect” that may partly explain both the lack of diaphragmatic unloading observed with NIV and patients’ preference for HFNC. We believe this central argument deserves closer statistical scrutiny.

Among all physiological parameters reported in Table 2, EELV is the only variable that was not included in the authors’ own Bayesian analysis, which spanned ten other parameters (Supplementary eTable-3). For EELV, only a frequentist post-hoc comparison (p = 0.004 vs HFNC30, p = 0.024 vs HFNC50) supports the claim that NIV promotes greater lung hyperinflation than HFNC.

To address this asymmetry, we reanalyzed the published summary statistics for ΔEELV (mean ± SD, n = 15) using a standard Bayesian approach: a normal likelihood with unknown variance and a non-informative Jeffreys prior, equivalent to the classical Student’s t-distribution used in frequentist confidence intervals – matching the rigor the authors applied elsewhere in the article. Under this standard model, NIV showed a higher posterior probability of a greater EELV increase than HFNC50 (P = 97.8%) and HFNC30 (P = 99.5%), consistent with the authors’ interpretation. As only aggregate statistics were available, conditions were modeled as independent samples rather than through the original paired crossover structure – a conservative simplification, since ignoring the (typically positive) intra-patient correlation likely underestimates the true precision of these estimates.

However, because EELV was never subjected to a robustness check, we additionally examined how this evidence behaves under a heavier-tailed error distribution – obtained by lowering the posterior’s degrees of freedom from n-1 to 2, holding the mean and standard error fixed – appropriate given the small subgroup (n = 15) and a standard deviation up to three times the mean for this variable – both of which increase vulnerability to a few extreme individual responses. Across a continuum of assumed degrees of freedom (Fig. 1), the probability that NIV increases EELV more than HFNC50 – the comparison underlying the article’s central argument against NIV – falls from P = 97.8% under the standard model to P = 95% under a heavy-tailed model (df = 4), dropping below the threshold approximately equivalent to a two-tailed p < 0.05 (P = 97.5%) across nearly the entire plausible range of assumptions. The NIV-HFNC30 comparison, in contrast, remained robust to this same check (99.5% under the normal model, 97.9% under the heavy-tailed model).Fig. 1Continuous sensitivity of the EELV evidence to the assumed tail shape of the posterior distribution, from heavy-tailed (df = 2, robust to extreme values) to the standard normal-theory model (df = n−1, the same rigour applied by the authors to all other physiological parameters; dotted vertical line). This pattern is a reminder that a frequentist p < 0.05 does not guarantee a stable or robust level of Bayesian evidence. Lindley’s classical demonstration that statistical significance and posterior support can diverge under reasonable changes in assumptions – most famously formalized for large samples as Lindley’s paradox [2] – illustrates the same underlying principle in a different regime: here, a small sample with a standard deviation up to three times the mean yields posterior probabilities that are highly sensitive to the assumed tail behavior, even though the original frequentist test reported significance. A p-value alone conveys no information about how readily that significance would erode under an equally defensible alternative set of assumptions – precisely the mismatch Lindley cautioned against when treating p < 0.05 as a fixed, transferable unit of evidence.

The asymmetric fragility should temper the causal narrative built around EELV. Two further points reinforce this caution. First, the endpoint the authors themselves designated as primary – absolute TFdi – showed no significant difference between any of the four conditions (p = 0.113) [1]. The narrative that NIV “failed” to unload the diaphragm rests entirely on a secondary, high-variance metric, not on the prespecified primary outcome. Second, an increase in EELV during pressure-support ventilation is the expected physiological consequence of externally applied PEEP counteracting intrinsic PEEP and dynamic hyperinflation – a mechanism well established in the NIV literature, and one the authors themselves invoke for HFNC in their introduction. Labelling this increase as inherently “undesired” is therefore an interpretive choice rather than an empirical finding; no correlation between ΔEELV and ΔTFdi, or between ΔEELV and patient preference, was reported to substantiate a harmful mechanistic link.

We do not dispute that HFNC was preferred by patients and offers genuine comfort advantages, nor that it may be a valuable adjunct, particularly between NIV sessions [3]. However, NIV remains the guideline-recommended first-line therapy for hypercapnic COPD exacerbation, with mortality and intubation benefits established in larger trials [4,5]. A small, single-center physiological study may not be sufficient to reframe NIV as physiologically inferior to HFNC without applying to its key argument the same statistical rigor used for the article’s other conclusions. In physiology, as in statistics, appearances of certainty often dissolve under closer inspection; we offer this reanalysis not to settle the debate, but to keep it open.
