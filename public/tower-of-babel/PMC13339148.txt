Initiating Photocontrolled Atom Transfer Radical Polymerization from a Redox-Activated Functional Group
Authors: Isobelle F. McClements, Megan E. Driscoll, Brett P. Fors
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Initiating controlled radical polymerizations directly from common functional groups is desirable for synthesizing advanced polymer architectures, such as surface grafts and polymer conjugates. Previously, photocontrolled atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) has been performed with alkyl halide initiators, limiting its utility in these advanced polymer applications. In this study, we demonstrate the initiation of photo-ATRP directly from the α-carbon of primary amines. We employ an iridium photocatalyst to trigger radical deamination of redox-activated primary amines, initiating controlled radical polymerization in the presence of an exogenous bromide source under visible light irradiation. The resulting polymers have narrow molar mass distributions (Đ ∼ 1.2–1.3), good agreement between theoretical and experimental molar masses, exceptionally high α-chain-end fidelities (>99%), and active bromide ω-chain-ends. This study lays the groundwork for utilizing non-alkyl halide initiators to initiate well-controlled ATRP polymerizations, opening the door for the use of redox-activated functional groups as ATRP initiators for creating advanced polymer architectures.

Controlled radical polymerizations (CRPs) are powerful tools to synthesize polymers with targetable molar masses, low dispersity (Đ), high chain-end fidelity, and the ability to chain extend to make block polymers. One of the most widely used methods of controlled radical polymerization is atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP). ATRP operates through a reversible-deactivation redox process between an alkyl halide species and a catalyst. Under thermal conditions, the catalyst alternates between oxidation states to mediate the homolytic cleavage and reformation of the carbon–halogen bond, with propagation only occurring when the carbon–halogen bond is cleaved, generating an alkyl radical on the propagating chain. This controlled activation–deactivation mechanism minimizes termination events and allows for controlled chain growth by primarily keeping chains in the halide-capped deactivated state. , Due to the ability to yield precisely controlled polymer architectures, ATRP has attracted interest from industry, with over 1,500 patents filed with a wide range of applications, as well as having utilization in pharmaceutical and biomedical research. ,

While ATRP is traditionally performed under thermal conditions, in 2012 Hawker and co-workers developed a method for photocontrolled ATRP under visible light irradiation. This strategy utilized a tris­(2-phenylpyridine)iridium (Ir­(ppy)3) photocatalyst to reduce an alkyl bromide initiator under visible light excitation, yielding Br– and an alkyl radical, which initiated polymerization. The propagating polymer chain-end was reversibly deactivated via oxidation by IrIV and capping with Br–, giving rise to photocontrolled ATRP in situ (Figure ).

Initiation of ATRP from a standard alkyl bromide initiator and from a redox-activated functional group, both leading into the standard photo-ATRP mechanism.

Photocontrolled ATRP has gained popularity for its avoidance of harsh stimuli, such as high temperatures, by leveraging visible light to trigger the reversible activation–deactivation of the polymerization. − Photocontrolled polymerizations allow for spatiotemporal control over the reaction as polymerization only proceeds under exposure to light, which has utility in orthogonal catalysis and patterning. ,

Furthermore, the Ir photocatalyst has higher functional group tolerance than the Cu catalyst typically used in thermal ATRP, allowing for the polymerization of monomers inaccessible in thermal ATRP, such as methacrylic acids.

While considerable research efforts have been devoted to advancing ATRP catalysts, − little work has focused on expanding the scope of initiating species. Researchers have reported that a reverse ATRP method can be used to initiate ATRP from a non-alkyl halide initiator, though this approach suffers from several limitations. − Reverse ATRP utilizes the thermal decomposition of a conventional radical initiator, such as azobis­(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN), to initiate polymerization. The radical species then abstracts a halogen from a transition metal catalyst in the higher oxidation state, often Cu­(II), to form the dormant species and the reduced catalyst which enters the standard ATRP cycle. This method helps to overcome the air-sensitivity of the Cu­(I) catalyst traditionally used for ATRP, but requires temperatures exceeding 100 °C, a greater than stoichiometric amount of Cu­(II), and the molecular weights of the resulting polymers cannot be changed independently from the amount of Cu­(II) catalyst used. Furthermore, the α-chain-end functionality is limited, as the initiating species are confined to standard free radical initiators. Currently, the reported methods for thermal- or photocontrolled-ATRP require an alkyl halide-functionalized substrate to initiate polymerization. For instance, to graft ATRP from a target substrate, an alkyl halide initiator must first be installed before polymerization. − The synthesis of these initiating species typically involve multiple steps, difficult purifications, issues with selectivity, and changes the structure around the initiating site.

Methods have been developed for initiating a different type of CRPreversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT)directly from common functional groups, such as C–H bonds, carboxylic acids, and amines, allowing for unique polymer architectures to be synthesized. − In contrast, the initiation of ATRP from common functional groups has been largely unexplored. ATRP has been reported to yield polymers with higher α-chain-end fidelity than RAFT, produce odorless polymers, and allows for an array of unique postpolymerization end-group modifications. As such, initiation of ATRP using common functional groups would be greatly advantageous.

In 2025, our group reported the initiation of a photocontrolled RAFT polymerization directly from the α-carbon of primary amines. This method utilized amine-derived Katritzky salts, which formed electron-donor–acceptor (EDA) complexes with a dithiocarbamate electron donor. The Katritzky salt initiator underwent radical deamination after a visible light induced single electron transfer to form an alkyl radical which initiated a photocontrolled RAFT polymerization, using the dithiocarbamate electron donor as a chain-transfer agent. This work allowed for the grafting of polymers directly from the α-carbon of a primary amine without the installation of an initiating group.

Inspired by this work as well as Hawker’s original photo-ATRP, we hypothesized that Katritzky salts could also be used as photo-ATRP initiators. Specifically, we envisioned that these salts could undergo radical deamination upon reduction by photoexcited Ir­(III), yielding 2,4,6-triphenylpyridine and an alkyl radical, which could add into monomer to initiate polymerization. − The active chain-end would then be oxidized by Ir­(IV), regenerating ground-state Ir­(III) and the bromide-capped polymer in the presence of TBABr. We posited that the polymerization could then enter the standard photo-ATRP mechanism reported by Hawker in 2012 (Figure ). This strategy not only initiates ATRP directly from the α-carbon of a primary amine using a facile, one-step synthesis but also sets precedent for the initiation of ATRP from common functional groups without requiring prior modification to an alkyl halide. Furthermore, the synthesis of these Katritzky salt initiators is highly selective for primary amines, allowing for increased selectivity over other nucleophiles compared to the acyl substitutions commonly used for ATRP initiator installation. − This work opens the door to a tunable method of initiating ATRP from various redox active groups by shuttling a non-alkyl halide initiator into photo-ATRP equilibrium.

To begin our studies, l-phenylalanine ethyl ester (5a) was chosen as a model initiator due to the visibility of the chain-end using 1H NMR spectroscopy and its similarity in structure to reported initiators for photo-ATRP of methacrylates. , The N-terminus was transformed to a redox-active Katritzky salt through a reaction with 2,4,6-triphenylpyrylium. − When the Katritzky salt was exposed to methyl methacrylate (MMA), tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBABr), and Ir­(ppy)3 in dimethylacetamide (DMAc), we obtained a polymer with excellent agreement between the experimental and theoretical molar masses (M n) and low Đ (Table , entry 1). Furthermore, the α-chain-end fidelity of the resulting polymer was determined to be >99% through 1H NMR spectroscopy and GPC analysis (Figure S30). Notably, when the reaction mixture excludes initiator, no polymerization occurs. When the polymerization proceeds with no TBABr or Ir­(ppy)3 added, a loss of control is observed (Table , entries 7–8). This indicates that the reaction is mediated by the photocatalyst and relies on the added bromide for reversible deactivation. While initiation may occur through direct photolysis of the Katritzky salt as shown in entry 7, we believe that the reduction of the Katritzky salt by excited Ir­(ppy)3 would occur much more rapidly. To confirm the reduction of the Katritzky salt by the photocatalyst, we performed photoluminescence quenching studies. We observed concentration dependent photoluminescence quenching, which indicates that Ir­(ppy)3 is reducing the initiator (Figure S11).

To ensure that a range of molar masses could be successfully targeted, we varied the ratio of monomer to initiator ([M]:[I], Table , entries 1–6). Through this method, we synthesized well-defined polymers with predictable M n spanning 2.5 kg/mol to 20 kg/mol while maintaining narrow Đ. However, we observed low molar mass tailing as we further increase the monomer to initiator ratio. To mitigate this, we decreased the catalyst concentration from 0.01 mol % to 0.0019–0.0025 mol % for molar mass targets 20 kg/mol or above, which narrowed the Đ (Table S5). We hypothesize overloading the photocatalyst resulted in a high radical concentration, which led to undesirable termination events during the polymerization. We found that molar masses ≤ 20 kg/mol could be obtained while maintaining Đ < 1.3. For molar masses >20 kg/mol, we noticed an increase in Đ while maintaining good molar mass matching (Table , entry 6). These results are comparable to the original report of photo-ATRP mediated by Ir­(ppy)3 using an alkyl bromide initiator, where Đ were reported to be between 1.19 and 1.25 with the highest molar mass being 20 kg/mol. We then sought to expand the scope of monomers by polymerization of benzyl (Bn), ethyl (Et), tertbutyl (tBu), and methoxyethyl (ME) methacrylate which also yielded well controlled polymerizations (Table , entries 9–12).

Next, we investigated if initiation and polymerization were occurring via our proposed reaction mechanism. First, we probed whether the kinetic profile of our polymerization agreed with standard CRP kinetics, which would be expected if we had efficient initiation and the propagation followed the proposed standard photo-ATRP mechanism. When monitoring monomer conversion over time, we observed the expected first-order kinetics as well as a linear relationship between conversion and molar mass while maintaining low Đ (Figure a and b). This first-order kinetic profile is indicative of a constant radical concentration, which is one of the characteristics of ATRP. Similarly to prior reports of photo-ATRP mediated by Ir­(ppy)3, we began to observe an increase in dispersity as we exceeded a conversion of 60%. We hypothesize that this is due to an accumulation of irreversible termination events and photocatalyst degradation over long reaction times. We then probed the phototemporal control of the polymerization to determine if the propagation only occurred in the presence of light. By performing cycles of on/off irradiation, we found that conversion and molar mass growth was only observed during periods where the light was on, confirming that the reaction can be temporally controlled and providing evidence that the reaction proceeds through a photocatalytic cycle (Figure c).

Furthermore, we wanted to confirm that the polymer chains were being capped by the exogenous Br– source and that the polymerization was truly proceeding through the prior reported photo-ATRP mechanism. To test this, we analyzed a precipitated polymer using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization–Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) spectrometry. The MALDI-TOF spectrum showed peaks which corresponded to bromide-capped poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) initiated by the deaminated phenylalanine-derived Katritzky salt (Figure a). A second species with molar masses corresponding to hydride-capped chains was also present, which could be indicative of chain death or degradation of the labile bromide chain-end during MALDI-TOF analysis (Figure S29). , To confirm that our polymers are reversibly deactivated with a Br– chain end and that there is not significant chain termination, we performed a chain extension (Table S10). When a PMMA macroinitiator isolated through precipitation was exposed to BnMA and Ir­(ppy)3 in DMAc, a block copolymer with low Đ and good matching between theoretical and experimental molar masses was synthesized (Figure b). This confirmed not only the presence of the bromide chain-end, but also that all chains could be reactivated to continue propagation and that there was not a significant number of terminated C–H chain-ends. Furthermore, this demonstrates that only one equivalent of exogenous bromide is required for complete deactivation of the chain-ends.

Finally, we wanted to broaden the scope of initiators and confirm that this system is effective for a range of amines. We primarily investigated amino acid ethyl esters as substrates due to their similarity in structure to common ATRP initiators. We were able to initiate from select amino acid ethyl esters with good molar mass matching, low Đ, and exceptionally high chain-end fidelities. It is important to note that amino acid ethyl esters with redox active side groups, such as tryptophan and tyrosine, experience a loss in control (Figure g-h). We hypothesize that these redox-active side groups can be oxidized by Ir­(IV), leading to unwanted side reactions or catalyst death. − Notably, we found that it was also possible to initiate a moderately controlled polymerization from commercially available amines that are α- to radical stabilizing groups (Figure e-f), though we observed an expected decrease in control due to the difference in structure of the initiating species and the methacrylate monomer, leading to unbalanced rates of initiation, propagation, and deactivation. , Katritzky salt initiators derived from unactivated amines did not initiate polymerization (Figure S19). To further understand why these unactivated initiators did not initiate polymerization we performed cyclic voltammetry studies (Figure S12). We found that while an activated initiator, 5b, exhibited irreversible reduction, unactivated initiator, 5i, exhibited reversible reduction. This indicates that the activated Katritzky salts undergo rapid mesolytic cleavage upon reduction yielding a propagating radical, while the unactivated initiators undergo a slower process which does not lead to effective initiation. This allows for selective polymerization from activated primary amines in the presence of unactivated amines.

In summary, we have demonstrated the initiation of a photocontrolled atom transfer radical polymerization directly from the α-carbon of primary amines. We achieved a system with low Đ, excellent molar mass matching and α-chain-end fidelities, active bromide ω-chain-ends, phototemporal control, and a broad scope of activated amine-derived initiators. This method expands the scope of ATRP to tunable non-alkyl halide initiators, a previously unexplored concept, showing that it is possible to utilize redox active groups as initiators that can be shuttled into the photo-ATRP cycle. This study lays the groundwork for initiating ATRP directly from common functional groups, allowing for the synthesis of advanced polymer architectures.
