Assessment of malaria prevalence and determinants among clinically suspected individuals attending Dejen Primary Hospital, East Gojjam Zone, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Ayele Emnetu, Asmamaw Habtamu, Agumas Lemlemu, Solomon Yeshanew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia, despite ongoing efforts toward its control and elimination. Generating updated evidence on its epidemiology and identifying the factors linked to infection are vital for guiding targeted interventions. This study assessed the prevalence of malaria and its associated determinants among clinically suspected individuals attending Dejen Primary Hospital, Dejen District, northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was carried out from April to November 2024. A total of 385 individuals presenting malaria-related symptoms at Dejen Primary Hospital were enrolled. Data on socio-demographic characteristics and malaria-related variables were gathered using a structured questionnaire. Blood samples were collected and examined for malaria parasites through standard microscopy procedures. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify key determinants of malaria infection.ResultsOverall, 46.2% of the study participants tested positive for malaria. Plasmodium falciparum was the predominant species, accounting for 56.1% of malaria cases. Individuals without formal education had higher odds of malaria infection (AOR = 2.16; 95% CI 0.46–3.28; p = 0.001). Similarly, residing in close proximity to mosquito breeding sites was identified as a significant determinant (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 0.76–3.25, p = 0.020). Moreover, participants who did not use insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) (AOR = 1.72; 95% CI 0.33–1.84; p = 0.001) or did not receive indoor residual spraying (IRS) (AOR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.27–2.05; p = 0.035) were more likely to be infected compared to those who utilized these preventive measures.ConclusionMalaria remains highly prevalent among symptomatic individuals attending Dejen Primary Hospital. The findings highlight that low educational status, living near mosquito breeding sites, and inadequate utilization of key preventive measures significantly increase the risk of infection. Background

Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in many tropical and subtropical regions. According to the World Malaria Report 2024, an estimated 263 million malaria cases and nearly 600,000 deaths occurred globally in 2023 [1]. These figures reflect a stagnation in progress since 2017, driven by climate variability, increasing vector resistance, supply chain disruptions, and ongoing humanitarian crises in endemic settings [1, 2]. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to bear more than 94% of the global malaria burden, with Plasmodium falciparum responsible for the majority of severe clinical outcomes and deaths [1–3].

Ethiopia remains among the most malaria-affected countries in Africa. Malaria transmission in the country is typically unstable and heterogeneous due to diverse topography and climatic conditions [4, 5]. Approximately 75% of the landmass is considered malaria-endemic, placing nearly 70% of the population at risk [6]. Despite sustained investments in diagnostic capacity, vector control interventions, and community-based health services, the country has experienced a resurgence of malaria cases in recent years [6–8]. National surveillance data indicate that over 7.3 million malaria cases and 1,157 deaths were reported in 2024, representing the highest annual caseload recorded in the past seven years [6].

The epidemiology of malaria in Ethiopia is strongly influenced by eco-climatic variation. Stable transmission predominantly occurs in lowland (Kolla) areas below 1,500 m above sea level, while mid-altitude (Woina Dega) areas (1,500–2,500 m) experience unstable and epidemic-prone transmission due to seasonal climatic fluctuations [8, 9]. Highland (Dega) areas above 2,500 m have historically been considered malaria-free; however, recent warming trends have facilitated the expansion of vector habitats into highland fringe areas, indicating a shifting altitudinal risk profile [9]. The country exhibits two major transmission seasons: September to December, following the main rainy season, and April to May, after the minor rains, both associated with increased vector density and intensified human movement related to agricultural activities [6, 10].

Multiple interrelated factors continue to influence malaria transmission and control outcomes in Ethiopia. Ecological changes, including irrigation expansion, deforestation, construction activities, and rapid urbanization, create favorable breeding habitats for Anopheles mosquitoes and alter human–vector contact patterns [10–12]. Socioeconomic determinants, such as limited access to healthcare, delayed treatment-seeking behavior, and reliance on traditional medicine, further contribute to disease persistence in rural communities [13, 14]. Behavioral factors, including inconsistent use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), outdoor evening activities, and declining indoor residual spraying (IRS) coverage, also increase transmission risk [14]. Although access to rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) has improved, challenges remain in ensuring timely diagnosis, treatment adherence, and sustained vector control coverage.

Recent conflict-related disruptions, particularly in parts of the Amhara region, have introduced additional challenges to malaria control efforts. Insecurity has affected disease surveillance, interrupted IRS implementation, delayed ITN distribution, and strained healthcare delivery systems, especially in rural districts with limited infrastructure [15]. Consequently, several areas in the region, including the present study setting, have reported increases in malaria incidence during 2023–2024, raising concerns about local outbreaks and the effectiveness of ongoing interventions [4, 16].

Although Dejen district is recognized as an area with seasonal malaria transmission, recent epidemiological data remain limited, and no updated health facility-based assessment has been conducted. The lack of current, localized evidence constrains decision-makers in evaluating disease burden, identifying at-risk populations, detecting changes in parasite species composition, and implementing targeted control strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of malaria and identify associated determinants among clinically suspected individuals attending Dejen Primary Hospital in East Gojjam Zone, northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Description of study area

The study was conducted at Dejen Primary Hospital, located in Dejen town, the capital of Dejen District, East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia. The town lies approximately 335 km south of Bahir Dar and about 230 km northwest of Addis Ababa, positioned along the cliff of the Blue Nile Gorge. The district is situated in the geographical coordinates of latitude (38.1239492) and longitude (10.1769828), with elevation ranging from 1,002 to 2,587 m above sea level [17]. The district comprises 22 administrative Kebeles (2 urban and 20 rural) (Fig. 1). The area experiences an average temperature of 20–24 °C and receives 800–1,200 mm of rainfall per year [18]. Based on Ethiopia’s traditional agro-ecological classification, Dejen encompasses highland (41%), midland (31%), and lowland (28%) zones, creating ecological variability conducive to seasonal and unstable malaria transmission [19]. According to the 2023 district health office report, Dejen hosted malaria infection high-risk population groups, including 1,019 pregnant women, 4,093 children under five, 940 infants under one year, 721 people living with HIV, and 22 tuberculosis patients, underscoring the district’s vulnerability to malaria infection (Dejen District Health Office Report; unpublished report).

Study design

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted to assess the prevalence of malaria and associated determinants among individuals clinically suspected of malaria who attended Dejen Primary Hospital, Dejen District, northwest Ethiopia.

Study participants

The study population included all febrile individuals with a body temperature above 37.5 °C who visited the hospital during two distinct periods (April to June and September to November 2024), chosen to coincide with the major and minor malaria transmission seasons, respectively. Individuals who were severely ill and unable to provide consent, or had received anti-malarial treatment within the previous two weeks, were excluded. Both male and female patients across all age groups were included to ensure representation of the population most affected by malaria in the district.

Sample size determination

The single population proportion formula was used to calculate the required sample size. Based on a reported malaria prevalence of 41.2% among febrile individuals attending healthcare facilities in Jawi District [16], the sample size was estimated using the formula: n=Zα/22xp1-pd2, where n: minimum sample size, P: estimation prevalence rate of malaria in the district (0.41), (Zα/2)2: the standard normal variable, and at 95% Confidence Interval (CI) (1.96), d: margin of error (5%) [20]. The estimated sample size was 371 study participants. Five percent of the total estimated sample size for withdrawal or missed data was added, and the sample size increased to 390. Five study participants withdrew from the study, and the final sample size became 385.

Sampling procedure

A consecutive sampling technique was employed to recruit study participants. All individuals who presented to Dejen Primary Hospital with signs and symptoms suggestive of malaria during the two malaria transmission periods were considered eligible. Participants were enrolled until the predetermined sample size of 385 was achieved.

Methods of data collection and quality assurance

Socio-demographic and malaria-related data

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ socio-demographic characteristics including age, sex, residence, family size, marital status, occupation, income, educational level, and type of sleeping space, as well as malaria-related factors such as history of infection, availability and use of ITNs, application of IRS, proximity to mosquito breeding sites, and housing characteristics (walls and roofs). The questionnaire was developed based on relevant literature and expert consultation, then pretested on 5% of the target population (excluded from the main study) to assess content validity and internal consistency. The reliability of the questionnaire was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77, indicating acceptable internal consistency. A back-translation method was applied to ensure accuracy between English and Amharic, and the average completion time was approximately 15 min per participant.

Blood sample collection and processing

Finger-prick blood samples were obtained from each participant by puncturing the central pad of the finger with a sterile disposable lancet. Duplicate thick and thin blood smears were prepared within 15–30 min of collection, following WHO (1991) guidelines [21]. Thin smears were fixed with 100% methanol, and both thick and thin smears were stained with 3% Giemsa solution for 30 min, then rinsed with tap water and air-dried in an upright position. Thick smears were examined under 100 × oil immersion microscopy to detect malaria parasites, while thin smears were used for species identification if parasites were detected. All slides were examined independently by two senior medical laboratory technologists, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third senior laboratory professional.

Data quality assurance

Standard operating procedures (SOPs) were strictly followed during blood collection, smear preparation, staining, and microscopic examination to ensure the accuracy and reliability of laboratory results. Laboratory reagents and instruments were evaluated by an experienced technologist for quality control. The questionnaire was translated into Amharic to enhance clarity and consistency, and trained laboratory personnel conducted face-to-face interviews with participants or guardians of minors following blood sample collection. These measures collectively ensured the reliability and validity of both laboratory and questionnaire-based data.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were thoroughly checked for completeness, accuracy, and consistency before being entered into Microsoft Excel and subsequently exported to SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics, malaria prevalence, and other study variables. Associations between independent variables (potential determinants) and the dependent variable (malaria infection) were initially assessed using bivariable logistic regression, and variables with p-values < 0.25 were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent explanatory determinants of malaria [22]. Variables with p-values < 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 385 individuals who had malaria-related symptoms and visited Dejen Primary Hospital participated in the study, with a response rate of 98.7%. Among these participants, half (50.1%, n = 193) were males. In terms of age category, the majority (52.7%, n = 203) of the study participants were found greater than 15 years old, and children less than 5 years were accounted for 36.6% of the study participants. Furthermore, significant portions of study participants were students (34.0%, n = 131), practicing farming (44.9%, n = 173), 46.2% lacked formal education, and 69.6% came from rural areas. However, 66.5% of study participants did not utilize ITNs (Table 1).

Malaria prevalence among study participants

The overall malaria prevalence among febrile patients in Dejen Primary Hospital was 46.2% (95% CI 0.75–1.39, p = 0.021) (Table 1). Among these, (56.1%, n = 98) of individuals who were positive for malaria were infected with P. falciparum, and (44.9%, n = 80) with Plasmodium vivax. Whereas, P. falciparum and P. vivax mixed infections were not detected in the present study (Fig. 2).

Malaria infection across the study period

The majority of the malaria infections (63.5%, n = 113) were reported during the major malaria season (September–November), in which the highest infection record (30.3%, n = 54) was reported in the month of November, 2024. Yet, slight infection decrements were recorded in the months of May (12.4%, n = 22) and June (9.0%, n = 16) of the same year (Fig. 3).

Potential explanatory determinants of malaria in the study area

Eleven factors appeared in the final regression with p < 0.05 and were found to be significant explanatory determinants of malaria infection among the target population in the study area. Individuals who did not have formal education (AOR = 2.16; 95% CI 0.46–3.28, p = 0.001) were two times more likely to be infected with malaria than individuals who completed secondary education or higher. The possibility of malaria infection was 2 times higher among individuals who lived within a nearby distance from mosquito breeding sites (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 0.76–3.25, p = 0.020) than those who lived distant. Similarly, study participants whose houses were mud-plastered (AOR = 2.32; 95% CI 0.63–3.91, p = 0.026) were found to have a higher risk of infection. In addition, study participants who did not utilize ITNs (AOR = 1.72; 95% CI 0.33–1.84, p = 0.001) and IRS (AOR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.27–2.05, p = 0.035) were found more likely to be infected with Plasmodium than those who did utilize them. Similarly, individuals who had a history of malaria previously were also found 2 times more likely to be infected with malaria than those who did not (Table 2).

Discussion

Malaria remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia and across sub-Saharan Africa, where the majority of global malaria cases and deaths continue to be recorded. Although substantial progress has been made through the scale-up of ITNs, IRS, improved diagnostic capacity, and widespread use of ACTs, the region still bears the highest burden of infections [1, 2]. In Ethiopia, malaria transmission is characteristically heterogeneous and often unstable due to the country’s diverse ecological and climatic conditions, making communities particularly susceptible to seasonal outbreaks and recurrent resurgences [4, 6, 7]. The persistence of malaria in these settings highlights the importance of generating localized evidence to inform tailored interventions. Thus, the present study provides updated insights into malaria prevalence and key determinants among clinically suspected individuals attending Dejen Primary Hospital in Dejen District, northwest Ethiopia.

This facility-based cross-sectional assessment, therefore, revealed a high prevalence of malaria, confirming that the disease remains a significant public health challenge in the district. The overall prevalence of 46.2% is notably higher than estimates reported in the National Malaria Indicator Survey [23], indicating ongoing transmission despite the intensification of national malaria control and elimination efforts. The magnitude of infection observed aligns with reports from other areas of northwest Ethiopia, such as Jawi District (41.2%) [16] and Teda Health Center (39.0%) [24], suggesting that this wider geographic region may constitute persistent transmission hotspots.

In contrast, the prevalence documented in the present study exceeds findings from several other malaria-endemic regions in Ethiopia. Lower prevalence has been reported from Shewa Robit Health Centre (19.0%) [25], Mizan Tepi University Teaching Hospital (20.7%) [26], Lake Tana and adjacent communities (24.7%) [27], East Shewa Zone (25%) [28], and Hamusit Health Centre (29.0%) [29]. Such variations underline the distinct spatial heterogeneity of malaria transmission in Ethiopia, which is influenced by differences in altitude, local microclimates, vector ecology, population mobility patterns, and disparities in the implementation and utilization of preventive interventions. Moreover, the prevalence observed in Dejen substantially exceeds the national pooled estimate (25.8%) [30]. A recent meta-analysis by Kendie et al. also reported a lower pooled prevalence of malaria among adults in Ethiopia (13.6%) [31], further emphasizing the importance of geographically tailored and context-specific strategies to effectively address the malaria burden in this district.

The limited use of ITNs (49.8%) and the relatively low coverage of IRS (49.6%) observed in this study may have contributed to the persistence of local malaria transmission. Moreover, the environmental and ecological features of Dejen District predominantly midland and lowland topography with numerous water bodies provide highly conducive habitats for Anopheles mosquitoes, thereby enhancing transmission potential [32]. In addition to these ecological and behavioral factors, several broader contextual influences may explain the elevated prevalence reported. The study period coincided with a national malaria resurgence in 2023/2024, during which malaria cases increased by approximately 150% and 120% compared with the corresponding periods in 2021 and 2022, respectively [33].

Furthermore, ongoing armed conflict and substantial population displacement within and across neighboring districts have likely disrupted malaria prevention and control activities, reduced access to essential health services, and increased human-vector contact, collectively contributing to amplified transmission [34, 35]. Additional factors may also explain the observed increase in malaria burden. Notably, shortages of funding for procuring IRS chemicals have hindered the replacement of existing insecticides, whose effectiveness has diminished [36], particularly against the emerging Anopheles stephensi vector [33, 37]. The recent surge in malaria cases has also occurred against a backdrop of extreme weather events. In several parts of the country, heavy rains and flash flooding following one of the most severe droughts in the past four decades have destroyed homes and livestock, creating conditions that further favor mosquito breeding and complicate malaria control efforts [6, 33].

In this study, P. falciparum (56.1%) was more prevalent than P. vivax (44.9%), which aligns with the general malaria pattern in Ethiopia, where P. falciparum typically causes the majority of cases. National reports indicate that P. falciparum accounts for around 72% of confirmed malaria infections, while P. vivax represents roughly 28% [38]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of 79 studies involving over 1.6 million cases across Ethiopia found that approximately two-thirds of infections were due to P. falciparum, with P. vivax responsible for about one-third [39].

The lack of mixed P. falciparum and P. vivax infections in this study may be due to several factors. Mixed infections often occur at low prevalence in moderate transmission areas and may be missed by standard diagnostic methods such as microscopy or rapid tests, especially when one species is dominant, or parasite densities are low [40, 41]. Additionally, local ecological and epidemiological factors, including vector species, biting behavior, seasonality, human immunity, and treatment practices, can favor single-species infections, and proportions of P. falciparum and P. vivax vary by region, altitude, and season [42]. Finally, diagnostic limitations, particularly the reliance on microscopy, may fail to detect low-density P. vivax infections, potentially explaining the absence of mixed infections [43].

Several socio-demographic and environmental factors were identified as significant determinants of Plasmodium infection in this study. Individuals without formal education were at greater risk of malaria (AOR = 2.16; 95% CI 0.46–3.28; p = 0.001), highlighting the crucial role of health education in promoting awareness and preventive behaviors. Proximity to mosquito breeding sites also emerged as a key risk factor, with residents living nearby showing increased susceptibility (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 0.76–3.25; p = 0.020), underscoring the influence of environmental conditions on transmission.

Housing characteristics further affected infection risk; participants residing in mud-plastered houses (AOR = 2.32; 95% CI 0.63–3.91; p = 0.026) or houses with thatched roofs (AOR = 3.72; 95% CI 2.68–5.18; p = 0.001) were significantly more likely to be infected compared to those living in cement-walled or iron-sheet-roofed houses. Additionally, the presence of openings in walls and eaves contributed to increased malaria risk, emphasizing the importance of structural housing improvements as part of control strategies.

Male participants were also at higher risk (AOR = 2.42; 95% CI 1.02–2.67; p = 0.026), likely reflecting greater exposure due to outdoor activities. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for integrated interventions that address both socio-demographic vulnerabilities and environmental determinants to effectively reduce malaria transmission in the study area. The determinants identified in this study are consistent with findings from previous reports across diverse socio-demographic and environmental settings in Ethiopia [4, 16, 24–29].

Conclusion

Malaria remains a significant public health problem in the study area, with notable prevalence among clinically suspected individuals attending the health facility. Low educational status, residence near mosquito breeding sites, male sex, mud-built housing, and inadequate use of insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying were associated with malaria prevalence. These findings highlight the need to strengthen integrated malaria control measures by reinforcing the consistent use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), effective implementation of indoor residual spraying (IRS), and elimination of mosquito breeding sites. In addition, strengthening community awareness and improving housing conditions are essential for reducing malaria transmission.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional design of this study may limit the ability to establish causal relationships or evaluate temporal variations in malaria transmission. The use of a facility-based sample may introduce selection bias, as it excludes individuals who did not seek care, including those with mild or asymptomatic infections or limited access to health services. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader community. Furthermore, data were collected between April and November, a period that corresponds to peak malaria transmission in the area, which may not accurately represent seasonal variations in malaria prevalence throughout the year.
