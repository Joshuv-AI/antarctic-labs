Abstract
. Hanika, Schneider, and Stumme introduced geometric data set as a generalization of metric measure space for the computation of the observable diameter, and extended the observable distance between metric measure spaces to that between geometric data sets. In this paper, we begin by proving the non-separability of the observable distance between geometric data sets. We then extend the box distance between mm-spaces to that between geometric data sets and prove its completeness and non-separability.

1.
Introduction

Gromov
[
3
]
developed the geometry of mm-spaces, based on the concentration of measure phenomenon and theory of collapsing manifold. A triplet
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})
, or simply
X
, is an
mm-space
if
d_{X}
is a complete separable metric on
X
, and
\mu_{X}
is a Borel probability measure on the metric space
(X,d_{X})
. He defined the observable distance
d_{\operatorname{conc}}
based on measure concentration and the box distance
\Box
based on collapsing theory on the set of all mm-spaces, say
\mathcal{X}
, and constructed a natural compactification of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
. Various properties of these distances and spaces are known, with particular attention given to the separability of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
and the completeness and separability of
(\mathcal{X},\Box)
.

Pestov
[
7
]
treated data as mm-spaces and used the observable diameter (see Definition
3.20
) to explain a form of the curse of dimensionality through the concentration of measure phenomenon. However, efficient computation of the observable diameter of an mm-space
X
is not yet known, and straightforward computation over the set of all 1-Lipschitz continuous functions, say
\lip 1(X)
, in the definition takes exponential time with respect to the number
\#X
of set of data sample
X
. He proposed restricting the function family to compute the observable diameter.

Following this, Hanika, Schneider, and Stumme
[
4
]
defined the geometric data set as a generalization of mm-spaces to compute the curse of dimensionality through the observable diameter as follows: A triplet
(X,F_{X},\mu_{X})
, or simply
X
, is defined to be a
geometric data set
if
F_{X}
is a set of real valued functions on
X
such that

d_{X}(x,y)\coloneqq\sup_{f\in F_{X}}|f(x)-f(y)|,\ x,y\in X,

is a complete separable metric, and if
\mu_{X}
is a Borel probability measure on the metric space
(X,d_{X})
. An mm-space can be interpreted as a special case where
F_{X}=\lip 1(X,d_{X})
. They extended the observable distance between mm-spaces to geometric data sets and similarly generalized the observable diameter to geometric data sets. They then confirmed the characteristics of the observable diameter of geometric data sets as a statistical measure.

Hanika et al.’s paper aims to utilize the observable diameter as a statistical measure and does not generally prove the properties of the observable distance. While the separability of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
is known, the topological properties of
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
are entirely unknown. Similarly, although the completeness and separability of
(\mathcal{X},\Box)
are established, the box distance on
\mathcal{D}
has yet to be defined.
In this paper, we prove the non-separability of
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
and further extend the box distance to
\mathcal{D}
, showing its completeness and non-separability.

We state a theorem about the non-separability of
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
. For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, define a
single-point geometric data set

\ast_{A}
as

\ast_{A}\coloneqq\{\ast\},\quad F_{\ast_{A}}=\{\ast\mapsto x\mid x\in A\},\quad\mu_{\ast_{A}}=\delta_{\ast}.

Then, we have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

The set
\{\ast_{A}\mid A\subset\mathbb{N}\}
is an uncountable discrete subset of
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
. In particular,
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
is non-separable.

This is in contrast to the separability of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
.

Next, we extend the box distance
\Box
to that between geometric data sets. The box distance between mm-spaces
X
and
Y
is defined using only distance and measure. Nakajima showed the following representation
[
5
]
:

\Box(X,Y)=\inf_{\pi,S}\max\{1-\pi(S),\dis S\},

where
\pi
runs over all couplings (Definition
2.9
) between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, and
S
runs over all closed sets in
X\times Y
. The
distortion
‘
\dis S
’ of
S
is defined as

\dis S\coloneqq\sup\{|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|;(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S\}.

To extend the box distance
\Box
to
\mathcal{D}
, the value
\dis S
with respect to the distances
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
needs to be replaced by the values with respect to the function families
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
. Introducing an extended pseudo-distance for real-valued functions
f
and
g
on
S
,

{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f,g)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in S}|f(x)-g(x)|,

and denoting its Hausdorff distance by
{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}
, we show the following lemma:

Lemma 1.2
.

We have

\dis S=2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\lip 1(X)\circ\pr_{1},\lip 1(Y)\circ\pr_{2}).

Here,
\pr_{n}
denotes the projection from the product to the
n
-th component for
n=1,2
, and
F\circ f
represents the set of all compositions of each element in the function family
F
with
f
. Using this lemma, we extend the box distance to
\mathcal{D}
as follows:

Definition 1.3
.

We define the box distance between two geometric data sets
X
and
Y
as

\Box(X,Y)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi,S}\max\{1-\pi(S),2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})\},

where
\pi
runs over all couplings between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, and
S
runs over all closed sets in
X\times Y
.

The box distance defined here satisfies the axioms of a distance. Lemma
1.2
implies that the box distance defined here on
\mathcal{D}
is a generalization of the conventional box distance. It is known that the inequality
d_{\operatorname{conc}}\leq\Box
holds between the conventional observable distance and the box distance, and this inequality is easily proved also for geometric data sets.

Furthermore, the box distance on
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the following property.

Theorem 1.4
.

(\mathcal{D},\Box)
is complete but not separable.

The non-separability of
(\mathcal{D},\Box)
is shown by
d_{\operatorname{conc}}\leq\Box
and Theorem
1.1
. In particular, this contrasts with the completeness and separability of
(\mathcal{X},\Box)
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Chapter 2, we introduce basic definitions, such as mm-space and coupling. In Chapter 3, we define geometric data set, describe basic geometric data sets such as the single-point geometric data set, and discuss the (semi)continuity of the observable diameter of geometric data sets. Next, we refer to the definition of the partial order relation between geometric data sets called feature order, and construct the quotient geometric data set, which is the dominated geometric data set, from the subset of features. The quotient geometric data set is used to prove the completeness of the box distance. In Chapter 4, we confirm the non-separability of the observable distance between geometric data sets and apply similar techniques to Nakajima’s characterization
[
5
]
of the observable distance between mm-spaces for that between geometric data sets. In Chapter 5, we extend the box distance to geometric data sets and show that this extension retains completeness but not separability.

In the next paper
[
9
]
, we will consider how much the techniques on
\mathcal{X}
can be applied to the observable distance and box distance on
\mathcal{D}
. Concerning
\mathcal{X}
, the natural compactification of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
was constructed by Gromov
[
3
]
, and Shioya
[
8
]
showed that this compactification is metrizable. The obvious obstacle in applying the same method to geometric data sets is the non-separability of
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
and
(\mathcal{D},\Box)
. Here, by considering a suitable subset of
\mathcal{D}
, it is possible to keep
d_{\operatorname{conc}}
and
\Box
separable. This subset is the set of all elements
X
of
\mathcal{D}
with a function family
F_{X}
that is appropriately “closed” with respect to a suitable function family
\mathcal{L}\subset\lip 1(\mathbb{R})
, so let us denote it by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
. It can be shown that
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
and
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}},\Box)
are separable. Furthermore, by using the same techniques as the compactification of
(\mathcal{X},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
, a natural compactification of
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
can be constructed and shown to be metrizable.

\supp\mu\coloneqq\bigl\{x\in X\mid\mu(U)>0\ \textrm{for all open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$}\bigr\}.

A triple

Definition 2.7

3.
Geometric Data Set

3.1.
Feature Space and Geometric Data Set

Definition 3.1

(Tame)
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set and
F
a non-empty subset of the power set
\mathbb{R}^{X}
. We define a function
d_{F}\colon X\times X\to[0,+\infty]
by

d_{F}(x,y)\coloneqq\sup_{f\in F}|f(x)-f(y)|,\quad x,y\in X.

d_{F}
is symmetric and satisfies a triangle inequality.

F
is said to be
tame
if
d_{F}(x,y)<+\infty
for any
x,y\in X
.

Definition 3.2

(Feature space)
.

A pair
(X,F)
is called a
feature space
if
X
is a non-empty set,
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{X}
is tame, and
d_{F}
is a distance function. For a feature space
(X,F)
, any element of
F
is called a
feature
.

Remark 3.3
.

We sometimes say that
X
is a feature space, for which the associated feature set is denoted by
F_{X}
and the distance function
d_{F_{X}}
by
d_{X}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Then we have
d_{\lip 1(X)}=d_{X}
.

Proof.

Take any
x,y\in X
. For any
f\in\lip 1(X)
, it follows that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d_{X}(x,y),

which proves
d_{\lip 1(X)}(x,y)\leq d_{X}(x,y)
. We define
g\in\lip 1(X)
as

g(z)\coloneqq d_{X}(x,z),\ z\in X.

Since

d_{X}(x,y)=|g(x)-g(y)|\leq d_{\lip 1(X)}(x,y),

we have
d_{\lip 1(X)}(x,y)=d_{X}(x,y)
. The arbitrariness of
x
and
y
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

For a feature space
(X,F)
, we have
F\subset\lip 1(X,d_{F})
.

Let
(X,F)
be a feature space,
p\in\lip 1(\mathbb{R})
,
Y
a non-empty set, and
\varphi\colon Y\to X
a map. We denote
p\circ F\coloneqq\{p\circ f\mid f\in F\}
and
F\circ\varphi\coloneqq\{f\circ\varphi\mid f\in F\}
.

Definition 3.6

(Product feature space)
.

The

of
(X,F)

(Y,G)

(X\times Y,F\otimes G)

F\otimes G\coloneqq F\circ\pr_{1}\cup\ G\circ\pr_{2}.

Remark 3.7

d_{F\otimes G}

d_{\infty}\left((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\right)\coloneqq\max\{d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2}),d_{G}(y_{1},y_{2})\}

.

Remark 3.9
.

We sometimes say that

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X
be an mm-space and
Y
be a metric space. On
\lip 1(X,Y)
, the pointwise convergence and
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}
-convergence are equivalent to each other. In particular,
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}
is a metrization of not only the convergence of measure but also the pointwise convergence.

Proof.

First, we show that if a sequence in
\lip 1(X,Y)
converges pointwise, then it
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}
-converges. Let
f_{n},f\in\lip 1(X,Y)
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, and assume that
f_{n}
converges pointwise to
f
as
n\to\infty
. Take any
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a compact subset
K\subset X
such that
\mu_{X}(K)>1-\varepsilon
. By the compactness of

, there exist finitely many points

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{M}\in K\text{ such that }K\subset\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}U_{X}(x_{m};\varepsilon).

Since
f_{n}
converges pointwise to
f
as
n\to\infty
and
M
is finite, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

d_{Y}(f_{n}(x_{m}),f(x_{m}))<\varepsilon\quad\textrm{for any}\ m=1,2,\ldots,M\text{ and }n\geq N.

For any
x\in K
, there exists
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
with
d_{X}(x,x_{m})<\varepsilon
. It follows that
d_{Y}(f_{n}(x),f(x))<3\varepsilon
for
n\geq N
. Since
\mu(X\setminus K)<\varepsilon
and
x\in K
is arbitrary, we have
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}(f_{n},f)\leq 3\varepsilon
for any
n\geq N
.

Next, we show that if a function sequence in
\lip 1(X,Y)

d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}
-converges, then it converges pointwise. Let
f_{n},f\in\lip 1(X,Y)
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, such that
f_{n}
does not converge pointwise to
f
as

. There exists
x\in X
,
\delta>0
, and a subsequence
\{f_{\iota(n)}\}_{n}
such that

d_{Y}(f_{\iota(n)}(x),f(x))>\delta\ \textrm{for}\ n=1,2,\ldots.

Take

so small that
\mu(U_{X}(x,\delta-r))>r
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,r)
and any

,

\displaystyle\mu_{X}(\{x\in X\mid d_{Y}(f_{\iota(n)}(x),f(x))>\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{X}(U_{X}(x;\delta-\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\geq r>\varepsilon.

Since it shows that
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}(f_{\iota(n)},f)\geq r
,

, we prove that

does not
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}
-converge to

as

.
∎

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
X
and
Y
be geometric data sets. Then a domination
p\colon X\to Y
is 1-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Take any
x,y\in X
and any
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
g\in F_{Y}
such that

d_{Y}(p(x),p(y))<|g(p(x))-g(p(y))|+\varepsilon.

Since

, there exists
f\in F_{X}
such that

|g\circ p(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon,\quad|g\circ p(y)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.

Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{Y}(p(x),p(y))

\displaystyle<|g\circ p(x)-g\circ p(y)|+\varepsilon

\displaystyle<|f(x)-f(y)|+3\varepsilon\leq d_{X}(x,y)+3\varepsilon.

By the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, we have
d_{Y}(p(x),p(y))\leq d_{X}(x,y)
. Also, by the arbitrariness of
x
and
y
, we prove

.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

In
[
8
, Definition 2.10]
, we say that an mm-space

Remark 3.21

The observable diameter of an mm-space

Lemma 3.23

(

Lemma 3.24

(

Proposition 3.25
.

Let

-observable diameter of

3.3.
Quotient Geometric Data Set

Lemma 3.26
.

Let
X
be a complete separable metric space and
d^{\prime}
a pseudometric on
X
. If
d^{\prime}(x,y)\leq d_{X}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
, then there exist a complete separable metric space
Y
and a 1-Lipschitz continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
such that the following
(1)
,
(2)
, and
(3)
hold.

(1)

The image
f(X)
is tight on
Y
.

(2)

For any
x,y\in X
, we have
d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))=d^{\prime}(x,y)
.

(3)

Let
Z
be a metric space and
g\colon X\to Z
a 1-Lipschitz continuous map. If
d_{Z}(g(x),g(y))\leq d^{\prime}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
, then there exists a unique 1-Lipschitz continuous map
\tilde{g}\colon Y\to Z
such that
\tilde{g}\circ f=g
. In particular,
Y
is unique up to isometries.

Proof.

Let us define
Y
and
f
. We define an equivalence relation
\mathord{\sim}
on
X
by saying that
x\sim y
if and only if
d^{\prime}(x,y)=0
. Let
p\colon X\to X/\mathord{\sim}
be the natural projection and

\tilde{d}(p(x),p(y))\coloneqq d^{\prime}(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\in X.

Then,
(X/\mathord{\sim},\tilde{d})
is a well-defined metric space, and
p
is 1-Lipschitz continuous. Let
Y
be the completion of
(X/\mathord{\sim},\tilde{d})
,
q\colon X/\mathord{\sim}\to Y
the natural distance preserving map, and
f\coloneqq q\circ p
. We see that
f
is 1-Lipschitz continuous.

Since
f(X)=q\circ p(X)=q(X/\mathord{\sim})
, (1) is obvious. In particular,
Y
is separable. Since
q
is a distance preserving map, (2) is also clear. We prove (3). Let Z be a metric space and
g\colon X\to Z
a 1-Lipschitz continuous map. We assume that

d_{Z}(g(x),g(y))\leq d^{\prime}(x,y)\text{ for any }x,y\in X.

We define

g^{\prime}\colon X/\mathord{\sim}\to Z\text{ by }g^{\prime}(p(x))\coloneqq g(x)\text{ for }x\in X.

Since

d_{Z}(g(x),g(y))\leq d^{\prime}(x,y)=0\text{ for any }x,y\in X\text{ with }x\sim y,

the well-definedness of
g^{\prime}
holds. From the definition by
g^{\prime}
, we have the continuity of
g^{\prime}
. By the universal property of the completion
Y
, there exists a unique continuous map
\tilde{g}\colon Y\to Z
satisfying
\tilde{g}\circ q=g^{\prime}
. Since
g^{\prime}\circ p=g
, we have
\tilde{g}\circ f=g
. Let us show the 1-Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{g}
. For all
x,y\in X
, we have

\displaystyle d_{Z}(\tilde{g}(f(x)),\tilde{g}(f(y)))

\displaystyle=d_{Z}(g(x),g(y))\leq d^{\prime}(x,y)=d_{Y}(f(x),f(y)).

By (1), this completes the proof of the 1-Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{g}
. Finally, we prove the uniqueness of
\tilde{g}
. Take any maps
h,k\colon Y\to Z
that
h\circ f=k\circ f
, that is,
h\circ q\circ p=k\circ q\circ p
. By the projectivity of
p
, we have
h\circ q=k\circ q
. From the universal property of
Y
, we see
k=h
.
∎

Definition 3.27

(Quotient geometric data set, quotient domination)
.

Let
X
be a geometric data set and
G\subset\overline{F_{X}}
a subfamily. A geometric data set
Y
is a
quotient geometric data set
of
X
by
G
and a map
f\colon X\to Y
is a
quotient domination
if the following conditions (1) and (2) are satisfied:

(1)

The equality
F_{Y}\circ f=G
holds.

(2)

For any geometric data set
Z
and any domination
g\colon X\to Z
, if
F_{Z}\circ g\subset\overline{G}
, then there exists a unique domination
\tilde{g}\colon Y\to Z
such that
\tilde{g}\circ f=g
.

By definition, if a quotient geometric data set of
X
by
G
exists, it is unique up to isomorphism, so we denote it by
X/G
.

Moreover, for a geometric data set
X
and
G\subset Lip_{1}(X)
, even if
G\not\subset F_{X}
, we also define
X/G
as
(X,Lip_{1}(X),\mu_{X})/G
. In this case, we still refer to it as a quotient geometric data set; however, the quotient domination is not defined.

Proposition 3.28
.

Let
X
be a geometric data set. For any subset
G\subset F_{X}
, there exist a quotient geometric data set
Y
of
X
by
G
and a quotient domination
f\colon X\to Y
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.26
, there exist a complete separable metric space
Y
and a 1-Lipschitz continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
such that the following (a), (b), and (c) hold.

(a)

The image
f(X)
is tight on
Y
.

(b)

For any
x,y\in X
, we have
d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))=d_{G}(x,y)
.

(c)

Let
Z^{\prime}
be a metric space and
g\colon X\to Z^{\prime}
a 1-Lipschitz continuous map. If
d_{Z^{\prime}}(g(x),g(y))\leq d_{G}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
, then there exists a unique 1-Lipschitz continuous map
\tilde{g}\colon Y\to Z^{\prime}
such that
\tilde{g}\circ f=g
.

We set

F_{Y}\coloneqq\{h\in\lip 1(Y)\mid h\circ f\in G\}\text{ and }\mu_{Y}\coloneqq f_{*}\mu_{X}.

The conditinon (c) for
Z^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}
implies (1). In particular, we see that
F_{Y}\circ f\subset\overline{F_{X}}
. By Lemma
2.3
and (a), we have
\supp\mu_{Y}=Y
. Let us prove
d_{Y}=d_{F_{Y}}
. Take any
x,y\in X
. We have

\displaystyle d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))

\displaystyle=d_{G}(x,y)=\sup_{g\in G}|g(x)-g(y)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in F_{Y}}|h\circ f(x)-h\circ f(y)|=d_{F_{Y}}(f(x),f(y)).

By (a), we obtain
d_{Y}=d_{F_{Y}}
. In particular,
Y
is a geometric data set, and
f
is a domination.

We prove (2). Let
Z
be a geometric data set and
g\colon X\to Z
a domination such that
F_{Z}\circ g\subset\overline{G}
. By applying (c) for
Z^{\prime}=Z
, there exists a unique 1-Lipschitz continuous map
\tilde{g}\colon Y\to Z
such that
\tilde{g}\circ f=g
. Since

F_{Z}\circ\tilde{g}\circ f=F_{Z}\circ g\subset\overline{G}=\overline{F_{Y}\circ f}=\overline{F_{Y}}\circ f

and given the universal property of
f
, it follows that
F_{Z}\circ\tilde{g}\subset\overline{F_{Y}}
. Since

\mu_{Z}=g_{*}\mu_{X}=\tilde{g}_{*}f_{*}\mu_{X}=\tilde{g}_{*}\mu_{Y},

we find that
\tilde{g}
is a domination. The uniqueness of
\tilde{g}
is obvious since Proposition
3.18
and (c).
∎

4.
Observable Distance

4.1.
Definition and Characterization of Observable Distance

Definition 4.1

(Parameter)
.

We set
I\coloneqq[0,1]
and denote by
\lambda
the one-dimentional Lebesgue measure on
I
. Let
(X,\mu)
be a Borel probability measure space. A Borel measurable map
\varphi\colon I\to X
is called a
parameter
of
\mu
if
\varphi_{*}\lambda=\mu
.

Definition 4.2

(Observable distance)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two geometric data sets. We define the
observable distance

d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X,Y)
between
X
and
Y
as the infimum of

{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\lambda}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(F_{X}\circ\varphi,F_{Y}\circ\psi)
,
where
\varphi
and
\psi
run over all parameters of
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, respectively.

Remark 4.3
.

In
[
3
]
, the observable distance
d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X,Y)
between two mm-space
X
and
Y
is defined as the infimum of
{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\lambda}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(\lip 1(X)\circ\varphi,\lip 1(Y)\circ\psi)
, where
\varphi
and
\psi
run over all parameters of
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, respectively. We see that
d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X,Y)=d_{\operatorname{conc}}(D(X),D(Y))
for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 4.4

(Concentrate, concentration topology)
.

Let
X_{n}
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, and
X
be geometric data sets. We say that the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

concentrates
to
X
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X_{n},X)=0.

If
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
concentrates to X, we write
X_{n}\xrightarrow{\conc}X
. We call the topology introduced by
d_{\operatorname{conc}}
on
\mathcal{D}
the
concentration topology
.

Remark 4.5
.

We could consider the different topology induced from a pseudometric defined as
d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\mathcal{X}(X),\mathcal{X}(Y))
for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{D}
. This topology is not finer than the concentration topology since

d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\mathcal{X}(\ast_{\{0\}}),\mathcal{X}(\ast_{\{1\}}))=d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\ast,\ast)=0<1=d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\ast_{\{0\}},\ast_{\{1\}}),

where
d_{\ast}\coloneqq 0
and
\mu_{\ast}
is the Dirac measure. Moreover, this topology does not coincide with the concentration topology. Indeed, there exists the following example. For
N=1,2,\ldots
, we defined N-points discrete geometric data set by

X_{N}\coloneqq\left(\{1,\ldots,N\},\{d^{N}_{m}\mid m=1,2,\ldots,N\},\mu_{N}\right),

where
\mu_{N}
is the normalized counting measure, and

d^{N}_{m}(n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\mathrel{\mathrm{if}}n=m,\\
1&\mathrel{\mathrm{if}}n\neq m,1\leq n\leq N.\end{cases}

Let us evaluate the observable distance between
X_{N}
and
\ast_{\{1\}}
. We set the parameters
\varphi_{N}\colon I\to X_{n}
and
\psi\colon I\to\ast
by

\varphi_{N}(t)\coloneqq\frac{\lfloor N\cdot t\rfloor}{N}+1,\quad\psi(t)\coloneqq\ast

for
N=1,2,\ldots
, where
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the maximal integer of less than or equal to
x
. Since

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\lambda}(d^{N}_{m}\circ\varphi_{N},1\circ\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left(\{t\in I\mid d^{N}_{m}\circ\varphi_{N}(t)\neq 1\circ\psi(t)\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left(\left(\frac{m-1}{N},\frac{m}{N}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{N}

for
m=1,2,\ldots
, we have

d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X_{N},\ast_{\{1\}})\leq{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\lambda}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(F_{X_{N}}\circ\varphi_{N},\{1\}\circ\psi)\leq\frac{1}{N}\to 0\mathrel{\textrm{as}}N\to\infty.

However,
\{\mathcal{X}(X_{N})\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
does not concentrate to
\mathcal{X}(\ast_{\{1\}})
. For
N=1,2,\ldots
, we define
h_{N}\in\lip 1(X_{2N})
by

h_{N}(n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\mathrel{\mathrm{if}}1\leq n\leq N,\\
1&\mathrel{\mathrm{if}}N<n\leq 2N.\end{cases}

Since
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X_{2N}}(h_{N},x)\geq 1/2
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\mathcal{X}(X_{2N}),\mathcal{X}(\ast_{\{1\}}))\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Remark 4.6
.

The metric space
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
is not separable. In fact, for any subset
\eta\subset 2^{\mathbb{N}}
, the only existing parameter for
\ast_{\eta}
is obtained as
\varphi(t)\coloneqq\ast
. For any
\xi\subset 2^{\mathbb{N}}
different from
\eta
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{conc}}(\ast_{\eta},\ast_{\xi})

\displaystyle={\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\lambda}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(\{\ast\mapsto n\mid n\in\eta\}\circ\varphi,\{\ast\mapsto n\mid n\in\xi\}\circ\varphi)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{{\left(d_{\mathbb{R}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(\eta,\xi),1\right\}=1.

The uncountability of the set
2^{\mathbb{N}}
of all subsets of
\mathbb{N}
implies that
(\mathcal{D},d_{\operatorname{conc}})
is not separable.

4.2.
The Semi-Continuity of Observable Diameter and the Conversation of Feature Order Relation with respect to the Observable Distance

The semi-continuity of observable diameter with respect to the observable distance is obvious from the following proposition.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
X,Y
be geometric data sets. For any real numbers
\delta>d_{\operatorname{conc}}(X,Y)
and
\kappa\in[0,1-\delta]
, we have

\od\bigl(X;\ -(\kappa+\delta)\bigr)\leq\od(Y;\ -\kappa)+2\delta.

Proof.

For any
f\in F_{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}),g\in F_{Y}
such that

d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})<\delta.

Lemma
2.8
implies

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{P}}(f_{*}\mu_{X},g*\mu_{Y})

\displaystyle=d_{\operatorname{P}}(f_{*}(\pr_{1})_{*}\pi,g_{*}(\pr_{2})_{*}\pi)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})<\delta.

From this and Lemma
3.23
, we have

\displaystyle\pd\bigl(f_{*}\mu_{X};\ -(\kappa+\delta)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\pd(g_{*}\mu_{Y};\ -\kappa)+2\delta

\displaystyle\leq\od(Y;\ -\kappa)+2\delta.

The arbitrariness of
f
implies

\od\bigl(X;\ -(\kappa+\delta)\bigr)\leq\od(Y;\ -\kappa)+2\delta.

∎

To show that the feature order relation is preserved in the concentration topology, we need some lemmas.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
X
be an mm-space,
x_{0}\in X
, and
c\geq 0
a real number. Then the set

\bdd(X,x_{0},c)\coloneqq\{f\in\lip 1(X)\mid|f(x_{0})|\leq c\}

is compact with respect to the pointwise topology and the topology of convergence in measure.

Proof.

The equicontinuity of
\lip 1(X)
is clear. For any
x\in X
and
f\in\bdd(X,x_{0},c)
, we have

|f(x)|\leq d_{X}(x,x_{0})+c.

This implies that
\bdd(X,x_{0},c)
is pointwise bounded. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem,
\bdd(X,x_{0},c)
is compact with respect to the pointwise topology. We have the compactness with respect to the topology of convergence in measure from Lemma
3.12
.
∎

Lemma 4.16

5.
Box Distance

The box distance is a complete distance between two mm-spaces induced by Gromov. In this section, we also define the box distance between two geometric data sets and obtain its completeness.

Let
X
be a set,

a metric space, and

on the total set of maps from

{d^{A}_{\infty}}(f,g)\coloneqq\sup\left\{d_{Y}(f(x),g(x))\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x\in A\right\}.

Similarly, we define
{d^{\emptyset}_{\infty}}\equiv 0
.

5.1.
Definition

Definition 5.1

(Box distance)
.

and

\dis S\coloneqq\max\left\{\left|d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\right|\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in S\right\}.

For any
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
, we define the

of

as

\displaystyle\dis\pi\coloneqq\inf\left\{\max\left\{1-\pi(S),\dis S\right\}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}S\in\mathcal{F}(X\times Y)\right\}.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X,Y
be two geometric data sets and
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
. Then we have
\dis\pi\leq\Box_{\pi}(F_{X},F_{Y})
.

Proof.

For any real number
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
S\in\mathcal{F}(X\times Y)
such that

\Box_{\pi}(F_{X},F_{Y})\leq\max\left\{1-\pi(S),2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})\right\}+\varepsilon.

Take any
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in S
. There exist
f\in F_{X}
and
g\in F_{Y}
such that

\displaystyle d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|+\varepsilon,

\displaystyle{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})

\displaystyle\leq{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})+\varepsilon.

From the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle<|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq|f(x_{1})-g(y_{1})|+|g(y_{1})-g(y_{2})|+|g(y_{2})-f(x_{2})|+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq|g(y_{1})-g(y_{2})|+2{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})+2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})+3\varepsilon.

The symmetry between
X
and
Y
proves

|d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})|\leq 2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})+3\varepsilon.

By the arbitrariness of
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
, we obtain

\dis S\leq 2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})+3\varepsilon.

The definition of
S
proves

\displaystyle\dis\pi

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\pi(S),\dis S\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\pi(S),2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})+3\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\Box_{\pi}(F_{X},F_{Y})+4\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
\dis\pi\leq\Box_{\pi}(F_{X},F_{Y})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
X,Y
be two geometric data sets and
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
a coupling. Then we have
\dis\pi=\Box_{\pi}(\lip 1(X),\lip 1(Y))
.

Proof.

By Proposition

, we see
\dis\pi\leq\Box_{\pi}(\lip 1(X),\lip 1(Y))

\Box_{\pi}(\lip 1(X),\lip 1(Y))\leq\dis\pi

For any real number
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

such that

\max\left\{1-\pi(S),\dis S\right\}\leq\dis\pi+\varepsilon.

Let us evaluate
{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\lip 1(X)\circ\pr_{1},\lip 1(Y)\circ\pr_{2})
. Take any

and define

by

We prove
g\in\lip 1(Y)
. For any

,

\displaystyle g(y_{1})-g(y_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{(x,z)\in S}\left(f(x)+d_{Y}(z,y_{1})\right)-\inf_{(x,z)\in S}\left(f(x)+d_{Y}(z,y_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{(x,z)\in S}\left(f(x)+d_{Y}(z,y_{1})-f(x)-d_{Y}(z,y_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{(x,z)\in S}\left(d_{Y}(z,y_{1})-d_{Y}(z,y_{2})\right)\leq d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}).

The arbitrariness of
y_{1},y_{2}
implies
g\in\lip 1(Y)
. Let us see
{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})\leq(\dis S)/2
. For any
(x,y)\in S

\displaystyle g(y)-f(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\dis S+\left(f(x)+d_{Y}(y,y)\right)-f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\dis S

\displaystyle f(x)-g(y)

\displaystyle=f(x)-\left(\frac{1}{2}\dis S+\inf_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S}\left(f(x^{\prime})+d_{Y}(y^{\prime},y)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S}\left(f(x)-f(x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y^{\prime},y)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\dis S

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S}\left(d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right)-\frac{1}{2}\dis S\leq\frac{1}{2}\dis S.

By the arbitrariness of
x,y
, we have
{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f\circ\pr_{1},g\circ\pr_{2})\leq(\dis S)/2
. The symmetry between
X
and
Y
implies

{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\lip 1(X)\circ\pr_{1},\lip 1(Y)\circ\pr_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}\dis S+\varepsilon.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\Box_{\pi}(\lip 1(X),\lip 1(Y))

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\pi(S),2{{\left({d^{S}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\lip 1(X)\circ\pr_{1},\lip 1(Y)\circ\pr_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\pi(S),\dis S\right\}+2\varepsilon<\dis\pi+3\varepsilon.

The arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
proves
\Box_{\pi}(\lip 1(X),\lip 1(Y))\leq\dis\pi
. This completes the proof.
∎

because we have

\displaystyle\mu(X^{\prime}_{\infty})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{N=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(\left\{\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\{(x_{n},x_{n+1})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\supp\pi_{n}\times\prod_{n=N}^{\infty}S_{n}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{N=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{\infty}X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n-1}\times S_{n}\times X_{n+2}\times\cdots\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{N=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\Box(X_{n},X_{n+1})\right)=1.

We set
F_{X_{\infty}}\coloneqq\lip 1(X_{\infty})
,
\mu_{X_{\infty}}\coloneqq\mu|_{X_{\infty}}
, and
\varphi_{n}\coloneqq\pr_{n}|_{X_{\infty}}
for each
n=1,2,\ldots
.

By the definition of
d_{X^{\prime}_{\infty}}
, we see that
\varphi_{n}
is 1-Lipschitz continuous. Take any
n=1,2,\ldots
. Since

\displaystyle(\varphi_{n})_{*}\mu_{X_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(\pr_{n})_{*}\mu=\mu_{X_{n}},

we see that
\varphi_{n}
is domination.

Consider the distace between
F_{X_{n}}\circ\varphi_{n}
and
F_{X_{n+1}}\circ\varphi_{n+1}
. From the definition of
\mu_{X_{\infty}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(F_{X_{n}}\circ\varphi_{n},F_{X_{n+1}}\circ\varphi_{n+1})

\displaystyle={\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi_{n}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(F_{X_{n}}\circ\pr_{1},F_{X_{n+1}}\circ\pr_{2})

\displaystyle={\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi_{n}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(\overline{F_{X_{n}}}\circ\pr_{1},\overline{F_{X_{n+1}}}\circ\pr_{2}).

By the definition of
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi_{n}}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{\pi_{n}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(\overline{F_{X_{n}}}\circ\pr_{1},\overline{F_{X_{n+1}}}\circ\pr_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\{1-\pi_{n}(S_{n}),{{\left({d^{S_{n}}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\overline{F_{X_{n}}}\circ\pr_{1},\overline{F_{X_{n+1}}}\circ\pr_{2})\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\{1-\pi_{n}(S_{n}),2{{\left({d^{S_{n}}_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}}(\overline{F_{X_{n}}}\circ\pr_{1},\overline{F_{X_{n+1}}}\circ\pr_{2})\}=\Box(X_{n},X_{n+1}).

Thus,

{\left(d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X_{\infty}}\right)_{\operatorname{H}}}(F_{X_{n}}\circ\varphi_{n},F_{X_{n+1}}\circ\varphi_{n+1})\leq\Box(X_{n},X_{n+1}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
X
be a geometric data set,
K
a compact subset of
X
, and
Y
a metric space. Then,
d^{K}_{\infty}
is uniformly continuous with respect to
(\lip 1(X,Y),d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X})
.

Proof.

Take any real number
\varepsilon>0
. There exists finitely many points

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\in X\text{ such that }K\subset\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{X}(x_{n};\varepsilon).

We set

s\coloneqq\min\left\{\mu_{X}(U_{X}(x_{n};\varepsilon))\mid n=1,\ldots,N\right\},\ \delta\coloneqq\min\left\{\frac{s}{2},\varepsilon\right\}.

We take any
f,g\in\lip 1(X,Y)
and
x\in K
and assume that
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}(f,g)<\delta
. There exists
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
such that
d_{X}(x,x_{n})<\varepsilon
. Since

\mu_{X}(\left\{x\in X\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}d_{Y}(f(x),g(x))<\delta\right\})\geq 1-\delta

and

\mu_{X}(U_{X}(x_{n};\varepsilon))\geq s\geq 2\delta,

there exists
x^{\prime}\in U_{X}(x_{n};\varepsilon)
such that
d_{Y}(f(x^{\prime}),g(x^{\prime}))<\delta\leq\varepsilon
. Thus, we have

d_{Y}(f(x),g(x))\leq d_{Y}(f(x^{\prime}),g(x^{\prime}))+2d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})<3\varepsilon.

The arbitrariness of
x
proves that
d^{K}_{\infty}(f,g)<3\varepsilon
. We take any
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\in\lip 1(X,Y)
that
d_{\operatorname{KF}}^{X}(f,g)<\delta
. We have
d^{K}_{\infty}(f,g)<3\varepsilon
in the same way as above. From the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d^{K}_{\infty}(f,f^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq d^{K}_{\infty}(f,g)+d^{K}_{\infty}(g,g^{\prime})+d^{K}_{\infty}(g^{\prime},f^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq d^{K}_{\infty}(g,g^{\prime})+6\varepsilon.

The symmetry between
(f,f^{\prime})
and
(g,g^{\prime})
implies
d^{K}_{\infty}(g,g^{\prime})\leq{d^{S}_{\infty}}(f,f^{\prime})+6\varepsilon
. Thus, we have

|d^{K}_{\infty}(f,f^{\prime})-d^{K}_{\infty}(g,g^{\prime})|\leq 6\varepsilon.

The arbitrariness of
f,f^{\prime},g,g^{\prime}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.21
.

Let
X,Y
be two geometric data sets,
Z
a feature space,
\nu
a Borel probability measure on
Z
, and
\varphi\colon Z\to X
,
\psi\colon Z\to Y
two maps. We assume that

F_{X}\circ\varphi\subset F_{Z},\ F_{Y}\circ\psi\subset F_{Z},\ \varphi_{*}\nu=\mu_{X},\ \psi_{*}\nu=\mu_{Y}.

Then, we have

\Box(X,Y)\leq\max\left\{1-\nu(S),2{d^{S}_{\infty}}(F_{X}\circ\varphi,F_{Y}\circ\psi)\right\}.

Proof.

Since the measure
(\varphi,\psi)_{*}\nu
is a coupling between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\Box(X,Y)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-(\varphi,\psi)_{*}\nu((\varphi,\psi)(S)),2{d^{\overline{(\varphi,\psi)(S)}}_{\infty}}(F_{X}\circ\pr_{1},F_{Y}\circ\pr_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\nu(S),2{d^{S}_{\infty}}(F_{X}\circ\varphi,F_{Y}\circ\psi)\right\}.

This completes the proof.
∎
