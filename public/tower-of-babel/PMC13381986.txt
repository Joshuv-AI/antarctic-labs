Trehalose and sucrose in chicken semen cryopreservation: effects on post-thaw sperm quality and molecular stress-response markers☆
Authors: Azindokht Babapour, Manuel Hidalgo, Agnieszka Śmieszek, Skarlet Napierkowska, Sandra Krywko Valencia, Wojciech Niżański, Agnieszka Partyka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryopreservation is essential for avian genetic resource conservation but often compromises post-thaw sperm quality. This study evaluated the effects of trehalose and sucrose as non-permeating cryoprotectants on chicken semen cryopreserved using a straw-based protocol with reduced dimethylformamide (DMF). Pooled semen from Green-legged Partridge roosters was assigned to a cryopreserved control group containing 6% DMF or to treatment groups containing 3% DMF supplemented with trehalose (400 or 500 mM) or sucrose (300 or 500 mM). Post-thaw sperm quality was assessed using CASA and flow cytometry parameters, together with targeted qPCR analysis of BAK1 and HSP60. Trehalose, particularly at 500 mM, was associated with a more favorable overall functional profile, including higher motility and lower DNA fragmentation. It also showed lower lipid peroxidation- and apoptosis-related values in selected comparisons. However, the response varied depending on the parameter assessed, with S300 showing higher values for mitochondrial membrane potential. BAK1 and HSP60 expression increased after cryopreservation, but no differences were detected between the cryopreserved control and trehalose 500 mM groups, suggesting that these transcriptional changes mainly reflected the freeze-thaw process rather than a trehalose-specific molecular response. Overall, 500 mM trehalose combined with reduced DMF showed a promising functional profile for chicken semen cryopreservation, while the targeted molecular findings provide complementary insight into freeze-thaw-associated stress responses.Keywords: Avian, Sperm, Semen cryopreservation, Gene expression, BAK1, HSP60

Introduction

Over the past few decades, semen cryopreservation has become a widely recognized and non-invasive approach for the ex-situ conservation of avian genetic resources, particularly for preserving rare breeds and maintaining genetic diversity for future breeding and restoration programs (Blesbois, 2011; Ehling et al., 2012; FAO, 2013; Long, 2006; Rakha et al., 2016; Svoradová et al., 2018; Thélie et al., 2019). Although primordial germ cell (PGC)-based approaches provide an important alternative tool for avian genetic resource conservation and breed reconstitution, semen cryopreservation remains a practical and cost-effective method for routine application (Blesbois, 2011; Ichikawa and Horiuchi, 2023; Mathan et al., 2023; Nandi et al., 2016). However, frozen-thawed poultry semen often shows reduced quality and fertilizing capacity due to cryoinjuries affecting sperm membranes, DNA, proteins, and stress- or apoptosis-related signaling pathways, partly because of the unique physiology and morphology of avian sperm (Donoghue and Wishart, 2000; Holt, 2000b; Long, 2006; Mehdipour et al., 2020; Mosca et al., 2016; Najafi et al., 2021; Rakha et al., 2016).

The selection of appropriate cryoprotective agents (CPAs) is undoubtedly one of the most critical determinants for the success of a poultry semen freezing protocol (Mosca et al., 2016). Permeable CPAs can reduce intracellular ice formation and improve membrane fluidity; However, their effects are concentration- and exposure-dependent and may be associated with cytotoxicity, membrane destabilization, or protein denaturation (Chian and Quinn, 2010; Holt, 2000a). In contrast, non-permeating CPAs, including sugars such as sucrose and trehalose, act mainly as extracellular osmoprotectants by reducing osmotic stress and extracellular ice formation, thereby potentially decreasing the required concentration of permeating CPAs in sperm cryopreservation (Aisen et al., 2002; Mosca et al., 2016). Accordingly, combining reduced concentrations of permeating CPAs, such as DMF, with non-permeating CPAs may be a strategy to improve post-thaw sperm quality while limiting CPA-related toxicity.

Sucrose and trehalose are non-permeating disaccharide CPAs that can stabilize sperm membranes and reduce osmotic- and freezing-induced damage, supporting their use in reduced-DMF protocols (Raju et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2018). These disaccharides have been extensively studied across various mammals (Aboagla, 2003; Consuegra et al., 2019; El-Sheshtawy et al., 2015; Gutiérrez-Pérez et al., 2009; Hidalgo et al., 2020; Iaffaldano et al., 2014; Woelders et al., 1997). Additionally, trehalose was successfully used in the cryopreservation of turkey and chicken semen (Blanco et al., 2011; Mosca et al., 2016); However, the concentration-dependent comparison of sucrose and trehalose in chicken semen, particularly with reduced DMF, remains unclear.

Although mature spermatozoa were previously considered transcriptionally inactive, their RNA content is now increasingly recognized as a source of information related to semen quality and fertility status (Miller et al., 1999; Pérez-Rico et al., 2014). However, sperm RNA analysis remains technically challenging due to the low RNA content (15 fg/cell compared with 10–30 pg in somatic cells) and sensitivity to degradation (Lian et al., 2021; Pérez-Rico et al., 2014; Umar et al., 2024). Thus, targeted gene expression analysis can complement functional assessment of cryopreserved semen.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of sucrose and trehalose on post-thaw chicken sperm quality using functional assessments and targeted molecular markers. We hypothesized that the addition of sucrose or trehalose to reduced-DMF extender would improve post-thaw sperm quality and that these effects would be concentration-dependent.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were obtained from commercial sources of analytical grade. The chemicals supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), including sucrose (catalog no. S0389), trehalose (catalog no. T5251), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; catalog no. 319937), and PNA lectin from Arachis hypogaea conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488, Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP; catalog no. 646547), and the components required for preparation of the Lake and Ravie extender, according to the original formulation (Lake and Ravie, 1984). Fluorescent dyes, including propidium iodide (PI), JC-1, SYBR-14, Fluo 3 AM, C11-BODIPY581/591, acridine orange (AO), Merocyanine (M540), and YO-PRO-1, as well as SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase Enzyme (catalog no. 11766050) and TRIzol reagent (catalog no. 15596026), were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). The Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus Kit (catalog no. R2070) was purchased from Zymo Research Corp. (Irvine, CA, USA). Further details regarding the application of these reagents are provided in the subsequent methodological sections.

Animals

Ten roosters from the Green-legged Partridge breed were included for this experiment. Roosters were kept in individual cages (109 cm × 70 cm × 73 cm) at 18 to 20°C, 14 h light:10 h dark photoperiod, and fed with commercial feed for breeding flocks. Fresh water was provided constantly.

Semen collection was approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Wrocław University of Environmental and Life Sciences (Statement n. 17/2024). All experiments were conducted in compliance with the national Act on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific or Educational Purposes (Dz. U. poz. 266).

Semen collection

Semen was collected by dorso-abdominal massage (Burrows and Quinn, 1937) twice per week throughout the 3-week experimental period. Semen was always collected by the same person under the same conditions. Collected samples were evaluated initially by a light microscope. Clean samples were pooled to provide sufficient material for the experiment.

Experimental design

Cryopreservation

Lake and Ravie (LR) extender was used as the base extender for all groups (Lake and Ravie, 1984). Five experimental groups were included: a cryopreserved control group (Cont; final DMF concentration 6%) and four treatment groups, each containing 3% DMF: trehalose at 400 or 500 mM (T400 and T500), and sucrose at 300 or 500 mM (S300 and S500).

After pooling, semen samples were divided into aliquots, diluted 1:1 (v/v) with the corresponding control or disaccharide-supplemented extender, and cooled to 5°C for 1 h. Dimethylformamide (DMF) was then added to reach the final concentration assigned to each group: 6% for Cont and 3% for the treatment groups. After 5 min of equilibration at 5°C, 100 µL of diluted semen (∼100 × 10⁶ spermatozoa) was loaded into 0.25 mL French straws, which were then inserted into 0.5 mL French straws to provide an additional protective outer layer during freezing and handling. Straws were sealed using the HSA-1 Straw Sealer (CryoLogic Pty. Ltd., Melbourne, VIC, Australia).

Sealed straws were frozen horizontally on a floating rack in a freezing unit for 20 straws (Minitube, Tiefenbach, Germany; rack Ref. 5015043/0600) for 10 min in liquid nitrogen vapor, then plunged into liquid nitrogen and stored in liquid nitrogen containers. For thawing, straws were immersed in a 37°C water bath for 30 s. After thawing, samples were maintained at 37°C for an additional 10 min before functional sperm quality assessments.

Assessment of sperm quality

Sperm motility was assessed using a CASA system (Ceros II, Hamilton Thorne, Beverly, MA, USA) with settings optimized for chicken semen. Thawed samples were diluted 1:50 in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified medium (DMEM), and 3 µl of diluted semen was loaded into an analysis chamber (Leja, Nieuw-Vennep, Netherlands) maintained at 36° C. Five random fields were analyzed per sample. The recorded motility parameters included total motility (MOT, %), progressive motility (PROG, %), average path velocity (VAP) (µm/s), straight-line velocity (VSL, µm/s), and curvilinear velocity (VCL, µm/s). A representative CASA field of frozen-thawed chicken spermatozoa, including the movement tracks generated by the CASA system, is provided in Supplementary Figure 1.

Flow cytometric analyses were performed using a Guava EasyCyte 5 cytometer equipped with a 488 nm argon-ion laser. The data was acquired and analyzed using GuavaSoft 3.1.1 software. Acquisition, gating, and exclusion of debris and aggregates were performed according to previously validated protocols for chicken semen flow cytometry assessment (Napierkowska et al., 2024). The assessed parameters included plasma membrane integrity, mitochondrial membrane potential, acrosome integrity, early apoptosis, lipid peroxidation, intracellular calcium ion levels, and DNA fragmentation.

Plasma membrane integrity was assessed using the Live/Dead Sperm Viability Kit according to a previously described protocol (Partyka et al., 2010). Samples were stained with SYBR-14 (333 nM, 10 min) and propidium iodide (PI; 24 µM, 3 min) at room temperature. SYBR-14+/PI− cells were classified as spermatozoa with intact plasma membranes (PMI).

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining (3 mM, 20 min, 37°C) in combination with PI, added 3 min before analysis. PI-positive spermatozoa were excluded as dead cells, and live spermatozoa were classified as having high, medium, or low mitochondrial membrane potential according to JC-1 fluorescence patterns.

Acrosome integrity was assessed using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated PNA (1 µg/mL, 5 min, room temperature) in combination with PI. Spermatozoa were classified as live intact acrosome (PI−/PNA−), live damaged acrosome (PI−/PNA+), dead intact acrosome (PI+/PNA−), or dead damaged acrosome (PI+/PNA+). Lipid peroxidation was assessed using C11-BODIPY581/591, as previously described (Partyka et al., 2011). Samples were stained with C11-BODIPY581/591 (1 µL, 2 µM, 30 min, 37°C) and PI (5 min, room temperature) before flow cytometry analysis.

Early apoptosis and membrane lipid disorder were assessed using M540 and YO-PRO-1, as previously described (Partyka et al., 2017). Diluted spermatozoa were stained with M540 (2.7 µM) and YO-PRO-1 (25 nM) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were classified into four subpopulations: viable non-disordered cells (YO-PRO-1−/M540−), viable cells with membrane lipid disorder (YO-PRO-1−/M540+), apoptotic cells with membrane lipid disorder (YO-PRO-1+/M540+), and apoptotic cells without membrane lipid disorder (YO-PRO-1+/M540−).

Intracellular calcium levels were assessed using Fluo-3 AM (0.5 mM, 15 min, 37°C) followed by PI addition, as previously described (Harrison and Mairet, 1993). Live and dead spermatozoa were classified according to low or high intracellular calcium levels.

DNA fragmentation was assessed using Acridine orange (AO) staining. Diluted semen (1 × 10⁶ spermatozoa/mL) were treated with acid lysis solution (100 µL suspension + 200 µL lysis solution, 30 s), stained with AO solution (600 µL, 6 µg/mL, 3 min), and analyzed by flow cytometry. Spermatozoa with denatured DNA were used to calculate the DNA fragmentation index (DFI).

Gene expression

Fresh semen served as the baseline (non-cryopreserved) control. Cryopreserved samples included a cryopreserved control (Cont) and the trehalose 500 mM group (T500). Gene expression was assessed in Cont and T500 to compare molecular changes after cryopreservation in the treatment that showed the best post-thaw performance. The qPCR analysis was performed using eight biological replicates per group.

Fresh semen samples were used for RNA isolation following an initial evaluation. Cryopreserved samples (Cont and T500) were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s prior to RNA extraction. For all samples, 45 × 10⁶ spermatozoa were used for RNA isolation. RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent and TCEP (Umar et al., 2024), followed by a 10-minute incubation at 55 °C in a water bath. Subsequently, the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus Kit, combined with DNase I treatment was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of extracted RNA was determined using a DeNovix DS-11+ Spectrophotometer. The RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase Enzyme according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was performed in a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

The obtained cDNA was used as a template for qPCR measurements. The PCR primers used in the experiment were designed with the Primer-BLAST software (Table 1). Each qPCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 20 μL containing 150 ng of cDNA template and gene-specific primers (forward and reverse) at a concentration of 200 nM. The thermal cycling conditions during reaction included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melt curve analysis was carried out from 65 °C to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments every 5 seconds to confirm amplification specificity. The analyses were carried out using a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The analysis included evaluation of relative expression mRNA levels of BAK1, a pro-apoptotic gene involved in the regulation of programmed cell death, and HSP60, a gene associated with protein folding and cellular stress responses.

For qPCR assay verification, testicular tissue cDNA was used as a positive control to confirm product amplification. The fluorescence threshold was checked and applied consistently across qPCR analyses before Ct determination. The relative expression of BAK1 and HSP60 was determined using GAPDH as the reference gene. BAK1 and HSP60 transcript levels were subsequently expressed as normalized quantities (Qn), according to a previously established method (Lis-Bartos et al., 2018). In addition, fresh semen served as the calibrator group (relative expression = 1), allowing direct comparison of gene expression between the cryopreserved groups (Cont and T500).

Amplification efficiency for qPCR was assessed from individual amplification curves using a curve-fitting approach based on reaction kinetics (Zhao and Fernald, 2005). Assay specificity was confirmed by melting-curve analysis, which showed single product-specific peaks without evidence of non-specific amplification. No-template controls (NTC) and no-reverse-transcription controls (No-RT) were included to monitor reagent contamination and genomic DNA-derived amplification, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The experimental unit was the pooled semen sample used for each replicate. All functional analyses were performed using 6 biological replicates per group, and qPCR reactions were performed using 8 biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA software (version 13.3, StatSoft, Inc., 2018) and GraphPad Prism 10. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed before applying ANOVA. Parametric tests, including ANOVA, were applied when the data followed a normal distribution. For nonparametric scenarios, the Wilcoxon matched pairs test, the sign test, Friedman’s ANOVA, and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance were used. The specific post hoc procedure and sample size are stated in detail in the corresponding table and figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05. Results are presented as mean ± SEM.

Results

In the experiment, trehalose at 500 mM and 400 mM concentrations significantly enhanced sperm motility (P < 0.01), but also increased the proportion of slow spermatozoa (P < 0.05). On the other hand, no significant differences in progressive motility (PROG) were observed between the control and any of the test groups (Table 2). Sucrose at the concentration of 300 mM significantly enhanced sperm VCL when compared to the control group and sucrose 500 mM (P < 0.05). Sucrose at 500 mM concentration significantly declined the VAP compared to the control group and sucrose at 300 mM (P < 0.05). In terms of VSL, all treatment groups exhibited a reduction compared to control. The S500 group showed the lowest VSL value compared with other treatment groups (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of different non-permeating cryoprotectants on post-thaw chicken sperm quality using an innovative and improved straw-based cryopreservation approach. This method may offer practical advantages in terms of standardization, aseptic handling, and repeatability (Hidalgo, 2021), and the control group showed higher viability than previously reported for pellet-based semen cryopreservation in chicken (Mehaisen et al., 2020; Partyka et al., 2010). Under the present experimental conditions, trehalose, particularly at 500 mM, demonstrated a more favorable overall functional profile among the tested treatments.

Sucrose and trehalose are well-established non-permeating cryoprotectants used to reduce cryoinjury in semen cryopreservation. Sucrose and trehalose are well-established non-permeating cryoprotectants used to reduce cryoinjury in semen cryopreservation across species (Caturla-Sánchez et al., 2018; Hidalgo et al., 2020; Quan et al., 2012). In the present study, T400 and T500 were associated with enhanced post-thaw sperm motility, indicating their beneficial influence under the present experimental conditions. Sucrose did not improve overall sperm motility, though increased selected velocity parameters, including VCL in S300 and VAP in S500. Similar species- and concentration-dependent responses have been reported in other mammals (Caturla-Sánchez et al., 2018; Hidalgo et al., 2020; Jhamb et al., 2023; Quan et al., 2012; Soltani, 2023). Plasma membrane integrity (PMI) is a key indicator of post-thaw sperm quality due to its sensitivity to thermal and osmotic stress (Bakst and Sexton, 1979; Holt, 2000b; Zhang et al., 2013). According to our results, S300 and T500 showed higher PMI than S500 and T400, suggesting better membrane preservation under these specific treatment conditions. This finding is consistent with previous reports suggesting non-permeating cryoprotectants may support membrane preservation, although their effects can vary depending on species, concentration, and cryopreservation protocol (Hidalgo et al., 2020; Quan et al., 2012).

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed as a key indicator of mitochondrial functional status under stress conditions (Aitken et al., 2016). Our findings illustrated that S300 resulted in a higher proportion of spermatozoa with HMP than T400, which showed the lowest value of this parameter, while neither S500 nor T500 differed significantly from the control. This pattern suggests a concentration-dependent response in mitochondrial membrane potential, which aligns with previous investigations (Jhamb et al., 2023; Thananurak et al., 2019). However, under the present experimental conditions, the relatively higher HMP observed in S300, particularly compared with T400, should be interpreted in the context of the overall functional profile.

Acrosomal integrity is an important indicator of post-thaw cryoinjury, as the acrosome plays an essential role in penetrating the outer layers of the egg during fertilization (Abouelezz et al., 2017; Lemoine et al., 2011; Mocé et al., 2010; Olexikova et al., 2019). In this investigation, trehalose-containing groups and S500 showed better acrosomal integrity, whereas S300 was associated with lower acrosome preservation. This emphasizes the potential distinct effects of these cryoprotectants on sperm acrosome preservation. Previous studies have reported beneficial but species- and extender-dependent effects of trehalose and sucrose on acrosomal integrity (Hu et al., 2010; Olexikova et al., 2019; Quan et al., 2012; Shiva Shankar Reddy et al., 2010).

Trehalose has been associated with reduced oxidative damage and LPO in frozen-thawed sperm (Jhamb et al., 2023; Soltani, 2023). Our findings in rooster sperm, showed that T500 had lower LPO-related values compared with S300. Although differences were not consistent across all treatment groups, a clear trend toward reduced LPO levels was noted in both live and dead cells with T500 (Fig. 4). The absence of significant changes in LPO levels compared to the control group suggests that the antioxidant efficacy of the tested non-permeating CPAs may depend on concentration and experimental context.

Despite the positive effect of trehalose in reducing apoptosis in prior studies, findings in chicken semen suggest a more variable response and were not confirmed by flow cytometry (Gholami et al., 2023; Miranda et al., 2018; Varela et al., 2020).To our knowledge, no previous research has examined the impact of sucrose on apoptosis in post-thaw chicken semen. In the present study, early apoptosis was not reduced by any disaccharide treatment compared with the control; However, T500 showed a lower proportion of apoptotic cells than S300. This highlights the complexity of trehalose’s effects on spermatozoa in certain species.

In our study, S300 was associated with higher calcium levels in live sperm cells, whereas T400 showed lower values for this parameter. These observations may indicate treatment-associated differences in calcium regulation after cryopreservation. Since disturbances in calcium homeostasis during cooling can contribute to cryoinjury and reduced post-thaw sperm quality, these results may provide additional insight into the functional status of frozen-thawed sperm. Calcium is essential for fertilization-related sperm functions, including motility, capacitation, and the acrosome reaction (Finkelstein et al., 2020; Publicover et al., 2007; Tokmakov et al., 2014), which may explain why proper calcium regulation may contribute to protection against oxidative stress and cell death (Napierkowska et al., 2024; White, 1993).

Our results showed that trehalose supplementation lowered DNA fragmentation compared with S300, suggesting trehalose’s better preservation of DNA integrity under the present experimental conditions. Since sperm DNA integrity is important for post-thaw sperm function, embryo development, and it can be impaired by cryopreservation (Evenson et al., 2002; Hammadeh et al., 1999), this finding supports the relevance of trehalose for maintaining DNA quality after freezing. In chicken semen, a twofold increase in DFI has previously been reported after the freeze–thaw process (Partyka et al., 2010). Similar protective patterns of trehalose on sperm DNA integrity have also been reported in other species (Borecki et al., 2025; El-Sheshtawy et al., 2015; Jhamb et al., 2023).

BAK1 and HSP60 expression was assessed as a focused molecular complement to the functional sperm quality analyses, with BAK1 representing apoptosis-related changes, and HSP60 indicating mitochondrial stress-related chaperone response. These markers were therefore used to provide a focused molecular assessment complementary to the functional sperm quality parameters. As T500 showed the most promising overall functional profile among the trehalose treatments, qPCR was restricted to a targeted comparison between the cryopreserved control and T500 rather than a comprehensive molecular comparison across all treatments.

Based on our observations, BAK1 expression was upregulated in the T500 group compared with fresh semen, but did not differ from the frozen-thawed control. This suggests that the observed increase mainly reflected the freeze-thaw process rather than a clearly detectable trehalose-specific effect. As BAK1 is involved in the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, its upregulation is consistent with a cryopreservation-induced apoptotic response (Gross et al., 1999; Jeong et al., 2005). However, the comparable BAK1 levels between T500 and the frozen-thawed control indicate that T500 did not further increase this apoptosis-related response under the present experimental conditions. In the present study, HSP60 was upregulated in both cryopreserved groups, indicating a freeze-thaw-related stress response. This is consistent with the role of HSP60 as a mitochondrial stress-related chaperone and with previous reports of increased HSP60 protein expression after semen cryopreservation, including in chicken (Cheng et al., 1989; Gupta et al., 2010; Partyka et al., 2022). However, comparable HSP60 expression levels among targeted groups indicate that T500 did not further intensify this stress-related response. The increased expression of BAK1 and HSP60 in cryopreserved samples indicates that the freeze-thaw process was associated with cellular stress; however, neither marker differed significantly between the cryopreserved Cont and T500 groups, suggesting that the observed transcriptional changes primarily reflect the cryopreservation itself rather than a detectable trehalose-specific response. Therefore, the qPCR findings should be viewed as a focused molecular assessment rather than a full mechanistic explanation for the improved functional profile of T500. Since gene expression was evaluated only in the cryopreserved Cont and T500 groups, no molecular conclusions can be drawn regarding sucrose-based protocols.

Conclusion

Present outcomes demonstrate that the use of non-permeating cryoprotectants, particularly trehalose, can effectively preserve several post-thaw sperm quality parameters in chicken semen cryopreserved using a straw-based method with a reduced concentration of permeating cryoprotectant. Increasing trehalose concentration while lowering the permeating cryoprotectant resulted in the most consistent improvements in sperm functionality, including enhanced motility, maintained viability and DNA integrity, and reduced oxidative stress.

Trehalose, especially at 500 mM, provided effective cellular stabilization during the freeze–thaw process, with no additional changes detected in the selected apoptosis- and stress-related markers compared with the cryopreserved control. By comparison, sucrose (particularly at 300 mM) was associated with increased signs of cryodamage in selected parameters, including acrosomal damage, lipid peroxidation, increased intracellular calcium levels, and higher DNA fragmentation. From a practical perspective, the inclusion of trehalose may still be relevant because it could support extender optimization and potential reduction of DMF concentration.

Overall, these findings support the potential of trehalose-based formulations with reduced permeating cryoprotectant concentrations as a promising strategy to improve the robustness of straw-based poultry semen cryopreservation, with possible applications in semen bio-banking and artificial insemination programs. Future studies including a broader range of apoptosis- and fertility-related genes may further clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms.
