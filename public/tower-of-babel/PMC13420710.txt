The effect of simultaneous application of plant-rich ketogenic diet and intermittent fasting on early-to-mid-stage Alzheimer's disease: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Beyza Tağraf, Dilara Kaygusuz, Deniz Yerlikaya, Özden Erkan Oğul, Lütfü Hanoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study evaluates the effect of simultaneous application of plant-rich ketogenic diet and intermittent fasting as an individualized adjunct to standard treatment on cognitive function in early/mid-stage Alzheimer’s patients.MethodsThis assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial enrolled 60 Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients (CDR 1–2; aged 55–85), randomized to intervention (n = 30; intervention + standard care) or control (n = 30; standard care). Cognition and brain structure were assessed at baseline and 6 months. Individual cognitive changes were analyzed using Reliable Change Index (RCI).ResultsCognitive change scores differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). The mean Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score increased by +0.9 in the intervention group, while a decrease of −1.5 points was observed in the control group. RCI analysis indicated stability in 93% of intervention and 70% of control participants, with cognitive improvement in 7% and 3.3%, respectively.DiscussionThe intervention method has the potential to stabilize cognitive decline in AD and promote improvement at the individual level. The response to the intervention is closely related to the individual's metabolic phenotype and structural brain integrity.Ethical approvalIstanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on March 6, 2025, under number 242 and the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06898424.

Introduction

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by β-amyloid and tau pathology, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation, though their complex interactions remain incompletely understood [1].

Current treatments provide only symptomatic relief, with anti-amyloid agents showing limited efficacy. Growing evidence links AD to glucose hypometabolism and chronic inflammation as key drivers of neurodegeneration [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]].

Metabolic-based interventions have thus gained attention: ketogenic nutrition provides alternative neuronal energy via ketone bodies, while intermittent fasting (IF) enhances insulin sensitivity and attenuates inflammation [8,9].

AD has been classified into five metabolic subtypes — inflammatory, glycotoxic, toxic, vascular, and atrophic — each contributing to cognitive decline through distinct mechanisms. These subtypes are not mutually exclusive and may co-occur, reflecting the heterogeneous biological underpinnings of the disease [10]. The inflammatory type is characterized by microglial activation and neuroinflammation in the brain. Targeting neuroinflammation is central to treatment in this subtype. The glycotoxic subtype refers to insulin resistance and excess glucose exerting toxic effects on the brain [11,12]. In this type, dietary interventions and metabolic management strategies are recommended to reduce the negative effects of high glycemic levels on brain function [13]. The toxic subtype is linked to the neurotoxic effects of environmental exposures, including heavy metals, organochlorine residues, and polychlorinated biphenyls [14]. The vascular subtype involves cerebrovascular disease and impaired blood flow, with hypertension and atherosclerosis as key risk factors. This classification marks a shift toward individualized, subtype-specific treatment strategies for AD [14].

The ketogenic diet and IF are dietary strategies with potential systemic and neurological benefits. By inducing ketosis, the ketogenic diet generates ketone bodies — particularly β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate — serve as alternative cerebral energy substrates supporting neuronal metabolism and cognitive function [15]. However, conventional ketogenic diets rely heavily on animal-based foods and saturated fats may be associated with adverse cardiovascular effects and may be difficult to maintain over the long term [[16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, the present study adopted a plant-rich ketogenic approach aimed at supporting ketosis while promoting cardiovascular health and long-term dietary sustainability.

IF regulates energy metabolism through timed fasting periods, triggering autophagy and demonstrating potential to suppress neuroinflammation, reduce oxidative stress, and support neuroplasticity and cognitive function [12].

This study evaluates the effects of a plant-rich ketogenic diet combined with intermittent fasting on global cognitive function in AD. The intervention is a multi-component, individualized protocol hypothesized to improve the clinical course by enhancing ketone-based brain energy metabolism, reducing neuroinflammation, and preserving synaptic integrity.

Methods

Participants and randomization

Our study was conducted as a randomized, assessor-blinded trial at the Memory Center of Istanbul Medipol University Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained from the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on March 6, 2025, under number 242 and the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. This study was prospectively registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06898424) prior to the recruitment of the first participant. Participants were aged 55–85 years, had a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) [19] score of 1 or 2, met the NIA-AA [1], for the clinical diagnosis of AD, had no alcohol or drug addiction, no chronic kidney failure, no other neuropsychiatric disease diagnosis. Participants with existing amalgam fillings or previous amalgam removal not performed according to the Safe Mercury Amalgam Removal Technique (SMART) were excluded in order to minimize potential confounding related to mercury exposure within the toxic subtype classification framework [20]. Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention (n = 30) or control (n = 30) group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Group allocation was concealed from the assessor conducting cognitive evaluations (assessor-blinded). Intervention group

The intervention group was subjected to a 6-month plant-rich ketogenic diet and IF simultaneously. The dietary recording method required individuals to detail all foods and beverages consumed, as well as their physical activities. These records, collected to analyse the dietary habits. They were categorized based on anthropometric measurements, food consumption data, biochemical markers indicating the metabolic effects of nutrient intake, and clinical evaluations revealing the possible consequences of an unbalanced diet. After the intervention group completed the tests, the new dietary protocol [10] was explained to the patient and, if applicable, their close relatives. The interventional data were collected through face-to-face/online interviews and dietitian follow-ups at three different time points: baseline, 3th months, and 6th months.

Control group

The control group received standard medical care, including routine physician follow-up, without any dietary intervention or nutritional guidance.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure was global cognitive function, assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) at baseline and 6 months. Cognitive assessments were performed by the research intern physician who had completed neurology coursework and received training in the administration of the cognitive assessment instruments used in the study. All assessments were conducted under the supervision of a neurologist.

Secondary outcome measures

Structural magnetic resonance images obtained at baseline (acquired as part of routine clinical care) were visually assessed using the Modified Visual Magnetic Resonance Rating Scale (MRRS). The MRRS is a visual scoring system based on seven parameters that semi quantitatively rates structural changes specific to AD and other neurodegenerative disorders. The parameters evaluated include sulcal atrophy, ventricular atrophy, medial temporal atrophy, periventricular and subcortical white matter lesions, basal ganglia infarcts, and infratentorial infarcts. Each parameter is graded from normal to advanced pathological change [22].

Identification of metabolic subtypes

In order to tailor the nutrition protocol to each individual, all participants were categorized into one of the following groups based on their metabolic profiles prior to intervention: inflammatory, glycotoxic, toxic, vascular, or atrophic. A patient may belong to more than one category. The inflammatory type is characterized by hs-CRP > 0.9, pain, and arthritis. The glycotoxic type is characterized by BMI > 30 kg/m² and insulin resistance (IR). The toxic type is characterized by fatigue and elevated heavy metal levels in the blood. The vascular type is characterized by CVD and hypertension. The atrophic type is characterized by age-related cerebral atrophy [23]. Based on this pathophysiological classification, dietary planning was carried out for each individual through a clinically meaningful adaptation process [10].

Simultaneous application of the plant-rich ketogenic diet and intermittent fasting

Taking into attention the pathophysiological characteristics specific to each metabolic subtype, the plant-rich ketogenic diet and IF intervention were designed in an individualized model. This individualized approach targeted not only macronutrient balance but also inflammation, insulin resistance, and detoxification pathways. Inflammatory type patients eliminated foods that cause inflammation from their diet. The diet for the glycotoxic group was calorie-restricted. The toxic group was informed about avoiding exposure to toxic substances. Salt consumption was monitored in vascular type patients. For all groups, foods were categorized as ‘proteins, dairy products, fats, seeds, vegetables, legumes, fruits, grains, spices and sauces, beverages, sweeteners’. For each group, sections titled ‘prohibitions’ and ‘replacements’ were created and provided to patients in tabular form. The intervention group was informed that they needed to fast for 12 h a day without interruption and that they should stop eating 3 h before bedtime [10].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Sample size was determined using G*Power software. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine the effects of time, group, and time × group interactions on study outcomes. The primary analysis was based on the group × time interaction. When significant interaction effects were identified, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed to determine the source of the differences. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with age included as a covariate, was conducted to assess whether baseline age differences influenced cognitive outcomes. The Reliable Change Index (RCI) was calculated to evaluate individual-level changes [24,25]. In the analyses conducted, results were considered statistically significant if the p-value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05). As a sensitivity analysis, a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was additionally fitted to examine the robustness of the primary findings obtained from the mixed ANOVA. The model included time, group, and their interaction as fixed effects, with a random intercept for participant ID to account for individual variability. Parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with degrees of freedom approximated via the Satterthwaite method. Estimated marginal means were used to derive between-group and within-group mean differences, along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and effect sizes. The full results of this sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The mean age of individuals in the study was 71 years (SD = 7) in the intervention group and 72 years (SD = 9) in the control group. An ANCOVA including age as a covariate confirmed a significant group effect on cognitive outcomes (F(1,57) = 14.95, p < 0.001), while age was not a significant predictor (F(1,57) = 1.23, p = 0.273), indicating that the 0.9-year mean age difference between groups did not confound the results. The mean MMSE baseline score was 19 (SD = 5.1) in the intervention group and 20 (SD = 5.2) in the control group. The MMSE average after six months was 20 (SD = 5) in the intervention group and 18 (SD = 5) in the control group. The average difference in MMSE scores was +0.9 in the intervention group and –1.5 in the control group. A sensitivity analysis using a linear mixed-effects model confirmed these findings, showing a significant between-group difference in change over time, with a significant within-group improvement in the intervention group and a significant within-group decline in the control group (Supplementary Table S1).

Subgroup analysis – RCI outcomes by group

According to the Reliable Change Index (RCI) results calculated to evaluate clinically meaningful individual changes, 93% of individuals in the intervention group remained cognitively stable, 7% showed improvement, and no regression was observed in any individual. In contrast, 70% of the control group remained stable, 27% showed deterioration, and only 3% showed improvement. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that a personalized multimodal dietary intervention may support cognitive stabilization in individuals with Alzheimer's disease. Given the modest efficacy of current treatments, lifestyle strategies may help prevent decline and complement therapy, showing both group-level and clinically relevant individual benefits. Between-group mean difference showed a significant interaction between the time and group factors. This finding suggests that cognitive improvements in the intervention group were attributable to the intervention rather than natural ageing. This means there was a statistically significant and measurable difference in their cognitive performance. The cognitive change in the intervention group was statistically significant, both in group averages and with the RCI. RCI identifies clinically meaningful changes at the individual level, determining whether performance shifts exceed measurement error or natural variation [24,25]. A significant proportion of intervention group participants exceeded the RCI threshold, while no such individual improvement was observed in the control group. When comparing responders and non-responders to the dietary intervention in the intervention group, a statistically significant difference was found between MRRS scores. In the intervention group, when the participants were divided into subgroups according to metabolic subtypes, there was an observed difference in their cognitive change levels in line with these metabolic characteristics. Non-glycotoxic individuals showed higher changes in MMSE scores compared to glycotoxic individuals. A significant negative correlation between glycotoxic status and MMSE change score was observed.

Impaired energy metabolism in AD has prompted growing interest in dietary strategies as a means of supporting neurological health. Previous studies have examined full ketogenic diets [26,27]. They have demonstrated that ketone bodies can provide energy for neurons [28,29]. However, such trials are usually short-term and small-scale, and they don't look at the cardiovascular and metabolic risks linked to a high fat content [18,30]. On the other hand, diets that are plant-rich — especially the Mediterranean diet — have been extensively investigated in relation to neurodegenerative diseases because of their anti-inflammatory properties, high antioxidant levels, and protective effects on cardiovascular risk. These dietary models have a positive effect on overall health, but they do not reliably induce ketosis [31,32]. Our study is based on a plan that keeps the body in a state of ketosis. It combines a metabolic background that supports ketone production with anti-inflammatory content. Thus, a holistic approach targeting intracellular energy balance and reduction of structural and metabolic stress has been proposed.

The significantly higher MRRS scores in non-responders suggest that advanced structural brain changes may limit cognitive response to intervention. MRRS estimates overall structural damage by evaluating parameters including frontal and temporal atrophy, enlarged sulci, hippocampal volume loss, and periventricular white matter changes [22]. These structural markers are among the determinants of both disease severity and progression rate in AD [22]. In our trial, findings suggest that neuro-metabolic interventions may be more effective in individuals with limited structural damage, and that intervention efficacy may depend on structural brain integrity. In particular, advanced atrophy in regions critical for cognitive function may limit treatment response by restricting neuronal plasticity [[33], [34], [35]]. Thus, structural assessment scales such as MRRS can be considered clinical tools that can predict not only the presence or severity of the disease but also the potential response to intervention. Beyond structural factors, metabolic status also emerged as a key modulator of intervention response. Glycotoxic physiology is characterised by oxidative stress, the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) — both endogenously formed through chronic hyperglycemia and exogenously derived from dietary sources such as cooked fats and proteins — and mitochondrial dysfunction [36]. These processes may trigger neuroinflammatory responses that disrupt synaptic plasticity and neuronal network function [37,38]. This negative correlation supports the hypothesis that neurometabolic load — encompassing glucose hypometabolism, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress — may modulate the cognitive response to dietary intervention. The attenuated benefits in glycotoxic individuals suggest that pathological hyperglycemia may modulate the intervention's mechanisms of action. In addition, our finding suggests that insulin resistance, metabolic flexibility, and cellular energy utilisation pathways may be among the biological factors that influence the intervention response. Our study highlighted that the MRRS score and glycotoxicity can independently influence the response to nutritional intervention. Yet, these factors may also combine to further restrict response. In glycotoxic individuals, mechanisms such as oxidative stress, microvascular dysfunction, and impaired energy metabolism reduce neuronal resilience [37]. A high MRRS score, meanwhile, signals reduced structural reserve and the inability of existing neuronal infrastructure to withstand intervention [22]. Together, these conditions create a pathophysiological environment that limits intervention potential at both metabolic and structural levels, suggesting that individuals with high MRRS scores and a glycotoxic profile may face greater challenges in cognitive response. Indeed, glycotoxicity may drive brain atrophy through chronic inflammation and AGE accumulation, further increasing structurally detectable damage [23]. This pathophysiological interaction demonstrates that structural and metabolic axes are not only parallel to each other but also interact with each other.

The differentiation of the intervention group according to metabolic phenotype calls for the reevaluation of multi-component and individualised approaches, in the perspective of neurocognitive objectives [10]. In the classification of the intervention group according to metabolic phenotypes, it was observed that the majority of individuals belonged to the "glycotoxic" and "inflammatory" subtypes. This distribution explains the variability in intervention response and may also be related to the unique clinical and cultural characteristics of the population in which the study was conducted. National cohort analyses based on the Electronic Health Record database (E-nabız) have shown that diabetes prevalence is high in Turkey, insulin resistance is a common metabolic problem, and diabetes is an important determinant of AD [39]. Furthermore, environmental and cultural factors, including a sedentary lifestyle, high consumption of refined carbohydrates, and the prevalence of metabolic syndrome components, contribute to the formation of this metabolic pattern [40]. Our findings indicate the prevalence of glycotoxic and inflammatory subtypes within the Turkish population could potentially result in variations in the quantitative and qualitative responses of individuals to the same therapeutic intervention. Therefore, when evaluating the effectiveness of nutrition-based interventions, it is essential to consider not only the intervention's content, but also the metabolic profile of the population in which it is applied. This underscores the importance of tailoring strategies to population characteristics and adopting a comprehensive classification that considers glycotoxicity, inflammation, and structural damage simultaneously. Our study presents several methodological and conceptual strengths. Given the multifactorial nature of neurodegeneration, the multimodal design of the intervention reflects the complexity of AD pathophysiology more comprehensively than single-component dietary approaches. Additionally, the assessor-blinded design and the use of RCI allowed for evaluation of clinically meaningful changes at the individual level, beyond group-level statistics. However, the study has some limitations. The sample size has been demonstrated to limit statistical power in subgroup analyses. Moreover, the study is single-centred and has a limited follow-up period. This reduces the applicability of the findings and the ability to assess long-term effects.

Therefore, it is recommended that not only metabolic parameters but also brain imaging findings be considered in the pre-intervention assessment process. When MRRS and metabolic subtypes are evaluated together, a more comprehensive and accurate clinical classification can be made to predict the potential response to intervention. This approach may contribute to clinical practice in terms of targeting and personalizing cognitive intervention strategies. Metabolic screening prior to intervention may therefore be an important clinical step.

In conclusion, the simultaneous application of plant-rich ketogenic diet and intermittent fasting approach shows potential for cognitive stabilisation and improvement in patients with AD. The effect of the intervention is associated with factors such as individual brain structure and metabolic status. Structural damage levels defined by MRRS and metabolic co-factors such as glycotoxicity are important variables determining the response to the intervention. Considering that sociocultural factors also shape intervention success, models like this intervention should be evaluated within personalized, multidimensional intervention strategies.
