Defining and measuring ‘Lift’ in soft tissue filler-based facial rejuvenation: a critical review and proposed framework
Authors: Steven Harris, Alain Michon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The term “lift” is among the most frequently used outcomes, technique names, and product descriptors in the soft tissue filler literature, yet it has no consensus operational definition, no agreed measurement standard, and no validated threshold for clinical significance. We undertook a structured narrative critique of how “lift” is operationalised in the peer-reviewed filler literature for hyaluronic acid, calcium hydroxylapatite, poly-l-lactic acid, polycaprolactone, and hybrid products, and compared reported effect sizes against the documented accuracy of the measurement modalities used. The term “lift” is operationalised in multiple categorically distinct ways across this corpus. All published quantitative measurements reflect changes at the cutaneous surface rather than independent tracking of deeper structures. Reported mm-magnitude effects are often similar in magnitude to, and in some cases substantially exceeded by the standard deviation of the measurement and the documented accuracy of the imaging modality. Dose-response analyses, where performed, demonstrate a non-monotonic rather than dose-dependent relationship between injected volume and measured displacement. Studies comparing multiple rater groups suggest that less independent raters may report larger improvements. No reviewed study reports modality accuracy alongside effect size or pre-specifies a clinically meaningful threshold. These observations do not establish that fillers do not produce lift; they establish that the construct of filler-mediated lift has not yet been operationalised with the precision its claims now require. We propose a five-standard framework toward an operational definition.

Introduction

“Lift” is among the most frequently used terms in the soft tissue filler literature, appearing in study titles, clinical guidelines, product descriptions, and patient-facing communication. Yet despite its ubiquity, “lift” in this literature has no consensus definition, no agreed measurement standard, and no validated threshold for clinical significance. The published literature reports “lift” using fundamentally different operationalisations — surface vector displacement, soft tissue position relative to bony landmarks, evaluator scoring on subjective scales, photographic comparison, volumetric change, angular change at anatomical landmarks, and patient-reported satisfaction — without consistently acknowledging that these may not represent the same underlying construct.

A useful distinction, often elided in the published literature, is between surface displacement — measurable change at the cutaneous envelope detected by photogrammetric or optical imaging, and deep structural lift — gravitational repositioning of identifiable deeper anatomical structures (skin, superficial musculo-aponeurotic system, retaining ligaments) against the vector of facial descent. A filler may produce measurable surface displacement without producing deep structural lift, and surface displacement is a valid clinical observation in its own right. Relatedly, “lift” is used in the literature in at least two distinct ways: phenomenologically, to describe a visible superior or restored appearance after an aesthetic intervention; and mechanically, to describe the structural repositioning of deeper anatomical components against gravity, as in surgical rhytidectomy. A field may reasonably choose to define lift phenomenologically rather than structurally; visible superior displacement of the cutaneous envelope is itself a valid operational definition. Our argument is not against that choice. It is against the coexistence of multiple incompatible definitions under a single apparently objective term, within an evidence base that uses one word for both without specifying which is being measured. The construct validity of “lift” in this literature is therefore the central issue we address.

Importantly, this paper does not argue that fillers fail to produce clinically appreciable aesthetic improvement, nor that superior tissue displacement cannot occur after filler injection. Rather, it examines whether the construct currently termed “lift” has been operationalised with sufficient methodological precision to support mechanistic and quantitative claims within the literature.

The term was imported from surgical rhytidectomy, where it carries a specific mechanical meaning: identifiable tissue components are mobilised, repositioned in a defined vector, and re-secured in a new anatomical location.1,2 Filler injection produces mechanical effects of its own — local tissue expansion, tension on surrounding structures, contour modification, light reflex modulation — that are real and clinically valuable, but are not the same effects as those produced by surgical mobilisation and suspension. The borrowed vocabulary therefore carries a connotation of mechanism the published evidence base has not yet demonstrated the intervention reproduces in the same form.

This paper examines the operational status of “lift” as a construct in the filler literature and proposes minimum standards toward a more defensible definition. We focus exclusively on injectable soft tissue fillers (hyaluronic acid, calcium hydroxylapatite, poly-l-lactic acid, polycaprolactone, hybrid products), excluding thread-based lifting, energy-based devices, topical agents, surgical procedures, and combined-modality studies in which filler contribution cannot be isolated. Our central argument is that the field would benefit from an explicit operational definition of filler-mediated lift, and the published evidence base does not yet provide one.

Methods

This is a structured narrative critique, not a PRISMA-compliant systematic review. Our aim was methodological mapping: to identify how the construct of “lift” is defined and measured in filler studies and to compare reported effect sizes against the documented accuracy of the measurement modalities used. We did not undertake a systematic review because the objective was not pooled efficacy estimation, but conceptual analysis of how “lift” has been operationalised across heterogeneous study designs, measurement modalities, and outcome frameworks.

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE between 20 and 23 May 2026 using the string: (“lift” OR “lifting effect” OR “facial lift” OR “liquid facelift” OR “non-surgical facelift”) AND (“filler” OR “hyaluronic acid” OR “calcium hydroxylapatite” OR “calcium hydroxyapatite” OR “poly-l-lactic acid” OR “polycaprolactone” OR “biostimulator”). Additional papers were identified by reference scanning. Studies were eligible if they investigated injectable soft tissue fillers and used the term “lift” or its derivatives as an outcome, technique, or procedural label. Studies were excluded if the primary intervention was non-injectable or if filler was combined with non-injectable modalities such that filler contribution to lift could not be isolated. For each eligible study we extracted: filler type, study design, sample size and demographics, the operational definition of “lift” used, the measurement modality and its documented accuracy, the reported effect size and its variance, and the presence of a pre-specified threshold for clinical significance. Studies were grouped by measurement construct rather than by chronology, indication, or filler type, to make the conceptual heterogeneity of the literature visible. Reported effect sizes were compared against published validation data for the measurement modalities used.

Results

Fourteen eligible studies identified through the structured search are summarised in Table 1, spanning cadaveric, prospective and retrospective clinical studies, randomised comparator studies, case series, editorial letters, and narrative reviews from 2018 to 2025. Across this corpus, “lift” was operationalised in multiple categorically distinct ways: three-dimensional surface displacement at the cutaneous envelope measured by Vectra-class imaging;1,3, 4, 5 surface volume change in cubic centimetres reported in lieu of displacement;2,6 facial tension vector change on the cutaneous surface;3 angular change at anatomical landmarks reported as “lift”;7 injection technique or strategy named as a “lift” without independent verification of mechanical tissue elevation;4,8, 9, 10, 11 procedure label or effect descriptor applied without measurement;12,13 and umbrella term in narrative trend articles or marketing-adjacent literature.14 Together, these categories describe different phenomena grouped under a single word (Fig. 1). Reported effect magnitudes and their variance

Where mm-magnitude surface displacement was reported, the magnitudes were consistently small. Haidar et al. measured local effects of 0.11 mm (medial midface), 0.57 mm (temple), 0.74 mm (perioral), 0.81 mm (lateral midface), 0.29 mm (jawline), and 1.11 mm (forehead) on cadaveric tissue after standardised injection.1 Urdiales-Gálvez et al. reported facial tension vector increases of 2.0–2.2 mm at 180 days in living patients after hybrid filler injection.² Volumetric changes ranged 0.03–1.16 cm³ across the relevant studies.2,6

Suwanchinda et al. assessed the posterior temporal supraSMAS lifting technique in 15 patients (mean age 37.1 ± 9.4 years) by subjective percent-improvement scoring, and in 3 cadaveric donors (mean age 85.67 ± 9.7 years) by Vectra H1 imaging.4 No mm vector data were reported for the clinical sample. In the cadaveric sample, three observations bear on the methodological interpretation of filler-mediated lift: (i) increased injected volume produced smaller skin displacement vectors, with 1 mL producing larger vectors than 3 or 4 mL, attributed by the authors to gravitational redistribution of product once compartmental containment was exceeded; (ii) the donor with significant skin laxity showed no measurable skin displacement, prompting the authors’ observation that the technique “might show minimal-to-no effects in patients with skin laxity or excess of skin”;4 and (iii) fanning injection and adjunctive subcision, both common clinical practices, also reduced the magnitude of measured vectors.

Hernandez et al. extended the same technique to the in vivo clinical setting in 12 patients (24 temples; 10 HA, 14 CaHA) using Vectra H2 imaging at three months.5 Mean horizontal lifting was 0.13 ± 1.0 mm; mean vertical lifting 0.02 ± 0.5 mm; three-month volume changes were +0.26 ± 1.1 mL in the temple and −0.22 ± 3.5 mL in the middle/lower face. In each case the standard deviation exceeded the mean by a factor of approximately 8 for horizontal lift, 25 for vertical lift, 4 for temple volume, and 16 for midface volume.

Imaging-modality accuracy

The relevant question is not whether sub-millimetre changes are theoretically detectable by Vectra-class imaging, under standardised conditions they may be, but whether the studies in this corpus reported the within-subject repeatability, registration accuracy, and region-specific validation needed to support the interpretive confidence placed on their measurements.

Several distinct concepts of accuracy are relevant. Vectra H2 has reported global three-dimensional trueness with RMS error exceeding 1.0 mm, with regional trueness below 1.0 mm³; Vectra H1 has demonstrated mean absolute error below 2 mm for linear measurements.15 Repeatability data for Vectra H2 show mean absolute difference of 0.13 anthropometric units for periocular landmarks;16 reproducibility between Vectra H2 and the static Vectra M3 yields inter-device MAD of 0.62 units.16 Registration error (alignment of pre/post scans) and landmarking error are rarely reported separately in the filler literature, though they dominate within-subject measurement of small longitudinal change. Regional accuracy within a confined area is typically better than global accuracy across the full face.17

A pre/post within-subject comparison of a small region can, in principle, detect changes smaller than the global RMS error, if the specific repeatability and registration performance for that comparison are validated. No study in the present corpus reports those specific dimensions for its specific regional measure. Taken together, these considerations mean that interpreting sub-millimetre longitudinal surface changes requires study-specific validation rather than reliance on favourable global device-performance figures alone. Detectability of small longitudinal surface change is not equivalent to proof that the measured change reflects the specific biological construct being claimed.

Rater independence and effect size

Where outcome scoring is performed by multiple rater groups within the same study, less independent raters may report larger improvement, as illustrated by Hernandez et al. The reported three-month GAIS score was 3.08 ± 0.6 from three independent blinded observers, 4.33 from the treating physician, and 4.83 from the patient.5 The patient–physician ICC was 0.317, which the authors themselves described as indicating poor consistency between the two parties most directly invested in the procedure.5

Pre-specified clinical thresholds

In no reviewed study reporting mm-magnitude lift was a pre-specified threshold disclosed for what magnitude of measured change would constitute a clinically meaningful lift. Statistically significant differences within or near the device’s documented error envelope have been reported without comparison against a benchmark of clinical significance derived from surgical, perceptual, or other principled sources.

Discussion

Four patterns emerge from this review, considered alongside a note on methodological symmetry with the surgical literature and a brief comment on scope.

Construct fragmentation

“Lift,” as used in the filler literature, does not refer to a single phenomenon. It refers to a family of phenomena that share a word but not a mechanism, a measurement, or a clinical implication. A study that measures three-dimensional surface displacement is not measuring the same thing as a study that names an injection technique, reports volumetric change in cubic centimetres, or reports angular change at an anatomical landmark. Yet all are reported, cited, and translated into clinical language under the same heading.

The fragmentation is evident in the paper titles themselves. Casabona et al. frame “lifting” and “volumizing” as distinct despite outcomes based on injected volume and global aesthetic scoring.6 Cohen et al. assess “Vectorial Facial Sculpting” using GAIS alone10 while Hong and Yi describe a hypothesised mechanism without independent measurement.11 Although each paper uses “lift,” they operationalise it differently, or not at all. The convergence in headline claims exceeds what the underlying data warrant.

Surface displacement is not necessarily deep structural lift

Within the subset of studies reporting quantitative measurement, the construct being measured is surface displacement, not deep structural lift. This is a consequence of the imaging modalities available rather than of authorial intent: optical photogrammetric systems image the cutaneous envelope and do not independently track deeper structures relative to fixed bony landmarks. A filler placed beneath the skin will produce some surface displacement by virtue of its volume alone; surface displacement therefore does not, on its own, demonstrate repositioning of deeper anatomical structures against gravity.

Where reported surface displacement is small and its standard deviation exceeds the mean, as in Hernandez et al.,5 surface displacement itself becomes difficult to distinguish from measurement noise; the authors of that study themselves noted that “the calculated changes might be statistically significant in a theoretical (=cadaveric) model but it is unclear whether one can extrapolate these results to the in vivo clinical setting.”5 The Suwanchinda et al. finding of inverse dose-response is consistent with this picture: multiple mechanisms (compartmental overflow, vector dissipation, tissue compliance, rheology, gravitational loading, nonlinear biomechanics) could plausibly contribute to non-monotonic dose-response, but the observation complicates a simplistic dose-dependent mechanical lifting model and is one any operational definition would need to accommodate.4

Rater independence and the outcome gradient

Within studies that include multiple rater groups, effect-size estimates may increase as the rater becomes less independent of the procedure. The Hernandez et al. GAIS gradient — 3.08 (independent observers), 4.33 (treating physician), 4.83 (patient), with a patient–physician ICC of 0.317 — illustrates this and is consistent with established findings on unblinded outcome rating.5 This is methodologically important because subjective scales often serve as the primary outcome where objective measurement is absent, sub-threshold, or sits within measurement noise.

A note on methodological symmetry

We have focused on the filler literature, but the methodological standards we propose apply equally to surgical lift research. Surgical outcome measurement is itself subject to subjective assessment, inter-observer variability in photographic grading, and inconsistent endpoints. The asymmetry we describe is one of magnitude: in deep plane rhytidectomy and related procedures, intraoperative vector measurements and observed tissue displacement are of the order of millimetres to centimetres, well above the documented error envelope of any reasonable measurement modality.18 Whether such effects are also clinically meaningful or proportionate to risk is a question for that literature to answer. The point of comparison is mechanistic, not evaluative.

The category translation problem

“Lift,” in surgical practice, refers to identifiable tissue components being mobilised, repositioned, and re-secured — anatomically defined, mechanically specified, and clinically observable at magnitudes of millimetres to centimetres of tissue displacement.18 Filler injection does not reproduce this mechanical process; it produces a different set of effects — secondary tissue displacement, contour redistribution, light-reflex modulation, and perceptual changes in facial support — that are real and clinically valuable, but distinct from the direct mobilisation and fixation of tissue performed in surgical rhytidectomy. The borrowed surgical vocabulary therefore translates incompletely. Measurement alone does not bridge this translation gap; the gap is partly conceptual and requires conceptual work.

At the same time, aesthetic improvement in facial rejuvenation is not purely a mechanical construct. Perception of age, attractiveness, and facial support is influenced by contour transitions, projection, and light reflex patterns that may change after filler treatment, independent of measurable deep tissue repositioning.19, 20, 21 Such perceptual effects may themselves constitute clinically valuable outcomes. The issue raised here is therefore not whether fillers produce meaningful aesthetic benefit, but whether phenomenological improvement and mechanically defined “lift” are being treated as equivalent constructs within the literature.

Scope and implications

We have deliberately excluded combined-modality studies in which filler is administered alongside thread lifting or energy-based devices: filler contribution to lift cannot be isolated where mechanically active interventions are co-administered, so such studies lie outside the scope of any review aiming to evaluate filler-mediated lift on its own terms.

As a structured narrative critique, this paper draws on the most-cited and most-influential investigations of filler-mediated lift rather than on an exhaustive enumeration. If studies exist that operationalise “lift” consistently across investigators, with effect sizes well above documented measurement variance and pre-specified clinical thresholds, the field would benefit from their being foregrounded. We have not encountered such studies in the contemporary literature; we do not claim they do not exist.

The patterns above interact. The fragmented construct permits findings from one operationalisation of “lift” to be cited as evidence for another, despite differences in mechanism, measurement, and interpretation. Subjective outcome gradients further amplify this ambiguity, while borrowed surgical terminology may imply a degree of mechanistic equivalence not yet established by the available evidence. A more explicit operational definition would not preclude the use of fillers, nor diminish the clinical value of the contour, light-reflex, and perceptual effects they may produce. It would clarify what is being measured, what is being inferred, and what remains conceptually unresolved.

This paper has several limitations. As a structured narrative critique, the selected corpus is illustrative rather than exhaustive and may not capture all contemporary operationalisations of filler-mediated lift. Selection bias is therefore possible. Some proprietary or unpublished validation data for imaging systems may also exist outside the peer-reviewed literature. Additionally, advances in imaging registration, machine-learning-assisted landmarking, and longitudinal facial analysis may improve the precision of future assessments beyond the performance characteristics discussed here. The purpose of this critique is therefore not to foreclose future operational refinement, but to evaluate the methodological status of the construct as presently represented in the published literature.

Proposed framework

We propose five minimum standards for the use of the term “lift” in filler literature, clinical practice, and patient communication.1. Definitional precision. Authors should specify, in any study using the term “lift,” which construct is being measured: surface displacement, surface volume change, vector change, angular change, evaluator-rated improvement, patient-rated rejuvenation, or another defined outcome. Where the construct is mechanical repositioning of deeper tissue, that should be stated explicitly and operationalised by an appropriate modality. Where the construct is phenomenological — visible improvement as perceived by patient or observer — that should also be stated explicitly. The constructs are not equivalent and should not be reported under a single undifferentiated label.2. Measurement-modality validation. Quantitative claims of lift should be accompanied by the documented accuracy of the measurement modality in the dimensions relevant to the claim — trueness, repeatability, reproducibility, regional versus global error, registration error, landmarking error — reported in the same units and at the same anatomical scale as the claimed effect. The standard deviation should be reported alongside the mean. Where the effect size falls within the documented error envelope, or where the standard deviation exceeds the mean, the claim should be qualified accordingly.3. A priori clinical significance threshold. Studies should specify, before data collection, the magnitude of measured change that would constitute a clinically meaningful effect, justified by reference to surgical lift magnitudes in equivalent regions, known thresholds of patient perception, or other reasoned benchmarks. Post-hoc statistical significance in the absence of such a threshold should not be reported as evidence of clinically meaningful lift.4. Control, blinding, and confounding. Prospective studies should include appropriate controls (contralateral untreated side, matched cohort, or sham injection) to permit attribution of observed change to the intervention rather than to weight fluctuation, photographic drift, head positioning, oedema, or other confounds. Subjective outcome scoring should be performed by raters blinded to the intervention; where multiple rater groups are reported, the primary outcome should be the blinded independent rating.5. Language fidelity. Clinical, educational, and patient-facing communication should align with the construct that has actually been measured. Where the measured outcome is contour change, surface displacement, or evaluator-rated improvement, those terms should be available alongside the broader term “lift.” This is not a recommendation against the phenomenological use of “lift” in patient communication; it is a recommendation that the literature and the consent process distinguish what has been shown from what has been claimed.

The standard deviation should be reported alongside the mean. Where the effect size falls within the documented error envelope, or where the standard deviation exceeds the mean, the claim should be qualified accordingly.

3. A priori clinical significance threshold. Studies should specify, before data collection, the magnitude of measured change that would constitute a clinically meaningful effect, justified by reference to surgical lift magnitudes in equivalent regions, known thresholds of patient perception, or other reasoned benchmarks. Post-hoc statistical significance in the absence of such a threshold should not be reported as evidence of clinically meaningful lift.

4. Control, blinding, and confounding. Prospective studies should include appropriate controls (contralateral untreated side, matched cohort, or sham injection) to permit attribution of observed change to the intervention rather than to weight fluctuation, photographic drift, head positioning, oedema, or other confounds. 5. Language fidelity. Clinical, educational, and patient-facing communication should align with the construct that has actually been measured. Where the measured outcome is contour change, surface displacement, or evaluator-rated improvement, those terms should be available alongside the broader term “lift.” This is not a recommendation against the phenomenological use of “lift” in patient communication; it is a recommendation that the literature and the consent process distinguish what has been shown from what has been claimed.

Conclusion

In the soft tissue filler literature, “lift” currently functions as an umbrella term encompassing multiple distinct constructs, including facial surface displacement, volumetric augmentation, angular change, evaluator-rated improvement, procedural nomenclature, and phenomenological rejuvenation. These constructs are not methodologically or mechanistically equivalent, yet are frequently discussed interchangeably within the literature.

Where quantitative measurements are reported, they are derived from changes at the cutaneous surface rather than direct tracking of deeper anatomical structures. Reported effect magnitudes are often small relative to measurement variance, while clinically meaningful thresholds for defining “lift” remain largely unspecified. At the same time, fillers may produce clinically valuable aesthetic and perceptual improvements independent of mechanically defined tissue repositioning.

The present critique does not argue against the clinical utility of fillers, but for greater definitional precision regarding the construct being measured and claimed. A more defensible operational framework for filler-mediated “lift” will require explicit construct definition, validated measurement methodology, clinically meaningful thresholds, and terminology that accurately reflects what has actually been measured.
