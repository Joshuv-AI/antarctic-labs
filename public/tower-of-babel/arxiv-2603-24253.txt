Deletion Does Not Measure Contribution in Coupled-Channel Dynamics
Jin Lei, Hao Liu

Abstract
In projected descriptions of quantum dynamics, the importance of an eliminated degree of freedom is routinely assessed by deleting it and measuring the system’s response. This conflates two effects: the channel’s intrinsic contribution and the reorganization of the surviving model space. Here we disentangle them in continuum-discretized coupled-channels (CDCC) scattering, decomposing the Feshbach dynamic polarization potential (DPP) channel by channel while keeping the full Green’s function intact, and comparing with conventional bin-deletion from the coupled equations. For d d + 58 Ni the two approaches reproduce the same elastic S S -matrix to 0.45%, yet a channel ranked first by one diagnostic is ranked fifth by the other. A frozen-basis protocol, zeroing couplings without reducing the basis, yields rankings that track the DPP closely ( ρ DPP , frozen = 0.94 \rho_{\rm DPP,frozen}=0.94 ) and are uncorrelated with standard deletion ( ρ frozen , del = − 0.37 \rho_{\rm frozen,del}=-0.37 ), establishing that the discrepancy is dominated by model-space reorganization. Pairwise analysis reveals quantum anti-synergy: adjacent channels partially cancel through off-diagonal Green’s-function coherence, in all 10 tested pairs by the DPP and 8 of 10 by deletion. The asymmetry between excluding a degree of freedom from the effective interaction and deleting it from the model space is algebraic and general; basis-preserving decoupling, implementable in any coupled-channel code, isolates the reorganization component.



Introduction.
—A central strategy in quantum physics is to
divide a system’s degrees of freedom into those treated explicitly
and those absorbed into an effective description of the rest. The
optical potential, the many-body self-energy, and the
renormalization-group effective Hamiltonian all arise this
way
7
;
8
;
9
; so do Feshbach
resonances in ultracold gases
4
and the reduced
dynamics of open quantum
systems
5
. The full and effective
descriptions yield identical observables, but they decompose the
physics differently. In particular, they give different answers to the
question: which degrees of freedom matter most? In the full
description, one removes a degree of freedom and measures the
system’s response. In the effective description, one can decompose
the effective interaction into contributions from each eliminated
degree of freedom without disturbing the rest. These are operationally
distinct procedures, one restructures the dynamics; the other
dissects the interaction. Yet whether they agree has not been tested.

This Letter performs the test. The question is general:
configuration-interaction shell models embedded in the
continuum
17
, coupled-cluster effective
Hamiltonians, and renormalization-group decimation all face the same
ambiguity whenever a basis state is removed to probe its
importance. We expose and quantify the distinction in
continuum-discretized coupled-channels (CDCC)
scattering
1
;
21
, where deletion diagnostics
have been the sole tool for assessing channel
importance
18
;
2
for half a century: they have been used to
compare resonant and nonresonant breakup
states
10
;
11
;
12
;
13
, to quantify continuum-continuum
coupling
3
;
20
, and to identify dominant
partial-wave components
14
. Complementary studies have
mapped the structure and range of coupling form
factors
16
and the convergence of the model space with
respect to angular momentum and excitation
energy
6
. In every case, however, the assessment
requires re-solving the coupled equations after modifying the
coupling matrix or basis, and the implicit assumption is that the
measured response equals the channel’s intrinsic contribution.

The full-coupling dynamic polarization potential (DPP) recently
computed by Liu, Lei, and Ren
15
provides the
effective-interaction counterpart needed for the comparison. The DPP
encodes the effect of all continuum channels in an exact nonlocal
potential for the elastic channel. Because it is a bilinear sum over
channel pairs [Eq. (
1
)], one can exclude a channel from
this sum while leaving the full-space Green’s function intact, a
decomposition that has no counterpart in the coupled-channel
framework. We compare DPP channel-exclusion with CDCC bin-deletion,
using the same code, potential, and bin structure for

d
+
58
Ni. A frozen-basis protocol, zeroing couplings without
reducing the basis, isolates model-space reorganization as the
dominant source of the discrepancy and provides a
reorganization-free importance diagnostic implementable in any
coupled-channel code without a DPP calculation.

Decomposing the effective interaction.
—Within the Feshbach
formalism
7
;
8
, the elastic-channel
effective Hamiltonian contains the DPP

\Delta U(R,R^{\prime})=\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}U_{0\gamma}(R)\,g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(R,R^{\prime})\,U_{\gamma^{\prime}0}(R^{\prime}),

(1)

where
U_{0\gamma}
couples the elastic channel to continuum channel

\gamma
(labeled by orbital angular momentum
l
and
excitation-energy interval), and
g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
is the
full-coupling Green’s function retaining all continuum-continuum
couplings
15
. The off-diagonal elements

g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
(
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}
) encode quantum coherence
between eliminated states, the terms absent in prior weak-coupling
treatments
3
;
18
.

Grouping the double sum by channel participation decomposes the DPP
into direct and bridge contributions for each channel
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\Delta U^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}+B_{\alpha},

(2)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=U_{0\alpha}\,g_{\alpha\alpha}\,U_{\alpha 0},

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\gamma\neq\alpha}\bigl(U_{0\alpha}\,g_{\alpha\gamma}\,U_{\gamma 0}+U_{0\gamma}\,g_{\gamma\alpha}\,U_{\alpha 0}\bigr),

so that
\Delta U=\sum_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}B_{\alpha}

(the factor
\frac{1}{2}
avoids double-counting). The direct term

D_{\alpha}
represents virtual excitation
elastic
{}\to\alpha\to{}
elastic via the diagonal propagator

g_{\alpha\alpha}
. The bridge term
B_{\alpha}
captures multi-state
paths elastic
{}\to\alpha\to\gamma^{\prime}\to{}
elastic mediated by the
off-diagonal
g_{\alpha\gamma^{\prime}}
: a state with weak direct coupling
can still be important if it bridges strongly coupled states, a

gateway channel
whose intrinsic contribution is modest but
whose removal severs mediation pathways for its neighbors.

To assess channel
\alpha
, we remove it from the sum in
Eq. (
1
) but keep
g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
unchanged.
This is an algebraic operation on the already-solved DPP, not a
re-solution of any coupled equations: the full-space Green’s function
is computed once and held fixed, so every other channel’s propagation
is unaffected. Each channel is therefore assessed against an identical
background, a property that standard deletion lacks.
We quantify the
S
-matrix impact through

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}=\frac{|S_{\mathrm{full}}-S_{\mathrm{no}\,\alpha}|}{|S_{\mathrm{full}}|},

(3)

where
S_{\mathrm{full}}
and
S_{\mathrm{no}\,\alpha}
are the
elastic
S
-matrix elements at fixed total angular momentum
J
.
Because the Green’s function is held fixed, each

\Delta U^{(\alpha)}
[Eq. (
2
)] is uniquely defined
regardless of which other channels are simultaneously excluded;

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
maps that potential-level contribution onto the
elastic observable. Note that
S
depends nonlinearly on
\Delta U
,
so
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
is not additive across channels; nor is the
exclusion measure
\Delta U^{(\alpha)}
itself, since bridge terms
shared between channels
\alpha
and
\beta
enter both

B_{\alpha}
and
B_{\beta}
. What the fixed-
g
construction guarantees
is not additivity but
independence
: assessing
\alpha
does not
alter the background against which
\beta
is assessed.

By contrast, standard bin-deletion removes
\alpha
from the model
space and re-solves the coupled equations in the reduced space

r=\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{\alpha\}
. Writing

g^{(-\alpha)}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
for the Green’s function of the
reduced space, the residual DPP after excluding versus deleting

\alpha
takes the same structural form but with different
propagators (
g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
versus

g^{(-\alpha)}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
). Their difference isolates the
reorganization algebraically:

\Delta U_{\rm del}^{(-\alpha)}-\Delta U_{\rm excl}^{(-\alpha)}=\!\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in r}\!U_{0\gamma}\!\left(g^{(-\alpha)}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}-g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\right)\!U_{\gamma^{\prime}0}.

(4)

This
reorganization term
vanishes only if
\alpha
decouples
from every surviving channel; generically it does not. The deletion
diagnostic therefore measures not the channel’s intrinsic
contribution but its removal response: intrinsic contribution plus
the reorganization of the surviving space.
Equation (
4
) is the central result of this Letter: it
holds for any Feshbach-projected coupled-channel system and makes no
reference to the specific Hamiltonian, basis, or scattering
formalism. The frozen-basis protocol (zeroing
\alpha
’s couplings
while retaining its basis state) eliminates the dominant
reorganization mechanism by preserving the quadrature grid,
approximating DPP exclusion from within the coupled-channel
framework.

To probe inter-channel coherence, we define the pairwise interaction
term

I=\frac{|\Delta S_{\alpha\beta}|-|\Delta S_{\alpha}|-|\Delta S_{\beta}|}{|\Delta S_{\alpha}|+|\Delta S_{\beta}|},

(5)

where
\Delta S_{\alpha}=S_{\mathrm{full}}-S_{\mathrm{no}\,\alpha}
.
Anti-synergy (
I<0
) signals destructive interference carried by
the off-diagonal propagator
g_{\alpha\beta}
.

We test these ideas on
d
+
58
Ni at
E_{\mathrm{lab}}=21.6
MeV
(
J=8

l=0,1,2
(18 bins

potential
19

identical code, Hamiltonian, bin structure, and numerical parameters;
only the channel-removal protocol differs.

The direction of the inversion is physically informative. The DPP,
which probes coupling strength weighted by the full propagator, ranks
high-energy bins highest: their larger breakup momenta produce
stronger overlap with the short-range nuclear potential at the
surface. Standard deletion ranks low-energy bins highest: removing a
bin near the breakup threshold causes the largest disruption to the
surviving continuum discretization, inflating the apparent importance
of a channel whose intrinsic coupling is weak. This
inversion, intrinsically weak channels scoring highest under
deletion, is the hallmark of reorganization-dominated attribution.

A frozen-basis control experiment isolates the origin of the
discrepancy. Standard CDCC deletion removes channel
\alpha
from
the model space entirely: the coupled equations shrink in dimension,
and the surviving channels propagate through a reduced

\mathcal{Q}
-space in which the continuum quadrature has been altered.
The measured response therefore conflates two effects: the loss of

\alpha
’s coupling and the readjustment of the surviving basis. To
separate them, we repeat the CDCC calculation with all coupling matrix
elements involving
\alpha
set to zero, both elastic-to-
\alpha

and continuum-to-
\alpha
, while retaining
\alpha
’s basis state in
the coupled equations. The coupled equations remain
N\times N
: channel
\alpha
is
dynamically inert but still occupies its slot in the basis, so the
Gauss-Legendre quadrature grid is identical to the full calculation.
Standard deletion, by contrast, shrinks the system to

(N{-}1)\times(N{-}1)
and rebuilds the quadrature without

\alpha
. The frozen-basis and DPP channel-exclusion therefore
isolate the same single variable,
\alpha
’s coupling, by
different routes: one within the coupled equations, the other within
the effective potential.

The result is unambiguous (Fig.
1
). Frozen-basis
rankings track the DPP closely (
\rho_{\rm DPP,frozen}=0.94
for
l=2
,
0.77

for
l=0
) and are essentially uncorrelated with standard deletion
(
\rho_{\rm frozen,del}=-0.37
and
0.09
). This three-way comparison establishes
that the ranking discrepancy between DPP and standard deletion is
dominated by the basis reorganization that standard deletion
induces: when that single confound is removed, the two operationally
distinct diagnostics converge.

Quantum anti-synergy.
—The bridge terms in
Eq. (
2
) predict a specific consequence of
inter-channel coherence: adjacent bins, whose wave functions share
similar spatial envelopes but carry slightly shifted oscillation
phases (
\Delta k\propto\sqrt{\Delta\varepsilon}
), should
partially cancel each other’s contribution through the off-diagonal
propagator
g_{\alpha\beta}
. Removing only
\alpha
from the DPP
sum eliminates both its direct term and its bridge cross terms with

\beta
; the surviving cross terms from
\beta
’s side partially
compensate, inflating
|\Delta S_{\alpha}|
. Removing both eliminates
the compensating terms as well, so

|\Delta S_{\alpha\beta}|<|\Delta S_{\alpha}|+|\Delta S_{\beta}|
:
anti-synergy (see End Matter for the phase-structure analysis).

That deletion confirms anti-synergy in 8 of 10 pairs shows that
inter-channel coherence is strong enough to survive reorganization
noise in most cases. That it fails in the two cases where
simultaneous two-bin deletion produces the largest quadrature
disruption is not a weakness of the comparison but a
self-consistency check: the reorganization interpretation correctly
predicts which pairs will deviate and in which
direction.

Anti-synergy also compounds the reorganization problem identified
above. When two channels partially cancel through

g_{\alpha\beta}
, deleting one removes the cancellation and inflates
the survivor’s apparent importance. Gateway channels, those
dominated by bridge terms, are especially susceptible: their
deletion severs mediation pathways for neighboring channels, producing
a large reorganization response that far exceeds their intrinsic
contribution. Deletion diagnostics therefore measure not only a
channel’s own coupling but also the coherence it shares with its
neighbors, a confound that the DPP, evaluating each channel against
the intact propagator, avoids by construction.

Discussion.
—The central finding is that conventional
channel-deletion diagnostics mix two physically distinct effects:
the deleted channel’s intrinsic contribution to the breakup
coupling and the reorganization of the surviving model space. The
frozen-basis control experiment decisively identifies reorganization
as the dominant source of the ranking discrepancy: when the
discretization basis is held fixed, CDCC rankings converge to the
DPP rankings (
\rho_{\rm DPP,frozen}=0.94
for
l=2
). Standard deletion, which
allows the quadrature to readjust, produces rankings essentially
uncorrelated with the frozen-basis result
(
\rho_{\mathrm{frozen,del}}=-0.37
). The DPP decomposition therefore provides a
reorganization-free diagnostic that reveals the channel
contributions standard deletion obscures. Deletion-based channel hierarchies in the
literature
18
;
10
;
11
;
3
are therefore best interpreted as truncation-sensitivity
rankings; additional basis-preserving analysis is needed before
promoting them to statements about intrinsic channel contribution.

A natural objection arises: if the DPP decomposition reveals
intrinsic channel contributions, should it not also be the better
guide for model-space truncation? A truncation benchmark shows that
it should not, and that this is precisely the point. Retaining the
top
N
channels (out of 12
l=0,2
bins;
l=1
always kept)
according to each ranking: at moderate truncation (
N=9
), the
deletion-ranked selection reproduces the full
S
-matrix within
6%, while the DPP-ranked selection gives 20%; at aggressive
truncation (
N=6
), both give
\sim
18%. Deletion outperforms
because deletion ranking already encodes the reorganization that
truncation will cause; it is, in effect, a self-consistent
predictor of its own consequences. The DPP ranking, by construction,
strips out that reorganization to expose the underlying channel
structure. The two diagnostics therefore answer different questions:
which channels should one keep? (deletion) versus which
channels contribute most to the effective interaction of the intact
system? (DPP). Neither subsumes the other; together they provide a
complete picture.

Two levels of generality should be distinguished. The

conceptual
inequivalence between DPP exclusion and
coupled-channel deletion follows from the Feshbach projection algebra
itself: the DPP sum [Eq. (
1
)] and the coupled-channel
Green’s function
\mathbf{G}_{QQ}
(whose elements are the

g_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
) are mathematically independent
objects, so excluding a channel from the former leaves

\mathbf{G}_{QQ}
invariant while deleting it from the latter does
not. This asymmetry holds in any projected coupled-channel framework.
How large the resulting ranking discrepancy is, however, depends on
the system and the partial wave: the strength of inter-channel
coherence, the density of the discretization, and the coupling
topology all modulate the reorganization magnitude. The
d
+
58
Ni calculation demonstrates
that, even in a mild system with non-resonant continuum, the effect
is already large enough to invert the importance hierarchy. The
frozen-basis control converges to DPP rankings in both the

l=0
and
l=2
sectors (
\rho_{\rm DPP,frozen}=0.77
and
0.94
), which
differ markedly in coupling structure, confirming that the
effect is robust across angular-momentum regimes within the test
system. Importantly, the frozen-basis protocol does not require a DPP
implementation: it operates entirely within the standard
coupled-channel framework. Any CDCC code that currently performs
bin-deletion can implement frozen-basis deletion by zeroing coupling
matrix elements rather than removing basis states. Comparing the two
rankings for a given system provides an immediate, self-contained
diagnostic of the reorganization content in standard deletion
results.

The quantum anti-synergy effect, confirmed by both methods in 8 of
10 adjacent pairs, is the observable consequence of inter-channel
coherence encoded in the off-diagonal Green’s function

g_{\alpha\beta}
. It demonstrates that the eliminated states are
not independent: their individual contributions to the effective
interaction interfere, making the whole nonadditive.

The magnitude of the reorganization effect demonstrated here
should be viewed as a lower bound. The test system

d
+
58
Ni, with its non-resonant continuum, non-halo
projectile, and moderate Coulomb field, is among the mildest
applications of CDCC. In halo-nucleus reactions (
6
He,

11
Li,
11
Be), the projectile wave function extends far
beyond the nuclear surface, producing much larger spatial overlap
between continuum bins and correspondingly stronger off-diagonal
Green’s-function elements
g_{\alpha\beta}
. Both the
reorganization of the surviving model space and the inter-channel
anti-synergy scale with this overlap, so the ranking distortions
reported here, already sufficient to invert the importance hierarchy
in a mild system, are expected to be substantially larger in
precisely the exotic systems where CDCC truncation decisions matter
most.

The frozen-basis protocol introduced here, decoupling a
degree of freedom without shrinking the basis, provides a practical
diagnostic for isolating the reorganization component of deletion
tests whenever the conceptual inequivalence identified above is
suspected to be numerically significant.

Phase structure of anti-synergy.
—

For two adjacent bins
\alpha,\beta
with the same
l
, the
off-diagonal Green’s function

g_{\alpha\beta}(R,R^{\prime})=(2\mu/\hbar^{2})\sum_{m}u_{\alpha}^{m}(R)\,h_{\beta}^{m}(R^{\prime})/\mathcal{W}^{m}(R^{\prime})

(
R<R^{\prime}
) is built from the regular and irregular solutions of the
coupled
\mathcal{Q}
-space equations
15
. Adjacent
bins share similar radial envelopes but differ in oscillation phase
by
\Delta\phi\sim\Delta k\cdot R
, where

\Delta k\propto\sqrt{\Delta\varepsilon}
.

The
S
-matrix shift from excluding channel
\alpha
contains both
diagonal and bridge contributions:

\Delta S_{\alpha}\propto\mathrm{Im}\langle\varphi_{P}^{J}|D_{\alpha}+B_{\alpha}|\varphi_{P}^{J}\rangle.

(6)

Because
g_{\alpha\beta}
carries a phase offset relative to

g_{\alpha\alpha}
, the vectors
\Delta S_{\alpha}
and

\Delta S_{\beta}
point in nearly opposite directions in the complex
plane. For the
l=2
pair [4–6]
\times
[6–8] MeV at
J=8
,
the angle between
\Delta S_{\alpha}
and
\Delta S_{\beta}
is

{\sim}\,138^{\circ}
(nearly anti-parallel). When both are removed, the
compensating cross terms
U_{0\alpha}\,g_{\alpha\beta}\,U_{\beta 0}

also vanish, so
|\Delta S_{\alpha\beta}|
is smaller than

|\Delta S_{\alpha}|+|\Delta S_{\beta}|
.

Anti-synergy requires (i) comparable coupling strengths for both
channels, (ii) adjacent energy bins (small
\Delta k
), and
(iii) same orbital angular momentum (sufficient spatial overlap in

g_{\alpha\beta}
). These conditions hold for 8 of the 10 tested
pairs; the two exceptions involve either highly asymmetric importance
or mid-continuum gaps that amplify reorganization in the CDCC
comparison.
