Decoding preeclampsia: A fusion of multi-view machine learning and multi-omics to identify putative inflammation-related mechanisms
Authors: Yuting Guo, Yuchao Liang, Lingxuan Liu, Yan Zhou, Xiaoyan Yang, Xin Ming, Pengwei Hu, Jie Wu, Debang Li, Dongxia Hou, Shuqin Xia, Xiaohua Wang, Yongchun Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preeclampsia (PE) is a leading cause of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality worldwide, with placental inflammation recognized as a central pathogenic feature, yet the upstream triggers and inflammatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we combined placental single-cell transcriptomics with gut metagenomic and metabolomic profiling to characterize inflammatory signatures in PE. Stratified analyses across clinical subgroups—defined by fetal number, onset timing, and fetal sex—revealed that placental single-cell transcriptomics coupled with multi-view machine learning consistently prioritized bacteria-associated inflammatory features across all subgroups. Superimposed on this shared foundation, we identified subgroup-specific trajectories: twin PE exhibited IL-1-dominant inflammation with compensatory antioxidant metabolic shifts, while singleton PE showed IFN-II-associated immune activation. Early-onset PE displayed sexual dimorphism—male fetuses featured bacterial defense pathways, lipid metabolic programs, and trophoblast-confined glycolysis, while female fetuses exhibited angiogenesis, chemotaxis, nitric oxide signaling pathways, and glycolytic reprogramming in immune cells, whereas late-onset PE exhibited comparatively attenuated inflammatory activity. Gut metagenomic profiling revealed enrichment of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-producing taxa and depletion of beneficial commensals in PE, accompanied by metabolomic alterations that aligned with inflammatory pathways also highlighted in placental analyses. Collectively, these findings reveal a conserved bacteria-associated inflammatory program in PE that is modulated by clinical context and linked to gut microbial dysbiosis.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is the second major cause of maternal death worldwide, impacting 5%–7% of pregnant women and leading to approximately 70,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 fetal and newborn deaths annually.1,2 It is clinically defined by the new onset of hypertension and proteinuria or other end-organ dysfunctions after 20 weeks of gestation.3,4 Despite its significant health burden, the molecular mechanisms driving PE remain incompletely elucidated, hampering the development of targeted therapies. PE is classified by time of onset into early-onset preeclampsia (EPE, <34 weeks) and late-onset preeclampsia (LPE, ≥34 weeks), and by fetal number into singleton preeclampsia (PE_S) or twin preeclampsia (PE_D). Each subtype exhibits distinct clinical features, risk factors, and pathogenic mechanisms.5,6,7 Furthermore, fetal sex is an important risk factor, with growing evidence suggesting it influences placental development and maternal adaptation. However, the exact pathogenic mechanisms involved remain unclear.8,9,10

Immune dysregulation at the maternal-fetal interface is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of placental inflammation, a process now considered fundamental to the development of PE.11 As high-throughput sequencing technologies, notably single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), advance rapidly, the molecular landscape of PE has been comprehensively characterized.12,13 In 2017, Tsang et al. utilized single-cell and cell-free plasma RNA transcriptomics to reveal the trophoblast dysfunction in PE placentas, and demonstrated the potential of combining single-cell transcriptomics with non-invasive diagnostics.14 A recent single-cell study (2023) revealed distinct inflammatory profiles between EPE and LPE, which noted elevated expression of inflammation-associated genes such as SPP1 in EPE.15 With advances in high-precision sequencing and multi-omics approaches, increasing evidence supports the important role of inflammation and its pro-inflammatory factors in PE pathogenesis.16,17,18 However, the association between initial inflammatory triggers and the development of PE during pregnancy remains to be fully elucidated.

The gut microbiota, commonly known as the “second genome”, is indispensable for regulating key host physiological processes, such as metabolism, immune response, and overall health maintenance.19 Research has found that the intestinal microbiota of PE patients shows significant ecological imbalance.20 This imbalance not only alters the structure of the microbial community but also affects the host’s physiological state through metabolic reprogramming.16 However, how intestinal microbiota dysbiosis is associated with placental inflammation remains unclear.

In this study, we therefore aimed to systematically characterize inflammatory features associated with PE by performing parallel analyses of single-cell transcriptomics, metagenomics, and metabolomics. Building on evidence that gut microbiota dysbiosis is linked to systemic inflammation and placental pathology, we applied multi-view machine learning to high-resolution single-cell data from 208,047 placental cells, exploring mechanistic variations across fetal sex, onset time, and fetal number. Our analyses revealed significant enrichment of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory pathways, disrupted immune cell crosstalk—such as enhanced IFN-II and IL-1 signaling—and dysregulated placental metabolic processes. Further, metagenomic and metabolomic profiling identified microbial shifts characterized by increased LPS-producing bacteria and decreased beneficial taxa, alongside elevated sphingomyelin and other metabolites correlated with inflammatory processes. Together, these findings are consistent with a potential gut-placenta axis, in which gut microbiota dysbiosis may be linked to placental inflammatory responses via metabolic and immune pathways, offering insights into potential mechanisms and therapeutic targets for PE.

Results

Construction of a multi-layer PE placenta cell atlas

In this study, a case-control investigation was designed to conduct an unbiased and comprehensive characterization of placental cell types (Figure 1A), including 53 single-cell placental samples (2 PE_S, 3 PE_D, 2 PN_S, 2 PN_D, 4 early-onset controls (EC), 12 EPE, 6 late-onset controls (LC), 9 LPE, 5 normal pregnancies (PN), and 8 PE cases, see materials and methods and Table S1). We constructed a placental single-cell atlas from 208,047 high-quality placental cells derived from both PE cases and PN cases, and identified 13 distinct cell types based on their markers (villous cytotrophoblasts [VCT], unfused syncytiotrophoblasts [SCT-pre-fused], syncytiotrophoblasts expressing pregnancy-specific beta-1 glycoproteins [SCT-PSG], extravillus trophoblasts [EVT], decidual stromal cells [dS], endothelial cells [Endo], Hofbauer cells, dendritic cells [DCs], T cells, neutrophils, B cells, monocytes, and decidual natural killer cells [dNK]) (Figures 1A–1C, S1, section 4 of supplemental information). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for each cell type between the PE and PN groups revealed a significant enrichment (adjusted p < 0.05) in processes including LPS response, cell growth regulation, oxidative stress response, and detoxification (Figures 1D and 1E), indicating critical roles for inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and LPS stimulation in PE pathogenesis. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of PE, samples were divided into six groups based on fetal number, fetal sex, and onset timing (Figure S1), with sample and cell numbers summarized in Table S2: twin pregnancy (PN_PE_D), singleton pregnancy (PN_PE_S), early-onset male fetus (EPE_EC_0), early-onset female fetus (EPE_EC_1), late-onset male fetus (LPE_LC_0), and late-onset female fetus (LPE_LC_1). We retrieved genes associated with seven classic PE pathogenic mechanisms—oxidative stress, hypoxia, angiogenesis, inflammation, ferroptosis, autophagy, and cellular senescence—and performed intersection analyses with DEGs from the six PE subgroups (Figure 1G). Results showed that PN_PE_D exhibited a markedly higher number of overlapping DEGs with these pathway genes compared to PN_PE_S, indicating more complex molecular mechanisms in twin pregnancy-related PE. EPE demonstrated more pronounced gene expression alterations than late-onset PE, whereas no clear differences in DEG counts were observed between male and female fetal subgroups. Notably, across all six subgroups, oxidative stress-related genes showed the highest number of intersections with DEGs, underscoring the central role of oxidative stress in PE pathogenesis. Cellular senescence-related genes ranked second in intersection frequency, further highlighting their potential involvement. These observations provide important clues for understanding the molecular underpinnings of distinct PE clinical subtypes.

Singleton and twin pregnancies shape distinct inflammatory-metabolic trajectories in PE

To determine the distinct molecular mechanisms underlying PE in singleton and twin pregnancies, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of key pathogenesis-related genes, including those involved in hypoxia, angiogenesis, autophagy, ferroptosis, inflammation, cellular senescence, and oxidative stress. Machine learning models were developed using multiple commonly used algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), LightGBM (LGBM), and gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT). Based on cross-validation performance, LGBM and GBDT were selected for downstream analyses (Table S3). The models were trained using the overlap between these genes and DEGs, with the aim of prioritizing discriminative gene-level features between the PN_PE_S and PN_PE_D groups. The LGBM and GBDT models demonstrated good classification performance (mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] > 0.9), suggesting that these pathogenesis-related genes carry strong discriminative signals between groups (Figures 2A and 2B). To evaluate the contribution of key genes in the predictive models, shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis was performed, identifying the top 10 feature genes for each of the seven classic mechanistic models in both the PN_PE_D and PN_PE_S groups (Figures S2A–S2N). Subsequently, these prioritized genes were integrated for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, systematically revealing the functional divergence in placental cells between singleton and twin PE. Notably, GO enrichment analysis revealed that pathways related to microbial-host interaction, including response to lipopolysaccharide and cellular response to molecule of bacterial origin, were significantly enriched (adjusted p < 0.05) in both groups (Figures 2C and 2D). KEGG analysis further corroborated these findings, showing pronounced enrichment in key inflammatory and stress-response signaling pathways, such as Toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor, IL-17, and TNF signaling (Figures S2O and S2P). Additionally, pathways involved in oxidative stress response (e.g., cellular response to oxidative stress) and nitric oxide metabolism were also upregulated. This conserved enrichment profile highlights a pervasive, microbial-triggered inflammatory axis across PE subtypes, while setting the stage for uncovering their distinct pathogenic mechanisms.

Moreover, cell communication analysis revealed distinct patterns of enhanced signaling in the placentas of twin and singleton groups. At the pathway level, information flow analysis quantified the differential activity of IL-1 and IFN-II signaling across subgroups (Figures S3A and S3B). In the PN_PE_D group, IL-1 signaling was enhanced, particularly in the communication from DCs and monocytes to EVTs (Figures 2E and 2F, S3C). Ligand-receptor analysis further indicated that this enhanced signaling at the DC-EVT interface was primarily driven by the coordinated activation of multiple IL-1-related pairs, including IL1B-IL1R2, IL1B-(IL1R1+IL1RAP), IL1A-IL1R2, and IL1A-(IL1R1+IL1RAP) (Figures 2J, S3D, and S3E). In contrast, the PN_PE_S group showed stronger IFN-II signaling, primarily originating from dNK and T cells and targeting DCs and monocytes via the IFNG-(IFNGR1+IFNGR2) receptor complex (Figures 2G–2I; S3F–S3H).

The scMetabolism analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles between the PN_PE_D and PN_PE_S groups, indicating differential metabolic reprogramming occurring across these pregnancies. In the PN_PE_D group, notable upregulation of cysteine and methionine metabolism and glutathione metabolism pathways was observed, particularly in dS and Endo cells (Figures 2K and 2L). Comparatively, the PN_PE_S group showed an increase in the inositol phosphate metabolism pathway, especially in immune cells, such as B cells, dNK, Hofbauer cells, and T cells (Figure 2M).

Similar machine learning models were applied to the EPE subgroups to explore discriminative molecular features associated with fetal sex. SHAP-based interpretation identified the top 10 contributing genes for each model (Figures S4A–S4G). The models demonstrated reasonable classification performance (mean AUC >0.8). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of prioritized genes revealed that both subgroups exhibited convergent enrichment of hypoxia- and oxidative stress-related pathways, including HIF-1 signaling and reactive oxygen species metabolic processes (Figures 3C and 3D; S4H and S4I), indicating a shared hypoxic-inflammatory microenvironment in EPE. Subgroup-specific enrichment patterns were also observed. In the male fetus subgroup (EPE_EC_0), GO analysis demonstrated significant enrichment (adjusted p < 0.05) of defense response to bacterium and regulation of pattern recognition receptor signaling, together with pathways related to cold-induced thermogenesis, long-chain fatty acid transport, and PPAR signaling (Figures 3C; S4H), reflecting coordinated immune activation and metabolic remodeling. In contrast, the female fetus subgroup (EPE_EC_1) exhibited enrichment of angiogenesis, chemotaxis, and regulation of nitric oxide-mediated signaling (Figure 3D). KEGG analysis further identified activation of PI3K-Akt and IL-17 signaling pathways in this subgroup (Figure S4I).

Global communication profiling revealed subgroup-specific shifts in outgoing signaling strength. In the EPE_EC_0 group, Endo and VCT exhibited enhanced signaling capacity compared with controls (Figure S4J), whereas in the EPE_EC_1 group, EVT and monocytes showed increased outgoing signals alongside reduced VCT communication (Figure S4K). Both subgroups displayed enhanced IL-1 and CXCL chemokine signaling relative to controls (Figures S4L and S4M). Notably, IL-1 signaling networks exhibited a shared activation pattern in both EPE subgroups, with enhanced communication from monocytes, Hofbauer cells, and DCs to EVT and SCT-PSG, mediated by IL1B–IL1R2 and IL1B–(IL1R1+IL1RAP) ligand-receptor interactions (Figures 3E–3J). These findings highlight IL-1-dominant immune communication as a common feature of EPE.

The scMetabolism analysis revealed distinct cell-type-specific patterns of glycolytic reprogramming between EPE_EC_0 and EPE_EC_1. In the male fetus subgroup (EPE_EC_0), enhanced glycolysis/gluconeogenesis activity was predominantly confined to trophoblast subsets (VCT, SCT-PSG and SCT-pre-fused). In contrast, the female fetus subgroup (EPE_EC_1) exhibited widespread glycolytic upregulation in EVT and multiple immune cell lineages, including DCs, dS, B cells, T cells, Hofbauer cells, and monocytes (Figures 3K and 3L).

Sex-specific molecular signatures and pathway activation patterns in LPE

This comparatively lower extent of transcriptional divergence observed in LPE may explain the relatively limited discriminative capacity of the LPE gene-based models (mean AUC <0.7), particularly in the LPE_LC_1 group (Figures 4A–4B). To improve feature discrimination in LPE_LC_1, genes across all mechanistic categories were integrated, resulting in an increased classification performance (AUC = 0.815). In the LPE_LC_0 group, GO enrichment of oxidative stress-related genes (Os) demonstrated significant overrepresentation (adjusted p < 0.05) of LPS response and immune cell chemotaxis pathways, including response to lipopolysaccharide, response to molecule of bacterial origin, leukocyte chemotaxis, neutrophil migration, granulocyte migration, and cellular response to interleukin-1 (Figure 4C). In contrast, the LPE_LC_1 group showed no significant enrichment (adjusted p < 0.05) of pro-inflammatory pathways in the integrated gene set analysis. Instead, GO analysis revealed enrichment of cell growth, viral life cycle, response to temperature stimulus, and protein refolding (Figure 4D), suggesting a cellular state oriented toward stress adaptation and host defense rather than classical inflammation. KEGG analysis of the LPE subgroups further identified enrichment of several immune-related signaling pathways, including Toll-like receptor, IL-17, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and PI3K-AkT signaling (Figures S5C and S5D).

Global communication profiling showed subgroup-specific shifts in signaling capacity. In the LPE_LC_0 group, dS and Endo exhibited enhanced outgoing signaling compared with controls (Figure S5E). In the LPE_LC_1 group, Endo and Hofbauer cells showed increased signaling output, while dS remained comparable to controls (Figure S5F). Analysis of information flow in cellular communication identified both shared and sex-biased features. Both LPE subgroups displayed enhanced IL-16, VEGF, CXCL, PDGF, and GAS signaling relative to controls (Figures S5G and S5H). Male-specific signaling enhancements included IGF, GDF, OSM, PTN, SEMA3, and WNT pathways, implicating dysregulated metabolic-growth axis and neurovascular guidance in male-bearing LPE placentas. Female-specific enhancements were characterized by concerted activation of IL-1, TGF-β, and BAFF signaling, suggesting exacerbated immunometabolic dysfunction and adipose tissue–mediated insulin resistance in female-bearing LPE. Given the prominent and shared role of IL-16, we further dissected its ligand-receptor interactions. In the LPE_LC_0 group, IL16-CD4 signaling was predominantly enhanced from B cells to DCs, Hofbauer cells, and monocytes (Figures 4E, 4F, and 4I). In the LPE_LC_1 group, both B cells and T cells served as signal senders, with enhanced IL16-CD4 communication to DCs and Hofbauer cells (Figures 4G, 4H, and 4J).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with placental LPS enrichment and inflammatory signatures in PE

Given the consistent enrichment of LPS response and other bacteria-related inflammatory pathways across placental subgroups, we next investigated whether gut microbial dysbiosis may represent a potential contributor to LPS-associated inflammatory signaling. To this end, metagenomic and metabolomic analysis was performed on fecal samples collected from an independent cohort comprising 38 PE and 36 PN (Figure 5A). Class-level analysis of gut microbiota composition revealed distinct differences between the PN and PE groups (Figure 5B). Consistently, Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis further identified enrichment of multiple Gram-negative and LPS-producing taxa in PE, including Escherichia coli (E. coli), Phocaeicola coprophilus, and Prevotella pectinovora (Figure 5C). Metabolomic profiling further identified 325 upregulated and 323 downregulated metabolites in PE (Figure 5D). Differential metabolites were significantly enriched in sphingolipid metabolism, pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, and inflammation-related pathways. Notably, metabolic pathways previously highlighted in placental single-cell analyses, including cysteine and methionine metabolism, glycolysis, and inositol phosphate metabolism, also showed coordinated alterations in the gut metabolome. Correlation analysis demonstrated positive associations between LPS-producing bacteria and inflammation-associated metabolites, whereas beneficial taxa showed inverse correlations (Figures 5E and 5F). Together, these findings suggest coordinated alterations between gut microbial composition, metabolite profiles, and placental inflammatory signatures in PE.

Discussion

PE is widely conceptualized as a placental disorder characterized by immune dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired vascular remodeling. Increasing evidence supports a central role for inflammatory activation in PE pathogenesis.21 However, whether these inflammatory programs constitute a conserved pathogenic core, and to what extent and in what manner they are differentially shaped by pregnancy-specific biological variables—including fetal number, disease onset timing, and fetal sex—remains incompletely understood. By integrating in-house sequencing data with three independent public scRNA-seq datasets, we constructed a comprehensive placental single-cell atlas encompassing 53 placental samples and 208,047 high-quality cells. Through systematic stratified analyses, we delineate a context-dependent inflammatory framework in which a conserved LPS-associated inflammatory axis is progressively remodeled by pregnancy context and fetal biological variables.

Despite heterogeneity in enriched pathways across clinical subgroups, a central and unifying feature was the consistent enrichment of bacteria-associated immune programs. This observation is supported by prior animal model studies showing that low-dose LPS exposure during pregnancy recapitulates key PE-like features, and that TLR4-mediated signaling induced by bacterial endotoxin—particularly LPS, a major component of Gram-negative bacterial outer membranes—can trigger placental inflammatory responses relevant to PE.22,23

Twin and singleton PE displayed fundamentally different downstream molecular trajectories, despite sharing a common inflammatory foundation. Twin PE demonstrated broader molecular dysregulation, with hypoxia-, angiogenesis-, autophagy-, ferroptosis-, and senescence-related genes exhibiting stronger discriminative capacity. Notably, twin PE was characterized by IL-1-dominant inflammatory signaling from dendritic cells and monocytes to EVT, together with upregulation of cysteine and methionine metabolism and glutathione metabolism. This aligns with previous evidence showing elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in placental dysfunction and dysregulated cysteine, methionine, and glutathione metabolism in PE placental tissues.24,25,26,27 As cysteine and methionine contribute to glutathione synthesis, the principal intracellular antioxidant, these metabolic alterations are consistent with redox imbalance and potential compensatory activation of antioxidant pathways in PE.28,29,30,31,32,33 In contrast, singleton PE displayed a more pronounced IFN-II-associated immune activation pattern, with dNK and T cells signaling toward antigen-presenting cells, consistent with evidence of elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ in PE.34 These findings indicate that pregnancy type shapes distinct inflammatory-metabolic configurations within a shared PE inflammatory framework.

Fetal sex emerged as an additional layer of pathogenic heterogeneity, particularly in EPE. Sexual dimorphism in placental gene expression and immune regulation has been increasingly recognized, with male fetuses often associated with heightened inflammatory vulnerability.35 In our data, transcriptomic prioritization in male EPE highlighted pathways related to defense response to bacterium and regulation of pattern-recognition receptor signaling, together with enrichment of lipid metabolic programs, including PPAR signaling and long-chain fatty acid transport. These findings are consistent with reports that male placentas exhibit altered lipid handling and inflammatory sensitivity.36 Conversely, female EPE demonstrated enrichment of angiogenesis, chemotaxis, and regulation of nitric oxide-mediated signal transduction, together with broader glycolytic activation in immune cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that angiogenic and nitric oxide pathways are tightly regulated by hypoxia-inducible signaling in the placenta, and that hypoxia-driven HIF-1 activation promotes glycolytic reprogramming in immune cells.37,38,39 In this context, the vascular and metabolic signatures observed in female EPE may reflect a sex-specific pattern of adaptation to hypoxic stress. Collectively, these results suggest that although early-onset PE shares a hypoxic-inflammatory background, fetal sex may influence the cellular and metabolic routes through which pathology manifests. In LPE, inflammatory activation appeared comparatively attenuated, in line with prior reports describing milder placental transcriptomic perturbation and greater maternal contribution to disease pathophysiology.40 Nevertheless, heterogeneity within LPE remained evident. Whereas one subgroup retained enrichment of innate immune-associated signatures, including LPS- and interleukin-1-related responses, the other preferentially engaged pathways linked to cellular maintenance and stress responsiveness. This divergence suggests that LPE may encompass both residual inflammatory activation and stress-adaptive states, rather than representing a uniformly mild phenotype.

Combined analysis of gut metagenomic and metabolomic data indicates that PE is accompanied by marked alterations in maternal microbial composition and metabolic profiles. In particular, enrichment of Gram-negative and LPS-producing taxa—including Escherichia coli, Phocaeicola coprophilus, and Prevotella pectinovora—was observed in PE. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing gut microbiota dysbiosis in PE, characterized by altered microbial composition and shifts toward inflammation-associated microbial profiles, including an increased abundance of Proteobacteria.20,41 Prior studies have suggested that LPS derived from gut dysbiosis may enter the circulation and promote inflammatory responses through activation of the TLR4-signaling pathway.42,43,44 Notably, placentas from preeclamptic pregnancies have been reported to exhibit heightened inflammatory signatures, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between microbial-derived endotoxin and placental inflammatory pathology.45 In contrast, several commensals with reported anti-inflammatory properties, such as Faecalibacterium, Akkermansia, and Alistipes, were depleted in PE, consistent with previous studies.20,41,46,47 These genera have been associated with short-chain fatty acid production, mucosal barrier maintenance, and attenuation of inflammatory signaling.48,49,50 Concordantly, gut metabolomic shifts further aligned with inflammatory and lipid-related programs, including pathways also highlighted in placental single-cell analyses.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small number of independent biological samples, particularly within certain subgroups, may limit the generalizability of our conclusions and affect statistical power. Second, sample selection bias cannot be entirely excluded, as samples were primarily collected from specific hospitals or regions. Third, while we focused on specific bacterial genera, the analysis of other microbial species and their interactions was not fully explored, which may play critical roles in modulating inflammation and health outcomes. Lastly, our findings are primarily based on maternal samples, and the involvement of fetal microbiota remains to be investigated, as it may also contribute to the pathophysiology of PE.

Conceptually, these findings support a context-structured model of PE pathogenesis, in which a recurrent bacteria-associated inflammatory backbone is differentially configured by pregnancy type, disease onset timing, and fetal sex. By delineating shared and context-dependent molecular trajectories across placental and maternal compartments, this study provides a conceptual framework for refined disease stratification and highlights potential avenues for precision-oriented therapeutic exploration in PE. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings in larger, longitudinal cohorts and to further elucidate the underlying causal mechanisms of the putative gut-placenta axis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v.4.3.2). Adjusted p values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. For scRNA-seq data, differential gene expression analyses between groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. DEGs were defined as genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 and an adjusted p value <0.05. The full lists of upregulated and downregulated DEGs across placental cell types are provided in Tables S4 and S5.

For GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, statistical significance was defined as adjusted p value <0.05.

For metabolomic profiling, differential metabolite analysis between the PE and PN groups was performed using a fold change threshold of |log2FC| > 1, and the complete list of significantly altered metabolites is provided in Table S6. For visualization and group-wise comparison of selected key metabolites involved in representative metabolic pathways, Student’s t test was applied, and statistical significance was indicated as p ≤ 0.05 (∗), p ≤ 0.01 (∗∗), and p ≤ 0.001 (∗∗∗).

ScRNA-seq data processing

All downstream analyses were carried out with R using the package Seurat (see section 4 of supplemental information).52,53,54,55 To minimize technical variation arising from different datasets, sequencing platforms, and library preparation protocols, batch effects were corrected using Harmony with dataset identity (orig.ident) specified as the batch variable.53 Harmony correction was performed in the PCA space, and the resulting integrated data were used for all downstream analyses.

A total of 43 clusters were identified and annotated into 13 placental cell types based on typical marker genes. Differential gene expression between the PE and PN groups was performed for each cell type using the Seurat function FindMarkers based on Harmony-integrated data, aiming to reduce batch-driven noise while preserving shared biological signals across samples. Genes were required to be expressed in at least 25% of cells in either group (min.pct = 0.25). Identified DEGs were subsequently subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to identify relevant biological pathways.56,57

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat (v.1.6.1) based on known ligand-receptor interactions from CellChatDB.58 Ligand-receptor interactions and communication probabilities between cell types were inferred under default parameters. Communication networks between major placental cell types were constructed, and signaling pathway activity and information flow were compared between groups. Selected signaling pathways and ligand-receptor interactions were further analyzed and visualized using R packages including igraph and ggplot2. To assess metabolic pathway activity at single-cell resolution, we used the R package scMetabolism (v.0.2.1).59 Gene expression count data from the Harmony-integrated dataset were used as input for metabolic scoring. Metabolic pathway activities were computed using the sc.metabolism.Seurat function with the VISION-based scoring algorithm and KEGG-curated metabolic gene sets. The resulting metabolic activity scores were used for downstream comparisons across cell types and phenotypic groups. Visualization of pathway activity distributions was performed using the BoxPlot.metabolism function. The numbers of cells analyzed for each placental cell type are provided in Table S7.

Source of genes related to pathogenesis of PE

This study centers on genes associated with the pathogenesis of PE, which were obtained from diverse databases. 1,125 and 76 genes related to oxidative stress and angiogenesis respectively were retrieved from the Genecard (https://www.genecards.org/).60 200 genes related to inflammatory and hypoxia-related genes were obtained from the GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/).61 370 ferroptosis-related genes were sourced from the FerrDb V2 (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/).62 Additionally, 232 autophagy-related genes were acquired from the HADb (http://www.autophagy.lu/), and 1,259 senescence-related genes were extracted from the CellAge (https://genomics.senescence.info/cells/).63,64

Machine learning model construction and optimization

Machine learning models were constructed using the intersecting set of DEGs between pathogenesis-related genes and cell-type-specific DEGs. This strategy was adopted to reduce feature dimensionality while retaining genes with both statistical significance and established relevance to PE pathogenesis. To ensure robust and unbiased model selection, multiple commonly used machine learning algorithms spanning linear, distance-based, and tree-based methods were initially evaluated under a unified preprocessing and validation framework (see Section 5 of the supplemental information).

The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (80%) and an internal held-out test set (20%). The same preprocessing transformations were applied to both the training and held-out test sets, with all preprocessing parameters learned exclusively from the training set. Hyperparameter tuning and model training were performed solely on the training data. Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search coupled with 5-fold cross-validation (see section 5 of supplemental information).65,66,67,68,69

Model selection was primarily based on the AUC obtained from cross-validation. Based on comparative performance across cross-validation folds, LGBM and GBDT demonstrated consistently superior and more stable predictive performance across multiple subgroups and functional categories and were therefore selected as the final models.70,71 The final optimized LGBM and GBDT models were evaluated on the held-out test set to estimate internal generalization performance, while comparative test-set results of all evaluated models are provided in Table S8.

Both algorithms are well suited for modeling high-dimensional, nonlinear transcriptomic data and support SHAP-based interpretation, enabling biologically meaningful identification of key pathogenesis-related genes.

Interpretability of model features

To interpret the machine learning predictions, SHAP values were computed by the SHAP Python package (v.0.39.0).72 SHAP, which is derived from Shapley values in game theory, provides a unified approach for understanding feature importance in machine learning models. In this study, SHAP values were employed to calculate and rank the pathogenesis-related genes involved in PE.
