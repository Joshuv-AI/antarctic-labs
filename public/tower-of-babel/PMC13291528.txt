Concerns on the human-induced biospheric carbon sink in the Taklamakan Desert
Authors: Nan Xu
Published: 2026

Recently, Noor et al. presented an interesting study on the positive effects of afforestation along the margins of the Taklamakan Desert on vegetation cover and photosynthetic activity (1). The restoration efforts are commendable. However, before attributing the observed changes directly to a human-induced biospheric carbon sink, several aspects may deserve further discussion.

First, the long-term trend in net ecosystem exchange (NEE) carries statistical uncertainty. The reported NEE trend is –5.2 × 10−12 kg m−2 s−1 y−1, with an error bar of exactly the same magnitude (±5.2 × 10−12), giving a signal-to-noise ratio of approximately one. Without P-values or CI, it remains unclear whether this trend is significantly different from zero. Therefore, the conclusion of a strengthening carbon sink may need more rigorous statistical support.

Second, establishing a causal link between increased vegetation and the afforestation project remains challenging. Although precipitation shows no significant trend, other potential factors—such as artificial irrigation, groundwater changes, or CO2 fertilization—have not been ruled out. Without systematic comparison with non-afforested areas (e.g., the desert interior or similar untreated drylands), the observed greening trend could be more convincingly linked to the planting project with additional evidence.

Third, the well-documented abiotic CO2 exchange in the Taklamakan Desert deserves attention. Yang et al. showed that shifting sands undergo significant abiotic CO2 uptake and release driven by thermal expansion/contraction and salt-alkali chemistry, yielding a net abiotic sink of about 1.6 million t CO2 y−1 (2004–2017), a sink that is weakening under climate warming (2). Noor et al. rely primarily on a biospheric model (MiCASA) that does not resolve abiotic processes (1). Thus, the observed ~3 ppm CO2 drawdown in the wet season could be partially or largely due to abiotic mechanisms rather than solely to photosynthesis from planted vegetation. Distinguishing between these two mechanisms is important for accurate attribution.

Furthermore, extending the carbon uptake rate measured along the desert margins (1.74 t CO2 ha−1 y−1) to the entire desert (yielding 58.7 million t CO2 y−1) or to all of China (1.67 billion t CO2 y−1) faces considerable practical difficulties. The hyperarid desert interior consists largely of shifting sands and lacks the water resources needed to support perennial vegetation on a large scale. Moreover, large-scale afforestation could interfere with the existing abiotic carbon sink. The national extrapolation also overlooks current land use, water constraints, and ecological trade-offs. These estimates might therefore be better viewed as theoretical upper bounds rather than near-term achievable mitigation targets.

In summary, Noor et al. provide a thought-provoking study, but further clarification on trend significance, causal attribution, and separation of abiotic carbon exchange would be beneficial (1). In the future, until more comprehensive evidence becomes available, it may be prudent to interpret the afforestation project along the desert margins as a promising local effort, rather than a readily scalable carbon solution.
