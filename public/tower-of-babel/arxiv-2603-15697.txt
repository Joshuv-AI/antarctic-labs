Optical Magnus effect on gravitational lensing
Yusuke Nishida

Abstract
The optical Magnus effect refers to transverse shift of a trajectory of light caused by its polarization and appears as a correction to geometrical optics at the linear order in wavelength. Here, we start from Maxwell’s equations in a curved spacetime to derive the equation of motion for a wave packet of circularly polarized light, which confirms the known result involving the helicity-dependent anomalous velocity with some generalization and clarification. We then study possible consequences of the optical Magnus effect on gravitational lensing in the Schwarzschild spacetime as well as under a weak gravitational potential in an expanding spacetime. Among others, by formulating the lens equation modified to incorporate the optical Magnus effect, the Einstein ring is found impossible to emerge from a point source for any axially symmetric thin lens. Analytic solutions to the modified lens equation are also obtained for simple lens models, illuminating how image formation is affected by the optical Magnus effect.



I
Introduction

Polarization has been an important probe in high-precision astrophysics and cosmology, providing us with new insights unavailable from intensity measurements alone.
Among others, the
B
-mode polarization of the cosmic microwave background is useful to constrain models of cosmic inflation and primordial gravitational waves because it cannot be produced by density fluctuations
Seljak:1997a
;
Seljak:1997b
;
Kamionkowski:1997
;
Kamionkowski:2016
.
The polarization images taken by the Event Horizon Telescope and the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer already brought out information on structures and dynamics of magnetized plasmas around supermassive black holes
Akiyama:2021a
;
Akiyama:2021b
;
Liodakis:2022
.
Furthermore, possible polarization modes of gravitational waves are expected to serve as diagnostics to test alternative theories of gravity
Eardley:1973a
;
Eardley:1973b
;
Will:2014
.
The polarization of light is typically considered to be transported simply from a source to an observer, with its trajectory following a null geodesic of background spacetime independent of the wavelength and polarization of light.
This is an assumption relying on the geometrical-optics approximation valid in the limit of negligible wavelength
Misner:1973
;
Schneider:1992
.

However, the trajectory of light is to acquire dependence on its polarization by incorporating the wave nature of light at the linear order in wavelength.
This phenomenon in classical optics is known as the optical Magnus effect or the spin Hall effect of light
Dooghin:1992
;
Liberman:1992
;
Bliokh:2004a
;
Bliokh:2004b
;
Onoda:2004
;
Bliokh:2015
;
Ling:2017
.
It manifests itself as transverse shift of a trajectory of circularly polarized light when it propagates through an inhomogeneous optical medium.
Because a spacetime under a static gravitational potential can be modeled as an optical medium with a spatially varying refractive index
Felice:1971
, a gravitational analog of the optical Magnus effect is expected and has indeed been derived with various approaches such as the Foldy-Wouthuysen transformation
Berard:2006
;
Gosselin:2007
, the Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin approximation
Frolov:2011
;
Yoo:2012
, the path integral formulation
Yamamoto:2017
;
Yamamoto:2018
, and the Mathisson-Papapetrou-Dixon equations
Duval:2017
;
Duval:2019
.
See also Refs.
Oancea:2020
;
Frolov:2020
;
Andersson:2021
;
Harte:2022
for recent developments as well as Refs.
Oancea:2019
;
Andersson:2023
for reviews on this subject.

The purpose of our work is to study possible consequences of the optical Magnus effect on gravitational lensing, which is the deflection of light by a massive object predicted by Einstein’s theory of general relativity
Misner:1973
;
Schneider:1992
.
In order to make our work self-contained, we first derive the equation of motion for a wave packet of circularly polarized light starting from Maxwell’s equations in a curved spacetime in Sec.
II
.
We then apply the resulting equation of motion to the gravitational lensing in the Schwarzschild spacetime in Sec.
III
as well as under a weak gravitational potential in an expanding spacetime in Sec.
IV
.
Finally, our findings are summarized in Sec.
V
together with some outlook.
We set the speed of light to
c=1
in Sec.
II
and Appendices, whereas
c
is presented explicitly in the other sections.

II
Optical Magnus effect

II.1
Maxwell’s equations

In order to derive the gravitational analog of the optical Magnus effect, we start from Maxwell’s equations in a curved spacetime,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!a}F^{ab}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\!a}\tilde{F}^{ab}=0,

(1)

where
F^{ab}
is the electromagnetic tensor and
\tilde{F}^{ab}=-\epsilon^{abcd}F_{cd}/2
is its dual with
\epsilon^{0123}=-1/\sqrt{-g}
.
The metric in the foliated spacetime is decomposed as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\alpha^{2}dt^{2}+\gamma_{ij}(dx^{i}+\beta^{i}dt)(dx^{j}+\beta^{j}dt)

(2)

in terms of the lapse function
\alpha
, shift vector
\beta^{i}
, and spatial metric
\gamma_{ij}
on the spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
.
The timelike unit vector normal to
\Sigma_{t}
reads
n^{a}=(1/\alpha,-\beta^{i}/\alpha)
and thus
n_{a}=(-\alpha,0,0,0)
.
The electric and magnetic fields measured by an Eulerian observer are then provided by

\displaystyle E^{a}=F^{ab}n_{b},\qquad B^{a}=\tilde{F}^{ab}n_{b}.

(3)

Because of
n_{a}E^{a}=n_{a}B^{a}=0
, the electromagnetic tensor and its dual can be expressed as

\displaystyle F^{ab}

\displaystyle=n^{a}E^{b}-n^{b}E^{a}+\epsilon^{abc}B_{c},

(4)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{ab}

\displaystyle=n^{a}B^{b}-n^{b}B^{a}-\epsilon^{abc}E_{c},

(5)

with
\epsilon^{abc}\equiv n_{d}\epsilon^{dabc}
.

Maxwell’s equations in Eq. (
1
) are projected along
n_{a}
by
n_{b}\nabla_{\!a}F^{ab}=0
, leading to

\displaystyle D_{i}E^{i}=0,\qquad D_{i}B^{i}=0,

(6)

where
D_{i}
is the covariant derivative compatible with
\gamma_{ij}
.
On the other hand, they are projected onto
\Sigma_{t}
by
({\delta^{i}}_{b}+n^{i}n_{b})\nabla_{\!a}F^{ab}=0
, leading to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E^{i}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{\beta}+\alpha K)E^{i}+\epsilon^{ijk}D_{j}(\alpha B_{k}),

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B^{i}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{\beta}+\alpha K)B^{i}-\epsilon^{ijk}D_{j}(\alpha E_{k}),

(8)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}E^{i}=\beta^{j}D_{j}E^{i}-E^{j}D_{j}\beta^{i}
is the Lie derivative along the shift vector and
K=-\nabla_{\!a}n^{a}
is the trace of extrinsic curvature tensor.
See Refs.
Thorne:1982
;
Alcubierre:2009
;
Baumgarte:2010
for further details.
Obviously, Eqs. (
6
), (
7
), and (
8
) represent Gauss’s law, Ampère’s law, and Faraday’s law in a curved spacetime, respectively.

We now assume an irrotational spacetime with
\beta^{i}=0
and introduce a densitized complex field as
\Psi^{i}\equiv\sqrt{\gamma}\,(E^{i}+iB^{i})/\sqrt{2}
.
By employing
\alpha K=-\partial_{t}\ln\!\sqrt{\gamma}+D_{j}\beta^{j}
and the Levi-Civita symbol
\varepsilon^{ijk}=\sqrt{\gamma}\,\epsilon^{ijk}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, each pair of equations for electric and magnetic fields can be combined into

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\Psi^{i}

\displaystyle=0,

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Psi^{i}

\displaystyle=-i\varepsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\Psi_{k}\right).

(10)

The second equation leads to the continuity equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{i}j^{i}=\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\gamma_{ij}\right)\Psi^{*i}\Psi^{j},

(11)

where
\Psi^{*i}
is the complex conjugate of
\Psi^{i}
and

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\gamma_{ij}\Psi^{*i}\Psi^{j},\qquad j^{i}=-i\varepsilon^{ijk}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\gamma}\Psi^{*}_{j}\Psi_{k}

(12)

are proportional to the energy density and the Poynting vector, respectively.
Therefore, the total energy of electromagnetic field is conserved only when
\alpha\gamma_{ij}/\sqrt{\gamma}
is independent of time.
For example, it is achieved for
\alpha=a(t)\sqrt{A({\bm{x}})}
and
\gamma_{ij}=a(t)^{2}C({\bm{x}})\delta_{ij}
, which is the case considered in the following discussion.

II.2
Wave packet motion

Maxwell’s equations under

\displaystyle ds^{2}=a(t)^{2}[-A({\bm{x}})dt^{2}+C({\bm{x}})\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}]

(13)

are thus brought into the Dirac-like equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\Psi^{i}(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Psi^{i}(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}[v({\bm{x}})\Psi^{k}(t,{\bm{x}})].

(15)

Here,
a(t)
is the scale factor,
t
is the conformal time,
{\bm{x}}
are the comoving coordinates,
v({\bm{x}})\equiv\sqrt{A({\bm{x}})/C({\bm{x}})}
is an effective velocity, and the summation over repeated indices is taken regardless of their positions.

We then introduce the Fourier representation of complex electromagnetic field,

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int\!\frac{d{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\psi(t,{\bm{k}}){\bm{e}}({\bm{k}})e^{\lambda i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}},

(16)

where
{\bm{e}}({\bm{k}})\equiv[\hat{\bm{e}}_{1}({\bm{k}})+i\hat{\bm{e}}_{2}({\bm{k}})]/\sqrt{2}
is a complex polarization vector defined in terms of three mutually orthogonal unit vectors
\hat{\bm{k}}
,
\hat{\bm{e}}_{1}({\bm{k}})
,
\hat{\bm{e}}_{2}({\bm{k}})
satisfying
\hat{\bm{k}}=\hat{\bm{e}}_{1}({\bm{k}})\times\hat{\bm{e}}_{2}({\bm{k}})
.
Gauss’s law in Eq. (
14
) is ensured by
{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{e}}({\bm{k}})=0
, whereas
\lambda=\pm 1
specifies the helicity corresponding to right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations.
The latter can be understood because Eq. (
15
) for constant
v
is solved by
\psi(t,{\bm{k}})\sim e^{-\lambda iv|{\bm{k}}|t}
, so that the electric field evolves as
{\bm{E}}(t,{\bm{x}})\sim\cos(v|{\bm{k}}|t-{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\hat{\bm{e}}_{1}({\bm{k}})+\lambda\sin(v|{\bm{k}}|t-{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\hat{\bm{e}}_{2}({\bm{k}})
for a given wavevector.

Because of the energy conservation, the complex electromagnetic field can be normalized as

\displaystyle\int\!d{\bm{x}}\,v({\bm{x}})|\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})|^{2}=1,

(17)

where the integrand represents an energy density normalized by the total energy.
We now assume that the electromagnetic field forms a wave packet localized in both position and wavevector spaces.
Namely,
|\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})|^{2}
is localized at
{\bm{x}}\approx\bar{\bm{x}}(t)
and
|\psi(t,{\bm{k}})|^{2}
at
{\bm{k}}\approx\bar{\bm{k}}(t)
, where
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)
and
\bar{\bm{k}}(t)
are a mean position and a mean wavevector of the wave packet at a given time, respectively.
This assumption allows us to make the approximations of

\displaystyle x^{i}|\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\bar{x}^{i}(t)|\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})|^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle k_{i}|\psi(t,{\bm{k}})|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\bar{k}_{i}(t)|\psi(t,{\bm{k}})|^{2}.

(19)

For example, the mean position under the above normalization is provided by

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{i}(t)\approx\int\!d{\bm{x}}\,v({\bm{x}})x^{i}|\bm{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})|^{2}.

(20)

By employing the Fourier representation in Eq. (
16
) together with
x^{i}e^{\lambda i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}=-\lambda i\partial_{k_{i}}e^{\lambda i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}
and
\psi(t,{\bm{k}})\equiv e^{-\lambda i\varphi(t,{\bm{k}})}|\psi(t,{\bm{k}})|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{i}(t)\approx[\partial_{k_{i}}\varphi(t,{\bm{k}})-\mathcal{A}^{i}({\bm{k}})]_{{\bm{k}}\to\bar{\bm{k}}(t)},

(21)

where
\mathcal{A}^{i}({\bm{k}})=-\lambda i{\bm{e}}^{*}({\bm{k}})\cdot\partial_{k_{i}}{\bm{e}}({\bm{k}})
is the so-called Berry connection.

Ampère’s and Faraday’s law in Eq. (
15
) is obtained by the principle of least action from

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int\!dtd{\bm{x}}\left[v({\bm{x}})\Psi^{*i}(t,{\bm{x}})i\tensor{\partial}_{t}\Psi^{i}(t,{\bm{x}})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.{}-\varepsilon^{ijk}[v({\bm{x}})\Psi^{*i}(t,{\bm{x}})]\tensor{\partial}_{j}[v({\bm{x}})\Psi^{k}(t,{\bm{x}})]\right],

(22)

with
\Psi^{*}\tensor{\partial}\Psi\equiv[\Psi^{*}(\partial\Psi)-(\partial\Psi^{*})\Psi]/2
.
The equation of motion for the wave packet is then derived by regarding the action for the complex electromagnetic field as that for the wave packet
Chang:1996
;
Sundaram:1999
.
The Fourier representation in Eq. (
16
) substituted into the above action leads to

\displaystyle S\approx\lambda\int\!dt\,[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,{\bm{k}})-v({\bm{x}})|{\bm{k}}|]_{{\bm{x}}\to\bar{\bm{x}}(t),{\bm{k}}\to\bar{\bm{k}}(t)}.

(23)

The first term can be expressed as
\partial_{t}\varphi(t,\bar{\bm{k}}(t))\approx\dot{\varphi}(t,\bar{\bm{k}}(t))-\dot{\bar{k}}_{i}(t)[\bar{x}^{i}(t)+\mathcal{A}^{i}(\bar{\bm{k}}(t))]
in terms of the total time derivative and the mean position in Eq. (
21
).
Therefore, the action reads
S\approx\lambda\int\!dt\,L
with the Lagrangian provided by

\displaystyle L=\bar{k}_{i}(t)\dot{\bar{x}}^{i}(t)-v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|-\dot{\bar{k}}_{i}(t)\mathcal{A}^{i}(\bar{\bm{k}}(t)).

(24)

Finally, the Euler-Lagrange equation with respect to
\bar{x}^{i}(t)
leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{k}}_{i}(t)=-|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|\partial_{i}v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t)),

(25)

whereas that with respect to
\bar{k}_{i}(t)
leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{x}}^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))\partial_{k_{i}}|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|+\varepsilon^{ijk}\dot{\bar{k}}_{j}(t)\mathcal{B}_{k}(\bar{\bm{k}}(t))

\displaystyle=v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))\frac{\bar{k}_{i}(t)}{|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|}-\lambda\varepsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))\frac{\bar{k}_{k}(t)}{|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|^{2}},

(26)

where
\bm{\mathcal{B}}({\bm{k}})\equiv\partial_{\bm{k}}\times\bm{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})=\lambda{\bm{k}}/|{\bm{k}}|^{3}
is the so-called Berry curvature.
The resulting two equations constitute the equation of motion for the wave packet of circularly polarized light.
It is valid as long as a variation of potential is negligible over a spatial extension of wave packet, requiring
|\bm{\partial}\ln v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))|\ll|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|
.
The first term of Eq. (
II.2
) together with Eq. (
25
) is equivalent to the null geodesic of background spacetime in Eq. (
13
) as shown in Appendix
A
.
On the other hand, the second term of Eq. (
II.2
) transverse to both potential gradient and wavevector is known as the anomalous velocity, providing a correction to geometrical optics at the linear order in wavelength.
Because its sign is opposite for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations, the trajectory of light is to acquire dependence on its polarization, which is nothing other than the gravitational analog of the optical Magnus effect.

We note that although the consistent expression can be found in Refs.
Berard:2006
;
Gosselin:2007
;
Yamamoto:2017
;
Yamamoto:2018
, our alternative derivation allows an expanding spacetime as well as a strong gravitational potential described within Eq. (
13
).
It also clarifies that the optical Magnus effect is a purely classical phenomenon with no quantum mechanics at all.
In particular,
\hbar
never appears unless it is introduced artificially as a bookkeeping parameter to perform the systematic expansion over wavelength.
Furthermore, the same equation motion is expected for a wave packet of circularly polarized gravitational wave by regarding
\lambda\to\pm 2
as its helicity
Yoo:2012
;
Yamamoto:2018
;
Andersson:2021
.
A heuristic derivation with our approach applied to linearized Einstein’s equations is presented in Appendix
B
.
In the following sections, we simplify the notation as
\bar{x}^{i}(t)\to x^{i}
,
\bar{k}_{i}(t)\to k_{i}
,
|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|\to k
, and
v(\bar{\bm{x}}(t))\to v
.

III
Schwarzschild spacetime

We first apply the resulting equation of motion to the gravitational lensing in the Schwarzschild spacetime with particular attention to its photon sphere and black hole shadow.
The metric in isotropic coordinates is provided by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\frac{\left(1-\frac{r_{s}}{4r}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{r_{s}}{4r}\right)^{2}}(cdt)^{2}+\left(1+\frac{r_{s}}{4r}\right)^{4}\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j},

(27)

where
r_{s}=2GM/c^{2}
is the Schwarzschild radius and
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}>r_{s}/4
.
Then, the equation of motion is brought into

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-k\frac{x^{i}}{r}\partial_{r}v,

(28a)

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=v\frac{k_{i}}{k}-\lambda\varepsilon^{ijk}\frac{x^{j}}{r}\frac{k_{k}}{k^{2}}\partial_{r}v,

(28b)

with
v=c\,[1-r_{s}/(4r)]/[1+r_{s}/(4r)]^{3}
.

III.1
Photon sphere

The photon sphere consists of unstable circular orbits of light around a massive object
Misner:1973
.
Because

\displaystyle\frac{dr}{dt}=v\frac{x^{i}k_{i}}{rk},\qquad\frac{dk}{dt}=-\frac{x^{i}k_{i}}{r}\partial_{r}v,

(29a)

\displaystyle\frac{d(x^{i}k_{i})}{dt}=(v-r\partial_{r}v)k

(29b)

are obtained from the above equation of motion, constant
r
is possible only when
v=r\partial_{r}v
.
It is achieved outside the event horizon at

\displaystyle r=\frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{4}r_{s},

(30)

corresponding to
R=r\,[1+r_{s}/(4r)]^{2}=3r_{s}/2
in the Schwarzschild coordinates.
This is the radius of photon sphere, which is found unaffected by the optical Magnus effect.

When
r
is constant,
k
is also constant because of
x^{i}k_{i}=0
.
Then, Eq. (
28
) under
v=r\partial_{r}v
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x^{i}}{dt^{2}}=-\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{r^{2}k^{2}}\right)x^{i},

(31)

so that the orbit is provided by
x^{i}(t)=\cos(\omega t)x^{i}(0)+\sin(\omega t)\dot{x}^{i}(0)/\omega
with

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{v}{r}\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{r^{2}k^{2}}}.

(32)

This is the frequency of circular motion of light, which is found to acquire dependence on its wavelength due to the optical Magnus effect.
In particular, the resulting frequency increases as the wavelength increases equally for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations.

III.2
Black hole shadow

When a general orbit of light is considered,
E\equiv vk
is conserved.
On the other hand,
L_{i}\equiv\varepsilon^{ijk}x^{j}k_{k}
is not conserved in the presence of optical Magnus effect
footnote
,

\displaystyle\frac{dL_{i}}{dt}=\lambda\left(\frac{x^{i}}{r}-\frac{k_{i}}{k}\frac{x^{j}k_{j}}{rk}\right)\partial_{r}v,

(33)

but
L^{2}=r^{2}k^{2}-(x^{i}k_{i})^{2}=r^{2}k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
is conserved.
Therefore,
b\equiv cL/E
is a constant of motion, providing the impact parameter at
r\to\infty
.
The radial equation of motion can be expressed in terms of
b
as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{v}\frac{dr}{dt}\right)^{2}=1-\left(\frac{bv}{cr}\right)^{2},

(34)

where the right-hand side reaches its minimum at the radius of photon sphere in Eq. (
30
).
When the minimum is positive (negative), the orbit of light can (cannot) cross the photon sphere, corresponding to a plunging (scattering) orbit.
The critical impact parameter separating the plunging and scattering orbits then reads

\displaystyle b=\frac{cr}{v}\Big|_{\text{Eq.(\ref{eq:photon})}}=\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}r_{s}.

(35)

This is the radius of black hole shadow
Misner:1973
, which is again found unaffected by the optical Magnus effect.

\lambda=+1
incident from
{\bm{x}}\to(2.6,0,-\infty)
in units of
r_{s}=1
, represented by
x
(upper panel) and
y
(lower panel) coordinates as functions of
z
.
The solid (blue), dashed (green), and dotted (red) curves correspond to initial wavevectors of

are obtained by the replacement of

Because the anomalous velocity does not contribute to Eq. (
34
), the radial motion is entirely unaffected by the optical Magnus effect.
However, the trajectory of light is indeed affected in the three-dimensional space.
As a demonstration, three trajectories obtained by numerical integration of Eq. (
28
) are shown in Fig.

for different wavelengths but with the same impact parameter
b=2.6\,r_{s}
slightly above the critical value.
Although the trajectory of light is restricted to a plane in the geometrical-optics approximation, it deviates toward transverse directions opposite for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations due to the optical Magnus effect.
In particular, the resulting deviation increases as the wavelength increases within the validity range of
k^{-1}\ll r_{s}
.

IV
Weak gravitational potential

We then apply the equation of motion for a wave packet of circularly polarized light to the gravitational lensing under a weak gravitational potential
\phi=\phi({\bm{x}})\ll c^{2}
in an expanding spacetime.
The metric is provided by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=a(t)^{2}\left[-(c^{2}+2\phi)dt^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}\right)\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\right],

(36)

so that the equation of motion is brought into

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2k}{c}\partial_{i}\phi,

(37a)

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=v\frac{k_{i}}{k}-\frac{2\lambda}{c}\varepsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\phi\frac{k_{k}}{k^{2}},

(37b)

with
v=c+2\phi/c
.
In this section, we work up to the linear order in
\phi
and neglect
O(\phi^{2})
without further notes.

IV.1
Deflection and transverse shift

The above equation of motion can be solved perturbatively.
We consider a trajectory of light in the form of

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=(b,0,ct)+O(\phi),

(38)

\displaystyle{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{c}\frac{d{\bm{x}}}{dt}+O(\phi)=(0,0,k)+O(\phi),

(39)

where
b
is the impact parameter.
By changing the parameter from
t
to
z=x^{3}
with
dz/dt=c+O(\phi)
, Eq. (
37
) for
m=1,2
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{m}}{dz}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2k}{c^{2}}\partial_{m}\phi,

(40a)

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{m}}{dz}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{m}}{k}-\frac{2\lambda}{c^{2}k}\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{n}\phi,

(40b)

with
\varepsilon^{mn}\equiv\varepsilon^{mn3}
.
Then, the integration over
z
under
k_{m}|_{z\to-\infty}=0
leads to

\displaystyle k_{m}=-\frac{2k}{c^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{z}\!dz^{\prime}\partial_{m}\phi

(41)

and thus

\displaystyle[x^{m}]_{-\infty}^{z}=-\frac{2}{c^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{z}\!dz^{\prime}(z-z^{\prime})\partial_{m}\phi-\frac{2\lambda}{c^{2}k}\int_{-\infty}^{z}\!dz^{\prime}\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{n}\phi,

(42)

where
\phi
in the integrands is evaluated at
{\bm{x}}=(b,0,z^{\prime})
and
\int_{-\infty}^{z}dz^{\prime\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{z^{\prime\prime}}dz^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{z}dz^{\prime}\int_{z^{\prime}}^{z}dz^{\prime\prime}
is employed to perform the integration over
z^{\prime\prime}
.
The first term provides the usual deflection of light by the gravitational lensing, whereas the second term is to provide the transverse shift due to the optical Magnus effect.

When the gravitational potential is spherically symmetric, the first and second terms in Eq. (
42
) contribute only to
x=x^{1}
and
y=x^{2}
, respectively, because of
\partial_{m}\phi=\delta_{m1}(b/r)\partial_{r}\phi
.
For example, the integration over
z^{\prime}
can be performed analytically for
\phi=-GM/r
, so that the trajectory of light reads

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=b-r_{s}\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}+z^{2}}+z}{b},

(43)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda r_{s}}{kb}\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}+z^{2}}+z}{\sqrt{b^{2}+z^{2}}},

(44)

with
r_{s}=2GM/c^{2}
.
Therefore, the deflection angle is found to be

\displaystyle\alpha=\left[\frac{dx}{dz}\right]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}=-\frac{2r_{s}}{b},

(45)

whereas the transverse shift to be

\displaystyle\Delta y=[y]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}=\frac{2\lambda r_{s}}{kb}.

(46)

In particular, the resulting transverse shift is opposite for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations and increases in magnitude as the wavelength increases within the validity range of
k^{-1},r_{s}\ll b
.

IV.2
Lens equation

We now formulate the lens equation incorporating the transverse shift due to the optical Magnus effect.
We set the origin at an observer and direct the
z
axis toward a source located on a plane at
z=z_{s}>0
.
By considering a reverse trajectory of light from the observer to the source in the form of
{\bm{x}}=(0,0,ct)+O(\phi)
and
{\bm{k}}=(0,0,k)+O(\phi)
, the integration of Eq. (
40
) over
z
under
x^{m}|_{z=0}=0
leads to

\displaystyle x^{m}(z_{s})

\displaystyle=z_{s}\frac{k_{m}}{k}\bigg|_{z=0}-\frac{2}{c^{2}}\int_{0}^{z_{s}}\!dz\,(z_{s}-z)\partial_{m}\phi

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2\lambda}{c^{2}k}\int_{0}^{z_{s}}\!dz\,\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{n}\phi,

(47)

where
\phi
in the integrands is evaluated at
{\bm{x}}=(x^{1}(z),x^{2}(z),z)
.
Then, by introducing two-dimensional angular coordinates on the celestial sphere as
\theta_{m}(z)\equiv(-1)^{m}x^{m}(z)/z
, we obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{m}(z_{s})

\displaystyle=\theta_{m}(0)-\frac{2}{c^{2}}\int_{0}^{z_{s}}\!dz\,\frac{z_{s}-z}{z_{s}z}\partial_{\theta_{m}}\phi

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2\lambda}{c^{2}k}\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{n}\phi\,\bigg|_{z=0}+\frac{2\lambda}{c^{2}k}\int_{0}^{z_{s}}\!dz\,\frac{1}{z_{s}z}\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{\theta_{n}}\phi.

(48)

The first line constitutes the usual lens equation for the gravitational lensing
Schneider:1992
, which is found to be modified by the optical Magnus effect with the additional second line.

The resulting lens equation is a nonlinear integral equation for
\theta_{m}(z)
, which can be made tractable in the thin-lens approximation.
Namely, by assuming that the gravitational potential produced by a mass density
\rho({\bm{x}})
is thin compared to its distances to observer and source, we regard it as localized on a lens plane at
z=z_{l}\in(0,z_{s})
,

\displaystyle\phi({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\approx\delta(z-z_{l})\int\!dz\,\phi({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\delta(z-z_{l})2Ga_{l}z_{l}^{2}\int\!d\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\Sigma(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\ln|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|,

(49)

where
a_{l}
is the scale factor at
z=z_{l}
and
\Sigma(\bm{\theta})=a_{l}\int dz\,\rho(z_{l}\theta_{1},z_{l}\theta_{2},z)
is the surface mass density projected onto the lens plane.
This assumption substituted into Eq. (
IV.2
) leads to

\displaystyle\beta_{m}=\theta_{m}-\partial_{\theta_{m}}\psi(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{k_{s}D_{sl}}\varepsilon^{mn}\partial_{\theta_{n}}\psi(\bm{\theta}),

(50)

with the lensing potential provided by

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{\theta})=\frac{4G}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{sl}D_{l}}{D_{s}}\int\!d\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\Sigma(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\ln|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|.

(51)

Here,
\bm{\beta}\equiv\bm{\theta}(z_{s})
and
\bm{\theta}\equiv\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}(z_{l})
are angular coordinates of source and image, respectively,
D_{l}=a_{l}z_{l}
,
D_{s}=a_{s}z_{s}
, and
D_{sl}=a_{s}(z_{s}-z_{l})
are angular diameter distances from observer to lens, from observer to source, and from lens to source, respectively, and
a_{s}
and
k_{s}=k/a_{s}
are the scale factor and the physical wavenumber at
z=z_{s}
, respectively.
Therefore, the gravitational lensing and the optical Magnus effect contribute to the lens equation as longitudinal and transverse derivatives of the lensing potential, respectively, whereas the latter is multipfied by the factor of
\Lambda\equiv\lambda/(k_{s}D_{sl})
.
Its validity range reads
k_{s}^{-1}\ll l\ll D_{l},D_{sl}
with
l
being a thickness of lens.

IV.3
Axially symmetric thin lens

IV.3.1
Generalities

Possible consequences of the optical Magnus effect can be studied in more detail when the lensing potential is axially symmetric under
\Sigma(\bm{\theta})=\Sigma(\theta)
.
By introducing the mean convergence,

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}(\theta)\equiv\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\theta)}{\theta}=\frac{4G}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{sl}D_{l}}{D_{s}}\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\int_{0}^{\theta}\!d\theta^{\prime}2\pi\theta^{\prime}\Sigma(\theta^{\prime}),

(52)

the modified lens equation in the thin-lens approximation is brought into

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)&\Lambda\bar{\kappa}(\theta)\\
-\Lambda\bar{\kappa}(\theta)&1-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(53)

which relates the magnitudes of
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\theta}
by

\displaystyle\beta^{2}=\left([1-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)]^{2}+[\Lambda\bar{\kappa}(\theta)]^{2}\right)\theta^{2}.

(54)

Each solution for
\theta
in terms of
\beta
predicts an image of a given source formed at

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\frac{\theta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)&-\Lambda\bar{\kappa}(\theta)\\
\Lambda\bar{\kappa}(\theta)&1-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(55)

In particular,
\beta=0
corresponds to the exact alignment of source, lens center, and observer on a single line, where the image can appear as an Einstein ring at
\theta=\theta_{E}>0
satisfying
\bar{\kappa}(\theta_{E})=1
in the absence of optical Magnus effect.
However, when the optical Magnus effect is incorporated, Eq. (
54
) no longer allows solutions at
\theta>0
for
\beta=0
.
Therefore, the Einstein ring is found impossible to emerge, being one of the most significant consequences of the optical Magnus effect.

The expansion
\kappa
, twist
\omega
, and shear
\gamma_{m}
of image are provided by the Jacobian matrix of the lens equation
Schneider:1992
.
From

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta_{m}}{\partial\theta_{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\kappa-\gamma_{1}&-\gamma_{2}-\omega\\
-\gamma_{2}+\omega&1-\kappa+\gamma_{1}\end{pmatrix}_{mn},

(56)

we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa(\theta)

\displaystyle=\bar{\kappa}(\theta)+\frac{\theta}{2}\bar{\kappa}^{\prime}(\theta),\qquad\omega(\theta)=-\Lambda\kappa(\theta),

(57)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}-\theta_{2}^{2}}{\theta^{2}}-\Lambda\frac{2\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right)\frac{\theta}{2}\bar{\kappa}^{\prime}(\theta),

(58)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}{\theta^{2}}+\Lambda\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}-\theta_{2}^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right)\frac{\theta}{2}\bar{\kappa}^{\prime}(\theta).

(59)

The inverse of its determinant also leads to the magnification factor expressed as

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\frac{1}{[1-\kappa(\theta)]^{2}+[\Lambda\kappa(\theta)]^{2}-(1+\Lambda^{2})[\kappa(\theta)-\bar{\kappa}(\theta)]^{2}}.

(60)

Therefore, the optical Magnus effect is found to affect the twist, shear, and magnification of image.
In particular, its twist is produced solely by the optical Magnus effect and increases in magnitude as the wavelength of light increases within the validity range.

We note that when the magnification factor is divergent at
\theta=\theta_{c}
, the circle with radius
\theta_{c}
in the image plane and the corresponding circle with radius
\beta_{c}=\beta(\theta_{c})
in the source plane are referred to as a critical curve and a caustic, respectively.
Because the magnification factor can be expressed in terms of
\beta^{2}
in Eq. (
54
) as

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\left(\frac{d\beta^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(61)

\theta_{c}
and
\beta_{c}
are provided by an extremum point and the corresponding extremum value of
\beta(\theta)
, respectively.
Therefore, when a source crosses the caustic, a pair of images appears or disappears on the critical curve.
In particular, we obtain
\theta_{c}=\theta_{E}+O(\Lambda^{2})
and
\beta_{c}=|\Lambda|\theta_{E}+O(\Lambda^{2})
for
|\Lambda|\ll 1
, where
\theta_{E}
is the Einstein radius satisfying
\bar{\kappa}(\theta_{E})=1
.

In order to illuminate how image formation is affected by the optical Magnus effect, we consider
\theta=\theta_{E}+\delta\theta
with
|\delta\theta|\sim\beta\sim|\Lambda|\ll 1
, so that Eq. (
54
) is solved by

\displaystyle\theta=\theta_{E}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\Lambda^{2}\theta_{E}^{2}}}{\theta_{E}\bar{\kappa}^{\prime}(\theta_{E})}+O(\Lambda^{2}).

(62)

Whereas such two solutions exist for
\beta>|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
, they merge at
\theta=\theta_{E}
for
\beta=|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
and then disappear for
\beta<|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
.
The angular coordinates where the pair of images merges are obtained from Eq. (
55
),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}_{\beta=|\Lambda|\theta_{E}}=\frac{\theta_{E}}{|\Lambda|}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Lambda\\
\Lambda&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\beta}_{1}\\
\hat{\beta}_{2}\end{pmatrix}+O(\Lambda),

(63)

which is a point on the Einstein radius but rotated by
\pm 90
degrees from the direction of source in the limit of
|\Lambda|\ll 1
.
In particular, the resulting rotation of image is opposite for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations of light.
This is another significant consequence of the optical Magnus effect, which is to be demonstrated with simple lens models.

IV.3.2
Simple lens models

Apart from the general discussion, the modified lens equation in the thin-lens approximation can be solved analytically for a point mass lens with
\rho=(M/a_{l}^{3})\delta({\bm{x}})
.
The mean convergence reads

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\theta_{E}}{\theta}\right)^{2},\qquad\theta_{E}^{2}=\frac{4GM}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{sl}}{D_{s}D_{l}},

(64)

so that Eq. (
54
) is solved by
\theta=\theta_{\pm}
with

\displaystyle\theta_{\pm}^{2}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}+\theta_{E}^{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}+\theta_{E}^{2}\right)^{2}-(1+\Lambda^{2})\theta_{E}^{4}}.

(65)

Whereas such two solutions exist for
\beta^{2}>\beta_{0}^{2}\equiv 2\,(\sqrt{1+\Lambda^{2}}-1)\,\theta_{E}^{2}
, they merge at
\theta^{2}=\theta_{0}^{2}\equiv\sqrt{1+\Lambda^{2}}\,\theta_{E}^{2}
for
\beta=\beta_{0}
and then disappear for
\beta<\beta_{0}
.
Because the magnification factor is provided by

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\frac{\theta^{4}}{\theta^{4}-(1+\Lambda^{2})\theta_{E}^{4}},

(66)

the radii of critical curve and caustic are found to be
\theta_{c}=\theta_{0}
and
\beta_{c}=\beta_{0}
, respectively, for the point mass lens.

Similarly, the same equation can be solved analytically for a singular isothermal sphere with
\rho=\sigma_{v}^{2}/(2\pi Ga_{l}^{2}r^{2})
.
The mean convergence reads

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}(\theta)=\frac{\theta_{E}}{\theta},\qquad\theta_{E}=\frac{4\pi\sigma_{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{sl}}{D_{s}},

(67)

so that Eq. (
54
) is solved by
\theta=\theta_{\pm}
with

\displaystyle\theta_{\pm}=\theta_{E}\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\Lambda^{2}\theta_{E}^{2}}.

(68)

Whereas only the upper solution exists for
\beta>\sqrt{1+\Lambda^{2}}\,\theta_{E}
, the lower solution appears at
\theta=0
for
\beta=\sqrt{1+\Lambda^{2}}\,\theta_{E}
.
Therefore, the two solutions exist for
|\Lambda|\theta_{E}<\beta\leq\sqrt{1+\Lambda^{2}}\,\theta_{E}
, but they merge at
\theta=\theta_{E}
for
\beta=|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
and then disappear for
\beta<|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
.
Because the magnification factor is provided by

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\theta-\theta_{E}},

(69)

the radii of critical curve and caustic are found to be
\theta_{c}=\theta_{E}
and
\beta_{c}=|\Lambda|\theta_{E}
, respectively, for the singular isothermal sphere.

V
Summary

Finally, our findings are summarized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we derived the equation of motion for a wave packet of circularly polarized light starting from Maxwell’s equations in a curved spacetime.
Although the known result involving the helicity-dependent anomalous velocity is confirmed, our alternative derivation allows an expanding spacetime as well as a strong gravitational potential.

In Sec.
III
, we applied the resulting equation of motion to the gravitational lensing in the Schwarzschild spacetime.
Although the radii of photon sphere and black hole shadow are unaffected by the optical Magnus effect, the frequency of circular motion of light is found to increase as the wavelength increases.
We also demonstrated that a general orbit of light is no longer restricted to a plane but deviates toward transverse directions opposite for right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations due to the optical Magnus effect.

In Sec.
IV
, we studied the gravitational lensing under a weak gravitational potential in an expanding spacetime.
By formulating the lens equation modified to incorporate the optical Magnus effect, the Einstein ring is found impossible to emerge from a point source for any axially symmetric thin lens.
This is because the optical Magnus effect makes a caustic acquire nonzero radius so that a source inside no longer forms images near the Einstein radius.
When the source is located outside, a pair of images appears and their locations can deviate significantly toward transverse directions even for an infinitesimal wavelength of light.

Possible consequences of the optical Magnus effect on gravitational lensing are thus illuminated as a matter of principle.
Since they are typically suppressed by a factor of the wavelength of light over astrophysical length scales, future work should be devoted to finding observable signatures sensitive to the optical Magnus effect.
It is also worthwhile to extend our derivation of the equation of motion to an arbitrary spacetime metric for a wave packet of circularly polarized light as well as gravitational wave, which may provide us with insights complementary to previous approaches
Oancea:2020
;
Frolov:2020
;
Andersson:2021
;
Harte:2022
.

Appendix A
Geodesic equation

The geodesic equation is provided by

\displaystyle\frac{dk^{a}}{ds}+{\Gamma^{a}}_{bc}k^{b}k^{c}=0,

(70)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{a}}{ds}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{a}g_{bc}k^{b}k^{c}=0,

(71)

where
s
is the affine parameter and
k^{a}=dx^{a}/ds
is the wavevector satisfying
g_{ab}k^{a}k^{b}=0
for light.
Under the background spacetime in Eq. (
13
), the null condition leads to

\displaystyle(k^{0})^{2}=\frac{C({\bm{x}})}{A({\bm{x}})}(k^{j})^{2}=\frac{(k_{j})^{2}}{a(t)^{4}A({\bm{x}})C({\bm{x}})},

(72)

whereas the geodesic equation for
i=1,2,3
reads

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{i}}{ds}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}a(t)^{2}\partial_{i}A({\bm{x}})(k^{0})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}a(t)^{2}\partial_{i}C({\bm{x}})(k^{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(k_{j})^{2}}{2a(t)^{2}A({\bm{x}})}\partial_{i}\!\left[\frac{A({\bm{x}})}{C({\bm{x}})}\right].

(73)

By changing the parameter from
s
to
t=x^{0}
with
dt/ds=k^{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dk_{i}}{dt}=\frac{ds}{dt}\frac{dk_{i}}{ds}=-|{\bm{k}}|\partial_{i}v({\bm{x}}),

(74)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
25
) under
v({\bm{x}})=\sqrt{A({\bm{x}})/C({\bm{x}})}
and
|{\bm{k}}|=\sqrt{(k_{j})^{2}}
.
Similarly, by employing
k^{i}=dx^{i}/ds
, we also obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{i}}{dt}=\frac{ds}{dt}\frac{dx^{i}}{ds}=v({\bm{x}})\frac{k_{i}}{|{\bm{k}}|},

(75)

which is equivalent to the first term of Eq. (
II.2
).

Appendix B
Gravitational wave

Einstein’s equations linearized with respect to an expanding flat spacetime

ds^{2}=a^{2}(-dt^{2}+\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j})
are provided by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}h_{ij}+\frac{2\dot{a}}{a}\partial_{t}h_{ij}-\partial_{k}\partial_{k}h_{ij}=0,

(76)

where
h_{ij}\ll 1
is a tensor perturbation in transverse-traceless gauge satisfying
\partial_{i}h_{ij}=h_{ii}=0

Maggiore:2018
.
We then introduce gravito-electric and gravito-magnetic fields as

\displaystyle E_{ij}=-a\partial_{t}h_{ij},\qquad B_{ij}=a\varepsilon^{ikl}\partial_{k}h_{lj},

(77)

which are also symmetric, transverse, and traceless
Barnett:2014
.
Because of
\varepsilon^{ikl}\partial_{k}B_{lj}=-a\partial_{k}\partial_{k}h_{ij}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}E_{ij}+\varepsilon^{ikl}\partial_{k}B_{lj},

(78)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B_{ij}

\displaystyle=+\frac{\dot{a}}{a}B_{ij}-\varepsilon^{ikl}\partial_{k}E_{lj},

(79)

and such a pair of equations can be combined into

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Psi_{ij}=-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\Psi^{*}_{ij}-i\varepsilon^{ikl}\partial_{k}\Psi_{lj},

(80)

with
\Psi_{ij}\equiv(E_{ij}+iB_{ij})/\sqrt{2}
.
Because the first term does not allow the energy conservation, we assume
\dot{a}=0
from now on, leading to the continuity equation in the usual form of
\partial_{t}(\Psi^{*}_{ij}\Psi_{ij})+\partial_{k}(-i\varepsilon^{kil}\Psi^{*}_{ij}\Psi_{lj})=0
.

The equation of motion for a wave packet of gravitational wave can be derived in parallel to Sec.
II.2
.
One important difference is that the Fourier representation of complex gravito-electromagnetic field reads

\displaystyle\Psi_{ij}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int\!\frac{d{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\psi(t,{\bm{k}})e_{ij}({\bm{k}})e^{\lambda i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}.

(81)

Here,
e_{ij}({\bm{k}})\equiv e_{i}({\bm{k}})e_{j}({\bm{k}})
is defined in terms of the complex polarization vector in Eq. (
16
), so that
\Psi_{ij}(t,{\bm{x}})
is symmetric, transverse, and traceless, and
\lambda=\pm 1
specifies the sign of helicity corresponding to right-handed and left-handed circular polarizations.
It is then straightforward to obtain the Lagrangian,

\displaystyle L=\bar{k}_{i}(t)\dot{\bar{x}}^{i}(t)-c|\bar{\bm{k}}(t)|-\dot{\bar{k}}_{i}(t)\mathcal{A}^{i}(\bar{\bm{k}}(t)),

(82)

where
c
is presented explicitly and the Berry connection is provided by
\mathcal{A}^{i}({\bm{k}})=-\lambda ie^{*}_{jk}({\bm{k}})\partial_{k_{i}}e_{jk}({\bm{k}})
.

We now consider a curved spacetime described by Eq. (
13
) with
a(t)=1
.
Possible corrections to the above Lagrangian include replacing
c
by
v({\bm{x}})=\sqrt{A({\bm{x}})/C({\bm{x}})}
as well as adding terms involving its derivatives.
Under such replacement, the Euler-Lagrange equations with respect to
\bar{x}^{i}(t)
and
\bar{k}_{i}(t)
lead to Eqs. (
25
) and (
II.2
), respectively, but with
\lambda
replaced by
2\lambda
because the Berry curvature is provided by
\bm{\mathcal{B}}({\bm{k}})=2\lambda{\bm{k}}/|{\bm{k}}|^{3}
.
Therefore, the optical Magnus effect with twice the anomalous velocity is expected for gravitational waves with circular polarizations, which should be referred to as the
gravitational Magnus effect
.
In particular, all the consequences discussed in Secs.
III
and
IV
are applicable by regarding
\lambda\to\pm 2
as the helicity of circularly polarized gravitational wave.
However, our heuristic derivation of the equation of motion does not exclude other additional corrections at the linear order in wavelength.
