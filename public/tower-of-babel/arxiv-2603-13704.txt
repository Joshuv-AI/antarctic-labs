A Kernel-Based Nonparametric Test for Conditional Independence of Functional Data
Yin Tang, Bing Li

Abstract
Conditional independence is a fundamental concept in many areas of statistical research, including, for example, sufficient dimension reduction, causal inference, and statistical graphical models. In many modern applications, data arise in the form of random functions, making it important to determine whether two random functions are conditionally independent given a third. However, to the best of our knowledge, existing conditional independence tests in the literature apply only to multivariate data, and extensions to the functional setting are not available. To fill this gap, we develop a kernel-based test for conditional independence of random functions based on the conjoined conditional covariance operator (CCCO). We rigorously derive the asymptotic distribution of the CCCO estimator using a recently established sharpened convergence rate for the regression operator ( Choi et al. 2026 ) . Based on this result, we construct a test statistic using the spectral decomposition of the operator appearing in the asymptotic distribution. The proposed method is illustrated through applications to an activity and biometrics dataset and a macroeconomic dataset.



Keywords
: conditional independence, functional data, conjoined conditional covariance operator, reproducing kernel Hilbert space

1
Introduction

Conditional independence is a fundamental concept underlying a wide range of multivariate data analysis methods. For instance, in Sufficient Dimension Reduction, we need to find
\beta
such that
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp X|\beta^{\mathsf{T}}X

(
Li 2018b
)
. In graphical models, nodes
i
and
j
are disconnected if and only if

\displaystyle X_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp X_{j}|\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\}\setminus\{X_{i},X_{j}\},

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
represent the random variables on nodes
1,\ldots,p
. See
Lauritzen 1996
and
Koller and Friedman 2009
. In causal inference, the common cause principle indicates that two dependent random variables
X
and
Y
may have a common cause
Z
such that
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z

(
Peters et al. 2017
)
. Conditional independence plays a central role in all three applications.

It is thus of great importance to test whether two random variables are conditionally independent given a third.
Li and Fan 2020
provides a comprehensive review of nonparametric conditional independence (CI) tests for continuous variables.
For example, under the Gaussian assumption, partial correlation and conditional correlation can be used to characterize the conditional dependency between two random variables. In fact, the Gaussian assumption can be relaxed as indicated in
Baba et al. 2004
. Representative works in this area include the discretization-based test
(
Huang 2010
)
, metric-based tests
(
Su and White 2008
;
Huang et al. 2016
)
, the permutation-based two-sample test
(
Doran et al. 2014
)
, and the mutual independence test under transformations
(
Cai et al. 2022
)
.
Also, some regression-based methods can be employed for this purpose under some additivity or linearity assumptions (see, for example,
Zhang et al. 2017
).

To further relax modeling assumptions, kernel-based methods have recently
gained prominence by embedding probability distributions into feature spaces
induced by kernels.
Within the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) framework, conditional independence can be characterized via the
Conjoined Conditional Covariance Operator (CCCO); see, for example,

Fukumizu et al. 2004
;
Fukumizu et al. 2007b
;
Lee et al. 2016
.
Building on this characterization,
Zhang et al. 2011
proposed a
kernel-based conditional independence test whose statistic is closely related
to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the CCCO introduced in

Fukumizu et al. 2007b
.
Related developments include
Huang et al. 2022
, which introduces a kernel
partial correlation coefficient as a measure of conditional independence, and

Sheng and Sriperumbudur 2023
, which investigates the connection between
distance-based and kernel-based measures of conditional independence.

In this paper, we extend kernel-based conditional independence tests to the functional data setting, where the three random elements involved in the test are random functions or vectors of random functions. Such functional observations are increasingly prevalent in modern data analysis, particularly with the rapid growth of applications involving smart wearable devices. See, for example,
Ramsay and Silverman 2007
and
Acar‐Denizli and Delicado 2025
. A flexible conditional independence testing procedure would constitute a
valuable addition to the toolbox of functional data analysis, enabling the
study of complex interconnections among functional variables. Such
interconnections arise in a wide range of problems, including
function-on-function regression
(
Sun et al. 2018
;
Sang and Li 2026
)
,
functional graphical models
(
Qiao et al. 2019
;
Li and Solea 2018
)
,
and functional sufficient dimension reduction

(
Ferré and Yao 2003
;
Li and Song 2017
;
Li and Song 2022
)
.

In addition to the importance of conditional independence testing for
functional data, we would like to highlight a particularly important
theoretical contribution of our extension. As the pioneering work on
kernel-based conditional independence testing,

Zhang et al. 2011
provided only a sketch of the proof for the
asymptotic null distribution, without a detailed list of conditions under
which the asymptotic distribution holds.

A key difficulty arises from the fact that the test statistic involves
the inverse of a covariance operator. In practice, the inverse must be
estimated using Tikhonov regularization, which slows down the
convergence rate of a related regression operator. Until recently, the
best known convergence rate for this operator was

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{-1},

(1)

where
n
is the sample size,
\epsilon_{n}
is the Tikhonov regularization
parameter, and
\beta>0
characterizes the smoothness of the regression
relationship between the two random variables involved. See, for
example,
Li and Song 2017
. The optimal rate implied by
(
1
) is
n^{-1/4}
, which can be achieved when

\beta\geq 1
with the optimal tuning
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-1/4}
.
However, our calculations (see Section
5
) indicate that this rate is not
sufficiently fast to guarantee the asymptotic distribution claimed in

Zhang et al. 2011
. Only recently has the convergence rate of the regression operator been
sharpened to

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{(\beta\wedge 1)-1}+\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1}+n^{-1}\epsilon_{n}^{-(3\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{-(\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)},

(2)

where
\alpha>1
characterizes the decay rate of the eigenvalues of the
covariance operator; see
Choi et al. 2026
. As shown in
Theorem
2
, if

\displaystyle\beta>\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha},\qquad\frac{\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}>\frac{1}{4},\qquad\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-\frac{\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}},

then the convergence rate in (
2
) is faster than

n^{-1/4}
, which is sufficient to establish the asymptotic distribution
of the test statistic in
Zhang et al. 2011
.

Although our analysis is carried out in the functional setting, the above
result also applies to the multivariate setting. Consequently,
Theorem
2
provides the first rigorous justification
of the asymptotic null distribution for the kernel-based conditional
independence test. Given the widespread use of this test, establishing
its asymptotic validity is both important and significant.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we review the background on RKHS and several linear operators needed for constructing the test statistic at the population level. In Section
3
, we introduce the test statistic
for conditional independence. In Section
4
, we rigorously develop the asymptotic distribution of the proposed kernel-based test statistic for conditional independence for functional data. We also carefully discuss the key conditions needed in the asymptotic development.
In Section
5
, we introduce
practical methods for approximating random functions involved in constructing the test statistic. Section
6

presents the sample-level implementation, including estimation of the
eigenvalues appearing in the asymptotic distribution using an
acceleration method, selection of tuning parameters, and the resulting
algorithms. Section
7
reports results from simulation
studies, and Section
8
illustrates the proposed
method through applications to the WISDM and WDI datasets.

2
Background of RKHS

2.1
Construction of nested Hilbert spaces

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space, let
T
be an interval in
\mathbb{R}
which, without loss of generality, is assumed to be
[0,1]
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{X},{\cal{H}}_{Y},{\cal{H}}_{Z}
be a separable Hilbert spaces of functions defined on
T
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{X},{\cal{B}}_{Y},{\cal{B}}_{Z}
be the Borel
\sigma
-fields generated by the open sets in
\mathcal{H}_{X},{\cal{H}}_{Y},{\cal{H}}_{Z}
, respectively. Let
{\cal{B}}_{X}\times{\cal{B}}_{Y}\times{\cal{B}}_{Z}
be the product
\sigma
-field and let
({\cal{H}}_{X}\times{\cal{H}}_{Y}\times{\cal{H}}_{Z},{\cal{B}}_{X}\times{\cal{B}}_{Y}\times{\cal{B}}_{Z})
be the product measurable space. Furthermore, let
(X,Y,Z):\Omega\to\Omega_{X}\times\Omega_{Y}\times\Omega_{Z}
be a random element measurable with respect to
{\cal{F}}/({\cal{F}}_{X}\times{\cal{F}}_{Y}\times{\cal{F}}_{Z})
, and let
P_{X}=P\circ X^{-1}
,
P_{Y}=P\circ Y^{-1}
,
P_{Z}=P\circ Z^{-1}
, and
P_{XYZ}=P\circ(X,Y,Z)^{-1}
denote the distributions of
X
,
Y
,
Z
, and
(X,Y,Z)
, respectively. Our goal is to construct a test for whether
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
holds.

Having defined the random elements
X,Y,Z
, we then introduce positive definite kernels

\displaystyle\kappa_{X}:{\cal{H}}_{X}\times{\cal{H}}_{X}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\kappa_{Y}:{\cal{H}}_{Y}\times{\cal{H}}_{Y}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\kappa_{Z}:{\cal{H}}_{Z}\times{\cal{H}}_{Z}\to\mathbb{R}.\quad

Let
\mathcal{G}_{X},{\cal{G}}_{Y},{\cal{G}}_{Z}
be the RKHS’s generated by
\kappa_{X},\kappa_{Y},\kappa_{Z}
, respectively. For further information about RKHS, see, for example,
Aronszajn 1950
;
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2004
. We assume that
\kappa_{X}
is uniquely determined by the inner product in
\mathcal{H}_{X}
; that is, for any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
,
\kappa_{X}(f,g)
is a function of
\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}
,
\langle g,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}
and
\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}
.
Li and Song 2017
;
Li and Solea 2018
refer to this special structure as nested Hilbert spaces:
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is nested in
\mathcal{G}_{X}
in the sense that the inner product in
{\cal{H}}_{X}
determines the kernel in
{\cal{G}}_{X}
. We make the same assumptions on
\kappa_{Y}
,
\kappa_{Z}
,
\mathcal{G}_{Y}
,
\mathcal{G}_{Z}
as well.

2.2
Mean element and covariance operator

Before introducing the mean element and covariance operator, we first make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The kernels
\kappa_{X}
,
\kappa_{Y}
and
\kappa_{Z}
satisfies
E[\kappa_{X}(X,X)]<\infty
,
E[\kappa_{Y}(Y,Y)]<\infty
and
E[\kappa_{Z}(Z,Z)]<\infty
.

With the reproducing kernel
\kappa_{X}
specified, we define the mean element
of
X
in the RKHS
\mathcal{G}_{X}
. Under Assumption
1
, there is a unique element
\mu_{X}\in{\cal{G}}_{X}
satisfying

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{X},f\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}=E[f(X)],\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{G}_{X}.

This
element is defined as the mean element of
X
.
We denote this element by

E[\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)]
, so that the identity

\displaystyle\langle E[\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)],f\rangle_{{\cal{G}}_{X}}=E\langle\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X),f\rangle_{{\cal{G}}_{X}}=E[f(X)]

is satisfied.
We define the mean elements

\mu_{Y}=\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{Y}(\cdot,Y)]
and

\mu_{Z}=\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]
in the same way.

We also need the definition of characteristic kernels, also known as probability-determining kernels (see
Fukumizu et al. 2004
,
Zhang et al. 2011
).

Definition 1

(characteristic kernel)
.

Let
(A,{\cal{F}}_{A})
be a measurable space, and

\kappa:A\times A\to\mathbb{R}
be a reproducing kernel. Let
X:\Omega\to A
and
Y:\Omega\to A
be random elements that are measurable with respect to
{\cal{F}}/{\cal{F}}_{A}
. We say that
\kappa
is characteristic if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\kappa(\cdot,X)]=\mathbb{E}[\kappa(\cdot,Y)]\ \Rightarrow\ P\circ X^{-1}=P\circ Y^{-1}.

According to Proposition 2 of
Zhang et al. 2024
, the Gaussian kernel and Laplace kernel in
L^{2}(T)
, defined by

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(\cdot,z)=

\displaystyle\exp(-\gamma_{1}\left\|\cdot-z\right\|^{2}),\quad\gamma_{1}>0

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}(\cdot,z)=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\gamma_{2}\left\|\cdot-z\right\|\right),\quad\gamma_{2}>0,

respectively, are both characteristic kernels on their corresponding RKHS’s.

We then define the covariance operator as follows. Under Assumption
1
, there is a unique bounded linear operator
\Sigma_{XX}:\mathcal{G}_{X}\to\mathcal{G}_{X}
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\langle f,\Sigma_{XX}g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}=\mathrm{cov}\left[f(X),g(X)\right]

for all
f,g\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
. This operator is called the covariance operator of
X
. It can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XX}=E\left[\left(\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)-\mu_{X}\right)\otimes\left(\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)-\mu_{X}\right)\right].

(3)

It can be shown that
\Sigma_{XX}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
\mathcal{G}_{X}
under Assumption
1
; see, for example,
Fukumizu et al. 2007a
. Linear operators
\Sigma_{YY}
and
\Sigma_{ZZ}
are defined similarly.
Letting
\tilde{\kappa}
be centralized kernel
\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,x)=\kappa_{X}(\cdot,x)-\mu_{X}
,
we can represent (
3
) as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XX}=E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\right].

(4)

Similarly, we can rewrite
\Sigma_{YY}
and
\Sigma_{ZZ}
as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{YY}=E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right],\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{ZZ}=E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\right].

We further define the (cross-)covariance operator between
(X,Y)
,
\Sigma_{XY}:\mathcal{G}_{Y}\to\mathcal{G}_{X}
, as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY}=E\left[\left(\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)-\mu_{X}\right)\otimes\left(\kappa_{Y}(\cdot,Y)-\mu_{Y}\right)\right]=E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right].

(5)

Equivalently, for all
f\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle f,\Sigma_{XY}g\right\rangle=\mathrm{cov}\left[f(X),g(Y)\right].

A well-known fact is that, if
\kappa_{X}
and
\kappa_{Y}
are characteristic, then
X
and
Y
are independent if and only if
\Sigma_{XY}=0
. See, for example,
Fukumizu et al. 2007b
. This fact is often used to construct tests for independence
(
Gretton et al. 2005
;
Sejdinovic et al. 2013
)
. We define
\Sigma_{XZ}
,
\Sigma_{YZ}
,
\Sigma_{YX}
,
\Sigma_{ZX}
and
\Sigma_{ZY}
similarly.

2.3
Conditional Covariance Operator (CCO)

Based on the definitions of the mean element and the covariance operator,
we define the conditional covariance operator (CCO) of
(X,Y)
given
Z
,
denoted by
\Sigma_{XY|Z}:\mathcal{G}_{Y}\to\mathcal{G}_{X}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY|Z}=\Sigma_{XY}-\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZY},

(6)

where
\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\Sigma_{ZZ}
(see, for example,
Li 2018a
;
Hsing and Eubank 2015
). Further define

B_{X|Z}=\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger},\qquad B_{Y|Z}=\Sigma_{YZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}.

Note that
B_{X|Z}
and
B_{Y|Z}
are precisely the adjoint operators
of the regression operators introduced in Chapter 13 of
Li 2018b

and in
Li 2018a
. We adopt a different notation here (than, say,
R_{ZX}^{*}
and
R_{ZY}^{*}
) for
convenience in subsequent developments.

To establish an equivalent representation of
\Sigma_{YX|Z}
,
we first present the following lemma and corollary,
which connect the operators
B_{X|Z}
and
B_{Y|Z}

to the regression between two centralized kernels.
We begin by stating an additional technical assumption.

Assumption 2
.

1.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
, the function
E[g(X)|Z=\cdot]
is an element of
\mathcal{G}_{Z}
;

2.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}
, the function
E[g(Y)|Z=\cdot]
is an element of
\mathcal{G}_{Z}
.

Lemma 1
.

If Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied, then, for all
f\in\ker(\Sigma_{ZZ})^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{G}_{Z}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
and
h\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle B_{X|Z}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=\langle f,E[g(X)|Z=\cdot]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}},\quad\langle B_{Y|Z}f,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Y}}=\langle f,E[h(Y)|Z=\cdot]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}.

(7)

Proof.

We will only show the first part of (
7
), as the second part is the same statement with
X
replaced by
Y
and
g
replaced by
h
. By the definition of an adjoint operator,

\displaystyle\langle B_{X|Z}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=\langle\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=\langle f,\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZX}g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}.

The rest of the proof follows from Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 of
Fukumizu et al. 2004
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we have

\displaystyle E[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z=z]=B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),\quad E[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z=z]=B_{Y|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z).

(8)

Proof.

Again, we will only show the first part of (
8
). Take
f=\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z)
and
g=\kappa_{X}(x,\cdot)
in the first equation in (
7
), and we have

\displaystyle\langle B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),\kappa_{X}(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)|Z=\cdot]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}.

(9)

The right-hand side of (
9
) is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)|Z=\cdot]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}&=E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)|Z=z]-E\{E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)|Z]\}\\
&=E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)|Z=z]-E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)]\\
&=E[\kappa_{X}(x,X)-\mu_{X}(x)|Z=z]\\
&=E[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(x,X)|Z=z].\end{split}

The left-hand side of (
9
) is

\displaystyle\langle B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),\kappa_{X}(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=\langle B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),\kappa_{X}(\cdot,x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{X}}=[B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z)](x).

Equating the two sides of (
9
) gives the desired result (
8
). It remains to check that
f=\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z)\in\ker(\Sigma_{ZZ})^{\perp}
. For any
h\in\ker(\Sigma_{ZZ})
, we have
\Sigma_{ZZ}h=0
, so

\displaystyle 0=\langle h,\Sigma_{ZZ}h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}=\mathrm{var}[h(Z)],

which implies that
h
is a constant function almost surely. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z),h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Z}}=h(z)-E[h(Z)]=0

almost surely. This implies that
\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z)\in\ker(\Sigma_{ZZ})^{\perp}
.
∎

As we can see from (
8
),
B_{X|Z}
and
B_{Y|Z}
can be viewed as the regression coefficients of
\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)
on
\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)
, which is the motivation for choosing
X|Z
and
Y|Z
as the subscripts of
B
.
Also, from Corollary
1
, we derive the equivalent representation of
\Sigma_{XY|Z}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Sigma_{XY|Z}
be defined by (
6
). Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY|Z}=E\left[\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)-E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\right)\otimes\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)-E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right)\right].

(10)

Proof.

We expand the right-hand side of (
10
) as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&E\left[\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)-E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\right)\otimes\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)-E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right)\right]\\
=&E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]-E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]\\
&-E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\otimes E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right]+E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right]\end{split}

(11)

By (
5
), the first term on the right-hand side is
\Sigma_{XY}
. We now consider the other three terms separately. By Corollary
1
, we have

\displaystyle E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]=B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger},\quad E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]=B_{Y|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=\Sigma_{YZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}.

Hence the second term on the right-hand side of (
11
) is

\displaystyle\begin{split}E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]=&E\left[B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]\\
=&B_{X|Z}E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]\\
=&\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZY}.\end{split}

By the law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)\right]=

\displaystyle E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right],

\displaystyle E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)\otimes E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right]=

\displaystyle E\left[E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\otimes E\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right].

Consequently, the four terms on the right-hand of (
11
) are simply

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY}-\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZY}-\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZY}+\Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZY}=\Sigma_{XY|Z},

as desired.
∎

Note that the
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
terms in
\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,x)
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{Y}(\cdot,y)
are nonrandom. Two direct corollaries of Proposition
1
are as follows.

Corollary 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY|Z}=E\left[\left(\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)-E\left[\kappa_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z\right]\right)\otimes\left(\kappa_{Y}(\cdot,Y)-E\left[\kappa_{Y}(\cdot,Y)|Z\right]\right)\right].

(12)

Corollary 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for any
f\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,\Sigma_{XY|Z}g\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}=E\left\{\mathrm{cov}[f(X),g(Y)|Z]\right\}.

For notational convenience, define the conditional centered kernel as

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{X|Z}(\cdot,x|z)=\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,x)-E\!\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,X)|Z=z\right]=\tilde{\kappa}_{X}(\cdot,x)-B_{X|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z).

(13)

By construction,
\tilde{\kappa}_{X|Z}(\cdot,x|z)\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
.
Analogously, we define
\tilde{\kappa}_{Y|Z}(\cdot,y|z)\in\mathcal{H}_{Y}
.
With these notations, the representation in (
10
) can be written more compactly as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{XY|Z}=E\!\left[\tilde{\kappa}_{X|Z}(\cdot,X|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Y|Z}(\cdot,Y|Z)\right].

(14)

3
Test Construction for Conditional Independence

3.1
Construction of Test Statistic

The conditional covariance operator
\Sigma_{XY|Z}
is inadequate for testing conditional independence because, by Corollary
3
,
\Sigma_{XY|Z}=0
if and only if, for all
f\in{\cal{G}}_{X}
and
g\in{\cal{G}}_{Y}
,

\displaystyle E\{\mathrm{cov}[f(X),g(Y)|Z]\}=0.

Clearly, this does not imply conditional independence between
X
and
Y
given
Z
. Instead, conditional independence is implied by

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}[f(X),g(Y)|Z]=0.

(15)

For this reason, we need to make a modification of
\Sigma_{XY|Z}
so that the modified version corresponding to (
15
).

Let
\ddot{X}=(X,Z)
and
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}=\kappa_{X}\kappa_{Z}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}
be the RKHS generated by
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}
. Similarly, let
\ddot{Y}=(Y,Z)
and
\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}=\kappa_{Y}\kappa_{Z}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
be the RKHS generated by
\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}
. We define the modified conditional covariance operator as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{Z\ddot{Y}},

(16)

This operator was first introduced by
Fukumizu et al. 2008
. While one may regard the above operator as a special case of
\Sigma_{XY|Z}
with
X
and
Y
replaced by
\ddot{X}
and
\ddot{Y}
, it is convenient to assign the former a special name. Following
Li and Kim 2024
, we call
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
the conjoined conditional covariance operator, and abbreviate it by CCCO.
The next theorem shows that the CCCO can characterize conditional independence. Before stating the theorem, we need to present a modified version of Assumptions
1
and
2
using
\ddot{X}
and
\ddot{Y}
.

Assumption 3
.

The kernels
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}
,
\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}
and
\kappa_{Z}
satisfies
E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\ddot{X},\ddot{X})]<\infty
,
E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\ddot{Y},\ddot{Y})]<\infty
and
E[\kappa_{Z}(Z,Z)]<\infty
.

Assumption 4
.

The following two parts hold:

1.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}
, the function
E[g(\ddot{X})|Z=\cdot]
is an element of
\mathcal{G}_{Z}
;

2.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
, the function
E[g(\ddot{Y})|Z=\cdot]
is an element of
\mathcal{G}_{Z}
.

Theorem 1
.

If Assumptions
3
and
4
hold and
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}
is characteristic, then,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z.

(17)

This theorem was first established in Theorem 8 and Corollary 9 of
Fukumizu et al. 2004
, though the equivalent condition they gave was

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Y|Z}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z,

where
X
,
Y
,
Z
are random vectors.

Fukumizu et al. 2007b
stated the equivalence (
17
) without an explicit proof.
On the other hand, when
X
,
Y
,
Z
are random functions, although the proof is similar, the definitions of their corresponding Borel
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{B}_{X}
,
\mathcal{B}_{Y}
,
\mathcal{B}_{Z}
are more complicated, and we refer to Sections 1.1-1.2 of
Bosq 2012
and Section 2.2, items 5-8 of
Shiryaev 2016
.
For these reasons, we include the proof of Theorem
1
in Appendix
A
for completeness.

By (
17
), testing the hypothesis

H_{0}:X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
is equivalent to testing
H_{0}:\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=0
. This motivates us to use the statistic

\displaystyle T_{n}=n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2},

as the test statistic, where
\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|X}
is the sample estimate of
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
, which will be constructed in the next subsection, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Under
H_{0}
, we expect
T_{n}
to be small, whereas under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\neq 0
,
T_{n}
should tend to be large. We therefore reject
H_{0}
for sufficiently large values of
T_{n}
.
In this paper, we will focus on the test statistic of the form
n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
. Other variants, such as
n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
and
n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
,
can be developed analogously.

3.2
Estimating CCCO and its Hilbert-Schmidt norm

We now construct an estimator of

\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
.
The construction proceeds in two parts.
In the first part, we derive the estimator

\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}

and therefrom the corresponding test statistic

T_{n}=n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

In the second part, we develop an implementable numerical procedure
based on coordinate representations.

Henceforth, we will use
E_{n}(\cdots)
to denote the sample average. For example, for a generic kernel
\kappa
and a sample of random variables
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
, we use
E_{n}[\kappa(\cdot,X)]
is the function
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa(\cdot,X_{i})
. We let
\hat{\kappa}(\cdot,x)
denote the centered version

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}(\cdot,x)=\kappa(\cdot,x)-E_{n}[\kappa(\cdot,X)].

Recall that we have used
\tilde{\kappa}(\cdot,x)
to represent the version centered by the true mean
E[\kappa(\cdot,X)]
. Thus,
\hat{\kappa}
can be regarded as the estimate of
\tilde{\kappa}
.

We begin the construction by estimating
B_{\ddot{X}|Z}=\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}
.
Each covariance operator can be estimated by replacing the population expectations with sample means. Specifically, the empirical estimators of
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}
and
\Sigma_{ZZ}
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}=E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)],\quad\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}=E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]

Since
\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}
is not invertible, we apply Tikhonov regularization when estimating
\hat{B}_{X|Z}
. That is, we can estimate
B_{\ddot{X}|Z}
by

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}=\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1},

where
\epsilon_{n}>0
is a tuning constant.
We further estimate
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{x}|z)
by

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{x}|z)=\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{x})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z).

(18)

We perform exactly the same sequence of operations to obtain the estimates of
B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{y}|z)
as

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}=\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{y}|z)=\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,z).

We construct an estimator of
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
by simply replacing the expectation in (
14
) by sample mean and replacing
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
by
\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
. That is,

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}|Z)].

(19)

Substituting (
18
) into (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=&E_{n}\{[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\}\\
=&E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]-E_{n}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]\\
&\quad-E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]+E_{n}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\\
=&\hat{\Sigma}_{XY}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZY}-\hat{\Sigma}_{XZ}\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}+\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*},\end{split}

(20)

where, for a linear operator
A
,
A^{*}
stands for the adjoint operator of
A
.
The squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=&\left\|E_{n}\left\{[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\\
=&E_{n}\left[\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}^{\prime})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}}\right.\times\\
&\quad\quad\quad\left.\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}^{\prime})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}}\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{i}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{j})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}}\right.\times\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{i}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{j})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}}\right].\end{split}

The above expression is at the operator level. In order to implement it numerically we need to turn operators to matrices via coordinate mapping (see, for example, Section 12.3 of
Li 2018b
). The subspace
{\mathrm{ran}}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{X}})
is spanned by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}=\{\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})-E_{n}[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})]:i=1,\dots,n\}=\{\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i}):i=1,\dots,n\}.

Similarly,
{\mathrm{ran}}(\hat{\Sigma}_{YY})
and
{\mathrm{ran}}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ})
are spanned by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}=\{\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i}):i=1,\dots,n\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}_{Z}=\{\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{i}):i=1,\dots,n\},

respectively. Let
K_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the Gram matrix of
\kappa_{X}
acting on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
; that is,
\left({K}_{X}\right)_{ij}=\kappa_{X}(X_{i},X_{j})
. Similarly, we define
K_{Y}
and
K_{Z}
as the Gram matrices for
\kappa_{Y}
and
\kappa_{Z}
.
Since
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}=\kappa_{X}\kappa_{Z}
and
\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}=\kappa_{Y}\kappa_{Z}
, the Gram matrices
K_{\ddot{X}}
and
K_{\ddot{Y}}
of
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}
and
\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}
can be represented as

\displaystyle K_{\ddot{X}}=K_{X}\odot K_{Z},\quad K_{\ddot{Y}}=K_{Y}\odot K_{Z},

(21)

where
\odot
represents the Hadamard product between matrices; that is, for example,
(K_{\ddot{X}})_{ij}=(K_{X})_{ij}(K_{Z})_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\dots,n
.
Furthermore, let
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}=HK_{\ddot{X}}H
be the centralized Gram matrix where
H=I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{T}
, where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
1_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional vector whose entries are 1. Equivalently,
(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}})_{ij}=\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{j}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}}
. Similarly,
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}=HK_{\ddot{Y}}H
and
\tilde{K}_{Z}=HK_{Z}H
. We now give the coordinate mappings for some related operators as follows (in the notations described in, for example,
Li 2018b
,
Li and Solea 2018
):

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{X}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{Y}\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}]_{\mathcal{B}_{ZZ}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z},

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{Y}\ddot{X}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z},

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{Z\ddot{X}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{Y}Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z},\quad\,_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{Z\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}.

Using the above results, and applying the rules for coordinate mapping described in
Li and Solea 2018
, we derive the coordinate mappings of
\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}
as follows:

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}

\displaystyle=\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}=n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z}(n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1},

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\,_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{Z\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=(n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}.

Therefore, the coordinate mapping of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=&\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\Sigma}_{Z\ddot{Y}}-\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}+\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}-\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{Z\ddot{Y}}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}-\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}\\
&\quad\quad+\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}-n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}\\
&\quad\quad+\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}\\
=&n^{-1}[I-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}]^{2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}.\end{split}

Next, we derive the expression for
\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
. Let

\displaystyle R_{Z}=I-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}=n\epsilon_{n}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}.

(22)

Then
\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}
can be abbreviated as

n^{-1}R_{Z}^{2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}
.
By Theorem 8 (part 4) of
Li and Solea 2018
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=&\left\|\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{-1/2}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|n^{-1}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}R_{Z}^{2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
=&n^{-2}\mathrm{trace}\left(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}R_{Z}^{2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}R_{Z}^{2}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}\right)=n^{-2}\mathrm{trace}\left(R_{Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}R_{Z}R_{Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}R_{Z}\right).\end{split}

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}|Z}=R_{Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}R_{Z},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}=R_{Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}R_{Z}.

(23)

Then,
\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
can be further abbreviated as

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=n^{-2}\mathrm{trace}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}).

(24)

The test statistic is then expressed as
T_{n}=n^{-1}\mathrm{trace}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}|Z})
, which coincides the form given in equation (13) of
Zhang et al. 2011
.

4
Asymptotic Distribution of
T_{n}

In this Section we derive the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
. We make the following additional assumptions.

Assumption 5
.

The kernels
\kappa_{X}
and
\kappa_{Y}
are bounded, and the kernel
\kappa_{Z}
satisfies
E[\kappa_{Z}^{2}(Z,Z)]<\infty
.

This assumption is satisfied, for example, by the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel and the Laplace kernel.

Assumption 6
.

There exists some
\beta>0
such that
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}=S_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{1+\beta}
and
\Sigma_{\ddot{Y}Z}=S_{\ddot{Y}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{1+\beta}
for some bounded linear operators
S_{\ddot{X}Z}:\mathcal{G}_{Z}\to\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}
and
S_{\ddot{Y}Z}:\mathcal{G}_{Z}\to\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
.

As argued in
Li 2018b
and
Li 2018a
, this assumption imposes a certain degree of smoothness in the relation between functions in the domain space
{\cal{G}}_{Z}
and those in the range spaces
{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{X}}
and
{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{Y}}
.
Let

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ZZ}=\textstyle{\sum}_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}(\phi_{k}\otimes\phi_{k})

(25)

be the eigen decomposition of
\Sigma_{ZZ}
, where
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\dots
are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{ZZ}
, and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots
are the corresponding eigenfunctions. The Karhunen-Loéve expansion of
\kappa_{Z}(\cdot,Z)
can be written as

\displaystyle\kappa_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=\mu_{Z}+\textstyle{\sum}_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi_{k}\phi_{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\xi_{k}=\langle\kappa_{Z}(\cdot,Z)-\mu_{Z},\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{Z}}.

(26)

Note that
\xi_{k}
’s are uncorrelated random variables with mean 0 and variance
\gamma_{k}
.
We add an additional assumption on the decay rate of eigenvalues in (
25
).

Assumption 7
.

\gamma_{j}\asymp j^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
, and for all
j=1,2,\dots
.

This assumption is common in functional data analysis to regulate the behavior of the distribution of a random function. See, for example,
Cai and Hall 2006
;
Sang and Li 2026
.

We first state a lemma regarding the convergence rate of the
\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
, which was established in a recent work by
Choi et al. 2026
.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumptions
4
–
7
for some
\beta>0
and
\alpha>1
, if
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=O_{P}(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1-1}+n^{-1}\epsilon_{n}^{-(3\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{-(\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)})

and

\displaystyle\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=O_{P}(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1-1}+n^{-1}\epsilon_{n}^{-(3\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)}+n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{-(\alpha+1)/(2\alpha)}).

The proof of Lemma
2
mimics that of Theorem 13 of
Choi et al. 2026
and Theorem 9 of
Sang and Li 2026
, and we place the full proof in our setting in Appendix
A
for completeness.

Furthermore, Theorem 16 of
Choi et al. 2026
provides the optimal choice of
\epsilon_{n}
, which yields the fastest convergence rates for

\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
and

\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
.
We restate that result below in the form of a corollary tailored to our setting.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose the conditions in Lemma
2
hold and
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
.

1.

If
\beta>\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}
, then the optimal choice of
\delta
is
\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}
, and the optimal convergence rate of
\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
and
\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
is
O_{P}(n^{-\frac{\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}})
;

2.

If
\beta\leq\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}
, then the optimal choice of
\delta
is
\frac{1}{2}
, and the optimal convergence rate of
\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
and
\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
is
O_{P}(n^{-\frac{\beta}{2}})
.

We now state the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
based on the optimal rate of
\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
in Corollary
4
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2
–
7
hold for some
\beta>0
and
\alpha>1
. Further suppose that
\beta>\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}
and
\frac{\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}>\frac{1}{4}
. Let
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-\frac{\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)}{2\alpha(\beta\wedge 1)+\alpha+1}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}N(0,\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}),

(27)

where
\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}:\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}\to\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
is the linear operator defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=&E\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}|Z)-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]\otimes\right.\\
&\ \quad\left.[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}|Z)-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]\right\}.\end{split}

(28)

Proof.

We first define two intermediate linear operators:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle E_{n}\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\},

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle E_{n}\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}.

Using these operators we make the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}+\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)}+\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)}-{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}

By the central limit theorem for Hilbert-space-valued random elements,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}N(0,\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}).

(29)

So we need to show that

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),\quad\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(30)

To show the first equation in (
30
), we begin with the decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)})\\
=&\sqrt{n}\left(E_{n}\left\{[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\quad\left.-E_{n}\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}\right)\\
=&\sqrt{n}\big\{E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]\\
&\quad\quad\quad-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]\\
&\quad\quad\quad-E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)-\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)-\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}^{*}\big\}.\end{split}

(31)

We now consider each term on the right-hand side of (
31
) separately. By construction, the first term is

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}]\otimes[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}]-[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})-\mu_{\ddot{X}}]\otimes[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})-\mu_{\ddot{Y}}]\right\}.

The right-hand side can be decomposed into eight terms, among which the two terms involving
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})
and
-\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})
cancel out, leaving the following six terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[-\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})+\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}}+\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})-\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}}\big].\end{split}

Next, applying the relations
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})=\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}
and
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{i})=\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}
, we further reduce the right-hand side above to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sqrt{n}(-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}+\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}+\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}}+\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}})\\
=&\sqrt{n}(-\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}+\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}}+\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\mu_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mu_{\ddot{Y}})\\
=&-\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}-\mu_{\ddot{X}})\otimes\left(\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\mu_{\ddot{Y}}\right).\end{split}

By Chebychev’s inequality, we have
\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{X}}-\mu_{\ddot{X}}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
and
\hat{\mu}_{\ddot{Y}}-\mu_{\ddot{Y}}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. Hence the right-hand side is of the order
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. Thus we have proved

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

By the similar argument, we can show that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})]=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)-\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)-\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

By Lemma
2

\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}=O_{P}(1)
and
\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}=O_{P}(1)
. Hence

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})+O_{P}(1)O_{P}(n^{-1/2})+O_{P}(n^{-1/2})O_{P}(1)+O_{P}(1)O_{P}(n^{-1/2})O_{P}(1)

\displaystyle=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})=o_{P}(1).

This proves the first equation in (
30
).

To show the second equation in (
30
), we begin by rewriting
\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(2)}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}^{(1)})
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sqrt{n}\Big(E_{n}\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}\\
&\qquad-E_{n}\left\{[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\otimes[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]\right\}\Big).\end{split}

(32)

Define random elements in
\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}\in{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{X}}
and
\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}\in{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{Y}}
as follows

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}=\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z),\quad\epsilon_{\ddot{Y}}=\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z).

Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}-(\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z})\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=\epsilon_{\ddot{Y}}-(\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z})\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)

Substituting these into (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sqrt{n}(B_{\ddot{X}|Z}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z})E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\epsilon_{\ddot{Y}}]+\sqrt{n}E_{n}[\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)](B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z})^{*}\\
&\qquad+\sqrt{n}(B_{\ddot{X}|Z}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z})E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)](B_{\ddot{Y}|Z}-\hat{B}_{\ddot{Y}|Z})^{*}\end{split}

\displaystyle E(\epsilon_{\ddot{X}}|Z)=E[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})|Z]-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)=0.

\displaystyle E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\epsilon_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot)]=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

Apply the same argument to

5
Approximation for Random Functions

5.1
Approximating trajectories by kernel-based smoother

In practice, the processes
X(t)
,
Y(t)
, and
Z(t)
are not observed
continuously over
T
; instead, they are observed only at a finite
collection of discrete time points. The underlying curves must
therefore be reconstructed from these discrete observations.
In the following, we describe the procedure for estimating these
curves, using
X(t)
as an illustration. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. samples from
X
. Assume, for sample
i
, we can only observe
X_{i}(t)
at time points in
T_{i}=\{t_{i1},\dots,t_{im_{i}}\}\subseteq T
. We use an RKHS smoother to approximate the functions (see, for example, Section 6.2 of
Li and Song 2017
or Section 7.2 of
Kokoszka and Reimherr 2017
). Let
\kappa_{T}:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive definite kernel, and let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space spanned by
\mathcal{L}=\{\kappa_{T}(\cdot,t):t\in T\}
.

For subject
i
, we use the subset

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{\kappa_{T}(\cdot,t):t\in T_{i}\}
to construct an approximation
of the function
X_{i}(t)
, and denote the resulting approximation by

\hat{X}_{i}(t)
. Let
K_{T}^{(i)}
be the kernel matrix generated by

\kappa_{T}
evaluated on
T_{i}
, that is,

\displaystyle(K_{T}^{(i)})_{rs}=\kappa_{T}(t_{ir},t_{is}),\quad r,s=1,\dots,m_{i}.

(39)

Furthermore, let

X_{i}(T_{i})=\{X_{i}(t_{ir}):r=1,\dots,m_{i}\}

denote the observed values of
X_{i}(t)
at the time points in
T_{i}
.

We now represent
X_{i}(t)
in the coordinate system induced by

\mathcal{L}_{i}
, and denote the corresponding coordinate vector
by
[X_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
. By the definition of the coordinate mapping,
for
s=1,\dots,m_{i}
,

X_{i}(t_{is})=\sum_{r=1}^{m_{i}}([X_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}})_{r}\,\kappa_{T}(t_{is},t_{ir}).

Stacking the observations
X_{i}(t_{is})
together yields

\displaystyle X_{i}(T_{i})=K_{T}^{(i)}[X_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.

To solve for
[X_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
, we account for the possible
singularity of
K_{T}^{(i)}
by applying Tikhonov regularization.
The regularized coordinate mapping is given by

\displaystyle[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}=(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}I)^{-1}X_{i}(T_{i}),

(40)

where
\delta_{n}>0
is a regularization tuning parameter.
We use the notation
[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
instead of

[X_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
to emphasize that this is the coordinate of the estimated curve
\hat{X}_{i}
.
Let

\kappa_{T}(\cdot,T_{i})=\{\kappa_{T}(\cdot,t_{ir}):r=1,\dots,m_{i}\}.

The coordinate vector
[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
then yields the
following representation of the reconstructed curve:

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{i}(\cdot)=[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\kappa_{T}(\cdot,T_{i}).

5.2
Approximating inner products and distances

To construct the second layer kernel
\kappa_{X}
(again, using
X
as an illustration), we need to approximate the inner products
\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle
and the distances
\|X_{i}-X_{j}\|
, both taken in the space
L^{2}(T)
.
To estimate the inner product

\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle=\int_{T}X_{i}(t)X_{j}(t)\,dt,

we approximate
X_{i}
and
X_{j}

by their reconstructions
\hat{X}_{i}
and
\hat{X}_{j}
, respectively, and
evaluate the integral numerically.
Specifically, we evaluate
\hat{X}_{i}
and
\hat{X}_{j}
at
l+1

equally-spaced points in
T
, denoted by

U=\{u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{l}\}.

For simplicity, we assume that
l
is even and that
u_{0}
and
u_{l}

are the left and right endpoints of
T
.
Applying Simpson’s rule to approximate the integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{l}}\hat{X}_{i}(t)\hat{X}_{j}(t)\,dt\approx\hat{X}_{i}(U)^{\mathsf{T}}D\hat{X}_{j}(U),

(41)

where
\hat{X}_{i}(U)
is the vector
\{\hat{X}_{i}(u_{r}):r=0,1,\dots,l\}
and same for
\hat{X}_{j}(U)
,

\displaystyle D=(h/3)\mathrm{diag}(1,4,2,4,\dots,2,4,1),

and
h=(u_{l}-u_{0})/l
. Using the coordinate mappings, we have

\displaystyle\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle\approx\langle\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}\rangle=\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{l}}\hat{X}_{i}(t)\hat{X}_{j}(t)dt\approx[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\kappa_{T}(T_{i},U)\,D\,\kappa_{T}(U,T_{j})[\hat{X}_{j}]_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}

(42)

where
\kappa_{T}(T_{i},U)
is the
m_{i}
by
l
matrix
\{\kappa_{T}(t_{ir},u_{s}):r=1,\dots,m_{i},s=0,\dots,l\}
, and
\kappa_{T}(U,T_{j})
is the
l
by
m_{j}
matrix
\{\kappa_{T}(u_{r},t_{js}):r=0,\dots,l,s=1,\dots,m_{j}\}
.
The distance
\|X_{i}-X_{j}\|
can then be estimated by

\displaystyle\|\hat{X}_{i}-\hat{X}_{j}\|=\sqrt{\langle\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{i}\rangle-2\langle\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}\rangle+\langle\hat{X}_{j},\hat{X}_{j}\rangle},

(43)

where the inner products on the right-hand side are given the right-hand side of (
42
).

5.3
Simplification under balanced observation schedule

An important special case of the observation schedule is where all subjects are observed according to the same set of equally-spaced time points, which is known as the balanced observation scheme. In symbols,
T_{1}=\dots=T_{n}=U
, where
U
is the common set of time points. In this case we can simplify the estimation procedure by directly applying Simpson’s rule (
41
) to
X_{i}(U)
and
X_{j}(U)
as an approximation of
\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle
. That is,

\displaystyle\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle=\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{l}}X_{i}(t)X_{j}(t)dt\approx X_{i}(U)^{\mathsf{T}}DX_{j}(U).

(44)

Based on the inner product, we can calculate the inner product matrix and the pairwise distance matrix needed for constructing the second layer kernel
\kappa_{X}
(as well as
\kappa_{Y}
and
\kappa_{Z}
), Note that we do not need to involve
\kappa_{T}
and the tuning parameter, and the calculation is significantly simplified.

6
Sample-Level Implementation

6.1
Estimation of eigenvalues

The linear operator
\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
defined by (
28
) depends on the unknown population distribution of
(X,Y,Z)
. So, to apply Theorem
2
and Corollary
5
in hypothesis testing of conditional independence we must first approximate this linear operator.

Since
\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
is a self-adjoint linear operator on
{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{X}\otimes\ddot{Y}}=\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
, we begin by characterizing the tensor product space
{\cal{G}}_{\ddot{X}\otimes\ddot{Y}}
at the sample level. By Theorem 2.7.13 of
Hsing and Eubank 2015
,
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}

is a reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}\otimes\ddot{Y}}
.

To estimate the eigenvalues of
\Gamma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
, we perform coordinate mapping on its empirical version
\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
under the following basis:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}=\left\{{\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i},\star,\ddot{Y}_{j})}:i=1,\dots,n;j=1,\dots,n\right\},

where

\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{x},\star,\ddot{y})=\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{x})\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\star,\ddot{y})
.

In the operator level,
\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
is estimated by replacing all expectations in (
28
) by the corresponding sample means, as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})].

We then turn the linear operators in the right-hand above into
n\times n
matrices by coordinating mapping. Applying the rules of coordinate mapping described in Lemma 12.3 of
Li 2018b
, the coordinate of the linear operator
\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
with respect to the spanning system
\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}^{\mathsf{T}}G_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}},\end{split}

(45)

where
G_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}
is the Gram matrix of the set
\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}
; that is, the
((i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}))
-th entry of
G_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}(G_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}})_{(ij)(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}=&\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{j}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i^{\prime}})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{j^{\prime}})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\ddot{X}\otimes\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{i^{\prime}})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\ddot{X}}}\langle\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{j}),\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{j^{\prime}})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\ddot{Y}}}\\
=&\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\ddot{X}_{i},\ddot{X}_{i^{\prime}})\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}}(\ddot{Y}_{j},\ddot{Y}_{j^{\prime}})\\
=&(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}})_{ii^{\prime}}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}})_{jj^{\prime}}.\end{split}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}},

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product between two matrices. Also, on the right-hand side of (
45
),

\displaystyle[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}\otimes[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}},

where the first term is

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}=&[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k})-\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}\\
=&[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}-\,_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}}}[\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}\\
=&e_{k}-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}e_{k}\\
=&[I-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}]e_{k}\\
=&n\epsilon_{n}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}e_{k}\\
=&R_{Z}e_{k},\end{split}

and same arguments apply to the second term, which gives

\displaystyle[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{Y}}}=R_{Z}e_{k},

where
R_{Z}
is defined by (
22
).
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}_{k}|Z_{k})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{Y}_{k}|Z_{k})]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=\left(R_{Z}e_{k}\right)\otimes\left(R_{Z}e_{k}\right).

We plug this result into the coordinate mapping of
\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}
, and we have

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[(R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(R_{Z}e_{k})][(R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(R_{Z}e_{k})]^{\mathsf{T}}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}).

(46)

Since the rank of the matrix
\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}
is at most
n
, we compute its first
n
eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\hat{\lambda}_{n}
and use them as estimates of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
’s in (
38
).

6.2
Acceleration of computation

Since the coordinate mapping
\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}
in (
46
), is an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix, the direct computational cost for its eigenvalues is very high even for moderate sample sizes. However, since the rank of this matrix is only
n
, the cost can be substantially reduced by a carefully designed algorithm. In the following we adapt the idea in Section 6.3 of
Tang and Li 2024
to accelerate the computation.

Specifically, since
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}
and
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}
are symmetric, (
46
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}\otimes\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2})[(R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(R_{Z}e_{k})][(R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(R_{Z}e_{k})]^{\mathsf{T}}(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}\otimes\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2})\\
=&\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}R_{Z}e_{k})/\sqrt{n}\right]\left[(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}R_{Z}e_{k})\otimes(\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}R_{Z}e_{k})/\sqrt{n}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\\
=&\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[(L_{\ddot{X}}e_{k})\otimes(L_{\ddot{Y}}e_{k})/\sqrt{n}\right]\left[(L_{\ddot{X}}e_{k})\otimes(L_{\ddot{Y}}e_{k})/\sqrt{n}\right]^{\mathsf{T}},\end{split}

where
L_{\ddot{X}}=\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}}^{1/2}R_{Z}
and
L_{\ddot{Y}}=\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}}^{1/2}R_{Z}
.
Let
L=(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
be the
n^{2}\times n
matrix, whose
k
th column is
L_{k}=(L_{\ddot{X}}e_{k})\otimes(L_{\ddot{Y}}e_{k})/\sqrt{n}
. Then,

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}L_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}=LL^{\mathsf{T}}.

Since the nonzero eigenvalues of
LL^{\mathsf{T}}
are same as those of
L^{\mathsf{T}}L
, we only need to calculate the eigenvalues of the
n\times n
matrix
L^{\mathsf{T}}L
, substantially reducing the computing time. The computer memory needed is also substantially reduced, because we never need to save the
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix
\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[\hat{\Gamma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}]_{\mathcal{B}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}
, and the largest matrix we need to save is the
n^{2}\times n
matrix
L
.

6.3
Tuning

In this section, we describe the procedure for selecting tuning parameters.
Three types of tuning parameters are involved: the kernel bandwidths,
the regularization parameter
\delta_{n}
associated with
K_{T}
, and the
regularization parameter
\epsilon_{n}
associated with
K_{Z}
.
We propose a step-by-step procedure for selecting these parameters sequentially.

6.3.1
Kernel bandwidths

For all kernels, we use Gaussian radial basis functions (RBFs) as
reproducing kernels. Each kernel involves a tuning parameter controlling
the bandwidth. Specifically, the kernel on
T
is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa_{T}:T\times T\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(t_{1},t_{2})\mapsto\exp\{-\gamma_{T}(t_{1}-t_{2})^{2}\},

(47)

where
\gamma_{T}>0
is a bandwidth parameter.
Similarly, the kernel on
\mathcal{G}_{X}
is

\displaystyle\kappa_{X}:\mathcal{G}_{X}\times\mathcal{G}_{X}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\exp\{-\gamma_{X}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}\},

(48)

where
\gamma_{X}>0
is the corresponding bandwidth parameter.
The kernels
\kappa_{Y}
and
\kappa_{Z}
are defined analogously with tuning
parameters
\gamma_{Y}
and
\gamma_{Z}
.

Following the criterion in Section 6.4 of
Li and Solea 2018
,
we choose the bandwidth parameters according to the average pairwise
distances. Specifically,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{T}}}={l+1\choose 2}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\sum_{r=k+1}^{l}|u_{k}-u_{r}|,

(49)

where
U=\{u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{l}\}
are the
l+1
equally-spaced points on
T
as defined in Section
5.2
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{X}}}={n\choose 2}^{-1}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\sum_{b=a+1}^{n}\|X_{a}-X_{b}\|.

(50)

The parameters
\gamma_{Y}
and
\gamma_{Z}
are chosen in the same way.

6.3.2
Tikhonov regularization constant
\delta_{n}

To achieve appropriate scaling, we reset
\delta_{n}
to
\delta_{n}\lambda_{\max}(K_{T}^{(i)})
, where
\lambda_{\max}(K_{T}^{(i)})
is the largest eigenvalue of
K_{T}^{(i)}
. Inspired by the approach in Section 6.4 of
Li and Solea 2018
, we employ the generalized cross-validation (GCV) criterion

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{GCV}_{T}(\delta_{n})=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|X_{i}(T_{i})-K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}X_{i}(T_{i})\|_{F}^{2}}{\{1-\mathrm{trace}[K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}]/m_{i}\}^{2}}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|Y_{i}(T_{i})-K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}Y_{i}(T_{i})\|_{F}^{2}}{\{1-\mathrm{trace}[K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}]/m_{i}\}^{2}}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|Z_{i}(T_{i})-K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}Z_{i}(T_{i})\|_{F}^{2}}{\{1-\mathrm{trace}[K_{T}^{(i)}(K_{T}^{(i)}+\delta_{n}\lambda_{max}(K_{T}^{(i)})I_{m_{i}})^{-1}]/m_{i}\}^{2}}.\end{split}

(51)

In practice, we minimize
GCV_{T}(\delta_{n})
over a prespecified range
I_{T}

to determine the optimal tuning parameter
\delta_{n}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{*}={\arg\min}_{\delta_{n}\in I_{T}}\mathrm{GCV}_{T}(\delta_{n}).

(52)

6.3.3
Tikhonov regularization constant
\epsilon_{n}

Similarly, let
\kappa_{XY}
be a reproducing kernel on the tensor-product space

\mathcal{G}_{X\otimes Y}=\mathcal{G}_{X}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{Y}
, defined by

\kappa_{XY}(\cdot,x;\star,y)=\kappa_{X}(\cdot,x)\,\kappa_{Y}(\star,y).

The corresponding Gram matrix for
\mathcal{H}_{X\otimes Y}
is

K_{XY}=K_{X}\odot K_{Y},

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. The centered Gram matrix is therefore

\tilde{K}_{XY}=H(K_{X}\odot K_{Y})H.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{Z}=\mathrm{ran}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ})=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B}_{Z})
and

\mathcal{R}_{XY}=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B}_{XY})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{XY}=

\displaystyle\left\{\hat{\kappa}_{XY}(\cdot,X_{i};\star,Y_{i}):i=1,\dots,n\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\kappa_{XY}(\cdot,X_{i};\star,Y_{i})-E_{n}[\kappa_{XY}(\cdot,X;\star,Y)]:i=1,\dots,n\right\}.

We predict functions in
\mathcal{R}_{XY}
using functions in

\mathcal{R}_{Z}
, which amounts to projecting

\hat{\kappa}_{XY}(\cdot,X_{i},\star,Y_{i})
onto
\mathcal{R}_{Z}
.
The empirical projection is

\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{Z(XY)}\hat{\kappa}_{XY}(\cdot,X_{i},\star,Y_{i}),

which minimizes the empirical variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{n}\!\left\{\hat{\kappa}_{XY}(X,X_{i},Y,Y_{i})-[A\hat{\kappa}_{XY}(\cdot,X_{i},\star,Y_{i})](Z)\right\}.

(53)

among all bounded linear operators
A:{\cal{G}}_{X\otimes Y}\to{\cal{G}}_{Z}
.
Under Tikhonov regularization, the coordinate representation of

\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{Z(XY)}
with respect to the bases

\mathcal{B}_{Z}
and
\mathcal{B}_{XY}
is

\,{}_{\mathcal{B}_{Z}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{Z(XY)}]_{\mathcal{B}_{XY}}=(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\tilde{K}_{XY}.

Hence the empirical error in (
53
) becomes

\|\tilde{K}_{XY}-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\tilde{K}_{XY}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
stands for the Frobenius matrix norm.

As with
\delta_{n}
, we replace
\epsilon_{n}
by

\epsilon_{n}\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{K}_{Z})
to ensure appropriate scaling.
The generalized cross-validation (GCV) criterion is

\displaystyle\mathrm{GCV}_{Z}(\epsilon_{n})=\frac{\|\tilde{K}_{XY}-\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{K}_{Z})I)^{-1}\tilde{K}_{XY}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{\{1-\mathrm{trace}[\tilde{K}_{Z}(\tilde{K}_{Z}+n\epsilon_{n}\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{K}_{Z})I)^{-1}]/n\}^{2}}.

(54)

The optimal parameter is chosen as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}^{*}={\arg\min}_{\epsilon_{n}\in I_{Z}}\mathrm{GCV}_{Z}(\epsilon_{n}).

(55)

for some prespecified range
I_{Z}
.

6.4
Algorithm

We summarize the procedures developed before as the following algorithm.

1.

Choose the kernel
\kappa_{T}
for
\mathcal{H}
, along with its corresponding bandwidth. For example, if
\kappa_{T}
is the Gaussian kernel (
47
), then set the bandwidth by (
49
).

2.

Compute the matrices
K_{T}^{(i)}
based on
\kappa_{T}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
, by (
39
).

3.

Choose the regularization parameter
\delta_{n}
by minimizing
GCV_{T}(\delta_{n})
in (
51
) over a prespecified range.

4.

Based on the observations
X_{i}(T_{i})
,
Y_{i}(T_{i})
,
Z_{i}(T_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
, compute the coordinate mapping
[\hat{X}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
,
[\hat{Y}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
,
[\hat{Z}_{i}]_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
by (
40
) for
i=1,\dots,n
.

5.

Set the grid points
U
and compute the matrix
\kappa_{T}(T_{i},U)
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Compute the inner products

\langle\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}\rangle
,
\langle\hat{Y}_{i},\hat{Y}_{j}\rangle
and
\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle

by (
42
),
and further compute

\|\hat{X}_{i}-\hat{X}_{j}\|
,
\|\hat{Y}_{i}-\hat{Y}_{j}\|
and
\|\hat{Z}_{i}-\hat{Z}_{j}\|

by (
43
).

6.

Choose the bandwidths for the Gaussian kernels
\kappa_{X}
,
\kappa_{Y}
,
\kappa_{Z}
by (
50
).

7.

Compute the Gram matrices
K_{X}
,
K_{Y}
,
K_{Z}
based on the kernels
\kappa_{X}
,
\kappa_{Y}
,
\kappa_{Z}
, and further compute
K_{\ddot{X}}
,
K_{\ddot{Y}}
based on (
21
).

8.

Choose the regularization parameters
\delta_{n}
and
\epsilon_{n}
by minimizing
\mathrm{GCV}_{T}(\delta_{n})
in (
51
) and
\mathrm{GCV}_{Z}(\epsilon_{n})
in (
54
), respectively, over their prespecified ranges.

9.

Compute
R_{Z}
using (
22
), and further compute
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and
\tilde{K}_{\ddot{Y}|Z}
using (
23
).

10.

Compute the test statistic
n\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
via (
24
).

11.

Compute the eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{1}
, …,
\hat{\lambda}_{n}
of
L^{\mathsf{T}}L
as defined in Section
6.2
.

12.

Perform the test using the asymptotic distribution in (
38
).

One side note is that the first five steps can be simplified under some special cases, where all observations are on the same set of equally-spaced grid points. The simplified steps are to be carried out according to Section
5.3
.

7

We first generate the random functions
\epsilon_{i}(t)
for
i=1,2,3
by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\xi_{k}\kappa_{T}(t,t_{k}),

is the Brownian motion kernel,

.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

\Leftarrow
:
Applying (
12
) to
(\ddot{X},\ddot{Y},Z)
and the iterative law of conditional expectation, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}

\displaystyle=E[(\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})|Z])\otimes(\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})|Z])]

\displaystyle=E\{E[(\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})|Z])\otimes(\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})|Z])|Z]\}.

(56)

Since
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
, we also have
\ddot{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp\ddot{Y}|Z
, and consequently,

\displaystyle E[(\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})|Z])\otimes(\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})|Z])|Z]

\displaystyle=E\{E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})|Z]|Z]\otimes E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})-E[\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}(\cdot,\ddot{Y})|Z]|Z]\}=0.

Thus
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=0
.

\Rightarrow
: Let
f\in\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
. Then, by (
56
) and
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}=0
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\langle f,\Sigma_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}|Z}g\rangle_{\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}}

\displaystyle=E[(f(\ddot{X})-E[f(\ddot{X})|Z])(g(\ddot{Y})-E[g(\ddot{Y})|Z])]

\displaystyle=E[f(\ddot{X})g(\ddot{Y})]-E\{E[f(\ddot{X})|Z])]E[g(\ddot{Y})|Z])]\}

This implies that, for all
h\in\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}
,

\displaystyle E_{P_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}}[h(\ddot{X},\ddot{Y})]=E_{E_{Z}(P_{\ddot{X}|Z}\otimes P_{\ddot{Y}|Z})}[h(\ddot{X},\ddot{Y})]

(57)

where
E_{Z}(P_{\ddot{X}|Z}\otimes P_{\ddot{Y}|Z})
is the probability measure on
(\Omega_{X}\times\Omega_{Y}\times\Omega_{Z},{\cal{B}}_{X}\times{\cal{B}}_{Y}\times{\cal{B}}_{Z})
defined by

\displaystyle E_{Z(P_{\ddot{X}|Z}\otimes P_{\ddot{Y}|Z})}(A\times B)=E_{Z}[E(I_{A}(\ddot{X})|Z)E(I_{B}(\ddot{Y})|Z)]

for all Borel sets
A\subset\Omega_{X}\times\Omega_{Z}
and
B\subset\Omega_{Y}\times\Omega_{Z}
. Since
\kappa_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\kappa_{\ddot{Y}}
is characteristic, we know that
\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{X}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ddot{Y}}+\mathbb{R}
is dense in
L^{2}(P_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}})
(see, for example, Proposition 5 of
Fukumizu et al. 2009
). Thus, (
57
) implies

\displaystyle P_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}=E_{Z}(P_{\ddot{X}|Z}\otimes P_{\ddot{Y}|Z}).

Therefore, for all Borel sets
A\in\mathcal{B}_{X}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
and
C\in\mathcal{B}_{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle P[(X,Y,Z)\in A\times B\times C]

\displaystyle=P_{\ddot{X}\ddot{Y}}[(A\times C)\times(B\times C)]

\displaystyle=E_{Z}[P_{\ddot{X}|Z}(A\times C|Z)P_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(B\times C|Z)]

\displaystyle=E_{Z}[E_{\ddot{X}|Z}(I_{A}(X)I_{C}(Z)|Z)E_{\ddot{Y}|Z}(I_{B}(Y)I_{C}(Z)|Z)]

\displaystyle=E_{Z}[E_{X|Z}(I_{A}(X)|Z)E_{Y|Z}(I_{B}(Y)|Z)I_{C}(Z)]

\displaystyle=E_{Z}[P_{X|Z}(A|Z)P_{Y|Z}(B|Z)I_{C}(Z)],

while on the other hand,

\displaystyle P[(X,Y,Z)\in A\times B\times C]

\displaystyle=E_{XYZ}[I_{A}(X)I_{B}(Y)I_{C}(Z)]

\displaystyle=E_{Z}\{E_{XY|Z}[I_{A}(X)I_{B}(Y)|Z]I_{C}(Z)\}

\displaystyle=E_{Z}[P_{XY|Z}(A\times B|Z)I_{C}(Z)].

Thus,

\displaystyle E_{Z}[P_{X|Z}(A|Z)P_{Y|Z}(B|Z)I_{C}(Z)]=E_{Z}[P_{XY|Z}(A\times B|Z)I_{C}(Z)]

for all Borel sets
A\in\mathcal{B}_{X}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
and
C\in\mathcal{B}_{Z}
, which implies that

\displaystyle P_{X|Z}(A|Z)P_{Y|Z}(B|Z)=P_{XY|Z}(A\times B|Z)\quad\text{almost surely }P_{Z}

for all Borel sets
A\in\mathcal{B}_{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
, which gives
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We only prove the rate of
\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
, as the other one can be proved similarly. We first decompose
\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}
and use the triangular inequality to bound its operator norm as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\hat{B}_{\ddot{X}|Z}-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=&\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
\leq&\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
&+\|[\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
&+\|(\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z|Z})(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
&+\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z|Z}[(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}]\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
&+\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z|Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}},\end{split}

(58)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z|Z}=E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)],

and
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}
is defined by (
13
).

We analyze the five terms in (
58
) one by one. For the second term of (
58

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{ZZ}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\left[(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\Sigma_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)\right](\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\Sigma_{ZZ})(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\|\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\Sigma_{ZZ}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\|(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}.

Note that
\|\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\Sigma_{ZZ}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by Hilbert space central limit theorem, and
\|(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\leq\epsilon_{n}^{-1}\|I\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=\epsilon_{n}^{-1}
. Regarding the first term

, using
\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}=S_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{1+\beta}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=&\|S_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{1+\beta}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
\leq&\|S_{\ddot{X}Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\|\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\beta}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
\leq&\|S_{\ddot{X}Z}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\|(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1+\beta}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}\\
=&O(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1-1}).\end{split}

(59)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}(\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=&\epsilon_{n}O(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1-1})O_{P}(n^{-1/2})O_{P}(\epsilon_{n}^{-1})\\
=&O_{P}(n^{-1/2}\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1-1}).\end{split}

(60)

For the first term of (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}[\Sigma_{ZZ}-(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)](\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{n}\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}.

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}(\Sigma_{ZZ}+\epsilon_{n}I)^{-1}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{OP}}=O_{P}(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta\wedge 1}).

(61)

For the third term of (
58
), since

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z}-\Sigma_{\ddot{X}Z}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{ZZ}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ddot{X}Z|Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}E_{n}[\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]-E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]

\displaystyle E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}}(\cdot,\ddot{X})\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]-B_{\ddot{X}|Z}E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)\otimes\hat{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]-E_{n}[\tilde{\kappa}_{\ddot{X}|Z}(\cdot,\ddot{X}|Z)\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{Z}(\cdot,Z)]
