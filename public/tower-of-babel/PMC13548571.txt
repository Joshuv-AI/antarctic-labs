Development and validation of machine-learning diagnostic models for identifying frailty in older adults with tuberculosis: a multicentre observational study protocol
Authors: Jinhui Xu, Jiamei Wang, Jian Tai, Liulu Zhang, Shanbi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Frailty in Tuberculosis (FIT) study aims to assess frailty in older adults with tuberculosis (TB) using machine learning (ML) to develop diagnostic models to identify prevalent frailty. Despite its relevance, limited research exists on frailty among this population, and the potential of ML in identifying frailty remains underexplored.MethodsThe study will enroll older adults with TB hospitalized at multiple centers in Guizhou Province, China from June 2026 to May 2027. Frailty, which increases vulnerability to poor outcomes, is understudied despite being particularly relevant in the older TB population. The collected data will include clinical, laboratory, and demographic variables, which will be analyzed using ML algorithms such as logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. Rigorous validation, including repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation and external validation, will ensure model reliability and generalizability. The primary outcome is the baseline frailty status assessed within 1 week after admission, whereas secondary outcomes include hospital stay, healthcare-seeking delay, and 30-day mortality.DiscussionBy integrating ML methods, the FIT study aims to support early frailty identification, promote targeted interventions, and enhance clinical decision-making. Ultimately, the findings may support clinical decision-making for older adults with TB by providing an accessible and efficient approach to frailty assessments that guide personalized care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04445-2.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, is one of the most prevalent infectious diseases worldwide, and it continues to pose significant challenges regarding diagnosis and treatment. In 2021, an estimated 10.6 million people developed TB, resulting in 1.6 million deaths, including 187,000 deaths among individuals living with human immunodeficiency virus [1]. In the older adult population, several coexisting factors make TB a specific issue. First, older adults, particularly those aged 65 and older, are at increased risk for TB because of global aging trends [2]. This demographic is especially vulnerable to TB reactivation, which is primarily linked to age-related immune decline and the presence of multiple comorbidities [3]. Often, older adults experience TB as a reactivation of latent infections acquired earlier in life. Second, the presentation of TB in older adults is frequently atypical or obscured by other chronic conditions, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation [4]. Third, older adults are more prone to adverse drug reactions, drug interactions, and treatment complications because of their use of multiple medications and age-related declines in organ function [3]. Furthermore, effective management of TB in this population necessitates tailored diagnostic and treatment approaches, including comprehensive geriatric assessments and adherence support, to optimize outcomes and minimize complications [5].

In older adults, frailty is a multidimensional and potentially dynamic syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve across multiple organ systems, resulting in increased vulnerability to illness and other health stressors and a greater risk of morbidity, mortality, and loss of independence. Several complementary conceptual models of frailty have been proposed. The physical frailty phenotype defines frailty according to observable physical characteristics, whereas the deficit accumulation approach quantifies frailty using a Frailty Index based on the proportion of health deficits present in an individual. Multidimensional or integrative models further incorporate psychological and social domains. The present study adopts an integrative framework because the Tilburg Frailty Indicator assesses physical, psychological, and social dimensions of frailty. Frailty may be precipitated or exacerbated by chronic disease, physical inactivity, poor nutrition, psychological stress, and aging [6]. It is more prevalent among hospitalized patients and individuals with chronic diseases than among community-dwelling populations and may provide clinically relevant prognostic information beyond conventional measures of disease severity [7–9]. Nearly one-third of hospitalized older adults are frail. Frailty frequently coexists with anemia, cognitive impairment, and chronic heart failure and is associated with functional decline, reduced treatment tolerance, prolonged hospitalization, loss of independence, and increased mortality [10, 11]. Identifying frailty is therefore important for recognizing vulnerable patients, guiding comprehensive assessment and supportive interventions, and reducing adverse clinical outcomes.

Frailty is particularly relevant to older adults with tuberculosis (TB), because several features of TB, including persistent systemic inflammation, malnutrition, reduced physical activity, and progressive functional decline, may further deplete physiological reserve and increase vulnerability to adverse outcomes. Conversely, pre-existing frailty may impair patients’ ability to tolerate prolonged illness and anti-TB treatment, suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship between TB and frailty. However, direct evidence supporting this relationship remains limited. A previous single-center study in China reported a frailty prevalence of approximately 50% among older adults with pulmonary TB. Factors associated with frailty included advanced age, a prolonged disease course, elevated inflammatory markers, comorbidities, higher depression scores, and sputum positivity [12]. Additionally, a nomogram model incorporating these factors has been developed to identify frailty in this population [12].

Machine learning (ML) represents a transformative approach in data science that is capable of uncovering complex patterns and supporting diagnostic classification by analyzing large datasets. Unlike traditional statistical methods, ML can handle high-dimensional variables, non-linear relationships, and complex interactions, making it particularly powerful in clinical contexts [13]. ML techniques, such as random forest, support vector machines, and neural networks, have displayed superior accuracy in handling complex data compared with conventional regression models [14–16]. The advantages of ML include its ability to manage vast amounts of data, capture subtle correlations, and generate diagnostic classifications without relying on prespecified linear assumptions, thereby offering insights into the relationships between clinical characteristics and health outcomes [17, 18]. However, one notable disadvantage of ML is its “black box” nature, which makes the decision-making process challenging to interpret. To address this issue, explainable artificial intelligence methods have been developed to enhance transparency and allow clinicians and researchers to better understand model decisions, thereby improving trust and facilitating broader adoption in clinical practice [19, 20]. In clinical settings, ML has been increasingly used to support decision-making, diagnostic classification, patient phenotyping, and clinical pattern recognition by analyzing clinical characteristics from databases or larger cohorts [21–23]. Compared with conventional statistical approaches, ML models often demonstrate superior classification performance, including improved accuracy and sensitivity in complex clinical scenarios. ML has also been applied in frailty research, in which it helps identify associations between various clinical factors and the frailty phenotype [24–26]. One study reported high sensitivity and specificity for ML-based frailty detection, suggesting that this approach may facilitate the early identification of individuals at risk of frailty and support timely intervention [27, 28]. Notably, ML methods have also been applied to process sensor data to assess frailty, displaying promising results for the real-time monitoring and evaluation of physical function in older adults [29, 30]. However, little research has investigated the frailty status of older adults with TB, especially through ML approaches.

Using data from multiple centers in Guizhou Province, China, we aim to assess the prevalence of frailty in hospitalized older patients, identify factors associated with frailty, and examine its association with various clinical outcomes, such as delays in seeking healthcare. The development of simple and efficient diagnostic models for frailty assessment is of significant research value, particularly in China, where an aging population and complex demographics necessitate accessible and reliable tools for effective intervention [31].

In this study, machine learning (ML) methods will be applied to clinical and laboratory data to develop diagnostic models for frailty identification. We hypothesize that ML can accurately identify frailty in older adults with TB and, through explainable methods, support clinicians in interpreting model outcomes to enhance patient care. The intended users of the model are clinicians and trained healthcare personnel involved in TB inpatient management and geriatric assessment. Importantly, the model is not designed to replace comprehensive geriatric assessment or clinical judgement, but rather to function as an efficient screening-support tool for identifying patients who may benefit from further geriatric evaluation and targeted interventions. The developed diagnostic models will undergo both internal and external validation to evaluate their discrimination, calibration, generalizability and interpretability for clinical application. The Frailty in Tuberculosis (FIT) study aims to provide deeper insights into frailty burden and its clinical correlates in vulnerable populations and facilitate targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes. We expect our model to reduce the time and resources required for frailty diagnosis compared with existing methods while also enabling remote self-assessment for broader accessibility.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicentre observational study will comprise two components: a cross-sectional diagnostic-modelling component to identify prevalent frailty at baseline and a prospective follow-up component to assess subsequent clinical outcomes. It will be conducted at a provincial referral TB and teaching hospital in Guiyang, as well as at multiple centers across Guizhou Province, China. The study will begin in June 2026 and conclude in May 2027, with the final 3 months dedicated to external validation. The methodology adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for observational studies [32]. Frailty status and candidate predictors for the diagnostic model will be assessed at baseline, within 1 week after admission. Participants will then be followed until discharge and, where applicable, for up to 30 days after admission to assess secondary clinical outcomes.

Recruitment

All older adults admitted and hospitalized for TB will be included in the study. The diagnosis of TB will be established through a comprehensive assessment and categorized into two types: (1) confirmed TB, with the diagnosis based on nucleic acid amplification tests (e.g., Xpert), culture examination, and pathological evidence; and (2) clinical diagnosis, which will be based on clinical evaluation, radiological findings, and sputum smear analysis.

Sample size

This study will include all eligible hospitalized older adults with tuberculosis (TB) who provide informed consent during the recruitment period. The sample size for developing the baseline frailty diagnostic model was reconsidered according to published guidance for multivariable diagnostic and prediction model development with binary outcomes. The calculation will account for the anticipated prevalence of frailty, the number of candidate predictor parameters, expected model performance and criteria to minimize overfitting, including a target global shrinkage factor of at least 0.90, a small difference between apparent and optimism-adjusted model performance, and adequate precision in estimating the overall prevalence of frailty [33]. Based on a previous single-center study reporting a frailty prevalence of approximately 50% among older adults with pulmonary TB [12], a prevalence of 50% was used as the initial planning assumption. Approximately 560 participants will therefore be recruited to obtain at least 500 analyzable participants. Because the prevalence of frailty in the present multicenter population may differ, particularly because of variation in TB subtypes and eligibility criteria, the number of candidate predictor parameters will be prespecified and restricted according to the observed number of frailty events and the prespecified sample-size criteria. Given this sample size, the number of candidate predictor parameters will be restricted and prespecified before modelling. If the final number of candidate predictors is not supported by the available number of events or the prespecified sample size criteria, the candidate predictor set will be reduced before model development according to clinical relevance, prior evidence, feasibility of measurement and data quality.

The dataset will be divided into a model-development dataset and an independent validation dataset. External validation will be performed using participants who are not used at any stage of model training, feature selection or internal validation. The validation dataset will be derived from participating centres not included in model development and/or from a temporally separated recruitment period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is baseline frailty status, assessed within 1 week after admission using the self-reported 15-item Tilburg Frailty Indicator. This instrument is the sole measure used to classify participants as frail or non-frail in the present study. It was developed on the basis of the integrative model of frailty and excludes disability and comorbidity; a score of ≥ 5 indicates frailty [34]. Secondary outcomes include length of hospital stay, healthcare-seeking delay, in-hospital clinical outcomes, discharge status, treatment-related complications recorded during routine care and all-cause mortality within 30 days after admission. Length of hospital stay is defined as the total number of days from admission to discharge. Healthcare-seeking delay is defined as the interval between the onset of the first TB-related symptom and hospital admission. TB-related symptoms include cough, sputum production, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, haemoptysis, or other symptoms that prompted medical consultation or hospital admission. Thirty-day mortality is defined as death from any cause occurring within 30 days after admission.

Frailty assessment will be performed by trained research nurses or investigators using standardized procedures. Assessors will receive uniform training before study initiation. Where feasible, assessors of frailty status will be blinded to laboratory and imaging predictors, and personnel extracting candidate predictors will be blinded to frailty classification. Any deviations from blinding will be documented.

The primary outcome was selected because early identification of baseline frailty is the central objective of the diagnostic-modelling component and may help clinicians prioritize patients for comprehensive geriatric assessment and targeted supportive interventions during hospitalization. Secondary outcomes were selected because they represent clinically meaningful short-term outcomes in hospitalized older adults with TB and may reflect the clinical relevance of baseline frailty. Healthcare-seeking delay will be analysed as a pre-admission clinical characteristic rather than as a post-baseline clinical outcome.

Data collection

Data will be collected at baseline, during hospitalization, at discharge, and, where applicable, up to 30 days after admission. Baseline clinical and laboratory data will be collected within 1 week after admission and will include demographic characteristics, TB-related symptoms, medical history, laboratory findings, imaging results, comorbidities and final diagnosis (Supplementary Table 1). Laboratory data will include complete blood counts and inflammatory markers, and imaging data will include chest radiography and computed tomography, where available.

The date of onset of the first TB-related symptom before admission will be obtained through a structured interview with the patient or caregiver at baseline and cross-checked, where available, against referral notes, outpatient records and admission medical records. In addition, baseline assessments will include Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 [35], the Social Support Rating Scale [36], the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale [37], the Activity of Daily Living Scale [38], and the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form [39]. These instruments assess nutritional, functional, and psychosocial characteristics and will be considered candidate predictors; they will not be used as alternative measures to define frailty.

Only baseline clinical, laboratory and assessment variables collected within 1 week after admission will be used as candidate predictors for the baseline frailty diagnostic model. These variables will be used by machine-learning models to identify patterns associated with prevalent frailty. Participants will then be followed during hospitalization and, where applicable, up to 30 days after admission to ascertain secondary outcomes. Post-baseline information will be used only to describe the in-hospital clinical course and assess secondary outcomes, and will not be used as candidate predictors in the baseline frailty diagnostic model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis will be conducted using SPSS version 29.0, with significance indicated by P < 0.05. Categorical data will be summarized using frequencies (n) and percentages (%), whereas the distribution of continuous data will be assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. If the data follow a normal distribution, then they will be presented as the mean ± standard deviation; otherwise, data will be described using the median and interquartile range.

Missing data will be summarized for each variable before analysis. Because the mechanism of missingness cannot be definitively established, missing baseline predictor values will be handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model will include frailty status, candidate predictors, important auxiliary variables, study centre and relevant outcome variables. The number of imputed datasets will be determined according to the extent of missingness, with at least 20 imputations planned. Model development and validation procedures will be repeated within each imputed dataset where applicable, and estimates from regression-based secondary analyses will be combined using Rubin’s rules. Complete-case analyses will be performed as sensitivity analyses.

Candidate predictors for the baseline frailty diagnostic model will be prespecified on the basis of clinical relevance, prior literature, biological plausibility, feasibility of measurement within 1 week after admission, routine availability and data quality [40]. Univariable analyses may be presented to describe crude associations, but statistical significance in univariable analyses will not be used as a criterion for predictor inclusion. Variables with excessive missingness, very low prevalence or substantial redundancy will be reviewed before modelling. Where feature reduction is required, model-based or penalized feature-selection procedures will be embedded within the internal resampling process to minimize information leakage. Longitudinally collected post-baseline variables will not be used as candidate predictors in the baseline frailty diagnostic model.

The frailty diagnostic model will be developed using Python 3.13. The outcome variable will be prevalent frailty status assessed at baseline, and only candidate predictors measured within 1 week after admission will be used for model development. Several machine-learning algorithms will be evaluated, including logistic regression, decision tree, naïve Bayes, random forest and XGBoost. Data preprocessing, transformation, imputation, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning and model training will be performed within the resampling framework to minimize information leakage. Python libraries, including NumPy, pandas and scikit-learn, will be used to construct and train the models.

Model performance will be evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, classification performance and clinical utility. Discrimination will be assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Calibration will be assessed using calibration plots, calibration intercept and slope, and the Brier score [41, 42]. Classification performance will be summarized using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy and F1 score. The term “precision”, if used, will refer specifically to positive predictive value. The primary model output will be the predicted probability of baseline frailty. For classification purposes, the primary threshold will be selected within the development dataset using the Youden index. Alternative clinically relevant thresholds that prioritize sensitivity will be explored in sensitivity analyses, given the intended use of the model as an early frailty screening-support tool. Threshold selection will be performed only in the development dataset and then applied unchanged to the validation dataset. Decision-curve analysis will be performed, where appropriate, to evaluate potential clinical utility across a range of threshold probabilities [43].

For model interpretability, global variable importance will be reported for tree-based models and, where appropriate, SHAP-based summaries will be used to describe the contribution of individual predictors to model outputs. These analyses will be used to explain model behaviour and improve transparency, but will not be interpreted as evidence of causal risk factors.

Internal validation will be conducted within the development dataset using repeated five-fold cross-validation. All preprocessing steps, including imputation, feature selection, data transformation and hyperparameter tuning, will be performed within each training fold and then applied to the corresponding validation fold. External validation will be performed using an independent validation dataset that is not used at any stage of model development, internal validation, feature selection or hyperparameter tuning. This dataset will be derived from participating centres not included in model development and/or from a temporally separated recruitment period, using the same eligibility criteria, predictor definitions, frailty assessment and outcome definitions. Model performance in the external validation dataset will be reported using the same discrimination, calibration, classification and clinical-utility metrics as those used in internal validation.

Secondary analyses will examine the association between baseline frailty status and subsequent clinical outcomes. Baseline frailty status will be treated as the primary exposure. Outcomes will include length of hospital stay, healthcare-seeking delay, in-hospital clinical outcomes, discharge status, treatment-related complications recorded during routine care and 30-day mortality after admission, where available. Binary outcomes will be analysed using logistic regression and reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Length of hospital stay will be analysed using linear regression if approximately normally distributed; otherwise, appropriate generalized linear models or non-parametric methods will be used. Time-to-event outcomes, if applicable, will be analysed using Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable models will adjust for prespecified clinically relevant covariates, including age, sex, TB diagnostic category, comorbidities, nutritional status and study centre, as appropriate. These secondary analyses will be reported separately from the development and validation of the baseline frailty diagnostic model.

Because this is a multicentre study, centre-level heterogeneity will be considered throughout the analysis. Study centre will be recorded for each participant. Participant characteristics, frailty prevalence, missing-data patterns and key predictor distributions will be described by centre where sample size permits. Study centre will be included as a covariate or stratification factor in secondary association analyses, as appropriate. For model development and validation, model performance will be assessed overall and, where feasible, by centre or centre group. If substantial between-centre variation in model calibration or discrimination is observed, this will be reported and considered when interpreting model generalizability.

The development, validation and reporting of the frailty diagnostic model will follow the TRIPOD + AI statement for clinical prediction models using regression or machine-learning methods [44].

Conceptual framework and covariate selection

The primary modelling objective is diagnostic rather than causal: the model aims to identify prevalent frailty at baseline using variables available within 1 week after admission. Candidate predictors will therefore be selected according to clinical relevance, prior evidence, biological plausibility, feasibility, routine availability and data quality, rather than on the basis of causal inference alone.

For secondary analyses examining associations between baseline frailty and clinical outcomes, a prespecified conceptual framework will be used to distinguish baseline confounders, potential mediators and post-baseline outcomes. Age, sex, TB diagnostic category, comorbidities, nutritional status and study centre will be considered core adjustment variables because they may influence both baseline frailty status and subsequent clinical outcomes. Post-baseline variables that may lie on the causal pathway between frailty and outcomes will not be adjusted for in the primary secondary-outcome models, to avoid overadjustment. These analyses will be interpreted as observational associations rather than evidence of causality.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and caregivers will be consulted during protocol implementation regarding the acceptability of frailty assessment, clarity of questionnaire administration, and feasibility of collecting baseline predictors. Their feedback will be used to refine participant-facing materials and dissemination plans. Patients will not be involved in model training or statistical analysis. Educational sessions on frailty and TB management will be organized to raise awareness and promote engagement among participants and the public. The research findings will also be disseminated to participants and shared through academic publications. Additionally, patients’ representatives will be involved in discussions regarding the dissemination plan.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of Taijiang County People's Hospital. The study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants will provide informed consent before data collection. The study results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and shared with healthcare professionals and policymakers to inform TB and frailty management strategies in older populations.

Data management

A centralized data management system will be employed to ensure participant confidentiality and data integrity. All data will be securely stored: digital materials will be stored in encrypted databases, and physical copies will be stored in locked filing cabinets, accessible only to authorized personnel. Digital data will be further safeguarded by storage on a secure, password-protected computer. Data management protocols will adhere to strict ethical standards, avoiding the collection of any identifying information. Instead, unique identification numbers will be assigned to maintain participant anonymity. Regular audits will verify data accuracy, and data sharing agreements will guarantee that any shared information remains anonymized.

Conclusion

The FIT study protocol outlines a comprehensive approach to understanding frailty in older adults with TB, leveraging the power of the ML approach to enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making. By employing data from multiple centers across Guizhou Province, China and using advanced ML algorithms, we aim to develop models that can identify prevalent frailty early during hospitalization, ultimately guiding targeted interventions and improving patient outcomes.

The study’s multicentre observational design, cross-sectional diagnostic modelling framework, and validation methods will support the reliability of the proposed frailty-identification model. Addressing a critical gap in frailty research, this protocol could facilitate the development of more efficient and accessible assessment tools, potentially transforming the management of TB in vulnerable older adults. We anticipate that the developed diagnostic models will support clinicians in providing personalized care and lead to better resource allocation. Ultimately, this research will enhance the quality of life for older adults with TB by offering precise and timely assessments of frailty, fostering interventions that mitigate risks and improve healthcare outcomes.
