Quantum clock and Newtonian time
Dorje C. Brody 1 and Lane P. Hughston 2

Abstract
An extension of standard quantum mechanics is proposed in which the Newtonian time parameter appearing in the unitary evolution operator is replaced with the time shown by a ‘quantum clock’. A quantum clock is defined by the following properties: (a) the time that the clock shows is non-decreasing, (b) the clock ticks at random with random tick sizes, and (c) on average the clock shows the Newtonian time. We show that the leading term in the evolution equation for the density matrix associated with any quantum clock model gives the von Neumann equation. Modifications to the von Neumann equation are worked out in detail in a parametric family of examples for which the tick sizes have a gamma distribution. The leading correction to the von Neumann equation is given by the Lindblad equation generated by the Hamiltonian, but there are higher-order terms that generalize the von Neumann equation and the Lindblad equation. Lower bounds on the parameters of these quantum clock models are derived by use of the precision limit of an atomic clock.



In quantum mechanics, the time
evolution of the state of a system is formulated on the basis of a time variable that is grounded in Newtonian
physics, in which time is a fixed structure external to the system
Isham
.
Yet, empirically, the timescales of physical processes,
especially in the microscopic realm, appear to be dependent on the nature of
the environment to which the system is exposed. For example, chemical
reactions can vary from femtoseconds to years, depending on a range of
factors including the concentration and density of the reactants, the pressure, the temperature, and the presence of catalysts. Thus, at high concentration a process can move forward rapidly,
whereas in a sparse environment a process progresses slowly. If a
system engages in many interactions with its environment, then time in effect
passes more quickly for the system; whereas for a nearly isolated system with little by way of interaction, time passes slowly.

This observation motivates
us to explore the question whether it is reasonable to assume that the time
variable
t\geq 0
parameterizing the unitary evolution operator

\{{\hat{U}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
governing the
dynamics of a quantum system is deterministic. Specifically, our purpose
here is to examine the effect of replacing Newtonian time
t
with a quantum
clock
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where the time that the quantum clock
shows is an increasing random process. We shall
derive the dynamical equation satisfied by the state of a quantum system
driven by a random unitary operator process
\{{\hat{U}}({\Gamma}_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
, with a clock
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
that
ticks forward randomly by random amounts.

The exact nature of the dynamical
equation is dependent on the statistical properties of the chosen clock. We demand, however, that for a viable quantum
clock
it holds that
T_{0}=0
almost surely, where time zero is interpreted as the
moment the clock becomes operational, at which the state of the system faithfully reflects its initial preparation. Additionally, we
require that its expectation over the relevant
statistics is such that
{\mathbb{E}}[{\Gamma}_{t}]=t
for all
t\geq 0
. Then we can interpret the clock as a counting device that
measures the frequency and magnitude of the system’s interaction with its
environment. In a generic environment we might expect the
system to interact with environmental particles frequently by small
magnitudes, but occasionally an environmental particle may have a greater
impact. Then the counting “instrument”
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}
will increase by
very small amounts rather frequently, but will also increase by larger amounts
occasionally. It is also not unreasonable to suppose that the interactions of the system with its environment over disjoint intervals of the associated Newtonian time are independent. If these interactions are such that

{\mathbb{E}}[{\Gamma}_{t}]=t
, then we can interpret the Newtonian time
t
as an
emergent variable from an underlying quantum clock model.

With these requirements, we shall show that the leading
contribution to the dynamics of the density matrix of the system is
the von Neumann equation, regardless of the details of the statistical
properties of the clock.
However, the randomness of
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}
will generate a modification of the von
Neumann equation, the particulars of which depend on the statistics of the
clock. In the present paper we look in detail at the case
for which the tick sizes follow a gamma distribution. This example illustrates the
above-indicated feature of
having a large number of very small ticks and a small number of much larger ticks, coordinated in such a way that

{\mathbb{E}}[{\Gamma}_{t}]=t
for
t\geq 0
.

The model contains
a parameter
\kappa
that determines the balance of the rates at which jumps occur for jumps of different sizes. The Newtonian time is
recovered by taking the limit as
\kappa
gets large. If the time shown by a quantum clock is viewed
as a small perturbation away from the Newtonian time, then we expect
\kappa

to be large, and hence we can expand the dynamics as a series
in
\kappa^{-1}
. In this case the dynamical equation to
leading order is given by an equation of the Lindblad type, where
the Lindblad operator is the Hamiltonian. The model exhibits a
generic structure for higher-order corrections. These corrections show
that although we have a deterministic dynamical equation of a
completely-positive trace-preserving Markovian type,
there exists a large family of dynamical equations that generalize
both the von Neumann equation and the Lindblad equation.

The notion of a quantum clock introduced here does not directly concern
quantum devices that can be used to estimate Newtonian time, nor the accuracy limits of such devices, for which there is an extended
literature

(b) how Newtonian time can arise from this counting device. Although our
model is based on an underlying unitary evolution, the dynamics of the state is not
time-reversal symmetric in a quantum clock model. In our picture the fundamental breakdown in the
unitarity of the evolution of the density matrix arises when
we average over the statistics of the random clock.

A natural question to ask concerning any model for a quantum clock
is whether it is consistent with the observed accuracy of atomic
clocks. We find that the known accuracy of atomic clocks gives rise to a
lower bound on the parameter
\kappa
. Taking the case of a

{{}^{133}\rm Cs^{+}}

\kappa>10^{19}
Hz is sufficient to ensure the model

empirical observation. This bound on
\kappa
can be made tighter by several orders of
magnitude with an atomic clock of
higher precision; however, it remains some twenty orders of magnitude
smaller than Planck’s frequency of
10^{43}
Hz. Hence the Newtonian
time can be replaced by a quantum clock with tick sizes ranging far away from the Planck scale.
We conclude by arguing that the observation of
small errors in atomic clocks would indicate that our approach
might be a viable alternative to standard quantum theory equipped with the usual background
Newtonian time.

We begin by considering a generic non-decreasing clock process
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

that this process has the physical dimensions of time. As usual in the theory of jump processes we require that
\{{\Gamma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
should be right continuous with left limits

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t^{-}}=\lim_{s\uparrow t}{\Gamma}_{s}

\displaystyle\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}={\Gamma}_{t}-{\Gamma}_{t^{-}}.

(2)

Then for all

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq t}\Delta{\Gamma}_{s}

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t^{-}}.

In terms of the quantum clock we thus have

\displaystyle\Delta{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{t}=\mbox{$\rm e$}^{-{\rm i}{\hat{H}}\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t^{-}}\,\mbox{$\rm e$}^{{\rm i}{\hat{H}}\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t^{-}},

We use a bold font to highlight the fact that
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{t}

In what follows we are interested, more generally, in the way the density matrix changes over any nonvanishing time interval of duration
\epsilon

\displaystyle\Delta{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{t}=\mbox{$\rm e$}^{-{\rm i}{\hat{H}}\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon}\,\mbox{$\rm e$}^{{\rm i}{\hat{H}}\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon}.

To proceed we expand (

\displaystyle\Delta{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{t}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\mbox{$\rm i$}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon},{\hat{H}}]\,\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(-\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n}}{n!}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon}\frac{(\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n}}{n!}\right.

(7)

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{k}}{k!}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{t-\epsilon}\frac{(\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\right](\Delta{\Gamma}_{t})^{n}\,.

This is the general expression of a unitary evolution of the state
associated with randomly evolving time. To obtain a dynamical equation for
the density matrix, however, we must average (

clock. We assume that the increments of the clock time are independent. Upon taking the average, the evolution equation is no longer unitary.
Nevertheless, by assumption we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta{\Gamma}_{t}]=\Delta t

Neumann equation.

The deviation away from the unitary motion governed by the von Neumann
equation arises because for a typical quantum clock, the expectation

{\mathbb{E}}[(\Delta{\Gamma}_{t})^{n}]

\displaystyle c_{n}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[(\Delta{\Gamma}_{t})^{n}]}{\Delta t}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{$\rm d$}{\hat{\rho}}_{t}}{\mbox{$\rm d$}t}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\rm i$}[{\hat{\rho}}_{t},{\hat{H}}]+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}c_{n}\left[\frac{(-\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n}}{n!}{\hat{\rho}}_{t}+{\hat{\rho}}_{t}\frac{(\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n}}{n!}\right.

(9)

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{k}}{k!}{\hat{\rho}}_{t}\frac{(\mbox{$\rm i$}{\hat{H}})^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\right].

The first few terms in the dynamical equation, more specifically, are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{$\rm d$}{\hat{\rho}}_{t}}{\mbox{$\rm d$}t}

\displaystyle-\mbox{$\rm i$}[{\hat{H}},{\hat{\rho}}_{t}]+c_{2}\left({\hat{H}}{\hat{\rho}}_{t}{\hat{H}}-\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}({\hat{\rho}}_{t}{\hat{H}}^{2}+{\hat{H}}^{2}{\hat{\rho}}_{t})\right)

The idea that time may be discrete at or below the Planck time scale is often
entertained in the literature. Our
analysis shows that the discreteness of time can arise at a larger time
scale without being inconsistent with empirical properties of atomic clocks.
The novelty in our model is the notion that the frequency and
the magnitude of the tick size of the clock are random. As a consequence,
Newtonian time can be seen to emerge from a statistical averaging over an
ensemble of quantum clocks.

Although the dynamical evolution of the quantum state is unitary,
the counting aspect of a quantum clock makes it time-irreversible.
The idea of a time-irreversible random unitary motion has been considered by
Peres
Peres
and Adler
Adler2000
in a different context;
but the Peres-Adler ansatz is based on a Gaussian white noise
Hida
to
model system-environment interaction strengths, and this does not give rise to
a quantum clock model. The accuracy of atomic clocks, with an error of a second
over billions of years, imposes a lower bound on the jump rate parameter of the model.
The fact that an atomic
clock admits a small error suggests that the quantum clock models introduced here
might provide a feasible alternative to the standard quantum theory, which is built on an
ambient Newtonian time. Though here we have looked at time-homogeneous
clocks, where the statistical properties of the ticks do not depend on
the Newtonian time, one can envisage a model in which the jump rate is time
inhomogeneous – for instance, a model wherein the jump rate is initially low (so
the clock can tick by large amounts over a short period of Newtonian time), but
then gradually increase.
Such a model may find applications in the quest for a
quantum theory of cosmology. For instance, if the speed of light is constant but time changes according to a quantum clock, then the distance travelled by light will be a constant multiple of the value shown on the clock.
