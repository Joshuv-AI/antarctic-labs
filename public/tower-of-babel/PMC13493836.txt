Combined effects of tactile stimulation and aerobic exercise on BDNF-related pathways and neurofunctional outcomes in an early-stage 5xFAD mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Deniz Aslan, Nuray Alaca, Merve Açıkel Elmas, Dilek Özbeyli, Serap Arbak, Anıl Can, Filiz Onat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) lacks effective early-stage non-pharmacological interventions capable of simultaneously targeting neurotrophic signaling and neurofunctional decline. This study examined the early-stage effects of tactile stimulation (TS), aerobic exercise (Ex), and their combination (TS + Ex) on behavioral, molecular, and histopathological outcomes in 5xFAD mouse model of AD. Twenty-eight male transgenic 5xFAD mice (7–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to four groups: control (n = 7), TS (n = 7), Ex (n = 7), and TS + Ex (n = 7). Interventions were applied for eight weeks, 5 days per week, with daily 30-minute sessions. Behavioral assessments were conducted, and biochemical analyses measured brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), irisin, lactate, total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), and myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels in brain, muscle, skin, and serum tissues. Histological, immunofluorescent, and ultrastructural evaluations were performed in the hippocampus. Compared to control, the TS + Ex group showed significant improvements in motor performance, neurotrophic factor levels, irisin and lactate concentrations, and antioxidant capacity in 5xFAD mice (p = 0.05-<0.001, partial η²=0.498–0.902). Additionally, increased doublecortin expression (p = 0.032, partial η²=0.571) and improved histopathological architecture were observed. Aerobic exercise alone improved motor function, anxiety-related behaviors, modulated several biochemical and histological outcomes compared with the control (p = 0.05 − 0.004, partial η²=0.332–0.498). TS alone produced limited effects, with notable increases only in hippocampal BDNF (p < 0.001, partial η²=0.712) and lactate levels (p = 0.015, partial η²=0.605) compared with the control group. The combination of TS and Ex may exert synergistic neuroprotective effects, suggesting that multimodal non-pharmacological strategies could represent promising early-stage interventions for mitigating AD–related pathology.Keywords: 5xFAD mouse model, Fibroblast growth factor-2, Irisin, Lactate, Myokine, Tropomyosin receptor kinase BSubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia worldwide, characterized by progressive decline in memory, cognition, decision-making, and behavior, particularly in the elderly1,2. With population aging, AD has become a major public health challenge. Approximately 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide in 2021, a number projected to rise to 139 million by 2050, with AD accounting for 60–70% of cases3–5. This demographic trend underscores the profound personal, societal, and economic burden of AD6,7. Beyond cognitive decline, AD also affects motor and sensory systems, limiting daily activities. Early motor symptoms such as gait disturbances, balance impairment, and muscle weakness8,9, together with sensory losses in hearing, vision, and touch, impair synaptic plasticity and cognition10. Notably, tactile dysfunction has been suggested as an early biomarker of cognitive decline11, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that are complementary, holistic, and target both cognitive and sensorimotor systems12–15.

Degenerative changes in the tactile system with aging can impair both motor and cognitive functions, mainly through mechanoreceptor loss, dorsal column deterioration, and thinning of the primary somatosensory cortex11,16. Tactile stimulation (TS), a non-invasive intervention, has therefore attracted interest for its potential to enhance neuroplasticity14,15. By activating cutaneous receptors, TS initiates neural signaling cascades that influence sensory processing and neural modulation17,18. Animal studies have demonstrated that TS alters dendritic morphology, particularly in brain regions responsible for cognitive and emotional regulation such as the prefrontal cortex and amygdala. Furthermore, TS has been shown to increase the levels of neurotrophic compounds, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), acetylcholine, and fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), thereby supporting neuroplasticity17–21. However, in the context of AD, the literature on the effects of TS remains quite limited. Two of the few studies addressing this issue were conducted by14,15, demonstrating that TS applied to both neonatal and adult APP model mice significantly reduced amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque accumulation and led to improvements in cognitive function, motor performance, and anxiety-like behaviors. The authors hypothesized that these beneficial effects may be mediated by FGF-2 released from the skin, which crosses the blood–brain barrier and promotes neurogenesis, cellular proliferation, and differentiation within the central nervous system. However, these studies did not perform direct biochemical measurements of FGF-2; rather, their hypothesis was grounded in prior experimental evidence22 and theoretical frameworks17,18. Thus, TS may act not only as sensory input but also as a neurotherapeutic trigger of skin–brain communication, warranting further confirmation in AD models.

Similar to TS, aerobic exercise represents another non-pharmacological strategy that enhances synaptic plasticity and upregulates neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and FGF-223,24. Unlike TS, however, aerobic exercise additionally provides a systemic bioenergetic challenge, increasing oxygen consumption, energy expenditure, and cerebral blood flow. Through this bioenergetic context, aerobic exercise triggers adaptive processes that protect against neurodegeneration. Indeed, aerobic exercise has been reported to reduce Aβ accumulation, suppress oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, increase cerebral perfusion, and support synaptic integrity25–27. At the microscopic level, aerobic exercise stimulates hippocampal neurogenesis and enhances BDNF expression via both central and peripheral mediators. These include aerobic exercise-induced myokines and metabolites such as irisin and lactate, which complement broader mechanisms of neuroplasticity28–31. Irisin, cleaved from the membrane protein fibronectin type III domain containing 5 (FNDC5), contributes to the maintenance of synaptic function by increasing BDNF levels, and in AD models, peripheral administration of irisin has been shown to reduce synaptic loss and cognitive decline29,30,32,33. Similarly, lactate, released from skeletal muscle during aerobic exercise and utilized by neurons, enhances BDNF expression in the hippocampus via sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) activation, thereby promoting neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive performance31,34. Notably, peripheral administration of lactate has also been experimentally shown to improve cognitive outcomes35. Thus, aerobic exercise acts not only through myokine signaling but also through broader bioenergetic and vascular mechanisms supporting brain health. Accordingly, emerging evidence suggests that the neuroprotective effects of aerobic exercise may be particularly relevant when interventions are initiated during the early pathological stages of AD, before extensive neuronal damage and plaque accumulation occur36.

The 5xFAD transgenic mouse line (Tg6799), also known as Five Familial Alzheimer’s Disease (5xFAD), expresses five familial Alzheimer’s disease mutations under the control of the neuron-specific Thy1 promoter and is characterized by rapidly developing amyloid pathology37,38. In this model, amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation begins at approximately two months of age but is initially asymptomatic, whereas cognitive deficits typically emerge around four months, representing the prodromal stage of the disease39. By six months of age, pronounced Aβ deposition, neuroinflammation, and significant cognitive and neurotransmission deficits are observed. Therefore, 5xFAD mice are widely considered a suitable experimental model for investigating early pathological processes and evaluating the preventive potential of disease-modifying strategies40,41. Indeed, one of the critical factors in studies investigating aerobic exercise interventions in Alzheimer’s disease models is the timing of intervention initiation. When aerobic exercise is introduced after amyloid plaque formation has already begun, its ability to prevent plaque accumulation and associated cognitive decline may be limited42. For this reason, many experimental studies initiate interventions during early adulthood (approximately 6–9 weeks of age) to examine whether therapeutic strategies can modulate early pathological processes before extensive plaque accumulation and neuronal loss occur43–47. This approach is particularly relevant for lifestyle-related interventions such as aerobic exercise, as it allows investigation not only of restorative effects after disease progression but also of their potential preventive and neuroprotective roles during early pathological stages.

Although the neuroplasticity-enhancing effects of tactile stimulation (TS) and aerobic exercise have been independently demonstrated in the context of AD14,15,26, no experimental study to date has systematically compared these two interventions or examined their potential combined effects in an AD model. While classical neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and FGF-2 have been partly investigated17–19,21, the responses of aerobic exercise–related molecules such as irisin and lactate to TS, as well as their potential roles in AD pathology, remain poorly understood. To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the combined effects of TS and aerobic exercise in an early-stage 5xFAD mouse model, particularly with respect to behavioral, neuropathological, and molecular outcomes. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the individual and combined effects of TS and aerobic exercise in an early-stage 5xFAD mouse model of AD. We evaluated their influence on cognitive and motor performance, Aβ accumulation, oxidative stress, neurogenesis, and neuropathological alterations, together with their modulation of BDNF, TrkB, FGF-2, irisin, and lactate.

Methods

Study design and animals

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Furthermore, all methods are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Acıbadem University, approval number 2022/83. A total of 28 male transgenic 5xFAD mice (age: 7–8 weeks; mean body weight: 24±1.46 g) were obtained from the Acıbadem University Experimental Animal Center (ACU-DEHAM). Animals were housed in groups of five under standard laboratory conditions, including a controlled temperature of 20–22 °C and a 12:12-hour light–dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory chow (Carfil Quality, Oud-Turnhout, Antwerp, Belgium). Throughout the experimental period, all animals were maintained in the ACU-DEHAM laboratory, with regular monitoring for ventilation, cleanliness, and overall hygiene, in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH Publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1985). A detailed schematic of the experimental protocol is provided in Fig. 1.

Schematic showing the experimental timeline. TS: tactile stimulation.

The sample size was calculated a priori using GPower software (version 3.1.7, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The t-test family (two-tailed independent samples t-test for the difference between two means) was selected. The alpha level (Type I error) was set at 0.05, and the statistical power (1 − β) was set at 0.95. The effect size (Cohen’s d) was estimated based on the findings of Belaya et al. (2020)43, which reported an approximate 18% difference in BDNF levels between groups. Considering that the within-group standard deviation was approximately 85% of this difference, the estimated effect size was calculated as d ≈ 1.06. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was 6 mice per group. To account for potential attrition or data loss during experimental or post-experimental procedures (e.g., unexpected mortality, exclusion during experimental procedures, or unusable tissue samples during biochemical analyses), the sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in a final sample size of 7 mice per group48,49.

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 7 per group) using a sealed-envelope method. The control group consisted of 5xFAD mice without any intervention, housed under the same environmental conditions as the experimental groups.

Tactile stimulation procedure

TS procedure was applied to the animals in the TS and TS + Ex groups by the same trained researcher to ensure procedural consistency. The intervention lasted for 8 consecutive weeks, 5 days per week, with each daily session lasting 30 min. During the procedure, each mouse was individually placed in a clean, transparent Plexiglas cylinder (20 cm in diameter and 25 cm in height) to minimize environmental stress and prevent physical interaction with other animals. A soft brush with fine bristles was used to gently stroke the dorsal and neck regions of the animal in a slow and repetitive manner, following a standardized rhythm and direction to provide uniform tactile input. This controlled setting minimized external stimuli, reduced handling-related variability, and allowed uninterrupted visual monitoring of each session by the researcher19. To avoid stress habituation or fatigue, the mice were gently transferred into the apparatus prior to each session and returned to their home cages immediately afterward. In the TS + Ex group, tactile stimulation was consistently administered prior to the aerobic exercise session to standardize sequencing across animals.

Exercise training procedure

Mice in the Ex and TS + Ex groups underwent a structured swimming-based aerobic exercise program, consisting of 30 min per day, 5 days per week, over an 8-week period. The regimen included two distinct phases: an adaptation phase and an aerobic exercise phase. On the first day of adaptation, each mouse was individually introduced to a round plastic swimming tank (60 cm diameter × 150 cm length × 45 cm depth) filled with water maintained at a constant temperature of 32 ± 1 °C to prevent hypothermia or thermal stress. During this initial exposure, animals swam for 10 min. Over the course of one week, swimming duration was progressively increased in 5-minute increments each day until the animals were capable of maintaining continuous swimming for 30 min without signs of exhaustion. This gradual adaptation period was implemented to familiarize the animals with the aquatic environment, minimize stress-related responses associated with swimming, and standardize aerobic exercise tolerance prior to the main aerobic exercise intervention, as commonly recommended in rodent swimming exercise protocols50,51.

Following adaptation, animals entered the full swimming exercise phase, during which they completed 30-minute sessions daily. To ensure consistency, all sessions were conducted at the same time of day under the direct supervision of the same researcher. Brief and minimal handling (typically lasting only a few seconds) was performed only when necessary to separate animals that overlapped or clustered together, or to assist those showing difficulty during swimming, thereby ensuring safe and equal participation in the aerobic exercise protocol52,53. This swimming protocol was selected based on previous study54 demonstrating that it corresponds to approximately 60% of maximal oxygen consumption, thereby qualifying as a moderate-intensity aerobic intervention. It should be noted that this value represents an estimate based on established rodent swimming exercise protocols rather than a direct physiological measurement. Swimming was considered advantageous for this model due to its low risk of joint or musculoskeletal injury, reduced likelihood of physical trauma, and compatibility with the natural swimming ability of rodents55,56. These measures collectively ensured both the welfare of the animals and the reproducibility of the intervention.

Behavioral tests

Twenty-four hours after the final session of aerobic exercise and/or tactile stimulation, behavioral assessments were conducted to evaluate short-term memory, motor coordination, and anxiety-like behaviors. First, the novel object recognition test was performed to assess short-term memory. Then, the rotarod test was conducted to measure balance and coordination. Finally, the elevated plus maze test was used to evaluate anxiety-related behavior. On each testing day, animals were transported from the adjacent housing room to the testing room. For identification purposes, tails were marked with a non-toxic permanent marker displaying the assigned subject ID. Prior to testing, mice were habituated to the testing environment for at least 60 min. Between animals, all apparatus surfaces were disinfected with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry fully before introducing the next subject. Lighting conditions in the testing room were maintained at ~ 500 lx, consistent with the ambient lighting of the housing facility. The rotarod test was conducted under standard housing conditions with a 12:12 h light-dark cycle57.

The rotarod test

The rotarod test was employed to evaluate motor coordination, balance, and muscle strength. Prior to testing, animals underwent a habituation session to acclimate to the apparatus. During the test, each mouse was placed individually on the rotating rod, and performance was assessed over three consecutive trials. The maximum latency to fall (in seconds) among the three trials was recorded for analysis57,58.

Novel object recognition

To evaluate recognition memory, a subtype of episodic memory within declarative memory, the Novel Object Recognition test was conducted. The test was performed in an open-field arena (31 × 24 × 45.5 cm), with two identical objects placed in the left and right corners. The floor was covered with replaceable paper, and mice were habituated to the empty arena for 10 min, 24 h prior to testing. On the testing day, animals were placed at the center of the arena facing away from the objects and allowed to explore two identical objects (sample phase) for 10 min, recorded via video camera. Testing was performed in an isolated room to prevent distractions. After a 1-hour retention interval, one of the objects was replaced with a novel object differing in shape and color. Mice were reintroduced for 3 min under the same conditions (test phase). Object exploration was defined as direct contact with the nose touching the object. Between trials, all objects were cleaned with 70% isopropyl alcohol and allowed to dry for 1 min. Memory performance was quantified using the recognition index and discrimination index as follows59,60:

Recognition Index = (Time spent exploring the novel object)/(Time spent exploring the novel object + Time spent exploring the familiar object).

Discrimination Index = (Time spent exploring the novel object – Time spent exploring the familiar object)/(Time spent exploring the novel object + Time spent exploring the familiar object).

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze apparatus consists of a central platform (10 × 10 cm) with two open arms (50 × 10 cm) and two closed arms (50 × 10 × 20 cm) extending from it. The maze is elevated 50 cm above the ground. The test procedure begins by placing the mouse in the center of the maze facing an open arm, and allowing it to explore freely for 5 min. Behavior is recorded using a camera mounted above the apparatus. The following parameters are measured: latency to enter the open arm, frequency of entries into open and closed arms, and time spent in open arms, closed arms, and the central platform. An entry is defined as all four paws of the animal being within an arm. After each trial, the apparatus is cleaned with a 10% ethanol solution to eliminate odor cues. An anxiety index is calculated based on according to the formula described by Pellow et al. (1985)61:

Anxiety Index = 1 – [(Time spent in open arms (s)/Total test duration (s)) + (Number of open arm entries/Total number of entries (open + closed))] ÷ 2.

Collection of blood and tissue samples

Twenty-four hours after the completion of behavioral testing, animals will be euthanized under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia (50 mg/kg). Blood samples will be collected via cardiac puncture. For serum preparation, blood will be immediately centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min without allowing coagulation, and the resulting serum will be stored at − 80 °C. Tissue samples, including the right hippocampus, right gastrocnemius muscle, and dorsal skin tissue, will be dissected. Serum and tissue samples will be stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses using ELISA (Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay) and for histological evaluation of the left hippocampus.

Biochemical measurements

BDNF, FGF-2, irisin, and lactate levels in serum, muscle, and hippocampal samples, as well as TrkB levels in the hippocampus, gastrocnemius muscle, and skin will be quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Elabscience, Texas, USA for FGF-2, irisin lactate; BT-lab, Shanghai, China for BDNF and TrkB), following the manufacturer’s instructions (Reader: Biotek ELx800; Washer: Biotek ELx50, Germany). Additionally, BDNF and FGF-2 levels in skin tissue samples will also be measured using the same ELISA kits.

Total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS)62,63 and myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels were measured in serum, gastrocnemius muscle, and cortical brain tissue using commercially available kit (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Istanbul, Turkey), in accordance with the methods described in the original literature62–64. The TAS measurement method is based on the ability of antioxidants to decolorize the characteristic color of the more stable ABTS (2,2-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) radical. This method has high sensitivity, with a coefficient of variation below 3%63. The TOS method relies on the oxidation of the ferrous ion–o-dianisidine complex to ferric ion by oxidants present in the sample. The oxidation reaction is accelerated by glycerol molecules abundantly present in the reaction medium. The ferric ion then forms a colored complex with xylenol orange under acidic conditions. The intensity of this color, measured spectrophotometrically, is directly related to the total amount of oxidant molecules in the sample. The test was calibrated with hydrogen peroxide62. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity was assessed using commercial colorimetric assay kits (Relassay, Turkey) measuring both chlorination and peroxidation activities. In the chlorination assay, MPO-catalyzed hypochlorous acid formation was quantified via taurine chloramine–mediated TNB consumption, whereas in the peroxidation assay, MPO activity was determined by H₂O₂-dependent oxidation of o-dianisidine, monitored kinetically at 412 nm64.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses

Following sacrifice, mouse brains were rapidly removed, and hippocampal tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until sectioning. For histological evaluation, 5–6 μm thick coronal sections were obtained using a sliding microtome (Leica Microsystems, Germany) and mounted onto gelatin-coated slides. Sections were stained with Cresyl Violet for general morphological assessment and Congo Red to detect amyloid deposition. For Congo Red staining, sections were washed in distilled water, incubated in saturated NaCl for 20 min, then immersed in Congo Red solution for 30 min. After staining, slides were mounted with Entellan and examined under a light microscope (Zeiss A1 Axio Scope, Germany). Histological analysis was performed using Cresyl violet-stained sections, while Congo red-stained sections were utilized to examine amyloid beta aggregates.

For immunohistochemical analysis, frozen brain samples were bisected horizontally, embedded in gel medium on a freezing stage, and sectioned at 6–8 μm thickness using a cryostat set to − 20 °C. Sections were fixed in alcohol and stored at − 20 °C until further processing. Prior to immunofluorescent staining, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 5 min. Non-specific binding was prevented using a blocking serum solution. Sections were incubated overnight at + 4 °C with primary antibodies against Amyloid Precursor Protein (APP) (1:250, Bioss bs-0112R) and Doublecortin (DCX) (1:250, Bioss bs-3113R). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a humidified chamber with a fluorescent secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit, 1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111–545-003). After triple PBS washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam, UK). Stained sections were examined and imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 700, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was quantified by measuring the integrated density using ImageJ software (v1.44, NIH)65.

Transmission electron microscopic examination

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), cortex tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2) at 4 °C for 4 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Samples were dehydrated in ascending ethanol series (70%, 90%, 96%, 100%), treated with propylene oxide, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. After polymerization at 60 °C, ultra-thin sections (~ 60 nm) were obtained using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7, Germany), mounted on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were examined and photographed using a transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos L120c, The Netherlands)66.

Statistical analyses

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test, confirming that the assumptions for parametric testing were met. Therefore, group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Tukey–Kramer post hoc test to identify pairwise differences. These tests were chosen because they are appropriate for comparing more than two independent groups when parametric assumptions are satisfied. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were reported as partial η², along with the corresponding F values for each ANOVA test, to provide an estimate of the magnitude of the observed effects. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

In the present study, all analyses were primarily conducted in comparison with Group C. Although additional pairwise comparisons between the intervention groups were also performed, no statistically significant differences were detected among them. Therefore, the following results are mainly reported with reference to Group C.

Behavioral tests

The mean ± standard error of behavioral test results for all groups, along with the corresponding F values and partial η² effect sizes, are presented in Table 1. In the rotarod test, one-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference among groups (p = 0.004). Compared to the Group C, both aerobic exercise groups showed significantly increased performance (p = 0.045) for Group Ex; p = 0.006 for Group Ex + TS). In the novel object recognition test, no significant between-group differences were found for either the recognition index (p = 0.077) or the discrimination index (p = 0.068). In the elevated plus maze test, significant differences were observed in the time spent in the closed arms (p = 0.034) and open arms (p = 0.004), with Group Ex showing improved exploratory behavior compared to Group C. However, there was no significant difference among groups in the anxiety index (p = 0.187).

Biochemical measurements

BDNF and TrkB levels

According to one-way ANOVA, significant group differences were observed in BDNF levels in the hippocampus (F(3,17) = 14.03, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.712, Fig. 2A), gastrocnemius muscle (F(3,19) = 15.59, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.715, Fig. 2B), and skin tissue (F(3,16) = 10.29, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.659, Fig. 2C). However, no significant difference was found in serum BDNF levels (F(3,17) = 0.618, p = 0.613, partial η² = 0.098, Fig. 2D). In all intervention groups, BDNF levels in the hippocampus (p < 0.001 for Group TS; p = 0.026 for Group Ex; p = 0.022 for Group Ex + TS; Fig. 2A) and gastrocnemius muscle (p = 0.050 for Group TS; p = 0.007 for Group Ex; p < 0.001 for Group Ex + TS; Fig. 2B) were significantly increased compared to the control group. In skin tissue, BDNF levels were significantly higher than control only in the TS + Ex group (p < 0.001, Fig. 2C).

Regarding TrkB levels, no significant differences were detected in the hippocampus (F(3,17) = 1.472, p = 0.258, partial η² = 0.211, Fig. 2E) or skin tissue (F(3,21) = 0.396, p = 0.757, partial η² = 0.053, Fig. 2G). However, a significant group effect was observed in the gastrocnemius muscle (F(3,17) = 5.546, p = 0.007, partial η² = 0.494, Fig. 2F). Post hoc analysis showed that TrkB levels in the gastrocnemius muscle were significantly elevated in both aerobic exercise groups compared to control (p = 0.048 for Group Ex; p = 0.001 for Group Ex + TS, Fig. 2F).

FGF-2 levels

Significant group differences in FGF-2 levels were observed in the hippocampus (F(3,14) = 42.56, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.901, Fig. 2H) and serum (F(3,16) = 5.739, p = 0.007, partial η² = 0.518, Fig. 2K), whereas no significant differences were found in the gastrocnemius muscle (F(3,18) = 0.109, p = 0.954, partial η² = 0.018, Fig. 2I) or skin tissue (F(3,15) = 1.080, p = 0.387, partial η² = 0.178, Fig. 2J). Post hoc analysis revealed that FGF-2 levels in both the hippocampus (p < 0.001; Fig. 2H) and serum (p = 0.005; Fig. 2K) were significantly elevated in the TS + Ex group compared to the control group.

Irisin and lactate levels

No statistically significant differences were observed in irisin levels in the hippocampus (F(3,17) = 1.266, p = 0.318, partial η² = 0.183, Fig. 2L) or gastrocnemius muscle (F(3,17) = 2.038, p = 0.147, partial η² = 0.265, Fig. 2M) according to one-way ANOVA. However, serum irisin levels showed a significant group effect (F(3,17) = 9.302, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.621, Fig. 2N). Although all intervention groups showed a trend toward increased irisin levels in the hippocampus and muscle compared to the control group, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2L and M). In contrast, post hoc analysis revealed that serum irisin levels were significantly higher in both aerobic exercise groups compared to the control group (p = 0.008 for Group Ex, p = 0.023 for Group Ex + TS, Fig. 2N).

One-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in lactate levels across groups in the hippocampus (F(3,18) = 9.180, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.605, Fig. 2P), gastrocnemius muscle (F(3,15) = 8.696, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.635, Fig. 2R), and serum (F(3,13) = 9.522, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.687, Fig. 2T), whereas no significant differences were observed in skin tissue (F(3,17) = 2.122, p = 0.135, partial η² = 0.272, Fig. 2S). Hippocampal lactate levels were significantly increased in all intervention groups compared to the control group (p = 0.015 for TS group, p < 0.001 for Group Ex, p = 0.001 for Group Ex + TS, Fig. 2P). In contrast, lactate levels in both muscle (p = 0.001 for Group Ex, p = 0.03 for Group Ex + TS, Fig. 2R) and serum were significantly higher than control only in the aerobic exercise groups (p = 0.014 for Group Ex, p = 0.002 for Group Ex + TS, Fig. 2T).

TAS, TOS and MPO levels

Discussion

In this study, TS, aerobic exercise, and their combination (TS + Ex) were tested in 5xFAD mice, a transgenic model representing the early pathological stage of AD. The TS + Ex group showed the strongest effects, with improved motor performance, increased neurotrophic factors (BDNF, TrkB, FGF-2, irisin), enhanced antioxidant capacity, reduced MPO levels, and preserved hippocampal structure with higher DCX expression. Aerobic exercise alone also improved motor and anxiety outcomes with parallel molecular and histological benefits. TS alone, however, had limited effects, mainly increasing hippocampal BDNF and lactate.

In our study, aerobic exercise significantly improved motor performance in the 5xFAD mice. The rotarod test results align with previous findings showing that aerobic exercise enhances motor coordination and endurance in AD models67–69. TS has previously been shown to support motor development in both clinical and preclinical contexts, such as improving motor recovery after stroke70 and enhancing neuromotor outcomes in preterm infants71. In animal models, TS has been reported to increase sensitivity to somatosensory input, promote dendritic growth in the motor cortex, and improve motor function14,15,17,72,73. However, in our study, the group that received TS alone did not show a statistically significant improvement in rotarod performance. This discrepancy may be related to factors such as intervention duration, stimulation frequency, or the stage of disease. Although the motor improvement observed in the TS + Ex group was greater than that in the aerobic exercise group, it did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that longer interventions or modified stimulation parameters may be required.

Previous studies have shown that both TS and aerobic exercise can enhance cognitive functions by promoting hippocampal synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and stress regulation14,15,74–76. However, in our study, no statistically significant improvements were observed in the recognition or discrimination indices in the novel object recognition test. The lack of short-term memory enhancement across all intervention groups may indicate that these treatments did not produce sufficient neurochemical effects under the current experimental conditions. Nevertheless, a study by Oblak et al. (2021) reported that 5xFAD mice exhibit a hyperactivity phenotype, suggesting that cognitive tasks involving motor components—such as the novel object recognition test—may not be entirely suitable for this model77.

Our findings from the elevated plus maze test showed that only the aerobic exercise group exhibited a significant increase in time spent in the open arms, indicating reduced anxiety-like behavior. This result aligns with previous literature demonstrating the anxiolytic effects of aerobic exercise in AD models43,75. Although the increased time spent in the closed arms in the TS + Ex group may suggest that TS weakened the anxiolytic effect of aerobic exercise, this outcome is more likely due to a conditioned association formed by the animals between the enclosed environment and the prior TS procedures, potentially resulting in behavioral conditioning effects. Indeed, previous studies have shown that TS reduces anxiety, increases sensitivity to benzodiazepines, and lowers cortisol levels77–82.

In AD, Aβ accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation reduce BDNF levels in brain regions related to cognition and mood, leading to synaptic dysfunction and loss of neuroplasticity29,83,84. TS enhances acetylcholine release and activates the somatosensory system, thereby promoting the expression of BDNF and FGF-2, and supporting BDNF production through stress regulation and suppression of the HPA axis20,21,85. Animal studies have reported that TS increases BDNF mRNA levels and improves learning and memory processes86. In our study, the increase in hippocampal BDNF levels following TS is consistent with these findings21.

Aerobic exercise also significantly increases BDNF levels, particularly in the hippocampus, promoting dendritic branching, neurogenesis, and synaptogenesis, which contribute to compensating for synaptic loss in AD29. Regular aerobic exercise has also been shown to modulate cortisol levels through the HPA axis, thereby reducing the inhibitory effect on BDNF expression87. Consistent with these findings, previous studies using 5xFAD and other AD models have reported significant increases in BDNF levels following aerobic exercise interventions42,88–91. Our study supports these findings, showing increased BDNF levels in both the hippocampus and muscle tissue in the aerobic exercise groups. This further suggests that BDNF functions not only as a neurotrophin but also as a myokine originating from skeletal muscle92.

Neurotrophins are critical growth factors for skin innervation93. The role of BDNF in tactile perception and mechanosensory processes is well established94–97. In our study, a significant increase in skin BDNF levels was observed only in the TS + Ex group, suggesting that the combined effect of mechanical stimulation and physiological activity may be necessary to induce a localized neurotrophic response. In contrast, no differences were detected in serum BDNF levels, indicating that circulating BDNF may not accurately reflect local expression. Therefore, local measurements appear to provide a more reliable assessment of intervention effects.

TrkB, the high-affinity receptor for BDNF encoded by the NTRK2 gene, plays a central role in neuronal survival, differentiation, and plasticity through intracellular pathways such as MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and PLCγ/Ca²⁺ upon activation via autophosphorylation98–100. In the present study, a significant increase in TrkB protein levels were observed only in the skeletal muscle of both aerobic exercise groups, while no such elevation was detected in the brain or skin tissues. This tissue-specific variation suggests that TrkB expression is regulated not only transcriptionally but also post-transcriptionally. Indeed, increases in TrkB mRNA reported in the brain following aerobic exercise do not always translate to corresponding protein changes101, potentially due to post-translational modifications, protein stability, or region-specific signaling dynamics. Furthermore, AD-related neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and synaptic loss may suppress TrkB expression or stability in neural tissues. In contrast, the observed upregulation of TrkB in skeletal muscle highlights the peripheral neurotrophic effects of aerobic exercise.

Muscle contraction is known to enhance BDNF expression102,103, and exogenous BDNF can stimulate acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction via TrkB activation104,105. Although studies on aerobic exercise-induced TrkB expression in skeletal muscle are limited, similar increases in phosphorylated TrkB have been reported in the soleus muscle following swimming exercise in a myocardial infarction model106, possibly mediated by AMPK and PGC-1α signaling pathways102,103. Moreover, TrkB agonism in skeletal muscle has been shown to activate AKT and ERK1/2 pathways, enhancing energy expenditure and contributing to anti-obesity effects107,108. Therefore, the TrkB increase observed in muscle tissue in this study underscores the broader significance of the BDNF–TrkB axis in peripheral muscle health and represents one of the pioneering experimental findings on this topic.

FGF-2 is a growth factor expressed in various cell types within the central nervous system, with neurotrophic, mitotic, and angiogenic properties109,110. It supports neurogenesis and cell proliferation and protects against mitochondrial apoptosis111. While some studies suggest that excessive FGF-2 may hinder neuronal maturation in AD112,113, others report decreased FGF-2 levels in the aged hippocampus114,115. Despite these conflicting findings, several studies show that FGF-2 protects against Aβ-induced cytotoxicity and promotes neurogenesis111,116, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target in AD. The literature on FGF-2 expression following TS is limited. Although some studies have suggested that TS may increase FGF-2 levels during the perinatal period20,21, in our study, TS alone showed a relative increase in FGF-2 levels across tissues, but this increase was not statistically significant in any of them. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological factors such as the use of adult AD model animals and the application of ELISA, which cannot distinguish between different isoforms of FGF-2. Tissue-specific responses also appear to play a role. Aerobic exercise is known to upregulate FGF-2 expression, particularly in muscle and hippocampal tissues117; however, in our study, this increase was not statistically significant in any of the aerobic exercise groups. Notably, a significant increase in FGF-2 levels was observed only in the hippocampal tissue and serum of the TS + Ex group. These findings suggest that FGF-2 responses are both tissue- and intervention-specific, and that combined stimulation may contribute to a more pronounced response compared to single interventions. The absence of significant changes in muscle and skin may also reflect the more subtle or context-dependent nature of peripheral FGF-2 regulation in adult AD models. Future studies using complementary techniques (e.g., Western blot, immunohistochemistry) and targeting multiple time points will be needed to clarify these mechanisms.

Irisin, a myokine cleaved from the membrane protein FNDC5 during aerobic exercise, has been shown to induce BDNF expression, particularly in the hippocampus, thereby providing neurotrophic support33. Reduced irisin levels have been reported in Alzheimer’s patients, while exogenous administration has been found to improve BDNF levels and cognitive functions29,32,33. These effects are associated with suppression of inflammation, inhibition of apoptosis, and BDNF enhancement via SIRT1 activation31,33. In our study, significant increases in serum irisin levels were observed only in the aerobic exercise groups. The increases in irisin levels in the muscle and hippocampal tissues across all intervention groups did not reach statistical significance. The lack of significant changes in irisin levels in muscle and hippocampal tissue may be explained by several factors. First, the ELISA method may have limited sensitivity for detecting subtle tissue-level changes; more precise methods such as Western blot or immunohistochemistry might yield clearer results. Second, irisin is a transient myokine, rapidly induced during acute aerobic exercise but not sustained in tissue, suggesting that its hippocampal effects may be mediated predominantly by circulating rather than locally expressed irisin. Furthermore, the effect of tactile stimulation on irisin remains unclear and may operate through alternative pathways, such as direct BDNF activation or modulation of neuroinflammation.

On the other hand, lactate—another metabolite elevated through aerobic exercise—not only supports energy metabolism but also promotes neuroplasticity through the SIRT1–BDNF axis31,34,118. Consistent with previous studies, our findings revealed significant increases in lactate levels in hippocampal, muscle, and serum in the aerobic exercise groups, while TS resulted in a significant increase only in hippocampal tissues. Moreover, increased hippocampal lactate was paralleled by elevated BDNF levels. These findings suggest that both aerobic exercise and TS may support cognitive processes by activating the lactate–SIRT1–BDNF pathway and those TS could potentially serve as an aerobic exercise-mimetic intervention.

In our study, the control group showed marked granule cell disorganization, pyknotic nuclei, and intense Aβ accumulation in the hippocampus. These structural damages were less pronounced in the TS and aerobic exercise groups, with the TS + Ex group showing fewer and smaller plaques, consistent with reports that environmental enrichment and aerobic exercise may suppress Aβ pathology15,119,120. However, APP expression did not differ significantly between groups, possibly due to intervention duration, timing, or methodological sensitivity.

Regarding neurogenesis, DCX expression increased in both TS and aerobic exercise groups but reached statistical significance only in the TS + Ex group, suggesting a stronger effect on hippocampal plasticity121,122. Transmission electron microscopy further supported these findings, showing preserved mitochondria, intact myelinated fibers, and higher synaptic density in the TS + Ex group, while the control group displayed pronounced degeneration.

An important limitation of this study is the use of adult rather than aged transgenic animals. While AD typically manifests in elderly populations, early intervention models are often applied in preclinical research to examine preventive effects of aerobic exercise and sensory stimulation at initial stages of pathology. Evidence suggests that once amyloid plaque formation has begun, aerobic exercise cannot fully prevent plaque accumulation or cognitive decline42. Accordingly, many 5xFAD studies start interventions in young adult mice (6–9 weeks old), when pathology first becomes detectable, to assess preventive rather than restorative effects43,123. However, morphological, biochemical, and functional characteristics—as well as responses to interventions—differ substantially between adult and aged animals. Extending these findings to aged models is therefore necessary. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should be interpreted primarily within the context of early pathological stage modulation rather than reversal of advanced AD pathology. Another limitation relates to the aerobic exercise protocol. Exercise intensity was estimated based on previously validated swimming protocols corresponding to moderate-intensity aerobic exercise in rodents rather than direct physiological measurements such as oxygen consumption (VO₂). Although the same exercise conditions were applied to all animals to minimize variability, biological individuality among animals may result in differences in the exact physiological load experienced during training. In addition, the adaptation procedures and manual handling required in swimming-based exercise models represent inherent methodological limitations of this type of intervention. Future studies incorporating direct physiological measurements or alternative exercise paradigms may allow more precise characterization of exercise intensity. Another limitation of this study is that only the gastrocnemius muscle was analyzed for irisin expression and oxidative stress markers, which may limit the interpretation of fiber-type–specific metabolic adaptations to aerobic exercise. Muscles with a predominantly oxidative fiber composition, such as the soleus or extensor digitorum longus, may provide clearer insights into aerobic adaptations at the fiber-type level. However, the gastrocnemius muscle was selected in the present study because it contains both oxidative and glycolytic fibers and provides a sufficient tissue volume for multiple biochemical analyses. Future studies examining more oxidative muscles may provide a more detailed understanding of skeletal muscle adaptations to aerobic exercise. A further limitation relates to the evaluation of inflammatory responses. Inflammatory status was assessed using myeloperoxidase (MPO) as an indicator of inflammatory activity. However, classical inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 were not evaluated in this study. Inclusion of a broader cytokine panel would allow a more comprehensive assessment of inflammatory mechanisms and should be considered in future research. Due to limited tissue availability, oxidative stress and inflammatory markers (MPO, TOS, and TAS) were assessed only in cortical tissue, which may limit the interpretation of region-specific responses in other brain areas. Finally, although several molecular mediators—including irisin, lactate, FGF-2, and BDNF—were evaluated, some tissue-level changes did not reach statistical significance. This may reflect methodological sensitivity, as ELISA lacks isoform specificity and the ability to detect subtle variations compared with Western blot or immunohistochemistry. In addition, many of these molecules exhibit transient responses to aerobic exercise, and multiple time-point measurements may be required to fully characterize their dynamic regulation. Furthermore, correlation and regression analyses across tissues were not performed due to the relatively small sample size, which limited statistical power. Future studies with larger cohorts, complementary techniques, and longitudinal designs will be needed to better elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these interventions.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that the combined intervention of TS and aerobic exercise exerts neuroprotective effects in the 5xFAD mouse model representing an early pathological stage of AD. The combined intervention resulted in significant improvements across behavioral, biochemical, histopathological, and ultrastructural domains, including enhanced motor performance, increased levels of BDNF, TrkB, FGF-2, irisin, and lactate, as well as improved antioxidant and anti-inflammatory profiles. These molecular changes were accompanied by enhanced neurogenesis and better preservation of neuronal architecture. While aerobic exercise alone demonstrated robust effects, particularly in antioxidant status and behavioral outcomes, tactile stimulation alone yielded more limited benefits, primarily confined to hippocampal BDNF and lactate levels.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with our initial hypothesis that the combined intervention would provide superior benefits compared with either approach alone. The statistical differences observed between the combined and single-intervention groups support this assumption, although the modest efficacy of TS alone indicates that further validation is required.

Importantly, because the interventions were implemented in young adult animals representing an early pathological stage of Alzheimer’s disease, the observed effects should primarily be interpreted as modulation of early disease processes rather than reversal of advanced neurodegeneration. These findings therefore highlight the potential value of combined non-pharmacological interventions targeting both central and peripheral mechanisms as early preventive or disease-modifying strategies.

Future studies should investigate the long-term effects of these interventions, evaluate their impact in aged or advanced disease models, and explore their translational applicability in clinical populations.
