Bronchoalveolar lavage microbiota signatures and stage-associated alterations in early-stage and advanced-stage non-small cell lung cancer: a pilot study
Authors: Yajing Li, Qin Chen, Xing Bin, Song Xu, Hui Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aims of this study were to characterize the microbial flora in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of patients with early-stage (stage I, II, IIIA) and advanced-stage (stage IIIB, IIIC, IV) non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and to explore the associations between microbial flora and lung cancer stage.MethodsWe collected BALF from NSCLC patients (early-stage group 26 cases; advanced-stage group 31 cases). Absolute quantitative metagenomic sequencing was performed to identify differential taxa, genes, and enriched pathways. Flow cytometry was used to profile T cell subsets. We correlated the microbial species with immune cell and gene expression. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the ability of differential taxa to distinguish advanced-stage from early-stage NSCLC.ResultsDokdonia (q = 0.040, LDA = 5.704) and Cocleimonas (q = 0.026, LDA = 5.329) were enriched in the early-stage group, whereas Barnesiella (q = 0.046, LDA = 4.784), Pedobacter (q = 0.040, LDA = 4.913) and unclassified Bacteroides (q = 0.046, LDA = 4.932) were significantly enriched in the advanced-stage group. The microbial genes gmhD (q < 0.001, LDA = 3.926), rfaD (q < 0.001, LDA = 3.918), nudF (q = 0.004, LDA = 4.283) and sfsA (q = 0.004, LDA = 3.915) were expressed remarkably in the advanced-stage group. The advanced-stage group exhibited altered T cell subset distributions, including a higher proportion of CD8⁺ T lymphocytes (q < 0.001), whereas it showed a lower proportion of CD4⁺ T cells and a decreased CD4/CD8 ratio (q < 0.001; q < 0.001). Bifidobacterium was negatively associated with the CD4/CD8 ratio (q = 0.015) and positively significant correlated with the genes which enriched in the advanced-stage group.ConclusionsThis study delineated the microbial structure and function of early-stage and advanced-stage of NSCLC. We identified discriminating taxa, genes, and pathways linked to cancer progression, characterized the T cell subset distributions in the advanced-stage of NSCLC. Bifidobacterium abundance was associated with altered T cell subset distributions and stage-related microbial genes, providing hypotheses for future mechanistic studies on microbiota-driven NSCLC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08501-7.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most life-threatening malignancies worldwide. According to the 2022 global cancer statistics, it was the most diagnosed cancer that year, accounting for nearly 2.5 million new cases, equivalent to one in every eight cancers diagnosed globally (12.4% of all cancers). It is also the leading cause of cancer related mortality, responsible for 18.7% of all cancer deaths [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common histological subtype of lung cancer. Survival outcomes vary markedly by stage. A hospital-based multicenter study from China reported 5-year overall survival rates of 76.9%, 56.1%, 32.6%, and 21.4% for patients with stages I, II, III, and IV, respectively [2]. For patients with early-stage (e.g., stage IA) NSCLC who undergo surgical resection, 5-year survival rates reach 77–92%. In contrast, patients with advanced-stage (e.g., stage IV) disease have 5-year survival rates of ≤ 10% [3]. Therefore, the stage of the disease is a critical determinant of patient prognosis. The goal is to implement precise interventions that delay stage progression and prolong survival.

Recent studies have confirmed that the pulmonary environment is not sterile, instead, its microbial community structure differs between physiological and pathological conditions [4]. Researchers agree that healthy individuals also harbor a low density of pulmonary microbiota, composed primarily of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria [5].However, as Mathieu et al. point out, which specific genera truly constitute the “healthy pulmonary microbiome” remains contentious [6]. A microbial ecosystem that remains in a stable equilibrium is defined as a state of symbiosis, characterized by an abundance of potentially beneficial species living in harmonious interrelationships [7]. Consequently, when the balance among species is disrupted, the relationship between beneficial microbes and pathogens can be reversed, leading to dysbiosis. As the lung is a critical site for immune–microbiota interactions, numerous studies have observed that lung cancer is associated with the enrichment of specific microbial taxa [8], alterations in the pulmonary microbiota may promote lung carcinogenesis and progression through defective immune surveillance [9].

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are central to antitumor immunity, mediating immune surveillance and the destruction and elimination of malignant pulmonary epithelial cells. Key cellular subsets include CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), CD4⁺ helper T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), each exerting distinct immunoregulatory functions [10, 11]. Among CD4⁺ T-cell subsets, Th1 cells primarily activate CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, whereas Tregs suppress CD8⁺ T-cell activity to maintain immune tolerance [12]. CD8⁺ T cells are the principal immune effectors that directly kill tumor cells [13]. The pulmonary microbiota plays a pivotal role in orchestrating this antitumor immunity [9]. Studies have shown that a high abundance of Fusobacterium nucleatum within tumor tissues of NSCLC patients is associated with reduced cytotoxic T-cell activity. By inducing T-cell dysfunction, F. nucleatum may foster an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, resulting in poor responses to immune-checkpoint inhibitors [14]. Nevertheless, whether the pulmonary microbiota correlates with lung cancer stage and local immune status, and whether it related to tumor progression, remains unresolved.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is a well-established technique for assessing the cellular composition of the respiratory tract. Because tumor tissue is often difficult to obtain from patients with advanced lung cancer, BAL is regarded as a safe and repeatable procedure that enables microbiological analysis and immune-cell phenotyping of the milieu surrounding the tumor [15]. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) provides a more objective and representative snapshot of the lung cancer microbial microenvironment than oropharyngeal swabs or sputum [16, 17]. Importantly, prior studies have demonstrated the differences between BALF and tumor tissue microbiome, but there is overlap between the microbiota of BALF and paired tumor tissues [18, 19]. The cellular atlas of BALF mirrors the composition of cells lining the alveolar surface, offering a window into the immune status of pulmonary lesions [20]. Therefore, BALF is widely regarded as an ideal sample reflecting the pulmonary microbial environment [21, 22].

Most prior studies have compared the lung microbiota between lung cancer patients and healthy controls to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers. Yet in real-world practice, most patients already present with advanced-stage disease at first diagnosis, forgoing the chance of surgery and facing 5-year survival rates below 10%. Clarifying how the pulmonary microbiome and local immunity differ across NSCLC stages is therefore essential for developing precision interventions that can delay stage progression. The present study targets patients with NSCLC to pinpoint differential taxa between early-stage (I, II, IIIA) and advanced-stage (IIIB, IIIC, IV) disease. We further aim to provide an initial mechanistic framework characterizing stage specific microbial signatures that are associated with cancer progression.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

A prospective cohort study enrolled 57 patients histopathologically diagnosed with NSCLC between October 2024 and May 2025. All enrolled patients were newly diagnosed with NSCLC and had not received any prior anti-cancer therapy. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age 30–85 years with no contraindication to bronchoscopy; (2) No prior bronchoscopy examination, and NSCLC was newly confirmed by pathology on the current procedure; (3) Complete clinical data available; (4) At least one measurable lesion. Exclusion criteria: (1) Co-existing obstructive pneumonia, asthma, tuberculosis, COPD, HIV, diabetes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or any other disorder that could alter the pulmonary microbiome or require antibiotic therapy; (2) History of occupational dust or other unusual exposures; (3) Antibiotic, corticosteroid, cytokine (e.g., interleukins, interferons), immunosuppressive agent, or high-dose probiotic use within 3 months before admission; (4) Bronchoscopy samples obtained without strict aseptic technique or visibly contaminated.

On this basis and using the 9th edition TNM staging system [23], enrolled patients were stratified into an early-stage group (stage I + II + IIIA) and an advanced-stage group (stage IIIB + IIIC + IV). Histopathology of bronchoscopy obtained specimens served as the gold standard. To minimize confounding of the final sequencing results, age, sex, height, weight, and smoking history were balanced between the two groups. No statistically significant differences detected. Ultimately, 57 patients were enrolled, 26 in the early-stage group and 31 in the advanced-stage group.

To obtain BALF from the tumor-involved lung, we evaluated the procedural risk of bronchoscopy lavage for each patient based on thoracic imaging. Lavage was subsequently carried out at the site suspected to harbor malignancy, followed by endobronchial mucosal biopsy and brushing, specifically targeting the segmental bronchus supplying the primary lesion. This site-directed approach maximizes the likelihood of capturing lesion-associated microbiota rather than generic airway flora. To further minimize contamination from the upper airways and contralateral lung, bronchoscopy was first performed on the contralateral lung and then on the affected side, with strict aseptic technique maintained throughout. Fifteen milliliters of BALF were collected from every patient: 10 ml were immediately processed for flow-cytometric analysis on the same day, and the remaining 5 ml were snap-frozen within 1 h and stored at − 80 °C for metagenomic sequencing.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants which included age, sex, height, weight, smoking history, tumor markers, histologic subtype, and TNM stage were recorded and will be followed prospectively. The study was reviewed and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on November 28, 2025 (ChiCTR2500113496), approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Chest Hospital (No. 2024-KY-019-01), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and carried out only after written informed consent had been obtained from each participant.

Preparation of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid

Subjects were placed in the supine position and anesthetized with nebulized lidocaine hydrochloride followed by intravenous propofol. After tracheal intubation or laryngeal-mask insertion, a pre-sterilized OLYMPUS BF-N20 fiber-optic bronchoscope was advanced to the segmental bronchus supplying the primary lesion. When the lesion completely occluded the segmental orifice, the scope was wedged just proximal or immediately above the nearest patent airway. Lavage was performed with five 20 mL aliquots of room-temperature, sterile 0.9% saline (total 100 mL). Fluid was recovered under continuous suction at ≤ 13.3 kPa into a sealed, sterile collection trap, ensuring a return rate of 40–60%. The pooled bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was filtered through double-sterilized gauze and the total volume recorded.

Extraction of genomic DNA and metagenomic sequencing

BALF specimens stored at − 80 °C were thawed at room temperature. Five milliliters of lavage fluid per patient was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was used for DNA extraction. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the FastDNA® SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was verified by agarose-gel electrophoresis, and concentration was determined using the Qubit 3.0 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific); only samples meeting quality criteria (concentration ≥ 10 ng/µL and clear bands without smearing) were processed. All DNA extractions were performed in a dedicated pre-PCR clean room with UV-irradiated reagents and surfaces.

Contamination control

To monitor potential contamination during laboratory processing, the following controls were included: (i) Extraction blank controls: Sterile water was processed in parallel with BALF samples through the entire DNA extraction workflow (one blank per batch of 8–10 samples); (ii) Library preparation negative controls: A no-template control (NTC) was included during adapter ligation and PCR amplification; (iii) Sequencing negative controls: A blank library (sterile water) was sequenced on each sequencing run to assess background noise. All negative controls yielded negligible DNA concentrations (< 0.1 ng/µL by Qubit) and were excluded from downstream analysis.

Absolute quantification

Absolute quantification was achieved using an internal standard spike-in approach. Nine random DNA sequences with base arrangements distinct from any known microbial gene sequences were designed and synthesized by G&C Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China) and divided into four 10-fold gradient according to copy number content. These internal standard sequences were spiked into each DNA sample at 0.1% of total DNA mass.

DNA library preparation and sequencing were performed by Shanghai G&C Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The DNA with spike-in standards was sheared by the Covaris® system (Covaris, USA), end-repaired, and ligated with sequencing adapters. The 500–600 bp range of the sequencing library was size-selected and recovered using MagicPure® Size Selection DNA Beads (TransGen, Beijing). Libraries were qualified on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA), quantified with the Kapa Library Quantification kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) using the ABI 9700HT Fast Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina), generating 10G bps of 2 × 150 bp paired end reads per sample. In total, 842.18 Gb of raw data were obtained across all 57 samples (mean ± SD: 14.78 ± 2.35 Gb per sample; range: 10.2–18.6 Gb).

Data processing and analysis

Quality filtering

Raw sequencing reads were first quality-controlled with FastQC (v0.11.9) and fastp (v0.20.0). Quality filtering parameters were: (i) removal of reads containing > 1 ambiguous base (N); (ii) removal of reads with < 60% of bases having Phred quality score ≥ 20 (Q20); (iii) trimming of low-quality bases at the 3’ end with Phred score < 20; (iv) removal of reads < 100 bp in length after trimming. Only paired reads with both mates passing quality filters were retained for downstream analysis. Clean data quality metrics included Q30 ≥ 92.1% and GC content between 40 and 60%. Clean reads from each sample were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with default parameters. Contigs ≥ 500 bp were retained for downstream analysis.

Assembly and gene catalog construction

Clean reads from each sample were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with default parameters. Contigs ≥ 500 bp were retained. Open reading frames were predicted with Prodigal. Predicted ORFs were clustered at 90% amino-acid identity and 80% coverage using MMseqs2, yielding a non-redundant gene catalog.

Absolute abundance calculation

Clean reads were mapped back to the non-redundant gene catalogue using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4) with end-to-end alignment mode and sensitive parameters. Only reads with mapping quality ≥ 20 and ≥ 80% alignment coverage was counted. The internal standard spike-in DNA sequences were identified in the sequencing data and removed. For each sample, a standard curve was constructed with the logarithmic value of read count per unit length of each spike-in sequence as the horizontal coordinate, and the logarithmic value of the known copy number of each gradient sequence as the vertical coordinate. Based on this standard curve, the absolute copy number of each gene fragment was quantitatively calculated using an in-house R script (v4.4.1).

Taxonomic and functional annotation

Genes were annotated taxonomically and functionally by alignment against reference genome and protein databases (NR, eggNOG, KEGG). Community profiles were generated at phylum through species levels.

Contamination filtering

Taxa with mean relative abundance < 0.01% across all samples or present in < 10% of samples were excluded. Taxa abundantly detected in negative controls were flagged and removed.

Diversity and statistical analyses

Alpha-diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, Pielou evenness) were calculated using QIIME2 and R (v4.4.1). Beta-diversity was assessed by principal component analysis (PCA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray-Curtis distance. ADONIS (permutational multivariate analysis of variance, 9999 permutations) was performed to test for significant differences in microbial community composition between early-stage and advanced-stage groups. Differential taxa, genes, and pathways were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with FDR correction (q < 0.05) and further validated by LEfSe (LDA > 2).

Flow cytometry detection

Three milliliters of lavage fluid filtered through double-layer sterile gauze were transferred into a flow-cytometry tube and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, 20 µL of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (CD3-FITC, CD8-PE, CD45-PerCP and CD4-APC; CD3-FITC/CD8-PE/CD45-PerCP/CD4-APC kit, Z6410002, Beijing Quantobio Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) were added to the pellet, vortex-mixed and incubated for 15 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Erythrocytes were then lysed by the addition of 1 mL of red-cell lysis buffer (R1010, Solarbio, Beijing) and followed by incubation for 10 min at RT in the dark. Tubes were centrifuged again at 1600 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed once with 1 mL of sheath fluid under the same centrifugation conditions. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 200 µL of sheath fluid and acquired on a flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026, Biodragon, Changzhou). Data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.10.0.

Compensation and instrument settings

Before sample acquisition, compensation controls were prepared for each fluorochrome using single-stained compensation beads and unstained cells. A minimum of 10,000 events were collected per compensation tube. The compensation matrix was automatically calculated using FlowJo v10.10.0 and manually verified for accuracy. Instrument performance was monitored daily using standard calibration beads, and voltage settings were optimized using unstained cells and kept constant throughout the study.

Gating strategy and validation

The following sequential gating strategy was applied: (1) Forward scatter area (FSC-A) vs. side scatter area (SSC-A) to exclude debris and identify the leukocyte population; (2) FSC-A vs. forward scatter height (FSC-H) for doublet discrimination; (3) CD45⁺ gating to select lymphocyte; (4) CD3⁺ gating within CD45⁺ cells to identify T lymphocytes; (5) CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subset identification within the CD3⁺ population. The CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio was calculated from the percentages of CD3⁺CD4⁺ and CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells within the total CD3⁺ population. Gating consistency was validated by backgating analysis to confirm that gated populations localized appropriately in preceding plots. A representative gating hierarchy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.4.1). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the clinical characteristics of participants in the early-stage and advanced-stage groups. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), and comparisons between groups were made using the independent-samples t-test. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed are expressed as median and interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)], with between-group comparisons conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are described as frequency and percentage [n (%)], and comparisons of unordered categorical variables between groups were performed using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact probability test. Differential taxa, genes, and pathways were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with FDR correction (q < 0.05) and further validated by LEfSe (LDA > 2). Spearman correlation analysis was performed among differential microbial taxa, immune cell, and differentially expressed genes, and p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the subjects

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 57 enrolled participants, comprising 26 early-stage and 31 advanced-stage NSCLC patients. No significant differences were observed between the early and advanced groups with respect to age, sex, height, weight, smoking status, or histologic subtype. ECOG performance status differed significantly (p < 0.001), with 84.62% of early-stage patients scoring 0 versus 25.81% of advanced-stage patients. This distribution reflects the expected clinical manifestation of disease burden: advanced stage NSCLC inherently imposes greater systemic functional impairment through tumor load, metastatic dissemination, and cancer-related systemic inflammation, making ECOG an integral component of stage-related morbidity rather than an independent variable or study imbalance. RECIST therapeutic response was assessed prospectively at follow up after definitive therapy and differed between groups (p < 0.001). All BALF samples were collected at initial diagnosis before any oncological treatment; therefore, neither treatment exposure nor RECIST response, which both occurring after sampling, could influence the pre-treatment microbial and T cell subsets.

Microbial composition and diversity of BALF in early-stage versus advanced-stage NSCLC patients

BALF samples were subjected to 2 × 150 bp sequencing on the Illumina platform, yielding a total of 842.18 Gb of raw data (about 8.42 × 10¹¹ bases). After quality control with FastQC and related tools, 806.80 Gb of high-quality sequences with Q30 ≥ 92.1% were retained for downstream microbial community analyses. Figures 1a, b depicts the most abundant microbial taxa across all BALF samples, showing their absolute abundances, the top six taxa are displayed as bar plots. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Cyanobacteria, Actinomycetota, and Verrucomicrobiota were predominant (Fig. 1a, c). At the genus level, Haminiimonas, Prevotella, Streptococcus, Cuspidothrix, Haemophilus, and Veillonella were the most abundant (Fig. 1b, d). Figure 1e compares the four α-diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, Pielou’s evenness index (Pielou_e)) between the early-stage and advanced-stage groups, and all p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. No significant difference was observed for the Chao1 index (q = 0.735). However, Shannon (q = 0.010) and Pielou_e (q = 0.025) indices were significantly higher in the advanced-stage group, and Simpson index was significantly lower (q = 0.010). Collectively, these results indicate greater microbial diversity in the advanced NSCLC. Principal-component analysis (PCA) further showed separation along the first principal component (PC1, 25.47% variance explained), suggesting compositional divergence between groups (Fig. 1f). Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS, stress = 0.11) corroborated this segregation. ADONIS based on Bray-Curtis distance confirmed significant differences in microbial community composition between early-stage and advanced-stage groups (R² = 0.045, F = 2.59, p = 0.015), indicating that disease progression is accompanied by marked restructuring of the intrapulmonary microbial community.

Differential taxa in the BALF microbiota between the early-stage and advanced-stage NSCLC Patients

After filtering out genera with a mean relative abundance < 0.01% to reduce background noise, we compared the metagenomic profiles of early-stage (n = 26) and advanced-stage (n = 31) NSCLC patients. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Cyanobacteria, Actinomycetota and Verrucomicrobiota dominated the advanced-stage group, yet no difference reached statistical significance by the Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (q < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). To increase robustness, we cross-validated findings with LEfSe (LDA > 2). Five genera were consistently identified as differentially abundant: Dokdonia (q = 0.040, LDA = 5.704), Cocleimonas (q = 0.026, LDA = 5.329), Barnesiella (q = 0.046, LDA = 4.784), Pedobacter (q = 0.040, LDA = 4.913) and unclassified Bacteroides (q = 0.046, LDA = 4.932). Dokdonia and Cocleimonas were markedly enriched in the early-stage group, whereas Barnesiella, Pedobacter and unclassified Bacteroides were significantly expanded in the advanced-stage group (Fig. 2b, c). Notably, although Bifidobacterium did not reach statistical significance in the inter-group comparison, its mean abundance was visibly higher in the advanced-stage group (Fig. 1d), suggesting a potential stage-associated trend that warranted further exploratory analysis.

To evaluate the translational value of the identified differential taxa, we performed ROC curve analysis to assess their ability to distinguish advanced-stage from early-stage NSCLC. The five consensus differential genera identified by cross-validation (LEfSe and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests) were evaluated both individually and in combination. Among the three advanced-stage enriched genera, Barnesiella demonstrated the highest individual discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.708), followed by unclassified_Bacteroidota (AUC = 0.705) and Pedobacter (AUC = 0.690). Notably, the two early-stage enriched genera, Cocleimonas and Dokdonia, exhibited AUCs below 0.5 (0.349 and 0.404, respectively). This pattern is biologically consistent with their stage-specific enrichment: the absence or low abundance of these taxa in advanced-stage patients contributes to the discriminatory model, reflecting their characteristic presence in early-stage disease. A combined logistic regression model integrating all five differential genera achieved an AUC of 0.846 (95% CI: 0.747–0.944) for predicting advanced-stage NSCLC (supplementary Figure. 2). At the optimal threshold determined by the Youden index, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 64.5% and a specificity of 96.1%.

Differential genes and metabolic pathways in the BALF microbiome of early-stage versus advanced-stage NSCLC patients

Differential gene analysis was performed on the microbial taxa identified between the early-stage and advanced-stage NSCLC groups. Cross validation using LEfSe and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests consistently identified gmhD, rfaD, nudF, sfsA, and K09983 as differentially expressed genes, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests’ p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method (Table 2; Fig. 3). K09983, which represents a class of genes with conserved cross-species functional annotations, was significantly expressed in the early-stage group, though its function remains unclear. The genes gmhD, rfaD, nudF, and sfsA were significantly expressed in the advanced-stage group and are considered differential genes. gmhD and rfaD are responsible for synthesizing lipopolysaccharides [24], nudF and sfsA are involved in fungal cytokinesis [25] and bacterial maltose metabolism [26], respectively.

T-cell subset distributions in BALF from early-stage versus advanced-stage NSCLC patients

T lymphocytes are essential components of the tumor microenvironment and can influence the growth and differentiation of cancer cells [27]. Four immune markers were analyzed using t-test, with p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Flow cytometric analysis of lesion-derived BALF showed that the advanced-stage group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of total CD3⁺ T lymphocytes (q = 0.025) and CD8⁺ T cells (q < 0.001) than the early-stage group, whereas the percentages of CD4⁺ T cells (q < 0.001) and the CD4/CD8 ratio (q < 0.001) were markedly lower (Fig. 5). These findings demonstrate altered T cell subset distributions in advanced-stage NSCLC. The CD4/CD8 ratio, a metric frequently used in clinical settings, differed significantly between groups and may reflect stage-related changes in local immune cell composition.

Correlation analysis between differential microbial taxa, immune cells, and differential genes in BALF

Previous studies have shown that modulating the pulmonary microbiota can enhance cellular immunity and thereby elicit antitumor effects [9], motivating our investigation of correlations between microbial taxa and T cell subset distributions. Recent perspectives in lung cancer microbiome research argue that studies should not focus solely on taxa differing in abundance; instead, attention should be directed to interaction networks between microbes and host factors to identify genuine ‘keystone’ organisms whose high connectivity maintains pulmonary microbial homeostasis and merits functional validation [28]. Therefore, we performed the Spearman correlation analysis among differential microbial taxa, immune cell, and differentially expressed genes in early versus advanced-stage NSCLC and the p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Consistent with this view, although Bifidobacterium was not among the statistically differential genera between groups, its mean abundance was visibly elevated in the advanced-stage group (Fig. 1d). The analysis showed that Bifidobacterium abundance was negatively correlated with the proportion of CD4⁺ T cells and the CD4/CD8 ratio (q = 0.038; q = 0.015) but positively correlated with the percentages of CD3⁺ T cells and CD8⁺ T cells (q = 0.020; q = 0.010). Moreover, Bifidobacterium showed significant positive associations with the advanced-stage enriched microbial genes gmhD, rfaD, sfsA, and nudF (q = 0.009; q = 0.009; q = 0.009; q = 0.010), whereas it tended to be negatively associated with the early stage-enriched gene K09983 (q = 0.314) (Table 4). These multiple significant connections spanning immune cell subsets and stage-specific microbial genes identify Bifidobacterium as a highly connected taxon within the BALF ecosystem. We emphasize that these are exploratory correlations and do not establish causality. Bifidobacterium may be as a candidate keystone taxon for hypothesis-driven functional studies. (Fig. 6).

Discussion

A substantial body of evidence has established the tumor microenvironment as a complex network composed of neoplastic cells, stromal elements, immune infiltrates and non-cellular components. Within this ecosystem, the tumor associated microbiome, which was long overlooked, but is now emerging as a critical functional entity that commands intense investigative attention [29].The microbial community within tumor tissue constitutes an integral component of the tumor microenvironment and influences the development of tumors [30].

Across all enrolled NSCLC patients, Herminiimonas was markedly enriched at the genus level in BALF; however, its abundance did not differ between the early-stage and advanced- stage groups. This genus comprises Gram-negative rods that were first isolated from oligotrophic environments such as mineral water, groundwater, soil, and rock surfaces [31].To date, no definitive evidence indicates that this genus possesses virulence factors capable of directly invading pulmonary tissue. Previous gut microbiota studies employing either 16 S rRNA or 16 S rDNA sequencing have consistently detected Herminiimonas [32–34]. These findings suggest that Herminiimonas may enter the lower respiratory tract passively via inhaled air or aerosols as an environmentally derived background bacterium. It likely becomes established only when host immunity, local oxygen tension, or nutrient conditions are altered. All enrolled patients were diagnosed with lung cancer, and the shared airway microenvironment—characterized by chronic hypoxia and excessive mucus secretion—provided a similar ecological niche for Herminiimonas. This resulted in comparable abundances that lacked stage specificity. Taken together, we propose that Herminiimonas functions as an “environment-host shared neutralist” whose presence is independent of tumor stage.

Multiple previous studies have shown that, compared with healthy individuals, lung cancer patients exhibit reduced pulmonary microbial diversity and an enrichment of oral commensal genera such as Veillonella, Streptococcus, and Prevotella [21, 35–41]. Other investigations have also reported an enrichment of Clostridiaceae and Bacteroidaceae within lung tissues of the patients who suffer from lung cancer [42, 43]. These findings are all consistent with our results. Additionally, some researchers have reported an enrichment of Capnocytophaga in the BALF of lung cancer patients [37, 44, 45]. Zhang et al. reported enrichment of Enterobacter and Fusobacterium in BALF from lung cancer lesions, accompanied by decreased α-diversity [46], these same species were also detected in our dataset. Lee et al. likewise observed an enrichment of Firmicutes in lung cancer patients [35], however, conflicting results also exist, previous studies have reported a significant increase in Proteobacteria and a decrease in Firmicutes, along with marked differences in microbial composition between squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma tissues [8]. These two major taxa were not detected in the present study.

Beyond distinguishing lung cancer from healthy individuals, the pulmonary microbiota also influences cancer progression. Jin et al. demonstrated that tumor burden in lung cancer correlated closely with local airway bacterial abundance, but not with gut bacterial load. They isolated a bacterial pool from advanced lung adenocarcinoma lesions and instilled it intratracheally into mice at the onset of lung adenocarcinoma. This treatment markedly accelerated tumor growth. Concurrently, Sphingomonadaceae was enriched in the mice’s BALF, leading the authors to conclude that the local airway microbiota made a substantive contribution to lung cancer progression [38]. Evidence also indicates that the sputum microbiota differs significantly between stage I–III NSCLC patients and those with distant metastases. In the sputum of NSCLC patients with distant metastases, the relative abundances of Coriobacteriaceae, Actinomyces, Streptococcus, and Pseudomonas are markedly increased, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa being significantly enriched in the subgroup of brain metastasis group [47]. Our study likewise demonstrates enrichment of Sphingomonadaceae in the advanced-stage group. In addition, we newly identified significantly increased content of Barnesiella, Pedobacter, and unclassified Bacteroidaceae in the advanced-stage group, with concomitant reductions in their abundance during early-stage disease. Our findings align with those of Sun et al. [48], collectively confirming significant enrichment of Bacteroidetes in the advanced-stage group. Regarding microbial diversity between the early and advanced-stage NSCLC, Lu et al. observed decreased α-diversity in the sputum microbiota of NSCLC patients with distant metastases compared to stage I–III patients [47], this contrasts with our results, in which α-diversity was marginally higher in the advanced stage group. This discrepancy likely stems from differences in patient stratification and the type of microbiome specimen collected. Tsay et al. found that the lower airway microbiota of advanced-stage (IIIB–IV) lung cancer patients more closely resembled the oral flora, with enrichment of oral commensals such as Veillonella, Prevotella, and Streptococcus. These patients also had poorer RECIST responses. The authors validated this observation in an animal model; mice inoculated with V. parvula exhibited shortened survival and increased tumor burden [36]. We likewise detected enrichment of these oral taxa in NSCLC patients; however, their abundance did not differ significantly between the advanced-stage and early-stage groups. Collectively, these findings underscore that dysbiosis is associated with lung cancer progression.

Meanwhile, immune cells constitute a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, and the balance of the pulmonary microbiota is both maintained by and interacts with the resident immune cells. A series of animal experiments have demonstrated that modulating lung microbial composition can enhance cellular immunity against tumors [9]. Jin et al. showed that the lung tumor microbiota in mice can expand local γδ T17 cells, prompting these cells to secrete IL-17 and IL-22. These cytokines not only recruit pro-tumorigenic neutrophils but also directly stimulate tumor cell proliferation, thereby accelerating lung cancer progression [38]. Cheng et al. found that Prevotella and Veillonella were significantly elevated in BALF from stage IIIB–IV lung cancer lesions. Patients with high levels of these genera had markedly fewer CD8⁺ T cells in peripheral blood, indicating weakened antitumor immunity [21]. We observed a significant increase in total T lymphocytes and CD8⁺ T cell counts, along with a marked reduction in CD4⁺ T cell numbers, in the advanced-stage NSCLC group. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study populations: the previous work used murine models or patients with specifically high levels of selected taxa, and assessed peripheral blood rather than local immunity. Multiple comparative analyses have demonstrated that BALF is better at reflecting the local immune status of lung cancer than peripheral blood [20, 49, 50]. Therefore, we analyzed T cell subsets in BALF obtained directly from the tumor involved lobe to obtain an accurate picture of local cellular immunity. Our data show altered T cell subset distributions in advanced-stage NSCLC, characterized by elevated CD8⁺ T cell percentages and reduced CD4⁺ T cells. The significance of these compositional changes for antitumor immunity remains to be determined through functional studies. The CD4/CD8 ratio differed markedly between groups and may serve as a simple metric reflecting stage-associated immune profile alterations, though its functional implications require further investigation.

A notable limitation of this study is the restricted flow cytometry panel, which was limited to CD3, CD4, and CD8 surface markers. While this panel allowed quantification of major T cell subsets, it did not assess T cell functional status, exhaustion state, or regulatory T cell populations. Without markers for exhaustion (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3, TOX), cytotoxic function (Granzyme B, Perforin, IFN-γ), or regulatory T cells (CD25, FOXP3), our interpretation of immune status remains preliminary. Future studies incorporating multiplexed flow cytometry are essential to dissect the functional heterogeneity of T cell subsets in BALF and to determine whether the observed CD8⁺ T cell expansion in advanced-stage NSCLC represents active cytotoxicity or terminal exhaustion.

The interpretation of research results on bifidobacteria requires careful consideration of the background in existing literature. Most studies reporting the anti-tumor effects of bifidobacteria have focused on the gut microbiota [51–55], among which bifidobacterial species can play an anti-tumor role by promoting dendritic cell secretion of IL-12 [51], enhancing CD8⁺ T cell function [53, 55], and activating CD4+ Th1 cells through their metabolic product inosine [54]. However, Bifidobacterium is increasingly recognized as a genus with dual functions that are highly dependent on the environment. Several key differences must be considered: Firstly, our study detected Bifidobacterium in BALF from the tumor-involved lung lobe, not in the gut. The pulmonary and intestinal microbiomes represent distinct ecological niches with different community structures, metabolic activities, and host interactions. Bifidobacterium in the lung may originate from oropharyngeal aspiration or hematogenous spread rather than representing a resident commensal population, and its functional properties in this microenvironment may differ fundamentally from those in the gut. Secondly, recent evidence emphasizes that the anti-tumor effects of bifidobacteria require specific environments. In the absence of immunotherapy or pro-inflammatory signals, the metabolic product of bifidobacteria, inosine, may even inhibit immune responses [54]. Finally, our observation of Bifidobacterium enrichment in advanced-stage NSCLC is not unprecedented. Wang et al. [56] independently identified Bifidobacterium dentium and Bifidobacterium longum as significantly enriched in the BALF of lung cancer patients compared to benign controls. They incorporated both species into a high-performing diagnostic model (AUC = 0.931). This convergence across independent cohorts supports the biological relevance of pulmonary Bifidobacterium in lung cancer, distinct from its intestinal counterpart. Prior literature on Bifidobacterium in lung cancer is sparse and conflicting: gut-derived Bifidobacterium has been linked to both antitumor immunity [57] and smoke-promoted tumorigenesis [58] These differences emphasize that without considering anatomical location, strain identity, host genetics, and disease background. Bifidobacterium cannot be uniformly classified as “beneficial” or “harmful”.

Moreover, we propose Bifidobacterium as a candidate keystone taxon in the BALF microenvironment. A growing consensus holds that lung cancer microbiome studies should move beyond simple abundance comparisons and prioritize interaction networks to identify keystone taxa, the organisms whose ecological and functional influence exceeds their numerical representation [28]. This framework is particularly relevant for low-biomass pulmonary ecosystems, where minor taxa can exert disproportionate effects on community stability and host immunity. In our study, Bifidobacterium exemplifies this paradigm: although it did not achieve statistical significance in inter-group abundance comparisons, its mean level was visibly higher in advanced-stage patients (Fig. 1d), and it critically exhibited the strongest connectivity within the BALF microbe-immune-gene network. Specifically, Bifidobacterium abundance correlated significantly with four T cell subset parameters and four advanced-stage enriched microbial genes (gmhD, rfaD, sfsA, nudF), a breadth of associations unmatched by any differentially abundant genus. These findings position Bifidobacterium as a candidate keystone taxon whose stage-associated trend and high network centrality may reflect functional relevance beyond what abundance metrics alone capture. We caution that this designation remains hypothetical. Correlation does not imply causation, co-culture assays testing the effect of microbial components or metabolites on T-cell activation, proliferation, or exhaustion phenotypes and the validation in an independent patient cohort are essential next steps to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Unlike previous investigations that focused on microbiota differences between the lung cancer patients and healthy controls, the present study focused exclusively on NSCLC patients and examined the microbial community and local cellular immunity within BALF across different stages. We delineated stage-specific pulmonary microbial signatures at initial diagnosis, applied multiple complementary statistical filters to ensure robustness. More importantly, we provided a preliminary mechanistic framework linking airway microbes to cancer stage progression. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size of this pilot study (n = 57) limits statistical power and generalizability, our findings require validation in independent cohorts, and the larger, multi-ethnic cohorts are required. While public shotgun metagenomic BALF datasets with detailed TNM staging are currently unavailable, we identified PRJNA303190 (BioProject number), which is 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing of BALF from NSCLC patients, n = 31). Despite methodological differences (V3-V4 region vs. whole-genome shotgun), we performed a limited comparison at the genus level. Both our study and the cited study found an enrichment of Legionella in the advanced-stage group. In our study, Legionella showed a statistically significant difference in the Wilcoxon test but did not reach the threshold of LDA > 2 in the LEfSe analysis. In addition, the cited study also found an increase in Thermus in the advanced-stage group. This cross-platform comparison should be interpreted cautiously, given known biases in 16 S vs. shotgun taxonomic resolution. Second, although BALF sampled from the lung lobes invaded by tumors which can reflect the lesion associated microbiome better than from remote sites, the lavage inevitably includes adjacent non-malignant airway mucosa and therefore cannot perfectly mirror the intratumoral microbial milieu. We have recognized that definitive validation requires direct comparison with matched tumor tissue samples or integration with existing tumor microbiome datasets. Third, the marked difference in ECOG performance status between the early and advanced stage groups warrants careful interpretation. ECOG performance status co-varied with disease stage in an expected and clinically meaningful manner. We emphasize that this is not a methodological confounder but an intrinsic clinical attribute of disease stage. While this reflects real-world disease biology rather than methodological confounding, we cannot empirically disentangle whether observed microbial and immune associations are directly attributable to intratumoral processes or to stage-related systemic physiological deterioration captured by ECOG. This limitation is inherent to cross-sectional stage-comparison designs and can only be addressed through prospective cohort studies or experimental models that track the temporal evolution of these intertwined factors. Fourth, despite rigorous contamination controls, we acknowledge that complete elimination of background signals in low-biomass samples like BALF remains challenging. The inclusion of multiple negative controls and abundance filtering mitigates but does not fully exclude this risk. Fifth, we acknowledge that several potential confounding factors were not fully assessed in this study. Tumor burden (e.g., total tumor volume), systemic inflammatory status (e.g., C-reactive protein) and dietary habits, all of which can shape both gut and lung microbiota through the gut-lung axis, were not systematically recorded. The residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, and future prospective studies should incorporate comprehensive assessment of tumor burden metrics, inflammatory biomarkers, and dietary factors. Finally, we identified keystone taxa associated with stage and local immunity and proposed a plausible mechanism; however, functional validation remains to be performed in the next phase of this research program.

Conclusions

In this study, we delineated the microbial landscape of lesion-derived BALF from early versus advanced-stage NSCLC patients by quantitative metagenomic sequencing. We identified discriminating taxa, genes, and pathways, characterized the T cell subset distributions in the advanced-stage of NSCLC, and identified associations between Bifidobacterium, immune cell profiles, and cancer stage, providing hypotheses for future mechanistic studies on microbiota-driven NSCLC progression.
