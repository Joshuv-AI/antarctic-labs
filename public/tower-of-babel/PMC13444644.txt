AI-augmented MALDI-TOF MS screening reveals a high burden of undiagnosed monoclonal gammopathy in adult patients
Authors: Junda Huang, Zewen Li, Enzhong Chen, Guangqing Liang, Xin Zhao, Meng Lan, Shaocong Liang, Yifeng Ou, Mengxue Zou, Xuguang Bao, Yamei Lv, Zhencheng Fang, Hongwei Zhou, Nianyi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMonoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance is a common precursor of multiple myeloma, yet its prevalence and clinical distribution remain poorly defined due to the limited scalability of conventional electrophoretic workflows. Here, we developed an artificial intelligence-augmented matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) platform that integrates machine learning, rule-based detection of weak monoclonal signals and glycosylation assessment for automated M-protein screening. Following training and validation on 5,218 retrospective serum samples, the platform was deployed in a real-world cohort of 12,263 adult patients. Screening identified M-proteins in 7.5% of patients, reaching 10.1% among individuals aged ≥ 50 years, and revealed a substantial burden of previously undetected monoclonal protein abnormalities. Beyond hematologic disorders, M-protein positivity was associated with a broad spectrum of infectious, neoplastic, and metabolic conditions. These findings establish a scalable strategy for population-level M-protein screening and highlight adult patients as an underrecognized high-risk population that may benefit from systematic surveillance.

Introduction

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is a premalignant plasma cell disorder with a well-established risk of progression to multiple myeloma (MM) and related malignancies.1,2,3,4 Early detection prior to malignant transformation or end-organ damage enables risk-stratified monitoring and is associated with improved outcomes upon progression. Population-based evidence from Sweden and the United States consistently indicates a 13%–15% overall survival advantage for patients whose MGUS was identified prior to malignant transformation.5,6,7,8 Despite these benefits, the absence of systematic screening strategies results in most MGUS cases being detected incidentally, frequently after irreversible end-organ damage (e.g., renal failure or pathological fractures) has occurred.

The inpatient setting represents an important, yet underutilized, opportunity for systematic MGUS screening. Hospitalized patients often present with non-specific symptoms-such as unexplained cytopenias, renal impairment, or acute skeletal events, which can be manifestations of plasma cell dyscrasias.9,10,11 This makes the inpatient population “diagnostically enriched” for monoclonal gammopathies. Indeed, prevalence data12,13 indicate that while MGUS affects approximately 3%–5% of individuals aged ≥50 years in the general community,1 a limited-scale investigation in acute-care settings reported higher prevalence of 6.94%.12 However, because these clinical presentations often overlap with other common conditions, MGUS frequently goes unrecognized in the absence of targeted screening. A persistent clinical paradox exists: despite this concentrated risk, routine inpatient protocols rarely incorporate M-protein screening. This oversight leaves a vast reservoir of undiagnosed disease, leading to missed opportunities for early intervention. The consequences of this diagnostic gap extend beyond clinical outcomes to substantial economic strain. Health economic modeling suggests that the adherence to guideline-recommended screening yields a 2-fold downstream cost saving per dollar invested.14 In contrast, failure to detect disease before progression to MM triggers an exponential rise in healthcare expenditure, with excess annual costs for first-to third-line therapy reaching $46,024, $100,329, and $101,942, respectively.15

The central barrier to closing this gap lies in the practical limitation of the current diagnostic protocol, immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE). IFE is labor intensive, low throughput, and relatively costly, which make it impractical for large-scale screening, even in high-yield settings like tertiary-care hospitals.16,17,18,19 While advanced matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) platforms (e.g., Q-MASS-FIX and EXENT) offer superior diagnostic sensitivity, their reliance on immunoenrichment introduces significant reagent costs and workflow complexities that severely restrict population-level scalability.20,21,22 To bypass enrichment, several direct MALDI-TOF MS strategies have emerged, yet they face critical translational hurdles. Existing approaches often target low-molecular-weight regions (2–10 kDa) lacking etiological relevance to intact M-proteins23 or depend on subjective manual spectral interpretation, impeding automation.24,25 Most importantly, these pioneering studies remain heavily constrained by small-scale training cohorts, fundamentally lacking robust validation in large, unselected real-world populations.

To address this unmet need, we developed a high-throughput “dilute-and-shoot” MALDI-TOF MS screening strategy that directly analyzes serum samples without immunoenrichment. By eliminating antibody-dependent capture and multiple manual processing steps, our workflow substantially simplifies sample preparation and improves throughput. We further integrated a machine learning algorithm for automated spectral interpretation to achieve diagnostic performance comparable to IFE. This platform is fundamentally designed for scalability and speed, making it uniquely suited for evaluating large, high-risk cohorts where systematic screening was previously infeasible.

In this proof-of-concept study, we deployed this artificial intelligence (AI)-augmented MALDI-TOF MS platform to screen an unselected cohort of 12,263 adult patients. Our objective was to quantify the previously unrecognized burden of monoclonal gammopathy in the patient population and to elucidate its associations with specific comorbid conditions, thereby evaluating the platform’s utility for clinical risk stratification and its potential to redefine screening protocols in high-risk medical settings.

Results

Development and analytical validation of the AI-augmented MALDI-TOF MS platform

We developed a high-throughput platform that integrates fully automated MALDI-TOF MS with an AI-based algorithm for detecting serum M-proteins. The analytical core of the platform is a two-stage evaluation pipeline. First, a random forest classifier was trained on 5,218 manually curated serum spectrum, using six quantitative shape features (peak width, amplitude, and their ratios) extracted from Gaussian-fitted κ and λ light-chain peaks. The cohort selection and exclusion workflow for model development is summarized in Figure S1. This machine learning model was primarily designed to identify overt monoclonal peaks with distinct morphological deformation. During 10-fold cross-validation, the random forest classifier demonstrated robust performance in distinguishing κ-positive, λ-positive, and negative spectra, achieving an accuracy of 0.96, with per-class sensitivity exceeding 0.95 (Figure 2A; Table 1). Using the same input features and an identical 10-fold cross-validation scheme, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), and logistic regression models achieved accuracies of 0.94, 0.95, and 0.94, respectively (Tables S1–S3). To minimize false negatives, spectra classified as negative by the random forest model were subjected to a secondary rule-based analysis to identify weak monoclonal light-chain signals not captured by Gaussian-derived feature classification (Figure 2B). We rigorously validated the optimized pipeline. Analysis of purified immunoglobulin standards confirmed accurate light-chain detection, with patient-derived monoclonal light chains showing characteristically sharper peaks than polyclonal profiles (Figures S2A and S2B). In a blinded comparison with IFE, the platform identified all 752 IFE-positive samples (100% concordance) among 906 prospectively analyzed sera, including 695 with overt spectral abnormalities and 57 with subtle deviations (Table 2). Because the subtle abnormalities accounted for over half of those discordant results, we employed a two-part validation strategy to confirm they represented true biological signals rather than algorithmic noise. First, clinical verification of 47 samples with subtle spectral abnormalities showed that while serum protein electrophoresis (SPE) detected no M-protein (0 g/L), IFE and/or serum free light chain (sFLC) analysis provided independent evidence of monoclonality in 22 samples (46.8%) (Figure 2B; Table S4). Second, serial dilution experiments demonstrated the superior analytical sensitivity of AI-assisted MALDI-TOF MS. The platform resolved M-protein down to 0.14 g/L in a representative sample—where SPE and IFE failed—and paired comparisons across 30 patient samples confirmed its significantly higher maximum dilution factors and lower limits of detection overall (Figure S2C). These findings confirmed that the subtle abnormalities correspond to low-level monoclonal proteins below the detection threshold of conventional electrophoretic methods.

The platform further demonstrated robust analytical performance. The κ/λ ratio exhibited a stable, near-normal distribution in 1,004 M-protein-negative individuals (Figure S3A), and representative mass spectra from the λ and κ regions of 66 M-protein-negative serum samples are shown in Figure S3B. Analytical reproducibility was maintained across a wide concentration range, with coefficients of variations below 15% for κ/λ ratio and Mspike/total light chain (TLC) ratio (Figure S3C), and the detection performance remained unaffected by common clinical interferents (Figure S3D).

High prevalence of previously undiagnosed M-protein among adult patients aged ≥50 years

We prospectively applied this validated platform to screen an unselected cohort of 12,263 adult patients spanning a broad spectrum of disease categories (Figure 3A; Table S5). The cohort had a median age of 55 years (interquartile range [IQR], 40–67), and 50.5% were female. The algorithm identified M-protein positivity in 923 individuals (prevalence: 7.5% [95% CI: 7.1%–8.0%]). Stratification by age revealed a marked increase in prevalence with older age. The positivity rate was 3.4% (161/4,694) in patients aged <50 years, rising sharply to 10.1% (762/7,569) in those aged ≥50 years and reaching 14.0% (338/2,411) in individuals aged ≥70 years. Younger positive cases were predominantly characterized by subtle spectral abnormalities (128/161), whereas older positives included a higher proportion of overt monoclonal peaks (271/762), alongside subtle abnormalities (491/762) (Figure 3C).

The vast majority of screen-positive cases (921/923, 99.8%) had an M-protein concentration below 30 g/L and no clinical endpoints indicative of active malignancy, corresponding to monoclonal protein levels typically observed in MGUS. Two individuals showed M-protein concentrations of ≥30 g/L, corresponding to levels commonly observed in smoldering MM (Figure 3D).

To assess the biological and clinical validity of these AI-flagged signals, all 923 screen-positive samples underwent confirmatory SPE, and a subset (285/923, 30.9%) were further evaluated by IFE. This analysis confirmed that algorithm-detected signals, including subtle spectral abnormalities, corresponded to immunologically verifiable M-proteins. Notably, participants aged ≥50 years had higher SPE-quantified M-protein levels and higher IFE positivity rates than those aged <50 years (Table 3) across both detection categories. For instance, in the overt peak group, the mean M-protein concentration was 5.29 g/L in the older group versus 2.17 g/L in the younger group, with corresponding IFE positivity rates of 89.2% and 66.7%, respectively. Association between M-protein positivity and systemic comorbidities

Integration of screening results with comprehensive electronic health records data identified significant clinical correlates of M-protein positivity. Screen-positive status was associated with elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers, specifically high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), procalcitonin (PCT_F), and white blood cell (WBC) count (all p < 0.001; Figure 3E). To evaluate the independent relationship between the M-protein status and broad disease categories, we performed propensity score matching (1:2) on age and sex between the M-protein-positive and -negative participants. Logistic regression analysis within the matched cohorts revealed that M-protein positivity was independently associated with multiple disease categories. The strongest association was with pre-existing hematologic disorders (odds ratio [OR]: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.68–2.71; p < 0.001). Significant associations were also observed with infectious diseases (OR: 1.47; 95% CI: 1.13–1.91), endocrine/metabolic conditions (OR: 1.47; 95% CI: 1.24–1.73), neoplasms (OR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.10–1.56), digestive diseases (OR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.02–1.43), and genitourinary disorders (OR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.02–1.42) (Figure 3F). The comorbidity profile of M-protein-positive patients was distinct from that of the general patient cohort, underscoring a unique clinical signature associated with monoclonal gammopathies in this setting.

Discussion

This study establishes that an AI-augmented MALDI-TOF MS platform enables efficient, large-scale screening for monoclonal gammopathies, uncovering a substantial and previously underestimated disease burden in adult patients. By applying this high-sensitivity tool to an unselected cohort of 12,263 patients, we demonstrate its technical superiority over conventional electrophoresis and redefine the epidemiological landscape and clinical context of M-proteins in this high-risk setting.

The principal advancement of our platform lies in its enhanced sensitivity, which proved instrumental in redefining the scale of underdiagnosis. It identified M-proteins in 10.1% of individuals aged ≥50 years, with 89.5% of positive cases (682/762) harboring concentrations below the reliable quantitation limit of routine SPE. This finding starkly illustrates the systematic underdiagnosis inherent to current, electrophoresis-based clinical pathways.26 Beyond binary detection, our algorithm-derived quantitative M-spike/TLC ratio correlated strongly with systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., hsCRP and ferritin), even at low M-protein concentrations. This suggests that the low-level clonal expansion may coexist with and potentially be influenced by a pro-inflammatory state, challenging the classical perception of MGUS as a biochemically inert condition.27,28

The suitability of this platform for population-scale screening is supported by two key advantages. First, it overcomes the logistical and throughput limitations of IFE, which is labor intensive and difficult to scale, by providing an automated workflow integrating sample preparation and MALDI-TOF MS acquisition. Compared with previously reported MALDI-TOF MS approaches that rely on immunoenrichment or manual spectral interpretation, our “dilute-and-shoot” strategy further simplifies the workflow and improves scalability by eliminating affinity-capture steps and enabling fully automated analysis. Second, the AI-assisted spectral analysis improves detection sensitivity by identifying subtle monoclonal peaks that may be overlooked by conventional electrophoretic interpretations. These findings are supported by concordance with orthogonal clinical assays (IFE and sFLC) and serial dilution experiments. By enabling the detection of monoclonal clones that would otherwise be missed by standard electrophoretic techniques, the platform enhances analytical sensitivity and provides a robust framework for earlier identification of MGUS. This capability has important implications for both clinical practice and population-based screening, as it enables the detection of MGUS at a potentially earlier stage.

The observed prevalence of 10.1% is a transformative epidemiological finding, and it is more than double the rates reported in community-based cohorts. While age is a known risk factor,29 this stark disparity highlights that acute or chronic illness necessitating hospitalization is itself a major, independent risk amplifier for monoclonal gammopathies. The pattern of disease associations reinforces this paradigm. The strong link with hematologic disorders (OR: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.68–2.71) suggests shared pathogenic mechanisms. Furthermore, significant associations with infectious, neoplastic, and metabolic diseases coherently point toward a unifying pathophysiological backdrop of persistent immune activation and chronic antigenic stimulation.9 Therefore, M-protein in adult patients may often represent a biomarker of sustained immune dysregulation, rather than being an incidental finding.

These insights carry direct clinical implications. They provide a compelling rationale for viewing the patient setting as a strategic venue for targeted, risk-stratified screening. The operational scalability of our workflow (e.g., rapid turnaround and full automation) makes such a strategy feasible for high-volume clinical laboratories. Detecting these clones allows for the initiation of appropriate monitoring and can inform the management of associated comorbidities, potentially mitigating future complications.

In conclusion, we present and validate a high-throughput tool that transforms M-protein assessment from a low-throughput, confirmatory test toward a high-throughput, discovery-oriented screening assay. By revealing the substantial hidden burden and systemic disease links of monoclonal gammopathies in adult patients, our work provides a strong evidence base for integrating targeted screening into the care of this high-risk population. The next crucial steps involve prospective, outcome-driven trials to evaluate the clinical utility of this strategy and mechanistic studies to decipher the complex dialogue between emergent clones and the systemic disease environment.

Limitations of the study

Our study has limitations that outline essential future research directions. First, the single-center design necessitates external validation in other centers and multi-ethnic populations. Second, due to its cross-sectional design, this study could neither capture longitudinal changes in clonal persistence or burden nor establish subsequent disease progression. Accordingly, longitudinal follow-up to determine the persistence and clinical progression risk of the newly detected, often low-level monoclonal clones is essential for translating screening findings into clinical benefit. Third, while the algorithm performed excellently for typical cases, its generalizability to rare M-protein variants requires ongoing training with diverse datasets. Fourth, therapeutic monoclonal antibodies may potentially interfere with MALDI-TOF MS spectral interpretation in certain clinical contexts. Finally, the present workflow was primarily designed as a qualitative high-throughput screening strategy and does not provide precise quantification of the M-protein burden.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study consisted of two independent phases: (1) retrospective model training and 10-fold cross-validation, and (2) prospective real-world clinical deployment.

For model training, a retrospective cohort of 5,218 serum samples collected between October 2022 and July 2024 was used for model development and performance evaluation using 10-fold cross-validation. These samples were obtained from hospitalized patients who underwent routine clinical evaluation for suspected monoclonal gammopathy. All cases had available serum protein electrophoresis (SPE) and immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE) results, which were used to define ground-truth labels and to categorize samples into normal, κ-abnormal, or λ-abnormal groups (2,516 normal, 1,564 κ-abnormal, and 1,138 λ-abnormal). Inclusion criteria for this retrospective cohort were: (1) availability of sufficient residual serum for MALDI-TOF MS analysis, and (2) completion of both SPE and IFE as part of standard clinical workup. Samples with incomplete SPE/IFE results, insufficient serum volume, or incomplete clinical data were excluded. In addition, to preliminarily characterize the distribution of the κ/λ ratio in non-diseased individuals, a subset of 1,004 samples from the normal group was independently analyzed using the MALDI-TOF MS platform.

The prospective deployment cohort was completely independent of the training dataset and consisted of 12,263 consecutively enrolled adults (≥18 years) who presented to the outpatient, emergency, or inpatient departments between July 1 and December 1, 2023. This cohort represented an unselected real-world clinical screening population without prior enrichment for known monoclonal gammopathies. The primary inclusion criterion was the availability of sufficient residual serum for both MALDI-TOF MS analysis and confirmatory testing by IFE. Key exclusion criteria were: (1) a prior diagnosis of any plasma cell dyscrasia (e.g., MGUS, smoldering or symptomatic multiple myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinemia); (2) samples with improper storage, multiple freeze-thaw cycles, or severe hemolysis/lipemia/icterus that could interfere with mass spectrometry. For patients with multiple visits, only the first eligible sample was included. To further evaluate assay performance under blinded conditions, a randomly selected subset of 906 individuals from this cohort underwent parallel, blinded comparison between MALDI-TOF MS–based classification and IFE results.

This observational study was conducted at Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University. The retrospective cohort used for model training and 10-fold cross-validation (n=5,218) and the real-world clinical deployment cohort (n=12,263) were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee under protocols 2022-KY-135-03 and 2023-KY-145-02, respectively. As anonymized residual serum samples were used, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Method details

Clinical diagnosis classification and ICD-11 coding

All clinical diagnoses were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11).30 ICD-11 classifications were independently assigned by at least two trained and qualified staff members who reviewed discharge diagnoses from the electronic medical record system. Relevant laboratory data were additionally evaluated to support and verify the diagnostic assignments. In cases of discrepancy, a third trained reviewer performed independent adjudication to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Reference standard testing: Serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation

SPE was performed using the V8 capillary electrophoresis system (Helena Laboratories). IFE, serving as the diagnostic reference standard, was conducted on the SPIFE TOUCH platform (Helena Laboratories). All IFE gels were independently reviewed by two senior laboratory technologists. Any discrepancies were adjudicated by a third expert reviewer to reach a final consensus diagnosis (positive or negative for monoclonal protein). These analyses were completed within 48 hours of sample collection.

Automated serum sample preparation

This workflow is based on a “dilute-and-shoot” serum preparation strategy without immunoenrichment or immunocapture. For high-throughput MALDI-TOF MS analysis, sample preparation was fully automated using a Biomek i5 liquid handling workstation according to a standardized protocol. Briefly, 2 μL of residual serum was diluted in 16 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by addition of 2 μL of an internal standard consisting of recombinant Burkholderia pseudomallei Hcp1 protein (∼20.9 kDa) prepared in PBS at a final concentration of 0.4 mg/mL, used for m/z calibration and spectral alignment. To dissociate immunoglobulins into their constituent heavy and light chains, the mixture was reduced by introducing 2 μL of 400 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) supplemented with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The final reaction mixture was subsequently incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature. The denatured sample was then mixed with sinapic acid matrix solution (10 mg/mL in 30:70 acetonitrile: water) and spotted onto a target plate.

MALDI-TOF MS analysis

Mass spectrometry was performed using an IntelliBio MALDI-TOF MS system operating in positive linear mode over a mass range of m/z 5,000–100,000. Instrument parameters were configured as follows: acceleration voltage, 20 kV; laser energy, 15 μJ; pulse frequency, 3000 Hz; focus mass, 23,100 Da; Spectrum Size, 0.4 mm; Motion Scanning Speed, 1.00 mm/sec; Detector Voltage, -0.6 kV; Extraction Voltage, -3.25 kV; Source Voltage, -20 kV; Absolute Input Scale, 1.000 V; Sample Rate, 800.00 ps. Each spectrum was generated from the accumulation of 800 laser shots to improve signal stability and signal-to-noise ratio.

To ensure mass axis accuracy and inter-plate consistency throughout high-throughput acquisition, external calibration was routinely performed using a three-protein standard mixture consisting of cytochrome C (m/z 12,362; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# C8857), myoglobin (m/z 16,952; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A8971), and aldolase (m/z 39,212; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A9096). Analytical robustness and batch stability were further monitored by including three quality control (QC) serum samples (Bio-Rad) on each plate. An analytical run was considered acceptable only when the measured κ/λ light chain ion ratio of these QC samples remained within 2.0 ± 0.1. Inter-batch reproducibility was additionally assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV), which was maintained <5% throughout the study.

AI-assisted spectral analysis and M-protein detection algorithm

We developed a dedicated software pipeline for automated M-protein detection based on MALDI-TOF MS spectral pattern recognition. The analytical workflow consisted of three sequential steps: (1) detection of overt monoclonal peaks using a random forest classifier, (2) identification of subtle spectral abnormalities through rule-based analysis, and (3) assessment of glycosylated light-chain–associated peaks.

Machine learning classification of overt monoclonal peaks

Gaussian fitting of κ- and λ-region light-chain peaks

Raw MALDI-TOF MS spectra were processed through baseline correction, noise reduction, and normalization to an internal standard, whereas an external multi-protein calibration mixture applied to each MALDI target plate was used for mass-axis calibration and cross-plate alignment to ensure consistency in m/z assignment across runs. Following preprocessing, monoclonal light-chain candidate peaks were identified within predefined κ (m/z 23,100–24,600) and λ (m/z 22,200–23,100) regions by selecting the local maximum intensity within each interval.

To robustly characterize peak morphology, each candidate peak was further modeled using Gaussian function fitting. Specifically, for a given peak apex at (xm,ym), spectral points within a narrow window around the apex (defined as x∈[xm-0.001xm,xm+0.001xm]) were extracted for model fitting. Let these points be denoted as (xi,yi), the Gaussian model was defined as:(Equation 1)y=C∗A∗exp⁡(−(x−xm)22σ2)where x denotes m/z, y denotes signal intensity, xm is the peak apex location, σ represents peak width, and C is the amplitude scaling parameter. The normalization constant was defined as A=12πσ, and Cwas parameterized as C=ym/A.

Parameter optimization

To obtain robust estimates of σand C, an optimization procedure was performed. For each candidate peak, a grid-search–based parameter estimation strategy was applied to minimize the residual error between the observed spectrum and the fitted Gaussian curve. Specifically, the optimal parameters were determined by minimizing the sum of squared residuals:(Equation 2)min⁡σ∑i=1nyi−C·A·exp⁡−xi−xm22σ22

The value of σyielding the minimum residual was selected as the optimal peak width, and the corresponding C value was retained as the amplitude descriptor. This procedure was independently applied to both κ- and λ-region peaks, generating four primary parameters: σκ,Cκ,σλ,Cλ. In addition, ratio-based features (σλ/σκ, Cλ/Cκ) were computed to capture relative asymmetry between light-chain distributions.

Feature construction and classification model

In total, six peak-shape–derived features were used as input variables for machine learning–based classification: σκ,σλ,Cκ,Cλ,σλ/σκ,Cλ/Cκ. These features were used to train a random forest (RF) classifier implemented in MATLAB. The RF model was trained on 5,218 labeled spectra, including 2,516 normal samples, 1,564 κ-abnormal samples, and 1,138 λ-abnormal samples, for classification into three categories: normal, κ-abnormal, and λ-abnormal. To further evaluate robustness and model generalizability, additional machine learning algorithms, including k-nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), and logistic regression, were implemented and evaluated using the same feature set and validation framework.

The performance of the three-class machine learning model was rigorously assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. Model efficacy was quantified by per-class recall (sensitivity) and overall classification accuracy. For each class i, per-class recall was defined as the proportion of correctly classified samples relative to the total number of true cases in that class:(Equation 3)Recalli=TPiTPi+FNiwhere TPi and FNi represent the numbers of true positives and false negatives for class i, respectively. Overall classification accuracy was calculated as the total number of correctly classified samples across all classes divided by the total number of samples.

Rule-based detection of subtle monoclonal peak

The random forest classifier based on six Gaussian-derived peak-shape features was designed to identify overt monoclonal peaks. Spectra initially classified as “normal” subsequently underwent rule-based analysis to detect weak monoclonal light-chain signals not captured by Gaussian-derived feature classification. This secondary analysis focused on four characteristic spectral patterns within the κ and λ light-chain regions. (1) Left-shoulder weak monoclonal detection (λ region): The algorithm first identified the primary λ apex (xλ) within the m/z 22,400–23,100 range. The ascending slope on the left side of the λ peak was then examined for abnormal shoulder-like protrusions. A potential weak monoclonal signal was flagged when the expected smooth monotonic increase of the spectral curve was interrupted by a continuous intensity decrease spanning at least 25 consecutive data points. (2) Intermediate weak peak detection (between λ and κ apices): The trough region between the λ and κ peaks was analyzed to identify hidden local maxima suggestive of low-intensity monoclonal signals. Candidate peaks were identified using a “rise-and-fall” pattern recognition strategy. Specifically, within the 25 data points preceding a candidate apex, at least 15 points were required to show increasing intensity, while within the following 25 data points, at least 15 points were required to show decreasing intensity. (3) Right-shoulder weak monoclonal detection (κ region): To detect subtle peaks masked by the κ descending slope (from the κ apex xmax to the local minimum xmin), we implemented a two-stage screening and verification process: (i) Candidate Screening: We first calculated a “Peak Sharpness Index” for each data point. This metric was derived by determining the vertical intensity difference between a point and its n-th neighbors (where n represents the peak width index, defined as the distance to the nearest point with a higher intensity). The points were ranked by their Sharpness Index, and the top two candidates were selected for quantitative verification. (ii) Slope Verification (k value): To rigorously distinguish signal from noise, the intensity was normalized (maximum set to 1.0). For each candidate, we calculated the average linear slope over a 50-point sliding window for both the upstream (k1) and downstream (k2) directions. The final slope score was defined as k = max(k1, k2). Candidates with a score k > 0.00072621 were confirmed as weak monoclonal signals. (4) Apex weak monoclonal assessment (λ and κ apices): To capture low-abundance monoclonal proteins overlapping with the primary peaks, the specific shapes of the λ and κ apices were analyzed. Using the same Normalized Slope Analysis described in Step 3 [calculation of k = max(k1, k2) after normalization], primary apices exceeding the slope threshold of 0.00072621 were re-classified as weak monoclonal positives.

Calculation of upstream and downstream slope metrics (k1 and k2): After intensity normalization (maximum intensity within the region of interest set to 1.0), the average linear slope around a candidate peak apex at position xmwas calculated over a fixed window of 50 data points.

The upstream slope (k1) was defined as:(Equation 4)k1=150∑i=050I(xm−i)−I(xm−i−1)xm−i−(xm−i−1)

The downstream slope (k2) was defined as:(Equation 5)k2=150∑i=050I(xm+i+1)−I(xm+i)(xm+i+1)−xm+iwhere I(x)denotes the normalized spectral intensity at mass-to-charge position x. The final slope score was defined as: k=max⁡(k1,k2). This metric captures the maximal local sharpness of the peak and was used to distinguish weak monoclonal signals from background fluctuations.

Detection and experimental validation of glycosylated light chains

Light chain glycosylation (25,000-27,000 Da) was assessed using a similar approach: potential peaks were first located based on local prominence, and then evaluated using the 50-point slope analysis. Peaks with a normalized slope exceeding 0.0016311 were flagged as potential glycosylation-associated signals and subsequently evaluated by PNGase F digestion. Briefly, 5 μL of serum was mixed with 1 μL of 10× Glycoprotein Denaturing Buffer and heated at 95–100°C for 10 min to denature immunoglobulins and expose potential N-linked glycosylation sites. After cooling, 2 μL of 10× GlycoBuffer 2, 2 μL of 10% NP-40, and 1 μL of PNGase F were added, and the reaction volume was adjusted to 20 μL with nuclease-free water. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Glycosylation was considered present when the abnormal high-mass shoulder or broad peak markedly decreased or disappeared after PNGase F treatment, with restoration of a typical κ- or λ-light-chain peak pattern.

Final M-protein classification

A final M-protein-positive result was assigned if any of the following criteria was met: (i) identification of an overt monoclonal peak by the random forest classifier; (ii) detection of a subtle monoclonal spectral abnormality by the rule-based analysis; or (iii) identification of a glycosylated monoclonal light-chain signal subsequently confirmed by PNGase F digestion. All algorithm-positive results and a random subset of algorithm-negative results were flagged for manual review by a technologist blinded to the IFE result.

Propensity score–matched logistic regression analysis

To evaluate the association between M-protein positivity and major disease categories while reducing confounding by demographic differences, propensity score matching was performed between M-protein–positive and M-protein–negative participants using age and sex as matching variables. Nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was applied at a 1:2 ratio, whereby each M-protein–positive participant was matched to two M-protein–negative participants. Following matching, logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between M-protein positivity and major disease categories derived from electronic health records. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) and MATLAB. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages), and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Machine learning model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. For disease association analyses, propensity score matching was performed using age and sex as matching variables, followed by logistic regression analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Sample sizes and significance thresholds are provided in the corresponding figure legends (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Schematic illustrations were created using BioRender and further refined using Adobe Illustrator 2021.
