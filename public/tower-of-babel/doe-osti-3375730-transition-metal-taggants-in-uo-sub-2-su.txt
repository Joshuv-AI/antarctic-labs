 ABSTRACT

The incorporation of transition metals into nuclear fuel has gained attention both to improve fuel
properties and as a possible nuclear forensics tool. Recent experimental studies by Ulrich et al.1 and
Adorno Lopes et al.2 have investigated Ni and Fe as candidate transition metal dopants for potential
nuclear forensics purposes and found that there is minimal alteration to key UO2 fuel properties. In the
present work, we performed density functional theory (DFT) investigations into possible defect structures
for Ni and Fe incorporation. The dynamic stability of these defect structures was validated by calculating
the phonon density of states. We found that transition metal incorporation likely occurs via substitution at
U sites in the fluorite crystal structure with a nearby O vacancy for charge-balancing, which agrees with
experimentally proposed structures. Additionally, this defect structure does not cause long-range
alterations to the crystal parameters—an important consideration for use as a nuclear fuel taggant. Future
needed work involves computational investigation of additional defect concentrations using larger
supercells, additional transition metal charge states, and thermal effects. Such investigations can be
carried forward into sintering models for a more complete understanding of the suitability of Ni and Fe as
fuel taggants.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Assessment methods used for tracing nuclear material origins are a longstanding interest in the nuclear
forensics community. As nuclear reactors gain attention as possible major contributors to meeting ever-
increasing energy demands, methods for deterring material diversion and identifying the origin of
material found outside regulatory control are also gaining importance. One possible approach for
provenance determination gaining attention is the addition of taggants to nuclear fuel3, 4—that is,
intentionally adding specific isotopic ratios of chemical dopants to the fuel matrix during manufacture.5-8
Special considerations are necessary in identifying taggant candidates for further investigation. In
addition to persisting in the UO2 lattice throughout the fuel life cycle and being measurable using routine
techniques, a taggant must not substantially affect the fuel’s production procedures or its safety or
performance. Ideally, taggants would even improve these properties.1 For example, Cr has been
extensively studied as a UO2 fuel dopant owing to its reported increase to grain size compared to undoped
UO2, thereby allowing higher fuel burnup.9-20 To avoid costly reviews and approvals, an ideal taggant
would also fall within existing fuel composition standards.21

Although Cr has been extensively studied, other 3d transition metals (TMs), such as Fe and Ni, have not
been subject to close examination as dopants in UO2. Recent investigations have shown that Ni positively
affects grain size at low concentrations (up to 500 ppm), but higher concentrations reduce grain size
relative to undoped UO2.2 Iron has also been found to reduce grain size compared to undoped UO2 at all
concentrations investigated (up to 3,000 ppm).1 Both elements introduce modest UO2 lattice changes,
although Raman analyses support retention of the ordered fluorite crystal structure in both cases.1, 2 Nickel
doping up to 500 ppm resulted in small lattice contractions, whereas lattice parameter changes with Fe
were dependent on doping concentrations.1, 2

To provide additional insight into the microstructural effect of Ni and Fe taggants on UO2 fuels, we used
DFT to investigate possible taggant incorporation sites in UO2. We examined the commonly investigated
interstitial and substitutional sites as well as coupled defect sites, such as substitution with a nearby
vacancy and multiple TM substitutions at a single U site. We compare unit cell volume changes with
recent experimental measurements and also calculate the phonon density of states (PDOS) to inform the
dynamic stability of each site.

 2. METHODS

We used DFT as implemented in Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.4)22-25 to model the spin-
polarized electronic structure of pristine and TM-doped UO2. VASP parameters were tested for
convergence using the unit cell, whereas all production calculations used a 2  2  2 supercell to reduce
interactions of defects with their periodic images. This resulted in cells of approximately 11  11  11 Å
in the production calculations. The exchange–correlation functional of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzhoff
modified for solids (PBEsol)26 was combined with projector augmented–wave (PAW)27, 28
pseudopotentials to describe the interatomic interactions. This choice returned a unit cell volume only
slightly larger than the experimental value: 165.8 Å3 for the DFT system compared with 163.5 Å3 from
experiments.29 The plane-wave basis set was expanded to 800 eV, and the electron occupations were
described using the Gaussian smearing method with a smearing width of 0.2 eV for all relaxations. The
Brillouin zone was sampled using a Γ-centered k-point grid with a spacing of 0.35 Å−1, equivalent to a 4 
4  4 grid with 18 k-points in the pristine UO2 supercell. All coordinates and lattice constants, initially
taken from the work of Desgranges et al.,29 were allowed to fully relax until forces between atoms were
less than 0.01 eV/Å and the electronic energy was converged to less than 1  10−6 eV. Additional details
about computational benchmarks in the present work are available in Appendix A.

Effective Hubbard +U corrections of 4.0, 4.5, and 6.0 eV were applied to U, Fe, and Ni atoms,
respectively, using Dudarev’s method based on previous values in literature.30-36 Although the Hubbard
correction improves the electronic description of insulators, it can result in relaxation to metastable
electronic states. We attempted to ramp the +U values, as suggested by Meredig et al., to avoid relaxation
to a metastable state;37 however, we encountered significant electronic convergence issues that prohibited
the use of this approach. We have not explicitly accounted for the possibility of metastable states in the
present work; previously, Miskowiec showed that for UF4, the electronic analog of UO2, the primary
difference between the ground and metastable states was the shape of the valence f orbitals in the
electronic density of states.38 Additionally, Dorado et al. found minimal differences between the atomic
structures of ground and metastable states in UO2.32 However, both authors reported electronic states
much higher in energy than the lowest energy electronic state found in their respective works (0.8 eV by
Miskowiec38 and 3.4 eV by Dorado et al.32). As such, we provide the relative energies of the various
defect structures (Appendix B, Table B1) but have omitted a comparison of the energetics of the defect
systems, and most of our discussion is focused on the structural implications of the defects. In support of
a structural investigation of TM taggant incorporation, we have calculated phonons using the finite
displacement method where displacement structures were determined using Phonopy,39, 40 and forces were
obtained from VASP.

3.2 TRANSITION METAL-DOPED UO2

In the following sections we consider several possible incorporation sites for Fe and Ni TM dopants
(Figure 2). First, we consider the often-studied interstitial (Figure 2b) and substitution (Figure 2c) defects.
These defects do not allow for charge-balancing in the considered systems without changes to the
oxidation state of nearby U atoms.1 In pristine UO2, U takes a +4 charge, whereas O takes a −2 charge.
TMs can assume a variety of charge states. Here, Fe can assume +2 and +3 states, and Ni can assume +1,
+2, +3, and +4 states, although Ni is most commonly found in the +2 state. Because both Fe and Ni can
adopt +2 charges, we proceed with this assumption. Thus, in the interstitial defect, in which a TM is
added to the UO2 lattice without modifying the number of U and O atoms, the cell could be charge-
balanced by reduction of two U sites from +4 to +3. Likewise, substitution at a U site can be charge-
balanced by oxidation of two U sites from +4 to +5. However, we also consider three defects that afford
charge-balancing without the need for redox of neighboring U atoms. Two are TM substitutions at U sites
with nearby O vacancies (Figures 2d and 2e). Although the defects are generally the same, two different
vacancy locations were considered: nearest neighbor coordination sphere, referred to occasionally as
substitution with vacancy 1, and second nearest neighbor coordination sphere, referred to as substitution
with vacancy 2. Additionally, we consider a cluster defect, in which two TM atoms are substituted at a U
site (Figure 2f). We discuss the local and nonlocal structural changes and vibrational properties of each
defect site in the following sections.

3.2.1 Interstitial Doping

The fluorite UO2 structure has only one high-symmetry interstitial site, and Fe and Ni intercalation at this
site causes an increase in the lattice volume (Table 1). For Fe, the lattice constants of the supercell are
11.02  10.94  11.02 Å (Appendix B, Table B1), changes of Δa = 0.02 Å, Δb = 0.04 Å, and Δc = 0.01 Å
relative to the undoped system. For Ni, the lattice constants of the supercell are 11.00  10.93  11.01 Å
(Appendix B, Table B1) (Δa = 0.00 Å, Δb = 0.04 Å, and Δc = 0.00 Å [Table 1]). The U–O distances in
the interstitially doped UO2 structures vary slightly from the undoped case. For both Fe and Ni, the
presence of the intercalated dopant causes the nearest neighbor U atoms to move slightly outward, leading
to a subtle increase in U–O bond lengths near the interstitial and decreased bond lengths away from the
defects. The degree of U–O bond length variation differs between the two TMs because of their
difference in atomic radius: U–O bonds with the Fe interstitial defect are 2.34–2.44 Å (Δr = −0.03 to
+0.07 Å), whereas those with the Ni interstitial defect are 2.36–2.39 Å (Δr = −0.01 to +0.02 Å). The
behavior difference of the U–O bonds extends from the difference in U–TM distances: the U–Fe distances
are 2.83-2.84 Å, whereas in the Ni-doped case they are shorter, at 2.76-2.78 Å. Thus, because the U–Ni
distances are shorter, the U environment in the Ni-doped case is distorted less, resulting in a smaller effect
on the U–O distances near the Ni defect compared to Fe doping.

The PDOS of the interstitially doped UO2 is shown in Figure 3. The PDOS for both TM dopants at the
interstitial site show imaginary phonons, depicted as negative values in the PDOS plots in Figure 3. The
Fe-doped system has low-density imaginary modes across a wide frequency range. Inspection of the
phonon band structure (Appendix B, Figure B1) reveals that there are only a few imaginary bands that are
highly dispersive across the Brillouin zone. The imaginary bands for the Ni interstitial are flat with no
dispersion (Appendix B, Figure B1). The eigenvectors on atoms with the largest displacement magnitudes
for the largest frequency imaginary modes at the Γ point for both TMs are provided in Appendix B,
Figure B2 and show that extensive atomic motion would be needed to reduce the forces near the defect.

 5

Such extensive atomic motion would likely remove the TM atom from the interstitial site, so we did not
to pursue further optimization of the structure via displacement of atoms to remove the imaginary modes.

 Figure 3. PDOS of (left) Fe and (right) Ni interstitially doped UO2.

Because of the symmetry of the fluorite structure, all the U atoms in UO2 are equivalent, which means
there is only one unique substitution site. Substitution of a U atom with a TM leads to a slight contraction
of the lattice. The final relaxed supercell lattice constants are 10.94  10.93  10.93 Å (Δa = −0.06 Å, Δb
= 0.04 Å, and Δc = −0.08 Å [Table 1]) for Fe doping and 10.94  10.94  10.91 Å (Δa = −0.07 Å, Δb =
0.05 Å, and Δc = −0.09 Å [Table 1]) for Ni doping. This result is opposite to the expansion observed for
interstitial doping, as are the changes to the U–O distances. Focusing on the nearest neighbor U
polyhedra, the U–O distances toward the TM defect are shortened, whereas the U–O distances away from
the TM are elongated. The U–O distances range from 2.28 to 2.41 Å (Δr = −0.09 Å to +0.04 Å) for Fe
substitution and 2.29 to 2.43 Å (Δr = −0.08 Å to +0.06 Å) for Ni substitution. In the substitution defect,
the TM takes the place of a U atom and has an O coordination sphere around it. The TM–O distances are
2.38 and 2.39 Å for Fe and Ni, respectively, which is only slightly longer than the U–O distance in
undoped UO2 (2.37 Å). The qualitative changes in bond length are reasonable when considering the
difference in atomic radius between the TMs and U: because the TMs are smaller the neighboring atoms
move closer to the substitutional site compared with U occupation in the undoped case.

The presence of imaginary phonon modes in the PDOS for the substitutionally doped systems provided in
Figure 4 show that, like the interstitial defects, the structures of the substitutional defects are not
dynamically stable. The phonon band structures (Appendix B, Figure B3) show that the Fe defect has
imaginary bands with significant dispersion; the Ni defect modes are relatively flat, like in the interstitial
case. The eigenvectors of the largest frequency imaginary modes are provided in Appendix B, Figure B4.

3.2.2 Substitutional Doping with Nearest Neighbor O Vacancy

By creating an O vacancy near the substitution site, the UO2 system can achieve charge-balancing without
requiring the U sites to change oxidation state assuming the TMs take on a charge of +2. When the O
vacancy is placed in the nearest neighbor coordination sphere, the TM coordinates closer to the side of the
cubic TM–O polyhedral opposite the vacancy. This allows the TM to bind to four O atoms with shorter
TM–O distances versus the regular substitution structure (Figure 5). Without nearest neighbor O
vacancies, the Fe–O and Ni–O distances are 2.38–2.39 Å, but with the nearest neighbor O vacancies these
distances decrease to 2.04–2.08 Å and 1.94–2.00 Å for Fe–O and Ni–O, respectively. The O vacancy and
TM–O interactions lead to larger variations in the U–O bond lengths compared to the other defects. For
Fe, the U–O bond lengths range from 2.25 to 2.55 Å (Δr = −0.12 Å to +0.18 Å relative to undoped),
whereas for Ni the U–O bond lengths range from 2.26 to 2.50 Å (Δr = −0.11 Å to +0.13 Å). These
structural changes result in a slight overall contraction of the UO2 cell compared to the undoped case
(Table 1). Fe doping results in supercell dimensions of 10.96  10.98  10.97 Å, whereas Ni doping
results in supercell dimensions of 10.96  10.96  10.97 Å (Appendix B, Table B1).

3.2.3 Substitutional Doping with Second Nearest Neighbor O Vacancy

In addition to creating an O vacancy in the nearest neighbor O shell around the substituted U site, we also
placed a vacancy at the second nearest neighbor O site. When the distance between the TM and charge-
balancing vacancy is increased by moving the vacancy to the second nearest neighbor sphere, the volume
increases compared to vacancy incorporation at the nearest neighbor location (Table 1). The volume of
the cell with substituted doping and second nearest neighbor O vacancy is slightly larger than the volume
of pristine UO2. The relaxed lattice constants are 10.99  10.97  10.97 Å and 10.98  10.96  10.98 Å
for Fe and Ni, respectively. The UO2 lattice also shows some distortions arising from the defect
(Appendix B, Figure B7). The U–O bond lengths range from 2.22 to 2.61 Å (Δr = −0.15 Å to +0.24 Å
relative to pristine) for Fe and from 2.23 to 2.57 Å (Δr = −0.14 Å to +0.20 Å relative to pristine) for Ni.

The PDOS for both TMs substituted at a U site with second nearest neighbor O vacancy show
dynamically stable structures (Figure 7). Although small imaginary modes are calculated for both TM
defects (<3 cm−1), the eigenvectors reveal that these modes are acoustic in nature (Appendix B, Figures
B8 and B9). In addition to the presence of high-frequency shoulders as in the nearest neighbor vacancy
case, peaks 40–50 cm−1 above the pristine UO2 PDOS edge are evident with second nearest neighbor O
vacancies. These features arise from vibration of a significantly displaced O atom in the first coordination
sphere below the O vacancy (Figure 8).

3.2.4 Cluster Substitution

If the TMs take on a charge of +2, charge-balancing can also be achieved by substituting two TM taggant
atoms for a single U atom, which we refer to as a cluster defect. We find that the closer the two TM atoms
bind to one another, the longer the nearest TM–O bonds are. Specifically, the Fe–Fe bond is 2.56 Å, and
the nearest neighbor Fe–O bonds are 1.91–1.92 Å. The Ni–Ni bond is shorter, at 2.31 Å, but the nearest
neighbor Ni–O bonds are 2.00 Å. This is a logical finding because as the TMs move toward each other,
they inherently move away from the O coordination sphere around the site. The U–O bonds are similar
for the TMs, ranging from 2.30 to 2.43 Å (Δr = −0.07 Å to +0.06 Å relative to pristine) for the Fe cluster
defect and from 2.29 to 2.41 Å (Δr = −0.08 Å to +0.04 Å relative to pristine) for the Ni cluster defect. The
qualitative volume change relative to pristine UO2 differs between the TMs. With Fe, the volume
increases to 165.87 Å3 (ΔV = +0.84 Å3), whereas the Ni cluster defect contracts the volume to 164.80 Å3
(ΔV = −0.22 Å3).

The vibrational response also differs between the two TMs. Figure 9 shows that the PDOS of UO2
containing an Fe cluster substituted for a U has no significant imaginary modes: the imaginary modes that
are present are small (<3 cm−1) and acoustic in nature (Appendix B, Figure B10). In contrast, the Ni
cluster defect has a relatively flat imaginary band at 63 cm−1 (Appendix B, Figure B11). There is a high-
frequency peak 25 cm−1 above the pristine UO2 PDOS edge with the Fe cluster defect that arises from
phonons on the eight TM-bound O atoms.

 4. DISCUSSION

Recent experimental studies have investigated Fe and Ni incorporation into UO2.1, 2 By blending Fe2O3
into UO2, Ulrich et al. investigated several concentrations of Fe-tagged UO2.1 Adorno Lopes et al.
performed a similar study by blending NiO with UO2 at multiple taggant concentrations.2 Notably, both
studies found limited TM taggant solubility into the UO2 lattice, while the remaining concentration of
taggants resided in secondary phases. Specifically, Ulrich et al. observed white precipitates in their Fe-
tagged UO2 identified as metallic Fe via energy dispersive spectroscopy,1 and the Ni solubility limit was
found by Adorno Lopes et al. to be approximately 500 ppm in the sintered pellet, though the identity of
this phase was not available.2

The changes to the lattice constants and, consequently, the volume, differed substantially between the two
TM taggants. Ulrich et al. reported a possible small lattice contraction at low concentrations (250 ppm
Fe), although the change was smaller than the uncertainty of the measurement; expansion at intermediate
concentrations (500 ppm Fe); and more significant contraction at the highest concentration (3,000 ppm
Fe). A slight expansion was also evident at 1,000 ppm Fe but was within the experimental uncertainty.
Adorno Lopes et al. found a consistent decrease in lattice volume with taggant concentrations up to
1,000 ppm Ni, which was the highest concentration examined in their study. However, the total volume
change for the Ni-tagged system was substantially smaller than it was for the Fe-tagged system.

Figure 10 compares the percent volume change of the experimentally Fe- and Ni-tagged UO2 with each
defect structure investigated in the present work. Note that the experimental volume changes cannot be
compared directly to the computational volume changes because of the significant differences in taggant
concentration. Because of the size of the supercell in the present work, the defect concentration is twice
that of the largest experimentally Fe-tagged case and six times that of the Ni-tagged experimental system.

Without experimental validation, it is unknown how the lattice would change at higher concentrations.
Because of the differences in taggant concentrations between experimental and computational work, no
defect sites can be ruled out based on lattice changes from TM taggants alone.

 Figure 10. Comparison of predicted volume changes for each defect type from this work with experimental
 volume change ranges reported in Ulrich et al.1 and Adorno Lopes et al.2

Based on the phonon analysis, our work shows that simple interstitial and substitution defects are unlikely
to be dynamically stable sites for Fe and Ni incorporation in UO2. Although these defect structures could
afford charge-balancing through redox of neighboring U atoms, this scenario appears unlikely based on
the present findings. In fact, charge-balancing by defect coupling is more favorable from a dynamics
perspective.

We find substitution with first and second nearest neighbor O vacancy to be dynamically stable for both
TMs considered. Additionally, the cluster defect is stable for Fe. The Ni cluster defect, however, has a
nonnegligible imaginary vibrational mode whose eigenvectors reveal that large atomic displacement of
the Ni atoms would be needed to stabilize the defect. Such displacements would likely alter the defect
structure away from the cluster defect. The dynamically stable structures also show modest changes in the
PDOS when compared to pristine UO2, particularly at high frequencies where shoulders or new features
are evident because of changes in the O sublattice. These changes may be of use in identifying the
presence of the TM defects in UO2, but more investigation is needed to determine if these features can be
measured with Raman or infrared spectroscopy.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

In the present work, we have investigated the defect incorporation structure of Fe and Ni taggants in UO2
via two charge-balancing mechanisms: redox and defect coupling. We find that redox charge-balancing
via interstitial TM and TM substitution at a U site are not dynamically stable. However, substitution with
a charge-balancing O vacancy shows only minor imaginary acoustic modes, which are likely artifacts of
the computational supercell size used in the finite displacement calculations. Additionally, cluster
defects—structures in which two TMs are substituted at a single U site—are plausible for both TMs,
although the DFT calculations reveal a sizeable imaginary optical mode for the Ni case. These findings
provide foundational support for experimentally hypothesized defect incorporation structures. However,
to provide more definitive support to such understandings, additional defect concentrations, TM charges,
and thermal effects should be computationally analyzed.

 APPENDIX A. COMPUTATIONAL METHODOLOGY BENCHMARKS

The exchange-correlation functional, cutoff to the expansion of the planewave basis set, spacing between
k-points for sampling the Brillouin zone, and Gaussian electronic smearing width were all benchmarked
for the UO2 unit cell consisting of 12 atoms in the present work.

To benchmark the exchange-correlation functional, the unit cell volume—determined with PBE,48
revPBE,49 RPBE,50 and PBEsol26—were compared to the experimental value29 and are shown in Table
A1. Because it was parameterized to improve the description of lattice constants, the unit cell relaxed with
PBEsol had the smallest deviation from the experimental volume and was selected for the remainder of
the study.

Cutoff energies ranging from 400 to 1,000 eV were used to calculate the single-point energy of the
PBEsol-relaxed UO2 unit cell and compared, as shown in the left panel of Figure A1. The energy was
converged by 800 eV, so this value was used for the remainder of the study. In the right panel of Figure
A1, the energy of UO2 was compared using a range of k-point spacings from 0.05 to 0.8 Å-1. Note that
less space between k-points corresponds to a tighter grid and more k-points and should therefore be more
accurate. Figure A1 (right) shows minimal changes in energy for k-spacings up to 0.55 Å-1, so 0.35 Å-1
was selected as the k-spacing for the remainder of the work. This spacing had 18 k-points compared to
only 6 k-points for larger values.

 Figure A1. (left) Energy vs. cutoff energy plot for UO2 benchmarking of the cutoff to the planewave
  expansion. (right) Energy vs. number of k-points per Å-1 for UO2 benchmarking for sampling the k-space.

Gaussian smearing was used in the present work to handle partial occupancies because it is robust and
handles both metallic and insulating systems well when an appropriate smearing width (σ) is chosen. In
the present work, values ranging from 0.01 to 0.15, 0.2, and 0.5 eV were tested. Minimal changes were
observed in the external pressure calculated by VASP and energy as σ  0, even at 0.5 eV; however,
results for 0.01 and 0.2 eV were identical, so 0.2 eV was selected and used in the remainder of the work.
