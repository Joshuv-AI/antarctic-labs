Covalent pan-TEAD inhibitors block YAP activity and demonstrate brain penetrance in a Hippo-dependent cancer model

ABSTRACT
TEAD transcription factors enable the oncogenic activity of deregulated Hippo signaling and are a promising therapeutic target in oncology. Targeting the TEAD lipid pocket is an established path to inhibit the oncogenic activities of cofactors YAP and TAZ. Here we present two pan-TEAD inhibitors, GNE-8025 and its in vivo brain-penetrant derivative GNE-2181, that covalently bind the lipid pocket at a conserved cysteine. Both small molecules show growth inhibition of YAP-driven tumor cells in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, we show that GNE-8025 increases the activity of a broad range of MAPK pathway inhibitors in vitro as well as the KRASG12C inhibitor Divarasib both in vitro and in vivo. In addition, GNE-2181 inhibits growth of an intracranial tumor model in vivo. Altogether we present a next-generation class of TEAD inhibitors representing a significant advancement towards potent, specific, and effective Hippo-targeting cancer therapies.

Introduction

Initially discovered in Drosophila melanogaster, the Hippo pathway is a highly conserved kinase cascade with tumor suppressive activity1–3. A primary activity of the Hippo pathway is influencing the phosphorylation status and subcellular localization of the transcriptional cofactors YAP and TAZ. When the pathway is active, upstream factors including NF2, MST1/2, and LATS1/2 function together to phosphorylate YAP and TAZ which results in their cytosolic sequestration and proteasomal degradation. When the pathway is inactive (or mutated), unphosphorylated YAP and TAZ translocate to the nucleus where they bind to TEAD transcription factors and induce transcriptional activation. Nuclear interaction with TEAD proteins enables YAP and TAZ to drive the transcription of genes involved in cellular proliferation and survival4–7. Loss of Hippo pathway regulation results in uncontrolled cellular growth and oncogenic transcriptional programs and frequently arises in cancer. Deregulated Hippo signaling in cancer can occur through various mechanisms—mutation, overexpression, amplification, epigenetic reprogramming—and has been shown to drive oncogenesis and tumor maintenance in a variety of contexts7–12. In vitro and in vivo cancer models have firmly established that loss of regulated Hippo signaling can drive tumorigenesis and metastasis13–19. Additionally, nuclear YAP/TAZ activity has been identified as a resistance mechanism to targeted therapies toward KRAS, EGFR, ALK, RAF, MEK, AR, and CDK4/620–29. Thus, inhibiting the transcriptional programs of YAP and TAZ hold tremendous potential as cancer therapy, including to potentially mitigate resistance mechanisms.

Although YAP and TAZ are highly unstructured, TEAD is well-structured and is amenable to structure-based drug design. Among various strategies for inhibiting TEAD30,31, the most prominent strategy is to target the central hydrophobic lipid pocket to inhibit palmitoylation and induce allosteric changes that disrupt the interaction with YAP/TAZ29,32–34. Subsequent to the discovery of the druggable lipid pocket, many lipid pocket-binding TEAD-targeting molecules have been identified, both non-covalent35,36 and covalent37–39. We have developed several reversible, pan-TEAD inhibitors that engage the lipid pocket to inhibit palmitoylation33, block YAP/TAZ binding to TEAD29, and induce degradation of TEAD40. Consistently, these TEAD-targeting modalities impart an anti-proliferative response, particularly in Hippo-deregulated cancer contexts such as mesothelioma. Key markers of efficiently targeting TEAD in a Hippo-deregulated state include disruption of the YAP/TAZ–TEAD interaction, downregulation of a core transcriptional signature of YAP/TAZ target genes, and inhibition of cellular proliferation. Currently, various TEAD inhibitors for cancers are in preclinical and clinical development, and most of these clinical molecules target the lipid pocket41. These efforts reflect growing recognition of the Hippo pathway as a therapeutic target, especially in tumors driven by aberrant YAP/TAZ activity. Among several emerging applications, brain metastases (BMs) from lung cancer present a particularly compelling opportunity. Lung cancer remains the most common primary tumor to metastasize to the brain, yet therapeutic options for brain metastasis (BM) patients remain limited. In our previously published work42, a subset of brain metastases harbored amplifications in YAP, offering opportunities for targeted therapies for BM patients. However, therapeutically targeting this pathway has remained a major challenge. In this study, we present a brain-penetrant pan-TEAD inhibitor and evaluate its efficacy using both in vitro and in vivo models of lung cancer brain metastasis.

In order to effectively target the Hippo pathway in cancer, inhibitors with optimal properties must be developed. Here, we describe the development and preclinical characterization of two exquisitely potent, covalent pan-TEAD inhibitors: GNE-8025 and GNE-2181. GNE-8025 is an acrylamide-based covalent inhibitor with nanomolar affinity for all four TEAD paralogs. GNE-2181 is also an acrylamide-based covalent inhibitor, which binds to TEAD and blocks YAP interaction. We report that GNE-2181 demonstrates brain penetrance and efficacy in an intracranial brain metastases in vivo tumor model, presenting a brain penetrant TEAD inhibitor. Overall, we observe that these covalent modalities enhance potency compared to reversible lipid pocket binding compounds, retain pan-TEAD potency, and enable accessibility across the blood-brain barrier. Collectively, these preclinical studies advance our understanding of the potential of TEAD inhibition, and suggest a path towards targeting brain tumors and brain metastasis with TEAD-targeting small molecules.

Results

GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 bind potently and covalently to the lipid pocket of TEAD proteins

TEAD proteins contain a cysteine residue within the lipid pocket which is the site of endogenous, posttranslational palmitoylation32,34. This cysteine is conserved across all four paralogs of TEAD and can be targeted covalently by small molecules to engage the lipid pocket and allosterically disrupt the interaction between TEAD and YAP31,43. In the course of our efforts to discover potent small molecule inhibitors of TEAD, GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 were identified (Fig. 1A). Both compounds contain a chemically reactive acrylamide warhead which covalently engages the lipid pocket Cys380 residue. As a control, we generated the non-covalent compound, Compound 1, which is structurally similar to GNE-8025, but with the acrylamide warhead replaced by an unreactive, non-covalent amide. We performed in vitro time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assays to biochemically measure the effect of these molecules on engaging the lipid pocket and disrupting the YAP–TEAD interaction. These biochemical assays demonstrate that both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 exhibit strong activity against all four isoforms of TEAD, with the exception of TEAD3 for GNE-8025 which presents a more modest potency of 1 µM (Fig. 1A). GNE-2181 displays similar potency towards all four TEADs. In contrast, the non-covalent Compound 1 exhibited much lower activity, indicating that covalent attachment to TEAD significantly drives the potency of GNE-8025. Next, we assessed activity of these compounds on the growth of three separate cell lines in culture. We tested NCI-H226 and MSTO-211H, two Hippo-driven workhorse cell lines, and VMRC-LCD, a Hippo-independent cell line that lacks expression of YAP and TAZ and does not respond to TEAD inhibitors29,44. Proliferation of the Hippo-driven cell lines was potently inhibited by both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 exhibiting nanomolar EC50 values, but the Hippo-independent VMRC-LCD cells had a much weaker response with >2500-fold higher EC50 values. To understand the structural basis of compound engagement with TEAD, we determined co-crystal structures of TEAD2 bound to GNE-8025 and TEAD2 bound to GNE-2181 (Supplementary Table 1). The compounds showed clear and unambiguous electron density within the lipid binding pocket of TEAD2 (Fig. S1A, B). Both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 form a covalent linkage with the lipid pocket cysteine (C380) of TEAD2 through the acrylamide warhead, while the amide carbonyl of both compounds form a hydrogen bond with the backbone amide nitrogen of Cys380 (Fig. 1B, C). The conserved central pyrazolopyridine core of GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 is sandwiched in between Phe233 and Ile408, forming strong hydrophobic interactions. Both compounds form hydrogen bonds with Gln410, while GNE-8025 has an additional hydrogen bond with the side chain of Ser345. The p-trifluoromethoxyphenyl group, which is present in both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181, occupies the large hydrophobic portion of the lipid binding pocket lined by Phe428, Leu383, and Ile408. These structural elucidations confirm lipid pocket engagement and targeting of the conserved cysteine palmitoylation and give insights into the elements that enable such potent, covalent TEAD inhibitors.

In order to assess the TEAD target selectivity of GNE-8025 and GNE-2181, we performed proteomic profiling to globally assess cysteine reactivity on all cellular proteins. We treated NCI-H226 cells with GNE-8025, GNE-2181, or DMSO and then used desthiobiotin-iodoacetamide (DTB-IA) to label free cysteines (Fig. S1C). Biotin biotinylated peptides were recovered by streptavidin pulldown and samples were labeled with tandem mass tag (TMT) isobaric labels to enable quantitation between sample treatments. Overall, we quantified 13,874 DTB-IA labeled cysteine sites which map to 5759 proteins including C367 on TEAD4, C359 on TEAD1, and C368 on TEAD3. (Note that the tryptic peptides are identical for TEAD1(C359) and TEAD3(C368), although the biochemical potency data (Fig. 1A) indicates that TEAD1 is more readily engaged by GNE-8025). TEAD2 was not detected in this experiment, likely due to low expression. With both GNE-8025 treatment and with GNE-2181 treatment we observed decreased cysteine reactivity compared to the DMSO control, with a log2FC of −2.25 for GNE-8025 and −2.73 for GNE-2181 (Fig. S1D–F). We observed high levels of compound-induced alkylation on the target TEAD cysteines for both compounds, indicative of robust covalent target engagement. Specifically, with GNE-8025 we observed ~80% alkylation of TEAD1(C359)/TEAD3(C368) and ~65% alkylation on TEAD4(C367). With GNE-2181 we observed ~85% alkylation on TEAD1(C359)/TEAD3(C368) and ~65% alkylation on TEAD4(C367). TEAD proteins showed the highest fold change in cysteine reactivity, indicating high specificity (Fig. S1G, H), and there were only two other profiled cysteines with greater than 50% labeling (adjusted p-value cutoff of ≤ 0.01). The first is C85 on PXL2A (peroxiredoxin-like 2A), which was alkylated >50% by GNE-8025. The second is C177 on DHRS1 (dehydrogenase/reductase SDR family member 1) which was alkylated >50% by GNE-2181. We next determined the off-target rate for these two molecules by calculating the number of cysteine sites with >50% compound-induced alkylation, other than cysteine sites on TEAD proteins, divided by the total number of cysteine sites. For both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 the off-target rate was 0.01%. Altogether, these cysteine reactivity experiments show that GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 are selective inhibitors of TEAD proteins and demonstrate very low off-target activity.

Next we examined GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 for their cellular efflux transport properties in vitro and their pharmacokinetic properties in vivo. For GNE-8025, we observed that the compound was an efflux substrate in both MDR1 and BCRP in bidirectional transport assays (Fig. 1D). In mice, GNE-8025 demonstrated low plasma clearance, moderate half-life, and high oral bioavailability (Fig. 1E). Additionally, GNE-8025 did not achieve high free brain exposure in mice (Fig. 1E, unbound partition coefficient (Kpuu) = 0.12, measured in normal brain tissue). To improve compound exposure in the brain, GNE-2181 was designed. By replacing the benzylic acrylamide warhead of GNE-8025 with an azetidine acrylamide and the 1,2-diol with a benzylic alcohol, we removed two hydrogen bond donors and increased overall lipophilicity. Indeed, the efflux ratio of GNE-2181 in both MDR1 and BCRP assays was notably improved (Fig. 1D) and the compound demonstrated a high degree of brain exposure (Fig. 1E). Similar to GNE-8025, GNE-2181 maintained an excellent pharmacokinetic profile with low clearance, long half-life, and high bioavailability. The measured Kpuu for GNE-2181 in mice was 3.2, reflecting a high degree of free brain exposure (also measured in normal brain tissue). Overall, we report the development and characterization of two potent, TEAD-specific, covalent inhibitors, and we demonstrate brain-penetrance for GNE-2181.

Cellular screening of GNE-8025 reveals YAP/TAZ-dependence and highlights pleural mesothelioma as a sensitive indication

To better understand the potency of a covalent TEAD inhibitor across a wide spectrum of cell lines, we focused on GNE-8025 and conducted a comprehensive in vitro dose-response screen to assess the impact on the proliferative response of cell types derived from a wide spectrum of cancer tissues. We employed a high-throughput, multiplexed methodology known as PRISM (Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures)45 to profile 774 cancer cell lines (Supplementary Data 1). Growth inhibition was measured by a decreased Area Under the Curve (AUC) from seven-point GNE-8025 dosing. Enforcing an AUC cutoff of 0.6, we observed substantial impact of pleura-derived cells and, to a lesser extent, cells derived from soft tissue, kidney, bladder/urinary tract, biliary tract, and the brain (CNS/brain) (Fig. 2A). Little to no dependency was observed in hematological malignancies (lymphoid) or in skin. Correlation of the AUC with lineage identity statistically confirmed that the strongest association is with pleura-derived cell lines (Fig. 2B). Correlation of the AUC with a set of biological pathway gene expression signatures showed strong association with a signature for YAP/TAZ activation, as well as angiogenesis and epithelial to mesenchymal transition (Fig. 2C). One prominent mechanism of Hippo pathway deregulation is NF2 deficiency by mutation or genetic deletion46–48. Pleural mesothelioma cancers are frequently NF2-deficient, and are often acutely sensitive to TEAD inhibitors29,46,48,49. We observe that many of the pleura-derived cell types that demonstrate sensitivity to GNE-8025 in our PRISM analysis are mesothelioma (Fig. 2A), thus we proceeded to focus on this malignant subtype and profile a panel of NF2-null or NF2-proficient mesothelioma cell lines. GNE-8025 showed a strong dose-dependent anti-proliferative effect on NF2-deficient mesothelioma cell lines (Fig. 2D, E). Benchmarking GNE-8025 to previously published TEAD degrader GNE-691540 and reversible TEAD inhibitor compound GNE-788329 showed superior efficacy in both NCI-H226 and MSTO-211H cells (Fig. S2A, B). NF2-proficient mesothelioma cell lines showed a moderate response to GNE-8025, with the exception of MSTO-211H and to a lesser extent NCI-H2810, which are known to inactivate Hippo pathway signals through other mechanisms (LATS1-PSEN fusion in MSTO-211H, and TEAD1 overexpression in NCI-H2810) (Fig. 2D). As a negative control, we also assayed several Hippo-independent cancer cell lines that do not depend on YAP/TAZ: in ES-2 cells YAP/TAZ is exclusively cytosolic, and in SK-N-FI, QGP-1, and VMRC-LCD cells YAP/TAZ proteins are not expressed. These Hippo-independent cell lines were insensitive to GNE-8025 treatment (Fig. 2E, Fig. S2C–F). Cell cycle analysis in the Hippo-deregulated cell lines NCI-H226 and MSTO-211H showed a G0/G1 arrest upon treatment with GNE-8025 for 48 h (Fig. 2F, Fig. S2G). These results demonstrate that GNE-8025 potently inhibits the proliferation of Hippo-deregulated cells and reinforces mesothelioma as a malignancy where TEAD inhibitors are effective at impeding cancer cell proliferation.

GNE-8025 reconfigures transcriptional outputs in Hippo-deregulated mesothelioma cells

We next investigated the transcriptional underpinnings that drive the cellular sensitivities to TEAD inhibitors. We performed a comparative RNA-seq experiment in two cell lines, NF2-null NCI-H226 cells and Hippo-independent ES-2 cells, and used three different modalities of TEAD inhibitors: covalent GNE-8025, TEAD degrader GNE-691540, and reversible GNE-788329. Our analysis focused on three transcriptional signatures: YAP/TAZ target genes and Proliferation genes. In NCI-H226 cells, treatment with a TEAD inhibitor induced a decrease in gene expression scoring for YAP/TAZ targets and proliferation genes (Fig. S3A). In ES-2 cells, relative to NCI-H226 cells, we observe minimal transcriptional responses. Altogether, GNE-8025 demonstrated comparable in vitro efficacy to the reversible TEAD inhibitor GNE-7883 and outperformed the TEAD degrader GNE-6915 in NCI-H226 cells. While GNE-8025 showed transcriptional activity comparable to GNE-7883 and a stronger response than GNE-6915 in this context, we do not interpret these data as evidence of covalent inhibitors being categorically superior. Instead, we highlight that covalency was an essential feature in this chemical series to achieve potent TEAD inhibition. This was particularly important given the highly lipophilic nature of the TEAD central pocket, which can complicate optimization of potency without compromising solubility, selectivity, or brain exposure. Covalent binding enabled us to enhance potency without relying solely on increasing lipophilicity, thereby preserving favorable pharmacokinetic properties.

To further examine the transcriptional signatures that are induced upon TEAD inhibition, we used QuantSeq, a 3’ mRNA sequencing method. QuantSeq enables cost-effective transcriptome sampling by focusing only on the 3’ ends of polyadenylated RNA50. We performed QuantSeq to analyze GNE-8025 treatment in a panel of fourteen NF2-null mesothelioma cell lines as well as three benchmark cell lines: MSTO-211H (Hippo-dependent), SK-N-FI (Hippo-independent), and VMRC-LCD (Hippo-independent). Upon GNE-8025 treatment, NF2-null mesothelioma lines exhibited downregulation of YAP/TAZ target genes and proliferation genes and an upregulation of apoptosis genes (Fig. S3B). Together, these transcriptional results confirm that GNE-8025 is effective in targeting Hippo-deregulated mesothelioma and establish that cellular sensitivity is accompanied by a change in YAP/TAZ target transcriptional signature.

GNE-2181 inhibits the growth of YAP/TAZ-dependent cancer cells

GNE-2181 is a potent covalent TEAD inhibitor that demonstrates comparable efficacy to GNE-8025 both in vitro and in cells (Fig. 1A). Given the impact of GNE-8025, we next profiled GNE-2181 in the same panel of mesothelioma cell lines to compare the cellular effects of both covalent compounds. Similar to GNE-8025, GNE-2181 had a strong dose-dependent anti-proliferative effect on NF2-deficient mesothelioma cell lines, and a modest effect in NF2-proficient mesothelioma cell lines with the exception of MSTO-211H (Fig. 3A, B). Benchmarking GNE-2181 to GNE-691540 and GNE-788329 showed superior efficacy in both NCI-H226 and MSTO-211H cells (Fig. S2A, B). As expected, the Hippo-independent cancer cell lines ES-2 and SK-N-FI were insensitive to GNE-2181 treatment (Fig. 3B, Fig. S2E, F). Cell cycle analysis in the Hippo-deregulated cell lines NCI-H226 and MSTO-211H showed a G0/G1 arrest upon treatment with GNE-2181 for 48 h (Fig. 3C, Fig. S2H), similar to what we observed with GNE-8025 treatment (Fig. 2F, Fig. S2G). RNA-seq analysis of NCI-H226 cells treated for 48 h with GNE-2181 showed a decrease in expression for YAP/TAZ target genes, proliferation genes and MAPK associated genes (Fig. 3D). No significant transcriptional signature changes were observed with GNE-2181 treatment of ES-2 cells. Covalent TEAD inhibitors enhance the activity of clinically relevant small molecules

Nuclear YAP/TAZ transcriptional activity is a robust resistance mechanism to a variety of targeted cancer therapeutic agents, particularly modalities that target RAS/MAPK signaling29,30,51. To assess whether or not our covalent TEAD inhibitors result in a combination benefit with MAPK-targeting molecules, we screened 80 different cell lines. We measured the growth inhibition of various drug combinations using a 5-by-5 or 9-by-9 co-dosing combination matrix design. These viability response matrices enabled us to calculate the Highest Single Agent (HSA) and Bliss scores. The HSA score measures combination benefit by comparing the combination effect to the most effective single agent, and the Bliss score measures synergy by comparing the observed combination effect to the expected additive effect assuming independent drug actions. In line with previous findings with GNE-788329, both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 showed a significant combination benefit on in vitro growth inhibition over single agents, as indicated by positive HSA scores, when combined with small molecule inhibitors (SMIs) targeting RTKs such as EGFR (erlotinib52and osimertinib53) and ALK (alectinib54) (Fig. 4A). A beneficial combination effect was also observed with SMIs targeting the RAS/MAPK pathway downstream, including KRAS (KRASG12C: divarasib55, sotorasib56, adagrasib57, and JDQ-44358; KRASG12D: MRTX113359), RAF (belvarafenib60), and MEK (cobimetinib61) (Fig. 4A) (Supplementary Data 2). Bliss scores were largely positive across the observed combinations but were substantially increased for the combination of TEAD inhibitors with KRASG12C inhibitors, indicating drug synergy. Specifically, the KRASG12C H2122 cell line co-treated with the KRASG12C inhibitor Divarasib and GNE-8025 or GNE-2181 demonstrated not only combination benefit, as shown by HSA excess values, but also synergistic growth inhibition as revealed by positive Bliss excess values (Fig. 4B). Synergistic inhibition was also observed sporadically in cell lines for the combination other MAPK pathway and TEAD inhibitors, such as in a EGFR-mutant NSCLC cell line NCI-H1975 co-treatment with GNE-8025/GNE-2181 and the EGFR inhibitor osimertinib (Fig. S4A), and in a melanoma cell line WM1791C upon co-treatment with GNE-8025/GNE-2181 and the RAF inhibitor Belvarafenib (Fig. S4B). To understand the cellular changes induced by these synergistic drug combinations, we performed RNA-seq to measure gene expression signature scores of cancer cells co-treated with MAPK and TEAD inhibitors. RNA-seq analysis revealed that GNE-8025 reduced the expression of YAP/TAZ target genes in KRASG12C mutant NCI-H2122 cells, but did not impact apoptosis, proliferation, or MAPK genes (Fig. 4C). In contrast, Sotorasib reduced the expression of MAPK target genes, mildly induced apoptosis genes and caused upregulation of YAP/TAZ target genes and proliferation genes, suggesting the existence of a compensatory mechanism to KRASG12C inhibition driven by the Hippo pathway. Combination of the two agents enhanced these transcriptional signatures. We observed decreased expression of YAP/TAZ target genes, MAPK target genes, and proliferation genes, along with an increase in expression of apoptosis genes. These results align with previous findings that show an increased dependence on YAP/TAZ transcriptional activity upon chemical KRASG12C blockade29. Similar responses were observed for mutant EML4-ALK fusion cell line NCI-H2228 (Fig. 4D) co-treated with the ALK inhibitor alectinib and GNE-8025. Similar data were also generated in the KRASG12C mutant lines NCI-H358 (Fig. S5A) and NCI-H2030 (Fig. S5B), and in the mutant EML4-ALK fusion cell line SNU-2292 (Fig. S5C). These findings provide a mechanistic basis at the gene expression level for combining TEAD inhibitors with MAPK inhibitors, as the combination effectively downregulates both YAP/TAZ and MAPK target and proliferation genes while inducing an apoptotic response, enhancing therapeutic efficacy in cancer cell lines and supporting a combination therapy strategy for cancers driven by mutations in RAS/MAPK signaling.

GNE-8025 is a potent and specific pan-TEAD inhibitor with in vivo anti-tumor activity

To further evaluate the anti-tumor activity of GNE-8025 in vivo, we injected NCI-H226 cells subcutaneously into the flanks of C.B-17 SCID mice. As a pharmacodynamics (PD) readout, the YAP/TAZ target score and Proliferation score were analyzed by RNA-seq, and as a pharmacokinetics (PK) readout, we collected the blood and measured the concentration of free compound. We treated mice for four days of daily (QD) oral (PO) dosing of GNE-8025 at 1, 3, 10, 20, and 50 mg/kg, or for four days of twice daily (BID) dosing at 10 mg/kg (Fig. 5A). Analysis of tumors collected 7 h post-last dose showed a dose-proportional decrease in the YAP/TAZ target score up to the 20 mg/kg dose, with no further significant improvement at 50 mg/kg. This decrease correlated directly with the detected concentration of free compound in the blood. Both 20 mg/kg QD and 10 mg/kg BID dosing achieved the same magnitude of YAP/TAZ target score inhibition. To assess the durability of response, the YAP/TAZ target score was analyzed 24 h post-last treatment in tumors treated with 3 mg/kg QD and 50 mg/kg QD of GNE-8025. No significant change in the YAP/TAZ target score was observed between 7 and 24 h (Fig. 5A). At 31 and 48 h post dosing the YAP/TAZ score in tumors treated 50 mg/kg QD started to rebound. The transcriptional trend of the YAP/TAZ score was mirrored by the Proliferation score, indicating that these two transcriptional axes are targeted with similar dynamics and are both regulated by TEAD (Fig. 5A). Two of the key YAP/TAZ target genes used to monitor YAP/TAZ–TEAD transcriptional outputs are ANKRD1 and CTGF. To evaluate if regulation of the Hippo target genes ANKRD1 and CTGF could be used as individual PD biomarkers, in situ hybridization analysis was performed on tumors treated with 10 mg/kg BID of GNE-8025 (Fig. 5B). Signal quantification showed a significant reduction in both ANKRD1 and CTGF transcripts upon treatment (Fig. 5C), consistent with the YAP/TAZ target score RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 5A). Similar results were obtained when immunohistochemically analyzing the proliferation markers Ki67 and p-HH3 in tumor samples treated with 20 mg/kg QD for four days and collected 7 h post-last dose (Fig. 5D, E). Thus, monitoring individual transcripts prove indicative for probing the impact of GNE-8025 on YAP/TAZ transcriptional activity.

To assess how PD readout and efficacy correlated and to directly compare QD dosing to BID dosing, we performed a 21-day efficacy study. Tumor-bearing mice were treated for 21 days with GNE-8025 QD PO at 1, 3, 10, 20, and 50 mg/kg or BID at 1.5, 5, and 10 mg/kg. While treatment at 1 and 3 mg/kg QD did not significantly impact tumor growth inhibition, a significant increase in efficacy was observed with all other doses starting at 1.5 mg/kg BID, plateauing at 20 mg/kg QD or 10 mg/kg BID, while body weight was only minimally impacted (Fig. 5F, Fig. S6A, B). These results demonstrate that twice daily dosing improved efficacy of GNE-8025 in a mouse tumor model.

To further understand how twice daily dosing of GNE-8025 modulated YAP/TEAD activity, we performed an additional PK/PD study in an NCI-H226 xenograft model. This comprehensive PK/PD study was performed with BID PO doses ranging from 0.025 to 25 mg/kg. We observed a dose-proportional decrease in the YAP/TAZ target score, starting at 1.5 mg/kg, as well as an increasing amount of free drug that corresponds to the increasing doses (Fig. 5G). In order to correlate the PD readout with TEAD transcriptional activity, ATAC-seq analysis was performed on these tumors to assess the in vivo effect of GNE-8025 on chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5H). We then used ChromVAR to calculate how chromatin accessibility at YAP/TAZ–TEAD binding sites change with these different in vivo doses of GNE-8025. Consistent with the transcriptional YAP/TAZ target score analysis, a dose-dependent decrease in chromatin accessibility was observed for binding sites of TEAD1, YAP/TEAD1, TAZ/TEAD1, and YAP/TAZ/TEAD1, starting at 1.5 mg/kg BID and plateauing at 10 mg/kg BID. Taken together, these data indicate that treatment with GNE-8025 influences chromatin accessibility and YAP/TAZ–TEAD chromatin to inhibit the transcription of TEAD target genes in vivo.

To better understand the dynamics of pathway rebound after TEAD inhibition, we administered GNE-8025 at low (1.5 mg/kg BID) and high (10 mg/kg BID) doses and collected tumors after two or four days. We analyzed tumors for PK/PD markers of YAP/TAZ signature and free drug in blood. After two days of GNE-8025 administration, a significant inhibition of the YAP/TAZ target score was observed (Fig. 5I). Intriguingly, tumors collected after two or four days of dosing demonstrated a similar transcriptional response and circulating exposure, indicating that GNE-8025 rapidly achieves maximum pathway inhibition. Staggered collection times after four days of dosing showed that at 1.5 mg/kg BID the YAP/TAZ score rebounded above the threshold of the EC50 at 24 h, while at 10 mg/kg BID the YAP/TAZ score was suppressed for more strongly, to 31 h post last dose. Thus, more circulating GNE-8025 likely extends the window for transcriptional inhibition of TEAD.

Finally, to verify the degree of correlation among PK, PD and compound activity, an efficacy experiment with GNE-8025 was performed with doses matching the PK/PD experiment. Consistent with the PK/PD study (Fig. 5F, G), significant anti-tumor activity was observed at 1.5 mg/kg BID (TGI 116%) and improved up to the 10 mg/kg BID dose, with minimal impact on mouse body weight (Fig. 5J, Fig. S6C, D). Comparable results were obtained in another mouse xenograft model of mesothelioma, MSTO-211H (Fig. 5K, Fig. S6E, F). Efficacy of GNE-8025 at 1.5 and 10 mg/kg was also confirmed in the pleural mesothelioma PDX model PXF1752 (Fig. 5L and Fig. S6G, H). Lastly, to confirm that the compound’s activity was limited to Hippo-deregulated models, an in vivo efficacy experiment was performed in the negative control ES-2 xenograft model. Consistent with in vitro data (Fig. 3B, Fig. S2E), the ES-2 xenograft model did not respond to GNE-8025 treatments, confirming the compound’s specificity (Fig. 5M, Fig. S6I, J). Overall, these data indicate that GNE-8025 is an active pan-TEAD inhibitor, demonstrating efficacy and durability in Hippo pathway inhibition and tumor growth reduction.

To assess the growth inhibition activity of GNE-2181 in vivo, we conducted a PK/PD study over four days using the NCI-H226 xenograft model. This study was run in parallel with tumors treated with GNE-8025 at 10 mg/kg BID. Furthermore, daily dosing of NCI-H226 xenografts for 21 days with GNE-2181 resulted in significant tumor growth inhibition, starting at 10 mg/kg (Fig. 6D and Fig. S7A), with minimal impact on mouse body weight overall (Fig. S7B). TEAD inhibitors have demonstrated the ability to overcome resistance to various targeted therapies31. Therapy resistance frequently emerges in the advanced stages of cancer progression, often coinciding with the occurrence of brain metastases. Brain metastases present a significant treatment challenge due to the limited permeability of small molecules through the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Additionally, we observed in our PRISM screen that CNS/brain-derived cells were especially sensitive to the covalent inhibitor GNE-8025 (Fig. 2A). This result indicates that TEAD inhibitors with brain penetrant capability could be efficacious in brain malignancies. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the efficacy of our brain-penetrant TEAD inhibitor, GNE-2181, in a model of brain metastasis with advanced stage lung cancer.

Here we used an orthotopic xenograft in the brain to model and test intracranial tumor growth. Results obtained using similar orthotopic mouse models have previously been shown to be representative of intracranial drug response for other targeted therapies in patients62. We generated the mouse model of lung cancer brain metastasis by injecting luciferase-tagged (FmC) NCI-H226 lung cells into 6-week-old nude mice. Five days after tumor implantation, we started daily treatments of GNE-2181 and treated for more than 21 days at 10 or 50 mg/kg (Fig. 6E). Brain, kidney, liver and muscle tissue distribution of GNE-2181 was maintained upon treatment (Fig. S7C). Over the course of the experiment we monitored body weight to assess good tolerability of the compound in this mouse model (Fig. S7D). To assess tumor growth, we imaged the mice using bioluminescence imaging (BLI) after 7 and 21 days of treatment. Compared to the vehicle control, mice treated with GNE-2181 presented a significant reduction in intracranial tumor growth at week 3 compared to the control group (Fig. 6F, G, Fig. S7E), with a concomitant reduction of the YAP/TAZ target score and proliferation marker (Ki67) particularly at the dose of 50 mg/kg (Fig. 6H, I). In conclusion, these data demonstrate GNE-2181 to be a potent, orally bioavailable pan-TEAD inhibitor with brain-penetrant properties, capable of effectively inhibiting brain tumor growth in vivo in the NCI-H226 orthotopic model.

Combination of Divarasib and GNE-8025 leads to enhanced antitumor activity and signaling inhibition in KRASG12C models

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining the KRASG12C inhibitor divarasib (GNE-6036) with the GNE-8025, we conducted an in vivo efficacy study in the NCI-H2122 (KRASG12C) xenograft model. We first assessed the impact of increasing doses of GNE-8025 either as a single agent or in combination with Divarasib dosed at 25 mg/kg qd po. Single agent GNE-8025, administered at a dose range from 0.5 to 10 mg/kg BID showed minimal antitumor activity. Divarasib monotherapy at 25 mg/kg QD showed almost tumor stasis (98% TGI). The combination of GNE-8025 to this fixed dose of Divarasib resulted in a dose-dependent enhancement of efficacy, transitioning from growth inhibition to tumor regression. To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed synergy, we performed pharmacodynamic modulation by analyzing the YAP/TAZ and MAPK transcriptional signatures in the tumors. Discussion

This study describes the development of two potent and selective TEAD inhibitors that covalently bind TEAD1-4: GNE-8025 and GNE-2181. The observed anti-tumor activity, both in vitro and in vivo, is particularly effective in tumor cells with deregulated Hippo pathway signaling and shows a clear correlation of PK, PD, and efficacy. The covalent drug design strategy presented here offers several unique advantages. First, we demonstrate that higher potency can be achieved by designing an inhibitor that binds covalently within the lipid pocket of TEAD. Second, unlike other covalent TEAD inhibitors, both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 inhibit all TEAD isoforms, although not to equal potency. Notably, both compounds are not equipotent across TEAD isoforms. The Phe-->Tyr change from TEAD1,2,4 compared to TEAD3 (Tyr230) is a key determinant for selectivity of the compounds. The arene side-chain of the Phe/Tyr occupies the same binding site as the compounds and causes steric hindrance. This made binding to TEAD3 particularly challenging because the hydroxyl of the Tyr clashed with the GNE-8025. The warhead of GNE-2181 shifted the core of the molecule such that the TEAD3 Tyr230 had reduced steric hindrance with the compound and hence the compound was pan-TEAD potent. It remains to be seen how the differential activity of TEAD inhibitors across TEAD paralogs (TEAD1-4) influence the final outcome of efficacy as well as compensatory mechanisms of resistance. This will also become more relevant as additional TEAD inhibitors enter the preclinical and clinical space and will represent a resource to evaluate a broad range of combinations with other therapies.

To understand the cellular consequences of TEAD inhibition, we leveraged a cellular PRISM screen and QuantSeq analysis to survey a wide panel of cell lines. In this report PRISM technology revealed that Hippo pathway inactivation by both genetic and non-genetic mechanisms, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and FGFR signaling may be determinants of TEAD dependency in solid tumors. QuantSeq enabled the quantification from gene expression of specific signatures for YAP/TAZ signaling, as well as MAPK signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis, thereby elucidating the mechanisms of action and properties of GNE-8025 and GNE-2181.

Beyond the anti-tumor effects in Hippo-deregulated models, we have identified a pan-TEAD covalent inhibitor with brain-penetrant properties. We have shown that the evolution of GNE-8025 into GNE-2181 increases the Kpuu, resulting in higher brain penetrance. The ability to inhibit all four TEAD proteins in the brain is crucial, as multiple independent studies have underscored the central role of Hippo signaling in metastasis formation. In preclinical models, it has been shown that YAP depletion inhibits the ability of the lung cancer cell line NCI-H2030 to colonize the brain upon intracardiac injection63. Similarly, YAP deletion inhibits invasiveness of the NF2-mutant cell line NCI-H205264. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that in clinical models brain metastases from lung adenocarcinoma show a nearly tenfold increase in YAP amplifications (0.8% vs. 7%) compared to primary tumors42 and that the YAP/TEAD axis drives metastasis formation65. Therefore, future studies leveraging GNE-2181 will help shed light on the role of the Hippo pathway on each stage of metastasis such as migration, colonization and growth. Moreover, YAP has been implicated in promoting radiation resistance through FGF2 activation in glioma66, suggesting that GNE-2181 could be a promising therapeutic option in this clinical scenario, alone or in combination with radiotherapy, even though the compound has been tested only in a single models of brain cancer. Future studies utilizing GNE-2181 across various brain tumor models will be crucial in evaluating its effectiveness as a combination therapy for treating and/or preventing metastatic disease and for tackling YAP driven rare CNS tumors such as meningiomas and schwanommas. Last but not least, accurate assessment of potential brain toxicology aspects should be assessed both in the preclinical and clinical space. In conclusion, the preclinical characterization and in vivo pharmacological profiles of both GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 highlight their potential as effective treatments for tumors and brain metastases driven by Hippo pathway deregulation.

Our research complies with all of the relevant ethical regulations. CD-1, C.B-17 SCID, C-B-17 SCID.bg, NMRI Nude, and Nu/Nu (nude-CRL) mice (aged 5 to 15.9 weeks) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories and maintained in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011). Genentech is an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care-accredited facility and all non-intracranial animal activities in this research study were conducted under protocols approved by the Genentech Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The maximum tumor size permitted by the ethics committee/institutional review board is 2 cm3. We confirm that this maximal tumor burden was not exceeded in the majority of our studies. In one specific instance involving the experiment shown in Fig. 7C, the tumor volume of one animal reached 2068 mm3. This occurred due to the rapid growth kinetics of the NCI-H2122 xenograft between scheduled measurement intervals. Upon detection of this measurement, the animal was euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation using veterinary approved methods followed by post-mortem manual cervical dislocation for confirmation of euthanasia. No other animals across all reported experiments exceeded the institutional limit. Source data are provided for all of the in vivo experiments.

Animal studies conducted using the intracranial mouse models were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Massachusetts General Hospital (protocol # 2017N000019). All experiments were conducted on 6-week-old nu/nu mice from Charles River Laboratories. For intracranial tumors, tumor size never exceeded the humane endpoint permitted by MGH-IACUC, animals were euthanized when they developed clinical/neurological symptoms (e.g., circling, rolling, persistent seizures), weight loss >20% from baseline or body condition score 2 or less.

Cell lines and experimental compounds

All cell lines used in this study were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). They were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco Life Sciences) in a humidified incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell line authentication was conducted for Short Tandem Repeat (STR) Profiling using the Promega PowerPlex 16 System (Promega Corporation). This was performed once when receiving new cell lines and compared to external STR profiles of cell lines (when available) to determine cell line ancestry. Cell line authentication was routinely conducted by SNP-based genotyping using Fluidigm multiplexed assays at the Genentech cell line core facility. Cells were assessed with a Vi-CELL Cell Viability Analyzer (Beckman Coulter); viability of at least 90% was required for experiments and screening.

Experimental compounds and sotorasib were synthesized by Genentech. Detailed descriptions of the compound structures and synthetic protocols for experimental compounds are provided later in the “Methods” Section.

Cell viability and colony formation assays

For single-agent treatments with pan-TEAD experimental compounds or in combination with KRASG12C, KRASG12D or EGFR experimental compounds, cells were seeded at 1000 cells/well on tissue culture-treated flat-bottom 96-well plates, or at 300 cells/well for NCI-H226 and 100 cells/well for MSTO-211H cells on tissue culture-treated flat-bottom 384-well plates. 12–24 h post plating, cells were treated with multipoint titration ranges as indicated of the desired chemical compounds using the HP D300 drug dispenser. Afer 6 days, ATP abundance as a measurement of cell growth was assessed using CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assays (Promega Corporation), and luminescence was read by a 2104 EnVision Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). All cell viability data were collected and calculated for at least 5 replicates per time point, per condition. IC50 values for the inhibitors were determined by fitting the nonlinear regression curves generated by GraphPad Prism.

To assess colony formation, cells were seeded at 1.5 × 103 cells/well in 40 mL 0.29% soft agar on a base layer of 40 mL 0.6% soft agar and treated with 40 mL 3× concentrated experimental compound. On day 7, 40 mL 4× concentrated experimental compound/well was added. Colonies were imaged and counted on days 7, 10, and 14 using a GelCount colony counter (Oxford Optronix) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of drug combinations

Cell viability data were processed to relative viability to obtain HSA (Highest Single Agent) scores. HSA scores were calculated as the average of the 10% highest HSA excess values observed across the full dose ranges tested. HSA excess values for each dose combination tested were calculated by subtracting the observed response against the expected response under the HSA model. As an observed response, we used a smoothened version of the experimental drug combination matrix of relative viability obtained by fitting dose-response curves along every fixed dose of each drug and averaging the fitted values. Data import, processing, and calculations were performed using the R package gDR67.

Cell cycle analysis by EdU incorporation, labeling, and immunofluorescence

8000–10,000 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates the day before treatment. Cells were treated with 1 μM TEAD inhibitors for 48 h. For EdU (5-ethynyl 2´-deoxyuridine) incorporation, cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU for 20 min at 37 °C prior to fixation. Cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized, and blocked in PBS containing 10% FBS, 1% BSA, 0.1% TX-100, and 0.01% NaN3 for 30 min. The click reaction was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (ThermoFisher Cat. #C10337). After washing with PBS, cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (ThermoFisher) diluted in blocking buffer for 20 min, followed by PBS wash. Cells were imaged in PBS using the PerkinElmer Opera Phenix system, utilizing widefield at 10x magnification (binning 2).

Image analysis

Images were segmented, quantified, and analyzed using custom scripts based on previously published MATLAB code68. Initially, uneven illumination in each imaged color channel was corrected by averaging the background of multiple wells and normalizing the images using the calculated profile. A global background value for each field of view was then determined and subtracted from every color channel. Nuclear masks of the cells were segmented by combining both global and adaptive local thresholding approaches using the Hoechst channel. Cells near the image borders were excluded from the analysis to ensure accuracy. Additionally, segmented areas that were excessively large, small, or irregularly shaped were also removed to increase the fidelity of the segmentation. The nuclear mask was subsequently used to quantify intensity levels in the other imaged colors. Hoechst staining was quantified by calculating the total integrated intensity for each cell, while all other fluorescence values were determined from the median intensity value within each cell.

RNA analyses

For treatment, cells were seeded at 1–2 × 105 cells/well on tissue culture treated 6-well plates, and were treated after 12–24 h with experimental compounds. Cell lines were lysed on-plate for RNA isolation using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (catalog number 74034), and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For analysis by RNA Fluidigm, 100 ng RNA was subjected to a complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis reaction using the Applied Biosystems High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 4368814), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. A pre-amplification reaction was then performed with Taqman PreAmp Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After amplification, samples were diluted 1:4 with Tris-EDTA, and quantitative PCR using Taqman Real-time PCR assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Supplementary Data 3) was conducted on Fluidigm 96. 96 Dynamic Arrays were performed using the BioMark HD system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluidigm data were analyzed with RealTime StatMiner for quantitative PCR in the SpotFire program.

RNA-seq was performed as previously described46. Specifically, RNA integrity was confirmed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). About 500 ng of RNA was used for library synthesis using the TrueSeq RNA Sample Preparation kit v2 (Illumina). Size of the libraries was confirmed using 2200 TapeStation and High Sensitivity D1K screen tape (Agilent Technologies), and the concentration was determined by a qPCR-based method using the Library Quantification Kit (KAPA). The libraries were multiplexed and then sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2500 (Illumina) to generate 30 M single-end 50-base pair reads. For RNA-seq data analysis, RNA-seq reads were first aligned to ribosomal RNA sequences to remove ribosomal reads. The remaining reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCm38) using GSNAP version 2013-10-10, allowing a maximum of two mismatches per 75 base sequence (parameters: ‘-M 2 -n 10 -B 2 -i 1 -N 1 -w 200000 -E 1 — pairmax-rna = 200000—clip-overlap)69. Transcript annotation was based on the Ensembl genes database (release 77). To quantify gene expression levels, the number of reads mapped to the exons of each RefSeq gene was calculated. Differential gene expression was performed with DESeq2. A pre-filter was applied so that only genes with at least a median RPKM value of 10 in one were analyzed. P-values for other genes were simply set to 1 and log-fold changes were set to 0 for visualization purposes, but such genes were not included in the multiple testing correction. Q-values were obtained by correcting P-values for multiple hypotheses using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Genes were considered if they had a Q-value of less than 0.05 and were protein-coding. Counts were transformed to log2 counts per million, quantile-normalized, and precision-weighted with the “voom” function of the limma package.

ATAC-seq library preparation

ATAC-seq was performed essentially as previously described70. Briefly, cryo-preserved cells were thawed, washed with 1× PBS and counted. About 100,00 cells were then lysed for 5 min on ice in 100 µl of Lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, pH7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP-40, 0.1% Tween-20 and 0.01% digitonin). 1 ml of wash buffer (10 mM Tris, pH7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween-20) was then added and the pellets were spun down at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 50 µl of tagmentation buffer (25 µl 2× TD buffer, 16.5 µl PBS, 0.5 µl 10% tween 20, 0.5 µl of 1% Digitonin, 2.5 µl Tn5 transposase and 5 µl H2O). The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by DNA isolation using the Qiagen MinElute Cleanup Kit (Cat. No. 28206). The DNA was then amplified using NEBNext 2× PCR Master mix (Cat. No. M0541L) and analyzed by Tapestation (Agilent Technologies) before submitting for sequencing.

ATAC-seq analysis

ATAC-seq reads were analyzed using the ENCODE ATAC-seq pipeline (version 1). Briefly, reads were trimmed of adapters by cutadapt (version 1.9.1) and mapped to hg38 by Bowtie2 (version 2.2.6). Bam files were converted to tagAlign format, which was then adjusted for Tn5 by shifting +4 bp for positive strand and by −5 bp for negative strand. TagAlign files were used to call peaks using MACS2 (version 2.1.0) (12). Chromatin accessibility at TEAD/YAP binding sites were computed using ChromVar using publicly available TEAD/YAP ChIP-seq (28825706).

QuantSeq

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates using seeding densities previously determined to achieve approximately 80% confluence at the final time point. The following day, cells were treated with various TEAD inhibitors. After 24 h compound treatment, cells were lysed on-plate with RLT lysis buffer and stored in −80 °C until RNA isolation. Total RNA isolation was done using the Qiagen RNeasy 96 Kit (catalog number 74181) and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). About 30 ng total RNA was used to generate libraries for sequencing using Lexogen QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit FWD (catalog number 193.384) and UMI Second Strand Synthesis Module for QuantSeq FWD (catalog number 081.96) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantseq libraries were confirmed using 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies) with High Sensitivity D1000 kits (catalog number 5067-5584 and 5067-5585 and library concentrations were determined by the Library Quantification Kit (KAPA). 384 libraries were pooled, multiplexed, and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2000 (Illumina) to generate 3 M reads per library. For data analysis, UMI sequences were extracted from fastq files using UMI2index (Lexogen). BBTools (v39.06) was used to perform quality filtering, adapter trimming and poly-A tail removal. The STAR aligner (2.7.10b) was used to align the clean reads to GRCh38. Reads with the same alignment position and UMI were collapsed into a single representative read using collapse_UMI_bam (Lexogen). Finally, the number of aligned reads were counted using HTseq-count (v2.0.5).

Gene signatures

Refer to Supplementary Data 4 for the YAP/TAZ, MAPK, proliferation and apoptosis signatures used in RNA-seq and Fluidigm analysis.

PRISM analysis

AUC were calculated from the dose response relative viability data measured in the PRISM multiplexed cell line screen experiments using the gDR package. Linear models were used to fit these vectors, containing information on all 774 cell lines, against each of the following datasets: in log(TPM + 1) units; expression signature scores obtained from gene expression values and tissue lineage classifications. Each of these datasets was obtained from the Dependency Map Project (https://depmap.org) and model fits were performed with custom R code as a wrapper for the cdsr_models R package (https://github.com/broadinstitute/cdsr_models). Correlation and p, and q values were extracted from the model fits and used for plotting. A Q-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistics & reproducibility

Beside the RNA-seq studies that have their own statistical analysis, all of the statistical analysis for in vitro studies was done using Student’s t-test (two-tailed, unpaired) to compare the treatment groups with the control group. All in vitro experiments were performed at least 3 times. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*, p < 0.05). Significant values as well as number of replicates are noted for each experiment in the respective figure legends.

Protein purification

All purification steps were conducted at 4 °C utilizing chromatography stations (BioRad NGC system) and columns acquired from GE Healthcare. Between purification steps, the protein was maintained on ice or at 4 °C in a cold room or refrigerator. All buffers were prepared at room temperature, with pH adjusted accordingly, and subsequently cooled to 4 °C. Chromatography buffers were filtered (0.22 μm) and degassed before use. Reducing agents were freshly added just before use.

Frozen cell pellets derived from a 2 L Rosetta2 expression culture were thawed in 300 mL of Lysis Buffer (50 mM Hepes, pH 7.2; 500 mM NaCl; 10% Glycerol; 5 mM DTT, supplemented with 2 protease inhibitors/100 mL buffer and 20 μL benzonase/100 mL buffer). Cell disruption was achieved by passing the suspension once through a microfluidizer. The insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 16,700 × g for 40 min. The resulting cleared lysate was applied to 2 mL of Ni-NTA resin, followed by washing with 10 column volumes (CV) of Lysis Buffer containing 20 mM imidazole, and elution with 5 CV of Lysis Buffer containing 300 mM imidazole in five separate fractions. Fractions containing His-tagged TEAD2 were pooled, TEV protease added, dialyzed against Lysis Buffer overnight at 4 °C.

This dialyzed sample was then applied to 1 mL of Ni-NTA resin, and the flow-through and elutes was collected and subjected to size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on an S75 column with SEC Buffer (20 mM Hepes, pH 7.2; 150 mM NaCl; 5 mM DTT). Appropriate fractions were pooled and concentrated using a 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) concentrator.

Protein crystallization, X-ray data collection, and structural determination

Apo TEAD2 crystals were grown using the hanging drop method on an EasyXtal 15-Well DG-Tool (#132107) with 500 μL solution containing 0.2 M K/Na tartrate, 0.1 M Bis-Tris propane at pH 6.5, and 20% PEG 3350. The drop ratio used was 1:1 of 5 mg/mL Apo TEAD to mother liquor, and the setup was stored at 19 °C. Initial crystals served as seeds for growing larger crystals. Rectangular crystals appeared after 3 to 5 days and reached optimal size after 7 days of growth.

The Apo TEAD2 crystals were transferred to a fresh EasyXtal 15-Well DG-Tool with 300 μL of soaking solution containing 0.2 M K/Na tartrate, 0.1 M Bis-Tris propane at pH 6.5, and 35% PEG 3350. A 9 μl of soaking solution was placed on a screw cap, and the TEAD2 crystals were then added to the drop. Subsequently, 1 μl of a 200 mM compound solution was added to the 9 μl of soaking solution, and the crystals were incubated for 5 days at 19 °C. Finally, the crystals were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent data collection.

X-ray diffraction data was collected at SSRL on beamline 12-1. Diffraction data was reduced using Global Phasing’s autoProc71 procedure using outputs from XDS72. Resolution cutoffs were determined using I/σ ~ 1 as the primary criteria. Anisotropic data was further processed using Global Phasing’s STARANISO protocol (http://staraniso.globalphasing.org), and outputs were used for structure solution and refinement.

Initial phases were determined using a molecular replacement search with a TEAD2 model that removed the lipid modification (5EMV). Several rounds of model building and refinement were done using Coot73 and Phenix74, respectively. Final models of TEAD2 bound to G8025 and G2181 had final Rwork/Rfree values of 0.20/0.25 and 0.19/0.24, respectively. All structure figures were drawn using PyMol (https://www.pymol.org).

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis

Lysate generation

All NCI-H226 cell pellets were thawed on ice and resuspended in 500 µL of ice-cold hypotonic lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCL pH 7.4, 1% NP-40, 1X Halt (protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail; Pierce), and benzonase nuclease (1/1000; EMD Millipore)). Pellets were resuspended via gentle pipetting and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with end-over-end rotation. Lysates from 3 conditions were generated for proteomic analysis. The treatment conditions were DMSO, GNE-2181, and GNE-8025. Each condition was performed in 3 bioreplicates, except for DMSO in 2 bioreplicates. Following lysis, NaCl was added to a final concentration of 150 mM. Total protein concentrations were quantified using the Pierce micro-BCA assay and lysates were used immediately for cysteine labeling.

Cysteine labeling

Samples were treated with 200 µM desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (from a 10 mM stock) for 1 h in the dark at room temperature, followed by dithiothreitol (DTT) at 5 mM final concentration for 30 min in the dark. SDS was added to each lysate (1% final), followed by 2 M EPPS buffer (pH 8.5) added at a ratio of 1:10 buffer:sample, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Samples were cooled to room temperature and reduced cysteine residues alkylated with 30 mM iodoacetamide for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. DTT was added to a final 20 mM concentration for 15 min and the samples were frozen.

Protein digestion

The single-pot, solid-phase-enhanced, sample preparation (SP3) technology protocol75,76 was applied to purify ~500 µg protein across all samples. Briefly, the magnetic beads were prepared by combining E3 and E7 Sera-Mag Carboxylate-Modified Magnetic SpeedBeads (1:1; vol:vol) (Cytiva). The beads were washed three times with HPLC H2O prior to resuspension in lysis buffer (1% SDS, 100 mM EPPS, pH 8.0) for a final bead slurry concentration of 50 µg/µL. Approximately 100 µL of the pre-washed bead slurry and 375 µL 100% ethanol was added to each lysate containing 500 µg of protein. Proteins were incubated with the bead slurry for 15 min at 25 °C with gentle end-over-end rotation. Following protein binding, the supernatant was discarded and the bead-captured proteins were washed three times with 80% ethanol and air dried. The purified protein-captured beads were resolubilized in 100 mM EPPS pH 8.0 and digested with lysyl endopeptidase (LysC, Wako Chemicals) at a 1:10, enzyme:protein (w/w), at room temperature overnight. Following digestion with LysC, trypsin (Promega) was added to all digests at a 1:50, enzyme:protein (w/w), and incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. The digested peptides (supernatant) were purified and separated from the magnetic beads. Three post-digestion washes were performed to wash and ensure elution of all peptides from the beads with 0.2% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid. With every wash and elution, the supernatant was transferred and combined with the initial supernatant and dried to completion by vacuum centrifugation. The percentage of missed cleavages was monitored across 3 random samples to ensure optimal digestion efficiency prior to isobaric labeling.

TMT labeling

All peptides (500 µg/sample) were resolubilized in 200 mM EPPS, pH 8.0. Isobaric labeling of peptides was performed with the 11-plex tandem mass tag (TMT) reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were labeled by adding TMT11 reagents (channels 126-131C) at a ratio of 1.5:1 (TMT:protein, w/w) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. A small portion (1%) of each sample was mixed together, desalted via C18 stage-tip, and analyzed via LC-MS to determine labeling efficiency and loading ratio. The TMT-labeled peptides were quenched with hydroxylamine (0.5% final) and acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (2% final). The samples were combined according to the total summed TMT signal observed in the labeling efficiency check such that all TMT channels have equal loading. The final peptide mix was desalted with 500 mg tC18 Sep-Paks (Waters) and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

Cysteine-containing peptide enrichment

The desalted peptide mix was resuspended in 500 µL streptavidin enrichment buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) and biotinylated peptides were enriched with high-capacity streptavidin agarose (1.0 mL slurry per 5 mg of total peptide input) for ~1 h with end-over-end rotation at room temperature. Following enrichment, the non-cysteine containing peptide flow through was collected and reserved for companion proteomics assays. The cysteine-containing peptide-bead slurry was washed over a gravity flow column to remove nonspecific binding with the following sequential washes: 3× 10 mL 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP40, 3× 10 mL 1× PBS pH 7.4, and finally, 3× 10 mL HPLC grade H2O. The enriched cysteine-containing peptides were eluted with a total of 1.5 mL of 50% acetonitrile and 0.1% TFA and dried via vacuum centrifugation.

Microfractionation

The dried TMT-labeled cysteine-containing peptides were resolubilized in 0.1% TFA and fractionated by high pH reverse phase chromatography using an Agilent 1290 Series HPLC. Cysteine-containing peptides were loaded onto a 2.1 × 50 mm column packed with 1.8 µm ZORBAX Extend-C18 material (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) equilibrated with buffer A (5% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8) and separated utilizing a 6 min linear gradient from 5% to 50% buffer B (90% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Nine fractions were collected and consolidated into 3 total fractions and vacuum dried. The fractions were resuspended in 0.2% formic acid and desalted on C18 StageTips and vacuum dried. The fractions were analyzed using a 120 min SPS-MS3 method on an Orbitrap Lumos instrument.

LC-MS/MS data acquisition

All data were acquired using an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer operating in nano-flow mode using an Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo). Separation was achieved using an in-house packed C18 Column (Sepax GP-C18 resin) that was 30 cm in length with 100 µM inner diameter. A real-time search77 approach was utilized during data acquisition to only trigger quantitative spectra on high-confidence peptide identifications. Online spectral identification was accomplished via a custom software client that monitors spectral acquisition through a vendor supplied instrument application programming interface and assigns peptide sequences through a probabilistic model, in real-time. The RTS client utilized a peptide database composed of in-silico predicated human tryptic peptides and variable inclusion of DTB-IAA on cysteine residues.

Mass spectrometry quantification

MS/MS data was searched using the Comet search algorithm against a concatenated forward-reverse target-decoy database (UniProtKBconcat, downloaded October 2023) consisting of Homo sapiens proteins and common contaminant sequences. Spectra were assigned using a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm and fragment ion settings for low resolution MS/MS with a fragment ion bin tolerance and bin offset of 1.0005 and 0.4, respectively. Static modifications included carbamidomethyl cysteine (+57.0215 Da), TMT tag (+229.1629 Da) on both the N-termini of the peptides and lysine residues. Variable modifications included oxidized methionine (+15.994 Da) and desthiobiotin on cysteine residues (+239.1628 Da). Trypsin specificity with up to 2 miscleavages was specified. Peptide spectral matches were filtered at 2% false discovery rate (FDR). The TMT reporter ion quantification was performed using an in-house Mojave module78 by calculating the highest peak within 20 ppm of theoretical reporter mass windows and correcting for isotope purities. A subset of peptides with desthiobiotin-labeled cysteine was selected for further statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis of mass spectrometry data

The R package MSstatsTMT v.2.0.1 was used to preprocess PSM-level quantification before statistical analysis79. Briefly, the preprocess filtered out TMT peaks in MS3 scans whose sum was less than 30,000 across all channels. Peptides shorter than 7 residues were also filtered out. MSstatsTMT summarized the peptide quantitation to the unique cysteine site level by using a model fitted with Tukey Median Polish summarization (TMP), with imputation of missing values below a censoring threshold of 28. The differential abundance analysis between conditions was performed based on a linear mixed effect model per site. The inference procedure was adjusted by applying an empirical Bayes shrinkage. The resulting p- values from the linear mixed effect model were also adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing by using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

PXF1752 efficacy study was dosed with GNE-8025 (1.5 or 10 mg/kg) in Methocel A15 LV 0.60%, Tween 80 0.2%, twice daily by oral gavage.

Tumor sizes and mouse body weights were recorded twice weekly over the course of the study. In house, tumor volumes were measured in two perpendicular dimensions (length and width) using Ultra Cal IV calipers (model 54-10-111; Fred V. Fowler). Tumor volumes were then calculated as: tumor size (mm3) = (longer measurement × shorter measurement) × 0.5. Body weights were measured using an Adventurer Pro AV812 scale (Ohaus Corporation). Percentage animal weight changes were calculated as: body weight change (%) = [(current body weight/initial body weight) − 1) × 100]. The efficacy studies used ten mice per group and PK/PD studies used four to sixteen mice per group. The sample sizes were chosen based on historical studies; our models had been run numerous times previously and we have an understanding of their performance.

Analyses and comparisons of tumor growth were performed using a package of customized functions in R (Version 4.1.0; The R Foundation, which integrates software from open source packages (e.g., lme4, mgcv, gamm4, multcomp, settings, and plyr) and several packages from tidyverse (e.g., magrittr, dplyr, tidyr, and ggplot2)80. Briefly, as tumors generally exhibit exponential growth, tumor volumes were subjected to natural log transformation before analysis. All raw tumor volume measurements less than 8 mm3 were judged to reflect complete tumor absence and were converted to 8 mm3 prior to natural log transformation. Additionally, all raw tumor volume measurements less than 16 mm3 were considered miniscule tumors too small to be measured accurately and were converted to 16 mm3 prior to natural log transformation. The same generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) was then applied to fit the temporal profile of the log-transformed tumor volumes in all study groups with regression splines and automatically generated spline bases. This approach addresses both repeated measurements from the same study subjects and moderate dropouts before the end of the study. Estimates of group-level efficacy were obtained using the calculation of percent tumor growth inhibition (TGI). This value represents the percent difference in tumor growth between the treatment and reference groups according to each group fit baseline-corrected area under the curve (AUC). This AUC is obtained after back-transforming tumor volumes to the original scale and correcting for starting tumor burden. Positive values indicate antitumor effects, with 100% denoting stasis and values > 100% denoting regression (negative values indicate a pro-tumor effect). The 95% confidence intervals are based on the fitted model and variability measures of the data obtained using parametric bootstrapping. Also, the calculation of a Dunnett contrast of endpoint Gain Integrated in Time (eGaIT) estimates was used to evaluate group-level efficacy. Such contrast of eGaIT represents the difference in growth rates between the treatment and reference groups which are derived from the area under the curve (AUC) of the fits on the natural log scale over a common time period. To obtain the growth rate from the fit AUC on this scale in this time range, the AUC is corrected for starting tumor burden and then subjected to slope equivalence “normalization.” Mathematically, this “normalization” is attained by dividing the estimated baseline-corrected AUC value by half of the square of the common study period, resulting in units of natural log units per day. When tumors exhibit log-linear growth (i.e., the fit is a line on the natural log scale), slope equivalence “normalization” of the AUC results in the calculation of the slope of the fit. In cases where tumors demonstrate non–log-linear growth (i.e., the fit is curved on the natural log scale), slope equivalence “normalization” results in the calculation of the constant log-linear growth rate required to yield the observed baseline-corrected AUC for the fit. The more negative the contrast value, the greater the antitumor effect; the more positive the contrast value, the greater the pro-growth effect. The 95% confidence intervals are based on the fitted model and variability measures of the data. Contrasts for which the 95% confidence interval does not include 0 are statistically significant with P < 0.05.

GNE-2181 efficacy in an intracranial model of lung brain metastases

NCI-H226 lung cancer cells were transduced with firefly-luciferase (fluc)-mCherry (FmC) using lentivirus to enable in vivo monitoring of tumor growth by bioluminescence imaging (BLI) as previously described42. 5 × 105 H226-FmC cells were injected intracranially in 6-week-old nude mice to generate an orthotopic model of lung brain metastasis (n = 30 animals; 10 mice per group). Animals were randomized based on pre-treatment BLI. Then, five days after tumor implantation, two different dosages of GNE-2181 (10 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg) were tested and administered daily via oral gavage for three weeks. Intracranial tumor growth was monitored using BLI and measured in radiance at 4 different time points: pre-treatment (week 0), mid-treatment (week 2), post-treatment (week 3), and late post-treatment (week 6). Mice were also monitored for body weight.

gMDCK-MDR1 and gMDCK-hBCRP permeability assay

To create the gMDCK cell line, Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells obtained from the ATCC were modified using CRISPR Cas9 to knock out the endogenous canine Mdr1 gene. The human ABCG2 (BCRP) and MDR1 genes were stably overexpressed in these cells. The cell line was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, pen-strep, puromycin, and plasmocin. Following maintenance, cells were seeded at 2.5 × 105 cells/mL on Millipore Millicell-24 well plates and allowed to mature for 5 days. Once the monolayers were established, they were equilibrated in a transport buffer consisting of Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution with 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) for 60 min at $37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. This preparation was followed by the introduction of test compound dose solutions, which were prepared at 1 µM in transport buffer and included 100 µM of lucifer yellow as an integrity marker. The experiment was initiated by adding these dose solutions to the donor chambers while the receiver chambers were filled with transport buffer. Permeability was then evaluated in both the apical to basolateral (A:B) and basolateral to apical (B:B) directions. Throughout the assay, the receiver chambers were sampled at 60, 120, and 180 min, with fresh transport buffer used for replenishment after each collection. To conclude the study, compound concentrations in the receiving and donor compartments were determined via LC-MS/MS analysis, while lucifer yellow levels were quantified using a fluorescence plate reader at an excitation of 425 nm and emission of 530 nm. The apparent permeability (Papp) for the A:B and B:A directions was then calculated using the following equation: Papp = (dQ/dt)•(1/AC0), Where: dQ/dt = rate of compound appearance in the receiver compartment; A = surface area of the insert; and C0 = initial substrate concentration at time 0 min.

In vivo pharmacokinetics (PK) characterization in mice

The blood clearance (CL), terminal half-life (t1/2) and oral bioavailability (F) of GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 were determined in CD-1 mice following single oral (1 mg/kg of body weight) or intravenous (0.3 mg/kg of body weight) administrations (n = 3 per group). Blood samples (15–20 µL) were collected from each animal at 0.033 (IV only), 0.083 (IV only), 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 (oral only) hour post dose for PK profiling. Once collected, the blood samples were diluted 5-fold by immediately placed into labeled polypropylene micro-centrifuge tubes which contained 60–80 µL of 1.7 mg/ml Potassium (K2) EDTA in water on wet ice, mixed, then frozen and stored in a freezer set to maintain −60 °C or lower until LC/MS-MS analysis.

Brain exposure assessment and subsequent Unbound brain-to-plasma partition coefficient (brain Kpuu) estimation was done using both CD-1 and C.B-17 SCID mice (aged 6–9 weeks) via oral administration in multiple studies and the results were analyzed and pooled after evaluation. A daily dose of 20–1000 mg/kg for GNE-8025 and 30–750 mg/kg for GNE-2181 were given to the animals at various dosing regimen (QD, BID) to obtain steady state concentration in the brain and in the systemic circulation in these studies. Blood samples (15–20 µL) and brain samples were collected from each animal at 4 h post single dose or 7 or 24 h post multiple doses depending on the studies. The blood sample preparation was the same as described above. The brain tissue samples were collected terminally and rinsed in cold saline and then blotted on filter paper, weighed and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately. The samples were stored in dry ice, and then transferred to a freezer set to maintain −60 °C or lower. Tissue homogenization was done using water at the ratio of 1:4 (1 g tissue will use 4 mL pure water). Final homogenates were kept at −60 °C or lower until LC/MS-MS analysis. All the study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (USA).

The LC-MS/MS system was a Waters Acquity UPLC System coupled to a Sciex Triple Quad 6500 Plus mass spectrometer (AB Sciex). The aqueous mobile phase for the analysis was 0.1% formic acid and 2 mM ammonium formate in water/ACN (v:v, 95:5) (A), and the organic mobile phase was 0.1% formic acid and 2 mM ammonium formate in ACN/water (v:v, 95:5) (B). Chromatographic separations were achieved with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm 2.1 × 50 mm) at column temperature of 50 °C. The gradient was maintained at 10% B for 0.2 min, then ramped up from 10% B to 95% B from 0.2–0.9 min and maintained at 95% B from 0.9 to 1.4 min and then re-equilibrated at 10% B from 1.41 to 1.6 min. The flow rate was set at 0.7 mL/min. Quantification was completed with an optimized multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transition settings for both the analyte and the internal standards. The lower limit of quantitation in all matrices was 5.1 ng/mL.

Concentrations of GNE-8025 and GNE-2181 in blood and tissue samples obtained from the LC MS/MS method were used to calculate PK parameters. Specifically, CL, t1/2 and F% were estimated from the single dose IV and oral PK study using non-compartmental methods with the IV bolus or extravascular input model of Phoenix™ WinNonlin®, version 8.3 (Certara). Brain Kpuu was determined using the terminal unbound concentrations of drug in brain tissue homogenate (Cu,brain) and in plasma (Cu,plasma), under the assumption of steady state. Brain Kpuu derived from multiple time points at different doses were pooled and averaged as appropriate.

To assess GNE-2181 distribution in other tissues, mice were dosed with GNE-2181, formulated in 25% polyethylene glycol (PEG), 5% Solutol HS15 and water, via oral gavage once daily for 7 days at the dose volume of 10 ml/kg at dose levels of 0, 30, 150 or 750 mg/kg/day. Whole blood (10 μl) was collected via the tail vein using a positive displacement pipette into cluster tubes that hold 40 μl K2 EDTA water for analysis. At terminal necropsy, 24 h after the final dose, the following tissues were collected and snap-frozen for TK analysis at each necropsy: liver, kidney, brain, and skeletal muscle. Hemolyzed Blood Samples (HBS) and tissue GNE-2181 concentrations were quantified by Labcorp Laboratory Inc. (Madison, WI) using a non-validated LC/MS/MS method. TK parameters were determined by non-compartmental methods using a validated version of PhoenixTM WinNonlin®, version 8.1.1 (Certara).

PD process and analysis

Plasma and tumor samples were snap-frozen on dry ice and stored frozen at –80 °C until further processing. For RNA extraction, tumors were pulverized, and approximately 10 mg of tumor powder was homogenized with Trizol/chloroform using a GenoGrinder. The upper aqueous layer containing RNA was isolated using MagMax total RNA isolation kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop.

To quantify the gene expression changes using fluidigm, 100 ng of RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystem, 4374966) followed by pre-amplification using TaqMan PreAmp Master Mix (Applied Biosystem, 4391128) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Pre-amplified samples were diluted 5 times using Tris-EDTA, pH 8.0 buffer and subjected to quantitative PCR on 96. 96 Dynamic Array Chip for Gene Expression (Fluidigm BMK-M-96.96) using BioMark HD system. The data analysis was performed using the Spotfire program.

Immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridization

Xenografts were formalin-fixed, routinely processed, and paraffin-embedded for immunohistochemical and in situ hybridization evaluations. Ki-67 immunohistochemistry was performed on a Dako autostainer with 0.334 µg/ml of the D3B5 rabbit monoclonal anti-Ki-67 antibody (Cell Signaling Technologies) with hydrogen peroxide and bovine serum albumin blocks, and 20 min of Target pH 6 antigen retrieval (Agilent). The reaction was detected with Powervision rabbit polymer-HRP (Leica) with diaminobenzidine as the chromogen and hematoxylin as the counterstain. Rabbit monoclonal IgG (DA1E, Cell Signaling) was used in place of primary antibody as a negative control. Human intestines were used as a positive tissue control. Phospho-histone H3 immunohistochemistry was performed on the Ventana Discovery XT platform (Roche) with the Upstate Biotechnology rabbit-polyclonal anti-phospho-histone H3 (Ser 10) antibody (Millipore) at 1 µg/ml with CC1 standard antigen retrieval. The reaction was detected with the OmniMap rabbit detection systems (Roche) using diaminobenzidine as the chromogen and hematoxylin as the counterstain. Polyclonal naïve rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling) was used as a negative control, and a tumor xenograft was used as a positive control for the phospho-histone H3 assay.

ANKRD1 and CTGF in situ hybridization was performed using RNAscope (Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD)) with probes targeting nucleotides 550–1584 and 660–1670 of each transcript, respectively. In situ hybridization was performed on the Bond (Leica) platform with ER retrieval (Leica) at 95 °C for 15 min and RNAscope 2.5 LSx Protease III digestion (ACD). Hybridization was detected with the RNAscope LSx Reagent kit-RED and hematoxylin was used as a counterstain.

All slides were scanned with Hamamatsu Nanozoomer XR or S360 whole-slide scanners (Hamamatsu). Ki-67 and phospho-histone H3 immunolabeling was quantified using MatLab (MathWorks) based image segmentation of the tumor and immunolabeled nuclei to calculate the number of immunolabeled cells per unit area (mm2). In situ hybridization was manually scored. ANKRD1 in situ hybridization was scored as the following: (0) less than 1 dot/ 10 cells, (1) 1–3 dots/cell rare to no cells with clusters of dots, (2) 1–9 dots/cell with scattered individual cells having clusters of dots, (3) cells with 10 or more dots are distributed throughout the tumor, primarily as individual cells, (4) multiple aggregates of cells with clusters of dots/ cell. CTGF in situ hybridization was scored as the following: (0) less than 1 dot/ 10 cells, (1) 1–3 dots/ cell, (2) 4–9 dots per cell, (3) regional increase in cells with >10 dots or clusters of labeling, (4) extensive distribution of cells with clustered labeling.
