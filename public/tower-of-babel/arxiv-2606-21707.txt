Quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes estimation of sums of random variables
Stefano Favaro, Sandra Fortini

Abstract
The estimation of sums of functions of observable and unobservable variables is a long-standing problem in statistics with applications across many domains. Empirical Bayes methods provide a natural framework for this task under mixture models, but existing approaches often rely on restrictive parametric assumptions or apply only to limited classes of functionals in nonparametric settings. We propose a nonparametric methodology, referred to as quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes, that addresses these limitations through a recursive estimation of the mixing distribution based on Newton’s algorithm. The resulting plug-in estimate of the target sum is computationally efficient, scalable, and applicable to a broad class of utility functions, while enabling uncertainty quantification via asymptotic credible intervals derived from a Gaussian central limit theorem. We establish large sample asymptotic theoretical guarantees by proving a merging between the quasi-Bayes and Bayes estimates and by showing consistency under a correctly specified frequentist model. Synthetic-data and real-data analyses demonstrate the practical accuracy and stability of the method, with performance comparable to, and in some cases better than, existing empirical Bayes procedures.



Keywords:
Empirical Bayes, Newton’s algorithm, quasi-Bayes, sum of random variables

Introduction

1.1
Problem setting

Consider
n\geq 1
i.i.d. real-valued random vectors
(X_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},\theta_{n})
with unknown distribution, where the
X_{i}
’s are observable and the
\theta_{i}
’s are either unobservable or constants. Given a utility function
u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a broad class of statistical problems concerns the estimation of sums

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(X_{i},\theta_{i});

(1)

see, for instance,
Robbins 1988
;
Robbins and Zhang 2000
;
Zhang 2005
. As an illustration we recall the motorist example of
Robbins 1977
. For each individual
i
in a pool of
n
motorists, let
X_{i}
denote the number of accidents this year,
Y_{i}
the (unobservable) number of accidents the next year, and
\theta_{i}
the expected number of accidents or intensity,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Depending on the choice
u
, a variety of quantities can be expressed in the form (
1
). For example, if
u(x,\theta)=\theta I(x=\kappa)
with
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, then
S_{n}
is the total intensity of motorists with
\kappa
accidents this year; if
u(x,\theta)=I(x>\theta)
then
S_{n}
is the number of motorists with a number of accidents this year above their intensity; if
u(x,y)=yI(x\leq\kappa)
with
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, then
S_{n}
is the total number of accidents next year for motorists with no more than
\kappa
accidents this year.

Early studies on the estimation of sums of random variables
S_{n}
focused primarily on treatment effects in clinical trials
(
Robbins and Zhang 1988
;
Robbins and Zhang 1989
;
Robbins and Zhang 1991
)
. A much broader range of applications is reviewed in
Zhang 2005
, highlighting both the generality and practical relevance of this class of problems. Examples include species sampling problems in biological and physical sciences, e.g., the estimation of the number of species in a population, the missing mass, and the number of unseen species
(
Good 1953
;
Good and Toulmin 1956
;
Efron and Thisted 1976
;
Bunge and Fitzpatrick 1993
;
Mao and Lindsay 2002
)
; data confidentiality, e.g., the estimation of measures of disclosure risks
(
Bethlehem et al. 1990
;
Skinner and Elliot 2002
;
Rocher et al. 2019
)
; and network data analysis, e.g., the estimation of node degrees based on source-destination data
(
Vardi 1996
;
Tebaldi and West 1998
;
Zhang 2005
)
.

1.2
Background on empirical Bayes

Robbins and Zhang 2000
and
Zhang 2005
developed a general theory for the efficient estimation of
S_{n}
, based on a mixture model for the unknown distribution of the
(X_{i},\theta_{i})
’s. Specifically, let

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptk(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptG,

where
k(\cdot\mid\theta)
is a known kernel density with respect to a dominant measure
\lambda
, and
G
is the unknown mixing distribution on
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Within the model (
2
), empirical Bayes (EB) provides a principled approach to estimate
S_{n}

(
Robbins 1956
)
. Specifically, interpreting
G
as a prior distribution on the
\theta_{i}
’s, the optimal estimate of
S_{n}
under the squared-error loss is the posterior mean

\hat{S}_{n}(G)=\mathds{E}_{G}[S_{n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{G}[u(X_{i},\theta_{i})\mid X_{i}],

(3)

i.e. the Bayes estimate. The EB approach then consists of estimating
G
in
(
3
)
from the observable data
X_{i}
’s, a strategy commonly referred to as
g
-modeling; see, for instance,
Efron 2014
;
Efron 2019
.

Parametric
g
-modeling assumes that
G
belongs to the parametric family
\{G_{\tau}:\tau\in\mathcal{T}\}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is a Euclidean space. An estimate of
G
is then obtained by replacing
\tau
with an estimate
\hat{\tau}_{n}
, yielding
G_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}
. Substituting
G_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}
into (
3
) produces an EB estimate of
S_{n}
, which is asymptotically efficient provided that
\hat{\tau}_{n}
is itself asymptotically efficient for
\tau

(
Zhang 2005
, Theorem 2.2)
. While typically computationally tractable, this approach relies on strong (parametric) modeling assumptions on the form of
G_{\tau}
, making it vulnerable to model misspecification. Nonparametric
g
-modeling instead replaces
G
in (
3
) with a nonparametric estimate
\hat{G}_{n}
, for instance based on maximum likelihood, minimum distance or Bayesian methods. While this is a natural extension of parametric
g
-modeling, EB estimates of the form
\hat{S}_{n}(\hat{G}_{n})
have not been investigated in the literature; their theoretical properties are unknown, and the approach is expected to be computationally demanding due to the infinite-dimensional nature of estimating
G
. A more tractable nonparametric alternative is the “
u,v
” method of
Robbins 1988
, which is closely related to the so-called
f
-modeling strategy
(
Efron 2014
)
. Although
Zhang 2005
shows that “
u,v
” estimates of
S_{n}
are asymptotically efficient, their construction applies only to a restricted class of utility functions
u
.

1.3
Preview of our contributions

We propose a nonparametric
g
-modeling approach for estimating sums of random variables
S_{n}
that combines modeling flexibility with computational scalability and provable theoretical guarantees, thereby addressing key limitations of existing parametric and nonparametric methods.

The proposed approach relies on a recursive estimation of the mixing distribution
G
. Specifically, starting from an initial guess
G_{0}
for
G
, for
n\geq 1
we iteratively update
G_{n-1}
upon observing
X_{n}
according to the procedure of
Smith and Makov 1978
, referred to as Newton’s algorithm
(
Newton et al. 1998
;
Martin and Ghosh 2008
)
. This recursive scheme can be interpreted as a quasi-Bayesian learning model and yields a predictive construction that is asymptotically equivalent, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, to a Bayesian model
(
Fortini and Petrone 2020
;
Fortini and Petrone 2025
)
. A quasi-Bayes EB estimate of
S_{n}
is then obtained from (
3
) by replacing
G
with
G_{n}
. The resulting estimate
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
is straightforward to evaluate and computationally efficient, as the update of
G_{n}
incurs a constant per-observation computational cost as data accrue. Viewing Newton’s algorithm as a learning model allows uncertainty quantification for
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
; in particular, we construct asymptotic credible intervals based on a central limit theorem.

We establish theoretical guarantees for the quasi-Bayes EB estimate
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
. First, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, we prove a merging of the distributions induced by the quasi-Bayesian learning process and the corresponding Bayesian learning process, namely the posterior distribution. This result thus rigorously justifies the interpretation of
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
as an approximate Bayesian estimate of
S_{n}
in large samples. Second, under the assumption that the observations
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. from the model (
2
) with a “true” mixing distribution
G^{\ast}
, we prove frequentist guarantees in terms of consistency of
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
relative to the oracle estimate
\hat{S}_{n}(G^{\ast})
, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. In particular, we provide sufficient conditions under which
n^{-1}|\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})-\hat{S}_{n}(G^{\ast})|\to 0
almost surely as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, and we then derive a convergence rate under additional assumptions.

We empirically validate the proposed methodology on both synthetic and real datasets, focusing on Poisson and Gaussian mixture models. To this end, we consider examples of
S_{n}
drawn from
Zhang 2005
. The quasi-Bayes EB estimate
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
is compared with parametric EB estimates and, whenever available, with nonparametric “
u,v
” estimates. The results indicate that
\hat{S}_{n}(G_{n})
attains accuracy that is generally comparable to, and in some cases improves upon, these alternatives, while remaining computationally efficient and accommodating a broader class of sums through the choice of the utility function
u
.

2
Quasi-Bayes EB for sums of random variables

2.1
Preliminaries and modeling assumptions

For
n\geq 1
, let
(X_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},\theta_{n})
be defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and distributed according to the mixture model (
2
), with unknown mixing distribution
G
supported on
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We consider the problem of estimating sums of random variables
S_{n}
, as defined in (
1
).

The Bayesian
g
-modeling approach to estimate
S_{n}
, here referred to as Bayes EB following the terminology of
Deely and Lindley 1981
, consists of placing a prior distribution on
G
. Equivalently, it is assumed the existence of a random probability distribution
\tilde{G}
on
\Theta
, such that for
i=1,\ldots,n

X_{i}\mid\tilde{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}f_{\tilde{G}}(x)=\int_{\Theta}k(\cdot\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)

(4)

and

\theta_{i}\mid X_{1:n},\,\tilde{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\frac{k(X_{i}\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(X_{i}\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}.

(5)

The mixture model (
4
)-(
5
) offers an equivalent formulation of (
2
), under which the Bayes estimate (
3
) becomes

\hat{S}_{n}(\tilde{G})=\mathds{E}_{\tilde{G}}[S_{n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{\tilde{G}}[u(X_{i},\theta_{i})\mid X_{i}],

(6)

and
\tilde{G}
is estimated through the posterior distribution of
\tilde{G}
given
X_{1:n}
, e.g. the posterior mean.

Remark 2.1
.

The term Bayes EB was introduced by
Deely and Lindley 1981
, who proposed a Bayesian
g
-modeling approach to the compound decision problems of
Robbins 1951
; see also
Efron 2014
;
Efron 2019
. Their treatment is largely parametric, although they briefly discuss the possibility of placing a nonparametric prior on
G
, through a Dirichlet process
(
Ferguson 1973
)
. Recently,
Ignatiadis and Kankanala 2026
pursued this direction by employing a Dirichlet process prior within the Bayes EB approach to mean estimation in Gaussian mixture models.

Here, we develop a quasi-Bayesian
g
-modeling approach to estimate
S_{n}
. Like the Bayesian framework described above, the quasi-Bayesian framework treats the model as a learning model rather than a data-generating process
(
Fortini and Petrone 2020
;
Fortini and Petrone 2025
)
. However, a fundamental difference lies in how the learning process for the
\theta_{i}
’s is specified: while the Bayesian framework makes use of the posterior distribution of
\tilde{G}
given
X_{1:n}
, yielding

\theta_{i}\mid X_{1:n},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\mathds{E}\left[\frac{k(X_{i}\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(X_{i}\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\mid X_{1:n}\right],

(7)

the quasi-Bayesian framework employs a recursive estimation of the mixing distribution
\tilde{G}
through Newton’s algorithm
(
Smith and Makov 1978
;
Newton et al. 1998
)
, as we now describe.

The quasi-Bayes framework assumes the existence of an unknown probability distribution
\tilde{G}
that governs the distribution of
X_{1:n}
, as specified in (
4
). In addition, it specifies how to learn the
\theta_{i}
’s based on
X_{1:n}
, by replacing the posterior-based learning process (
7
) with

\theta_{i}\mid X_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}G_{n}(\theta\mid X_{i})=\frac{k(X_{i}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(X_{i}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)},

(8)

where
G_{n}
is a recursively updated estimate of
\tilde{G}
, computed via Newton’s algorithm, i.e. for
n\geq 0

G_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\theta)=(1-\alpha_{n+1})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\alpha_{n+1}\frac{k(X_{n+1}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(X_{n+1}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)},

(9)

starting from an initial guess
G_{0}
, and using a sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of real numbers in
(0,1)
satisfying
\sum_{n\geq 1}\alpha_{n}=+\infty
and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\alpha_{n}^{2}<+\infty
. From (
9
),
G_{n}
is updated as the observation
X_{n+1}
becomes available, by taking a weighted average, with respect to
\alpha_{n}
, of
G_{n}
itself and of its posterior distribution based on
X_{n+1}
. The sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is the learning rate
(
Fortini and Petrone 2020
)
, for which a standard choice is
\alpha_{n}=(\alpha+n)^{-\gamma}
for
\alpha>0
and
\gamma\in(1/2,1]
.

The quasi-Bayesian learning process (
8
)-(
9
) differs conceptually and technically from that developed in
Favaro and Fortini 2024
for mean estimation in Poisson mixture models, though both rely on Newton’s algorithm. The distinction lies not only in the target of estimation but also in the learning mechanism. In
Favaro and Fortini 2024
, Newton’s algorithm defines a sequential predictive model for the observations, as the recursive estimate of the mixing distribution determines the predictive distribution of future data. In this sense the learning proceeds in a “forward direction”, from the data toward prediction of future observations. In the present work, instead, Newton’s recursion is used to approximate Bayesian learning of the latent
\theta_{i}
’s by replacing the unknown mixing distribution
\tilde{G}
with its recursive estimate
G_{n}
in posterior expectations, i.e. (
8
)-(
9
). The learning therefore proceeds in a “backward direction”, from the data toward inference on the latent parameters.

Under the mixture model (
4

be a

)-(

(\mathcal{A}2)

is absolutely continuous with respect to

\Theta
.

(\mathcal{A}3)

The kernel

is strictly positive, bounded, continuous in
\theta

\lambda
-a.e.

, and dominated by a function

in

.

(\mathcal{A}4)

The mixture model
\{\int_{\Theta}k(x\mid\theta)G(\mathrm{d}\theta):G\in\overline{\mathbb{G}}\}

(\mathcal{A}5)

\displaystyle{\sup_{\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{k^{2}(x\mid\theta)}{k^{2}(x\mid\theta^{\prime})}k(x\mid\theta^{\prime\prime})\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty.}

Estimation and credible intervals

We estimate

See
B.2
for the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Assumption (ii) is trivially satisfied when the kernel is discrete, since in this case equicontinuity is with respect to the discrete topology. From Theorem
2.4
, denoting by
z_{1-\beta/2}
the
(1-\beta/2)
-quantile of the standard Gaussian distribution, as
n\rightarrow\infty

\mathds{P}^{(n)}(\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-z_{1-\beta/2}\sqrt{B_{n}}\leq S_{n}\leq\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}+z_{1-\beta/2}\sqrt{B_{n}}\mid X_{1:n})\rightarrow 1-\beta,\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}\text{-a.s.}

(12)

Thus, with respect to
\mathds{P}^{(n)}
, an asymptotic credible interval for
S_{n}
is given by
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}\pm z_{1-\beta/2}\sqrt{B_{n}}
. In particular, the proof of Theorem
2.4
suggests that the interval length is of order
n^{1/2}
.

3
Frequentist guarantees

3.1
Consistency

Consider the mixture model (
2
) with the mixing distribution
G
replaced by a “true” distribution, say the oracle prior
G^{*}
on
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
, such that the observations
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. with density function

f_{G^{*}}(x)=\int_{\Theta}k(x\mid\theta)G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\hskip 18.49988ptx\in\mathbb{R}

(13)

with respect to
\lambda
. If the distribution
G^{*}
were known, one could estimate
S_{n}
through the oracle estimate

\hat{S}_{n}(G^{\ast})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Theta}u(X_{i},\theta)\,G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid X_{i}).

The next theorem establishes frequentist guarantees in terms of consistency of the quasi-Bayes EB estimate
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
relative to
\hat{S}_{n}(G^{\ast})
, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, under
G^{\ast}
. We denote by
\mathds{P}^{\ast}
the probability measure under which the
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. with density function
f_{G^{*}}
as in (
13
) with respect to
\lambda
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under assumptions
(\mathcal{A}1)
-
(\mathcal{A}5)
, if
G^{*}\in{\mathbb{G}}
, the utility function
u(x,\theta)
is
G
-a.e. continuous in
\theta
for every
G\in\mathbb{G}
and for
\lambda
-almost every
x
, and the following condition holds:
there exist measurable non negative functions

a
and
b
such that

|u(x,\theta)|\leq a(\theta)\,b(x)\;\mbox{ for all }(x,\theta),\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}a(\theta)<\infty,\hskip 9.24994pt\int b(x)\,f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

(14)

Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty

\frac{1}{n}|\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-\hat{S}_{n}(G^{\ast})|\rightarrow 0\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}^{*}\mbox{-a.s.}

(15)

See
C.1
for the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

3.2
Rate of convergence

Under the additional assumption that
G_{n}
in Newton’s algorithm (
9
) is supported on a finite grid
\Theta_{\diamond}\subset\Theta
, we establish a rate of convergence for
n^{-1}|\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-\hat{S}_{n}(G^{*}_{\diamond})|
, where
G^{*}_{\diamond}
is defined as

G^{*}_{\diamond}=\arg\min_{G\in\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}}\mathrm{KL}(f_{G^{*}}\,\|\,f_{G}),

(16)

with
\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}
denoting the class of probability distributions supported on
\Theta_{\diamond}
and
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot||\cdot)
being the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Such a discretization of
\Theta
is known to be a standard assumption in the analysis of convergence rates for Newton’s algorithm, as it enables a quantification of asymptotic behavior without loss of generality
(
Martin and Ghosh 2008
;
Martin 2012
)
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
G_{n}
in Newton’s algorithm (
9
) be defined from an initial guess
G_{0}
on
\Theta_{\diamond}
. Under
(\mathcal{A}4)
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{k(x\mid\theta)^{2}}{k(x\mid\theta^{\prime})^{2}}f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{\diamond},

(17)

then, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
,
G_{n}
converges
\mathds{P}^{*}
-a.s. to
G^{*}_{\diamond}

(
Martin and Tokdar 2009
, Corollary 4.7)
. Then, we denote by
\hat{S}_{n}(G^{*}_{\diamond})
the oracle estimate of
S_{n}
based on the KL discretization
G_{\diamond}^{*}
of the oracle prior
G^{*}
, i.e.,

\hat{S}_{n}(G^{*}_{\diamond})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Theta_{\diamond}}u(X_{i},\theta)\,G_{\diamond}^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid X_{i}).

(18)

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
be the quasi-Bayes EB estimate of
S_{n}
, with an initial guess
G_{0}
supported on a grid
\Theta_{\diamond}
, with
\alpha_{n}=(\alpha+n)^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
. If the utility function
u
is bounded and

(\mathcal{A}4)
and (
17
) hold,
then

\bigl|\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-\hat{S}_{n}(G^{*}_{\diamond})\bigr|=o(n^{\delta})\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}^{\ast}\text{-a.s.}

(19)

for every
\delta>\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}
, where
\mathds{P}^{\ast}
is the probability measure under which the
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. with density function
f_{G^{*}}
as in (
13
).

See
C.2
for the proof of Theorem
3.2
. The next proposition constructs a finite grid for the Poisson and Gaussian kernels ensuring a KL approximation error of order at most
\eta
, together with asymptotics.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
k(\cdot\mid\theta)
be either the Poisson kernel with mean (intensity)
\theta
or the Gaussian kernel with mean
\theta
and known variance
\sigma^{2}
. Furthermore, assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{k}f_{G^{*}}(x)\,\lambda(dx)\leq m_{k}<+\infty
for some
k\geq 2
(Poisson kernel) or
k>2
(Gaussian kernel). For
\eta>0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}
denote the class of probability distributions supported on a grid
\Theta_{\diamond}
, where,

•

in the Poisson case

\Theta_{\diamond}=\left\{\frac{i\eta}{2}\text{ : }i=1,\dots,d\right\}

with

d=\inf\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\text{ : }\dfrac{j\eta}{2}\geq e,\dfrac{\log(j\eta/2)\,m_{k}}{j^{k-1}}\leq\dfrac{\eta^{k}}{2^{k}}\right\};

•

in the Gaussian case

\Theta_{\diamond}=\{i\sigma\sqrt{\eta}\text{ : }i=-d,\dots,d\}

with

d=\inf\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\text{ : }\dfrac{m_{k}}{j^{k-2}\sigma^{k}}\leq\eta^{k/2}\right\}.

Then the distribution
G^{*}_{\diamond}=\arg\min_{G\in\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}}\mathrm{KL}(f_{G^{*}}||f_{G})
exists, it is unique, and it satisfies
\mathrm{KL}(f_{G^{*}}||f_{G^{*}_{\diamond}})<\eta
. Moreover, if
\sup_{x,\theta}|u(x,\theta)|<+\infty
,
\alpha_{n}=(\alpha+n)^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty

(20)

with
h(x)=d^{2x}
and
h(x)=\exp\left\{\frac{4d\sqrt{\eta}|x|}{\sigma}\right\}
in the Poisson and Gaussian kernel, respectively, then (
19
) holds.

See
C.3
for the proof of Proposition
3.3
. Explicit constructions of a grid that guarantees a KL approximation error of at most order
\eta
can be given for other kernels, such that the exponential family, though the Poisson and Gaussian kernels are the most common
(
Zhang 2005
, Section 2)
. A construction for a general kernel

k
, based on its structural properties, is presented in
C.4
.

4
Examples with Poisson kernel

For the Poisson mixture model, we present examples of
S_{n}
drawn from
Zhang 2005
. Let
n\geq 1
and consider the real-valued random vectors
(X_{1},Y_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n},\theta_{n})
distributed as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid X_{i},\,\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Poisson}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n

(21)

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Poisson}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt{G},

where
\text{Poisson}(\cdot\mid\theta)
is the Poisson kernel of mean (intensity)
\theta\in\Theta
, and
{G}
is the unknown mixing distribution on
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Given the observed
X_{i}
’s, we address the estimation of
S_{1,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\theta_{i}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)
,
S_{2,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}Y_{i}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)
and
S_{3,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}I(X_{i}>\theta_{i})
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
is a given threshold
(
Robbins 1977
;
Robbins 1988
;
Robbins and Zhang 1988
;
Robbins and Zhang 1989
;
Robbins and Zhang 1991
;
Robbins and Zhang 2000
)
. Note that, under the model (
21
),
\mathds{E}_{G}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}]=\mathds{E}_{G}[\theta_{i}\mid X_{i}]
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
; accordingly the estimates of
S_{2,n}
coincides with those of
S_{1,n}
after replacing
G
by its estimate.

Remark 4.1
.

In the celebrated motorist example
(
Robbins 1977
)
,
S_{1,n}
denotes the total intensity of motorists with at most
\kappa
accidents this year,
S_{2,n}
denotes the total number of accidents next year among motorists with at most
\kappa
accidents this year (for
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
), and
S_{3,n}
denotes the number of motorists whose number of accidents this year exceeds their intensity.

4.1
Parametric
g
-modeling and “
u,v
” method

Let
{G}
in (
21
) be an Exponential distribution with rate (inverse scale)
\tau
. Under this assumption, we apply parametric
g
-modeling to obtain EB and Bayes EB estimates of
S_{r,n}
,
r=1,2,3
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
(X_{1},Y_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n},\theta_{n})
be random vectors as in (
21
),
{G}
be an Exponential distribution with rate (inverse scale)
\tau
, here denoted by
{G}_{\tau}
. Then

\hat{S}_{r,n}({G}_{\tau})=\mathds{E}_{\tilde{G}_{\tau}}[S_{r,n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+X_{i}}{1+\tau}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)\hskip 18.49988ptr=1,2

(22)

and

\hat{S}_{3,n}({G}_{\tau})=\mathds{E}_{\tilde{G}_{\tau}}[S_{3,n}\mid X_{1:n}]=n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+X_{i},(1+\tau)X_{i})}{\Gamma(1+X_{i})},

(23)

where
\Gamma(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}y^{x-1}\text{e}^{-y}\mathrm{d}y
and
\Gamma(x,z)=\int_{(z,+\infty)}y^{x-1}\text{e}^{-y}\mathrm{d}y
. EB estimates of
S_{1,n}
,
S_{2,n}
and
S_{3,n}
are obtained as plug-in estimates by replacing
\tau
in (
22
) and (
23
) with the maximum likelihood estimate

\hat{\tau}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n}=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}.

By placing on
\tau/(1+\tau)
a (standard) Beta prior with (hyper)-parameter
(\alpha,\beta)
, the Bayes EB estimate of
S_{1,n}
,
S_{2,n}
and
S_{3,n}
are obtained as plug-in estimates by replacing
\tau
in (
22
) and (
23
) with the Bayesian estimate

\hat{\tau}^{\text{\tiny{[B]}}}_{n}=\frac{n+\alpha}{\beta+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}.

The resulting EB and Bayes EB estimates of
S_{r,n}
, for
r=1,2,3
, here denoted by
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{r,n}
and
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[B]}}}_{r,n}
respectively, are asymptotically efficient
(
Zhang 2005
, Theorem 2.2)
.

Now, we consider
{G}
in (
21
) completely unknown, namely we do not specify any parametric assumption. In this nonparametric setting, the “
u,v
” method of
Robbins 1988
estimates
S_{n}
in (
1
) with
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}v(X_{i})
if there exists a function
v
that solve the “
u,v
” equation

\sum_{x\geq 0}(v(x)-u(x,\theta))\frac{\text{e}^{-\theta}\theta^{x}}{x!}=0\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\,\theta\in\Theta.

(24)

For the quantities
S_{1,n}
and
S_{2,n}
, Equation (
24
) admits the unique solution
v(x)=xI(x-1\leq\kappa)

(
Robbins 1988
;
Robbins and Zhang 2000
)
. Hence, the “
u,v
” estimates of
S_{1,n}
and
S_{2,n}
are

\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{1,n}=\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{2,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}I(X_{i}-1\leq\kappa).

(25)

The estimates
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{1,n}
and
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{2,n}
are asymptotically efficient, as
n\rightarrow+\infty

(
Zhang 2005
, Theorem 2.5)
. With regards to
S_{3,n}
, the “
u,v
” equation (
24
) does not admit an explicit solution
v
, which makes not possible the derivation of a corresponding “
u,v
” estimate
(
Zhang 2005
, Section 2.3)
.

5
Real-data analysis

We apply the quasi-Bayes EB approach to a dataset of the total number of goals scored by National Hockey League players in the 2017–2018 and 2018–2019 seasons, available from
https://www.hockey-reference.com/
. We restrict attention to the
n=745
players for whom goal totals are available in both seasons. We analyze these data under the Poisson mixture model (
21
), where, for player
i
,
Y_{i}
is the total number of goals scored in the 2018–2019 season,
X_{i}
is the total number of goals scored in the 2017–2018 season, and
\theta_{i}
represents the latent scoring intensity. In this setting, the
X_{i}
’s are observed data used for estimation, whereas the
Y_{i}
’s are treated as future unobserved data that are used only for out-of-sample validation. Thus, the 2017–2018 season is used to learn the distribution

6
Discussion and concluding remarks

6.1
Examples with Gaussian kernel

Although the examples in the paper are developed for the Poisson kernel, the proposed quasi-Bayes EB methodology can be applied to other kernels, a natural example being the Gaussian kernel. Let
n\geq 1
and consider real-valued random vectors
(X_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},\theta_{n})
distributed as follows:

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathrm{Gaussian}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i},1),\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n,

(26)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptG,

where
\mathrm{Gaussian}(\cdot\mid\theta,1)
denotes the Gaussian kernel with mean
\theta
and variance
1
, and
G
is an unknown mixing distribution on
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
. Following
Zhang 2005
, in
F
we consider the estimation of
S_{1,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}^{n}\theta_{i}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)
and
S_{3,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}I(X_{i}>\theta_{i})
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed threshold. For these functionals, the quasi-Bayes EB estimate is compared with parametric EB and Bayes EB estimates, as well as with the “
u,v
” estimate, which is available for
S_{3,n}
only.

The numerical results reported in
F
show broadly comparable performance across the competing estimates. For
S_{1,n}
, the quasi-Bayes EB estimate is competitive with the parametric estimates, while the classical “
u,v
” estimator is not available. For
S_{3,n}
, all estimates, including the “
u,v
” estimate, achieve similar empirical accuracy, with no method clearly dominating the others. Overall, the quasi-Bayes EB estimate remains competitive for both functionals.

6.2
Multidimensional extension

Throughout the paper, we have focused on univariate mixture models, where
\theta
take values in a subset of
\mathbb{R}
. The proposed quasi-Bayes EB methodology extends naturally, with only minor modifications, to multidimensional settings under a coordinate-wise independence assumption. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\dots,X_{i,d})
be a
d
-dimensional random vector with independent coordinates, where each
X_{i,j}
has density function
k(\cdot\mid\theta_{i,j})
. Writing
\bm{\theta}_{i}=(\theta_{i,1},\dots,\theta_{i,d})\in\Theta^{d}
,

k(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\bm{\theta}_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}k(x_{i,j}\mid\theta_{i,j}),

and the corresponding marginal density is obtained by mixing over a distribution
G
on
\Theta^{d}
. Given a measurable vector-valued utility function
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\Theta^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
, we consider the estimation of

\mathbf{S}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{i}).

In this multidimensional framework, Newton’s algorithm extends verbatim, yielding a recursive update of the mixing distribution
G_{n}
on
\Theta^{d}
. The quasi-Bayes EB estimate
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
is then obtained by replacing
G
with
G_{n}
in the posterior mean
\mathds{E}_{G}[\mathbf{S}_{n}\,|\,\mathbf{X}_{1:n}]
. See
G
for details.

All the theoretical properties established in Sections
2
and
3
extend to the multidimensional framework with minor technical modifications. While the computational cost increases with the dimension
d
, Newton’s algorithm preserves constant per-observation complexity.

6.3
Alternative nonparametric EB strategies

An important direction for future research is the development and theoretical analysis of alternative nonparametric
g
-modeling strategies for estimating sums of random variables
S_{n}
under the mixture model (
2
). A natural approach is nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation of
G

(
Lindsay 1995
)
, which yields a principled plug-in EB procedure, though it may be computationally demanding. Another promising alternative is nonparametric Bayes EB, which places a prior on
G
, for instance via Dirichlet process mixture models
(
Ferguson 1973
;
Lo 1984
)
. While such methods provide EB estimates of
S_{n}
based on posterior means and offer substantial flexibility, they typically require MCMC or related sampling techniques for implementation
(
Favaro and Teh 2013
)
. Establishing rigorous theoretical guarantees for these nonparametric EB approaches and systematically comparing them with the quasi-Bayes EB methodology developed here remains an open and interesting problem.

From a purely empirical perspective, for the estimation of
S_{1,n}
and
S_{3,n}
under the Poisson mixture model (
21
), we implemented a nonparametric EB strategy by replacing
\tilde{G}
with its nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation, computed via the vertex direction algorithm
(
Lindsay 1995
;
Jana et al. 2025
)
. Numerical results are reported in
H
, including a detailed comparison with respect to the quasi-Bayes EB and the “
u,v
” estimates from Sections
4
. For
S_{1,n}
, the resulting nonparametric EB estimates are competitive with both the quasi-Bayes EB and the “
u,v
” estimates. In contrast, for
S_{3,n}
the nonparametric EB estimates perform substantially worse than the quasi-Bayes EB estimates, while “
u,v
” estimates are not available in this case. We also report CPU times, which indicate that the nonparametric EB approach is noticeably computationally slower than the quasi-Bayes EB procedure.

6.4
Specie sampling problems

Another promising direction for future research is to adapt the quasi-Bayes EB methodology developed in this paper to species sampling problems
(
Good 1953
;
Good and Toulmin 1956
;
Efron and Thisted 1976
;
Mao and Lindsay 2002
)
. In this setting, quantities of interest such as the number of species in the population, the missing mass, and the number of unseen species can be expressed as sums of random variables under Poisson mixture models. Unlike the examples considered in Section
4
, however, the associated “
u,v
” integral equations do not admit solutions, and corresponding
u,v
estimates are therefore unavailable
(
Zhang 2005
, Section 3.2)
. While parametric EB approaches remain feasible under parametric assumptions on the mixing distribution, developing quasi-Bayes EB estimates with theoretical guarantees in this setting would be of independent interest. Additional details on the species sampling formulation and its connection with EB estimation of sums are provided in
I
.

6.5
Further directions for future research

As in
Favaro and Fortini 2024
, establishing convergence rates for quasi-Bayes EB estimates currently relies on discretizing the parameter space
\Theta
, a standard and practically convenient assumption that would ideally be avoided; obtaining rate results in infinite-dimensional settings would require new tools to control stochastic approximation errors without finite-dimensional approximations. In addition, while this paper adopts a
g
-modeling strategy, an alternative
f
-modeling approach, directly modeling the marginal distribution of the observations, remains largely unexplored within the quasi-Bayes framework and may offer complementary insights. Finally, although the quasi-Bayes approach yields asymptotic credible intervals for the target sum, their frequentist coverage properties remain unknown; identifying conditions under which such intervals achieve valid coverage is an important open problem.

G\in{\mathbb{G}}
, and let

\mathcal{U}=\{\,u_{G}(\cdot):\,G\in{\mathbb{G}}\,\}.

We first prove that
\mathcal{U}
has an integrable envelope with respect to the measure
F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x):=f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)
. At this aim, denote

A=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}a(\theta)
and
M(x)=Ab(x).

Then, by (
14
),

\sup_{G\in\mathbb{G}}|u_{G}(x)|=\sup_{G\in\mathbb{G}}\int|u(x,\theta)|G(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)\leq M(x)

and

\int M(x)f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty,

proving the envelope property.
Denoting for
n\geq 1

u_{n}(x)=\frac{\int u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{f_{G_{n}}(x)},\hskip 9.24994pte_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{d}x),

we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}|\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-\hat{S}_{n}^{G^{*}}|=|\int u_{n}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{G^{*}}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\int u_{n}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{G^{*}}(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)|+|\int u_{G^{*}}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{G^{*}}(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)|.

Since
|u_{G^{*}}(x)|\leq M(x)
for all
x
and since
M
is integrable with respect to
F_{G^{*}}
, then the last term converges to zero
\mathds{P}^{*}
-a.s. as
n\rightarrow\infty
by the strong law of large numbers. On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\int u_{n}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{G^{*}}(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int u_{n}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{n}(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\right|+\int|u_{n}(x)-u_{G^{*}}(x)|F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)

To prove that both terms of the sum converge to zero
\mathds{P}^{*}
-a.s. as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we first prove that
u_{n}(x)
converges to
u_{G^{*}}(x)
,
\lambda
-a.e. as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
By Corollary 4.6 in
Martin and Tokdar 2009

f_{G_{n}}(x)=\int k(x\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\rightarrow f_{G^{*}}(x)=\int k(x\mid\theta)G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda\mbox{-a.e. and in }L^{1},

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, by Corollary 4.7 in
Martin and Tokdar 2009
,
G_{n}
converges to
G^{*}

\mathds{P}^{*}
-a.s. in the weak topology. For a fixed
x
,

\sup_{G\in\mathbb{G}}\int_{\Theta}|u(x,\theta)|k(x\mid\theta)G(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq A\,b(x)\,\sup_{x,\theta}k(x\mid\theta).

Hence, the function
u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)
is uniformly integrable with respect to
G_{n}
. Moreover, as a function of
\theta
, it is
G^{*}
-a.s.
continuous by assumption. Therefore,

\int u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\rightarrow\int u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta),\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda\mbox{-a.e.}

It follows that
u_{n}(x)
converges to
u_{G^{*}}(x)
,
\lambda
-a.e. as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The map
G\mapsto u_{G}
is continuous as a map
from
{\mathbb{G}}
with the weak topology into
L^{1}(F_{G^{*}})
. Indeed, if
G_{n}^{\prime}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{G}
converging weakly to
G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}
, then

\displaystyle\int\left|u_{G_{n}^{\prime}}(x)-u_{G^{\prime}}(x)\right|F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int\left|\frac{\int_{\Theta}u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)G_{n}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(x\mid\theta)G_{n}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}-\frac{\int_{\Theta}u(x,\theta)k(x\mid\theta)G^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}k(x\mid\theta)G^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\right|F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\rightarrow 0,

where the convergence holds since
\mathcal{U}
has an integrable envelope with respect to
F_{G^{*}}
.

Since the map
G\mapsto u_{G}
is continuous and
{\mathbb{G}}
is precompact by (
\mathcal{A}
1),
its image
\mathcal{U}
is precompact (hence totally bounded) in
L^{1}(F_{G^{*}})
(continuous image of a precompact set is precompact).
Therefore
\mathcal{U}
admits finite
L^{1}(F_{G^{*}})
-bracketing numbers at every

\varepsilon>0
, and it follows that

\mathcal{U}
is a
F_{G^{*}}
-Glivenko–Cantelli class; see Theorem 2.4.1 in
van der Vaart and Wellner (1996).
Consequently,

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Big|\int v(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int v(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\Big|\;\longrightarrow\;0\hskip 18.49988ptP^{*}\text{-a.s.},

and in particular,

\Big|\int u_{G_{n}}(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int u_{G_{n}}(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\Big|\;\leq\;\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Big|\int v(x)e_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int v(x)F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\Big|\;\longrightarrow\;0\hskip 18.49988ptP^{*}\text{-a.s.}

On the other hand, since
|u_{n}(x)-u_{G^{*}}(x)|
are dominated by
2M(x)\in L^{1}(F_{G^{*}})
, then by dominated convergence theorem

\int|u_{n}(x)-u_{G^{*}}(x)|F_{G^{*}}(\mathrm{d}x)\rightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Lemma C.2

Under the assumptions of Theorem

||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\diamond}^{*}||^{2}=o(n^{-\zeta})\hskip 9.24994ptP^{*}-a.s.

(\mathcal{B}4)

(\mathcal{B}1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{k(x\mid\vartheta_{i})k(x\mid\vartheta_{j})}{f_{\mathbf{g}_{\diamond}^{*}}(x)^{2}}f_{G^{*}}(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

i=1,\dots,d
. Then,
\min_{G\in\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}}\mathrm{KL}(f_{G^{\ast}}\,\|\,f_{G})\leq\eta
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}
is the set of all probability distributions supported on
\Theta_{\diamond}
.

Proof.

For every
i=1,\dots,d
, let
w_{i}=G^{*}((\vartheta_{i-1},\vartheta_{i}])
and let
G^{\dagger}
be the probability measure

G^{\dagger}=G^{*}((-\infty,\vartheta_{0}])\delta_{\vartheta_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\delta_{\vartheta_{i}}+G^{*}((\vartheta_{{d}},+\infty))\delta_{\vartheta_{{d}}}.

Then

G^{\dagger}=\delta_{\vartheta_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\vartheta_{0}}G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{\vartheta_{i}}\int_{I_{i}}G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\delta_{\vartheta_{{d}}}\int_{\vartheta_{d}}^{+\infty}G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta),

where
I_{i}=(\vartheta_{i-1},\vartheta_{i}]
for
i=1,\dots,{d}
. Since the KL divergence is convex, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(f_{G^{*}}||f_{G^{\dagger}})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\vartheta_{0}}\mathrm{KL}(k(\cdot\mid\theta)||k(\cdot\mid\vartheta_{0}))G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\int_{\vartheta_{d}}^{+\infty}\mathrm{KL}(k(\cdot\mid\theta)||k(\cdot\mid\vartheta_{d}))G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{I_{i}}\mathrm{KL}(k(\cdot\mid\theta)||k(\cdot\mid\vartheta_{i}))G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq\eta.

∎

Synthetic-data illustrations: Poisson kernel

We present a detailed and exhaustive synthetic-data analysis for the estimation of
S_{r,n}
,
r=1,2,3
, under the Poisson mixture model. Beyond estimation, we also consider quasi-Bayes credible intervals, which, for a fixed leve
1-\alpha
, are constructed according to (
12
). The quasi-Bayes credible interval is then compared with the corresponding oracle credible interval of level
1-\alpha
, which is the
(1-\alpha)
-level posterior quantile obtained under the “true”, or oracle, prior
G^{\ast}
that generate the
\theta_{i}
’s in the Poisson and Gaussian mixture models. For instance, consider the Poisson mixture model (
21
). To define the oracle credible intervals, let

G^{\ast}(d\theta_{i}\mid X_{i})=\frac{\text{Poisson}(X_{i}\mid\theta_{i})\,G^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{i})}{\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(X_{i}\mid\vartheta)\,G^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n,

denote the posterior distribution of
\theta_{i}
given
X_{i}
under the prior
G^{\ast}
. Because of the definition of the model (
21
), conditionally on
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, the posterior distribution of
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
factorizes as
\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}G^{\ast}(d\theta_{i}\mid X_{i})
, the oracle credible interval for
S_{1,n}
is defined as the equal-tail posterior credible interval of level
1-\alpha
associated with the posterior distribution function

F^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathds{P}_{G^{\ast}}(S_{1,n}\leq t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\hskip 18.49988ptt\in\mathbb{R},

that is
I^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}=[L^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}},U^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}]
, where

L^{\ast}_{T_{\kappa,n}}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq\alpha/2\},

and

U^{\ast}_{T_{\kappa,n}}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq 1-\alpha/2\}.

Similarly, we define the oracle credible interval for
S_{3,n}
. In particular, this is defined as the equal-tail posterior credible interval of level
1-\alpha
associated with the posterior distribution function

F^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathds{P}_{G^{\ast}}(S_{3,n}\leq t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\hskip 18.49988ptt\in\mathbb{R},

that is
I^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}=[L^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}},U^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}]
, where

L^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq\alpha/2\}

and

U^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}(t\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq 1-\alpha/2\}.

In the numerical experiments, the oracle posterior distribution functions
F^{\ast}_{S_{1,n}}
and
F^{\ast}_{S_{3,n}}
are evaluated numerically, and the corresponding equal-tail quantiles are used to construct the oracle credible intervals.

F
Examples: Gaussian kernel

For the Gaussian mixture model, we present examples of
S_{n}
drawn from
Zhang 2005
. Let
n\geq 1
and consider the real-valued random vectors
(X_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},\theta_{n})
distributed as follows:

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Gaussian}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i},\,1)\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n

(F.6)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptG,

where
\text{Gaussian}(\cdot\mid\theta,\,1)
is the Gaussian kernel of mean
\theta
and variance
1
, and
G
is the unknown mixing distribution on
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
. Given the observed
X_{i}
’s, we address the estimation of
S_{1,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\theta_{i}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)
and
S_{3,n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}I(X_{i}>\theta_{i})
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
is a given threshold.

F.1
Parametric
g
-modeling and “
u,v
” method

Let
G
in (
F.6
) be a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Under this assumption, we apply parametric
g
-modeling to obtain EB and Bayes EB estimates of
S_{r,n}
,
r=1,3
.

Proposition F.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
(X_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},\theta_{n})
be random vectors as in (
F.6
), with
G
be a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, here denote by
G_{\mu,\sigma^{2}}
. Then

\hat{S}_{1,n}(G_{\mu,\sigma^{2}})=\mathds{E}_{G_{\mu,\sigma^{2}}}[S_{1,n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu+\sigma^{2}X_{i}}{1+\sigma^{2}}I(X_{i}\leq\kappa)

(F.7)

and

\hat{S}_{3,n}(G_{\mu,\sigma^{2}})=\mathds{E}_{G_{\mu,\sigma^{2}}}[S_{3,n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\frac{1}{2}\text{Erfc}\left(\frac{\mu-X_{i}}{\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(1+\sigma^{2})}}\right),

(F.8)

where
\text{Erfc}(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{(x,+\infty)}\text{e}^{-y^{2}}\mathrm{d}y
. EB estimates of
S_{1,n}
and
S_{3,n}
are obtained as plug-in estimates by replacing
\mu
and
\sigma
in (
F.7
) and (
F.8
) with the maximum likelihood estimate

\hat{\mu}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

and

\hat{\sigma}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}-1}.

By placing on
(1+\sigma^{2})^{-1}
a Gamma prior with (hyper)-parameter
(a_{\sigma^{2}},b_{\sigma^{2}})
and on
\mu
a Gaussian prior with (hyper)-parameter
(a_{\mu},((1+\sigma^{2})^{-1}b_{\mu})^{-1/2})
, the Bayes EB estimates of
S_{1,n}
and
S_{3,n}
are obtained as plug-in estimates by replacing
\mu
and
\sigma
in (
F.7
) and (
F.8
) with the Bayesian estimate

\hat{\mu}^{\text{\tiny{[B]}}}_{n}=\frac{a_{\mu}b_{\mu}+n\hat{\mu}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n}}{n+b_{\mu}}

and

\hat{\sigma}^{\text{\tiny{[B]}}}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{b_{\sigma^{2}}+2^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\hat{\mu}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n})^{2}+\frac{nb_{\mu}}{2(n+b_{\mu})}(\hat{\mu}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{n}-a_{\mu})^{2}}{a_{\sigma^{2}}+n/2}-1}.

The resulting EB and Bayes EB estimates of
S_{r,n}
, for
r=1,3
, here denoted by
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[ML]}}}_{r,n}
and
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[B]}}}_{r,n}
respectively, are asymptotically efficient
(
Zhang 2005
, Theorem 2.2)
.

Now, we consider
G
in (
F.6
) completely unknown, namely we do not specify any parametric assumption. In this nonparametric setting, the “
u,v
” method of
Robbins 1988
estimates
S_{n}
in (
1
) with
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}v(X_{i})
if there exists a function
v
that solve the “
u,v
” equation

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(v(x)-u(x,\theta))\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-\theta)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=0\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\,\theta\in\Theta.

(F.9)

For the quantity
S_{3,n}
, Equation (
F.9
) has the unique solution
v(x)=2^{-1}
. Hence, the “
u,v
” estimate of
S_{3,n}
is

\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{3,n}=\frac{n}{2}.

(F.10)

The estimate
\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny[``u,v"]}}_{3,n}
is asymptotically efficient, as
n\rightarrow+\infty

(
Zhang 2005
, Theorem 2.5)
. With regards to
S_{1,n}
, the “
u,v
” equation (
F.9
) does not admit an explicit solution
v
, which makes not possible the derivation of a corresponding “
u,v
” estimates
(
Zhang 2005
, Section 2.4)
.

G
Multidimensional extension

The proposed quasi-Bayes EB methodology extends naturally, with only minor modifications, to multidimensional settings under a coordinate-wise independence assumption. Let
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\dots,X_{i,d})
be a
d
-dimensional random vector with independent coordinates,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where each
X_{i,j}
has density function
k(\cdot\mid\theta_{i,j})
. In particular, writing
\bm{\theta}_{i}=(\theta_{i,1},\dots,\theta_{i,d})\in\Theta^{d}
,

k(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\bm{\theta}_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}k(x_{i,j}\mid\theta_{i,j}),

and the corresponding marginal density function of the
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s is obtained by means of mixing over a distribution
G
on
\Theta^{d}
. Specifically, if
\bm{\theta}_{i}=(\theta_{i,1},\dots,\theta_{i,d})
has the probability distribution
G
on
{\Theta}^{d}
, then the marginal density function of
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\dots,X_{i,d})
with respect to
\lambda^{k}
is

f_{G}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{{\Theta}^{d}}k(\mathbf{x}\mid\bm{\theta})G(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}).

(G.11)

Given a measurable vector-valued utility function
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\Theta^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
, we consider the estimation of

\mathbf{S}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{i}).

In this multidimensional framework, Newton’s algorithm extends verbatim, yielding a recursive update of the mixing distribution
G_{n}
on
\Theta^{d}
. Specifically, the Newton’s algorithm becomes

G_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})=(1-\alpha_{n+1})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})+\alpha_{n+1}\frac{k(\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\mid\bm{\theta})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})}{f_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})},

(G.12)

where, for every
G
,
f_{G}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{{\Theta}^{d}}k(\mathbf{x}\mid\bm{\theta})G(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})
, we denote by
G_{0}
a fixed probability mass function on
{\Theta}^{d}
and
(\alpha_{n})
is a sequence of positive numbers (namely the learning rate) satisfying
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}=+\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}<+\infty
. Note that, while the computational cost increases with the dimension
d
, Newton’s algorithm (
G.12
) preserves constant per-observation complexity.

Based on Newton’s algorithm (
G.12
), the quasi-Bayes EB estimate
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
is defined as

\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}=\mathds{E}^{(n)}(\mathbf{S}_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{{\Theta}^{d}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\bm{\theta})\,G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{j}).

All the properties discussed in Section
2
extend to the multidimensional
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
, and the corresponding proofs follow essentially the same arguments. In particular, Theorem
2.4
becomes

\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\left(B_{n}^{-1/2}\big(\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}_{n}\big)\in\cdot\;\middle|\;\mathbf{X}_{1:n}\right){\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}_{s}(0,I)\hskip 9.24994pt\mathds{P}\mbox{-a.s.},

where
\mathcal{N}_{s}(0,I)
denotes the
s
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution, and
B_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{n,j}
, with

\Sigma_{n,j}=\int_{{\Theta}^{d}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\bm{\theta})\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\bm{\theta})^{T}G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{j})-\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{j})\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{j})^{T},

and

\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{j})=\int_{{\Theta}^{d}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\bm{\theta})\,G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{j})\mu(d\bm{\theta}).

Very similarly, all the properties established in Section
3
extend to the multidimensional
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\text{\tiny{[Q-B]}}}_{n}
.

H
Nonparametric EB

Consider the Poisson mixture model (
21
) with
G
completely unknown, namely without any parametric assumption on
G
, and consider the problem of estimating
S_{1,n}
and
S_{3,n}
. We implement a nonparametric EB approach by replacing
G
with its nonparametric maximum likelihood estimate, i.e.

\hat{G}_{n}=\argmax_{G}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{G}(y_{i}),

(H.13)

where

p_{G}(y_{i})=\mathds{P}(Y_{i}=y_{i})=\int_{\Theta}\frac{\theta^{y_{i}}\text{e}^{-\theta}}{y_{i}!}G(\mathrm{d}\theta)

and the maximization (
H.13
) is taken over all distributions on
\Theta

(
Lindsay 1995
)
. In particular,
\hat{G}_{n}
is computed via the vertex direction algorithm specialized to the Poisson setting
(
Jana et al. 2025
, Section 5)
. Hence, the corresponding nonparametric EB estimates of
S_{1,n}
and
S_{3,n}
are

\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[N-ML]}}}_{1,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{\hat{G}_{n}}[\theta_{i}\,|\,X_{i}]I(X_{i}\leq\kappa).

(H.14)

and

\hat{S}^{\text{\tiny{[N-ML]}}}_{1,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{\hat{G}_{n}}[I(X_{i}>\theta_{i})\,|\,X_{i}],

(H.15)

respectively. As an alternative to nonparametric maximum likelihood one may also consider minimum distance estimates, as proposed in
(
Jana et al. 2025
)
for the Poisson compound decision problem.

I
Species-sampling problems

Consider
N\geq 1
random samples (with replacement) from a population of
d
species. Given observed species’ frequencies in the samples, species sampling problems aim to estimate unobserved characteristics of the population, as well as to predict the species composition of
M\geq 1
additional unobservable samples from the same population. Examples include the number
d
of species in the population, the probability of discovering a new species, and the number of new species in the additional samples. Letting
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
denote the frequencies of the
i
-th species in the initial and additional samples, respectively, for
i=1,\ldots,d
, we consider the model

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid X_{i},\,\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i})\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,d

(I.16)

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\mid G

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptG,

where
G
is the unknown mixing distribution on the set
\mathbb{R}^{+}

(
Zhang 2005
, Section 3)
. Now, let

p_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}},

be the probability of the
i
-th species in the population, for
i=1,\ldots,d
. In particular, recall that conditionally on the sample size
N=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\sim\text{Poisson}(\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\lambda_{i})
, the random variables
X_{i}
’s are distributed according to a Multinomial distribution with parameter
(N,p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})
.

We let
\mathds{P}_{G}
the probability measure that specifies the joint distribution of the
(Y_{i},X_{i},\lambda_{i})
’s in the model Poisson mixture model (
I.16
). In particular, under
\mathds{P}_{g}
, the likelihood function is

L_{\mathds{P}_{G}}(G;\,n_{1},\ldots,n_{N})=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\int_{\Lambda}\frac{\text{e}^{-\lambda}}{k!}\lambda^{k}G(\mathrm{d}\lambda)}{1-\int_{\Lambda}\text{e}^{-\lambda}G(\mathrm{d}\lambda)}\right)^{n_{k}},

(I.17)

viewed as a function of the unknown mixing distribution
G
, where
n_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}I(X_{i}=k)
denotes the number of species with frequency
k
in the sample, for
k=1,\ldots,N
(frequencies of frequencies). That is,
\sum_{1\leq k\leq N}n_{k}
is the number of species in the sample and
\sum_{1\leq k\leq N}kn_{k}=N
.

Hereafter, we interpret the quantity
d
as the number of species represented in the population out of a total of
n
species in a hypothetical superpopulation, assuming
p_{i}=0
if the
i
-th species is not present in the population. Therefore, under this assumption, for a given

p^{\ast}<\mathds{P}_{G}(X>0)=\mathds{E}_{G}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}I(X_{i}>0)\right],

we denote by
\mathds{P}_{p^{\ast},G}
the probability measure that specifies the joint distribution of the random variables
(Y_{i},X_{i},\lambda_{i})
’s in the Poisson mixture model (
I.16
) when
d
is replaced by
n
, and such that

\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G)}(X_{i}=x)=(1-p^{\ast})I(x=0)+p^{\ast}\frac{\mathds{P}_{G}(X_{i}=x)}{\mathds{P}_{G}(X>0)}I(x>0).

(I.18)

The probability measure
\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},g)}
takes into account the probability
p^{\ast}
that a species is represented in the population, merging with
\mathds{P}_{G}
as
p^{\ast}\rightarrow 1
. It is reasonable to take
p^{\ast}
close to
1
if
N
is sufficiently large. Under
\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G)}
, the likelihood function is from (
I.17
)-(
I.18
), and denoted by
L_{\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G})}
.

The Poisson mixture model (
I.16
) with
d
replaced by
n
, and probability measure
\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G)}
in (
I.18
), allows to write the number
d
of species in the population as a sum
S_{n}
. That is, we write

D_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(p_{i}>0).

Similarly, other species sampling problems can written as sums
S_{n}
, for a suitable choice of the utility
u
. For instance, the probability of discovering a new species can be written as follows:

M_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}I(p_{i}>0,X_{i}=0),

i.e. the missing mass
(
Good 1953
;
Efron and Thisted 1976
;
Mao and Lindsay 2002
)
. Furthermore, the number of new (hitherto unseen) species in the additional (unobserved) sample is

U_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(p_{i}>0,X_{i}=0,Y_{i}>0)

i.e. the unseen
(
Good and Toulmin 1956
;
Efron and Thisted 1976
)
. While many other examples of species sampling problems could be considered, their estimation follows similar lines.

Parametric EB and Bayes EB estimates of
D_{n}
,
M_{n}
and
U_{n}
can be derived as in Proposition
4.2
, by assuming the unknown mixing distribution
G
to be parameterized by
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, for an Euclidean space
\mathcal{T}
, here denoted by
G_{\tau}
. For instance, to estimate
D_{n}
one has the following

\hat{D}_{n}(G_{\tau})=\mathds{E}_{(p^{\ast},G_{\tau})}[D_{n}\mid X_{1:n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G_{\tau})}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}}>0\right),

(I.19)

from which an EB estimate follows by replacing
\tau
in (
I.19
) with the maximum likelihood estimate

\hat{\tau}_{n}=\text{Argmax}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}L_{\mathds{P}_{(p^{\ast},G_{\tau}})}(G_{\tau};\,n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}).

Similarly, a parametric Bayes EB estimate of
D_{n}
follows by replacing
\tau
in (
I.19
) with a Bayesian estimate of
\tau
, under suitable prior assumptions for
\tau
. Parametric EB and Bayes EB estimates of the quantities
M_{n}
and
U_{n}
are obtained along the very same lines
(
Zhang 2005
)
.

No “
u,v
” estimates can be derived for
D_{n}
,
M_{n}
and
U_{n}
, as the corresponding “
u,v
” (characteristic) integral equations do no admit solutions for
v
; see
Zhang 2005
.
