Diagnosis and management of subdural empyema with negative cultures: The value of magnetic resonance imaging and early surgical
Authors: Quoc Phuong Nguyen, Duy Thanh Doan, Thi Thu Huong Dinh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubdural empyema (SDE) is a neurosurgical emergency with 6–15% mortality. Culture-negative cases pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationA 51-year-old male with chronic alcohol abuse presented with high fever (39°C), severe headache, meningismus, and altered mental status (GCS 13). CSF analysis revealed protein 28 g/L (60-fold elevated), uncountable cells due to viscous fluid, and Gram-positive cocci. Initial non-contrast CT suggested chronic subdural hematoma, but surgical exploration revealed frontal subdural empyema with frank purulent discharge. All cultures (pus, CSF, blood, urine) were negative despite elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 303 mg/L, PCT 2.12 ng/mL, WBC 16.7 × 10⁹/L). Introduction

Subdural empyema (SDE) is a collection of pus between the dura mater and the arachnoid mater in the subdural space. Although uncommon, it accounts for approximately 15–20% of localised intracranial infections [1], with a mortality rate of 6–15% and high risk of neurological sequelae if not diagnosed and treated promptly [2], [3]. Studies demonstrate that SDE predominantly affects adult males (male: female ratio = 3:1) [4], with common etiologies including sinusitis, trauma and/or neurosurgical procedures, and other causes, with sinusitis accounting for 56% of cases [3], [5].

The most common causative organisms are Streptococcus (particularly the Streptococcus milleri group), Staphylococcus aureus, and anaerobic bacteria [5]. A major challenge frequently encountered in clinical practice is negative CSF and specimen cultures, commonly attributed to prior antibiotic use before sample collection or fastidious organisms. This creates difficulties in selecting specific antimicrobial therapy and assessing prognosis.

We report a case of subdural empyema with markedly elevated CSF protein (28 g/L, 60 -fold above the upper limit of normal), uncountable CSF cell count due to excessively viscous fluid, and Gram-positive cocci on microscopy. However, all specimen cultures (CSF, pus, blood, urine) were negative despite markedly elevated inflammatory markers. This case highlights the challenges in diagnosing and treating severe central nervous system infections when the causative microorganism cannot be identified.

Case description

A 51-year-old male farmer from Thai Nguyen province presented with no underlying chronic medical conditions, no history of head trauma, neurosurgical procedures, or recent ear-nose-throat infections. Notably, the patient had a history of heavy alcohol consumption (an important risk factor for immunosuppression).

Non-contrast brain CT (Sept 14) demonstrated a hypodense subdural collection in the right frontal region, initially suspected to be chronic subdural haemorrhage. Contrast-enhanced MRI was not available before surgery: MRI was not accessible at the referring hospital, the patient was transferred urgently with a deteriorating level of consciousness, and at our center intraoperative opening of the dura revealed frank pus, so surgery was not delayed for additional imaging.

Contrast-enhanced brain MRI (Sept 17) was performed on surgical day 3 once the patient was haemodynamically stable, and confirmed right frontal subdural empyema with peripheral dural enhancement on post-gadolinium T1-weighted sequences and markedly restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC.

Follow-up brain CT (Sept 22) post-surgery showed a significant reduction in the empyema collection with no mass effect (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Discussion

This case presents three challenges in the diagnosis and management of subdural Empyema: (1) abnormal CSF characteristics with markedly elevated protein, (2) limitations of CT imaging in differential diagnosis, and (3) negative CSF and purulent fluid cultures despite post-operative evidence of frank purulent discharge and obvious infection.

In our case, the CSF protein level of 28 g/L was 60-fold above the upper limit of normal and 5–10 times higher than typical values in bacterial meningitis or tuberculous meningitis. Combined with CSF cell counts described as "uncountable due to excessive viscosity", this suggests a more severe condition than simple meningitis - intracranial Empyema. According to Nathoo et al., craniotomy is the preferred surgical approach for SDE management, allowing complete drainage and, more importantly, decompression of the underlying hemisphere [6]. Such cases require emergency surgical intervention combined with antibiotic therapy than antibiotics alone.

Initial non-contrast CT demonstrates a "subdural hypodense collection" interpreted as suspected chronic subdural haemorrhage, leading to initial misdiagnosis. Both chronic subdural haemorrhage and subdural Empyema can appear hypodense on routine brain CT. Multiple studies have demonstrated that routine CT has lower sensitivity than MRI in diagnosing subdural empyema [5], [7], [8]. Empyema contains numerous inflammatory cells, cellular debris, and bacteria that restrict diffusion, producing a bright signal on DWI - a characteristic feature that helps differentiate it from chronic haemorrhage. In this case, only upon surgical opening of the dura was "frank purulent discharge" discovered instead of blood, revealing the limitations of routine CT and emphasizing the role of contrast-enhanced MRI in evaluating suspected intracranial infections.

Negative culture results from all specimens posed challenges in accurately identifying the microbiological aetiology for clinicians' diagnosis and treatment. Despite direct CSF microscopy showing Gram-positive cocci and markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 303 mg/L, PCT 2.12 ng/mL, WBC 16.7 ×10⁹/L), all culture results from CSF, pus, blood, and urine were negative. Three hypotheses can be proposed: (1) the patient self-medicated (possibly including antibiotics) from September 11th before sampling on September 14th, suppressing bacterial growth in vitro; (2) particular Streptococcus species are fastidious organisms requiring special CO₂-enriched media; (3) anaerobic bacteria may have died upon oxygen exposure if strict anaerobic conditions were not maintained. Some studies report negative culture rates in intracranial Empyema reaching 50–70% [4], [9]. In such cases, molecular methods such as 16S rRNA PCR, Broad-range polymerase chain reaction (BR-PCR), or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) are recommended for pathogen identification at the molecular level due to their high sensitivity. In Vietnam, these tests are not widely available in most hospitals, necessitating reliance on empirical diagnosis based on clinical presentation, inflammatory markers, and microscopy/culture results.

The patient received empirical antibiotic therapy for severe central nervous system infection pending culture results. In this case, the combination of meropenem and linezolid provides coverage for potential causative organisms of subdural empyema. Meropenem 2 g intravenously every 8 h was selected as the backbone of therapy because it covers streptococci (including the Streptococcus anginosus/milleri group, which is the most frequent cause of SDE), aerobic Gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes commonly recovered from intracranial suppuration, and because it achieves reliable CSF concentrations at the dose used for nervous central system infection. Linezolid 600 mg intravenously every 12 h was added to extend Gram-positive coverage to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and to provide a second agent with excellent CSF and abscess-cavity penetration in a patient with chronic alcohol use and direct microscopy showing Gram-positive cocci; vancomycin was avoided because of less predictable CSF penetration, dependence on therapeutic drug monitoring, and concern about additive nephrotoxicity in a patient with chronic alcohol use. After negative cultures, this empirical regimen was continued unchanged because it already covered the most likely pathogens based on clinical syndrome, intraoperative findings and direct microscopy. Intravenous meropenem and linezolid were given for a total of 14 days, with clinical, biochemical (CRP, procalcitonin, full blood count) and radiological (follow-up CT) monitoring; No adjustment for treatment failure or adverse events was required.

In this case, the decision for craniotomy was timely and appropriate, as opening the dura revealed "frank purulent discharge under pressure," indicating high-pressure, viscous pus. After evacuation of pus and copious irrigation of the subdural space with warmed 0.9% sodium chloride until the effluent ran clear, the dura was closed mainly in a watertight fashion; duraplasty with a graft was not required because the dural edges were healthy and approximated without tension. The bone flap was cleaned macroscopically and the infection was confined to the subdural space without evidence of osteomyelitis or refractory cerebral oedema; it was therefore replaced and fixed in situ at the index operation (primary cranioplasty), and no staged or delayed cranioplasty was needed. A soft Nelaton catheter was left in the subdural space and connected to closed, dependent, gravity drainage to evacuate residual pus, prevent re-accumulation, and allow daily monitoring of drain output. It was removed when output was low and clear and inflammatory markers were improved, and no recurrent collection, CSF leak, wound infection or bone-flap infection occurred during inpatient stay or follow-up. This patient had the risk factor of heavy alcohol consumption, leading to impaired leukocyte function, reduced macrophage activity, cellular immunosuppression, and respiratory mucosa damage. Alcoholics have significantly increased infection risk, particularly with Streptococcus and Staphylococcus, consistent with the clinical finding of Gram-positive cocci in CSF in this case.

Treatment outcome

The patient achieved complete recovery after 24 days of treatment, demonstrating the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary approach including timely surgery (within 6 h of hospital admission), early initiation of broad-spectrum combination antibiotics, and intensive supportive critical care. Studies show that subdural empyema mortality ranges from 6% to 15%, with neurological sequelae rates up to 56%. Favorable prognostic factors in this case included GCS 13, rapid diagnosis and surgery, early antibiotic initiation, and continuously declining inflammatory markers from day 5 onward, indicating good response.

Conclusion

Patients with subdural Empyema have high negative culture rates; non-contrast brain CT can easily be confused with chronic haemorrhage, necessitating MRI for differential diagnosis. Emergency surgical drainage is crucial for prognosis, alongside early initiation of broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics. Consider 16S PCR/mNGS when conventional culture methods fail to identify the causative microorganism.
