Moral perspective-taking can reduce polarization around climate policy in the United States
Authors: Kristin F Hurst, Zachary M Hurst, Mohammad Atari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolarization is a barrier to the large-scale collective action urgently needed to address the climate crisis. Moral perspective-taking (MPT) combines counter-attitudinal argument generation with moral framing as a strategy for reducing polarization. Across four online studies with participants in the United States, we experimentally tested the efficacy of MPT for depolarization around divisive climate-related topics (transition from fossil fuels, fracking bans). We found consistent evidence that MPT can help moderate views on a divisive environmental topic, thus helping to decrease polarization. All four studies found significant effects of MPT on policy support. Further, studies 1 and 2 found that the intervention increased cognitive empathy for people with opposing views and studies 3 and 4 found it decreased resistance to opinion change and influenced willingness to take action on the issue. Our findings suggest that MPT can be used as a flexible tool to reduce polarization that is scalable and adaptable across multiple contexts.Keywords: political polarization, climate change polarization, moral perspective-taking

The collective action needed to address challenges such as the climate crisis requires group agreement on a course of action. Polarization impedes this process. Moral perspective-taking (MPT) asks people to create moral arguments that counter the attitudes they hold as a strategy for reducing polarization. We experimentally tested the efficacy of a MPT intervention for depolarization around divisive climate-related topics in a United States context. We found consistent evidence that it can help moderate views, thus increasing the likelihood of collective action. Our findings suggest that MPT can be used as a flexible tool to reduce polarization that is scalable and adaptable across multiple contexts.

Introduction

Polarization stymies effective climate action and acts as a barrier to the adoption of climate friendly policies (1–3). Defined as “a situation in which strongly held opposing opinions form around an issue in society, creating a sense of ‘us versus them’ divides” (1) (p. 478), polarization impedes the cooperation and collective action needed to solve complex problems by decreasing people's willingness to see opposing views as legitimate, increasing partisan animosity, and making it difficult to find common ground (1, 4–6). Recognizing the urgency of this challenge, scholars have tested several approaches designed to reduce climate change polarization. These include strategic message reframing (7–11), engaging in small group deliberation or sharing values to identify common ground (12), correcting misperceptions (13), and making superordinate identities salient to reveal commonalties (14). Despite these advances, the persistence of the problem points to the need for more research to identify practical strategies that are scalable and effective across contexts.

One set of strategies for reducing polarization employs perspective-taking techniques. Perspective-taking, broadly defined as “the act of ‘contemplating others’ psychological experiences” (15) (p. 1), has been associated with outcomes relevant to reducing polarization including reduced outgroup bias, increased cooperation, conflict resolution, and empathy for outgroup members (6, 15–18). One study, for example, found that perspective-taking can diminish racial bias and increase positive interracial attitudes (15) while another found that it reduces the accessibility of stereotypes and in-group favoritism (18). Perspective-taking based approaches have been used in the environmental domain to increase empathy toward wildlife, nature (19–21), and future generations (22). However, they have yet to be systematically tested in the context of climate change polarization.

Perspective-taking interventions involve imagining the thoughts, feelings, and/or experiences of an outgroup member (15, 17), or crafting arguments from another person's point of view (16). Many perspective-taking techniques align with research on self-persuasion which has found that crafting arguments in favor of one's opposing stance can be an effective tool for attitude change (23–27). This is thought to be because people produce rationales and justifications they themselves find most convincing, even when they are ostensibly trying to convince another person (23). For example, role playing studies (25–27), have found that exercises that ask participants to pretend to be someone on the opposing side of an issue by generating and verbalizing opinions that person would hold leads to counter-attitudinal attitude change. In another example, across four studies, Briñol and others (23) found that asking participants to craft counter-attitudinal arguments was particularly effective when the target of persuasion was the self, as opposed to another person (eg another student for whom the issue was relevant).

Importantly, interventions based on counter-attitudinal argument generation may backfire when people craft arguments that are incongruent with their own values (28). Thus, such interventions are most likely to be effective when people are prompted to make value-congruent arguments. This observation aligns with the literature on moral framing, which suggests that morally grounded arguments that align with the underlying moral values of message recipients are more likely to resonate with individuals and increase receptivity to viewpoints they may have otherwise dismissed or rejected (7, 29–33). We tested, a moral perspective-taking (MPT) intervention (34) which combines counter-attitudinal argument generation with moral reframing. It involves asking participants to craft persuasive counter-attitudinal arguments using their own moral frame of reference. Across four online studies, we tested the efficacy of MPT for depolarization around divisive climate-related topics in a United States context (transition from fossil fuels, fracking bans). The goal of the intervention is to prompt participants to use their own moral frame of reference to reflect on the merits of a stance they may have not previously considered. This action, in turn, could lead them to moderate their own views, while also increasing empathy for people with opposing views.

Results

Study 1

Study 1 provided an initial test of MPT in the context of transitioning the US economy away from fossil fuels. A sample of 215 participants who were identified through prescreening as being opposed to transitioning away from fossil fuels (Mage = 45, 52% men, 94% white) read a factsheet on the pros and cons of the clean energy transition and were randomly assigned to the MPT condition (N = 102) or a comparison condition (N = 113). Both the MPT condition and the comparison condition asked participants to write a short, morally grounded essay on the topic of the transition away from fossil fuels. We define morality broadly as the set of standards that “communicate what is right and wrong in human conduct” (p. 878) (34). Guided by this definition, we asked participants to craft arguments for why someone might consider the transition away from fossil fuels to be the right [wrong] thing to do. The only difference between the two conditions is that those in the MPT condition were asked to write in support of a stance they disagreed with coming into the study while those in the comparison condition wrote in support of their own opinion. More specifically, those in the MPT condition were given the following prompt:

In the space below, please write a short essay (2–3 paragraphs) outlining why someone might believe that transitioning the U.S. economy to 100% renewable energy by 2050 is the right thing to do. Your task is to make the strongest and most compelling argument you can in favor of the transition to renewable energy, even if you don't personally support this position.

In contrast, those in the comparison condition were given the following prompt:

In the space below, please write a short essay (2-3 paragraphs) outlining why someone might believe that transitioning the U.S. economy to 100% renewable energy by 2050 is the wrong thing to do. Your task is to make the strongest and most compelling argument you can against the transition to renewable energy, even if you don't personally support this position.

They then responded to survey items about their views on the topic and openness to different perspectives. We expected that, compared to the comparison condition, those in the MPT condition would report (i) more support for a clean energy policy (policy support) (H1a), (ii) greater intentions to take action in support of a clean energy policy (behavioral intentions) (H1b), (iii) more cognitive empathy, defined as taking the “mental perspective of others, allowing one to make inferences about their mental or emotional states” (35) (p. 727) (cognitive empathy) (H1c), and (iv) greater willingness to consider different perspectives (perspective-taking) (H1d).

We conducted a series of means comparisons to test for differences in the outcome variables between the two experimental conditions. Across all four studies, we present bias corrected and accelerated bootstrapped confidence intervals for policy support and behavioral intentions because these variables did not meet the assumption of normality (see Table S1). Otherwise, one-sided t tests are reported, given the directional nature of our hypotheses. When Levene's tests for equality of variances were significant (P < 0.05), we report results with equal variance not assumed. In support of H1a, we found a significant difference in policy support between conditions such that those in the MPT condition expressed significantly more policy support (M = −1.97, SD = 1.58) than those in the comparison condition (M = −2.44, SD = 1.34, t (199) = −2.36, P = 0.016, BCa 95% CI [−0.871, −0.081], d = 0.32). Further in support of H1c, there was a significant difference in cognitive empathy between conditions such that those in the MPT condition expressed more cognitive empathy for those with differing views on the topic (M = 1.24, SD = 1.56) than those in the comparison condition (M = 0.85, SD = 1.66, t (213) = −1.75, P = 0.042, 95% CI [−0.816, 0.050], d = 0.24). No significant differences were detected for behavioral intentions (MPT: M = −2.22, SD = 1.24; Comparison: M = −2.40, SD = 1.15, t (213) = −1.15, P = 0.259, BCa 95% CI [−0.519, 0.147], d = 0.16) or perspective-taking (MPT: M = 0.85, SD = 1.39; Comparison: M = 0.85, SD = 1.36, t (213) = 0.009, P = 0.496, 95% CI [−0.368, 0.372], d = 0.001) (see Fig. 1). Thus, H1b and H1d were not supported.

Mean score of the four outcome variables by experimental condition for study 1. Note: Error bars represent 95% CI. All variables were measured on 7-point scales from −3 to 3 with 0 as the midpoint. Policy support (A) and behavioral intentions (B) were measured on likelihood scales, cognitive empathy (C) from “not at all” to “completely,” and perspective-taking (D) on an agreement scale.

Study 2

The goal of study 2 was to replicate the results of study 1, while testing a new potential outcome variable: resistance to opinion change. We also removed the measure of perspective-taking and added measured manipulation checks (see the Methods section below). We recruited a sample of 197 participants (Mage = 46, 54% men, 84% white) prescreened for their opposition to the transition away from fossil fuels. Participants were randomly assigned to the MPT condition (N = 97) or the comparison condition (N = 100) and followed the same procedures as described for study 1.

We proposed the following preregistered hypotheses for study 2a (https://osf.io/869ph/overview?view_only=e1766578e6214a8d98f7dbea886fec01):

H1: Participants in the MPT condition will express (1) more support for a clean energy policy (H1a), (2) more cognitive empathy for people who disagree with them on the issue (H1b), and (3) less resistance to opinion change (H1c) compared to those in the comparison condition.

In support of H1a and H1b, there was a significant difference in policy support and cognitive empathy between conditions. Specifically, those in the MPT condition expressed more policy support (M = −1.44, SD = 1.87) than those in the comparison condition (M = −1.92, SD = 1.55, t (186.46) = −1.95, P = 0.044, BCa 95% CI [−0.950, 0.013], d = 0.28). Likewise, those in the MPT condition expressed more cognitive empathy (M = 1.28, SD = 1.38) than those in the comparison condition (M = 0.83, SD = 1.57, t (195) = −2.16, P = 0.016, 95% CI [−0.873, −0.039], d = 0.31). No significant differences were detected for behavioral intentions (MPT: M = −1.81, SD = 1.47; Comparison: M = −2.00, SD = 1.40, t (195) = −0.95, P = 0.342, BCa 95% CI [−0.591, 0.200], d = 0.13) or resistance to opinion change (MPT: M = 0.68, SD = 1.36; Comparison: M = 0.92, SD = 1.48, t (195) = 1.18, P = 0.119, 95% CI [−0.161, 0.640], d = 0.17), thus, H1c was not supported (Fig. 2).

Policy support (A) and behavioral intentions (B) were measured on likelihood scales, cognitive empathy (C) from “not at all” to “completely,” and resistance to opinion change (D) on an agreement scale.

Study 3

The goal of study 3 was to replicate the results of the first two studies using a different environmental issue: support for a ban on hydraulic fracturing (fracking). We also included two new measures. These were a measure of presupport for the environmental policy to test for pre–post intervention change in policy support and a dichotomous (1 = yes, 0 = no) measure of donation behavior. For this measure, we gave participants the option to donate some or all of a $25 raffle prize to an environmental organization.

A sample of 302 participants (Mage=44, 55% men, 81.5% white) prescreened for their opposition to a ban on fracking completed an online survey in which they were randomly assigned to the MPT condition (N = 150) or the comparison condition (N = 152) after reading a short factsheet on the pros and cons of fracking. We adapted the study materials to the issue of fracking and otherwise followed the same procedures as used in studies 1 and 2. We preregistered the following hypothesis for study 3 (https://osf.io/sf8ak/?view_only=4788e22890e547e49b37b766e5b11850):

H1: Participants who write a (counter-attitudinal) MPT essay will show more support for a fracking ban (H1a), greater cognitive empathy toward those who hold different opinions (H1b), and less resistance to opinion change (H1c) compared to those in the comparison condition.

In support of H1a, we found a significant difference between conditions for policy support such that those in the MPT condition expressed more policy support (M = −1.19, SD = 1.88) than those in the comparison condition (M = −1.79, SD = 1.64, t (293.60) = −2.97, P = 0.005, BCa 95% CI [−0.995, −0.205], d = 0.34). While, based on the results of studies 1 and 2, we did not predict a main effect on behavioral intentions, in this study, we did find that those in the MPT condition expressed greater intentions to take action in support of a fracking ban (M = −1.24, SD = 1.81) than those in the comparison condition (M = −1.77, SD = 1.53, t (290.23) = −2.78, P = 0.009, BCa 95% CI [−0.905, −0.194], d = 0.32). Further, in support of H1c the intervention led to less resistance to opinion change, compared to the comparison (MPT: M = 0.46, SD = 1.43; Comparison: M = 0.72, SD = 1.32, t (300) = 1.66, P = 0.049, 95% CI [−0.048, 0.575], d = 0.19). We did not, however, find a significant increase in cognitive empathy among those in the MPT condition, compared to the comparison, thus failing to support H1c (MPT: M = 1.16, SD = 1.39; Comparison: M = 1.14, SD = 1.27, t (300) = −0.16, P = 0.435, 95% CI [−0.327, 0.276], d = 0.02). Finally, there was no difference in donation behavior between conditions (MPT: 16.0% yes, Comparison: 16.6% yes, χ2 (1) = 0.017, P = 0.896) (Fig. 3).

Policy support (A) and behavioral intentions (B) were measured on likelihood scales, cognitive empathy (C) from “not at all” to “completely” and resistance to opinion change (D) on an agreement scale.

Pre–post intervention change

We conducted a 2 (condition: MPT vs. comparison) × 2 (time: pre vs. post) mixed ANOVA to assess change in policy support for people in each condition. The model was significant (F (1, 295) = 32.611, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.10). Critically, the condition by time interaction was also significant indicating that change in policy support from pre- to post differs by condition (F (1, 295) = 12.16, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.04). Specifically, those in the MPT condition increased their support for a fracking ban following the intervention (presupport: M = −1.99, SD = 1.21, postsupport: M = −1.17, SD = 1.90,b) to a significantly larger degree than those in the comparison group (presupport: M = −1.98, SD = 1.19, postsupport: M = −1.78, SD = 1.64). These results indicate that the differences in policy support reported above were driven by the much larger change among those in the MPT condition, compared to those in the comparison condition.

Study 4

The goal of study 4 was to replicate the results of previous studies among people who hold the proenvironmental stance. We expected the MPT intervention (in this case writing an essay in opposition to a fracking ban) to have similar effects among proenvironmental individuals such that writing a morally grounded counter-attitudinal essay would serve to moderate their views on the topic, make them less resistant to opinion change and, potentially, more empathetic toward people who hold alternate views from their own. A sample of 301 participants (Mage = 41, 54% men, 65.4% white) prescreened for their support of a fracking ban were randomly assigned to the MPT condition (N = 150) or the comparison condition (M = 151). We followed the same procedures as described for study 3 with the exception that the donation behavior variable was dropped. We preregistered the following hypotheses (https://osf.io/5p8t9/overview?view_only=c1049c6682514a11a7bd7496b8356902):

H1: Participants who write a (counter-attitudinal) MPT essay will show less support for a fracking ban (H1a), greater cognitive empathy toward those who hold different opinions (H1b), and less resistance to opinion change (H1c) compared to those in the comparison condition.

In support of H1a, we found a significant difference between conditions in policy support such that those in the MPT condition expressed less support for the fracking ban (M = 0.73, SD = 1.95) than those in the comparison condition (M = 1.83, SD = 1.36, t (267.75) = 5.70, P < 0.001, BCa 95% CI [0.722, 1.474], d = 0.66). Likewise, those in the MPT condition expressed lower intentions to take action in support of a fracking ban (M = 0.14, SD = 1.77) than those in the comparison condition (M = 0.88, SD = 1.56, t (299) = 3.82, P < 0.001, BCa 95% CI [0.373, 1.095], d = 0.44), and less resistance to opinion change (MPT: M = 0.46, SD = 1.48; Comparison: M = 0.78, SD = 1.41, t (299) = 1.96, P = 0.026, 95% CI [0.002, 0.654], d = 0.23) (supporting H1c). The difference in cognitive empathy between conditions was only marginally significant, thus failing to fully support H1b (MPT: M = 1.47, SD = 1.25; Comparison: M = 1.26, SD = 1.42, t (299) = 1.38, P = 0.085, 95% CI: [−0.515, 0.092], d = 0.16) (Fig. 4).

Pre–post intervention change

We conducted a 2 (condition: MPT vs. comparison) × 2 (time: pre vs. post) mixed ANOVA to assess change in policy support for people in each conditionc. The model was significant (F (1, 294) = 43.58, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.13). Critically, the condition by time interaction was also significant indicating the change in policy support from pre- to post differs by condition (F (1, 294) = 36.23, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.11). Specifically, those in the MPT condition decreased their support for a fracking ban following the intervention (presupport: M = 2.04, SD = 1.00, postsupport: M = 0.72, SD = 1.95), to a significantly larger degree than those in the comparison group (presupport: M = 1.89, SD = 0.99, postsupport: M = 1.83, SD = 1.36). Again, these results indicate that the differences in policy support reported above were driven by those in the MPT condition moderating their views on the topic.

Discussion

Across four studies, we found consistent evidence that MPT can help people moderate their views on a divisive environmental topic, thus helping to decrease polarization. All studies found significant effects of MPT on policy support while studies 3 and 4 found that this change can be attributed to shifts in views among those in the MPT conditions (rather than the comparison condition). These findings align with other research on self-persuasion (23–27), which has generally found that counter-attitudinal argument generation can be a useful tool for changing attitudes across a wide range of issues and topics. We build on this prior work in two key ways. First, we tested the effectiveness of counter-attitudinal argument generation in a novel (ie not before tested) context—that of reducing polarization around climate policy in the Unites States. Further, by adding a self-generated form of moral reframing we sought to enhance the intervention such that—beyond simply moderating opinions—it would also increase openness to people with different perspectives. As we explain below, in this latter aim, we were only partially successful.

The effect of MPT on other outcome variables was inconsistent across studies. In studies 1 and 2, we found a significant effect of MPT on cognitive empathy. However, the effect on cognitive empathy was not significant in study 3 and only marginally significant in study 4. We additionally found a significant effect of MPT on resistance to opinion change in studies 3 and 4, but not study 2 (it was not measured in study 1). This pattern of findings suggests that MPT has the potential to increase openness to different perspectives but does not seem to do so consistently. The discrepancies across our studies may be attributable to the abstract versus more concrete context of the intervention prompts (ie fossil fuels broadly in the case of studies 1 and 2 vs. fracking specifically in the case of studies 3 and 4). The more abstract context of the former may have triggered affective reasoning while the more concrete context of the latter may have triggered cognitive reasoning because in the latter case participants have more information upon which to draw. These cognitive and affective pathways have empirical support in perspective taking studies, but when they are enacted remains unclear (36). Future research that varies the concreteness of the intervention prompts can help clarify these underlying mechanisms.

Finally, we found significant effects of MPT on behavioral intentions in studies 3 and 4. However, there was no effect of MPT on this variable in studies 1 and 2 nor on the measure of donation behavior in study 3. In general, it tends to be easier to influence internal states, such as policy support, than it is to change behavior. This may particularly be so in the present case since—in three of our four studies—we measured behavioral responses that conflicted with participants’ prestudy policy positions. A limitation of this pattern of findings is that the study design obscures the direction of the effects (eg for study 3 did the MPT condition increase behavioral intentions among participants, or rather, did writing in support of one's own position decrease behavioral intentions?). Future work can include a pure control group to clarify this question.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the strength of MPT lies in helping people recognize the merits of specific arguments they may have otherwise dismissed, and may sometimes increase openness to perspectives of those holding opposing viewpoints. The inconsistency of the findings on openness to differing perspectives align with Ogbeche et al. (37), which found no effects of the intervention in terms of openness to differing viewpoints in an in-person collaborative setting. Rather, the cognitive effort required to come up with what the participant themself would consider to be convincing arguments to support a stance they disagree with may have helped people experience new realizations or insights that put their prior assumptions or convictions in doubt, thus leading them to moderate their own views. This observation is consistent with prior research, which has linked cognitive elaboration to attitude change and even behavior change (38, 39). This pattern of findings is also consistent with the idea that MPT may serve to decrease attitude certainty, even if it does not lead people to entirely switch positions on an issue. For example, prior research has found people are more certain in their attitudes when they feel that (among other factors) their attitudes are based on complete and accurate information (40). It's possible that the insights and realizations gained through the MPT exercise made people question the extent to which their previously held attitudes were based on complete and/or accurate information, thus decreasing attitude certainty. Future research can include measures of attitude certainty to explore this proposition.

As we work to solve urgent environmental challenges such as climate change, we suggest that MPT can be used as a flexible tool to reduce polarization that is adaptable across a wide range of settings. Furthermore, MPT is easier to scale than some of the other strategies for decreasing polarization such as deliberative democracy-based approaches, which require more involved processes. From a practical standpoint, we see several promising applications of MPT. For example, it can be incorporated as an introductory exercise in facilitated, multistakeholder group settings (eg policy-centered focus groups, public energy siting hearings, etc.) where polarized issues are being considered or discussed. Additionally, individuals can use MPT as an independent exercise when they are engaging in conversations with partisans or as a self-check to reduce unrecognized, overly polarized views they may hold.

These findings should be considered in light of several limitations. First, while the overarching intention of the intervention was to evaluate the combined effect of moral reframing and counter-attitudinal argument generation, we recognize that the inability to parse the effects of the two components of the intervention limits our ability to understand what is driving the outcomes. Future studies can be designed to isolate the effect of moral framing from counter-attitudinal argument generation. Additionally, our online experimental design is limited in external validity and does not allow us to rule out the possibility of experimenter demand effects. Future research should test the MPT intervention in face-to-face settings with consequential outcomes as well as evaluate the longevity of the effects. Additionally, examining the congruence between individuals’ moral foundations and the moral content of the arguments they make and the relationship between this congruence and the effectiveness of the intervention is another avenue for future research that would likely yield meaningful insights. We also note that the null effects of the perspective-taking measure in study 1 may be due to this variable capturing trait-level factors.

Finally, while our intervention was effective in moving policy support away from the extreme in all four studies, our effects were relatively small with mean differences in support between the MPT and comparison conditions ranging from 0.5 to 1.1 on a 7-point scale. Thus, we emphasize that the intervention served to moderate views rather than eliminate polarization entirely. We argue, however, that even this modest change can lead to positive outcomes. Researchers of environmental polarization (1, 6) have asserted that when attitudes predominately cluster at either extreme of an issue and few people hold moderate views, it becomes more difficult to reach common ground. In this way, by pulling attitudes and opinions in from the extremes, MPT can foster mindsets more conducive to collaboration, thus serving as a valuable strategy for facilitating collective action and environmental problem solving.

Materials and methods

All studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Southern Illinois University Carbondale, protocol #22193 and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study 1

Participants and procedures

A sample of 1,000 self-identified conservative participants, recruited from Prolific.co were prescreened for their views on the transition away from fossil fuels by responding to the following question: “To what extent do you agree or disagree with the following statement: The U.S. economy should transition from fossil fuels to 100% clean energy by 2050.” The prescreening sample was limited to political conservatives to more efficiently identify participants opposed to the fossil fuels transition (41). Participants responded on a scale from −3 (Strongly disagree) to 3 (Strongly agree). We invited those who responded below the midpoint on this scale (0) to participate in the full experiment. In total, 460 participants scored between −3 and −1 on this measure (M = −0.24, SD = 1.94). Of those, 223 responded to the full survey experiment (48% response rate). The prescreening survey also included demographic measures including age, gender, political ideology, race, and education.

We dropped eight of the 223 participants who completed the experiment for failing the instructional attention check (please select “somewhat disagree” as your response to this item). This left us with a final sample of 215. A post hoc sensitivity analysis conducted in G*Power revealed that this sample size was sufficient for a t test to detect a small to medium effect (d = 0.34) at 80% power. The mean age of participants was 45 years old (with ages ranging from 19 to 77 years). Slightly over half of the sample (52%) identified as men and the vast majority as white or Caucasian (94%). There were two participants (1.0%) who identified as black or African American, three (1.5%) as American Indian, Eskimo or Aleut, three (1.5%) as Asian or Pacific Islander, three (1.5%) as multiracial, and two (1.0%) as other. Slightly over half of the sample (55%) reported having a bachelor's degree or higher. Finally, as expected, the sample skewed highly conservative with a mean of 6.0 (SD = 0.84) on a seven-point scale of political ideology (1 = extremely liberal to 7 = extremely conservative).

Those who participated in the full experiment read and agreed to an online consent form before advancing to an online survey. The survey began by asking them to read a short factsheet on the pros and cons of the transition away from fossil fuels, adapted from the website “Clean Energy Ideas” (the full text of the factsheet is available here: https://osf.io/tqf7c/overview?view_only=54fbaebaa257433eaf8ee66b3cda418b). It was not designed to persuade people in a particular direction but rather to (i) provide a basic foundational knowledge for people who knew little about the issue prior to joining the study and (ii) provide balanced facts-based information participants could use to craft their arguments when writing their essay. After reading the factsheet, participants were randomly assigned to either the MPT condition (N = 102) or to the comparison condition (N = 113), each of which involved writing a short, morally grounded essay either in support of (MPT condition) or in opposition to (comparison condition) the transition away from fossil fuels. After writing their essay, participants responded to a set of survey items to measure (i) support for a clean energy policy (policy support), (ii) intentions to take action in support of a clean energy policy (behavioral intentions), (iii) cognitive empathy for people with different views on the topic (cognitive empathy), and (iv) willingness to consider different perspectives on the topic (perspective-taking). Exploratory measures were included in the survey to assess engagement with the factsheet and information processing. However, since all participants engaged with the factsheet before participating in the intervention, these two measures are not analyzed as outcome variables in the study.

A comparison of preintervention support for the transition away from fossil fuels (ie the prescreening variable) and basic demographics—age, education, gender, and race—between conditions found no differences in these variables (all ps > 0.10), indicating successful randomization.

Measured variables

We included four measured outcome variables: policy support, behavioral intentions, cognitive empathy, and perspective-taking. A description of how each was measured is provided below.

Policy support

Policy support was measured with a single item. Participants were asked how likely they would be to vote in support of a policy to transition the US economy to 100% renewable energy by the year 2050. They responded on a 7-point scale from −3 = extremely unlikely to 3 = extremely likely with 0 as the midpoint (neither likely nor unlikely).

Behavioral intentions

Behavioral intentions was measured with four items. Participants were asked how likely they would be to engage in each of the following behaviors in support of a policy to transition the US economy to 100% renewable energy by the year 2050: (i) vote for a candidate who supports this policy, (ii) write to their representative in support of this policy, (iii) share an article on social media in support of this policy, and (iv) talk to their friends and family about the importance of this policy. Items were measured on a 7-point scale from −3 = not at all likely to 3 = extremely likely with 0 as the midpoint (neither likely nor unlikely) (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.91).

Cognitive empathy

Cognitive Empathy was measured with three items adapted from the perspective-taking dimension of the interpersonal reactivity index developed by Davis (42). Thus, the measure aligns with our conceptualization of cognitive empathy described above as it focuses on taking the perspective of others. Participants were asked to what extent each of the following considerations factor into their stated level of support for the renewable energy policy: (i) I tried to think about everybody's side of the issue before I made my decision about whether to support this policy, (ii) I did not waste time thinking about other people's arguments on this issue (reverse coded), and (iii) I believe there are two sides to the issue and tried to look at both before making my decision. Items were measured on a 7-point scale from −3 = not at all to 3 = completely (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.84). A fourth item was included in the survey (I tried to understand people who might disagree with me about this issue by imagining how things look from their perspective), but was dropped from the scale in this study because it substantially reduced the internal consistency of the scale (Cronbach's Alpha = −0.099 with the item included).

Perspective taking

In addition to the cognitive empathy measure described above, we tested an alternative measure of perspective-taking that combined some aspects of empathetic perspective taking (42) with a measure of social curiosity adapted from Kashdan et al. (43). Perspective taking has been found to be affected by interventions with effects possible on reasoning, empathy, and behavior in intergroup settings (36). Participants were asked to what extent they agreed or disagreed with the following statements about the transition to renewable energy (from −3 = strongly disagree to 3 = strongly agree): (i) Before criticizing somebody for their stance of the issue I would try to imagine how I would feel if I were in their place, (ii) if I disagreed with someone, I would try to “put myself in their shoes” for a while, (iii) I find it difficult to see things from the “other person's” point of view on the topic of renewable energy (reverse coded), (iv) If I were talking to someone about this issue, I would ask a lot of questions to understand their point of view, (v) If I were talking to someone who is excited about this issue, I would be curious to find out why and, (vi) I’m interested to know why other people feel the way they do about this issue (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.89).

Essay coding

Our intervention was based on the assumption that people would be prompted to come up with morally grounded reasons someone might think differently than they do on an issue. To check this assumption, we coded the essays for morally grounded arguments using Moral Foundations Theory as a guide (44). Specifically, we identified language embedded in each argument related to (i) care, (ii) loyalty, (iii) fairness, (iv) authority and, (v) purity. We found that 77% of participants used at least one morally grounded argument in their short essay, broadly demonstrating that being prompted to consider issues in terms of “right” and “wrong” triggers moral thinking for most people. A chi-square analysis found that this did not differ between conditions such that approximately the same percentage of participants used at least one moral argument in each of the two conditions (χ2 (1) = 2.029, P = 0.154).

Study 2

Participants and procedures

We prescreened 1,606 participants on Prolific.co for their views on the transition away from fossil fuels. Of those, 318 scored below the midpoint (0) on same measure of presupport used in prescreening survey for study 1. Note that while we oversampled for conservative participants (N = 926 self-identified conservatives out of 1,606 total participants), we did not limit our sample only to conservatives because we incorporated the presurvey into a larger study on environmental attitudes in which we aimed to collect data from a more representative sample of participants. For reference, only 15 self-identified liberal participants expressed opposition to the transition away from fossil fuels. This change is a slight deviation from our preregistration in which we initially planned to screen a sample of 1,000 self-identified conservatives rather than incorporate the screening question into a larger survey. We have no reason to think this deviation in screening methods influenced the experimental results.

Of the 318 participants invited to participate in the full experiment, we received completed responses from 198 (63% response rate). No participants failed the instructional attention check (please select “agree” as your response to this item). However, one participant was excluded from the analysis for missing data on key variables (policy support and behavioral intentions), leaving us with a final sample of 197. A post hoc sensitivity analysis conducted in G*Power revealed that this sample size was sufficient for a t test to detect a small to medium effect (d = 0.36) at 80% power. One hundred participants were randomly assigned to the comparison condition and 97 to the MPT condition. The mean age of participants was 46 years old (with ages ranging from 20–77 years). As before, slightly over half of the sample (54%) identified as men and a large majority as white or Caucasian (84%). There were 11 participants (5.6%) who identified as black, nine (4.5%) as Asian or Pacific Islander, six (3.0%) as multiracial, and five (2.5%) as other (one participant failed to respond). In terms of education, half of the sample (50.5%) reported having a bachelor's degree or higher. Finally, the sample skewed highly conservative with a mean of 5.73 (SD = 1.21) on a seven-point scale of political ideology (1 = extremely liberal to 7 = extremely conservative). The experimental procedures and interventions were the same as described for study 1. A comparison of preintervention support for the transition away from fossil fuels (ie the screening variable) and basic demographics between conditions found no differences in these variables (all ps > 0.10), indicating successful randomization.

Measures

Policy support, cognitive empathyd (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.86) and behavioral intentions (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.94) were measured as described in study 1. Resistance to opinion change was measured with two items measured on a 7-point scale from −3 = strongly disagree to 3 = strongly agree with 0 as the midpoint (neither agree nor disagree): (i) There is no argument that would change my opinion on the topic of fossil fuels and (ii) I am confident in my opinion on the topic of fossil fuels (r = 0.56) (45).

As with study 1, we coded the essays to evaluate whether participants employed moral reasoning in their essays. Our coding revealed that 88.4% of participants used at least one morally grounded argument in their short essay. A chi-square analysis found that moral argumentation did not differ between conditions such that approximately the same percentage of participants used at least one moral argument in each of the two conditions (χ2 (1) = 2.253, P = 0.133).

We included two additional measured items as manipulation checks (measured on a 7-point scale): (i) As you wrote your essay, how deeply did you think about arguments in favor of the transition away from fossil fuels? and (ii) As you wrote your essay, how deeply did you think about arguments in opposition to the transition away from fossil fuels? If the manipulation worked as intended, we expected those in the MPT condition to indicate that they thought more deeply about arguments in favor of the transition away from fossil fuels and less deeply about arguments in opposition to the transition, compared to those in the comparison condition. The t tests confirmed that those in the MPT condition thought more deeply about arguments in favor of the transition away from fossil fuels (M = 5.46, SD = 1.47) relative to those in the comparison condition (M = 4.16, SD = 1.99, t (182.23) = −5.23, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−1.796, −0.812], d = 0.74). In contrast, those in the comparison condition reported thinking more deeply about arguments in opposition to the transition away from fossil fuels (M = 5.54, SD 1.55) relative to those in the MPT condition (M = 4.84, SD = 1.80, t (195) = 2.95, P = 0.002, 95% CI [0.234, 1.176], d = 0.42).

Study 3

Participants and procedures

We prescreened 2,001 participants on Prolific.co for their views on hydraulic fracturing (fracking). Specifically, we asked participants to respond to the following question: “To what extent do you agree or disagree with the following statement? The US government should ban fracking by the year 2035” (−3 = strongly disagree to +3 = strongly agree). Of the initial sample, 781 scored below the midpoint on this scale and were invited to participate in the full experiment. To efficiently identify a sufficient number of people who opposed a fracking ban, we oversampled self-identified conservatives (there were 1,165 self-identified conservatives in the sample of 2,001). Based on the results of an a priori power analysis conducted in G*Power (powered for a t test with an effect size of 0.3, alpha = 0.05, power = 0.8) we closed the experiment after receiving 300 completed responses. Due to the real-time nature of data collection on Prolific, we ended with a final sample of 302 (N (MPT) = 150, N (comparison) = 152). No participants failed the instructional attention check (please select “agree” as your response to this item). The mean age of participants was 44 years old (with ages ranging from 19 to 80 years). Slightly over half of the sample (55%) identified as men and a large majority as white or Caucasian (81.5%). There were 27 participants (8.9%) who identified as black or African American, 13 (4.3%) as Asian or Pacific Islander, 3 (1%) as American Indian, Eskimo or Aleut, 4 (1.3%) as multiracial, and 4 (1.3%) as other (5 people did not respond). In terms of education, over half of the sample (58.2%) reported having a bachelor's degree or higher. Finally, the sample skewed highly conservative with a mean of 5.42 (SD = 1.41) on a seven-point scale of political ideology (1 = extremely liberal to 7 = extremely conservative).

The procedures were the same as those described for studies 1 and 2 with a few minor modifications. First, all material was adapted to focus on the issue of fracking. This included the question stems for key variables as well as the factsheet on the pros and cons of fracking, which was excerpted from an article published in the Encyclopedia Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/procon/fracking-debate#ref391654). Second, we added two variables to more thoroughly understand the impact of the intervention. These were (i) a preintervention measure of policy support, included in the prescreening survey, to allow us to test for pre–post intervention change in policy support and (ii) a dichotomous measure of donation behavior, to test the effect of the intervention on a consequential behavior. Finally, we added the word ethical [unethical] to the intervention language to better trigger moral-based thinking (“In the space below, please write a short essay (2-3 paragraphs) outlining why someone might believe that banning fracking is the wrong and unethical [right and ethical] thing to do”). Our goal was to strengthen this aspect of the intervention as, according to our theorizing, the more people are able to situate arguments in their own moral frame of reference, the more intuitively compelling their reasoning will feel (33). A comparison of preintervention support for a fracking ban (ie the prescreening variable) and basic demographics between conditions found no differences in these variables (all ps > 0.10), indicating successful randomization.

Measures

Policy support, cognitive empathy (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.82), behavioral intentions (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.93) and Resistance to opinion change (r = 0.58), and the manipulation checks were measured as described in studies 1 and 2, with items adapted slightly to align with the issue of fracking (see the online supplemental information for the complete language of all measures, Tables S2–S7). The preintervention measure of policy support was the exact same as the postintervention measure of policy support. Donation behavior was measured by entering participants into a raffle to win a $25 bonus payment and allowing them to indicate whether they would like to donate some or all of their winnings to an environmental organization (1 = yes, 0 =no)e (46).

The essay coding revealed that 95.0% of participants used at least one morally grounded argument in their short essay. A chi-square analysis found that moral argumentation did not differ between conditions such that approximately the same percentage of participants used at least one moral argument in each of the two conditions (χ2 (1) = 0.343, P = 0.558).

In support of our manipulation, t tests comparing how deeply participants thought about arguments in favor of [in opposition to] a ban on fracking between conditions showed that those in the MPT condition thought more deeply about arguments in favor of a fracking ban (M = 5.43, SD = 1.48) relative to those in the comparison condition (M = 4.72, SD = 1.96, t (287.43) = −3.68, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−1.090 −0.330], d = 0.42). In contrast, those in the comparison condition reported thinking more deeply about arguments in opposition to a fracking ban (M = 5.17, SD 1.65) relative to those in the MPT condition (M = 4.47, SD = 1.72, t (300) = 3.59, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.316, 1.080], d = 0.41).

Study 4

Participants and procedures

Study 4 followed the same procedures as those described for study 3 with the exception that only participants who were supportive of a ban on fracking were invited to participate in the experiment. We identified these participants from the same prescreening survey used for study 3. Of the 2,001 initially prescreened participants, 857 scored above the midpoint (4) on the screening question and were invited to participate in the full experiment. Informed by the same a priori power analysis conducted for study 3, we closed the experiment after receiving 300 completed responses. Due to the real-time nature of data collection on Prolific we ended with a final sample of 301 (N (MPT) = 150, N (comparison) = 151). No participants failed the instructional attention check. The mean age of participants was 41 years old (with ages ranging from 19 to 81 years). As with the other samples, a slight majority of the participants (54%) identified as men. However, the sample was somewhat more racially diverse than previous studies with only 65.4% identifying as white or Caucasian (81.5%), 18.3% as black or African American, 11.0% as Asian or Pacific Islander, 2.7% as multiracial, 0.3% as American Indian, Eskimo or Aleut, and 0.7% as other (five people did not respond). In terms of education, a clear majority (63.4%) reported having a bachelor's degree or higher. Finally, the sample skewed slightly liberal with a mean of 3.70 (SD = 1.90) on a seven-point scale of political ideology (1 = extremely liberal to 7 = extremely conservative). A comparison of preintervention support for fracking (ie the screening variable) and basic demographics between conditions found no significant differences in these variables (all ps > 0.05), indicating successful randomization.

Measures

Policy support, cognitive empathy (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.83) behavioral intentions (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.87), Resistance to opinion change (r = 0.52) and the manipulation checks were the same as those used in study 3. We did not include the donation behavior measure in this study.

Our essay coding found that a full 97.7% of participants used at least one morally grounded argument in their short essay. A chi-square analysis found that moral argumentation did not differ between conditions such that approximately the same percentage of participants used at least one moral argument in each of the two conditions (χ2 (1) = 0.197, P = 0.657). Next, t tests comparing how deeply participants thought about arguments in favor of [in opposition to] a ban on fracking between conditions showed that those in the MPT condition thought more deeply about arguments in opposition to a fracking ban (M = 5.40, SD = 1.65) relative to those in the comparison condition (M = 4.61, SD = 1.81, t (299) = 3.99, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.405, 1.190], d = 0.46). In contrast, those in the comparison condition reported thinking more deeply about arguments in favor of a fracking ban (M = 5.68, SD 1.53) relative to those in the MPT condition (M = 4.83, SD = 1.76, t (293.97) = −4.48, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−1.227, −0.478], d = 0.52).
