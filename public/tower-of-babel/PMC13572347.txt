Associations between death anxiety, health literacy, and illness perception in Iranian individuals with heart failure an analytical cross sectional study
Authors: Alireza Iraj, Arvin Mirshahi, Seyed Arash Mousavi, Miaad Barkabian, Meysam Khoshavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure is a chronic condition that poses a global health concern. Death anxiety, which involves fear or worry about death and dying, is common among individuals with heart failure and has been linked to negative clinical and psychological outcomes. Several factors may contribute to death anxiety; this study aims to investigate the relationship between death anxiety, health literacy, and illness perception in individuals with heart failure.MethodsThis analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from April 2024 to June 2025 among 256 individuals with physician-confirmed heart failure who were referred to a tertiary heart failure clinic in Tehran, Iran. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older and able to communicate in Persian, while individuals with a history of anxiety disorders or related pharmacological treatments were excluded. Data were collected using validated instruments, including Templer’s Death Anxiety Scale, the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire, and the Health Literacy for Iranian Adults questionnaire. Demographic data were obtained using a researcher-developed questionnaire. Statistical analyses, including independent t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and linear regression, were performed using SPSS version 22, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 50.6 ± 12.2 years; 62.9% were male and 76% had a diploma or lower. Death anxiety was high (8.07 ± 3.18), with insufficient health literacy (64.24 ± 17.61) and moderate illness perception (46.91 ± 12.43). Higher death anxiety was observed in women (9.49 ± 3.19 vs. 7.22 ± 2.89, P < 0.001), those with lower education (8.28 ± 3.17 vs. 7.37 ± 3.16, P < 0.05), insufficient income (8.68 ± 3.39 vs. 7.54 ± 2.91, P < 0.004), unemployment (9.23 ± 2.93 vs. 7.19 ± 3.09, P < 0.001), negative family history (9.52 ± 2.97 vs. 6.84 ± 2.83, P < 0.001), and in heart failure classes I–II (8.84 ± 3.19 vs. 7.41 ± 3.04 in classes III–IV, P < 0.001). Death anxiety was negatively correlated with health literacy (β = − 0.44, SE = 0.01, CI [− 0.66, − 0.22], P < 0.001) and positively correlated with negative illness perception (β = 0.058, SE = 0.016, CI [0.027, 0.089], P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis study indicates that higher health literacy and a positive illness perception can reduce death anxiety in individuals with heart failure. Targeted interventions, like educational programs and cognitive-behavioral therapies, can be effective in suppressing death anxiety. Additionally, addressing socioeconomic barriers and integrating palliative care with psychosocial support can enhance the quality of life and clinical outcomes of individuals with heart failure.Keywords: Heart failure, Death anxiety, Health literacy, Illness perception

Background

Heart failure is a chronic condition caused by structural or functional abnormalities of the heart, leading to inadequate blood supply to peripheral organs [1]. It represents a growing global health crisis [2], affecting over 64 million individuals worldwide [3] and imposing a substantial economic burden of approximately $284.17 billion on healthcare systems due to hospitalizations and treatment costs [4]. Heart failure is associated with high mortality rates, frequent hospital readmissions [5], and reduced social and physical functioning, ultimately leading to a diminished quality of life [6]. Individuals with heart failure often experience depression, stress, and anxiety related to mortality, affecting both patients and their families [7]. Individuals confronting life-threatening chronic illnesses, such as heart failure, frequently face uncertainty about their prognosis, which may contribute to death anxiety [8–10].

Death anxiety refers to the fear of mortality triggered by thoughts of dying [10], often arising from concerns about suffering, uncertainty about the afterlife, and the concept of extinction [11]. It is associated with feelings of helplessness, loss of meaning, and reduced enjoyment of life [12], negatively impacting quality of life and even life expectancy [13–15]. Moreover, death anxiety may diminish wisdom and positive life perspectives, contribute to alienation from healthcare providers, and reduce trust in the treatment team [16, 14].

In patients with heart failure, death anxiety can further compromise quality of life and worsen an already fragile prognosis [17, 18]. Its severity is influenced by multiple factors, including demographic variables (e.g., gender, age, marital status), psychological and developmental factors (e.g., ego integrity, mental health status, meaning in life), as well as sociocultural and economic conditions [10, 16, 17, 19–23]. Evidence suggests that individuals with heart failure experience substantial levels of death anxiety, underscoring the importance of addressing its consequences in this population [24].

Health literacy refers to the ability of individuals to obtain, process, and understand health information and services in order to make informed health decisions [25], It is a critical factor for patients with heart failure, as it directly influences self-care and adherence to medical treatment [26]. Limited health literacy is associated with adverse health outcomes including delayed care-seeking due to poor recognition of disease progression and reduced capacity for self-monitoring [26, 27]. It is also linked to unfavorable physical outcomes, such as elevated blood pressure and higher hemoglobin A1c levels [28], as well as negative psychological consequences [29].

In contrast, higher health literacy is associated with lower levels of stress, depression, and anxiety among patients with chronic conditions, including heart failure [30]. Among these patients, inadequate health literacy has been linked to increased hospitalization rates and higher mortality risk [31, 32]. Conversely, adequate health literacy can improve treatment adherence, enhance psychological well-being, and promote better overall quality of life. Given the complex and chronic nature of heart failure, addressing health literacy remains essential for optimal disease management [28].

In individuals with heart failure, illness perception plays a significant role in shaping psychological outcomes such as anxiety and depression [33]. Illness perception refers to patients’ cognitive and emotional representations of their condition, including beliefs about symptoms, consequences, timeline, controllability, and causes [34, 35]. These perceptions are strongly associated with psychological well-being and treatment adherence, often exerting a greater influence than socio-demographic or functional factors [34, 33].

Negative illness perceptions have been linked to poorer physical, social, and role functioning, higher levels of psychological distress, and reduced well-being and vitality [36]. They may also hinder recovery and return to work by prolonging treatment, increasing absenteeism, and reducing motivation for therapy [24, 37]. In contrast, more positive illness perceptions are associated with better social functioning, improved well-being, lower distress, and more favorable disease outcomes [36]. Therefore, addressing patients’ illness perceptions is essential in the management of heart failure [38].

Despite the high prevalence of death anxiety among Iranian healthcare providers, caregivers, and patients [39–41], research on this issue in patients with heart failure remains limited. Evidence suggests that up to 70% of cardiovascular patients experience moderate to severe death anxiety [42]. highlighting its clinical importance. However, most existing studies have examined death anxiety in isolation, with limited attention to its relationship with modifiable factors such as health literacy and illness perception.

Given the global burden of heart failure and its associated mortality [3, 24, 37], identifying psychosocial factors that influence patients’ psychological outcomes is essential. Health literacy and illness perception are known to affect self-care, treatment adherence, and quality of life, yet their association with death anxiety in heart failure patients—particularly in Iran—remains underexplored. This represents a clear gap in the literature.

Therefore, this study aims to assess death anxiety, health literacy, and illness perception, and examine their relationships among patients with heart failure referred to the Heart Failure Clinic at Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex (IKHC), Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), Iran.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among patients with heart failure referred to the Heart Failure Clinic at Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex (IKHC), affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), from April 2024 to June 2025. The study was approved by the TUMS Research Ethics Committee (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1403.015) and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [45].

Study population and sampling method

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method based on predefined eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of heart failure (any classification), and ability to communicate in Persian. Exclusion criteria included a history of anxiety disorders, diagnosed psychiatric illnesses, or use of related medications. All participants received information about the study and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Study measurements

Data were collected using four instruments: [1] a researcher-developed demographic questionnaire [2], the Persian version of Templer’s Death Anxiety Scale (TDAS) [3], the Persian version of the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), and [4] the Health Literacy for Iranian Adults Questionnaire (HELIA). Demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, education, occupation, comorbidities, and number of children.

Templer’s death anxiety scale (TDAS)

The Templer Death Anxiety Scale was initially developed by Templer in 1970. It stands as the most widely utilized instrument for assessing death-related anxiety, which was translated and validated into Persian by Rajabi and Bahrani [46]. The questionnaire’s reliability in the study by Ghasempour et al. was reported as 0.65 using Cronbach’s alpha test [47]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the Templer Death Anxiety Scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which was calculated to be 0.79, indicating acceptable to good reliability in this sample. This self-administered questionnaire consists of 15 yes/no items with scores ranging from 0 to 15, where higher scores indicate greater death anxiety. A “yes” response signifies the presence of death anxiety in the individual. The cutoff score for this questionnaire is 8, with scores above this indicating a high degree of death anxiety. The total death anxiety score is calculated by assigning 1 point for a “true” response to questions 1 through 9 and 15, and 0 points otherwise. Conversely, 1 point is assigned for a “false” response to items 10 through 14, and 0 points otherwise [48].

Brief illness perception questionnaire (BIPQ)

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) was developed by Broadbent et al. in 2006 [17]. This questionnaire was translated into Persian by Saeideh Bazazian et al. in 2010, and its validity and reliability—including cross-cultural validity assessed through confirmatory factor analysis—have been confirmed [49]. A Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.8 has been reported for this questionnaire [50]. The questionnaire consists of items scored on a 0–10 scale (excluding the causal item) and assesses cognitive (consequences, timeline, personal control, treatment control, identity) and emotional (concern, emotions) representations, along with illness comprehensibility. Total scores range from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating more negative (poorer) illness perception [51, 52]. Scores of 0–27, 28–55, and 56–80 represent high, moderate, and low illness perception, respectively.

Health literacy for Iranian adults questionnaire (HELIA)

The Health Literacy for Iranian Adults Questionnaire (HELIA), developed by Montazeri in 2014, was utilized to evaluate the health literacy levels of urban Iranian individuals aged 18 to 65 years [53]. The questionnaire’s face and content validity were confirmed by 10 health experts. Reliability, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be 0.86, showing good internal consistency. Factor analyses supported the identification of five main factors consistent with the instrument’s conceptual dimensions [54]. This instrument comprises 33 items presented on a five-point Likert scale, including dimensions: reading (4 items), access (6 items), understanding (7 items), evaluation (4 items), and decision-making and application of health information (12 items)[53]. For the “reading” skill items, scoring follows a five-point Likert scale, where responses are rated as follows: “very easy” (score 5), “easy” (score 4), “neither difficult nor easy” (score 3), “difficult” (score 2), and “very difficult” (score 1). The scores for all other items are as follows: “always” (score 5), “most of the time” (score 4), “sometimes” (score 3), “rarely” (score 2), and “never” (score 1). To derive the overall score for each dimension, the raw score for each participant in that dimension is calculated and subsequently converted to a score on a 0-100 scale using a specific formula. Each Section Score [(Row score – Minimum possible) / (Maximum possible – Minimum possible)] ×100. The total health literacy score is calculated by averaging standardized scores across five key dimensions. Based on Montazeri et al.‘s classification, scores are interpreted as follows: 0 to 50 indicates inadequate health literacy, 50.1 to 66 represents not-so-sufficient health literacy, 66.1 to 84 indicates sufficient health literacy, and 84.1 to 100 reflects excellent health literacy [53].

Sample size calculation

Following a review of similar articles and utilizing the standard deviation reported in the study by Hashim et al. [55], the sample size for this study was calculated to be 278 participants, taking into account a 10% attrition rate.

Statical analysis

Descriptive analyses for qualitative data included frequency and percentage, while mean and standard deviation, and ANOVA for normally distributed quantitative data in this study. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD for continuous) characterized patient demographics and clinical features. Paired t-tests and one-way ANOVA evaluated within-subject change and between-group differences, respectively. Linear and multiple linear regression analyses were performed, reporting unstandardized β, SE, t, p, and adjusted R². Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and analyses were conducted using SPSS (v.22).

Results

Demographic information

A total of 324 individuals were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 68 individuals were excluded due to not meeting the inclusion criteria or declining to participate. Finally, 256 participants were included in the analysis. Furthermore, there were no missing data. The mean age of participants was 50.6 ± 12.2 years [Min:18, Max: 83]. 161 participants (62.9%) were male, and 195 participants (76%) had a high school diploma or lower education level. Additionally, 139 participants (54.3%) had heart failure in classes III or IV (Table 1).

Death anxiety

The mean death anxiety score was 8.07 ± 3.18. Women reported significantly higher death anxiety than men (P < 0.001). Higher levels were also observed among individuals with lower education, unemployed participants, and those with insufficient income (all P ≤ 0.05). Participants without a family history of cardiovascular disease experienced greater death anxiety (P < 0.001). Relationship between death anxiety, health literacy, and illness perception

The study results indicated a significant negative relationship between death anxiety and health literacy (β = −0.44, P < 0.001), meaning individuals with higher health literacy experienced less death anxiety. Furthermore, the study found a significant positive relationship between death anxiety and negative illness perception (β = 0.058, P < 0.001), indicating that individuals with a more negative perception of their illness experienced severe death anxiety (Table 3).

Discussion

Death anxiety is a persistent fear of dying, often driven by concerns about suffering and uncertainty regarding what follows death. This study examined death anxiety, illness perception, and health literacy among individuals with heart failure, as well as factors influencing death anxiety. The findings showed higher death anxiety among women, single individuals, those with lower education, unemployed individuals, and those with insufficient income. Additionally, individuals without a family history of cardiovascular disease and those with better functional status (NYHA classes I–II) reported greater death anxiety. A significant negative relationship was observed between death anxiety and health literacy, while a positive relationship was found between death anxiety and negative illness perception.

Our findings indicate that individuals with heart failure experience considerable levels of death anxiety. This is consistent with Çamcı et al. (2025), who reported that over 60% of such patients experience high death anxiety [56], as well as Merati et al. (2024) [18], and Asghari et al. (2018), who found moderate to severe levels in the majority of patients [17]. Evidence suggests that psychological support, education, and perception-based interventions can reduce death anxiety and improve outcomes [18, 24]. Emerging approaches, such as art-based interventions [57] and counseling programs [58], may also be beneficial. These findings highlight the need for future research focusing on intervention-based strategies, including palliative and supportive care.

Gender was a significant factor, with women experiencing higher death anxiety, consistent with prior studies [17, 59, 60]. Similar patterns have been observed in patients with chronic diseases [9, 61] and in the general population [62, 63]. However, some studies found no significant association [17, 64]. possibly due to differences in study populations or settings. Sociocultural roles, emotional expression, and caregiving responsibilities may contribute to higher anxiety in women [65, 66]. Additionally, women’s greater use of healthcare services may increase awareness of disease severity. Gender differences in expressing anxiety—verbal in women versus nonverbal in men—may also influence results [67]. To reduce death anxiety in individuals with heart failure, especially women, interventions such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and schema therapy are suggested [68, 69, 70].

Insufficient income was another significant predictor of death anxiety. This aligns with studies by Bayrak et al. (2019) [60], Ghiyasi et al. (2018) [71], and Bakhtiari et al. (2019) [62], which linked lower financial status to higher death anxiety. Financial strain, particularly concerns about treatment costs and future security, may intensify psychological distress [72]. Addressing economic challenges through social support services and financial assistance may help reduce death anxiety and improve quality of life.

Although not statistically significant, single individuals reported higher death anxiety than married individuals, consistent with the studies of Bayrak et al. (2019) [60] and Bakhtiari et al. (2019) [62] and McLeod et al. (2016) [73], though Asghari et al. (2018) report conflicting findings [17]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in social support systems. Single individuals may experience greater fear of isolation and lack of support, whereas married individuals benefit from emotional and financial support. Strengthening social support networks may therefore reduce death anxiety [74].

Unemployment was also associated with higher death anxiety, consistent with findings by Bayrak et al. [60], and Asghari et al. [17]. Conversely, a study on individuals with chronic conditions did not report a significant association between employment status and death anxiety [62]. These inconsistencies may be due to differences in study populations. In heart failure, the burden of symptoms and treatment costs may exacerbate the psychological impact of unemployment. Given the established link between unemployment and mental health disorders, providing supportive socioeconomic packages and employment and mental health programs are recommended [75].

Illness perception and death anxiety

Participants demonstrated a moderate level of illness perception. A significant positive relationship was found between death anxiety and negative illness perception. This finding is consistent with Batool et al. (2022), who reported a similar association in patients with breast cancer [76].

Likewise, Turan et al. (2024) and Cham et al. (2024) found that more negative illness perceptions were associated with higher death anxiety in patients with advanced cancer and those receiving palliative care [52, 77]. Furthermore, study by Salmanian and Marashian (2021) on individuals with breast cancer emphasized that illness perception is an important predictor of death anxiety [78]. It seems that when individuals, due to a negative perception of their illness, evaluate their condition as uncontrollable, they develop negative thoughts about their future, which can conjure images of themselves dying in their minds, leading to increased death anxiety, especially in individuals with chronic diseases. It can be suggested that to reduce death anxiety in individuals, packages aimed at educating to promote a positive, realistic, and clear understanding of their illness should be prepared. Interventions aimed at improving illness understanding and promoting more adaptive perceptions have been shown to reduce death anxiety, as reported by Babaei Vahed et al. (2022) [24] and Soleimani et al. (2018) [79]. Thus, perception-based educational programs may be beneficial.

Health literacy and death anxiety

Health literacy levels among participants were generally insufficient, consistent with findings by Ahmad Zadeh et al. (2021) [29] and Turan et al. (2024) [77]. Fabbri et al. (2022) indicated that low health literacy in individuals with heart failure is associated with a 1.91 times higher risk of death and increased hospitalizations, emphasizing the priority of improving health literacy [31, 32]. Similar findings have been reported by Merati et al. (2024) [18] and Kefeli et al. (2025) [63], as well as an study linking higher mental health literacy with lower anxiety levels [30].

Adequate health literacy is associated with improved quality of life [80], while evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of individuals with heart failure continue to have inadequate health literacy [78]. Health literacy is shaped by cultural, social, and individual factors, as well as healthcare and educational systems, which influence how individuals’ access and understand health information. Policies in these areas can create both challenges and opportunities for improving health literacy. Higher health literacy is also associated with better self-care, reduced hospitalizations, and improved prognosis, highlighting its importance in chronic disease management, including heart failure [81]. In an interventional study, Bahrami et al. (2019) demonstrated that health literacy promotion programs improved health literacy and reduced death anxiety among women with breast cancer [34]. Therefore, promoting health literacy may not only improve physical health outcomes but also reduce death anxiety. Designing targeted educational programs to enhance health literacy is strongly recommended.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it provides a comprehensive evaluation of death anxiety in individuals with heart failure by simultaneously examining its relationship with health literacy and illness perception—two modifiable and clinically relevant factors. Second, the use of validated Persian versions of standardized instruments (TDAS, BIPQ, and HELIA) enhances the reliability and cultural appropriateness of the findings. Additionally, the relatively adequate sample size and inclusion of diverse sociodemographic variables allowed for a more robust analysis of factors associated with death anxiety.

However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences between death anxiety, health literacy, and illness perception. The use of convenience sampling from a single center may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or settings. Self-reported data may also be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Finally, other potentially relevant factors, such as severity of comorbid conditions, social support, and coping mechanisms, were not assessed and may have influenced the results.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study demonstrated that individuals with heart failure experience high levels of death anxiety, have a moderate perception of their illness, and possess insufficient health literacy. Death anxiety was negatively associated with health literacy and positively associated with negative illness perception. However, no significant relationship was observed between health literacy and illness perception. These findings suggest that interventions targeting modifiable factors—particularly health literacy and illness perception—may help reduce death anxiety. Structured educational programs aimed at improving health literacy should be developed and implemented, especially for individuals at higher risk of death anxiety. In addition, psychological interventions, which have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing death anxiety, may be incorporated into routine care. Strengthening social support through family involvement, charitable resources, and social work services may also enhance psychological well-being and reduce death anxiety. Future research should move beyond observational designs and focus on developing and evaluating targeted interventions to reduce death anxiety in individuals with heart failure. Longitudinal and interventional studies are recommended to better understand causal relationships and assess the effectiveness of educational, psychological, and supportive care programs. Furthermore, exploring these relationships in diverse populations and healthcare settings would enhance the generalizability of findings.
