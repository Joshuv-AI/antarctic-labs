Sodium nitroprusside and chitosan alleviate drought stress in spinach by modulating nutrient balance, phenolics production, and photosynthesis
Authors: Faezeh Khatami, Vahid Niknam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought perturbs water potential in the plants, causing oxidants accumulation and impairing cellular functions. The mineral nutrients are critical for adjusting water potential and modulating antioxidant activity and photosynthesis. This work investigated the impact of sodium nitroprusside (SNP) and chitosan (CS) on six key nutrients (Na, K, P, Ca, Mg, Fe) in leaves of spinach under polyethylene glycol (PEG)-induced drought. The 3-leaved seedlings were irrigated with PEG (5%, 10%, and 15%) and one-day later treated by foliar spray of SNP (25 and 50 µM) and CS (15 and 30 mg/L). The physiological responses were studied by measuring the concentrations of hydrogen peroxide, malondialdehyde, chlorophylls, carotenoids, phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and nutrients using UV/Vis spectrophotometry and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry. Increasing drought intensity enhanced hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde contents. Drought stress increased Na, K, and Mg and decreased Ca and P. Iron remained constant due to its dual function in catalyzing oxidants and in activating antioxidant enzymes and photosynthesis. The SNP and CS applications enhanced photosynthesis and alleviated oxidative stress by enhanced production of phenolics and carotenoids. The elicitors caused higher Ca, P and Fe, and lower Na, K and Mg than those of non-elicited controls. Co-application of both elicitors caused the highest Fe, accompanied by the highest chlorophylls. The intricate interplay between six nutrients were critical to minimize oxidative damage and to improve photosynthetic performance. Overall, the Fe, Mg and Ca interplay were important for photosynthetic performance and antioxidant activity. Moreover, the Na, K and P interplay is essential for osmotic adjustment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58696-4.

Introduction

Spinach, Spinacea oleracea L., is a leafy green annual dicot herb belonging to Chenopodiaceae family and to Chenopodioideae subfamily1. This plant is native to central and western Asia and a popular edible vegetable (named Persian plant in written records2. Spinach possesses micro- and macro-nutrients essential for healthy human consumption including dietary fibers, vitamins (B, K, C), antioxidants, phenolics (flavonoids, anthocyanins), minerals (Ca, Fe, Mg, K, Mn, Cu, P) and other health promoting compounds. It has known for its therapeutic benefits because of anti-oxidant, anti-hypertension, anti-inflammatory, anti-aging and anti-bacterial activities1,3.

The annual production of spinach in Iran is estimated ~ 101k tons based on FAO statistics1 with an average yield of ~ 22 tons per ha, the world’s average yield is ~ 30 tons per ha 2. Drought stress has imposed adverse effects on agricultural production, especially in countries such as Iran with ~ 88% arid and semi-arid climates4. To mitigate the adverse effects of drought stress, it is critical to understand the mechanisms of plant adaptation to drought and then apply effective drought management practices.

Drought significantly changes the water potential and mineral composition of the rhizosphere, thereby impairing osmotic uptake of water by roots. This disturbance disrupts the cellular osmotic balance and induce abscisic acid, as stress signal, which initiate several protective responses particularly stomata closure to reduce water loss through transpiration5. The stomata closure is one of the first reactions of most plants to water deficit5. However, this physiological response limits CO2 uptake. Lowering intracellular CO2 concentration will restrict calvin cycle activity and decrease NADP+ regeneration through photosynthetic carbon assimilation6. Accordingly, it results in excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and consequently severe oxidative damages to cellular components7. Photoinhibition and photo-destruction of photosynthetic pigments are other consequences of oxidative stress8. Oxidative damages to lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, photosynthetic pigments and enzymes disrupt normal cell functions9.

The plant cells developed several adaptive processes to tolerate the drought depending on the growth stage, age and species of the plant as well as the intensity and duration of drought10. Two critical cellular processes for plant adaptation are photosynthesis and osmotic adjustment5. The photosynthetic apparatus generates the chemical energy for antioxidant defense processes. It also generates secondary metabolites like carotenoids to quench ROS and stabilize photosynthetic complexes11. The osmotic adjustment is mainly conducted by plant vacuoles. Moreover, the plant vacuoles produce secondary metabolites and more importantly sequester and digest the damaged components and toxic compounds. The phenolic compounds (e.g., flavonoids and anthocyanins) are the most substantial groups of secondary metabolites and antioxidants, produced from the shikimate-phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway in the vacuoles12,13. The flavonoids and anthocyanins significantly increased in pea and graph berries under drought, enabled plants to mitigate oxidative and dehydration stresses14.

Flavonoids are low molecular weight compounds and efficient chain-breaking antioxidants that can inhibit lipid peroxidation and reduce oxidative damages. Additionally, flavonoids possess anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory activities, and also beneficial for cardiovascular health upon human consumption15. Spinach is a well-known rich source of flavonoids16. Anthocyanins, a subset of flavonoids, are colored water-soluble pigments. Anthocyanins, recognized biomarkers of abiotic stress, act as signaling molecules and ROS scavengers, protecting photosynthetic machinery from photooxidation under drought stress17.

The vacuole operation is tightly regulated by transport of solutes across tonoplast. The principal solutes in vacuoles are sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), chloride (Cl−), calcium (Ca2+), phosphates (PO42−), magnesium (Mg2+), sulfate (SO42−), and nitrates (NO3−). The principal elements involved in the photosynthesis and defense processes are iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), boron (B), and molybdenum (Mo). Under drought, the concentrations of N, K, Mg, Na and Cl will increase while those of Ca, P, and Fe will decrease18.

The macro- and micro-nutrients play significant roles in cell structure, cell wall extensibility, turgor-related processes, respiration, and transpiration10,19. For example, potassium and sodium, as macronutrients, are essential ions for osmotic adjustment and also key second messengers for diverse cellular processes20. Potassium regulates the opening and closing of stomata, and controls carbohydrate metabolizing enzymes to enhance the accumulation and translocation of such osmolytes as sucrose21. Phosphorous, a macronutrient, is an important structural and functional component of biological molecules such as nucleic acids, phospholipids, and vitamins. It also plays a critical role in biological energy transfer processes, synthesis and transport of carbohydrates, nitrate reduction in roots, nuclear division and growth, transpiration rate and stomatal conductance. Magnesium, as a macronutrient and co-factor of chlorophylls, is essential for the light absorption, chlorophyll synthesis, and Rubisco activation22. Mg plays a key function in activating several enzymes, in the phloem export of photosynthetic products22, and in mediating plant tolerance to abiotic stresses23,24. Adequate Mg levels in plants is required for efficient transport of carbohydrates from source to sinks, otherwise any impairment can lead to reduced carbon fixation and consequently excessive ROS accumulation22. Mg also has the capacity to form complexes with ADP or ATP, which are the true substrates of most enzymes using adenine nucleotides25. Ca, as a macronutrient, plays a second messenger role in plant signal transduction, and mediates plant adaptation to drought by activating the plasma membrane ATPase enzyme and calcium-calmodulin26. Iron, an essential micronutrient, is co-factor of several enzymes and proteins in the electron transport chain (photosystem I, photosystem II, ferredoxins, and cytochromes), chlorophyll synthesis, and nitrogen assimilation (nitrate reductase).

To induce adaptive mechanisms in spinach, the exogenous application of sodium nitroprusside (SNP) and chitosan (CS) were reported as an effective drought management practice1. Chitosan has been used as a non-toxic, biodegradable, and biocompatible natural biopolymer for agricultural applications. It reduces the negative effects of drought through the stress transduction pathway and the use of secondary messengers. Chitosan foliar application prevents water loss by transpiration and maximizes water usage27. The SNP, as the most prevalent NO-releasing substance, mediates diverse signaling pathways involved in plant response to biotic and abiotic stresses. The antioxidant properties of NO are demonstrated by its ability to trigger antioxidant activities and reduce ROS28.

Given the prominent role of mineral nutrients in several cellular functions, the current research aimed at investigating the impact of SNP and CS on six principal nutrients (Fe, Ca, Mg, P, Na, and K) of spinach leaves under PEG-induced drought. The profile of the nutrients can lead us to cross-talks between them and how this intricate interplay can improve the plant tolerance to drought through effective performance of photosynthetic and antioxidant systems. The physiological functions of these systems were studied by assessing the contents of chlorophylls, carotenoids, phenols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins at certain levels of PEG-induced drought under applications of CS, and SNP.

Results

Oxidative status in the spinach plants

The PEG-induced drought increased the H2O2 contents and consistently raised the MDA level (Fig. 1). The highest drought intensity (PEG15%) led to the highest peroxide and MDA contents, almost 2-folds compared to those of control PEG0% (Fig. 1). The CS and SNP applications to the plants caused a significant reduction in the H2O2 and MDA contents of the plants at low drought intensities (Fig. 1). The CS and SNP had nearly similar effects. Both concentrations of CS and SNP showed identical trends on the H2O2 and MDA profiles. Higher concentrations of 30 mg L− 1 CS and 50 µM SNP were more effective in alleviating oxidative stress than the lower concentrations of 15 mg L− 1 CS and 25 µM SNP. Under PEG5%, the plants treated by either elicitor had shown similar H2O2 contents with those of PEG0% (Fig. 1a). At ≥PEG10%, the individual application of the elicitors had almost no significant effects on the peroxide contents compared to control non-elicited plants. However, the combined application of both elicitors significantly reduced the peroxide contents at all PEG levels.

The MDA contents of the elicited plants were decreased compared to control at all PEG-induced drought intensities (Fig. 1b). Higher concentrations of CS and SNP were more effective in reducing MDA contents. The highest reduction in the MDA content compared to the control was observed in the plants co-treated by 30 mg L− 1 CS and 50 µM SNP (Fig. 1b).

Photosynthetic pigments in the spinach plants

The contents of chlorophyll a, b and total chlorophylls showed a decreasing trend with increasing PEG-induced drought intensity (Fig. 2a and c). At < PEG10%, the individual applications of both CS and SNP led to higher chlorophylls than those of the non-elicited plants (CS = 0 and SNP = 0) under PEG treatment (Fig. 2). At PEG10%, the lower concentration of CS (15 mg L− 1) and SNP (25 µM) caused a significant increase in the chlorophylls compared to the non-elicited control plants, while higher concentrations of CS (30 mg L− 1) and SNP (50 µM) imposed no significant effects. At > PEG10%, application of both CS concentrations had no significant effects on the chlorophylls, while the application of both SNP concentrations slightly increased (~ 30%) the contents of chlorophylls compared to the non-elicited plants (CS = 0 and SNP = 0). The co-application of CS = 15 mg L− 1 + SNP = 25 µM and CS = 30 mg L− 1 + SNP = 50 µM synergistically increased the contents of chlorophylls almost 77% and 1.3-fold, respectively. The combined applications of CS and SNP resulted in the highest contents of chlorophylls at all PEG levels compared to the non-elicited plants. The highest chlorophylls were observed in the plants co-treated with SNP (50 µM) and CS (30 mg L− 1) under no PEG-induced drought (PEG0%). The lowest chlorophylls were measured in the non-elicited plants under PEG15%.

The contents of carotenoids raised with stepwise increase in drought intensity from PEG 0% to PEG 15% in both control and the elicited plants (Fig. 2d). The CS and SNP treatments increased the contents of carotenoids in plants under similar PEG level. Higher concentrations of CS and SNP resulted in higher carotenoid contents in the plants under the same drought condition (Fig. 2d). The highest carotenoids were measured in the plants co-treated with both elicitors under PEG15%, ~ 84% higher than the non-elicited plants.

Antioxidant pigments in the spinach plants

Total phenols, total flavonoids, and anthocyanins consistently enhanced with the increasing PEG levels, Fig. 3. The exogenous application of CS and SNP induced the production of phenols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins. Higher levels of both elicitors and also their combined application resulted in higher levels of these antioxidants. Both CS and SNP had similar induction effects. The moderate levels of either elicitors (25 µM SNP or 15 mg L− 1 CS) led to nearly identical enhancements in the metabolites. The combined application had a synergistic effect, led to approximately two-fold enhancement compared to control (Fig. 3). The highest metabolite contents were observed in the spinach plants co-treated with 50 µM SNP and 30 mg L− 1 CS under PEG15% (Fig. 3).

Mineral composition in the spinach plants

Based on preliminary experiments and literature evidence1,29,30, different concentrations of CS and SNP were screened to identify the effective dose for mitigating drought stress in spinach plants. Although both concentrations showed similar trends in regulating oxidative stress, photosynthetic activity, and phenolic metabolism, the plants exposed to CS at 30 mg L− 1 and SNP at 50 µM had greater physiological improvement under drought stress conditions (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Therefore, these concentrations were selected for subsequent nutrient analysis (Table 1) to further elucidate the physiological mechanisms of drought tolerance from the perspective of mineral nutrient composition and homeostasis.

The PEG-induced drought significantly increased the contents of intracellular Na, K, and Mg, while there was a singificant decrease in the contents of Ca, P and Fe (Table 1). There was almost 25% enhancement in the former elements only upon exposure to PEG15%. The Ca and P showed a ~ 27% reduction only upon exposure to the highest PEG15% treatment. Fe had the lowest reduction (6%) and remained almost constant (Table 1).

Under SNP treatment, the contents of Na, K, and Mg first raised in the plants and then remained constant with increasing drought intensities. On the contrary, the content of P and Fe first decreased and then remained constant without any significant changes (Table 1). Under CS treatment, the contents of Na, K, and Mg increased monotonically with increasing drought intensities. However, the contents of Ca and P continuously decreased with stepwide increase in PEG level of the plants. The effects of combined elicitors (CS + SNP) was similar to the plants exposed only to CS elicitor.

Comparing the nutrient contents in the elicited and non-elicited plants under each PEG level, it was found that the application of CS and SNP significantly decreased the contents of Na, K, and Mg and significantly increased the contents of Ca, P and Fe (Table 1). In this regard, the effect of CS in decreasing the Na and Mg contents and increasing the Ca, P and Fe contents was greater than SNP, while the SNP had greater effect than CS on the K content. The highest contents of Ca, P, and Fe and the lowest contents of Na, K, and Mg were observed in the control plants co-treated with both SNP (50 µM) and CS (30 mg L− 1) elicitors. The co-application of both CS and SNP had a synergistic effect on the Fe contents, with approximetly 5-fold increase compared to the plants under PEG-induced drought (Table 1).

Discussion

Drought has a significant effect on water potential and mineral composition surrounding the plant roots5. It will decrease the plant water contents (Supplementary Fig. S1), lead to excessive accumulation of ROS (Fig. 1a), and consequently damage to the cellular components such as membranes and photosynthetic system. The oxidative damage impairs the physiological functions or, at high intensities, induces the programmed cell death31. The chlorophyll biosynthesis and the activity of enzymes in the Calvin cycle will be reduced in the damaged chloroplasts32. Consistently, there was a decrease in the chlorophylls and an increase in the MDA contents (Fig. 1b) under increasing drought intensity (Fig. 2a and c).

The increase in the total carotenoids (Fig. 2d) and phenolics (Fig. 3) under drought can be attributed to the antioxidant activity of these metabolites. Carotenoids have a protective role against ROS and protect chlorophyll against photooxidation11. Phenolics, in particular flavonoids and anthocyanins, are essential non-enzymatic antioxidants for alleviating drought stress. These major group of antioxidants play a key role in scavenging free radicals, stabilizing cell membranes, and preventing lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing drought resistance12. The contents of phenol, flavonoids, and anthocyanins in the plants elicited by either CS and SNP were almost equal under PEG 5% and PEG 0% (Fig. 3), in agreement with the contents of hydrogen peroxide (Fig. 1a) and the relative water contents (Supplementary Fig. S1) which were nearly equal at the conditions as well. Noteworthy, there was also an increase in the antioxidant enzymes under drought (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The physiological responses can be correlated with the significant changes in the nutrients of the plant cells. The damaged lipid membranes are unable to regulate transport across membranes. Additionally, the plants actively alter the uptake of mineral nutrients to sustain cellular homeostasis and essential metabolic processes under the adverse drought conditions33. Also, the interaction of minerals significantly affects the nutrient composition34. The exogenous application of SNP and CS significantly affected the nutrient uptakes possibly through induction of osmo-protective agents and hormones, activation of ion-receptor genes, and improvement of photosynthesis and antioxidant activity with notable consequences on plant physiology16.

K is required for maintaining photosynthetic CO2 fixation and carbohydrate metabolism, and so protecting chloroplast from oxidative damage35. Adequate K minimizes ROS generation by virtue of activating a series of antioxidant enzymes. The accumulation of the Na and K ions in vacuoles is a cost-effective choice for plants to adjust the water potential under drought33. Agreeably, there were significant increases in Na and K concentrations under PEG-induced drought (Table 1). Consistently, sufficient K supply was suggested as an effective strategy in minimizing the detrimental effects of drought by improving water use efficiency in crop plants22.

The exogenous application of CS improves the moisture holding capacity of the plants and possibly contributed to lower concentrations of Na and K required for osmotic adjustment (Table 1). This property of CS can be attributed to its abscisic acid-dependent stomatal closure27. That is why, in agriculture, CS is mainly used for coating on seeds, leaves, and fruits. The application of CS oligomers was proposed to compensate the negative impact of drought stress on coffee, pepper, rice, corn, gerbera plants36. However, stomatal closure is accompanied by excessive accumulation of ROS7. Besides, CS was reported to stimulate the production of key osmotic regulators such as proline16 and γ-aminobutyric acid30 in drought stressed plants. Thus, it can be one possible reason for lower hydrogen peroxide and MDA contents in the elicited plants (Fig. 1). The exogenous application of nitric oxide can improve the relative water content in plants37, and consequently decrease the Na and K contents. Consistently, the SNP treatment significantly enhanced the relative water content of the plants under PEG-induced drought (Supplementary Fig. S1). Additionally, the SNP treatment triggers the vacuolar H+-ATPase activity which will increase the Na+/H+ antiport activity37. As a result, it possibly caused lower Na accumulation and Na stress-induced oxidative damage (Table 1; Fig. 1).

The uptake and translocation of P is severely restricted in soil-grown plants under drought stress22. The reduced mobility of phosphorous in the plants grown in dry soil conditions can be attributed to the large number of molecular exudates from plants22. Consistently, there was a decrease in phosphorous levels under PEG-induced drought (Table 1). This observation was one possible reason for the lower photosynthetic rate at higher drought intensities (Fig. 2a and c) in that P is critical to the stomatal conductance, photosynthetic rate, and membrane stability38. CS treatment induces the adsorption of nutrients with negative charges into the leaves. In agreement with the present findings (Table 1), there were significant increase in the phosphorous of the spinach leaves treated with CS 39. The CS and SNP both stimulate the antioxidant activity which will positively influence the P intake. As a result, the CS was more potent than SNP in increasing the P intake in the elicited plants (Table 1).

The drop in Ca under drought stress (Table 1) can be attributed to the ROS generation and cell membrane damage (Fig. 1). Calcium is a critical component of cell membrane structures. The CS and SNP application mediated the negative effects of oxidative stress, which possibly enhanced the Ca contents as it is required to supply the required nutrients lost during membrane damage. The combined application of both elicitors was more effective than individual application, thus higher Ca than other cases (Table 1). The changes in Ca concentration under different treatments were consistent with the production of photosynthetic pigments and secondary metabolites (Figs. 2 and 3). Higher Ca concentration cause higher energy transport and antioxidant defense activity40. Consistently, the NO application improved the Ca uptake in wheat plants and activated the Ca-mediated antioxidant defense mechanism37. Besides, changes in Ca under these treatments can also be related to the K- and Na-induced Ca antagonism41.

Under drought stress, plants activate magnesium uptake, transport, and remobilization mechanisms to maintain chloroplast function, sustain photosynthetic capacity, and improve stress tolerance23,42. Consistently, higher Mg concentrations were observed in the plants subjected to higher PEG-induced drought (Table 1). The Ca and Mg antagonism also affects their concentrations in the plants. In fact, the homeostatic balance between them is critical for optimal growth and development of plants43. Upon application of SNP and CS, an increase in the concentration of Ca, as a competing cation, possibly resulted in a lower Mg concentration (Table 1). Under CS and SNP treatments, higher photosynthetic efficiency and higher antioxidant activity (Figs. 2 and 3) possibly minimized ROS accumulation and photosynthetic system disruption, obviated the need for excess magnesium to enhance photosynthetic CO2 fixation35. Higher Mg content in the plants exposed only to SNP compared to the plants under CS treatment might be attributed to the role of NO in inducing the chlorophyll synthesis37(Table 1).

The iron contents were not significantly changed with increasing drought intensity possibly because of two contradicting effects. Lower iron content increases the auxin synthesis, which is linked to the root development34. Higher root development led to higher osmotic water uptake. The Fe is an essential co-factor of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase44. As a result, the iron uptake will possibly increase with high drought levels possibly because of higher antioxidant enzymes (Supplementary Fig. S2). On the contrary, the excessive iron in the cells will catalyze the production of ROS via Fenton reactions45. As a result, the plants might prefer lower iron contents under increasing drought as a possible defense mechanism to prevent ROS generation (Table 1). The K intake also impedes the absorption of iron34, in agreement with the present findings (Table 1). Higher K content decreases the Fe antagonistically. These contradictory effects possibly led to almost constant Fe in the plants exposed to increasing drought intensities.

Higher iron contents in the plants subjected to SNP and CS (Table 1) can possibly be understood by considering the recognized role of these elicitors in inducing antioxidant enzymes and also photosynthetic pigment biosynthesis (Figs. 2 and 3), as consistently reported in literature16,30. Consistently, the contents of chlorophylls surged upon co-application of SNP and CS due to both higher synthesis rate (Fig. 2a and c) at high iron contents22 and also less degradation rate at sharply reduced ROS (Fig. 1a). The carotenoids and phenolics raised to quench singlet oxygen and to protect photosynthetic systems at elevated drought levels (Fig. 2d). SNP stimulates mitochondrial aconitase (a component of the Krebs cycle) gene expression to IRP1 (iron regulatory protein) through increasing the plant’s access to iron46. Also, the NO application to the plants modulate the iron redox status and consequently increase the intracellular iron content29. Additionally, SNP directly increase the extracellular Fe content because of the chemical structure of SNP, Na2[Fe(CN)5NO].2H2O, which includes iron in Fe(II) form. As a result, the contents of Fe synergistically increased possibly because of high Fe demand.

Further investigations on other mineral elements involved in the antioxidant defense pathways and plant water status need to be investigated in the future research attempts. Similar studies can help us understand the key role of different nutrients in enhancing water use efficiency in plants. This understanding can lead us to modified culture media for cultivations of plants in arid and semi-arid regions.

Conclusion

The CS and SNP effectively alleviated the PEG-induced drought stress in the spinach. The application of the elicitors individually or in combination caused a similar trend in decreasing the oxidative stress and improving the production of photosynthetic pigments and phenolics. The combined application of both elicitors at 30 mg CS L− 1 and 50 µM SNP showed the highest mitigating effects. These treatments had significant effects on the profile of mineral nutrients. The contents of Na, K, and Mg increased and those of Ca, P, and Fe decreased under drought. The CS and SNP applications increased the contents Ca, P, and Fe and decreased the contents of Na, K, and Mg, while keeping the same trend of changes with drought intensity. The intricate interplay between these nutrients is critical to osmotic adjustment, antioxidant metabolism, and photosynthesis.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and culture conditions

Commercial spinach seeds were bought from Pakan Bazr-e-Esfahan Co in Esfahan-Iran. The seeds were sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (1%) for 5 min, followed by thorough wash with double distilled water (dd H2O). The uniform sterilized seeds were germinated by placing them between two layers of filter paper in Petri dishes containing dd H2O. Until the emergence of radicles (~ 72 h), the dishes were kept in darkness at 25 °C. The seedlings were then transferred to pots filled with peat and perlite (1:1) in a growth room, illuminated with light florescence lamps at an intensity of ~ 47 µmol m− 2 s− 1 under 16/8 h photoperiod and 25/18 °C day/night temperature.

The plants were grown in Hoagland culture media composed of 0.607 g L− 1 KNO3, 0.945 g L− 1 Ca(NO3)2.4H2O, 0.230 g L− 1 NH4H2PO4, 0.246 g L− 1 MgSO4.7H2O, and 2 mL of stock micronutrient solution47. The composition of micronutrients in the stock were 1.864 g L− 1 KCl, 0.773 g L− 1 H3BO3, 0.169 g L− 1 MnSO4.H2O, 0.288 g L− 1 ZnSO4.7H2O, 0.062 g L− 1 CuSO4.5H2O, 0.040 g L− 1 H2MoO4. The iron was supplemented by 1 mL NaFeDTPA (30 g L− 1, 10% Fe).

The spinach plants at the three-leaf stage were irrigated with four PEG concentrations (0, 5, 10 and 15%), and after one day, the plants were treated by foliar spray of CS (15 and 30 mg L− 1), SNP (25 and 50 µM), and CS + SNP (15 + 25 and 30 + 50). These growth conditions were selected based on preliminary experimental works and literature review1. After 7 d, the samples were re-sprayed with the same elicitors. The plants undergone the PEG-induced drought for three weeks, and subsequently were harvested in the vegetative growth stage. The harvested plants were washed with dd H2O and then used for chemical assays immediately. The leaves were also double washed with dd H2O to remove any residual materials on the leaves and then dried in an air-circulated oven at 65 °C for 24 h. The fresh weight to dry weight conversion factor was determined 5.79.

Photosynthetic pigments

The contents of photosynthetic pigments including chlorophyll a, b (Chl a, Chl b) and carotenoids (Cx+c) were determined according to the standard method48. The photosynthetic pigments were estimated and then expressed as mg g− 1 dry weight (DW).

Hydrogen Peroxide

The contents of hydrogen peroxide were measured by the standard method49. The spinach leaves (0.1 g) were ground to homogenization in an ice bath with 5 mL TCA 0.1% w/v. The sample was centrifuged at 11,289 ×g for 20 min. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mL phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.0) and 1 mL KI (1 M). The solution absorbance was then read at 390 nm using the spectrophotometer. After calculation, the contents were expressed as µg g− 1 FW.

Malondialdehyde

The contents of MDA, as a biomarker of lipid peroxidation, was measured using the standard method50. The spinach leaves (0.1 g) were homogenized in 5 mL TCA 0.1%w/v. The extract was centrifuged at 7840×g for 15 min. The supernatant (1 mL) was mixed with TCA/TBA reagent (4 mL) and the solution was heated at 95 °C for 30 min and then cooled on ice immediately. The solution was centrifuged at 7840×g for 15 min. The supernatant’s absorbance was read at 532 nm and the value was subtracted from the non-specific absorbance reading at 600 nm. The MDA contents were estimated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM− 1 cm− 1 and expressed as mg g− 1 FW.

Flavonoids

The contents of total flavonoids were determined by following the standard method in literature51. In brief, the spinach leaves (1 g) were ground to homogenization in 5 mL methanol (80%) using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The tissue homogenate was centrifuged at 7840×g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant (0.5 mL) was mixed with 1.5 mL methanol, 0.1 mL aluminum chloride (10%), 0.1 mL potassium acetate (1 M), and 2.8 mL ddH2O. The mixture was incubated in a water bath (80 °C, 30 min) and then cooled down to room temperature. The sample absorbance was read at 415 nm by the spectrophotometer. The concentration of flavonoids was estimated by a correlation curve using Rutin as standard, and expressed as µg per g DW.

Anthocyanins

The anthocyanin content was determined according to the standard method52. In brief, the harvested leaves (1 g) were homogenized in HCl-methanol solvent (1:99 v/v) using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate suspension was centrifuged at 7,840×g for 10 min. The supernatant was incubated in darkness at 4 °C for 24 h, and then its absorbance was read at 550 nm by the spectrophotometer. The anthocyanins concentration was quantified by the extinction coefficient of 33,000–1 cm–1, and expressed as µmol per g DW.

Phenols

Total phenol content was determined by the available method in literature53. In brief, the harvested leaves (1 g) were ground to homogenization in 1 mL methanol (80%) at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min. A 100 µL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 900 µL dd H2O and 500 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (10%), and the mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. Thereafter, 1.5 mL of sodium carbonate solution (7.5%) was added to the reaction and the solution was further incubated at 25 °C for 30 min in dark. Ultimately, the sample absorbance was read at 765 nm by the spectrophotometer. The total phenol was quantified by a calibration curve using gallic acid as standard and expressed as mg GAE per g DW.

Minerals

The spinach leaves were washed with dd H2O and the sample was then dried in an air-circulated oven until reach to the constant weight. The minerals (Ca, Na, K, Fe, Mg, and P) were then extracted from the dried leaves (0.5 g) by digestion in a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and perchloric acid (2:1v/v). Subsequently, the solution was analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), Model VARIAN 725-ES, USA, under optimized operating conditions. The concentrations of Fe, Mg, P, Na, K, and Ca were determined at the specific wavelengths of 261.2 nm, 279.8 nm, 213.6 nm, 589.6 nm, 766.5 nm, and 317.9 nm, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments and treatments of the spinach plants were done in triplicates. The plant leaves were sampled and used for further chemical assays. The means of the data were then analyzed by one-way analysis of variance in the SPSS Statistics Version 22.0 to identify statistically significant differences at p < 0.05. The results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).
