Single-center experience with aortopexy in pediatric tracheomalacia: a case series
Authors: Ali Tadayon, Shayan Yousufzai, Mehdi Forooghi, Shahdokht Abooali, Parisa Zare, Hossein Hosseini, Alireza Yousefi, Mostafa Poorkhosravani, Mostafa Moqadas, Omidreza Azh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTracheomalacia in infants often results from vascular compression and is frequently associated with multiple congenital anomalies. Aortopexy is a well-established surgical treatment for severe cases; however, long-term outcomes vary significantly, especially in patients with syndromic conditions.ObjectiveThis study aims to present the clinical, surgical, and long-term outcomes of aortopexy performed in six infants diagnosed with severe tracheomalacia and multiple congenital anomalies, focusing on determining whether persistent morbidity and mortality are attributable to the airway intervention itself or to underlying comorbidities.MethodsWe conducted a single-center case series involving six infants diagnosed with tracheomalacia confirmed by rigid bronchoscopy. All subjects underwent open aortopexy combined with thymectomy (partial, subtotal, or total). Data collected included preoperative respiratory support status, bronchoscopic findings—specifically the degree of airway collapse and posterior wall dynamics—perioperative details, and long-term follow-up outcomes. Due to the small sample size and lack of a control group, no formal analysis was performed to determine the independent effects of thymectomy.ResultsEach patient presented with multiple congenital anomalies, with symptom onset ranging from 6 days to 1 year of age. Surgical approaches included thoracotomy in five cases and cervicotomy in one. Preoperative respiratory support varied: two patients required invasive mechanical ventilation, two received non-invasive support, one was on supplemental oxygen, and one required no respiratory assistance. Thymectomy was successfully performed in all cases (partial in four, subtotal in one, and total in one) without intraoperative complications. No major early postoperative complications were observed; however, two patients required reintervention—one for tracheal stenosis and one for tracheostomy plug removal—and two patients died from comorbidity-related causes (acute respiratory distress syndrome and pulmonary hypertension complicated by COVID-19). Among the four patients with extended follow-up (up to eight years), persistent morbidities such as tracheal stenosis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and scoliosis were common, with half reporting poor or fair quality of life.ConclusionAortopexy effectively alleviates airway obstruction in infants with tracheomalacia secondary to vascular compression, especially when guided by intraoperative bronchoscopy. However, long-term outcomes are primarily influenced by associated congenital anomalies rather than procedural failure. Although routine thymectomy was feasible, its additional benefit remains unproven. Future multicenter prospective studies and investigations into adjunctive surgical techniques, such as posterior tracheopexy, are warranted to improve prognosis in this complex patient population.Graphical abstract Keywords: Congenital tracheomalacia, Anterior aortopexy, Pediatrics surgery, Sternotomy

Introduction

Tracheomalacia, characterized by the collapsibility and intermittent narrowing of the trachea, may present with a variety of signs and symptoms in pediatrics. The most reported clinical presentation of tracheomalacia is wheeze, stridor, and cough [1]. The known etiologies for this condition are congenital defects in muscles or cartilaginous architectures of the upper airways, iatrogenic weakening of the trachea after interventions, and the pressure effect of adjacent structures [2]. Although no definite test is considered the gold standard for detecting tracheomalacia, chest computed tomography, bronchoscopy, and fluoroscopy could be diagnostic, and provide further evaluations regarding the site of collapse and nearby structures [3]. However different surgical interventions are offered for the management of respiratory outcomes, one of the most frequently applied surgical techniques is aortopexy [4].

A recent study indicated that aortopexy is effective in improving stridor, physical activity tolerance, ventilator or oxygen dependence, and recurrent respiratory infections, despite a small fraction (6%) of patients suffering from persistent respiratory symptoms [5]. Similarly, a review of articles demonstrated a success rate of 84% for aortopexy, while 59.2% of patients reported complete relief [6]. However, the quality of life remained poor in a proportion of the individuals undergoing aortopexy, since their physical and emotional well-being was significantly affected by the associated comorbidities, including congenital anomalies [7]. Data support the coincidence of tracheomalacia and certain congenital diseases, including cardiac anomalies, esophageal atresia or fistula, vertebral defects, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia [6, 8].

Among the main surgical approaches for aortopexy, including sternotomy, open thoracotomy, cervicotomy, and the thoracoscopic approach, the reported mortality ratio and success rates are variable from 2% to 12.4% and 69% to 93%, respectively [6, 9–11]. Since open surgeries were accompanied by significant symptom improvements and extremely low recurrence rates compared to the thoracoscopic techniques, they are often preferred [6, 12].

Aortopexy is a well-established surgical procedure for treating tracheomalacia, with reported success rates ranging from 70% to 93%, depending on patient selection and surgical technique [4, 6]. However, most published studies include heterogeneous patient populations, and the specific impact of multiple congenital anomalies on long-term outcomes has not been thoroughly investigated [7, 8].

This case series presents an eight-year, single-center experience with aortopexy performed to treat tracheomalacia caused by vascular compression. Unlike larger meta-analyses, our patient-level data allow for a focused examination of whether the persistent morbidity and mortality observed in a cohort characterized by an almost universal presence of congenital anomalies can be attributed to the aortopexy procedure itself or to the underlying comorbid conditions. Additionally, we describe our institutional practice of routine thymectomy; although the limited size of this series precludes assessment of its independent impact, it may generate hypotheses for future research. A summary of the unique contributions of this series relative to existing literature is provided in the Discussion section. To provide an overview of the clinical and demographic characteristics of patients, along with intra-operative and post-operative outcomes, this study presents a single-center experience with aortopexy for infants suffering from vascular pressure-induced tracheomalacia. By reporting eight years of retrospective data, we aim to enhance insights that may inform surgical decision-making. Our experience of long-term follow-up indicates a 100% prevalence of congenital anomalies in aortopexy candidates, which could affect the patients’ quality of life and disabilities. Contrary to the literature, we hypothesize that the main causes of mortality and disability among patients could be attributed to their congenital diseases, rather than post-operation complications.

Materials and methods

This case series was conducted at Namazi Hospital, a referral educational medical center in Shiraz, Fars Province, Iran. Data regarding patients aged from 12 days to 1 year, who underwent an aortopexy operation between 2017 and 2024, were included. Patients diagnosed with tracheomalacia were considered candidates for aortopexy surgery if evaluation by rigid bronchoscopy demonstrated the pulsatile pressure effect of the aorta or its main branching vessels on the trachea, accompanied by a remarkable collapse in the anterior aspect. Patients were excluded when information on the pre-operative bronchoscopy outcome or a definitive diagnosis of extrinsic pressure on the trachea was lacking. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Reference ID: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1403.312). Patient confidentiality was respected, and all details were anonymized.

Classification of tracheomalacia extent

Tracheomalacia was classified based on findings from rigid bronchoscopy into focal and diffuse types. Focal tracheomalacia was defined as collapse confined to a single tracheal segment—such as the mid or distal trachea—with a craniocaudal length of 2 cm or less. In contrast, diffuse tracheomalacia involved collapse across two or more contiguous segments or extended over more than 2 cm, encompassing long-segment involvement or regions from the upper trachea to the carina. This classification was established intraoperatively by the senior author.

The dynamics of the posterior tracheal wall were evaluated during rigid bronchoscopy by observing its behavior throughout spontaneous respiration. Collapse was characterized as predominantly anterior when the anterior tracheal wall bulged posteriorly, with minimal or no anterior prolapse of the membranous posterior wall. Conversely, mixed or circumferential collapse was identified when the posterior wall also bulged anteriorly during expiration, with or without involvement of the lateral walls. Dynamic assessment was conducted both before and after gentle anterior displacement of the aorta or innominate artery using the bronchoscope or a probe, facilitating differentiation between extrinsic vascular compression and intrinsic tracheomalacia.

Rigid bronchoscopy was performed under general anesthesia with spontaneous ventilation maintained and without neuromuscular blockade to preserve natural respiratory movements. A bronchoscope with an outer diameter of 3.5–4.0 mm (Karl Storz) was introduced trans-orally, allowing inspection of the trachea from the subglottic region to the carina during both the inspiratory and expiratory phases. The degree of airway collapse was visually graded as a percentage reduction in luminal diameter. Additionally, dynamic evaluation of posterior wall intrusion was conducted by observing the membranous wall during expiration, both before and after gentle anterior displacement of the aorta or innominate artery using the bronchoscope tip.

Surgical protocol

Aortic arch was accessed via open thoracotomy or cervicotomy, and then was fixed to the posterior aspect of the sternum using a non-absorbable stitch (Prolene©). The procedure was accompanied by total or partial thymectomy. Rigid bronchoscopy was performed immediately after surgery to confirm the appropriate decompression and improved airway narrowing.

Indications and extent of thymectomy

Thymectomy—whether partial, subtotal, or total—was performed according to the surgeon’s judgment based on intraoperative anatomical findings. Partial thymectomy, involving resection of the left lobe only, was undertaken when the thymus was small and easily mobilized. Subtotal thymectomy, which entailed removal of both lobes while preserving the thymic capsule, was performed when the thymus partially obscured the aortic arch. Total thymectomy was reserved for a single patient with an exceptionally narrow upper mediastinal space, where even partial thymic tissue impeded adequate visualization. The extent of thymectomy was neither randomized nor subjected to comparative analysis.

Chest computed tomography scan (CT-S) and its limitations

Chest computed tomography (CT) was performed using a 64-slice scanner (Siemens® SOMATOM Definition) during spontaneous quiet breathing, without sedation or mechanical ventilation. Imaging was conducted with patients in the supine position, using a slice thickness of 1.0 to 1.5 mm, a pitch of 1.0, and a tube voltage of 120 kVp. Neither dynamic (4D) CT nor respiratory gating techniques were employed. All CT scans were reviewed by a pediatric radiologist solely for anatomical localization. It is important to note that conventional static CT has inherent limitations in assessing dynamic posterior membranous prolapse; therefore, all determinations regarding the pattern and severity of airway collapse, including posterior wall dynamics, were based exclusively on rigid bronchoscopy findings. Consequently, static chest CT was used only for anatomical reference, while dynamic evaluation of posterior wall intrusion was performed solely through rigid bronchoscopy.

Data collection

Clinical information, including demographics, clinical presentation, bronchoscopy results, intraoperative notes, early postoperative outcomes, and potential readmissions or reoperations, was obtained from patient medical records. All subjects had been assessed by an expert pediatrician, and detailed data regarding their congenital diseases, clinical symptoms, and life-threatening events necessitating respiratory support were documented. Medical records documenting an admission completely irrelevant to the respiratory diseases were assessed thoroughly, since they provide essential data about the patients’ chronic diseases that might be related to their outcomes and quality of life. To augment data collection on quality of life and long-term follow-up, Telephone-based evaluations were applied. In the case that parents were complaining of a specific disability, detailed medical history was scrutinized to find out the underlying cause. Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA) was utilized for data gathering. Continuous variables were given as ranges, categorical variables as absolute frequencies, and descriptive analyses were used.

Results

A total of six pediatric patients diagnosed with tracheomalacia were included. Of these, four were male and two were female. Gestational age (GA) at birth ranged from 32 to 38 weeks, with a corresponding birth weight between 1.5 kg and 2.7 kg. One male patient’s GA and birth weight were not recorded.

Tracheomalacia etiology was classified as cardiovascular (n = 3), post-intubation (n = 1), secondary to esophageal atresia (EA) and aortic compression (n = 1), and mixed (laryngocele and cardiovascular) in one patient. Multiple congenital anomalies were identified, including VSD, ASD, IUGR, polydactyly, micrognathia, TEF, dysmorphic features, VACTERL association, PDA, digital and vertebral anomalies, and cystic fibrosis. Maternal gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was reported in one case.

Focal tracheomalacia was identified in two patients: one case involved the distal trachea, and the other involved the mid-trachea. Diffuse tracheomalacia was observed in three patients, characterized as follows: one extended from the upper trachea to the carina, one involved a long segment, and one spanned from the tracheostomy site to the carina. In one patient, the site of tracheomalacia was not fully documented, precluding classification. Bronchoscopic evaluation revealed that airway collapse was predominantly anterior in three patients—specifically, the two with focal tracheomalacia and one with distal tracheal compression. In contrast, the remaining three patients with diffuse or long-segment involvement exhibited mixed or circumferential collapse, including posterior membranous wall intrusion. Notably, within this mixed group, relief of anterior compression did not prevent the posterior tracheal wall from bulging anteriorly during expiration, suggesting an intrinsic component of posterior tracheomalacia.

Age at symptom onset ranged from 6 days to 1 year. Presenting symptoms included respiratory distress (n = 2), cyanosis (n = 3), stridor (n = 2), wheezing, dysphagia, and feeding difficulty. Tracheomalacia severity was classified as severe in five patients and mild in one. The preoperative respiratory support requirements among the patients varied: two individuals required invasive mechanical ventilation, two needed non-invasive respiratory support such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or high-flow nasal cannula, one received supplemental oxygen via nasal prongs, and one patient did not require any respiratory support, being monitored solely for feeding difficulties and episodes of cyanosis. Rigid bronchoscopy performed on all six patients revealed anterior pulsatile compression caused by either the aorta or the innominate artery at the site corresponding to the observed airway collapse. In cases involving proximal or extensive segmental airway involvement, the compressive vessel was intraoperatively identified as either a high-riding aortic arch or an innominate artery positioned more cephalad than typically observed. Collapse rate was 100% in three patients and 70–80% in one. Collapse sites included the upper trachea to carina (n = 1), mid trachea (n = 1), distal trachea (n = 1), long tracheal segment (n = 1), and the tracheostomy site (n = 1); one case lacked site documentation (Table 1).

All patients underwent surgical correction. Five patients received an open thoracotomy approach, while one underwent cervicotomy. The interval between diagnosis and surgery ranged from 3 days to 3 years. Thymectomy was performed in all cases—partial in four patients, subtotal in one, and total in another. No formal criteria were used to determine the extent of thymectomy; the decision was made intraoperatively and depended on the surgeon. We did not observe any intraoperative complications attributable to thymectomy, such as phrenic nerve injury or bleeding. Operative times ranged from 90 to 210 min; however, we could not attribute this variability specifically to the extent of thymectomy versus other factors, including surgical approach, patient anatomy, or concurrent procedures. Given the small cohort size (N = 6) and the absence of a non-thymectomy control group, we did not perform statistical comparisons of outcomes based on thymectomy extent. This stenosis was not present preoperatively; it developed in the mid-trachea three months postoperatively, likely as an acquired fibrotic sequela of severe, long-standing dynamic collapse rather than as a primary fixed stenosis. Two patients required reintervention—one for stenosis management and one for tracheostomy plug removal. Persistent tracheomalacia was identified postoperatively in two patients. Discussion

In this cohort of infants with severe tracheomalacia secondary to vascular compression and associated congenital anomalies, our results support previous findings that aortopexy is a technically feasible intervention with significant symptomatic benefit, with most patients demonstrating improvement in airway patency and resolution of life-threatening events such as cyanosis or dying spells.

Contributions of this series beyond the existing literature

This study presents four notable observations derived from a small patient cohort, which are often obscured in larger aggregated datasets.

First, the presence of comorbidities was universal, with all six patients exhibiting multiple congenital anomalies. This prevalence exceeds the 70–90% range typically reported in heterogeneous cohorts [6, 13]. While this may reflect a tertiary referral bias, it also underscores that aortopexy is infrequently performed for isolated tracheomalacia in routine clinical practice.

Second, both fatalities (2 out of 6 patients) were attributable to non-surgical causes—acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in a premature infant and pulmonary hypertension complicated by COVID-19—despite intraoperative confirmation of adequate tracheal decompression. Importantly, no mortality was directly linked to aortopexy failure.

Third, among the four patients with long-term follow-up, three experienced poor or fair quality of life, primarily due to feeding difficulties, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), scoliosis, and recurrent infections. These conditions predated the aortopexy procedure and were unrelated to airway patency. The only patient reporting a good quality of life was the sole individual without significant comorbidities.

Fourth, routine thymectomy (partial in four patients, subtotal in one, and total in one) was feasible without evident immunologic consequences during follow-up. However, it remains unclear whether thymectomy enhanced surgical exposure or improved outcomes compared to thymic mobilization alone. This technical aspect warrants prospective investigation. Although partial or complete thymectomy appeared to facilitate visualization in our experience, this was not quantitatively assessed. Future prospective studies employing standardized exposure metrics and control groups are necessary to clarify this issue.

Clarifying the indications for aortopexy in non-distal tracheal lesions

Traditionally, aortopexy is indicated for anterior vascular compression at the distal trachea near the aortic arch or innominate artery. In contrast, our series identified collapse sites involving the upper trachea, mid-trachea, extended segments, and tracheostomy sites. Bronchoscopic and intraoperative findings confirmed anterior pulsatile compression by the aorta or innominate artery at these respective levels. Anatomical variations, such as high-riding aortic arches or elongated innominate arteries, can impinge upon more proximal tracheal segments. In cases of long-segment or mixed pathology—such as intrinsic malacia combined with focal vascular compression—aortopexy may alleviate only the compressive component, leaving residual posterior or diffuse collapse unaddressed. This likely contributed to persistent symptoms and suboptimal quality of life in three of four patients with long-term follow-up. Therefore, although aortopexy was technically successful in all cases, alternative or adjunctive interventions—such as posterior tracheopexy or combined anterior–posterior stabilization—should be considered for patients exhibiting predominant posterior membranous intrusion or extensive intrinsic malacia, as supported by recent literature [13, 16, 17]. We acknowledge that the retrospective nature of this series precludes determination of the optimal surgical approach for each anatomical pattern and advocate for prospective comparative studies.

Impact of diffuse versus focal tracheomalacia on outcomes

Within our cohort, the two patients with focal tracheomalacia demonstrated superior long-term outcomes: one remained asymptomatic eight years postoperatively, while the other experienced only a mild cough and maintained a good quality of life. In contrast, all three patients with diffuse (long-segment or upper-to-carina) collapse exhibited persistent symptoms and fair-to-poor quality of life despite bronchoscopic confirmation of adequate anterior decompression. This observation aligns with existing literature suggesting that diffuse or long-segment tracheomalacia is less responsive to isolated aortopexy, which primarily addresses anterior vascular compression but does not stabilize posterior or membranous collapse along the tracheal length [6, 13, 17]. For such patients, adjunctive procedures such as posterior tracheopexy or combined anterior–posterior stabilization warrant consideration. Although our small sample size precludes statistical analysis, we present these findings to generate hypotheses for future prospective research.

Posterior tracheal wall dynamics and their surgical implications

In this series, three patients with diffuse or long-segment tracheomalacia exhibited mixed or circumferential airway collapse, including posterior membranous intrusion during expiration. This finding is clinically significant because aortopexy exclusively addresses anterior vascular compression and does not stabilize the posterior tracheal wall. The persistent symptoms and suboptimal quality of life observed in these patients may be attributable to untreated posterior malacia. One patient developed mid-tracheal stenosis requiring balloon dilation, highlighting that severe, prolonged tracheomalacia can lead to secondary fibrotic narrowing despite successful aortopexy. This underscores that aortopexy mitigates dynamic airway collapse but does not reverse pre-existing mucosal or cartilaginous damage.

During the study period (2017–2024), posterior tracheopexy was not available at our institution; consequently, all patients underwent aortopexy as the sole surgical intervention. In current practice, patients with mixed or predominantly posterior airway collapse would be candidates for posterior tracheopexy or combined anterior–posterior stabilization [13, 16, 17]. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our historical series and recommend that future patients with diffuse tracheomalacia and posterior wall intrusion undergo dynamic bronchoscopic assessment to guide the selection among aortopexy, posterior tracheopexy, or combined approaches.

Our reliance on static computed tomography (CT) for preoperative evaluation (Fig. 1) presents a limitation, as standard CT imaging does not reliably capture dynamic posterior membranous prolapse. Consequently, all characterizations of collapse patterns (anterior versus mixed/circumferential) were derived from rigid bronchoscopy, which remains the gold standard for dynamic airway assessment.

Strengths of our study include rigorous procedural details, consistent use of intraoperative bronchoscopy, and reporting of comprehensive short- and long-term clinical metrics.

However, our study is limited by its retrospective single-center nature, small sample size, and heterogeneity in follow-up intervals and outcome metrics.

These constraints are echoed in the literature and contribute to the serious risk of bias noted in most retrospective cohorts [6].

A significant limitation of the present study is the inability to isolate and assess the independent impact of thymectomy—or the extent of thymic resection—on clinical outcomes. Although all patients underwent thymectomy (ranging from partial to subtotal or total), the heterogeneity of congenital anomalies, variability in surgical approaches (thoracotomy in five cases, cervicotomy in one), and the limited sample size preclude any robust comparative analysis among thymectomy subgroups. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the extent of thymectomy influenced parameters such as airway patency, duration of ventilatory support, length of intensive care unit stays, complication rates, frequency of reintervention, or long-term quality of life. Nonetheless, based on intraoperative observations by the senior surgeon, routine thymectomy (partial or complete) appeared to enhance visualization of the aortic arch and anterior mediastinum, thereby facilitating the placement of suspension sutures without necessitating excessive retraction. However, this technical advantage was not systematically documented. Operative durations varied between 90 and 210 min, but it is not possible to attribute this variability specifically to the extent of thymectomy rather than to other factors such as surgical approach, patient anatomy, or concurrent procedures. Future investigations should prospectively evaluate operative time, intraoperative complications (including phrenic nerve injury and bleeding), and surgeon-assessed quality of exposure to determine whether thymectomy confers meaningful technical benefits.

Moreover, the heterogeneity in sites of airway collapse (upper, mid, distal, and long-segment) limits the ability to determine which anatomical patterns are optimally managed by aortopexy alone versus combined surgical approaches. Additionally, bronchoscopic assessment of posterior tracheal wall dynamics was not systematically quantified, and the absence of posterior tracheopexy as a treatment modality during the study period restricts comparative analysis between anterior-only and combined interventions. The lack of a standardized preoperative classification system distinguishing focal from diffuse disease within this retrospective cohort further limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding differential outcomes based on anatomical extent.

Regarding the role of thymectomy in aortopexy, all six patients in this series underwent the procedure, which differs from most published reports where thymic resection is not routinely described as a standard component of aortopexy [12,16,18]. The rationale at our institution was primarily technical: thymectomy (partial, subtotal, or total) provided unobstructed access to the ascending aorta and aortic arch, facilitated safer suture placement, and minimized the need for forceful retraction of mediastinal structures. In one case, total thymectomy was deemed essential due to limited upper mediastinal space required to achieve adequate exposure.

Nonetheless, several clinically relevant questions remain unanswered. First, does thymectomy significantly improve operative exposure? This study did not quantify the quality of exposure nor compare outcomes to historical controls without thymectomy. Second, does the extent of thymectomy (partial versus total) affect postoperative outcomes such as immunologic function, infection risk, or long-term health? Although the thymus plays a crucial role in T-cell maturation, particularly during infancy, no patient in this series exhibited clinically apparent immunodeficiency during follow-up periods of up to eight years. Third, is thymectomy essential for successful aortopexy? Existing literature suggests that adequate exposure can often be achieved through thymic mobilization without resection [12, 16]. Fourth, does thymectomy increase operative time, bleeding risk, or the risk of phrenic nerve injury? These complications were not systematically recorded in the present study.

In light of these unresolved issues, it is important to recognize that this case series primarily generates hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions. We advocate for a prospective, multicenter study comparing aortopexy performed with and without routine thymectomy, using standardized metrics to assess exposure quality, operative duration, complication rates, and long-term immune function, in order to inform evidence-based recommendations.

The success rate observed aligns closely with a recent comprehensive systematic review of 17 studies involving 473 pediatric patients, which reported an overall aortopexy success rate of 84%, though 40% continued to experience persistent symptoms postoperatively [6].

Our open aortopexy technique, routinely involving thymectomy and intraoperative bronchoscopy, aligns with practices linked to higher success rates.

The same review indicated that sternotomy and thoracotomy achieved success rates of 92.6% and 84.2%, respectively, while thoracoscopic approaches had similar efficacy only when accompanied by pericardiotomy, thymectomy, and pledgeted sutures (86.4% vs. 92.6%, p = 0.41) [6].

This supports our surgical strategy emphasizing the importance of technical refinements—such as pericardiotomy and pledgeted sutures—over the choice of approach itself.

Consistent with the literature, our patient cohort was almost universally affected by significant comorbidities—most commonly esophageal atresia and cardiac anomalies—mirroring the > 90% rate of secondary tracheomalacia in literature, as shown by Zanini et al., who reported primary tracheomalacia in only 7.6% of cases [6].

Bairdain et al. had similar findings, with 70% of their patients having concurrent esophageal atresia, 40% having congenital heart disease, and 85% having another condition overall [13].

These associated anomalies likely contributed to increased perioperative morbidity, as reflected in our cohort’s prolonged ventilatory support and mortality events.

As reported in other series, bronchial involvement—particularly in tracheobronchomalacia—carries a higher risk of prolonged intubation, tracheostomy dependency, and even mortality [10, 14].

Our follow-up data indicate that while short term results are encouraging, long-term morbidity persists, manifested as tracheal stenosis, gastroesophageal reflux, skeletal abnormalities (e.g., scoliosis), and variable quality of life metrics.

Similar long term respiratory sequelae have been documented; for example, Abdel Rahman et al. reported 80% permanent symptom relief at mean 7.8 years, though respiratory flow metrics (e.g. TEF25%) remained modestly reduced in some patients [15].

Moreover, Zanini’s systematic review underscores that up to 40% of patients report persistent symptoms, despite technically successful procedures [6].

This enduring morbidity highlights that aortopexy addresses the mechanical airway issue but does not modify the trajectory of underlying syndromic or chronic pulmonary disorders.

Emerging alternatives such as posterior tracheopexy—whether isolated or combined with aortopexy—have shown promising results in select institutional cohorts [13, 16, 17], particularly in patients with predominant membranous tracheal collapse.

While our series did not include these approaches, these techniques merit evaluation in future comparative studies, particularly for patients exhibiting posterior wall or bronchial collapse.

Conclusion

When performed via open thoracotomy or sternotomy with thymectomy and intraoperative bronchoscopy, aortopexy remains a safe and effective intervention for infants with severe tracheomalacia due to vascular compression. Short-term benefits include relief of airway obstruction and reduction of acute life-threatening events. However, the persistence of residual morbidity in a substantial minority of patients underscores that underlying congenital anomalies significantly influence outcomes. Our findings suggest that surgical technique—particularly meticulous intraoperative assessment and tissue handling—may have greater prognostic value than the access route alone. To further improve patient outcomes, we recommend multicenter prospective studies with standardized diagnostic and outcome metrics, including objective pulmonary function testing and validated quality-of-life assessments. Additionally, comparative evaluation of posterior tracheopexy or hybrid techniques may clarify best practices for complex or syndromic presentations. These insights underscore the need for individualized perioperative planning and emphasize the multidisciplinary nature of care for syndromic tracheomalacia. Finally, the key lesson from this brief series is not about the efficacy of aortopexy—which is already well established—but rather that, in children with syndromic tracheomalacia, long-term prognosis may depend far more on associated anomalies than on the airway procedure itself. Future outcome studies should therefore stratify patients by comorbidity burden and report quality-of-life metrics, rather than focusing solely on tracheal patency or symptom scores.
