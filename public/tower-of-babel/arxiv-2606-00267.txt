StressDream : Steering Video World Models for Robust Policy Evaluation and Improvement
Junwon Seo, Sushant Veer, Ran Tian, Wenhao Ding, Apoorva Sharma, [0.2em] Karen Leung, Edward Schmerling, Marco Pavone, Andrea Bajcsy, [0.7em] Carnegie Mellon University, [0.2em] University of Washington

Abstract
Video world models (WMs) have shown promise for policy evaluation and improvement by imagining realistic future observations conditioned on ego-robot actions. While WMs can model distributions over futures, policy evaluation and improvement typically rely on nominal imaginations, which can miss high-impact outcomes of robot actions unless prohibitively many samples are drawn. To enable robust policy evaluation and improvement over WM imaginations, we propose StressDream , which steers imaginations toward high-impact yet plausible outcomes specified at inference time by optimizing the initial noise of diffusion-based WMs. However, optimizing high-dimensional noise is challenging: the optimization must reason about nuanced, scene-dependent target events in generated videos while avoiding out-of-distribution (OOD) noise that yields implausible imaginations. We address this with two complementary objectives: a semantic objective with a Vision-Language Model that provides informative gradients by reasoning about the generated video, and a plausibility objective that prevents the optimized noise from drifting OOD. With state-of-the-art video world models for autonomous driving and robotic manipulation, we show that StressDream effectively steers imaginations toward high-impact yet plausible outcomes specified by text at inference time, such as task failures, enabling robust policy evaluation and improvement by identifying actions whose plausible futures include undesirable outcomes.



Keywords:
World Models, Inference-time Steering, Policy Learning and Evaluation

1
Introduction

Video world models (WMs), or learned simulators of physical environments, have attracted significant interest in robotics, promising policy evaluation and improvement with substantially less costly real-world interaction
[
1
,
117
,
78
]
. At their core, video WMs are generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching
[
52
,
59
,
73
]
, learning
distributions
over future observations conditioned on ego robot actions, and thereby capturing the inherent uncertainty of physical interactions or behaviors of other agents
[
7
,
17
,
39
,
61
]
(
top
, Fig.
). For example, in robotic manipulation, dropping an open bag from high above the table may or may not cause its contents to spill, leading to either task success or failure; in autonomous driving, the same ego-vehicle decision can elicit qualitatively different outcomes depending on the responses of surrounding agents, such as braking or maintaining speed.

Despite learning rich distributions over future outcomes, policy evaluation and improvement with WMs typically rely on
nominal
imaginations
[
17
,
38
,
42
,
41
]
, which can under-explore the diverse outcomes they are trained to model.
Naively sampling imaginations from WMs can easily miss critical outcomes without drawing prohibitively many samples, making it ill-suited to the role of WMs: exploring
plausible
,
high-impact
outcomes of robot actions for policy evaluation and improvement
[
128
,
91
,
108
,
99
]
, where plausibility refers to outcomes supported by the learned WM distribution.
For example, when a manipulator drops an open bag from high above the table, the WM’s distribution may include outcomes both with and without spilling; in contrast, when the bag is placed close to the table, spilling may be unlikely or absent from the outcome distribution (
bottom
, Fig.
). The ability to imagine plausible, high-impact outcomes can therefore allow the latter action to be preferred during policy evaluation, while the former is downweighted or suppressed during policy improvement. This motivates the central question of our work:

How can we efficiently steer video world models at inference time to imagine plausible, high-impact outcomes for policy evaluation and improvement?

Our key idea is to optimize the
initial noise
of a diffusion-based WM, steering generation toward high-impact events specified at inference time (e.g., spills or collisions), within the distribution of plausible outcomes.
The challenge, however, is to guide this optimization in
extremely high-dimensional
noise spaces (e.g.,
1
M dims
[
38
]
): the optimization must reason about nuanced, scene-dependent target events from generated videos, while naive optimization can easily push the initial noise into out-of-distribution (OOD) regions
[
95
,
106
]
, producing implausible videos.
To address this, we propose
StressDream
, which combines a
semantic
objective, using a Vision-Language Model (VLM) that reasons about
what happens in the generated video
to guide generation toward target events, with a
plausibility
objective that keeps the optimized noise within the in-distribution region.
Together,
StressDream
provides dense gradient signals for searching the high-dimensional noise space, while preserving plausibility of generations, enabling video WMs to explore plausible, high-impact outcome distributions for
robust
policy evaluation and improvement.

Statement of Contributions.

We propose
StressDream
, a novel inference-time steering method for diffusion-based video world models. Our algorithm optimizes the high-dimensional Gaussian initial noise to steer generations toward high-impact outcomes while preserving plausibility. Through experiments, we show: (i) in controlled settings with access to the true system dynamics,
StressDream
steers WM imaginations toward target task-failure events only when such outcomes are plausible;
(ii) on state-of-the-art video world models for autonomous driving
[
38
]
and robotic manipulation
[
42
]
,
StressDream
enables robust policy evaluation by detecting high-impact outcomes, such as task failures, with substantially higher recall (
54\%\rightarrow 94\%
);
and (iii) this robust evaluation improves policy by promoting robust actions that avoid potential failures, increasing the success rate of a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy
[
57
]
(
39\%\rightarrow 71\%
).

Background: Action-conditioned Video World Models

Video World Models.

An action-conditioned video world model
f_{\theta}
, parameterized by
\theta
, models distributions of future observations
\mathbf{o}:=o_{t:t+H}
, where
t
denotes the time and
H
is the prediction horizon
[
42
,
91
,
108
,
70
,
65
,
125
]
. Each observation
o=[I^{1},\cdots,I^{n},q]\in\mathcal{O}
consists of
n
camera images
I^{i}
and proprioceptive state
q
, such as the robot joint configuration. The prediction is conditioned on an observation history
\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}}:=o_{t-h:t}
and a future ego-action sequence
\mathbf{a}:=a_{t:t+H}
:

\mathbf{o}\sim f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}),\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{o}:=o_{t:t+H},\quad\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}}:=o_{t-h:t},\quad\mathbf{a}:=a_{t:t+H}.

(1)

Diffusion Models as Video World Models.
Recent video world models
[
38
,
42
,
125
]
often instantiate
f_{\theta}
as a diffusion model
[
52
,
101
]
, learning a transformation between the data distribution,
\mathbf{x}^{0}:=\mathbf{o}\sim p^{\text{data}}(\mathbf{o})
, and a tractable noise distribution, typically a standard Gaussian
\mathbf{x}^{T}:=\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, where
D
is a noise dimension, indexed by diffusion time
\tau\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
:

\mathbf{x}^{\tau}=\alpha^{\tau}\,\mathbf{x}+\sigma^{\tau}\,\bm{\epsilon},\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{x}^{0}:=\mathbf{o},\quad\mathbf{x}^{T}:=\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D}),

(2)

where
\alpha^{\tau}
is a decreasing function of
\tau
and
\sigma^{\tau}
is an increasing function. Generation proceeds in the reverse direction by iteratively mapping a noisier sample to one at a less noisy diffusion time
:

d^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}):=\,\mathbf{x}^{\tau-1}=\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}-\sigma^{\tau}\,g^{\tau}_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}\right)\right)+\sigma^{\tau-1}\,g^{\tau}_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}\right),

(3)

where
g^{\tau}_{\theta}
is the noise predictor (see Appendix
A
). Starting from an initial Gaussian sample
\mathbf{x}^{T}=\bm{\epsilon}
, repeated application of this reverse update produces the predicted future observations
\mathbf{x}^{0}=\mathbf{o}
.

Initial Noise as a Control Variable.
Following (
3
), which corresponds to the probability-flow ODE
[
103
]
, for fixed conditioning inputs
(\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})
, the imaginations are a

deterministic
function of the initial noise:
\mathbf{o}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}).
Thus, all stochasticity in generation is governed by the initial noise, implying that selecting the initial noise controls which video is generated from the WMs.

Setup: Policy Evaluation and Improvement with Video World Models

Policy Evaluation and Improvement with Video World Models.

Given an action-conditioned video world model
f_{\theta}
, we aim to evaluate candidate action sequences by imagining their future outcomes, which can then be used for policy evaluation
[
91
,
108
,
70
]
and improvement
[
42
,
41
,
99
]
.
The predicted futures
\mathbf{o}
are evaluated by a test-time criterion function
\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}(\mathbf{o})\in\mathbb{R}
, which measures whether
plausible
outcomes with
high impact
on policy evaluation and improvement occur in the generated videos.
These evaluations can be used to improve policies by selecting actions that avoid such outcomes
[
130
,
48
]
, or by training a policy
\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}})
using world-model imaginations
[
42
,
41
]
.

However, when multiple futures are possible under the same action sequence,
robust
policy
[
80
,
5
]
should account for the distribution of outcomes that are plausible under the ego robot action:

Steering Imagination via Inference-time Noise Optimization.
Since the initial noise acts as a control variable for the generation of WMs (Sec.
2
), the goal of the inner optimization corresponds to steering the WM imagination toward a high-impact outcome that remains plausible under the learned model. However, because evaluating each noise requires an expensive denoising process, repeated random sampling is highly inefficient and likely to miss rare but plausible critical outcomes within a limited sampling budget. Instead, we leverage the
gradient
of a differentiable criterion function to steer generation more efficiently, optimizing the initial noise directly via gradient ascent:

\bm{\epsilon}_{i+1}=\bm{\epsilon}_{i}+\eta\,\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\,\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}\!\left(\mathbf{o}_{i}\right)\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{o}_{i}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon}_{i},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a}),

(5)

where
\eta
denotes a step size. Although this first-order gradient ascent requires a small number of iterative updates, each involving forward and backward passes through both the WM and the criterion function, the dense gradient signal makes it substantially more efficient than zeroth-order random sampling for discovering noises that induce plausible, high-impact video generations
[
106
,
60
,
34
]
.

Challenge
: High-Dimensional Noise Space.

While gradient-based optimization in (
5
) can steer diffusion model generation at inference time
[
106
,
34
]
, applying it to video world models is challenging because their noise spaces are extremely high-dimensional, e.g.,
\approx 1
M in driving
[
38
]
and
\approx 50
K in manipulation
[
42
]
. The optimization must guide generation toward meaningful, scene-dependent target events while preserving plausibility under the learned WM distribution. However, naive optimization can easily push the noise into out-of-distribution (OOD) regions of Gaussian noises, producing implausible videos (Fig.
), and the test-time criterion
\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}
must be differentiable while reasoning about nuanced, task-relevant events that vary across scenes. Moreover, solving (
5
) is inefficient because exact gradients require backpropagating through the iterative denoising process.

4
StressDream
: Steering High-Dimensional Video World Models

To address the challenges of steering video world models, we propose
StressDream
, which defines a differentiable optimization criterion by combining a
semantic
objective using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with a
plausibility
objective that prevents OOD noise:
\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}=\mathcal{C}^{\text{sem}}+\mathcal{C}^{\text{pla}}
. The semantic objective uses inference-time text specifications of high-impact outcomes to reason about whether the target events occur in the generated video (Sec.
4.1
). The plausibility objective preserves the statistical properties of the standard Gaussian noise prior, thereby maintaining the plausibility of WM generations (Sec.
4.2
). Finally, we approximate the noise gradient to avoid full backpropagation through the iterative denoising process, enabling efficient optimization (Sec.
4.3
).

4.1
Semantic Objective: Steering towards Target Events with VLM Gradients

Since video WMs operate across diverse scenes and tasks, the high-impact target events for steering may change with the policy context, requiring a
semantic
objective that can differentiably score whether scene-dependent target events occur in generated videos. We therefore leverage the general video-understanding capability of a Vision-Language Model (VLM) as the semantic objective. Given an inference-time text prompt
\bm{l}
describing the target high-impact outcome, the VLM measures how well the generated video
\mathbf{o}
matches the prompt. For example, in robotic manipulation, one may specify “the coffee beans spill,” while in driving, one may specify “a collision occurs.”

To obtain a differentiable score, we use Qwen-VL
[
8
]
and prompt it to output a single token,
yes
or
no
, indicating whether
\mathbf{o}
matches
\bm{l}
. We then define
\mathcal{C}^{\text{sem}}
as the difference in log token probabilities:

\mathcal{C}^{\text{sem}}(\mathbf{o}\,;\,\bm{l})=\log p^{\text{VLM}}(\texttt{yes}\mid\mathbf{o},\bm{l})-\log p^{\text{VLM}}(\texttt{no}\mid\mathbf{o},\bm{l}).

(6)

Using the VLM’s single-token probabilities makes the objective differentiable, providing rich gradient signals for optimizing high-dimensional noise that generates an inference-time target event.

4.2
Plausibility Objective: Keeping High-dimensional Noise in the Typical Set

Diffusion models are trained to generate samples from noise drawn from a Gaussian prior. When the noise leaves the
typical set

[
25
,
13
,
83
]
, where most training-time noise samples lie, the resulting out-of-distribution (OOD) noise can produce implausible videos that are unlikely under the WM distribution or have degraded visual quality. Importantly, in high dimensions, the typical set is not the same as the region of highest probability density: some noise vectors, such as the zero vector, may have high density but are extremely unlikely to be sampled from the Gaussian prior (See Appendix
B.1
for more details). Since gradient-based noise optimization can perturb the noise away from this typical set
[
95
,
106
,
34
,
96
,
49
]
, we employ a
plausibility objective
that encourages the noise to remain within the
typical set
of the Gaussian prior, with a combination of functions of the noise that capture key statistics of high-dimensional Gaussian noise:
\mathcal{C}^{\text{pla}}(\bm{\epsilon})=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{C}^{\text{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon})+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{C}^{\text{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon})+\lambda_{3}\mathcal{C}^{\text{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon})
.

Norm Concentration.
First, since the squared norm of Gaussian noise follows a chi-square distribution,

\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{D}
, it concentrates sharply around the noise dimension
D

[
95
]
. We therefore regularize deviations of the noise radius from the typical Gaussian shell:

\mathcal{C}^{\text{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\left(\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}-\sqrt{D}\right)^{2}.

Isotropy.

While norm concentration enforces typicality at a
global
scale, the optimized noise may still contain
local
correlations or structured coordinate patterns that are unlikely under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior
[
106
]
. To encourage coordinate-wise independence, we randomly permute the entries of
\bm{\epsilon}
and partition them into
m
subvectors
\{\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
D=mk
. Under the Gaussian prior, these subvectors should behave as i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{k})
, and therefore their empirical second moment
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}
should be close to
\mathbf{I}_{k}
. We therefore penalize deviations from blockwise isotropy as
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\frac{1}{k}\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{I}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}
, averaged over multiple random permutations.

Spectral Whiteness.

Even when the noise remains typical in coordinate space, optimization can introduce
frequency-domain
artifacts
[
30
]
. Since Gaussian noise has a flat expected power spectrum
[
104
,
112
]
, we encourage the noise to remain spectrally white. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote a unitary 2D discrete Fourier transform applied over the spatial dimensions of each noise slice, and compute the power spectrum
\mathbf{P}=|\mathcal{F}(\bm{\epsilon})|^{2}
. We aggregate
\mathbf{P}
into
B
spatial-frequency bins to obtain bin-averaged powers
\{\widehat{p}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
. To preserve spectral flatness, we minimize the variance of these bin-averaged powers,
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(\widehat{p}_{b}-\bar{p})^{2}
, where
\bar{p}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\widehat{p}_{b^{\prime}}
is the mean power across bins.

Approximating Noise Gradients for Efficient Steering

Noise optimization in (
5
) requires computing the gradient of the criterion with respect to the initial noise,

\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\,\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}\!\left(f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})\right)=\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\,\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}\!\left(d^{1}\!\big(\cdots d^{T}(\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})\big)\right),

where
d^{\tau}(\cdot)
denotes one denoising step in (
3
). Although diffusion models are differentiable in principle, backpropagating through the full iterative denoising process (e.g.,
50
steps
[
14
]
) can incur high memory cost and vanishing gradient
[
33
,
3
]
. To make optimization efficient, we adopt a score-distillation
[
3
]
(Appendix
B.4
), which approximates the gradient with respect to the noise using the gradient at the generated sample:

\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}\!\left(\mathbf{o}\right)\approx\beta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{o}}\mathcal{C}^{\text{test}}(\mathbf{o}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{o}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})=d^{1}\!\big(\cdots d^{T}(\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})\big),

(7)

where
\beta
is a scalar coefficient. This approximation only requires backpropagation through the differentiable criterion function, avoiding full backpropagation through the iterative denoising process.

5
Case Study: Steering Dubins Car Video World Model

We first study
StressDream
in controlled settings where the system dynamics and uncertainty are known. Since we know the true dynamics, this setup allows us to test whether WM steering can effectively identify high-impact futures (i.e., failures) only when they are possible under the system.

Naughty
3D Dubins Car.

We consider an image-based setup of a discrete-time 3D Dubins car with state
s=[p_{x},p_{y},\theta]
, continuous angular-velocity action
a_{t}\in[-1.25,\,1.25]\,\mathrm{rad/s}
, fixed speed
v=1\,\mathrm{m/s}
, and timestep
\Delta t=0.05\,\mathrm{s}
. The
naughty
Dubins car introduces uncertainty by randomly flipping the sign of the control input:
\delta_{t}=-1
with probability
p=0.2
, and
\delta_{t}=1
otherwise:

s_{t+1}=s_{t}+\Delta t\,[\,v\cos(\theta_{t}),\;v\sin(\theta_{t}),\;\delta_{t}a_{t}\,],\qquad\delta_{t}\in\{-1,1\}.

(9)

Each state is rendered as a
3\times 128\times 128
RGB image showing the environment and vehicle. In this setup, instead of the VLM-based
\mathcal{C}^{\text{sem}}
, we use the safety score defined as
\mathcal{C}(s)=p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}-0.25^{2}
, which induces a circular failure set of radius
0.25\,\mathrm{m}
centered at the origin:
\mathcal{F}:=\{s\mid\mathcal{C}(s)\leq 0\}.

Video World Model.
We train a WM on an offline dataset of
4{,}000
random observation–action trajectories. We train a safety-score predictor to regress the ground-truth score from an image, and train a diffusion world model,
f_{\theta}(o_{t+1}\mid\epsilon,o_{t},a_{t})
, to generate one-step future observation
(H=1)
conditioned on the current observation and action. The resulting noise dimension is
1024
.

Figure 1:
Results: Naughty Dubins Car.

(a): TPR and TNR for detecting possible failures in WM imaginations.
(b): Overlaid nominal and steered imaginations.

StressDream
steers WM imaginations, detecting plausible failures that nominal imagination misses. See Appendix
C
&
Website
for more results and details.

Experiment Setup.
We aim to detect action sequences that have a possibility of entering the failure set using WM imaginations. We construct an evaluation dataset of
5{,}000
random initial state and action sequences. For each trajectory, we compute the ground-truth minimum safety score achievable under the stochastic dynamics in (
9
), and label it as
positive
if the g.t. minimum score is below zero, indicating that the trajectory can enter the failure set. We then perform open-loop world-model rollouts using only the initial image
o_{0}
and the action sequence, and classify an imagined trajectory as a failure, i.e., positive, if its minimum predicted safety score along the rollout is below zero.

Baselines.

StressDream
steers imagination by minimizing the predicted safety-score for
10
optimization steps. We compare against sampling without optimization:
Nominal
(
N
=
1
), and
Best-of-N
(
N
=
10
). We ablate
StressDream
without
\mathcal{C}^{\text{pla}}
, and compare against classifier guidance (
CG
)
[
26
,
69
]
, which applies gradients during denoising instead of optimizing the initial noise.

StressDream
reliably detects failures only when they are plausible.
Fig.
1
shows that
StressDream
accurately detects potential failures in WM imaginations, achieving a high true positive rate, i.e., correctly identifying trajectories that can fail, while maintaining a high true negative rate, i.e., not falsely classifying safe trajectories as failures. In contrast,
StressDream
without the plausibility objective in Sec.
4.2
yields a low true-negative rate, indicating implausible failures are imagined without the typical set constraint.
CG
similarly produces many false positives by directly modifying the denoising trajectory, generating implausible imaginations. Random sampling methods (
Nominal
,
Best-of-N
) generate plausible imaginations, but are less effective at identifying rare possible failures compared to
StressDream
, often missing them even with multiple samples.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we propose
StressDream
, which steers video world model imaginations toward high-impact outcomes specified at inference-time, while remaining within the distribution of plausible outcomes.
StressDream
optimizes the high-dimensional initial noise of diffusion-based video world models using a combination of semantic and plausibility objectives: VLM gradients steer generation toward high-impact outcomes specified by inference-time prompts, while typical-set constraints keep the high-dimensional noise typical under the Gaussian prior. With WMs for autonomous driving and robotic manipulation, we demonstrate that
StressDream
effectively steers imaginations while preserving plausibility, enabling robust policy evaluation by identifying actions with potential failures and improving policies by favoring robust actions that avoid such failures.

Limitations.

StressDream
requires specifying high-impact outcomes with text descriptions, so its effectiveness depends on prompt quality and may suffer from reward hacking, where the score increases without meaningful changes in generations
[
34
,
55
]
, highlighting the need for generalizable and robust reward models for robotics
[
105
,
71
]
. In addition,
StressDream
may fail to preserve plausibility or to discover relevant failures when the base WM is limited: it may steer within flawed videos produced by the WM, or fail to imagine target outcomes absent from the WM’s training distribution. Thus, our notion of plausibility is limited to what the WM supports, and does not necessarily imply physical plausibility in the real world, underscoring the need for diverse robot data to train high-fidelity WMs with physically consistent predictions. Runtime also remains expensive, as current WMs take several minutes per imagination
[
38
,
42
]
, though this could be reduced by efficient models
[
102
,
64
,
32
]
. We refer readers to Appendix
F
for a more detailed discussion.

A.2
Diffusion Models

Recent generative models are often based on diffusion or flow-matching formulations, which generate samples from noise by simulating a learned differential equation. This section expands on the diffusion-model formulation in Sec.
3
, which provides the foundation for recent video world models capable of generating high-quality videos. We omit conditioning on
(\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})
throughout for brevity.

Forward Probability Path.
Score-based diffusion models
[
52
,
59
,
101
]
and flow-matching models
[
6
,
73
]
define generative models
f_{\theta}
whose sampling process can be described by a stochastic or ordinary differential equation (SDE or ODE). The denoising network
g^{\tau}_{\theta}
, which parameterizes these dynamics, is trained to recover the reverse generative process, typically by predicting the score of the perturbed data distribution, the injected noise, or the conditional vector field.

Starting from clean data
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{o}\sim p^{\text{data}}(o)
and isotropic Gaussian noise
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
, the forward process, indexed by diffusion time
\tau\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, defines a perturbed sample as

\mathbf{x}^{\tau}=\alpha^{\tau}\,\mathbf{x}+\sigma^{\tau}\,\bm{\epsilon}.

(10)

For variance-preserving parameterizations such as DDPM, the boundary conditions satisfy

\alpha^{0}=1
,
\sigma^{0}=0
, and

\alpha^{T}\to 0
,

\sigma^{T}\to 1
.
More generally, we only require that the terminal state be noise-dominated. Representative examples include the variance-preserving schedule of DDPM
[
52
]
, where
(\alpha^{\tau})^{2}+(\sigma^{\tau})^{2}=1
, and the variance-exploding schedule of EDM
[
59
]
, where
\alpha^{\tau}\equiv 1
and
\sigma^{\tau}
increases with diffusion time.

Training Objective.

The denoiser
g^{\tau}_{\theta}
is trained to recover the injected Gaussian noise from a perturbed future observation, conditioned on the observation history and action sequence. With the noise-prediction parameterization,

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p^{\text{data}},\,\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),\,\tau}\left[\,w(\tau)\,\big\|\,\bm{\epsilon}-g^{\tau}_{\theta}\!\big(\alpha^{\tau}\mathbf{x}+\sigma^{\tau}\bm{\epsilon}\big)\big\|_{2}^{2}\,\right],

(11)

where
w(\tau)
is a time-dependent weighting
[
59
]
. The clean-sample, score, and velocity parameterizations are linearly equivalent
[
59
,
103
]
; we take
g^{\tau}_{\theta}
as a noise predictor, and the analysis transfers to the other parameterizations.

Generation via Deterministic ODE Updates.

At inference time, generation starts from an initial Gaussian noise sample
\mathbf{x}^{T}=\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
and iteratively maps it to a clean sample by following the reverse probability-flow ODE. The deterministic update used in (
3
) can be viewed as a first-order Euler step of this ODE, with DDIM
[
101
]
and EDM
[
59
]
as representative instances. Given the current noisy sample
\mathbf{x}^{\tau}
, the predicted clean sample is obtained by inverting the forward path (
10
):

\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{0\mid\tau}=\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\tau}-\sigma^{\tau}\,g^{\tau}_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}\right)}{\alpha^{\tau}}.

(12)

The sample is then advanced to the next, less-noisy time
\tau-1
by reapplying the forward path to
\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{0\mid\tau}
, while using the predicted noise as the noise direction:

\mathbf{x}^{\tau-1}=\alpha^{\tau-1}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{0\mid\tau}+\sigma^{\tau-1}\,g^{\tau}_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}\right).

(13)

Iterating this deterministic update from
T
to
0
defines the generative model sampling
\mathbf{x}\sim f_{\theta}
. Substituting (
12
) into (
13
) recovers the denoising update in (
3
).

Probability-Flow ODE and Initial-Noise Determinism.

In the continuous-time limit, the deterministic updates in (
13
) correspond to an Euler discretization of the
probability-flow ODE
associated with the forward path in (
10
)
[
103
,
59
]
. This ODE transports samples from the Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
to the data distribution
p^{\text{data}}
along deterministic trajectories. Therefore, the generated sample can be written as

\bm{\epsilon}\mapsto f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon})=\mathbf{x}^{0},

where
f_{\theta}
denotes the deterministic sampler induced by the trained denoiser. This makes the generated sample a deterministic and differentiable function of the initial noise, justifying the use of
\bm{\epsilon}
as a control variable and allowing gradients of downstream criteria to be backpropagated through the sampler.

Action-conditioned Video World Models

Video world models for robotics have shown promise for policy evaluation and improvement by simulating hard-to-model systems directly from high-dimensional sensor observations

For robotic applications, video world models are conditioned on actions to enable controllable future generation

To generate coherent future frames that remain consistent with the conditioning observations, recent video world models incorporate observation history in several ways. Application: Policy Evaluation & Improvement.

Action-conditioned video world models can support robot learning without repeated real-world rollouts, enabling scalable data generation, planning, policy evaluation, and policy improvement. Because current state-of-the-art video generation models are often slow and memory-intensive, they are typically used offline rather than for real-time planning. Prior work has primarily leveraged action-conditioned video prediction in two ways: (i) using imagined rollouts to evaluate or optimize policies without repeated hardware trials

[

While our method can be applied to these broader settings, our experiments focus specifically on the value of steering video world models for robust policy evaluation and improvement. Rather than evaluating a complete robotic policy in a closed loop, we evaluate candidate trajectories that could be produced by a policy and assess their potential failure risks. Similarly, rather than improving policies through closed-loop exploration inside the world model, we use the steered world model to identify failure-prone expert trajectories and reweight them during policy fine-tuning.

A.4
Inference-time Steering of Generative Models

Given a generative model
f_{\theta}
that maps an initial noise sample
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
to a generated sample
\bm{x}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon})
, inference-time optimization aims to find generations that maximize a user-specified criterion
\mathcal{C}
, while preserving the plausibility of the generated samples with a regularizer
\mathrm{Reg}(\bm{x})
that penalizes generations that deviate from the original model distribution. This can be written as

\bm{x}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{x}}\;\mathcal{C}(\bm{x})-\alpha\,\mathrm{Reg}(\bm{x}).

(14)

A natural way to realize this objective is to adapt the generative model itself so that it samples from a reward-tilted distribution,

f_{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x})\propto f_{\theta}(\bm{x})\exp\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}(\bm{x})}{\alpha}\right),

(15)

which upweights high-criterion samples while remaining close to the original model distribution. However, directly fine-tuning the generator is often impractical for inference-time control: the criterion may change across tasks or users, and fine-tuning requires significant computation for each new criterion. Moreover, preserving closeness to the original distribution can require density estimates that are intractable or prohibitively expensive for high-dimensional generative models
[
115
,
4
]
. As a result, model adaptation can drift away from the original data distribution and produce implausible generations
[
33
,
54
]
.

Initial Noise Optimization.
Initial noise optimization instead keeps the generative model fixed and optimizes only the input noise according to an inference-time criterion evaluated on the generated sample:

\bm{\epsilon}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\epsilon}}\;\mathcal{C}\!\left(f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon})\right).

(16)

This formulation allows arbitrary criteria to be specified at inference time without modifying the model parameters. If the optimized noise remains plausible under the original Gaussian prior, the resulting generation is still induced by the pretrained model distribution, so additional regularization on the generated sample itself is not required.

When
\mathcal{C}
is differentiable, the optimization can be solved by backpropagating through the diffusion sampler
[
116
,
60
,
34
]
. For non-differentiable criteria, prior work estimates gradients of the criterion
[
106
]
, amortizes the optimization with a hypernetwork
[
33
]
, or formulates steering as a reinforcement learning problem
[
18
,
113
]
. Since backpropagating through many iterative denoising steps is computationally expensive, several methods further distill diffusion models into one-step generators to make optimization more tractable
[
34
,
84
,
33
]
.

A.5
Typical Set of High-Dimensional Gaussian Noise

The typical set describes where samples from a distribution are expected to lie with high probability.
It is therefore a notion of probability
mass
, rather than simply probability
density

[
25
,
83
]
.
For a random vector
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, let

p(\bm{\epsilon})
denote the joint density of the full
D
-dimensional Gaussian prior.
The
\delta
-typical set can be written as

\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{(D)}=\left\{\bm{\epsilon}:\left|-\frac{1}{D}\log p(\bm{\epsilon})-h(\mathcal{N}(0,1))\right|\leq\delta\right\},

(17)

where
h(\mathcal{N}(0,1))=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e)
is the differential entropy of a one-dimensional standard Gaussian.
The one-dimensional entropy appears because the standard Gaussian prior factorizes across coordinates,

p(\bm{\epsilon})=\prod_{i=1}^{D}p(\epsilon_{i})
.
Thus,
-\frac{1}{D}\log p(\bm{\epsilon})
is the average information content per coordinate, analogous to the empirical entropy of an i.i.d. sequence.
For the isotropic Gaussian prior,

-\frac{1}{D}\log p(\bm{\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2D}\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

Therefore, typicality implies

\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}/D\approx 1
.
In high dimensions, Gaussian samples concentrate on a thin shell of radius approximately
\sqrt{D}
, rather than near the mode.
This illustrates the key distinction between density and typicality: the zero vector has the highest density under the Gaussian prior, but is extremely unlikely to be sampled in high dimensions. Although the Gaussian distribution has full support over
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, most of its probability mass concentrates on a small subset of that space as
D
grows.
Equivalently, a typical sample is not characterized only by its likelihood, but by a collection of sample statistics that take values close to their expectations under the Gaussian prior.

Appendix B
Inference-time Steering of Video World Models

B.1
Regularizing Noise within the Typical Set of High-Dimensional Gaussian Distribution

In this section, we elaborate on Sec.
4.2
, where we impose typical-set constraints during noise optimization. In high-dimensional Gaussian distributions, regions of high probability
density
do not necessarily coincide with regions of high probability
mass
, where typical samples concentrate. For example, the zero vector has the highest density under a standard Gaussian, yet it is almost never sampled in high dimensions; instead, typical samples lie on a thin shell with squared norm approximately
d
. We refer to this high-mass region as the
typical set
. As illustrated in Fig.
8
, the typical set is distinct from the high-density region, and sampling noise outside the typical set can lead to implausible generations. This distinction is important for diffusion models, which are trained to denoise samples initialized from typical Gaussian noise. If optimization pushes the initial noise toward atypical, low-mass regions of the prior, the denoiser may be evaluated on inputs rarely seen during training, leading to approximation errors and, consequently, implausible or OOD generations.

Figure 8:
Probability density vs. mass in a high-dimensional Gaussian distribution.
Noise sampled from the typical set leads to plausible generations, whereas noise outside the typical set–despite having high probability density–produces implausible generations, e.g., humans becoming blurry or transforming into vehicles.

A common way to encourage typicality is to regularize the norm of the optimized noise, since Gaussian samples exhibit strong norm concentration in high dimensions. Several prior methods have found norm-based regularization sufficient for preserving plausibility in diffusion generation
[
95
,
34
,
106
]
. However, the norm captures only one of the numerous sample statistics of a Gaussian sample. In practice, we find that additional typicality constraints are important for high-dimensional video world models. Thus, as described in Sec.
4.2
, we regularize multiple statistics of the optimized noise, including norm concentration, isotropy, and spectral whiteness, each motivated by concentration properties of high-dimensional Gaussian samples.

Figure 9:
Examples of atypical Gaussian noise.

Left
: typical noise sampled from a Gaussian prior.
Others
: noises perturbed to violate norm concentration, isotropy, and spectral whiteness, respectively.

Fig.
9
illustrates examples of Gaussian noise visualized as 2D images, where each atypical sample violates one statistic of the Gaussian typical set. Violating norm concentration produces noise with globally biased values compared to typical Gaussian noise. Violating isotropy introduces local coordinate patterns that do not appear in typical i.i.d. noise, while violating spectral whiteness produces structured, non-white patterns in the frequency domain.

B.2
Concentration of Gaussian Typical Set Regularizers

Norm Concentration.

For Gaussian noise
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, the squared norm satisfies

\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{D}
, and therefore concentrates sharply around
D
. Equivalently, the Euclidean norm, or radius
\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}
concentrates around
\sqrt{D}
. Moreover, if
R=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}
, then
R
follows a chi distribution with
D
degrees of freedom, whose standard deviation remains bounded independently of
D
and approaches
1/\sqrt{2}
as
D\to\infty
. Thus, although the typical radius grows as
\sqrt{D}
, the absolute width of the shell containing most Gaussian probability mass remains approximately constant. This motivates penalizing deviations from the typical Gaussian shell in radius:

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\left(\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}-\sqrt{D}\right)^{2}.

Lemma 1

(Norm concentration of Gaussian noise)
.

Let
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}}{D}-1\right|>2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{D}}+2\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{D}\right)\leq\delta.

Equivalently, the probability that
\bm{\epsilon}
lies outside this typical norm shell is at most
\delta
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, its coordinates

\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{D}
are independent standard normal random variables. Hence,

\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\epsilon_{j}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{D}.

Let
X=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}
. By the Laurent–Massart inequality
[
66
]
, for any
x>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X-D\geq 2\sqrt{Dx}+2x\right)\leq e^{-x},\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left(D-X\geq 2\sqrt{Dx}\right)\leq e^{-x}.

Taking
x=\log(2/\delta)
, each tail event has probability at most

\delta/2
. Therefore, by a union bound, with probability at least

1-\delta
,

-2\sqrt{D\log(2/\delta)}\leq X-D\leq 2\sqrt{D\log(2/\delta)}+2\log(2/\delta).

Dividing by
D
gives

\left|\frac{X}{D}-1\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{D}}+2\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{D}.

Substituting
X=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1

(High-probability scale of the radial norm regularizer)
.

For

|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|=\left(\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}-\sqrt{D}\right)^{2},

we have, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|\leq\left(2\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}+2\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\sqrt{D}}\right)^{2}.

Consequently,

|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{norm}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|=O\!\left(\log(1/\delta)+\frac{\log^{2}(1/\delta)}{D}\right)

with high probability.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left|\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}-D\right|\leq 2\sqrt{D\log(2/\delta)}+2\log(2/\delta).

Let
R=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}
. Since

|R-\sqrt{D}|=\frac{|R^{2}-D|}{R+\sqrt{D}}\leq\frac{|R^{2}-D|}{\sqrt{D}},

we obtain

|R-\sqrt{D}|\leq 2\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}+2\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\sqrt{D}}.

Squaring both sides gives the desired bound.
∎

Corollary 2

(Dimension-independent width of the Gaussian shell)
.

Let
R=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}
with

\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
. Then the standard deviation of
R
is bounded independently of
D
:

\operatorname{std}(R)=O(1).

More specifically,
R
follows a chi distribution with
D
degrees of freedom, and

\operatorname{std}(R)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad\text{as}\qquad D\to\infty.

Proof.

Since
R=\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}
and

\|\bm{\epsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{D}
, the random variable
R
follows a chi distribution with
D
degrees of freedom. The chi distribution has moments

\mathbb{E}[R^{2}]=D,\qquad\mathbb{E}[R]=\sqrt{2}\,\frac{\Gamma((D+1)/2)}{\Gamma(D/2)}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{Var}(R)=D-2\left(\frac{\Gamma((D+1)/2)}{\Gamma(D/2)}\right)^{2}.

This variance remains bounded as
D
grows, and in fact converges to
1/2
. Hence

\operatorname{std}(R)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

showing that the width of the Gaussian shell is asymptotically independent of the dimension.
∎

Isotropy Concentration.

We now show that the isotropy regularizer is small with high probability under the Gaussian prior. Let

\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, and suppose

D=mk
. We randomly permute the coordinates of
\bm{\epsilon}
and partition them into

m
blocks

\bm{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\epsilon}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Since the Gaussian prior has i.i.d. coordinates, this random permutation does not change the distribution, and the blocks satisfy

\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{k}).

Thus, their empirical second moment

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}

should be close to
\mathbf{I}_{k}
under the Gaussian prior. We measure deviations from this blockwise isotropy using

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\frac{1}{k}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{I}_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Lemma 2

(Small probability of anisotropic Gaussian block covariance)
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that, for any

\delta\in(0,1)
, if
m\gtrsim\log(2k^{2}/\delta)
, then

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|>C\frac{k\log(2k^{2}/\delta)}{m}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

For each entry of
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
,

\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i,a}\epsilon_{i,b}.

First, consider the diagonal terms. Since

\epsilon_{i,a}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,

\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i,a}^{2},\quad\text{therefore,}\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa}]=1,\quad\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa})=\frac{2}{m},\quad\Rightarrow\,\mathbb{E}\left[(\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa}-1)^{2}\right]=\frac{2}{m}.

For the off-diagonal terms
a\neq b
, the products

\epsilon_{i,a}\epsilon_{i,b}
have mean zero and variance one. Hence

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}]=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab})=\frac{1}{m},\quad\text{which gives}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{m}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{I}_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa}-1)^{2}\right]+\sum_{a\neq b}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=k\cdot\frac{2}{m}+k(k-1)\cdot\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}+k}{m}.

Dividing by
k
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|\right]=\frac{k+1}{m}.

For the high-probability statement, note that each diagonal error

\widehat{\Sigma}_{aa}-1
is an average of centered sub-exponential random variables, and each off-diagonal entry
\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}
is an average of products of independent Gaussians, which are also sub-exponential. Therefore, by Bernstein-type concentration
[
56
]
, for all entries,

\mathbb{P}\left(|\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}-\delta_{ab}|>t\right)\leq 2\exp(-cmt^{2})

for
0<t\lesssim 1
, where
c>0
is a universal constant. Applying a union bound over the
k^{2}
entries and taking

t=C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2k^{2}/\delta)}{m}},

we obtain, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\widehat{\Sigma}_{ab}-\delta_{ab}|\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2k^{2}/\delta)}{m}}\qquad\text{for all }a,b.

Hence,

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{I}_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq k^{2}{C^{\prime}}^{2}\frac{\log(2k^{2}/\delta)}{m}.

Dividing by
k
yields

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{iso}}(\bm{\epsilon})\leq C\frac{k\log(2k^{2}/\delta)}{m}

with probability at least
1-\delta
, for a universal constant
C>0
.
∎

This shows that the normalized isotropy penalty is small under the training-time Gaussian prior whenever
m\gg k
. Unlike norm concentration, which only constrains the total energy

\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}})
, the isotropy penalty also discourages unequal coordinate variances and nonzero off-diagonal correlations. Therefore, it detects structured coordinate patterns that may still have a typical global norm but are unlikely under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior.

Spectral-Whiteness Concentration.

The isotropy regularizer checks whether randomly grouped coordinates have approximately identity covariance, but it does not directly rule out spatial frequency structure. For example, optimized noise may have excessive low-frequency energy, producing smooth spatial patterns, or excessive high-frequency energy, producing oscillatory artifacts. Under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior, the noise should instead be spectrally white: its energy should be spread evenly across spatial frequencies.

To formalize this, consider one spatial slice of the noise and let

\widetilde{\bm{\epsilon}}=U\bm{\epsilon}

be its discrete Fourier transform, where
U
is an orthonormal Fourier basis. For real-valued noise, this can be interpreted as the equivalent real sine–cosine basis. Since the standard Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant,

\widetilde{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).

Thus, no frequency band should systematically contain more energy than another. Individual Fourier powers
|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{j}|^{2}
are noisy, but averages over sufficiently large frequency bins concentrate.

Let
\{\mathcal{B}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
be disjoint frequency bins, and define the average power in each bin by

\widehat{p}_{b}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{b}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{j}|^{2}.

We penalize deviations from a flat binned spectrum using

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon}):=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\widehat{p}_{b}-\bar{p}\right)^{2},\qquad\bar{p}:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{p}_{b}.

Lemma 3

(Small probability of atypical spectral energy)
.

Let
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, and let

\widetilde{\bm{\epsilon}}=U\bm{\epsilon}
, where
U
is an orthonormal discrete Fourier transform. Let
\{\mathcal{B}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
be disjoint frequency bins, and define
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}
as above. Let

n_{\min}:=\min_{b}|\mathcal{B}_{b}|.

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|>4\left(2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{n_{\min}}}+2\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{n_{\min}}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

Since
U
is orthonormal and the standard Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant,

\widetilde{\bm{\epsilon}}=U\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).

Thus, in the equivalent real orthonormal Fourier representation, the Fourier coefficients are independent standard normal random variables.

For each frequency bin
\mathcal{B}_{b}
,

|\mathcal{B}_{b}|\widehat{p}_{b}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{j}|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{|\mathcal{B}_{b}|}.

By the Laurent–Massart inequality
[
66
]
and a union bound over the
B
bins, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
b=1,\ldots,B
,

\left|\widehat{p}_{b}-1\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{|\mathcal{B}_{b}|}}+2\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{|\mathcal{B}_{b}|}.

Since
|\mathcal{B}_{b}|\geq n_{\min}
, we have

\max_{b}\left|\widehat{p}_{b}-1\right|\leq\eta,\qquad\eta:=2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{n_{\min}}}+2\frac{\log(2B/\delta)}{n_{\min}}.

Moreover,

|\bar{p}-1|=\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(\widehat{p}_{b}-1)\right|\leq\eta.

Therefore, for every bin,

|\widehat{p}_{b}-\bar{p}|\leq|\widehat{p}_{b}-1|+|\bar{p}-1|\leq 2\eta.

Substituting this into the definition of the regularizer gives

|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\widehat{p}_{b}-\bar{p}\right)^{2}\leq 4\eta^{2}.

Equivalently,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\,\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{\epsilon})\,|>4\eta^{2}\right)\leq\delta,

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma
3
shows that the spectral-whiteness penalty is small under the Gaussian prior when each frequency bin contains sufficiently many coefficients. Thus, a large value indicates an atypical spatial frequency structure. This regularizer is complementary to isotropy: isotropy checks covariance after random coordinate grouping, whereas spectral whiteness checks whether energy is evenly distributed across spatial frequencies.

B.4
Score-Distillation for Approximating Initial-Noise Gradients

This section justifies the gradient approximation used in (
8
). We omit the conditioning variables
(\mathbf{o}^{\text{hist}},\mathbf{a})
for readability. Let the deterministic sampler define the reverse trajectory

\mathbf{x}^{\tau-1}=d^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}),\qquad\mathbf{x}^{T}=\bm{\epsilon},\qquad\mathbf{x}^{0}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon}),

where each denoising step follows the update in (
3
):

d^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})=\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}-\sigma^{\tau}g_{\theta}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})\right)+\sigma^{\tau-1}g_{\theta}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}).

For a differentiable criterion
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0})
, the exact initial-noise gradient is obtained by the chain rule:

\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0})=\left[\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\frac{\partial d^{\tau}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}\right]^{\!\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{0}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0}),

where the product is ordered along the reverse denoising trajectory. The Jacobian of each step is

\frac{\partial d^{\tau}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}=\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\mathbf{I}+\left(\sigma^{\tau-1}-\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\sigma^{\tau}\right)\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g},\,\text{where}\,\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g}=\frac{\partial g_{\theta}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}.

Thus, exact backpropagation requires multiplying by all denoiser Jacobians and storing the full denoising computation graph.

Proportional-Jacobian approximation.
Ahn et al.
[
3
]
empirically observe that the denoiser Jacobian is approximately diagonal and often behaves like a timestep-dependent multiple of the identity. Under the idealized approximation

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g}\approx\rho^{\tau}\mathbf{I},

the sampler Jacobian becomes

\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\left[\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\mathbf{I}+\left(\sigma^{\tau-1}-\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\sigma^{\tau}\right)\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g}\right]\approx\beta\mathbf{I},\,\text{where}\,\beta:=\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\left[\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}+\left(\sigma^{\tau-1}-\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\sigma^{\tau}\right)\rho^{\tau}\right].

Substituting this into the exact chain rule yields

\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\mathcal{C}(f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon}))\approx\beta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{0}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0}),\qquad\mathbf{x}^{0}=f_{\theta}(\bm{\epsilon}).

This is the approximation used in the main text: instead of differentiating through the sampler, we compute the criterion gradient with respect to the generated clean latent and use it as a scaled proxy for the initial-noise gradient.

Diagonal-Jacobian variant.
If the denoiser Jacobian is diagonal but not exactly proportional to the identity,

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g}\approx\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\rho}^{\tau}),

then the same derivation gives a diagonal preconditioner

\nabla_{\bm{\epsilon}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\approx\mathbf{B}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{0}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{0}),

where

\mathbf{B}=\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\operatorname{diag}\!\left[\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\mathbf{1}+\left(\sigma^{\tau-1}-\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\sigma^{\tau}\right)\bm{\rho}^{\tau}\right].

The scalar approximation
\mathbf{B}\approx\beta\mathbf{I}
is therefore a practical simplification: it assumes the diagonal entries vary slowly enough that their average effect can be absorbed into the optimization step size.

Connection to stop-gradient MSD.
Multistep score distillation
[
3
]
keeps the forward denoising trajectory unchanged but applies a stop-gradient operator to the denoiser outputs during backpropagation. In our notation, the stop-gradient version of one denoising step is

d^{\tau}_{\mathrm{SG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})=\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\tau}-\sigma^{\tau}\operatorname{SG}\!\left[g_{\theta}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})\right]\right)+\sigma^{\tau-1}\operatorname{SG}\!\left[g_{\theta}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\tau})\right],

where
\operatorname{SG}[\cdot]
is the identity in the forward pass and has zero derivative in the backward pass. Therefore, this removes
\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{g}
from the backward pass, leaving only the explicit linear dependence on
\mathbf{x}^{\tau}
:

\frac{\partial d^{\tau}_{\mathrm{SG}}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}=\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\mathbf{I}.

Under the same proportional-Jacobian approximation, both the full-gradient and stop-gradient Jacobian products are scalar multiples of the identity:

\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\frac{\partial d^{\tau}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}\approx\beta\mathbf{I},\qquad\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\frac{\partial d^{\tau}_{\mathrm{SG}}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}=\left(\prod_{\tau=T}^{1}\frac{\alpha^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}\right)\mathbf{I}.

Therefore, the stop-gradient gradient and the full sampler gradient differ primarily by timestep-dependent scalar factors. Our approximation uses this same principle, but applies it directly to optimize the initial noise: the clean-latent criterion gradient is used as a proxy direction for the initial-noise gradient, with the unknown scalar absorbed into
\beta
and the learning rate.

The noise-gradient approximation enables efficient gradient-based steering without backpropagating through the iterative denoising process of large-scale video diffusion models. However, it relies on the assumption that the Jacobian of the diffusion sampler is approximately diagonal
[

]
. Indeed, prior work on noise optimization for text-to-image generation often relies on one-step distilled generators to make noise optimization tractable without differentiating through the full iterative sampling process.

We therefore empirically evaluate whether this approximation provides a useful gradient signal in the initial-noise space for generating high-reward samples in the output space. Fig.

Baseline: Classifier Guidance.

We also compare against classifier guidance
[
26
,
69
]
, which modifies the denoising process at each diffusion step using the gradient of the failure criterion. In the world-model setting, however, such guidance is difficult to apply directly. The failure predictor
\ell_{\theta}
is trained on clean latent states, not on noisy intermediate diffusion states. Therefore, evaluating
\ell_{\theta}
directly on
z_{t}^{\tau}
can provide unreliable gradients, while exact guidance would require backpropagating through the denoiser at every denoising step.

To make classifier guidance tractable, we use a one-step clean-latent approximation
[
69
]
. Given a noisy latent
z_{t+1}^{\tau}
, we estimate the corresponding clean latent by inverting the forward diffusion path:

\hat{z}_{t+1}^{0\mid\tau}=\frac{z_{t+1}^{\tau}-\sigma^{\tau}g_{\theta}^{\tau}\left(z_{t+1}^{\tau},z_{t},a_{t}\right)}{\alpha^{\tau}}.

The guidance gradient is then computed on this approximately clean latent:

\nabla_{z_{t+1}^{\tau}}\ell_{\theta}\left(\hat{z}_{t+1}^{0\mid\tau}\right)\approx\frac{1}{\alpha^{\tau}}\nabla_{\hat{z}_{t+1}^{0\mid\tau}}\ell_{\theta}\left(\hat{z}_{t+1}^{0\mid\tau}\right),

which avoids backpropagating through the denoising network, but it can introduce bias because the one-step clean prediction may be misaligned with the final generated latent after the full denoising trajectory. This issue is particularly pronounced in action-conditioned world models, where small guidance errors can push the generation toward high-failure latents that are inconsistent with the conditioning action sequence or the learned dynamics.

Empirical Density Estimation.

We additionally evaluate whether the generated samples themselves remain
in-distribution
by fitting a flow-matching density surrogate in the latent observation space
[
124
]
. Let

z=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(o)
denote the latent representation of an observation from the training dataset. We train a flow-matching vector field
q_{\psi}
that transports training latents to standard Gaussian noise. Specifically, for
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
and
\tau\in[0,1]
, define the linear interpolation

z^{\tau}=z+\tau(w-z).

The vector field is trained to predict the displacement toward the Gaussian endpoint:

q_{\psi}(z^{\tau},\tau)\approx w-z.

Given a generated latent
\hat{z}_{t}
, we estimate its corresponding Gaussian endpoint with a one-step prediction from
\tau=0
:

\widehat{w}_{t}=\hat{z}_{t}+q_{\psi}(\hat{z}_{t},0).

If
\hat{z}_{t}
is in-distribution, then
\widehat{w}_{t}
should be close to a sample from
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
. Since the Gaussian log-density is proportional to
-\|\widehat{w}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
, we use

s_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\hat{z}_{t}):=\|\widehat{w}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

as the latent OOD score. A higher score indicates that the generated latent is farther from the distribution of encoded in-distribution observations. This density-based OOD score complements the noise-space regularizers by checking whether the resulting generated samples, not only the optimized initial noise, remain plausible.

failure

D.4
Evaluation Metrics

Autonomous Driving.

To evaluate steering, we use held-out metrics that are not used during optimization. This is important because a steered generation can increase the optimization score without producing a meaningful or plausible change in the video. We therefore evaluate two complementary aspects: (i)
target alignment
, i.e., whether the generated video clearly depicts the target event described by the text prompt, and (ii)
plausibility
, i.e., whether the generated video remains physically reasonable and free from severe visual artifacts that indicate out-of-distribution generation.

For autonomous driving, we adopt WorldModelBench (WMB)
[
68
]
, which evaluates video world-model generations using both text-prompt alignment and video-quality criteria. We use WMB because it includes driving data in its evaluation distribution and, empirically, provides meaningful scores for whether generated driving videos both match the target prompt and remain visually plausible. WMB reports three scores: instruction following
(0\text{--}3)
, physics adherence
(0\text{--}5)
, and commonsense consistency
(0\text{--}2)
. We use the instruction-following score to measure target alignment, and use physics adherence and commonsense consistency as proxies for generation plausibility.

In addition, we report a target-alignment score from an external VLM judge. This judge is used only for evaluation and is not used during steering. We use the following prompt template with Gemini
[
107
]
to obtain a
0\text{--}10
alignment score:

You are an expert evaluator of autonomous driving world models.
Your ONLY task is to judge how well the video follows the text instruction.
Ignore visual quality issues such as blur, flickering, warping, or low resolution: these are expected artifacts of world-model generation and must NOT affect your score.

TEXT DESCRIPTION: {prompt}

SCORING RUBRIC (0-10):

10: The video is aligned with the text description clearly and completely.

7-9: The video is aligned with the text description but with minor deviations (e.g., slightly wrong direction or timing).

4-6: The video is vaguely related to the text description, but the specific action does not happen.

1-3: The video is completely unrelated to the text description.

0: The video content is completely unrelated to the text description.

Robotic Manipulation.
We use human evaluation to determine whether each generated trajectory corresponds to task success or failure. We additionally report scores from Robometer
[
71
]
, a general-purpose robotic reward model. However, we rely on human evaluation as the primary metric for a more accurate assessment of generated trajectories, since reward models provide continuous scores and require prompt engineering to infer task success.

Appendix F
Limitations & Discussions

Suboptimality of Noise Optimization.

While
StressDream
provides a practical instantiation of steering video WM imaginations via gradient-based noise optimization, the optimized noise may still be suboptimal. The optimization in
StressDream
should be viewed as a
local refinement
of the initial noise rather than a global search over all possible futures. Exhaustively searching the high-dimensional noise space is infeasible: if
\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
, then
\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},2\mathbf{I}_{D})
, so
\|\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/2\sim\chi_{D}^{2}
, and therefore
\|\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\|_{2}
concentrates around
\sqrt{2D}
. In contrast, the average
L_{2}
distance between the initial and optimized noises in our experiments is far smaller, indicating that
StressDream
performs local refinement rather than global exploration. The typical-set constraints make gradient updates reliable mainly within a moderate local region; overly large updates can push the noise OOD and make generations implausible. A natural extension is to combine Best-of-
N
-style search with gradient-based refinement
[
75
,
126
]
, or to learn a value function
[
54
]
to guide denoising. Finally, for video WMs that require autoregressive rollouts, later generations condition on previously generated futures. The optimal solution would therefore require jointly optimizing a sequence of noises across rollout segments, but the current implementation solves it sequentially.

Preserving Plausibility.

StressDream
preserves plausibility by regularizing the optimized noise, encouraging it to remain in the typical set of the high-dimensional Gaussian. However, our notion of plausibility is defined with respect to the learned WM distribution: it assumes that generations from Gaussian noise are plausible under the WM. Thus,
StressDream
can keep steering within the outcomes the WM supports, but it cannot correct a base WM that already produces physically implausible generations. One could incorporate video-quality or physical-consistency metrics as part of the steering objective
[
89
,
34
,
33
]
, to explicitly bias generations toward higher-quality imaginations. Conversely,
StressDream
also cannot imagine outcomes that may be plausible in the real world but are absent from the WM distribution. This highlights the importance of training WMs on diverse interaction data, including failures and rare outcomes, rather than success-only data
[
62
]
, so that their predictive distributions can support policy evaluation and improvement.

Computational Inefficiency.
Although
StressDream
is sample-efficient for generating plausible target outcomes compared to random sampling by leveraging dense VLM gradients, its runtime is still primarily bottlenecked by video WM generation, which can take several minutes per imagination with current models
[
38
,
50
,
42
,
1
]
. Inference-time steering methods for text-to-image generation often reduce this cost with step-distilled or shortcut models that generate samples in one or a few denoising steps, making both noise optimization and gradient computation more tractable
[
34
,
33
]
. Our method is orthogonal to these advances: as video WMs become faster,
StressDream
directly benefits from improved sampling efficiency, and shortcut models may enable more accurate gradient computations without relying on the current approximation. Another promising direction is to amortize the iterative optimization by learning a noise generator for fixed target outcomes, similar to
33
,
113
, but enforcing the typical-set constraints in high-dimensional initial noise space remains challenging. Lastly,
StressDream
incurs additional overhead from backpropagating through the criterion function, making each optimization step more expensive (Appendix
B.6
for more details). However, random sampling still requires evaluating generated samples with expensive verifiers, so systematic steering within a finite sampling budget remains useful for discovering high-impact outcomes from WMs.

Reward Hacking.
Although our gradient-based optimization with the Vision-Language-Model’s gradient is empirically effective, it can sometimes introduce reward hacking, where the criterion increases without inducing meaningful changes in the generated video
[
34
,
106
,
55
]
. Prior work mitigates this issue by combining multiple reward functions or adding image-/video-quality metrics that directly penalize degenerate generations
[
34
,
89
]
.
StressDream
can also incorporate these strategies. In practice, however, we observe that VLM-based criteria trained on internet-scale data are substantially more robust than reward functions learned from scratch: they tend to assign high scores only when the target event is semantically evident in the generated video, whereas task-specific learned rewards are more susceptible to adversarial artifacts. This limitation may be further mitigated by more efficient video WM architectures with robust latent representations
[
90
]
, shortcut/distilled diffusion models with fewer denoising steps
[
47
]
, and stronger reward models with better video understanding and robustness to adversarial artifacts
[
71
]
. Moreover,
StressDream
requires specifying target high-impact outcomes with text prompts, which may need to be adapted for each scene, and the criterion’s effectiveness depends on prompt quality (see Appendix
B.3
).
