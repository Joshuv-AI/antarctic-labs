Ginkgo biloba extract mitigates behavioral deficits, oxidative stress, and hippocampal remodeling in a rat model of PTSD
Authors: Morvarid Meamar, Zeynab Mohamadi Yarijani, Houman Parsaei, Abbas Ali Vafaei, Ali Rashidy-Pour, Payman Raise-Abdullahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is associated with cognitive impairments, anxiety, fear extinction deficits, and neurobiological alterations, particularly in the hippocampus. Oxidative stress, reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and dendritic remodeling are key contributors to these dysfunctions. This study investigated whether Ginkgo biloba extract, known for its antioxidant and neuroprotective properties, could ameliorate PTSD-like symptoms and hippocampal abnormalities in male rats exposed to the single prolonged stress (SPS) model. Adult male Wistar rats were exposed to SPS and treated with Ginkgo biloba extract (20 or 200 mg/kg) for 14 days. Behavioral assessments included anxiety-like behavior, fear extinction, spatial learning and memory, and working memory. Hippocampal tissue was analyzed for oxidative stress markers (MDA, SOD, TAC), BDNF expression (Western blot), and CA3 dendritic morphology (Golgi-Cox impregnation method). SPS exposure induced marked behavioral deficits, elevated oxidative stress, reduced BDNF expression, and dendritic atrophy in CA3 pyramidal neurons. Ginkgo biloba extract, particularly at a dose of 200 mg/kg, significantly attenuated anxiety-like behaviors, facilitated fear extinction, improved memory performance, reduced oxidative damage, restored BDNF levels, and reversed dendritic retraction and branching deficits in the CA3 region of the hippocampus. These findings demonstrate that Ginkgo biloba extract exerts neuroprotective effects against trauma-induced behavioral, molecular, and structural abnormalities in a validated animal model of PTSD. While promising, these results are based on preclinical data, and further studies are necessary to determine whether similar benefits can be translated to clinical populations.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a complex and often chronic mental health condition that arises following exposure to severe psychological trauma, such as natural disasters, accidents, or interpersonal violence. Core features of PTSD include intrusive recollections, hyperarousal, avoidance behaviors, and disturbances in mood, cognition, and memory [1]. In addition to these hallmark psychological symptoms, converging evidence from both human and animal studies indicates that PTSD is associated with significant neurobiological alterations, particularly in brain regions involved in emotional regulation, memory processing, and fear extinction [2].

The pathophysiology of PTSD involves complex interactions among neuroendocrine, neurochemical, and neuroinflammatory systems. A central feature is dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which alters glucocorticoid signaling and contributes to maladaptive stress responses [2]. Chronic stress exposure has been associated with enhanced oxidative stress, characterized by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and impaired antioxidant defenses, leading to cellular damage within stress-sensitive brain regions [3, 4]. In parallel, neuroinflammatory processes, including microglial activation and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine release, further contribute to neuronal dysfunction and synaptic alterations. Among affected brain regions, the hippocampus is particularly vulnerable due to its high density of glucocorticoid receptors and its critical role in memory processing and emotional regulation. Stress-induced alterations in hippocampal structure and function, including dendritic remodeling and impaired neuroplasticity, have been consistently reported in both preclinical and clinical studies. These neurobiological changes are closely linked to dysregulation of key neurotransmitter systems, including glutamatergic and GABAergic signaling, as well as reduced neurotrophic support, particularly brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to the persistence of PTSD symptoms and represent important targets for therapeutic intervention [2, 4].

The hippocampus, a medial temporal lobe structure central to learning, memory, and contextual processing of fear, is consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of PTSD [5–7]. Structural imaging studies have reported reduced hippocampal volume in individuals with PTSD; however, the directionality of this association remains a subject of ongoing debate. While substantial evidence suggests that chronic stress and elevated glucocorticoid exposure can lead to hippocampal atrophy over time, other studies indicate that smaller hippocampal volume may represent a pre-existing vulnerability factor that increases susceptibility to PTSD following trauma exposure. For instance, twin studies have demonstrated that individuals with smaller hippocampi prior to trauma exposure are more likely to develop PTSD, suggesting that hippocampal structure may serve as both a risk factor and a consequence of the disorder [5, 8]. Preclinical studies have further revealed that exposure to traumatic stress can lead to molecular and cellular changes in the hippocampus, including increased oxidative stress, impaired antioxidant defenses, and disruption of neurotrophic support systems, such as reduced BDNF expression [9, 10]. Within the hippocampus, distinct subregions contribute differentially to memory processing and stress responses. The CA3 subregion is particularly relevant in the context of stress-related disorders due to its unique anatomical and functional properties. CA3 pyramidal neurons are characterized by extensive recurrent excitatory collateral connections, which support associative memory processing and pattern completion [11]. Importantly, this region exhibits a high density of glucocorticoid receptors, rendering it especially sensitive to stress hormones and highly vulnerable to stress-induced structural remodeling [12, 13].

Experimental evidence has consistently demonstrated that exposure to chronic or traumatic stress induces dendritic retraction and reduced branching complexity specifically in CA3 pyramidal neurons, alterations that are closely associated with impairments in spatial memory and emotional regulation [13]. These structural changes are thought to arise from the combined effects of prolonged glucocorticoid exposure, excitotoxic glutamatergic signaling, and oxidative stress mechanisms [12, 14]. In contrast, other hippocampal subregions, such as the dentate gyrus, are more strongly associated with adult neurogenesis and pattern separation, while CA1 primarily contributes to memory consolidation and hippocampal output. Given its well-documented susceptibility to stress-induced plasticity and its critical role in hippocampal-dependent cognitive processes, the CA3 subregion represents a particularly relevant target for investigating the structural and molecular mechanisms underlying PTSD-like pathology and for evaluating potential neuroprotective interventions.

Oxidative stress, defined as an imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defense mechanisms, has emerged as a critical contributor to stress-induced neuronal dysfunction and cognitive impairment. Elevated levels of lipid peroxidation products, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), along with reductions in endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), have been observed in both patients with PTSD and animal models of traumatic stress [3]. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that reductions in BDNF expression, particularly within the hippocampus, may impair synaptic plasticity and hinder the extinction of traumatic memories, further perpetuating the disorder [15].

The single prolonged stress (SPS) model has been widely utilized to mimic PTSD-like symptoms in rodents, as it produces long-lasting behavioral and neurobiological alterations resembling key aspects of the human condition [16]. Animals exposed to SPS typically exhibit increased anxiety-like behavior, impairments in fear extinction, and cognitive deficits in tasks assessing spatial learning and memory. At the molecular level, SPS has been shown to induce oxidative damage and reduce hippocampal BDNF expression, making it a well-established model to investigate potential therapeutic strategies targeting these pathological mechanisms [16, 17].

Bioactive compounds derived from herbs and plant-based foods have demonstrated therapeutic potential in experimental models of PTSD. Preclinical studies suggest that several plant-derived agents can modulate fear memory, stress hormone regulation, neurotransmission, and neuroinflammation, offering promise as complementary strategies for managing trauma-related disorders [18]. In light of growing interest in complementary and plant-based interventions for neuropsychiatric disorders, Ginkgo biloba, an extract derived from the Ginkgo tree’s leaves, has received considerable scientific attention. Ginkgo biloba is recognized for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties, and has been extensively studied for its potential to enhance cognitive function and mitigate neurodegeneration [19]. Several preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that Ginkgo biloba extract enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, reduces lipid peroxidation, modulates neurotransmitter systems and intracellular signaling, and promotes neurotrophic factor expression, particularly BDNF, in models of cognitive impairment and stress-related disorders [19–21]. However, despite these promising properties, its potential protective effects in the context of PTSD, especially concerning hippocampal oxidative stress and BDNF regulation, remain largely unexplored.

Given the critical role of hippocampal oxidative damage, neurotrophic deficits, and structural synaptic alterations in the development of PTSD-like symptoms, investigating whether Ginkgo biloba extract can mitigate these molecular and morphological changes while improving behavioral outcomes is of considerable scientific interest. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of oral administration of Ginkgo biloba extract at two different doses on anxiety-like behavior, fear extinction, spatial learning and reference memory, and spatial working memory in male rats exposed to the SPS model of PTSD. In parallel, hippocampal oxidative stress markers, BDNF protein expression, and neuronal morphology were assessed using biochemical assays, Western blotting, and Golgi-Cox impregnation techniques, respectively. We hypothesized that Ginkgo biloba extract would alleviate trauma-induced behavioral deficits, reduce oxidative and neurotrophic disruptions, and preserve hippocampal neuronal architecture, thereby supporting its potential as a complementary therapeutic agent for stress-related disorders.

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of seventy-two adult male Wistar rats (220–250 g, 10 weeks old) were used in this study. The animals were housed in groups under standard laboratory conditions, including a controlled ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and a 12-hour light/dark cycle (6:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m.). Food and water were available ad libitum throughout the experiment. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals established by the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Semnan University of Medical Sciences (IR.SEMUMS.AEC.1402.015).

Experimental design

At the beginning of the experiment (day 0), seventy-two adult male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 12 per group) as follows:

(1) Sham + Saline (SH-SAL); (2) Sham + Ginkgo biloba 20 mg/kg (SH-GB 20); (3) Sham + Ginkgo biloba 200 mg/kg (SH-GB 200); (4) Single Prolonged Stress + Saline (SPS-SAL); (5) SPS + Ginkgo biloba 20 mg/kg (SPS-GB 20); (6) SPS + Ginkgo biloba 200 mg/kg (SPS-GB 200).

On day 1, animals in the SPS groups were subjected to the SPS protocol to induce PTSD-like symptoms, while rats in the sham groups were handled similarly without exposure to stress procedures. Beginning on day 2, rats received oral gavage of standardized Ginkgo biloba extract or saline for 14 consecutive days, continuing until day 15. The extract was freshly prepared by dissolving the appropriate amount of Ginkgo biloba extract in normal saline prior to administration. The selected doses (20 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg, dissolved in 1 ml saline) were based on previous reports demonstrating the antioxidant and neuroprotective properties of Ginkgo biloba in rodent models [22]. Control animals in the SH-SAL and SPS-SAL groups received an equivalent volume of saline by oral gavage. Behavioral assessments were conducted in the following sequence: On day 16, anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated using the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM). On day 17, rats underwent contextual fear conditioning. Fear extinction trials were conducted over four consecutive days (days 18–21) to assess the capability for fear extinction. Spatial learning and memory were evaluated using the Morris Water Maze (MWM) from days 22 to 26, with a probe trial conducted on the final day. Spatial working memory performance was evaluated with the T-maze task on days 27 and 28. All behavioral testing was performed between 09:00 and 13:00 to minimize circadian variability in corticosterone levels.

At the conclusion of behavioral testing (day 29), animals were weighed, deeply anesthetized with CO2, and euthanized by decapitation. The adrenal glands were carefully removed and weighed to assess HPA axis activity. Brains were rapidly extracted, and the hippocampi were dissected on ice for parallel biochemical and morphological evaluations. One cerebral hemisphere was allocated for biochemical analyses, including the quantification of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) using standardized commercial kits. Additionally, hippocampal BDNF expression was assessed by Western blot analysis. The contralateral hemisphere was processed using the Golgi-Cox impregnation technique to examine dendritic morphology in the CA3 region of the hippocampus, with particular focus on pyramidal neuron dendritic length and branching. These combined assessments were designed to elucidate the neurobiological mechanisms underlying the behavioral effects of Ginkgo biloba extract in the SPS model of PTSD (Fig. 1).

Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a randomization procedure. All behavioral testing, as well as biochemical, molecular, and morphological analyses, were conducted by investigators blinded to the group allocations.

Single prolonged stress (SPS) protocol

On day 1, animals in the SPS groups were subjected to the SPS protocol, a well-established rodent model of PTSD that mimics key behavioral and neuroendocrine features of the disorder. The SPS procedure was performed as previously described with minor modifications [23]. Briefly, rats were first exposed to restraint stress for 2 h using well-ventilated restraining tubes to induce psychological stress. Immediately following restraint, animals were subjected to forced swim stress in a cylindrical container filled with water (24 ± 1 °C) for 20 min. After a 15-minute recovery period, rats were exposed to diethyl ether vapor until loss of consciousness to model an acute traumatic experience. After completion of the SPS procedure, animals were returned to their home cages. Beginning on day 2, rats received daily oral administration of Ginkgo biloba extract or vehicle for 14 consecutive days (days 2–15), according to the experimental design described below. This period encompassed the post-stress phase during which PTSD-like behavioral and neurobiological alterations develop.

Drug administration

A standardized Ginkgo biloba extract (EGb 761) was used in the present study. The extract was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Netherlands, Cat. No. 05485001) and standardized to contain approximately 24% flavonoid glycosides and 6% terpene lactones, including ginkgolides A, B, C, and bilobalide, consistent with widely used EGb 761 formulations. For administration, the extract was freshly dissolved in sterile normal saline prior to each use and delivered by oral gavage in a total volume of 1 mL per animal. Two doses were selected: 20 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg. These doses were chosen to represent a low-to-moderate and a high pharmacologically active range, respectively, enabling evaluation of potential dose-dependent effects. Previous studies have shown that Ginkgo biloba extract within this dose range exerts significant antioxidant, neuroprotective, and cognitive-enhancing effects in rodent models of neurological and stress-related disorders [24, 25]. Treatment was administered once daily for 14 consecutive days, beginning 24 h after exposure to the SPS protocol. This subchronic treatment duration was selected based on the temporal dynamics of the SPS model, in which PTSD-like behavioral and neurobiological alterations emerge during the post-stress incubation period. The 14-day regimen allows sufficient time for Ginkgo biloba to modulate oxidative stress pathways, neurotrophic signaling, and synaptic plasticity mechanisms implicated in stress-induced hippocampal dysfunction.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed on day 16 using the EPM, a widely used behavioral paradigm based on rodents’ natural aversion to open and elevated spaces. The apparatus was constructed from wood and consisted of two opposing open arms (50 × 10 cm) and two enclosed arms of the same dimensions, surrounded by 40 cm-high walls. All four arms extended from a central square platform (10 × 10 cm), and the maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. The test was conducted in a dimly lit and quiet room to minimize external stressors.

At the beginning of each trial, rats were gently placed in the central platform facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. Animal behavior was recorded during this period. An arm entry was defined as the placement of all four paws into an arm. The primary behavioral parameters measured included the time spent in the open arms, the number of entries into the open arms, and the total number of arm entries, the latter serving as an indicator of general locomotor activity. Increased time spent and entries into the open arms were interpreted as reduced anxiety-like behavior. The maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues [26].

Contextual fear conditioning (CFC)

To evaluate conditioned fear responses and extinction learning, a rodent fear conditioning apparatus (TSE Systems, Bad Homburg, Germany) was used. The conditioning chamber was constructed from transparent Plexiglas and equipped with a floor consisting of 25 parallel stainless steel bars (6 mm in diameter, spaced 12 mm apart) that delivered foot shocks. Continuous, low-level background noise and consistent illumination were maintained throughout all experimental sessions to standardize environmental conditions. The chamber was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol after each trial to eliminate scent cues.

On day 17 of the study, rats underwent the fear conditioning protocol. Each animal was placed in the chamber and allowed to explore freely for 3 min. This was followed by the delivery of three footshocks (intensity: 1 mA, duration: 2 s) separated by 40-second intervals. After the final shock, the animals remained in the chamber for an additional 1 min, after which they were returned to their home cages.

Fear extinction trials were conducted over four consecutive days, starting 24 h after conditioning. During each extinction session, rats were returned to the same conditioning chamber for 5 min, under identical environmental conditions, but without receiving any footshocks. A trained observer, blinded to group assignments, assessed freezing behavior, defined as complete immobility of the animal in a characteristic crouched posture, with the exception of respiratory movements. Freezing behavior was monitored at 5-second intervals, and the percentage of time spent freezing was calculated for each extinction session. Morris water maze (MWM)

The MWM was employed to assess spatial learning and memory performance in the rats. The apparatus consisted of a large circular pool with a diameter of 150 cm and a height of 60 cm, divided into four quadrants: northwest, northeast, southwest, and southeast. The pool was filled with water to a depth of approximately 35 cm and maintained at 22 ± 1 °C to ensure animal comfort. A circular escape platform, 10 cm in diameter, was positioned 1.5 cm below the water surface in the center of the southwest quadrant. The platform location remained unchanged throughout all training sessions. However, to prevent the use of non-spatial strategies, the rats were released into the pool from randomly selected starting points (north, east, south, or west) in each trial. An overhead video camera was mounted 2 m above the center of the pool to record the animals’ movements. The tracking signals were transmitted to a computer-based monitoring system (Noldus EthoVision XT 7), which automatically analyzed and documented various parameters of the rats’ swimming patterns and spatial navigation. To assist spatial orientation, distinct visual cues were placed on the surrounding walls of the testing room, enabling the animals to locate the hidden platform using external environmental landmarks.

The testing procedure was conducted over five consecutive days. During the first four days (days 22–25), each rat underwent four training trials per day, with an inter-trial interval of approximately 30 s. In each trial, the rat was gently released into the water from one of the randomly selected starting positions and allowed to search for the hidden platform for up to 60 s. If the animal successfully located the platform, it was allowed to rest on it for 15 s before being removed. If the platform was not found within the allotted time, the rat was guided to it and allowed to remain there for 15 s.

On day 26, a probe trial was conducted to evaluate memory retention. For this trial, the platform was removed from the pool, and the rats were released from a novel starting point. Each animal was allowed to swim freely for 60 s, and the time spent in the target quadrant (where the platform had been located during training) was recorded as an index of spatial memory. At the end of each trial, the rats were dried and returned to their home cages. The pool water was replaced regularly, and the entire setup was cleaned as needed to maintain experimental consistency.

T-maze

Spatial working memory was evaluated using a T-maze apparatus constructed from transparent Plexiglas in the shape of a capital “T.” The maze consisted of a central stem (50 cm long, 10 cm wide) and two identical goal arms (40 cm long, 10 cm wide each) extending perpendicularly from the top of the stem. The walls of the maze were 20 cm high, and the entire structure was elevated 50 cm above the floor to restrict escape. Sliding doors were positioned to separate the goal arms from both the central alley and the start box. The testing environment was uniformly illuminated at approximately 50 lx by an overhead lamp positioned 2 m above the apparatus.

The task procedure consisted of two distinct phases: a sample (forced-choice) phase followed by a choice phase. During the sample phase, one of the goal arms was blocked, and the animal was placed at the start arm and allowed to enter the open arm. After the rat entered the arm with all four paws, it was confined for approximately 30 s. The animal was then removed from the maze. Following an inter-trial interval of 30–60 s, the choice phase was conducted. In this phase, both goal arms were open, and the animal was returned to the start arm and allowed to freely choose between them. Entry into the arm opposite to that visited during the sample phase was recorded as a correct alternation, reflecting intact working memory. Each animal underwent 10 trials, and performance was expressed as the percentage of correct alternations. The maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues [27].

Hippocampal sampling and adrenal gland assessment

At the end of the experimental procedures (day 29), animals were weighed and deeply anesthetized using CO₂ inhalation, followed by euthanasia via decapitation. Brains were rapidly removed, and the hippocampus was carefully dissected on an ice-cold surface. For biochemical and molecular analyses, hippocampal tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. For morphological analysis using the Golgi-Cox impregnation method, whole brains were collected and processed according to the protocol described below. All procedures were performed rapidly to preserve tissue integrity and minimize post-mortem degradation.

In addition, adrenal gland weight was assessed as a physiological indicator of HPA axis activity and chronic stress exposure. Both adrenal glands were carefully removed, cleared of surrounding fat and connective tissue, and weighed. To account for body size differences, adrenal weights were normalized to body weight and expressed as milligrams of adrenal tissue per gram of body weight.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement

Hippocampal MDA levels were measured using a colorimetric TBARS assay as previously described [28]. We used a commercially available colorimetric assay kit (Teb Pazhouhan Razi, Tehran, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, approximately 100 mg of hippocampal tissue was homogenized in 0.9 mL of ice-cold 150 mM potassium chloride (KCl) solution supplemented with butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) to minimize ex vivo lipid peroxidation during sample preparation. The homogenates were then sonicated on ice to ensure complete tissue disruption. Following sonication, samples were centrifuged at 1600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. For the assay, 100 µL of the tissue homogenate was mixed with 100 µL of detergent reagent and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Next, 200 µL of freshly prepared chromogenic reagent containing thiobarbituric acid (TBA) was added to each sample. The reaction mixtures were heated in a boiling water bath for 60 min to facilitate the formation of MDA-TBA adducts. After incubation, the samples were cooled on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove any precipitates. The absorbance of the resulting supernatants was measured at 530–540 nm using a microplate reader. MDA concentrations were determined by reference to a standard curve generated with known MDA concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 µM. Final MDA levels were expressed as µM per mg of hippocampal tissue. All samples were assayed in duplicate, and the method demonstrated acceptable reproducibility, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation of 6.7% and 7.2%, respectively.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity measurement

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in hippocampal tissue samples was determined using a commercially available colorimetric assay kit (Nasdox™, Navand Salamat, Iran), based on the established principle of pyrogallol autoxidation inhibition under alkaline conditions. This assay provides an indirect but reliable measure of SOD activity, reflecting the tissue’s capacity to neutralize superoxide radicals and mitigate oxidative damage [29]. Approximately 100 mg of hippocampal tissue was homogenized in 500 µL of ice-cold lysis buffer (diluted 1:10 with double-distilled water) using a mechanical tissue homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for enzymatic analysis. The assay was performed in 96-well microplates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Freshly prepared working reagents were used for all steps. Reagent 1 (R1) was diluted and its pH adjusted to 8.2, while Reagent 2 (R2) was prepared by mixing appropriate volumes of R2a and R2b to achieve a final pH of 7.4.

For each reaction, 50 µL of the tissue supernatant was added to the wells designated for test samples, and an equal volume of deionized water was added to the control wells. Subsequently, 200 µL of R1 and 50 µL of R2 were added to all wells. The plate was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader. SOD activity was calculated based on the degree of inhibition of pyrogallol autoxidation, expressed as units per milligram of protein, in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. All assays were performed in duplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) measurement

The total antioxidant capacity (TAC) of hippocampal tissue samples was assessed using a commercial assay kit (Naxifer™, Navand Salamat, Iran) based on the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) method. This well-established colorimetric assay provides an index of the tissue’s non-enzymatic antioxidant capacity by measuring its ability to reduce ferric (Fe³⁺) to ferrous (Fe²⁺) ions, forming a stable colored complex under acidic conditions [30]. For the assay, 50–100 mg of hippocampal tissue was homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio using a glass homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and immediately processed for TAC measurement. The working solution was prepared by dissolving Reagent 2a powder in Reagent 2b, then mixing the resulting solution with Reagent 3 in a 1:1 ratio. Subsequently, five volumes of Reagent 1 were added to complete the preparation. A standard curve was constructed using serial dilutions of the provided standard solution, yielding Fe²⁺ equivalents ranging from 0 to 1.0 mM. The assay was conducted in 96-well microplates. For each well, 5 µL of tissue supernatant or standard solution was added in duplicate, followed by 250 µL of the freshly prepared working solution. After incubation at room temperature for 5 min, absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a microplate reader. TAC values were calculated from the standard curve using linear regression and expressed as mmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per milligram of protein. All measurements were performed in duplicate, and the mean values were used for statistical analyses.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed to evaluate protein expression levels in hippocampal tissue samples, following standard protocols with minor modifications [31]. Approximately 100 µL of Pro-PREP™ protein extraction buffer (iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea) was added to each dissected hippocampus, followed by mechanical homogenization on ice to maintain protein integrity. Homogenates were incubated at − 20 °C for 20 min and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C (Hettich Universal 320R, Germany). The resulting supernatants were collected, and total protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (50 µg per sample) were mixed with 2× Laemmli sample buffer containing SDS and reducing agents, denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels with a 5% stacking gel using a TV100 vertical electrophoresis system (Scie-Plas Ltd, UK). A pre-stained protein ladder (Fermentas, USA) was included as a molecular weight marker.

Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, USA) at 300 mA for 75 min in standard transfer buffer. Membrane transfer efficiency was confirmed with Ponceau S staining. Membranes were then blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% BSA prepared in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20). For detection, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with primary antibodies against BDNF (dilution 1:5000, Cat. No. ab108319, Abcam, UK) and β-actin (loading control; dilution 1:2500, Cat. No. ab8227, Abcam, UK). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP-conjugated) secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Cat. No. ab6721, Abcam, UK). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and images were captured on radiographic film. Densitometric analysis was performed using GelAnalyzer software. The relative expression of BDNF was normalized to β-actin by calculating the ratio of their integrated optical densities.

Golgi-Cox impregnation and morphological analysis

The CA3 subregion of the hippocampus was specifically selected due to its well-documented susceptibility to stress-induced structural remodeling. In particular, pyramidal neurons located in the proximal CA3 region near the hilus were analyzed, as this area receives dense mossy fiber input from the dentate gyrus and plays a critical role in hippocampal excitatory circuitry [12, 13]. These neurons are especially sensitive to stress-related alterations in synaptic activity and dendritic architecture. Dendritic morphology was assessed by quantifying total dendritic length and branching complexity, which are widely accepted indicators of neuronal structural plasticity. Although apical and basal dendrites subserve distinct functional roles, apical dendrites receive long-range cortical inputs and basal dendrites contribute to local circuit integration, global dendritic measures were used in this study to provide an overall assessment of neuronal integrity and stress-induced remodeling [32].

Following deep anesthesia with CO₂ and subsequent decapitation, brains were rapidly collected and immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h at room temperature for initial fixation. After fixation, brain specimens were transferred into a freshly prepared Golgi–Cox impregnation solution consisting of 5% potassium dichromate, 5% mercuric chloride, and 5% potassium chromate dissolved in distilled water (5:5:4:10 ratio). Tissues were maintained in this solution for 50 days at room temperature in the dark to prevent light-induced degradation. To ensure consistent impregnation quality, the solution was replaced weekly throughout the incubation period. Following impregnation, tissues were processed using standard histological procedures. Brain samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Coronal Sect. (25 μm thick) were obtained using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany), targeting the hippocampal region between − 1.56 mm and − 3.36 mm relative to Bregma. Sections were mounted on gelatin-coated glass slides and allowed to air-dry. A modified Golgi-Cox impregnation protocol was then completed, followed by dehydration, clearing, and cover slipping with a permanent mounting medium (DPX).

Morphometric analysis was performed using a bright-field microscope (Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a [40× or 60×] objective. Only well-impregnated neurons with clearly visible and continuous dendritic arborization, minimal overlap with neighboring cells, and no truncation artifacts were included, in accordance with established selection criteria. For each animal, 3–5 coronal sections containing the CA3 region were analyzed within the defined stereotaxic range. From these sections, approximately 5–10 pyramidal neurons per animal were randomly selected for analysis. Sections were sampled systematically using non-adjacent intervals to avoid repeated sampling of the same neuronal population. Quantification of dendritic morphology, including total dendritic length and branching complexity, was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). All analyses were conducted by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups [33]. Minor modifications to the standard Golgi-Cox protocol, including extended impregnation time and periodic replacement of the impregnation solution, were implemented to enhance consistency and quality of neuronal impregnation and improve visualization of dendritic arborization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All data were tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test to ensure the assumptions of parametric tests were met. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Depending on the nature of each dataset, different statistical approaches were applied. A two-way ANOVA was employed to examine the main effects and potential interactions between the two independent variables. Additionally, for experiments involving repeated measurements over time, such as the extinction trials in the contextual fear conditioning task and spatial learning performance in the Morris Water Maze, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. In all analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed in a blinded manner to minimize experimental bias.

The effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on anxiety-like behaviors were assessed using the elevated plus maze. A two-way ANOVA for time spent in the open arms revealed significant main effects of treatment (F2, 42 = 40.77, p < 0.0001) and SPS exposure (F1, 42 = 91.55, p < 0.0001), along with a significant interaction between the two factors (F2, 42 = 14.35, p < 0.0001). Effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on fear extinction

The effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on fear extinction were evaluated using a contextual fear conditioning paradigm. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA on freezing behavior across extinction trials revealed significant main effects of group (F5, 42 = 6.82, p < 0.0001) and extinction trial (F3, 126 = 143.8, p < 0.0001), as well as a significant group × extinction trial interaction (F15, 126 = 2.34, p = 0.005). All animals displayed high levels of freezing during the first extinction session, confirming successful fear conditioning.

Post hoc Tukey’s analysis showed that SPS exposure significantly impaired fear extinction, as indicated by elevated freezing levels in the SPS-SAL group compared to SH-SAL controls during extinction trials 2 (p < 0.05), 3 (p < 0.01), and 4 (p < 0.01). Notably, 14 days of Ginkgo biloba treatment significantly facilitated fear extinction in SPS-exposed rats, reflected by reduced freezing at both tested doses. The higher dose of 200 mg/kg produced a more robust effect (p < 0.01 vs. SPS-SAL) compared to the 20 mg/kg dose (p < 0.05 vs. SPS-SAL) (Fig. 3A).

Effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on spatial working memory

The effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on spatial working memory were evaluated using a T-maze task. A two-way ANOVA on the percentage of correct alternations revealed significant main effects of treatment (F2, 42 = 11.75, p < 0.0001) and SPS exposure (F1, 42 = 47.76, p < 0.0001).

Notably, 14 days of Ginkgo biloba administration at the higher dose (200 mg/kg) significantly attenuated this deficit (p < 0.001 vs. SPS-SAL), suggesting a protective effect of Ginkgo biloba on SPS-induced working memory impairment (Fig. 4E).

Effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on hippocampal BDNF expression

The effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on hippocampal BDNF expression were assessed using western blot analysis. A two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of treatment (F2, 18 = 18.45, p < 0.0001) and SPS exposure (F1, 18 = 44.75, p < 0.0001), indicating that both factors significantly influenced hippocampal BDNF levels.

Post hoc Tukey’s analysis showed that administration of Ginkgo biloba extract at the higher dose (200 mg/kg) significantly increased BDNF expression in healthy rats compared to SH-SAL controls (p < 0.05), suggesting a potential neurotrophic effect of Ginkgo biloba under non-stressed conditions. Importantly, treatment with the high dose of Ginkgo biloba (200 mg/kg) effectively reversed the SPS-induced reduction in BDNF levels (p < 0.01 vs. SPS-SAL), indicating the protective effect of Ginkgo biloba on hippocampal BDNF expression following traumatic stress (Fig. 6D).

The effects of Ginkgo biloba treatment and SPS exposure on the dendritic architecture of hippocampal CA3 pyramidal neurons were evaluated using Golgi-Cox impregnation. A two-way ANOVA on total dendritic length revealed significant main effects of treatment (F2, 42 = 7.64, p = 0.001), SPS exposure (F1, 42 = 23.68, p < 0.0001), and a significant interaction between the two factors (F2, 42 = 3.25, p = 0.04). These findings suggest that Ginkgo biloba extract confers a dose-dependent neuroprotective effect on hippocampal CA3 neuronal architecture following traumatic stress exposure (Fig. 7A-C).

Discussion

The present study provides novel and comprehensive evidence that Ginkgo biloba extract mitigates a broad spectrum of behavioral, biochemical, molecular, and structural abnormalities induced by SPS, a well-established rodent model of PTSD. Our results demonstrate that Ginkgo biloba treatment, particularly at the higher dose of 200 mg/kg, attenuates anxiety-like behaviors, facilitates fear extinction, and improves hippocampal-dependent cognitive functions, including spatial learning, reference memory, and working memory. At the molecular level, Ginkgo biloba effectively reversed SPS-induced oxidative damage and restored hippocampal BDNF expression. In parallel, the extract preserved neuronal integrity by rescuing dendritic atrophy in CA3 pyramidal neurons, highlighting its neuroprotective impact on hippocampal structural plasticity.

The SPS model has been extensively used to mimic core features of PTSD, including heightened anxiety, impaired fear extinction, cognitive deficits, and dysregulation of neurobiological systems such as the HPA axis and hippocampal plasticity [16, 17]. In addition to SPS, alternative paradigms such as the stress re-exposure stress (SRS) model have been developed to better capture specific symptom domains of PTSD. The SRS model incorporates repeated re-exposure to trauma-associated cues and has been shown to induce persistent hyperarousal and intrusive memory, alongside HPA-axis alterations [34]. Compared with SPS, which relies on a single sequence of stressors followed by an incubation period, SRS models may offer advantages for studying re-experiencing phenomena and cue-induced relapse. However, SPS remains particularly well-suited for investigating fear extinction deficits, long-term neuroendocrine dysregulation, and hippocampal-dependent cognitive impairments, which were central to the aims of the present study [16]. Consistent with previous reports, SPS exposure in our study led to marked increases in anxiety-like behavior, as reflected by reduced open-arm exploration in the EPM, as well as significant impairments in fear extinction in the contextual fear conditioning paradigm [35–37]. These alterations were accompanied by deficits in spatial learning and memory in the Morris water maze and impaired working memory in the T-maze task, confirming the detrimental impact of traumatic stress on hippocampal-dependent cognitive and emotional processes.

Importantly, Ginkgo biloba extract effectively counteracted these behavioral deficits. Its anxiolytic-like and cognitive-enhancing effects are consistent with previous preclinical and clinical studies [20, 38]. Beyond cognitive improvement, Ginkgo biloba extract (EGb 761) has demonstrated therapeutic potential across a range of psychiatric conditions. As summarized by Montes et al. [39], EGb 761 exerts multiple neuroprotective actions, including antioxidant activity, modulation of neurotransmission, neuroendocrine regulation, and upregulation of neurotrophic factors, all of which are highly relevant to stress-related disorders [39]. Similarly, Banin et al. [38] reported that Ginkgo biloba administration attenuated anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in ovariectomized rats, further supporting its role in modulating emotional and metabolic dysfunctions [38]. These findings reinforce the rationale for investigating Ginkgo biloba as a complementary intervention in PTSD.

The protective and memory-enhancing impact of Ginkgo biloba has been recognized for several decades. Notably, as early as 1991, Winter demonstrated that daily administration of Ginkgo biloba extract significantly improved learning and memory performance in mice using an operant conditioning paradigm. The study showed that EGb 761 facilitated the acquisition, performance, and long-term retention of a two-lever response sequence, indicating its ability to enhance both memory formation and retrieval processes [40]. In the present study, the beneficial cognitive effects of Ginkgo biloba observed in the MWM and T-maze tasks align with previous studies reporting its memory-enhancing and neuroprotective properties [22]. Long-term administration of EGb 761 has been shown to enhance spatial memory and exploratory behavior in aged rats. Using the MWM and hole-board tests, Blecharz-Klin et al. [41] demonstrated that EGb 761 improved memory retention, which was accompanied by increased serotonin levels in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. These findings suggest that the cognitive-enhancing effects of Ginkgo biloba may, in part, be mediated by modulation of monoaminergic neurotransmission, particularly serotonin pathways, which are critically involved in memory consolidation [41].

The neuroprotective properties of Ginkgo biloba have been attributed to its rich flavonoid and terpenoid composition, which exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neurotrophic effects [42]. These bioactive compounds likely contribute to the observed improvements in both emotional and cognitive domains following the administration of Ginkgo biloba extract. Interestingly, although EGb 761 is widely recognized for its neuroprotective properties, some studies suggest that it may also enhance fear memory acquisition under certain conditions [43]. However, accumulating evidence, including the present findings, indicates that Ginkgo biloba facilitates fear extinction and restores emotional regulation [44]. Given that impaired extinction learning is a central mechanism underlying PTSD persistence [35–37], these findings suggest that Ginkgo biloba may have therapeutic value as an adjunct to exposure-based interventions.

Oxidative stress represents a key mechanism underlying stress-induced neuronal dysfunction. Excessive ROS production leads to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, ultimately impairing synaptic function and neuronal viability [3, 4]. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage due to its high metabolic demand and dense expression of glucocorticoid receptors [2]. In the present study, SPS exposure significantly increased MDA levels and reduced SOD activity and TAC, indicating disruption of redox homeostasis. Elevated MDA reflects lipid membrane damage, while reduced antioxidant defenses compromise the ability to neutralize ROS. This imbalance contributes to dendritic retraction and impaired synaptic plasticity, particularly in CA3 pyramidal neurons [13]. In this context, Ginkgo biloba’s ability to restore antioxidant capacity and reduce oxidative damage is likely central to preserving hippocampal function and cognitive performance.

Beyond its antioxidant effects, Ginkgo biloba exerts additional neuroprotective actions that are highly relevant to PTSD pathophysiology. Notably, it possesses anti-inflammatory properties, including suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulation of microglial activation [39]. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a critical contributor to stress-related disorders. Impairments in mitochondrial bioenergetics, enzyme activity, and membrane potential can exacerbate oxidative damage and neuronal vulnerability. Importantly, interventions targeting mitochondrial function have been shown to ameliorate PTSD-like symptoms and restore cellular homeostasis [45]. In this context, Ginkgo biloba has been reported to enhance mitochondrial function, improve ATP production, and stabilize mitochondrial membranes [46]. Additionally, Ginkgo biloba modulates synaptic plasticity through its effects on neurotrophic signaling pathways, particularly the BDNF–TrkB axis, thereby promoting dendritic remodeling and synaptic connectivity. These combined mechanisms, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, mitochondrial protective, and neurotrophic, likely underlie the broad neuroprotective effects observed in the present study.

The role of BDNF signaling in these effects is particularly noteworthy. BDNF is essential for neuronal survival, dendritic growth, and synaptic plasticity. Its effects are primarily mediated through activation of TrkB receptors, which trigger intracellular pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK/ERK, and PLCγ, leading to cytoskeletal stabilization and enhanced synaptic connectivity [47, 48]. In CA3 neurons, BDNF–TrkB signaling is critical for maintaining dendritic complexity and facilitating recovery from stress-induced damage [49, 50]. Our findings demonstrating restoration of BDNF expression alongside improved dendritic architecture strongly support the involvement of this pathway in mediating the neuroprotective effects of Ginkgo biloba.

Chronic stress-induced dendritic remodeling is a hallmark of hippocampal dysfunction in PTSD. In agreement with previous studies [14, 49, 51, 52], SPS exposure reduced dendritic length and branching complexity in CA3 pyramidal neurons. Importantly, Ginkgo biloba treatment reversed these structural deficits in a dose-related manner, with the higher dose producing more pronounced effects on dendritic branching. These findings suggest that Ginkgo biloba preserves synaptic connectivity and structural integrity in stress-compromised hippocampal circuits, which likely contributes to the observed improvements in cognitive and emotional behaviors.

The translational relevance of the present findings is supported by clinical evidence indicating the beneficial effects of Ginkgo biloba extract (EGb 761) in humans. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated improvements in cognitive function in patients with mild cognitive impairment and dementia, as well as reductions in anxiety symptoms [53, 54]. These effects are thought to be mediated through mechanisms similar to those observed in preclinical models, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neurotrophic actions [39]. Although direct clinical evidence in PTSD populations remains limited, these findings support the potential of Ginkgo biloba as a complementary therapeutic strategy for stress-related disorders and highlight the need for targeted clinical trials.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although the SPS model reproduces key behavioral and neurobiological features of PTSD, it cannot fully capture the complexity, heterogeneity, and multifactorial nature of the human condition, including individual variability, comorbidities, and environmental influences. Second, the experiments were conducted exclusively in male rats, which limits the generalizability of the findings, particularly given well-established sex differences in stress responsivity and PTSD prevalence. Third, only two doses of Ginkgo biloba extract were examined, precluding a comprehensive evaluation of the dose-response relationship and the identification of an optimal therapeutic range.

In addition, translating effective doses from rodent models to humans remains a significant challenge. Differences in metabolism, pharmacokinetics, and brain physiology may influence both the efficacy and safety profile of Ginkgo biloba, and therefore, the doses used in the present study may not directly correspond to clinically relevant human doses. Furthermore, although the study focused on hippocampal oxidative stress, BDNF expression, and dendritic morphology, other brain regions critically involved in PTSD, such as the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, were not assessed. While increased BDNF expression was observed, downstream signaling through TrkB and associated intracellular pathways was not directly measured, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Finally, the specific bioactive constituents responsible for the observed effects of Ginkgo biloba were not individually characterized, which may be important for understanding its precise mechanisms of action.

These limitations highlight important directions for future research. Future studies should include both male and female subjects to explore potential sex-specific effects, and should span broader dose ranges and treatment durations to better define the optimal therapeutic window. In addition, beyond oxidative stress and BDNF signaling, further investigations should examine the effects of Ginkgo biloba on neuroinflammatory pathways, mitochondrial function, glutamatergic signaling, and synaptic plasticity across multiple brain regions. Given the growing evidence for microRNAs as molecular regulators and potential biomarkers in neurological disorders [55], evaluating how Ginkgo biloba modulates brain microRNA expression may provide deeper mechanistic insights. Importantly, well-designed clinical trials are required to determine the efficacy, safety, and appropriate dosing of Ginkgo biloba in individuals with PTSD, thereby establishing its translational and therapeutic relevance.
