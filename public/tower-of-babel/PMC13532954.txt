Prognostic Factors for Patients Under 45 Undergoing Surgery for Colorectal Liver Metastases: A SEER Population‐Based Study
Authors: Gabrielle Grob, Fang Fang, Matvey Karpov, Eric Feliberti, Marybeth Hughes, Rachel Burke, Winifred Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesColorectal cancer (CRC) has been on the rise in adults younger than 50. Surgical resection is a potentially curative treatment option for patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). The aim of this study was to describe patient and tumor characteristics associated with disease‐specific survival in young patients (< 45 years) versus older patients (≥ 45 years) with isolated CRLM.MethodsThe SEER database was queried for patients with CRLM who underwent colon and liver resection from 2010 to 2021. Patients under age 20, with metastases beyond the liver, or who had not had surgery for liver metastases were excluded. Cox regression was used to analyze demographic factors, tumor characteristics, and treatment order.ResultsThe study group consisted of 2222 patients, with 299 patients less than 45 years old, and 1923 patients at least 45 years old. Higher nodal burden was associated with worse survival in both groups. Elevated CEA was associated with worse survival only in older patients. Younger patients experienced initial longer disease‐specific survival.ConclusionsTypical prognostic factors for older patients do not have the same impact on disease‐specific survival for younger patients. Further study may help describe patient and tumor selection for surgical resection of colorectal liver metastases in young patients.Keywords: colorectal cancer, liver metastases, surgery, young

Introduction

The overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) has decreased in the United States since the 1990s following implementation of national screening guidelines [1]. For adults under the age of 50, incidence of CRC has increased, and these patients are more likely to present with metastatic disease at diagnosis [2, 3]. In 2018, the American Cancer Society updated guidelines to lower the recommended screening age from 50 to 45 [4]. It is unclear if this new guideline improves outcomes for young patients with CRC [5].

Among patients that present with metastatic CRC, approximately one‐third have isolated liver metastases, and surgical resection is a potentially curative treatment option [6]. A common scoring system for patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) is the Fong Clinical Risk Score, which gives a point each for node‐positive primary tumors, disease‐free interval of less than 12 months prior to discovery of liver metastases, more than one liver tumor, pre‐operative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level greater than 200 ng/mL, and tumor size greater than 5 cm [7]. In this model, each of one these factors have been associated with lower 5‐year overall survival rates.

While the Fong Clinical Risk score has been widely used to guide clinical decision‐making for patients with CRLM, it may not account for unique prognostic factors that apply to the growing number of young patients with CRC. Current National Cancer Center Network guidelines do not reference objective criteria for surgical selection of colorectal liver metastases. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate patient and tumor factors associated with disease‐specific survival in young patients (< 45 years old) as compared to older patients (≥ 45 years).

Materials and Methods

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER 1975–2021) database was queried for patients with CRLM who underwent primary colon resection as well as liver resection from 2010 to 2021. SEER is a national cancer registry that evaluates local, regional, and national cancer burden and serves as a continuous quality control and quality improvement program on cancer data. Patients under age 20, with metastases beyond the liver, or who had not had surgery for liver metastases were excluded. Patients were divided into two groups based on their age at diagnosis, either less than 45 years or 45 years and older. The 45‐year age cutoff was based on the updated screening guidelines recommending colonoscopy beginning at age 45. The cutoff separates patients that would have not yet begun colon cancer screening from patients who are of typical colon cancer screening age.

Data on patient demographics (sex, race, household income, rural vs. suburban residence) and tumor factors (T stage, positive lymph nodes, pre‐operative CEA) were analyzed with Chi‐square test and Mann‐Whitney U test to compare differences by age group. Kaplan‐Meier analysis was used to calculate disease‐specific and overall survival. Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to analyze prognostic factors such as age, sex, rural residency, T stage, regional node positivity, and elevated CEA for overall and disease‐specific survival. P values were reported with significance set at p = 0.05. Stata (StataCorp) statistical software was used for statistical analysis.

Results

The study group consisted of 2222 patients, with 299 patients less than 45 years old and 1923 patients 45 years and older. There was no significant difference in sex, race, income, rural residence, T staging or pre‐op CEA between the two groups (less than 45 years old vs. 45 years and older) (Table 1). The younger age group had a higher proportion of patients with advanced nodal disease (44% N2 vs. 34% N2, p < 0.01) than the older patients. There was a difference in order of therapy between groups, with older patients more likely to have received adjuvant chemotherapy and younger patients more likely to have received perioperative chemotherapy.

Median overall survival for the younger group was 61 months and for the older group was 49 months. 5‐ and 10‐year overall survival for the younger group was 51.4% (CI 43.5%–58.8%) and 26.5% (CI 18.1%–35.6%) respectively. For the older group, 5‐ and 10‐year overall survival was 41.4% (CI 38.6%–44.2%) and 23.7% (CI 20.8%–26.7%) respectively (Table 2). Kaplan‐Meier analysis suggests an initial modest overall survival advantage for young patients, though the survival curves cross at approximately 7 years from diagnosis, suggesting diminished effect by 10 years (Figure 1). Median disease‐specific survival for the younger group was 63 months and for the older group was 52 months. Disease‐specific survival for the younger group at 5 years was 52.1% (CI 44.1%–59.4%) and at 10 years was 26.8% (CI 18.3%–36.0%). Disease‐specific survival for the older group at 5 years was 44.4% (CI 41.4%–47.2%) and at 10 years was 27.7% (CI 24.6%–30.9%) (Table 3). A similar survival advantage was observed for disease‐specific survival, favoring young patients early on (Figure 2). 5‐year disease‐specific survival favors young patients initially, though this effect was lost when assessing 10‐year disease‐specific survival (Tables 2 and 3).

In the older group, multiple factors had significant prognostic value (Tables 4 and 5). Higher household income among older patients was associated with lower risk of disease‐specific death (HR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.95, 0.99). In terms of lymph nodes, nodal disease burden (N1 HR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.10, 1.64; N2 HR = 1.82, 95% CI = 1.49, 2.22) was associated with higher risk of disease‐specific death. Wald test on the equality of hazard ratios revealed that N2 was associated with a significantly higher mortality risk than N1 disease for the older age group (z = −3.77, p < 0.001). Older patients with elevated pre‐operative CEA had higher risk of disease‐specific death (HR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.13,1.51). Neoadjuvant (HR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.45, 0.95) and perioperative chemotherapy (HR = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.23, 0.51) among older patients were both associated with lower risk of disease‐specific death as compared to adjuvant chemotherapy.

For younger patients, unlike in the older group, there was no significant difference in disease‐specific death for N1 as compared to N2 disease (Wald test for equality, z = −0.56, p = 0.579). As shown in Table 5, elevated CEA in younger patients was not significantly associated with disease‐specific death (HR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.66, 1.48). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy in the younger group was associated with increased disease‐specific mortality as compared to adjuvant chemotherapy (HR = 2.32, 95% CI = 1.32, 4.07).

Discussion

With increasing incidence of CRC in young adults, there is a need to better understand unique prognostic factors in this age group. The aim of this study was to evaluate which patient and tumor factors may be more distinguishing for younger patients considered for liver surgery for colorectal metastases.

There are no widely embraced criteria for selecting patients for liver surgery in metastatic colon cancer. One of the historic objective scores is the Fong clinical risk score, which incorporates several tumor‐specific factors (node positive primary, initial CEA > 200 ng/mL), disease‐free interval, and metastatic features (tumor size, tumor number) to estimate the likelihood of disease recurrence after liver surgery. Other notable factors include the Nordlinger score, the GAME score, and CERR score which have not been widely adopted for surgical selection. These include a variety of additional potential factors including patient age, serosal involvement, presence of KRAS mutation, or estimated tumor burden score based on radiographic distribution [8]. Of note, the National Cancer Center Network guidelines, which serve as reference for many surgeons, do not specifically recommend any objective score for incorporation in surgical decision‐making. These selection criteria, while potentially helpful, were created without keeping young patients with metastatic disease in mind and may not be applicable for that patient population.

In this study, we sought to use the SEER database to identify potentially helpful patient and tumor factors for selecting young patients for liver surgery for colorectal liver metastases. There are several limitations with using the SEER database for this query. Foremost, the database does not collect granular data on the number of liver metastases, size of the greatest metastatic lesion (surrogates for tumor burden score), vascular involvement, and only limited information on tumor mutations (i.e., KRAS, BRAF). There is also absent data on procedural information including resection, ablation, and margin status; these would be important tumor and quality metrics which would ostensibly factor into the calculus for offering surgery or deferring to other treatment options. Our selection criteria included only patients who underwent some form of liver surgery and cannot account for patients who were deemed non‐surgical candidates and therefore offered systemic therapy or liver‐directed therapy. Here, we describe the included patient and tumor factors which may be considered for additional assessment.

Both age groups in this study had very similar baseline characteristics which allowed them to be easily compared. One difference was that the younger group had a slightly higher nodal disease burden than the older group (44% N2 vs. 34% N2, p = 0.007). In early‐stage CRC, it has been shown that younger patients more often present with positive lymph nodes than older patients, suggesting young patients present with more regionally advanced disease [8, 9, 10]. Notably, elevated CEA was not prognostic in this study group.

A study by Ahmed et al. found that even in stage IV CRC, nodal status may be a significant prognostic factor [11]. Our Cox model identified a higher risk of mortality in both age groups for N1 and N2 disease vs N0 disease, which aligns with this report. This effect may be related to an increased likelihood of developing future metastases beyond the liver. Knijn et al. evaluated autopsies of patients with metastatic CRC, with and without positive regional lymph nodes, and found that regional lymph node positivity was associated with increased likelihood of developing peritoneal metastases and distant lymph node metastases but had no impact on the development of liver and lung metastases [12]. This suggests a different dissemination pattern based on lymph node positivity that may account for the prognostic value of positive regional lymph nodes noted in our study. In the older group of our study, there was a statistically significant difference between the hazard ratios for N1 and N2 disease, with higher mortality noted among patients with N2 disease. In the younger group, there was no statistically significant difference between N1 and N2 disease. This finding may represent a limitation to the prognostic value of N stage for younger patients with metastatic CRC and may indicate that other tumor factors or biomarkers need to be explored. Alternatively, it may reflect the smaller sample size in the younger group with insufficient patients to determine the difference between N1 and N2 disease.

In terms of survival, the younger age group had higher overall and disease‐specific survival compared to the older age group for the first 6 to 7 years of the study, after which survival curves overlap. There are some broad generalizations that might be inferred given the limited tumor and treatment information that was available through SEER. The difference in overall survival in the first 5 years reflects that younger patients have an initial survival advantage, possibly due to less comorbidities, better tolerance for surgery, or better tolerance of adverse effects of chemotherapy. The initial difference in disease‐specific survival is more difficult to explain, as most studies on metastatic CRC have found no difference in survival between younger and older patients [13, 14, 15, 16], despite the fact that young patients often receive more aggressive multimodal systemic therapy regimens than the usual patient population. After 5 years, the Kaplan Meier curves for overall survival and disease‐specific survival overlap, which may suggest that tumor biology (i.e. disease recurrence and progression) eventually closes the gap in long‐term survival between younger and older patients with CRLM.

An unexpected finding of this study was that elevated CEA at pretreatment was not prognostic for patients < 45 years old (HR 0.98, 95% CI = 0.66, 1.48). This is contrary to the Clinical Risk Score developed by Fong et al. in which elevated CEA is one of five factors negatively predictive of long‐term survival [7]. Our findings suggest that elevated CEA may only be a relevant prognostic indicator for older patients with metastatic CRC. While most studies have found elevated CEA to be negatively prognostic for both younger and older patients with CRC, there is some evidence of a distinct pattern of CEA in young patients, with a smaller proportion of young patients expressing elevated CEA than older patients [17, 18]. A meta‐analysis by Venturino and Colloca on patients with resectable CRLM, found the negative prognostic effect of preoperative CEA > 20 ng/mL to be more pronounced among older patients than younger patients, which does align with the present study and suggests CEA may be less relevant for prognostication in younger patients than older patients [19].

Alternative prognostic factors have been suggested, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). Wong et al. found that in patients with indeterminate routine evaluations including CEA and CT scan, detection of ctDNA was associated with increased likelihood of having metastatic disease [20]. A study by Tie et al. found pretreatment ctDNA level to correlate more strongly with tumor burden than CEA level [21]. Alternatively, Newhook et al. identified that post‐hepatectomy patients with detectable ctDNA had worse recurrence‐free survival compared to patients who had non‐detectable ctDNA [22]. While none of these studies specifically analyzed young patients versus the usual patient population, this may inform whether an alternate biomarker may be more prognostic for selecting patients for surgery. Further research is required to assess the utility of ctDNA, either in addition to, or in place of, CEA for young patients with advanced CRC.

The impact of surgery and chemotherapy order on survival was different for younger and older patients in our study. Historically, perioperative chemotherapy has not been shown to improve long‐term survival [23], and long‐duration treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been associated with hepatotoxicity and poor postoperative outcomes [24]. Specifically, Nordlinger et al. found perioperative chemotherapy to slightly reduce mortality (HR = 0.87 for overall survival), but this was not statistically significant [23]. Multiple systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have attempted to answer the question of whether neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to hepatic resection versus upfront hepatic resection offers superior outcomes in patients with resectable CRLM, yet the results are mixed [25, 26, 27]. In our study, among older patients, neoadjuvant and perioperative chemotherapy were associated with a statistically significant decrease in mortality as compared to adjuvant chemotherapy. This suggests that neoadjuvant chemotherapy may significantly improve survival for older patients, which somewhat conflicts with the findings from Nordlinger et al. In the younger group, however, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was associated with increased mortality compared to adjuvant therapy. It is unclear why this effect would exist only among younger patients while older patients appeared to respond well to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. One potential explanation could be that younger patients with borderline‐resectable disease were more likely to be offered neoadjuvant chemotherapy and hepatectomy than older patients with borderline‐resectable disease. Perhaps the younger patients in our study that received neoadjuvant chemotherapy had a higher burden of disease than the typical patient that would be offered hepatectomy and thus were predestined to face worse outcomes. In the future, it would be important to separate patients into younger and older age groups in another randomized clinical trial to evaluate if a true difference exists in response rates to neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

Limitations of this study include the lack of liver surgery‐specific information, such as margin status, number of liver tumors, whether ablation was used, as well as lack of information on tumor mutation status. Additionally, the exact chemotherapy regimen used for each patient and number of cycles administered was not known. Lastly, the SEER database does not report on recurrence‐free survival, which limits the ability to understand each patient's complete clinical course. This is particularly important in metastatic colon cancer as patients may be subject to additional therapy for recurrent disease as it evolves. To address the lack of surgery‐specific information as well as information on mutation status, this study could be repeated using a multi‐institutional, prospective database.

Conclusions

For young patients with CRLM undergoing metastasectomy, some of the usual factors associated with disease‐specific survival in older patients do not appear to be prognostic. The finding that elevated CEA was not prognostic for young patients is one clue that suggests metastatic CRC behaves differently in young patients, and alternate prognostic factors must be discovered to guide treatment‐related decisions. While this study was able to evaluate the effects of positive regional lymph nodes and elevated preoperative CEA, it would be useful to evaluate the other elements of the Fong Clinical Risk Score, such as largest tumor size, number of tumors, and interval between discovery of primary tumor and liver metastases to determine the utility of this model in young patients. Further study is warranted to better understand patient and tumor selection for surgical resection of colorectal liver metastases in young patients.

Synopsis

For young patients (less than 45 years) with colorectal liver metastases, historic tumor factors may not have prognostic value for survival benefit from surgery.
