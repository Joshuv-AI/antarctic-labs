StructSAM: Structure- and Spectrum-Preserving Token Merging for Segment Anything Models
Duy M. H. Nguyen, Tuan A. Tran ‡ \,{}^{\ddagger}, Duong Nguyen ‡ \,{}^{\ddagger}, Siwei Xie ‡ \,{}^{\ddagger}, Trung Q. Nguyen, Mai T. N. Truong, Daniel Palenicek, An T. Le, Michael Barz, TrungTin Nguyen, Tuan Dam, Ngan Le, Minh Vu, Khoa Doan, Vien Ngo, Pengtao Xie, James Zou, Daniel Sonntag, Jan Peters, Mathias Niepert

Abstract
Recent token merging techniques for Vision Transformers (ViTs) provide substantial speedups by reducing the number of tokens processed by self-attention, often without retraining. However, their direct application to the Segment Anything Model (SAM) family is nontrivial: SAM’s image encoder mixes windowed and global attention, and its mask decoder relies on dense, prompt-conditioned features for precise boundary prediction. We systematically evaluate representative token-merging methods on SAM and Medical SAM in a strict off-the-shelf setting, and find that existing destination-selection heuristics can erode boundaries and leak prompt information as merge rates increase. We propose StructSAM , a resolution-preserving merge–unmerge framework tailored to SAM. StructSAM computes a lightweight token-energy score from first-order feature gradients, uses grid-based flatness screening to protect boundary/prompt regions, and merges tokens within flat areas toward low-energy destinations with explicit token recovery. We further provide a spectral graph coarsening view showing that score-guided merging yields bounded Laplacian spectral distortion compared to random or window-restricted baselines. Across eight natural and medical benchmarks, StructSAM reduces encoder FLOPs by 25–30% (up to 40%+ with prompt-aware merging) with minor drops in mIoU/Dice, consistently outperforming ToMe, PiToMe, ToMeSD, VidToMe, and ALGM at the same compute.

Introduction

SAM
(
17
)
has recently emerged as a foundation model for segmentation, demonstrating remarkable generalization across diverse visual domains. By combining a powerful Vision Transformer (ViT) image encoder
(
9
)
with prompt-conditioned mask decoding, SAM enables flexible, interactive segmentation using points, boxes, and masks, often without any task-specific training. This versatility has led to rapid adoption beyond natural images, including medical imaging, e.g., MedSAM
(
28
)
, robot surgery
(
38
)
, and embodied AI systems
(
19
;
30
)
, where robust segmentation is a critical component. Despite these strengths, SAM’s practical deployment is severely constrained by its computational cost
(
44
)
. In particular, SAM follows a heavy encoder–light decoder design: for large variants such as ViT-L and ViT-H, the image encoder alone accounts for over 98% of the total model parameters and FLOPs, making inference expensive even when the downstream task is lightweight or interactive.

Recognizing this bottleneck, recent efforts to improve SAM’s efficiency have largely focused on model compression strategies, including knowledge distillation to smaller architectures
(
44
;
50
)
, lightweight re-designs of the vision backbone
(
41
)
, and post-training quantization
(
27
;
45
)
. While effective, these approaches typically require retraining or fine-tuning the image encoder on large-scale datasets or applying task-specific calibration during deployment. Such requirements limit their applicability in scenarios where SAM is used off-the-shelf, where training data is unavailable, or where domain-specific fine-tuning is undesirable due to cost and stability concerns.

In practice, SAM’s pre-trained representations are already highly expressive across many domains,
motivating approaches that retain the original model weights while reducing inference cost
. Token merging
(
2
;
35
)
has recently emerged as a promising strategy for accelerating Vision Transformers by dynamically reducing the number of tokens processed by self-attention layers. By grouping similar patch tokens, token merging has shown substantial gains in both classification
(
2
)
and segmentation tasks
(
31
)
for standard ViTs, often without retraining.
However, directly applying existing token merging methods to SAM is nontrivial
. Unlike conventional ViTs, (i) SAM’s image encoder
interleaves windowed and global attention
and preserves fine-grained spatial details, which is crucial for mask prediction. Moreover, (ii) segmentation tasks inherently
require dense, structured outputs
, making aggressive token reduction
(
4
;
16
)
incompatible without careful unmerging or feature upsampling mechanisms.

Motivated by these challenges, we systematically study representative token-merging techniques originally developed for ViTs in images and videos
(
3
;
35
;
20
)
and dense segmentation settings
(
31
;
4
)
, and adapt them to the SAM family, including SAM and Medical SAM. We evaluate commonly used SAM backbones (ViT-B and ViT-L) on boundary-sensitive natural-image benchmarks and on cross-domain medical segmentation datasets. Crucially, all experiments are conducted in a
strict off-the-shelf setting
, without any fine-tuning, to assess the accuracy–efficiency limits of inference-time token merging for foundation segmentation models. Our findings show that prior approaches, which typically rely on random, global, or window-restricted destination selection, struggle to preserve object boundaries and prompt-relevant regions, leading to noticeable degradation as the merge rate increases.

To mitigate this issue, we propose a structure- and spectrum-preserving token-merging framework tailored to SAM-style architectures, called StructSAM. StructSAM (i) identifies boundary-critical tokens using a lightweight energy score computed from first-order finite differences on the encoder feature map
(
51
;
10
)
, inspired by connections to spectral graph energy
(
1
;
13
)
. Tokens near object boundaries exhibit large feature gradients and are therefore protected from merging, while tokens in visually flat regions can be safely merged. Building on this distinction, StructSAM (ii) groups tokens into grid-based cells and ranks cells by flatness to select mergeable regions with spatial coherence, and (iii) merges tokens within selected cells toward low-energy destinations while explicitly unmerging to recover the original token resolution required by SAM’s mask decoder. When box prompts are available, a prompt-aware variant further restricts aggressive merging to tokens outside the prompted region for additional speedups. Finally, we show that our merging strategy admits a spectral graph-theoretic interpretation
(
15
;
35
)
, under which it provably preserves intrinsic spectral properties of the original token space under mild conditions, offering a principled explanation for its stability and effectiveness.

In summary, our work contributes the following points:

•

We present the first systematic evaluation of
inference-time
token merging for the SAM family (SAM and MedSAM) in a strict off-the-shelf setting, revealing why existing merging strategies can fail under boundary- and prompt-sensitive segmentation.

•

We propose StructSAM, a boundary- and prompt-aware,
structure-preserving
merging strategy that leverages gradient-based token energy and cell flatness to protect informative regions while merging redundant background tokens. StructSAM achieves strong accuracy–efficiency trade-offs, reducing FLOPs by 25–30% (and up to 40%+ with prompt-aware merging) while maintaining segmentation quality across natural and medical benchmarks.

•

We provide a spectral graph-theoretic analysis showing that, under mild assumptions, our score-guided merging yields a provable bound on spectral distortion, offering a principled explanation for its robustness in dense segmentation and the limitations of prior random or similarity-only merging approaches.

Related Work

Compression and Pruning Methods for SAM.

To improve SAM’s efficiency, most existing methods focus on
backbone replacement or structured compression
. Approaches such as MobileSAM
(
44
)
, FastSAM
(
47
)
, EdgeSAM
(
50
)
, and EfficientSAM
(
41
)
replace the original ViT-H encoder with lightweight architectures, including TinyViT
(
40
)
, or EfficientViT
(
46
)
, often combined with task-specific training strategies. While effective, these methods typically require training new models from scratch, incurring high data and training costs and potentially weakening SAM’s pre-trained generalization. More recent work compresses the original SAM via structured pruning and distillation
(
6
)
, which better preserves pre-trained knowledge but introduces additional optimization complexity and limited flexibility across domains and hardware settings.

In contrast, StructSAM investigates
SAM off-the-shelf acceleration
via inference-time token merging, without modifying model weights or requiring retraining, offering a lightweight alternative to architectural compression.

Token Pruning and Merging in Transformers.

Existing methods for reducing transformer complexity mainly fall into dynamic token pruning and token merging. Token pruning has been explored in both NLP
(
12
;
48
)
and vision transformers
(
42
;
39
)
, where tokens are dynamically removed based on learned importance. These methods typically require training and introduce input-dependent token counts, complicating batching and limiting practical speedups. Token merging methods, led by ToMe
(
3
)
and follow-up works
(
5
;
33
)
, merge similar tokens using lightweight bipartite matching, achieving better efficiency-accuracy trade-offs. While attention- or guidance-based improvements exist, these approaches remain sensitive to token partitioning and distribution imbalance. More principled clustering or graph-based methods
(
25
;
35
)
offer stronger guarantees but incur substantial overhead, making them less suitable for efficient ViT inference. However, these methods typically
reduce tokens progressively across layers
, which is not well-suited to SAM’s architecture and dense segmentation setting.

Token Reduction for Semantic Segmentation.

A number of methods have been developed to reduce tokens in transformer-based segmentation. Early token halting approaches pause high-confidence tokens to avoid further processing, but this can limit information flow in later layers
(
34
;
24
)
. Token clustering strategies such as ELViT
(
21
)
and AiluRus
(
18
)
merge neighboring tokens within a single layer, yet offer limited efficiency gains due to clustering overhead and one-shot reduction. Content-aware token sharing
(
26
)
uses a policy network to merge image patches, but it incurs additional computational cost and primarily addresses local redundancies. More recent work, ALGM
(
31
)
, performs token merging in two adaptive stages: first locally in early layers and then globally in intermediate layers, based on cosine similarity, achieving substantial throughput improvements and even enhancing mIoU without retraining.

In comparison, our algorithm introduces a
structure-aware token-merging strategy
that uses image-gradient-based energy scores to identify protected boundary tokens and mergeable regions, thereby avoiding the random or purely local window–based merging decisions commonly adopted in existing approaches.

Method

3.1
SAM architecture

Figure 2
:
StructSAM overview.
Feature-gradient energy identifies structurally important regions, forming a
protected set
that is kept at full resolution. Visually flat regions are selectively merged (one representative per mergeable cell) and followed by lightweight token recovery (unmerging), so SAM’s mask decoder still receives a dense feature grid.

SAM employs a transformer-based image encoder for dense image representation learning.
Images are first embedded into a grid of visual tokens via a patch embedding layer and
processed by a hierarchical encoder that produces multi-scale features, which are then
consumed by a lightweight mask decoder.

To balance efficiency and global context modeling, SAM interleaves

local
and
global attention
within the encoder
(Figure
1
). Given an image token set

\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, tokens are partitioned into disjoint spatial
windows
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
such that

\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{P}_{k}=\mathcal{X},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{j}=\emptyset\ \text{for }i\neq j.

At most layers, self-attention is computed independently within each window to capture
local spatial dependencies. For a token
x_{i}
, local attention is defined as

\mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{local}}(x_{i})=\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{P}(i)}\mathrm{softmax}_{j}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{j}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j},

where
\mathbf{q}_{i}=\mathbf{q}(x_{i})
,

\mathbf{k}_{j}=\mathbf{k}(x_{j})
, and

\mathbf{v}_{j}=\mathbf{v}(x_{j})
.

To enable long-range information exchange, SAM periodically applies

global attention
over locally updated tokens

\tilde{x}_{i}=\mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{local}}(x_{i})
:

\mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{global}}(\tilde{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{softmax}_{j}\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{j}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}.

This design captures global context while amortizing the cost of full self-attention across selected layers.

Why SAM needs explicit token recovery.

Many token reduction methods for classification ViTs progressively reduce the token count across layers, since only a pooled representation is used at the output.
In SAM, however, the mask decoder requires a
dense
feature grid at the original token resolution, and the encoder’s window partitioning and relative positional embedding assume a consistent 2D token layout.
Therefore, we perform
merge–compute–unmerge
inside each attention block: we temporarily merge tokens only to reduce attention cost, and then immediately unmerge to restore a full-resolution grid for the next block and for the final decoder.

3.2
Resolution-preserving merge–unmerge interface

To reduce the computational cost of self-attention in the

Segment Anything Model (SAM)
, we introduce a

token merging mechanism
tailored to SAM’s mixed local–global
attention architecture. At an encoder layer
\ell
, let the full-resolution token set be

\mathcal{X}^{(\ell)}=\{x_{1}^{(\ell)},\dots,x_{N_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\}
.
We define a merging operator

f_{\ell}:\mathcal{X}^{(\ell)}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell)},\qquad|\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell)}|<|\mathcal{X}^{(\ell)}|,

which aggregates spatially redundant tokens while preserving boundary- and prompt-relevant information.
Self-attention is computed on the merged tokens and then lifted back to the original resolution by an explicit unmerging operator
f_{\ell}^{-1}
:

\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell+1)}=\mathrm{Attn}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell)}\right),\qquad\mathcal{X}^{(\ell+1)}=f_{\ell}^{-1}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell+1)}\right),

where
\mathrm{Attn}
denotes either local or global attention depending on the layer.
This design enables efficient attention computation while preserving
the
segmentation accuracy of SAM.

Plug-in baselines.

Under this merge–unmerge interface, existing token merging strategies (e.g.,
ToMe
,
Pi-ToMe
,
ToMe-SD
,
VidToMe
,
ALGM
) correspond to different choices of how
f_{\ell}
selects merge candidates and destinations.
We compare these methods using the same interface in the Experiments section, validating across different SAM encoder sizes and datasets.

3.3
StructSAM: gradient-guided structure-aware token merging

We overview our method in Figure
2
.
At each transformer layer
\ell
of the SAM image encoder, we consider a set of image tokens

\mathcal{X}^{(\ell)}=\{x_{1}^{(\ell)},x_{2}^{(\ell)},\dots,x_{N_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\},

which can be reshaped into a 2D feature map

\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times C_{\ell}},\ \mathrm{where}\ |\mathcal{X}^{(\ell)}|=H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}.

Each token
x_{i}^{(\ell)}
is associated with a spatial position

p(i)=(h(i),w(i)),

corresponding to its height and width indices on the token grid.
These spatial indices match those used in SAM’s decomposed relative positional embedding, providing a consistent notion of spatial locality across attention and feature processing.
Our goal is to reduce the number of tokens participating in self-attention at layer

\ell
by merging spatially redundant tokens, while preserving tokens that are
critical for object boundaries and prompt-conditioned segmentation.

Feature gradient–based energy estimation.

We interpret token features at layer
\ell
as a discrete feature field

\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}:(h,w)\mapsto\mathbf{f}^{(\ell)}_{h,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\ell}},

and approximate local feature gradients via finite differences (or Sobel operators
(
11
)
) over adjacent tokens:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}(h,w)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{f}^{(\ell)}_{h,\,w+1}-\mathbf{f}^{(\ell)}_{h,\,w-1},

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}(h,w)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{f}^{(\ell)}_{h+1,\,w}-\mathbf{f}^{(\ell)}_{h-1,\,w}.

The gradient magnitude

\mathbf{G}^{(\ell)}(h,w)=\sqrt{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}(h,w)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{y}\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)}(h,w)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

serves as a lightweight
energy
score: high values correspond to strong local feature variations (typically boundaries) and are therefore protected from merging.
As shown in Figure
7
and Table
5
, this first-order energy provides a comparable indicator to fully connected graph–based energy measures
(
35
)
, while achieving substantially lower computational overhead.

Cell partitioning aligned to attention windows.

To enable controlled and spatially coherent merging, we partition the token grid into non-overlapping spatial cells of size
s\times s
.
For local-attention layers, we apply this partition
within each attention window

\mathcal{P}_{k}
to prevent cross-window collapse; for global-attention layers we treat the entire grid as a single window.
Let

\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}=\{\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(\ell)},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{M}^{(\ell)}\},\quad\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{I}^{(\ell)},\quad\mathcal{C}_{i}^{(\ell)}\cap\mathcal{C}_{j}^{(\ell)}=\emptyset.

Cell flatness and protected set.

We assign each cell a flatness score

\phi(\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)})=-\max_{(h,w)\in\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}}\mathbf{G}^{(\ell)}(h,w).

Cells with higher flatness correspond to visually smooth regions with low structural variation, while cells containing large gradient responses are more likely to include object boundaries.
Given a target
merge rate

r\in[0,1)
(fraction of tokens removed at layer
\ell
), we sort cells by
\phi
and select the first
M_{\mathrm{merge}}
cells as
mergeable
, where
M_{\mathrm{merge}}
is chosen so that the post-merge token count matches
(1-r)H_{\ell}W_{\ell}
.
Since each mergeable
s\times s
cell reduces
s^{2}
tokens to one token, each selected cell removes
s^{2}-1
tokens, and we choose
M_{\mathrm{merge}}
accordingly.
All remaining cells form the
protected set
whose tokens are kept at full resolution.

Destination and source token selection.

Within each mergeable cell
\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}
, we designate a single

destination token
as the most stable representative in that region:

x^{(\ell)}_{m,\mathrm{dst}}=\arg\min_{x_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}}\mathbf{G}^{(\ell)}(h(i),w(i)).

All remaining tokens in the same cell form the
source set

\mathcal{S}_{m}^{(\ell)}=\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}\setminus\{x^{(\ell)}_{m,\mathrm{dst}}\}.

Cell-wise token merging.

For each mergeable cell, we merge its source tokens into the destination token by averaging:

\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}^{(\ell)}_{x^{(\ell)}_{m,\mathrm{dst}}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}|}\sum_{x_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(\ell)}}\mathbf{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}.

The merged token set
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}^{(\ell)}
is formed by (i) all tokens in protected cells, and (ii) the destination tokens for mergeable cells.

Token recovery (unmerging).

After computing self-attention on the merged token set, we restore token representations to their original spatial resolution through a lightweight
token recovery
operation.
For each mergeable cell
m
, the updated destination feature is duplicated back to all tokens in

\mathcal{S}_{m}^{(\ell)}\cup\{x^{(\ell)}_{m,\mathrm{dst}}\}
.
Tokens in protected cells keep their own updated features.
This unmerging step preserves the original token layout required for dense, prompt-conditioned mask prediction, while incurring negligible computational overhead.
We provide end-to-end pseudocode in the appendix.

Prompt-aware variant.

While SAM’s image encoder is prompt-independent, prompts specify
which
structures the decoder will emphasize.
When box prompts are available, we map the box to token coordinates and apply a lower merge rate inside the prompted region and a higher merge rate outside, preserving prompt-relevant detail while enabling additional speedups in background areas.

4
Graph Coarsening View and Spectral Stability in StructSAM

We analyze token merging in
StructSAM
through the lens of
spectral graph theory
.
At a high level, tokens within each attention window define a weighted graph, and StructSAM’s
merge–unmerge procedure induces a
graph coarsening
followed by a canonical
lifting

that restores the original resolution required for dense mask prediction.
Concretely, mergeable cells act as coarse nodes (one destination per cell), while protected regions
remain at full resolution; the score used to select mergeable cells and destinations is the
feature-gradient energy from
Figure

2
(see Sec.
3
).

At encoder layer
\ell
, tokens in each attention window
\mathcal{P}_{k}

(spatial windows for local attention or
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{X}
for global
attention) form a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,k}
with normalized Laplacian

\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k}
.
After merging and lifting, we obtain a lifted graph

\mathcal{G}_{\ell,k,l}
on the original token set.
We quantify structural distortion at layer
\ell
via the
spectral discrepancy

\text{SD}_{\ell}\;\triangleq\;\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\bigl\|\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}-\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}\bigr\|_{1},

(1)

where
\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}
denote the eigenvalues of the
original and lifted Laplacians, respectively (eigenvalues are compared in sorted order).

Under mild assumptions on (i) window-wise separability and (ii) score-guided destination selection
(formally stated in Appendix
D
), we show that boundary-aware, score-guided merging yields stable spectral behavior.

Theorem 1

(Informal: Layerwise spectrum stability of score-guided merging)
.

Fix an encoder layer
\ell
with windows
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}
.
Let
\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{SG})
denote the spectral discrepancy induced by StructSAM’s
score-guided merging, and
\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{Base})
that of a non–score-guided
baseline (e.g., random or stride-based destination selection).
Then, under the assumptions:

1.

If merges occur within coherent regions with high probability and within-region token similarities concentrate, then
\mathbb{E}[\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{SG})]
is tightly controlled and can vanish in the ideal limit;

2.

If a baseline produces cross-region merges with non-negligible probability, then
\liminf\,\mathbb{E}[\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{Base})]>0
, i.e., it exhibits an irreducible spectral drift.

Theorem

1
, proved in Appendix
D
, provides a theoretical explanation for why StructSAM preserves segmentation quality at high merge rates, while heuristic destination selection can degrade dense prediction performance.

6
Conclusion

We present the first systematic study of token merging for SAM-based segmentation, showing that methods designed for classification ViTs often fail to preserve structure in dense, prompt-conditioned settings. Motivated by this gap, we introduce StructSAM, based on the insight that first-order, gradient-based energy cues are sufficient to identify boundary-critical tokens without costly graph interactions. By framing token merging as a local, structure-driven process with explicit recovery, we provide an efficient and principled approach to scaling foundation segmentation models.

Impact Statement

This work enables efficient deployment of foundation segmentation models in low-resource environments by substantially reducing inference cost without retraining or architectural modification. By preserving fine-grained structural details under aggressive token reduction, our method broadens access to high-quality segmentation for applications with limited compute, memory, or energy budgets, including medical imaging and embedded vision systems.

Appendix A
Limitations

Despite these advantages, StructSAM has several limitations.
First, our current formulation relies on fixed, hand-crafted gradient operators (e.g., Sobel or central differences), which may be suboptimal for highly textured regions or domain-specific imagery.
Second, the use of predefined cell partitions introduces an additional design choice that may require tuning for different input resolutions or architectures.
Finally, while our method is evaluated primarily in the context of SAM-based segmentation, its effectiveness for other vision tasks or transformer architectures remains to be fully explored.

These limitations suggest several promising directions for future work.
An interesting extension would be to learn adaptive or task-specific gradient operators that retain the efficiency of first-order information while improving robustness.
In addition, dynamically adjusting cell structures or sampling strategies based on content or model depth could further enhance flexibility.
More broadly, we believe that StructSAM opens up new opportunities for rethinking token merging as a
local, structure-driven
problem rather than a global graph optimization task, and we hope this perspective will inspire more efficient designs for scalable vision transformers as well as other relevant domain applications in 3D perception learning
(
36
)
or robotic manipulation with object-centric conditional inputs
(
7
;
14
;
37
)
.

Bounding Box Generation

Following the standard SAM evaluation protocol, we use bounding box prompts derived from ground truth segmentation masks. For each ground truth mask, we compute the tight
axis-aligned bounding box by extracting the minimum and maximum coordinates of foreground pixels (threshold ¿ 128). The resulting boxes are provided to SAM in the format of top-left
and bottom-right corners as spatial prompts. This deterministic box generation ensures reproducible evaluation while simulating realistic user-provided region annotations. For our
prompt-aware token merging strategy, pixel-space boxes are converted to token-space coordinates by dividing by the patch size (16 pixels), enabling differentiated merging policies
for tokens inside versus outside the prompted region.

This appendix provides a formal counterpart of the informal statement in

Theorem

.
In particular, we make explicit (i) the window-wise graph construction used by SAM’s

Connection to StructSAM scoring.

In StructSAM, the score map
S
used in the unified destination rule below is instantiated by the
feature-gradient energy
S(h,w)=\mathbf{G}^{(\ell)}(h,w)
defined in the Method section
(first-order finite differences or Sobel gradients on the encoder feature map).
Mergeable cells are selected by high flatness

\phi(\mathcal{C}_{m})=-\max_{(h,w)\in\mathcal{C}_{m}}S(h,w)
,
and the destination token in each mergeable cell is the minimum-score token (corresponding to
\sigma=-1
in
Equation

8
).
This choice is designed to (i) avoid merging across strong gradients (boundaries) and (ii) select stable representatives in visually flat regions.

Encoder-level spectral distortion.

Let
L
be the number of encoder layers. We measure the cumulative spectral distortion across
the encoder by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{enc}}\;\triangleq\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\text{SD}_{\ell},

(2)

where
\text{SD}_{\ell}
is the layerwise spectral discrepancy defined in
Equation

1
. Thus,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{enc}}
is simply the sum of per-layer spectral discrepancies.

D.1
Preliminaries: coarsening, lifting, and eigenvalue inclusion

We reuse
Graph Coarsening
and

Definitions

and
2

Definition 1

(Graph Coarsening)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathbf{W})
be a weighted graph with
|\mathcal{V}|=N
and adjacency matrix

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be a partition of
\mathcal{V}
into
n
disjoint subsets, i.e.,

\mathcal{V}=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{V}_{i}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i}\cap\mathcal{V}_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
.
The
coarsened graph
of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
is the weighted graph

\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{c},\mathcal{E}_{c},\mathbf{W}_{c})
, where each subset
\mathcal{V}_{i}
is collapsed into a single node

\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{c}
.
Its adjacency entries are defined by block-averaging:

\mathbf{W}_{c}[i,j]\;=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{i}|\,|\mathcal{V}_{j}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{j}}\mathbf{W}[u,v],\qquad i,j\in[n].

(3)

Let
\mathbf{D}
be the degree matrix of
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathbf{D}[p,p]=d_{p}:=\sum_{q=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}[p,q]
,
and denote the combinatorial and normalized Laplacians by

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{W},\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}.

(4)

Similarly, define
\mathbf{D}_{c}
,
\mathbf{L}_{c}=\mathbf{D}_{c}-\mathbf{W}_{c}
, and

\mathcal{L}_{c}=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{D}_{c}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}_{c}\mathbf{D}_{c}^{-1/2}
for
\mathcal{G}_{c}
.
We denote the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{L}
by
(\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{u})
and those of

\mathcal{L}_{c}
by
(\bm{\lambda}_{c},\mathbf{u}_{c})
.

Definition 2

(Graph Lifting)
.

Given a coarsened graph
\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{c},\mathcal{E}_{c},\mathbf{W}_{c})
induced by a partition

\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
,
the
lifted graph

\mathcal{G}_{l}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{l},\mathbf{W}_{l})
is defined on the original node set
\mathcal{V}
by assigning
each pair of nodes
u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
,
v\in\mathcal{V}_{j}
the adjacency weight of their corresponding coarse nodes:

\mathbf{W}_{l}[u,v]\;=\;\mathbf{W}_{c}[i,j],\qquad\forall\,u\in\mathcal{V}_{i},\ v\in\mathcal{V}_{j},\ i,j\in[n].

(5)

Let
\mathbf{D}_{l}
be the degree matrix of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
with
\mathbf{D}_{l}[p,p]=d_{lp}:=\sum_{q=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}_{l}[p,q]
.
Define the combinatorial and normalized Laplacians of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
as

\mathbf{L}_{l}=\mathbf{D}_{l}-\mathbf{W}_{l},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{l}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}_{l}\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}.

(6)

We denote the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{L}_{l}
by
(\bm{\lambda}_{l},\mathbf{u}_{l})
.

Lemma 1

(Eigenvalue inclusion under lifting)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{c}
be a coarsened graph obtained from
\mathcal{G}
by a partition
\mathcal{P}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{l}
be the
lifted graph from
\mathcal{G}_{c}
via
Definition

2
.
Then the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{l}
contain all eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{c}
, and the remaining

(N-n)
eigenvalues equal
1
.
Equivalently, as multisets,

\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{l})\;=\;\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{c})\ \cup\ \{1\}^{N-n}.

Proof of
Lemma

1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times n}
be the cluster-membership matrix,
\mathbf{P}[u,i]=\mathbf{1}\{u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}\}
.
From
Equation

5
, there exists
\mathbf{W}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

\mathbf{W}_{l}=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{W}_{c}\,\mathbf{P}^{\top}
.
Hence
\mathbf{W}_{l}
has rank at most
n
, and so does

\mathbf{M}_{l}:=\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}_{l}\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{M}_{l}
has at most
n

\mathcal{L}_{l}

(N-n)

.

It remains to identify the other

Hence

Combining with the

\ell

(7)

(Windowed separability and score-guided cell purity)
.

Fix a layer
\ell
and window

(S1) Within-region concentration.

If

\mathbb{E}[\cos(v_{u},v_{v})]\to 1
.

2.

(S2) Margin across regions.

There exists

such that for

\cos(v_{u},v_{v})\geq m>\cos(v_{u},v_{w})
whenever

and
v_{w}\in\mathcal{V}_{0,\ell,k,j}^{(s)}
.

3.

(S3) Score-guided cell purity (unified

Let
\mathcal{C}_{m}
be a mergeable grid cell inside window
(\ell,k)
.
Let

be the cell score map used to select a destination token, and let

\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}
. Pick the destination coordinate by

\displaystyle(u_{m}^{\star},v_{m}^{\star})

\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}_{m}}\ \sigma\,S(u,v),

(8)

\displaystyle x_{m}^{\mathrm{dst}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow x(u_{m}^{\star},v_{m}^{\star})\in\mathbf{B}.

Equivalently, in each cell select the token corresponding to the maximum value in
S
if

represents

(

represents

gradient magnitudes
(

\mathbf{B}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta_{\ell,k,s}
, the selected destination belongs to the
dominant true region inside
\mathcal{C}_{m}
, and each true region present in the window contributes

Formal theorem statement

Theorem 2

(Formal version of

)
.

\ell

(\ell,k)

0<d_{\min}\ \leq\ d_{\ell,k}^{(s)}(i)\ \leq\ d_{\max},\qquad 0\leq\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}[i,j]\ \leq\ w_{\max},

(9)

for all valid indices (degrees are bounded away from

Consider two merging policies applied window-wise:
(i)

(ii) a

Then there exist constants

,

, and

, depending only on

(d_{\min},d_{\max},w_{\max})
and window sizes, such that:

1.

(Telescoping spectral bound)

For each layer
\ell

\text{SD}_{\ell}\ \leq\ c_{\mathrm{sp}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\sum_{s=n_{\ell,k}+1}^{N_{\ell,k}}\Delta_{\ell,k,s},

(10)

is a one-step (merge-step) discrepancy term defined in

Equation

below.

2.

(Vanishing distortion for score-guided merging)

Under
1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{SG})\big]\ \leq\ c_{\mathrm{sp}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\sum_{s=n_{\ell,k}+1}^{N_{\ell,k}}\Bigl(c_{\mathrm{row}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|v_{a_{\ell,k,s}}-v_{b_{\ell,k,s}}\|_{2}\big]\;+\;c_{0}(1-m)\,\delta_{\ell,k,s}\Bigr).

(11)

In particular, if
\max_{\ell,k,s}\delta_{\ell,k,s}\to 0
and

\mathbb{E}[\cos(v_{a_{\ell,k,s}},v_{b_{\ell,k,s}})]\to 1

.

3.

(Non-vanishing distortion for non–score-guided baselines)

Suppose there exists
\delta>0
and an infinite subsequence of merge steps such that

\inf_{\ell,k,s}\ \mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}\right)\ \leq\ 1-\delta.

(12)

Then

\liminf\ \mathbb{E}\big[\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{Base})\big]\ \geq\ c_{0}(1-m)\,\delta,

so the baseline exhibits an irreducible spectral drift.

D.5
Proof of
Theorem

2

We proceed in three steps: (i) relate one merge to a local change in adjacency rows,
(ii) convert adjacency perturbations to Laplacian perturbations,
(iii) apply eigenvalue perturbation inequalities and telescope across merge steps.

One-step discrepancy quantity.

At merge step
s
, window
(\ell,k)
merges indices
(a_{\ell,k,s},b_{\ell,k,s})
.
Define

\Delta_{\ell,k,s}\;\triangleq\;\bigl\|\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}[a_{\ell,k,s},:]-\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}[b_{\ell,k,s},:]\bigr\|_{1}\;+\;\bigl\|\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}[:,a_{\ell,k,s}]-\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}[:,b_{\ell,k,s}]\bigr\|_{1}.

(13)

For symmetric affinities, the two terms coincide; we keep both to cover general constructions.

Proposition 1

(Row/column drift under correct vs incorrect merges)
.

Fix
(\ell,k,s)
. Under bounded weights
\mathbf{W}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}\in[0,w_{\max}]
and unit-norm tokens,
there exist constants
c_{\mathrm{row}},c_{0}>0
(depending only on the affinity construction) such that:

1.

On
\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}
,
\Delta_{\ell,k,s}\leq c_{\mathrm{row}}\|v_{a_{\ell,k,s}}-v_{b_{\ell,k,s}}\|_{2}
.
Moreover, since
\|v_{a}-v_{b}\|_{2}^{2}=2(1-\cos(v_{a},v_{b}))
for unit vectors,

\Delta_{\ell,k,s}\;\leq\;c_{\mathrm{row}}\sqrt{2\bigl(1-\cos(v_{a_{\ell,k,s}},v_{b_{\ell,k,s}})\bigr)}.

2.

On
\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}^{c}
, if the window contains at least two regions and
1
(S2)
holds, then
\Delta_{\ell,k,s}\geq c_{0}(1-m)
.

Proof of
Proposition

1
.

We give an explicit route for cosine-type affinities; other affinities follow similarly under Lipschitzness.

Assume tokens are
\ell_{2}
-normalized, and edge weights are constructed from cosine similarity via a
Lipschitz map
\psi
(possibly followed by clipping/normalization):

\mathbf{W}[i,j]=\psi(\cos(v_{i},v_{j}))
, with
|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|\leq L_{\psi}|x-y|
.
Then for any fixed
j
,

|\mathbf{W}[a,j]-\mathbf{W}[b,j]|\leq L_{\psi}\,|\cos(v_{a},v_{j})-\cos(v_{b},v_{j})|=L_{\psi}\,|v_{j}^{\top}(v_{a}-v_{b})|\leq L_{\psi}\,\|v_{a}-v_{b}\|_{2},

since
\|v_{j}\|_{2}=1
. Summing over
j
and using bounded window size yields

\|\mathbf{W}[a,:]-\mathbf{W}[b,:]\|_{1}\leq c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{row}}\|v_{a}-v_{b}\|_{2}
, and similarly for columns.
This proves the first claim with
c_{\mathrm{row}}=2c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{row}}
.

For the second claim, when
\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}^{c}
occurs, the pair comes from different regions

i\neq j
. By
1
(S2), their within-region similarities are at least
m
,
while cross-region similarities are strictly below
m
. Hence there exists a subset of tokens
(with nonzero cardinality whenever at least two regions are present) for which one of the rows has
entries at least
m
and the other has corresponding entries below
m
, producing an
\ell_{1}

row mismatch bounded below by a constant multiple of
1-m
. The same holds for columns, giving

\Delta_{\ell,k,s}\geq c_{0}(1-m)
for some
c_{0}>0
.
∎

Proposition 2

(Adjacency-to-Laplacian perturbation)
.

Fix
(\ell,k,s)
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}
be the normalized Laplacian before applying the merge at step
s
,
and let
\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}
be the lifted normalized Laplacian after the merge.
Under the boundedness condition
Equation

9
, there exists
c_{\mathrm{lap}}>0
such that

\bigl\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}-\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}\bigr\|_{F}\ \leq\ c_{\mathrm{lap}}\ \Delta_{\ell,k,s}.

(14)

Proof of
Proposition

2
.

Write
\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
.
A single merge modifies
\mathbf{W}
only through the merged pair’s rows/columns (coarsening plus lifting),
so
\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{l}
has support restricted to those indices, and its Frobenius norm is controlled by

\Delta_{\ell,k,s}
. Moreover, degrees are uniformly bounded away from
0
and above by

Equation

9
, so
\|\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\|_{2}
and
\|\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}\|_{2}
are uniformly bounded.
Using triangle inequality and submultiplicativity,

\bigl\|\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}-\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}_{l}\mathbf{D}_{l}^{-1/2}\bigr\|_{F}\leq c\,\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{l}\|_{F}+c^{\prime}\,\|\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{D}_{l}\|_{F},

and both
\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{l}\|_{F}
and
\|\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{D}_{l}\|_{F}
are controlled by
\Delta_{\ell,k,s}

because degrees are row sums of
\mathbf{W}
. This yields
Equation

14
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Hoffman–Wielandt inequality)
.

For symmetric matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with eigenvalues

\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{N}
and
\beta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{N}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}\ \leq\ \|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}.

Consequently,

\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\ \leq\ \sqrt{N}\,\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}.

Proof of
Lemma

2
.

The squared inequality is the Hoffman–Wielandt theorem. The
\ell_{1}
bound follows from
Cauchy–Schwarz:
\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{N}\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}
.
∎

Proposition 3

(One-step eigenvalue drift)
.

Fix
(\ell,k,s)
. Under
Equation

9
, there exists
c_{\mathrm{sp}}>0
such that

\bigl\|\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}-\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}\bigr\|_{1}\ \leq\ c_{\mathrm{sp}}\ \Delta_{\ell,k,s}.

(15)

Proof of
Proposition

3
.

Apply
Lemma

2
with
\mathbf{A}=\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}
,
then use
Proposition

2
:

\bigl\|\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}-\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}\bigr\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{N_{\ell,k}}\ \bigl\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}-\mathcal{L}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}\bigr\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{N_{\ell,k}}\,c_{\mathrm{lap}}\,\Delta_{\ell,k,s}.

Absorb
\sqrt{N_{\ell,k}}\,c_{\mathrm{lap}}
into
c_{\mathrm{sp}}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Step 1: Telescoping.

For a fixed window
(\ell,k)
, telescope across merge steps:

\|\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}-\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{s=n_{\ell,k}+1}^{N_{\ell,k}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k}^{(s)}-\bm{\lambda}_{\ell,k,l}^{(s-1)}\|_{1}\leq c_{\mathrm{sp}}\sum_{s=n_{\ell,k}+1}^{N_{\ell,k}}\Delta_{\ell,k,s},

where the last inequality uses
Proposition

3
.
Summing over windows
k=1,\dots,K_{\ell}
yields
Equation

10
.

Step 2: Score-guided vanishing distortion.

By
1
(S3),

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}^{c})\leq\delta_{\ell,k,s}
.
Using
Proposition

1
and the law of total expectation,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\ell,k,s}]\leq c_{\mathrm{row}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|v_{a_{\ell,k,s}}-v_{b_{\ell,k,s}}\|_{2}\big]+c_{0}(1-m)\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}^{c})\leq c_{\mathrm{row}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|v_{a_{\ell,k,s}}-v_{b_{\ell,k,s}}\|_{2}\big]+c_{0}(1-m)\,\delta_{\ell,k,s}.

Combine with
Equation

10
to obtain
Equation

11
.
Finally,
\mathbb{E}[\cos(v_{a},v_{b})]\to 1
implies

\mathbb{E}\|v_{a}-v_{b}\|_{2}\to 0
(unit vectors), hence the right-hand side vanishes when

\max_{\ell,k,s}\delta_{\ell,k,s}\to 0
.

Step 3: Baseline non-vanishing distortion.

If
Equation

12
holds, then along the corresponding steps

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\ell,k,s}^{c})\geq\delta
.
By the lower bound in
Proposition

1
,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\ell,k,s}]\geq c_{0}(1-m)\delta
.
Plugging into
Equation

10
yields

\liminf\,\mathbb{E}[\text{SD}_{\ell}(\mathrm{Base})]\geq c_{0}(1-m)\delta
, proving the claim.
∎

Remark (A concrete sufficient condition for
Equation

12
).
The assumption in
Equation

12
is mild for stride-based or random destination selection.
For example, suppose a window contains at least two true regions with proportions

p
and
1-p
for some
p\in(0,1)
, and the baseline selects destination tokens without using

S
(e.g., fixed stride independent of content).
Then, at many merge steps, the probability that a selected pair crosses regions is bounded below by
a constant multiple of
p(1-p)
, hence
\delta>0
in
Equation

12
.
This is the sense in which non–score-guided strategies exhibit an irreducible rate of erroneous merges,
which translates into a non-vanishing spectral drift through
Proposition

1
.
