Eigenvalue Statistics of Random Quantum Geometry
Mingpu Jiang, Jianhui Zhou

Abstract
The quantum geometric tensor is a fundamental property of quantum states, with broad applications in condensed matter physics, topological phases, and quantum phase transitions. The eigenvalues characterize the scale, anisotropy, and effective rank of random quantum geometry, going beyond scalar quantities such as the trace. Here we study the eigenvalue statistics of the quantum geometric tensor in finite-dimensional parameter-dependent random Hamiltonians. We obtain exact analytical results for the first two nontrivial cases, N = 2 N=2 and N = 3 N=3 , with N = 3 N=3 already showing genuine shape fluctuations. We further propose a finite- N N , arbitrary- D D description of QGT eigenvalue statistics and verify it by numerical simulations. Our results provide exact benchmarks and a practical framework for random quantum geometry in finite-dimensional disordered and chaotic systems.

II
model and spectral reduction

Consider the parameter-dependent random Hamiltonian family

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}y_{i}H_{i}

(1)

\displaystyle p(H_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{Exp}(-\frac{N}{2}\mathrm{tr}H_{i}^{2}).

(2)

where
\{H_{0},H_{i}\}
are independent and identically distributed

N\times N
Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE) matrices. When

this ensemble reproduces to the low-dimensional random-Hamiltonian
models studied previously in
(

;

;

;

;

)

At the origin of parameter space, the QGT associated with a non-degenerate
eigenstate
|n\rangle

H_{0}
is

G_{\alpha\beta}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=1,\not=n}^{N}\frac{\langle n|H_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|H_{\beta}|n\rangle}{(E_{n}-E_{m})^{2}}

(3)

with
\alpha,\beta\in\{y_{i},i=1,...,D\}
. This representation separates
the random transition matrix elements in the numerator from the level-spacing
factors in the denominator, and it forms the starting point for the
analysis below.

One can show that the distribution of
G^{(n)}
is invariant under
unitary rotations in parameter space. Indeed, we define a set of
D
-dimensional
complex vectors
\{X_{m}\},(X_{m})_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{(H_{\alpha})_{nm}}{E_{m}-E_{n}}.

Using Eq. (2), the QGT can be written as

G^{(n)}=\sum_{m=1,\not=n}^{N}X_{m}(X_{m})^{\dagger}.

(4)

When
H_{0}
is fix,
\{X_{m}\}
are independent centered complex
Gaussian vectors with conditional covariance

\mathbb{E}[(X_{m})_{\alpha}(X_{m^{\prime}})_{\beta}^{*}|H_{0}]=\frac{\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{N(E_{m}-E_{n})^{2}}.

(5)

The conditional covariance is isotropic in parameter space, thus the
joint Gaussian density is invariant under
U(D)
rotations.
(UX_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{m}
,
where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. Hence,
conditioned on
H_{0}
, the distribution of QGT is
U(D)
-invariance:

UG^{(n)}U^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G^{(n)},\mathrm{conditioned}\ \mathrm{on}\ H_{0}.

(6)

Because the numerators are generated by perturbation matrices independent
of
H_{0}
, whereas the denominators depend only on the level spacings
of
H_{0}
, averaging over
H_{0}
randomizes only the radial weights

(E_{m}-E_{n})^{-2}
and does not break the isotropy in parameter
space. Hence the conditional
U(D)
-invariance immediately extends
to the full distribution.

UG^{(n)}U^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G^{(n)}.

(7)

It states that, at the ensemble level, the orientation of the QGT
in parameter space has no preferred direction. The nontrivial, rotation-invariant
information is therefore contained in the nonzero eigenvalues of
G^{(n)}
.
In this respect, the statistics of random QGTs reduce naturally to
a spectral problem.

The spectral reduction also motivates a scale-shape parametrization
of the nonzero QGT eigenvalues. Let
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{r}

be the nonzero eigenvalues of the QGT, with
r\equiv\mathrm{rank}(G)=\mathrm{min}(N-1,D)
.
We define the scale variable and the normalized shape variables by

S\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i},\ \xi_{i}\equiv\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}}
.
Here
S
measures the overall strength of the QGT, while
\xi_{i}

describe how this strength is distributed among the principal directions
in parameter space. Including the Jacobian of the transformation from

\lambda_{i}
to
(S,\xi_{i})
, the joint eigenvalue density can
be written as

p_{G}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{r})=\frac{1}{s^{r-1}}p_{S}(s)p_{\xi}(\xi|s).

(8)

In the following sections, this decomposition will be used as the
common language for all finite-
N
results. The two-level case
N=2

provides the basic benchmark: the QGT has rank one, so the shape sector
is frozen and only the scale distribution remains nontrivial. The
first genuinely nontrivial shape distribution appears at
N=3
. A more systematic derivation is given in Appendix A.

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(E_{1}-E_{2})^{2}}(\omega^{\dagger}\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(E_{1}-E_{2})^{2}}(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D}|\langle 1|H_{\alpha}|2\rangle|^{2}).

(10)

The numerator is the total transition strength generated by all parameter

The shape sector

where

To obtain

Then

\,K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Gamma(D,2),\,U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Gamma(\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2})

Equation (12) gives the complete scale statistics of the rank-one

The agreement is essentially

The density of eigenvalues of QGT conditioned on

For
N=3
, the QGT of the middle eigenstate receives contributions
from two neighboring levels. The distribution therefore contains a
nontrivial shape sector, which describes the relative weight carried
by another two principal directions of the QGT.

We focus on the middle state
|2\rangle
and define
\omega_{\alpha}^{(i)}\equiv\langle 2|H_{\alpha}|i\rangle,\ \omega^{(i)}\equiv(\omega_{1}^{(i)},..,\omega_{D}^{(i)})^{T},\ \Delta_{i}\equiv|E_{2}-E_{i}|,\ i=1,3

The QGT is the sum of two correlative channels

G_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}\equiv\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{(1)}\omega_{\beta}^{(1)\dagger}}{(\Delta_{1})^{2}}+\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{(3)}\omega_{\beta}^{(3)\dagger}}{(\Delta_{3})^{2}}.

(13)

To separate amplitude and orientation, we introduce

X^{(1)}\equiv\frac{|\omega^{(1)}|^{2}}{\Delta_{1}^{2}},\,X^{(3)}\equiv\frac{|\omega^{(3)}|^{2}}{\Delta_{3}^{2}},

u^{(1)}\equiv\frac{\omega^{(1)}}{|\omega^{(1)}|},\,u^{(3)}\equiv\frac{\omega^{(3)}}{|\omega^{(3)}|},\,\phi\equiv|u^{(1)\dagger}u^{(3)}|^{2}.

(14)

Here
X^{(1)},\,X^{(3)}
measure the strength of two fluctuation
channels, while
\phi
measures their relative orientation in parameter
space. In Appendix C, we derive the for the eigenvalues of the QGT
explicitly. The two nonzero eigenvalues of the QGT are

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(X^{(1)}+X^{(3)}\pm\sqrt{(X^{(1)}-X^{(3)})^{2}+4X^{(1)}X^{(3)}\phi}).

(15)

As in previous section, we introduce scale and shape variables,
S\equiv\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-},\,\xi\equiv\frac{\lambda_{+}}{S}
.
The shape fluctuation in the
N=3
problem has a clear physical origin:
it arises from the competition between two virtual transition channels
and their relative orientation in parameter space.

To derive the exact distribution, it is convenient to proceed in two
steps. We first fix the two adjacent level spacings
\Delta_{1}

and
\Delta_{3}
, and derive the conditional distribution
p_{S,\xi}(s,\xi|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})
.
At fixed gaps, the fluctuation strengths and the angular variable
simplify considerably:
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(3)}
become independent
Gamma variables, while the orientation variable
\phi
follows a
simple Beta distribution. The remaining coupling is the mixing between
scale and shape can be handled by introducing the relative channel
weight
z=\frac{X^{(1)}}{X^{(1)}+X^{(3)}}
. At fixed
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3}
,
the density factorizes into a scale part and an angular part in the

\displaystyle p_{S,z}(s,z|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{3})^{2D}\frac{3^{2D}}{\Gamma^{2}(D)}(z(1-z))^{D-1}s^{2D-1}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Exp}(-3(\Delta_{1}^{2}sz+\Delta_{3}^{2}s(1-z)))

\displaystyle p_{\xi}(\xi,z)

\displaystyle=(D-1)[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)

\displaystyle\times[\frac{1}{z(1-z)}]^{D-1}.

(16)

Integrating over

\displaystyle\phantom{}p_{S,\xi}(s,\xi|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{(D-1)(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{3})^{2D}3^{2D-1}s^{2D-2}}{\Gamma(D)^{2}(\Delta_{1}^{2}-\Delta_{3}^{2})}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)[\mathrm{Exp}(-3(\Delta_{1}^{2}s+(\Delta_{3}^{2}-\Delta_{1}^{2})s\xi))

\displaystyle\phantom{}-\mathrm{Exp}(-3(\Delta_{1}^{2}s\xi+\Delta_{3}^{2}s(1-\xi))))].

(17)

The derivation of this expression is given in Appendix C. It already
shows the essential difference from

are no longer trivially separated, because both are influenced by

Averaging over energy spacings

The second stage is to average over the gap statistics of the
3\times 3

GUE matrix itself. Writing the three eigenvalues in terms of their

\displaystyle p_{\Delta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{81}{4\sqrt{3}\pi}\Delta_{1}^{2}\Delta_{3}^{2}(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{3})^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times\mathrm{Exp}(-(\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{3}^{2}+\Delta_{1}\Delta_{3})).

Substituting the conditional density into the gap average and changing
variables to
x\equiv\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{3}

we obtain

\displaystyle\phantom{}p_{S,\xi}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=K_{D}s^{2D-2}[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\int_{-1}^{1}dyx^{4D+5}(1-y^{2})^{2D+2}

Averaging over the transition amplitudes and the correlated adjacent

the final result can be written as a convergent hypergeometric series:

where

\displaystyle c_{l}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{(D-1)3^{2D+2l+4}2^{2l}\Gamma(2D+2l+4)}{\sqrt{3}\pi\Gamma(D)^{2}(2l+1)!}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times s^{2D+2l-1}[3(1+s)]^{-(2D+2l+4)}B(l+\frac{1}{2},2D+3)

IV.3
Simple gap approximations

The exact
N=3
formula not only acts as a benchmark, but also as
a guide for constructing controlled approximations. The main technical
difficulty lies in the joint distribution of the two adjacent gaps

\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{3}
. We therefore introduce simplified
gap sectors to isolate the contributions of two-channel geometry and
adjacent-gap correlations to the QGT statistics. Two approximations
are particularly instructive.

In the equal-spacing approximation (ESA), we set the two adjacent
gaps equal,
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{3}
. Physically, this approximation
suppresses fluctuations of the gap ratio and retains only the symmetric
two-channel geometry. The corresponding gap distribution is taken
as

p_{\Delta}^{ESA}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\Delta_{1}^{2}\mathrm{Exp}(-\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}}{2})\delta_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3}},

(21)

which corresponds to

\displaystyle p_{S,\xi}^{ESA}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{6^{2D}\Gamma(2D)}{B(2D,\frac{3}{2})\Gamma(D)\Gamma(D-1)}s^{2D-1}(1+6s)^{-(2D+\frac{3}{2})}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)^{2}.

(22)

The independent-spacing approximation (ISA) was introduced to compute
the trace distribution
(
7
)
. Here we extend this
idea to the eigenvalue distribution. In contrast to the ESA, one keeps
the two gap variables but neglects their correlations, replacing the
exact joint density by a product form,

p_{\Delta}^{ISA}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})=\frac{2}{\pi}\Delta_{1}^{2}\Delta_{3}^{2}\mathrm{Exp}(-\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{3}^{2}}{2}),

(23)

which gives

\displaystyle p_{S,\xi}^{ISA}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(D-1)3^{2D}\Gamma^{2}(D+\frac{3}{2})}{2\pi\Gamma^{2}(D)}s^{2D-1}(\frac{1+3s}{2})^{-(2D+3)}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times._{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},D+\frac{3}{2};\frac{3}{2},(\frac{3s}{1+3s})^{2}(2\xi-1)^{2}).

(24)

Because it keeps the two-channel competition but discards only the
gap correlations, the ISA approximation is generally closer to the
exact result than ESA, especially for the trace tails and for the
shape distribution at small
D
. However, as
D
increases, the
exact coupling between scale and shape becomes more important, and
the ISA approximation begins to deviate visibly in the bulk.

The distinction between these two approximations is physically instructive.
The ESA removes essentially all information about the fluctuation
of the gap ratio and is therefore best viewed as a minimal analytic
model. The ISA goes one step further by allowing the two virtual transition
channels to fluctuate independently, but it still misses the fact
that the two channels share the same middle level and therefore inherit
correlated spacing statistics from the underlying GUE spectrum. In
this sense, the difference between the ISA and the exact solution
measures the importance of adjacent-gap correlations for QGT eigenvalue
statistics.

2
illustrates the roles of the two-channel
geometry and the adjacent-gap correlations. Panels

3\times 3
GUE
spectrum.

The exact
N=3
solution therefore clarifies which ingredients are
essential for QGT eigenvalue statistics. The two-channel structure
is responsible for the emergence of shape fluctuations, while the
correlated adjacent gaps determine the quantitative correction to
simpler gap approximations. These observations suggest that a general
finite-
N
theory should retain the scale-shape structure of the
spectrum while incorporating the universal constraints visible in
the solvable cases. We now use this perspective to construct an approximate
model for arbitrary finite
N
and
D
.

V.1
Approximation of Scale
p_{S}(s)

V.1.1
Exact asymptotic input for the scale sector

The scale variable
S=\mathrm{tr}\,G
receives contributions from
all
D(N-1)
transition amplitudes. Small
S
requires all these
amplitudes to be simultaneously suppressed, giving
p_{S}(s)\sim s^{D(N-1)-1}
.
Large
S
is dominated by rare near-degeneracies in the spectrum,
giving the universal tail
p_{S}(s)\sim s^{-5/2}
. These two exponents
will be used as the main constraints on the scale ansatz.

The mean scale can also be evaluated analytically. In Appendix D,
we show that

\mathbb{E}[S]=\frac{(N-1)(D+(D-1)r^{2})}{2(r^{2}+1)^{2}}.

(25)

V.1.2
Beta-prime ansatz for
p_{S}(s)

Among simple positive distributions, the beta-prime family provides
the minimal interpolation between independent power laws at the head
and at the tail. It is therefore the natural first candidate for
p_{S}(s)
.
We write the beta-prime density as

p_{beta-prime}(s)=\frac{(\frac{s}{s^{*}})^{\alpha-1}(1+\frac{s}{s^{*}})^{-\alpha-\gamma}}{s^{*}B(\alpha,\gamma)}.

(26)

Matching the two asymptotic exponents and the mean scale, this gives

p_{S}(s)\approx\frac{4(4s)^{D(N-1)-1}(1+4s)^{-D(N-1)-\frac{5}{2}}}{B(D(N-1),\frac{3}{2})}

(27)

V.2
Leading-order shape ansatz from the ESA approximation

The shape sector is modeled by suppressing fluctuations of the energy
denominators. In this limit, the QGT becomes a Gram matrix formed
from random transition vectors. Its eigenvalue statistics are therefore
described by a finite-rank Wishart ensemble
(
28
)
.
Physically, this approximation keeps the random orientation of the
transition channels in parameter space while treating the energy denominators
as an overall scale:

G^{(n)}\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}}X^{(n)}X^{(n)\dagger},X_{\alpha m}\equiv\langle n|H_{\alpha}|m\rangle.

(28)

Here
X
is a
D\times(N-1)
matrix, so
X^{(n)}X^{(n)\dagger}

forms a Wishart matrix. The joint distribution of non-zero eigenvalues is given by

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{r}|\Delta)

\displaystyle\approx C_{r,\rho}(N\Delta^{2})^{D(N-1)}\mathrm{Exp}(-N\Delta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}^{\rho}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}

(29)

where
r=\mathrm{min}(D,N-1)
,
\rho=|D-(N-1)|
,
C_{r,\rho}=1/\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(j+1)\Gamma(\rho+j)
.
This leads to a concise representation of
p_{\xi}(\xi|s)
:

p_{\xi}(\xi|s)\approx\Gamma(D(N-1))C_{r,\rho}\delta_{1,\sum\xi_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\xi_{i}^{\rho}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})^{2}.

(30)

The right-hand side is independent of
S
, so the shape sector decouples
from the scale sector at leading ESA order.

The quality of this leading shape ansatz is tested in Fig.
3
.
We compare the normalized largest eigenvalue
\xi=\lambda_{\max}/\mathrm{tr}\,G
,
obtained from the full random-Hamiltonian simulation with the equal-spacing
Wishart shape model. For
N=3
, the shape distribution is only weakly
modified when the number of parameters is changed from
D=2
to
D=3
.
This indicates that the normalized QGT spectrum is mainly controlled
by the finite-rank structure of the Gram matrix, rather than by the
precise value of the parameter dimension. The Wishart shape model
therefore provides a useful baseline for arbitrary finite
N
and

D
.

The discrepancy becomes more visible for larger
N
. This is expected,
because the ESA suppresses the nonuniformity and correlations of the
energy denominators. As
N
grows, the QGT receives contributions
from many levels with different spacings, and the shape sector becomes
increasingly sensitive to these denominator fluctuations. Thus Figure
(3) should not be interpreted as an exact finite-
N
result, but
as a test of the effective finite-rank Wishart structure that remains
after the scale fluctuations have been separated.

VI
resonant-disordered realization

To connect the finite-dimensional random-Hamiltonian model with a
microscopic disordered system, we consider a low-dimensional effective
Hamiltonian emerging from a resonant subspace. Specifically, we study
a two-dimensional Anderson lattice with on-site disorder and finite-range
complex hopping, extended by external parameters
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{D})

that control additional random hopping layers. The full Hamiltonian is

H(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|+\sum_{ij}(J_{0})_{ij}+y^{\mu}(J_{\mu})_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|,

(31)

where the on-site energies
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
are independent and
identically distributed random variables with disorder strength
W
,
and the hopping amplitudes
\{(J_{0})_{ij},(J_{\mu})_{ij}\}
are
independent random hopping layers. The parameters
y_{\mu}
probe
the local quantum geometry of the effective resonant subspace.

(a)

Figure 4:
Numerical resonant-disordered realization.
(a)
Mean-normalized
level spacing
s=\Delta/\mathbb{E}[\Delta]
of the two-site self-energy

\Sigma(0)=H_{{\rm eff}}(0)-PHP
.
(b)
Window-normalized QGT trace
distribution for
S\leq 3
. Red curves denote the
2\times 2
GUE spacing
distribution and the exact
N=2
mixed-GUE trace distribution.

In the strong-disorder regime
W\gg|J_{ij}|
, most eigenstates are
localized
(
3
;
13
)
. Nevertheless,
rare near-degenerate sites or clusters satisfying
|\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}|\lesssim|J_{ij}|

can hybridize and form resonant eigenstates
(
17
;
9
;
2
)
.
The relevant low-energy dynamics near such a resonance is therefore
described not by the full Hilbert space, but by a finite resonant
subspace. This provides a microscopic route to the finite-
N
random
Hamiltonians studied above.

Let
P
be the projector onto a chosen resonant subspace and
Q=I-P
.
Eliminating the non-resonant sector gives the energy-dependent effective
Hamiltonian

H_{{\rm eff}}(E)=PHP+PHQ(E-QHQ)^{-1}QHP.

(32)

The second term describes virtual transitions through the non-resonant
localized states. If we decompose
QHQ=D+V
, where
D
contains
the localized onsite energies and
V
contains the residual couplings
among non-resonant states, then

(E-QHQ)^{-1}=G\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(VG)^{n},\qquad G=(E-D)^{-1}.

(33)

Therefore

H_{{\rm eff}}(E)=PHP+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(PHQ)G(VG)^{n}(QHP).

(34)

This expression shows that the matrix elements inside the resonant
subspace receive contributions from many virtual hopping paths. When
sufficiently many paths contribute, their random amplitudes, phases,
and energy denominators tend to wash out microscopic details. After
an irrelevant scalar energy shift is subtracted, the effective resonant
block can therefore be modeled by a Wigner-type Hermitian random matrix.
In the absence of additional antiunitary or chiral constraints, the
corresponding minimal ensemble is the GUE:

\Sigma\equiv H_{{\rm eff}}(E)-PHP

(35)

The parameter dependence of the microscopic hopping amplitudes induces
a corresponding parameter dependence of the effective resonant Hamiltonian.
Expanding around a reference point
\mathbf{y}_{0}
, we obtain

\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}y_{\mu}\left.\partial_{\mu}\Sigma\right|_{\mathbf{y}=0}+O(|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{0}|^{2}).

(36)

Keeping only the leading local dependence gives

\Sigma(y)=\Sigma_{0}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}y_{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\Sigma\big|_{y=0}.

(37)

which is precisely the linear mixed-GUE structure studied in the preceding
sections. In this sense, the random QGT eigenvalue statistics derived
above can be viewed as a universal local description of quantum geometry
near resonant disordered eigenstates.

In the numerical realization shown in Fig.
4
, we use
D=3
external
parameters and choose the hopping layers as

J_{0ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}J_{\mu ij}=\begin{cases}t\mathrm{exp}(-\frac{|i-j|}{l})(\frac{a_{\mu ij}+ib_{\mu ij}}{2})&|i-j|\leq R_{c}\\
0&|i-j|>R_{c}\end{cases},

(38)

with Hermiticity imposed by
J_{ji}^{(\mu)}=J_{ij}^{(\mu)*}
. The
onsite energies are uniformly distributed in
[-W/2,W/2]
.

For each selected pair, we compute the self-energy
\Sigma(\mathbf{y})=H_{PQ}(\mathbf{y})\left[E-H_{QQ}(\mathbf{y})\right]^{-1}H_{QP}(\mathbf{y}),

and remove its scalar part before extracting the level spacing. Figure

(4a)
shows the spacing distribution of
\Sigma(0)
, with
s=\Delta/\mathbb{E}[\Delta],\qquad\Delta=E_{2}-E_{1}.

The distribution exhibits clear level repulsion and is close to the

2\times 2
GUE Wigner surmise. The remaining small deviation is attributed
to finite-connectivity covariance anisotropy: in a finite-range lattice,
the diagonal self-energy difference and the off-diagonal self-energy
do not have exactly equal variances. Fig.
4
(b) shows the corresponding
QGT trace,
S=\mathrm{tr}\,G=\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}\frac{|\langle 1|\partial_{\mu}\Sigma|2\rangle|^{2}}{(E_{2}-E_{1})^{2}}.

The agreement with the exact
N=2
result shows that the virtual-path
self-energy of a two-site resonant block reproduces the leading mixed-GUE
quantum-geometric statistics.

This realization also clarifies why the cases
N=2
and
N=3
are
especially relevant. In a strongly disordered system, large resonant
clusters are rare, while two-level and three-level resonances are
the most common nontrivial hybridized structures. The exact finite-N
results obtained in this work therefore provide solvable benchmarks
for random quantum geometry and effective descriptions of the QGT
associated with rare resonant states in disordered systems. In Appendix
E, we show that the probability of finding an
N
-site resonant block
decreases rapidly with
N
.

VII
conclusion and discussion

In this work, we developed an arbitrary-
D
theory for the eigenvalue
statistics of the QGT in parameter-dependent random Hamiltonians.
The central point is that the QGT should be treated not only through
scalar observables such as the trace, but through its full nonzero
spectrum, which resolves the scale and shape of the QGT. By exploiting
the
U(D)
invariance of the QGT distribution, we reduced the problem
to the statistics of its eigenvalues, which provide a natural description
of the scale, anisotropy, and effective rank of the QGT. We obtained
exact results for the first two nontrivial cases,
N=2
and
N=3
.
In particular, the
N=3
case gives the first exact finite-
N
description
of nontrivial shape fluctuations.

Motivated by these exact solutions, we further proposed a unified
model for arbitrary finite
N
and
D
, based on a scale-shape decomposition
of the eigenvalue distribution. The model correctly captures the universal
head and tail behavior of the trace and gives a simple approximation
for the shape sector. Numerical simulations show good agreement with
the exact formulas and support the general model. The resonant-disordered
construction discussed above gives a simple physical interpretation
of the finite-dimensional mixed-GUE ensemble. Our results provide
a useful non-asymptotic framework for random quantum geometry and
offer a basis for future studies of disordered, chaotic, and finite-dimensional
quantum systems. Possible extensions include other random-matrix symmetry
classes, non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, and many-body settings where
QGT eigenvalues may diagnose the structure of quantum-state sensitivity
in high-dimensional parameter spaces.

Appendix A
proof of the
U(D)
invariance

In this appendix we give a more explicit proof of the statistical

U(D)
invariance used in the main text. For any
U\in U(D)
and
any Borel set
A\subset\mathrm{Herm}(D)
, the statement is

\mathrm{Pr}\!\left(G^{(n)}\in A\right)=\mathrm{Pr}\!\left(UG^{(n)}U^{\dagger}\in A\right),

(39)

where
\mathrm{Herm}(D)
means the set of
D\times D
Hermitian
matrices.

Let
V
be a unitary matrix that diagonalizes
H_{0}
,

V^{\dagger}H_{0}V=\mathrm{diag}(E_{1},\ldots,E_{N}).

(40)

Since
H_{\alpha}
is independent of
H_{0}
, and since the GUE
ensemble is invariant under unitary conjugation, the rotated matrices

\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}=V^{\dagger}H_{\alpha}V,\qquad\alpha=1,\ldots,D,

(41)

remain mutually independent GUE matrices after conditioning on
H_{0}
.
Their conditional distribution does not depend on the eigenvectors
of
H_{0}
.

For each
m\neq n
, define the transition vector

(X_{m})_{\alpha}=\frac{(\widetilde{H}_{\alpha})_{nm}}{E_{m}-E_{n}}.

(42)

Then the QGT can be written as the Gram matrix

G^{(n)}=\sum_{m\neq n}X_{m}X_{m}^{\dagger}.

(43)

Conditioned on
H_{0}
, the vectors
X_{m}
are independent centered
complex Gaussian vectors with covariance

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X_{m})_{\alpha}(X_{m^{\prime}})_{\beta}^{*}\,|\,H_{0}\right]=\frac{\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{N(E_{m}-E_{n})^{2}}.

(44)

The covariance is proportional to the identity in the parameter indices.
Therefore, for every
U\in U(D)
,

(UX_{m})_{m\neq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(X_{m})_{m\neq n}

(45)

after conditioning on
H_{0}
, where
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equality
in distribution. It follows that

UG^{(n)}U^{\dagger}=\sum_{m\neq n}(UX_{m})(UX_{m})^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G^{(n)}

(46)

under the same conditioning. Averaging over
H_{0}
preserves the
equality in distribution and gives the desired
U(D)

invariance.

Equivalently, the QGT ensemble is invariant under unitary conjugation
in parameter space. This implies that the nontrivial invariant information
is contained in the eigenvalue statistics of
G^{(n)}
.

Appendix B
distribution of the scale variable for
N=2

We now derive the distribution of the only nonzero QGT eigenvalue
for
N=2
. In this case the scale variable is simply

S=\lambda_{\max}=\frac{K}{U},

(47)

where

K=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D}|\langle 1|H_{\alpha}|2\rangle|^{2},\qquad U=(E_{2}-E_{1})^{2}.

(48)

The two random variables
K
and
U
are independent because the
transition matrix elements are independent of the eigenvalues of
H_{0}
.

B.1
The Density of
K,U

We first determine the distribution of
K
. For each parameter direction,
the off-diagonal matrix element
\langle 1|H_{\alpha}|2\rangle
is
a complex Gaussian variable. Hence
|\langle 1|H_{\alpha}|2\rangle|^{2}

is exponentially distributed. Its characteristic function is

\chi(t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{it|\langle 1|H_{\alpha}|2\rangle|^{2}}\right]=\frac{2}{2-it}.

(49)

Since
K
is the sum of
D
independent copies of this
variable, its characteristic function is

\chi_{K}(t)=\left(1-\frac{it}{2}\right)^{-D}.

(50)

Therefore

p_{K}(x)=\frac{2^{D}x^{D-1}e^{-2x}}{(D-1)!}.

(51)

Next, let
\Delta=E_{2}-E_{1}
. For a
2\times 2
GUE matrix, the
two level spacing distribution is
(
7
)

p_{\Delta}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,y^{2}e^{-y^{2}/2}.

(52)

Thus
U=\Delta^{2}
has density

p_{U}(u)=\frac{1}{2^{3/2}\Gamma(3/2)}u^{1/2}e^{-u/2}.

(53)

B.2
The Density of
S

Since
S=K/U
, we use the change of variables
K=su
. The Jacobian
gives

p_{S}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u\,p_{K}(su)p_{U}(u)\,du.

(54)

Evaluating the integral yields

p_{S}(s)=\frac{4^{D}}{B(D,3/2)}s^{D-1}(1+4s)^{-(D+3/2)}.

(55)

Thus the scale variable follows a beta-prime distribution. This completes
the derivation of the
N=2
result used in the main text.

Appendix C
eigenvalue distribution for
N=3

This appendix gives the derivation of the exact
N=3

eigenvalue distribution. We focus on the middle level.

C.1
The Expression of Eigenvalues

The QGT receives two contributions, one from each neighboring level,
and can be written as

G=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{\dagger},

(56)

where

\mathcal{A}=\frac{\omega^{(1)}}{\Delta_{1}},\qquad\mathcal{A}=\frac{\omega^{(3)}}{\Delta_{3}}.

(57)

Let
\Phi=(A,B)
be the
D\times 2
matrix formed by the two transition
vectors. Then

G=\Phi\Phi^{\dagger}.

(58)

The nonzero eigenvalues of
G
coincide with those of the
2\times 2

matrix
\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi
. Therefore

\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\dagger}A&A^{\dagger}B\\
B^{\dagger}A&B^{\dagger}B\end{pmatrix}.

(59)

Solving this two-dimensional eigenvalue problem gives

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left[X^{(1)}+X^{(3)}\pm\sqrt{(X^{(1)}-X^{(3)})^{2}+4X^{(1)}X^{(3)}\phi}\right],

(60)

where

X^{(1)}\equiv\frac{|\omega^{(1)}|^{2}}{\Delta_{1}^{2}},\,X^{(3)}\equiv\frac{|\omega^{(3)}|^{2}}{\Delta_{3}^{2}},

u^{(1)}\equiv\frac{\omega^{(1)}}{|\omega^{(1)}|},\,u^{(3)}\equiv\frac{\omega^{(3)}}{|\omega^{(3)}|},\,\phi\equiv|u^{(1)\dagger}u^{(3)}|^{2}.

(61)

Here
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(3)}
are the two channel weights, while

\phi
measures the relative orientation of the two transition vectors.

C.2
Conditional density
p_{S,\xi,z}(s,\xi,z|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

C.2.1
Density of
S
and
z
at fixed spacings

We first keep the two adjacent spacings
\Delta_{1}
and

\Delta_{3}
fixed. Under this conditioning, the two channel weights

X^{(1)}
and
X^{(3)}
are independent Gamma variables. It is also
convenient to introduce the relative weight
z=\frac{X^{(1)}}{X^{(1)}+X^{(3)}}.

The scale variable is
S=X^{(1)}+X^{(3)}
, and the shape variable
is chosen as
\xi=\lambda_{+}/S
. By the same argument used in
N=2

case,

\displaystyle p_{X^{(1)}}(x|\Delta_{1})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{1}^{2D}\frac{3^{D}x^{D-1}\mathrm{Exp}(-3\Delta_{1}^{2}x)}{\Gamma(D)}

(62)

\displaystyle p_{X^{(3)}}(y|\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{3}^{2D}\frac{3^{D}y^{D-1}\mathrm{Exp}(-3\Delta_{3}^{2}y)}{\Gamma(D)}.

(63)

Therefore

\displaystyle p_{S,z}(s,z|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=sp_{X^{(1)}}(sz|\Delta_{1})p_{X^{(3)}}(s(1-z)|\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{3})^{2D}\frac{3^{2D}}{\Gamma^{2}(D)}(z(1-z))^{D-1}s^{2D-1}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Exp}(-3(\Delta_{1}^{2}sz+\Delta_{3}^{2}s(1-z))).

(64)

Here we have used the conditional independence of

and
X^{(2)}

at fixed
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{3}

C.2.2
Density of the angular variable

The angular variable
\phi
has a simple distribution. By unitary
invariance, one may fix one of the two unit vectors and regard
\phi

as the squared modulus of one component of a random unit vector in

\phi=|u^{(1)\dagger}u^{(3)}|^{2}=|u_{1}^{(1)}|^{2}=\frac{|\omega_{1}^{(1)}|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{D}|\omega_{i}^{(1)}|^{2}}.

(65)

Let
X=|\omega_{1}^{(1)}|^{2}
,

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(3)\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1,3)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(D-1,3).

(66)

And two variables are independent. Hence

\phi=\frac{X}{X+Y}\sim\mathrm{Beta(1,D-1)}.

(67)

Indeed, setting
t=X+Y
gives

\displaystyle p_{t,\phi}(t,\phi)

\displaystyle=tp_{X}(\phi t)p_{Y}(t(1-\phi))

(68)

with

\displaystyle p_{\phi}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int p_{t,\phi}(t,\phi)dt

(69)

C.2.3
Density of

We now transform from

\phi=\frac{z(1-z)-(\xi-\xi^{2})}{z(1-z)},\qquad 1-\phi=\frac{(\xi-\xi^{2})}{z(1-z)},

C.2.4
Conditional joint density of

Combining the previous results gives

\displaystyle\phantom{}p_{S,\xi}(s,\xi|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{(D-1)3^{2D+2l+4}2^{2l}\Gamma(2D+2l+4)}{\sqrt{3}\pi\Gamma(D)^{2}(2l+1)!}

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times s^{2D+2l-1}[3(1+s)]^{-(2D+2l+4)}B(l+\frac{1}{2},2D+3)

\displaystyle\phantom{}\times{}_{2}F_{1}(2D+2l+4,l+\frac{1}{2};l+2D+\frac{7}{2},-\frac{1+3s}{3(1+s)}).

(88)

C.4
Approximation Distributions

We next derive the two approximate distributions used for comparison
in the main text. The equal-spacing approximation is obtained by setting
the two adjacent gaps equal. This removes the fluctuation of the gap
ratio and reduces the problem to a symmetric two-channel geometry.

The independent-spacing approximation keeps the two gaps fluctuating
but neglects their correlation. In this case the exact GUE spacing
density is replaced by a product form. Starting from the conditional
density
p_{S,z}(s,z|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})
, we first integrate
over
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{3}
, using

p_{\Delta}^{ISA}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})=\frac{2}{\pi}\Delta_{1}^{2}\Delta_{3}^{2}\mathrm{Exp}(-\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{3}^{2}}{2}).

Thus

\displaystyle p_{S,\xi}^{ISA}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int p_{S,z}(s,z|\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})p_{\xi}(\xi,z)p_{\Delta}^{ISA}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{3})d\Delta_{1}d\Delta_{3}dz.

(89)

The Gaussian integrals give

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{3})^{2D+2}e^{-3(\Delta_{1}^{2}sz+\Delta_{3}^{2}s(1-z))}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Exp}(-\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{3}^{2}}{2})d\Delta_{1}d\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma^{2}(D+\frac{3}{2})}{4(3sz+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}(3s(1-z)+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}}.

(90)

We are then left with the integral over the relative weight
z
:

\displaystyle p_{S,\xi}^{ISA}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}_{D}s^{2D-1}[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)

\displaystyle\times\int_{1-\xi}^{\xi}\frac{dz}{(3sz+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}(3s(1-z)+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}},

where
K^{\prime}_{D}=\frac{(D-1)3^{2D}\Gamma^{2}(D+\frac{3}{2})}{2\pi\Gamma^{2}(D)}.

To evaluate the remaining integral, define

p=D+\frac{3}{2},u=2\xi-1,z=\frac{1+y}{2},c=\frac{1+3s}{2},q=\frac{3s}{2}.

(91)

Then

\displaystyle J(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{1-\xi}^{\xi}\frac{1}{(3sz+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{1}{(3s(1-z)+\frac{1}{2})^{D+\frac{3}{2}}}dz

\displaystyle=uc^{-2p}._{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},p;\frac{3}{2},\frac{q^{2}u^{2}}{c^{2}}).

Thus

\displaystyle p_{S,\xi}^{ISA}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}_{D}s^{2D-1}[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)^{2}c^{-2p}

\displaystyle\times._{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},p;\frac{3}{2},\frac{q^{2}u^{2}}{c^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{(D-1)3^{2D}\Gamma^{2}(D+\frac{3}{2})}{2\pi\Gamma^{2}(D)}s^{2D-1}(\frac{1+3s}{2})^{-(2D+3)}

\displaystyle\times[\xi(1-\xi)]^{D-2}(2\xi-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},D+\frac{3}{2};\frac{3}{2},(\frac{3s}{1+3s})^{2}(2\xi-1)^{2}).

(92)

Appendix D
finite-
N
scale-shape model

In this appendix we collect the ingredients used to construct the
finite-
N
, arbitrary-
D
model in the main text. The model is based
on two observations. First, the scale variable
S=\mathrm{tr}\,G

is strongly constrained by its small-
S
behavior and large-
S

tail. Second, when the fluctuations of the energy denominators are
suppressed, the shape sector reduces to the eigenvalue statistics
of a finite-rank Wishart matrix.

D.1
Tail and Head Behavior

We first discuss the asymptotic behavior of the scale density. Berry
and Shukla use codimention method to discuss head and tail behavior
of trace when
D=3

(
7
)
.Here we extend this
argument to arbitrary finite
D
.

For large
S
, the dominant events are near-degeneracies of
H_{0}
.
If a pair of levels
E_{n}
and
E_{m}
becomes close, then

S\sim\frac{K^{(n,m)}}{\Delta_{nm}^{2}},

where
K^{(n,m)}\equiv\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D}\langle n|\partial_{\alpha}H|m\rangle\langle m|\partial_{\alpha}H|n\rangle

is the transition strength and
\Delta_{nm}=|E_{n}-E_{m}|
.

The small-spacing behavior of the GUE spectrum gives
p(\Delta)\sim\Delta^{2}

(
18
)
, Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(S>s)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(\frac{K^{(n,m)}}{\Delta_{nm}^{2}}>s)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(\Delta_{nm}<\sqrt{\frac{K^{(n,m)}}{s}})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{Pr}(\Delta_{nm}<\sqrt{\frac{k}{s}})p_{K^{(n,m)}}(k)dk.

(93)

For fixed
k
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(\Delta_{nm}<\sqrt{\frac{k}{s}})

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{s}}}\Delta^{2}d\Delta

\displaystyle=(\frac{k}{s})^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(94)

This leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(S>s)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{s^{\frac{3}{2}}}\int k^{\frac{3}{2}}p_{K^{(n,m)}}(k)dk

(95)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s^{\frac{3}{2}}}\mathbb{E}[(K^{(n,m)})^{\frac{3}{2}}].

(96)

Since
K^{(n,m)}
follows a Gamma distribution, the moment
\mathbb{E}[(K^{(n,m)})^{\frac{3}{2}}]

is finite. Differentiating with respect to
s
gives the large-
S

tail

p_{S}(s)\sim s^{-5/2}.

The same argument applies to individual matrix elements of the QGT,
with only the prefactor modified.

For head behavior, recall that

p_{K^{(n,m)}}(k)\sim k^{D-1}.

(97)

Conditioned on each
\Delta_{nm}^{-2}
,

p_{\Delta_{nm}^{-2}K^{(n,m)}}(k)\sim\Delta_{nm}^{2D}k^{D-1}.

(98)

Convolution method gives

p_{S}(s|\Delta)\sim\prod_{m=1,m\not=n}^{N}\Delta_{nm}^{2D}s^{D(N-1)-1}.

(99)

Averaging over the GUE spacings gives

p_{S}(s)\sim s^{D(N-1)-1}\mathbb{E}(\prod_{m=1,m\not=n}^{N}\Delta_{nm}^{D}).

(100)

\mathbb{E}(\prod_{m=1,m\not=n}^{N}\Delta_{nm}^{D})
is clearly finite
by the regularity of the GUE joint eigenvalue density. Hence
p_{S}(s)\sim s^{D(N-1)-1}
.

For
D=3
, these result is consistent with the result of Berry and
Shukla.

D.2
Mean value of the QGT trace

We also need the mean value of the trace. It is useful to introduce
hyperspherical coordinates in parameter space. This calculation applies
at a general point in parameter space.

Recall the Hamiltonian is

H=H_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}y_{i}H_{i}.

(101)

Introduce hyperspherical coordinates by

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle=r\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{2})

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle=r\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{2})\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{3})

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=r\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{i})\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{k+1})

\displaystyle y_{D-1}

\displaystyle=r\prod_{i=2}^{D-1}\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{i})\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{D})

\displaystyle y_{D}

\displaystyle=r\prod_{i=2}^{D}\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{i}).

(102)

Let

u(r,\phi)=\frac{1}{r}(y_{1},...,y_{D})^{T},\overrightarrow{H}=(H_{1},...,H_{D})^{T}.

(103)

We rewrite
H
as
H=H_{0}+ru^{T}\cdot\overrightarrow{H}.

Define

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}\prod_{k=2}^{k}\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{k})&k>1\\
1&k=0,1\end{cases}

(104)

\displaystyle\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{D+1})

\displaystyle\equiv 1,\phi_{1}\equiv r.

(105)

Then

y_{k}=r\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{i})\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{k+1})=rP_{k}\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{k+1}).

(106)

Now we have to consider
\partial_{\phi_{k}}H,\,k\in[D]
. We will
show that
\{\partial_{\phi_{k}}H,\,k\in[D]\}
are centered independent
GUE matrices. Their variance are
\{\frac{1}{N},\,\frac{r^{2}P_{i-1}^{2}}{N}
,

k\in[D]\}
.

This fact can be proved by several steps.

First,we expand

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi_{1}}H

\displaystyle=u^{T}\cdot\overrightarrow{H}

(107)

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi_{k}}H

\displaystyle=r\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\cdot\overrightarrow{H}.

(108)

Define

\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{1}}\equiv u^{T}.

(109)

The covariance is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\phi_{k}}H\partial_{\phi_{s}}H]

\displaystyle=r^{2-\delta_{k1}-\delta_{si}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial u_{i}^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\frac{\partial u_{i}^{T}}{\partial\phi_{s}}\mathbb{E}[H_{i}H_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2-\delta_{k1}-\delta_{si}}}{N}\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{s}}.

(110)

A direct differentiation gives

\frac{\partial u_{i}^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}=\begin{cases}0&i<k-1\\
-P_{k}&i=k-1\\
P_{i}cot(\phi_{k})cos(\phi_{i+1})&i>k-1\end{cases}.

(111)

From this expression one obtains the orthogonality relation

\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{s}}=P_{k-1}^{2}\delta_{ks}

(112)

Indeed, for
k<s
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{s}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cot}(\phi_{k})[-P_{s-1}^{2}\mathrm{cot}(\phi_{k})\mathrm{cos}(\phi_{s})\mathrm{sin}(\phi_{s})

\displaystyle+\mathrm{cot}(\phi_{s})\sum_{i=s}^{D}P_{i}^{2}\mathrm{cos}^{2}(\phi_{i+1})]

\displaystyle=0,

(113)

where we have used
\sum_{i=s}^{D}P_{s}^{2}cos^{2}(\phi_{i+1}))=P_{s}^{2}
.

When
k=s
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial u^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k-1}^{D}\frac{\partial u_{i}^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}\frac{\partial u_{i}^{T}}{\partial\phi_{k}}

\displaystyle=P_{k-1}^{2}.

(114)

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\phi_{k}}H\partial_{\phi_{s}}H]=\frac{r^{2-2\delta_{k1}}}{N}P_{k-1}^{2}\delta_{ks}.

(115)

Thus the derivative matrices are independent centered GUE matrices
with variances
1/N
for
\partial_{r}H
and
r^{2}P_{k-1}^{2}/N

with
k>1
.

We can now compute the mean QGT components in these coordinates. For

k>1
,

\mathbb{E}[G_{\phi_{k}\phi_{k}}^{(n)}]=\frac{r^{2}P_{k-1}^{2}}{N}\sum_{m\not=n}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{(H_{0}+rH_{1})_{nm}^{2}}]_{H_{0}+rH_{1}}.

(116)

The combined matrix
H_{0}+rH_{1}
is again a GUE matrix, but with
variance enlarged by a factor
1+r^{2}
. Rescaling its eigenvalues
gives

\mathbb{E}[G_{\phi_{k}\phi_{k}}^{(n)}]=\frac{(N-1)r^{2}}{2(r^{2}+1)}P_{k-1}^{2},

(117)

where we used the GUE identity
(
22
;
32
)

\sum_{m\not=n}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{(E_{n}-E_{m})^{2}}]=N(N-1).

(118)

Similarly, for the radial component,

\mathbb{E}[G_{rr}^{(n)}]=\frac{(N-1)}{2(r^{2}+1)^{2}}.

(119)

Finally, the trace must be taken with the inverse metric in hyperspherical
coordinates. Since

ds^{2}=dr^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{D}r^{2}P_{k-1}^{2}d\phi_{k}^{2},

(120)

we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g^{ij}G_{ij}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(N-1)(D+(D-1)r^{2})}{2(r^{2}+1)^{2}}.

(121)

Appendix E
dominance of small resonant blocks in strongly disordered systems

In this appendix we give a simple estimate showing why small effective
matrix dimensions naturally dominate resonant states in a strongly
disordered Anderson-type system. The purpose is not to derive the
full random-matrix ensemble from a microscopic model, but to justify
why the finite-
N
cases studied in the main text are physically
relevant for rare resonant eigenstates in disordered systems.

Consider an Anderson-type Hamiltonian

H(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|+\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}(\mathbf{y})|i\rangle\langle j|,

(122)

where the onsite energies are independent random variables. We write

\epsilon_{i}=Wx_{i},
where
W
measures the disorder strength and

x_{i}
has a bounded density
\rho(x)
, with
\rho_{\infty}\equiv\sup_{x}\rho(x)<\infty.

The hopping amplitudes
J_{ij}
are assumed to be short-ranged and
bounded,
|J_{ij}|\leq J_{0}.
In the strong-disorder regime
W\gg J_{0}
,
most eigenstates are localized. Nevertheless, rare pairs or clusters
of sites can become resonant when their onsite energy mismatch is
comparable to the hopping amplitude.

To quantify this statement, define a resonant graph
\mathcal{G}_{{\rm res}}=(V,E_{{\rm res}}),
where
the vertices are lattice sites and an edge
(i,j)
belongs to
E_{{\rm res}}

if

|\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}|\leq A|J_{ij}|.

(123)

Here
A
is a constant of order one, specifying the resonance criterion.
Let
C(i)
denote the connected resonant cluster containing site

i
. If
|C(i)|\geq k
, then there exists at least one connected
tree
T
with
k
vertices containing
i
, such that
all
k-1
edges of
T
are resonant. Therefore

\{|C(i)|\geq k\}\subset\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{i,k}}R_{T},

(124)

where
\mathcal{T}_{i,k}
is the set of connected trees with
k

vertices containing
i
, and
R_{T}
is the event that every edge
of
T
is resonant.

Let
z
be the coordination number of the lattice. A standard depth-first-search
estimate gives

|\mathcal{T}_{i,k}|\leq z^{2(k-1)}.

(125)

For a fixed neighboring site
i
, the conditional probability that
the edge
(i,j)
is resonant satisfies

\mathrm{Pr}\left(|\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}|\leq A|J_{ij}|\,\middle|\,\epsilon_{i}\right)\leq\frac{2A|J_{ij}|}{W}\rho_{\infty}\leq\frac{2AJ_{0}}{W}\rho_{\infty}.

(126)

Iterating this conditional bound along a tree
T
with
k-1
edges
gives

\mathbb{P}(R_{T})\leq\left(\frac{2AJ_{0}\rho_{\infty}}{W}\right)^{k-1}.

(127)

Using the union bound, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(|C(i)|\geq k)\leq\left(\frac{2Az^{2}J_{0}\rho_{\infty}}{W}\right)^{k-1}.

(128)

Equivalently, when
W>W_{c}\equiv 2Az^{2}J_{0}\rho_{\infty},
the
probability of a
k
-site resonant cluster is exponentially suppressed:

\mathbb{P}(|C(i)|\geq k)\leq\exp[-(k-1)/\xi_{{\rm res}}],

(129)

with
\xi_{{\rm res}}^{-1}=\ln\left(\frac{W}{2Az^{2}J_{0}\rho_{\infty}}\right)>0.

This estimate shows that, in the strong-disorder regime, large resonant
clusters are rare. The dominant nontrivial resonant objects are therefore
small clusters. A single isolated localized state does not produce
nontrivial level hybridization. The first nontrivial case is a two-site
resonance, corresponding to an effective
N=2
Hamiltonian. The next
correction is a three-site resonance, corresponding to an effective

N=3
Hamiltonian. Larger effective matrix dimensions are possible,
but their probability is parametrically suppressed by powers of
J_{0}/W
.
