 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This project will conduct a Techno-Economic Analysis comparing the three-phase, 125 kW, 2.2-
 level topology, SiC-based PV inverter from Participant and a commercially available comparable
 product.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Estimate material costs for each inverter.

 Participant will collaborate with Contractor to estimate material costs for their 2.2 level
 topology. Contractor will estimate material costs at commercialized volumes for two comparable
 inverters: one with power conversion topologies using silicon devices and the second using
 silicon carbide devices. These costs will be used to understand economic comparison between
 inverter technologies in Task 2 and generate System Advisor model files in Task 4.

 Task 1 Results:

 To define an appropriate commercialized production volume for cost estimation, we reviewed
 publicly available manufacturing and shipment data from leading U.S. inverter manufacturers.
 Among the top nine U.S. producers, SolarEdge and Siemens are publicly traded and manufacture
 three-phase photovoltaic (PV) direct current to alternating current (DC−AC) inverters. We
 selected SolarEdge as the reference point because its smaller scale, product family, and
 manufacturing footprint were more analogous to the inverter system analyzed in this project.
 Originally, 125kw was planned as a baseline, but the project team found 300kw to be a more
 relevant technological baseline and decided to use this throughout the modeling effort.

 SolarEdge’s three-phase commercial inverter line includes 10 kW, 17.3 kW, 30 kW, and 40
 kilowatt (kW) products1. Fiscal Year (FY) 2024 disclosures indicated shipments of 3,563
 megawatts (MW) (AC) of inverters and 576 megawatt−hours (MWh) of batteries for PV
 applications2. Additional reporting showed:

 • A U.S. manufacturing plant in Austin reaching ~500 MW quarterly run-rate by mid-
 20243.
 • Q3 2025 shipments of 1,471 MW (AC) of inverters, equivalent to approximately 36.8k
 units of 40 kW or 147.1k units of 10 kW on a unit-equivalent basis.4
 • As of June 2025, the Austin plant had produced roughly 250,000 inverters since opening
 in late 2023 and reached a quarterly manufacturing run rate of 50,000 residential Home
 Hub inverters5,6, corresponding to an annualized rate of ~200,000 units per year.

 Based on these production and shipment volumes, we defined the commercialized volume for
 our cost model as approximately 200,000 units per year, representing a realistic, mature-
 production scale for a competitive inverter manufacturer operating at high throughput.

 2

 We further developed a structured methodology to identify material categories, estimate
 representative components, and assign cost values consistent with commercialized-volume
 procurement. Our approach involved conducting reference design research and selecting
 components. Since a complete bill of materials was not provided, the project team reviewed
 publicly available inverter reference designs to map the system architecture and ensure all
 functional blocks were accounted for. This meant the material costs may have been
 underestimated and the labor and assembly estimates may also be insufficient. This included
 thermal management systems, sensor sets, converter stages, control electronics, and connectivity
 subsystems. For each functional block, we identified the most widely used component types that
 aligned with the client’s power level and topology. We typically selected commonly used
 commercial components appropriate for the system’s ratings.

 Cost data was taken from major electronics distributors (e.g., Mouser, DigiKey) and
 supplemented with manufacturer data (e.g., Infineon, Cincon). For Power Control Boards, cost
 estimates were based on standard board manufacturing calculators from commercial vendors.
 Power and control printed circuit board (PCB) details were assumed using client guidance. Each
 component and its total unit cost was normalized to commercial-volume production of 10,000
 units per month.

 Our cost model produced the list of material costs for the silicon carbide – based inverter design
 below. These values represent commercial-volume pricing and include all functional blocks
 described above. Based on expert interviews, the material costs for silicon (Si) and silicon
 carbide (SiC) were found to be equivalent, as indicated by the provided bill of materials.

 Task 2: Estimate economics at commercialized volume

 Utilizing cost estimates produced in Task 1, Contractor will use a bottom-up cost model to
 generate a minimum sustainable price for each inverter analyzed. This model will include
 material costs from Task 1, manufacturing, labor, and other associated costs. Economic estimate
 will be generated using outputs from Task 1. This will be to generate System Advisor Model
 (SAM) input files for Task 4.

 Task 2 Results:

 The minimum sustainable price (MSP) of the SiC inverter was the lowest cost at which this
 model of inverter needed to be sold to achieve break even. This was based on the inverters being
 manufactured in consistent and large quantities; in this case, the annualized production rate of
 was 200,000 units per year. The MSP was assessed using a bottom-up cost approach. The
 inverter piece was treated as a purchased and pre-assembled part, and it was $4,081/unit, or
 $0.0136/Wac for the materials, as shown in Task 1 above. The key assumptions for the bottom-
 up cost model are listed below.

 In the bottom-up cost model, the annual production volume set the size of the assembly line and
 determined the required labor, equipment, and facility space. Facility availability and plant
 capacity utilization defined how efficiently the line operated and how fixed costs were spread
 across each unit. Location affected labor rates, electricity prices, and assembly building costs.
 Building costs were based on the floor space needed for production, storage, and support areas.
 Financial assumptions were used to convert capital investments into annual costs through
 depreciation.

 To reflect the full cost structure of the business, indirect operating expenses were added as
 percentage markups, including selling, general, and administrative (SG&A) expenses at 19% of
 revenue and research and development (R&D) expenses at 8.3%, representing ongoing product
 development and corporate overhead. Financing assumptions, including a 10.6% cost of equity,
 5.2% corporate interest rate, and 3% inflation, were used to evaluate capital recovery and long-
 term cost escalation. In addition, a 28% corporate tax rate was applied to pre-tax earnings to
 estimate after-tax costs and ensure consistency with reported financial performance. Finally,
 labor rates were applied to the number of workers needed to meet the production target, and
 electricity rates were used to estimate energy costs for operating the assembly equipment and
 facility. Equipment and building space costs were then allocated per unit to calculate the inverter
 manufacturing cost. The modeled factory gate pricing is shown below.

 To enable a direct economic comparison in Task 2, we also researched costs for an equivalent
 inverter design using silicon power-conversion devices. According to the Wood Mackenzie
 market report7, the national average price for silicon-based power electronic inverter was
 $0.089/Wac in 2025, which was lower than the 300kW bidirectional SiC inverter estimated here,
 which was $0.51Wac. The latest bottom-up cost modeling of a three-phase commercial inverter
 showed a cost of $65/kw without tariffs.

 Task 4: LCOE analysis

 Pending cost metrics completed in Task 1, Contractor will generate (SAM) file for each inverter
 topology that can be modified by the participants in the future to understand effects of their
 inverters cost, performance, and lifetime on the calculated levelized cost of energy.

 Task 4 Results:

 The System Advisor Model (SAM) file was compiled using the latest iteration of SAM
 2025.4.16. The file was made available for the Participants; however, the system design had to
 be modified if the parameters of the inverter changed. The 2024 commercial system with a 1-axis
 tracking median ratio was 1.338 and a calculated Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE) of
 $0.0759. The Participant’s system was designed using a First Solar Series 7 panel and single axis
 tracking. The Participant’s invertor loading ratio (ILR) was set to 1.38. The LCOE for the
 Performer’s inverter, using the system described, came to $0.0509/kWh. The increased
 efficiency and specific invertor parameters made the LCOE of the system lower than the
 benchmarked 2024 commercial system, despite the significantly higher inverter cost.
