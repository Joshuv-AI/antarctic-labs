Vaginal Estriol Tablet Therapy for Rectovaginal Fistula after Low Rectal Surgery: Endoscopic Evaluation of Efficacy and Healing Mechanism
Authors: Takashi Tamura, Fumikazu Koyama, Yosuke Iwasa, Tadataka Takagi, Kosuke Fujimoto, Goki Ejiri, Chihiro Yoshikawa, Takashi Inoue, Masayuki Sho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and study aim: Rectovaginal fistula (RVF) remains a challenging complication after low rectal surgery, lacking a standardized, effective, and less-invasive treatment. Current strategies often require multiple invasive procedures following stoma creation. This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes and potential healing mechanisms of vaginal estriol tablet (VET) therapy for RVF following low rectal surgery. Patients and methods: A retrospective analysis was performed on the medical records and serial endoscopic images of five patients with RVF after rectal tumor surgery who received VET treatment (0.5 mg daily) between 2008 and 2024. We assessed the fistula closure rate, recurrence rate, and endoscopic findings, with a focus on mucosal changes. Results: All five patients had a diverting stoma. The fistula closure rate was 100% (5/5). The median time to closure was 125 days (range, 7-168). Endoscopic examination revealed vaginal mucosal neovascularization in three patients, all of whom exhibited vaginal-side primary healing. The recurrence rate was 20% (1/5), and this patient achieved successful closure after stoma reconstruction and VET readministration. No adverse events were observed. Conclusion: VET therapy following stoma creation may be an effective, non-invasive treatment option for small to moderate-sized RVF after low rectal surgery. It potentially promotes vaginal tissue regeneration and neovascularization, suggesting a novel, targeted approach to RVF healing.

Introduction

Rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is one of the most serious complications of low rectal surgery, occurring in 2.2-3.0% of female patients undergoing low anterior resection[1,2]. The common causes of RVF include the development of abscesses from anastomotic failure, vaginal injury due to a dog-ear deformity at the rectal transection line, or impaction of the vaginal wall by surgical staplers[3,4]. These complications result in abnormal communication between the rectum and vagina, causing painful symptoms such as fecal or gas leakage from the vagina, significantly impairing quality of life. No standard treatment for RVF has been established, but in most cases, a diverting stoma is created first[2]. Retrospective studies report spontaneous closure with stoma diversion alone in only 37.5-71.4% of cases[2,5-7]. Failure to heal often necessitates further invasive surgical interventions, including transanal, transvaginal, or transperitoneal approaches[2,4,5,8]. However, even complex reconstructive procedures such as gracilis muscle flap repair show a limited cure rate of 53-73%[9-11], frequently leading to significant morbidity and requiring reoperation with uncertain outcomes. In recent years, attempts have been made to close fistulas endoscopically, but the success rate for RVF with clip systems is only 43.7%. Stent placement has an 80% success rate, but it cannot be applied to the area immediately above the anal canal, which is the most common site of RVF[12,13].

One fundamental challenge is the patient demographic. Many patients with post-surgical RVF are elderly, postmenopausal women with underlying atrophic vaginal tissues. Current treatment strategies focus predominantly on addressing the rectal side of the fistula, often neglecting the critical contribution of the vaginal compartment to the healing process. Estriol exerts specific beneficial effects on vaginal tissue. Specifically, these include promotion of mucosal keratinization, improvement of blood flow, thickening of the muscular layer, and improvement of the self-cleaning mechanism[14]. Vaginal estriol tablets (VET) are widely used for the treatment of postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis and have excellent safety[15]. While these regenerative effects on vaginal tissue are well documented, their application to RVF is limited to isolated case reports[16]. We hypothesized that VET, by specifically targeting and improving the atrophic vaginal tissue environment, could create optimal conditions for fistula closure from the vaginal side in this vulnerable patient population. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the changes in vaginal endoscopic findings and clinical outcomes in patients treated with VET for RVF after low rectal surgery.

Methods

Patients and study design

We conducted a retrospective study of patients with rectovaginal fistula (RVF) following surgery for rectal tumors who were treated with vaginal estriol tablets (VET) between 2008 and 2024. From the hospital's prospective database, 23 cases of RVF treated in the Department of Surgery during this period were identified. Among these, 14 cases related to gynecological diseases and 2 cases involving inflammatory bowel disease were excluded. Of the remaining 7 cases associated with rectal tumor surgery, 2 cases were excluded as they were resolved via fistula resection and gracilis muscle flap repair without VET administration. Consequently, 5 cases treated with VET were included in this study to evaluate the efficacy of the treatment. The rationale for VET administration was based on the presence of atrophic vaginal changes in postmenopausal patients or the failure and concern of surgical treatment, including stoma diversion. Treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating surgeons. The present study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Nara Medical University (No. 3412), and conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained through an opt-out process as approved by the Ethics Committee.

Vaginal Estriol Tablet Treatment

In all five cases, VET (estriol 0.5 mg) was administered by daily vaginal insertion following stoma creation. Patients were instructed on proper insertion technique and advised to continue treatment until endoscopic confirmation of complete fistula closure.

Endoscopic Examination

All patients were diagnosed with RVF based on clinical symptoms and endoscopic findings. Transanal endoscopy was performed using standard video endoscopes (EC-590ZW, EC-L590ZW, EC-760Z-V/M; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan, and CF-Q260AI, CF-HQ290I, PCF-H290TI; Olympus Optical Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Transvaginal endoscopy was performed using flexible gastroscopes (EG-590ZW, EG-L590ZW, EG-590WR; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan, and PCF-H290TI; Olympus Optical Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Serial endoscopic examinations were performed during active treatment at the discretion of the treating physicians. Both transvaginal and transanal approaches were used to document healing progression. Particular attention was paid to changes in mucosal quality, the presence or absence of neovascularization, and the sequence of fistula closure from either side.

Definitions

Healing was defined as complete symptom resolution with endoscopic confirmation of fistula closure on both vaginal and rectal sides. Regarding the side of primary healing, we defined: Vaginal-side primary healing as the closure of the vaginal fistula opening with a depression remaining at the rectal opening; Rectal-side primary healing as the closure of the rectal fistula opening with a depression remaining at the vaginal opening; and Simultaneous healing as the closure of both openings at the same time.

In vaginal endoscopy, the presence of neovascularization was defined as the detection of vascular proliferation beyond a homogeneous distribution of single, hairpin-shaped capillary loops. Narrow-band imaging (NBI) and blue laser imaging (BLI) were employed whenever possible to assess the presence or absence of mucosal neovascularization.

Extracted Data

The following information was extracted from electronic medical records: age, menstrual status, details of rectal tumor, surgical method, curability, time interval from rectal surgery to onset of RVF, details of RVF, whether or not a stoma was created, time interval from onset of RVF to VET administration, duration of VET administration, observation period, details of additional treatment, whether or not the fistula closed, whether or not the stoma closed, whether or not there was recurrence, treatment course at the time of recurrence, endoscopic and radiological images of RVF, and adverse events of VET. To evaluate the effect of VET on RVF associated with rectal tumors, the RVF closure rate and the recurrence rate with VET were calculated.

Results

Patients' Characteristics

All RVF cases resulted from anastomotic failure. Details of rectal tumor surgery are shown in Table 1. The median age at the onset of RVF was 58 years (range, 48-66 years). Four patients were postmenopausal, and one was premenopausal. Three tumors were adenocarcinoma, one GIST, and one neuroendocrine tumor. Three patients underwent low anterior resection, one intersphincteric resection (ISR), and one transanal local excicion. One patient underwent a combined hysterectomy (Case 3) and another underwent a posterior vaginal wall resection (Case 4). None of the patients received radiation therapy or chemotherapy before surgery. All five patients achieved curative resection.

Outcome of VET treatment

Details and course of RVF are shown in Table 2 and Figure 1. The median time from surgery to onset of RVF was 30 days (range, 5-3336 days). The median fistula diameter was 5 mm (range, 3-10 mm). The fistula was located in the lower rectum in four patients and in the middle rectum in one. All patients had diverting stomas at the time of VET initiation. The median time from RVF onset to VET administration was 5 days. VET was administered at a dose of 0.5 mg once daily. RVF closure was achieved in all five patients, thus resulting in a 100% fistula closure rate. The median time from the start of VET treatment to RVF closure was 125 days (range, 7-168). Endoscopic Findings

A total of 34 endoscopic examinations (17 transanal and 17 transvaginal) were performed throughout the treatment course (Table 3). Endoscopic evidence suggestive of vaginal mucosal neovascularization was observed using NBI or BLI in three cases during VET treatment. Notably, all three cases with apparent neovascularization demonstrated vaginal-side primary healing, while the remaining two cases showed simultaneous closure of both the vaginal and rectal sides. The case that best illustrates our findings is presented in Figure 2. This case (Case 1) involved a 58-year-old woman with refractory RVF who had previously undergone three unsuccessful gracilis muscle flap repairs (one on the left, two on the right). Serial endoscopic documentation showed progressive improvement in vaginal mucosal quality over the first month of VET treatment, followed by clear evidence of neovascularization (Figure 2d and 2e). The fistula closed after 4 months of treatment. Complete healing was maintained throughout the 39-month follow-up period after stoma closure. These findings are descriptive endoscopic observations; no histological confirmation or quantitative vascular assessment was performed.

Recurrence and Adverse Effects of VET Treatment

One patient (Case 2) experienced RVF recurrence 7 months after stoma closure but was successfully treated with repeat VET therapy following stoma reconstruction and gracilis muscle flap repair. The recurrence rate was 20%, but fistula closure was possible with re-administration of VET. No adverse events related to VET were observed in any of the five cases, including menopausal symptom exacerbation or thrombotic complications.

Discussion

This retrospective review, which includes serial endoscopic records, provides the first detailed visualization of the healing process of RVF treated with VET, offering unprecedented insights into the mechanism of vaginal fistula closure. The observed vaginal-first healing pattern with VET therapy suggests that the quality and regenerative capacity of the vaginal tissue may be a rate-limiting factor in spontaneous RVF closure. This finding fundamentally challenges the conventional approach focused on rectal-side repair and supports a treatment strategy prioritizing vaginal tissue regeneration. The mechanism of VET's efficacy may be related to its comprehensive effects on postmenopausal vaginal atrophy. Most post-surgical RVF patients are elderly women with thin, poorly vascularized vaginal walls lacking the structural integrity necessary for effective healing. Administration of VET improves mucosal keratinization, promotes local blood flow, and facilitates tissue thickening[14]. These effects create optimal conditions for healing fistulas originating in the vaginal compartment. The neovascularization observed in our patients provides direct endoscopic evidence of VET's regenerative effects on compromised vaginal tissue.

In evaluating the outcomes of this study, it is important to acknowledge that all patients possessed a diverting stoma. It is difficult to isolate the independent therapeutic effect of VET from the potential contributory effects of the stoma. Nevertheless, the 100% closure rate achieved in this study is significantly superior to results reported with traditional surgical or endoscopic interventions. Previous studies have documented spontaneous closure rates for rectovaginal fistulas (RVFs) with stoma creation alone ranging from 37.5% to 71.4%[2,5-7]. In addition, success rates for gracilis myocutaneous flap repair have been reported to range from 53% to 73%[9-11], consistent with our institution's success rate of 75%. Notably, even with advanced endoscopic techniques, optimal results were not achieved. The over-the-scope clip system achieved a limited success rate of 43.7%[12]. Endoscopic stent insertion achieved 80%, but its application in the area immediately above the anal canal, which is the most common site of RVF, is difficult[13].

In contrast, the exceptional effectiveness of VET was achieved through a minimally invasive approach involving daily tablet insertion, without the complications associated with complex reconstructive surgery. Moreover, in a patient who experienced recurrent RVF (Case 2), fistula closure was finally achieved by combining stoma creation, gracilis myocutaneous flap repair, and re-administration of VET. Although the successful fistula closure in this case was not solely attributable to VET, it suggests that VET may be beneficial for fistula closure even in recurrent cases. The fact that VET demonstrated high efficacy in cases where conventional treatments often fail strongly suggests that this approach may represent a groundbreaking treatment option for the management of RVF.

Early initiation of VET therapy appears crucial for optimal outcomes. Our median 5-day interval from RVF onset to treatment initiation may prevent chronic inflammation and facilitate rapid tissue regeneration. This contrasts with traditional surgical approaches that often delay definitive intervention until conservative measures fail, potentially allowing tissue quality to deteriorate further.

The safety profile of VET in our series was excellent, with no adverse events, including menopausal symptom exacerbation or thrombotic complications. This finding aligns with extensive literature documenting the safety of vaginal estrogen therapy[15]. The local administration route minimizes systemic absorption while maximizing tissue-specific effects, making this treatment suitable even for patients with relative contraindications to systemic hormone therapy.

VET treatment is non-invasive and straightforward, as it only requires the insertion of a vaginal tablet once a day. It can also be used in combination with various conventional treatments. It can also be applied to diseases other than rectal tumor surgery. Recently, we reported a case in which an ileal pouch-vaginal fistula caused by pouchitis after ulcerative colitis surgery was successfully treated with antibiotics and VET without stoma construction[17]. However, this treatment is not suitable for patients with estrogen-sensitive tumors or thrombosis.

This study has several important limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the small sample size (n=5) provides limited statistical power and prevents meaningful subgroup analyses or multivariate assessment of factors influencing treatment success. Second, the single-center, retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as treatment decisions were not randomized and may have been influenced by unmeasured patient or provider factors. Third, all patients had concurrent diverting stomas, and no control group was available, making it impossible to separate the independent therapeutic contribution of VET from that of fecal diversion. The relatively short median time to closure (125 days) and endoscopic evidence of vaginal mucosal regeneration suggest a potential beneficial role for VET; however, a controlled comparison would be necessary to confirm this hypothesis. Fourth, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the specific patient population studied. Four of five patients were postmenopausal, and all underwent curative surgery without neoadjuvant or adjuvant radiotherapy. The effectiveness of VET in premenopausal women with higher endogenous estrogen levels remains undefined. Similarly, applicability to patients with radiation-induced RVF or inflammatory bowel disease-associated RVF, whose tissue quality and healing potential may differ, cannot be inferred from the present data. Fifth, the proposed healing mechanism based on endoscopic neovascularization lacks histological or molecular confirmation. Therefore, the proposed mechanism should be considered hypothetical and requires validation through controlled studies with histological and molecular analyses. Finally, the optimal dosing regimen (0.5 mg daily), treatment duration, and initiation timing require investigation through systematic trials, as our treatment protocol was empirically derived rather than evidence-based. Larger prospective controlled studies are warranted to definitively establish the role of VET in the management algorithm for post-surgical RVF.

In conclusion, our preliminary experience suggests that VET therapy, when combined with fecal diversion, may represent a useful conservative treatment option for post-surgical RVF, particularly for low-lying, small to moderate-sized fistulas in postmenopausal women.
