Beyond admission day: operative intent and physiological readiness in rapid chest wall reconstruction
Authors: Ting-Yu Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the multicenter registry analysis by Spering et al., which compared rapid sequence chest wall reconstruction (CWR) on the day of admission with early CWR on days 1–3 in patients with severe chest wall injuries [1]. The authors should be commended for addressing a clinically important question within the early treatment window discussed in contemporary chest wall injury care. Their study showed that patients selected for day-0 CWR had higher mortality than those treated on days 1–3, whereas sepsis, multi-organ failure, ventilation duration, ICU stay and hospital stay were broadly similar after matching. The authors appropriately concluded that day-0 CWR should not be interpreted as harmful per se, but as a marker of a distinct high-risk trauma phenotype. We suggest that this interpretation can be strengthened by moving from a calendar-based timing framework to one based on operative intent and physiological readiness.

First, day-0 CWR should not be considered a single therapeutic strategy. In clinical practice, the same calendar category may include at least three different operative intents: life-saving or damage-control chest wall stabilization, concomitant CWR performed during another mandatory emergency operation, and planned very early CWR intended to restore respiratory mechanics in a patient who is otherwise stabilizable. These scenarios are not clinically equivalent. In a patient with an open chest, major air leak, penetrating chest wall disruption or mandatory thoracotomy, CWR may be part of an immediate life-saving operation. In contrast, in a patient with flail chest, controlled hemorrhage and improving physiology, CWR is better understood as an early definitive procedure aimed at restoring chest wall mechanics, improving analgesia and facilitating ventilation.

This distinction matters because the therapeutic objective determines the appropriate comparator and endpoint. If CWR is performed as part of emergency damage control, mortality will mainly reflect the severity of shock, hemorrhage, brain injury or associated organ damage. If CWR is performed as planned early respiratory reconstruction, more proximate endpoints such as ventilator-free days, extubation failure, rescue tracheostomy, pneumonia conditional on ventilator exposure, pain control and mobilization may better capture the intended benefit. Pooling these different operative intents under the same “rapid sequence” label risks making admission day appear to be the exposure, when the clinically relevant exposure is why the chest wall was reconstructed immediately. Recent articles in Critical Care have emphasized that time-dependent ICU treatment decisions require explicit definition of time zero, eligibility, treatment strategies and clinically meaningful contrasts before causal interpretation is attempted [2, 3].

The authors’ data support this distinction. Predictors of day-0 CWR included penetrating injury, relevant extra-thoracic injury and the need for another emergency operation, whereas other thoracic injuries such as hemothorax, lung contusion and lung laceration reduced the likelihood of rapid CWR. This suggests that day-0 CWR was often embedded in a broader operative trajectory of polytrauma, rather than being driven solely by isolated chest wall instability. This is clinically important because early trauma mortality is strongly shaped by bleeding, coagulopathy, resuscitation and evolving physiological instability [4].

Second, the decision for CWR timing may be better anchored to physiological readiness than to admission calendar day alone. Current international recommendations support early surgical stabilization when indicated, ideally within 72 h, while emphasizing individualized timing according to injury pattern, operative context and physiological status rather than calendar day alone [5, 6]. A practical next step would be to define a readiness-for-fixation framework that combines chest wall phenotype with systemic trauma physiology. Such a framework could include control of major hemorrhage, improvement in base excess or lactate, correction of coagulopathy, acceptable vasopressor requirement, manageable pulmonary contusion, adequate oxygenation reserve, completion of essential imaging and the presence or absence of mandatory concomitant surgery.

Under this model, the clinically relevant comparison would not simply be day 0 versus days 1–3. It would be immediate fixation despite ongoing physiological instability versus early fixation after predefined stabilization milestones. For hemodynamically stable patients with severe chest wall instability, planned CWR within 24–72 h may provide the benefits of early fixation while allowing time for resuscitation, imaging, analgesic optimization and multidisciplinary planning. For unstable patients, day-0 CWR should remain reserved for situations in which chest wall reconstruction is inseparable from life-saving operative management. Because CWR plausibly acts through respiratory mechanics and liberation from ventilatory support, ventilator-related and weaning outcomes may be more proximal to the intended treatment effect than all-cause in-hospital mortality alone [7, 8].

These comments do not weaken the contribution of Spering et al. Rather, they highlight what may be the most clinically useful message of their study: rapid CWR is not a homogeneous intervention. The question is not whether day-0 surgery is good or bad, nor whether earlier is always better, but which operative intent and physiological state justify immediate reconstruction. Future registries should record why CWR was performed, whether it was the primary operation or part of another emergency procedure, and whether predefined stabilization milestones had been achieved before fixation. Such information may better define patients who benefit from immediate CWR and those for whom planned early stabilization within 24–72 h is the safer and more effective strategy.
