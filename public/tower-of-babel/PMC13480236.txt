Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy–Induced Conduction System Quiescence With Concomitant Fascicular Ventricular Tachycardia and Complete Heart Block
Authors: Lea Carina Rivera, Caleb Wutawunashe, Natasha Qureshi, Anish Adhikari, Neil Bernstein, Sandrine Lebrun, Atul Bali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTakotsubo cardiomyopathy (TCM) is a stress-induced cardiomyopathy that mimics acute coronary syndrome without obstructive coronary artery disease. Complications include persistent, life-threatening arrhythmias, even after ejection fraction recovery.Case SummaryA 73-year-old woman presented with syncope, and electrocardiogram showed alternating wide complex tachycardia and complete heart block (CHB) (heart rate: 30-150 beats/min). Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 15% with apical/mid akinesis and basal hyperkinesis, and left heart catheterization showed nonobstructive coronary artery disease. Persistent ventricular tachycardia and CHB precluded antiarrhythmic therapy. After failing biventricular capture given an electrically quiescent conduction system, a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was placed.DiscussionCatecholamine excess in TCM induces microvascular dysfunction, leading to both tachy- and bradyarrhythmias, likely from catecholamine-induced electrical instability and concomitant vagal stimulation. History of Presentation

A 73-year-old woman presented after a witnessed syncopal event preceded by acute-onset lightheadedness and flailing movements of the bilateral upper and lower extremities. Family members denied tongue biting, bowel or urinary incontinence, or a postictal state. On admission, temperature 36.5 °C, blood pressure 154/100 mm Hg with fluctuating heart rate of 30 to 150 beats/min, oxygenating 97% on room air. Physical examination showed a well-appearing female in no acute distress. Extraocular movements were intact, and oropharyngeal mucosa was dry. Lungs were clear bilaterally. Cardiac examination revealed an irregular, bradycardic rhythm, normal S1 and S2, and no murmurs, rubs, or gallops. There was no jugular venous distention or peripheral edema, though extremities were cool with 2+ radial and dorsalis pedis pulses with an irregular rhythm. Abdominal examination showed no tenderness. Neurologic examination was grossly unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included a former 10 pack-year smoking history (quit in 1997), childhood murmur, peripheral neuropathy, and anxiety. Medications included gabapentin, quetiapine, zolpidem, and alprazolam. Family history included paternal fatal myocardial infarction (unknown age), and a sibling with atrial fibrillation and mitral valve prolapse.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included cardiac arrhythmias, including tachy-brady syndrome, atrial fibrillation with slowed ventricular response, and high-grade atrioventricular (AV) block; structural heart disease–related syncope, including aortic stenosis and cardiomyopathies; acute coronary syndrome; vasovagal syncope; convulsive syncope; orthostatic hypotension; and, less likely, seizure, stroke, or other acute neurological events.

Investigations

Laboratory results revealed leukocytosis 12.98 K/μL, potassium 3.1 mmol/L, magnesium 2.2 mg/dL, lactate 3.4 mmol/L, normal high-sensitivity troponin T of 25 ng/L, and pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 364 pg/mL. Serial electrocardiograms showed a wide complex tachycardia (WCT) with right bundle morphology, left-axis deviation, QTc 555, and AV dissociation concerning for fascicular ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1). Repeat electrocardiogram 30 minutes later showed complete heart block (CHB) with a junctional escape rhythm at 32 beats/min (Figure 2). A transvenous pacer was placed into the right ventricle via the right internal jugular vein and adjusted to a pacer rate of 60 beats/min with 10 mA capture threshold. The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit for SCAI (Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions) stage B arrhythmia-mediated cardiogenic shock.

Limited transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed an ejection fraction (EF) of 15% with mid and apical akinesis and hypercontractility of the basal segments (Figure 3). Complete TTE showed similar findings, with an EF of 25%. Inflammatory markers, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, were normal—making myocarditis unlikely—and infectious work-up was negative, prompting an ischemic evaluation.

Right heart catheterization revealed mildly elevated filling pressures, cardiac output 2.9 L/min, and cardiac index 2.0 L/min/m2 (Figure 4). Diagnostic left heart catheterization showed a left ventricular end-diastolic pressure of 16 mm Hg, nonobstructive coronary artery disease with 40% stenosis in the mid left anterior descending artery and a mid left anterior descending artery myocardial bridge with systolic compression. Left ventriculogram was consistent with TTE findings, confirming the diagnosis of Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TCM) (Figure 5).

Management (Medical/Interventions)

The patient continued to experience alternating episodes of fascicular ventricular tachycardia and CHB with junctional escape, precluding antiarrhythmic therapy, including verapamil, given her newly reduced EF and the concern of potentiating persistent bradyarrhythmias. Attempts to decrease the transvenous pacing rate below 60 beats/min resulted in worsening lactic acidosis (3.5 mmol/L), but later improved to 1.2 mmol/L with an increased pacer rate to 80 beats/min. The decision was made to proceed with the placement of a biventricular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator for secondary prevention. Intraoperatively, there was failure to achieve capture at 5 V at the coronary sinus. Compromised structural integrity of the interventricular septum also prevented proper lead placement, therefore a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was placed instead, with the atrial pacing lead placed in the high right atrium and the ventricular lead in the right ventricle.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After device implantation, repeat TTE showed modest improvement, with EF 20%. The patient was started on guideline-directed medical therapy with valsartan, metoprolol succinate, and spironolactone. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors were deferred given a history of urinary tract infections. The patient was then transferred to a general cardiology service and later discharged. At 5-month follow-up, repeat TTE showed recovery of EF to 63% and resolution of left ventricular dysfunction. At 15-month follow-up, the patient remained pacemaker dependent, with 56% right atrial pacing and 99% right ventricular pacing.

Discussion

Although TCM is considered to be a transient condition, with expected improvement in cardiac function in the weeks to months after symptom onset, numerous studies have shown an increasing incidence of cardiac complications during the acute phase of the disease.1 Though the exact process is unknown, it is believed that the activation of β1- and β2-adrenergic receptors (ARs) in response to psychological and/or physical stressors contributes to macro- and microvascular dysfunction, including direct myocardial injury and electrical instability.2,3 The distribution of the β1-AR and β2-AR differs across the myocardium, with the highest density of β2-AR at the cardiac apex and the highest density of β1-AR at the cardiac base.2 Under normal physiologic conditions, epinephrine and norepinephrine stimulate both receptor subtypes, activating downstream G-protein-mediated signaling pathways and augmenting myocardial inotropic and chronotropic effects. Elevated norepinephrine levels during a catecholamine surge preferentially stimulate β1-AR, maintaining signaling through the G-protein alpha subunit (Gαs) pathway. In contrast, preferential stimulation of β2-AR by epinephrine induces a phenomenon known as stimulus trafficking, whereby phosphorylation of the receptor by G-protein coupled receptor-2 (GRK2) promotes a switch in coupling from the stimulatory Gαs subunit to the G-protein alpha inhibitory subunit (Gαi), ultimately generating a negative inotropic effect localized at the cardiac apex and inducing the characteristic “apical ballooning” associated with TCM.3

Catecholamine-induced myocardial injury can present as either focal or global myocardial edema, serving as arrhythmogenic substrate even in the absence of significant structural heart disease or fibrosis.4 Differences in the distribution of βAR within the myocardium may contribute to regional variations in myocardial stunning and conduction disturbances, predisposing patients to ventricular and supraventricular arrhythmias and repolarization abnormalities.3,4 Our patient presented with hypokalemia (3.1 mmol/L) and marked QTc prolongation, which may be explained by direct β2-AR activation and stimulation of membrane-bound Na+/K+-ATPase, promoting intracellular potassium uptake. This transient systemic hypokalemia during acute sympathetic activation may further increase susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias, contributing to the electrical instability observed at presentation.

Despite increased reports in recent years, the temporal relationship between TCM and high-degree AV block remains incompletely understood and difficult to ascertain. Prior studies have suggested that CHB serves as the physiologic stressor precipitating TCM, with reports of high-degree AV block persisting even after recovery of left ventricular dysfunction, such as in our patient, who continued to require permanent pacing even up to 15 months postdischarge and 10 months after EF recovery.5 In contrast, the demographic characteristics of patients typically affected by TCM offers an alternative hypothesis. TCM predominantly occurs in older adults—particularly postmenopausal women—at an age when some degree of degenerative conduction disease is relatively common.1, 2, 3,6 Such conduction abnormalities, however, may only become clinically apparent during the acute phase of TCM, triggered by myocardial dysfunction and autonomic imbalance.6 Though our patient had no known history of cardiac disease, the possibility of subclinical conduction disease cannot be excluded and may have contributed to the development of CHB on presentation.

Although we cannot characterize the exact chronology of our patient's arrhythmias, the initial WCT may represent an early phase tachyarrhythmia, likely secondary to catecholamine-induced electrical instability. However, given the subsequent development of a CHB, an alternative explanation is that the WCT reflects an accelerated escape rhythm in the setting of an evolving high-grade AV block. Although escape rhythms in CHB are classically slow, accelerated junctional rhythms may occur in the setting of increased sympathetic tone. Thus, the WCT may represent a compensatory escape mechanism rather than a primary tachyarrhythmia, suggesting that conduction disease may have preceded and contributed to the development of TCM in our patient. The subsequent development of CHB may reflect progression to a vagally mediated bradyarrhythmia in response to autonomic dysfunction from catecholamine excess.7,8 Additionally, the discovery of an electrically quiescent myocardium during pacemaker lead placement further supports the theory that acute myocardial stunning and edema in TCM may impair myocardial excitability and conduction.3

Although many arrhythmias in TCM are self-limiting and generally resolve as myocardial function recovers, conduction disease—and CHB in particular—frequently requires permanent pacing, though the optimal timing for pacemaker placement is unknown.8 There have seldom been reports of CHB in patients with TCM, and the persistence of these conduction abnormalities raises the question of whether these patients continue to undergo residual electrical remodeling even after the removal of the stressor, resulting in a protracted recovery course and even possible device failure after implantation.9 Furthermore, given the increasing recognition of autonomic dysfunction in TCM, further studies on the use of autonomic modulators, including beta-blockers and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, may provide insight into other treatment modalities to manage this patient population.10

Conclusions

TCM has been shown to have significant arrhythmogenic potential, demonstrating that even transient myocardial dysfunction can precipitate benign to life-threatening conduction abnormalities. Given the acuity of these arrhythmogenic complications, early diagnosis and intervention may be necessary to mitigate morbidity and mortality.2,7,8 To our knowledge, there have been limited reports of patients presenting with both a tachy- and bradyarrhythmia in association with the development of TCM, underscoring the necessity of continued research to fully understand the mechanisms underlying these conduction abnormalities and to direct the timing of device implantation.
