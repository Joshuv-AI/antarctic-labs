Injectable chondroitin sulfate-glycosylated decellularized extracellular matrix microgels activate Wnt/β-Catenin signaling to promote functional muscle regeneration in VML
Authors: Siyu Liu, Sheng Ding, Shuwei Wang, Chunxiao Zhou, Xiang Liu, Hao Zhang, Long Yang, Zujian Feng, Deling Kong, Ningbei Yin, Weiwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolumetric muscle loss (VML) overwhelms the endogenous regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle, leading to serious fibrosis, which further reduces muscle strength and severely affects patients’ quality of life. While decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) scaffolds offer tissue-specific biochemical cues for muscle regeneration, conventional decellularization protocols deplete critical glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), particularly chondroitin sulfate (CS), which are essential for growth factor sequestration and signaling pathway activation. Here, we developed injectable CS-glycosylated dECM microgels via microfluidic technology to restore GAG functionality to promote comprehensive muscle regeneration. We demonstrate that CS functionalization enhances myoblast adhesion, proliferation, migration, and differentiation in vitro. RNA sequencing analysis reveals that CS-mediated enhancement operates principally through activation of canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling, with coordinated upregulation of downstream myogenic programs. In a rat tibialis anterior VML model, administration with high-CS formulations (H-CS@ECM) greatly promote muscle regeneration, function recovery and vascularization, with the muscle exhibiting enhanced-, blood perfusion, neuromuscular connectivity, and contractile protein expression at 8 weeks. Our findings suggest that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels possess significant translational potential as a minimally invasive and pro-regenerative implant material for VML repair. More broadly, this work establishes GAG modification as a generalizable design principle for enhancing dECM scaffold performance across diverse endogenous tissue regeneration applications, without the addition of exogenous growth factors, cytokines or drugs.Keywords: Volumetric muscle loss, Muscle regeneration, Injectable microgel, Decellularized extracellular matrix, Chondroitin sulfate

Introduction

Volumetric muscle loss (VML) injuries, commonly arising from trauma, vehicular accidents, tumor ablation, and surgical debridement [1,2], where the lost muscle exceeds 20% of the total muscle volume, exceeding the regenerative capacity of endogenous repair mechanisms, represent a major contributor to permanent disability [3,4]. The catastrophic nature of VML stems from the simultaneous destruction of multiple tissue components essential for regeneration, including myofibers, satellite cells, basal lamina, vascular networks, and neural innervation, resulting in chronic functional deficits, scar tissue formation, and severely diminished quality of life [5,6]. Currently, free functional muscle transfer (FFMT) represents the gold standard surgical intervention for clinical treatment of VML. However, it carries substantial limitations including donor site morbidity, limited tissue availability, prolonged operative times, requirement for microsurgical expertise, and crucially, inconsistent functional restoration with only approximately 50% of patients returning to pre-injury activity levels [7,8]. These limitations have catalyzed intensive research into regenerative medicine approaches that harness biomaterial scaffolds to guide de novo muscle tissue formation without sacrificing healthy donor tissue.

Among regenerative strategies, decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) scaffolds have emerged as particularly promising due to their inherent tissue-specific biochemical composition, preservation of native ultrastructure, and capacity to modulate immune responses toward constructive remodeling rather than chronic inflammation [[9], [10], [11]]. The critical role of biomaterial characteristics in directing macrophage polarization and shaping the inflammatory microenvironment has been extensively documented in recent comprehensive reviews [12,13], highlighting the importance of designing scaffolds that actively promote pro-regenerative immune phenotypes. Muscle-derived dECM retains crucial biomolecular signals including collagen networks, basement membrane components, and residual growth factor binding sites that collectively provide instructive cues for cellular infiltration, proliferation, and differentiation [14]. Clinical studies employing acellular biological scaffolds have demonstrated modest improvements in muscle strength and tissue volume in VML patients, with some showing evidence of satellite cell infiltration and de novo myofiber formation [15,16]. However, current acellular scaffolds consistently fail to regenerate physiologically meaningful amounts of functional muscle tissue, with improvements often modest in magnitude and potentially reversible over time [17,18]. To address these limitations, recent research has focused on engineering dECM derivatives with enhanced bioactivity through targeted biochemical modifications and modular microgel formulations [19,20].

A fundamental limitation of current dECM scaffolds lies in the significant depletion of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) during the decellularization process [21,22]. GAGs constitute critical ECM components that regulate diverse cellular behaviors through their capacity to sequester, present, and protect growth factors, thereby establishing biochemical gradients essential for tissue morphogenesis and regeneration [23,24]. Accordingly, recent advances in regenerative biomaterials have increasingly emphasized the reconstruction of GAG-related matrix functions and the broader engineering of microenvironments, including modulation of growth factor availability, immune responses, and tissue-specific regenerative signaling. These strategies have been applied in advanced hydrogel and scaffold systems to promote functional tissue repair, including pediatric growth plate regeneration [12,25,26]. In native skeletal muscle, GAGs mediate crucial interactions with growth factors including fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF2), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [27,28]. However, conventional detergent-based decellularization protocols result in dramatic GAG losses, with retention rates typically below 30% of native tissue levels [29]. Critically, the residual GAGs in decellularized scaffolds exhibit severely compromised functionality, demonstrating negligible binding affinity for essential matrix-associated growth factors, leading to insufficient activation of endogenous stem cells [30,31]. The above highlights the importance of considering GAGs in decellularized matrices and their derivatives. Among the depleted GAG species, chondroitin sulfate merits particular attention as it represents the most abundant sulfated GAG in native muscle ECM and plays multifaceted roles in tissue development and repair. CS regulates stem cell quiescence and activation [32,33], modulates inflammatory responses [34], promotes angiogenesis [35,36], and crucially, exhibits high binding affinity for various key growth factors that govern myogenic lineage commitment and skeletal muscle development [[37], [38], [39]]. Furthermore, CS has demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties by modulating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype associated with tissue repair [40]. Despite this mechanistic understanding, approaches to restore CS functionality within dECM scaffolds and harness its regenerative potential for muscle tissue repair remain largely unexplored.

Injectable microgels have emerged as a particularly promising delivery system for VML repair due to their minimally invasive administration, excellent defect conformability, and ability to support cellular infiltration and tissue integration [[41], [42], [43]]. In this study, we designed an injectable glycosylated dECM microgel, incorporating CS as the sulfated GAG material to restore GAG component lost during the decellularization process. We developed an emulsion-based microfluidic platform to fabricate uniform CS-glycosylated dECM microgels (CS@ECM) (Scheme. 1A). We comprehensively characterized the structure, mechanical properties, and injectability of microgels, whilst also investigating the biocompatibility and the regulatory effects on myoblasts alongside the underlying mechanisms. Finally, we demonstrated the pro-regenerative effects on muscle of the CS@ECM microgels in the rat tibialis anterior VML model (Scheme. 1B). These findings provide mechanistic insights into how GAG restoration within tissue-engineered scaffolds can recapitulate the instructive biochemical microenvironment of native ECM to guide functional tissue repair.

Results

Preparation and characterization of dECM

To develop a biomimetic scaffold for tissue engineering applications, we first established a protocol for the extraction and processing of dECM from porcine skeletal muscle tissue (Fig. 1A). Fresh skeletal muscle tissue was harvested from porcine hindlimb and subjected to sequential decellularization and lyophilization steps to generate dECM powder suitable for further processing. Macroscopic examination revealed that the natural muscle tissue underwent a progressive color change from pink to white and semi-transparent following decellularization, with the final lyophilized product appearing as a fluffy white powder (Fig. 1B). The dECM powder was readily dissolved in acidic pepsin and neutralized with NaOH to create homogeneous solutions for downstream applications.

Histological analysis confirmed the efficacy of the decellularization process. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of native muscle tissue showed well-organized muscle fiber architecture with densely packed myofibrils and prominent cell nuclei (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the dECM exhibited a complete absence of cellular components while preserving the fibrous ECM architecture. Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated robust collagen content in both native tissue and dECM, though the dECM showed more dispersed collagen fibers following the decellularization process. DAPI staining revealed abundant nuclear material in native muscle tissue but complete absence of detectable DNA in the dECM, confirming thorough removal of cellular components.

Quantitative analysis revealed that DNA content decreased from 619.5 ± 61.4 ng/mg to 9.23 ± 0.25 ng/mg, falling significantly below the international standard threshold of 50 ng/mg for decellularized products (p < 0.001, Fig. 1D). Despite the dramatic reduction in cellular content, the dECM retained substantial collagen, with measurements of 41.2 ± 5.6 μg/mg compared to 62.5 ± 3.8 μg/mg in native tissue (p < 0.01, Fig. 1E). This retention of approximately 65% of the original collagen content indicates successful preservation of the primary structural component of the ECM during the decellularization process. SDS-PAGE analysis of total protein extracted from dECM revealed distinct protein profiles from native muscle (Fig. 1F). The dECM showed a markedly simplified protein profile dominated by bands consistent with collagen α-chains (130 kDa) and β-chains (approximately 260 kDa), with minimal presence of intracellular contractile proteins. Pure collagen I (Col I) served as a reference standard, confirming the identification of collagen bands in the dECM samples.

To further characterize the biochemical composition of the dECM, we performed quantitative analysis of major glycosaminoglycan (GAG) species. Native muscle tissue contained 1.92 ± 0.14 μg/mg of GAGs, which was reduced to 0.41 ± 0.04 μg/mg in the dECM, representing approximately 21% retention (p < 0.001, Fig. 1G). Chondroitin sulfate (CS) content decreased from 807.79 ± 18.24 ng/mg in native tissue to 84.27 ± 9.78 ng/mg in dECM (p < 0.001, Fig. 1H). Heparan sulfate (HS) showed similar trends, declining from 351.2 ± 5.67 ng/mg to 70.03 ± 3.17 ng/mg (p < 0.001, Fig. 1I). Dermatan sulfate (DS) was reduced from 47.42 ± 1.3 ng/mg in native tissue to 9.66 ± 0.2 ng/mg in dECM (p < 0.001, Fig. 1J). We observed a substantial reduction in the absolute content of sulfated glycosaminoglycans after decellularization, particularly chondroitin sulfate.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that our decellularization protocol effectively removes cellular components and associated DNA while preserving key ECM constituents such as collagen. However, functional levels of bioactive GAGs are significantly depleted. The loss or dysfunction of specific GAGs resulting from decellularization may prevent the binding of matrix-associated growth factors crucial for cell growth and differentiation [23,30,44], significantly impacting cellular metabolism. This suggests that GAG supplementation should be incorporated into the fabrication of dECM-derived materials.

Fabrication and characterization of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels

Having established a robust decellularization protocol yielding high-quality muscle dECM, we next sought to engineer this material into a modular, cell-compatible delivery platform. Referencing our prior research [45], a 5 wt% dECM solution was selected for subsequent studies. To further enhance the biological activity of dECM, chondroitin sulfate was introduced as a GAGs component. First, chondroitin sulfate was oxidized with NaIO4 to synthesize CS-aldehyde (CS-ald), with the aldehyde substitution degree determined via titration with hydroxylamine hydrochloride at 13.8%. Subsequently, CS-ald was mixed with dECM, forming covalent Schiff's base crosslinks between the aldehyde and amino groups to generate glycosylated dECM. To screen for the optimal glycosylation concentration, a gradient of CS-ald to dECM mass ratios (1:20, 1:10, 1:2, and 1:1) was evaluated. As shown in Fig. S1, the elastic modulus increased monotonically with CS content. However, at the extreme ratio of 1:1, the overabundance of aldehyde groups generated excessive Schiff base crosslinks, producing an overly stiff and brittle matrix (∼20 kPa) that deviated from the native mechanical niche of skeletal muscle (∼10–15 kPa). Furthermore, CCK-8 assay revealed that C2C12 cell viability and proliferation peaked at the 1:2 ratio, whereas cell responses were markedly restricted at the 1:1 ratio (Fig. S2), potentially due to the supra-physiological stiffness and elevated local osmotic pressure. Two representative modification ratios of 1:20 (L-CS@ECM) and 1:2 (H-CS@ECM) were selected for subsequent evaluations. We developed an emulsion-based microfluidic fabrication strategy to generate three groups of microgels: ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM, where L and H represent ECM microgels with low and high CS contents, respectively. The fabrication process involved mixing the CS-dECM/dECM solution with GelMA solution and photoinitiator, then the mixture was encapsulated in an oil phase to form uniform droplets, which were subsequently crosslinked via UV photopolymerization to generate stable microgels (Fig. 2A).

Microscopic examination revealed the successful generation of uniform microgels across all formulations (Fig. 2B). Bright-field imaging showed spherical microgels with smooth surfaces and consistent size distributions. Fluorescent imaging of rhodamine-labeled microgels confirmed homogeneous fluorophore distribution throughout the microgel volume, indicating uniform polymer crosslinking and structural integrity. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis provided detailed insights into the microarchitecture of different microgel formulations (Fig. 2C). Pure ECM microgels exhibited a moderately porous surface topology with interconnected pore structures. The low-CS content microgels (L-CS@ECM) showed slightly increased surface roughness and pore definition compared to unmodified ECM microgels. Notably, high-CS content microgels (H-CS@ECM) displayed the most pronounced surface texture with well-defined pore structures and increased surface area, likely attributable to the hygroscopic properties of CS enhancing hydrogel swelling during freeze-drying preparation. Quantitative analysis revealed that the pore size across all groups was approximately 8–9 μm, with no significant differences among the three formulations (Fig. 2D). Similarly, porosity analysis demonstrated that all microgel types possessed comparable porosity values of approximately 50-55%, with no statistically significant differences detected among ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM formulations (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that CS incorporation does not substantially alter the bulk structural properties of the microgels, ensuring consistent physical characteristics across formulations. The average particle sizes of the ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM microgels were all concentrated around 95–100 μm (Fig. 2F). The uniformity in size across formulations confirms the robustness and reproducibility of the microfluidic fabrication process.

Chemical characterization by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed successful CS incorporation into the microgel matrix (Fig. 2G). ECM microgels displayed characteristic peaks associated with collagen, including amide I (∼1640 cm−1), amide II (∼1540 cm−1), and amide III (∼1240 cm−1) bands. CS-glycosylated microgels showed additional peaks at ∼1033 cm−1 and ∼1225 cm−1, corresponding to sulfate group vibrations characteristic of chondroitin sulfate. The intensity of these sulfate-associated peaks increased progressively from L-CS@ECM to H-CS@ECM. Quantitative analysis of chondroitin sulfate content in three microgel groups revealed significantly elevated levels in both L-CS@ECM and H-CS@ECM groups, confirming successful incorporation of the CS component (Fig. 2H). This constitutes direct evidence of successful cross-linking of the glycosylated ECM.

Mechanical properties, biocompatibility and safety evaluation of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels

Subsequently, to assess the mechanical properties, biocompatibility and safety of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels, we performed comprehensive studies including swelling rate, rheological properties, in vivo degradation, injectability, organ toxicity, and hemocompatibility. Swelling behavior analysis revealed that CS incorporation significantly enhanced water uptake capacity (Fig. 2I). All formulations demonstrated rapid initial swelling within the first 60 min, followed by gradual approach to equilibrium by 300 min. ECM microgels reached an equilibrium swelling ratio of approximately 730%, while L-CS@ECM microgels achieved approximately 720%. H-CS@ECM microgels exhibited the highest swelling capacity, reaching approximately 870% at equilibrium. This enhanced swelling behavior of CS-glycosylated microgels reflects the hygroscopic nature of sulfated GAGs and suggests improved capacity for nutrient transport and metabolite exchange within the microgel environment. Mechanical characterization revealed that the elastic moduli of all microgel formulations fell within the physiological range of native resting skeletal muscle (∼10-15 kPa, with a typical resting value of ∼12 kPa) [46]. Stress-strain curves demonstrated characteristic viscoelastic behavior (Fig. 2J). CS incorporation produced a modest increasing trend in elastic modulus (ECM: 6.34 ± 1.01 kPa; L-CS@ECM: 8.63 ± 0.53 kPa; H-CS@ECM: 10.68 ± 1.1 kPa; Fig. 2K). Stress-relaxation testing demonstrated that all formulations exhibited time-dependent stress relaxation characteristic of viscoelastic hydrogels (Fig. 2L). Quantification of stress relaxation levels after 5 s revealed that ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM microgels relaxed to 85.34 ± 2.65%, 81.48 ± 3.13%, and 75.15 ± 1.66% of their initial stress, respectively (Fig. 2M). Although the additional crosslinking mediated by sulfate groups on CS chains [47,48] contributed to a slight trend in matrix viscoelasticity, these subtle physical variations remained entirely within the physiological and adaptive window of skeletal muscle cells.

Assessment of injectability demonstrated that CS-glycosylated microgels maintain excellent flow properties suitable for minimally invasive delivery (Fig. S3). The microgels could be readily injected through 21G needles without clogging or excessive force requirements. Microscopic examination of microgels before and after injection revealed that all formulations retained their spherical morphology and structural integrity following the injection process. This excellent injectability combined with post-injection shape recovery ensures that the microgels can be delivered minimally invasively while maintaining their designed structural and functional properties at the target site. Evaluation of in vivo degradation behavior was conducted using Cy5-labeled microgels injected subcutaneously in mice, with fluorescence imaging performed over 4 weeks (Fig. S4A and B). IVIS spectral imaging revealed that all microgel formulations were readily detected at day 0 following injection. The fluorescence intensity progressively decreased over time, indicating gradual biodegradation. ECM microgels showed relatively rapid degradation, with approximately 53% of the initial signal remaining at 1 weeks and 11% at 3 weeks (Fig. S4C). CS- glycosylated microgels exhibited slightly slower degradation kinetics, with L-CS@ECM retaining approximately 59% at 1 weeks and 5% at 3 weeks, and H-CS@ECM maintaining 56% at 1 weeks and 10% at 3 weeks. This controlled degradation profile is favorable for tissue engineering applications, as it provides sustained structural support during the critical early phases of tissue regeneration while allowing eventual replacement by native tissue.

To evaluate potential systemic toxicity, we performed comprehensive histological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) at 8 weeks following microgel injection (Fig. S5). H&E staining of organ sections revealed no evidence of pathological changes across all treatment groups. These findings indicate that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels do not elicit systemic toxicity and are well-tolerated in vivo. Hemocompatibility assessment is critical for any biomaterial intended for clinical translation, as blood contact can occur during injection or through capillary ingrowth during vascularization. Hemolysis assays revealed excellent blood compatibility across all microgel formulations (Fig. S6A). Visual inspection of red blood cell suspensions incubated with microgels showed no visible hemolysis, with supernatants remaining clear similar to the negative control (normal saline). In contrast, the positive control (deionized water) produced complete hemolysis with bright red supernatants. Quantification of hemolysis rates confirmed these observations (Fig. S6B). All microgel formulations produced hemolysis rates below 4% (ECM: 2.53 ± 0.76%, L-CS@ECM: 3.84 ± 1.24%, H-CS@ECM: 3.76 ± 1.26%), well below the 5% threshold established by ISO standards for acceptable hemolytic activity. These results demonstrate that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels possess excellent hemocompatibility and pose minimal risk of hemolytic reactions.

Collectively, these studies establish that we have successfully prepared CS-glycosylated dECM microgels with controllable CS incorporation, uniform structure, and tunable mechanical properties. These microgels exhibit favorable biodegradation kinetics, excellent injectability, no systemic toxicity, and outstanding hemocompatibility. These characteristics position this biomaterial platform as a promising candidate material in regenerative medicine applications.

CS-glycosylated dECM microgels support myoblast viability, proliferation, and migration

To evaluate the biological performance of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels, we assessed their capacity to support C2C12 myoblast viability, proliferation, and functional behavior. C2C12 cells were cultured on ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM microgels, and cell viability was monitored over a 5-day culture period using live/dead fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3A). At day 1, all three microgel formulations supported robust cell attachment with predominantly live cells (green fluorescence) and minimal dead cells (red fluorescence). By day 3, cell density increased substantially across all groups, with cells forming interconnected networks throughout the microgel surfaces. At day 5, confluent cell coverage was observed on all microgel types, with H-CS@ECM microgels appearing to support the highest cell densities. Throughout the culture period, dead cell populations remained minimal across all conditions, indicating excellent cell compatibility of the microgel substrates. Quantitative analysis of cell proliferation and viability using the CCK-8 assay confirmed the live-dead staining results (Fig. 3B and C). At day 5, proliferation continued to increase with cell viability remaining robust across all groups, with ECM OD value at 1.65 ± 0.02, L-CS@ECM at 1.75 ± 0.03, and H-CS@ECM reaching 1.86 ± 0.05 (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). Regarding cell viability, H-CS@ECM microgels showing slightly elevated viability (123.3 ± 3.9%) compared to ECM (109.2 ± 2.1%) and L-CS@ECM (115.9 ± 2.3%) (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM). To confirm the cross-species applicability of this pro-proliferative efficacy and align with the rat-derived host niche used in vivo, parallel evaluations were conducted using the rat-derived L6 myoblast cell line. The results consistently demonstrated that the H-CS@ECM formulation significantly enhanced L6 cell proliferation and sustained high viability, consistent with the trends captured in C2C12 cultures (Fig. S7). These results indicate that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels, particularly at high concentrations, enhance myocyte proliferation and sustained high viability, thereby providing a supportive microenvironment for long-term cell culture applications.

To assess the impact of CS functionalization on myoblast migratory capacity, we performed transwell migration assays (Fig. 3D). Representative images of crystal violet-stained cells that migrated through the transwell membrane demonstrated clear differences among experimental groups (Fig. 3E). Control cultures (no microgels) showed baseline migration with an average of 79.3 ± 12.5 cells per field. ECM microgels enhanced migration to 119.9 ± 9.62 cells per field. L-CS@ECM microgels further increased migration to 120.8 ± 17.83 cells per field. Notably, H-CS@ECM microgels elicited the most robust migratory response, with 191.7 ± 17.72 cells per field migrating through the membrane (Fig. 3F). This represents a 2.4-fold increase over control conditions and a 1.6-fold increase over unmodified ECM microgels (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs all other groups). These results indicate that CS incorporation, particularly at high concentrations, significantly enhances the chemotactic properties of dECM microgels, promoting myoblast migration.

To examine longer-term cellular interactions with the microgel substrates, we performed non-adherent culture experiments in which C2C12 cells were co-cultured with microgels on ultra-low attachment plates (Fig. 3G–I). Phalloidin staining revealed cell attachment and spreading on all microgel types across the 5-day culture period (Fig. 3G). At day 1, cells exhibited rounded morphology with initial attachment to microgel surfaces. By day 3, cells demonstrated increased spreading with visible actin stress fiber formation, indicating active cytoskeletal remodeling and matrix engagement. At day 5, cells formed elaborate networks spanning multiple microgels, with particularly robust cell-microgel aggregates observed in the H-CS@ECM condition. Quantification of cell-microgel attachment revealed progressive, dose-dependent increases in cellular association with CS-glycosylated microgels (Fig. 3I). At day 1, ECM microgels recruited 26.2 ± 5.9 cells per microgel, L-CS@ECM recruited 32.5 ± 3.7 cells, and H-CS@ECM recruited 39.7 ± 1.9 cells (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). By day 3, attachment increased across all formulations, with ECM at 40.9 ± 7.3 cells, L-CS@ECM at 41.6 ± 5.7 cells, and H-CS@ECM at 51.7 ± 7.6 cells (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). At day 5, the trend continued with ECM supporting 47.8 ± 6.3 cells per microgel, L-CS@ECM at 55.7 ± 4.9 cells, and H-CS@ECM achieving the highest attachment at 66.4 ± 6.2 cells per microgel (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). This time- and dose-dependent increase in cell-microgel association demonstrates that CS glycosylation enhances cellular recruitment and sustained interaction with the dECM substrate.

Collectively, these cellular function studies establish that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels provide an excellent platform for myoblast culture, supporting cell viability, proliferation, migration, and attachment. The enhanced cellular responses observed with H-CS@ECM microgels suggest that GAG functionalization of tissue-derived ECM materials represents a promising strategy for optimizing cell-biomaterial interactions in regenerative medicine applications.

CS-glycosylated dECM microgels promote myogenic differentiation of myoblasts

Having established that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels support robust myoblast proliferation, migration, and attachment, we next investigated their capacity to promote myogenic differentiation. C2C12 myoblasts were cultured on ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM microgels for 5 days, and the expression of key myogenic markers was assessed by confocal laser scanning microscopy. Immunofluorescence staining revealed expression of early (MyoD), middle (Desmin) and late (MyHC) myogenic differentiation markers were elevated across all microgel formulations (Fig. 4A). Notably, cells cultured on CS-glycosylated microgels exhibited more robust and widespread expression of these markers compared to unmodified ECM microgels. Desmin (a muscle-specific intermediate filament protein) and MyHC (a definitive marker of mature contractile myofibers) showed particularly pronounced expression in H-CS@ECM cultures, with well-organized filamentous structures evident throughout differentiated myofibers. MyoD, a master regulatory transcription factor for myogenic commitment, displayed strong nuclear localization in cells on all microgel types, with enhanced signal intensity observed in CS-glycosylated conditions. Quantitative analysis of immunofluorescence images also confirmed the visual observations (Fig. 4B–D). The proportion of Desmin and MyHC-positive myofibers increased in a CS dose-dependent manner, with ECM microgels supporting 38.5 ± 7.2% and 34.3 ± 7.7% positive cells, L-CS@ECM achieving 58.5 ± 7.3% and 37.4 ± 6.5%, and H-CS@ECM reaching 80.3 ± 10.2% and 61.2 ± 10.2% (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM). Similarly, the proportion of MyoD-positive cells showed significant enhancement with CS functionalization, with percentages of 26.3 ± 1.8% for ECM, 35.3 ± 5.7% for L-CS@ECM, and 61.9 ± 11.2% for H-CS@ECM (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM; p < 0.01 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). These findings demonstrate that CS incorporation substantially enhances the myogenic differentiation capacity of dECM microgels.

To further validate the differentiation of C2C12 cells at the molecular level, we performed quantitative RT-PCR analysis to assess the expression of key myogenic regulatory genes (Fig. 4E). Analysis of MyoD mRNA expression revealed an increase with H-CS@ECM cultures showing 1.87 ± 0.33-fold higher expression compared to ECM controls (p < 0.01). MyoG (myogenin), another critical transcription factor governing terminal myogenic differentiation, exhibited even more pronounced upregulation, with H-CS@ECM microgels eliciting 2.09 ± 0.53-fold increase over ECM controls (p < 0.05). Expression of MyHC was significantly elevated in CS-functionalized conditions, with H-CS@ECM achieving 1.59 ± 0.44-fold enhancement compared to ECM (p < 0.01). We also performed time-course Western blot analysis (Days 1, 3, and 5) to track the dynamic expression of MyoD (early-stage), MyoG (mid-stage), and MyHC (late-stage). The results (Fig. S8) demonstrated that MyoD expression was highest at Day 1 and progressively declined thereafter, whereas both MyoG and MyHC increased steadily from Day 1 to Day 5, consistent with the sequential activation of the myogenic cascade. Notably, the H-CS@ECM group exhibited the highest expression of all three markers at their respective peak time points, MyoD at Day 1, MyoG at Day 3, and MyHC at Day 5, compared to the ECM and L-CS@ECM groups, confirming that CS incorporation substantially accelerates and enhances the myogenic differentiation capacity of dECM microgels. Furthermore, this pro-myogenic capacity was successfully recapitulated in rat-derived L6 myoblasts, where H-CS@ECM microgels robustly accelerated terminal myogenic commitment and upregulated MyHC expression (Figs. S9 and S10), thereby validating the cross-species regenerative efficacy of the glycosylated dECM platform. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels provide biochemical cues that enhance myogenic differentiation beyond what can be achieved with unmodified ECM alone. The enhancement of both early and late myogenic markers suggests that GAG-mediated signaling plays a crucial role in directing myoblast fate determination and differentiation progression.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway activation by CS-glycosylated dECM microgels

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced myogenic differentiation observed on CS-glycosylated microgels, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of C2C12 cells cultured on ECM versus H-CS@ECM microgels. Volcano plot analysis revealed substantial transcriptional reprogramming induced by CS functionalization, with numerous genes showing significant differential expression (Fig. 5A). A total of 713 genes were significantly upregulated and 767 genes were downregulated in H-CS@ECM cultures compared to ECM controls, indicating broad effects of CS on cellular transcriptional programs.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes provided insights into the biological processes most significantly affected by CS functionalization (Fig. 5B). The enriched GO terms included muscle system process, muscle cell differentiation, and muscle contraction, all of which directly relate to myogenesis and skeletal muscle formation. Additional enriched terms such as cell adhesion and cell migration were consistent with our earlier observations of enhanced cell-microgel interactions and migratory capacity. KEGG pathway analysis further corroborated these findings, identifying Wnt signaling pathway and focal adhesion among the most significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5C). Other enriched pathways included ECM-receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecules, and calcium signaling pathway, all of which are intimately linked to cell-matrix interactions and mechanical transduction processes that influence stem cell fate decisions. To investigate the role of Wnt signaling more comprehensively, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This analysis revealed significant enrichment of gene sets associated with both focal adhesion and Wnt signaling pathway in H-CS@ECM versus ECM cultures (Fig. 5D and E). The enrichment plots demonstrated coordinated upregulation of multiple genes within these pathways, with normalized enrichment scores indicating statistically significant pathway activation. Heatmap visualization of differentially expressed genes specifically within the Wnt signaling pathway revealed widespread upregulation of key pathway components in CS-functionalized conditions (Fig. 5F). These included canonical Wnt ligands, receptors, and downstream effectors, suggesting comprehensive activation of this critical developmental signaling cascade.

To validate the RNA-seq findings and confirm Wnt pathway activation at the protein level, we performed immunofluorescence analysis of β-catenin, the central mediator of canonical Wnt signaling. CLSM imaging revealed marked differences in β-catenin expression and localization across microgel formulations (Fig. 5G). Cells cultured on ECM microgels displayed relatively modest β-catenin staining, primarily localized at cell-cell junctions. In contrast, L-CS@ECM cultures showed increased β-catenin expression with some nuclear accumulation, while H-CS@ECM cultures exhibited the most pronounced staining with robust nuclear localization throughout the cell population. Quantitative image analysis confirmed these observations, with β-catenin-positive area increasing from 10.7 ± 4.2% in ECM to 17.1 ± 6.1% in L-CS@ECM and 31.7 ± 5.8% in H-CS@ECM (p < 0.001, Fig. 5H). Nuclear accumulation of β-catenin is a hallmark of canonical Wnt pathway activation and indicates transcriptional activity of Wnt target genes [49].

To validate that the observed increase in β-catenin translates to functional Wnt signaling output, we assessed the expression of established Wnt target genes by RT-PCR (Fig. 5I). AXIN2, a direct transcriptional target of β-catenin/TCF complexes and a reliable readout of canonical Wnt activity, showed significant upregulation in H-CS@ECM cultures, with 1.88 ± 0.41-fold increase compared to ECM controls (p < 0.05). Similarly, c-MYC, another well-characterized Wnt target gene involved in cell proliferation and differentiation, exhibited 1.81 ± 0.5-fold elevation in H-CS@ECM conditions (p < 0.05). The coordinated upregulation of these canonical Wnt target genes provides definitive evidence that CS functionalization activates Wnt signaling in myoblasts. Western blot analysis provided further confirmation of increased β-catenin protein levels in CS-glycosylated conditions (Fig. 5J). Densitometric quantification revealed that the β-catenin expression achieved 0.89 ± 0.14 in H-CS@ECM microgels (p < 0.05 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM, Fig. 5K), consistent with pathway activation at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels.

To elucidate whether CS enhances Wnt signaling by directly manipulating extracellular ligand localization, we characterized the molecular interaction kinetics between immobilized CS-ald and recombinant Wnt3a protein. The solid-phase binding assay demonstrated that the adsorption of Wnt3a to the CS matrix followed a classic, concentration-dependent saturation curve rather than non-specific physical trapping (Fig. S11A). The corresponding apparent dissociation constant was calculated to be Kd = 43.5 nM, demonstrating a high-affinity capture capability that aligns with the therapeutic ligand-sequestration traits of native GAGs. To further assess whether this interaction was associated with GAG-binding domains, a competitive displacement assay utilizing free heparin was introduced (Fig. S11B). Free heparin markedly reduced Wnt3a binding to immobilized CS-ald in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 0.3 μg/mL, establishing that this specific retention is cross-linked to the sulfation-dependent identification of the conserved Heparin-Binding Domain (HBD) of Wnt proteins. Collectively, these results indicate that covalently anchored CS networks can function as a solid-phase ligand-retention platform to sequester Wnt3a and may thereby contribute to the local enrichment of Wnt signals within the microgel matrix.

To definitively establish the causality between CS-mediated Wnt pathway activation and enhanced myogenic differentiation, we introduced XAV939, a specific canonical Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor, during the in vitro co-culture of C2C12 myoblasts with microgels. CLSM imaging and subsequent quantitative analysis revealed that the nuclear accumulation and overall expression of β-catenin stimulated by H-CS@ECM were effectively abrogated upon XAV939 treatment. Specifically, β-catenin-positive area drastically decreased from 31.8 ± 6.7% in the H-CS@ECM group and 32.2 ± 7.6% in the DMSO vehicle control group to 14.1 ± 4.9% in the XAV939-treated group (p < 0.001), with the residual fluorescent signals restricted to the cytoplasm and cell membrane (Fig. S12A and B). Crucially, the downstream myogenic commitment was directly impacted by this pharmacological blockade; the percentage of MyHC+ myofibers, which peaked at 44.5 ± 9.1% in H-CS@ECM and 40.5 ± 10.1% in +DMSO conditions, plummeted back to a level of 16.7 ± 5.4% in the +XAV939 group (p < 0.001, Fig. S12A and C).

To validate that the morphological abrogation of Wnt signaling translates to transcriptional and translational suppression, the expression of established downstream targets and contractile proteins was assessed. RT-PCR results confirmed that XAV939 administration thoroughly suppressed the transcription of Wnt target genes; the relative mRNA levels of c-MYC and AXIN2 were drastically downregulated from 2.27 ± 0.46-fold and 2.71 ± 0.58-fold in the H-CS@ECM group to 0.99 ± 0.23-fold and 1.09 ± 0.31-fold in the inhibitor group, respectively, fully reverting to the levels observed in the ECM control (p < 0.001, Fig. S12D and E). Furthermore, Western blot analysis provided translational confirmation of this targeted pathway disruption (Fig. S13A). Densitometric quantification revealed that total β-catenin protein expression in the H-CS@ECM + XAV939 group was significantly reduced to 0.38 ± 0.1 compared to the H-CS@ECM group (0.77 ± 0.1, p < 0.01). Concurrently, the expression level of MyHC protein exhibited a parallel decline, dropping to 0.33 ± 0.02 in the inhibitor group, which was consistent with the suppression observed at the gene and microscopic levels (Fig. S13B and C).

Taken together, these transcriptomic and molecular validation studies reveal that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels activate the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in myoblasts. This pathway activation likely contributes to the enhanced myogenic differentiation observed on CS-glycosylated substrates, as Wnt signaling plays well-established roles in muscle stem cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation [50]. Alterations in the GAG content and sulfation level of the extracellular matrix may influence the equilibrium of Wnt signaling [51,52]. Mechanistically, CS-glycosylated dECM microgels enhance the matrix's ability to bind Wnt factors, restricting their diffusion and thereby increasing the local Wnt concentration in the pericellular space. This activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. The capacity of CS modification to regulate this critical developmental pathway highlights the potential of GAG functionalization as a strategy for directing cell fate in regenerative medicine applications.

CS-glycosylated dECM microgels promote muscle regeneration in VML model of rat

Having established the capacity of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels to enhance myoblast function and activate pro-myogenic signaling pathways in vitro, we next sought to evaluate their therapeutic potential in vivo using a rat VML model. A standardized muscle defect was created in the tibialis anterior muscle of adult Sprague-Dawley rats, and the defect sites were treated with either Sham (normal), PBS (control), HA, ECM microgels, L-CS@ECM microgels, and H-CS@ECM microgels via direct injection (Fig. 6A). Animals were monitored for 8 weeks to assess muscle regeneration, functional recovery, and integration of the biomaterial with host tissue.

Macroscopic examination of the injury sites revealed progressive improvements in tissue appearance across all treatment groups compared to PBS-control and HA groups (Fig. 6B). At day 0 immediately following injury and treatment, all groups exhibited visible muscle defects. By 4 weeks post-treatment, PBS and HA-treated defects remained largely unhealed with visible indentations and lack of tissue continuity. In contrast, microgel-treated groups showed varying degrees of tissue fill and integration, with H-CS@ECM-treated sites displaying the most complete restoration of normal muscle contour. At 8 weeks, the differences became even more pronounced, with H-CS@ECM-treated muscles achieving near-complete restoration of normal tissue architecture, while PBS controls and even ECM microgel-treated sites retained visible defects. Quantitative assessment of muscle mass recovery corroborated the macroscopic observations (Fig. 6C). The muscle mass ratio (injured limb/contralateral normal limb) at 4 weeks showed significant improvements with microgel treatment, with PBS at 0.59 ± 03, HA at 0.60 ± 0.06, ECM at 0.65 ± 0.06, L-CS@ECM at 0.65 ± 0.06, and H-CS@ECM achieving 0.77 ± 0.02 (p < 0.01 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM and L-CS@ECM). By 8 weeks, the mass ratios further improved across all groups, with PBS reaching only 0.69 ± 0.03, HA at 0.70 ± 0.06, ECM at 0.77 ± 0.03, L-CS@ECM at 0.76 ± 0.04, and H-CS@ECM achieving 0.90 ± 0.03 (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). These data demonstrate that CS functionalization substantially enhances the regenerative capacity of dECM microgels in vivo, with high-CS content formulations promoting near-complete restoration of muscle mass.

Histological analysis provided detailed insights into the quality of tissue regeneration. H&E staining revealed distinct differences in tissue architecture across treatment groups at both 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 6D). PBS and HA-treated defects showed persistent tissue gaps filled with disorganized connective tissue and minimal muscle fiber regeneration even at 8 weeks. ECM microgel-treated sites demonstrated improved cellular infiltration and some muscle fiber formation, though with substantial remaining scar tissue. L-CS@ECM microgels promoted more extensive muscle fiber regeneration with better tissue organization. Notably, H-CS@ECM-treated muscles displayed the most complete regeneration, with well-organized muscle fiber bundles showing characteristic peripheral nuclei positioning and cross-striated appearance reminiscent of normal muscle tissue. High-magnification images revealed that H-CS@ECM-treated sites contained abundant centrally nucleated regenerating fibers at 4 weeks, with a portion maturing to peripherally nucleated fibers by 8 weeks, indicating progressive myofiber maturation. Masson's trichrome staining, which distinguishes collagenous scar tissue (blue) from muscle tissue (red), provided assessment of fibrotic tissue deposition (Fig. 6E). PBS and HA-treated defects showed extensive collagen deposition at both time points, with blue-stained fibrotic tissue occupying the majority of the defect area. ECM microgel treatment reduced but did not eliminate fibrosis. CS-glycosylated microgels further decreased collagen deposition, with H-CS@ECM-treated sites showing predominantly red-stained muscle tissue with minimal blue-stained fibrotic regions.

Detailed morphometric analysis of regenerating muscle fibers provided further evidence of superior regenerative outcomes with CS-glycosylated microgels (Fig. 6F–H). The number of regenerating muscle fibers (identified by central nucleation) per field showed progressive increases with CS content (Fig. 6F). At 4 weeks, PBS and HA-treated sites contained only 23.2 ± 4.9 and 23.3 ± 3.3 centrally nucleated fibers per field, while ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM showed 32.3 ± 5.8, 31.2 ± 4.4, and 49.0 ± 6.0 fibers per field, respectively (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). At 8 weeks, fiber counts remained elevated in microgel-treated groups, though with some reduction as fibers matured and nuclei relocated to peripheral positions. Analysis of regenerated muscle fiber diameter revealed that CS-glycosylated microgels promoted formation of larger, more mature fibers (Fig. 6G). At 4 weeks, average fiber diameters were 15.5 ± 4.3 μm for PBS, 15.7 ± 3.8 μm for HA, 20.5 ± 4.4 μm for ECM, 18.8 ± 6.1 μm for L-CS@ECM, and 31.9 ± 6.1 μm for H-CS@ECM (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). By 8 weeks, fiber diameters increased further, with H-CS@ECM achieving 42.2 ± 5.0 μm compared to normal muscle fiber diameters of approximately 45-50 μm, indicating substantial maturation. Fiber cross-sectional area (FCSA) analysis provided a comprehensive assessment of overall muscle fiber development (Fig. 6H). At 4 weeks, FCSA values showed progressive increases from PBS (585.3 ± 77.3 μm2), HA (608.4 ± 59.9 μm2) to ECM (661.3 ± 106.9 μm2), L-CS@ECM (700.5 ± 121.5 μm2), and H-CS@ECM (976.4 ± 141.1 μm2, p < 0.05). By 8 weeks, H-CS@ECM-treated muscles achieved FCSA of 1643.1 ± 120.7 μm2, approaching the values observed in normal muscle tissue (approximately 1600–1800 μm2). These morphometric data collectively demonstrate that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels promote robust muscle fiber regeneration with enhanced fiber size and maturation compared to unmodified ECM scaffolds. Quantification of collagen area were performed (Fig. 6I). At 4 weeks, collagen area percentages were 47.3 ± 2.5% for PBS, 44.5 ± 2.9% for HA, 39.1 ± 7.7% for ECM, 37.2 ± 4.4% for L-CS@ECM, and 29.7 ± 5.5% for H-CS@ECM (p < 0.01 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM; p < 0.05 for H-CS@ECM vs L-CS@ECM). By 8 weeks, the trend continued with PBS at 27.4 ± 5.1%, HA at 25.3 ± 3.4%, ECM at 23.2 ± 2.4%, L-CS@ECM at 25.0 ± 3.7%, and H-CS@ECM at 16.1 ± 2.9% (p < 0.05 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM). These findings indicate that CS functionalization not only promotes muscle regeneration but also actively suppresses fibrotic scar formation.

Together, these in vivo studies establish that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels represent a highly effective therapeutic strategy for volumetric muscle loss. The materials promote extensive muscle fiber regeneration, reduce fibrotic scarring, and facilitate restoration of near-normal tissue architecture. The dose-dependent benefits of CS incorporation suggest that GAG-mediated signaling plays a crucial role in orchestrating the complex cellular events required for successful muscle regeneration.

Notably, CS-glycosylated microgels, particularly H-CS@ECM, exhibited extensive cellular infiltration throughout the microgel mass, with cells penetrating deep into the scaffold and establishing dense networks within the biomaterial. Quantification of infiltrating cells per microsphere confirmed the enhancement of cellular recruitment by CS functionalization (Fig. 7B). ECM microgels recruited 105.5 ± 20.1 cells per microsphere, while L-CS@ECM attracted 114.2 ± 27.2 cells and H-CS@ECM achieved 159.9 ± 31.7 cells per microsphere (p < 0.001 for H-CS@ECM vs ECM). This early burst of cellular infiltration is critical for establishing the cellular foundation necessary for subsequent tissue regeneration and suggests that CS creates a highly chemotactic microenvironment that actively recruits host cells.

This in vivo activation of Wnt signaling at the injury site suggests that CS-mediated signaling effects translate effectively to the complex in vivo environment and may contribute to the observed enhancements in satellite cell activation and myogenic differentiation.

These early time point analyses reveal that CS-glycosylated dECM microgels rapidly establish a pro-regenerative microenvironment in vivo characterized by extensive cellular infiltration, robust satellite cell recruitment and activation, accelerated myogenic differentiation, and sustained Wnt signaling. These early cellular and molecular events likely establish the foundation for the superior long-term regenerative outcomes observed at 4 and 8 weeks.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals functional muscle regeneration after VML injury by CS-glycosylated dECM microgels

To evaluate the function of the CS@ECM microgels in vivo at 8 weeks, we employed RNA-seq analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized to compare gene expression patterns between control and H-CS@ECM groups (Fig. 9A). In PCA plots, the proximity of samples within a group indicates high reproducibility, while distances between groups reveal significant differences in gene expression. The Venn diagram illustrates the distribution of overlapping and unique differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the H-CS@ECM and control groups (Fig. 9B). Volcano plots analysis revealed a total of 1593 DEGs, including 172 genes downregulated and 1421 genes upregulated in H-CS@ECM groups (Fig. 9C).

(D, E) Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes. Next, GO analysis was performed (Fig. 9D). Compared to the control, H-CS@ECM treated muscle showed significant enrichment in GO terms related to cell differentiation, cytoskeleton organization, and cell proliferation. Additional terms associated with cell adhesion (such as focal adhesion, integrin binding, ECM binding and GAG binding) were enriched in the H-CS@ECM group. Notably, Wnt signaling pathway emerged as an enriched biological process, suggesting that CS-mediated enhancement of myogenic differentiation also involve in vivo activation of Wnt signaling. KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed the functions of DEGs (Fig. 9E). The results indicated enrichment of glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate and ECM-receptor interaction in H-CS@ECM group, pathways crucial for cell-matrix interactions in muscle regeneration and myogenic differentiation. Additionally, H-CS@ECM also exhibited enrichment in calcium signaling pathway and cytoskeleton in muscle cells pathway, both of which directly relate to myogenesis and skeletal muscle formation. Furthermore, GSEA analysis demonstrated upregulation of gene sets associated with cytoskeleton in muscle cells, ECM-receptor interaction and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate in H-CS@ECM groups (Fig. 9F–H).

The results of GO analysis, KEGG analysis, and GSEA analysis collectively indicated that H-CS@ECM significantly upregulates genes related to cell adhesion, proliferation and myogenic differentiation, thereby enhancing the efficiency of muscle function repair and promoting muscle regeneration, crucial for the treatment of VML. To validate the in vivo RNA-seq findings at the protein and mRNA level, the expression of downstream target genes and β-catenin protein levels was analyzed. RT-qPCR confirmed that Wnt target genes (AXIN2 and c-MYC) were significantly upregulated in the H-CS@ECM group compared to controls (Fig. S17A and B). Western blot analysis showed correspondingly higher β-catenin protein expression in the H-CS@ECM group (Fig. S17C and D). These in vivo results are consistent with the Wnt activation trends observed in our in vitro C2C12 experiments and corroborate the in vivo β-catenin immunofluorescence data at 1 week. In summary, H-CS@ECM provided an artificial muscle growth microenvironment for VML, particularly by upregulating integrin-mediated cell adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate pathways related to muscle-promoting signals. This upregulation promoted Wnt signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the myogenic differentiation and muscle function in vivo.

Discussion

This study establishes chondroitin sulfate-glycosylated decellularized extracellular matrix microgels as a promising strategy for volumetric muscle loss repair. Through CS glycosylation to muscle-derived dECM, we demonstrate the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and comprehensive functional muscle regeneration encompassing myofiber formation, vascularization, and neural connectivity, while offering the compelling advantages of off-the-shelf availability, minimally invasive delivery, and absence of donor site morbidity. These findings establish a roadmap for next-generation ECM-based therapeutics that harness endogenous regenerative mechanisms through biomimetic restoration of native microenvironmental cues, with implications extending beyond muscle regeneration to diverse applications in regenerative medicine.

The dramatic depletion of glycosaminoglycans during conventional decellularization protocols represents a fundamental but historically underappreciated barrier to functional ECM scaffold performance. Our quantitative analysis confirmed that standard decellularization reduces CS content to approximately 21% of native muscle tissue levels, corroborating findings from prior studies demonstrating GAG losses exceeding 70% across diverse tissue types [22,29,30]. Critically, this depletion is not merely quantitative but profoundly functional—residual GAGs in decellularized scaffolds exhibit severely compromised growth factor binding affinity, as demonstrated by surface plasmon resonance studies showing negligible binding constants for FGF2, HGF, and other morpho genetically critical factors [30]. This biochemical deficiency creates a microenvironment incapable of recruiting satellite cell and activating myogenic differentiation. Our CS glycosylation strategy directly addresses this limitation through covalent conjugation of CS to dECM proteins. This approach not only restores CS content, but also preserves the native collagen architecture and collagen components that characterize muscle-specific ECM. Importantly, this modification occurs without compromising the fundamental advantages of decellularization, including immunogenicity reduction and maintenance of three-dimensional ultrastructure. The microgel format provides additional benefits through enhanced surface area-to-volume ratios that facilitate cellular infiltration and adhesion, modular assembly into larger constructs through endogenous remodeling, and crucially, minimally invasive delivery through standard injection needles—a capability that distinguishes our approach from bulk scaffold implantation strategies requiring open surgical procedures [[53], [54], [55]].

Unmodified dECM solutions at neutral pH and low concentrations typically exhibit weak viscosity and lack the requisite chemical functional groups necessary for rapid photo-crosslinking. To overcome this manufacturing constraint, we strategically introduced GelMA bearing functional methacryloyl groups to undergo free-radical photopolymerization under UV light, thereby serving as a robust "structural backbone". While GelMA offers excellent cell compatibility and essential RGD motifs for initial cell anchorage, extensive literature confirms that pure GelMA scaffolds deficiency the complex, tissue-specific biochemical microenvironment required to orchestrate functional muscle regeneration in severe VML defects [56]. Within this hybrid material platform, GelMA acts strictly as the physical scaffold enabling microfluidic fabrication, whereas the incorporated dECM/CS matrix functions as the biomimetic niche driving biological execution. Consequently, the dose-dependent improvements in regenerative outcomes observed from ECM to L-CS@ECM to H-CS@ECM can logically be attributed to the graded, functional restoration of the CS carbohydrate code.

A central contribution of this work lies in elucidating the molecular mechanisms through which CS glycosylation enhances myogenic differentiation. Our RNA sequencing analysis provides definitive evidence that canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling represents the principal pathway activated by CS-glycosylated dECM. Immunofluorescence confirmed robust nuclear accumulation of β-catenin—the hallmark of active canonical Wnt signaling—in cells cultured on H-CS@ECM. The mechanistic basis for CS-mediated Wnt activation likely involves growth factor sequestration and presentation mechanisms [33,57]. Sulfated GAGs, particularly CS, exhibit high binding affinity for Wnt ligands through electrostatic interactions mediated by their negative charge density. By concentrating Wnt proteins in the pericellular space and restricting their diffusion away from cells, CS establishes localized signaling gradients that amplify pathway activation [51,52]. This mechanism is consistent with our observation of increased expression of β-catenin, AXIN2 (a direct Wnt target gene), and c-MYC in H-CS@ECM cultures. The coordinated upregulation of these canonical Wnt targets provides molecular validation that CS functionalization elicits genuine pathway activation rather than artifact. Critically, our in vivo studies demonstrate that CS-mediated Wnt activation translates effectively to the complex regenerative microenvironment. β-Catenin expression at injury sites increased from 9.7% (ECM) to 14.9% (H-CS@ECM) at 1 week, confirming sustained pathway activation in the presence of native growth factors, inflammatory mediators, and cellular heterogeneity. This in vivo Wnt activation temporally coincides with enhanced satellite cell recruitment and accelerated myogenic differentiation, establishing a mechanistic link between CS-mediated signaling and functional regenerative outcomes. These findings align with the established roles of Wnt signaling in satellite cell biology, where canonical Wnt activation promotes satellite cell proliferation, prevents premature differentiation, and coordinates the transition to committed myoblasts [58]. This mechanistic axis was further consolidated by the pharmacological blockade experiments using XAV939. The complete abrogation of -catenin nuclear translocation and the subsequent impairment of the myogenic program demonstrate that the regenerative efficacy of CS@ECM microgels does not arise from bypass side-effects, but is specifically and causally governed by the canonical Wnt/-catenin signaling cascade.

Furthermore, the 3–4week degradation trajectory of CS@ECM microgels exhibits a precise spatiotemporal match with the endogenous kinetics of functional muscle remodeling. Histological analysis at Week 8 confirmed that the microgels were thoroughly degraded without noticeable residual material, leaving the defect bed fully occupied by nascent myofibers and host ECM. During the initial 1–3 weeks (satellite cell recruitment and expansion), the physical persistence of the scaffold is strictly required to provide topographic guidance and the essential Wnt signaling niche. From Week 4 onward, as myotubes commit to fusion and generate extensive endogenous matrix, the timely biodegradation of the exogenous microgels vacates critical structural space. This evacuation facilitates downstream myofiber hypertrophy, tissue densification, and the cell–cell signaling necessary for neural and vascular maturation. Conversely, prolonged persistence of biomaterials beyond several months frequently precipitates physical geometric constraints, permanent fibrotic encapsulation, and chronic foreign body reactions [59]. Consequently, the synchronized elimination of our microgel platform ensures an unobstructed transition from structural template guidance to autonomous tissue maturation, driving the substantial functional recovery demonstrated at 8 weeks.

Preclinical evaluations of experimental dECM scaffolds typically achieve 60-75% muscle mass recovery in rodent models, with functional force recovery ranging from 50 to 65% of normal tissue [15,17,60]. In comparison, H-CS@ECM achieved 90% mass recovery and 87% functional recovery, representing a substantial advancement over current benchmarks. Recent studies employing growth factor-functionalized scaffolds or cell-seeded constructs have demonstrated promising results, with some achieving 80-97% muscle mass recovery and substantial functional improvements [[61], [62], [63]]. However, these approaches typically require either ex vivo cell culture, which introduces regulatory complexity and manufacturing challenges, or incorporation of recombinant growth factors, which substantially increases cost and raises concerns regarding uncontrolled dosing and off-target effects. Our CS functionalization strategy offers comparable efficacy through a purely acellular, growth factor-free approach that leverages endogenous regenerative mechanisms. Furthermore, macrophage polarization, particularly the transition from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, is recognized as a critical determinant of skeletal muscle regeneration. CS is known to possess immunomodulatory properties, interacting with receptors such as CD44 on macrophages to attenuate pro-inflammatory cytokine production and promote M2 polarization. M2 macrophages secrete pro-regenerative cytokines and growth factors (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF) that help resolve inflammation and support angiogenesis, creating a permissive microenvironment that may work synergistically with Wnt signaling to facilitate satellite cell activation, myoblast migration, and myotube formation. therefore, identifying macrophage–stromal cross-talk will be a direction in this system for future investigation.

The translational feasibility of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels is enhanced by several favorable characteristics. First, the constituent materials have established safety profiles and extensive histories of clinical use in FDA-approved and CE-marked medical devices. Second, the manufacturing process employs standard techniques that are amenable to scale-up and batch consistency requirements for commercial production. Third, the injectable delivery modality represents a transformative improvement in clinical feasibility compared to bulk scaffold implantation. Moreover, the microgel format also offers superior conformability to irregular defect geometries compared to preformed scaffolds, potentially improving material-tissue integration and reducing dead space that promotes hematoma formation. Beyond the specific application to muscle regeneration, this work holds broader applicability across regenerative medicine. The restoration of GAG functionality addresses a fundamental limitation that affects decellularized scaffolds derived from all tissue types—GAG depletion.

Several limitations of the current study warrant acknowledgment and present opportunities for future investigation. First, translation to clinical application will require validation in large animal model. Porcine or ovine VML models represent appropriate next steps for assessing scalability of the approach and for conducting dose-finding studies to establish optimal injection volumes and CS concentrations for larger defects. Second, while we have identified Wnt/β-catenin signaling as a principal mechanistic driver, the complexity of CS-mediated effects likely involves crosstalk between multiple signaling cascades. More detailed mechanistic studies are necessary to dissect the relative contributions of individual pathways and identify potential targets for synergistic enhancement. Third, our work does not capture the structural heterogeneity of native CS populations, which vary in molecular weight, sulfation pattern (4-sulfated versus 6-sulfated), and domain organization. Structure-function studies examining how CS molecular weight and sulfation influence Wnt binding affinity, signaling activation, and regenerative outcomes could enable further optimization of CS formulations.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of dECM

Adult porcine skeletal muscle was purchased from Tianjin Er-shang Yingbin Meat Food Co. Porcine skeletal muscle was cut into small pieces and eluted with 1% SDS for 72 h, and then washed in sterile water for 72 h. DNase and RNase mixture were used to remove DNA and RNA at constant shaking speed of 150 r/min for 10 h at 37 °C. After washing for 48 h, the decellularized samples were freeze-dried and ground into powder using a cryogenic tissue grinder [[64], [65], [66]]. Then, dECM powder was dissolved in 0.01 M HCl solution and digested with pepsin at a ratio of 10:1 for 48h at 37 °C. After digestion, the pH was adjusted to 7.4.

H&E and Masson staining was performed to observe the morphology and DAPI fluorescence staining was used to detect residual cells before and after decellularization. Quantitative assay of DNA, collagen, and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content in dECM was performed. SDS-PAGE was performed to analyze the protein composition of dECM. Details for these experiments were described in Supplementary Materials.

Synthesis and characterization of CS-aldehyde

CS-aldehyde (CS-ald) was synthesized via oxidation with NaIO4. The NaIO4 solution was slowly added dropwise to the chondroitin sulfate solution. This mixture was further incubated in the dark with stirring at 26 °C overnight. Excess ethylene glycol was added to quench the reaction. Then, the solution was dialyzed in DI water for 5 days. CS-ald was obtained by freeze-drying. The aldehyde groups in CS-ald were determined using a hydroxylamine hydrochloride titration assay [67], and detailed experimental procedures were described in Supplementary Materials.

Fabrication and characterization of CS-glycosylated dECM microgels

Based on previous studies, we selected a 5% (w/v) dECM solution for microgel fabrication [45]. Glycosylated-dECM microgels were fabricated using a flow-focusing microfluidic droplet technology. The microfluidic device was operated under the following precise parameters: the continuous phase consisted of mineral oil supplemented with 2% (v/v) Span-80, while the aqueous phase contained 5% (w/v) GelMA, 5% (w/v) dECM, and corresponding concentrations of CS-ald (2.5% (w/v) or 0.25% (w/v)) dissolved in PBS, with 0.25% (w/v) LAP serving as the photoinitiator. Driven by independent pumps, the flow rates were fixed at Qoil = 10 μL/min and Qaq = 2 μL/min. The resulting emulsion droplets were crosslinked via free-radical polymerization under 405 nm visible light illumination. To obtain purified hydrogel microgels, the collected matrices were washed three times with isopropanol and deionized water to thoroughly remove excess oil and additives.

Stereoscopic microscopy (Zeiss, Germany) was used to observe the macroscopic structure of microgels. SEM analysis (FEI Sirion 200, USA) was used to observe the microstructure of microgels. The diameter, pore size and porosity of microgels were measured using Image Pro Plus software. Microgels labeled with rhodamine B were observed using a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Germany).

The component of different microgels was analyzed by FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The contents of CS in different microgels were detected using ELISA kits for CS. Details of mechanical properties, swelling rate, injectability, in vivo degradation, blood compatibility assay of microgels were provided in Supplementary Materials.

Proliferation and differentiation of myoblasts

To evaluate the cell proliferation, C2C12 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were co-cultured with microgels in a low attachment 6-well plates. After culturing for 1, 3 and 5 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 3% BSA in 0.1% PBST. The cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor-labeled phalloidin (Yeasen, China) and DAPI for 30 min. Images were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, LSM900, Germany). The numbers of adherent cells on the microgel were quantified using Image Pro Plus software. Details of CCK-8 assay, Live/Dead staining, and Transwell migration assay were provided in Supplementary Materials.

To evaluate the myogenic differentiation, C2C12 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were co-cultured with microgels on the differentiation medium (containing 2% horse serum). After cultivation for 5 days, the cells-loaded microgels were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 3% BSA. Then the samples were incubated with Desmin rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam, ab15200), MyHC mouse monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab37484), MyOD rabbit polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 18943-1-AP), and β-catenin rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam, ab6302) overnight at 4 °C. To assess the cross-species validity using rat-derived L6 myoblasts, cell-loaded microgels from parallel L6 cultures were fixed, blocked, and incubated with primary antibody against MyHC (Abcam, ab37484) overnight at 4 °C. To evaluate the causal role of Wnt signaling, a pharmacological blockade assay was introduced. C2C12 cells were assigned to four groups: (1) ECM, (2) H-CS@ECM, (3) H-CS@ECM + DMSO (vehicle control), and (4) H-CS@ECM + XAV939 (10 μM, MCE, USA). The medium containing XAV939 or DMSO was replenished every 48 h. After 5 days, the samples were fixed, blocked, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against β-catenin (Abcam, ab6302) and MyHC (Abcam, ab37484). After washing with DPBS, the samples were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h. Then the samples were incubated with DAPI for 5 min and observed with the confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, LSM900, Germany). The percentage of positive cells/area were quantified using Image Pro Plus software. RNA sequencing was used to explore the potential signaling pathways of myogenic differentiation. RT-qPCR and Western blot (including the pharmacological inhibition validation samples) were used to determine the molecular mechanism for myogenic differentiation. Details for these experiments were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Rat VWL model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nankai University, Tianjin, China (2025-SYDWLL-000485), and complied with the requirements of the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Sixty male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (aged 8–10 weeks with the weight range of 300–350 g) were used to construct VML models in this study. All animals were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For all experiments n = 5 rats per group was set for obtaining numerical data.

To prepare the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle VML model, SD rats were randomly assigned to 6 groups: sham, control, HA, ECM, L-CS@ECM, and H-CS@ECM group. Isoflurane gas was used to anesthetize the animals throughout the procedure. The VML injury model were established according the previous research [68]. The skin of rats’ right legs was incised, and the fascia and muscle were separated to expose the tibialis anterior muscle. A 10 mm × 6 mm × 4 mm muscle defect was resected in the tibialis anterior using a scalpel, accounting for a volumetric loss of over 40% of the tibialis anterior. The HA group was injected with a PBS solution containing 1 wt% HA. The therapeutic microgel suspension was prepared by mixing PBS solution of 1 wt% HA with microgels in a volume ratio of 1:1. Subsequently, approximately 200 μL of microgel suspension (20 mg dry weight each) was injected into the defect site. After injection, the clinical 3 M breathable patch was fixed to the defect site using 9-0 sutures. The skin was closed with 3-0 monofilament nylon sutures. Finally, rats were euthanized at 1, 4 and 8 weeks, and the regenerated muscle was obtained for H&E, Masson, and immunofluorescence staining analysis to assess the muscle regeneration. Electrophysiology, laser doppler perfusion imaging, WB assay, and RNA sequencing were performed to evaluate functional recovery of regenerated muscle at 8 weeks. Detailed experimental procedures were shown in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least three times independently. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism software was used for statistical analysis. Single comparisons were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's post hoc test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Multivariant comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. For all tests, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗P < 0.01 indicate statistical significance, ns: no significance.
