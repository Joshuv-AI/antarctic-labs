Scalable Bi-causal Optimal Transport via KL Relaxation and Policy Gradients The authors thank Jose Blanchent, Yinbin Han, Wenhao Yang, and other participants at the Stanford Student OR Seminar for helpful comments and feedback.
Haoyang Cao, Jesse Hoekstra, Renyuan Xu, Yumin Xu, and Ruixun Zhang

Abstract




Abstract

Bi-causal optimal transport (OT) is a natural framework for comparing and coupling stochastic processes under nonanticipative information constraints, with important applications in robust finance, sequential uncertainty quantification, and multistage stochastic optimization.
In particular, a learned bi-causal coupling naturally serves as a simulator for generating joint sample paths that respect both prescribed marginal laws and the underlying information flow.
Its practical use, however, is limited by the computational difficulty of enforcing bi-causal coupling constraints over path space, especially for continuous distributions and long horizons. We develop a scalable stochastic-optimization framework for computing bi-causal OT couplings under general marginals. Our approach introduces a Kullback–Leibler (KL)-penalized relaxation that replaces hard marginal constraints with tractable divergence penalties while preserving the recursive structure of the problem. We establish dynamic programming principles for both the original and relaxed formulations, prove that the relaxed problem converges to the original bi-causal OT problem as the penalty grows, and derive explicit policy-gradient representations for the relaxed objective. Building on these results, we propose a practical policy-gradient algorithm with unbiased mini-batch estimators, variance reduction, and nonasymptotic regret guarantees. Numerical experiments show that the method accurately captures marginal laws and temporal dependence, and performs well in applications including robust subhedging and time series statistical downscaling. These results provide a scalable computational approach to bi-causal OT and broaden its applicability in settings where nonanticipative information constraints are essential.

Keywords
: Bi-causal optimal transport; Policy gradient; Kullback–Leibler regularization; Dynamic programming; Sequential modeling

Introduction

Bi-causal optimal transport (OT), also known as adapted transport, extends classical OT to sequential settings by restricting admissible couplings to be
non-anticipative
with respect to the natural filtration of two stochastic processes
(
48
,
67
,
11
,
10
)
. Concretely, given two discrete-time processes, a coupling is bi-causal if, at every time step
n
, the conditional law of each process’s next state depends only on the joint history up to time
n
and coincides with its own prescribed transition—so neither process can exploit or anticipate the other’s future.
The bi-causal OT problem then seeks the coupling in this restricted set that minimizes a cumulative expected cost, yielding a distance between stochastic processes that respects their temporal information structure.

This information-aware structure makes bi-causal OT a natural framework for learning couplings between stochastic processes under non-anticipative constraints, with broad applications in operations research and quantitative finance. Moreover, once computed, a bi-causal coupling also serves as a
principled simulator
for generating joint sample paths that are consistent with prescribed marginals and information flows. Among these applications, two settings particularly highlight the need for scalable bi-causal transport: robust hedging and time series statistical downscaling. In robust hedging, pricing bounds are defined through worst-case expectations over models that must be consistent with prescribed marginals and adapted to the market filtration
(
23
,
5
,
65
,
54
)
, so the coupling must respect the underlying information flow; at the same time, the learned coupling provides a simulator for generating dynamically consistent stress scenarios. In statistical downscaling, one seeks to transform low-fidelity time series into high-fidelity ones while preserving temporal dependence, where naive marginal matching can lead to dynamically inconsistent samples
(
83
,
58
)
; here, the bi-causal coupling similarly acts as a simulator for generating temporally consistent high-fidelity trajectories. More broadly, any sequential modeling or uncertainty quantification task in which the underlying information flow must be explicitly respected
(
26
,
67
)
can benefit from the bi-causal OT framework
(
50
,
47
)
.

The main obstacle to the broader adoption of bi-causal OT is
computational scalability
. Existing methods are most effective either for discrete distributions or for empirical samples from continuous laws at small scale, where one can exploit Sinkhorn-type iterations via entropic regularization or linear-programming formulations
(
68
,
34
)
. However, the resulting computational and memory costs typically increase rapidly with the sample size, time horizon, and state dimension. For continuous distributions, the recently proposed fitted value-iteration methods based on dynamic programming provide an important alternative
(
13
)
. Their computational cost, however, can remain substantial, since the bi-causal constraint must be enforced at each iteration, often via an additional inner iterative procedure. In addition, the sample complexity of their method suffers from the curse of dimensionality. These challenges become even more pronounced when bi-causal OT must be repeatedly evaluated within a larger optimization or statistical pipeline
(
5
,
11
,
36
)
. As a result, scalable computational methods for bi-causal OT under general marginals remain limited.

Our work and contributions.

This paper addresses the above gap by developing an efficient end-to-end stochastic-optimization framework for computing bi-causal OT couplings with general marginals, including continuous ones. The central difficulty is that bi-causal OT imposes hard sequential marginal constraints on a path-space coupling, so the coupling must respect the underlying information structure, even though direct optimization over the resulting global feasible set is intractable. Our key idea is to replace the hard marginal-matching constraints, which render the problem combinatorially difficult, with a Kullback–Leibler (KL)-penalized formulation that remains differentiable, computationally tractable, and amenable to policy-gradient methods, while preserving the recursive structure needed for computation.

The resulting framework learns joint laws that capture both marginal dynamics and temporal information flow. Such couplings can be used to generate dynamically consistent trajectories, and in some cases admit decision-theoretic interpretations, such as in robust hedging. Our primary focus, however, is on the learning and computation of the coupling itself. For clarity, we focus on the Markov setting, although our analysis extends to more general adapted marginals. Our main contributions are as follows.

Relaxation theory, well-posedness, and asymptotic consistency.

We establish the dynamic programming principle for both the original bi-causal OT problem and the KL-relaxed problem (Lemmas
2
and
3
), providing the recursive characterization of the coupling and value functions underlying our algorithmic design. We further show that, as the penalty parameter tends to infinity, both the relaxed objective and the corresponding optimal couplings converge to their counterparts in the original bi-causal OT problem (Theorem
1
). This result establishes that the relaxed formulation is not merely computationally convenient, but a principled surrogate for the constrained problem.

Scalable policy-gradient algorithm with convergence guarantees.

Building on a Markov parameterization of bi-causal couplings, we derive explicit policy-gradient representations (Theorem
2
), construct unbiased gradient estimators with asymptotic variance reduction (Lemma
4
), and obtain a practical algorithm that can be implemented with expressive conditional models such as normalizing flows (Algorithm
4.3
). A key advantage of our policy-gradient formulation is that its sampling-based implementation is
dimension-free
, in the sense that it does not require discretization over the state or action spaces. In addition, we establish a nonasymptotic convergence guarantee showing that the average regret is of order
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
, where
K
denotes the number of episodes (Theorem
3
). Taken together, these results turn the recursive structure of bi-causal OT into an efficient computational procedure that scales beyond discrete settings.

Numerical validation and applications.

We evaluate the performance of our method across several experiments. In controlled synthetic settings, the proposed algorithm performs well in capturing both marginal fidelity in multimodal distributions and temporal dependence in high-dimensional and long-horizon problems (Section
19
). We then demonstrate its practical value in two representative applications: robust subhedging under adaptedness constraints, where the method recovers near-optimal pricing bounds with relative errors below
1\%
(Section
42
), and time series statistical downscaling, where OT-debiased conditioning substantially outperforms unconditional generation (Section
66
). In addition, our implementation is computationally faster than existing approaches in the literature, further supporting the practical scalability of the proposed framework.
Our code is publicly available on GitHub and all experimental results can be replicated.

2
Bi-causal optimal transport of Markov sequences

In this section, we introduce the mathematical setting for the bi-causal OT problem between Markov sequences. We first revisit the definitions of both couplings and bi-causal couplings, and introduce equivalent Markov bi-causal couplings. Then, we establish the dynamic programming principle for the bi-causal OT problem.

2.1
Set-up and notation

Sequential data and their distributions.

We consider
d
-dimensional time series data with length
N+1
. Formally, let
S=\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a state space with Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{B}(S)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}=S^{N+1}
be the set of paths on
S
with length
N+1
, and its Borel
\sigma
-algebra is
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})=\mathcal{S}^{\otimes(N+1)}
. To model two coupled time series
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
, we have the product space
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
and its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})
. Throughout the paper, we write
[N]:=\{1,\dots,N\}
and
[N]_{0}:=\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. For any
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{N})\in\mathcal{Y}
and
n\in[N]_{0}
, we write the prefix
\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})
and the suffix

\boldsymbol{y}_{n:N}=(y_{n},\dots,y_{N})
. We define
\mu\otimes\mu^{\prime}
as the product coupling of two independent marginal laws
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\mathcal{P}(S)
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y})
be the sets of probability distributions over the state space
S
, the path space
\mathcal{Y}
and the product space
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. For any path law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim\mu
, given any
n\in[N]
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n}\in S^{\otimes(n+1)}
, its one-step conditional kernel at step
n
,
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1})
, is given by

\mu(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1})=\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n}\in A\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{Y}_{0:n-1}=\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1}\big),\qquad\forall\,A\in\mathcal{S}.

(1)

and its marginal law is
\mu_{n}(B)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in B)
for any
B\in\mathcal{S}
. Moreover, if
\mu
is Markov with initial law
\mu_{0}
and transitions
\boldsymbol{T}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{N})
, where
T_{n}(\cdot\,|\,y)\in\mathcal{P}(S)
for any
n\in[N]
and
y\in S
, then

\mu({\rm d}\boldsymbol{y})=\mu_{0}({\rm d}y_{0})\prod_{n=1}^{N}T_{n}({\rm d}y_{n}\,|\,y_{n-1}),

and (
1
) reduces to
\mu(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1})=T_{n}(A\,|\,y_{n-1})
.

Couplings and bi-causality.

To model two coupled time series—such as two price processes, or a real sample path and a synthetic sample path—we introduce the notion of bi-causal couplings. They describe two stochastic processes that are non-anticipative in both directions, so neither process is allowed to use the other’s future when coupled.

In particular, we consider two distributions
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, and a probability distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y})
is said to be a coupling of
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
if
\pi
satisfies

\displaystyle\pi(A\times\mathcal{Y})=\mu(A),\quad\pi(\mathcal{Y}\times B)=\mu^{\prime}(B),\qquad\forall\,A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}).

(2)

Denote the set of all possible couplings of
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
by
\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
. For
(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
n\in[N]
, we define one-step
marginal
conditionals as

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{(1)}(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\big(Y_{n}\in A\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{Y}_{0:n-1}=\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1}\big),

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{(2)}(B\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\big(Y_{n}^{\prime}\in B\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{Y}_{0:n-1}=\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1}\big),

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{S}
. Throughout the paper, when there is no confusion, we denote
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi}[\cdot]
.

Furthermore, define the set of
bi-causal couplings
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\Big\{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{(1)}(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1})=\mu(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1})\textrm{ and }

(3)

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{(2)}(B\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1})=\mu^{\prime}(B\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1}),\forall A,B\in\mathcal{S},n\in[N]\Big\}.

Bi-causal couplings are central to our framework: they preserve the prescribed marginal dynamics of both processes while allowing dependence only through the joint past, and therefore exclude any use of the other process’s future. This non-anticipative structure makes the problem recursive, leading to the Markov kernel representation in Lemma
1
, the dynamic programming principle in Lemma
2
, and the subsequent relaxed formulation and algorithmic developments in Sections
3
–
4
.

In addition, we assume that
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
are Markov with transitions
\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{T}^{\prime}
, respectively. In this case,
\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
in (
3
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{(1)}(A\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1}^{\prime})=T_{n}(A\,|\,y_{n-1}),\quad\pi_{n}^{(2)}(B\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1}^{\prime})=T_{n}^{\prime}(B\,|\,y_{n-1}^{\prime}),

(4)

for any
A,B\in\mathcal{S}
,
n\in[N]
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{0:n-1}^{\prime}\in S^{\otimes n}
.

Remark 1
(Beyond the Markov setting)

The Markov setting in (
4
) is imposed purely for technical and notational convenience and is not intrinsic to the framework. More generally, one may allow the coupling kernels at time
n
to depend on the full past histories
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{0:n-1},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}_{0:n-1})
, rather than only on the current states
(Y_{n-1},Y^{\prime}_{n-1})
. The extension is conceptually straightforward: one simply replaces current-state-dependent kernels with history-dependent kernels, at the cost of additional notation but without any substantial new technical difficulty.

Bi-causal optimal transport problem.

Given admissible bi-causal couplings (
3
) and a sequence of bounded cost functions
c_{n}:S\times S\to[0,c]\subset\mathbb{R}
,
n\in[N]_{0}
, our goal is to find a coupling
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
that minimizes the expected cumulative discrepancy between the two time series while respecting the non-anticipative information structure:

W_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\right].

(5)

Here, the cost function
c_{n}(y_{n},y_{n}^{\prime})
measures the mismatch between two time series
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}
at step
n
.

One-step Markov joint kernels.

For Markov data, the above bi-causal couplings (
3
) can be equivalently characterized by their one-step transition kernels, which reduces the global pathwise constraint to a local one-step description and thereby makes the analysis tractable.

In particular, we fix any pair of Markov path laws
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi
with
\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
, and define the one-step Markov joint kernel at each time
n\in[N]
as

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\pi}(A\times B\,|\,y,y^{\prime}):=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\big(Y_{n}\in A,Y^{\prime}_{n}\in B\,\big|\,Y_{n-1}=y,Y^{\prime}_{n-1}=y^{\prime}\big),\ \forall A,B\in\mathcal{S},\forall y,y^{\prime}\in S.

(6)

In addition, denote the first and second marginal distributions of
Q_{n}^{\pi}(A\times B\,|\,y,y^{\prime})
in (
6
) by
Q_{n}^{\pi,(1)}(A\,|\,y,y^{\prime})
and
Q_{n}^{\pi,(2)}(B\,|\,y,y^{\prime})
, respectively.

In the Markov setting, define the collection of one-step bi-causal coupling kernels between
T_{n}
and
T_{n}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n}):=\Big\{Q:S\times S\to\mathcal{P}(S\times S):\

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}(A\,|\,y,y^{\prime})=Q(A\times\mathcal{Y}|y,y^{\prime})=T_{n}(A\,|\,y),

(7)

\displaystyle\hskip-80.0ptQ^{(2)}(B\,|\,y,y^{\prime})=Q(\mathcal{Y}\times B\,|\,y,y^{\prime})=T^{\prime}_{n}(B\,|\,y^{\prime}),\ \forall A,B\in\mathcal{S},\forall y,y^{\prime}\in S\Big\}.

Given any joint distribution
\pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(S\times S)
and kernels
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N})
with
Q_{n}:S\times S\to P(S\times S)
for
n\in[N]
, we can define the corresponding Markov coupling
\pi_{\pi_{0},\boldsymbol{Q}}
as

\displaystyle\pi_{\pi_{0},\boldsymbol{Q}}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{y},{\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}):=\pi_{0}({\rm d}y_{0},{\rm d}y_{0}^{\prime})\prod_{n=1}^{N}Q_{n}({\rm d}y_{n},{\rm d}y_{n}^{\prime}\,|\,y_{n-1},y_{n-1}^{\prime}).

(8)

Using (
7
) and (
8
), we denote the collection of Markov bi-causal couplings as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\Big\{\pi_{\pi_{0},\boldsymbol{Q}}:\ \pi_{0}\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\prime}),\ Q_{n}\in\Pi_{M}(T_{n},T_{n}^{\prime}),\ \forall n\in[N]\Big\}.

(9)

Then we establish the following equivalent characterizations of Markov bi-causal couplings.

Lemma 1
(Equivalent characterization of Markov bi-causal couplings)

Given that both
\mu
,
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
are Markov, the set of Markov bi-causal couplings defined in (
9
) coincides with the set of all bi-causal couplings, that is,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

2.2
Dynamic programming principle

The dynamic programming principle provides a recursive characterization of the coupling and enables us to solve the bi-causal OT problem (
5
) in the same spirit as sequential decision-making. It serves as the foundation for our relaxed formulation in Section
3
and the policy-gradient method in Section
4
.

For any
\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and any
y,y^{\prime}\in S
, define the terminal condition as

J_{N}^{\pi}(y,y^{\prime})=V_{N}(y,y^{\prime})=c_{N}(y,y^{\prime}).

(10)

For all
n\in[N-1]_{0}
, we denote the objective function at time
n
by

J_{n}^{\pi}(y,y^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=n}^{N}c_{k}(Y_{k},Y_{k}^{\prime})\,\big|\,Y_{n}=y,Y_{n}^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\right]=c_{n}(y,y^{\prime})+Q^{\pi}_{n+1}[J_{n+1}^{\pi}](y,y^{\prime}),

(11)

which is the expected remaining cumulative cost from time
n
onward. Here, for any kernel
Q:S\times S\to\mathcal{P}(S\times S)
, any bounded function
v:S\times S\to\mathbb{R}
and any
y,y^{\prime}\in S
, we define the operator

Q[v](y,y^{\prime})=\int v(z,z^{\prime})\,Q({\rm d}z,{\rm d}z^{\prime}\,|\,y,y^{\prime}),

(12)

which measures the future expectation of
v
from time
n
onward given the current state
(y,y^{\prime})
.

Next, we minimize the objective function
J_{n}^{\pi}
in (
11
) over the admissible bi-causal couplings
\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
to obtain the corresponding value function
V_{n}
, which is defined as

V_{n}(y,y^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}J_{n}^{\pi}(y,y^{\prime}).

(13)

For any
n\in[N-1]_{0}
, given
(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi
, denote the conditional law from time
n+1
to
N
by

\pi_{(n+1):N}(y,y^{\prime})={\rm Law}\Big(\boldsymbol{Y}_{(n+1):N},\boldsymbol{Y}_{(n+1):N}^{\prime}\,\Big|\,Y_{n}=y,Y_{n}^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\Big),\quad\forall y,y^{\prime}\in S.

(14)

Then,
J^{\pi}_{n}
in (
11
) is equivalent to the following expression,

J_{n}^{\pi}(y,y^{\prime})=c_{n}(y,y^{\prime})+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{(n+1):N}(y,y^{\prime})}\left[\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}c_{k}(Y_{k},Y_{k}^{\prime})\right].

Here, for notational simplicity, we abbreviate
\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{Y}_{(n+1):N},\boldsymbol{Y}_{(n+1):N}^{\prime})\sim\pi_{(n+1):N}(y,y^{\prime})}[\cdot]
as
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{(n+1):N}(y,y^{\prime})}[\cdot]
. For any bounded function
v:S\times S\to\mathbb{R}
, define the Bellman operator and Bellman optimality operator as follows,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{Q}_{n}[v]

\displaystyle=c_{n}+Q[v],\quad\forall Q\in\Pi_{M}(T_{n+1},T_{n+1}^{\prime});

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}[v]

\displaystyle=c_{n}+\inf_{Q\in\Pi_{M}(T_{n+1},T^{\prime}_{n+1})}Q[v].

(15)

We have the following dynamic programming principle for (
5
), which characterizes the optimal solution to the bi-causal OT problem.

Lemma 2
(Dynamic programming principle)

The following statements hold.

1.

For any
n\in[N-1]_{0}
,
V_{n}=\Gamma_{n}[V_{n+1}]
.

2.

W_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\underset{\pi_{0}\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\prime})}{\inf}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}[V_{0}(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})]=\underset{\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}{\inf}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\right]
.

The above lemma shows that the bi-causal OT problem (
5
) can be viewed as a
Markov decision process
with coupling constraints. This result implies that, to solve the bi-causal OT problem (
5
), we only need to optimize recursively over the initial coupling and the one-step Markov coupling kernels instead of the entire path-space set. This reduction in complexity is what makes the relaxed formulation in Section
3
and the policy gradient method in Section
4
tractable.

3
Transport-relaxed problem

To tackle the computational challenges induced by the coupling constraints in Lemma
2
, we introduce a relaxed formulation of the bi-causal OT problem (
5
) in this section. Specifically, we employ a KL penalization to regulate violations of the coupling constraints while preserving the dynamic programming principle.

3.1
Relaxed formulation

The original bi-causal OT problem (
5
) requires every one-step coupling kernel to match the prescribed marginal transitions exactly, which poses the main computational difficulty. We therefore enlarge the feasible set by allowing arbitrary joint transition kernels and then penalize deviations from the prescribed marginals and transitions. Let us first consider the following set of general transition kernels
Q
without marginal constraints,

\mathcal{U}_{\textrm{rel}}:=\Big\{Q:S\times S\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(S\times S)\Big\}.

Define the relaxed policy space over the entire horizon as

\begin{split}\Pi_{\textrm{rel}}=\biggl\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y})\,\,\biggl|\,\,&\exists\pi_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(S\times S),\,\exists Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N}\in\mathcal{U}_{\textrm{rel}}\text{ s.t. }\forall\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{B}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\bigotimes(N+1)},\\
&\pi(\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B}^{\prime})=\int_{\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B}^{\prime}}\pi_{0}({\rm d}y_{0},{\rm d}y_{0}^{\prime})\prod_{n=1}^{N}Q_{n}({\rm d}y_{n},{\rm d}y_{n}^{\prime}\,|\,y_{n-1},y_{n-1}^{\prime})\biggl\},\end{split}

(T_{n},T_{n}^{\prime})

For any
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm rel}
, define the marginals of

as

\pi^{(1)}_{0}(A)=\pi_{0}(A\times\mathcal{S}),\,\,\pi^{(2)}_{0}(B)=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S}\times B),\quad\textrm{ for all }A,B\in\mathcal{S}.

KL penalization.

Under the set of relaxed couplings
\Pi_{\textrm{rel}}
in (

), we use KL-divergence to penalize violations of the original bi-causal constraints. For any
\beta>0
, we consider the relaxed coupling problem

\displaystyle W_{\textrm{rel}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\textrm{rel}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\biggl[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})+\beta\Big(\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0},\pi_{0}^{(1)})+\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}^{\prime},\pi_{0}^{(2)})\Big)

(17)

\displaystyle+\beta\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu\otimes\mu^{\prime}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(T_{n}(\cdot\,|\,Y_{n-1}),Q_{n}^{\pi,(1)}(\cdot\,|\,Y_{n-1},Y_{n-1}^{\prime})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle+\beta\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu\otimes\mu^{\prime}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(T_{n}^{\prime}(\cdot\,|\,Y_{n-1}^{\prime}),Q_{n}^{\pi,(2)}(\cdot\,|\,Y_{n-1},Y_{n-1}^{\prime})\big)\Big]\biggl],

where the first two KL terms penalize deviations of the initial marginals, and the remaining terms penalize deviations of the one-step transition marginals over the entire horizon.

Moreover, the chosen orientation enforces absolute continuity of the reference with respect to the candidate, assigning an infinite penalty under support mismatch.
As a result, the relaxation is mass-covering and provides a stable surrogate for enforcing the bi-causal constraints

Lemma 3
(Dynamic programming principle of the relaxed problem)

The following statements are true:

1.

For any

3.2
\Gamma
-convergence: recovering the original bi-causal optimal transport

Having introduced the relaxed problem above, a fundamental question is whether the original bi-causal OT problem can be recovered in the limit as the penalty parameter
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. Addressing this question is essential for validating the relaxation, as it ensures that the penalized formulation is not only computationally tractable but also asymptotically consistent with the original constrained problem. In what follows, we show that, as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, both the relaxed bi-causal OT coupling and the value in (
17
) converge to their respective counterparts in the original problem (
5
). To establish this convergence result, we first introduce the following standard assumption in OT.

Assumption 1

For each
n\in[N]_{0}
, the cost function
c_{n}(y,y^{\prime})
is nonnegative and lower semi-continuous.
Moreover, the original problem (
5
) has finite infimum, i.e.,

{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}\Big)\,d\pi<\infty.

In Assumption
1
, nonnegativity and lower semi-continuity ensure weak stability of the transport objective under weak convergence
(
82
,
73
)
; the finiteness condition rules out degenerate cases where the bi-causal constraint renders the value
+\infty
, so the limit problem remains well-defined and nontrivial
(
71
,
82
)
.

We define the closures of
\Pi_{\rm rel}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\rm bc}
under the weak convergence of measures (
\rightharpoonup
) by
\overline{\Pi}_{\textrm{rel}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\textrm{bc}}
, respectively.
For notational simplicity, we denote the summation of the expectation of KL divergences by

\mathrm{KL}^{(1)}\big(\mu,\pi\big):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{KL}_{n}^{\!(1)}\big(\mu,\pi\big),\quad\mathrm{KL}^{(2)}\big(\mu^{\prime},\pi\big):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{KL}_{n}^{\!(2)}\big(\mu^{\prime},\pi\big).

Furthermore, we introduce two functionals: the limiting constrained functional

that enforces the bi-causal constraints through indicator terms, and the penalized functional
F_{\beta}
that replaces these hard constraints by KL penalties.

\displaystyle F(\pi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\right]+\iota\Big(\mathrm{KL}^{(1)}\big(\mu,\pi\big)\Big)+\iota\Big(\mathrm{KL}^{(2)}\big(\mu^{\prime},\pi\big)\Big),

(26)

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(\pi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})+\beta\mathrm{KL}^{(1)}\big(\mu,\pi\big)+\beta\mathrm{KL}^{(2)}\big(\mu^{\prime},\pi\big)\right],

(27)

where

if

\{\pi^{\beta}\}_{\beta>0}\subset\overline{\Pi}_{\mathrm{rel}}

as
\beta\to+\infty

(28)

Then we are ready to state the
\Gamma
-convergence in the following theorem, which captures the variational convergence of the penalized functionals to the constrained problem, guaranteeing convergence of both infimum values and minimizers.

Theorem 1
(
\Gamma
-convergence)

Under Assumption
1
, the following two claims hold.

(i)

Infimum values under relaxation converge to the optimal value of the original problem in (
5
): as
\beta\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\overline{\Pi}_{\mathrm{rel}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})+\beta\mathrm{KL}^{(1)}\big(\mu,\pi\big)+\beta\mathrm{KL}^{(2)}\big(\mu^{\prime},\pi\big)\right]\longrightarrow\min_{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\right].

(ii)

Every weak limit point of any sequence of
\epsilon_{\beta}
-almost minimizers
\{\pi^{\beta}\}_{\beta>0}
as
\beta\to+\infty
, denoted by
\pi^{*}
, is the optimal solution of the original problem in (
5
) satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\Big[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\Big]=\min_{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\prime})\right].

Here, under the notion of weak convergence, no uniqueness of minimizers is required, either for the relaxed
problems or for the original problem.

Remark 2
(Proof sketch)

The main technical challenge is to show that a KL-penalized problem posed on a strictly larger coupling class still recovers the original bi-causal constraint in the limit. This step is delicate because the penalty must enforce feasibility while preserving compactness under weak convergence. Meanwhile, it is necessary to establish not only convergence of the penalized values, but also convergence of almost minimizers to the feasible Markov bi-causal set.

We address these difficulties through a three-step
\Gamma
-convergence argument. First, lower semicontinuity of the transport cost and the KL terms yields the
\Gamma
-liminf bound. Second, we construct recovery sequences on the feasible set to give the
\Gamma
-limsup bound. Finally, we prove that KL control yields asymptotic marginal consistency and tightness of almost minimizers. These properties imply that every weak limit point is feasible and optimal.

Theorem
1
establishes the asymptotic consistency of the relaxed bi-causal OT problem (
17
) with the original constrained problem (
5
). In particular, as
\beta\to\infty
, minimizers of the penalized objective (
17
) recover both the optimal value and the optimal coupling of the original problem (
5
).

\theta_{n}
and express it only through the remaining cost from time
n
onward. We address this point through the likelihood-ratio identity and the Markov factorization of the parameterized coupling.

(2)

For the KL component, the difficulty is that the penalty is defined through marginal kernels under the reference measure rather than under the current coupling. We handle this problem by differentiating under the reference measure and then representing the resulting marginal-score terms as score expectations through the Radon–Nikodym representation.

The proof therefore has two parts. We first derive the gradient of the value term by applying the likelihood-ratio identity and then using the Markov structure to reduce the weight to the continuation cost from time
n
onward. We next derive the gradient of the KL term under the reference dynamics and represent it as the expectation of marginal scores. Combining these two identities yields the decomposition in Theorem
2
.

Theorem
2
has several implications.
First, consistent with the decomposition of
J_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in (
30
), Theorem
2
shows that the gradient
\nabla J_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
also splits into two structurally distinct components:

(1)

\nabla J_{\mathrm{val}}
, which is an expectation under the current parameterized coupling weighted by the remaining cumulative cost; and

(2)

\nabla J_{\mathrm{KL}}
, which is an expectation under the prescribed marginal dynamics and captures the KL-penalty term.

This decomposition is central to the estimator construction in Section
4
.

Second, the representation in Theorem
2
expresses the gradient entirely in terms of the Stein’s score functions of the parameterized kernels. In particular, the gradient takes the form of weighted log-derivatives, with weights given by the cumulative future cost. This structure enables unbiased Monte Carlo estimation of the gradient using only samples from
\pi^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, without requiring differentiation through the underlying dynamics. As a result, the method is particularly attractive in high-dimensional settings, where explicit density evaluation or backpropagation through the full trajectory is computationally expensive.

Third, in terms of computational benefits, the KL contribution admits a particularly simple form: it is given by an expectation under the reference marginals and transition kernels, and hence can be computed without sampling from the current policy. This makes the regularization term particularly tractable to implement, while also clarifying the role of
\beta
in controlling the strength of the relaxation.

Finally, the above identity is closely related to the classical likelihood-ratio policy gradient theorem in reinforcement learning. In this correspondence, the cumulative future cost plays the role of the return-to-go, while the KL penalization is analogous to regularization terms commonly used in entropy-regularized or trust-region-based policy optimization.

4.2
Mini-batch gradient estimators and variance reduction

Theorem
2
gives an exact gradient formula for the relaxed objective. For practical implementation, however, this gradient must be estimated from samples. This subsection constructs unbiased mini-batch estimators for the value and KL terms, and introduces control variates for variance reduction.

More specifically, evaluating
\nabla J_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
relies on two sources of randomness: independent trajectories
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim\mu
and
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}\sim\mu^{\prime}
from the reference marginal distributions, and generated trajectories
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}\mkern-1.0mu^{\prime})\sim\pi^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
from the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-parameterized joint coupling. This sampling structure underlies both the estimator construction below and the implementation of Algorithm
4.3
in Section

.

To describe the implementation in detail, we introduce the iteration index
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the mini-batch size
B\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and the sample index
b\in[B]
within each mini-batch. At iteration
k
, given the parameter

B
i.i.d. trajectories

and
\{(\boldsymbol{Y}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime(b)})\}_{b=1}^{B}\sim\mu\otimes\mu^{\prime}
. To construct the control variates later, we also draw an auxiliary mini-batch of
B
i.i.d. generated trajectories

\{(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(b)},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{\prime(b)})\}_{b=1}^{B}\sim\pi^{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}},

independent of the batch
\{(\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(b)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{\prime(b)})\}_{b=1}^{B}
. Motivated by the gradient decomposition of
J_{\beta}
in (

) evaluated at

Per–trajectory statistics.

To mirror the gradient decomposition in Theorem
2
, we record three per-trajectory quantities: a remaining cost term
\widehat{V}_{k,n}^{(b)}
, a generated-sample score term
\widehat{S}_{k,n}^{(b)}
for the value component, and a reference-sample score term
\widehat{K}_{k,n}^{(b)}
for the KL component.
Specifically, for any
n\in[N]_{0}
and each trajectory index
b\in[B]
, to estimate the remaining cumulative cost in the value part
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}J_{\mathrm{val}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
, we denote the “per-trajectory cost” by

\widehat{V}_{k,n}^{(b)}:=\sum_{j=n}^{N}c_{j}\big(\widehat{Y}_{j}^{(b)},\widehat{Y}_{j}^{\prime(b)}\big).

(38)

Then, we define the “per-trajectory joint score” of the parameterized coupling evaluated on the generated trajectory
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(b)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{\prime(b)})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{k,n}^{(b)}

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\theta_{k,n}}\log q_{n}^{\theta_{k,n}}\big(\widehat{Y}_{n}^{(b)},\widehat{Y}_{n}^{\prime(b)}\,\big|\,\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{(b)},\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime(b)}\big).

(39)

Finally, for the KL-gradient term
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}J_{\mathrm{KL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
, we define the “per-trajectory marginal score” evaluated on the training data
(\boldsymbol{Y}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime(b)})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{k,n}^{(b)}

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\theta_{k,n}}\log q_{n}^{\theta_{k,n},(1)}\big(Y_{n}^{(b)}\,\big|\,Y_{n-1}^{(b)},Y_{n-1}^{\prime(b)}\big)+\nabla_{\theta_{k,n}}\log q_{n}^{\theta_{k,n},(2)}\big(Y_{n}^{\prime(b)}\,\big|\,Y_{n-1}^{(b)},Y_{n-1}^{\prime(b)}\big).

(40)

Mini-batch gradient estimators.

Using the per–trajectory statistics in (
39
)–(
40
), for each

and
\nabla_{\theta_{k,n}}J_{\textrm{KL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{val},n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{V}_{k,n}^{(b)}\,\widehat{S}_{k,n}^{(b)},\quad\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{KL},n}:=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{K}_{k,n}^{(b)},

(41)

and aggregate estimators as

\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{val}}:=\big(\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{val},0},\dots,\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{val},N}\big),\quad\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{KL}}:=\big(\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{KL},0},\dots,\widehat{G}_{k,\mathrm{KL},N}\big)

Here,

Control variates.

To reduce the variance of the estimators without introducing bias, we employ state–dependent control variates for the value component
(

,
42
)
. We consider linear control variates, which are standard and effective
(

)

Mathematically, for any

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{k,n}\in\arg\min_{w}\ \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Big\|\big(\widetilde{V}_{k,n}^{(b)}-w^{\top}\phi_{k,n}(\widetilde{Y}_{n-1}^{(b)},\widetilde{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime(b)})\big)\widetilde{S}_{k,n}^{(b)}\Big\|^{2},

where

Remark 4
(Proof sketch)

The main technical challenge is to derive nonasymptotic error bounds for a stochastic gradient method whose estimator depends on both the sampling noise and the penalty parameter
\beta
. Specifically, the first difficulty is to obtain a one-step error recursion that simultaneously captures the descent effect of the algorithm and the stochastic fluctuation of the gradient estimator. To address this point, we establish one-step error in Lemma
C.2
by combining unbiasedness of estimators in Lemma
4

C.1

C.3

The proof can be split into two parts. First, we establish the one-step descent inequality for the stochastic gradient iterate (as in Lemma
C.2
). We then unroll the recursion to derive the average-regret bound and the last-iterate proximity bound in Theorem
3
.

Y_{n,i}=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}+\varepsilon_{0,i},&n=0\\
\mu_{i}+\phi\,Y_{n-1,i}+\varepsilon_{n,i},&n=1,\ldots,N\end{cases},\qquad Y^{\prime}_{n,i}=\begin{cases}\mu^{\prime}_{i}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0,i},&n=0\\
\mu^{\prime}_{i}+\phi\,Y^{\prime}_{n-1,i}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n,i},&n=1,\ldots,N\end{cases}

(59)

where
\phi\in(-1,1)
is the autoregressive coefficient,
\mu_{i}
and
\mu^{\prime}_{i}
are asset-specific means, and

\{\varepsilon_{n,i}\}
and
\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n,i}\}
are innovation noises. Throughout, we model the distributions of the innovations to produce both Gaussian and non-Gaussian marginals while controlling the strength of temporal correlation.

We consider two complementary data-generating regimes, a unimodal setting and a bimodal setting with a time-constant selector:

22

•

Unimodal setting.

We set
\phi=0.5
and
\mu_{i}=\mu^{\prime}_{i}=-2+\frac{4(i-1)}{d-1}
, and draw the innovations from Gaussian and Beta distributions, respectively:

4
4
endnote:

4

The two shape parameters in Beta distribution control the distributional form (e.g., symmetric, skewed, and U-shaped), which makes it highly adaptable for modeling diverse phenomena
(
51
)
.

\varepsilon_{n,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5^{2}),\qquad\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n,i}\sim 4.0\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(2.0,5.0)-1.5,\qquad n=0,\dots,N.

(60)

All assets share the same innovation law and autocorrelation structure, so the adjacent-step correlation satisfies

\mathrm{Corr}(Y_{n,i},Y_{n-1,i})=\rho_{n,\mathrm{uni}}:=\phi\sqrt{\frac{1-\phi^{2n}}{1-\phi^{2(n+1)}}},\qquad n=1,\dots,N,

and the same expression holds for
\mathrm{Corr}(Y^{\prime}_{n,i},Y^{\prime}_{n-1,i})
.

•

Bimodal setting (time-constant selector).

We set
\phi=0.5
and
\mu_{i}=\mu^{\prime}_{i}=-0.5+\frac{0.5(i-1)}{d-1}
. For each asset
i
, we draw a selector

S_{i}=(S^{(y)}_{i},S^{(y^{\prime})}_{i})
once, with independent entries

S^{(y)}_{i},S^{(y^{\prime})}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
, and keep it fixed across time. The innovations are then sampled from the regime-dependent mixtures

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{n,i}&=(1-S^{(y)}_{i})\cdot\mathcal{N}(1.5,0.5^{2})+S^{(y)}_{i}\cdot\big(2.5\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(2.0,5.0)-2.5\big),\\
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n,i}&=(1-S^{(y^{\prime})}_{i})\cdot\mathcal{N}(-1.5,0.5^{2})+S^{(y^{\prime})}_{i}\cdot\big(2.5\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(5.0,2.0)+0.5\big).\end{split}

In this setting, the adjacent-step correlation is given by

\mathrm{Corr}(Y_{n,i},Y_{n-1,i})=\rho_{n,\mathrm{bi}}:=\frac{\rho_{n,\mathrm{uni}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{n}\kappa_{n-1}}}{\sqrt{(1+\kappa_{n})(1+\kappa_{n-1})}},\text{ with }\kappa_{n}:=\frac{\frac{1+\phi}{1+\phi^{n+1}}\cdot\mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{n,i}\mid S^{(y)}_{i}]\big)}{\frac{1-\phi}{1-\phi^{n+1}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{n,i}\mid S^{(y)}_{i})\big]}.

An analogous expression holds for
\mathrm{Corr}(Y^{\prime}_{n,i},Y^{\prime}_{n-1,i})
by replacing
(\varepsilon_{n,i},S_{i}^{(y)})
with
(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n,i},S_{i}^{(y^{\prime})})
. Because the selector is constant along the trajectory, the process is an AR(1) conditional on a latent regime, and hence exhibits a persistent random-intercept effect. Unconditionally, this increases adjacent-step dependence beyond the
\phi
-driven component and yields substantially larger correlations than in the unimodal setting.

6
Applications

We now illustrate how the proposed framework applies to two representative problems in which respecting temporal information structure is essential: robust hedging and time series statistical downscaling. While these applications arise in different domains, they share a common feature: the objective depends on pathwise interactions between stochastic processes under non-anticipative constraints. This makes them natural settings for bi-causal OT, and highlights the advantage of our approach in learning couplings that are both distributionally accurate and dynamically consistent.

6.1
Robust hedging

We apply Algorithm
4.3
to learn robust subhedging for financial derivatives under the adaptiveness constraint to the natural information filtration. We evaluate the learned coupling in terms of (i) marginal fidelity over time, (ii) temporal dependence, quantified by adjacent-step correlations, and (iii) pricing accuracy, measured by the learned subhedging value relative to the ground truth.

6.1.1
Framework

We first describe how robust hedging can be formulated as a bi-causal OT problem. Consider the dynamics of a financial market over discrete timestamps
n\in[N]_{0}
with
2d
tradable assets, and denote the price vector by
(Y_{n},Y^{\prime}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

6
6
endnote:

6

This two-group structure arises naturally in practice. For instance, they may represent a market index and a tradable ETF that tracks it, latent asset-pricing factors and the portfolios constructed to replicate them, or a benchmark portfolio and a hedging portfolio whose tracking error the investor seeks to minimize.

Trading strategies are required to be predictable with respect to the natural filtration

\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma\big(Y_{0},Y^{\prime}_{0},\dots,Y_{n},Y^{\prime}_{n}\big),\qquad n\in[N]_{0},

so that portfolio positions at time
n
depend only on the observed history up to time
n
.

For a target payoff/criterion
\xi
on asset prices
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{N})
and
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}=(Y^{\prime}_{0},\dots,Y^{\prime}_{N})
, the robust subhedging price is defined as the maximal initial capital whose terminal wealth can be guaranteed
not to exceed
the target payoff
\xi
under all admissible price dynamics:

\displaystyle\underline{p}(\xi):=\sup\biggl\{x\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;

\displaystyle\exists\,H=(H_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1},\ H_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},\ \text{with }H_{n}\text{ being }\mathcal{F}_{n}\text{-adapted, s.t. }

(61)

\displaystyle x+\sum_{n=1}^{N}H_{n-1}\cdot(Y_{n}-Y_{n-1},Y^{\prime}_{n}-Y^{\prime}_{n-1})\ \leq\ \xi(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\,,\quad\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}\textrm{-quasi-surely}\biggr\},

where
H_{n}
represents a trading strategy at step
n
, and
\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}
denotes the set of admissible bi-causal laws on path space satisfying prescribed marginal constraints
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
as well as the bi-causal information structure.

7
7
endnote:

7

Here, “
\Pi_{\rm bc}
-quasi-surely” means
\pi
-almost surely for every
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm bc}
.

In incomplete markets,
\underline{p}(\xi)
corresponds to a
robust lower (bid) bound
for
\xi
, complementing the robust upper (ask) bound from superhedging and forming a no-arbitrage price interval
(
14
)
.

44

Under mild conditions, the problem (
61
) admits a dual adapted-transport representation
(
54
, Section 3.1)
:

\underline{p}(\xi)\;=\;\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi}\left[\xi(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\right].

(62)

Thus,
\underline{p}(\xi)
is equivalent to the smallest attainable expected value of
\xi
over all admissible bi-causal models consistent with
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and the information constraint. To connect (
62
) with bi-causal OT, consider an additive payoff
\xi
specified by a sequence of nonnegative one-stage costs
\{c_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}

(
15
, Chapter 6)
:

\xi(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(y_{n},y^{\prime}_{n})\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\underline{p}(\xi)\;=\;\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}(Y_{n},Y^{\prime}_{n})\right].

(63)

Under this specification,
\underline{p}(\xi)
can be interpreted as a robust bid valuation for the cumulative cost (tracking error) incurred along the path
(
31
)
, under all bi-causal models consistent with
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
. The objective on the right-hand side of
\underline{p}(\xi)
in (
63
) coincides with a bi-causal OT problem (
5
) with stage costs
\{c_{n}\}
, so learning the optimal coupling
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
directly corresponds to learning the robust subhedging value. Accordingly, Algorithm
4.3
can be viewed as a numerical method for computing this robust bid bound under the imposed information constraint. In addition, an optimal hedging strategy

H_{n}
can be recovered from the value function

V_{n}
, for instance, via subgradients or martingale representation.

6.2
Time series statistical downscaling

We apply Algorithm
4.3
to a time series statistical downscaling problem motivated by mixed-frequency data commonly encountered in operations research---such as facility-level demand logs versus widely available aggregate forecasts, fine-grained local weather measurements versus coarse climate forecasts, or intraday asset returns versus quarterly accounting disclosures. We evaluate the generated samples in terms of (i) marginal fidelity over time and (ii) temporal dependence, quantified by adjacent-step correlations.

6.2.1
Framework

In many applications, high-resolution time series
\boldsymbol{X}
(e.g., local logs and measurements) are scarce, while low-resolution data
\boldsymbol{Y}
(e.g., aggregate forecasts) are abundant. The goal of statistical downscaling is to generate realistic high-resolution trajectories from low-resolution observations.

68

To connect these two scales, we introduce a designed projection space
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
, obtained by applying a known downsampling operator
C
to
\boldsymbol{X}
. This yields the forward modeling pipeline where
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
is a convenient but biased low-resolution representation induced by the high-resolution data, and the bi-causal OT step corrects this bias by aligning
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
with the observed low-resolution distribution
\boldsymbol{Y}
.

69

While this pipeline describes how the distributions are related, our ultimate goal is the inverse problem: to generate high-resolution trajectories
\boldsymbol{X}
from low-resolution observations
\boldsymbol{Y}
. The role of the intermediate space
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
is therefore to provide a structured bridge that links the scarce high-resolution data to the abundant low-resolution data.
See below for an illustration.

70

\boldsymbol{X}
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
\boldsymbol{Y}

bi-causal OT
debias

generation

74

A related two-stage framework was proposed by
83
, which first performs debiasing and then conditional generation. However, their framework is designed for non-sequential settings. In our setting, the data are time series, and the debiasing step must preserve temporal dependence and information flow. This makes the debiasing problem naturally a bi-causal transport problem.

75

Formally, let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{0},\dots,X_{N_{x}})\sim\mu_{X}
denote the high-fidelity high-resolution (HFHR) process, which is the ultimate target of modeling, and let
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{N_{y}})\sim\mu_{Y}
denote the observed low-fidelity low-resolution (LFLR) process, where
N_{y}\ll N_{x}
. We introduce a

linear downsampling operator

,
and define the projected high-fidelity low-resolution (HFLR) process

76

The process
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
is not intended to match the observed low-resolution data directly. Rather, it is a tractable representation of the high-resolution process on which the debiasing step can be performed. Because the projection map
C
is imposed by design rather than derived from the true data-generating mechanism, the induced law
\mu_{Y^{\prime}}
is generally biased relative to the observed low-resolution law
\mu_{Y}
.

77

Accordingly, we propose the following two-step approach, with technical details provided in Appendix
179
:

1.

Debiasing via bi-causal OT.

We learn a bi-causal transport map
T:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
T_{\#}\mu_{Y^{\prime}}=\mu_{Y}
, while preserving the temporal information structure. This step corrects the bias introduced by the projection space
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}
and aligns the projected low-resolution dynamics with the observed low-resolution data.

2.

High-resolution conditional generation.

After debiasing, we use the resulting low-resolution representation to generate an HFHR trajectory
\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}
through a conditional generative model.

Discussion

This paper develops a scalable computational framework for bi-causal OT under general marginals. By combining a KL-penalized relaxation with the recursive structure of bi-causal OT, we obtain an end-to-end stochastic-optimization method that approximates the original path-space problem without abandoning the underlying information constraints. On the theoretical side, we establish dynamic-programming principles for both the constrained and relaxed formulations, prove
\Gamma
-convergence back to the original problem, derive explicit policy-gradient formulas, and provide nonasymptotic optimization guarantees for our algorithm. On the numerical side, we show that the method preserves both marginal laws and temporal dependence, and that it performs well in robust hedging and time series statistical downscaling.

94

These results make adapted transport computationally feasible in operations research (OR) settings where sequential information matters. Many OR problems involve stochastic processes, multistage decisions, and filtration constraints, including scenario generation and reduction, multistage stochastic optimization, robust finance, and data-driven approximation of dynamical systems. In such settings, respecting information flow is essential for both modeling fidelity and decision quality. Our results suggest that bi-causal OT can serve not only as a structural notion of distance between stochastic processes, but also as a practical computational primitive inside larger optimization and uncertainty-quantification pipelines.

95

Several promising directions build naturally on the present framework. First, extending the analysis beyond the Markov setting to incorporate path-dependent costs and non-Markovian marginals would further expand its scope to important applications such as American option pricing and inventory management with demand memory. In this setting, recurrent or attention-based architectures offer a flexible modeling route beyond the one-step flow parameterization, together with interesting new questions for convergence analysis. Second, the current framework also opens the door to further advances in both computation and statistical theory, including finite-sample guarantees, adaptive penalty parameters, and accelerated training methods for long-horizon problems.

96

Overall, the framework proposed here narrows the gap between the theory of adapted transport and the computational demands of modern OR practice. It provides a basis for bringing bi-causal OT to a broader class of sequential modeling and uncertainty quantification problems.

\displaystyle=s_{1:(k+1)}a_{1}+\sum_{t=1}^{k+1}b_{t}\,s_{t+1:k+1},

where the last equality follows from the recursive definitions of

Appendix E
Additional details on the implemented normalizing-flow parameterization

In this section, we summarize the implemented normalizing-flow parameterization used in the synthetic experiments. For each
n\in[N]_{0}
, let
e_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{emb}}}
denote the sinusoidal embedding of time step
n
. The recursion is initialized by
\widehat{Y}_{-1}=0
and
\widehat{Y}_{-1}^{\prime}=0
. The implementation of the normalizing-flow architecture is described below.

177

1.

Normalization.

In implementation, the model is trained on coordinate-wise normalized trajectories (we normalize the data by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation coordinate-wise), and the corresponding affine Jacobian correction is added back when densities are evaluated on the original scale.

2.

Time-dependent conditioning variables.

For each
n\in[N]_{0}
, given the state
(\widehat{Y}_{n-1},\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime})
, we define

C_{n}:=(\widehat{Y}_{n-1},e_{n}),\qquad C^{\prime}_{n}:=(\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime},e_{n}),\qquad C_{n}^{(\rho)}:=(\widehat{Y}_{n-1},\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime},e_{n}).

The conditioning variables
C_{n}
and
C^{\prime}_{n}
are used for the two generated conditional marginals
\widehat{Y}_{n}
and
\widehat{Y}^{\prime}_{n}
, while
C_{n}^{(\rho)}
is used for the dependency network.

3.

Latent Gaussian-copula coupling.

Inspired by
(
56
)
, we introduce the dependence parameter produced by a neural network
\mathfrak{N}_{n}^{\theta_{n}}
:

\varrho_{n}=\mathfrak{N}_{n}^{\theta_{n}}(C_{n}^{(\rho)})\in[-\rho_{\max},\rho_{\max}]^{d},

(E.1)

where
\rho_{\max}
is a fixed upper bound of the correlation. Given two independent Gaussian vectors

\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)},\varepsilon_{n}^{(2)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, we define the latent pair
(U_{n},V_{n})
by

U_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)},\qquad V_{n}=\varrho_{n}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)}+\sqrt{1-\varrho_{n}^{2}}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{(2)}.

(E.2)

By construction,
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
have standard Gaussian marginals, and their dependence is determined by
\varrho_{n}
.

4.

Conditional marginal flows.

Conditional on
C_{n}
and
C^{\prime}_{n}
, the marginal densities are estimated by conditional spline coupling flows
(
33
)
. In our implementation, both flows use alternating coupling masks, and the associated conditioner networks are multilayer perceptrons (MLPs).

5.

Joint one-step density.

Let
u_{n},v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the latent variables obtained by inverting the conditional flows from
\widehat{Y}_{n}
and
\widehat{Y}_{n}^{\prime}
under conditioning
C_{n}
and
C^{\prime}_{n}
, respectively. Specifically, we define

\widehat{Y}_{n}=f_{\varphi}^{-1}(U_{n};C_{n}),\quad\widehat{Y}_{n}^{\prime}=g_{\psi}^{-1}(V_{n};C^{\prime}_{n}).

(E.3)

The conditional log-density of the implemented one-step kernel is

\log q_{n}^{\theta_{n}}(y_{n},y_{n}^{\prime}\mid y_{n-1},y_{n-1}^{\prime})=\log q_{n}^{\theta_{n},(1)}(y_{n}\mid c_{n})+\log q_{n}^{\theta_{n},(2)}(y_{n}^{\prime}\mid c_{n}^{\prime})+\log\ell_{\varrho_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n}),

where
\ell_{\varrho_{n}}
denotes the Gaussian-copula density with correlation vector
\varrho_{n}
.

The two marginal log-densities are given by the change-of-variables formula:

\log q_{n}^{\theta_{n},(1)}(y_{n}\,|\,c_{n})=\log\phi_{d}(u_{n})+\log\bigl|\mathrm{Jcb}_{n}^{(1)}(y_{n};c_{n})\bigr|,

and

\log q_{n}^{\theta_{n},(2)}(y_{n}^{\prime}\mid c_{n}^{\prime})=\log\phi_{d}(v_{n})+\log\bigl|\mathrm{Jcb}_{n}^{(2)}(y_{n}^{\prime};c^{\prime}_{n})\bigr|,

where
\phi_{d}
denotes the standard Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathrm{Jcb}_{n}^{(1)}
and
\mathrm{Jcb}_{n}^{(2)}
are the corresponding determinants of the inverse-flow Jacobians. The copula correction term is

\log\ell_{\varrho_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\varrho_{n,j}^{2})-\frac{u_{n,j}^{2}-2\varrho_{n,j}u_{n,j}v_{n,j}+v_{n,j}^{2}}{2(1-\varrho_{n,j}^{2})}+\frac{1}{2}(u_{n,j}^{2}+v_{n,j}^{2})\right].

6.

Sampling and likelihood evaluation.

Given
(\widehat{Y}_{n-1},\widehat{Y}_{n-1}^{\prime})
, we first compute
\varrho_{n}
using (
E.1
). Then, based on the latent pair
(U_{n},V_{n})
sampled from (
E.2
), we apply invertible mapping (
E.3
) to generate
(\widehat{Y}_{n},\widehat{Y}_{n}^{\prime})
. Repeating this procedure for
n\in[N]_{0}
yields one sample from the path law
\pi^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

At each iteration
k
, given sampled trajectories
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{\prime})\sim\pi^{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}
, the per-trajectory statistics in (
38
)–(
40
) are calculated. Then, the aggregated gradient estimator (
47
) can be constructed with the control variates (
46
). Finally, we apply the Adam optimizer to update the parameters using the estimated gradient.

Appendix F
Additional details on time series statistical downscaling

Debiasing.

We implement the debiasing step using Algorithm
4.3
. In each round of training, we construct projected low-resolution samples
\{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
from high-resolution trajectories
\{\boldsymbol{X}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
via
C\boldsymbol{X}^{(m)}=\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime(m)},m\in[M]
, and pair them with observed low-resolution samples
\{\boldsymbol{Y}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
. These paired samples are then used to learn a bi-causal coupling that transports
\mu_{Y^{\prime}}
toward
\mu_{Y}
while preserving sequential dependence.

For sampling, given an observed low-resolution path
\boldsymbol{Y}
, we apply the learned transport map
T
to obtain
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}=T^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Y})
. This yields a low-resolution trajectory that is consistent with the observed low-resolution law and suitable for downstream high-resolution generation.

181

In Section
20
, we explained the normalizing-flow parameterization used in Algorithm
4.3
; see Appendix
176
for implementation details. Concretely, given a projected low-resolution time series
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{0}^{\prime},\dots,Y_{N_{y}}^{\prime})
, we first map each coordinate to latent noise via
U_{n}=f_{\varphi}(Y_{n}^{\prime};C_{n})
. We then construct a correlated latent variable

V_{n}=\varrho_{n}U_{n}+\sqrt{1-\varrho_{n}^{2}}\,\varepsilon_{n},

where
\varepsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
\varrho_{n}
is the
n

g_{\psi}^{-1}

\boldsymbol{Y}=g_{\psi}(^{-1}(V_{n};C_{n})

\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}

\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}

Once a debiasing map is learned, we generate an HFHR trajectory conditional on it. To model the HFHR prior
\mu_{X}

\mathcal{E}_{y}=\{C\bar{X}_{T}=y\}

\tilde{X}

{\rm d}\tilde{X}_{t}=\left[\bar{b}(t,\tilde{X}_{t})+\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}^{\top}(t)\nabla_{x}\log h(t,\tilde{X}_{t};y)\right]{\rm d}t+\bar{\sigma}(t){\rm d}\tilde{W}_{t},

(F.1)

where
h(t,x;y)=\mathbb{P}(C\bar{X}_{T}=y\mid\bar{X}_{t}=x).

G.1
Evaluation metrics for synthetic experiments

This subsection introduces the evaluation metrics used in the synthetic experiments. These metrics assess the learned coupling from three perspectives: the transport objective value attained by Algorithm
4.3
, the accuracy of the generated distributions, and the preservation of temporal dependence.

192

•

Transport cost.

To quantify the objective value attained by the relaxed formulation (
17
), we report the empirical average cost induced by the learned coupling returned by Algorithm
4.3
:

\mathtt{Cost\_avg}:=\frac{1}{M(N+1)d}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big(\widehat{Y}_{n,i}^{(m)}-\widehat{Y}^{\prime(m)}_{n,i}\big)^{2}.

•

Wasserstein–2 distance for marginal distributions.

For each time step
n
and asset
i
, define the empirical marginal measures

\widehat{P}_{n,i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{Y_{n,i}^{(m)}},\quad\widehat{P}^{\prime}_{n,i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{Y^{\prime(m)}_{n,i}},\quad\widehat{Q}_{n,i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\widehat{Y}_{n,i}^{(m)}},\quad\widehat{Q}^{\prime}_{n,i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\widehat{Y}^{\prime(m)}_{n,i}}.

The averaged squared Wasserstein–2 distance
(
82
)
is given by

\mathtt{W2\_avg}:=\frac{1}{(N+1)d}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(W^{2}_{2}\big(\widehat{P}_{n,i},\widehat{Q}_{n,i}\big)+W^{2}_{2}\big(\widehat{P}^{\prime}_{n,i},\widehat{Q}^{\prime}_{n,i}\big)\Big),

where
W^{2}_{2}(P,Q):=\inf_{X\sim P,Y\sim Q}\mathbb{E}[(X-Y)^{2}]
and this metric quantifies discrepancies between one-step marginals across time and assets.

•

Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance for marginal distributions.

Let
\widehat{F}_{n,i}
,
\widehat{F}^{\prime}_{n,i}
,
\widehat{G}_{n,i}
, and
\widehat{G}^{\prime}_{n,i}
denote the empirical cumulative distribution
functions corresponding to
\widehat{P}_{n,i}
,
\widehat{P}^{\prime}_{n,i}
,
\widehat{Q}_{n,i}
, and
\widehat{Q}^{\prime}_{n,i}
, respectively.
The averaged Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) distance
(
77
,
60
)
is defined as

\mathtt{KS\_avg}:=\frac{1}{(N+1)d}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{F}_{n,i}(y)-\widehat{G}_{n,i}(y)\bigr|+\sup_{y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{F}^{\prime}_{n,i}(y^{\prime})-\widehat{G}^{\prime}_{n,i}(y^{\prime})\bigr|\Big),

which provides a complementary, distribution-free measure of marginal mismatch.

•

Sliced Wasserstein distance.

Let
Z^{(m)}=(Z^{(m)}_{1},\dots,Z^{(m)}_{N}):=\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{(m)})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and

\widehat{Z}^{(m)}=(\widehat{Z}^{(m)}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Z}^{(m)}_{N}):=\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(m)})
, where
p=2(N+1)d
. Given a projection direction
\alpha
, denote the marginal measures

\widehat{P}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\alpha^{\top}Z^{(m)}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widehat{Q}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\alpha^{\top}\widehat{Z}^{(m)}}.

The sliced Wasserstein distance (SWD)
(
20
)
is defined as

\mathtt{SWD}:=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})}\Bigl[W^{2}_{2}\bigl(\widehat{P}_{\alpha},\widehat{Q}_{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr],

and is approximated numerically via Monte Carlo sampling over random projection directions.

•

Maximum mean discrepancy.

For the joint trajectory distributions
P
and
Q
, the squared maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD)
(
43
)
is given by

\mathtt{MMD}^{2}:=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{m,l=1}^{M}\Big[k(Z^{(m)},Z^{(l)})+k(\widehat{Z}^{(m)},\widehat{Z}^{(l)})-2k(Z^{(m)},\widehat{Z}^{(l)})\Big],

where
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is a positive definite kernel. This metric captures discrepancies
in the full joint distribution of the stacked trajectories.

•

Adjacent-step correlation discrepancy.

To assess temporal dependence, for each time index
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we compute the empirical adjacent-step correlation

\widehat{\rho}_{n}^{(\mathrm{gen})}:=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\langle\widehat{Z}_{n}^{(m)}-\bar{Z}_{n},\,\widehat{Z}_{n-1}^{(m)}-\bar{Z}_{n-1}\rangle}{\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\widehat{Z}_{n}^{(m)}-\bar{Z}_{n}\|^{2}}\,\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\widehat{Z}_{n-1}^{(m)}-\bar{Z}_{n-1}\|^{2}}},\qquad\bar{Z}_{n}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{Z}_{n}^{(m)},

and
\widehat{\rho}_{n}^{(\mathrm{true})}
is defined analogously by replacing the generated samples
\widehat{Z}^{(m)}
with ground-truth samples
Z^{(m)}
. Let
\Delta_{n}:=\widehat{\rho}_{n}^{(\mathrm{gen})}-\widehat{\rho}_{n}^{(\mathrm{true})}
and define

\mathtt{T}_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|\Delta_{n}|,\qquad\mathtt{T}_{2}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{2}.

Statistical significance is assessed via a trajectory-level permutation test

(
41
,
84
)
, with
p
-values computed as

\mathtt{p(T)}=\frac{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}\{T^{(\ell)}\geq T^{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{B+1},

where
L
is the number of permutations and
T^{(\ell)}
is recomputed after permuting generated and ground-truth trajectories.

G.2
Additional evaluation metrics for robust hedging

This subsection records the additional pricing metrics used in the robust hedging experiment. In addition to the distributional and temporal-dependence metrics introduced in Section
191
, we assess pricing accuracy through the estimated subhedging price under the learned coupling and its relative error with respect to the closed-form benchmark.

195

The empirical subhedging price is defined by

\widehat{p(\xi)}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\widehat{Y}_{n}^{(m)}-\widehat{Y}^{\prime(m)}_{n})^{2},

where
\{(\widehat{Y}_{0}^{(m)},\widehat{Y}_{0}^{\prime(m)},\dots,\widehat{Y}_{N}^{(m)},\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\prime(m)})\}_{m=1}^{M}
are i.i.d. samples from the learned coupling. Since the optimal value
\underline{p}(\xi)
is available in closed form under our setup
(
45
, Lemma 2)
, we also denote the relative error by

\mathtt{RE}:=\frac{\bigl|\widehat{p(\xi)}-\underline{p}(\xi)\bigr|}{|\underline{p}(\xi)|}.

G.3
Additional evaluation metrics for time series statistical downscaling

This subsection records the additional evaluation metrics used in the time series statistical downscaling experiment. Beyond the general distributional and dependence metrics in Section
191
, we report the constraint relative root mean squared error (cRMSE), the Wasserstein--1 distance, and the kernel-density-estimated KL divergence (KLD).

198

Let
\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(k,m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
denote generated samples, where
k
indexes the conditioning value and
m
indexes the generated sample under that conditioning. Let
\{y^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
denote the
K
conditioning values. Each sample

\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(k,m)}=\{(\widehat{X}_{n,i}^{(k,m)})\}_{0\leq n\leq N_{x},\;1\leq i\leq d}

represents a length-
(N_{x}+1)
trajectory of
d
assets.

199

•

Constraint relative root mean squared error.

This metric quantifies the extent to which the generated sample satisfies the conditioning constraint. It is defined by

\mathtt{cRMSE}=\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\|C\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(k,m)}-y^{(k)}\|_{2}}{\|C\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(k,m)}\|_{2}}.

•

Wasserstein-1 distance.

Let
F_{n}
and
\widehat{F}_{n}
denote the empirical cumulative distribution functions of the reference and generated marginal distributions at time
n
, respectively, pooled over samples, conditions, and asset dimensions. Then

\mathtt{Wass}_{1}=\frac{1}{N_{x}+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{F}_{n}(t)-F_{n}(t)|\,dt.

This measures the discrepancy between pooled univariate marginal distributions across time, which is sensitive to differences in distributional location and shape.

•

Kernel-density-estimated Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Let
p_{n}
and
\widehat{p}_{n}
denote the kernel density estimates of the reference and generated marginal densities at time
n
, respectively. Then, define

\mathtt{KLD}=\frac{1}{N_{x}+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{n}(t)\log\frac{p_{n}(t)}{\widehat{p}_{n}(t)}\,dt,

which quantifies the discrepancy between pooled univariate marginal densities across time and particularly captures local density mismatch and tail undercoverage.
