Non-Exchangeable Mean Field Markov Decision Processes with common noise : from Bellman equation to quantitative propagation of chaos
Samy MEKKAOUI, Huyên PHAM

Abstract
We study infinite-horizon Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with a continuum of heterogeneous agents interacting through a common noise, without assuming exchangeability. We introduce the framework of Conditional Non-Exchangeable Mean Field MDPs (CNEMF-MDPs) in both a strong formulation and a label–state formulation. We establish the equivalence between these two formulations by showing that the control problem can be lifted to a standard MDP defined on the Wasserstein space 𝒫 λ ​ ( I × 𝒳 ) {\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X}) , where I I denotes the label (heterogeneity) space, 𝒳 {\cal X} is the individual state space, and λ \lambda specifies the fixed distribution of agent labels. Within this framework, we characterize the value function as the unique fixed point of an appropriate Bellman operator acting on 𝒫 λ ​ ( I × 𝒳 ) {\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X}) . Our second contribution is a quantitative analysis of the propagation of chaos for this non-exchangeable setting with common noise. We derive sharp finite-population bounds by comparing the Bellman operator of the finite N N -agent MDP, defined on the high-dimensional space 𝒳 N {\cal X}^{N} , with its infinite-agent counterpart. This comparison yields explicit constructions of near-optimal policies for the N N -agent system from ϵ \epsilon -optimal policies of the limiting CNEMF-MDP.

Introduction

The study of large-population and complex systems is a central topic in mathematical modeling, with broad applications across modern society, including social networks, power grid infrastructures, financial markets, and lightning networks. The analysis of such systems naturally leads to the study of McKean-Vlasov (MKV) equations, in which the dynamics of a representative agent depend on the distribution of the population. These equations have been extensively investigated within the frameworks of mean field games (MFG) and mean field control (MFC). We refer the reader to the seminal lectures of P.-L. Lions

where heterogeneity and asymmetric interactions break the symmetry assumptions typically underlying MFG and MFC models. Such systems lead naturally to non-exchangeable interactions. Outline of the paper.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 introduces the general framework of the control problem, presenting its various formulations and stating the assumptions required for the subsequent analysis. Section 3 is devoted to the main results: we establish the connection between the weak and strong mean-field formulations, as well as the link between the
N
-agent optimization problem and its mean-field counterparts, through a quantitative propagation-of-chaos result for the value functions and the construction of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{0}\big[f(u,X_{t}^{u},\alpha_{t}^{u},\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(X_{t}^{v},\alpha_{t}^{v})}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}a)\mathrm{d}v)\big](\omega)=\int_{{\cal X}\times A}f\big(u,x,a,\tilde{\phi}_{t}\big((\epsilon^{0}_{s})_{s\leq t}(\omega)\big)\mathbb{P}_{(X_{t}^{u},\alpha_{t}^{u})|(\epsilon^{0}_{s})_{s\leq t}=(\epsilon^{0}_{s})_{s\leq t}(\omega)}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}a).

The value function of the conditional non exchangeable mean field control Markov decision processes CNEMF-MDP is then defined by

\displaystyle V_{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(2.20)

2.4
The
N
-agent formulation

Let

representing the number of interacting particles/agents. We consider the following dynamics for the
N
-agent non exchangeable state process

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{0}^{i,N}&=x_{0}^{i},\\
X_{t+1}^{i,N}&=F_{N}(\frac{i}{N},X_{t}^{i,N},\alpha_{t}^{i,N},\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{(\frac{j}{N},X_{t}^{j,N},\alpha_{t}^{j,N})},\epsilon_{t+1}^{i/N},\epsilon_{t+1}^{0}),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.\end{aligned}\right.

where

and the control followed by agent

.

We now follow mainly the results from
[
32
]
, and state the Bellman equation for the
N
-agent problem, viewed as a MDP with state space
{\cal X}^{N}
, action space
A^{N}
, noise sequence
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{N})_{t\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
with
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{N}:=\big((\epsilon_{t}^{i/N})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket},\epsilon_{t}^{0}\big)
valued in
E^{N}\times E^{0}
. We consider the set
\mathbb{A}^{OL}_{N}
of randomized open-loop policies defined as sequences
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{N}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{N}
measurable function from

into

and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{N}
measurable function from
([0,1]^{N})^{\mathbb{N}}\times(E^{N}\times E^{0})^{t}
into
A^{N}
for

. Now, for each
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{N}\in\mathbb{A}^{OL}_{N}

(recall its definition in (

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{N}}:=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{N}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{N},(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}^{N})_{s\in\llbracket 1,t\rrbracket}),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.26)

The objective of the social planner is to maximize over the set
\boldsymbol{{\cal A}}_{N}
of
A^{N}
-valued process
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{t}^{i,N})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket,t\in\mathbb{N}}
the following criterion:

\displaystyle V_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\beta^{t}f_{N}\big(\frac{i}{N},X_{t}^{i,N},\alpha_{t}^{i,N},\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{(\frac{j}{N},X_{t}^{j,N},\alpha_{t}^{j,N})}\big)\Big],

(2.27)

and the value function of the

\displaystyle V_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}):=\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\boldsymbol{{\cal A}}_{N}}{\sup}V_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}).

(2.28)

and action space

(2)

\displaystyle\big|f_{N}(u,x,a,\mu)-f_{N}(u,x^{\prime},a^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\big|\leq L_{f}\big({\bf d}((x,a),(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}))+{\cal W}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\big)

Remark 2.10

The proposed setting slightly differs from those considered in

(ii)

\displaystyle\underset{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a})\in{\cal X}\times A}{\sup}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d\big(F(\frac{i}{N},x^{i},a^{i},{\mu}_{N}^{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{u},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a}],\epsilon_{1}^{i},\epsilon_{1}^{0}),F_{N}(\frac{i}{N},x^{i},a^{i},\mu_{N}^{\lambda}\big[\boldsymbol{u},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a}],\epsilon_{1}^{i},\epsilon_{1}^{0})\big)\Big]\leq\epsilon_{N}^{F}.

3
Main results

3.1
Bellman fixed point on
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})

Our first result is to show the equivalence between weak and strong formulations of the CNEMF-MDP, i.e., the equality between the value functions
V_{\text{S}}
and
V_{\text{W}}
relying on a lifting procedure on the space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
inspired by similar arguments as used in
[
31
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Equivalence of value functions between weak and strong formulation).

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{u})_{u\in I}
and
\xi
be random variables satisfying the hypothesis of Proposition
2.8
implying that
\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{u}}=\mathbb{P}_{\xi|U=u}

\lambda-\text{a.e}
. Then, under the
randomization hypothesis
stated under the weak and strong formulation, we have

\displaystyle V_{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=V_{W}(\xi)=\hat{V}(\mu),

(3.1)

where
\mu(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}x)=\lambda(\mathrm{d}u)\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{u}}(\mathrm{d}x)=\lambda(\mathrm{d}u)\mathbb{P}_{\xi|U=u}(\mathrm{d}x)
and where
\hat{V}
is defined in (
4.8
).
Moreover, there exists a map
\mathfrak{a}_{0}\in L^{0}({\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})\times I\times{\cal X}\times[0,1],A)
called optimal randomized feedback policy such that the randomized feedback control defined for
\lambda-\text{a.e }u\in I
by

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{S,u}=\mathfrak{a}_{0}(\mathbb{P}^{0}_{X_{t}^{v}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mathrm{d}v,u,X_{t},Z_{t}^{u}),\quad\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{W}=\mathfrak{a}_{0}(\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(U,X_{t})},U,X_{t},Z_{t}),\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s},\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

(3.2)

are respectively optimal for
V_{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
V_{W}(\xi)
.

Remark 3.2

Theorem
3.1
has several important implications for the underlying control problem, which can be summarized as follows.

(1)

The CNEMF-MDP can be formulated as a standard MDP control problem with state space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
. Moreover, by (
3.1
), the control problem is law-invariant. In particular, let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{u})_{u},\boldsymbol{\xi^{\prime}}=(\xi^{\prime u})_{u}
,
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\xi|U=u}=\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{u}}=\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{\prime u}}=\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{\prime}|U=u}

\lambda
-a.e, then, one has
V_{W}(\xi)=V_{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=V_{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi^{\prime}})=V_{W}(\xi^{\prime})=\hat{V}(\mu)
where
\mu(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}x)=\lambda(\mathrm{d}u)\mathbb{P}_{\xi^{u}}(\mathrm{d}x)
. Consequently, the value functions
V_{S}
and
V_{W}
can be identified with a single value function
\hat{V}
defined on
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
.

(2)

The existence of an optimal randomized feedback control for the weak control problem is equivalent to the existence of an optimal randomized feedback control for the strong control problem. In both cases, the optimal control is given by the same feedback map
\mathfrak{a}_{0}
.

3.2
Propagation of chaos for value functions

Our second result is to quantify the rate of convergence of the value function of the
N
-agent MDP
V_{N}
towards the value function of the CNEMF-MDP. Following Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.2
, we refer to
\hat{V}
independently for
V_{S}
or
V_{W}
.

Theorem 3.3

(Quantitative propagation of chaos for the CNEMF-MDP).

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.4
,
2.9
and
2.11
,
there exists some positive constant
C
such that for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(x^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}\in{\cal X}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|V_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\hat{V}(\mu_{N}^{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{u},{\boldsymbol{x}}])\Big|\leq C\Big(M_{N}^{\gamma}+\epsilon_{N}^{f}+(\epsilon_{N}^{F})^{\gamma}\Big),

(3.3)

where
\gamma=\text{min}\big(1,\frac{|\text{ln}(\beta)|}{\text{ln}(2L_{f})}\big)
.

Remark 3.4

Theorem
3.3
provides a refined quantitative analysis of propagation of chaos compared to the classical mean field framework in
[
32
]
. In particular, the error bound in (
3.3
) involves additional terms whose roles can be interpreted as follows.

(1)

In the CNEMF setting, the value function is defined on the space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
, which introduces an additional dimension corresponding to the agents’ labels. As a consequence, the quantity
M_{N}
, which depends on the dimension of the underlying state space, is directly affected.

(2)

The terms
\epsilon_{N}^{f}
and
\epsilon_{N}^{F}
are directly related to the convergence of
f_{N}
and
F_{N}
towards
f
and
F
, respectively, in the
L^{1}
sense. These error terms should be interpreted in light of interaction structures such as graphons: for instance, when assuming that a sequence of step graphons
(G_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
converges to a limiting graphon
G
in
L^{1}
, these quantities naturally quantify the discrepancy induced by the finite-agent approximation.

Remark 3.7

(1)

(2)

Remark 4.8

(Choice of the distance on
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
).

We now briefly explain an important point that motivates our choice of working with the standard Wasserstein distance on
{\cal P}(I\times{\cal X})
, and hence on the induced space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
. A seemingly natural alternative would be to endow
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
with the following distance. For
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
, define

\displaystyle\tilde{{\cal W}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\int_{I}{\cal W}(\mu^{u},\mu^{\prime u})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}u),

(4.27)

where
{\cal W}
denotes the usual
1
-Wasserstein distance on
{\cal P}({\cal X})
, and where
(\mu^{u})_{u\in I}
and
(\mu^{\prime u})_{u\in I}
denote the
\lambda(\mathrm{d}u)
-a.e. uniquely defined families of disintegrated probability measures.

However, it is not clear that the space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
is compact under the topology induced by the distance
\tilde{{\cal W}}
. In fact, compactness generally fails unless additional regularity assumptions are imposed on the label variable, more precisely on the map
u\mapsto\mu^{u}
. Since the space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
is too large in this sense, one could instead restrict attention to a regular subset, denoted by
{\cal P}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{reg}}(I\times{\cal X})
, consisting of probability measures whose disintegrations satisfy suitable regularity conditions ensuring compactness.

Such a restriction would however require verifying that the associated MDP is well posed on the space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{reg}}(I\times{\cal X})
. In particular, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, one would need to study the regularity of the mapping

\displaystyle u\;\longmapsto\;\mathbb{P}^{0}_{X_{t+1}\mid U=u}=F\big(u,\cdot,\cdot,\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(U,X_{t},\alpha_{t})},\cdot,\epsilon_{t+1}^{0}\big)\sharp\big(\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\mid U=u}\otimes\lambda_{\epsilon}\big).

(4.28)

To ensure that, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., the joint law
\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(U,X_{t})}
belongs to
{\cal P}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{reg}}(I\times{\cal X})
, one would have to restrict the class of admissible controls by imposing additional regularity with respect to the label variable. Since we do not wish to introduce such constraints on the control policies, we instead assume Lipschitz regularity of the coefficients
F
and
f
with respect to the label argument. This assumption allows us to work on the full space
{\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X})
endowed with the standard Wasserstein topology.

B.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mu_{t+1}=\hat{F}\big(\mu_{t},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t},\epsilon_{t+1}^{0}\big)\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a .s},\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

(B.4)

Now, it turns out that the strong formulation leads to the same MDP as the weak formulation with characteristics
\big({\cal P}_{\lambda}(I\times{\cal X}),\boldsymbol{A}={\cal P}(I\times{\cal X}\times A),\hat{F},\hat{f},\beta\big)
with controlled dynamics given by (
4.7
)-(
4.8
). Given
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{u})_{u\in I}
admissible according to Definition
2.5
, we can then introduce the relaxed operator
{\cal L}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{0}
as follows

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{0}:\mathcal{A}^{S}&\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{{\cal A}},\\
\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{t}^{u})_{u,t}&\longmapsto\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{R}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{R})_{t}:\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{R}:=\mathbb{P}^{0}_{(X_{t}^{v},\alpha_{t}^{v})}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}a)\mathrm{d}v,\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

(B.5)

Appendix C
Bellman equation for the
N
-agent MDP in the non exchangeable setting

In our non-exchangeable setting, the dynamic relation (
2.25
) can be rewritten as
{\bf X}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{F}_{N}({\bf X}_{t},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t+1})
where the measurable state transition map
\boldsymbol{F}_{N}:{\cal X}^{N}\times A^{N}\times(E^{N}\times E^{0})\to{\cal X}^{N}
is given for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}
,
{\bf a}=(a^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}
and
{\bf e}=\big((e^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket},e^{0})
by
\boldsymbol{F}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a},{\bf e}):=\Big(F_{N}(\frac{i}{N},x^{i},a^{i},\mu_{N}\big[\boldsymbol{u},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a}\big],e^{i},e^{0})\Big)_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}
.
The reward function
\boldsymbol{f}_{N}:{\cal X}^{N}\times A^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
\boldsymbol{f}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{N}\big(\frac{i}{N},x^{i},a^{i},\mu_{N}\big[\boldsymbol{u},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a}\big]\big)
.
Given
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\boldsymbol{{\cal A}}_{N}
, the expected gain (
2.27
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle V_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\beta^{t}\boldsymbol{f}_{N}({\bf X}_{t},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})\Big].

(C.1)

The MDP with characteristics
({\cal X}^{N},A^{N},\boldsymbol{F}_{N},\boldsymbol{f}_{N},\beta)
is then well posed. We then introduce the Bellman operator
{\cal T}_{N}:L^{\infty}_{m}({\cal X}^{N})\to L^{\infty}({\cal X}^{N})
defined for any
W\in L_{m}^{\infty}({\cal X}^{N})
by

\displaystyle\big[{\cal T}_{N}W\big]({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\underset{{\bf a}\in A^{N}}{\sup}\mathbb{T}_{N}^{a}W({\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal X}^{N}.

(C.2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{N}^{{\bf a}}W({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\boldsymbol{f}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a})+\beta\mathbb{E}\Big[W\big(\boldsymbol{F}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\bf a},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1})\big)\Big],\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal X}^{N},\quad{\bf a}\in A^{N}.

(C.3)

Following closely the arguments in Appendix B in
[
32
]
, we can check that
{\cal T}_{N}
admits a unique fixed point denoted by
V_{N}^{\star}
which is
\gamma
-Hölder. The objective is now to show that
V_{N}=V_{N}^{\star}
, i.e, the value function of the
N
-agent MDP is the unique fixed point of the Bellman operator
{\cal T}_{N}
. The proof of this standard result relies on the construction of
\epsilon
-optimal feedback policy and on a suitable verification result. We first recall the notion of (randomized) feedback policy in the context of the
N
-agent MDP.

Definition C.1

A feedback policy (resp randomized feedback policy) for the
N
-agent non exchangeable mean field MDP is a mapping
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in L^{0}({\cal X}^{N};A^{N})
(resp
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in L^{0}({\cal X}^{N}\times[0,1]^{N};A^{N})
. The associated feedback control is the unique control

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\pi}({\bf X}_{t}),\quad\text{resp},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{r}({\bf X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Z}_{t})),

(C.4)

where
\big\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{t}=(Z_{t}^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket},t\in\mathbb{N}\big\}
is a family of mutually i.i.d uniform random variables on
[0,1]
independant of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in L^{0}({\cal X}^{N};A^{N})
(resp
L^{0}({\cal X}^{N}\times[0,1]^{N};A^{N})
), we introduce the operator
{\cal T}_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
L_{m}^{\infty}({\cal X}^{N})
defined as

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}W({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\boldsymbol{f}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},\pi({\boldsymbol{x}}))+\beta\mathbb{E}\Big[W\big(\boldsymbol{F}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{\pi}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\epsilon_{1})\big)\Big],\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal X}^{N},

(C.5)

resp

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}W({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\boldsymbol{f}_{N}\big({\boldsymbol{x}},\pi({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{0})\big)+\beta W\big(\boldsymbol{F}_{N}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{\pi}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{0}),\epsilon_{1})\Big],\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal X}^{N},

(C.6)

where
\boldsymbol{Z}_{0}:=(Z_{0}^{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}
is a family of i.i.d uniform random variables independant of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.

Definition C.2

Fix
\epsilon\geq 0
. We say that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}
is an
\epsilon
-optimal (randomized) feedback policy for
V_{N}^{\star}
if for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal X}^{N}

\displaystyle V_{N}^{\star}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq{\cal T}_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}}V_{N}^{\star}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\epsilon.

(C.7)

We recall from the verification result (Lemma B.5 in
[
32
]
) that if
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}
is an
\epsilon
-optimal (randomized) feedback policy for
V_{N}^{\star}
, then the associated feedback control
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
from Definition
C.1
is
\frac{\epsilon}{1-\beta}
optimal for
V_{N}
, i.e satisfies
V_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}}}\geq V_{N}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\beta}
and
V_{N}\geq V_{N}^{\star}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\beta}
.

Theorem C.3

For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a (randomized) feedback policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}
that is
\epsilon
-optimal for
V_{N}^{\star}
. Consequently, the control
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\epsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{{\cal A}}
is
\frac{\epsilon}{1-\beta}
for
V_{N}
, and we have
V_{N}=V_{N}^{*}
, which thus satisfies the Bellman fixed point equation with operator
{\cal T}_{N}
defined in (
C.2
).

Proof.

The proof of this result can be found in Proposition B.2 in
[
32
]
.
