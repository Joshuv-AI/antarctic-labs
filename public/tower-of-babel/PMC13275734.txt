An AI-driven framework for diabetic foot ulcer classification, segmentation, and depth estimation
Authors: Saswati Debnath, Daniel Willson, Renukadevi M N, Shreenidhi H S, Jaydeep Kishore, Rohit Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic Foot Ulcers are a severe complication of diabetes that can lead to infection, amputation, and increased mortality, making timely and objective assessment essential. Conventional evaluation relies primarily on visual inspection and manual measurements, which are often subjective and inconsistent. This paper presents a comprehensive automated deep learning–based framework for DFU assessment that integrates classification, segmentation, relative depth estimation, and explainability. An EfficientNet-B0 model is employed for binary classification of ulcerated versus healthy skin. At the same time, a U-Net with an EfficientNet-B0 encoder is used for precise delineation of ulcer boundaries, enabling quantitative morphometric analysis such as area and width estimation. Model interpretability is incorporated through Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations, providing visual insights into the decision-making process. To characterize wound topology, relative pseudo-depth maps are generated from single RGB images using a MiDaS-based monocular depth estimation approach. Finally, an automated reporting module synthesizes outputs from all components into structured, clinician-friendly summaries. Experimental results demonstrate strong classification performance, accurate segmentation, and meaningful characterization of relative depth, highlighting the potential of the proposed framework as a research-oriented AI-assisted decision-support tool for DFU assessment. However, further clinical validation involving expert clinicians and prospective studies is required before real-world clinical deployment.Keywords: Diabetic foot ulcer, Multi-class classification, Deep learning, Hybrid model, Transformer, Computer-aided diagnosis, Explainable AI, Medical imagingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has escalated from a chronic metabolic disorder into a global public health crisis of unprecedented scale and velocity. The latest figures from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the World Health Organization (WHO) reveal a staggering reality: approximately 589 million adults worldwide are currently living with diabetes, representing one in every nine adults1,2. This epidemic is not static; projections indicate that the number of affected individuals will surge to 853 million by 2050, at which point one in every eight adults will have the condition1,2. This projected 46% increase starkly outpaces the estimated 20% growth in the global population, underscoring systemic failures in current public health strategies. The rise is predominantly fueled by type 2 diabetes, which constitutes over 90% of all cases and is linked to rapid urbanization, aging populations, sedentary lifestyles, and obesity2,3.

The burden of diabetes is mirrored by its immense economic impact. Global health expenditure attributable to diabetes and its complications has surpassed one trillion US dollars, marking a more than 300% increase in under two decades4. In the United States alone, the annual cost has reached $412.9 billion, with one in every four healthcare dollars spent on diabetes care5. Among the myriad complications that drive these costs, the Diabetic Foot Ulcer (DFU) stands out as one of the most common, severe, and financially draining, with the cost of care being 5.4 times higher than for a diabetic patient without one6.

The lifetime risk of a person with diabetes developing a DFU is 19–34%7. These ulcers, often initiated by minor trauma in neuropathic feet, precede more than 85% of non-traumatic lower-extremity amputations (LEAs)8. Globally, it is estimated that a leg is amputated due to diabetes every 30 seconds9. For patients who develop DFUs, the probability of amputation ranges from 20–31%8,9, and the 5-year mortality rate can reach 50–70%, surpassing many cancers, including breast and prostate cancer10. Thus, a DFU is not only a localized wound but a lethal biomarker of advanced systemic disease involving neuropathy, peripheral artery disease, and poor glycemic control7,11.

Despite this severity, the current standard for DFU assessment relies heavily on subjective methods–visual inspection, manual ruler measurements, and qualitative interpretation12. These methods are inconsistent, prone to inter-observer variability, and unable to reliably track wound progression. Quantitative techniques like digital planimetry exist but are cumbersome and rarely adopted in busy settings13. This unreliability hampers early identification of deteriorating wounds and contributes to delayed interventions, infection, amputation, and mortality8,12.

To address these challenges, research has increasingly turned to artificial intelligence (AI), computer vision, and deep learning. Automated analysis of wound images promises reproducible, quantitative, and objective assessment14. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have shown high performance in classification, detection, and segmentation of DFUs15 Yet most AI applications remain task-specific, fragmented, and limited in clinical utility. A truly transformative solution must integrate the full workflow–identification, measurement, characterization, and reporting–into a seamless pipeline.

Another critical barrier is the “black box” nature of AI. Despite high accuracy, models often lack transparency, undermining clinician trust and adoption16. Explainable AI (XAI) methods, such as Grad-CAM and LIME, are being explored to provide interpretability17,18. Trustworthy AI in medicine requires not only performance but also transparency, accountability, and clinical validation19. The complication of diabetes that will lead to infection and amputation, but also as an overwhelming burden on healthcare systems. The development of a sustainable AI-based deep learning framework was proposed in20 to initiate the transition to early detection and continuous monitoring of DFU.

This proposed work presents an integrated, task-specific DFU assessment framework that unifies classification, segmentation, pseudo-depth characterization, and explainability into a single, reproducible pipeline. The proposed system integrates five synergistic components–classification, segmentation, depth estimation, and interpretability.

Literature review

Goyal et al.14 proposed DFUNet, a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based architecture for diabetic foot ulcer analysis, which achieved an AUC of 0.961 on a dataset comprising 397 DFU images, outperforming conventional machine learning approaches as well as several existing CNN models. Goyal et al.16 extended DFU research to ischemia and infection recognition, contributing a dataset and methods for multi-label classification. Zhang et al. provided a comprehensive review of deep learning applications for DFU analysis, covering classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation. The authors summarized key performance metrics across tasks, noting that adaptive Faster R-CNN models achieved a mean average precision (mAP) of 91.4% in object detection, while U-Net architectures reached an accuracy of 94.96% for segmentation21. In contrast, deep learning approaches22, notably CNN, demonstrate the capability to autonomously extract features from given input samples, streamlined the process of disease classification. Giridhar et al.23 focused on the multi-class classification of DFUs into three stages (Ischemia, None, and Infection) using various deep learning models on the DFUC 2021 dataset. Their proposed system found that the DenseNet201 architecture achieved the highest performance, attaining an overall accuracy of 98% and F1-scores as high as 98% for the Ischemia and None stages. This highlights the effectiveness of DenseNet for rapid and accurate DFU stage classification. Liu et al.23 introduced a DL-based system for DFU ischemia and infection recognition using the EfficientNet architecture on the DFUC2021 dataset, achieving 99% accuracy in ischemia classification and 98% in infection classification through data augmentation techniques like geometric and color operations. Their work outperformed baselines such as ResNet and Inception, emphasizing EfficientNet’s efficiency in handling computational constraints while maintaining high accuracy. Alzubaidi et al.24 proposed a comparison of four hybrid deep CNN models for DFU classification, incorporating multi-branch networks with parallel convolutional layers to address gradient vanishing issues. Trained on a DFU dataset with augmentation, the four-branch model achieved a 95.8% F1-score, surpassing other branch configurations and prior methods. Nagaraju et al.25 developed the SSODL-DFUDC technique, combining Inception-ResNet-v2 for feature extraction with sparrow search optimization (SSO) for hyperparameter tuning and a stacked sparse autoencoder (SSAE) for classification. Evaluated on a Kaggle dataset of 1866 thermograms, it achieved 98.71% accuracy, outperforming other DL models in binary DFU detection. Devi et al.26 presented a hybrid CNN-SVM framework for DFU classification using hyperspectral images, involving adaptive median filtering, improved fuzzy c-means with particle swarm optimization for segmentation, and GLCM-based texture features. The model classified DFUs into four categories (normal, high-risk, ulcerated, and infected) with high accuracy on a custom dataset. Biswas et al.27 introduced DFU_MultiNet, a multi-scale feature fusion approach using VGG19, DenseNet201, and NasNetMobile for feature extraction on a public DFU dataset. The fused model achieved 99.06% accuracy, 99.08% F1-score, and 99.09% AUC, demonstrating superior performance in binary classification through transfer learning and augmentation. Muthulakshmi et al.28 conducted a comparative analysis of EfficientNet variants (B0 to B6) for ischemic and non-ischemic DFU differentiation on the DFUC2021 dataset. Using transfer learning and augmentation, EfficientNet-B1 and B6 achieved over 93% validation accuracy, with B1 balancing efficiency and performance. Jain et al.29 proposed an EfficientNet-based model for DFU classification, trained on 1055 images (ulcer and healthy skin). Optimized for validation loss (0.0496), it reached 95% accuracy, highlighting EfficientNet’s scalability for medical image tasks in resource-limited settings.

Almufadi et al.30 developed E-DFu-Net, evaluating seven pretrained DCNN models (including EfficientNetB0) for ischemia and infection classification. The proposed system achieved 97% ischemia and 92% infection accuracy, reducing overfitting through transfer learning. Shanmugam et al.31 introduced a neural network framework using EfficientNet-B0 on 1866 Kaggle thermograms, achieving 98.71% accuracy in binary DFU classification, emphasizing its potential for automated screening. Anusha et al.32 proposed a ConvMixer model for DFU detection and classification, preprocessing with the watershed algorithm, and achieving 97.16% accuracy, 97% precision, and 99.08% sensitivity on a diverse DFU dataset. Thotad et al.33 surveyed deep learning approaches for DFU detection, highlighting CNN-based progress. Wang et al.34 introduced a few-shot learning approach using Deep ResNet with transfer learning, improving DFU image classification where annotated data is scarce. Toofanee et al.35 introduced a novel classification approach, DFU-SIAM, which utilizes a Siamese Neural Network architecture. The model’s backbone is a unique ensemble of a Convolutional Neural Network (EfficientNetV2S) and a Vision Image Transformer (BEiT) to effectively learn image similarities. Their proposed method achieved state-of-the-art results on the DFUC2021 challenge dataset, demonstrating superior performance over previous works with a Macro F1-Score of 0.6228. Goyal et al.36 also developed robust mobile-friendly methods for real-time DFU detection and localization. Manoj et al.37 proposed an intelligent CNN-based framework using webcam imaging for non-invasive DFU detection, achieving 97.2% accuracy, 96.8% precision, and 97.5% recall. Hassib et al.38 presented an approach for Diabetic Foot Ulcer (DFU) segmentation by ensembling convolutional (DeepLabV3+) and transformer-based (SegFormer) models for the DFUC2022 challenge. Their experiments showed that the SegFormer model individually achieved the highest performance with a Dice coefficient of 69.89%, and the ensemble method did not yield further improvement in this metric. Kumar et al.39 addressed predictive modeling for early detection of diabetes using ML (RF achieved 83.6% accuracy onthe PIMA dataset), laying a foundation for DFU prevention strategies. A multitude of works have explored the detection and classification of DFU data, aiming to differentiate between abnormal skin and normal skin. Many writers have advocated for image processing and ML methods, focusing on the analysis of diverse features such as morphological textures, hues, and patterns. The efficacy of the proposed methodologies is intricately tied to the chosen algorithms and training strategies. DL has emerged as a prevalent and widely adopted approach for the development of medical image segmentation, classification, and detection tasks. Notably, DL models have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional approaches using DFU images from diabetic-affected patients14.

These studies collectively underscore a pronounced shift in the research landscape toward hybrid, transfer learning, and lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures for the classification and analysis of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). Among the various deep learning approaches, EfficientNet variants have frequently demonstrated superior performance when compared to conventional CNNs, owing to their balance of computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. Similarly, hybrid models and transfer learning strategies have gained traction as viable solutions for overcoming the inherent limitations of traditional feature-engineering-based methodologies, enabling models to leverage knowledge from related domains while adapting to the specific complexities of DFU image data.

Nevertheless, despite these significant advancements, several critical challenges continue to impede the large-scale deployment of such systems in clinical practice. Chief among these are dataset scarcity, which often restricts the ability of models to generalize across diverse patient populations; the risk of overfitting, particularly when training data is limited or highly imbalanced; and the lack of robust frameworks for real-world deployment, where variability in imaging conditions, acquisition devices, and patient-specific factors can degrade model performance.

Furthermore, the majority of current approaches remain constrained to binary or limited multi-class tasks, underscoring the need for more generalized architectures capable of addressing complex multi-class and multi-label scenarios, including the simultaneous recognition of ulcer severity, infection, and ischemia. Addressing these multifaceted challenges offers substantial opportunities for further advancements in automated DFU diagnostics, not only through the development of more sophisticated and resilient models but also via the curation of large-scale, standardized, and representative datasets, the incorporation of domain adaptation techniques, and the integration of explainable AI mechanisms to foster trust and interpretability. Collectively, these directions point toward a future in which automated DFU diagnostic tools can transition from experimental research prototypes to reliable, resource-efficient, and widely deployable solutions, thus supporting both early detection and improved patient outcomes on a global scale.

Background details

EfficientNet

EfficientNet originated from a systematic study of scaling Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets). Its foundational concept is that arbitrarily scaling a network’s depth, width, or input resolution is suboptimal. Instead, the model introduces a principled compound scaling method that uses a simple coefficient to uniformly balance all three dimensions. This approach was designed to achieve a better balance of accuracy and efficiency, addressing the challenge that the accuracy gains from scaling a single dimension tend to diminish quickly. The architecture’s baseline, EfficientNet-B0, was discovered through a neural architecture search that optimized for both accuracy and FLOPS (a measure of computational cost)40.

U-Net

U-Net is a convolutional network architecture designed specifically for fast and precise biomedical image segmentation. Its foundational concept is to effectively perform pixel-level classification on medical images even with very few annotated training samples, a common challenge in the biomedical field. The model’s architecture is famously symmetric and U-shaped, consisting of two main paths. The first is a contracting path (encoder) that follows a typical CNN structure to capture the image’s context by downsampling and increasing feature channels. The second is a symmetric expanding path (decoder) that uses transposed convolutions to upsample the feature maps, enabling precise localization of the features being segmented41.

Midas

MiDaS (Monocular Depth and Normal Estimation System) is a deep learning model designed to estimate the depth of a scene from a single 2D image. Its core concept is to predict relative depth, meaning it determines how far pixels are from the camera relative to each other, rather than predicting an absolute metric distance (e.g., in meters). This approach cleverly sidesteps the inherent ambiguity and ill-posed nature of monocular depth estimation, allowing the model to be trained on a diverse mixture of datasets with different camera parameters and depth scales. This training strategy makes MiDaS exceptionally robust and capable of generalizing to unseen environments (zero-shot transfer)42.

Grad-CAM

Grad-CAM is a widely used explainable AI (XAI) technique that produces visual explanations for the decisions made by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Its fundamental goal is to make CNNs more transparent by generating a “heatmap” that highlights the specific regions in an input image that were most influential in the network’s final prediction for a particular class. This allows users to understand why a model classified an image as, for example, “infected,” by showing exactly which parts of the image led to that conclusion43.

LIME

LIME is another cornerstone of the XAI technique designed to explain the predictions of any machine learning model, earning it the title of model-agnostic. The core idea is that while a complex model (like a deep neural network) may be globally intricate, its behavior for a single, specific prediction can be approximated by a much simpler, locally faithful, and interpretable model (e.g., a linear model). In essence, LIME answers the question “Why should I trust this specific prediction?” by showing which input features were most influential for that single instance44.

Dataset and pre-processing

Experiments employed the publicly available Kaggle DFU-dataset (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/laithjj/diabetic-foot-ulcer-dfu)45, which was collected at the diabetes hospital in Nasiriyah, Iraq, with appropriate informed consent and ethics approvals. Images were captured under varied illumination and viewpoints using a Samsung Galaxy Note 8 and an iPad. We utilized the patches subset (224 224 crops), consisting of 1,055 images (543 healthy and 512 ulcer), to ensure a standardized input size suitable for pre-trained models and to focus the analysis on the immediate ulcer region, thereby reducing computational overhead. For model consistency, all images were resized and normalized using the standard ImageNet mean and standard deviation before being fed into the classification and segmentation models. The dataset was subsequently partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets46–48. Using this wound segmentation data from the Foot Ulcer Segmentation Challenge 2021, we trained our U-Net model to better perform segmentation on the DFU wound region49.

Experiments are also conducted using the segmentation subset of the Diabetic Foot Ulcer Challenge (DFUC) 2022 dataset. The DFUC initiative provides clinically annotated wound images that enable standardized evaluation of deep learning models for ulcer analysis. In particular, the DFUC2022 segmentation dataset offers high-quality, expert-annotated masks, facilitating precise assessment of model performance in clinically relevant settings. The dataset and its evaluation protocols build upon earlier DFUC releases, including DFUC2020, which introduced large-scale data for ulcer detection and classification tasks50, and subsequent efforts that emphasized clinically interpretable segmentation annotations51. Furthermore, benchmark reports from DFUC challenges provide standardized evaluation frameworks and comparative analyses across state-of-the-art segmentation methods52,53.

Data augmentation and splitting

To enhance robustness against variations in imaging conditions and acquisition angles, on-the-fly data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set. The augmentation pipeline included random rotations (up to ), horizontal and vertical flips, and moderate color jittering in brightness, contrast, and saturation. These transformations were designed to improve generalization while preserving the underlying characteristics of ulcer and healthy skin regions.

The Kaggle DFU patch dataset, consisting of 1,055 images, was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets using a stratified 70/15/15 split to maintain class balance across all sets. Since the dataset comprises independent image patches without associated patient identifiers, patient-level separation was not applicable. Data augmentation was applied only during training, while validation and test sets remained augmentation-free to ensure unbiased performance evaluation.

This splitting strategy provides sufficient training data for model optimization while reserving independent subsets for hyperparameter tuning and final evaluation, enabling a realistic assessment of the model’s generalization capability.

Ulcer classification (EfficientNet-B0)

For binary classification of ulcer versus healthy skin, an EfficientNet-B0 model pre-trained on ImageNet was fine-tuned. EfficientNet-B0 was selected due to its favorable trade-off between classification performance and computational efficiency, making it suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Training was performed using a two-stage strategy to ensure stable convergence. In the first stage, the EfficientNet-B0 backbone was frozen and only the final classification layer was trained. In the second stage, all layers were unfrozen and fine-tuned using a lower learning rate to refine deep feature representations. The AdamW optimizer was employed for its effective decoupled weight decay regularization, along with a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler to facilitate smooth convergence.

The cross-entropy loss function was used for optimization, as the dataset exhibited only mild class imbalance. Model performance was monitored on the validation set throughout training, and the best-performing model weights were selected based on validation accuracy.

Ulcer segmentation and morphometry (UNet)

For spatial delineation of the ulcer region, we adopted a UNet architecture with an EfficientNet-B0 encoder, also initialized with ImageNet weights. This approach, often referred to as EfficientUnet, combines the powerful feature extraction capabilities of EfficientNet with the proven effectiveness of UNet’s encoder-decoder structure and skip connections for precise semantic segmentation. The predicted binary masks (thresholded at 0.5) are mapped back to the original image resolution. These masks were then used to compute quantitative, wound-level descriptors, including binary area (in pixels) and bounding-box dimensions (in pixels). When a scale is available, these were converted to physical estimates (mm and mm²), providing interpretable indicators for tracking wound progression.

These segmentation experiments are trained and validated on clinically annotated benchmark datasets from the DFUC challenge, ensuring standardized evaluation using expert-labelled ulcer masks51,52.

Depth estimation (MiDaS)

To provide a three-dimensional characterization of the wound, we estimated relative depth using the MiDaS (DPT-Large) model. This relative depth provides a quantitative proxy for wound severity, as ulcer depth is a key parameter in grading systems like the Wagner Ulcer Classification Scale. To contextualize the ulcer’s “depression,” we computed depth statistics within the segmented ulcer region relative to the median depth of a dilated perilesional “skin” band. Where a reliable depth-to-mm calibration is unavailable, depth values are reported in arbitrary units with an explicit note of this limitation. The full integration and necessary safeguards (e.g., fallback procedures if MiDaS fails) are encapsulated in our analysis module.

It is important to note that the depth values obtained from the MiDaS model represent relative pseudo-depth rather than clinically calibrated measurements. Therefore, the proposed depth estimation module does not provide direct clinical quantification of wound depth but serves as an auxiliary structural cue to enhance segmentation and spatial understanding of ulcer regions.

Experimental results and analysis

This section details the experimental setup used to train and evaluate our models, including the data splits, evaluation metrics, and the presentation of visual results.

Experimental setup

All experiments were implemented using the PyTorch deep learning framework and executed in a GPU-accelerated environment. Model training and evaluation were conducted on a system equipped with an NVIDIA T4 GPU. The EfficientNet-B0 architecture was employed for diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) classification, initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights using the timm library. Standard PyTorch and torchvision utilities were used for data handling, augmentation, and optimization.

Input images were resized to pixels. Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set and included random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotations up to , and color jittering to improve generalization. Validation and test samples were processed using deterministic resizing and normalization only. All images were normalized using ImageNet mean and standard deviation values.

Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer with cosine annealing learning rate scheduling. Cross-entropy loss was used as the objective function. No manual class re-weighting was applied, as the dataset exhibited only mild class imbalance. Implementation details and training settings

The framework is implemented using PyTorch and the timm library. To ensure strict reproducibility across all training folds and evaluations, a deterministic random seed (seed = 42) is fixed for all backend operations, including Python’s random, numpy, and PyTorch’s CUDA algorithms. All input images are resized to 256 × 256 pixels. To enhance model generalization and prevent overfitting, the training data is subjected to on-the-fly spatial and color augmentations. These include random horizontal and vertical flips (p = 0.5), random rotations (up to 30 degrees), and color jittering (brightness, contrast, and saturation variations of 20%, along with a hue variation of 10%). All images in the training, validation, and hold-out test sets are subsequently converted to tensors and normalized utilizing standard ImageNet mean and standard deviation values.

The model is optimized using the standard Cross-Entropy Loss function. Model weights are updated using the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 1e-2. To ensure smooth convergence, a Cosine Annealing learning rate scheduler is applied independently during each training stage. The efforts in the automated assessment of diabetic foot ulcers established the foundation for custom deep learning architectures. Notably, Goyal et al.56 introduced DFUNet, one of the first convolutional neural networks specifically engineered with parallel convolution blocks to extract features from a custom dataset of DFU images, proving the viability of deep learning for this task. Building upon this, Alzubaidi et al.57 developed DFU_QUTNet, which utilized an optimized deep network to significantly improve feature representation on the standardized Kaggle DFU dataset. Recently, the research paradigm has shifted from pure classification performance to clinical transparency and model trustworthiness. On the same Kaggle benchmark, Mahmud et al.54 proposed DFU_DIALNet, a reliable framework that achieves strong diagnostic accuracy while integrating GradCAM and LIME to visually validate its predictions. Similarly, Rathore et al.55 deployed a Siamese Neural Network (SNN) augmented with a suite of explainability tools. Their approach pushed the accuracy boundary to 98.76%, demonstrating that highly complex architectures can achieve state of the art results while ensuring the model’s focus remains clinically aligned with the actual ulcer topology.

Several studies report higher classification accuracies using alternative imaging modalities. Nagaraju et al.25 and Shanmugam et al.31 achieved accuracies above 98% using thermal DFU images. Although these results are impressive, thermal imaging systems are less accessible in routine environments. In contrast, the proposed framework operates on standard RGB images, which are more widely available in real-world and remote screening settings, making the achieved performance particularly relevant for practical deployment.

In terms of segmentation performance, Zhang et al.1 reported that U-Net-based architectures typically achieve segmentation accuracies of up to 94.96% in DFU applications. The proposed U-Net model with an EfficientNet-B0 encoder achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.8572 and an IoU of 0.7545 on the test set, demonstrating strong spatial agreement with the ground truth. These results are consistent with prior literature and confirm the suitability of the selected architecture for precisely delineating ulcer boundaries.

It is important to note that prior research on diabetic foot ulcers has employed distinct datasets, different research objectives, and varied methods for measuring effectiveness. Some studies focused exclusively on classifying the problem into two or more classes, whereas others focused on segmenting or employing multiple machine learning techniques. Because of differences in study design, the results of the comparisons listed in Table 8 are intended to provide general information to help determine the overall performance of different studies, rather than a strict one-to-one comparison. Where possible, comparisons of similar datasets and classification methods between studies are reported.

In general, the proposed framework demonstrates competitive performance in classification and segmentation tasks while maintaining architectural simplicity and practical applicability. By leveraging EfficientNet-B0 and U-Net with careful optimization, rather than complex hybrid designs, the framework achieves a favorable balance among accuracy, interpretability, and deployability, making it well-suited for automated DFU screening and decision support systems.

Clinical relevance and validation

The proposed framework is evaluated on publicly available and clinically acquired datasets, including the DFUC2022 dataset and the Kaggle DFU dataset. These datasets consist of wound images captured under real-world clinical conditions and annotated by domain experts, providing a realistic benchmark for evaluating model performance.

From a clinical perspective, the framework provides multiple outputs relevant for wound assessment. The segmentation module enables quantitative morphometric analysis, including ulcer area and width estimation, which are commonly used by clinicians to monitor wound progression. The explainability module (Grad-CAM and LIME) enhances transparency by highlighting image regions influencing model predictions, thereby supporting interpretability.

To assess robustness under real-world variability, the model was evaluated across multiple datasets, demonstrating consistent performance under variations in illumination, wound morphology, and imaging conditions. This cross-dataset evaluation serves as an indirect validation of generalization in clinically relevant scenarios.

However, it is important to note that direct clinical validation involving medical experts, prospective patient studies, or comparison with clinically calibrated measurement systems was not performed in this study. In particular, the depth estimation module is based on monocular pseudo-depth and provides relative structural information rather than absolute clinical measurements.

Therefore, the proposed framework should be considered as a decision-support tool rather than a replacement for clinical judgment. Future work will focus on clinical validation through expert evaluation, inter-observer agreement analysis, and validation against calibrated wound measurement techniques.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive, automated deep learning framework for assessing diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) that integrates classification, segmentation, pseudo-depth estimation, and explainability. EfficientNet-B0 is used as the base model for the classification of DFU, while an EfficientUNet architecture was utilized for precise segmentation of the ulcer boundary. The proposed framework achieved a classification accuracy of 96.86% on the held-out test set, along with strong segmentation performance, demonstrating its effectiveness in accurately identifying and localizing ulcer regions. The incorporation of MiDaS-based monocular depth estimation provides a novel, non-invasive mechanism for characterizing wound topology, offering complementary information related to ulcer severity that is typically unavailable through conventional image-based DFU assessment approaches. Although depth estimates are relative rather than absolute, they serve as a meaningful proxy for wound depression and volume, allowing longitudinal monitoring of ulcer progression. The integration of explainable AI techniques, namely Grad-CAM and LIME, enables visual interpretation of model decisions by highlighting relevant regions that drive predictions. This directly addresses the “black-box” concern commonly associated with deep learning models in healthcare. The explainability evaluation relies on computational metrics (IoU overlap between Grad-CAM attention maps and ground-truth masks, alongside LIME superpixel alignment). This approach provides a quantitative, reproducible proxy for assessing feature relevance without human subjectivity. An average IoU of 0.62 indicates substantial alignment between model attention and ulcer regions.

The proposed method is limited by dataset size and lack of multi-center validation, which may affect generalization. Pseudo-depth is not clinically validated and provides only relative structural information, while explainability evaluation lacks expert assessment. Explainability evaluations have not been performed using any expert evaluations. Future work will focus on evaluating the proposed framework on larger and more diverse datasets, along with the validation of clinical outcomes through expert assessment. Additionally, clinician-in-the-loop studies and qualitative scoring frameworks will be incorporated to rigorously assess the interpretability and clinical relevance of the explainability methods. Further work will address real-world deployment challenges, including device-dependent scaling, absolute depth calibration, robustness under varying imaging conditions, and improved generalization across heterogeneous patient populations.

Despite these limitations, the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance across classification, segmentation, pseudo-depth estimation, and explainability tasks, highlighting its potential as a research-oriented AI-assisted decision-support tool for DFU assessment. Future work will focus on multi-center clinical validation, expert assessment, clinician-in-the-loop studies, and evaluation under diverse real-world imaging conditions before deployment in clinical practice.
