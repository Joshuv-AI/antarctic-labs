Co-payments for healthcare by insured clients of Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme: evidence from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Roger Ayimbillah Atinga, Dominic Dormenyo Gadeka, Ambrose Azenyinkajigi Adama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClients insured by Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) who seek healthcare at credentialed facilities incur substantial out-of-pocket (OOP) payments for services covered by the scheme. While co-payments are part of OOP payments, the extent of these co-payments has received limited empirical attention. This study sought to determine co-payment levels and identify the social and demographic factors associated with co-payment for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs among insured clients of the scheme.MethodsWe used the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey data. This was a cross-sectional design, which employed a stratified two-stage random sampling technique. We analysed data involving 8,467 respondents with information on utilisation and payment for health services. Data were analysed using Stata version 16. Descriptive and inferential statistics were performed with statistical significance at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong insured clients who made any out-of-pocket payment, 58.4% reported it as a co-payment for drugs, 52.5% for inpatient care, and 48.4% for outpatient consultation. Respondents aged 25–34 years (AOR: 0.43; CI: 0.18–0.99; p < 0.05) had significantly reduced odds of co-payment for inpatient care, while urban residents had increased odds of co-payment for drugs (AOR: 1.42; CI: 1.03–1.95; p < 0.05) compared to their rural counterparts. Furthermore, being from the Eastern (p < 0.05), Upper West (p < 0.05) and Northern (p < 0.05) regions was associated with reduced odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation and drugs. However, compared to no formal education, having primary education statistically significantly increases the odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation (AOR: 2.66; CI: 1.13–6.27; p < 0.05) and drugs (AOR: 1.82; CI: 1.11–2.99; p < 0.05) while being rich significantly reduces the odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation (AOR: 0.40; CI: 0.19–0.84; p < 0.05).ConclusionInsured clients presenting at credentialed health facilities make co-payments for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs covered by the scheme. The study highlights the multifaceted determinants of co-payments. The extent of co-payments may impede Ghana’s progress toward achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC). There is a need to eliminate co-payments for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs for insured clients to realize the UHC goal.Keywords: Co-payment, Ghana, Out-of-pocket payment, Demographic and Health Survey, National Health Insurance Scheme

Background

Providing financial protection against the cost of ill-health is one of the fundamental goals of health system in achieving a healthy population [1]. High out-of-pocket (OOP) payments for health services are associated with catastrophic health expenditures and poverty [2, 3]. Millions of people around the world are impoverished due to healthcare spending above income levels. Within the last two decades, the proportion of the world’s population facing catastrophic OOP health spending has increased significantly, imposing huge financial risks on households without reliable income sources. Currently, about 1 billion people suffer catastrophic OOP health-related expenses, while 344 million are pushed deeper into extreme poverty, making essential healthcare services unavailable to those in need of care [4–6]. Populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) mostly face severe limitations in accessing healthcare due to financial constraints arising from OOP.

Social protection schemes are recognized as robust financing strategies that could mitigate the adverse effects of OOP payments, increase equitable access to essential health services, and contribute to achieving the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) agenda [7–9]. UHC seeks to ensure that all people and communities receive the health services they need and of sufficient quality and quantity, without exposing them to financial hardship [4, 5]. Although health insurance schemes have been introduced in many countries with varying degrees of success, some face significant challenges, such as co-payment, that threaten UHC efforts [10].

Co-payment refers to a percentage or fixed amount that insured patients pay out of pocket for certain health services covered by an insurance scheme [11]. While co-payment is a means to control demand-side factors of overutilization of healthcare, moral hazard and rising healthcare cost [12], it possesses several risk factors: undermines utilization by the poor and vulnerable, delays care seeking, weakens public support, and promotes catastrophic health expenditure, all of which compromise equity and universal access principles in social health insurance [13]. Co-payment may be legislated or informally introduced by health providers to cope with service delivery challenges or a low insurance tariff structure For example, in some high-income countries, such as UK, Italy, Austria, France, Spain, and Portugal, cost-sharing policies have been introduced in certain services, especially pharmaceuticals, with a threshold in OOP to contain healthcare spending [14]. In LMICs, however, co-payments backed by policies are limited, although the practice seems widespread, posing a threat to the sustainability of social health insurance systems [15]. In these settings, providers often find themselves both victims of structural inadequacies and agents of inequity, as the informal transfer of costs to patients helps sustain facility operations while undermining the protective function of insurance coverage [16].

In Ghana, since the adoption of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in 2005, elements of unauthorized or illegal payments by insured clients have attracted considerable public attention. The regulatory body, the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) has taken drastic measures to crack down on unauthorized charges by credentialed service providers because they undermine equitable access to health services [17]. In demonstration of zero tolerance for co-payment, the NHIA constituted national and regional taskforce committees with oversight responsibilities to monitor and report non-compliance, eliminate co-payments and restore public confidence and trust in the NHIS [17]. In spite of these systematic interventions, some credentialed service providers still charge insured patients fees, citing low tariffs and delayed reimbursement as underlying factors producing resource constraints and budget deficits [15]. Yet limited empirical attention has been given to this phenomenon, which obscures understanding of the magnitude of exposure to co-payments and the type of services affected.

Our literature search produced one study that revealed that 47% of insured clients reported paying OOP for consultation and outpatient services covered by the scheme [18]. Beyond this, sparse evidence, if any exists, to inform understanding of co-payment practices and their sociodemographic determinants for outpatient, drugs and inpatient services, which attract considerable demand from insured client populations. Social and demographic factors associated with co-payments play an important role in mediating their impact, as they influence the vulnerability of the insured, potentially leading to delayed care-seeking or even the abandonment of treatment [19–21]. Moreover, there is a knowledge gap about whether and the extent of co-payments in other covered services of the NHIS. The paucity of research is overwhelming, especially at a time when the NHIA and health policy makers need evidence-driven solutions to remove supply-side barriers to the scheme’s operations. Drawing on data from the eighth round of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), this study sought to determine co-payment for health services and identify the social and demographic factors associated with co-payment for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs by insured clients of the NHIS in Ghana. The findings will bring to light mechanisms for addressing disparities in utilization and improving the scheme’s operational efficiency toward achieving universal health coverage.

Conceptual framework of the influence of social and demographic factors on co-payment

The study was guided by an integrated framework (Fig. 1) of health financing equity [22], social determinants of health [23, 24] and Universal Health Coverage [25] to explain how social and demographic factors influence individuals’ exposure to co-payments and the resulting consequences. Individuals who are poor, less educated, living in rural areas, unemployed, older, or female are more susceptible to incurring additional fees even when insured [19–21]. These vulnerabilities increase their chances of making co-payments, which create financial burdens and reinforce inequality [22]. As co-payments increase, affected groups face limited access to care, delayed treatment, and catastrophic health expenditures, ultimately undermining the protective function of social health insurance and progress toward Universal Health Coverage [22, 24, 25]. As it is the focus of this paper, we only reported on the co-payment and social and demographic factors associated with it. Thus, we did not report data on the provider responses, equity outcomes and UHC but argue for the realization of the UHC goal through the elimination of co-payments based on the study findings.

Methods

Study design

The study was an analysis of secondary data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). This was a cross-sectional design, which employed a stratified two-stage random sampling technique. We analysed data involving 8,467 respondents with information on utilisation and payment for health services.

Data sources

The DHS, first conducted in Ghana in 1988 has since been conducted in a series of five-year intervals. The 2022 survey was conducted in the country’s 16 regions, drawing on a representative sample to generate statistical information on population and health dynamics, pertaining to but not limited to health status, healthcare service utilization, fertility and disease prevalence [26]. The data is freely and publicly available, hence no ethical approval was required for the present work. We specifically used data from the household members/persons file (PR file). Using stratified two-stage cluster sampling in rural and urban areas across all regions, the survey collected data from 18,450 households drawn from 618 clusters. A sample size of 22,058 respondents (household heads or other adult members capable of providing accurate information of the household), consisting of 15,014 women (aged 15–49) and 7,044 men (aged 15–59), were interviewed for the survey. More information about the methodology and sampling procedure deployed for the survey can be found elsewhere [27].

The survey data were collected using a structured questionnaire with several variables measuring population and health issues relevant to Ghana. However, we focused on indicators of utilisation and payment for health services among patients insured by the NHIS. The data showed that some insured patients paid OOP for outpatient department (OPD) and inpatient department (IPD) services during their visits to health facilities. These OOP are payments made with clients’ own money for services already covered by the NHIS and are not reimbursable. Structure and indicators of the survey questionnaire and how it was administered can be obtained in the DHS report [26]. The ‘Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology’ (STROBE) statement on items that should be included in reports of observational studies was followed in conducting the study.

Study variables

Outcome variables

Co-payments for OPD consultation, admission care and drugs were the outcome variables. These variables represent services that attract considerable demand from insured clients. The survey asked insured clients who indicated making OOP payments for OPD consultation, admission care and drugs whether the payments were co-payments. Responses to this question were captured as: 1 = co-payment; 2 = full payment; and 3 = don’t remember. We defined co-payment for outpatient services as payment made by the insured for consultation without being admitted, while co-payment for inpatient care was defined as payment made for admission care. These outcome variables were recoded as binary = 1 if the respondent made a co-payment, and 0 if otherwise.

Independent variables

These variables included household socio-demographic factors: sex (male, female), age (age in years), education (no education, primary, secondary, higher), residence (rural, urban), marital status (unmarried, married), religion (Christianity, Islam, Others), occupation (employed, unemployed), wealth status (poor, middle, rich), region (Western, Central, Greater Accra, Volta, Eastern, Ashanti, Western North, Ahafo, Bono, Oti, Northern, Savannah, North East, Upper East, Upper West), ethnicity (Akan, Ga/Dangme, Ewe, Guan, Mole-Dagbani, Grusi, Gurma, Mande, other), and exposure to mass media: newspaper, radio, TV (exposed, not exposed). Some of the survey variables were recoded for the purpose of this study. Exposure to mass media was included because it is often used to communicate health information and potentially influence insured clients’ behaviour. For example, gleaning from the mass media about how insured clients pay to receive quality health services or pay for unavailable services could influence co-payment behaviour.

In the Survey, exposure to mass media was created from three variables based on the frequency of watching television, listening to radio, or reading newspaper/magazine. This was captured as follows: “Do you watch television almost every day, at least once a week, less than once a week, or not at all? Do you read a newspaper or magazine at least once a week, less than once a week, or not at all? Do you listen to the radio at least once a week, less than once a week, or not at all?” The responses included the following: Not at all, less than once a week, and at least once a week. The responses from these questions were then categorized as: exposed (less than once a week and at least once a week)/Not exposed (not at all) after a composite measure of exposure from the three media sources was determined.

Wealth status was a composite measure derived from data on a household’s possession of specific assets, including televisions (TV), bicycles, construction materials, sanitation facilities, and types of water access. Principal component analysis was performed to transform these assets into a wealth index, allowing households to be situated on a continuous scale of relative wealth. The survey classified households into five wealth quintiles: poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest. These categories were further condensed into three groups: poor (poorest, poorer), middle, and rich (richer and richest).

Data analysis

The analyses were restricted to insured clients who reported making any out-of-pocket payment. Among these respondents, the outcome variables were coded as 1 = copayment and 0 = full payment or ‘don’t remember’. Therefore, the reported prevalence of co-payment was conditional on having incurred an out-of-pocket payment. The data were analysed using Stata version 16. First, descriptive statistics were used to present the demographic factors and co-payment for outpatient services, inpatient care and drugs. We treated co-payments for each service as distinct. Thus, in cases where the same respondent made co-payments for drugs, inpatient care, and outpatient consultations, it was counted once for each service type. We performed a bivariate logistic regression analysis as a first step to identify and select variables for multivariate logistic regression. Variables that showed statistical significance (p < 0.05) with co-payments were used for the multivariate regression. Before fitting the final model, we assessed multicollinearity between the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All the values of the VIF were less than the recommended threshold of 10, suggesting noncollinearity. We further used the Hosmer-Lemeshow to determine goodness-of-fit of the regression models. The regression results were presented as crude odds ratios (cORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs), along with their corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) set at 95%. A sample weight (v005/1,000,000) was applied to adjust for any over- or under-sampling in the survey. The STATA SVY command was utilized to address the complex survey design, enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Results

Prevalence of co-payment by service type among insured clients

Among insured clients (weighted percentages) who made any out-of-pocket payment, 58.4% reported it as a co-payment for drugs, 52.5% for inpatient care, and 48.4% for outpatient consultation (Fig. 2).

Distribution of co-payment respondents by socio-demographic characteristics and service type

The socio-demographic characteristics of respondents are shown in Table 1. The majority of the respondents who made co-payment for outpatient consultation, inpatient care and drugs were respectively: male (34.2% vs. 37.4% vs. 36.1%), had secondary education (27.5% vs. 26.7% vs. 32.8%), in formal employment (42.1% vs. 38.7% vs. 52.5%), aged between 35 and 44 (21.5% vs. 21.4% vs. 24.4%) and exposed to mass media (46.4% vs. 46.8% vs. 6.7%). Additionally, many rural respondents (29.4%) made co-payments for outpatient consultation compared to those from urban areas (24.9%). Conversely, more urban (27.4%) than rural (25.7%) respondents made co-payment for admission care (Table 1).

Social and demographic factors associated with co-payment for healthcare

The regression model of the socio-demographic factors associated with co-payment for outpatient consultation, inpatient care and drugs are shown in Table 2. Results of the adjusted models (AOR) showed that those aged between 25 and 34 had significantly reduced odds of co-payment by 43% (CI: 0.18–0.99; p < 0.05). When compared to no formal education, having primary education statistically significantly increases the likelihood of co-payment for outpatient consultation (AOR: 2.66; CI: 1.13–6.27; p < 0.05) and drugs (AOR: 1.82; CI: 1.11–2.99; p < 0.05). Furthermore, urban residents had increased odds of co-payment for drugs (AOR: 1.42; CI: 1.03–1.95; p < 0.05) compared to rural residents while being a Muslim is associated with reduced odds (71%) of co-payment for drugs compared to being a Christian. Being rich significantly reduces the odds (40%) of co-payment for outpatient consultation (CI: 0.19–0.84; p < 0.05).

Although not statistically significant, three regions, namely, Bono, Oti and Upper East regions showed increased odds of 2.42 (CI: 0.35–16.91), 1.64 (CI: 0.40–6.74) and 1.84 (CI: 0.34–10.12) respectively of co-payment for admission care. However, a reduced odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation was significantly associated with Bono region, 91% (CI: 0.02–0.46; p < 0.01), Upper West region, 89% (CI: 0.01–0.92; p < 0.05), Northern region, 88% (CI: 0.02–0.73; p < 0.05), Ahafo region, 84% (CI: 0.03–0.91; p < 0.05), Eastern region, 70% (CI: 0.10–0.93; p < 0.05) and Ashanti region 66% (CI: 0.12–0.98; p < 0.05). Additionally, a reduced odds of co-payment for drug was significantly associated with Greater Accra region, 75% (CI: 0.11–0.57; p < 0.01), Eastern region, 61% (CI: 0.18–0.88; p < 0.05), Bono region, 59% (CI: 0.18–0.96; p < 0.05), Northern region, 74% (CI: 0.09–0.73; p < 0.05), Savannah region, 75% (CI: 0.08–0.84; p < 0.05) and Upper West region, 86% (CI: 0.05–0.44; p < 0.05).

The regression results further show a high burden of co-payment among some ethic groups. For example, being a Mande is significantly associated with increased co-payment for outpatient consultation (AOR: 5.07; CI: 1.34–19.20; p < 0.05). Finally, being Ga/Dangme (AOR: 2.21; CI: 1.12–4.35), and Mande (AOR: 2.54; CI: 1.19–5.43) are significantly associated with increased co-payment for drugs.

Discussion

The study utilized data from the eighth round of the Ghana Demographic and Health Survey to determine co-payment levels and identify the social and demographic factors associated with co-payment for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs among insured clients of Ghana’s NHIS. We found that a significant proportion of the sample respondents − 58.4%, 52.5% and 48.4% made co-payments for drugs, inpatient care and outpatient consultation, respectively. The determinants of the co-payments, including age, region, wealth status, educational level, and ethnicity.

Although limited literature on co-payment exists for comparison, an earlier study in Ghana found that 47% of insured clients paid OOP for consultations and drugs covered by the scheme [18]. Outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs are some of the basic services covered by the NHIS. These findings undermine the NHIS’s core mandate of providing financial protection and equitable access [28, 29]. Clients often express dissatisfaction with supply-side barriers to accessing the needed range of services covered by the scheme [17, 20]. Inadequate or unavailability of essential medical inputs, including drugs and poor service quality have been documented in earlier studies [30]. These challenges, coupled with perceived low tariffs and reimbursement delays for services rendered, have often compelled health providers to levy insured clients in order to cope with operational bottlenecks [18]. Clients, meanwhile, are less empowered to resist co-payment or feel pressured to accept additional charges to have their condition treated.

Regardless of the reason for introducing co-payment, the practice has multiple adverse effects. First, it deters future and continuous use of health services which can undermine membership enrollment in the NHIS [31, 32]. A recent study among insured and uninsured members of the scheme demonstrated that although the scheme had a positive impact on healthcare utilisation, demand for OOP on the part of health providers was a major underlying factor for non-renewal of membership [33]. Second, households in Ghana have been shown to experience catastrophic health expenditure despite being enrolled in the NHIS [21]. Charging insured clients additional fee could further exacerbate economic wellbeing and push individuals down poverty margins. Thus, when not controlled, co-payment could significantly harm the NHIS goal of promoting access and use of health services toward improving overall health outcomes across populations.

We found that a range of sociodemographic characteristics were significantly linked to co-payment for healthcare services. Patients aged 25–34, as well as individuals from the Eastern, Upper West and Northern regions, exhibited lower odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation and drugs. Co-payment by those aged 25–34 age group, could be attributed in part to their frequent interactions with health facilities, particularly, the females of this age cohort who have high reproductive health needs and generally high health-seeking behaviour [34]. Frequent utilisation creates incentives for co-payments [35, 36]. Ghana currently implements a maternal user fee exemption policy embedded in the NHIS [37]. The policy covers several health service components related to women, including the sequence of maternal care, preventive information, and caesarean sections [38]. This phenomenon could explain the observed lower odds of co-payment in these age categories since there is less dependence on OOP payment for the services even though generally females often experience higher co-payment than men [26].

Older populations often tend to access healthcare more frequently, often due to chronic conditions. Our findings of lower odds of co-payment by those over 45 years could be because of economic factors. Economically disadvantaged individuals often lack access to quality health services [39, 40]. The regional influence on co-payment could be attributed to the variations in general poverty levels across the regions. The Bono, Upper West, Northern, and Ahafo regions have consistently ranked among the poorest in Ghana [41]. Consequently, service providers might be more inclined to expect co-payments from clients in other regions, as wealthier households are more likely to afford such health costs, unlike the poorest households, who may forgo healthcare services when faced with financial constraints [42, 43]. However, we also found that more resourced regions, including Ashanti and Eastern regions, had reduced odds of co-payment for outpatient consultation, while the Greater Accra and Eastern regions had reduced odds for co-payment for drugs. This may be linked to the plurality of service providers and the wide range of private health insurance organisations operating in these regions. Formally employed individuals often subscribe to employer insurance schemes to facilitate access to health services.

The findings showed that compared to having no formal education, attaining primary education, being rich, and belonging to a particular ethnic group, especially Ewe, Mole-Dagbani, Gurma, Ga/Dangme and Mande tribes, significantly increase the likelihood of patients making co-payments. This arises from the fact that having at least a primary education may enhance a patient’s health literacy and understanding of the healthcare system, potentially influencing their perception and management of co-payments. A previous study that examined the impact of clients’ involvement, price perception, and attitudes toward National Health Insurance co-payment adjustments on their healthcare-seeking behavior found that education level positively influences health-seeking behavior [44]. Additionally, healthcare utilization increases with wealth quintiles. The increased likelihood of rich patients making co-payments is because they often have higher healthcare utilization and can afford to make co-payments when necessary [45].

The findings have significant implications for using health insurance as a means to achieve UHC goal in Ghana. The considerable co-payment required from insured individuals for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs indicates that policy interventions addressing co-payment could potentially strengthen the scheme. This is particularly relevant since the NHIS clients’ co-payments for services covered by the scheme are not supported by the NHIA [17]. Our findings thus underscore the need for urgent reforms to strengthen NHIS financing and enforcement mechanisms to eliminate co-payments. Adjusting tariffs to reflect actual service costs, ensuring and sustaining timely reimbursements and stronger monitoring and accountability mechanisms are needed to curb unauthorized charges, ensuring equitable financial access to care and the realization of the UHC goal.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our study presents both strengths and limitations. A notable strength is the use of nationally representative survey data, which is credible due to its robust methodological design. Furthermore, the study’s reliance on a large number of respondents enhances statistical power of the findings and minimizes the margin of error. However, a key limitation is the cross-sectional design, which does not permit causal interpretations of the association between co-payments and socio-demographic characteristics. Additionally, the outcome variables were measured based on self-reports, potentially introducing recall bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides a comprehensive overview of co-payment in Ghana, while also highlighting the need for deeper analysis, possibly through qualitative evaluations, to further explore the intricacies of our findings.

Conclusion

Insured clients of Ghana’s NHIS seeking health care in credentialed health facilities make co-payments for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs that are covered by the scheme. The study highlights the multifaceted determinants of the co-payment, including age, region, wealth status, educational level, and ethnicity. The extent of co-payment can potentially impede Ghana’s progress towards achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC). There is a need to eliminate co-payments for outpatient services, inpatient care, and drugs for insured clients to realize the UHC goal.
