Effects of prehabilitation on outcomes among patients undergoing surgery for lung cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Dan Li, Yan Lv, Rui Wang, Jiaqi Li, Yanqiang Zhang, Yanni Rong, Xiaona Li, Le Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative complications are common after lung cancer surgery and can significantly impair patients’ recovery. Prehabilitation aims to optimize patients’ physiological and functional status through multimodal interventions. This study systematically evaluated the effects of prehabilitation on perioperative outcomes among patients undergoing lung cancer surgery.MethodsIn accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and other relevant databases from inception to January 2026. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing prehabilitation (including exercise-based components) with usual care were eligible for inclusion. The primary outcomes were preoperative changes in six-minute walk distance (Δ6MWD, defined as post-prehabilitation minus baseline values) and postoperative length of hospital stay (LOS). Secondary outcomes included postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) and preoperative changes in health-related quality of life (ΔHRQoL, defined as post-prehabilitation minus baseline values). A random-effects meta-analysis was performed, along with predefined subgroup analyses.ResultsFifteen RCTs involving 1,129 patients were included. The pooled analysis showed that prehabilitation significantly improved preoperative functional exercise capacity, with a mean increase in preoperative Δ6MWD of 37.58 m. Greater improvements were observed with interventions lasting >2 weeks. Prehabilitation also significantly reduce postoperative LOS. Additionally, it significantly reduced the risk of PPCs (odds ratio =0.39) and significantly improved preoperative ΔHRQoL.ConclusionsPrehabilitation effectively enhances functional capacity, reduces LOS, PPCs, and improves quality of life in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, supporting its role as a valuable perioperative management strategy.Keywords: Prehabilitation, lung cancer, outcome, six-minute walk distance (6MWD), randomized controlled trial (RCT)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Prehabilitation effectively enhances functional capacity, reduces postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), shortens length of hospital stay (LOS), and improves quality of life in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, supporting its role as a valuable preoperative management strategy.

What is known and what is new?

• Prehabilitation has been reported to reduce PPCs and promote recovery in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, but evidence remains limited by heterogeneity in intervention protocols and surgical approaches.

• This meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated both preoperative and postoperative outcomes and used change scores [e.g., change in six-minute walk distance (Δ6MWD)] to minimize baseline heterogeneity. In addition, multiple prespecified subgroup analyses based on intervention duration, setting, modality, and surgical approach were conducted, providing a more robust and clinically interpretable assessment of the effects of prehabilitation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support incorporating prehabilitation into preoperative care for patients undergoing lung cancer surgery to improve functional capacity, reduce PPCs, and shortens LOS. Future research should standardize intervention protocols and conduct large, high-quality randomized controlled trials to further clarify the optimal duration, modality, and patient populations that benefit most from prehabilitation.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide (1). Surgical resection is the standard treatment for patients with early-stage lung cancer (2). However, postoperative complications remain frequent, especially in older patients, those with poor preoperative functional status, and individuals with chronic pulmonary diseases or a history of smoking-related comorbidities (3-5). These complications can delay postoperative recovery, extend length of hospital stay (LOS), impair quality of life, and increase postoperative mortality (6). Therefore, optimizing patients’ preoperative physical condition and reducing perioperative risk through preoperative interventions has become an important aspect of perioperative management in lung cancer treatment (7).

Prehabilitation refers to the use of multidisciplinary interventions before surgery to address modifiable patient risk factors, such as poor nutritional status and reduced physical function, with the aim of improving overall physical and psychological condition and increasing tolerance to surgical stress (8,9). Prehabilitation usually includes exercise, nutritional support, and psychological support, forming a multimodal approach (10). Exercise interventions may be supervised or unsupervised and commonly involve aerobic, resistance, and/or breathing exercises to improve cardiopulmonary function and muscle strength (11). Nutritional interventions mainly focus on ensuring adequate energy intake and protein supplementation, while psychological interventions aim to reduce preoperative anxiety and improve patient cooperation and adherence to treatment (12). Prehabilitation was initially applied in patients undergoing elective lung and heart surgeries and has since been extended to those undergoing orthopedic, spinal, and cancer-related procedures, including colorectal, lung, and esophageal surgeries (13). The main goal is to help patients achieve better physical and mental condition before surgery, thereby reducing postoperative complications and improving recovery outcomes (11).

In the field of lung cancer surgery, prehabilitation has shown promising clinical benefits. Evidence indicates that prehabilitation can significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) and other severe complications while concurrently accelerating functional recovery and reducing LOS (14,15). However, existing studies on prehabilitation demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in protocol design, particularly regarding intervention duration and the modalities employed (16,17). Additionally, in the selection of anatomical lung resection techniques, conventional open surgery generally results in greater surgical trauma, whereas video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is less invasive and induces less systemic stress (18,19). These factors complicate direct comparisons across studies, thereby limiting the integration of evidence into clinical practice.

The present study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of prehabilitation on perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery. Furthermore, we conducted detailed subgroup analyses based on intervention duration, type of prehabilitation, and surgical approach to provide more precise guidance for clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0694/rc) (20,21).

Methods

The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under the registration number CRD420261284261.

Literature search

Relevant studies were identified through a comprehensive literature search of four electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CINAHL. The search was conducted from database inception to January 21, 2026, using a predefined search strategy, with full details provided in the Appendix 1. The search was restricted to human studies and full-length articles published in English in peer-reviewed journals. In addition, the reference lists of relevant original studies and review articles were manually screened to identify any further eligible studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for studies were defined according to the PICOS framework:

❖ P (patients): adult patients (aged ≥18 years) scheduled to undergo lung cancer surgery.

❖ P (patients): adult patients (aged ≥18 years) scheduled to undergo lung cancer surgery.

❖ I (intervention): intervention of interest of prehabilitation, defined as a prehabilitation program including only a physical (exercise) component (unimodal prehabilitation) or exercise combined with nutritional and/or psychological support (multimodal prehabilitation). Interventions specifically targeting respiratory function, such as inspiratory muscle training (IMT), were also considered eligible. No restrictions were imposed on exercise duration, frequency, type, or mode of delivery, including supervised or unsupervised programs.

❖ I (intervention): intervention of interest of prehabilitation, defined as a prehabilitation program including only a physical (exercise) component (unimodal prehabilitation) or exercise combined with nutritional and/or psychological support (multimodal prehabilitation). Interventions specifically targeting respiratory function, such as inspiratory muscle training (IMT), were also considered eligible. No restrictions were imposed on exercise duration, frequency, type, or mode of delivery, including supervised or unsupervised programs.

❖ C (comparison): control group defined as patients receiving usual care prior to surgery. Usual care generally consisted of standard preoperative thoracic surgical management, including routine education and counseling (e.g., smoking cessation advice, basic nutritional guidance, medication optimization, and explanation of surgical procedures), and may have included non-supervised basic breathing instructions (such as pursed-lip breathing, diaphragmatic breathing, or use of incentive spirometry). Importantly, patients in the control group did not receive any structured exercise program aimed at systematically improving overall physiological function or functional reserve, nor did they undergo any specialized, high-intensity, or supervised respiratory muscle training (e.g., IMT).

Studies were excluded if they did not specifically include patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, evaluated rehabilitation interventions delivered during the postoperative period, lacked a usual care control group, or employed a non-randomized study design.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (D.L., Y.L.) independently conducted the literature search, study screening, and data extraction according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer made the final decision. For each included study, the following basic characteristics were extracted: author, year, country, total sample size, sample sizes of prehabilitation group and groups, mean age, proportion of male participants, cancer stage, prehabilitation modality, prehabilitation setting, duration of prehabilitation, control group, and relevant outcomes. In addition, detailed data were extracted regarding the components of prehabilitation, including exercise modalities and intensity, nutritional strategies, and psychological interventions. Furthermore, information on adherence or compliance rates, reasons for non-adherence, and reported adverse events was also collected when available.

Risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers (D.L. and Y.L.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials, version 2 (RoB-2) (22). This tool assesses potential bias across five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported results. Each study was judged as having a low, some concern, or high risk of bias according to the RoB-2 criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (L.G.). Quality assessment was not used as an inclusion criterion for the meta-analysis to avoid potential selection bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 and Stata software 17 were used to conduct the systematic review and meta-analysis. Both continuous and dichotomous outcomes were included. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) or standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, depending on whether the same measurement scales were applied across studies. When continuous data were reported as medians with ranges and/or interquartile ranges, the corresponding means and standard deviations were estimated using the method described by Hozo et al. (23). For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratio (OR) with 95% CIs were extracted directly or calculated when necessary. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and the I2 statistic, with I2 values of <25%, 25–75%, and >75% indicating low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity (24), respectively. A random-effects model was applied to pool the results, taking into account between-study variability (20). Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results by changing the effect models. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots for asymmetry.

Results

Study identification

Figure 1 presents the process of literature identification and selection. A total of 892 records were obtained from four electronic databases, including PubMed (n=279), Embase (n=310), Web of Science (n=231), and CINAHL (n=71), along with one additional record identified through the citation of related articles (n=1) (25-27). After the removal of 368 duplicate records, 524 unique records remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. During this screening stage, 492 records were excluded because they were reviews, meta-analyses, or letters, or because they did not address the research question. Subsequently, 32 articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. After full-text review, 15 articles were excluded for the following reasons: the intervention was initiated during or after the postoperative period (n=8) (17,28-34), relevant outcome data were not reported (n=2) (35,36), and the same patient cohort was reported in multiple publications (n=5) (16,37-40). Ultimately, 17 studies were included in the final review (7,14,15,18,19,41-52).

Primary outcome: Δ6MWD

Eight datasets from seven studies measured functional exercise capacity using the 6-min walk test (6MWT) (7,14,15,45,46,48,51). In all included studies, the 6MWD was measured at baseline and again preoperatively. For the preoperative measurement, 4 studies measured the 6MWD immediately after completion of the prehabilitation intervention (7,15,48,51), 2 studies measured it 1 day before surgery (14,46), and 1 study measured it 2 days before surgery (45). The pooled analysis demonstrated that prehabilitation was associated with a statistically significant improvement in theΔ6MWD compared with usual care (MD =37.58 m; 95% CI: 21.84–53.33; P<0.00001, Figure 3A), with moderate heterogeneity (I2=61%). Subgroup analyses showed that interventions for >2 weeks resulted in greater improvements in functional exercise capacity (MD =65.13 m; 95% CI: 46.80–83.46) compared with those lasting ≤2 weeks (MD =26.09 m; 95% CI: 16.55–35.62) (Figure 3B), with a statistically significant between-subgroup difference (P=0.0002). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between multimodal and unimodal prehabilitation programs (MD =42.00 vs. 35.28 m; P=0.66, Figure 3C). Similarly, no significant differences were found between patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery versus open or mixed (open and minimally invasive) surgery (MD =27.73 vs. 48.87 m; P=0.17, Figure 3D). However, due to the limited number of included studies, further subgroup analyses based on prehabilitation setting were not performed. Sensitivity analysis indicated that, upon sequential omission of each individual study, the pooled effect size ranged from 26.99 to 42.74. Despite these fluctuations, the overall direction and magnitude of the effect remained largely consistent. This finding suggests that the combined estimate is stable and robust, with no single study exerting a disproportionate influence on the overall results (Figure 4).

Primary outcome: postoperative LOS

Eleven studies reported the postoperative LOS (14,15,19,42-45,47,48,51,52). The meta-analysis revealed that prehabilitation result in a significant reduction in LOS compared with that of the control group (MD=−1.21; 95% CI: −2.31 to −0.10; P=0.03; Figure 5A), with substantial heterogeneity observed (I2=83%). Subgroup analyses explored potential sources of variation. For interventions lasting >2 weeks, no significant reduction in LOS was observed (MD=−0.27; 95% CI: −1.87 to 1.34; P=0.75). For interventions lasting ≤2 weeks, A significant effect on LOS was found (MD=−2.21; 95% CI: −3.90 to −0.53; P=0.01). No significant difference was observed between these two duration-based subgroups (P=0.10; Figure 5B). Regarding the setting, A significant reduction in LOS was observed in the hospital-based subgroup, including interventions conducted in hospital only or in combination with home-based components (MD=−1.82; 95% CI: −3.46 to −0.19; P=0.03), But no significant reduction in LOS was observed the home-based subgroup (MD=−0.31; 95% CI: −1.71 to 1.10; P=0.67), with no significant difference between the two settings (P=0.17; Figure 5C). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that after exclusion of other studies, the effect size fluctuated between −0.96 and −1.52, but the direction of the effect remained consistently negative. This suggests that the pooled effect size estimate is robust and that no single study significantly altered the direction or statistical significance of the effect (Figure 6). However, due to the limited number of included studies, further subgroup analyses based on prehabilitation modality (multimodal versus unimodal) and surgical approach were not performed.

Secondary outcome: PPCs

Overall, 12 datasets from 11 studies reported the effect of prehabilitation on PPCs (14,15,18,41-43,45,48-51). The pooled results showed that prehabilitation was significantly associated with a lower risk of PPCs (OR =0.39; 95% CI: 0.23–0.64; P=0.0002; I2=42%; Figure 7A). In subgroup analyses, interventions lasting >2 weeks (OR =0.24; 95% CI: 0.11–0.52; P=0.0004) and interventions lasting ≤2 weeks (OR =0.52; 95% CI: 0.28–0.97; P=0.04) were both significantly reduced PPCs, with no significant difference observed between these two subgroups (P=0.12; Figure 7B). Prehabilitation significantly reduced PPCs in hospital-based studies or mixed (hospital-base and home-base) (OR =0.30; 95% CI: 0.20–0.47; P<0.00001), but not in home-based studies (OR =0.59; 95% CI: 0.17–2.01; P=0.40), with no significant difference observed between these two subgroups (P=0.31; Figure 7C). Regarding surgery type, the effect was more pronounced in patients who underwent open or mixed (open and minimally invasive) surgery (OR =0.27; 95% CI: 0.16–0.46; P<0.00001) compared with only minimally invasive surgery (OR =0.62; 95% CI: 0.31–1.21; P=0.16), with no significant difference observed between these subgroups (P=0.06; Figure 7D). However, due to the limited number of included studies, further subgroup analyses based on prehabilitation modality (multimodal versus unimodal) were not performed.

Secondary outcome: ΔHRQoL

Overall, five studies reported the impact of prehabilitation on ΔHRQoL (7,15,18,19,47). ΔHRQoL was defined as the change from baseline to the preoperative visit, and the between-group difference in change scores was used for pooling. Given the use of diverse measurement instruments across studies, including the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (7,18), European Organization for Research and Treatment (EORTC) quality of life questionnaire (QLQ) (15,47), and five-level EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (19), the SMD was applied as the summary effect size to enable comparability. The pooled results indicated that prehabilitation led to a significant improvement in HRQoL score compared with the control treatment (SMD =0.42; 95% CI: 0.22–0.63; P<0.0001; I2=35%; Figure 8). However, due to the limited number of included studies, further subgroup analyses based on prehabilitation modality (multimodal versus unimodal), surgical approach, prehabilitation duration, or prehabilitation setting were not performed.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 17 RCTs involving 1,129 patients with lung cancer. Analysis of the primary outcomes among these patients revealed that prehabilitation significantly improved functional exercise capacity, with a mean increase in Δ6MWD of 37.58 m (P<0.00001). Subgroup analysis indicated that interventions lasting >2 weeks yielded significantly greater improvements than those lasting ≤2 weeks (P=0.002). Significant reduction in postoperative LOS was also observed with prehabilitation (MD=−1.21; P=0.03), regardless of intervention duration or setting, and heterogeneity for LOS was high (I2=83%). Regarding secondary outcomes, prehabilitation significantly reduced the risk of PPCs by 61% (OR =0.39; P=0.0002). Additionally, prehabilitation significantly improved the ΔHRQoL (SMD =0.42; P<0.0001).

The 6MWD, measured in meters, provides an objective assessment of exercise capacity and is therefore a clinically relevant outcome for evaluating the impact of prehabilitation interventions (7,46,53). Skořepa et al. (10) reported that an increased 6MWD in the prehabilitation group compared with the control group in frail and high-risk patients undergoing major abdominal surgery, with a weighted MD of 40.1 m (95% CI 32.7–47.6 m, P<0.00001), which is consistent with the results of our study. In addition, the present study analyzed the Δ6MWD rather than preoperative 6MWD values or postoperative 6MWD values, as reported in other studies (10), which minimized the potential influence of baseline differences. Although one previous meta-analysis also used Δ6MWD, they lacked consistent inclusion criteria and combined different types of changes, such as differences before and after preoperative interventions and differences between postoperative and baseline values (15,27,28). In contrast, our study specifically included only the changes before and after preoperative interventions, thereby avoiding the heterogeneity introduced by mixing different types of change measurements. Further subgroup analysis indicated that interventions lasting >2 weeks resulted in significantly greater improvements compared with those lasting ≤2 weeks. However, due to limited data availability, the optimal duration of prehabilitation could not be determined. In future, larger, high-quality studies are needed to clarify the most effective intervention period.

In the present meta-analysis, the pooled results indicated that prehabilitation significantly reduces postoperative LOS in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery compared with usual care. This finding consistent with the results of several individual randomized trials, including those conducted by Morano et al. and Kökez et al. (42,48), which suggested that intensive preoperative physical therapy may shorten hospitalization. However, our results are contrast with a recent systematic review by Machado et al. (54), which reported that home-based exercise prehabilitation did not result in significant improvements in postoperative complications or LOS among colorectal cancer patients, even after sensitivity analyses restricted to high-quality studies. Although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary outcome, and subgroup analyses likewise did not identify statistically significant differences across predefined categories, substantial heterogeneity was present among the included studies (I2=83%). This heterogeneity likely reflects clinical and methodological variability, including differences in prehabilitation modalities (unimodal versus multimodal interventions), exercise intensity, and surgical approaches. Therefore, while prehabilitation appears to reduce LOS. Further large-scale, methodologically rigorous trials are required to clarify the impact of prehabilitation specifically on LOS.

Despite substantial advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care over the past decade, the incidence of PPCs after lung cancer resection remains considerable, ranging from 7.4–48% (mean, 18.4%) (55,56). As one of the most frequent and clinically significant complications after surgery, PPCs are associated with prolonged LOS, increased rates of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and readmission, reduced long-term survival, and substantial healthcare expenditures (57-59). In the present meta-analysis, which pooled 12 datasets from 11 RCTs, prehabilitation was associated with a significantly reduced risk of PPCs in patients with lung cancer (OR =0.39; 95% CI: 0.23–0.64; P=0.0002). These findings are consistent with recent evidence. Guo et al. (27) reported that preoperative pulmonary rehabilitation reduced the risk of PPCs by 61% in lung cancer patients. Similarly, Amirkhorsravi et al. (60) demonstrated that multimodal prehabilitation significantly decreased PPCs in patients undergoing upper abdominal surgery. Overall, our results reinforce the clinical value of prehabilitation in reducing PPCs among lung cancer patients.

To our knowledge, this meta-analysis included a relatively large number of RCTs that met predefined eligibility criteria, comparing prehabilitation with usual care in patients with lung cancer undergoing surgical resection. This comprehensive inclusion enhanced the overall strength and reliability of the evidence. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although 17 RCTs were included, the sample sizes of the individual studies were generally small, which may have limited statistical power and affected the precision of the pooled estimates. Second, we were unable to fully account for potential confounding factors, including heterogeneity in enrollment criteria, variations in baseline patient characteristics, and differences in prehabilitation protocols. Such clinical and methodological diversity, particularly with respect to intervention components and target populations, likely contributed to the substantial heterogeneity observed and limited the comparability across studies.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis of prehabilitation in patients undergoing lung cancer resection comprehensively evaluated both preoperative and postoperative outcomes. Prehabilitation significantly improved preoperative functional exercise capacity, as demonstrated by a clinically meaningful increase in preoperative Δ6MWD, and was associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative PPCs, a shorter postoperative LOS and improved preoperative ΔHRQoL. Importantly, by using Δ6MWD rather than absolute values, this study minimized the influence of baseline heterogeneity and provided a more robust assessment of intervention-related functional gains. In addition, multiple prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the effects of intervention duration, setting, modality, and surgical approach, further strengthening the interpretability of the findings. Overall, the present study highlights the feasibility and clinical relevance of prehabilitation as an effective strategy for patients with lung cancer.
