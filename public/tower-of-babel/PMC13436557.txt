From Theory to Practice: A Qualitative Study Exploring Change Managers’ Experiences Applying Change Management Frameworks in Hospital Redevelopment Across One Australian State
Authors: Sagda Osman, Ashfaq Chauhan, Corey Adams, Alexander Cardenas, Michelle Moscova, Kate Churruca, Sabe Sabesan, Deepak Bhonagiri, Elizabeth Manias, Rebecca J Mitchell, Natalie Taylor, Reema Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHospital redevelopment projects are among the largest and most costly changes undertaken in health systems. Despite abundant methodologies and frameworks that offer theoretical guidance, the literature reports persistent gaps in managing change of this scale effectively. To examine this gap between theory and implementation of change management in practice, we explored change managers’ experiences of leading change in hospital redevelopment projects across one Australian state, using a qualitative approach. We aimed to understand the change management approaches used, the perceived value and limitations of those approaches, the capabilities change managers need to lead change, and the barriers and enablers encountered through the change cycle.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 28 change managers of varying levels of experience and project scope in hospital redevelopments. Data were analysed using the Framework method. Subthemes were developed inductively from the interviews and then mapped deductively onto the three stages (planning, implementing, and sustaining change) of a statewide health infrastructure change management framework that guided practice in this setting. Inductive subthemes not able to be classified using the framework formed a fourth theme on change managers’ capabilities.FindingsFour themes were developed: 1) Planning for change, 2) Implementing change, 3) Sustaining and reinforcing change, and 4) Change managers’ capabilities and support. Participants described several effective approaches to managing change in hospital redevelopment, including progress tracking, prototypes, site visits, and ongoing stakeholder consultations, with local staff central to facilitating engagement. Executive sponsorship was repeatedly identified as an enabler of change, although securing it early and sustaining it across the project proved a recurring challenge. Further challenges included aligning change plans with broader project processes, transitioning change into business as usual once the project ended, and evaluating whether the change delivered its intended benefits. Some participants reported not being aware of available resources and how to utilise them.ConclusionChange management frameworks offered useful guidance particularly for less experienced change managers. However, participants often described challenges utilising these frameworks for effective stakeholder engagement, for sustaining change in long-term projects, or where change management and project processes were misaligned. The findings have practical implications for successful change management in redevelopment projects, including the importance of structured training and mentoring for change managers, a need for early and sustained executive and stakeholder engagement, planned transition to business as usual, and closer alignment of change management with project delivery so that the people side of change is supported throughout the project lifecycle.

Introduction

Health services around the world are experiencing significant redevelopment and improvement efforts in response to a global movement towards value-based healthcare and a pressing need for sustainability in healthcare delivery.1 These changes are necessary to meet the escalating health demands, driven by factors such as population growth and ageing societies, the rising prevalence of complex and often interrelated health issues,2 and the increasing impact of climate change on public health.3 Transformative redevelopment projects are among the most substantial and costly changes in health services, often spanning several years. For instance, in Australia, health redevelopment projects can cost anywhere from AU$50 million for a small or regional project to over AU$2 billion for a major redevelopment, such as the redevelopment of the Royal Adelaide Hospital.4–6 More than $2.5 billion worth of health infrastructure projects formed part of the 2022–23 budget of one Australian state government,7 and another state committed $2.1 billion to health infrastructure in its 2024–25 budget.8

The literature reports persistent gaps in the quality of change management in hospital redevelopment. For example, failure to align stakeholder interests and incentives can give rise to resistance to adopting and using innovations.9 The literature has also emphasised several potential barriers to successful change management efforts including limited leadership capability to facilitate and support the change process and insufficient organisational commitment to fostering a culture that enables and sustains change.9–11 Issues with resourcing change initiatives, due to workforce shortages, time allocation, and funding, were often described as key barriers to achieving desired outcomes and realising benefits.11 A notable Australian example is a $187 million redevelopment of a regional hospital, where issues with organisational culture and governance, poor planning and resourcing, and limited stakeholder engagement led to delays and operational challenges when transitioning to, and staffing, the new facility.12

Numerous methods, models, and frameworks are available for managing change more effectively in redevelopment projects of this scale. These approaches offer structured guidance to plan, deliver, and embed change in clinical and operational ways of working throughout the stages of change (planning, implementing, and sustaining) and across activities such as stakeholder engagement, workflow and model-of-care redesign, or transition planning.13,14 Examples of such approaches include widely used models such as Kotter’s eight-step model,15 the Prosci ADKAR model,16 and Lewin’s Three-Step Change Model.17,18 Many healthcare organisations may also develop their own context-specific structured approaches to guide change within their setting. Whatever their form, a common principle across these approaches is that effective change management requires engagement of all relevant stakeholders throughout the process, executive sponsorship and leadership, and the interpersonal skills of those leading and managing change.13 Taken together, the reported challenges of managing change effectively in practice, despite an abundance of structured approaches offering theoretical guidance, may point to a gap between the theory of change management and its implementation, and barriers operationalising these approaches in practice.

Previous research on change management in hospital redevelopment has by and large focused on experiences of staff and patients.19–21 However, the experiences of change managers in hospital redevelopment projects has received little attention despite these managers leading change day-to-day and working across the full change cycle. A systematic review of change management models in healthcare identified 38 studies across 10 countries,13 yet none of these studies explored change managers’ experiences in hospital redevelopment. Similarly, another recent systematic review examined the use of change management models and methods applied specifically to digital and virtual care transformation in healthcare, but did not consider change manager experiences.14 Moreover, a qualitative study of healthcare managers and leaders across an Australian state public health system examined their experiences of managing change, reporting limited use of structured change management methods, with hospital redevelopment featuring as only one of several change types reported by participants. Thus, the experiences of change managers specific to managing hospital redevelopment and applying structured change management approaches remain unexamined. Exploring their experiences of both managing large scale change in hospital redevelopment and applying structured approaches may offer valuable insights into where change management theory meets, or fails to meet, the realities of hospital redevelopment.

To distil implementation challenges and identify areas for enhanced support and improvement in managing change initiatives in a large public health system, we sought to capture the experiences of change managers in a sample of redevelopment projects in one Australian state. More specifically, this study aimed to explore: 1) the change management methodologies or approaches used to manage change in redevelopment activities; 2) the perceived value and limitations of those approaches in managing change; 3) the perceived capabilities required of change managers to manage change successfully; and 4) the perceived enablers and barriers to managing change at each stage of the change cycle.

Throughout the paper, we use the term “change manager” to describe someone who manages change activities in health redevelopment projects, but who may hold one of various job titles, including but not limited to change manager, change lead, redevelopment director, or change and transition lead. We use the term “structured approaches” as an umbrella term to refer to the methodologies, models, frameworks, tools used as a roadmap to guide change management, while acknowledging the nuances between these different terms.

Methods

Context

The study was conducted in an Australian state with delivery of care undertaken by geographically based health services and specialty networks that provide health care in a wide range of settings across metropolitan, regional, and rural areas. Within the Health department of the state, an organisation is responsible for the redevelopment projects oversees and is responsible for designing, planning, and constructing of all health infrastructure solutions across the state.

Study Design

The study involved conducting semi-structured interviews to explore the experiences of change managers across diverse projects in metropolitan, regional, and rural areas in one Australian state. The reporting of findings follows the guidelines of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).22

Recruitment and Sampling

A purposive sampling approach23 was used to recruit participants from clinical and non-clinical change roles in metropolitan, regional and rural health districts. The study information and the research team contact details were circulated by the organisation responsible for the redevelopment projects via Email to change managers. They were encouraged to contact the research team if interested in participating in the study. Participants were recruited through the organisation they were employed by and whose change management framework informed the analysis, therefore, several steps were taken to support open and critical responses. Participation was voluntary and interviews were conducted by researchers who are not employed by the organisation responsible for the redevelopment projects. Participants were assured any insights they share would remain anonymous, their responses would be de-identified, and that no individual would be identifiable to their employer. Interviewers made clear that critical views of existing frameworks, resources, and processes were of particular interest to the study.

Ethical Aspects

The ethical aspects of this study were reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of University of New South Wales [Ethics Approval Number: HC190534]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection where participants were informed about publication of deidentified responses as exemplary quotes.

Data Collection

A semi-structured interview technique24 was used to collect participants’ perspectives as it offered flexibility in exploring the topic while accommodating a diverse set of participants across various projects and levels of experience with change management. Individual interviews were conducted by three experienced qualitative researchers (RH, CA, AC) between September–October 2023.

An interview guide was developed to address the research objectives, and the questions were emailed to interviewees in advance. The guide was based on findings from a recent change management review,13 which synthesised principles common across established change management methodologies, including stakeholder engagement, executive sponsorship and leadership, the interpersonal capabilities of those leading change, and the staged process of planning, implementing, and sustaining change. These theoretical domains informed both the structure of the interview guide and the subsequent interpretation of the data. The guide also drew on qualitative work that preceded this study and was refined in collaboration with the project partners. The questions explored change management approaches in redevelopment projects, experiences and perceptions of the value and limitations of current approaches, change manager capabilities, and the perceived required support in redevelopment activities.

Interviews were conducted online and, where not possible, via telephone and lasted from 10 minutes to 57 minutes. The variation in interview length was due to participants’ availability. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim using the built-in transcription feature of Microsoft Teams. Data was collected and analysed iteratively, and interviews continued until theoretical saturation was reached where no new themes were generated. Saturation was assessed iteratively by the team where data collection and analysis proceeded in parallel, and recruitment stopped when additional interviews generated no new themes. While 30 interviews were conducted, two recordings were inaccessible due to technical issues and were excluded from the analysis, leaving 28 interviews to be analysed.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using the Framework Method25,26 which proceeds through five stages: familiarisation with the data, constructing a working analytical framework, indexing the data against that framework, charting, and interpretation. The analysis was both inductive and deductive. This method was selected due to its ability to provide a structured yet comprehensive account of the phenomenon being explored while also capturing the richness and nuances of participants’ unique experiences and backgrounds. Hence, the data was explored within the boundaries of the change management framework used by the organisation responsible for the redevelopment projects as a deductive framework.

Following transcription, two researchers (CA, SO) conducted line-by-line coding to identify words and phrases focusing on the themes developed from the data. The coding process was iterative, and the themes were refined accordingly throughout the analysis. Any discrepancies between the researchers were discussed between the team members until a consensus was reached.27 Following a team-based approach, four researchers (CA, RH, AC, SO) deductively mapped the themes onto the stages of the organisational change management framework. The framework is applied across the state’s public health infrastructure program to guide change in redevelopment projects. It comprises three stages (planning change, implementing change, and sustaining and reinforcing change) and draws on principles common to established change management approaches. To protect the confidentiality of the organisation, the framework is described here in general terms and is not cited directly, as a citation would identify the organisation. Any additional themes relevant to the study objectives that did not fit the framework formed a separate theme on change managers’ capabilities and support. The resulting four themes map onto the four study aims. The approaches used and their perceived value and limitations and enablers and barriers to managing change (ie, aims 1, 2, and 4) run through the planning, implementing, and sustaining themes, while the capabilities required and the support needed (ie, aim 3) are drawn together in the fourth theme. This allowed us to explore participants views on change management approaches, their limitations and the enablers and barriers to be read against the point in the project at which they arise, with the fourth theme on change managers’ capabilities and support cutting through all stages. Reflexivity

This study is part of a research project funded by the National Health and Medical Research Council partnership scheme. The organisation responsible for the redevelopment was a key partner in this project. However, the interviews and analysis were led by partner health services researchers with backgrounds in change management, co-design, implementation science, and qualitative research methods who were not employed by the organisation. The interviews were conducted by two experienced health services researchers with clinical backgrounds (CA and AC). CA is trained as a nurse and holds a Doctorate in Public Health (CA) while AC is trained as a physiotherapist and holds a PhD in health innovation. Analysis and interpretation were carried out by these two researchers together with RH and SO. RH is a professor of health services research with a background in psychology, whose work investigates how increasing stakeholder engagement can contribute to improved healthcare quality, experiences and outcomes. SO is a health services researcher with an engineering background and a PhD in health innovation, with expertise in evaluating complex health interventions. We worked to limit the influence of our backgrounds by coding as a team and checking interpretations against the data throughout.

Two authors (ACa and MM) were employed by that organisation at the time of the study where they supported recruitment and reviewed the final manuscript. However, they took no part in data collection, coding, or analysis. The organisation’s role in the project and in recruitment created a potential for bias where recruitment might have focused on participants that favoured the organisation’s approaches, or participants may be hesitant to openly criticise these approaches. We sought to reduce these risks by keeping the interviewing and analysis team independent of the organisation. Moreover, participants were encouraged by interviewers to share their views openly and were reassured that their responses were confidential.

The sample was fairly evenly split between metropolitan (n = 13, 46%) and regional (n = 14, 50%) locations, with one participant (4%) working across multiple areas. Years of experience ranged widely, from less than one year to over 15 years. The majority of participants (n = 23, 82%) reported their years of experience, with 11 (39%) participants with 1–5 years of experience, 9 (32%) having more than five years experience, and 3 (11%) participants with more than ten years experience in change management. Planning for Change

Interviewees expressed how they approached and planned for change in the early stages of the change process. Through three subthemes, change managers described the critical value of executive sponsorship and support in igniting interest in the change effort and positioning its perceived value. Framing the value of change in terms of the future state was seen as important to engage stakeholders in the change process.

Establishing Support for Change via Executive Sponsorship

Change managers described executive sponsors’ involvement early in establishing the change process as invaluable to provide credibility to the case for change and foster organisational commitment (Table 3, Q1). Crucial to the successful launch of change efforts was the sponsors’ belief in the value the proposed changes would bring to the organisation. Despite this importance, change managers reported a need for greater awareness among executive sponsors about the projects in which they were a sponsor, the project’s purpose or their role of being a sponsor (Table 3, Q2-Q6). Supporting Quotes for “Planning for Change” Theme

Clarity about the role of a change sponsor and its importance was often described by change managers as a key determinant of success to change efforts. Sponsors who were not engaged were often reluctant to fully commit to the project or recognise its value (Table 3, Q3). Participants found it hard to influence other stakeholders in the absence of strong executive sponsorship, resulting in a reliance on project managers to support the day-to-day change activities. While valued, project managers were described as having insufficient influence and limited presence to support project establishment through completion as they were often not available throughout the duration of the project (Table 3).

Change managers often described being outsiders coming into a hospital environment and needing executive support to be able to carry out planned changes and engage stakeholders to deliver desired outcomes (Table 3, Q4). Early engagement and support from executive is important in the success of a project, change managers expressed a need to have early engagement to appreciate the importance of change management in redevelopment projects (Table 3, Q5). Without a full understanding of the complexity and difficulty of the change process and about ways in which to support change managers, an executive’s ability to take timely decisions that support change managers carrying out their job effectively could be restricted (Table 3, Q6).

Projects with a high volume of turnover in executive leadership presented a further challenge, contributing to inconsistent expectations of the project and change management processes. Some change managers reported instances whereby senior leadership had different requirements and expectations, this added to the change load required (Table 3, Q8). In the absence of effective change reporting mechanisms and strong executive sponsorship from the planning stage, a gap in leadership around establishing accountability of the change processes and outcomes among change managers was reported. The value of progress tracking tools was identified as assisting in monitoring change across projects. These tools were also considered a useful resource to identify when and where support was needed from leadership and were useful to increase accountability (Table 3, Q9).

Envisioning the Future State

Interviewees emphasised the importance of reaching a shared vision when planning change. This shared vision was considered best achieved by envisaging the future state of change with stakeholders and highlighting the value it would bring. Yet, interviewees consistently shared challenges achieving this, particularly in the context of new technologies or tools that would be introduced as a result of the change process (Table 3, Q10). According to participants, challenges occurred in getting clinicians to visualise proposed changes due to the absence of a frame of reference or benchmark, leading to subjectivity and speculation about the implications of the change (Table 3, Q11). Interviewees described using prototypes and site visits to establish benchmarks and assist clinicians to envision changes. This approach assisted in establishing a frame of reference for change managers to map back to across the change process. Furthermore, it facilitated effective change management as it helped them understand how the change will affect their day-to-day workflows (Table 3, Q12). However, in the absence of evidence, interviewees described experiences in which the clinical staff deferred to the loudest voice or hierarchical decision-making (Table 3, Q13).

Driving Success via Stakeholders’ Engagement

Participants described early identification and mapping of stakeholders as the cornerstone of effective change planning and as essential to mitigate stakeholder resistance as the change process progressed (Table 3, Q14). Engagement was considered important for aligning and balancing stakeholders’ priorities and expectations to plan a change process with the greatest likelihood of success (Table 3, Q15). Although critical for planning, interviewees reported many challenges around successfully engaging stakeholders in redevelopment projects.

Central in overcoming challenges in engaging stakeholders was the use of local champions within the change site who had a deep connection with the value of the change and its purpose that could be conveyed within the site (Table 3, Q16). Interviewees emphasised the role local staff play as change champions in influencing other members to change their attitudes towards proposed changes and enable stakeholder engagement to be more successful. While large redevelopment projects offered opportunities for transformational change, they presented challenges in engaging stakeholders due to a stable workforce with few personnel changes (Table 3, Q17). This was primarily attributed to the lack of staff with fresh perspectives to act as change champions. Other reported difficulties were dealing with resistance to the notion of any change due to change legacy and staff preference for old ways of working (Table 3, Q18).

Participants noted that stakeholders go through different change journeys across the life of a redevelopment project, underscoring the importance of tailoring engagement techniques (Table 3, Q19). Strategies change managers applied to understand the standpoints of stakeholders included integrating periodic stakeholder group consultations with other methods such as the De Bono’s Six Thinking Hats technique to elicit concerns and to encourage staff to talk openly about their attitudes toward change (Table 3, Q20).

Implementing Change

Interviewees in various change roles and with varying levels of experience shared their approaches of managing the change process across three subthemes. Change managers used various frameworks and methodologies to effectively manage the change process and successfully navigate interpersonal issues when engaging stakeholders. While many change managers favoured structure and simplicity, flexibility was viewed equally as critical to accommodate different contexts and needs and to deal with unforeseen challenges and the dynamic complexity of change.

Balancing Structure and Flexibility in Frameworks

Depending on their expertise level and familiarity with change management methodologies, interviewees’ approaches to managing the change varied greatly. Such approaches included not using or following any formal framework or methodology at all, following simple and structured frameworks, or tailoring and adapting frameworks to fit the specific circumstances of the change process. A key perceived element for effective change management was balancing structure and flexibility in the methodology followed. On the other hand, less experienced change managers preferred using simple, structured frameworks (Table 4, Q24, Q25). These frameworks were perceived as straightforward and practical, making them easier to learn and apply in various change projects regardless of experience level. However, a few encountered challenges in achieving desired outcomes when using some of these methodologies, leading them to revert to traditional project management approaches to manage the change process (Table 4, Q26). Few change managers approached and managed the change without a specific framework or methodology. In such cases, change managers described having a lack of formal training and education on change frameworks and methodologies and identifying this as a gap in their support and training (Table 4, Q27, Q28).

Change as a Complex and Dynamic Process

Change managers described their experience on the ground as highly complex, with lack of alignment between initial change plans and its implementation being a common challenge. A key contributor to this misalignment between plans and reality was the mismatch between the technological and processes side of change and the people involved. While interrelated, change efforts often focused on the former, overlooking the latter, leaving stakeholders behind in the change journey and creating challenges as the project progressed. As such, understanding the complexity of change and its extended impacts on all relevant stakeholders was often perceived as crucial for an effective change process when working with change that involves introducing new models of care (Table 4, Q29, Q30).

Interviewees noted how the overlapping nature of project processes across design, change, and commissioning added a layer of complexity in navigating change (Table 4, Q31). They also reported challenges related to aligning different timelines and components across various departments to achieve desired changes (Table 4, Q32). Due to budget and time constraints, change managers perceived large change projects as not being adequately resourced, therefore relying on department managers to take on the role of change agents and carry out the change process, leading to added workload burden and time pressure (Table 4, Q33).

When managing a change process, interviewees described logistical difficulties related to adherence to timelines and initial plans due to the seasonal pressures such as rain and other weather-related challenges as they caused interruptions and delays in the project timeline (Table 4, Q34). Central in overcoming such challenges was adopting an agile approach to accommodate unplanned changes and deal with uncertainty in redevelopment projects (Table 4, Q35).

Navigating Interpersonal Issues

Navigating difficult conversations was frequently discussed. Many interviewees emphasised the impact of previous leadership and facilitation skills and personal qualities on successfully navigating these conversations towards the desired outcome (Table 4, Q36). In addition, while a few highlighted the role change management frameworks could play in providing structure to approaching such issues, many participants described difficulty in operationalising existing methodologies or frameworks to support them to manage heated and emotive conversations with stakeholders (Table 4, Q37). A key driver of challenging conversations was the issue of change fatigue, which was often pronounced in large and complex projects (Table 4, Q38).

Sustaining and Reinforcing the Change

Two key areas were the basis for two themes around experiences of enablers and barriers for sustaining and reinforcing change at the point of handover to the site. Unlike experiences of planning and implementing change, capturing evidence of whether change was sustained and ensuring benefits were realised was described as difficult for a change manager within the current approach to change management in redevelopment.

Transition planning of the project ownership post implementation was considered by many interviewees as critical for sustaining change by providing a pathway through the transition from the project period into business as usual (BAU). As such, without a clear transition plan, some participants noted that many change projects were handed over to BAU without clarity around ownership of the project post implementation (Table 5, Q40). In such circumstances, particularly when change was not fully embedded into BAU through continuation by site staff, change managers described a high risk of sites reverting to their original state rather than embracing and sustaining change (Table 5, Q41). An abrupt handover often meant that no clear path for progression or sustaining change post implementation was developed or discussed (Table 5, Q42).

Strategies to mitigate the risk of poor sustainment of change were described. Some change managers reported identifying change champions who have a deep connection with and high appreciation of the value of change to act as an informal successor for the change management role to sustain and reinforce change beyond the project period (Table 5, Q43, Q44). Whilst an effective strategy in some circumstances, the lack of consistency and ability to ensure that change champions have the continued capacity to take this role continued to pose limitations.

Realising Benefits and Evaluating Success

Successful change was often described in terms of benefits realisation through evaluation and assessment. Several methods were described by interviewees that were used to assess benefits and evaluate the impact of change included. These methods included post-occupancy evaluations, staff surveys, and regular meetings with end users to collect immediate feedback of small-scale changes (Table 5, Q45). Notable was the diversity of methods used between individuals and project, posing challenges for determining benefits realisation across projects and providing a consistent and recognised output to track benefits realisation for stakeholders to refer to. Participants reported that they were not aware of a standard tool or approach to measure and report the outcomes of the change process itself in benefits realisation, instead describing a method focused on measuring the project benefits which provided limited evidence of the success of the change process. Common challenges in evaluating change processes and their success were described as the qualitative and iterative nature of change processes and their misalignment with the project processes (Table 5, Q46).

Several interviewees discussed post-occupancy evaluations (POEs). They described the process of POEs in major redevelopment projects as lengthy as they were often conducted in groups across sites rather than per the specific project timeline. As such, some raised the issue of not having the opportunity to learn from these POEs, as they departed when the evaluation was concluded (Table 5, Q47), highlighting gaps in the awareness of change managers about the accessibility of POEs results via an online database. Hence, this lack of awareness limited change managers’ ability to learn about what was successful, what has been sustained, and what did not work towards their next project.

Change Managers’ Capabilities and Support

Interviewees described the experiences, skills and knowledge they found advantageous in navigating the challenges and meeting the demands of a change manager role, highlighting important capabilities for success in the role. Their diverse backgrounds and varying levels of experience in change management, infrastructure and redevelopment, clinical experience, and operational experience were discussed along with the personal qualities they applied towards successfully managing change. Interviewees further shared varied experiences on the orientation and training they received upon commencing their roles, as well as the support that was available to them to help them to build capacity for managing change.

Supporting Quotes for “Change Managers’ Capabilities and Support” Theme

Leadership skills and experience more broadly were instrumental for the change manager role. Skills from engaging with and managing stakeholders, collaborating across teams, being receptive to feedback from end users and stakeholders, and devising innovative feedback mechanisms and executing strategies to involve stakeholders in decision-making were all considered of value (Table 6, Q50). Those with past leadership experience were more confident to address the challenges of the change manager role, drawing heavily upon their leadership skillset. The broad and ill-defined scope of change manager’s role, particularly in large and complex projects, was often considered a core challenge (Table 6, Q51). In such circumstances, previous leadership experience was perceived as valuable in helping change managers navigate and tackle the broad range of issues that they encountered.

Insights from Prior Infrastructure, Operational and Clinical Experience

Few interviewees reported having past redevelopment and infrastructure experiences when they first transitioned into a redevelopment change manager role. While preferred, this prior experience was not regarded as an essential prerequisite for the change manager role (Table 6, Q52). However, those who had redevelopment and infrastructure experience described it as helpful in understanding change in a redevelopment context more quickly and hence facilitate managing it.

Several change managers viewed operational knowledge and experience as essential and highly beneficial for understanding the procedures associated with a project and the nature of change management that will be required. Key areas in which these prior experiences were helpful included navigating and understanding governance and hierarchical structures, identifying stakeholders, and understanding the impact of change on different parts of the organisation to manage this effectively (Table 6, Q53).

Most change managers transitioned into their role from a clinical role. They described their clinical background as highly beneficial when on the ground and engaging stakeholders who were clinical staff (Table 6, Q54, Q55). Key areas in which prior clinical experience was helpful included for understanding clinical requirements quickly, managing other staff and their own time (Table 6, Q56).

Upskilling Through Formal Training

Change managers described the absence of an established orientation program for those new to the role, and that support provided to change managers upon commencing their role was limited (Table 6, Q57-Q60). The impacts of this limited orientation and formal training at the outset were described as leaving less experienced managers constantly being in an experimental mode, following an ad-hoc learning process due to their limited change management knowledge (Table 6, Q59, Q60). Therefore, change managers often sought help and guidance on areas such as navigating the complexities of governance structure within the organisation and challenges locating resources required to inform their activities from contacts and direct managers (Table 6, Q61).

A number of interviewees expressed a desire for structured onboarding and workshops upon commencement to equip them with the knowledge and resources critical for understanding governance processes and accessing support along their journeys (Table 6, Q62). A desire for structured formal training was common among those from a clinical background with no prior change experience. For these interviewees, such training was seen as critical for upskilling and bridging their knowledge gaps in change management frameworks and methodologies as a basis for embarking on their roles.

For those who received formal change management training, they described learning about the use of frameworks developed by the organisation responsible for the redevelopments. Some interviewees reported these frameworks as easy to learn and apply to various projects and contexts because they were straightforward and structured. Others reported challenges around applying their learnings due to misalignment between the approach and the nature and timing of their redevelopment change. The time lag between when training was offered, and the application of learning was considered to limit the opportunity to put knowledge acquired during training into practice (Table 6, Q63). Consequently, tailored training to fit the individual needs and timelines of change managers was often described as central to ensuring actionable training and effective upskilling of change managers. Despite a desire for dynamic and ongoing training, interviewees also acknowledged their inability to dedicate time to training and upskilling in the context of a rapid and dynamic change management process (Table 6, Q64).

Learning Through Networking and Mentoring

Regardless of level of experience or sites, interviewees underscored the value of networking in sharing learning about how to overcome challenges in managing change (Table 6, Q65-Q67). Connecting with and visiting peer projects across local health districts was identified as valuable for learning about likely challenges, but also to gain interpersonal support when faced with problems as an isolated change manager. Opportunities to connect in person with peers were considered invaluable sources for accessing support and generating innovation about approaches to manage new challenges. Several specific events and resources were consistently identified as useful for promoting tacit learning and informal mentoring. Networking resources and events that were described as useful included annual change management conferences, weekly local change events across various health districts, and change management mailing lists (Table 6, Q66, Q67).

A number of interviewees highlighted a noticeable improvement in the frequency and the extent of support provided in the change network organised by the organisation responsible for the redevelopment projects as well as other local activities organised by LHDs in recent years (Table 6, Q68). Despite this, change managers working on regional or small projects expressed a desire for more frequent and structured networking opportunities to meet and connect with other change managers going through the same process and facing similar challenges. These change managers described feeling disconnected and siloed, and often unable to attend change conferences and other networking events organised by the organisation responsible for the redevelopment projects. As a result, they had limited access to the support required to navigate challenges (Table 6, Q69).

Interviewees stressed the value of mentorship for less experienced change managers because of the dynamic and changing nature of projects and the varying types of change-related challenges. More specifically, change managers valued the flexible approach mentorship offers to tackle challenges as opposed to having a fixed and structured set of resources or frameworks. Role-modelling with mentors was considered an opportunity for experiential learning that could boost the confidence of inexperienced change managers. By observing experienced change managers and then getting the chance to apply these skills themselves, change managers could develop their leadership, facilitation and negotiation skills (Table 6, Q70).

Despite describing an increased support from their employer in recent years, many change managers found it challenging to identify mentors who can provide sustained support. The perceived time demand and commitment required for mentoring was identified by these change managers as a significant barrier (Table 6, Q71). Senior change managers confirmed that they perceived a value in mentorship for upskilling and providing support to junior change managers. Most expressed interest in becoming mentors, but they described the activity as highly demanding in terms of time and effort and, as such, challenging to fit within an already time-constrained schedule of a senior change manager (Table 6, Q72).

Support for change managers to share their decisions and defend their actions through debriefing was described by a number of participants as insufficient. Many reported limited availability of debriefing opportunities due to the isolated nature of the change manager’s role in redevelopments as it is often a temporary position (Table 6, Q73). Change managers described lacking confidence in their decisions when faced with challenges and uncertainty in the absence of official debriefing opportunities. Mentorship was perceived as an avenue to debrief, reassess and prioritise actions informally, which may consequently increase change manager’s confidence in their decisions (Table 6, Q74). The need for support around decision making through debriefing or mentorship was particularly evident among those in rural and regional projects in which change managers described difficulties due to working on small projects where they were often the only team member (Table 6, Q75).

Scaffolding with Change Management Resources

Templates, toolkits and other materials made available to change managers by their employer were discussed as essential and invaluable resources (Table 6, Q76). Of these resources, change managers described change impact assessment, change management handbook, change reporting template, Gantt charts, and the implementation history assessment tools particularly helpful. Although valued, the complexity of many of the available resources and templates was identified as a central barrier to their effective use (Table 6, Q77-Q79). The inclusion of terminology that was not readily understood by stakeholders or practical for busy clinicians limited their application and required change managers to translate the resources for their use, consuming change managers’ time. These challenges were particularly evident for change managers with limited experience as they reported difficulties adapting these resources to their specific project needs. Moreover, the overwhelming volume of resources often left change managers perplexed and unable to decide on the best resource for their needs and circumstances (Table 6, Q80).

Redevelopment projects for major metropolitan sites versus smaller and often regional or rural projects identified different requirements. The components of the resources relevant to the nature of the redevelopment were not clearly demarked and change managers described this as leaving smaller sites with a large volume of superfluous documentation (Table 6, Q79). As such, they emphasised a need for orientation, mentorship, and further training on tailoring and adapting these templates to fit individual project needs. While some interviewees expressed challenges in identifying the resources available to them, one identified a need for a shared database or repository of resources from previous projects across the state to provide both immediate access to resources and long-term shared learning across projects beyond completion (Table 6, Q81).

Discussion

Findings from our study exploring the views of 28 change managers in an Australian state indicate that structured approaches available for managing change in healthcare redevelopment projects provided a helpful guide through the change process. However, these approaches and resources were mainly used for managing the process side of change and with limited utility for managing the people aspect of it. This may point to barriers in operationalising structured change management approaches effectively, especially when the change process is complex and dynamic. The study also identified key challenges in managing change more effectively in hospital redevelopment. These challenges included difficulties securing early and ongoing executive sponsorship, difficulty engaging the clinical staff, misalignment between change plans and project processes, challenges in transitioning to and embedding change into BAU, and the limited support and training available for change managers to manage change effectively.

Our findings underscore the importance of reaching a shared vision among stakeholders early in the change process to facilitate implementing and sustaining planned changes across various levels. These findings add to a broader body of evidence arguing that stakeholder engagement cannot be treated as a single step early in the change cycle but must be understood as an ongoing practice that builds trust, surfaces tacit knowledge, and legitimises the change in the eyes of those it affects.28,29 Thinking about the extent to which desired outcomes are co-designed and formalised at the outset with the input of the various stakeholders involved provides a shared framework for defining priorities and sustained change in terms of what sustained success looks like.30,31 Additionally, integrating monitoring and feedback throughout the change lifecycle can help encourage and sustain an organisational culture of continuous improvement. This approach could help address change or redevelopment fatigue that was featured in our findings in alignment with the international literature on change management.32–36 Reframing the introduction of change as part of a cultural shift in an organisation whose philosophy is to adapt, reflect and improve continuously may address this change fatigue better. In doing so, being responsive to change is inevitably a core responsibility of an individual’s role, which becomes more of an expectation rather than an extra task they are asked to do.37

Transition to BAU was frequently described by participants as an afterthought, planned late and handed over without clear ownership, which left embedded change vulnerable to reverting after handover. This is consistent with a review of sustainability in healthcare programs, which identified limited forward planning as a barrier to program sustainability.38 Hospital redevelopment complicates this issue as it delivers both a building and a new way of operating. This may help explain the challenges participants reported in evaluating intended benefits, which often begin to materialise only after the building and new ways of working are in use, by which point the project may no longer hold the ownership and governance needed to measure them.39,40 Several participants also expressed frustration that POEs conducted too late, or too far from when a project has concluded, which may suggest conflating POEs with routine evaluations. In Australia and New Zealand, the Australasian Health Infrastructure Alliance developed a POE Framework which sets out the POE process for projects and defined it as a structured analysis of the performance of a healthcare facility, typically undertaken 12 months after a facility has been occupied.41

As such, POEs are deliberately postponed, allowing sufficient time to lapse for transitional issues to settle and for the users to experience the building before assessing it.

Participants often described change in redevelopment as complex and dynamic, and reported that change plans often drifted out of step with project processes. One way to address this issue, suggested by the wider literature, is to use mapping tools alongside change management frameworks. For example, process mapping with an implementation lens can help people work through existing practices and identify where change might best fit, the steps in the process most affected by it, the behaviours of individuals that are associated with it, and how it will impact these behaviours.42,43 By doing so, process mapping can help work out and anticipate challenges and devise solutions that enable those involved to get ahead of the change curve.44 Furthermore, this approach would also likely support the challenges related to envisioning the future state of change as described by our study participants. They shared that being physically in the space where the change would happen was helpful, suggesting that it allowed them to think about how the proposed change will impact their workflows.

The interdependence some participants described, where a change in one department produced effects across many others, also points to a possible role for systems-based approaches. Training change managers in systems approaches may help them work with this dynamic complexity, understand how the parts of the system operate together and contribute to the workings of the organisation and the change process, and how these parts will need to adapt for change to take hold.45,46 An example of using systems approaches to utilise the collective tacit knowledge of stakeholders to manage change effectively and improve communication was explored by Banerjee and Lowalekar47 where they used a Theory of Constraints-based approach to achieve better communication and reach a shared vision, thus mitigating stakeholders’ resistance and effectively managing stakeholders’ relationships.

Implications for Practice

The findings have various implications for managing change in hospital redevelopment projects. Structured training and mentoring, especially for less experienced change managers, is essential for providing needed support. Early engagement with executives and stakeholders is critical for managing change in hospital redevelopment more effectively. Closer alignment of change management with project delivery is essential to ensure that the people side of change is supported across the full project lifecycle. This is most consequential at the transition to BAU, where sustaining the new ways of working and establishing whether the intended benefits were realised both fall outside the project’s funded life. Assigning ownership early on would help sustain change, consistent with the healthcare sustainability literature36 and with project management evidence.39–41

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The study contributes a growing body of empirical work on change in hospital redevelopment by examining a perspective that has received comparatively little attention; the experiences of change managers themselves; those tasked with operationalising change principles and guiding projects through the full change. A further strength is the scale and geographic diversity of the sample with 28 change managers recruited from across metropolitan, regional, and rural health services, covering projects that varied substantially in size, scope, and complexity. This breadth allows the findings to speak to challenges that recur across different redevelopment contexts rather than those of any one site. The use of the Framework method, combined with a team-based analytic approach in which coding was conducted and discussed by four researchers, strengthened the rigour and internal validity of our interpretation.

The study explored change managers’ views who constitute one cohort of stakeholders in redevelopment projects, recognising that their position gives them unique visibility across the full change cycle. However, other stakeholders include sponsors, health planners, consultants, and project managers, and may all have different views and experiences. This study limitation will be further investigated in some of our future work. Furthermore, a number of the projects referenced in this study occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic and with ongoing staff shortages. As with many qualitative studies that rely on self-reported experiences and perceptions, our data may be subject to recall bias or subjective interpretation. Participants were recruited through the organisation whose change management framework informed the analysis, which may have shaped both who volunteered and how freely expressed criticism of that framework and other available resources. The steps described in the methods were taken to reduce this risk but cannot remove it. Interview length varied considerably due to participants availability, from 10 to 57 minutes, so the depth of data differed across participants.

These findings point to several directions for future research. The variation we observed across settings and experience warrants further investigation. A sample purposively designed to contrast metropolitan, regional, and rural projects, and change managers of differing experience and seniority, could establish how the enablers and barriers identified here differ across these contexts and inform more tailored support. Furthermore, the support needs identified here, including structured onboarding, mentoring, peer networks, and adaptable resources, could be developed into interventions and evaluated for their effect on how well change is led and sustained.

Conclusion

Our study examined the experiences of change managers in relation to the use of structured change management approaches in managing health redevelopment change in an Australian context, and what would strengthen their capability to lead change. Our findings point to a gap less in the absence of these structured approaches than in how they were operationalised and adapted in practice; a gap between change management theory and implementation that this study set out to examine. Many participants reported limited executive sponsorship giving rise to managing change effectively, transitioning to business as usual, and evaluating whether change delivered its intended benefits. Practical ways to mitigate these challenges include structured mentoring and peer networks for change managers, especially those with less experience; securing early and sustained executive sponsorship and stakeholder engagement; and routine monitoring of the change process, all of which may help manage change in hospital redevelopment more effectively.
