A Quadratic Lower Bound for Noncommutative Circuits
Pratik Shastri

Abstract
We prove that every fan-in 2 2 noncommutative arithmetic circuit computing the palindrome polynomial Pal n , n ⁡ ( X , Y ) \Pal_{n,n}(X,Y) has size Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) \Omega(n^{2}) . The proof builds on and refines a previous work of the author [ 4 ] . The new ingredients in the proof were generated by Gemini 3.1 Pro.

Introduction

An arithmetic circuit for a noncommutative polynomial
f
is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) which represents a way to compute
f
, starting from variables and field constants (these label the leaves) and using addition and (noncommutative) multiplication as building blocks (these label the internal vertices). Its size is measured in terms of the number of vertices in the underlying DAG. The fan-in of a circuit is defined as the maximum in-degree of any of its vertices. A circuit is said to be homogeneous if it only ever computes homogeneous polynomials. Recently, Chatterjee and Hrubeš

\Omega(n\log n)

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, and let

X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},\qquad Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}

be disjoint sets of noncommuting variables, and let
\Pal_{n,d}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}\langle X,Y\rangle

denote the palindrome polynomial

\Pal_{n,d}(X,Y)=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{i_{j}}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}y_{i_{n+1-j}}\right).

A version of this polynomial already appears in Nisan’s work as a witness separating
formulas from circuits. On the one hand,
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
admits a simple homogeneous circuit of size
O(n^{2})
, obtained from the recursion

\Pal_{n,d}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\,\Pal_{n,d-1}\,y_{i}.

On the other hand, Nisan’s formula lower bound implies that any formula computing

\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
requires size
n^{\Omega(n)}
.

As alluded to before, in a previous work, the author proved a lower bound for circuits computing
\Pal_{n,n}
under certain restrictions on the
syntactic degree
of the circuit. The syntactic degree of a gate is defined recursively as follows: a leaf labeled by a variable has syntactic degree 1, and a leaf labeled by a field constant has syntactic degree 0. For a sum gate, the syntactic degree is the maximum of the syntactic degrees of its children, while for a product gate, it is the sum of the syntactic degrees of its children. The syntactic degree of the circuit is the maximum syntactic degree of any gate. It is well known that if a circuit is homogeneous and computes a polynomial of degree
d
, then, without loss of generality, its syntactic degree may be assumed to be exactly
d
. In
[
4
]
, the author proved the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Every fan-in
2
noncommutative arithmetic circuit computing
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
with syntactic degree
O(n)
has size
\Omega(n^{1+c})
for some constant
c>0
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
proceeds by constructing a "small" spanning set for the coefficient-space of
\Pal_{n,n}
, starting from a small circuit. The current paper uses similar techniques and improves upon
[
4
]
to prove a tight lower bound for
all
circuits, without any restriction on the syntactic degree.

Theorem 1.2
.

Every fan-in
2
noncommutative arithmetic circuit computing
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
has size

\Omega(n^{2})

Proof idea and notation

We now set up the notation used in the proof and describe the proof idea. We continue to use the coefficient space viewpoint from
[
4
]
, which we describe below. These ideas stem originally from Nisan’s work
[
3
]
. A polynomial

f\in\mathbb{F}\langle X,Y\rangle
is said to be
X,Y
-separated if in each nonzero monomial of

f
, every
X
-variable appears before every
Y
-variable. The palindrome polynomial

\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
is
X,Y
-separated. Accordingly, if
f
is
X,Y
-separated, we may write

f=\sum_{m\in Y^{*}}c_{m}\,m,\qquad c_{m}\in\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle.

We write
\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(f\right)\coloneqq c_{m}
, and we define

\CoeffX(f)\coloneqq\{\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(f\right)\mid m\in Y^{*},c_{m}\nequiv 0\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle.

For
f=\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
, the set
\CoeffX(f)
is exactly the set of all degree
n

monomials in the variables
X
. Since distinct noncommutative monomials are linearly
independent, it follows that

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\Span\bigl(\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\bigr)=n^{n}.

The rest of the proof is an upper bound on this same dimension in terms of the size of a
circuit computing
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
. Let
\Psi
be a fan-in
2
circuit of size
s

computing
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
. The spanning-set construction from
[
4
]

associates to
\Psi
a family of sets

\mathcal{B}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle

such that
\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}})
. Every element of
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}

arises from a polynomial
q
by repeatedly multiplying on the left by certain

X
-only polynomials coming from product gates in
\Psi
. Thus each such element admits a
parameterization of the form

p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,q,

where the multipliers
p_{i}
come from strictly decreasing syntactic degree classes.

In the previous paper, we bound the size of the spanning sets themselves. That is no longer
the right quantity here. For general circuits, the set
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
can be too large. The
new step is to apply the linear projection

\pi_{n}:\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle\to\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle

onto the homogeneous degree
n
component and count only those elements of
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}

whose image under
\pi_{n}
is nonzero.

The key observation is that every nonzero multiplier
p_{i}
has minimum degree at least
1
. It
follows that any nonzero parameterization of length
r
has minimum degree at least
r
. Hence if

r>n
, its degree
n
projection is zero. So, after projection, only parameterizations of
length at most
n
can survive.

At this point the problem becomes a counting problem. For each length
r\leq n
, choosing a
valid parameterization amounts to choosing a terminal factor
q
, and then choosing
r

distinct syntactic degree classes together with one multiplier from each chosen class. This
gives an upper bound of

(s+n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}

on the number of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
with nonzero degree
n
projection. Since

\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))
lies in the span of these projections, we obtain

n^{n}\leq(s+n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}.

An estimate now shows that this inequality is impossible when
s=o(n^{2})
. This
yields the required lower bound

s=\Omega(n^{2}).

We now set up some more notation used in the proof. For any nonzero polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle
, define
\mindeg(f)
to be the minimum
degree of a monomial appearing in
f
. The following lemma is immediate:

Lemma 2.1
.

For all nonzero
f,g\in\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle
,

\mindeg(fg)=\mindeg(f)+\mindeg(g).

We allow our circuits to have edges labeled by scalars from the field. For concreteness, we define our circuit model below.

Definition 2.2

(Circuit model)
.

A fan-in
2
noncommutative arithmetic circuit is a directed acyclic graph whose leaves
are labeled by field constants and variables and whose internal nodes (called gates) are labeled by
+

or
\times
. Every internal node has in-degree
2
. The
\times
gates all have designated left and right children. Each gate computes a noncommutative polynomial in the natural way. We allow edges labeled by field constants, these simply scale the polynomial carried by the edge.
The size of the circuit is the total number of vertices in its graph. The depth of a gate
g
is the length of the longest path from a leaf to
g
.

Construction of spanning set from
[
4
]

In this section, we recall the construction of spanning sets from
[
4
]
. Let
\Psi
be a fan-in
2
noncommutative arithmetic circuit of size
s
computing

\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
.

For each gate
g
of
\Psi
, let
\widehat{g}
denote the polynomial computed at
g
.
Recall that its syntactic degree, denoted
d(g)
, is defined in the standard way:
constants have degree
0
, variables have degree
1
, sum gates take the maximum,
and product gates add.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that for every product gate
g=a\times b
in the circuit,
both inputs have syntactic degree at least
1
, for if an input had syntactic degree
0
(a scalar constant),
the multiplication could simply be absorbed into the scalar weight of the outgoing edges of
g
.

Consequently, for every product gate
g=a\times b
, both children
a
and
b

must have strictly positive syntactic degrees. Since
d(g)=d(a)+d(b)
, it follows that the syntactic degrees of
a
and
b
are strictly smaller than that of
g
.

Let

\mathcal{D}_{\Psi}=\{l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots<l_{d^{\prime}}\}

be the set of distinct syntactic degrees appearing in
\Psi
.
For each
k\in[d^{\prime}]
, define

G_{k}\coloneqq\{g\mid\ d(g)=l_{k}\},\qquad P_{k}\coloneqq\{g\in G_{k}\mid\ g\text{ is a product gate}\},\qquad s_{k}\coloneqq|P_{k}|.

Since the sets
P_{k}
are pairwise disjoint,

\sum_{k=1}^{d^{\prime}}s_{k}\leq s.

(3.1)

For each gate
g
, decompose
\widehat{g}
as

\widehat{g}=\widehat{g}_{1}+\widehat{g}_{X}+\widehat{g}_{Y}+\widehat{g}_{XY}+\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{other}},

where:

•

\widehat{g}_{1}
is the constant term of
\widehat{g}
;

•

\widehat{g}_{X}
is the sum of all non-constant monomials of
\widehat{g}

involving only variables from
X
;

•

\widehat{g}_{Y}
is the sum of all non-constant monomials of
\widehat{g}

involving only variables from
Y
;

•

\widehat{g}_{XY}
is the sum of all monomials of
\widehat{g}
containing both

X
and
Y
such that every
X
-variable appears before every
Y
-variable;

•

\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{other}}
is the sum of all remaining monomials.

Now fix
k\in\{2,\dots,d^{\prime}\}
. For every product gate

g=a\times b\in P_{k},

the left input
a
has syntactic degree strictly smaller than
l_{k}
. Define

\mathcal{M}_{k}\coloneqq\{\widehat{a}_{X}:\text{there exists a product gate }g=a\times b\in P_{k}\}.

These are the
left
multipliers used in the spanning-set’s construction.

We define sets

\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle\qquad(1\leq k\leq d^{\prime})

inductively by

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},

and, for
k\geq 2
,

\mathcal{B}_{k}=\mathcal{B}_{k-1}\cup\{\widehat{g}_{X}\mid g\in P_{k}\}\cup\{p\,h\mid p\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\ h\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1}\}.

Claim 3.1
.

For every
k\in[d^{\prime}]
and every gate
g\in G_{k}
, the following inclusions hold:

•

\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{g}_{1}\bigr)\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
;

•

\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{g}_{X}\bigr)\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
;

•

\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{g}_{Y}\bigr)\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
;

•

\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{g}_{XY}\bigr)\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
.

In particular,

\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}).

Proof.

We argue by induction on the depth of the gate
g
. If
g
is a leaf, the claim is immediate from the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

Assume now that the claim holds for all gates of smaller depth. Note that if an edge from
a
to
g
carries a weight
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
, the value passed is
\alpha\widehat{a}
.

By the induction hypothesis, if
a\in G_{j}
for some
j\leq k
, then

\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{a}_{1}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{a}_{X}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{a}_{Y}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl(\widehat{a}_{XY}\bigr)\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{j}).

Since
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
is a vector space, it is closed under scalar multiplication. Therefore the corresponding coefficient sets

\CoeffX\!\bigl((\alpha\widehat{a})_{1}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl((\alpha\widehat{a})_{X}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl((\alpha\widehat{a})_{Y}\bigr),\quad\CoeffX\!\bigl((\alpha\widehat{a})_{XY}\bigr)

are also subsets of
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{j})
. It thus suffices to analyze the unweighted sum and product operations.

Case 1:
g
is a product gate.

Write

g=a\times b.

Observe that both
a
and
b
have syntactic degree strictly smaller than
l_{k}
.

A direct inspection of monomial types gives

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{1}\,\widehat{b}_{1},

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{X}

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{X}+\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{1}+\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{X},

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{Y}\widehat{b}_{Y}+\widehat{a}_{Y}\widehat{b}_{1}+\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{Y},

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{XY}

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{Y}+\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{Y}+\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{XY}+\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{1}+\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{XY}.

We verify the four inclusions in turn.

1.

The constant part.

The set
\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{1})
consists only of a constant.
Since
1\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{k}
, we have

\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{1})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k}).

2.

The
X
-only part.

Since
\widehat{g}_{X}\in\mathbb{F}\langle X\rangle
, we have

\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{X})=\{\widehat{g}_{X}\}.

By construction,
\widehat{g}_{X}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and hence

\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{X})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k}).

3.

The
Y
-only part.

The set
\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{Y})
again consists only of field constants, so

\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{Y})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{1})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k}).

4.

The
XY
-part.

Fix
m\in Y^{*}
. We need to show that

\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(\widehat{g}_{XY}\right)\in\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k}).

Since

\widehat{g}_{XY}=\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{Y}+\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{Y}+\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{XY}+\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{1}+\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{XY},

and
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
is a vector space, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(T\right)\in\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
for each
T\in\left\{\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{Y},\,\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{Y},\,\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{XY},\,\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{1},\,\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{XY}\right\}
.

We verify this for all five terms below.

•

For
\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{Y}
, the coefficient of
m
is a scalar
multiple of
\widehat{a}_{X}
. Since
g=a\times b\in P_{k}
, we have

\widehat{a}_{X}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, and so

\widehat{a}_{X}=\widehat{a}_{X}\cdot 1\in\mathcal{B}_{k},

because
1\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
.

•

For
\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{Y}
, the coefficient of
m
is a linear
combination of terms of the form
\operatorname{coeff}_{m^{\prime}}\!\left(\widehat{a}_{XY}\right)
, where
m^{\prime}

ranges over prefixes of
m
. By the induction hypothesis, each such term lies in

\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k-1})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
.

•

For
\widehat{a}_{X}\widehat{b}_{XY}
, the coefficient of
m
has the form

\widehat{a}_{X}\cdot\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(\widehat{b}_{XY}\right)

By induction,

\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(\widehat{b}_{XY}\right)\in\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k-1}),

and since
\widehat{a}_{X}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, it follows from the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
that
this term lies in
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
.

•

For
\widehat{a}_{XY}\widehat{b}_{1}
, the coefficient of
m
is a scalar
multiple of
\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(\widehat{a}_{XY}\right)
and the claim follows by induction.

•

For
\widehat{a}_{1}\widehat{b}_{XY}
, the same reasoning as above applies.

Therefore
\operatorname{coeff}_{m}\!\left(\widehat{g}_{XY}\right)\in\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
for every
m\in Y^{*}
, so

\CoeffX(\widehat{g}_{XY})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k}).

Case 2:
g
is a sum gate.

Write

g=a+b.

If
g\in G_{k}
, then
a
and
b
lie in classes
G_{k}
and
G_{t}
for some
t\leq k
.
By the induction hypothesis, all four coefficient sets for
a
lie in
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
,
and all four coefficient sets for
b
lie in
\Span(\mathcal{B}_{t})\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{k})
.
Since each of

\widehat{g}_{1},\quad\widehat{g}_{X},\quad\widehat{g}_{Y},\quad\widehat{g}_{XY}

is just the corresponding sum of the pieces for
a
and
b
, the claim follows.

Finally, the output gate computes
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
, and every monomial of
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
is of

XY
. Thus

\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}).

This finishes the proof of the claim.

Projection and Counting Projected Spanning Sets

We now turn to the new part of the argument. We project to homogeneous degree
n
via the linear map
\pi_{n}
and count only those elements in
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
whose degree
n
projections survive.

The construction of the sets
\mathcal{B}_{k}
uses only two kinds of generators: the base elements in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, and the new
X
-only outputs
\widehat{g}_{X}
of product
gates. Everything else is obtained by repeatedly multiplying on the left by elements from
the sets
\mathcal{M}_{k}
with the crucial restriction that we may only multiply by at most one element from each distinct syntactic degree class.

Definition 4.1

(Valid parameterization)
.

Let

\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\{\widehat{g}_{X}\mid g\text{ is a product gate of }\Psi\}.

A
valid parameterization
is an expression

v=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,q,

where

•

r\geq 0
;

•

q\in\mathcal{Q}
;

•

for each
i
, the multiplier
p_{i}
belongs to some set
\mathcal{M}_{m_{i}}
;

•

the class indices strictly decrease:

d^{\prime}\geq m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r}\geq 1.

When
r=0
, the parameterization is simply
v=q
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Every element of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
admits a valid parameterization in which all class indices are at
most
k
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
.

For
k=1
, every element of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
belongs to
\mathcal{Q}
, so it has a valid
parameterization of length
0
.

Now assume the statement holds for
\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
, and let
v\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

If
v\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
, we are done by induction.

Otherwise, either
v=\widehat{g}_{X}
for some product gate
g\in P_{k}
, in which case

v\in\mathcal{Q}
and hence has a valid parameterization of length
0
, or

v=p\,h

for some
p\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
and some
h\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
.

By induction,
h
has a valid parameterization

h=p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,q

with strictly decreasing class indices below
k
. Therefore

v=p\,p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,q

is a valid parameterization of
v
, and its class indices still strictly decrease.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For every gate
g
of
\Psi
, either
\widehat{g}_{X}=0
or

\mindeg(\widehat{g}_{X})\geq 1.

In particular, every nonzero
p\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
satisfies
\mindeg(p)\geq 1
.

Proof.

By definition,
\widehat{g}_{X}
is the sum of all non-constant monomials of
\widehat{g}

consisting only of
X
-variables. Thus, if
\widehat{g}_{X}\neq 0
, then every monomial in

\widehat{g}_{X}
has degree at least
1
, and hence
\mindeg(\widehat{g}_{X})\geq 1
.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Degree truncation)
.

If

v=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,q

is a valid parameterization with
r>n
, then

\pi_{n}(v)=0.

Proof.

If some factor among
p_{1},\dots,p_{r},q
is zero, then
v=0
, and hence
\pi_{n}(v)=0
.
So we may assume that all these factors are nonzero.

Each multiplier
p_{i}
belongs to some
\mathcal{M}_{m_{i}}
, so by
Lemma
4.3
,

\mindeg(p_{i})\geq 1\qquad(1\leq i\leq r).

Also, since
q\in\mathcal{Q}
is nonzero, we have
\mindeg(q)\geq 0
.

Applying Lemma
2.1
repeatedly, we obtain

\mindeg(v)=\mindeg(p_{1})+\cdots+\mindeg(p_{r})+\mindeg(q)\geq r.

If
r>n
, every monomial in
v
has degree strictly greater than
n
. Hence its
homogeneous degree
n
component is zero:

\pi_{n}(v)=0.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For any nonnegative reals
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}
and any integer
r\geq 0
,

e_{r}(a_{1},\dots,a_{t})\leq\frac{1}{r!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\right)^{r},

where
e_{r}
denotes the
r
-th elementary symmetric polynomial.

Proof.

Expand

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\right)^{r}=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in[t]^{r}}a_{j_{1}}\cdots a_{j_{r}}.

Fix
r
distinct indices
u_{1},\dots,u_{r}
. The squarefree monomial

a_{u_{1}}\cdots a_{u_{r}}

appears exactly
r!
times in this expansion, once for each permutation of the indices.
All other terms are nonnegative. Therefore

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\right)^{r}\geq r!\,e_{r}(a_{1},\dots,a_{t}),

which gives the result.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

The number of elements
v\in\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
with nonzero degree
n
projection satisfies

\bigl|\{v\in\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}\mid\pi_{n}(v)\neq 0\}\bigr|\leq(s+n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, every element of
\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
has a valid
parameterization. By Lemma
4.4
, any valid parameterization of
length greater than
n
has zero degree
n
projection. Thus every element counted on
the left admits a valid parameterization of length at most
n
.

So it suffices to count valid parameterizations of lengths
0,1,\dots,n
.

There are at most

|\mathcal{Q}|\leq|\mathcal{B}_{1}|+\#\{\text{product gates of }\Psi\}\leq(n+1)+s=s+n+1

choices for the terminal factor
q
.

Now fix a length
r
. To choose the multipliers
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
, we first choose

r
distinct syntactic degree classes

1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq d^{\prime}

and then choose one multiplier from each corresponding set

\mathcal{M}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{i_{r}}.

Since
|\mathcal{M}_{k}|\leq s_{k}
for every
k
, the number of such choices is at most

e_{r}(s_{1},\dots,s_{d^{\prime}}).

By Lemma
4.5
and (
3.1
),

e_{r}(s_{1},\dots,s_{d^{\prime}})\leq\frac{1}{r!}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d^{\prime}}s_{k}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{s^{r}}{r!}.

Multiplying by the number of choices for
q
and summing over
0\leq r\leq n
, we get

\bigl|\{v\in\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}\mid\pi_{n}(v)\neq 0\}\bigr|\leq(s+n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}.

∎

5
Main lower bound

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Psi
be a fan-in
2
noncommutative arithmetic circuit computing
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
.
If
\Psi
has size
s
, then

s=\Omega(n^{2}).

Proof.

By Claim
3.1
,

\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\subseteq\Span(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}).

Every element of
\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))
is homogeneous of degree exactly
n
, so
\pi_{n}

acts as the identity on this set. Since
\pi_{n}
is a linear map, we have the inclusion

\Span\bigl(\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\bigr)\subseteq\Span\bigl(\pi_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}})\bigr).

Hence

n^{n}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\Span\bigl(\CoeffX(\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y))\bigr)\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\Span\bigl(\pi_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}})\bigr).

The space on the right is spanned by the nonzero vectors
\pi_{n}(v)
with

v\in\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}
, so

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\Span\bigl(\pi_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}})\bigr)\leq\bigl|\{v\in\mathcal{B}_{d^{\prime}}\mid\pi_{n}(v)\neq 0\}\bigr|.

Applying Theorem
4.6
, we obtain

n^{n}\leq(s+n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}.

(5.1)

Since
\Pal_{n,n}(X,Y)
depends on all
2n
variables,
the circuit must contain at least one leaf for each of them. Thus

s\geq 2n>n.

Set
t_{r}\coloneqq\frac{s^{r}}{r!}
. For
1\leq r\leq n
,
\frac{t_{r}}{t_{r-1}}=\frac{s}{r}\geq\frac{s}{n}>1
, and so the sequence
t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
is strictly increasing. Therefore

\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{s^{r}}{r!}\leq(n+1)\frac{s^{n}}{n!}.

Substituting this into (
5.1
), we get

n^{n}\leq(s+n+1)(n+1)\frac{s^{n}}{n!}.

Using the standard bound
n!\geq\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}
, it follows that

n^{n}\leq(s+n+1)(n+1)\left(\frac{es}{n}\right)^{n}.

Assume for contradiction that
s\leq\frac{n^{2}}{2e}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Then
\frac{es}{n}\leq\frac{n}{2}
, and hence

n^{n}\leq(s+n+1)(n+1)\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{n}.

Dividing by
n^{n}
, we get

1\leq(s+n+1)(n+1)\,2^{-n}.

Substituting
s\leq n^{2}/(2e)
, the right hand side is bounded by

\left(\frac{n^{2}}{2e}+n+1\right)(n+1)\,2^{-n},

which tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. This is impossible. Therefore
s\leq n^{2}/(2e)
cannot hold for all sufficiently large
n
. This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎
