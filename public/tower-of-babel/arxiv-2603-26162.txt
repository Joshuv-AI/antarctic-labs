Shuffles of Context-Free Languages along Regular Trajectories
Corentin Barloy, Michaël Cadilhac, Kyle Ockerlund

Abstract
In single-core processors, when multiple processes execute concurrently, they are, in practice, intertwined by a scheduler as a single thread of execution. The language-theoretic operation that corresponds to this is the shuffle of two languages: in general, this is defined as the set of words obtained by interleaving words from the first and second language in an arbitrary fashion. It is well known that regular languages are closed under shuffles, while context-free languages (CFL) are not. Following an established line of research, this paper considers shufflings according to regular “trajectories,” that is, subject to scheduling constraints expressed by an automaton. Unsurprisingly, some trajectories, such as “a word from the first language first, then a word from the second,” allow for CFLs to be shuffled into CFLs, while some other trajectories do not. This paper provides a robust toolset to show that a given trajectory would always shuffle two nonregular CFLs into a nonCFL. In the case of deterministic CFLs (DCFLs), a salient trichotomy of trajectories depending on how they shuffle DCFLs is provided. These results are based on intricate expressiveness lemmas for CFLs and DCFLs of independent interest, the latter lemma relying on a recent result of Jančar and Šíma (MFCS’2021).

Introduction

In the theory of concurrency and parallel computation, many composition
operations ultimately boil down to a variant of the shuffle operation (see,
e.g.,
[
14
,
17
,
13
,
7
,
3
,
1
,
16
]

for early examples from the 1970s to the 1990s). The theoretical study of
shuffles was thus initiated, starting in the 1970s, as a means of formalizing
interactions and synchronization of processes.

Types of shuffles.
As concurrency may take many forms,
theoretical work has turned to restricting the “full” shuffle operation to
more constrained interleavings between processes. Bérard
[
2
]
, in
particular, introduced
literal shuffles
, that enforce that the processes
strictly alternate — she refers to it as modeling step-lock transmission in
single communication channels. Further widening the definition of shuffles,
Mateescu et al.
[
11
]
introduced
shuffles along a
trajectory
, where a trajectory is a language of words over
\{s,t\}
, and
such a word indicates in which order the processes are scheduled (
s
for the
first one,
t
for the second). For instance,
(s+t)^{*}
is the trajectory of
the full shuffle, and
(st)^{*}
would force that the two processes strictly
alternate, during the same length of time. Shuffles along trajectories
generalize many language-theoretic operations, including for example concatenation
(trajectory
s^{*}t^{*}
).

Questions about shuffles.
Historically, the most prominent
questions about shuffles revolved around closure properties. While regular
languages are easily seen to be closed under the full shuffle, Latteux famously
showed
[
10
]
, in the late 1970s, that the full shuffle of two CFLs is
a CFL if, and only if, one of the two languages is regular.
1
1

1

Let us note
here that this only holds if the two languages are over disjoint alphabets,
which is a standard assumption we will be making. Shuffling languages over a
common alphabet is, in addition to being less natural in most concurrent
scenarios, not well behaved: one can show that for any language
E\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
containing the empty word
\varepsilon
, with
\#\notin\Sigma
,
the full shuffle of
\{w_{1}\#w_{2}\mid w_{1}\in\Sigma^{*}\land w_{2}\in E\}

and
\{w_{1}\#w_{2}\mid w_{1}\in E\land w_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}\}
is simply

\Sigma^{*}\#\Sigma^{*}\#\Sigma^{*}
.

In fact, Ogden et al.
[
14
]
exhibited DCFLs that shuffle into
NP-complete languages. On the other hand, Bérard
[
2
]
shows that
there are nonregular CFLs which can be shuffled along the trajectory

(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})
into a CFL. She asks in particular if this is also true of
the trajectory
(s^{*}+t^{*})(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})
, a question that has been open since
then. Mateescu et al.
[
11
]
expand on these results, focusing in
particular on regular trajectories (Section 5 therein). They show, for
instance, that the shuffle of a CFL and a regular language along a regular
trajectory is always CFL.

This rugged landscape leaves open an important question: which regular
trajectories
do
preserve context-freeness (in the sense that the shuffle
of two CFLs is CFL)?
These questions can be seen as variants of — or a preliminary step to — the
so-called
parallelization problem
, which asks, given a nonCFL, whether it
is expressible as the shuffle along some trajectory of two CFLs. Crisp answers
to these questions would not only clarify the landscape of regular trajectories,
but allow to understand when regular schedulers over context-free processes
require additional computational power to be seen as a single thread of
context-free execution.

Contributions.
The contributions of this paper follow two broad
lines:

1.

In
\Cref
sec:coup, we investigate couplings within PDA: Briefly, two
subsections of a word are said to be coupled by a PDA if, for all accepting
runs, a symbol is pushed during the first subsection, and popped during the
second. We show, relying on some surgery on the runs, that if couplings are
rare, then the PDA recognizes a regular language. A strengthening is provided
for the case where the PDA, which may itself be nondeterministic, recognizes a
deterministic CFL, relying on a deep result of Jančar and Šíma
[
8
]
. The
proofs of these lemmas, dubbed the Coupling Lemmas, are provided in

\Cref
sec:couppda,sec:coupdpda, which can be safely skipped on first reading.
We note that these sections are entirely independent of shuffles; they simply
provide a toolbox to study how couplings appear.

2.

In
\Cref
sec:shuf, we turn to shufflings proper. We first study how
couplings survive a shuffle, leading to two so-called Resilience Lemmas. We
then show two salient results: (1) We provide infinite families of
trajectories that
always
shuffle two nonregular CFLs into a nonCFL; and
(2) We partition regular trajectories into three categories:

CFL
′

\babelhyphen
nobreaksafe
, under which shuffles of CFLs are always CFLs;

DCFL
′

\babelhyphen
nobreakhostile
, under which shuffles of nonregular DCFLs are always
nonCFL;
DCFL
′

\babelhyphen
nobreakmixed
, under which nonregular DCFLs shuffle sometimes
into CFLs, sometimes into nonCFLs. We give a decidable characterization of
each of these categories.

We note that we partially answer Bérard’s open question: we can show that the
shuffle along
(s^{*}+t^{*})(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})

never
preserves nonregular
DCFLs, but we cannot answer the same for CFLs. However, our results also imply
that the same is true for the trajectory
(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})
, for which Bérard
showed there
are
nonregular CFLs that shuffle into a CFL, hinting at the
fact that a full classification for CFL-preservation might prove to be much more
complex.

Preliminaries

We rely on standard concepts of automata theory, as presented for instance by
Sipser
[
18
]
. We make precise only the notations that are somewhat
idiosyncratic, but expect the reader to know about languages, finite

Words.
We usually write

for alphabets and

empty word. For a word

, we write

and

PDA, CFL, DCFL.
A PDA is a tuple

A=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},F)
with
Q
a set of states,

\Sigma
the input alphabet,
\Gamma
the tape alphabet,

\delta\colon Q\times(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\})\times(\Gamma\cup\{\varepsilon\})\to\mathcal{P}(Q\times(\Gamma\cup\{\varepsilon\}))
the
transition function,

the initial state, and

the set of final states.
That
\delta(q,a,s)\ni(q^{\prime},s^{\prime})
is to be interpreted as: from state
q
, reading

a
with
s
on top of the stack, go to state
q^{\prime}
and replace the top of the
stack with
s^{\prime}
. Note that any of
a,s,s^{\prime}
can be
\varepsilon
, which corresponds to not
reading from the word, not reading from the stack, or not writing to the stack,
respectively. When a (pushdown) automaton is under discussion, we will use the
elements of the tuple as above freely, without repeating
“
A=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},F)
.” In all of our PDA, we will make the standard
assumptions that every transition either pushes or pops one symbol, but not
both, and that there are no cycles of
\varepsilon
-transitions. The PDA is
deterministic if for every state
q
, letter
a\in\Sigma
and top of stack

s\in\Gamma
, there is only one valid transition that can be taken.

PDA define the class of context-free languages (CFLs) while deterministic PDA
define the class of deterministic context-free languages (DCFLs). Following
Jančar and Šíma
[
8
]
, we write
CFL
′
for the set of CFLs that are not regular,
and similarly for
DCFL
′
.

A
run

r
is a finite sequence of transitions in the PDA, such that

r[i]
ends where
r[i+1]
starts for every
i
and the stack semantic is
correct. The label
w
of
r
is the concatenation of the labels of its
transitions. The contents of the stack at the end of
r
is written

\mathsf{stk}(r)\in\Gamma^{*}
, with the top of the stack at the rightmost position, and we
abbreviate
\mathsf{stk}(r[:i])
as
\mathsf{stk}(r,i)
. Similarly,
\mathsf{stt}(r)
is the state
reached by
r
, and
\mathsf{stt}(r[:i])
is abbreviated as
\mathsf{stt}(r,i)
. A run is

accepting
if it starts in
q_{0}
with an empty stack and ends in a state
in
F
(the contents of the stack not being relevant for acceptance).

From runs to labels.
Since
r
may contain

\varepsilon
\babelhyphen
nobreaktransitions, we introduce notations for the correspondence between the
letters of its label
w
and the transitions of
r
: we let
r\langle i\rangle
be the
transition that reads
w[i]
. We let
r\langle i:j\rangle
be the transitions that read
the subword
w[i:j]
, excluding
\varepsilon

\babelhyphen
nobreaktransitions at the extremities — that is,

r\langle i:j\rangle
starts with
r\langle i\rangle
and ends with
r\langle j\rangle
. We let

r\langle i:j\rangle^{\varepsilon}
be similar to
r\langle i:j\rangle
but including
\varepsilon

\babelhyphen
nobreaktransitions at the
extremities — it still holds that the label of the subrun
r\langle i:j\rangle^{\varepsilon}
is

w[i:j]
. Finally, when
w=xyz
, where
y=w[i:j]
for some

, we
simply write, abusing notation,

for

and similarly for

r\langle y\rangle^{\varepsilon}
. In all such cases, the particular factorization of

in which

appears is provided to avoid potential ambiguity arising from multiple
occurrences of

. Note, in all of these notations, we see the transitions
extracted from

within the context
of

; for instance,

would formally be defined as the set of

in
r

of the transitions that read
w[i:j]
. We sometimes make use of this, and
allow writing, e.g.,

to mean that

is a transition in
the subrun of
r
that reads
y
.

6.4.1
Hard and easy strongly-connected components in DFA

Let

There is a finite computable set of accepting SCC-patterns such that the set

The

We further make use of common techniques in the study of semilinear sets.

7
Conclusion and Perspectives

We have examined the conditions under which shuffling two nonregular CFL along a
regular trajectory results in another CFL. In doing so, we investigated the
notion of
couplings
, i.e., portions of words where a PDA has to push and
pop respectively. We demonstrated that these couplings must exist (Coupling
Lemmas), that they can survive being shuffled (Resilience Lemmas), and applied
these tools to the study of trajectories of shuffles. There, we demonstrate that
some trajectories never shuffle two nonCFLs into CFLs, and further characterized
the behavior of said trajectories w.r.t. shuffling DCFLs.

The most pressing question left open by this work is to extend this latter
characterization to CFLs. Bérard
[
2
]
hints at the fact that this
will be much harder: Indeed,
(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})
can shuffle some nonregular
CFLs into a CFL, and it is not known if the same holds for

(s^{*}+t^{*})(st)^{*}(s^{*}+t^{*})
. We do not venture to conjecture one way or
another.

Going further than shuffles along regular trajectories, one could study

context-free
trajectories, an avenue of research started by Mateescu et
al.
[
11
]
. Further, (trajectory-based) variants of the so-called

synchronized
shuffle
[
21
]
, in which some letters act as
synchronizations, ought to be studied in the same way, in particular given their
relevance in molecular biology. (See also
[
20
]
for an extensive list
of references pertaining to this shuffle.)

Since semilinear sets are effectively closed under complement, it is sufficient to argue that we can find grids in them.
Let
\phi
be a formula defining a semilinear set

.

Without loss of generality, we assume that every lines involved in
\phi

To do that, we notice that we can shift the origin of the plan without affecting the existence of an antigrid.
Formally, there are only finitely many lines in
\phi

(i,j)
be a point such that every intersection point
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
occurs such that
i^{\prime}<i
or
j^{\prime}<j
.
Then define
S^{\prime}=(S-(i,j))\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
; it is again semilinear by replacing every occurrence of
x
(resp.
y
) in
\phi
by
x-i
(resp.
y-j
).
We can drop every half-plan atomic formula so that the remaining ones have non-intersecting lines.
It is clear that there is an antigrid is
S
iff there is one is
S^{\prime}
.

For
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
two non-intersecting lines, we can define an order that corresponds to the intuition that
l_{1}
is on the left of
l_{2}
.
To do that, notice that
l_{1}
separates then plan
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
in two regions: one with infinitely many points of the x-axis, and the other with infinitely many points of the y-axis.
Then
l_{1}\leq l_{2}
iff
l_{2}
belongs to the second to the former region.
This allows to enumerate all lines in
\phi
from left-to-right, according to that order:
l_{1}\leq l_{2}\leq\cdots\leq l_{n}
.
We consider the polyhedra
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n}
such that
p_{i}
is the region strictly between
l_{i}
and
l_{i+1}
.
Thus the
l_{i}
and

form a partition of
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
.
We sort them into cones and bands.
Any region between two non-parallel lines is called a
cone
.
Any region between two parallel lines is called a

.
We also consider that the lines are bands.
We call any of these two a

.
The partition now reads:
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}=\bigcup_{r\text{ region}}r=\bigcup_{c\text{ cone}}c\cup\bigcup_{b\text{ band}}b
.

We can now take into account the periodic information contained in
S
.
Here we assume that every modular atomic predicate uses the same modulus

Assume we only consider points in a fixed region

Then every half-plan atomic formula in

Thus adding
(p,0)

.
Let

(i,j)
is

has a grid iff

First, assume that

.
Without loss of generality, by taking a subgrid, we can assume that

and

.
The key observation about cones, that is not shared by bands, is that the grid intersects each one of them.
Indeed, the grid intersects any square of length

Let
c

(i,j)\in U\times V\cap c

By definition of the core, this implies that
(i\text{ mod }p,j\text{ mod }p)\in\mathsf{core}(c)

However, because of the period

Thus we have found a common point to the cores of every cones.

Second, assume that
\bigcap_{c\text{ cone}}\mathsf{core}(c)\neq\emptyset

Consider the grid

On any cone,

Remark C.44
.

The previous decidability algorithm yields a
\mathsf{PSPACE}
upper bound.
It stands for finding both grids and antigrids, as complementing a formula does not yield a blowup (and the algorithm works also with CNFs).
Complexity not being the main subject of the present article, we only briefly sketch why it stands.
We start by describing the size of a formula
\psi
: any atomic formula with parameters
c,d,e
(and
p
) has size
|c|+|d|+|e|+|p|
, and
|\psi|
is the sum of the size of all its atomic subformulas.
The first step of the algorithm is to shift the formula to avoid interesting lines.
By Cramer’s rule, the intersection point of two lines only incur a quadratic blowup.
Thus the new shifted formula only has a polynomial blowup.
Moreover, there is a linear number of lines involved, and thus a linear number of regions.
The common period
p
can be, on the worst case where every involved periods are different primes, exponentially large.
This implies that the size of the cores, and their computation, take an exponential time.
The final condition is simply the computation of the intersection of linear number of exponential sets, yielding an
\mathsf{EXPTIME}
algorithm.
This can be strengthened into
\mathsf{coPSPACE}
by noting that we can guess an element and check if it is in the intersection.
The checking takes polynomial time as arithmetic operation are polynomial is the bitsize, and thus checking membership in regions and in the semilinear set can be done in polynomial time.
We conclude by Savitch’s theorem.
Note that the main blowup comes from the size of the global period, therefore whenever the LCM of all periods is polynomially related to the size of
\psi
(for instance when they are all the same), we have a
\mathsf{PTIME}
algorithm instead.
