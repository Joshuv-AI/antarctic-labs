Diagnostically Challenging Cardiac Myxoma Presenting as Acute Myocardial Infarction

History of Presentation

We present a case of a 31-year-old woman who developed sudden-onset chest pain and syncope while walking. When first responders arrived, the electrocardiogram detected self-terminating ventricular tachycardia and inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figure 1). She was brought urgently to the hospital for acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Upon presentation, she looked generally unwell, diaphoretic, lethargic but conscious and able to answer questions. The patient had ongoing burning in the neck radiating to the chest. The patient had normal heart sounds, no extra heart sounds, and no murmurs. Otherwise, her physical examination was largely noncontributory.Figure 1Electrocardiogram Demonstrating Inferior ST-Segment Elevation

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of acute myeloid leukemia treated during childhood, splenectomy, and thyroid enlargement.

Differential Diagnosis

Our main differential diagnoses at presentation were acute coronary syndrome, spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD), coronary embolism, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, acute aortic syndrome, and pulmonary embolism.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations showed a high-sensitivity troponin elevation of 16 ng/L on presentation, which peaked at 2,066 ng/L. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was 2.54 mIU/L, and hemoglobin was 101 g/L. Emergent cardiac catheterization showed abrupt tapering of the left anterior descending artery, its diagonal branches, and the obtuse marginal branch of the left circumflex, findings consistent with an embolic phenomenon with differential diagnoses of spasm post–cardiac arrest and SCAD (Figure 2). The right coronary artery was normal, but its mid-portion gave rise to a small branch that perfused multiple left atrial microvessels suspicious for a feeding vessel for a cardiac myxoma (Figure 3). Left ventriculography showed mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and apical regional wall motion abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 54%, segmental left ventricular dysfunction with severe hypokinesis of the lateral and inferolateral walls, and an irregular left atrial mass (4 × 3 × 2 cm) arising from the interatrial septum with contour suggestive of a villiform myxoma prolapsing across the mitral annulus (Video 1). The diagnosis of left atrial cardiac myxoma with embolization causing acute myocardial infarction was made.Figure 2Coronary Angiogram Demonstrating Abrupt Termination of the Distal Left-Sided VesselsEmergent cardiac catheterization showed abrupt tapering of the left anterior descending artery, its diagonal branches, and the obtuse marginal branch of the left circumflex (yellow arrows pointing to this), findings consistent with an embolic phenomenon with differential diagnoses of spasm post–cardiac arrest and SCAD. Management

Given the ischemic presentation with arrhythmia, there was concern for ongoing embolic potential, and the patient was taken for emergency resection of the left atrial myxoma. The procedure was performed via median sternotomy with bicaval cannulation and transseptal incision, with extension onto the left atrial dome. The tumor was pedunculated, villiform, and friable. It was removed en bloc, and the resulting defect in the interatrial septum was repaired with autologous pericardium. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postoperative course was uncomplicated. She was discharged home on postoperative day 12 because she lived out of province and had to arrange transport home.

Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with an incidence of approximately 0.02%.1 Myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumor. They are most often sporadic (with a subset associated with the Carney complex), and the vast majority originate in the left atrium.2 Left atrial myxomas cause left-sided obstruction, syncope, and congestive heart failure. They can also be associated with constitutional symptoms. Myxomas can also present as systemic embolization and resulting complications, with irregular tumor surface, as present in this case, being an important risk factor.3 We present a case of left atrial myxoma that manifested as acute myocardial infarction.

This was a diagnostic challenge and thus can serve as a learning opportunity. Typically, acute myocardial infarction in a young woman would lead to a differential of stress cardiomyopathy or SCAD.4,5 Stress cardiomyopathy is characterized by transient regional systolic dysfunction, typically of the left ventricle. It mimics myocardial infarction but is differentiated by the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease or acute plaque rupture on angiography.4 SCAD is not caused by atherosclerotic plaque rupture, iatrogenic disease, or trauma; it is characterized by intimal tear, intramural hematoma, and false lumen formation, which can obstruct coronary blood flow and cause myocardial ischemia. SCAD is a cause of acute coronary syndrome in young women without any known cardiovascular risk factors.5

The diagnosis of stress cardiomyopathy was ruled out by echocardiography. Meanwhile, angiography was more difficult to exclude, as the abrupt change in vessel diameter between normal and diseased segments could suggest type 2 SCAD. However, the abnormal left atrial feeding vessel in association with coronary angiographic findings suggested an embolic phenomenon from a cardiac tumor, which was confirmed by echocardiography in the cardiac catheterization laboratory.

Conclusions

This case is an unusual presentation of an embolizing left atrial myxoma presenting as/ acute myocardial infarction in a young woman.
