Comparative Radiopacity of Additively Manufactured Permanent and Temporary Resins and Conventional Resin‐Based Composites
Authors: Sinem Coşkun‐Albayrak, Ayşenur Öncü, Sedanur Sakalli, Esra Yildirim‐Manav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate whether radiopacity differs among additively manufactured permanent resins, temporary resins, and conventional resin‐based composites at clinically relevant thicknesses.Materials and MethodsEight dental materials were evaluated and categorized into four groups: additively manufactured permanent resins (VarseoSmile Crown Plus, Saremco Print CrownTec), additively manufactured temporary resins (Arma Resin TEMP Teeth, Alias Temp C&B), conventional self‐cure resin‐based composites (Stela Automix, Stela Capsule), and conventional light‐cure resin‐based composites (Gradia Posterior, G‐ænial Injectable). Rectangular specimens (5 × 5 mm) were fabricated at two standardized thicknesses (1 and 2 mm) (n = 10). Extracted human tooth sections and a 21‐step aluminum wedge were used as reference standards. Radiographic images were obtained using a digital intraoral radiography system, and radiopacity values were measured in mmAl using ImageJ software. Statistical analysis was performed using Kruskal–Wallis, Dunn–Bonferroni, and Mann–Whitney U tests (α = 0.05).ResultsSignificant differences in radiopacity were observed among the tested materials (p < 0.05). Additively manufactured temporary resins demonstrated the lowest radiopacity values, whereas conventional resin‐based composites exhibited the highest radiopacity. Permanent additively manufactured resins showed intermediate radiopacity values. Increasing specimen thickness from 1 to 2 mm significantly increased radiopacity values for all materials (p < 0.001). Several temporary additively manufactured materials presented radiopacity values lower than dentin and enamel.ConclusionThe findings suggest that radiopacity varies significantly among restorative materials and is influenced by specimen thickness. In particular, the relatively low radiopacity of some additively manufactured resins may limit their radiographic detectability, which should be considered during material selection in clinical practice.Clinical SignificanceRadiopacity differences among restorative materials may influence the detection of recurrent caries and restoration margins. Lower radiopacity of some additively manufactured resins highlights the importance of careful material selection for accurate radiographic diagnosis.Keywords: 3D‐printed resins, additive manufacturing, digital radiography, radiopacity, resin‐based composites, restorative dentistry

Introduction

Radiopacity is a fundamental property of restorative and bioactive dental materials, as it directly affects radiographic diagnosis, treatment planning, and long‐term clinical monitoring [1, 2, 3]. Adequate radiopacity enables clinicians to differentiate restorative materials from adjacent dental hard tissues, thereby facilitating the detection of recurrent caries, marginal discrepancies, restoration overhangs, and material degradation [1, 2]. In contrast, materials with insufficient radiographic contrast may obscure critical diagnostic details and compromise clinical decision‐making [3]. For this reason, international standards such as ISO 4049 stipulate that polymer‐based restorative materials should exhibit radiopacity equal to or greater than that of an equivalent thickness of aluminum [4]. Compliance with this standard is clinically essential, as inadequate radiopacity may hinder the detection of restoration margins and recurrent caries, potentially affecting diagnostic accuracy and long‐term treatment outcomes.

The radiopacity of resin‐based materials is primarily governed by their inorganic filler content, including the type, concentration, and atomic number of radiopaque elements such as barium, strontium, zirconium, and ytterbium [5, 6]. In addition, resin matrix composition, filler loading, and polymerization characteristics may further influence radiographic behavior, leading to substantial variability among different classes of materials, including conventional hybrid composites, bulk‐fill systems, and flowable resins [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that even materials within the same category may show clinically relevant differences in radiopacity, emphasizing the need for material‐specific evaluation [2, 7].

With the rapid expansion of digital dentistry, additive manufacturing technologies have been increasingly integrated into restorative workflows [8, 9]. Additively manufactured permanent resin materials have been introduced as alternatives to Computer‐Aided Design and Computer‐Aided Manufacturing (CAD–CAM)‐milled composites for crowns, inlays, and onlays, offering advantages such as reduced material waste, simplified fabrication, and high manufacturing precision [8, 9, 10]. While their mechanical and esthetic properties have been investigated in recent studies, evidence regarding their radiopacity and compliance with ISO requirements remains limited [10, 11]. Recent investigations evaluating CAD–CAM and printable restorative materials have also demonstrated variability in radiopacity values depending on material composition and imaging parameters [12, 13, 14].

In parallel, three‐dimensional (3D)‐printed temporary resin materials have become widely used for interim restorations during diagnostic, surgical, and restorative phases of treatment [10]. These temporary restorations may remain intraorally for extended periods and are often present in close proximity to definitive restorations or bioactive bases. Despite their frequent clinical use, the radiopacity of 3D‐printed temporary resins has received comparatively little attention, raising concerns regarding their radiographic detectability and potential diagnostic ambiguity [10, 11]. Therefore, evaluating both permanent and temporary additively manufactured materials alongside conventional resin‐based composites is clinically relevant, as it allows a comprehensive comparison of their radiographic performance within the context of current restorative workflows.

Considering the increasing clinical use of additively manufactured dental materials and the limited evidence regarding their radiopacity, a comprehensive comparison with conventional resin‐based composites is required. Previous studies have demonstrated that radiopacity varies depending on filler composition, material category, and imaging parameters [2, 7, 15, 16]. Furthermore, specimen thickness has been reported to influence radiographic density due to X‐ray attenuation principles [17, 18, 19, 20].

Therefore, the aim of the present in vitro study was to evaluate and compare the radiopacity of permanent and temporary additively manufactured resin materials and conventional resin‐based composites at standardized thicknesses.

Based on the existing literature and scientific background, the following research hypotheses were formulated:

Significant differences would exist in radiopacity among permanent additively manufactured resins, temporary additively manufactured resins, and conventional resin‐based composites.

Specimen thickness would significantly influence the radiopacity of the tested materials.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This in vitro study was designed to comparatively evaluate the radiopacity of permanent and temporary additively manufactured resin materials and conventional resin‐based composites at two standardized thicknesses (1 and 2 mm). The study protocol followed the recommendations of ISO 4049 for radiopacity assessment of polymer‐based restorative materials [4].

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). An effect size of 0.40, based on a previous radiopacity study with a similar experimental design and corresponding to a medium effect size according to Cohen's classification, along with an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, was selected [12]. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 10 specimens per group would be sufficient to detect significant differences among materials. Therefore, 10 specimens were prepared for each material‐thickness combination.

Preparation of Tooth Reference Specimens

Extracted human premolars were collected following approval from the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (2025/220). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for the use of their extracted teeth for research purposes. Teeth were cleaned of soft tissue remnants and stored in distilled water until use. The premolars were then sectioned into standardized slices with thicknesses of 1.0 ± 0.03 mm and 2.0 ± 0.03 mm using a low‐speed precision cutting machine under continuous water cooling. One premolar section was used as a reference for each thickness.

Specimen Preparation

For each material, rectangular specimens (5 × 5 mm) were fabricated at two standardized thicknesses (1.0 ± 0.03 mm and 2.0 ± 0.03 mm), with 10 specimens prepared for each material–thickness combination (n = 10), resulting in a total of 160 specimens.

Fabrication of Additively Manufactured Resin Specimens

Specimens for the additively manufactured materials (permanent and temporary resins) were designed using CAD software and exported as Standard Tessellation Language (STL) files. All specimens were fabricated using a digital light processing (DLP) 3D printer (T‐Rox, Tolard, Turkey), with the largest flat surface oriented parallel to the build platform (0° build orientation). The layer thickness was standardized at 100 μm for all printed materials. Printing parameters, including exposure time and light intensity (405 nm wavelength), were controlled by the printer software according to the manufacturer's recommended settings and kept constant for all specimens.

Following printing, specimens were rinsed in 99% isopropyl alcohol to remove residual uncured resin, air‐dried, and post‐cured in a light‐curing unit (Otoflash G171, NK‐Optik GmbH, Germany) for 15 min under standardized conditions according to the manufacturers' recommendations. Environmental conditions during printing and post‐processing were maintained at room temperature. Support structures, when present, were removed prior to surface finishing.

Fabrication of Conventional Resin Composite Specimens

Specimens for conventional resin‐based composites were fabricated using a stainless‐steel split mold (5 × 5 mm). The mold was positioned between Mylar strips and glass slides to obtain flat and parallel surfaces.

Self‐cure bulk‐fill composites were inserted into the mold and allowed to polymerize under standardized laboratory conditions without light activation. Light‐cured composites were polymerized using a light‐emitting diode (LED) curing unit operating at an irradiance of 1200 mW/cm2 for 40 s, with the light guide positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface.

After polymerization, specimens were removed from the mold and visually inspected under magnification. Specimens exhibiting voids, surface defects, or edge irregularities were discarded and remade.

Surface Finishing and Thickness Standardization

All specimens were finished on both flat surfaces using silicon carbide abrasive papers (180‐, 320‐, and 600‐grit) under continuous water irrigation. Polishing was performed in a single direction for each grit, with rotation of the specimen by 90° between steps to ensure uniform reduction.

Final thickness was verified using a digital caliper with 0.01‐mm resolution. Only specimens within the predefined tolerance range (±0.03 mm) were included for radiographic evaluation.

Radiographic Procedure

Radiographic images were obtained using a wall‐mounted intraoral X‐ray device (NewTom RxDc, NewTom CEFLA, Imola, Italy) operating at 60 kVp and 7 mA, with an exposure time of 0.20 s [13, 14].

Specimens were positioned on number 2 photostimulable phosphor (PSP) plates (CS 7200 Imaging Plates, Carestream Dental, Atlanta, GA, USA) alongside enamel and dentin sections obtained from a sound human tooth. A custom‐made acrylic positioning device was used to standardize the object‐to‐focus distance (30 cm) and ensure perpendicular alignment of the X‐ray beam.

All radiographs were acquired using the same PSP plate and scanned immediately after exposure with a PSP scanner (CS 7200, Carestream Dental, Atlanta, GA, USA) to avoid potential variability related to plate sensitivity or fading. Radiopacity Measurement

Radiopacity was quantified by measuring mean gray values (MGVs) using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). All specimens were radiographed simultaneously with a 21‐step aluminum step wedge (99.5% purity; 0.5 mm thickness increments, range: 0.5–10.5 mm Al) under standardized exposure conditions.

For each specimen, three standardized regions of interest (ROI) (10 × 10 pixels) were selected from homogeneous areas, and the mean value was calculated. Radiopacity values were then converted into millimeters of aluminum equivalent (mmAl) using linear interpolation between adjacent steps of the aluminum wedge, as described previously [14, 15, 16]. For materials exceeding the highest aluminum step, extrapolation was performed using the calibration curve.

All measurements were obtained from 10 independent radiographs per material and performed by a single experienced oral radiologist blinded to the material identity.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Radiopacity values were expressed as mm Al. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, were calculated for each material at both specimen thicknesses (1 and 2 mm).

Because the data did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, nonparametric statistical tests were applied. Differences in radiopacity among the tested materials were analyzed separately for each thickness using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc tests to identify specific group differences. To evaluate the effect of specimen thickness (1 vs. 2 mm) within each material, Mann–Whitney U tests were used. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Inter‐Material Comparisons at 1 mm

At 1 mm thickness, statistically significant differences were observed among the tested materials (p < 0.05). The lowest radiopacity values were recorded for the temporary additively manufactured resins ART and ADT, with no significant difference between them (p > 0.05). Both materials exhibited radiopacity values lower than dentin and enamel.

Among the restorative materials, VCP, SPC, and GI demonstrated comparable radiopacity values. VCP showed significantly higher radiopacity than ART and ADT. SPC exhibited significantly higher radiopacity than the temporary materials and dentin, while not differing significantly from VCP or GI.

GI demonstrated significantly higher radiopacity than the temporary materials and dentin but did not differ significantly from SPC or SA. SA exhibited significantly higher radiopacity than ART, ADT, dentin, enamel, VCP, and SPC, while not differing significantly from GI or SC.

SC showed significantly higher radiopacity than the temporary materials, dentin, enamel, and the additively manufactured crown materials, but did not differ significantly from SA or GP. GP exhibited significantly higher radiopacity than all materials except SC.

Inter‐Material Comparisons at 2 mm

At 2 mm thickness, statistically significant differences were also observed among the materials (p < 0.05). The lowest radiopacity values were again recorded for the temporary additively manufactured resins ART and ADT. Enamel exhibited higher radiopacity than dentin and the temporary materials, but lower values than the composite restorative materials.

Among the restorative materials, VCP demonstrated higher radiopacity than dentin and enamel, whereas SPC showed significantly higher radiopacity than VCP and enamel. GI exhibited significantly higher radiopacity than both additively manufactured crown materials but lower radiopacity than SA, SC, and GP.

The highest radiopacity values were observed for SA, SC, and GP, with no statistically significant differences among these materials.

Intra‐Material Thickness Comparisons

Comparison of radiopacity values between 1 and 2 mm thicknesses revealed a statistically significant increase for all tested materials (Bonferroni‐corrected p < 0.001). At 2 mm thickness, all materials demonstrated higher radiopacity values compared with 1 mm.

Discussion

Radiopacity is a critical property of restorative dental materials because it directly affects radiographic diagnosis, treatment planning, and long‐term clinical monitoring. In the present study, significant differences in radiopacity were observed among additively manufactured permanent resins, temporary additively manufactured resins, and conventional resin‐based composites. These findings confirm the first research hypothesis and demonstrate that material composition plays a decisive role in radiographic detectability, particularly in emerging 3D‐printed restorative materials. The results further emphasize that radiopacity should be considered an essential parameter when evaluating new additive manufacturing materials introduced into clinical practice [1, 2, 3, 4].

The lowest radiopacity values were observed in temporary additively manufactured resins. This finding may reflect formulation priorities in temporary printable materials, where printability, surface accuracy, and ease of processing may be prioritized over radiographic performance. As a result, the relatively low inorganic filler content and limited incorporation of radiopacifying agents may reduce X‐ray attenuation in these materials. Previous studies have similarly reported that resin‐based CAD/CAM and 3D‐printed materials may demonstrate lower radiopacity compared with conventional restorative materials due to differences in filler composition and resin matrix characteristics [8, 9, 10, 11]. Nejat et al. also reported that certain CAD/CAM restorative materials exhibited radiopacity values lower than dentin, which may complicate radiographic identification of restoration margins and recurrent caries [12]. In agreement with these findings, temporary additively manufactured resins in the present study demonstrated radiopacity values lower than both enamel and dentin, suggesting potential diagnostic limitations in clinical situations.

In contrast, conventional resin‐based composites demonstrated the highest radiopacity values among all tested materials. Notably, the self‐curing bulk‐fill composites exhibited the greatest radiopacity. This finding may be attributed to the presence of high‐atomic‐number radiopaque fillers such as barium and ytterbium incorporated into their formulation. These elements increase X‐ray attenuation and enhance radiographic visibility. Previous studies have reported that composite materials containing radiopaque fillers exhibit significantly higher radiopacity values compared with materials containing primarily silica‐based fillers [5, 6]. The present findings support this observation and suggest that conventional filler technologies remain highly effective in achieving adequate radiographic contrast.

From a clinical perspective, adequate radiopacity facilitates the detection of recurrent caries, marginal discrepancies, and restoration overhangs. Therefore, materials with insufficient radiopacity may complicate radiographic interpretation and potentially increase the risk of diagnostic errors. The lower radiopacity observed in temporary printable materials may therefore represent a clinically relevant limitation, particularly in cases where temporary restorations remain intraorally for extended periods [1, 2].

Permanent additively manufactured resins demonstrated intermediate radiopacity values. These materials exhibited radiopacity values higher than enamel but lower than several conventional composites. This finding suggests that although permanent printable materials may provide acceptable radiographic detectability, their radiographic performance remains inferior to conventional composite materials. Radiopacity of additively manufactured resins may depend on several factors, including filler composition, filler loading, printing orientation, and post‐curing protocols [8, 9, 10]. As additive manufacturing technologies continue to evolve, improvements in resin formulation may enhance radiographic properties and clinical applicability.

The second research hypothesis, which proposed that specimen thickness would influence radiopacity, was also confirmed. Increasing specimen thickness from 1 to 2 mm significantly increased radiopacity values for all tested materials. This observation is consistent with the physical principles of X‐ray attenuation, where thicker specimens absorb a greater portion of the X‐ray beam and therefore produce higher radiographic density [14, 15]. Similar thickness‐dependent increases in radiopacity have been reported in previous studies evaluating restorative materials [13]. These findings suggest that radiographic detectability depends not only on material composition but also on restoration geometry, which may influence clinical interpretation.

Another important finding of the present study was the variability in radiopacity among materials within the same category. This variability suggests that material classification alone may not be sufficient to predict radiographic performance. Therefore, material‐specific evaluation remains essential when selecting restorative materials for clinical applications. Previous studies have also demonstrated significant variability in radiopacity among composite materials and CAD/CAM restorative materials, further supporting this observation [2, 7, 16].

In radiopacity studies, aluminum step wedges are commonly used as reference standards; however, comparison with natural dental tissues provides clinically relevant information. Materials with radiopacity equal to or greater than dentin are generally recommended to facilitate the detection of recurrent caries and restoration margins [16]. In the present study, temporary additively manufactured resins demonstrated radiopacity values lower than dentin and enamel, whereas conventional composite materials demonstrated higher radiopacity values. These findings suggest that conventional composites may provide improved radiographic detectability, while temporary printable materials may require further development to enhance radiographic performance.

According to ISO 4049, polymer‐based restorative materials should exhibit radiopacity equal to or greater than aluminum of the same thickness [4]. In the present study, some additively manufactured resins, particularly temporary materials, demonstrated relatively low radiopacity values. From a clinical perspective, radiopacity lower than dentin may compromise the detection of restoration margins and recurrent caries [1, 2, 16]. These findings suggest that compliance with ISO 4049 is critical for ensuring adequate radiographic detectability and highlight the need for improved radiopaque filler incorporation in additively manufactured dental materials.

Although this study was conducted under standardized in vitro conditions, several limitations should be considered. Radiopacity measurements obtained in vitro may not fully reflect clinical conditions, as various factors related to imaging and patient variability can influence radiographic interpretation [1, 3, 5, 6]. In addition, this study was limited to two‐dimensional radiographic imaging; therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to other imaging modalities such as cone‐beam computed tomography. Observer‐related variability was not assessed, which may affect clinical interpretation. Future studies incorporating observer‐based evaluation and clinical validation are recommended.

Overall, the findings of the present study demonstrate that radiopacity varies significantly among additively manufactured and conventional restorative materials and is influenced by specimen thickness. These results highlight the importance of considering radiopacity when selecting restorative materials, particularly with the increasing use of additive manufacturing technologies in restorative dentistry.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, it was concluded that: Research Hypothesis #1 was confirmed. Significant differences in radiopacity were observed among permanent additively manufactured resins, temporary additively manufactured resins, and conventional resin‐based composites. Temporary additively manufactured resins demonstrated the lowest radiopacity values, whereas conventional resin‐based composites demonstrated the highest radiopacity values.

Research Hypothesis #2 was confirmed. Increasing specimen thickness from 1 to 2 mm significantly increased radiopacity values for all tested materials.
