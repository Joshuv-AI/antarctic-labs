Intraperitoneal blue LED irradiation as a potential approach to suppress gastric cancer peritoneal dissemination in a murine model
Authors: Kengo Kai, Takumi Ishizuka, Jin Matsumoto, Koki Shimamawari, Baljinnyam Lkham-Erdene, Toshiki Kubota, Phyu Synn Oo, Kham Mo Aung, Hirofumi Matsui, Makoto Ikenoue, Atsushi Nanashima, Yoshitaka Hishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peritoneal dissemination remains a critical factor associated with a poor prognosis in gastric cancer. Blue light irradiation has recently been reported to exert wavelength-specific cytotoxic effects on cancer cells. This study evaluated the feasibility, safety, and antitumor efficacy of intraoperative intraperitoneal blue LED (IPBL) irradiation using a murine gastric cancer peritoneal dissemination model. BALB/c nude mice were intraperitoneally inoculated with HGC27 cells and treated with IPBL irradiation via a cylindrical fiber-optic diffuser. Tumor burden was quantitatively assessed by total tumor weight and peritoneal carcinomatosis index (PCI), and perioperative safety was evaluated by clinical findings, serum biochemistry, histopathology, and scanning electron microscopy. The total PCI was markedly reduced in the IPBL group in comparison to the control group (7.2 ± 6.9 vs. 21.3 ± 3.1, P < 0.01). No procedure-related mortality, ascites formation, or macroscopic peritoneal damage was observed. Histological analyses demonstrated preservation of the mesothelial layer, although mild submesothelial collagen deposition and mesothelial flattening were noted. These results indicate that IPBL irradiation is a feasible and well-tolerated intraoperative intervention that significantly suppresses peritoneal dissemination in a murine gastric cancer model and may serve as a promising surgical adjunct to reduce postoperative peritoneal recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57473-7.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, with approximately 23% of cases occurring in Eastern Asia1. In Japan, although the incidence of GC has gradually declined, it remains the third most common cancer in men and the fourth in women, with mortality ranking third overall2. Establishing effective therapeutic strategies for GC is therefore of considerable clinical importance. The recent decrease in mortality rates is largely attributed to advances in endoscopic techniques, enabling earlier detection. Even with recent advances including novel anticancer agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the median survival of patients with unresectable advanced GC remains relatively limited, ranging from 12.9 to 13.8 months in the Checkmate649, Keynote849, and ToGA trials3–5.

Peritoneal dissemination represents one of the most critical determinants of a poor prognosis in GC. Once cancer cells have seeded the peritoneal cavity, the disease is often associated with an extensive intraperitoneal tumor burden, rapid progression, and resistance to conventional systemic therapies6. The presence of peritoneal metastases limits the efficacy of surgical resection and contributes to high rates of recurrence even after curative-intent gastrectomy. Moreover, therapeutic options for established peritoneal disease are limited, and responses to standard chemotherapy are typically transient. As a result, peritoneal dissemination remains a major challenge in clinical management and is a key factor driving the urgent need for novel preventive and therapeutic strategies7,8.

Intraoperative peritoneal lavage cytology positivity is a particularly important prognostic factor, as it often precedes peritoneal dissemination and subsequent recurrence. The reported intraoperative frequency of positive lavage cytology ranges from 4% to 41%9, with higher rates observed in advanced tumors and incidence of approximately 10% in lesions invading the muscularis propria or deeper layers (T3 or beyond)3. A positive lavage result has been shown to be a stronger prognostic predictor than either tumor depth or lymph node involvement, underscoring its critical role in determining clinical outcomes4. Although palliative chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment for established peritoneal dissemination, optimal management strategies for patients with positive peritoneal lavage cytology without overt dissemination remain unsatisfactory. Gastrectomy combined with preoperative or postoperative chemotherapy is sometimes performed; however, median survival remains limited at 22.8 months2. The prognosis is particularly poor in patients with diffuse-type histology or in those who do not receive adjuvant therapy, with reported 5-year survival rates of only 21.8% even with S-1 or cisplatin-based regimens2. These findings highlight the urgent need for more effective approaches.

Phototherapy has recently emerged as a minimally invasive strategy capable of inducing selective cytotoxicity. In particular, blue light (wavelength 400–500 nm) has been reported to trigger apoptosis and inhibit proliferation in various cancer cell types, including malignant melanoma10, colon cancer11, malignant lymphoma12, pancreatic cancer13, leukemia14, and sarcoma15. In contrast, conventional phototherapies aimed at reaching deep-seated tumors, such as photodynamic therapy16 or photoimmunotherapy17, often employ red light (wavelength ~ 660 nm), which is less absorbed by hemoglobin. However, these approaches typically require the use of photosensitizers and strict light-shielding protocols, posing practical and logistical challenges. The strong absorption of blue light by hemoglobin limits tissue penetration, reducing efficacy in deeply invasive or highly vascularized tumors18. However, this physical characteristic may be advantageous in the context of preventing postoperative peritoneal dissemination in advanced GC, where residual free-floating cancer cells and microscopic tumor clusters are located on or near the peritoneal surface. Such cells are directly exposed to intraperitoneal light, making them ideal targets for blue light-based interventions.

Blue light has demonstrated wavelength-specific cytotoxic effects in vitro, selectively inhibiting the proliferation of GC cells while sparing non-malignant cells19. Its limited tissue penetration, previously regarded as a disadvantage for treating deeply invasive lesions, may therefore be beneficial for selectively targeting superficial peritoneal disease while minimizing damage to underlying tissue. In addition, blue light irradiation is non-thermal and can be spatially confined, reducing toxicity and making it well suited to intraoperative or immediate postoperative application. Despite these promising properties, intraoperative blue light irradiation has not yet been evaluated in vivo as a strategy to suppress postoperative peritoneal dissemination in advanced cancer. This lack of experimental evidence represents a critical gap in the assessment of both the therapeutic efficacy and safety of this approach.

In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of intraperitoneal blue light-emitting diode (LED) irradiation using a murine model in which GC cell lines were inoculated into the peritoneal cavity. Intraperitoneal blue LED (IPBL) irradiation markedly suppressed peritoneal dissemination, as evidenced by a significant reduction in both the number and size of peritoneal metastatic nodules. These findings suggest that intraoperative or immediate postoperative IPBL irradiation may represent a promising strategy to reduce the risk of peritoneal recurrence in patients with advanced GC.

Results

Blue light-emitting diode irradiation suppresses proliferation of gastric cancer cells

To evaluate wavelength-dependent effects of LED irradiation on GC cell growth, HGC27, RGK1, and RGM1 cells were exposed to blue (406 nm), orange (610 nm), or red (660 nm) light at an identical total energy density of 16.9 J/cm2. Cell viability was assessed 24 h after irradiation using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. A significant reduction in viability was observed in all cell lines following blue light irradiation, whereas orange or red light showed no detectable effects relative to non-irradiated controls (Fig. 1a).

We next examined the effect of blue light irradiation duration in HGC27 and RGK1 cells. Cells were exposed to blue light for 30, 60, or 120 min. In HGC27 cells, cell viability was reduced in a duration-dependent manner, with suppression detectable as early as 1 h after 30 min of irradiation. Longer exposure times resulted in marked cytotoxicity, reducing viability to approximately 30% after 60 min and to nearly undetectable levels after 120 min. In contrast, RGK1 cells showed no growth inhibition after 30–60 min of blue light exposure, and a transient increase in viable cell number was observed at 24 h after 30 min of irradiation. However, non-irradiated control data demonstrated a comparable proliferation pattern during the same observation period, suggesting that blue light irradiation did not induce sustained abnormal proliferative activity in RGK1 cells. Nevertheless, 120 min of blue light irradiation induced near-complete loss of viable RGK1 cells (Fig. 1b).

Consistent with these findings, microscopic observation demonstrated reduced cell density and morphological changes, including cell rounding, shrinkage, and partial detachment, particularly after prolonged blue light exposure. Representative phase-contrast images obtained before irradiation, 1 h after irradiation, and 24 h after irradiation are shown in Fig. 1c for HGC27 cells exposed to blue light for 60 min and RGK1 cells exposed for 120 min (Fig. 1c).

Blue light-emitting diode irradiation induces apoptotic cell death

To investigate the mechanism of blue light-induced cytotoxicity, apoptosis was assessed in HGC27 cells, on the basis of previous reports describing blue light-induced apoptosis11–21. Based on preliminary experiments using 30- and 60-min irradiation conditions in the MTT assay, a 45-min irradiation protocol was selected as an intermediate condition that allowed detection of apoptotic cells while avoiding both insufficient biological effects and excessive cell loss. TUNEL staining revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells following blue light irradiation, with TUNEL-positive cells increasing to 6.1 ± 2.8% at 1 h vs. 2.9 ± 1.4% in controls (Fig. 2a, b). An immunocytochemical analysis further demonstrated increased the expression of cleaved caspase-3 after blue light irradiation, supporting the involvement of apoptosis in blue light-induced cell death (Fig. 2c). However, because only limited time points were evaluated in the present study, the peak timing and overall kinetics of apoptotic responses following blue light irradiation remain unclear.

Characterization of the blue light-emitting diode probe for in vivo experiments

Measurement of the relationship between distance from the probe and light intensity demonstrated that an irradiance of ≥ 3 mW could be maintained within 5 mm from the probe tip (Fig. 3a). Based on these findings, an irradiation protocol was designed for 10-week-old nude mice, in which the abdominal cavity (estimated transverse diameter: 25–30 mm) was sequentially irradiated by dividing the target area into five regions. Each region received 5 min of blue light irradiation, resulting in a total irradiation time of 25 min (Fig. 3b). To evaluate potential thermal effects of the cylindrical fiber-optic diffuser used for in vivo IPBL irradiation, surface temperature changes were assessed using infrared thermography. Irradiation of the diffuser did not result in any detectable heat accumulation along the irradiated region (Fig. 3c). Thermal imaging demonstrated that the temperature of the LED fiber remained within a range comparable to ambient room temperature, and no localized temperature elevation was observed at the emission site of the cylindrical diffuser during irradiation. In addition, continuous thermal monitoring over a 30-min irradiation period demonstrated stable temperature profiles without progressive heat accumulation at the central light-emitting region of the probe. Furthermore, time-course evaluation of irradiance during continuous 30-min irradiation demonstrated stable output intensity without significant fluctuation (Fig. 3d). These findings indicated that both probe temperature and light output remained stable throughout the irradiation period under the experimental conditions used in this study.

Intraperitoneal blue light-emitting diode irradiation suppresses tumor progression in a peritoneal dissemination model

The experimental schedule is shown in Fig. 4a. IPBL irradiation was initiated two weeks after the intraperitoneal inoculation of HGC27 cells, and mice were euthanized four weeks later. No differences in general condition, food intake, or ascites formation were observed between groups. At six weeks after inoculation, body weight in the IPBL group was significantly lower relative to the control group (26.9 ± 1.3 g vs. 28.6 ± 1.1 g; P < 0.05; Fig. 4b).

Gross examination revealed marked differences in peritoneal dissemination (Fig. 4c). Although metastatic nodules were present in both groups, control mice showed extensive dissemination along the intestinal serosa and mesentery, whereas IPBL-treated mice exhibited a marked reduction in both nodule number and size. No port-site metastasis or ascites was observed, consistent with previous reports using HGC27 models22.

Autopsy analyses demonstrated that total tumor weight was significantly lower in the IPBL group than the control group (0.4 ± 0.5 g vs. 2.2 ± 0.4 g; P < 0.01; Fig. 4d). Similarly, total PCI was significantly reduced (7.2 ± 6.9 vs. 21.3 ± 3.1; P < 0.01). While the PCI in the abdominal wall regions did not differ significantly, the mesenteric PCI was markedly lower in the IPBL group (3.3 ± 3.7 vs. 11.8 ± 0.4; P < 0.01).

TUNEL staining of peritoneal nodules revealed apoptotic cells predominantly in central tumor regions in both groups (Fig. 4e). The distribution and frequency of TUNEL-positive cells were comparable, indicating no overt differences in apoptosis at the examined time point.

Intraperitoneal blue light-emitting diode irradiation induces mild structural changes in the peritoneum

The safety evaluation protocol is summarized in Fig. 5a. Non-tumor-bearing nude mice underwent IPBL irradiation and were euthanized four weeks later. No significant differences in body weight, ascites formation, or systemic abnormalities were observed (Fig. 5b). Serum creatinine levels were significantly lower immediately after irradiation in the IPBL group (0.19 ± 0.00 mg/dL vs. 0.27 ± 0.02 mg/dL; P < 0.05; Fig. 5c).

Macroscopic examination revealed no signs of peritoneal inflammation. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed preservation of the mesothelial layer in both groups, whereas Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated increased submesothelial collagen deposition in IPBL-treated mice (Fig. 5d). Scanning electron microscopy further revealed flattened mesothelial cells with reduced surface undulations in the IPBL group (Fig. 5e). These findings indicate that IPBL irradiation induces mild structural alterations of the peritoneum.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that IPBL irradiation significantly suppresses tumor progression in a murine model of GC peritoneal dissemination, without inducing overt systemic toxicity. IPBL-treated mice exhibited a marked reduction in total peritoneal tumor burden and PCI, particularly in mesenteric regions, which are clinically recognized as frequent sites of early peritoneal dissemination. Importantly, these antitumor effects were achieved without procedure-related mortality, ascites formation, or macroscopic intraperitoneal injury, indicating that IPBL irradiation represents a feasible and relatively safe local therapeutic intervention. To our knowledge, this study provides the first in vivo validation of IPBL irradiation as a potential strategy for controlling peritoneal dissemination of GC, underscoring its novelty and translational relevance.

In our in vitro experiments, blue light irradiation selectively suppressed the proliferation of GC cell lines, whereas orange and red light had no significant effects even under identical energy density conditions. In the present experimental setup, the total energy density was standardized across wavelengths; however, the prototype LED devices used in this study did not allow independent adjustment of output power. Therefore, irradiation duration was adjusted according to the output characteristics of each LED device to achieve equivalent total energy delivery. Although the observed biological responses demonstrated clear wavelength dependency, the potential influence of differences in irradiation duration cannot be completely excluded. This clear wavelength dependency is consistent with previous reports indicating that blue light-induced cytotoxicity is mediated primarily by intracellular photochemical reactions rather than thermal effects13,23. These reactions are thought to involve the generation of reactive oxygen species and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction11,24. Previous studies investigating the antitumor effects of blue light have employed a relatively broad wavelength range within the blue spectrum (approximately 380–500 nm)25, with wavelengths around 450 nm being among the most frequently evaluated19,23. In the present study, we selected 405 nm because the prototype LED system developed for this project utilized commercially available 405 nm LED chips. After confirming significant cytotoxic effects at this wavelength in vitro, we subsequently designed the in vivo intraperitoneal irradiation probe using the same wavelength to maintain consistency between the experimental platforms. It should also be noted that the in vitro experiments were not intended to directly reproduce the irradiation conditions used in the in vivo IPBL model. Rather, these experiments were designed as complementary mechanistic assessments to confirm the biological responsiveness of gastric cancer cells to blue light exposure. Because the irradiation systems used for the in vitro and in vivo experiments differed substantially in terms of device structure and optical characteristics, direct correspondence of irradiation parameters between the two platforms was not technically feasible nor intended in the present proof-of-concept study. Importantly, cellular responses to blue light irradiation differed between HGC27 and RGK1 cells, suggesting the presence of cell type-specific sensitivity. HGC27 cells exhibited rapid and pronounced growth inhibition even after relatively short irradiation periods, whereas RGK1 cells showed transient resistance or compensatory proliferation under lower irradiation doses. These findings highlight the necessity of optimizing irradiation parameters according to the biological characteristics of individual tumors and further suggest that intrinsic cellular properties (e.g., metabolic status, mitochondrial activity, and tolerance to oxidative stress) may critically influence susceptibility to blue light-induced cytotoxicity. Yoshimoto et al. reported that Opsin3, a photoreceptor expressed in cancer cells, plays a pivotal role in the antitumor effects induced by blue LED irradiation for colon cancer cell lines26. Moreover, the high expression of Opsin3 has been shown to be significantly associated with a poor clinical prognosis in several human cancers, indicating that molecular features such as the expression of Opsin3 may serve as important determinants of responsiveness to blue light-based therapies19. Although the role of Opsin3 in gastric cancer remains unclear, these findings raise the possibility that photoreactive molecular features may influence responsiveness to blue light-based therapies.

To enable in vivo application, we developed a cylindrical fiber-diffusing illumination device specifically designed for uniform intraperitoneal blue light delivery. Infrared thermographic monitoring confirmed the absence of significant temperature elevation during or after irradiation, supporting the conclusion that the observed antitumor effects were not attributable to thermal injury. From a clinical perspective, the non-thermal nature of IPBL irradiation is particularly advantageous, as it minimizes the risk of collateral tissue damage and facilitates integration into standard surgical workflows.

Although IPBL irradiation significantly reduced overall peritoneal tumor burden, a histological analysis of established tumor nodules did not reveal a clear increase in apoptotic markers at the time of evaluation. This observation suggests that IPBL may not primarily function as a cytotoxic treatment for established macroscopic tumors, but rather exerts its antitumor effects by inhibiting early tumor cell implantation, micrometastatic establishment, or initial proliferative expansion within the peritoneal cavity. Such a mechanism is consistent with the pronounced suppression observed in mesenteric dissemination and supports the concept that IPBL irradiation is particularly effective during early phases of peritoneal spread. Alternatively, apoptotic responses may have occurred transiently at earlier time points and were no longer detectable at later stages, highlighting the need for future time-course analyses to clarify the temporal dynamics of IPBL action in vivo. Furthermore, the absence of a sustained apoptotic response in established tumor nodules suggests that mechanisms other than apoptosis may substantially contribute to the antitumor effects of IPBL irradiation. Potential mechanisms may include modulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition, stemness-associated cellular phenotypes, oxidative stress responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, or cell-cycle regulation. However, these possibilities were not directly evaluated in the present study, and the precise molecular mechanisms underlying blue light-mediated suppression of gastric cancer progression remain to be elucidated.

From a safety standpoint, IPBL irradiation was well tolerated. Histological examination demonstrated preservation of the mesothelial layer continuity, while ultrastructural analyses revealed mesothelial cell flattening and mild submesothelial collagen deposition. These changes did not indicate overt inflammatory damage or tissue destruction, but rather suggested mild peritoneal remodeling. Notably, similar ultrastructural alterations have been reported in murine models subjected to hyperthermic CO2 insufflation, where early mesothelial changes were followed by structural recovery27. However, the reversibility of the mesothelial changes observed after IPBL irradiation was not directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, whether these alterations represent transient adaptive responses or persistent structural remodeling remains unclear. Accordingly, the potential long-term consequences of repeated or prolonged IPBL exposure, including the potential for adhesion formation or fibrosis, require further investigation.

An additional noteworthy finding was the reduction in serum creatinine levels observed after IPBL irradiation. Because irradiation was delivered intraperitoneally and independent of visual stimulation, this result suggests that IPBL may influence systemic physiological responses or inter-organ signaling pathways through mechanisms distinct from those previously described for environmental blue light exposure28. While the clinical significance of this observation remains unclear, it highlights the potential for IPBL to exert broader biological effects beyond local tumor control and warrants further mechanistic exploration.

Based on these in vivo findings, IPBL irradiation may be best positioned as a prophylactic or early-intervention strategy against peritoneal dissemination rather than a debulking therapy for established disease. As conceptually illustrated in Fig. 6, IPBL irradiation could be administered intraoperatively during gastrectomy, prior to reconstructive procedures, using conventional laparoscopic or robotic ports. This intraoperative window represents a clinically critical period during which free cancer cells and microscopic residual disease are likely to be present within the peritoneal cavity. Importantly, IPBL irradiation can be delivered under general anesthesia without additional invasiveness, enhancing its feasibility as an adjunct to standard surgical treatment. Unlike photodynamic therapy29 or photothermal approaches30, IPBL does not require exogenous photosensitizers or heat generation, further simplifying clinical implementation. Furthermore, blue light has already been introduced into clinical practice in endoscopic imaging modalities, such as narrow band imaging for enhanced visualization of vascular structures31. Although the energy parameters used in these diagnostic applications may differ from those required for IPBL irradiation, the accumulation of clinical experience supports the biological safety of blue light exposure, providing valuable insight for the potential clinical translation of IPBL.

This study was associated with several limitations. In the in vitro experiments, although the total energy density was standardized across wavelengths, irradiation duration differed because the prototype LED devices used in this study did not allow independent adjustment of output power. Therefore, the potential influence of irradiation duration on the observed biological effects cannot be completely excluded. The in vivo experiments were conducted using a single GC cell line and nude mouse models, potentially underestimating immune-mediated effects. Validation in immunocompetent models and across multiple GC subtypes will be essential to better define both efficacy and safety. Another limitation was the relatively small sample size used in the safety assessment experiments (n = 3 per group), which may not have been sufficient to detect subtle physiological or systemic effects associated with the procedure itself. Therefore, observations such as body weight trends should be Interpreted cautiously. Finally, the long-term impact of repeated IPBL irradiation on the peritoneal structure and function remains unknown. Addressing these Issues through longitudinal and immunologically relevant models will be critical for advancing IPBL irradiation toward clinical application.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that intraperitoneal blue LED irradiation effectively suppresses peritoneal dissemination of GC in vivo, while inducing minimal systemic toxicity and only mild structural alterations of the peritoneum. As a non-thermal and wavelength-specific phototherapeutic modality, IPBL irradiation represents a promising adjunct or alternative strategy for the local control of peritoneal metastasis. Further studies aimed at optimizing irradiation parameters, clarifying the underlying molecular mechanisms, and evaluating long-term safety will be essential for translating IPBL irradiation into clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Cell line and culture conditions

HGC27, a human undifferentiated GC cell line, and RGM1, a rat gastric mucosal cell line, were purchased from the Riken Cell Bank (Ibaraki, Japan). RGK1, a rat-derived GC cell line, was provided by the University of Tsukuba and was established from the same parental rat as RGM132. RGM1 and RGK1 cells were used as a paired model of non-neoplastic and malignant gastric cells derived from the same rat background to evaluate differences in biological responses to blue light irradiation independent of inter-individual variation. HGC27, RGK1 and RGM1 was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The medium was supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich) and penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B (Fujifilm Wako).

in vitro light irradiation

As reported in previous studies33, an LED light source was fabricated using 100 plug-in LED arrays mounted on an LED substrate (SPL-100-LC; Revox, Kanagawa, Japan). A heat-absorbing filter water filter (diameter, 100 mm; thickness 20 mm) was placed between the well plates and the LED light source to minimize heat emitted from the light source (Fig. 7a). The LED light intensity was 4.8 mW/cm2, 10.8 mW/cm2, and 18.8 mW/cm2 of blue (peak at 406 nm with full width at half maximum (FWHM) = 18 nm), orange (peak at 614 nm with FWHM = 19 nm), and red (peak at 668 nm with FWHM = 22 nm) light, respectively. The light intensity was measured with an optical power meter (8230E, ADC corporation, Saitama, Japan) equipped with an 82321B sensor. The emission spectrum was measured with an illuminance spectrophotometer (CL-500 A, Konica Minolta inc., Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. S1).

Light intensity was measured using an optical power meter (8230E; ADC corporation, Saitama, Japan) equipped with an 82321B sensor. Cells were cultured in complete medium for 24 h and then seeded in 96-well plates for cell viability assays or 24-well plates for TUNEL assays and immunocytochemistry. The cells were subsequently irradiated from the bottom of the well plates inside a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in the dark. Non-irradiated control cells were maintained under identical conditions without light exposure. Immediately after irradiation, the samples were subjected to further analyses.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, HGC27 or RGK1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. To compare the effects of three light sources (406, 610, and 660 nm) under equivalent energy exposure, cells were irradiated for 59, 26, and 15 min, respectively. After irradiation, the cells were cultured at 37 °C for additional 24 h prior to the MTT assay. In a separate experiment to evaluate the effect of the 406-nm light alone, cells were irradiated for 30, 60, or 120 min and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before the MTT analysis. Following incubation, MTT solution (5 mg/mL; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was added, and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The MTT reagent was removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Intracellular purple formazan was quantified measuring absorbance at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer (Immuno Mini NJ-2300; Nalge Nunc Int. Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of viable cells relative to untreated controls. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SE) of three independent experiments.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells were detected using a MEBSTAIN Apoptosis TUNEL Kit Direct (No.8445; Medical & Biological Laboratories, Nagoya, Japan) as previously described34. HGC27 cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates at density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubates at 37 °C for 24 h. After light irradiation for 45 min, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. The TUNEL reaction was then performed for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Fluorescence micrographs were captured using an Olympus BX53 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For quantitative analysis, TUNEL-positive cells were counted in 20 randomly selected microscopic fields, and the results are presented as the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells relative to the total number of cells counted.

Immunocytochemistry

Immunocytochemistry was performed as previously described35. HGC27 cells were seeded onto coverslips placed in 24-well plates at density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubates at 37 °C for 24 h. After light irradiation for 45 min, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS at RT for 10 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Fujifilm Wako) in methanol for 30 min, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incubation with 500 µg/ml normal goat IgG (I9140; Sigma-Aldrich) in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 30 min. The cells were incubated with a rabbit monoclonal anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (Asp175, clone 5A1E; 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h and washed with 0.075% Brij L23 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) for 1 h and washed in 0.075% Brij L23 in PBS. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC; Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan), nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin solution (Fuji Wako).

Animal and peritoneal dissemination models

Eight-week-old male BALB/c nu/nu mice were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). All mice were housed in standard cages under conventional (non-specific pathogen-free) conditions, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 °C, with ad libitum access to food and water. To evaluate the inhibitory effect of IPBL irradiation on peritoneal dissemination, mice were assigned to two groups: (1) a peritoneal dissemination model receiving IPBL irradiation (n = 6), and (2) a peritoneal dissemination model without IPBL irradiation (n = 6). For the safety assessment of IPBL, mice were divided into two additional groups: (3) a non-dissemination model subjected to IPBL irradiation (n = 3), and (4) a non-dissemination model without IPBL irradiation (n = 3). Upon arrival, the mice were housed separately according to their assigned groups. For Groups (1) and (2), peritoneal dissemination was induced on the day of housing by intraperitoneal inoculation of HGC27 cells. Cells were suspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells per 100 µL, and 100 µL of the suspension was injected into the peritoneal cavity using a 26-gauge needle inserted through the lower abdomen. Body weight was recorded twice weekly, and the general health of mice was monitored daily. Skin necrosis and other potential adverse events associated with IPBL irradiation were evaluated based on skin coloration, general condition, weight loss, and appetite. For the safety evaluation groups (3) and (4), tail-vein blood samples were collected at three time point: the time of housing, immediately after irradiation, and two weeks after irradiation. Blood samples were obtained by making a small incision in the tail vein using a razor blade and collecting blood with a capillary tube. Biochemical analyses were performed using a FUJI DRI-CHEM NX500sv analyzer (FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Plasma creatinine levels (CRE; mg/dL) were measured as an indicator of the renal function. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of the University of Miyazaki (approval no. 2024 − 530). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

in vivo light irradiation and tissue sampling

Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane delivered at a flow rate of 0.5 L/min. A cylindrical fiber-optic diffuser (PCD500-E-900; Furukawa Electric Group, Tokyo, Japan) was introduced into the peritoneal cavity via a 24-gauge catheter (SURFLO®; TERUMO, Tokyo, Japan) inserted through the lower abdomen (Fig. 7b). A single prototype probe was used throughout all in vivo experiments and was washed with water and disinfected with ethanol between procedures. The peritoneal cavity was irradiated using a fiber-coupled laser module (405 ± 5 nm, 50 mW; Edmund Optics Japan, Tokyo, Japan). In the non-irradiated control groups, mice underwent the same anesthesia, catheter insertion, and intraperitoneal probe placement procedures as the irradiation groups, and the probe was maintained in place for the same duration without light activation (sham procedure). The spatial profile of light intensity as a function of distance from the irradiation source was measured using a photodiode detector (S121C; Thorlabs, Newton, NJ, USA). To examine potential local heating effects, the temperature around the probe tip in air was monitored using an infrared thermography camera (R550Pro; Japan Avionics, Tokyo, Japan) during irradiation with a single wavelength within the 400–500 nm range at output power of 50 mW. Four weeks after light irradiation, the mice were euthanized by gradual CO₂ inhalation in accordance with institutional and relevant ethical guidelines. Disseminated peritoneal nodules were collected individually and weighed, while the peritoneal wall with the attached peritoneum was harvested as a separate tissue sample for subsequent analyses.

Peritoneal carcinomatosis index

Peritoneal dissemination was evaluated using a peritoneal carcinomatosis index (PCI), based on the concept commonly applied in human clinical settings36,37. In this murine model, the peritoneal cavity was divided into 12 anatomical regions, consisting of eight intraperitoneal regions (right/left subphrenic, right and left upper abdomen, subgastric, right and left flank, pelvic region) and four mesenteric regions (proximal, mid, distal, and ileocecal mesentery) For each region, a lesion score ranging from 0 to 3 was assigned according to the number and size of peritoneal nodules, as follows:

0, no macroscopic tumor;

1, minimal tumor involvement (1–2 mm in diameter);

2, moderate tumor burden (2–4 mm in diameter);

3, extensive tumor growth (> 4 mm in diameter or ≥ 5 nodules).

The PCI was calculated by summing the regional scores across all 12 compartments, yielding a maximum possible score of 36.

Histological analysis

Histological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS at RT overnight, followed by routine paraffin embedding. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and the resulting sections were subjected to H&E staining using standard protocols. Masson’s trichrome staining was performed on serial sections using a commercially available staining kit (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan). Apoptotic cells were detected using a TUNEL assay on paraffin-embedded tissue sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, 5-µm sections were treated with proteinase K (10 µg/ml) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by incubation in 1× terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase buffer and TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 90 min. After blocking, HRP-conjugated goat anti-biotin antibody was applied, and signals were visualized using DAB/Ni/Co/H2O2 solution.

Scanning electron microscopy

Peritoneal specimens from the left flank were collected as described above. Immediately after excision, the tissues were immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) and fixed overnight. After primary fixation, samples were washed in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 30 min and subsequently post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 1 h. The tissues were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%; 10 min at each step), infiltrated with tert-butyl alcohol, and subjected to freeze-drying. Once dried, the specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of platinum. All samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4800, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses, as well as the generation of graphs and plots, were performed using StatFlex (ver. 7, Artech, Osaka, Japan). Statistical comparisons between groups conducted using appropriate statistical tests, and significance levels were defined as follows: P < 0.05*, < 0.01**, < 0.001***. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and reproducible results were obtained.
