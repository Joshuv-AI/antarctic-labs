COINBench: Moving Beyond Individual Perspectives to Collective Intent Understanding
Xiaozhe Li, Tianyi Lyu, Siyi Yang, Yizhao Yang, Yuxi Gong, Jinxuan Huang, Ligao Zhang, Zhuoyi Huang, Qingwen Liu, Tongji University, Stanford University, CurrentsAI Research

Abstract
Understanding human intent is a high-level cognitive challenge for Large Language Models (LLMs), requiring sophisticated reasoning over noisy, conflicting, and non-linear discourse. While LLMs excel at following individual instructions, their ability to distill Collective Intent —the process of extracting consensus, resolving contradictions, and inferring latent trends from multi-source public discussions—remains largely unexplored. To bridge this gap, we introduce COIN-Bench , a dynamic, real-world, live-updating benchmark specifically designed to evaluate LLMs on collective intent understanding within the consumer domain. Unlike traditional benchmarks that focus on transactional outcomes, COIN-Bench operationalizes intent as a hierarchical cognitive structure (from explicit scenarios to deep causal reasoning). We implement a robust evaluation pipeline that synergizes rule-based method with an LLM-as-the-Judge approach. This framework incorporates COIN-Tree for hierarchical cognitive structuring and retrieval-augmented verification ( COIN-RAG ) to ensure expert-level precision in analyzing raw, collective human discussions. An extensive evaluation of 20 state-of-the-art LLMs across four dimensions— depth, breadth, informativeness, and correctness —reveals that while current models handle surface-level aggregation, they struggle with the analytical depth required for complex intent synthesis. COIN-Bench establishes a new standard for advancing LLMs from passive instruction-followers to expert-level analytical agents capable of deciphering the collective voice of the real world. Introduction

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs)
24
;
9
;
10
has catalyzed a fundamental paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, moving beyond mere text generation toward sophisticated reasoning and real-world problem-solving
28
. Current state-of-the-art models exhibit remarkable proficiency in structured, task-oriented environments, as evidenced by their performance on benchmarks such as SWE-bench
14
for software engineering, SPIDER2.0
17

22

reasoning agent
interacting with a
environment
, leaving a pivotal dimension of intelligence largely unexplored: understanding collective human intent. This task requires the sophisticated ability to distill coherent consensus from the noisy, fragmented, and often conflicting perspectives inherent in a population.

Real-world public discourse, such as community debates or consumer feedback, is characterized by "swarm-like" intelligence. Unlike linear instructions, these discussions involve an intricate interplay of divergent goals, emotional nuances, and implicit assumptions. To truly comprehend this environment, an LLM must go beyond parsing individual sentences; it must synthesize multi-source signals, resolve contradictions, and infer latent trends within an evolving discourse. This capability—acting as an expert-level analyst capable of navigating complex public opinion—represents a high-level cognitive frontier that current evaluation frameworks fail to capture.

To rigorously investigate this capability, we identify the
consumer domain
as an ideal microcosm for
Collective Intent
. Consumer discussions are far more than transactional records; they embody grounded yet complex human reasoning, rich in the noise and redundancy inherent to collective intelligence. While prior benchmarks have explored social or emotional dimensions
25
;
3
;
23
, they often rely on hand-crafted or semi-synthetic datasets that lack the "raw noise" of real-world discourse. Furthermore, recent consumer-focused benchmarks like Shop-R1
35
primarily treat intent as a surface-level prediction of shopper actions (e.g., purchase vs. non-purchase). We argue that such transactional outcomes overlook the deeper
Collective Intent
—the multi-layered cognitive structure distilled from a diverse population. There is a pressing need for a benchmark that shifts the focus from simple action prediction to the hierarchical synthesis of collective human voices.

To bridge this gap, we introduce
COIN-Bench
(Collective Intent Benchmark), a large-scale, dynamic, and live-updating framework designed to evaluate LLMs on their synthesis of real-world collective consumer intent.
COIN-Bench
encompasses over
200k opinions
across 9 major domains and 54 sub-categories, spanning 1,400+ products. Crucially, we depart from passive evaluation (e.g., multiple-choice questions) in favor of an
Active Probing Paradigm
. We task LLMs with acting as "meta-analysts" to reconstruct chaotic public discourse into structured questionnaires. This paradigm is underpinned by three core cognitive pillars:

•

Consensus Extraction
: Designing valid questionnaires requires models to implicitly perform denoising and clustering, identifying salient topics amidst the noise.

•

COIN-Tree
for Hierarchical Resolution
: Collective intent is inherently stratified. We map model outputs onto a hierarchical cognitive topology to strictly distinguish between surface-level aggregation (L1–L3) and deep causal reasoning or trend prediction (L4–L5).

•

COIN-RAG
for Grounded Verification
: To ensure correctness, we employ a retrieval-augmented verification pipeline that penalizes hallucinations by ensuring all inferred consensus is strictly grounded in the raw discussion context.

Evaluating 20 diverse LLMs reveals a critical performance dichotomy: while reasoning-enhanced models (e.g., OpenAI o1/o3) demonstrate superior depth in penetrating complex cognitive layers, a significant gap remains in their ability to achieve broad and expert-level analytical synthesis. Our findings suggest that even the most advanced models struggle with the multifaceted nature of collective intent, highlighting substantial room for future development.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

A Large-Scale, Live Benchmark
: We introduce
COIN-Bench
, featuring 200k+ real-world consumer discussions across 1,400+ products. Its dynamic nature ensures real-time updates and mitigates data contamination.

•

Robust Active Evaluation Pipeline
: We propose the Active Probing Paradigm, which transforms traditional passive structural questioning into a process of model-led self-exploration and autonomous Q&A. By integrating the
CoIn-Tree
with the
CoIn-RAG
verification framework, this pipeline ensures high-precision assessment, fundamentally shifting the evaluation focus from passive response generation to the active, structured reconstruction of collective intent.

•

Extensive Empirical Analysis
: We provide a comprehensive evaluation of 20 leading LLMs, revealing fundamental limitations in current models’ ability to synthesize deep, multi-source human intent.

BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION

2.1
Data Curation

Constructing a benchmark for collective intent requires a corpus that reflects the noisy, fragmented, and contradictory nature of real-world public discourse. We established an automated curation pipeline designed to retrieve high-density discussions while filtering out irrelevant noise. The process consists of three integrated stages:

Hybrid Acquisition (Search & Retrieval).
To maximize coverage, we employ a hybrid retrieval strategy combining semantic vector search with LLM-augmented API queries.
For a given focal product (seed entity), the system first generates context-aware keywords using an LLM to expand the search space.
It then retrieves discussion threads from diverse open-source web platforms.
To ensure semantic relevance, we utilize vector embeddings to measure similarity between the seed topic and retrieved content, retaining only threads surpassing a strict similarity threshold.

Denoising and Filtering.

Raw web data is inherently unstructured. We apply a cascade of quality filters:
(1)
Rule-based Filtering
removes low-information content (e.g., extremely short texts
<20
chars);
(2)
Semantic Filtering
employs an LLM-based verifier to exclude discussions that drift from the core topic;
(3)
Temporal Alignment
prioritizes recent discussions to ensure the intent reflects current market realities.
This pipeline ensures the final dataset preserves the complexity of swarm intelligence while maintaining high contextual fidelity.

2.3
Evaluation Dimensions

To rigorously quantify the LLM’s cognitive capability in collective intent synthesis, we categorize evaluation into four orthogonal dimensions:

Depth (Cognitive Penetration).

This dimension measures the level of reasoning required to derive the intent. We introduce a five-level taxonomy (
CoIn-Tree
levels):

•

Observational Understanding (L1–L3):
The model identifies explicit information directly present in the text, such as
Usage Scenarios
(L1),
Product Aspects
(L2), and
User Feelings
(L3).

•

Inferential Reasoning (L4–L5):
The model must synthesize dispersed information to perform advanced reasoning. This includes
Comparative Analysis
against competitors (L4) and
Predictive Tendency
regarding future improvements or market shifts (L5).

A high depth score indicates the model moves beyond surface summarization to expert-level insight.

Breadth (Semantic Coverage).

Collective intent is multifaceted. This dimension evaluates the model’s completeness in capturing the full spectrum of the crowd’s voice. A high breadth score signifies that the model has successfully identified the "long tail" of user concerns—covering diverse usage scenarios and edge cases—rather than fixating on a single dominant topic.

Correctness (Grounded Fidelity).

In swarm analysis, hallucination is the primary failure mode. This dimension evaluates whether the inferred intent is strictly grounded in the provided discussions. Using our
COIN-RAG
pipeline, we measure the factual alignment between the model’s generated consensus and the raw crowd evidence, ensuring the output is free from bias and unverified extrapolation.

Informativeness (Expressive Diversity).

An expert analyst should articulate insights with nuance. We assess this through
Lexical Richness
(vocabulary range) and
Semantic Distinctiveness
(lack of redundancy). High performance here indicates the model avoids generic boilerplate responses ("The product is good") and instead employs precise, varied language to capture the specific texture of the consumer intent.

3
Methodology

Evaluating
Collective Intent
presents a unique challenge: unlike factual QA, there is no single ’gold standard’ sentence that captures the entirety of a chaotic public discussion. Passive evaluation methods (e.g., multiple-choice selection) fail to test whether a model can actively synthesize fragmented views into a coherent whole.

To address this, we operationalize evaluation through an
Active Probing Paradigm
. We task the LLM with acting as a ’Meta-Analyst’ requiring it to
reconstruct
the unstructured crowd feedback into a structured
Questionnaire
. This design relies on the cognitive premise that to formulate a valid question-answer pair from noise, the model must successfully execute two high-level reasoning steps:

•

Salience Identification (The Question):
By formulating a relevant question stem, the model demonstrates it can identify meaningful topics amidst noise.

•

Consensus Aggregation (The Answer):
By designing options that reflect the majority perspective,
the model demonstrates a precise grasp of intents within diverse opinion clusters.

To rigorously assess the quality of this reconstructed intent, we propose a robust evaluation framework combining topological analysis (
COIN-Tree
) and evidence-based verification (
COIN-RAG
), as illustrated in Figure
2
.

3.1
COIN-Tree
: Hierarchical Cognitive Stratification

Moving beyond shallow keyword matching,
COIN-Tree
provides a
hierarchical intent graph
derived from real-world discussions to measure deep understanding. It allows to quantify the
Hierarchic
of the model’s understanding—distinguishing between surface-level observation and deep reasoning.

Construction.

We operationalize collective intent as a five-level weighted hierarchical tree (
CoIn-Tree
), where the root denotes the focal product. The construction process involves two key stages:
(i)
Branch Extraction:
We leverage an LLM to distill raw, unstructured discourse into standardized semantic branches based on a cognitive template (ranging from
Usage Scenarios
to
Future Tendencies
);
(ii)
Node Aggregation:
Semantically redundant nodes across disparate branches are consolidated into high-weight nodes.
The final weights are computed by integrating the frequency of node occurrences with real-world engagement metrics, such as view counts and upvotes, to reflect the popularity and consensus of specific viewpoints. Further technical details and the formal extraction prompt are provided in Figure
2
and Appendix
A.4
.

Surface Understanding (L1–L3):
Nodes at these levels capture explicit content directly observable in user discussions, including
Usage Scenarios
(L1), specific
Aspects
(L2), and
User Feelings
(L3) of the product.

Deep Reasoning (L4–L5):
These levels require the model to synthesize dispersed information. L4 (Comparison) represents comparative reasoning against competing products, while L5 (Tendency) represents predictive insights into future product improvements or market shifts.

Evaluation (Lighten Tree Nodes).

We evaluate the Depth and Breadth of the LLM by projecting its generated questionnaire onto the
COIN-Tree
nodes. Specifically, the content of each generated question is extracted into a semantic branch and matched shallow-deep against the
COIN-Tree
nodes using a Sentence Transformer. Successful matches ’lighten’ the corresponding nodes. The lightened sub-tree illustrates how well the model understands this human intent.

Depth Score:
Calculated as the ratio of the total weight of lightened nodes at a specific level (L1–L5) to the total weight of all nodes at that level. The overall depth is the average all five levels. A high score at L4/L5 indicates the model penetrated the “deep structure” of collective intent.

Breadth Score:
Calculated as the sum of weights of all lightened nodes. This reflects the model’s coverage of the multifaceted nature of the discussion.

3.2
COIN-RAG
: Evaluation of Grounded Correctness

In the context of swarm intelligence, ’correctness’ implies fidelity to the crowd’s consensus rather than external facts. To prevent the evaluation from favoring eloquent but hallucinated insights, we implement
COIN-RAG
, a retrieval-augmented verification pipeline acting as a “Fact-Checker.”

Construction.

COIN-RAG
serves to ground the LLM’s inferred intent in the raw evidence. We employ a two-stage retrieval process:

Dual-Embedding:
User discussions are vectorized using both TF-IDF (for precise keyword matching) and
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
(for semantic nuance). This ensures retrieval captures both specific terminology and implicit sentiments.

Joint Retrieval:
Key opinions extracted from the LLM’s generated questionnaire are queried against the vector database to retrieve the top-
k
most relevant raw discussion entries.

Evaluation.

We employ a reasoning-based verification step. The retrieved raw discussions (Ground Truth Evidence) are compared against the LLM’s generated intents (which are the question stems and given answers in the questionnaires). If the LLM’s synthesized consensus aligns with the majority perspective found in the retrieved evidence, it is deemed correct. This pipeline strictly penalizes hallucinations that drift away from the authentic collective voice.

3.3
Informativeness: Assessing Analytical Density and Diversity

Beyond structural accuracy, expert-level intent synthesis must be both information-dense and analytically diverse. We evaluate the
Informativeness
of generated questionnaires to ensure that models capture the full nuance of collective discourse rather than producing generic or repetitive text.

Lexical Richness

To reflect diverse population expression, models must employ precise and domain-relevant vocabulary. We measure lexical variety using the
Type–Token Ratio (TTR)

15
and phrase-level diversity using
Distinct-
n

18
. Higher scores indicate effective abstraction of raw crowd language into richer analytical expression.

Semantic Redundancy

A high-quality synthesis should maximize semantic coverage rather than concentrate on a narrow theme. We quantify redundancy by computing the average maximum cosine similarity between embeddings of generated question pairs
5
. Higher redundancy indicates limited analytical breadth, where the model repeatedly focuses on a single topic while overlooking other latent concerns and divergent perspectives. Detailed calculations are provided in Appendix
A.5
.

Hierarchy of Intent.

We observe a pronounced performance decay as we move from explicit to implicit intent layers within
CoIn-Tree
. While most models maintain reasonable scores in capturing
Usage Scenarios
(L1), their performance collapses when tasked with inferring
Future Tendencies
(L5). This "depth cliff" validates the hierarchical design of our benchmark and highlights a fundamental reasoning bottleneck: models can easily parse what is explicitly stated but struggle to synthesize what is implicitly anticipated by a collective population.

Reasoning vs. General Models.

Reasoning models, particularly
GPT-o3
, demonstrate a distinct superiority in the deepest cognitive layers (L5) and
Correctness
(80.35). Unlike general-purpose models that often aggregate noise,
GPT-o3
exhibits "critical deliberation"—the ability to filter out the vocal minority and anchor its synthesis in true majority consensus. This result suggests that navigating swarm-like discourse requires not just memory, but the ability to perform causal inference and temporal projection to resolve contradictions within the data.

Breadth vs. Precision.

An intriguing pattern emerges with large-scale open-source models like
Qwen3-30B-A3B
. While it excels in
Breadth
(53.20) and surface-level recall (L1), it suffers from high
Semantic Redundancy
(68.94) and lower
Correctness
. This indicates a "Verbosity Trap": the model achieves high coverage by exhaustively listing possible viewpoints (brute-force recall) rather than distilling them into a concise, high-entropy consensus. In contrast,
GPT-5
demonstrates superior semantic span, capturing the "long-tail" of niche concerns while maintaining higher analytical density.

Analytical Density and Information Richness.

Our
Informativeness
metrics reveal that
GPT-o3
achieves the highest lexical richness (85.52) and the lowest semantic redundancy (52.27). This implies that reasoning models actively restructure raw discussion points into a "distilled" analytical form, whereas general models often "parrot" dominant topics, leading to repetitive and diluted outputs. Furthermore, a significant performance gap remains between proprietary models and open-source models, particularly in
Correctness
, where even the strongest open-source models (e.g., Qwen3-32B) trail behind the GPT-4/5 series by nearly 15-20%.

Scaling Effects.

A clear scaling law is observed: models with fewer than 7B parameters (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B, DS-7B) fail significantly across all dimensions, particularly in
Correctness
and
Breadth
. This underscores that understanding collective intent is an emergent capability that requires a threshold of world knowledge and reasoning capacity to distinguish signal from noise in real-world public discourse.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we propose
COIN-Bench
, a comprehensive benchmark consisting of over 200k product-level discussions across 9 major domains, 54 sub-domains, and over 1,400 products, designed to evaluate the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) in understanding real-world human intent, particularly within consumer domains. Our evaluation framework measures LLMs’ ability to comprehend intent across four key dimensions: depth, breadth, correctness, and informativeness. We implement a robust evaluation pipeline to mitigate bias and hallucinations. Specifically, we construct
COIN-Tree
to assess LLMs’ depth and breadth of intent understanding, use
COIN-RAG
for evaluating correctness, and measure informativeness through lexical diversity and semantic richness.
Through extensive experiments on both closed-source and open-source models, we demonstrate that reasoning models outperform general models on average. However, significant gaps remain between closed-source and open-source models, and even the most advanced models struggle with deep and broad intent understanding. Our mission is to advance LLMs toward expert-level reasoning and improve their ability to understand complex real-world intent.

Limitations

Due to resource constraints, we were unable to include additional state-of-the-art models such as the Gemini 3 series, OpenAI GPT-5.2, and Claude 4.5 in our experiments.

A.4
COIN-Tree
construction details

First, the LLM (GPT-4o) is utilized to extract branches from each consumer discussion. During this process, the model is prompted to summarize a user’s discussion following the template: ⟨Product_series⟩, in the ⟨Usage Scenario⟩, its ⟨Aspect⟩, compared with ⟨Comparison⟩, gives consumers the ⟨Feeling⟩ perception, and the discussion suggests that ⟨Tendency⟩.

Next, the branches are used to construct the tree. All branches are connected to the tree root node, forming an initial tree. Sentence Transformers are then employed to merge semantically similar nodes layer by layer from the top down within the initial tree. During this process, nodes in the same layer that share the same parent node and are semantically similar are merged into one. The child nodes of each pre-merged node are then designated as the child nodes of the merged node. Additionally, the weight of the merged node is calculated as the sum of the weights of its child nodes. Ultimately, the fully merged tree is referred to as
COIN-Tree
, which will resemble the structure shown in figure X. Nodes with higher weights will appear in the shallow layers of the tree; this is because the core topics of discussion (e.g., usage scenarios, aspects, feelings) often overlap across discussions from different consumers, and such high-weight nodes represent the aspects of the product that users focus on most.

This process enables the clear presentation of user discussion content in a tree structure while highlighting discussion hotspots. Meanwhile, by updating the discussion data and reconstructing
COIN-Tree
, we can analyze changes in child nodes under the same parent node between the two trees—thereby identifying users’ immediate concerns and long-term strategic considerations.

After summarization, the key terms in the template are fetched to form a single branch. For the branches derived from one discussion, the initial weight of each node is equal, ranging from 1 to 3, and determined by the discussion’s upvotes and view count. Notably, not every discussion can be summarized to fill all six key terms—more successfully filled key terms correspond to a longer branch path, which in turn reflects a more in-depth consumer intent.
Next, all branches are connected to the tree root node, forming an initial tree. Sentence Transformers are then employed to merge semantically similar nodes layer by layer from the top down. During this process, nodes in the same layer that share the same parent node and are semantically similar are merged into one. The child nodes of each pre-merged node are then designated as the child nodes of the merged node. Additionally, the weight of the merged node is calculated as the sum of the weights of its child nodes. Ultimately, the fully merged tree is referred to as
COIN-Tree
. Nodes with higher weights will appear in the shallow layers of the tree; this is because the core topics of discussion (e.g., usage scenarios, aspects, feelings) often overlap across discussions from different consumers.

Lighten the Tree:
To assess the depth and breadth of a LLM’s understanding of consumer intent, content from questionnaires will be extracted into branches. Each branch will undergo semantic matching with the nodes in the
COIN-Tree
from top to bottom using a Sentence Transformer. Nodes that are successfully matched will be marked as "lightened," and the lightened nodes in the
COIN-Tree
will form a subtree. and the questionnaire will receive the score corresponding to that node.

Specifically, for each question in the questionnaire, the question stem and its four options will first be concatenated into four opinion statements. The LLM will then extract four branches from these four statements. These four branches will be matched with the nodes in the
COIN-Tree
from top to bottom—each branch will lighten a path and obtain a score based on the weights of the nodes along that path. The branch with the highest score for a given question will be used to "lighten" the
COIN-Tree
. Notably, nodes in the
COIN-Tree
cannot be repeatedly lightened by different questions. After iterating through all questions, the lightened nodes in the
COIN-Tree
will form a subtree.

A.5
Informativeness

Lexical Richness:
The evaluation of lexical richness relies on two key metrics: Type-Token Ratio (TTR)
15
and Distinct-n
18
. TTR quantifies the ratio of unique tokens to the total number of words in the text. It is defined as:

\displaystyle\text{TTR}=\frac{\text{Count(unique token)}}{\text{Count(tokens)}}

where a higher TTR indicates greater lexical richness. Distinct-n focuses on the
n-gram
level, measuring the ratio of unique
n-grams
to the total number of
n-grams
. This study focuses on
bi-grams
, and the Distinct-n is calculated as:

\displaystyle\text{Distinct-n}=\frac{\text{Count(unique bi-gram)}}{\text{Count(bi-grams)}}.

Semantic Redundancy
is evaluated using a self-referential manner
5
, where
the average maximum semantic similarity is computed between each question and all other questions in the questionnaire, as well as between each question’s options and all other questions’ options. Given a set of questions
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{n}\}
, the semantic similarity between any two questions
q_{i}
and
q_{j}
is calculated using cosine similarity:

\displaystyle\text{Sim}(q_{i},q_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{v_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{v_{j}}}{\|\mathbf{v_{i}}\|\|\mathbf{v_{j}}\|},

where
\mathbf{v_{i}}
and
\mathbf{v_{j}}
represent the vector embeddings of questions
q_{i}
and
q_{j}
, respectively. The redundancy score is then computed as the average of the maximum similarity values across all pairs of questions:

\displaystyle\text{Redundancy}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{j\neq i}\text{Sim}(q_{i},q_{j}).

Notably, a lower redundancy score indicates less repetition in question paradigms and option designs, which reflects the LLM’s ability to understand consumers’ intentions from multiple perspectives and conduct multi-source causal inference.
