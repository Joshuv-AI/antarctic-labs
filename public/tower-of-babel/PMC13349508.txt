Incidence and Predictors of New-Onset Heart Failure After Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation in Patients With a Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Masahiro Hisaoka, Yasuhiro Yoshiga, Hironori Ishiguchi, Masakazu Fukuda, Shohei Fujii, Shintaro Hashimoto, Goki Nakashima, Takuya Omuro, Shigeki Kobayashi, Motoaki Sano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence and predictors of new-onset heart failure (HF) after catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) remain unclear, particularly in patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (EF ≥50%) and no prior HF.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 480 consecutive patients with EF ≥50% and no prior HF who underwent initial AF ablation. Recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmias (ATs) and new-onset HF were assessed during follow up. Landmark analyses at 3 and 12 months after the first procedure were performed to evaluate predictors of new-onset HF and the association between recurrence of early ATs and subsequent HF. Additionally, recurrence of ATs after the final ablation was examined as a time-dependent covariate using a Cox proportional hazards model. During a median follow up of 70 months (interquartile range 60–84 months), 18 (3.75%) patients developed new-onset HF. Age >75 years was the only consistent independent predictor in both landmark analyses. Early recurrence of ATs within 3 or 12 months was not associated with HF development. However, recurrence of ATs after the final procedure was independently associated with new-onset HF in a time-dependent analysis (hazard ratio 4.028; 95% confidence interval 1.564–10.378; P=0.004).ConclusionsIn patients with preserved EF and no prior HF, new-onset HF after AF ablation was uncommon but clinically relevant. Durable rhythm control may be important to reduce HF risk.

Central Figure

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart failure (HF) are both highly prevalent conditions associated with significant increased mortality. AF and HF often coexist, and one may lead to the development of the other.1 Furthermore, these diseases share a common risk profile with multiple cardiovascular risk factors, increasing the likelihood of developing both AF and HF. AF and HF strongly influence each other’s outcome, resulting in increased hospitalizations and a two- to threefold increased risk of mortality compared with either condition alone.2 The Framingham Heart Study revealed the incidence of new-onset HF among AF subjects was 3.1–3.3% per person-years, and conversely the incidence of new-onset AF among HF subjects was 4.8–5.4% per person-years.3,4

Pharmacological rhythm control compared with rate control has not demonstrated any benefit on clinical outcomes in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).5,6 Recently, the EAST-AFNET 4 Trial demonstrated the benefit of early rhythm control for patients with AF as compared with rate control for the clinical outcomes.7 Furthermore, catheter ablation for AF improved the mortality, HF hospitalizations, and quality of life in patients with HFrEF as compared with optimal medical therapy.8,9 In contrast, in patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), catheter ablation for AF appears to improve the mortality, HF hospitalizations, and quality of life; however, there have been no randomized controlled trials.10,11 Although restoration and maintenance of sinus rhythm may theoretically prevent HF development by improving hemodynamics, atrioventricular synchrony, and myocardial function, AF ablation does not completely eliminate the risk of HF. Subclinical myocardial disease, atrial cardiomyopathy, and progression of comorbid conditions may contribute to HF onset even after apparently successful ablation.12 Previous studies have largely focused on patients with pre-existing HF, while the incidence and predictors of new-onset HF after AF ablation in patients without a history of HF remain unclear, especially in patients with preserved ejection fraction (EF).

Recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmias (ATs) after ablation represent a particularly important but complex factor. AF or atrial tachycardia recurrence may directly contribute to HF development through tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy or loss of atrial contribution to ventricular filling. Conversely, recurrence may simply reflect advanced atrial disease and underlying myocardial vulnerability, acting as a marker rather than a causal factor for HF. Importantly, many prior studies have treated recurrence as a time-fixed variable,13–15 which may introduce immortal time bias and obscure its true relationship with subsequent HF development. Because time-dependent Cox regression models assess the association between an outcome and covariates that change over time during follow up, it may solve this problem.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the incidence and predictors of HF after AF ablation in patients without a history of HF and with preserved EF, and to determine the correlation between new-onset HF and recurrent ATs.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was a single-center, retrospective, observational study and was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Informed consent was waived by using an opt-out system. This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation.

This study included consecutive patients without a history of HF and with an EF ≥50% who underwent their initial session of catheter ablation for AF between October 2011 and March 2018 at our institution. Patients were considered to have no prior HF if they had no prior hospitalization for HF, no documented HF diagnosis, and no HF symptoms, as evidenced by elevated natriuretic peptides or requiring HF-specific treatment before ablation, according to current guidelines.16 Recurrent ATs were defined as the recurrence of ATs of more than 30 s such as AF, atrial flutter (AFL), or atrial tachycardia. The overall population was classified into 2 groups: those with recurrent ATs, and those without ATs. Recurrent ATs were defined as 1 of the following 3 types for each analysis: (1) for the 3-month landmark analysis, ATs recurrence was defined as any ATs episode occurring within 3 months after the initial ablation; (2) for the 12-month landmark analysis, ATs recurrence was defined as any ATs episode occurring within 12 months; and (3) for the time-dependent analysis, recurrent ATs after the last procedure. Recurrences after 12 months were not captured by landmark grouping and were incorporated using a time-dependent Cox model, in which recurrence status changes over time. The blanking period for ATs recurrence was defined as 3 months after the procedure.

Study Endpoints and Definition of Clinical Events

The primary endpoint of this study was the cumulative incidence of new-onset HF after AF ablation. Additionally, univariate and multivariate analyses were used to evaluate the predictors of new-onset HF. New-onset HF was defined as the first symptoms consistent with HF, as evidenced by elevated natriuretic peptides or a history of hospitalization for HF, according to current guidelines.16 The secondary endpoint of this study was a comparison of the cumulative incidence of new-onset HF between the groups with and without recurrent ATs. For the 3- and 12-month landmark analyses, HF events occurring before each landmark time point were excluded. In addition, HF events occurring within the 3-month post-procedural period after the last procedure were excluded from the time-dependent analysis.

Catheter Ablation Strategy

Our procedural protocol and strategy have been previously described.17,18 All patients underwent pulmonary vein (PV) isolation (PVI) with radiofrequency (RF) energy with or without a contact force (CF) measurement function, or second-generation cryoballoon in the initial catheter ablation procedure. Patients with paroxysmal AF underwent PVI alone using RF energy with a 3D electroanatomical mapping system or second-generation cryoballoon. Patients with persistent AF also underwent PVI alone until September 2015, and since October 2015 underwent an empirical superior vena cava (SVC) isolation (SVCI) in addition to the PVI using RF energy. We did not perform atrial substrate modification, such as empirical complex fractionated atrial electrogram ablation or anatomical linear ablation. In the redo-procedures, isolation of the PVs and SVC was assessed. If re-conduction of the PVs and SVC was found, electrical re-isolation of the PVs and SVC was performed using RF energy. If both electrical PVI and SVCI were already achieved, we performed a posterior wall (PW) isolation of the left atrium. Regarding the patients who were clinically documented, or confirmed during the procedure, for tachyarrhythmias, such as atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT), atrial tachycardia, and AFL, ablation targeting the slow pathway/critical isthmus of the atrial tachycardia or AFL was also performed.

Management and Follow up

To exclude intracardiac thrombus, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed within 2 weeks before the procedure. Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) were discontinued after the ablation procedure according to our institutional protocol. During follow up, AADs could be resumed at the treating physician’s discretion, particularly in patients with symptomatic ATs recurrence, as part of standard clinical care. For at least 3 days after the procedure, electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring was performed. ECG and Holter ECG recordings were repeated at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months and every year thereafter after the procedure. Recurrence of ATs was defined as the detection of any sustained (>30 s) ATs after the blanking period. The indication for a repeat procedure was left to the discretion of the physician. Data regarding clinical events were collected by contacting the patients and primary care physicians of each patient in April 2023.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed variables are expressed as the mean±standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are expressed as medians and interquartile (first and third) ranges (IQR). The differences in the continuous variables between groups were assessed using the t-test and Mann–Whitney test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and proportions (%) and were compared using the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the event-free survival. Differences in the cumulative incidence of new-onset HF following the initial procedure between the groups were compared using the log-rank test. To precisely evaluate the association between recurrence of ATs and new-onset HF, we repeated the landmark analysis using an alternative landmark time (6 months) as a sensitivity analysis for the landmark approach. To identify any predictors for new-onset HF following the initial procedures, a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed. In the analysis where recurrence of ATs after the last procedure were treated as a time-dependent covariate, each patient’s follow-up time was divided into pre-recurrence and post-recurrence intervals; recurrence status was updated at the time of the first ATs recurrence after the last procedure. Time-dependent Cox regression models were used to evaluate the association between recurrence of ATs after the last procedure and new-onset HF. Variables reported to be associated with the prognosis of patients with HF in our and other studies16,19,20 were selected for the analysis. Variables with P values <0.05 in the univariate analysis were selected for the multivariable analysis. The results are expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Results with a P value <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 software.

Catheter Ablation Procedure

Figure 2 shows the details of the initial and total procedure. Ninety-five (20%), 224 (47%), and 161 (33%) patients underwent a non-CF-guided RF PVI, CF-guided PVI, or cryoballoon PVI, respectively, during the initial procedure (Figure 2A). Regarding treatment beyond the PVI, an SVCI, CTI ablation, focal atrial tachycardia ablation, linear ablation for macro-reentrant atrial tachycardia, slow pathway ablation for AVNRT, and Marshall vein ablation for atrial tachycardia were added to the PVI in 117, 134, 7, 1, 1, and 1 patients, respectively, during the initial procedure (Figure 2B). In total, an SVCI, CTI ablation, focal atrial tachycardia ablation, linear ablation of macro-reentrant atrial tachycardia, slow pathway ablation for AVNRT, Marshall vein ablation for atrial tachycardia, and a PW isolation were performed in 205, 178, 20, 22, 2, 4, and 8 patients, respectively (Figure 2B). Regarding complications, cardiac tamponade, cerebral infarctions, arteriovenous fistulae, and hematomas occurred in 4, 1, 5, and 4 patients, respectively, during the periprocedural period. Recurrence After the First and Last Catheter Ablation for AF

Figure 3 shows that the Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated maintenance of sinus rhythm after the first (Figure 3A) and last (Figure 3B) catheter ablation procedures for AF. Following the first ablation, sinus rhythm was maintained in 369 (77%) at 3 months and 321 (67%) out of 480 patients at 1 year, with a gradual decline over time due to recurrent ATs. After the last procedure, maintenance of sinus rhythm improved to 438 (91%) out of 480 patients at 1 year; however, with a gradual decline over time due to recurrent ATs. During follow up, 38 patients restarted AAD therapy after recurrence, and 442 patients remained off AADs at routine follow-up visits. Incidence of New-Onset HF

Figure 4A shows that the Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated a cumulative incidence for new-onset HF after the first procedure. The Kaplan–Meier estimates showed 2 events by 1 year and 11 events by 5 years. For landmark analyses, HF events within the respective landmark windows were excluded, resulting in 18 and 16 analyzable events from the 3- and 12-month landmarks, respectively. The comparison of the cumulative incidence curve for new-onset HF between the non-ATs recurrence and ATs recurrence at 3 and 12 months is shown in Figure 4B,C. There was no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of new-onset HF between the patients with and without ATs recurrence at the 3-month landmark (3.8% vs. 1.8% in the Kaplan–Meier estimated 5-year incidence; log-rank P=0.471), or at the 12-month landmark (3.3% vs. 1.8% in the Kaplan–Meier-estimated 5-year incidence; log-rank P=0.181). In a sensitivity analysis using an alternative landmark time point of 6 months, the results were consistent, with no significant difference in the incidence of new-onset HF between the patients with and without AT recurrence (2.6% vs. 2.1% in the Kaplan–Meier-estimated 5-year incidence; log-rank P=0.480). Predictors of New-Onset HF After Catheter Ablation for AF

Table 3 shows the results of the univariate and multivariable analyses of the predictors for new-onset HF at 3 (Table 3A) and 12 (Table 3B) months after the initial procedure. At 3 months after the initial procedure, significant factors in the univariate analysis were as follows: age >75 years; LAD >45 mm; and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) >100 pg/mL. After a multivariable adjustment, age >75 years alone (HR 3.209; 95% CI 1.235–8.339; P=0.017) remained the independent predictor of new-onset HF following the initial session. At 12 months after the initial procedure, significant factors in the univariate analysis were as follows: age >75 years; female sex; LAD >45 mm; and BNP >100 pg/mL. Age >75 years (HR 3.481; 95% CI 1.280–9.466; P=0.015), female sex (HR 2.987; 95% CI 1.078–8.277; P=0.035), and LAD >45 mm (HR 2.946; 95% CI 1.079–8.045; P=0.035) remained the significant predictors after multivariable adjustment. Table 3C shows the result of the time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models. Significant factors in the univariate analysis were as follows: age >75 years; BNP >100 pg/mL; and recurrence of ATs after the last procedure. Discussion

Our study produced the following 3 results: (1) after catheter ablation for AF, 18 (3.75%) of 480 patients without a history of HF and with EF ≥50% developed new-onset HF during a mean follow-up period of approximately 6 years; (2) age >75 years alone was an independent predictor of new-onset HF at 3 and 12 months following the initial procedure in patients with an EF ≥50% and no prior HF; female sex and left atrial enlargement were independent predictors of new-onset HF 12 months after the initial procedure; and (3) recurrent ATs within 3 months and 1 year did not predict new-onset HF, but time-dependent Cox regression models showed new-onset HF occurred following recurrent ATs after the last procedure.

New-Onset HF in Patients With AF

In patients with AF, subsequent development of HF is known to be associated with increased mortality. Therefore, the prevention of HF in AF patients is an important issue. In the Framingham Heart Study between 1948 to 1995, the incidence of new-onset HF among AF patients was 33 per 1,000 person-years.3 In a later Framingham Heart Study from 1980 to 2012, the incidence of new-onset HF among AF subjects was also 31.4 per 1,000 person-years. Despite the widespread availability of pharmacologic therapy for HF, little progress appears to have been made in preventing the onset of HF among AF patients. Furthermore, because it has been reported that the incidence of new-onset HFrEF and HFpEF among AF patients is comparable, it is necessary to keep in mind the possibility of developing HF not only in patients with a reduced EF but also in those with a preserved EF. In contrast, the incidence of new-onset HF in patients without AF is low at 4.8 per 1,000 person-years.4 In this study, in patients with an EF ≥50%, new-onset HF occurred in 6.4 per 1,000 person-years after catheter ablation for AF. Because the incidence of new-onset HF after catheter ablation for AF was apparently lower than that in the AF patients in the Framingham Heart Study, it was speculated that catheter ablation prevents new-onset HF in AF patients. The results of this study may suggest that catheter ablation for AF can prevent new-onset HF even in patients who have not yet developed HF, and prospective randomized controlled trials will be anticipated in the future.

Recurrence of ATs as a Predictor of HF Hospitalization After Catheter Ablation for AF

Catheter ablation for AF in patients with HF has been reported to be useful in preventing HF hospitalizations in patients with both HFrEF and HFpEF.8–11,21 Several studies have assessed the association between HF hospitalizations following catheter ablation and ATs recurrence. In the CASTLE-AF study, the AF burden at 6 months was predictive of hard clinical outcomes in HF patients with AF even though a first ATs recurrence >30 s after ablation was not associated with an improvement in the mortality and HF hospitalizations.13 In a report from Japan, the association between recurrence of ATs and death/HF hospitalizations was evaluated, but did not show that recurrence of ATs independently predicted HF hospitalizations after an adjustment in the multivariable analysis.14 In contrast, our previous study reported that recurrence of ATs independently correlated with HF hospitalizations in AF patients with HFrEF, but not with HFpEF.15,20 However, in previous studies, AF recurrence was treated as a time-fixed variable, defined as a recurrence during follow up, which may introduce immortal time bias because recurrence occurs at variable time points after ablation. Although landmark analyses have been used, they remain sensitive to landmark selection and may not fully capture the dynamic nature of arrhythmia recurrence. We performed landmark analyses at 3 months (end of blanking period) and 12 months (commonly used intermediate rhythm outcome time point) to assess whether early/intermediate recurrence predicts subsequent HF while reducing immortal time bias. Additionally, we performed time-dependent Cox regression to capture the dynamic relationship between recurrence and HF over long-term follow up. Landmark analyses are sensitive to the selection of landmark time and may misclassify patients who recur later; therefore, time-dependent modeling was used as the primary approach for ‘after the last procedure’ recurrence analysis. Our study revealed that recurrence of ATs after the last procedure as a time-dependent covariate independently predicted new-onset HF after catheter ablation in patients with EF ≥50% who had not yet developed HF; however, landmark analyses at 3 and 12 months after the initial procedure did not predict it. One of the reasons for the above results is that recurrent ATs that occurred over time, even at 1 year, required multiple procedures, so a landmark analysis may not be able to appropriately classify the ATs recurrence and non-recurrence groups. Recurrent ATs may impair cardiac hemodynamics through loss of atrioventricular synchrony, diastolic dysfunction, and intermittent tachycardia; however, recurrent ATs may also reflect more advanced atrial or myocardial disease predisposing patients to HF. The use of a time-dependent Cox regression allowed for a more accurate temporal assessment and reduced immortal time bias, but limitations inherent to retrospective observational studies remain. Accordingly, prospective studies are needed to determine whether preventing AF recurrence reduces the risk of new-onset HF after ablation.

Clinical Implications

The present study provides several clinically relevant insights into the development of new-onset HF after catheter ablation for AF in patients with EF ≥50% and no prior history of HF. Although the overall incidence of new-onset HF was relatively low over long-term follow up, its occurrence highlights that HF remains an important problem even in apparently low-risk patients. Advanced age, particularly age >75 years, emerged as a consistent predictor of HF development after AF ablation, underscoring the need for careful post-procedural surveillance in elderly patients. In addition, female sex and left atrial enlargement were associated with HF risk at later time points, suggesting that atrial structural remodeling and sex-related differences may contribute to HF vulnerability. While early recurrence of ATs did not predict HF, the temporal association between recurrence after the final procedure and subsequent HF emphasizes the importance of durable rhythm control. Collectively, these findings support individualized risk stratification and long-term rhythm management to mitigate HF risk after AF ablation. In recent years, medical therapies that are effective for HF complicated by AF have been introduced,22–26 and if any signs of HF are detected through careful observation after catheter ablation for AF, it may be possible to prevent hospitalizations due to HF through early therapeutic intervention.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design precludes definitive causal inference between recurrent ATs and new-onset HF, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as AF burden or subclinical myocardial disease cannot be excluded. Second, landmark analyses exclude events occurring prior to the landmark time by design, which may influence the observed temporal ordering between ATs recurrence and HF. Third, rhythm follow up relied on scheduled ECG and Holter monitoring, which may have underestimated asymptomatic or short-lasting recurrences. Fourth, although HF was defined according to current guidelines, detailed HF phenotyping and standardized echocardiographic reassessment at HF onset were not uniformly available. Fifth, because serial BNP measurements after ablation were not uniformly obtained, a standardized definition based on relative change could not be applied to all patients. Last, changes in ablation strategies and technologies over the long enrollment period may have influenced outcomes. Despite these limitations, the long-term follow up and time-dependent analyses provide clinically relevant insights.

Conclusions

In patients without prior HF and with EF ≥50%, new-onset HF after AF ablation was uncommon but clinically relevant. Advanced age was associated with a higher risk of subsequent HF. Because recurrent ATs after AF ablation may be associated with new-onset HF, patients who experience recurrent ATs should be carefully followed up while being monitored for HF, even patients without a history of HF and with an EF ≥50%.
