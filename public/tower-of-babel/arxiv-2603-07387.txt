Approximating Tensor Network Contraction with Sketches
Mike Heddes, Igor Nunes, Tony Givargis, Alex Nicolau

Abstract
Tensor network contraction is a fundamental mathematical operation that generalizes the dot product and matrix multiplication. It finds applications in numerous domains, such as database systems, graph theory, machine learning, probability theory, and quantum mechanics. Tensor network contractions are computationally expensive, in general requiring exponential time and space. Sketching methods include a number of dimensionality reduction techniques that are widely used in the design of approximation algorithms. The existing sketching methods for tensor network contraction, however, only support acyclic tensor networks. We present the first method capable of approximating arbitrary tensor network contractions, including those of cyclic tensor networks. Additionally, we show that the existing sketching methods require a computational complexity that grows exponentially with the number of contractions. We present a second method, for acyclic tensor networks, whose space and time complexity depends only polynomially on the number of contractions.

Tensor network contraction (TNC) is a fundamental mathematical operation with applications in various disciplines, including quantum physics, machine learning, database systems, graph theory, and probability theory. The general problem of TNC is known to be
NP
-hard
[
6
,
4
]
.
In
[
24
]
, the authors show that exact algorithms have a running time exponential in the treewidth of the tensor network.
In this paper, we introduce a polynomial-complexity
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximation for TNCs of acyclic tensor networks based on sketching.
Additionally, we present the first method capable of approximating arbitrary TNCs, including those of cyclic tensor networks.
To our knowledge, this is the first work to directly address the general problem with sketching, and it establishes a new bound for important instances of the problem.

Sketching, or dimensionality reduction, has attracted significant attention for its ability to swiftly scale down problems while maintaining an approximation of the solution space.
This technique has been applied to a number of fundamental problems in numerical linear algebra and machine learning, including least squares regression and low-rank approximation
[
33
]
.
Interest in these methods surged following the influential paper
[
3
]
, which introduced the
AMS sketch
—a method for estimating the second frequency moment of a data stream.
A notable improvement of the AMS sketch, called the
count sketch

[
7
]
, significantly reduced the sketching time complexity.
Both sketches later gained popularity as inner product estimators
[
2
,
8
]
.

In the realm of database theory, the estimation of inner products holds significant importance. It serves as a core aspect of query optimization, in particular, join size estimation. This importance has motivated the database community to further advance sketching methods.
Among the notable results are two generalizations of inner product sketches to accommodate multi-join queries: first of the AMS sketch
[
10
]
, and later of the count sketch
[
15
]
.
Building upon this, our work introduces a new perspective by showing that the problem of join size estimation, as addressed in these previous works, corresponds to the problem of approximating TNC.

Intuitively, expanding the estimation from the join size of single-joins to that of multi-joins, mirrors the broader generalization from approximating inner products to approximating TNCs.
Given this perspective, the motivation for our work arises from the observation that (1) the aforementioned join size estimators only support acyclic queries and (2) their accuracy deteriorates exponentially with an increase in the number of joins, which correspond to contractions. In fact, we show an exponential lower bound in Section
4.3
for the technique presented in
[
15
]
.

While our initial motivation stems from database theory, our reformulation of the underlying problem into one of TNC reveals the broader applicability of our proposed technique. In Section
5
, we discuss the important role that the more general problem of approximating TNC plays beyond databases.
For example, TNCs are used to simulate quantum computers
[
6
]
; in probability theory, TNC corresponds to marginalization in graphical models
[
29
]
; and counting the number of triangles in a graph can be reduced to a TNC.

Definition 4

(Approximate tensor network contraction (ATNC))
.

Our first method enables the approximation of

TNC, while prior sketching methods were only able to approximate

[
9
]

A TNC is

Key to this result is the

]
prevents it from approximating cyclic TNCs.
We then show an exponential lower bound for the current best methods in Section

Our second method eliminates the exponential dependence on the number of contractions for acyclic TNCs. To accomplish this, we interpret the tensor network as a tree structure and formulate acyclic TNC as a recursive function, consisting of a series of matrix multiplications with Kronecker products, in Section
4.4
. The Kronecker products are then sketched using the recursive sketching technique from
[
1
]
.
To ensure a space and time-efficient algorithm, the sketches are constructed incrementally from the leaves of the tree up to the root.
These two methods yield our main result, as stated in Theorem
1
. We state our main result for

2.1
Count sketch

The
count sketch
(see Definition
11
) was introduced to find frequent items in data streams
[
7
]
. It has since been used more broadly as a dimensionality reduction technique
[

]
. Each column of the count sketch matrix has a single random sign in a random row. The sign is determined by a 4-wise independent hash function (see Definition

Definition 10

(

A family of hash functions

the hashed values

are independent and uniformly distributed in

.

Definition 11

(Count sketch)
.

The count sketch matrix is a random matrix

, with each component specified as

, where
s:[n]\to\{-1,1\}
and
h:[n]\to[m]
are drawn from families of 4 and 2-wise independent hash functions, respectively. Given any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the count sketch of

is obtained by
Cx
.

(
[

, Lemma 2]
)

(Tensor sketch)
.

The

3
Related work

In this section, we review related works and compare them with our contributions.

Sketch-based tensor network contraction.

A generalization of the AMS sketch to enable the join size estimation of multi-join queries was proposed in
[
10
]
. Although not explicitly mentioned in
[
10
]
, the problem of join size estimation is equivalent to the problem of approximating a full TNC (see Section
5.1
).
Their approach involves sketching an order-
q
tensor using
q
independent AMS sketch matrices.
Once all the tensors are sketched, the approximation is obtained by performing the Hadamard product of the sketches, followed by an averaging of their components.
Since the AMS sketch matrix is dense, computing the
m
-dimensional sketch of an order-
q
tensor
{\mathcal{X}}
requires
O(mq\nnz({\mathcal{X}}))
time.
The method proposed in
[
15
]
improves this by a factor
m
by using sparse count sketch matrices to sketch the tensors.
Both methods only support acyclic tensor networks and have a space and time complexity that depends exponentially on the number of contractions (see Table
1
for a comparison with our methods).

Marginalization.

Robeva and Seigal
[
29
]
established that marginalization in undirected graphical models with discrete variables is equivalent to TNC. Consequently, classical inference algorithms for graphical models can be viewed as contraction algorithms for tensor networks. This includes exact methods such as belief propagation on acyclic tensor networks and the junction tree algorithm for cyclic tensor networks
[
30
]
. As shown in
[
30
]
and
[
24
]
, the running time of such exact algorithms is exponential in the treewidth of the tensor network. To mitigate this complexity, approximate methods such as loopy belief propagation
[
30
]
are often employed in practice, though they typically lack worst-case approximation guarantees.

Tensor decomposition.

Tensor decomposition methods aim to factor a given tensor into a tensor network, with the specific network structure determined by the chosen decomposition (e.g., CP, Tucker, or tensor train). This differs from the TNC problem studied in this paper, where the tensor network is given and the goal is to efficiently compute its contraction. A substantial body of work studies the use of randomized algorithms and sketching to accelerate tensor decompositions. For instance,
[
31
]
proposes algorithms for CP decomposition that use sketches to speed up tensor power iterations. In
[
23
]
and
[
21
]
sketched alternating least squares methods were presented for low-rank Tucker decomposition. More recently,
[
22
]
presented algorithms for tensor train, Tucker, and CP decompositions, as well as decompositions into arbitrary tensor networks. While these works demonstrate the effectiveness of sketching for tensor decomposition, they do not address the problem studied in this paper.

4
Methods

We propose two methods that are
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate tensor network contractions. We first describe our methods for full TNCs and later show that our methods also approximate partial TNCs, which result in a tensor of nonzero order.

After establishing four simplifying assumptions (without loss of generality), we start this section by describing our first method, which can approximate any TNC.
We then demonstrate the need for a new sketching technique by showing that the variance of the current best method requires an exponential dependence on the number of contractions. Motivated by this, we present a recursive expression for acyclic full TNC that serves as a foundation for the introduction of our second method, which has a polynomial dependence on the number of contractions.

4.1
Without loss of generality assumptions

To simplify our explanations and notation going forward, we make the following four assumptions about TNC, without loss of generality (WLOG):

WLOG 1.
Every mode is part of exactly one contraction. Otherwise,
virtual copies
of the modes participating in multiple contractions can be created. Consider the following TNC of vectors
a,b,c
:
\tc(a\times b\times c,\{(1,2),(2,3)\})
, where the single mode of
b
is part of two contractions. Defining
B(i,j)=b(i)[i=j]
, this can instead be written as
\tc(a\times B\times c,\{(1,2),(3,4)\})
.

WLOG 2.
Every contraction involves two tensors. When the modes being contracted belong to the same tensor, such as in the trace of a matrix, the exact contraction can trivially be computed in time proportional to the number of non-zero components of the tensor.

WLOG 3.
At most a single contraction exists between any two tensors. Multiple contractions can be merged into one larger contraction by reshaping the tensors. The following example of matrices
A
and
B
:
\tc(A\times B,\{(1,3),(2,4)\})
can be expressed as
\tc(\vecop(A)\times\vecop(B),\{(1,2)\})
.

WLOG 4.
Every mode has the same number of components. The methods discussed in this paper depend on the number of nonzero components of the tensors; therefore, zeros can be appended to smaller tensors to ensure that all modes have the same number of components.

It is easy to verify that these transformations do not increase the number of nonzero components or the Frobenius norm of the transformed tensors. Additionally, they do not introduce any extra tensors or contractions. These are sufficient conditions for our results to hold.

4.4
Improved approximation for acyclic tensor network contraction

For the second method, besides assuming a full TNC, we assume that the tensor network is acyclic.
Acyclic tensor networks are common in several important settings. In particular, many relational database queries have an acyclic join graph, and estimating the join size of these queries (which corresponds to a TNC) is critical for efficient database systems (see Section
5.1
).
Although tractable algorithms exist for TNC with acyclic tensor networks, the intermediate tensors may still be prohibitively large, motivating the need for approximate TNC.

To facilitate the explanation of our proposed solution, we first introduce some additional notation and concepts. Specifically, we introduce the notion of a root tensor
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}

We then define

\displaystyle\hat{\tc}({\mathcal{X}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{X}}_{p},E)=\vecop({\mathcal{X}}_{o})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{o}\hat{g}(o),\qquad\hat{g}(k)=\bigotimes_{l\in\Gamma(k)}\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l),

Going back to the remaining variance term, we have that

\displaystyle\Var\left\lparen x_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{g}(o)\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left\lparen x_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}\left\lparen{\textstyle\bigotimes_{l\in\Gamma(o)}}\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen-x_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}\left\lparen{\textstyle\bigotimes_{l\in\Gamma(o)}}\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})g(l)\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left\lparen x_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}\left\lparen{\textstyle\bigotimes_{l\in\Gamma(o)}}\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)-\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})g(l)\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\E\left[\lVert\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)-\mat({\mathcal{X}}_{l})g(l)\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle=\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\left[\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)-M_{l}(i,:)g(l)\right\rparen^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen

We then apply the law of total variance to obtain:

\displaystyle\Var\left\lparen x_{o}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{g}(o)\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\left[\Var\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)\middle|\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen\right]+\Var\left\lparen\E\left[M_{l}(i,:)R_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{l}\hat{g}(l)\middle|\hat{g}(l)\right]\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\left\lparen\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2q_{l}-2}-1\right\rparen\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\E\left[\lVert\hat{g}(l)\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen.

Combining the results thus far gives us

\displaystyle\Var\left\lparen y\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2q_{o}}-1\right\rparen\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2q_{l}-2}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\E\left[\lVert\hat{g}(l)\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\left\lparen\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2q_{l}-2}-1\right\rparen\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\E\left[\lVert\hat{g}(l)\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen,

The first term recursively aggregates all the tensor norms and
(1+8/m)
coefficients by traversing the tensor diagram from the root to the leaves. In total, there are
q_{o}+\sum_{k=1:k\neq o}^{p}q_{k}-1=q-p+1=t
of such
(1+8/m)
coefficients. Note that, for each leaf of the tree,
\hat{g}(l)=1
, which has zero variance. If we set
t^{\prime}=t-q_{o}
, then this results in the following:

\displaystyle\Var\left\lparen y\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t}\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}-\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t^{\prime}}\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle+\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\prod_{l\in\Gamma(o)}\left\lparen\!\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2q_{l}-2}-1\right\rparen\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\E\left[\lVert\hat{g}(l)\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var\left\lparen M_{l}(i,:)\hat{g}(l)\right\rparen,

The final inequality is derived from the observation that the overcounting of the norms of the leaf tensors results in the cancellation of the last two terms. However, it should be noted that we are overcounting by no less than the product of the norms, thus ultimately yielding

\displaystyle\Var\left\lparen y\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t}\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}-\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t^{\prime}}\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t^{\prime}}\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}-\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}},

which equals to
\left\lparen\!\left\lparen 1+\frac{8}{m}\right\rparen^{2t}-1\right\rparen\prod_{k=1}^{p}\lVert{\mathcal{X}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Proof.

(1)

(2)
