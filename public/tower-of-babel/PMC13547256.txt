TriPath3DNet: an efficient real-time model for multi-class classification in real-life surveillance videos of fixed duration
Authors: K Mohanarangan, P Palanisamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents TriPath3DNet, a novel, efficient, and interpretable 3D CNN architecture designed for real-time, multi-class classification of short, motion-triggered surveillance video clips under challenging real-world conditions–including occlusion, variable lighting, adverse weather, and subtle low-motion anomalies such as loitering or zone intrusion. TriPath3DNet integrates three complementary temporal pathways–short-term motion, long-term context, and Temporal Difference Encoding (TDE)–into a lightweight ResNet3D (R3D)-18 backbone to jointly model transient dynamics and sustained activities. Evaluated on four datasets–including the newly curated Virat1-RC, Virat2-RC, UCF-Crime, and our proprietary In-House Dataset (IHD)–TriPath3DNet achieves state-of-the-art or near state-of-the-art performance, with up to 95.37% accuracy, 99.42% AUC, and an inference latency of 129 to 137 ms per 50-frame clip (approx 2.6 ms per frame) on an 11 GB GPU, using only 33.46 M parameters. Notably, it outperforms both CNN- and transformer-based baselines–including MViTv1, MViTv2, and VideoSwin–by significant margins, especially on anomaly-dense benchmarks like UCF-Crime, where most vision transformers struggle. While MViTv2 achieves slightly higher accuracy on IHD (91.87% vs. 89.46%), TriPath3DNet delivers substantially better AUC (98.07% vs. 90.96%), indicating superior calibration for critical anomaly detection. Ablation studies confirm that each temporal branch contributes meaningfully to performance, and Grad-CAM visualizations demonstrate spatially precise and temporally coherent attention maps. By aligning architectural design with edge–cloud deployment constraints and the operational realities of industrial surveillance, our work bridges the gap between academic research and real-world video analytics.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern closed-circuit television (CCTV) surveillance demands minimal human intervention, making manual monitoring of hundreds of camera feeds impractical. Relying solely on human operators leads to fatigue, staffing challenges, and high operational costs. In contrast, AI assisted analytics enable real-time, scalable monitoring, allowing proactive incident prevention rather than post-mortem analysis1,2. AI-driven video analytics can simultaneously process multiple feeds, enhancing precision while reducing manpower and costs. High resolution and bitrate are crucial for detecting long-range objects and individuals up to 150 feet away. However, continuous analysis of high-frame-rate (5–30 fps) video streams using deep-learning models is computationally expensive and inefficient, as most frames contain no significant activity. Processing every frame can overload AI systems, introducing latency and degrading performance, particularly in time-sensitive events like intrusion, theft, or vandalism.

To overcome this, edge–cloud surveillance frameworks3 integrate lightweight motion detection at the edge, using frame differencing4. When notable motion is detected, the system triggers an event, prompting the AI model to analyze a short 50-frame events. Two common approaches in AI-based video analytics are object detection and video-level activity classification. Object detection operates frame-by-frame, focusing on spatial features such as color and shape–typified by the YOLO family of models5. However, these models often neglect temporal context, limiting their effectiveness for motion-centric surveillance tasks. Even with motion masks fused to YOLO detectors6, issues persist: static intruders or low-motion behaviors may go undetected, and low-contrast or partially occluded subjects can be missed in dim or cluttered environments. Conversely, small pixel variations (e.g., light reflections on stationary vehicles) can cause false alarms, reducing reliability.

Surveillance anomalies typically occur as short, isolated events during off-hours, such as unauthorized entry or unexpected vehicle movement. Leveraging this, our system employs a single-activity, multi-class video classification framework7,8 to detect threats precisely and efficiently.

At its core lies the proposed TriPath3DNet, a novel 3D CNN that fuses spatial and temporal information to enhance classification accuracy and contextual understanding of real-world surveillance. This holistic approach enables intelligent, human-like interpretation of activities for robust, real-time monitoring.

Fig. 1 illustrates the edge–cloud surveillance pipeline designed for scalable, real-time analytics across distributed CCTV installations. High-resolution cameras at key locations transmit streams to an edge node running lightweight motion detection. Only motion-triggered event clips (50 frames) are forwarded to the cloud AI engine hosting the TriPath3DNet classifier. The results are then sent to a centralized alert dashboard for operators or integrated security systems. This hybrid setup minimizes bandwidth, reduces inference cost, and enables low-latency analytics9–11, making it ideal for large-scale deployments.

Since most real-world surveillance scenarios fall under weakly supervised settings, we focus on works published from 2021 onward that address this regime.

Despite these advances, several critical gaps remain in current Video Anomaly Detection (VAD) research: (i) Most frameworks, including recent MIL, CLIP-based, and transformer designs, assume access to full or variable-length videos, which is unsuitable for real-time surveillance where anomalies often occur in short, motion-triggered clips; few methods explicitly address fixed-duration formats (e.g., 50 frames at 5 fps) required to trigger preventive action within minutes of incident onset. (ii) Stationary or subtle anomalies such as loitering, zone intrusion, or perimeter breach remain difficult to detect, and even advanced transformer-based and federated approaches struggle when subjects occupy small spatial footprints or appear under poor lighting, occlusion, or cluttered backgrounds. (iii) While recent works such as MA-PDM, self-supervised geometric scrambling, and GS-MoE explore fine-grained cues, lightweight multi-scale modeling across both spatial and temporal dimensions is rarely achieved, leaving variations in object scale (near vs. far) and activity duration (sudden trespass vs. prolonged lingering) underexplored in efficient architectures. (iv) No existing work leverages event-specific manual annotation that reflects real surveillance scenarios, where anomalies such as theft or intrusion occur as isolated activities during off-hours; current datasets like UCF-Crime, Virat, and ShanghaiTech contain overlapping, untrimmed, or multi-activity events, which poorly represent the clean, single-anomaly structure of industrial footage. (v) Although CLIP-based methods such as VERA and PLOVAD introduce verbalized learning and prompt tuning for unseen anomalies, most frameworks still lack robust open-vocabulary detection and interpretable outputs, with explainability limited to detection scores rather than anomaly rationales. (vi) Emerging federated approaches such as those by Wang and Su demonstrate promise in collaborative anomaly detection under domain shift, but scalability, communication efficiency, and robustness to heterogeneous client data remain open challenges for deployment in distributed surveillance networks. (vii) Few methods explicitly target industrial security anomalies such as restricted-zone intrusion, perimeter breach, or equipment tampering, with most focusing on generic violence or crowd datasets, leaving critical asset-protection scenarios underrepresented. (viii) Many recent works report performance only on high-end GPUs; while real-time CNN reconstruction (Elmetwally) and lightweight CLIP+Transformer designs (Dabouei) highlight deployment feasibility, overall inference efficiency and model complexity are frequently overlooked, hindering adoption on edge devices with limited compute and power budgets. In addition, although transformer-based architectures have recently gained attention in video understanding, their reliance on dense temporal attention and quadratic complexity limits their practicality in our experiments for short, low-motion, motion-triggered surveillance clips that require rapid decision-making.

Our work directly addresses these limitations by proposing a lightweight, multi-branch architecture that enables efficient, multi-scale spatio-temporal analysis tailored for short, fixed-duration surveillance clips, while emphasizing explainability, edge deployment, and alignment with real-world asset-protection tasks.

Materials and methods

The proposed TriPath3DNet model builds upon the base R3D-18 architecture. As shown in Fig. 2, it enhances the standard model by introducing three parallel temporal branches to capture motion at multiple scales. These include a short-term branch for rapid movements, a long-term branch for extended temporal patterns, and a TDE branch to emphasize subtle frame-to-frame variations. The outputs of these branches are fused via a convolution and subsequently processed through the R3D-18 layers for spatiotemporal feature extraction and final classification. This integration of temporal branches significantly improves performance. The heatmaps are generated by averaging feature maps across channels for each frame and overlaying them on the input frames using perceptual colormaps. Regions with high activation (typically appearing in yellow or red) indicate areas the model attends to for motion understanding, while low-activation regions (blue or dark) are considered less informative. The short-term branch focuses on fine-grained, rapid movements, the long-term branch captures slow and consistent motion patterns, and the TDE branch highlights abrupt changes across frames. The fused heatmaps demonstrate how the model integrates multi-scale temporal cues into a coherent representation.

Experimentation

Datasets

In this work, we curate Virat, and UCF-Crime as a methodological adaptation that aligns academic evaluation with real-world, edge–cloud surveillance. Specifically, (i) we enforce fixed-duration, motion-triggered inputs (e.g., 50 frames of action oriented content) instead of untrimmed, variable-length streams; (ii) we adopt a single-event, multi-class assumption by segmenting videos into 50-frame clips to mirror real-world alert workflows–such as intrusion, loitering, or vehicle entry–and thereby reduce label ambiguity; (iii) we restore temporal coherence and clear activity boundaries via motion-energy–based centering, ensuring each clip is anchored on its most dynamic segment; (iv) we rebalance classes toward threat-centric tasks, placing emphasis on low-motion yet high-risk events like zone intrusion or static trespass to enhance operational relevance; (v) we ensure computational feasibility under strict GPU memory and latency budgets by standardizing all inputs to 50-frame clips, enabling fair comparisons even against transformer-based state-of-the-art models. Collectively, these design choices constitute a core contribution of our work: reengineering public benchmarks to reflect the actual deployment setting of modern surveillance systems.

(i)Virat: The Virat dataset12 provides high-resolution outdoor surveillance videos with detailed activity annotations. However, the original clips are daylight-staged and lack contextual variation. To enhance real-world relevance, we curated two variants–Virat1-RC and Virat2-RC–by segmenting videos into 50-frame clips and redefining classes around intrusion and threat-related activities. The restructuring ensures temporal consistency and domain alignment (Fig. 6). Class-level statistics and descriptions are provided in Tables 2 and 12.2

Training protocol and hyperparameter configuration

We adopt a stratified split of 70:15:15 for training, validation, and testing. For IHD and Virat1/2-RC, the split is anchored to the minority class: the class with the fewest samples defines the per-split counts, and all other classes are downsampled proportionally to preserve balance across train/val/test. For UCF-Crime, we retain the original per-class proportions while enforcing the same 70:15:15 partition. All dataset splits were generated using a fixed random seed (42) and stratified sampling to ensure reproducibility and consistent class distributions. All models are trained for 50 epochs; the proposed architecture uses the hyperparameters in Table 3. The single checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy is selected for final test-set evaluation.

We evaluated the proposed TriPath3DNet against the baseline R3D-18 using Grad-CAM on LPS dataset, with representative per-frame visualizations at shown in Fig. 9. We summarize five interpretability indicators–FER), BSR, TC, Entropy, and Confidence–to quantify spatial alignment, background rejection, temporal stability, map compactness, and decisiveness, respectively. Quantitatively, TriPath3DNet achieves higher FER (0.1953 vs. 0.1928) and BSR (0.1953 vs. 0.1928), indicating that its Grad-CAM energy concentrates more within motion/GT regions while leaking less into the background. It also yields lower Entropy (10.034 vs. 10.155), reflecting sharper and more compact attention maps, and higher Confidence (0.915 vs. 0.871), denoting more decisive predictions. Although R3D-18 shows a slightly higher TC (0.9739 vs. 0.9682), the gap is marginal and both models are highly stable (TC >\!0.96). Overall, TriPath3DNet exhibits superior spatial precision, cleaner background suppression, and stronger predictive certainty while maintaining comparable temporal smoothness–supporting its advantage for interpretable and reliable activity localization in surveillance video.

Ablation analysis

To evaluate the contribution of each component in TriPath3DNet, we conduct an ablation study on the IHD and UCF-Crime datasets (Tables 9, Fig. 13 and Fig. 14). The full model (A1: short + long + TDE) achieves the highest accuracy–89.46% on IHD and 86.14% on UCF-Crime–with strong AUC (>94%) and consistent inference time (129–137 ms per clip). Removing TDE (A2) causes the largest performance drop on UCF-Crime (-7.50% accuracy), confirming its critical role in capturing subtle or sparse motion cues. Ablating long-term (A3) or short-term (A4) branches also degrades performance: short-term cues are especially vital for IHD (-5.12%), while long-term context benefits UCF-Crime (-7.05%), underscoring the necessity of multi-scale temporal modeling. Alternative fusion strategies (B1, C1), max pooling instead of average pooling (D1), and omission of Kinetics pretraining (E1) all yield inferior results. Notably, removing pretraining (E1) reduces UCF-Crime accuracy by 25.46%, highlighting the importance of large-scale initialization.

Conclusion

This work presents TriPath3DNet, a novel, efficient, and interpretable 3D CNN architecture designed specifically for real-time, multi-class classification of fixed-duration surveillance video clips. By integrating three complementary temporal pathways–short-term motion, long-term context, and Temporal Difference Encoding (TDE)–TriPath3DNet effectively captures both transient events and prolonged anomalous behaviors, enabling robust detection under challenging real-world conditions such as occlusion, poor lighting, and adverse weather.

Extensive evaluation across four datasets–including the newly curated Virat1-RC, Virat2-RC, and UCF-Crime, as well as our proprietary (IHD)–demonstrates that TriPath3DNet achieves state-of-the-art performance, with up to 95.37% accuracy, 99.42% AUC, and 137 ms per 50-frame clip. Notably, it outperforms both lightweight CNNs and recent vision transformers (e.g., MViT, VideoSwin) by significant margins, despite having only 33.46 M parameters. This highlights a key insight: while transformers excel at modeling long, data-rich sequences, they are often overkill for short, sparsely sampled surveillance clips (e.g., at 5 fps) where local spatio-temporal dynamics dominate–and where carefully designed CNNs can offer superior efficiency and accuracy.

Ablation studies confirm that each temporal branch contributes meaningfully to performance, with gains attributable to architectural design rather than model scale. Grad-CAM visualizations further reveal that TriPath3DNet produces spatially precise and temporally coherent attention maps, enhancing interpretability–a critical requirement for security-critical deployments.

To our knowledge, TriPath3DNet is the first framework to jointly address the core operational constraints of industrial asset-protection systems: (i) fixed-length, motion-triggered video inputs, (ii) the detection of subtle, low-motion anomalies–such as loitering or zone intrusion–under real-world conditions including occlusion, variable lighting, and environmental clutter. By aligning architectural design with edge–cloud deployment requirements and the 2-minute alert SLA, our work bridges the persistent gap between academic video analytics and practical surveillance needs, establishing a new efficiency–accuracy benchmark for real-time, deployable video classification.

Limitations. The current design of TriPath3DNet is optimized for single-activity, fixed-duration surveillance scenarios typical of asset-protection systems, where each motion-triggered clip contains one primary event of interest. Consequently, its performance may degrade on clips containing multiple concurrent or sequential activities, as the model assumes a single dominant class per input. Similarly, the architecture is not designed for long, untrimmed videos; both accuracy and computational efficiency are expected to suffer due to limited temporal receptive fields and fixed pooling structures. These limitations reflect intentional operational trade-offs rather than fundamental flaws, but they do restrict applicability to broader video understanding tasks.

Future Work. We plan to pursue three directions: (i) extending the framework to localize and classify multiple sequential events in longer, untrimmed videos; (ii) exploring hybrid architectures that fuse TriPath3DNet with lightweight vision transformers (e.g., MViTv1) to leverage global attention while preserving temporal efficiency; and (iii) developing motion-aware filtering mechanisms to suppress false alarms caused by environmental nuisances (e.g., swaying trees, fluttering flags)–which account for over 90% of false positives in current cloud-based systems and severely limit scalability. Given that MViTv1 achieves competitive AUC on IHD (98.29% vs. 98.07%) at ~10× higher speed, such hybrid models could yield a Pareto-optimal solution for both short and long surveillance sequences.
