A Comprehensive Evaluation of Vertex Elimination Algorithms for Algorithmic Differentiation
Alex Crane, Pål Grønås Drange, Eli Friedman, Paul D. Hovland, Jan Hückelheim, Andrew Lyons, Yosuke Mizutani, Macéo Ottavy, Blair D. Sullivan

Abstract
The algorithmic differentiation (AD) of mathematical functions can be interpreted as a sequence of vertex eliminations in an underlying directed acyclic graph. The problem of determining a minimum-cost elimination ordering, which we call Optimal Vertex Elimination , is NP -complete. Consequently, much effort has been devoted to the design of heuristics. Many of these heuristics are widely believed to perform well in practice, but this hypothesis has so far been difficult to test due to the lack of scalable exact methods. We design and engineer new integer programming formulations for Optimal Vertex Elimination and for a related objective we call Minimum Edge Count . Our implementations scale to graphs one-to-two orders of magnitude larger than existing techniques, enabling the assembly of a corpus of medium-sized graphs for which optimal solutions are known. This corpus facilitates a study of existing heuristics, confirming that on real data popular methods achieve high quality solutions. We also make several theoretical contributions. We give a tight analysis of the forward and reverse modes of AD, and extend our techniques to provide a simple algorithm for Optimal Vertex Elimination with approximation ratio parameterized by the size of a minimum source-sink separator. On the complexity side, we give the first approximation lower bounds for both problems.



1
Introduction

For over fifty years, the (linearized)
computational graph model has been used to guide the algorithmic differentiation (AD) of functions
[
4
,
18
]
.
In a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
D=(V=S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
,
source vertices
S
and sink vertices
T
represent independent and dependent variables, respectively.
The internal vertices
I
represent intermediate values, while directed edges model data dependencies.
By associating with each edge
(u,v)
a local partial derivative
\partial{v}/\partial{u}
, we obtain a chain-rule-based interpretation of the Jacobian:
the derivative of a dependent variable
t

with respect to an independent variable
s
is equal to the sum
over all
st
-paths of the product of the edge
weights (partial derivatives) along the path. Many popular procedures for computing derivatives can be interpreted as
a sequence of graph edits that preserve correctness of this chain-rule-based interpretation.

The
elimination
of an internal vertex
v\in I
is defined by deleting
v
and,
for each pair of in-neighbor
u\in N^{-}_{{D}}({v})
and out-neighbor
w\in N^{+}_{{D}}({v})
of
v
,
adding the product of the partial derivatives associated with

(u,v)
and
(v,w)
to the partial derivative associated with
(u,w)
. The edge
(u,w)
is created if it does not already exist; in this case the previously associated partial derivative is
0
. We write
{D}_{/{v}}
for the resulting DAG,
and generalize this notation to a
sequence

\sigma=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{\ell})
of eliminations, where
{D}_{\sigma}\vcentcolon=((({D}_{/{v_{1}}})_{/v_{2}})\ldots)_{v_{\ell}}
.
Viewed this way, the so-called forward mode
of AD computes derivatives by eliminating all internal vertices in topological order. The terminal
graph is bipartite on vertices
S
and
T
. Therefore, the derivative associated with each edge captures directly a non-zero entry in the Jacobian.
The so-called reverse mode of AD eliminates vertices in reverse topological order,
resulting in the same bipartite graph. Indeed, it is possible to eliminate the internal vertices in any order, obtaining an exact (up to round-off error) computation of the Jacobian.
However, not all elimination sequences require the same computational effort.

The
cost
of eliminating a vertex
v
is the
Markowitz degree

\mu({v})\vcentcolon=|N^{-}_{{D}}({v})|\cdot|N^{+}_{{D}}({v})|
, reflecting the number of multiplications required to maintain correctness of the chain-rule based interpretation of
D
. The
cost
of a sequence of eliminations is the sum of the costs of the involved eliminations.
While forward and reverse modes both eliminate every internal vertex (i.e., they produce
total elimination sequences
), the costs of these sequences may differ, as the elimination of a vertex may affect the Markowitz degrees of its not-yet-eliminated neighbors.
Since the number of multiplications greatly affects the computational effort associated with derivative evaluations, a central problem in AD is to find low-cost elimination sequences; see the standard textbook
[
12
]
, or a recent open problems paper
[
1
]
.

Input:

A DAG
D=(S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
.

Task:

Find a total elimination sequence of minimum cost.

Optimal Vertex Elimination

The original formulation of this problem is due to Griewank and Reese
[
11
]
,
who also proposed a greedy heuristic:
at each step, the internal vertex with minimum Markowitz degree is eliminated.
Many other greedy heuristics
have been proposed; see
Section

4
.

Additionally, a variety of randomized, search-based algorithms have been
developed, including algorithms based on simulated
annealing
[
23
,
24
]

and Monte Carlo tree
search
[
19
]
. Due to their running times, these methods are best used in situations where the search cost can
be amortized over many derivative evaluations.

Another randomized algorithm, based on Markov chain Monte Carlo, is
described in
[
21
]
.
This algorithm targets a different objective, introduced
in
[
13
]
as
scarcity
, and formalized
in
[
1
]
as the
Minimum Edge Count
problem. In this
problem, the elimination sequence
\sigma
need not be total, i.e., not all internal vertices need to be eliminated, and the objective changes from the cost of
\sigma
to the number of edges in
{D}_{\sigma}
.
We refer to the original paper
[
13
]
for a detailed motivation of scarcity.

Input:

A DAG
D=(S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
.

Task:

Find a (not necessarily total) elimination sequence
\sigma
minimizing
|E({D}_{\sigma})|.

Minimum Edge Count

Ideally, one would evaluate heuristics by comparing them to an
optimal elimination sequence or (tight) lower bounds. Unfortunately,
the decision problems underlying both
Optimal Vertex Elimination
and
Minimum Edge Count
are
NP
-hard
[
5
]
, and finding
good lower bounds has remained elusive except in very restricted structural classes. Nonetheless, in an
effort to provide a baseline for comparison, Chen
et al.

[
8
,
9
]
developed
integer linear programming (ILP) formulations for both problems. Unfortunately, these formulations remain computationally prohibitive
for all but very small graphs (around
30
vertices).

In
Section

2
, we introduce new, more efficient ILP formulations for both problems. These
enable us to find optimal solutions for medium-sized
graphs (a few hundred vertices for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
; over
1000
for
Minimum Edge Count
).
This in turn enables us to perform a comprehensive assessment
of heuristics and search-based
algorithms on a broad range of real and synthetic computational
graphs; see
Section

5
.

In
Section

3
, we give a tight bound on the performance of the forward and reverse modes of AD, and generalize our techniques to provide a parameterized approximation guarantee for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
.
In
Section

6
, we give the first approximation lower bounds for both
Optimal Vertex Elimination
and
Minimum Edge Count
.
We also provide a (stronger) approximation lower bound for the dual of the latter problem,
Maximum Edge Reduction
, which asks us to maximize
|E(D)|-|E(D_{\sigma})|
, rather than minimizing
|E(D_{\sigma})|
.
Throughout, we assume standard graph-theoretic notation, e.g., as seen in
[
10
]
.

Integer Linear Programming

In this section we present new ILPs for
Optimal Vertex Elimination

Minimum Edge Count

[
8
,

]

2.1
ILP for
Optimal Vertex Elimination

The ILP formulation provided by
[
8
,
9
]
has, in the worst case,
\Theta(n^{3})
variables and
\Theta(n^{4})
constraints. The ILP tracks the structure of the graph at timesteps
t=0,1,\ldots,|I|+1
, where the graph
D(t)=(V(t),E(t))
reflects the first
t
vertex eliminations. The bound on the number of constraints then comes from the need to encode events such as the creation of edge
(i,j)
via the elimination of vertex
k
at timestep
t
.
Here, we propose a new ILP with only
\Theta(n^{3})
constraints. Our main innovation is to abandon the timestep abstraction (though it remains useful for analysis) and encode only the relative order of vertex eliminations. Formally, our first set of variables is defined by:

\displaystyle x_{ij}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if $i,j\in I$ and $i$ is eliminated before $j$.}\\
1\quad\text{ if $i\in I$ and $j\notin I$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

We need two more sets of variables. The first tracks whether the edge
(i,j)
exists at any point in the elimination procedure, and the second tracks the cost of eliminations.
We say that a vertex triplet
(i,j,k)
is a
multiplication at

k
if
i
and
j
are in- and out-neighbors (respectively) of
k
when
k
is eliminated. Notice that the number of multiplications at
k
is precisely
k
’s Markowitz degree at the time of its elimination, i.e., the cost of the elimination.

\displaystyle e_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if $(i,j)\in\bigcup_{t}E(t)$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle z_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if $(i,j,k)$ is a multiplication at $k$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

We can now present our new ILP.

minimize

\displaystyle F=\sum_{k\in I}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}z_{ijk}.

(4)

subject to

\displaystyle x_{ij}+x_{jk}\leq x_{ik}+1

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j,k\in V.

(5)

\displaystyle x_{ij}=1-x_{ji}

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in I.

(6)

\displaystyle e_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\forall\ (i,j)\in E.

(7)

\displaystyle x_{ki}+x_{kj}+e_{ik}+e_{kj}\leq e_{ij}+3

\displaystyle\forall\ k\in I,\ \forall\ i,j\in V.

(8)

\displaystyle x_{ki}+x_{kj}+e_{ik}+e_{kj}\leq z_{ijk}+3\quad\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ k\in I,\ \forall\ i,j\in V.

(9)

\displaystyle x_{ij},e_{ij},z_{ijk}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in V,\ \forall\ k\in I.

(10)

The objective (
4
) is the total number of multiplications, i.e., the cost of the elimination sequence. Constraints (
5
) ensure that the relation defined by
x_{ij}=1
is transitive. Constraints (
6
) extend this to a total order on the internal vertices. Given a feasible solution, we may recover the vertex elimination order by examining the variables
x_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
.
Constraints (
7
) ensure that the initial structure of the graph is properly encoded.
The constraints (
8
) and (

) ensure correctness, and are motivated by the following observation: if the edge
(i,j)
exists at some point in the elimination process, then it must continue existing until either
i
or
j
is eliminated.
Then if
(i,k)
and
(k,j)
exist and
k
is eliminated before both
i
and
j
, the edge
(i,j)
must exist (
8
), and furthermore
(i,j,k)
is a multiplication at
k
(
9
).
Finally, constraints (
10
) ensure that all variables take binary values.

Optimizations
. We now present several optimizations intended to break symmetries and encode non-trivial lower bounds.
The first is the simplest. Let

We can then constrain some of our variables:

\displaystyle e_{ij}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in V\text{ such that }(i,j)\notin\widetilde{E}.

\displaystyle z_{ijk}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in V,k\in I\text{ such that }(i,k)\notin\widetilde{E}\text{ or }(k,j)\notin\widetilde{E}.

(12)

Our next optimizations provide useful lower bounds. For an internal vertex

,
we denote by

and

,

.
The following observation is easy to see.

Observation 1

(

]
)
.

For any

at the time of its elimination is at least

Note that

This motivates the following lower-bounding constraints:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq k}\sum_{j\not\in\{i,k\}}z_{ijk}

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{cut}({S},{k})\cdot\textnormal{cut}({k},{T})

\displaystyle\forall\ k\in I.

Constraints similar to (

) - (

]

Our last lower-bound constraint is new. Observe that without loss of generality, we may assume that every edge has at least one internal endpoint. Otherwise, the edge may be deleted as a safe preprocessing step, since neither endpoint can ever be eliminated.
A lower-bound follows.

Lemma 2

For every DAG

We give a proof in

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in I}\sum_{i\neq k}\sum_{j\not\in\{i,k\}}z_{ijk}\geq|E|/2.

Finally, to reduce the size of instances before solving, we use a widely accepted preprocessing rule. Note that while an argument is given in
[

Proposition 3

(
[

]

.

Let
D=(S\uplus I\uplus T,E)

2.2
ILP for
Minimum Edge Count

The ILP of
[
8
,
9
]
has
\Theta(n^{3})
variables and
\Theta(n^{4})
constraints, tracking events such as the creation of edge
(i,j)
via the elimination of vertex
k
at timestep

.
We again do away with the timestep abstraction, improving the number of constraints to
\Theta(n^{3})
. More significantly, we reduce the number of variables to
\Theta(n^{2})
.
The key tool is a recent result of
[
5
]
.

Lemma 4

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
D=(S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
be a DAG,
X\subseteq I
, and let
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
be any two permutations of
X
. Then
D_{\sigma_{1}}=D_{\sigma_{2}}
.

Lemma

4
essentially says that

is a vertex-selection problem, rather than a vertex-ordering problem. Henceforth, we will write
D_{X}
for the DAG resulting from eliminating the vertices
X\subseteq I
in any order; this notation is well-defined by
Lemma

4
.
Our first set of variables encodes the set
X
of eliminated vertices.

\displaystyle x_{i}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if $i\in X$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(15)

It remains to encode the structure of the DAG. We use another fundamental observation.

Observation 5
.

Let
D=(V=S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
,
X\subseteq I
, and
i,j\in V\setminus X
. Then
(i,j)\in E(D_{X})
if and only if there exists a directed path from
i
to
j
for which every internal vertex is in
X
.

We give a proof in
Section

A.1
. We can now use variables
y_{ij}
and

to encode, respectively, the existence of edges in the final DAG
D_{X}
, and at intermediate stages.

\displaystyle y_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $(i,j)\in E(D_{X})$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(16)

\displaystyle z_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if there exists $X^{\prime}\subseteq X$ with $(i,j)\in E(D_{X^{\prime}})$.}\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(17)

We can now present our new ILP for
Minimum Edge Count
.

minimize

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in V}y_{ij}

(18)

subject to

\displaystyle z_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\forall\ (i,j)\in E.

(19)

\displaystyle z_{ij}\geq z_{kj}+x_{k}-1

\displaystyle\forall\ i\in V,\ \forall\ k\in N^{+}(i),\ \forall\ j\in V.

(20)

\displaystyle y_{ij}\geq z_{ij}-x_{i}-x_{j}

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in V.

(21)

\displaystyle x_{i},y_{ij},z_{ij}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j\in V.

(22)

The objective (

) ensure correctness of the

is an out-neighbor of

to

2.3
Integrality Gaps

By relaxing constraints (
10
) or (
22
) to allow variable values in the range
[0,1]
, we obtain efficiently solvable linear programming (LP) relaxations for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
and
Minimum Edge Count
, respectively. It is natural to wonder whether rounding fractional solutions to these LPs may lead to high-quality approximations.
It turns out that this is unlikely to be a fruitful direction without additional assumptions.

Proposition 6
.

The fractional LP relaxation of the ILP defined by objective (
4
) and constraints (
5
)-(
14
) has an integrality gap of
\Omega(n^{1/4})
.

Proposition 7
.

The fractional LP relaxation of the ILP defined by objective (
18
) and constraints (
19
)-(
22
) has an integrality gap of
\Omega(n)
.

Both of our constructions (see
Section

A.1
) have vertices with degree linear in

, so it may still be interesting to explore rounding schemes in the bounded-degree setting.
Bounded in-degree, in particular, may be practically relevant for computational graphs.

3
Topological Ordering and Small Separators

The most classic vertex elimination schemes are the forward (
Fw
) and reverse (
Rev
) modes of AD, which eliminate internal vertices in forward (or reverse, respectively) topological order.
We now give a straightforward upper bound on the cost of these algorithms. Proofs of statements in this section can be found in
Section

A.2
.

Lemma 8
.

Eliminating
D=(S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
in forward topological order incurs cost at most
|S|\cdot|E|
. Eliminating in reverse topological order incurs cost at most
|T|\cdot|E|
.

Combining
Lemmas

2
and
8
immediately yields an approximation bound.

Theorem 9
.

The forward mode of AD is a
(2\cdot|S|)
-approximation for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
, and reverse mode is a
(2\cdot|T|)
-approximation. Both bounds are tight.

(e.g., see

,

,

]

for the edges from

to

in DAG

, dropping the subscript when the context is clear. Let

-

4
Summary of Evaluated Heuristics

In addition to
Fw
,
Rev
, and separator-based methods,
numerous other heuristics for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
have been proposed, which we summarize here.
A key is presented below.
The original
Greedy Markowitz
(
Mar
) rule of Griewank and Reese
[
11
]

greedily minimizes the cost of the current elimination.
Several variations have subsequently appeared. For a vertex
v\in I
, let
S_{v}\subseteq S
be the subset of sources from which
v
is reachable, and let
T_{v}
be defined similarly. Naumann
[
23
]
proposed
to modify
Mar
by considering the cost of eliminating
v

last
:
Relative Markowitz
(
rMar
) greedily minimizes
\mu({v})-|S_{v}|\cdot|T_{v}|
.
Two more variations, both proposed in
[
2
]
, consider the effect of eliminating
v
on the Markowitz degrees of the remaining vertices. Let
M({D})\vcentcolon=\sum_{v\in I}\mu({v})
denote the total Markowitz degree of
D
.

Maximum Total Markowitz Reduction
(
MR
) greedily selects
v
minimizing
M({D}_{/{v}})
. This idea can be refined as follows.
Define the
damage

d(v)
of eliminating
v
to be
d(v)\vcentcolon=M({{D}_{/{v}}})-M({D})+\mu({v})

6
Approximation Lower Bounds

The decision problems underlying
Optimal Vertex Elimination
and
Minimum Edge Count
have only recently been shown to be
NP
-complete
[
5
]
, and little else is known about their complexity.
We now establish the first approximation lower bounds for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
,
Minimum Edge Count
, and
Maximum Edge Reduction
.
For all three problems, we exclude a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) unless
\textnormal{{P}}=\textnormal{{NP}}
. In each case, the hardness holds even in bounded-degree DAGs; for
Minimum Edge Count
,
NP
-hardness was not previously known in bounded-degree DAGs. Finally, we exclude a sub-polynomial approximation for
Maximum Edge Reduction
, unless
\textnormal{{NP}}\subseteq\textnormal{{BPP}}
.
Complete proofs can be found in
Section

A.3
.

The existing
NP
-completeness reduction for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
is from
Vertex Cover

[
5
]
.
We show that approximations are preserved when the input graph is cubic.

A graph is
cubic
if every vertex has degree
3
.
Vertex Cover
remains
APX
-hard in cubic graphs
[
3
]
.

Theorem 12
.

Optimal Vertex Elimination
is
APX
-hard, even when restricted to DAGs with maximum in-degree 7, maximum out-degree 6, and maximum total-degree 9.

Henceforth, we focus on
Minimum Edge Count
and
Maximum Edge Reduction
, for which our constructions deviate from existing work.
First, we need an intermediate result.

Theorem 13

Maximum Independent Set
is

3

2

Our reduction makes use of a classic idea, e.g., as seen in
[

Our contribution is to show that the reduction is approximation-preserving when the input graph is cubic.

7
Conclusion

We have introduced and implemented novel ILP formulations for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
and
Minimum Edge Count
, enabling the assembly of a corpus of graphs for which optimal solutions are known.
We used this corpus to evaluate a wide range of heuristics, presenting measured approximation ratios.
On the theoretical side, we have given a tight analysis of the forward and reverse modes of AD, and have extended this analysis to provide a simple parameterized approximation guarantee.
Also, we have established the first approximation lower bounds for vertex elimination problems arising in AD.
Several interesting questions remain open, including the design of rounding schemes for our ILPs (in restricted settings), the tightness of the bound in
Theorem

11
, and an analysis of the forward and reverse modes of AD on evolution graphs.
Indeed, we find it surprising that these highly structured graphs are not better understood.
Finally, while our results exclude polynomial-time approximation schemes, the (non) existence of constant-factor approximations remains open.

A new preprocessing rule.
We conclude by introducing a new safe preprocessing rule for
Optimal Vertex Elimination
. To our knowledge, the only other known rule is
Proposition

3
.
Recall that we write
\mu_{D}^{*}(v)\vcentcolon=\textnormal{cut}({S},{v})\cdot\textnormal{cut}({v},{T})
for the minimum possible Markowitz degree of
v
. By
1

the cost of eliminating
v
in any elimination sequence is at least
\mu_{D}^{*}(v)
.
We write
\text{cost}(\sigma)
for the cost of an elimination sequence
\sigma
.

For two DAGs
D=(V=S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
and
D^{\prime}=(V^{\prime}=S^{\prime}\uplus I^{\prime}\uplus T^{\prime},E)
, we relax the subgraph relation by saying that

is

(denoted

) if

and
(E\setminus(S\times T))\subseteq E^{\prime}
.
Finally, for every internal node
v\in I
, we write

P^{+}_{D}(v)\vcentcolon=\{w\in N^{+}_{{D}}({v})\mid\exists u\in N^{-}_{{D}}({v})\text{ with }(u,w)\notin E\cup(S\times T)\}.

Intuitively,
P^{+}_{D}(v)
is the set of out-neighbors of

which cause

.
The following definition is symmetric.

P^{-}_{D}(v)\vcentcolon=\{u\in N^{-}_{{D}}({v})\mid\exists w\in N^{+}_{{D}}({v})\text{ with }(u,w)\notin E\cup(S\times T)\}.

Theorem 24
.

Let
D=(V=S\uplus I\uplus T,E)
be a DAG, and let
v\in I
be such that
\mu_{D}(v)=\mu_{D}^{*}(v)
,
P^{+}_{D}(v)\subseteq T
, and
|P^{+}_{D}(v)|\leq 1
.
Then there exists a minimum-cost total elimination sequence for
D
in which
v
appears first.

The rule can also be applied in the case where
\mu({v})=\mu_{D}^{*}(v)
,
P^{-}_{D}(v)\subseteq S
, and
|P^{-}_{D}(v)|\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma^{*}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{|I|})
be a minimum-cost total elimination sequence, and let
v_{k}=v
.
Let
\sigma=(v,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1},v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{|I|})
. We will show that
\text{cost}(\sigma)\leq\text{cost}(\sigma^{*})
.

We write
X_{i}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots v_{i-1}\}
for the set consisting of the first
i-1
vertices eliminated by
\sigma^{*}
.
Observe that for all
i>k
, we have
\mu_{{D}_{X_{i}\cup\{v\}}}(v_{i})=\mu_{{D}_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})
, since
X_{i}=X_{i}\cup\{v\}
.
Next, we observe that by our hypotheses, for all
i<k
we have

N^{-}_{D_{X_{i}\cup\{v\}}}(v_{i})=\begin{cases}N^{-}_{X_{i}}(v_{i})\backslash\{v\}&\text{ If }v\in N^{-}_{X_{i}}(v_{i})\\
N^{-}_{X_{i}}(v_{i})&\text{ Otherwise. }\end{cases}

N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}\cup\{v\}}}(v_{i})=\begin{cases}\left(N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})\backslash\{v\}\right)\cup\{t\}&\text{ If }v\in N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})\text{ and }P^{+}_{D}(v)=\{t\}\\
N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})\backslash\{v\}&\text{ If }v\in N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})\text{ and }P^{+}_{D}(v)=\emptyset\\
N^{+}_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})&\text{ Otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus,
\forall i<k
,
\mu_{D_{X_{i}\cup\{v\}}}(v_{i})\leq\mu_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})
. Lastly, since
\mu_{D}(v)=\mu^{*}_{D}(v)\leq\mu_{D_{X_{k}}}(v)

\text{cost}(\sigma)=\mu_{D}(v)+\underset{i\neq k}{\underset{i=1}{\overset{|I|}{\sum}}}\mu_{D_{X_{i}\cup\{v\}}}(v_{i})\leq\mu_{D_{X_{k}}}(v)+\underset{i\neq k}{\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}}\mu_{D_{X_{i}}}(v_{i})\leq\text{cost}(\sigma^{*}),

as desired.
