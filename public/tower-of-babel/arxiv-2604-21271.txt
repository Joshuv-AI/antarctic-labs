Downlink Channel Matrix Estimation from PMI-Only Feedback in FDD Systems: Maximum Likelihood and Sharp Excess Risk Bound
Jinchi Chen, Mingxi Hu, Peigang Jiang, Xin Meng, Ke Wei, Xianyin Zhang

Abstract
We study downlink channel estimation in a frequency-division duplex (FDD) massive MIMO system from PMI-only feedback under a 5G NR-type limited-feedback architecture. In this architecture, the user selects a preferred codeword from a shared codebook based on the reduced-dimensional channel and only reports its index (known as the precoding matrix indicator, PMI) back to the base station. Therefore, the channel must be estimated from these highly quantized, nonlinear PMI observations. Based on a probabilistic perturbation model, a constrained maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is proposed for this estimation problem, whose objective can also be interpreted as a relaxation of the hard empirical decision error. The Cramér–Rao bound is derived for the complex-valued model, with the global phase ambiguity handled via gauge-fixing. For the real-valued setting, a global excess-risk bound of order O ⁡ ( 1 / T ) O(1/\sqrt{T}) is established, which is then refined to a sharp local rate of order O ⁡ ( 1 / T ) O(1/T) under suitable identifiability conditions. Numerical results show that the MLE asymptotically attains the Cramér–Rao bound and outperforms several baseline methods on both synthetic data and realistic FDD channels.



1
Introduction

This paper studies downlink channel estimation in a frequency-division duplex (FDD) massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) system. We focus on the single-antenna user setting, where the base station has
d
antennas and the user has a single antenna. Thus the downlink channel can be represented by
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and the goal is to estimate
\bm{h}
at the base station from quantized feedback observations generated under a 5G NR-type limited-feedback mechanism.

In this mechanism, the base station and the user interact over multiple communication rounds, each consisting of a measurement phase and a feedback phase. More precisely,
at
t
-th communication round, the base station selects a dimensionality-reduction matrix
\bm{Q}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times p}
and transmits pilots, from which the user computes the effective channel. Assume that the base station and the user share a codebook

\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{v}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times N},

whose columns are candidate precoding vectors in the reduced-dimensional domain. The user then feeds back a precoding matrix indicator (PMI), which is the index of the codeword that maximizes the effective channel gain:

i_{t}=\argmax_{i=1,\dots,N}\big|\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{h}\big|^{2},\quad t=1,\cdots,T.

(1.1)

The corresponding channel quality indicator (CQI) is given by

\eta_{t}=\big|\bm{v}_{i_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{h}\big|^{2},\quad t=1,\cdots,T.

Since the base station does not directly observe
\bm{h}
, it must recover the channel solely from the feedback indices
\{i_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and possibly the CQI values
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
In this paper, however, we focus on the more challenging setting where the base station only observes the PMI sequence
\{i_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and does not use the CQI values
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Therefore, each measurement round provides only a discrete comparison outcome, rather than a direct linear measurement or a scalar gain value.

The estimation problem can therefore be stated as follows: given the measurement matrices
\{\bm{Q}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and the corresponding PMI feedback indices
\{i_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, recover the channel vector
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. This model captures the main difficulty of FDD downlink estimation under limited feedback: recovering a high-dimensional complex channel from highly nonlinear and compressed observations. While we focus on the single-antenna user case for simplicity, the proposed method can also be extended to the multi-antenna setting.

Related Work

Accurate downlink channel state information is essential for beamforming in FDD massive MIMO systems. This task is difficult because the BS cannot rely on channel reciprocity, while the channel dimension and the feedback cost both grow with the array size. A natural way to reduce this burden is to exploit long-term structure shared by uplink and downlink channels. [
2

To avoid this dependence on UL/DL structural extrapolation, many works aim at more direct downlink CSI acquisition. A common approach is to exploit channel sparsity and use compressive sensing for channel estimation and feedback reduction

Consequently, a gap remains between these techniques and the codebook-based limited-feedback interface of current cellular standards. To bridge this gap, another line of work adheres to the standard codebook-based limited-feedback architecture, and seeks to improve the feedback interface itself, for example through higher-resolution codebooks within the standard framework or through environment-aware and learned codebook design

1.2
Main Contributions

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

Maximum likelihood formulation.
We formulate direct downlink channel estimation from PMI-only feedback as a statistical inference problem. In contrast to existing works that rely on the joint use of PMI and CQI or target covariance recovery, beam refinement, or heuristic reconstruction, we focus on recovering the channel itself from ordinal codebook-comparison observations. To this end, we introduce a probabilistic perturbation model for PMI generation and derive a constrained maximum likelihood estimator (MLE). Moreover, we also show that the likelihood objective can be understood as a two-stage relaxation of the hard empirical decision error. Furthermore, we establish the Cramér–Rao bound (CRB) for the probabilistic model, properly accounting for the global phase ambiguity.

•

Sharp excess risk bound of MLE.
We establish a sharp local excess-risk bound of order
O(1/T)
for the MLE in the real-valued single-antenna user setting.
To this end, we first prove a global excess-risk bound of order
O(1/\sqrt{T})
. Combined with basic properties of the likelihood function, this bound guarantees that the estimator falls into a small neighborhood of the ground truth as
T
grows.
Within this local region, the population risk is strongly convex, allowing us to sharpen the global bound and obtain the fast
O(1/T)
rate under mild conditions.
It is worth noting that, although the analysis is carried out for the real-valued single-antenna user case, the MLE formulation itself is equally applicable to complex-valued channels and to the multi-antenna user setting.
We leave the extension of the sharp risk theory to these more general cases for future work.

•

Numerical validation.
We validate the proposed MLE method through numerical experiments on both synthetic data and FDD channels generated by QuaDRiGa. The experiments show that the MLE asymptotically attains the derived CRB as the number of feedback rounds grows. Furthermore, on the QuaDRiGa-generated FDD channels, the proposed method outperforms baselines such as the spectral method, alternating minimization, and subspace phase retrieval, demonstrating its effectiveness in both single-stream and two-stream scenarios.

2
MLE and Cramér-Rao Bound

2.1
MLE

In order to derive the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the PMI-only feedback, we approximate the feedback via a stochastic perturbation model. For ease of notation, let

\bm{a}_{t,i}=\bm{Q}_{t}\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times 1}.

Consider the following random model for the observed index at the
t
-th round:

\displaystyle I_{t}=\argmax_{i\in[N]}\left\{\frac{|\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}{\tau}+g_{t,i}\right\},

where
\tau>0
is a fixed temperature parameter, and
g_{t,i}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)
random variables whose probability density is given by
f(x)=\exp\bigl(-(x+e^{-x})\bigr)
. By the Gumbel-max trick, conditional on
\bm{h}
and
\bm{Q}_{t}
, it can be directly verified that the random variable
I_{t}
follows a multinomial distribution
[
17
]
. That is, for any
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{I_{t}=i\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=p_{t}(i;\bm{h})

where

\displaystyle p_{t}(i;\bm{x}):=\frac{\exp\big({|\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}/{\tau}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big({|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}/{\tau}\big)}.

In other words, we replace the hard
\arg\max
in (
1.1
) with a softmax distribution over the codewords, where
\tau
controls the sharpness of the approximation: as
\tau\to 0^{+}
, the softmax distribution tends to a one‑hot vector associated with the PMI feedback.

Define

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})=-\log p_{t}(i;\bm{x})=\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\frac{|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}{\tau}\right).

(2.1)

Then, given
T
independent observations
I_{1},\cdots,I_{T}
, the negative log-likelihood function is given by

\displaystyle L_{T}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\frac{|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}{\tau}\right).

(2.2)

We consider the following constrained maximum likelihood approach for the estimation of
\bm{h}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}\in\argmin_{x:\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R}L_{T}(\bm{x}),

(2.3)

where
R>0
is a fixed radius parameter. The constraint
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
is introduced to restrict the parameter space to a compact set. This is without loss of generality, since the channel vector
\bm{h}
has finite energy in practical systems, and
R
can be chosen sufficiently large such that the true channel lies in this set. From a statistical perspective, this constraint ensures well-posedness of the estimation problem and enables uniform control of the likelihood function and its derivatives, which is essential for establishing finite-sample guarantees.

Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x}):=\mathbb{E}\left\{L_{T}(\bm{x})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
and define the excess risk as

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h}).

(2.4)

A direct calculation yields that

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{t}(\cdot;\bm{h})\,\|\,p_{t}(\cdot;\bm{x})\bigr),

(2.5)

where
\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\cdot\,\|\,\cdot\bigr)
denotes the KL divergence of two probability distributions.
In Section
3
, we will establish the global and local excess risks for the MLE.

2.2
Alternative Interpretation of MLE

A straightforward way to estimate
\bm{h}
from the hard PMI observations is to minimize the empirical fraction of decision errors. That is, given the observations
\{I_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, consider

\displaystyle L_{T}^{0-1}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}1\Big\{I_{t}\notin\arg\max_{j\in[N]}|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}\Big\},

Noting that

\displaystyle I_{t}\notin\arg\max_{j\in[N]}|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\max_{j\in[N]}|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{t,I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}>0.

Therefore,
L_{T}^{0-1}(\bm{x})
can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle L_{T}^{0-1}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}1\Big\{\max_{j\in[N]}|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{t,I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}>0.\Big\}.

Though it is the most direct formulation of empirical error minimization under the hard PMI rule, the objective is discontinuous and difficult to optimize.

We now smooth this objective in two steps. First, replacing the discontinuous indicator loss by the margin yields

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{margin}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max_{j\in[N]}|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{t,I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}\right),\quad\bm{x}\neq 0.

This loss function is continuous, but still contains the nonsmooth max operator.
Thus, we further relax the
\max
function with its Moreau envelop (i.e., the log-sum-exp function),

\max\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}\}\rightarrow\tau\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(x_{i}/\tau)\right),

leading to

L_{T}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\tau\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(|\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}/\tau)-(\bm{a}_{t,I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x})^{2}\right)-\tau\log N,

which is exactly equivalent to the MLE loss. The parameter
\tau
controls the sharpness of this relaxation. A smaller
\tau
gives a sharper model and a sharper surrogate.

2.3
Cramér-Rao Bound

We use the Cramér–Rao bound as a benchmark for evaluating the performance of an estimator.
Let
{\cal M}
be the realification operator that maps a Hermitian matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
to a real symmetric matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
, i.e., for any Hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\bm{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{A})&-\Im(\bm{A})\\
\Im(\bm{A})&\Re(\bm{A})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Note that since
\bm{A}
is a Hermitian matrix, one has
\Re(\bm{A})=\Re(\bm{A})^{\mathsf{T}},\Im(\bm{A})=-\Im(\bm{A})^{\mathsf{T}}
, hence
{\cal M}(\bm{A})
is real symmetric.

Lemma 2.0

(Fisher information matrix)
.

Suppose that
\{I_{t}\}_{t=1,\cdots,T}
are independent with
I_{t}\sim p_{t}(\cdot;\bm{h})
. Let
\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}=[\Re(\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}~\Im(\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{A}_{t,i}=\bm{a}_{t,i}\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{H}}
and
\bm{g}_{t,i}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}
. Then the Fisher information matrix with respect to
\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{{\bm{\theta}}})=\frac{4}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{t}(i;\bm{h})\bm{g}_{t,i}\bm{g}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\frac{4}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{t}(i;\bm{h})\bm{g}_{t,i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{t}(i;\bm{h})\bm{g}_{t,i}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.

The proof of this lemma is presented Section
6.1
. The following lemma establishes the equivalence of the Fisher information matrix over the set
\{\bm{h}e^{-i\varphi}\}
.

Lemma 2.0
.

Let
\bm{h}_{\varphi}:=\bm{h}e^{i\varphi}
and define

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{h})\\
\Im(\bm{h})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},\quad{\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{h}_{\varphi})\\
\Im(\bm{h}_{\varphi})\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{R}_{\varphi}:=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\varphi\,\bm{I}_{d}&-\sin\varphi\,\bm{I}_{d}\\
\sin\varphi\,\bm{I}_{d}&\cos\varphi\,\bm{I}_{d}\end{bmatrix}.

Then

\displaystyle F({\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi})=\bm{R}_{\varphi}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{R}_{\varphi}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Proof.

First, it is easy to see that

p_{t}(i;\bm{h}_{\varphi})=p_{t}(i;\bm{h})
for any
t\in[T],\ i\in[N]
. Moreover, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{h}e^{i\varphi})\\
\Im(\bm{h}e^{i\varphi})\end{bmatrix}=\bm{R}_{\varphi}{\bm{\theta}}.

It can be easily checked that
\bm{R}_{\varphi}{\cal M}(\bm{A})={\cal M}(\bm{A})\bm{R}_{\varphi}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{t,i}({\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi})={\cal M}(\bm{A}_{t,i}){\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi}={\cal M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})\bm{R}_{\varphi}{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{R}_{\varphi}{\cal M}(\bm{A}_{t,i}){\bm{\theta}}=\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{g}_{t,i}({\bm{\theta}}).

Substituting
p_{t}(i;\bm{h}_{\varphi})=p_{t}(i;\bm{h})
and
\bm{g}_{t,i}({\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi})=\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{g}_{t,i}({\bm{\theta}})
into the formula for

F({\bm{\theta}})
, we obtain

\displaystyle F({\bm{\theta}}_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\left(\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})-\bm{R}_{\varphi}\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)\left(\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})-\bm{R}_{\varphi}\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bm{R}_{\varphi}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{R}_{\varphi}^{\mathsf{T}},

which completes the proof.
∎

With a slight abuse of notation, let
p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{\theta})
be the reparameterization of
p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})
using
\bm{\theta}
. Since

\displaystyle p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})=p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h}e^{\imath\varphi}),

it is not hard to see that

p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{\theta})=p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{\theta}).

Approximating
\bm{R}_{\varphi}
by the first order Taylor expansion yields

\bm{R}_{\varphi}\approx\bm{R}_{0}+\varphi\cdot\frac{d}{d\varphi}\bm{R}_{\varphi}\Big|_{\varphi=0}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{d}&0\\
0&\bm{I}_{d}\end{bmatrix}+\varphi\begin{bmatrix}0&-\bm{I}_{d}\\
\bm{I}_{d}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore one has

\log p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{\theta})\approx\log p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{\theta}+\varphi\cdot\bm{J}\bm{\theta})\quad\mbox{when $\varphi$ approaches $0$},

where

\displaystyle\bm{J}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\bm{I}_{d}\\
\bm{I}_{d}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Letting
\bm{u}=\bm{J}\bm{\theta}
, it follows that

\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{\theta})=0,

which implies

\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}F(\bm{\theta})\bm{u}=0.

The next lemma verifies this result rigorously.

Lemma 2.0
.

One has

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{u}=0.

Proof.

For any
t\in[T]
and
i\in[N]
, one has

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p_{t}(i;\bm{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\tau}{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right){\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\tau}{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{J}\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right){\bm{\theta}},

where we have used
\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}=-\bm{J}
. Moreover, by the definition of
{\cal M}
, one has
\bm{J}{\cal M}(\bm{A})={\cal M}(\bm{A})\bm{J}
, which implies that

\displaystyle(\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right))^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)\bm{J}

\displaystyle=-\bm{J}\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right).

It means that
\bm{J}\left(\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{M}(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)
is anti-symmetry, which implies that
\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p_{t}(i;\bm{h})=0
and
\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{u}=0
.
∎

To deal with the singular property of
F({\bm{\theta}})
caused by the phase ambiguity, we seek a lower dimensional representation of
\bm{\theta}
that can
be (locally) identified from the likelihood and thus yields a nonsingular Fisher information matrix.

Note that
\bm{u}=\bm{J}\bm{\theta}
is a tangent vector of the circle
\{\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{\theta}:~\varphi\in[0,2\pi]\}
at
\bm{\theta}
.
Let
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times(2d-1)}
be a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of
\bm{u}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Range(\bm{D})=\bm{u}^{\perp}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bm{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}=\bm{I}_{2d-1}.

It is evident that the subspace spanned by
\bm{D}
intersects with the equivalence class/circle
\{\bm{R}_{\varphi}\bm{\theta}:~\varphi\in(0,2\pi]\}
only at two isolated points
\bm{\theta}
and
-\bm{\theta}
. Moreover, one has

\bm{\theta}=\bm{D}\bm{\alpha}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\bm{\alpha}=\bm{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\theta}.

Consider the log-likelihood with respect to
\bm{\alpha}
(for the
t
-th observation):

\log p_{t}(I_{t};\bm{D}\bm{\alpha}).

Note
\bm{\alpha}
can be locally identified from the likelihood since
\bm{\alpha}
and
-\bm{\alpha}
are isolated. By the chain rule, it can be obtained that

F(\bm{\alpha})=\bm{D}^{\mathsf{T}}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{D}.

Moreover, it is evident that

\tr((\bm{D}^{\mathsf{T}}F({\bm{\theta}})\bm{D})^{-1})=\tr(F({\bm{\theta}})^{\dagger})

since
\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}F(\bm{\theta})\bm{u}=0
and
\Range(\bm{D})\perp\bm{u}
. Thus, for any locally unbiased estimator
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}
of the constrained parameter
{\bm{\alpha}}
, the constrained Cramér–Rao bound
[
37
,
36
,
5
]
gives

\mathbb{E}\left\{\|\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq\tr\left(F(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\right).

Next we establish the relation with the estimation error in the signal domain.
For an unbiased estimator
\widehat{\bm{h}}
, define its oracle phase-aligned representative by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}}:=\widehat{\bm{h}}\exp\!\left(-i\arg(\bm{h}^{\mathsf{H}}\hat{\bm{h}})\right).

Then

\bm{h}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi\in(0,2\pi]}\|\widehat{\bm{h}}-\bm{h}e^{i\varphi}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}}-\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}.

Let

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\hat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}})\\
\Im(\hat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}})\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}.

Since
\Im(\bm{h}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{al}})=0
, one has

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}\perp\bm{u}
and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}\in\Range(\bm{D}).

It follows that
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}=\bm{D}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}
and

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi\in(0,2\pi]}\|\widehat{\bm{h}}-\bm{h}e^{i\varphi}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{al}}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{D}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{al}}-\bm{\alpha})\|_{2}^{2}=\|\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{al}}-\bm{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}.

It follows that

\mathbb{E}\left\{\min_{\varphi\in(0,2\pi]}\|\widehat{\bm{h}}-\bm{h}e^{i\varphi}\|_{2}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq\tr\left(F(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\right).

The discussion above is based on a gauge-fixing technique when
F(\bm{\theta})
is singular due to an inherent ambiguity (here the global phase rotation). Note that we have used a gauge direction
\bm{u}=\bm{J}\bm{\theta}
which relies on the unknown true parameter
\bm{\theta}
and thus is not known
a priori
. This is not a problem for the purpose of theoretical analysis, since the Cramér–Rao bound is a statement about the estimation problem itself rather than a particular estimator. That being said, one can also construct an implementable gauge based on a fixed reference vector. We refer interested readers to
[
11
]
for a general treatment of constrained Cramér–Rao bounds under singular Fisher information matrices.

3
Sharp Excess Risk Bound of MLE

In the last section, we have established the Cramér–Rao bound (CRB) for the case
d=1
. It is well known that the CRB typically scales as
O(1/T)
, i.e., it is inversely proportional to the number of measurements. Empirical simulations (see Figure
1
) show that the maximum likelihood estimator attains the CRB when
T
is sufficiently large. Therefore, it is desirable to show that the reconstruction error of the MLE decays at the
O(1/\sqrt{T})
rate in the parameter space, or equivalently, that the excess risk converges at the
O(1/T)
rate. This section considers the real-valued case for ease of presentation. We first establish a global
O(1/\sqrt{T})
excess risk bound, and then prove a sharp local excess risk rate of
O(1/T)
.
In order to establish these results, we need to make several assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The radius
R
is chosen to be sufficiently large such that
\|\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq R
. The measurement matrices
\{\bm{Q}_{t}\}_{t=1,\cdots,T}
are fixed and orthogonal.

Remark 3.1
.

The assumption on the measurement matrices
\{\bm{Q}_{t}\}
is standard and widely adopted in the FDD massive MIMO literature
(
see for example
[
39
,
16
,
24
,
26
]
)
. In practical systems,
\bm{Q}_{t}
represents a dimensionality-reduction or precoding operation applied at the base station, which can be designed in advance and controlled across transmission rounds. The orthogonality condition
\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}=\bm{I}
has a clear physical meaning in communication systems. It corresponds to a semi-unitary precoding that preserves signal energy. This constraint is commonly satisfied when
\bm{Q}_{t}
is constructed from unitary transformations
(
e.g., DFT-based or Haar-distributed matrices
)
, which are routinely used in practical codebook design and pilot/beamforming strategies.

More notation is needed before we can state the other two assumptions. Recalling that
\bm{a}_{t,i}=\bm{Q}_{t}\bm{v}_{i}
, one has
\|\bm{a}_{t,i}\|_{2}=1
since
\bm{Q}_{t}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\bm{v}_{i}
is a unit-norm vector. Define

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{t,i}=\bm{a}_{t,i}\bm{a}_{t,i}^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad q_{t}(i;\bm{M})=\frac{\exp\bigl(\left\langle\bm{A}_{t,i},\bm{M}\right\rangle/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\bigl(\left\langle\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\right\rangle/\tau\bigr)}.

(3.1)

Assumption 2
.

There exists a constant
p_{\min}>0
such that for every
t\in[T]
,

q_{t}(i;\bm{M})\geq p_{\min}

holds for any
\bm{M}
that can be expressed as a convex combination of
m

(
with
m\geq 1
)
vectors within the radius
R
, i.e.,

\bm{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\bm{x}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\|\bm{x}_{k}\|_{2}\leq R,

where
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}=1
.

In fact, our analysis only requires Assumption
2
to hold for matrices of the form

\bm{M}=\lambda\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+(1-\lambda)\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}},

where
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. As a trivial consequence, Assumption
2
implies

p_{t}(i;\bm{x})\geq p_{\min},\quad\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R.

The following lemma provides an admissible choice for
p_{\min}
.

Lemma 3.0
.

Assumption
2
holds with

p_{\min}=\frac{1}{1+(N-1)e^{R^{2}/\tau}}.

The proof of this lemma is provided in Section
6.2
. Next, we present our last assumption.

Assumption 3
.

There exists a constant
\kappa_{0}>0
such that, for every
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\,\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle^{2}\geq\kappa_{0}\,\|\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Even though this assumption is only needed for
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
, it holds with high probability under a standard random design model uniformly for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.0
.

Assume that

|\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}_{j}|\leq\mu,\quad\forall\,i\neq j,

for some
\mu\in[0,1)
. Let
\bm{Q}_{1},\dots,\bm{Q}_{T}
be i.i.d. Haar-distributed on the Stiefel manifold

\mathrm{St}(d,p):=\{\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}:\bm{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{Q}=\bm{I}_{p}\}.

Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least

1-\Bigl(\frac{d(d+1)}{2}-1\Bigr)\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}T}{(d-1)(d+2)}\right),

one has

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\,\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle^{2}\geq\kappa_{0}(\delta)\,\|\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2},\quad\forall\,\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

\kappa_{0}(\delta):=(1-\delta)\frac{4N(N-1)(1-\mu^{2})}{d(d+2)}.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Section
6.3
. In addition, the following result which follows immediately from Assumption
3
will also be used, see Section
6.4
for a proof.

Lemma 3.0
.

Under Assumption
3
, for every
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{z}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{h}\bm{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle^{2}\geq\kappa_{0}\,\left\|\bm{z}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{h}\bm{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}.

Recalling the definition of the excess risk in (
2.4
), we are now ready to present our main results, whose proofs can be found in Section
5
. The first result is about the global excess risk bound.

Theorem 3.4

(Global excess risk bound)
.

Under Assumption
1
, there exist absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that, for every
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq\frac{C_{1}R^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}+\frac{C_{2}R^{2}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\ln(1/\gamma)}

holds with probability at least
1-\gamma
.

Before establishing the sharp local excess risk bound, we need a result which can guarantee that
\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}
will be sufficiently close to
\bm{h}
provided the excess risk is small.

Theorem 3.5

(From excess risk to parameter error)
.

Define

\beta_{0}=\frac{\kappa_{0}p_{\min}^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, if
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
satisfies

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon\leq\frac{\beta_{0}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{16},

then

\dist(\bm{x},\bm{h})\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\varepsilon}{\beta_{0}}}.

Building on Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
, we can finally establish the following sharp local excess-risk bound for the MLE.

Theorem 3.6

(Sharp local excess risk bound)
.

Let
\rho>0
be a constant such that

\rho\leq\min\!\left\{\frac{\|\bm{h}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},\,\frac{\beta_{0}}{8L_{H}}\right\}.

Define

\displaystyle L_{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{48R^{3}}{\tau^{3}}+\frac{24R}{\tau^{2}},

\displaystyle T_{1}(\gamma,\rho)

\displaystyle=C\,\frac{R^{4}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\ln(3/\gamma)})^{2}}{\tau^{2}\beta_{0}^{2}\rho^{4}},

\displaystyle T_{2}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=C\max\!\left\{\frac{\log(6d/\gamma)}{\tau^{2}\beta_{0}^{2}},\frac{\log(6d/\gamma)}{\tau\beta_{0}}\right\},

\displaystyle T_{3}(\gamma,\rho)

\displaystyle=C\,\frac{(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\ln(3/\gamma)})^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{\tau^{2}\beta_{0}^{2}\rho^{2}}.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, there exist absolute constants
C_{1}>0
such that if

T\geq\max\{T_{1}(\gamma/3,\rho),\,T_{2}(\gamma/3),\,T_{3}(\gamma,\rho)\},

then with probability at least
1-\gamma
,

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq C_{1}\,\frac{d+\ln(1/\gamma)}{\tau^{2}\beta_{0}T}\,\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}.

Numerical Experiments

4.1
Experiments for CRB

We first provide a simple numerical experiment to verify that the MLE asymptotically achieves the CRB in Section 2.3. The dimension of the test channel is set to
d=16
, while the reduced dimension is set to
p=4
. The channel
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
is generated from a standard complex Gaussian distribution, followed by normalization:

\bm{h}=\frac{\bm{g}}{\|\bm{g}\|_{2}},\qquad\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\bm{I}_{d}).

At the
t
-th communication round, we first draw a random complex Gaussian matrix
\bm{G}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times p}
with i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
entries. The measurement matrix
\bm{Q}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times p}
is then obtained as the
Q
factor of the reduced QR decomposition of
\bm{G}_{t}
. The codebook is chosen as the normalized DFT codebook

\bm{V}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\,\mathrm{DFT}(p),

and hence the number of codewords is
N=p
. The temperature parameter in the softmax observation model is set to be
\tau=0.05
.

Given the true channel
\bm{h}
and the measurement matrix
\bm{Q}_{t}
, the feedback index
I_{t}
is sampled according to the softmax distribution introduced in Section 2.1, namely,

\mathbb{P}(I_{t}=i)=p_{t}(i;\bm{h}),\qquad i\in[N].

After collecting the feedback indices from
T
communication rounds, we then compute the maximum likelihood estimator
\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}
.

Since the observation model is invariant under a global phase rotation, the channel is only identifiable up to a common phase. Therefore, we evaluate the reconstruction error using the phase-aligned mean squared error (MSE),

\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T},\bm{h}):=\min_{\varphi\in(0,2\pi]}\|e^{-i\varphi}\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}-\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}e^{-i\arg(\bm{h}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})}-\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}.

For each
T
, we run
100
independent Monte Carlo trials with the same ground-truth channel
\bm{h}
and report the average MSE.
As discussed in Section 2.3, we compute the CRB as

\mathrm{CRB}(T):=\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\bm{F}(\theta)^{\dagger}\bigr),

where
\bm{F}(\theta)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of the Fisher information matrix.

Figure
1
compares the empirical MSE of the MLE with the CRB as a function of the number of communication rounds
T
. We observe that, although the empirical MSE is noticeably above the CRB when
T
is small, the two curves become very close as
T
increases. Moreover, the CRB itself decays as
c/T
(as illustrated by the fitted line), and the empirical MSE follows the same trend for moderate and large
T
. This confirms that the
O(1/T)
decay rate established in Section
3
is tight.

T
. The dashed line is a fitted curve of the form
c/T
.

4.2.2
Methods

The proposed MLE method is compared against three baselines: spectral method, alternating minimization (AM), and subspace phase retrieval (Subspace-PR). These baseline methods are first reviewed, followed by the extension of the MLE to the general
r
-stream case.

Spectral method.

The spectral method is introduced by Yang
[
45
]
, which estimates the beam space by

\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{spec}}=\mathrm{eigvecs}\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{t}\bm{V}_{I_{t}}\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}},r\right).

Here
\mathrm{eigvecs}(\cdot,r)
denotes the matrix formed by the
r
dominant eigenvectors of the input matrix.

In particular, when
T=1
, the spectral method reduces to the two-stage precoding baseline
[
44
,
28
]
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{Type-I}}=\bm{Q}_{1}\bm{V}_{I_{1}},

(4.1)

where the dimensionality-reduction matrix
\bm{Q}_{1}
acts as the outer precoder and
\bm{V}_{I_{1}}
is the Type-I codebook
[
1
]
entry indexed by the PMI.

Alternating minimization (AM).

The alternating minimization method is introduced in
[
24
]
. In the single-stream case, the estimate is obtained as a solution to the following optimization problem that incorporates the CQI values:

\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{AM}}\in\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}\right|-\sqrt{\eta_{t}}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

By introducing auxiliary phases
\{\phi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, this problem can be reformulated as

\widehat{\bm{h}}_{\mathrm{AM}}\in\argmin_{\bm{x},\{\phi_{t}\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}\,e^{j\phi_{t}}-\sqrt{\eta_{t}}\right|^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2},

and can be solved via alternating minimization.

For the multi-stream case, the sequential extension based on equal CQI partition is used in
[
24
]
. Specifically, let

\bm{V}_{I_{t}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1,I_{t}}&\cdots&\bm{v}_{r,I_{t}}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\eta_{k,t}=\frac{\eta_{t}}{r},\quad k=1,\dots,r.

For each stream
k
, let
\bm{P}_{k}
denote an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of the previously estimated streams and assume
\bm{h}_{k}\approx\bm{P}_{k}\bm{u}_{k}
. The estimation can be obtained by first solving

\widehat{\bm{u}}_{k,\mathrm{AM}}\in\argmin_{\bm{u}_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left|\bm{v}_{k,I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{P}_{k}\bm{u}_{k}\right|-\sqrt{\eta_{t}/r}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}\|\bm{u}_{k}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad k=1,\dots,r,

and then set

\widehat{\bm{h}}_{k,\mathrm{AM}}=\frac{\bm{P}_{k}\widehat{\bm{u}}_{k,\mathrm{AM}}}{\|\bm{P}_{k}\widehat{\bm{u}}_{k,\mathrm{AM}}\|_{2}},\qquad\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{AM}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{h}}_{1,\mathrm{AM}}&\cdots&\widehat{\bm{h}}_{r,\mathrm{AM}}\end{bmatrix}.

Subspace phase retrieval (Subspace-PR).

Downlink reconstruction from PMI and CQI can be viewed as a quadratic inverse problem, or equivalently, a phase retrieval problem
[
14
]
. Furthermore, although uplink and downlink channels are not instantaneously reciprocal in FDD systems, they typically share partially common spatial structure under the same propagation environment
[
47
,
46
]
. This motivates the subspace phase retrieval (Subspace-PR) method introduced in
[
27
]
.

More precisely, let
\bm{B}_{k}=\mathrm{eigvecs}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ul}},k)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times k}
be a basis for the dominant uplink subspace. Then the downlink beam matrix is then parameterized as

\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{subspace\mbox{-}pr}}=\bm{B}_{k}\bm{S},\qquad\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times r},

which reduces the number of unknown parameters from the ambient antenna dimension
N_{t}
to the low-dimensional coefficient matrix
\bm{S}
. We consider the following two subspace phase-retrieval formulations:

\widehat{\bm{S}}_{\mathrm{subspace\mbox{-}pr}}\in\argmin_{\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times r}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B}_{k}\bm{S}\|_{F}^{2}-\eta_{t}\right|^{2},

and

\widehat{\bm{S}}_{\mathrm{subspace\mbox{-}pr}}\in\argmin_{\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times r}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B}_{k}\bm{S}\|_{F}-\sqrt{\eta_{t}}\right|^{2}.

The first formulation is solved via Wirtinger Flow (WF)
[
8
]
, and the second via Amplitude Flow (AF)
[
41
]
. We report the better of the two results for each test case.

MLE.

As mentioned earlier, the MLE method is capable of handling the multi-antenna and multi-stream case, where the estimate is given by

\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{MLE}}\in\argmin_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times r}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\left(\frac{\|\bm{V}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}-\|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}}{\tau}\right).

As with the subspace phase-retrieval method, we can also incorporate an uplink-informed subspace prior into the MLE, leading to the subspace-constrained formulation

\widehat{\bm{S}}\in\argmin_{\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times r}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\left(\frac{\|\bm{V}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B}_{k}\bm{S}\|_{F}^{2}-\|\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B}_{k}\bm{S}\|_{F}^{2}}{\tau}\right),

(4.2)

followed by

\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\bm{B}_{k}\widehat{\bm{S}},\qquad\widehat{\bm{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times r}.

This is the natural multi-stream and subspace-constrained extension of the MLE objective in (
2.2
). Note that, if the ground-truth channel satisfies
\bm{H}_{\star}\in\mathrm{span}(\bm{B}_{k})
with some coefficient matrix
\bm{S}_{\star}
, then the estimation problem is exactly equivalent to the original one but restricted to a known
k
-dimensional subspace. Therefore, the same theoretical analysis applies to the coefficient variable
\bm{S}
after replacing the ambient dimension
N_{t}
with the effective subspace dimension
k
.

It is worth noting that when
T=1
, the solution of the MLE is indeed given by Type-I codebook (two-stage precoding) baseline.
For simplicity, consider the single-stream case. Recall that the shared codebook is

\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{v}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times N}.

When only one feedback round is available, the MLE objective reduces to

L_{1}(\bm{x})=\ell_{1}(I_{1};\bm{x})=\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\left(\frac{|\bm{a}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}-|\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}}{\tau}\right).

(4.3)

Letting

\bm{x}=\alpha\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}},\qquad\alpha>0,

we have

|\bm{a}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}=\alpha^{2}|\bm{a}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}|^{2},\qquad|\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{x}|^{2}=\alpha^{2}\|\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}\|^{4}.

Substituting this into (
4.3
) gives

L_{1}(\alpha\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}})=\log\!\Bigg(1+\sum_{j\neq I_{1}}\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\big(|\bm{a}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}|^{2}-\|\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}\|^{4}\big)}{\tau}\right)\Bigg).

If no other effective codeword is collinear with
\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}
, then for every
j\neq I_{1}
,

|\bm{a}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}|^{2}<\|\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}\|^{4},

and hence each exponential term vanishes as
\alpha\to\infty
, which implies that

L_{1}(\alpha\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}})\to 0,\qquad\alpha\to\infty.

Therefore, without an explicit norm constraint, the optimal solution of the MLE tends to align with the direction of the selected effective codeword
\bm{a}_{1,I_{1}}=\bm{Q}_{1}\bm{v}_{I_{1}}
. The same argument applies to the subspace-constrained MLE whenever
\bm{Q}_{1}\bm{v}_{I_{1}}\in\mathrm{span}(\bm{B}_{k})
. In this case, one can parameterize the estimate as
\bm{B}_{k}\bm{s}
and recover the same directional behavior as in the unconstrained formulation. This condition is satisfied in our experiments, since
\bm{Q}_{1}
is chosen according to the same uplink-informed subspace
\mathrm{span}(\bm{B}_{k})
.

4.2.3
Implementation Details

We adopt gradient descent to find the solution of the MLE, with the maximum number of iterations set to
100
. The same iteration budget is also used in AM and the gradient-based methods for Subspace-PR. In addition, the algorithms are also terminated whenever the two consecutive iterates are sufficiently close to each other (relative change less than
10^{-3}
). In the single-stream case, this relative change is evaluated as

\frac{\bigl\|\mathbf{x}^{(t)}-e^{-j\phi_{t}}\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}\bigr\|_{2}}{\bigl\|\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}\bigr\|_{2}},\quad\phi_{t}=\arg\left((\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\right),

while in the multi-stream case it is evaluated as

\frac{\bigl\|\mathbf{X}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\bigr\|_{F}}{\bigl\|\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\bigr\|_{F}},\quad\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}}\bigl\|\mathbf{X}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{X}^{(t-1)}\bigr\|_{F}.

While the original paper adopts random initialization for AM, we find in our experiments that initializing AM with the spectral estimate
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{\mathrm{spec}}
yields better performance in the single-stream case, and we therefore adopts this scheme as the default. In the multi-stream case, since the streams are estimated sequentially under the orthogonality constraints, we follow the random initialization scheme used in
[
24
]
. As observed in
[
24
]
, the regularization parameter
\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}
has a substantial impact on performance. We thus tune
\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}
on a validation set constructed from uplink channel samples available at the base station, using the grid

\{0.001,\,0.01,\,0.1,\,1,\,10,\,100,\,1000\}.

Based on this validation, the selected values are
\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}=1
for the single-stream case and
\lambda_{\mathrm{AM}}=100
for the two-stream case.

For Subspace-PR, we use a subspace-aware spectral initialization. Specifically, we form the reduced-domain sample covariance

\bm{C}_{\mathrm{spec}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\bm{B}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t})\bm{V}_{I_{t}}\bm{V}_{I_{t}}^{\mathsf{H}}(\bm{B}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Q}_{t})^{\mathsf{H}},

and set

\bm{S}_{\mathrm{init}}^{\mathrm{spec}}=\mathrm{eigvecs}(\bm{C}_{\mathrm{spec}},r).

As the ablation study indicates, the performance of the MLE is insensitive to the choice of initialization. Hence, we adopt the first
r
columns of an identity matrix as the default in all experiments. For both the Subspace-PR and the MLE, the subspace dimension is set to
k=8
(i.e.,
\bm{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times 8}
). In addition, we set
\tau=1
for the MLE, which yields the best performance in the ablation study.

When
T=1
, we use a Type-I compatible dimensionality-reduction matrix,

\bm{Q}_{1}=\mathrm{eigvecs}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{ul}},8)\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\frac{\mathrm{DFT}(2)\otimes\mathrm{DFT}(2)}{2}\right),

which combines the user-specific uplink subspace information with the dual-polarized Type-I codebook structure
[
1
]
. When
T>1
, diversity across the matrices
\bm{Q}_{t}
becomes essential. We therefore consider the following two options (note that the first dimensionality-reduction matrix is always set to
\bm{Q}_{1}
):

•

Fully random design: each
\bm{Q}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{32\times 8}
is generated independently from a complex random Gaussian matrix, followed by orthogonalization.

•

Structured design with fixed outer and random inner layers:

\bm{Q}_{t}=\bm{Q}_{\mathrm{out}}\bm{U}_{t},

(4.4)

where
\bm{Q}_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathrm{eigvecs}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ul}},8)\in\mathbb{C}^{32\times 8}
is fixed, and
\bm{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{8\times 8}
is a random unitary matrix generated independently at each round. This construction preserves the dominant user subspace in the outer layer while injecting additional diversity through the random inner layer.

In the single-stream case, the proposed MLE achieves the best performance under both constructions of

5.1
Proof of Theorem
3.4

Define

\displaystyle Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R}\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})-L_{T}(\bm{x})+L_{T}(\bm{h})\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R}\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})\right)\right).

Since
\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T}
is the solution to (
2.3
) and
\|\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq R
, one has

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})

\displaystyle=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-L_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})+L_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-L_{T}(\bm{h})+L_{T}(\bm{h})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})

\displaystyle\leq\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-L_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})+L_{T}(\bm{h})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})

\displaystyle\leq Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T}).

Therefore, it suffices to bound
Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})
next.

•

Lipschitz of
\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})
.
Recall the definition
\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})
in (
2.1
). A direct calculus yields

\nabla\ell_{t}(i;x)=-\nabla\log p_{t}(i;x)=\frac{2}{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{t}(j;\bm{x})(\bm{A}_{t,j}-\bm{A}_{t,i})\bm{x},

where
\bm{A}_{t,j}=\bm{a}_{t,j}\bm{a}_{t,j}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Since
\|\bm{a}_{t,j}\|_{2}\leq 1
, one has

\|\nabla\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\tau}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}.

Therefore, for any
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\|\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq R
, there holds

\displaystyle|\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x}+u(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\rangle du\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{1}\|(1-u)\bm{x}^{\prime}+u\bm{x}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2R}{\tau}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

It also follows that

|\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{4R^{2}}{\tau}.

•

Apply bounded difference inequality to
Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})
.
It can directly verified that
Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})
satisfy the following bounded difference property:

\displaystyle|Z(i_{1},\cdots,i_{t},\cdots,i_{T})-Z(i_{1},\cdots,i_{t}^{\prime},\cdots,i_{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R}\left\{\left(\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{h})\right)-\left(\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{h})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4R^{2}}{\tau T}.

Thus the application of the bounded difference inequality (also known as McDiarmid’s inequality
[
40
]
) yields that

\displaystyle Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\leq\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}+\frac{CR^{2}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\log(1/\gamma)}

(5.1)

holds with probability at least
1-\gamma
, where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

•

Bound
\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}
.
By the symmetrization argument (see for example
[
40
, Exercise 8.11]
), there holds

\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}\leq\frac{2}{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}\left(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})\right)\right|\right\},

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{T}
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables.
Define

X_{\bm{x}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}\bigl(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})\bigr).

Conditioned on
\{I_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, it is not hard to see that for any
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\|\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq R
,
X_{\bm{x}}-X_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
is
\nu^{2}
-sub-Gaussian with

\nu^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x}^{\prime}))^{2}\leq\frac{4TR^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

Let
B=\{\bm{x}:~\|x\|_{2}\leq R\}
. Noting that
X_{\bm{h}}=0
, by the Dudely’s integral (see for example
[
40
, Theorem 8.1.3]
), one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}\left(\ell_{t}(I_{t};x)-\ell_{t}(I_{t};h)\right)\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(B,\sqrt{4TR^{2}/\tau^{2}}\|\cdot\|_{2},u)}du

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{R^{2}\sqrt{T}}{\tau}\sqrt{d},

where the upper bound for the covering number of a unit-norm ball can be found for example in
[
40
, Corollary 4.2.11]
.
This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}\lesssim\frac{R^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}.

(5.2)

Combining inequalities (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) together completes the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq\beta_{0}\rho^{2}/8.

when

. That is, let

Then
\mathbb{P}\{E_{1}\}\geq 1-\gamma/3
when

.

Define

Then, by Lemma

when

Let

and

By Lemma
5.4
, there also holds

.
Next, we are going to prove by contradiction (inspired by the proof of the fast rate in statistical learning, see for example
[

) that

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq\varepsilon_{*}

on the event

which occurs with probability at least
1-\gamma
.

Note that when

, one has

provided the constant in front of

is sufficiently large. It follows that

\varepsilon_{*}\leq\frac{\beta_{0}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{16}.

Thus for any
\bm{x}
such that
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
and

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\leq\varepsilon_{*},

Theorem
3.5
implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\bm{x},\bm{h})\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\varepsilon_{\star}}{\beta_{0}}}=r_{*}.

(5.3)

Assume
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\widehat{\bm{h}}_{T})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})>\varepsilon_{*}
. Due to the continuity of

, there exists

such that

satisfies

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x}_{\eta})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})=\varepsilon_{\star}.

Moreover, one has

and

.

Consequently, conditioned on

,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\star}

\displaystyle=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x}_{\eta})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})

\displaystyle F({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\left(\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}-\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)\left(\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}-\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left\{\bm{g}_{t,I_{t}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

Proof of Lemma
3.1

6.3
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Define

\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}=\{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\bm{M}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{M},\mathrm{trace}(\bm{M})=0\}.

Then it can be easily seen that
\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}
is a linear subspace of dimension
m=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}-1
. For every
t\in[T]
, define the self-adjoint positive operator
\mathcal{V}_{t}:\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}
,

\mathcal{V}_{t}(\bm{M})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\rangle(\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j}).

It is evident that for any
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}
, there holds

\langle\mathcal{V}_{t}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}.

Next the goal is to establish a lower bound of

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{V}_{t}.

Recall that
\bm{a}_{t,i}=\bm{Q}_{t}\bm{v}_{i}
. Thus,

\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j}=\bm{Q}_{t}(\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}.

For the expectation of
\mathcal{V}_{t}
, we have the following fact whose proof is present later in this section.

Fact 6.1
.

When
\bm{Q}_{t}
are uniformly distributed, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathcal{V}_{t}\}=\mu_{V}\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}},

where

\mu_{V}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Under the assumption
|\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}_{j}|\leq\mu
, one has

\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}=2(1-(\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}_{j})^{2})\geq 2(1-\mu^{2})

Therefore,

\mu_{V}\geq\frac{4N(N-1)(1-\mu^{2})}{(d-1)(d+2)}.

Moreover, for any
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}
, there holds

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{V}_{t}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j}\|_{F}^{2}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=m\mu_{V}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2},

where the last line follows from the definition of
\mu_{V}
. In other words, one has

0\preceq\mathcal{V}_{t}\preceq m\mu_{V}\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}}.

Therefore, the application of the matrix Chernoff inequality (see for example
[
38
, Theorem 5.1.1]
) yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\lambda_{\min}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{V}_{t}\right)\leq(1-\delta)\mu_{\min}\right\}\leq m\left[\frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right]^{\mu_{\min}/R}\leq m\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu_{\min}}{2R}\right),

where

\mu_{\min}=T\cdot\mu_{V}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R=m\cdot\mu_{V}.

It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{V}_{t}\right)\leq(1-\delta)\mu_{V}\right\}\leq m\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}T}{2m}\right)=\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta T^{2}}{(d+2)(d-1)}\right).

Thus, with probability at least

1-\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta T^{2}}{(d+2)(d-1)}\right),

there holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\geq(1-\delta)\frac{4N(N-1)(1-\mu^{2})}{(d-1)(d+2)}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\mbox{for any }\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}.

(6.1)

In order to apply this result to
\Delta_{\bm{x}}:=\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}
, consider

\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Delta_{\bm{x}})=\Delta_{\bm{x}}-\frac{\mathrm{trace}(\Delta_{\bm{x}})}{d}\bm{I}_{d}.

It is evident that
\mathrm{trace}(\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Delta_{\bm{x}}))=0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Delta_{\bm{x}})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Delta_{\bm{x}}\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{(\mathrm{trace}(\Delta_{\bm{x}}))^{2}}{d}

\displaystyle\geq(1-1/d)\|\Delta_{\bm{x}}\|_{F}^{2},

where the inequality follows from

\displaystyle(\mathrm{trace}(\Delta_{\bm{x}}))^{2}=(\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}\leq\|\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\Delta_{\bm{x}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Therefore applying (
6.1
) to
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Delta_{\bm{x}})
completes the proof.

Proof of Fact
6.1
.

We need to show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{E}\{\mathcal{V}_{t}\}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\langle\mathcal{V}_{t}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\rangle\}=\mu_{V}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2},\quad\mbox{for any }\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\langle\mathcal{V}_{t}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\{\langle\bm{A}_{t,i}-\bm{A}_{t,j},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle\bm{Q}_{t}(\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{Q}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle\bm{U}_{t}\bm{J}(\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\right\},

where
\bm{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are Haar-distributed on
O(d)
and

\bm{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{p}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}.

Since

\bm{J}(\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\bm{J}(\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2},

it suffices to show that for any
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle\bm{U}_{t}\bm{C}\bm{U}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\right\}=\frac{\|\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}}{m}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}.

Without loss of generality, assume

\bm{C}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})\mbox{ with }\sum_{r=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}=0.

Letting
\bm{U}_{t}=[\bm{u}_{1},\cdots,\bm{u}_{d}]
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle\bm{U}_{t}\bm{C}\bm{U}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}(\underbrace{\bm{u}_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{M}\bm{u}_{r}}_{:=X_{r}})\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}^{2}]+\sum_{r\neq s}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}\mathbb{E}\{X_{r}X_{s}\}.

Since
\bm{U}_{t}
is rotation invariant, there exist
a
and
b
such that

\mathbb{E}\{X_{r}^{2}\}=a\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\{X_{r}X_{s}\}=b\mbox{ for all }r,s\mbox{ and }r\neq s.

Since
\sum_{r=1}^{d}X_{r}=\mathrm{trace}(\bm{M})=0,
one has

0=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\sum_{r=1}^{d}X_{r}\right)^{2}\right\}=d\cdot a+(d-1)d\cdot b.

Therefore, one has

b=-\frac{a}{d-1}.

Thus, it remains to compute
a
, which can expressed as

a=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[(\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{M}\bm{u})^{2}].

To this end, for
\bm{u}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, there holds (see for example
[
13
,
21
]
, a detailed proof is also provided at the end of this subsection for the completeness)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}u_{\ell}]=\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}}{d(d+2)},

(6.2)

where
u_{i}
is the
i
-th entry of
\bm{u}
and
\delta_{ab}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Therefore,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{u}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{M}\bm{u}_{1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}M_{ik}u_{i}u_{k}\right)^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}M_{ik}M_{j\ell}u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}u_{\ell}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}M_{ik}M_{j\ell}\mathbb{E}\left\{u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}u_{\ell}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}M_{ik}M_{j\ell}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}M_{ik}^{2}+\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}M_{ii}M_{jj}+\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}M_{ij}M_{ji}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\frac{2}{d(d+2)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}M_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d(d+2)}\left\|\bm{M}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2},

where step
(a)
is due to (

), step

follows from that
\bm{M}
is symmetric and traceless. Moreover, since
\mathrm{trace}(\bm{C})=0
, one has

\sum_{r\neq s}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}=\left(\sum_{r=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}\right)^{2}-\sum_{r=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}^{2}=-\|\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle\bm{U}_{t}\bm{C}\bm{U}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{M}\rangle^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=a\sum_{r=1}^{d}\lambda_{r}^{2}+b\sum_{r\neq s}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}

\displaystyle=(a-b)\|\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\|\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}}{(d-1)(d+2)}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}}{m}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof of Equation (
6.2
).

Let
\bm{g}\sim N(0,\bm{I}_{d})
, and define

r:=\|\bm{g}\|_{2},\qquad\bm{u}:=\frac{\bm{g}}{\|\bm{g}\|_{2}}.

It is well known that
\bm{u}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, and that
\bm{u}
is independent of
r
. Since
\bm{g}=r\bm{u}
, we have, for any fixed
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{g})^{4}=r^{4}(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u})^{4}.

Taking expectations and using the independence of
r
and
\bm{u}
gives

\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{g})^{4}]=\mathbb{E}[r^{4}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u})^{4}].

Hence

\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u})^{4}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{g})^{4}]}{\mathbb{E}[r^{4}]}.

We now compute the numerator and denominator separately. Firstly, since
\bm{g}\sim N(0,\bm{I}_{d})
, the random variable
\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{g}
is Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}
. Thus

\mathbb{E}[(a^{\mathsf{T}}g)^{4}]=3\|a\|_{2}^{4}.

Secondly, since

r^{2}=\|\bm{g}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{d}g_{m}^{2},

we have
r^{2}\sim\chi_{d}^{2}
. A simple computation yields that

r^{4}=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}g_{m}^{2}\right)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{d}g_{m}^{4}+2\sum_{1\leq m<n\leq d}g_{m}^{2}g_{n}^{2}.

Taking expectations and using independence, one has

\mathbb{E}[r^{4}]=d\,\mathbb{E}[g_{1}^{4}]+2\binom{d}{2}\mathbb{E}[g_{1}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[g_{2}^{2}].

Since
\mathbb{E}[g_{1}^{4}]=3
and
\mathbb{E}[g_{1}^{2}]=1
, this gives

\mathbb{E}[r^{4}]=3d+d(d-1)=d(d+2).

Combining the two identities yields

\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u})^{4}]=\frac{3\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{4}}{d(d+2)}.

Expanding the left-hand side gives,

\mathbb{E}[(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u})^{4}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}u_{i}\right)^{4}\right]=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\,\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}u_{\ell}].

Notice that

\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{4}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}^{2}\right)=\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}\delta_{ij}\right)\left(\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{k}a_{\ell}\delta_{k\ell}\right)=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell},

Similarly,

\displaystyle\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{4}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}.

Averaging the above three identities yields

\displaystyle\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{4}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\,\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}}{3}.

Therefore,

\frac{3\|\bm{a}\|_{2}^{4}}{d(d+2)}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{d}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{\ell}\,\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}}{d(d+2)}.

Since this identity holds for every
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the coefficients of the quartic forms must agree, and hence

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}u_{\ell}]=\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}}{d(d+2)}.

6.5
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Choose

such that

\dist(\bm{x},\bm{h})=\|\bm{x}-s\bm{h}\|_{2}.

Define

\bm{u}=\bm{x}-s\bm{h},\qquad\bm{v}=\bm{x}+s\bm{h}.

Since
(s\bm{h})(s\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}
, we have

\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-(s\bm{h})(s\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\|\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\|\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}+2\left\langle\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(2\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}.

By construction,

\|\bm{u}\|_{2}=\|\bm{x}-s\bm{h}\|_{2}=\dist(\bm{x},\bm{h}).

Moreover, since
s
is chosen to minimize the distance, one has

\|\bm{x}-s\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{x}+s\bm{h}\|_{2}.

Equivalently,

\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{x}+s\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{x}-s\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}=4s\,\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{h}\geq 0.

Hence

\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}+2s\,\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{h}\geq\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 2\min\{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2},\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}\}.

Substituting this bound into the previous estimate yields

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{h}\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\dist(\bm{x},\bm{h})^{2}\cdot 2\min\{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2},\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2},\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}\}\,\dist(\bm{x},\bm{h})^{2}.

Taking square roots on both sides completes the proof.

Due to the sign symmetry, it suffices to consider the case

•

Lipschitz of softmax.

Given
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, denote the softmax function by

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})=\frac{\exp(z_{i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(z_{j})},\quad i=1,\cdots,N.

Then

\displaystyle\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})=\begin{bmatrix}-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\sigma_{1}(\bm{z})\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})^{2}\\
\vdots\\
-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\sigma_{N}(\bm{z})\end{bmatrix}.

One has

\displaystyle\|\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\sigma_{j}(\bm{z})+\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})(1-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}))+\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})(1-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}))

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}+(1-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})+(1-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}))

\displaystyle=1

and

\displaystyle|\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\bm{x}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})x_{i}-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}(\bm{z})x_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})|x_{i}|+\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}(\bm{z})|x_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})\bm{x}|\leq 2\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})-\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime}+u(\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}))^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime})du\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\nabla\sigma_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime}+u(\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}))^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime})\right|du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\tau T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bm{C}_{t}(\bm{x})-\bm{C}_{t}(\bm{y})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4}{\tau^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bm{S}_{t}(\bm{x})-\bm{S}_{t}(\bm{y})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4}{\tau^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bm{v}_{t}(\bm{x})\bm{v}_{t}(\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{v}_{t}(\bm{y})\bm{v}_{t}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left(\frac{48R^{3}}{\tau^{3}}+\frac{24R}{\tau^{2}}\right)}_{=L_{H}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

Taking expectations on both sides yields

\left\|\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{y})\right\|\leq L_{H}\,\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

•

Lower bound on population Hessian.
When
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\rho\leq\frac{\beta_{0}}{8L_{H}}
, one has

\left\|\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\right\|\leq L_{H}\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq L_{H}\cdot\rho\leq\frac{\beta_{0}}{8}.

Therefore, by Weyl’s inequality,

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x}))\geq\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h}))-\left\|\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})\right\|\geq\frac{7\beta_{0}}{8},

where the last line follows from Lemma
5.2
.

•

Lower bound on sample Hessian.
By (
6.4
) and (
6.5
), the difference

\nabla^{2}L_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})
can be written as

\nabla^{2}L_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})=-\frac{2}{\tau T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}-\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\Bigr),

which is independent of
\bm{x}
. Let

\bm{Z}_{t}:=\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}-\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

Then
\bm{Z}_{1},\dots,\bm{Z}_{T}
are independent, mean-zero, symmetric random matrices. Moreover,

\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}\right\|\leq\left\|\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}\right\|+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{A}_{t,I_{t}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\|\leq 2.

In addition,

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{Z}_{t}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}^{2}\right\|\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}\right\|^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 4T.

Therefore, Matrix Bernstein’s inequality (see for example
[
40
, Theorem 5.4.1]
) implies that, with probability at least
1-\gamma
,

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{Z}_{t}\right\|\leq\sqrt{8T\log(2d/\gamma)}+\frac{4}{3}\log(2d/\gamma).

Consequently,

\sup_{\{\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\rho\}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R\}}\left\|\nabla^{2}L_{T}(\bm{x})-\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})\right\|\leq\frac{4}{\tau}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2d/\gamma)}{T}}+\frac{8}{3\tau}\frac{\log(2d/\gamma)}{T}.

If
T\geq T_{2}(\gamma)
, then the right-hand side is at most
\beta_{0}/8
. Together with the lower bound for
\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\bm{x})
, one has
for every
\bm{x}
such that
\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\rho
and
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq R
,

\nabla^{2}L_{T}(\bm{x})\succeq\nabla^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\frac{\beta_{0}}{8}\bm{I}_{d}\succeq\frac{3\beta_{0}}{4}\bm{I}_{d}.

6.8
Proof of Lemma
5.4

Due to the sign symmetry (i.e,
L_{T}(\bm{x})=L_{T}(-\bm{x})
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(-\bm{x})
), we only need to consider

Z_{r_{*}}(I_{1},\dots,I_{T}):=\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}}\Bigl(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})-L_{T}(\bm{x})+L_{T}(\bm{h})\Bigr).

Similarly to the argument in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we can decompose the proof into three steps.

•

Local Lipschitz of
\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})
.
When
\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}
, one has

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2}.

Therefore, as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, one can show that

\displaystyle|\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i;\bm{x}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})}{\tau}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}\quad\mbox{for $\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}$ and $\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}$}.

•

Apply bounded difference inequality to
Z_{r_{*}}(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})
.
Recall that

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{x})-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muL\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{T}(\bm{h})-L_{T}(\bm{x})+L_{T}(\bm{h})

\displaystyle=\bar{L}_{T}(\bm{x})-\bar{L}_{T}(\bm{h})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h})\right).

One has

\displaystyle|Z_{r_{*}}(i_{1},\cdots,i_{t},\cdots,i_{T})-Z_{r_{*}}(i_{1},\cdots,i_{t}^{\prime},\cdots,i_{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}}|(\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{h}))-(\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{h}))|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq r_{*}}\left(|\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t};\bm{h})|+|\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(i_{t}^{\prime};\bm{h})|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{T}\frac{2(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})}{\tau}\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4r_{*}(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})}{\tau T}.

Thus, by bounded difference property, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there holds

\displaystyle Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\leq\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}+\frac{c_{0}r_{*}(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})}{\tau\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}.

•

Bound
\mathbb{E}\left\{Z_{r_{*}}(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}
.

By symmetrization,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{Z_{r_{*}}(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}\leq\frac{2}{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{h}\|\leq r_{*}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{h}))\right\},

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{T}
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. Since

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(I_{t},\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t},\bm{x}^{\prime}))^{2}\leq\frac{4T(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},

conditioned on
I_{t},t=1,\cdots,T
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}(\ell_{t}(I_{t};\bm{x})-\ell_{t}(I_{t},\bm{x}^{\prime}))\mbox{ is }\frac{4T(r_{*}+\|h\|_{2})^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\mbox{sub-Gaussian}.

Thus, a similar chaining argument yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{Z(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})\right\}\leq\frac{c_{1}r_{*}(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})\sqrt{d}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}.

Thus, with probability at least
1-\gamma/3
, there holds

\displaystyle Z_{r_{*}}(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{1}r_{*}(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})\sqrt{d}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}+\frac{c_{0}r_{*}(r_{*}+\|\bm{h}\|_{2})}{\tau\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\log(3/\gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log({1}/\gamma)})r_{*}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}}{\tau\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle\leq CK\frac{(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\gamma)})^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{\tau^{2}\beta_{0}{T}}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{*}=\frac{\beta_{0}r_{*}^{2}}{8}=\frac{K^{2}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\gamma)})^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{8\tau^{2}\beta_{0}{T}},

which implies

\displaystyle Z_{r}*(I_{1},\cdots,I_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{*}}{2}

provided
K\geq 16C
.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We develop a maximum likelihood estimator for downlink channel estimation from PMI‑only feedback in FDD systems under a 5G NR‑type limited‑feedback architecture. For the real‑valued single‑antenna user setting, a sharp excess‑risk rate of order
O(1/T)
is established. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the MLE attains the Cramér–Rao bound and outperforms baselines such as the spectral method, alternating minimization, and subspace phase retrieval on both synthetic data and QuaDRiGa‑generated FDD channels.

There are two lines for research for future work. First, since the experimental results indicate that the MLE shows superior performance on complex‑valued channels and in the multi‑stream case, it is natural to extend the sharp risk theory to these more general settings. The gauge-fixing technique used to resolve phase ambiguity in the CRB analysis may also prove useful in this extension. Second, it is of interest to study the lifted formulation based on
\bm{X}=\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{H}}
and to clarify its relation with the nonconvex maximum likelihood formulation, particularly regarding tightness, statistical efficiency, and whether the two estimators coincide.
