Drug-Induced Liver Injury Associated With an Over-The-Counter Supplement
Authors: David Pearose, Esteban Perez Montoya, John Gutierrez, John Cuenca, Rodolfo Singleterry, Ashequlla Qudratulla, Anish Saikumar, Mohammad Housini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUse of herbal and dietary supplements has increased worldwide, with a parallel rise in herbal and dietary supplement-associated liver injury in the United States. Limited regulation and inconsistent labeling complicate diagnosis. We report a 65-year-old woman who developed severe hepatocellular drug-induced liver injury after chronic ingestion of Artri King, an unregulated product previously flagged for undisclosed pharmaceutical ingredients, including diclofenac and corticosteroids. Evaluation excluded viral, autoimmune, and metabolic etiologies. Liver biopsy demonstrated a hepatocellular injury pattern, and liver tests improved after discontinuation. This case underscores the need for routine supplement screening and stronger regulatory oversight.

INTRODUCTION

Drug-induced liver injury remains a significant cause of acute hepatic dysfunction and liver transplantation, particularly due to unregulated herbal and dietary supplements (HDS).1–5 Artri King, an over-the-counter (OTC) supplement marketed for joint pain, is widely available through informal markets and online vendors, and has been flagged by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for containing undeclared pharmaceutical agents, including diclofenac, dexamethasone, and methocarbamol.6,7 Diclofenac, a known hepatotoxin, contributes to oxidative stress and immune-mediated hepatocellular injury.4 Dexamethasone can exacerbate injury through immune suppression and cytochrome P450 induction.5 Despite FDA warnings, this supplement remains easily accessible, underscoring persistent gaps in HDS regulation.6

A recent review identified HDS, including weight-loss agents, bodybuilding products, and multi-ingredient formulations, as responsible for a rising proportion of hepatotoxicity cases in the United States.3 These products often contain undeclared or mislabeled substances, complicating diagnosis and management. To our knowledge, biopsy-documented cases of liver injuries associated with the supplement have not been previously reported, and this case highlights key clinical and histopathologic features.

CASE REPORT

A 65-year-old Hispanic woman with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hypothyroidism, prediabetes, and obesity presented with progressive nausea, dizziness, dark urine, pale stools, and pruritus. Laboratory studies revealed aspartate aminotransferase 2,474 U/L, alanine transaminase 1,834 U/L, international normalized ratio 1.4 (peaked to 1.9), and total bilirubin 14.9 mg/dL. The patient had been taking an OTC supplement, 2 tablets 3 times daily, for 5 months for chronic joint pain. Hepatitis A, B, and C panels were nonreactive and testing for HIV, cytomegalovirus, Epstein Barr virus, and herpes simplex virus was negative.

The patient's clinical course was never complicated by hepatic encephalopathy and therefore did not meet criteria for acute liver failure. Additional evaluation included negative autoimmune serologies (antinuclear antibody, anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-LKM), negative metabolic testing (ceruloplasmin, iron studies, ferritin, and negative cholestatic markers for primary biliary cholangitis). Imaging revealed gallstones and a mildly dilated common bile duct, but no obstructive pathology. Upon discontinuation of the supplement, liver function improved markedly over 2 weeks. DISCUSSION

The supplement taken by this patient has previously been associated with adverse effects including Cushing syndrome, adrenal insufficiency, and hyperglycemia, findings attributed to undisclosed corticosteroid adulteration. The pathophysiology likely reflects a multiagent synergistic toxicity. Diclofenac, a known hepatotoxin identified in formulations of this OTC supplement, undergoes cytochrome-P450 mediated metabolism to reactive intermediates that induce mitochondrial dysfunction and hepatocyte apoptosis. Dexamethasone may further potentiate injury through immune modulation and enzyme induction, while methocarbamol may contribute additional metabolic stress. These mechanisms are consistent with the hepatocellular pattern of injury observed in this case and with previous reports of supplement-related liver injury involving multi-ingredient products. Previous reports of toxicity have largely emphasized endocrine and metabolic complications related to adulterant exposure. By contrast, our case expands the reported adverse-event spectrum by demonstrating severe hepatocellular drug-induced liver injury with histopathologic correlation and biochemical improvement following supplement discontinuation. The patient's R factor was 17.9, supporting a hepatocellular pattern of injury. The Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method score was 8. In addition, this case illustrates a unique challenge faced by clinicians in US cities along the Mexico border, such as San Antonio. Owing to high cross-border traffic, many patients obtain health products, pharmaceuticals, and supplements from Mexico, where labeling and regulation may differ significantly. These items are often omitted during medication reconciliation and may not be captured in US-based electronic medical records or prescription databases. In this case, the patient obtained Artri King in Mexico, underscoring how cross-border health practices can complicate diagnosis and delay identification of potentially hepatotoxic agents. Similar dynamics are likely encountered in other border regions including parts of Texas, Arizona, and California.

As HDS-related drug-induced liver injury continues to rise, this case reinforces the need for heightened clinical awareness, routine screening for supplement use, and stronger regulatory oversight.8

Unregulated supplements pose a serious hepatotoxic risk. Despite FDA warnings, this OTC supplement remains widely accessible, reflecting critical gaps in oversight and safety evaluation frameworks.9 This case demonstrates how commonly used supplements may lead to liver injury through undisclosed pharmaceutical adulterants, even after FDA-issued recalls.6 Awareness among clinicians and tighter regulation are urgently needed. Providers should routinely screen for supplement use in patients with unexplained liver injury.8,9
