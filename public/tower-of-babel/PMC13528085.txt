The relationship between social support and lying flat among Chinese university students: the chain mediation roles of meaning in life and psychological resilience
Authors: Yizhang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background"Lying flat" has emerged in recent years as a widely discussed sociocultural phenomenon in China, garnering significant attention across various sectors of Chinese society. Lying flat has become a representative psychological and behavioral phenomenon among Chinese university students, yet its underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently examined. Existing research suggests that social support, meaning in life, and psychological resilience may play key roles, but integrative frameworks are still scarce. Adopting the perspective of Positive Psychology 2.0 (PP2.0), this study aims to test a chain mediation model examining associations among social support, meaning in life, psychological resilience, and lying flat.MethodsThe study targeted a university student population and adopted a cross-sectional online survey design, yielding 2,323 valid responses from 30 provinces across China. Data collection instruments included the Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire, the Social Support Scale, the Meaning in Life Questionnaire, and the Psychological Resilience Scale. SPSS 27.0 was used for reliability and validity analyses, correlation analysis, and multicollinearity diagnostics. Subsequently, AMOS 28.0 was used to construct a structural equation model (SEM). Common method bias was examined, and the chain mediation role was tested while controlling for four demographic variables (gender, grade, university type, and household registration type).Results(1) Lying flat was significantly and negatively correlated with social support, meaning in life, and psychological resilience (r ranged from − 0.62 to − 0.43); (2) meaning in life mediated the association between social support and lying flat; (3) psychological resilience also mediated the association between social support and lying flat; and (4) meaning in life and psychological resilience jointly formed a chain-mediated role between social support and lying flat, with the mediating effect accounting for 70.2% of the total effect, and the model explaining 51.9% of the variance in lying flat.DiscussionThese findings underscore the roles of social support, meaning in life, and psychological resilience in understanding the lying flat phenomenon among university students, highlighting the complex interplay among these factors. Informed by these observed relationships and existing theoretical perspectives, an Integrative analytical Framework of “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” (RPCCF) is proposed as a heuristic framework to offer a possible interpretive perspective for understanding lying flat and help inform preliminary future discussions on educational or intervention-relevant considerations among Chinese university students.

Introduction

In recent years, a distinctive social phenomenon has emerged among Chinese university students, attracting extensive attention from both the public and academic communities. As a new life attitude and value orientation, lying flat not only reflects young people’s psychological responses during a period of social transition but also reveals the complex interaction between social structures and individual psychology. To clarify this concept, it is necessary to distinguish it from related psychological constructs. Unlike burnout (a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to high-demand situations; [1] and amotivation (the loss of behavioral intention due to the perceived lack of contingency between actions and outcomes; [2], the connotation of lying flat goes beyond simple exhaustion or loss of intention to act, and instead reflects a paradoxical state characterized by persistent psychological struggle and behavioral muddling along [3]. In addition, lying flat differs from learned helplessness (which emphasizes uncontrollable cognition formed after repeated failures; [4] and also from depression (a clinical syndrome characterized by pervasive mood disturbance and functional impairment; [5]. Rather than focusing on emotional exhaustion, motivational deficit, uncontrollable cognition, or psychopathological symptoms, lying flat refers to a passive adaptation orientation embedded in a specific cultural context that emerges after the dissolution of clear personal goals [6]. Specifically, it describes a pattern in which individuals, after losing clear goals, gradually fall into behavioral passive indolence and, under the combined influence of internal and external pressures, exhibit either a low mood or a tendency toward muddling along [3]. It should be further noted that although lying flat manifests behaviorally as withdrawal and passivity and may be accompanied by certain negative emotions, its meaning may not be limited to individual-level functional decline when examined within broader social structures and cultural contexts [7, 8]. With the continued prominence of issues such as “involution” and the “low-desire society” in Chinese society, young people’s coping strategies toward excessive competition, social anxiety, and a lack of meaning have gradually shifted from explicit “striving” to implicit “letting go” [9, 10]. This shift is deeply embedded in the unique socio-psychological structure of contemporary Chinese society. In a social environment characterized by intense competition and relatively constrained upward mobility, lying flat may also be understood as a strategic retreat adopted by individuals when their goals are blocked, or as a low-intensity response to structural overcompetition [9, 11]. Therefore, lying flat cannot simply be conceptualized as a clinical disorder or a pure motivational deficit. It is necessary to recognize its dual nature: it is both a passive adaptation pattern based on goal disengagement and a coping strategy embedded in the Chinese sociocultural context [6, 12]. This passive adaptive posture driven by loss of goals distinguishes it conceptually from existing psychological constructs and provides further justification for studying it as a novel socio-psychological variable. Therefore, exploring the underlying mechanisms and internal logic of lying flat from a psychological perspective carries important theoretical and practical significance. It should be noted that although lying flat may involve complex meanings such as strategic retreat or value reconstruction in the macro social context, at the level of psychological measurement this study focuses specifically on its individual-level manifestations of passive adaptation, namely passive indolence, loss of goals, low mood, and muddling along. The operational definition and measurement presented in this study do not negate the broader socio-cultural connotations of lying flat. Instead, they focus on quantifiable and testable individual psychological dimensions within an empirical research framework, thereby ensuring internal consistency between the conceptual definition and its measurement. A search conducted in the CNKI and Google Scholar databases showed that the earliest research on lying flat was published in July 2021. As of August 2025, a total of 143 Chinese and English academic articles has been published, including 26 English-language papers titled “lying flat” or “tang ping.” Current research on lying flat is mainly focused on examining the social roots of this phenomenon from macro perspectives such as social class, material economy, and cultural context. Some studies adopt a social class perspective, arguing that social structural changes and restricted social mobility create a gap between young individuals’ social expectations and their actual opportunities, thereby triggering a psychological state of passive withdrawal [7]. However, such studies rely primarily on textual interpretation to capture collective emotions and fail to quantitatively examine the relative contributions of different psychological factors at the individual level. Other studies interpret the phenomenon from a material-economic perspective, suggesting that economic development and improvements in the social security system have provided some young people with the material conditions to choose lying flat. After basic living needs are satisfied, the motivation for continuous high-intensity striving may weaken among some individuals [10, 13]. Nevertheless, these studies tend to simplify complex psychological processes into a one-directional logic of “conditions enabling behavioral choices,” lacking micro-level examination of individuals’ internal meaning-construction processes. In addition, some scholars explore the issue from cultural and value perspectives, arguing that the diffusion of postmodernism, consumerism, and hedonism has led some young people to question and distance themselves from the dominant narrative of “active striving” [14]. However, these studies often treat youth as homogeneous cultural recipients and overlook the heterogeneous responses that arise from differences in individuals’ psychological resources. Overall, although these studies reveal the social background of the lying-flat phenomenon from different macro perspectives, most research still remains within a social explanatory framework, and systematic exploration of the underlying individual psychological mechanisms remains relatively limited.

Notably, in recent years a small number of studies have begun to examine lying flat from the perspective of individual psychological variables by employing mediation or moderation models. For example, some research has shown that Active/Passive Involution can predict lying-flat behavior through the chain mediating effects of perceived stress and anxiety [15]. In addition, physical exercise has been found to influence lying-flat tendencies through the chain mediating mechanisms of Temporal Focus and meaning in life [16]. Related research also indicates that Personal Relative Deprivation may suppress Self-improvement motivation through System-justifying Belief, thereby providing supplementary evidence for understanding the psychological adaptation process underlying lying flat [17]. However, overall, existing studies still exhibit several limitations. First, from an overall trend perspective, previous research has primarily focused on risk factors (e.g., stress and anxiety), whereas the systematic integration of protective factors remains relatively limited. Second, existing models often display a pattern of “partial explanation” based on a single psychological resource. Although they reveal several meaningful associative pathways, systematic examination of the synergistic mechanisms among multiple psychological resources is lacking. Third, in the process of individual psychological adaptation, the collaborative mechanism between external environments and internal resources remains to be further clarified. Based on the above research status, it is necessary to introduce a more integrative theoretical perspective to systematically explain the psychological mechanisms underlying the lying-flat phenomenon.

Therefore, this study examines the issue from the perspective of individual psychological mechanisms and adopts Existential Positive Psychology 2.0 (PP 2.0) proposed by Wong [18, 19] as the overarching theoretical framework. Unlike traditional positive psychology, which mainly emphasizes strengths and happiness, existential positive psychology focuses on how individuals achieve integrative adaptation and growth under conditions of stress, adversity, and uncertainty through pillars and core features such as social connection, meaning-making, and resilience formation. PP2.0 emphasizes that the pursuit of meaning is the core motivation of human existence and the foundation of psychological well-being. At the same time, it stresses that meaning enhances individuals’ capacity to endure suffering and cope with adversity, suggesting that the experience of meaning can buffer stress, promote the formation and development of resilience, and help individuals achieve integrative growth in the face of difficulties. Although these propositions mainly belong to theoretical integration and interpretation rather than empirical testing based on path models, the logical structure of PP2.0 suggests an intrinsic relationship among social connection, meaning-making, resilience formation, and positive coping. Social connection provides the relational foundation for meaning-making, meaning-making provides motivation and direction for resilience formation, and resilience in turn supports positive coping and psychological health. Within this theoretical context, individual psychological adaptation is not the result of a single resource but a process in which multiple resources operate synergistically in a certain logical sequence.

The core constructs of PP2.0 exhibit a degree of alignment with the key psychological characteristics of lying flat among university students. PP2.0 emphasizes that social connectedness, meaning-making, and resilience are the pillars and core features that enable individuals to cope with adversity and uncertainty. In contrast, lying flat, as a form of passive adaptation, typically manifests precisely as the impaired states of these three aspects: weakened social connectedness leads to insufficient social support (social support can, to some extent, be regarded as a form or outcome of social connection, and the two are closely related; [20, 21]; the collapse of the meaning system results in a loss of existential value and sense of goals; and the depletion of resilience makes it difficult for individuals to maintain active coping. This internal correspondence enables PP2.0 to provide valuable theoretical clues for the logical relationships among the variables in this study.

Therefore, social support, meaning in life, and psychological resilience are not isolated variables. Incorporating these three variables into a unified model for systematic examination, and deriving the mediating order according to the theoretical logic of PP2.0, may help reveal the psychological mechanisms underlying the lying-flat phenomenon within the integrative framework of PP2.0, thereby providing a theoretically deeper explanation for the passive adaptation behaviors of contemporary university students.

To examine the internal logic of the above theoretical model in light of recent empirical research, the present study conducts a detailed analysis based on four core pathways. First, social support may be associated with lying flat. Sun [22] suggested that social support can alleviate the negative attitudes of young people who adopt lying flat coping styles. Tong [23] argued that the fairness and accessibility of social support can reduce young people’s perceptions of social injustice, thereby enhancing their willingness to take action. Social support, both materially and emotionally, can effectively weaken the sense of helplessness experienced by young people trapped in a cycle of “involution–exhaustion–withdrawal.”

Second, accumulated empirical studies have found that meaning in life plays an important role between social support and lying flat. Social support can fulfill individuals’ emotional and relational needs and is closely related to individuals’ sense of security, sense of belonging, and experience of meaning in life. Numerous studies have found a significant positive correlation between social support and meaning in life [24, 25]. Xu et al., [26] further demonstrated that individuals with higher perceived social support are more likely to experience goals and direction in life and can enhance subjective well-being through meaning in life. This suggests that when young individuals receive stable social support networks, their level of meaning in life tends to be higher and may be associated with lower levels of passive withdrawal and passive coping tendencies.

Third, abundant empirical evidence indicates that psychological resilience also plays an important role in the relationship between social support and lying flat. A large body of research has confirmed a significant positive association between social support and psychological resilience [27–30]. Social support is associated with maintaining a more active coping mindset when facing competition and stress, which is related to lower levels of avoidance and withdrawal behaviors. Wang et al., [31] found that individuals with greater social support exhibit significantly less avoidance under competitive pressure. Correspondingly, studies by Guo [32] and Zhou [33] reported that individuals with higher psychological resilience are more inclined to adopt active coping strategies rather than compromise or avoidance. It can therefore be inferred that social support may be associated with higher levels of psychological resilience, and higher psychological resilience may in turn be associated with lower lying flat levels.

Finally, many studies have shown a significant positive relationship between meaning in life and psychological resilience. Ostafin and Proulx [34] found that individuals with higher meaning in life exhibited lower psychological distress and negative thinking when facing stressful events, demonstrating stronger psychological recovery. Similarly, Mohseni et al., [35] reported that meaning in life significantly predicts psychological resilience, with the two variables positively correlated. Furthermore, Brown et al., [36] found that meaning in life can reduce psychological distress by enhancing psychological resilience. More importantly, narrative identity theory proposed by McAdams [37, 38] also provides theoretical support for this pathway. According to this theory, individuals construct and internalize a “life story” through which past experiences and future goals are integrated into a coherent and purposeful self-narrative, thereby giving life a sense of unity and meaning. Within this framework, meaning in life is conceptualized as a psychological structure gradually formed through individuals’ interpretation and integration of key life events. When individuals transform negative experiences into expressions of growth and agency through meaning-making and redemptive narratives, their life stories tend to exhibit higher levels of psychological integration and adaptive functioning [37]. This suggests that meaning in life not only reflects the degree of integration within self-narratives but also constitutes an internal psychological resource that supports individuals in maintaining functional stability under stressful conditions. Taken together, integrating multiple empirical findings with the core propositions of narrative identity theory, meaning in life can be regarded as an important mechanism promoting psychological adaptation. Higher levels of meaning in life help individuals better integrate and interpret adversity, thereby enhancing psychological resilience.

In summary, although existing research and theoretical models provides extensive indirect evidence for the relationships among social support, psychological resilience, meaning in life, and lying flat, an integrative mediating model has not yet been directly tested. Therefore, the present study aims to construct a chain mediation model to systematically examine the relationship between social support and lying flat and its underlying mechanisms. Based on this framework, the following hypotheses are proposed:Hypothesis 1 (H1): Social support is significantly negatively associated with lying flat.Hypothesis 2 (H2): Meaning in life mediates the relationship between social support and lying flat.Hypothesis 3 (H3): Psychological resilience mediates the relationship between social support and lying flat.Hypothesis 4 (H4): Meaning in life and psychological resilience jointly form a chain mediating mechanism between social support and lying flat. Specifically, social support is associated with the accumulation of meaning in life, which in turn is associated with higher resilience, showing a pattern of association with reduced lying flat.

Method

Participants

Data for this study were collected through the online survey platform “Wenjuanxing.” To enhance participant engagement, a paid participation approach was adopted, with each respondent who completed the questionnaire conscientiously receiving a cash reward of 3 to 5 RMB. To ensure data quality, the research team conducted strict screening procedures, removing invalid cases with excessively short response times or failures on attention-check items (e.g., "For this item, please select 'Strongly Agree'"). A total of 2,323 valid questionnaires were obtained. The effective sample covered 30 provincial-level administrative regions in China and exhibited good geographical representation.

Among the valid participants, 1,165 were female (50.15%) and 1,158 were male (49.85%). Grade distribution was as follows: 448 first-year students (19.29%), 609 s-year students (26.22%), 699 third-year students (30.09%), 389 fourth-year students (16.75%), and 178 postgraduate students (7.66%). Regarding academic disciplines, 768 were humanities and social science students (33.06%), 1,211 were students in science, engineering, agriculture, or medicine (52.13%), and 344 were students in arts or sports (14.81%). In terms of household registration, 1,241 students (53.42%) held rural household registration, while 1,082 (46.58%) held urban household registration. Regionally, 1,386 students (59.66%) were from universities in eastern China, 609 (26.22%) from central China, and 328 (14.12%) from western China. Concerning university type, 772 students (33.23%) were enrolled in China’s 985/211 or Double First-Class universities, while 1,551 students (66.77%) were from regular universities.

Instruments

Lying flat mentality questionnaire

The previously self-constructed Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire consists of four dimensions: passive indolence (6 items), loss of goals (4 items), low mood (4 items), and muddling along (3 items). The specific items are presented in Appendix 1. The questionnaire adopts a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of lying flat. The questionnaire was developed through the following procedures. First, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 45 university students. Grounded theory analysis was applied to the interview data to construct a theoretical model consisting of the four dimensions described above. Based on this model, corresponding items were developed, resulting in an initial questionnaire containing 28 items.

Second, the preliminary questionnaire was administered to 262 valid independent samples of Chinese university students, and the collected data were subjected to item analysis and exploratory factor analysis. The final version of the Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire consisted of 17 items across the four dimensions, with factor loadings ranging from 0.50 to 0.82.

Third, an online survey was conducted with 1,560 valid samples of university students to examine the reliability and validity of the questionnaire. Reliability testing showed a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.918. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated good model fit: χ2/df = 4.98, CFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, SRMR = 0.03, RMSEA = 0.05, demonstrating good reliability and validity.

Fourth, in another sample of 206 university students, discriminant validity and convergent validity were examined using a depression scale, a self-efficacy scale, and the Lying Flat Tendency Scale. Correlation analysis showed that the correlation coefficient between the self-constructed lying flat scale and the depression scale was r = 0.57, (HTMT = 0.78). The correlation with the self-efficacy scale was r = − 0.53, (HTMT = 0.75). These r values remain below the conventional threshold of 0.70 for construct redundancy. Crucially, the HTMT value between the constructs was substantially lower than the conservative criterion of 0.85 [39], suggesting that problematic overlap was not detected between the constructs. The correlation with the Lying Flat Tendency Scale was r = 0.76. The results indicated good discriminant validity and convergent validity (more complete evidence of construct validity is presented in Appendix 2). The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire in the present study was 0.92.

Social support scale

Based on Xiao’s [40] three-factor model of social support, Ye and Dai [41] developed a Social Support Scale for university students comprising three dimensions: subjective support (5 items), objective support (6 items), and support utilization (6 items). Each item is rated on a 1–5 scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social support. The scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.91, and its Cronbach’s α coefficient in the present study was 0.95.

Psychological resilience scale

The most widely used instruments for assessing psychological resilience are the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) and its short form (CD-RISC-10) developed by Connor and Davidson [42]. Chen et al., [43] revised the short version of the CD-RISC for Chinese populations and applied it to Chinese university students. The scale contains one dimension with 10 items, each rated on a 1–5 scale, with higher scores representing higher levels of psychological resilience. The scale’s Cronbach’s α coefficient in university student samples is 0.80, and its Cronbach’s α coefficient in the present study was 0.90.

Meaning in life questionnaire

Based on a dual-dimensional framework, Steger et al., [44] developed the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) to assess individuals’ levels of meaning in life. Liu and Gan [45] revised the MLQ into a Chinese version. During the revision process, the tenth item of the original scale (“I am always seeking something that makes my life feel significant”) did not meet psychometric standards and was therefore deleted. The final Chinese version of the Meaning in Life Questionnaire retains two dimensions: presence of meaning (5 items) and search for meaning (4 items). Each item is rated on a 1–7 scale, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of meaning in life. The scale’s Cronbach’s α coefficient in university student samples is 0.71, and its Cronbach’s α coefficient in the present study was 0.92.

SEM model construction and model fit assessment

AMOS 28.0 was used to construct the structural equation model (SEM). Because the original questionnaire contained a large number of items (53 items), an excessive number of observed variables may lead to unstable model fit indices, a problem that is difficult to avoid even with a large sample size [46, 47]. Therefore, following the recommendations of Landis et al., [48] and Wu and Wen [49], the item parceling method was adopted to correct estimation bias and improve communality and model fit. Item parceling refers to combining multiple items from the same scale into a new indicator and using the mean score of these items as the score of that indicator for analysis [50–52].

The specific parceling strategy was as follows: the Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire and the Social Support Scale, based on their multidimensional structures, were parceled according to dimensions into four and three parcels, respectively. For the unidimensional Psychological Resilience Scale, which contains only a single dimension, items were randomly assigned to three parcels following extensive methodological recommendations. This parceling strategy has been demonstrated to yield no systematic bias in structural path estimation [49, 53]. Although the Meaning in Life Scale theoretically contains two dimensions, given the high correlations between items and dimensions, and because this study focuses on relationships at the construct level, using only two parcels may lead to estimation bias [39, 54]. Therefore, following existing recommendations, a cross-domain parceling strategy was adopted to assign items into three parcels. This approach aimed to expand the number of indicators, thereby enhancing model identification and estimation precision [53, 55]. Each parcel group included items drawn from different dimensions to reduce model complexity while maintaining the completeness of construct coverage and its underlying factor structure [53].

Based on the parceled indicators, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted on the measurement model (see Fig. 1) to examine the model fit of the variables. The overall fit indices of the measurement model are shown in Table 3: χ2 = 720.323, df = 59, χ2/df = 12.209, NFI = 0.970, IFI = 0.972, TLI = 0.964, CFI = 0.972, RMSEA = 0.069, SRMR = 0.055. According to Guo et al., [56], when the sample size exceeds 1000, the χ2/df statistic tends to be abnormally inflated. Therefore, we further conducted a robustness check using the Robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) estimator. The robust fit indices indicated good model fit (Robust CFI = 0.970; Robust RMSEA = 0.054). Given the relatively large χ2/df ratio from the measurement model, we further examined local fit indices to assess whether substantive misspecification existed. Specifically, we adopted the misspecification detection approach proposed by Saris et al., [57], which is based on the Expected Parameter Change (EPC). This method is less sensitive to sample size and can effectively distinguish between statistical significance and practical significance. The results showed that although some modification indices (MI) and standardized residuals exceeded the recommended threshold due to the large sample size (N = 2,323), all corresponding EPC values were below 0.10, indicating that the model did not contain practically meaningful misspecification [57–59]. Taken together, the original model specification was considered reasonable and acceptable. The RMSEA value in this study is slightly close to the cutoff value of 0.08, primarily due to the large sample size (N = 2,323) and the fact that multiple latent variables and observed indicators remain even after item parceling. A large sample size inflates the chi-square value, which in turn slightly increases the RMSEA; meanwhile, having a relatively large number of observed indicators also produces minor effects on the fit indices [39].

Because the correlation between lying flat and psychological resilience was relatively strong (r = − 0.62), a more rigorous test was conducted to determine whether the two constructs are independent. Specifically, a competing model comparison approach was used. By comparing the four-factor model with a three-factor model in which lying flat and psychological resilience were merged, it was found that the three-factor model showed poorer fit, as shown in Table 3: χ2 = 2291.372, df = 62, χ2/df = 36.958, NFI = 0.905, IFI = 0.907, TLI = 0.883, CFI = 0.907, RMSEA = 0.124, SRMR = 0.066. Because of the large sample size, Δχ2 is almost inevitably significant when Δdf = 3 (Δχ2 = 1610.597, p < 0.001). Therefore, ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA were used as the criteria for evaluation. According to the recommendations of [60] and [61], when the change in CFI exceeds 0.01 and the change in RMSEA exceeds 0.015, it indicates a substantial deterioration in model fit. In this study, ΔCFI = 0.065 and ΔRMSEA = 0.055, both far exceeding the recommended thresholds, supporting the conclusion that lying flat and psychological resilience should be treated as two independent research constructs.

Common method bias test

Following the recommendation of Tang and Wen [62], the unmeasured latent method factor (ULMF) approach was adopted to examine common method bias. Specifically, the variance of the method factor was constrained to 1, while the paths from the factor to the indicators were freely estimated. Compared with the outdated Harman’s single-factor test, this method has higher testing validity. The model fit results were as follows: χ2 = 492.372, df = 47, χ2/df = 10.476, NFI = 0.979, IFI = 0.981, TLI = 0.969, CFI = 0.981, RMSEA = 0.064, SRMR = 0.029. Although the chi-square difference test was statistically significant due to the large sample size (Δχ2(12) = 227.951, p < 0.001), the key fit indices reflecting the practical significance of model improvement did not reach the threshold criteria. Specifically, the change in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI) was 0.009, which is below the recommended cutoff value of 0.01 [60]. In addition, ΔRMSEA = 0.005 and ΔSRMR = 0.026, both of which are smaller than the recommended thresholds of ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 [61]. Therefore, it can be concluded that the potential threat of common method bias to the relationships among the constructs in this study remains within an acceptable range.

Testing the chain mediating roles

To examine the chain mediating roles of meaning in life and psychological resilience between social support and lying flat, the final structural equation model was constructed (see Fig. 2). The model included gender, grade, university type, and household registration as control variables, because these demographic factors may influence individuals’ access to social resources and their psychological adaptation processes, thereby potentially confounding the relationships among the core variables. Because the above variables were categorical variables, they were subjected to dummy coding before entering the model, with male, first-year students, regular universities, and urban household registration serving as the reference categories. Discussion

In the present sample of Chinese university students, lying flat was significantly negatively correlated with social support, psychological resilience, and meaning in life. The negative association between social support and lying flat was partly accounted for by meaning in life and psychological resilience in mediation analyses. These findings suggest that higher levels of meaning in life, psychological resilience, and external social support are associated with lower levels of lying flat. Overall, individuals reporting greater social support also reported higher meaning in life and higher psychological resilience, and these factors were associated with lower levels of lying flat in the present sample.

The Relationship between social support and lying flat

The results indicate that higher levels of social support are associated with lower levels of lying flat. This finding is consistent with prior studies [22, 63]. This pattern also aligns with the Stress–buffering Model proposed by Cohen and Wills [64], which posits that social support may be associated with lower psychological burden under stress and, consequently, with fewer negative coping tendencies. According to Tong [23], a well-established social support system may enhance social recognition and work motivation among young people by improving income fairness and social security. In line with this theoretical perspective, the present study provides correlational evidence that individuals reporting greater support from family, peers, and the broader social environment tend to show lower level of lying flat.

The mediating role of meaning in life

From the perspective of PP 2.0, social support may provide individuals with the relational foundation they need and, by facilitating the construction of meaning, further promote positive adaptation. This study found that meaning in life partially mediates the relationship between social support and lying flat. That is, social support may be associated with lower level of lying flat through its association with meaning in life. This finding is also consistent with the core tenets of the Meaning–Making Model. Originally proposed by Park and Folkman [65] and systematically reviewed by Park [66], the model posits that when individuals face stress or life challenges, external support systems are theorized to be associated with internal meaning-making processes. Through re-evaluating situations or adjusting beliefs and goals, individuals restore their internal sense of meaning and directional clarity, which is considered to contribute to positive psychological adaptation [66]. Such meaning-making efforts are theorized to be related to fewer negative withdrawal behaviors and feelings of existential void. Moreover, numerous studies have shown that social support is associated with the experience of meaning in life, enhancing well-being and psychological adjustment [25, 26, 67]. However, previous studies have mainly focused on well-being or general mental health, and their explanatory frameworks have largely been established on general psychological adaptation models. They have not yet examined meaning in life within the specific youth subcultural phenomenon of lying flat, which possesses characteristics of passive adaptation. Unlike traditional mental health indicators such as depression and burnout, which reflect clinical or exhaustion states, lying flat is closer to a passive adaptive strategy adopted by individuals after perceiving stress. The core of this strategy lies in adjustment rather than emotional exhaustion. Therefore, its occurrence and change may be more sensitive to meaning-related resources such as meaning in life. Simply applying general psychological frameworks may not fully reveal the unique role of meaning mechanisms in this context. Therefore, the present study incorporated meaning in life into the research framework of lying flat and examined its mediating role between social support and lying flat. This provides support from theoretical perspectives such as PP2.0, for understanding this passive adaptive behavior with a distinct cultural context, and also responds to the theoretical need to extend general psychological frameworks to specific subcultural phenomena.

The mediating role of psychological resilience

From the perspective of PP2.0, psychological resilience can be understood as a stable capacity for action that is formed on the basis of social support and enables individuals to achieve positive adaptation. The results of the present study show that psychological resilience plays a significant mediating role between social support and lying flat. Higher levels of social support are associated with stronger psychological resilience and lower level of lying flat. This finding also aligns with the core principles of Resiliency Theory, which integrates perspectives from Garmezy [68], Masten [69], and Fergus& Zimmerman [70]. The theory emphasizes that individuals with sufficient external resources are theorized to engage internal adaptive mechanisms when facing external stress or uncertainty, which is considered to be associated with more positive responses to challenges and fewer negative withdrawal tendencies. Furthermore, some previous studies have also pointed out positive correlations between social support and psychological resilience [27–30, 71]. Luthar et al., [72] proposed that psychological resilience is a key characteristic enabling individuals to choose positive strategies and resist maladaptive behaviors. Individuals with high psychological resilience are more inclined to adopt constructive behaviors and exhibit less avoidance and withdrawal when under pressure [32, 33]. However, most of these studies have focused on variable relationships or stress-coping processes within mainstream psychological adaptation frameworks. For the culturally specific youth behavioral phenomenon of lying flat, the role played by psychological resilience has not yet been systematically examined. Simply applying universal resilience frameworks may not fully reveal the unique function of psychological resilience in emerging subcultural phenomena. Against this backdrop, the present study introduced psychological resilience into the research framework of lying flat and showed that there exists a statistically significant associative pathway among social support, psychological resilience, and lying flat. This not only extends the scope of traditional psychological resilience research but also provides theoretical support from perspectives such as PP2.0, for understanding the passive adaptive behaviors of contemporary Chinese youth.

The chain mediation of meaning in life and psychological resilience

The present study further found that the association between social support and lying flat can be explained through the chain mediating roles of meaning in life and psychological resilience. At the same time, the findings of the present study are also consistent with Protective Factor Model of Resilience [70], which posits that positive adaptation outcomes result from the interaction between risk factors and protective factors. Protective factors include both external resources and internal assets, which are theorized to be associated with adaptive mechanisms and with fewer negative outcomes under stress [70]. However, the model provides limited detail on specific internal psychological pathways. This study supplements it by incorporating the Psychological Capital Model [73], which proposes that individuals’ positive psychological resources (such as meaning in life) can promote levels of psychological resilience through processes of accumulation and activation, thereby predicting adaptive behaviors. In this way, it presents a progressive transformation logic of “external resources → internal capital → adaptive capacity → behavioral outcomes.” The chain pathway identified in this study is not only consistent with the above theoretical perspectives but also aligns with existing empirical evidence. Mohseni et al., [35] pointed out that a sense of meaning can enhance psychological resilience; Zhang [74] and Gao [75] found that social support can indirectly influence psychological resilience through meaning in life; Miao and Cao [76] further confirmed that meaning and resilience jointly contribute to reductions in stress levels. In addition, the findings of the present study are also consistent with the pathway of “social support → meaning in life → psychological resilience → adaptation” proposed by Li et al., [77], Zhang [78], Al-Mahrouqi et al., [79], and Arslan and Yıldırım [80]. It should be noted, however, that the above theories and studies have mainly focused on traditional mental health issues and have not involved lying flat as a culturally specific behavioral outcome. The present study provides a concrete empirical examination of this chain mechanism in the context of lying flat among university students, thereby extending the application boundary of PP2.0 and related theoretical models to the field of emerging subcultures and providing new evidence for their contextual applicability.

Compared with existing mediation studies that focus on specific antecedents such as involution pressure [15], relative deprivation [17], or nature connectedness [81], the core contribution of the present study lies in examining the deeper process of resource transformation and capacity accumulation in the formation mechanism of lying flat. Previous studies have mainly revealed the pathway of “stress → emotion → lying flat,” whereas the present study, by integrating meaning in life and psychological resilience, identifies a more fundamental “nourishing” pathway. Social support may not directly counteract stress; rather, it is first associated with meaning in life, providing individuals with a sense of existential direction and value (cognitive foundation). Meaning in life is further associated with psychological resilience, providing individuals with a stable psychological capacity to cope with adversity (capacity consolidation). The unique value of this chain mediation lies in the fact that it not only explains how lying flat may emerge but also indicates that the focus of intervention may need to shift from repairing deficits to cultivating meaning and resilience.

Theoretical examination of alternative models and path directions

To address potential alternative explanations, it should be noted that for two alternative chain mediation models (e.g., Support → Meaning → Resilience, as well as the reverse model involving lying flat predicting social support), within a cross-sectional data framework, the direction of paths cannot be determined solely based on model fit indices. Therefore, this study prioritizes examining the feasibility of the above alternative explanations from the perspective of theoretical logic.

From the perspective of theoretical logic, and based on the theoretical framework presented earlier, meaning in life, as the core cognitive appraisal system through which individuals evaluate the purpose, value, and existential meaning of life, constitutes the antecedent foundation for individuals to form a psychological resource structure [18, 19, 37, 38]. As an external protective resource, social support first influences individuals’ cognitive affirmation of their self-existence value and life goals (enhancement of meaning in life). On this basis, individuals further develop the regulatory capacity to face stress and setbacks (enhancement of psychological resilience), which was ultimately associated with lower levels of lying flat tendencies. In other words, meaning in life constitutes the cognitive prerequisite for psychological resilience to be activated and to operate continuously.

In contrast, the alternative model assumes a path in which “psychological resilience precedes meaning in life.” Although this path may be statistically viable, it presents structural limitations in terms of theoretical mechanisms. Psychological resilience is essentially a dynamic regulatory capacity, and its functioning depends on individuals having a clear cognitive understanding of behavioral goals and existential value [82, 83]. Without the support of a meaning system, the mobilization of resilience may have difficulty obtaining a sustained source of intrinsic motivation, thereby making it difficult to maintain individual psychological well-being [34, 80, 84]. Therefore, placing resilience before meaning, although feasible in model form, does not possess equivalent explanatory power in the generative logic of psychological resources.

In addition, regarding the reverse path suggesting that “lying flat may weaken social support,” this study also considers this possibility with caution. It is undeniable that passive escapist behaviors may influence individuals’ interpersonal interactions and access to support over the long term. However, multiple models on which this study is based such as PP2.0, the Stress-buffering Model, the Meaning-Making Model, and Resiliency Theory, all define social support as a preceding external resource variable, rather than an outcome variable. Therefore, in terms of the logical sequence of resource flow, social support is more consistent with the theoretical position of an “influencing source” rather than a “resultant product.” At the same time, from the perspective of variable-level attributes, social support is usually defined as a relatively stable contextual resource or environmental input factor, and has often been placed in the position of a predictor variable in many empirical studies [64, 85]. In contrast, behavioral responses or attitudinal tendencies are more often regarded as external manifestations after the integration of internal psychological resources, especially in SEM models [39, 86]. Therefore, in terms of structural modeling logic, positioning social support as an influencing source variable and treating lying flat as an outcome variable is more consistent with the hierarchical classification of psychological variables and modeling traditions.

In summary, although cross-sectional data cannot statistically rule out alternative directions, based on existing theoretical frameworks, the path structure “social support → meaning in life → psychological resilience → lying flat” demonstrates greater theoretical consistency and explanatory advantages. The hypothesized model of this study is not only supported by the data but also theoretically consistent with the generative logic of psychological resources.

An integrative analytical framework of “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” (RPCCF)

Based on the above analysis and the chain mediation findings, this study integrates key perspectives from the PP2.0, Stress–buffering Model, Meaning–Making Model, Resiliency Theory, Protective Factor Model of Resilience, and Psychological Capital Model, proposing an Integrative analytical Framework of “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” (RPCCF) to describe the preliminary psychological transmission chain underlying university students’ lying flat. It should be noted that the RPCCF is not intended to propose a mature or universally applicable theoretical model, but rather serves as a heuristic framework constructed based on prior research models and the variables of this study to interpret the present findings.

Meaning in life is conceptualized in RPCCF as a central link through which external resources may relate to psychological resilience. According to meaning–making theory, it reflects individuals’ recognition of existence value and life goals, with social support serving as an important basis. When students receive social support and engage in meaning-making processes, they may develop cognitions such as “I have support and I am valuable,” which are central to the experience of meaning in life.

Subsequently, stronger meaning in life is associated with higher psychological resilience. According to Resiliency Theory, psychological resilience refers to the capacity to maintain positive adaptation under stress or adversity. In this framework, psychological resilience is conceptualized as a potential outcome linked to meaning in life: students reporting more stable meaning in life tend to report greater tendencies to adjust mindset and seek solutions when facing academic pressure or interpersonal challenges that might be related to lying flat. Both meaning in life and psychological resilience are treated in the RPCCF as components of psychological capital that may be relevant for countering external pressures.

Ultimately, students with stronger psychological capital are more likely to adopt positive coping strategies when facing setbacks and are less likely to exhibit passive lying flat. Conversely, students reporting lower psychological capital (e.g., lower perceived social support or lower levels of meaning in life and psychological resilience) tend to report higher levels of lying flat.

In summary, the RPCCF helps us understand a conceptual sequence through which external resources such as social support may relate to internal psychological capital via meaning-making and resilience-building, which in turn are associated with lying flat. It is important to note that the Integrative analytical Framework of “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” (RPCCF) proposed in this study is not a newly established mature theoretical model; rather, it is a heuristic integrative interpretive framework formulated by synthesizing the core ideas of PP2.0, the stress–buffering model, meaning-making models, resilience theory, the protective factor model, and psychological capital models, and by incorporating the empirical findings of this study. Its core value lies in integrating the continuous transmission pathways among the variables, addressing the limitations of single theories that only explain isolated segments, and thereby providing a more coherent analytical approach to understanding the psychological mechanisms of lying flat, rather than replacing or surpassing existing theoretical systems.

Compared with single-theory models, the distinctiveness of this framework lies in incorporating external social resources, internal meaning-making, psychological resilience, and passive coping behavior into a continuous transmission chain, clarifying the progressive mechanism of “external resource input—internal psychological capital accumulation—coping behavior output,” which differs from traditional theories that focus only on isolated links or single-variable effects. Moreover, this framework is strictly grounded in the variable settings and university student sample of this study, and its explanatory power and applicability are confined to the scope of this research, and serving solely as a heuristic analytical tool for understanding the lying flat mentality among contemporary university students.

Research contributions and intervention implications

Lying flat, as a complex phenomenon reflecting social change and individual psychological adjustment, illustrates how young adults may respond to real-world pressures and navigate tensions between social structure, cultural expectations, and personal psychology. This study provides theoretical and practical contributions as follows: (1) From the perspective of PP 2.0, this study offers a psychological interpretive perspective to understand university students’ lying flat. While prior research has mainly focused on socio–economic or cultural symbolism explanations, this study's chain mediation analysis reveals that social support is associated with individual behavioral tendencies through internal psychological capitals, thereby providing a conceptual psychological explanatory framework. (2) The study integrates concepts from the PP 2.0 Stress–buffering Model, Meaning–Making Model, Resiliency Theory, Protective Factor Model of Resilience, and Psychological Capital Model, and preliminarily constructs an Integrative analytical Framework of “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” (RPCCF) as a heuristic framework for further exploration. Based on the above empirical support and theoretical contributions, this study proposes the following implications to provide directions for targeted psychological intervention and educational practice. First, the results show that the direct effect of social support on lying flat accounts for 29.82% of the total effect, indicating that social support, as an external resource, constitutes a foundational variable in the mechanism underlying the formation of university students’ lying flat tendency, and this association deserves attention. This finding suggests that universities and families may focus on the perceptibility and availability of support, for example, by optimizing the quality of teacher–student interaction, promoting peer connections, and creating a developmental feedback environment, so that external support can be more effectively transformed into psychological resources that individuals can mobilize. Second, this study finds that meaning in life plays an independent mediating role between social support and lying flat, and also serves as the starting point of the chain path. This suggests that helping students establish a clear value system and sense of goals may be associated with higher levels of psychological resilience and lower levels of lying flat tendency. Mental health education and ideological education in universities may further integrate the concept of meaning construction, for example by offering life education courses, organizing value-themed seminars, and guiding students to engage in self-narrative exploration in practice, thereby assisting students in clarifying personal goals and integrating past experiences. In this way, when facing academic pressure and social comparison, students may obtain a sense of stability from deeper existential value, thereby improving their level of personal resilience. Finally, the results of this study indicate that the effect transmitted through psychological resilience occupies a dominant position among all indirect paths (72.19%). This means that whether individuals ultimately achieve good adaptation still largely depends on the strength of their psychological resilience. Therefore, the cultivation of psychological resilience should become a priority in intervention strategies. Specifically, this can be approached from two dimensions. First, the nurturing role of social support for resilience should be utilized. Social support significantly and positively predicts psychological resilience, and measures that enhance the perception of support themselves function as fertilization for resilience cultivation. Creating an inclusive growth environment that encourages trial and error allows individuals to obtain buffering and recovery space when encountering setbacks, thereby tempering resilience through practice. When individuals possess sufficient internal resilience and external support, they do not need to cope with difficulties in a passive manner. Second, systematic psychological resilience training can be implemented. University mental health courses may introduce cognitive restructuring training to help students adjust maladaptive thinking when facing difficulties; promote mindfulness and emotion regulation skills to enhance emotional stability under stress; and conduct frustration tolerance training through stepwise challenge tasks (such as team projects), enabling students to gradually enhance their sense of self-efficacy in coping with uncertainty under controllable risks. Through systematic interventions focusing on capacity building, students may transform external support and internal beliefs into the capacity for active coping in complex environments.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations exist. First, due to the cross-sectional design, the relationships found among social support, meaning in life, psychological resilience, and lying flat should be understood as statistical associations rather than evidence of causal processes. Although the chain mediation model identified in this study has theoretical support, the temporal order implied by the model cannot be empirically confirmed within the current design, and therefore interpretations should remain cautious. Second, the self-developed Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire used in this study has undergone standardized testing in a relatively large sample, and this paper further examined the convergent validity and discriminant validity of the scale using an additional sample of 206 participants. However, as a recently developed measurement instrument, it still lacks sufficient empirical studies to provide further external validation of its reliability, validity, and stability. Therefore, the generalizability of conclusions based on this questionnaire requires further confirmation in future research. Third, the sample of this study was limited to university students and did not include other groups. Therefore, without further empirical verification, directly generalizing the findings to non-student populations or individuals at different life stages may involve potential risks. Fourth, the study relied primarily on self-report questionnaires, lacking experimental or behavioral validation of psychological processes underlying lying flat. Although the ULMF test indicated acceptable variance control, the significant χ2 difference suggests that common method variance cannot be entirely ruled out within this cross-sectional, single-session design. Fifth, cultural and contextual factors specific to China may affect the findings, and the generalizability of the findings to other cultural contexts remains uncertain; therefore, the cross-cultural generalization should be approached cautiously. Sixth, although the self-developed Lying Flat Mentality Questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity, its external validity and cross-sample stability warrant further scrutiny, as its current application boundary condition is limited to the population of Chinese university students. Seventh, family socioeconomic status (SES) or other potential variables were not operationalized in the current model due to data collection constraints. Given that omitted control variables might partially confound the observed structural pathways, this aspect warrants caution when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. Eighth, while the theoretical framework conceptualizes 'lying flat' as a reaction to environmental stressors (e.g., involution), the empirical model focused primarily on internal and interpersonal resources without directly measuring objective environmental pressure, meaning that the findings regarding the 51.9% explained variance may be partially context-dependent and potentially subject to overestimation.

Future research directions include: (1) using longitudinal designs to investigate antecedents and consequences of lying flat and assess its long-term effects on mental health, academic, and career development; (2) designing evidence-based interventions, such as meaning-making training, social support enhancement, and psychological resilience cultivation, while empirically testing their effectiveness. (3) future research should extend beyond university students to test whether the observed associations generalize across different ages and occupations; (4) employing experimental or neuroimaging methods to further explore psychological and neural mechanisms; (5) integrating additional social, economic, and individual variables to develop a more comprehensive understanding. (6) Future studies could extend the localized phenomenon of lying flat into a global cross-cultural context by comparing it with Western “Quiet Quitting” and Japan’s “Satori Generation.” Investigating how these cultural responses differ in their underlying psychological mechanisms would provide deeper theoretical insights into guiding contemporary youth’s value orientations globally.

Conclusion

The present study explored the associations among social support, meaning in life, psychological resilience, and lying flat among Chinese university students within the framework of PP 2.0. Across a large national sample, higher levels of social support were associated with lower levels of lying flat, and this association was consistent with the chain of associations involving meaning in life and psychological resilience. Based on these associations, this study proposes the “Resource–Psychological Capital–Coping” Framework (RPCCF) as an integrative heuristic framework, highlighting potential roles of external resources and internal psychological capital in relation to disengagement tendencies among young adults. This analytical framework may provide preliminary guidance for interventions aimed at enhancing social support, cultivating meaning in life, and strengthening psychological resilience among Chinese university students. Overall, these insights preliminarily reveal potential integrated pathways linking external support and internal psychological strengths to developmental outcomes in Chinese contemporary university students.
