Dimension of the singular set in the parabolic obstacle problem
Alejandro Martínez, Xavier Ros-Oton

Abstract
. In this paper we study the singular set in the parabolic obstacle problem for general obstacles φ ∈ C 2 , 1 \varphi\in C^{2,1} . We prove that the singular set has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n − 1 n-1 . Prior to our result, this was only known when Δ ​ φ ≡ − 1 \Delta\varphi\equiv-1 . Our approach combines a truncated parabolic frequency formula and monotonicity estimates with an iterative argument showing that the frequency is saturated for all values of the truncation parameter between 2 2 and 3 3 .

1.
Introduction

We consider the parabolic obstacle problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v-\Delta v\;&=\;0&\quad&\text{in }\{v>\varphi\}\cap\Omega\times(0,T)\\[3.00003pt]
v\;&\geq\;\varphi&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T)\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{t}v-\Delta v\;&\geq\;0&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

with boundary condition
v=g
and initial condition
v(x,0)=\varphi(x)
, where
\varphi\in C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega})
is the obstacle.

This problem can be formulated as a variational inequality and it arises (among other contexts) in the optimal stopping problem for the Brownian motion; see
[
9
]
. In particular, it arises also in Mathematical Finance, in the Black-Scholes model for pricing of American options
[
18
]
. We refer to the books
[
8
,
17
,
23
,
15
,
22
]
for further applications and motivations of (parabolic) obstacle problems.

Considering
u:=v-\varphi
and localizing to an interior cylinder, the parabolic obstacle problem becomes

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u-\Delta u\;&=\;-f(x)\,\chi_{\{u>0\}}&\quad&\text{in }B_{1}\times(-1,1)\\[3.00003pt]
u\;&\geq\;0&&\text{in }B_{1}\times(-1,1)\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{t}u\;&\geq\;0&&\text{in }B_{1}\times(-1,1)\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{t}u\;&>\;0&&\text{in }\{u>0\},\end{aligned}\right.

where
f:=-\Delta\varphi
is a smooth nonnegative function. When
f\equiv 1
, this is also known as the (one-phase) Stefan problem.

One of the central challenges in obstacle-type problems is to understand the regularity of the free boundary. For (
1.2
), little was known until the groundbreaking paper
[
4
]
. In this work, Caffarelli studied the case
f\equiv 1
and proved that the free boundary is locally
C^{\infty}
outside a closed set of singular points
\Sigma
, at which the contact set
\{u=0\}
has zero density.

More results on the singular set have been obtained in subsequent works. Using techniques adapted from the elliptic obstacle-type problem, it was shown in
[
3
,
20
]
that, at each fixed time
t
, the set of singular points
\Sigma_{t}
is locally contained in an
(n-1)
-dimensional
C^{1}
manifold. Other related works have explored structural and regularity aspects of parabolic obstacle problems: Hausdorff dimension of the free boundaries in related problems
[
27
]
; regularity for changing-sign solutions
[
5
]
; asymptotic behavior of solutions
[
7
]
and global regularity of the free boundary
[
14
]
among others.

Despite these results, one question remained unanswered: can one prove that the singular set is
(n-1)
-dimensional in
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
? This was finally established in the recent paper
[
12
]
in case
f\equiv 1
. Moreover, they proved also that the singular set can be covered by
C^{\infty}

(n-1)
-dimensional manifolds, up to a smaller set
\Gamma
of dimension
n-2
. Building on the work
[
12
]
, the recent paper
[
6
]
proved that such set
\Gamma
is actually parabolically countably
(n-2)
-rectifiable.

Our main goal in this paper is to extend the dimension bound obtained in
[
12
]
, previously known only for the constant case
f\equiv 1
, to arbitrary positive Lipschitz functions
f
. As explained below, this generalization is not trivial and requires not only controlling the errors in several monotonicity formulas, but also an iterative argument that is different from
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any open set, let
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(-T,T))
solve the parabolic obstacle problem (
1.2
) with
f\in C^{0,1}(\bar{\Omega}\times[-T,T])
and
f>0
. Let
\Sigma\subset\Omega\times(-T,T)
be the set of singular points. Then,

\dim_{\text{par}}(\Sigma)\leq n-1,

where
\dim_{\text{par}}
denotes the parabolic Hausdorff dimension.

For a definition of the parabolic Hausdorff dimension see Section
2
.

1.1.
The singular set

Under the assumption
f>0
, points on the free boundary
\partial\{u>0\}
can be classified into two categories: we call
(x_{0},t_{0})
a regular point if and only if

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{u(x_{0}+rx,t_{0}+r^{2}t)}{r^{2}}=\frac{f(x_{0})}{2}\max\{\textbf{e}\cdot x,0\}^{2}

for some
\textbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We call
(x_{0},t_{0})
a singular point if and only if

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{u(x_{0}+rx,t_{0}+r^{2}t)}{r^{2}}=p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}(x)

where
p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}(x)
is a quadratic polynomial of the form
\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax
, with
A
being a symmetric nonnegative definite matrix such that
\text{tr}(A)=f(x_{0})
. In both cases the convergence is in
C^{1,\alpha}_{x}\cap C^{0,\alpha}_{t}
locally in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We will also denote by
p_{2}(x):=p_{2,0,0}(x)
.

This classification can be found for example in
[
10
, Theorem 5.44]
for the elliptic obstacle problem and
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
for the fully nonlinear parabolic obstacle problem.

It is known that the free boundary is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of regular points; however, its behavior can be significantly more complicated near singular points. In
[
24
]
it was shown that, in the elliptic obstacle problem, if
f
is not strictly positive, the free boundary can be an arbitrary closed set; in particular, a set of dimension
n
. Moreover, under the hypothesis
f>0
, the free boundary can be any closed subset of a hyperplane; in particular, a set of dimension
n-1
. For a fixed time similar constructions can be done in the parabolic obstacle problem.

1.2.
Ideas of the proof

If
(x_{0},t_{0})
is a singular point, we have the following expansion

(1.3)

u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}+t)=p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}(x)+o(|x|^{2}+|t|),

where
p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}(x)
is the polynomial introduced previously. Since a singular point
(x_{0},t_{0})
is characterized by the existence of such a polynomial
p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}
, a natural way to classify singular points is by using the dimension of
\{p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}=0\}
. For
m\in\{0,1,2,...,n-1\}
we define

\Sigma_{m}:=\left\{(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Sigma:\dim\{p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}=0\}=m\right\},

hence the singular set decomposes as
\Sigma=\bigcup_{m=0}^{n-1}\Sigma_{m}
.

Several ingredients in our proof refine techniques introduced in
[
12
]
. We develop a sharper version of (
1.3
) in the top stratum
\Sigma_{n-1}
. To do this, we will use a truncated version of the frequency function

\phi^{\gamma}(r,w):=\frac{r^{2}\int_{\{t=-r^{2}\}}|\nabla(u-p_{2})|^{2}Gdx+\gamma r^{2\gamma}}{\int_{\{t=-r^{2}\}}(u-p_{2})^{2}Gdx+\gamma r^{2\gamma}}

where
\gamma
is a parameter to be chosen. Morally, this truncated version returns the value
\gamma
if the frequency is very high. In this case, we will often refer to it as ”frequency saturated”. The authors of
[
12
]
proved that when
f\equiv 1
the frequency function is almost-monotone for any value of
\gamma\in(2,\infty)
; however, this is not true in our case since, in our initial estimates, the frequency function is almost-monotone only for
\gamma\in\left(2,\frac{5}{2}\right)
. This constitutes the first step of our argument. Using this almost-monotonicity, we can improve the error term of (
1.3
), resulting in an error of
o(|x|^{\frac{5}{2}}+|t|^{\frac{5}{4}})
. With this improvement in the error term, we have sharper estimates and an almost-monotonicity formula can be obtained for
\gamma\in\left(2,\frac{11}{4}\right)
. Iterating this argument, we obtain an almost-monotonicity formula for
\gamma\in\left(2,3-\varepsilon\right)
.

Once this extended almost-monotonicity is established, the proof of Theorem
1.1
proceeds as follows.
If
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Sigma_{m}
with
m\leq n-2
, the expansion (
1.3
) cannot be improved. Nevertheless, a barrier argument similar to
[
11
,
12
]
yields
\dim_{\mathrm{par}}(\Sigma_{m})\leq n-2
.
If instead
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Sigma_{n-1}
, the improved almost-monotonicity allows us to strengthen the expansion to

(1.4)

u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}+t)=p_{2,x_{0},t_{0}}(x)+o(|x|^{3-\varepsilon}+|t|^{(3-\varepsilon)/2}),

for any
\varepsilon>0
.
This refined expansion implies
\dim_{\mathrm{par}}(\Sigma_{n-1})\leq n-1
, and combining the two cases completes the proof.

A key observation allowing us to assume only that
f
is Lipschitz is the following: if a subharmonic function is Lipschitz in all but one direction, then it is
C^{0,\alpha}
in that remaining direction for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
. This is proved in detail in
2.7
. We expect Lipschitz regularity of
f
to be the minimal regularity assumption under which (
1.4
) holds.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we introduce the main notation and definitions and collect several preliminary results used throughout the paper. In Section
3
we introduce several almost-monotonicity formulas and some crucial estimates. In Section
4
we analyze the second blow-up in the top stratum, i.e. at the critical points belonging to

.

2.1.
Notation and definitions

We start by introducing some notation.

Operators

We define the following operators

\displaystyle\textbf{H}:=(\Delta-\partial_{t})\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z:=(x\cdot\nabla+2t\partial_{t}).

We can rewrite parabolic obstacle problem (
1.2
) using them

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textbf{H}u=f\chi_{\{u>0\}}&\\
u\geq 0&\qquad\text{in }B_{1}\times(-1,1)\\
\partial_{t}u>0\quad\text{in }\{u>0\}\end{cases}

Bilinear form

For
r\in(0,1]
and functions
g,h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-1,0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define the following bilinear form

\displaystyle\langle g,h\rangle_{r}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(gh)(x,-r^{2})G(x,-r^{2})dx

where
G(x,t)
is the reversed heat kernel defined as follows

\displaystyle G(x,t):=\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}}e^{|x|^{2}/(4t)}.

In our applications
g
and
h
will be at least continuous although the bilinear form can be defined for more general functions.

Functionals D and H

We also define the following functionals, which correspond to dimensionless quantities, and are the parabolic version of the functionals that where introduced in the elliptic problem at
[
13
]
and adapted to the Stefan problem in
[
12
]
.

\displaystyle D(r,w):=2r^{2}\langle\nabla w,\nabla w\rangle_{r}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H(r,w):=\langle w,w\rangle_{r}

where
w:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-1,0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
will be, in our applications, at least
C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}(B_{1}\times(-1,1))
.

Frequency functions

We introduce the following functions

\displaystyle\phi(r,w):=\frac{D(r,w)}{H(r,w)},\qquad\phi^{\gamma}(r,w):=\frac{D(r,w)+\gamma r^{2\gamma}}{H(r,w)+r^{2\gamma}}

The function
\phi(r,w)
is known as the parabolic version of Almgren frequency function, and
\phi^{\gamma}(r,w)
is a truncated version introduced in
[
12
]
.

In the remainder of this section, we introduce several auxiliary notions that will be used throughout the analysis and the geometric setting adapted to the parabolic scaling.

Parabolic rescaling

Following the standard notation for parabolic PDE problems given a function
w:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-1,0)\to\mathbb{R}
we define

w_{r}(x,t):=w(rx,r^{2}t)

for
r>0
.

Note that
Z(w_{r})(x,t)=(Zw)(rx,r^{2}t)
and
\mathbf{H}(w_{r})(x,t)=r^{2}(\mathbf{H}w)(rx,r^{2}t)
.

Spatial cut-off

We fix a smooth spatial cut-off function
\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
verifying
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1/2})
and
\zeta\equiv 1
in
B_{1/4}
.

Family of polynomials

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of nonnegative
2
-homogeneous polynomials in the
x
variable which satisfy
\Delta p=f(0)
. In particular any
p\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies the parabolic obstacle problem (
2.1
) with
f(0)
instead of
f
.

Throughout the paper, we will frequently work on parabolic cylinders, which reflect the natural scaling of the problem. We denote them as follows:

Parabolic cylinders

Given
r>0
we define the parabolic cylinder
\mathscr{C}_{r}
as

\mathscr{C}_{r}:=B_{r}\times(-r^{2},0).

It will also be suitable to measure distance in space-time with respect to the parabolic geometry associated with the heat operator. For this purpose, we introduce the parabolic distance:

Parabolic distance and parabolic Hausdorff dimension

Given
(x,t),(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, we define the parabolic distance by

\text{dist}_{\text{par}}\left((x,t),(y,s)\right):=|x-y|+|t-s|^{1/2}.

The parabolic Hausdorff dimension is the Hausdorff dimension associated with the parabolic distance; see
[
21
, Chapter 4]
.

Spatial projection

We denote by
\pi_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the canonical projection
\pi_{x}(x,t):=x
.

Proposition 2.7

Let

Lemma 2.8
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0

be any subharmonic function such that

Proof.

Assume the claim is false. Then there exists a
\delta_{0}>0
and a sequence of subharmonic functions
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

[u_{k}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1/2})}>\delta_{0}[u_{k}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1})}+k\left(\|\nabla^{\prime}u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\right).

For each

Note

which implies

which rules out the case of

3.
Weiss and frequency formulae

We would like to apply the results of this section to
u-p_{2}
. However, since we are considering only solutions defined in
B_{1}\times(-1,1)
and our functionals
D
and
H
are defined in the whole space we have to use the cut-off function introduced in Section
2
. This fact generates an error term which can be controlled thanks to the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
12
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

Let
v\in C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}(\mathscr{C}_{1})
and
\zeta
a fixed cut-off function. Then for all
r\in(0,1/2)
we have

|\langle\zeta v,\textbf{H}(\zeta v)\rangle_{r}-\langle\zeta v,\zeta\textbf{H}v\rangle_{r}|+|\langle Z(\zeta v),\textbf{H}(\zeta v)\rangle_{r}-\langle\zeta Zv,\zeta\textbf{H}v\rangle_{r}|\leq CM_{v}^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{(8r)^{2}}}

where

M_{v}:=\sup_{B_{1/2}\times(-1/2,0)}|v|+|\nabla v|+|\partial_{t}v|+|\Delta v|,

and
C
depends only on
\zeta
.

Two key ingredients in the results from
[
12
]
were
\langle u-p,\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}\geq 0
and
\langle Z(u-p),\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}\geq 0
where
u
is a solution of (
2.1
) with
f\equiv 1
problem and
p\in\mathcal{P}
. However if
f\not\equiv 1
the previous estimates are no longer true, it may happen
\langle u-p,\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}<0
or
\langle Z(u-p),\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}<0
. For that reason we need to refine the estimates for small values of
r
, that is done in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u:B_{1}\times(-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a bounded solution of (
2.1
) and
(0,0)
a singular point. Given
p\in\mathcal{P}
set
w:=(u-p)\zeta
. Then, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r\in(0,r_{0})

(3.1)

|\langle w,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}|\leq Cr^{3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\langle Zw,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}|\leq Cr^{3}

where
C
is a constant depending only on
\zeta
and
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
.

Proof.

Since
u
is a solution of (
2.1
) we have
u\in C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}(\mathscr{C}_{1})
, hence
|(u-p_{2})_{r}|\leq Cr^{2}
and
|Z(u-p_{2})_{r}|\leq Cr^{2}
for some constant
C
. Since the difference between
p_{2}
and
p
is a second degree polynomial we obtain
|(u-p)_{r}|\leq Cr^{2}
and the constant depends on
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
. For the first bound, using the error estimates from
3.1
, note that

|\langle w,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}|\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{r^{2}}}+|\langle\zeta(u-p),\zeta\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}|,

since

|\langle\zeta(u-p),\zeta\textbf{H}(u-p)\rangle_{r}|\leq C\|(u-p)_{r}(\textbf{H}(u-p))_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\leq C\|(u-p)_{r}(f(rx)-f(0))\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\leq Cr^{3},

the first estimate follows absorbing the exponential error into the cubic term. The second bound is analogous using
Z(u-p)_{r}=\left(Z(u-p)\right)_{r}
, which implies
|Z(u-p)_{r}|\leq Cr^{2}
and the lemma follows. ∎

Remark 3.3
.

The exponentially small errors that appear when introducing the cutoff
\zeta
can be bounded using
3.1
, as has been done in the proof of
3.2
. Those error terms can always be absorbed by polynomial error terms.

We introduce now our first almost-monotonicity result:

Lemma 3.4

(Weiss’-type almost monotonicity formula)
.

Let

2.1
) and
(0,0)
a singular point. Given

For all

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}W(r,w)\geq-C,

depends only on
n
,
\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
and
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
.

(b)

.

(c)

For all

D(r,w)-2H(r,w)\geq-Cr^{5}

where
C
depends only on
n
,
\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
and
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
.

Proof.

(a)
Using
2.1
and
3.2

and

and the second one for controlling the terms

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ }{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}W(r,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\prime}(r,w)-2H^{\prime}(r,w)}{r^{4}}-4\frac{D(r,w)-2H(r,w)}{r^{5}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{r^{5}}\left(\langle Zw,Zw\rangle_{r}-2r^{2}\langle Zw,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}-4\langle w,Zw\rangle_{r}+4r^{2}\langle w,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}-4\langle w,w\rangle_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{r^{5}}(\langle Zw-2w,Zw-2w\rangle_{r}+4r^{2}\langle w,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}-2r^{2}\langle Zw,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r})

since the exponential error can be absorbed in the polynomial term. An analogous bound holds for the term

. The constant

.

(b)
To compute the limit note that, since
(0,0)

Integrating by parts and using
\Delta p_{2}=\Delta p

\nabla G=\frac{x}{2}G
and also

since

and

are

\displaystyle D(1,p_{2}-p)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\{t=-1\}}\nabla(p_{2}-p)\cdot\nabla(p_{2}-p)G

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\{t=-1\}}(p_{2}-p)G\Delta(p_{2}-p)-2\int_{\{t=-1\}}(p_{2}-p)\nabla(p_{2}-p)\cdot\nabla G

\displaystyle=\int_{\{t=-1\}}(p_{2}-p)x\cdot\nabla(p_{2}-p)G=2H(1,p_{2}-p).

(c)
The statement is a direct consequence of (a) and (b). Namely, integrating (

) from

to

and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Lemma 3.9
.

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}\phi^{\gamma}(r,w)+Cr^{\varepsilon-1}\geq\frac{2}{r}(F(r))^{2}.

(3.6)

\frac{\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mur}}}(H(r,w)+r^{2\gamma})}{H(r,w)+r^{2\gamma}}=\frac{2\left(D(r,w)+\gamma r^{2\gamma}+2r^{2}\langle w,\textbf{H}w\rangle_{r}\right)}{r\left(H(r,w)+r^{2\gamma}\right)}=\frac{2}{r}\left(\phi^{\gamma}(r,w)+F(r)\right).

and

\displaystyle\left|\int_{r}^{R}\frac{1}{s}F(s)ds\right|

3.1.
Variants of the Lemmas with extra assumption:

4.
The 2nd Blow-up in the Top Stratum

In this section we will analyze the structure of the blow-ups of

for singular points in
\Sigma_{n-1}
. The most important result of this section is that for any value of the parameter
\gamma\in(2,3)
we have
\lambda^{*}=\gamma
.

We start proving some estimates that will allow us to control different quantities by
\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}}
up to a cubic error term.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u:B_{1}\times(-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a bounded solution of (
2.1
) and
(0,0)
a singular point. Set

. Then there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all

To prove this we will need the following auxiliary lemma from
[
12
]
,

Lemma 4.2

(
[
12
, Lemma 6.1]
)
.

Let
u_{i}:\mathscr{C}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
solve

\begin{cases}\textbf{H}u_{i}=\chi_{\{u_{i}>0\}}(1+\varepsilon_{i}(x,t))&\text{in}\ \ \mathscr{C}_{2}\\

with

, and set

. Then

and

(4.2)

\left(\int_{\mathscr{C}_{1}}|\nabla w|^{2}+|w\textbf{H}w|+|\partial_{t}w|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C(\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+\bar{\varepsilon})

is a dimensional constant.

.

We will use
4.2
setting

and

. We have to check that

fulfills the hypotheses of the lemma. Note that

\textbf{H}u_{1}=\chi_{\{u_{1}>0\}}\left(\frac{f(rx)}{f(0)}\right)

and also, since

is a Lipschitz function with Lipschtiz constant

, we have

which fulfills the hypothesis with

provided

. The case of

is clear because

, hence it solves the PDE in weak sense since

has zero measure in

.

Therefore, by (

) we obtain

and the bound for

and

are obtained in a similar way from (

).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
u:B_{1}\times(-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a bounded solution of (
2.1
),
(0,0)\in\Sigma_{n-1}
. Set
w:=(u-p_{2})
, and without loss of generality assume
p_{2}=\frac{f(0)}{2}x_{n}^{2}
. Then there exists
r_{0}>0
and
\alpha>0
such that for all

it holds

\|\partial_{t}w_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}+\|\nabla^{\prime}w_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}+\|w_{r}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{x}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\leq C\left(\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r^{3}\right),

where
C
is a constant depending only on
n
and
\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
. Here
\nabla^{\prime}
denotes the gradient w.r.t. the first
n-1
variables and
\|\cdot\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{x}}
denotes the Hölder norm only w.r.t. the spatial variables.

Proof.

For
\textbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap\{p_{2}=0\}
define the incremental quotients for a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\textbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
h\in(0,1)

\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}g:=\frac{g(\cdot+\textbf{e}h,\cdot)-g(\cdot,\cdot)}{h}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{(t,h)}g:=\frac{g(\cdot,\cdot+h)-g(\cdot,\cdot)}{h}.

We want to compute the incremental quotients of
w_{r}=(u-p)_{r}
. Note that
\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}p=0
since

\textbf{e}\in\{p=0\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and also
\delta_{(t,h)}p=0
since
p
does not depend on time variable.

In the region
\{u>0\}
using that
f
is Lipschitz we have

\textbf{H}(\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r})\leq r^{2}\frac{f_{r}(x+\textbf{e}h)-f_{r}(x)}{h}\leq Cr^{3}.

In the region
\{u=0\}
we have

\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r}=\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}u_{r}=\frac{u_{r}(x+\textbf{e}h,t)}{h}\geq 0.

Hence the function
\left(\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r}\right)_{-}+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}
is subcaloric since

\textbf{H}\left((\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r})_{-}+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}\right)=\textbf{H}\left((\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r})_{-}\right)+Cr^{3}\geq 0.

Therefore, since
u\in C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}
we have

(\delta_{(\textbf{e},h)}w_{r})_{-}+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}\longrightarrow(\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r})_{-}+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}\quad\text{a. e. as }h\to 0

and the limit function is also subcaloric. We can repeat all the bounds in direction
-\textbf{e}
instead of
e
, since
\delta_{(-\textbf{e},h)}w_{r}\to-\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}
and
(-\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r})_{-}=(\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r})_{+}
we obtain that
|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}
is a subcaloric function.

Similarly for the time variable, in the region
\{u>0\}
we have

\textbf{H}\left(-\delta_{(t,-h)}w_{r}\right)=r^{2}\frac{f_{r}(x)\chi_{r}(x,t-h)-f_{r}(x)}{h}\leq 0.

Inside
\{u=0\}
, we have
-\delta_{(t,-h)}w_{r}\geq 0
.
Therefore
\left(-\delta_{(t,-h)}w_{r}\right)_{-}
is and its limit
(-\partial_{t}w_{r})_{-}
are subcaloric. Since
\partial_{t}w_{r}=\partial_{t}u_{r}\geq 0
we have
\left(-\partial_{t}w_{r}\right)_{-}=\partial_{t}w_{r}=|\partial_{t}w_{r}|
, thus the absolute value is also subcaloric.

By
2.5
we get

\sup_{\mathscr{C}_{4/3}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}\right)\leq C\left(\int_{\mathscr{C}_{5/4}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|+\frac{C}{2n}r^{3}|x|^{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/\varepsilon},

and using that
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\varepsilon}}
is a quasi norm

\sup_{\mathscr{C}_{4/3}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|\right)\leq C\left(\int_{\mathscr{C}_{5/4}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|\right)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/\varepsilon}+Cr^{3}

for some dimensional constant
C
. Since
L^{1,\infty}\subset L^{\varepsilon}
it holds:

\left(\int_{\mathscr{C}_{5/4}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|\right)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/\varepsilon}\leq C\sup_{\theta>0}\theta|\{|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|)>\theta\}\cap\mathscr{C}_{5/4}|.

By Calderon-Zygmund (
2.6
) we can control the

term by

\sup_{\theta>0}\theta|\{|\partial_{t}w_{r}|)>\theta\}\cap\mathscr{C}_{5/4}|\leq C\left(\|\textbf{H}w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/3})}+\|w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/3})}\right).

Moreover the term
|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|
can also be controled by interpolation inequalities (Ehrling’s lemma)

\|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{5/4})}\leq\|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/4})}\leq C\left(\|D^{2}w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/4})}+\|w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/4})}\right).

Combining the previous estimates we obtain

\sup_{\mathscr{C}_{4/3}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|\right)\leq C\left(\|\textbf{H}w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/3})}+\|w_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/3})}+r^{3}\right).

Note
\textbf{H}w_{r}\leq Cr^{3}
, taking
\xi
a smooth cut-off function which verifies
\xi\equiv 1
in
\mathscr{C}_{5/3}
and vanishes outside
\mathscr{C}_{2}
and integrating by parts we can estimate

\|\textbf{H}w_{r}-Cr^{3}\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{5/3})}\leq-\int_{\mathscr{C}_{2}}\xi\left(\textbf{H}w_{r}-Cr^{3}\right)=-\int_{\mathscr{C}_{2}}(\Delta+\partial_{t})\xi w_{r}+Cr^{3}\leq C\left(\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r^{3}\right).

thus

\sup_{\mathscr{C}_{4/3}}\left(|\partial_{t}w_{r}|+|\partial_{\textbf{e}}w_{r}|\right)\leq C\left(\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r^{3}\right).

Therefore we have the Lipschtiz estimate for the first

\|\nabla^{\prime}w_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{4/3})}\leq C\left(\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r^{3}\right).

By a rescaled version of
2.7
we obtain

\|w_{r}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\leq C\left(\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r^{3}\right),

and we are done.
∎

is bounded. For doing so note

and also

since
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{r^{3}}{H(r,w\zeta)^{1/2}}=0
we have
\tilde{w}_{r}\to q
for some

. Using the

estimates on

,
\partial_{t}w_{r}
and

from

and
4.3
we conclude that, up to subsequences, the convergence to

is locally uniformly.

For checking that

is a solution of the parabolic Signorini problem note

\textbf{H}\tilde{w}_{r}=\frac{r^{2}\left(f(rx)\chi_{\{u>0\}}-f(0)\right)}{H(r,w\zeta)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{Lr^{3}}{H(r,w\zeta)^{1/2}}

and recall that

\theta(r):=\frac{Lr^{3}}{H(r,w\zeta)^{1/2}}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad r\longrightarrow 0.

Define
\bar{w}_{r}:=\tilde{w}_{r}-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2n}\theta(r)
. Hence for

small enough
\textbf{H}\bar{w}_{r}\leq 0
and by Riesz representation theorem
\textbf{H}\bar{w}_{r}
defines a measure which converges to
\textbf{H}q
since
\bar{w}_{r}
also converges to
q
locally uniformly. Moreover it is verified
\textbf{H}q\leq 0
. By a similar argument
\bar{w}_{r}\textbf{H}\bar{w}_{r}\geq 0
implies
q\textbf{H}q\geq 0
. We conclude that
\textbf{H}q
is supported on
\{p_{2}=0\}
since for any set

we have

which implies
\textbf{H}q=0
.

∎

The following lemma will be useful to control the

-norm of

when the frequency is saturated.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
u:B_{1}\times(-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a bounded solution of (
2.1
),
(0,0)\in\Sigma_{n-1}
. Set
w:=(u-p_{2})
, let
\lambda^{*}
be given by
3.6
and assume
\lambda^{*}=\gamma
and
\lambda\in(2,3)
. Then, for
r
small enough it holds

(4.7)

\|w_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\leq Cr^{\lambda^{*}-\delta},

where
C
is a constant and
\delta>0
is any positive number.

Proof.

We will argue by contradiction, assume (
4.7
) does not hold.
Consider a particular subsequence
w_{r_{k}}
where each of the
r_{k}
is the biggest value such that (
4.7
) does not hold (taking
C=k
), i.e. we define

r_{k}:=\sup\left\{r>0:\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}\geq kr_{k}^{\lambda^{*}-\delta}\right\}.

Consider the sequence

v_{r_{k}}=\frac{w_{r_{k}}}{\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}},

by construction
\|v_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}=1
. Moreover, by
4.1

(4.8)

\|\nabla v_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}=\frac{\|\nabla w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}}{\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}}\leq C\frac{\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r_{k}^{3}}{\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}}.

The term
\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}/\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}
is bounded by construction of the subsequence since

\frac{1}{2^{(n+2)/2}}\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}=\|w_{2r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}<k\left(2r_{k}\right)^{\lambda^{*}-\delta}\leq 2^{\lambda^{*}-\delta}\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}.

Also we can assume that

is bounded, otherwise the claim of the lemma is trivial. Hence, the right hand side of (
4.8
) is bounded and
v_{r_{k}}\to q
strongly in

and weakly in
H^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{1})
. Since
\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}
and
\|\nabla w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}
are also controlled by
\|w_{r_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{2})}+r_{k}^{3}
we conclude that the convergence to

is uniform in
\mathscr{C}_{1}
.

For any
\tau\in(0,1)
consider

(4.9)

H(\tau,v_{r})=\int_{\{t=-\tau^{2}\}}v_{r}^{2}G=\frac{1}{\|w_{r}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}}\int_{\{t=-\tau^{2}\}}w_{r}^{2}G\leq C_{\tau}r^{2\delta},

where in the last inequality we have used
\int_{\{t=-\tau^{2}\}}w_{r}^{2}G=H(\tau r,w)\leq C_{\tau}r^{2\lambda^{*}}
. Taking the limit

in (

) we get

(4.10)

\int_{\{t=-\tau^{2}\}\cap\mathscr{C}_{1}}q^{2}G=0,

which is a contradiction since, by construction,
{\|q\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1})}}=1
.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
u:B_{1}\times(-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a bounded solution of (
2.1
),
(0,0)\in\Sigma_{n-1}
. Let
\lambda^{*}
be given by

. For any

it verifies

.

Proof.

We will divide the proof in two steps. In the first one we will prove the claim for the case

and in the second we will use an iterative argument.

•
Step 1.
Take
\gamma_{1}\in\left(2,\frac{5}{2}\right)
and assume

by

converges to a

-homogeneous solution of the Signorini problem. By

Since

the almost-monotonicity of the frequency formula

holds and then, by

and

expanding terms we obtain

and taking

(recall

since there are no solutions to the parabolic Signorini problem verifying (

.

Hence,
\lambda^{*}_{1}=\gamma_{1}
.

•
Step 2.
Take
\gamma_{1}\in\left(2,\frac{5}{2}\right)
, by

we know

, then by

and the estimates from
