Flexible artificial compound eye cameras for ultrawide continuous tracking in mixed reality
Authors: Heng Jiang, Chi Chung Tsoi, Yu Du, Yao Chai, Chi-hung Tang, Lanrui Sun, Weixing Yu, Mengchao Ma, Changrui Liao, Huaping Jia, Xuming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn nature, fruit flies Drosophila have evolved a simple but superb vision system characterised by a three-level synergy: natural compound eyes for panoramic perception, head muscles for continuous tracking in dim conditions, and neural circuits for dynamic scenes. This vision system serves as an ideal model for biomimicry, yet achieving true replication remains challenging, despite significant progress in artificial compound eyes recently. In this study, we present a flexible artificial compound eye camera that adopts such a three-level synergy. An artificial compound eye is constructed by plastic optical fibres and curved microlens arrays for real-time panoramic imaging. Two tethers simulate head muscle movements, achieving a 270° field of view for continuous tracking of weak signals. An artificial intelligence algorithm mimics neural processing, enabling the reconstruction of interactive mixed-reality scenes at rates up to 7000 fps. This unique integration of panoramic sensing, active tracking, and neural-like processing establishes a framework for vision-based metaverse applications and bioinspired wearable technologies.Subject terms: Fibre optics and optical communications, Imaging techniques, Imaging and sensing, Optoelectronic devices and components, Photonic devices

Introduction

Despite advances in imaging technology, continuously capturing and interpreting wide-field-of-view (FOV) visual information in complex environments remains a fundamental challenge. This is particularly true under low-light conditions, where weak object signals hinder reliable detection and tracking, limiting applications in mixed reality, visual restoration, and autonomous machine vision. Over millions of years of evolution, fruit flies (Drosophila) have evolved a visual system that integrates optics, motor control, and neural processing. Unlike single-chamber human eyes, which rely on plane-to-plane projection, each fruit fly possesses two natural compound eyes (NCEs), each covering an FOV of up to 180° (Fig. 1a)1–3. NCEs are composed of densely packed ommatidia-the basic optical units (Fig. 1c) arranged on a curved surface, enabling image formation primarily through angular projection (Fig. 1b) 4,5. Beyond static optics, NCEs are assisted by motor control: in dim environments, head bending driven by muscles extends the FOV, enhances light capture, and reduces motion blur, thereby allowing continuous tracking of moving objects6–8 (Supplementary Movie 1). Furthermore, recent studies indicate that visual signals from NCEs are processed by neurons to reconstruct corresponding virtual scenes in the brain9. Together, the angular projection optics, the motor control, and the neural processing of NCEs provide a robust biological blueprint for developing next-generation flexible artificial compound eyes (ACEs). However, most existing ACE designs have primarily focused on mimicking the function of NCEs, largely neglecting the critical roles of motor control and neural processing, which are so-called typical ACEs.

Typical ACEs can be traced back to the planar microlens arrays, whose FOV is restricted due to their flat configuration10–13 (Fig. 1giv), and curved microlens arrays (Fig. 1gv), which can be further classified into ACEs employing planar photodetector arrays (PDAs) and curved PDAs14–17, mimicking the distribution of microlenses in the outermost layer of NCEs (Fig. 1gi). Although ACEs with planar PDAs extend the FOV to approximately 140° by leveraging the curved distribution of microlenses 14,15, they still suffer from significant off-axis aberrations. To address this, researchers have attempted to introduce curved PDAs, enabling light from individual microlenses to be more accurately captured, thereby extending the FOV to around 160° and minimising off-axis aberrations 16,17. Nonetheless, these designs struggle to achieve both real-time imaging and dynamic motion detection simultaneously due to the discrete nature of the photodetectors. Further improvements are seen in ACEs integrating light guides (Fig. 1gvi), which facilitate light transmission between microlenses and PDAs, achieving an FOV of up to 180°, and faithfully mimicking the anatomic structure and function of NCEs (Fig. 1gii)18–22. Despite these advancements, the FOV remains confined to a hemispherical angle. Although metasurfaces are a promising alternative, their focusing efficiency drops markedly at large incident angles, for instance, falling from 82% at normal incidence to 45% at 85°23. More importantly, since the anatomical structures of typical ACEs are fixed, it is difficult for them to bend their head to extend the FOV and continuously track objects, especially under dim light, as a Drosophila does (Fig. 1giii). Likewise, due to the limitations of ACE hardware and the incomplete understanding of neural activities in the Drosophila brain, which was revealed recently9, the reconstruction of mixed reality based on ACE visual signals remains largely unexplored.

Here, we present the design of a Flexible Artificial Compound Eye Camera (FACEcam). The ACE head is comprised of a curved microlens array and a perforated dome into which flexible plastic optical fibres are threaded (Fig. 1e). Each microlens-fibre pair enables to limit the acceptance angle of the transmitted light, mimicking a natural ommatidium (Fig. 1f). Owing to the flexibility of the fibres, two tethers can bend the ACE head (Fig. 1d), extending the FOV to 270° and enabling continuous object tracking, thereby mimicking the head bending of Drosophila. This configuration achieves wide-angle imaging without off-axis aberrations and dynamic motion detection at frequencies up to 7000 frames per second (fps). Integrated with a convolutional neural network (CNN, middle of Fig. 1d), the system classifies visual signals in real time, paralleling the layered processing of the Drosophila visual system: lamina (shallow convolution), medulla (deep convolution), and lobula (fully connected layer) (Fig. 1a). This establishes a visual link between the physical and virtual worlds, allowing seamless integration of real-world elements into digital environments. Unlike our previous ACEs [21&22], FACEcam unifies the compound eye, the tethers, and the neural network (Fig. 1gvii), offering an intimate mimicry of the insect visual system and its cooperative mechanisms (Fig. 1giii). Consequently, moving objects can be continuously captured with a wide FOV and a high speed before being transferred into the virtual domain. This embodies the first principle of mixed reality (MR), real-time physical-digital coupling, while the second principle can be realised by manipulating virtual elements via real-world stimuli. Together, these functions allow FACEcam-based MR to realise an immersive and interactive experience that effectively dissolves the boundary between reality and virtual scenes.

Results

Working principle

In each ommatidium of NCEs, light is first collected by a corneal facet lens within a specific acceptance angle, then transmitted through a crystalline cone and a rhabdom (acting as a light guide) to photoreceptor cells24 (Fig. 1c). The ommatidia are further connected by axon bundles that form synaptic connections within lamina cartridges25. After initial signal processing in deeper neural centres, such as the medulla and lobula (Fig. 1a, b)26, the visual information is ultimately transmitted to central brain regions.

In Drosophila’s NCE, the acceptance angle (∆φ) of each ommatidium is given by ∆φ = d/f = 4.5°, while the inter-ommatidial angle (∆Φ) is defined as ∆Φ = D/R, which is between 4.7° to 5.5°27–29 (Fig. 1b, c), where d is the rhabdom diameter, f is the focal length of the facet lens, D is the arc distance between adjacent ommatidia, and R is the local radius of curvature30. Ideally, ∆φ should be close to ∆Φ to ensure complete angular coverage without losing information while minimising redundant overlap. Similarly, in FACEcam, under the focal-plane approximation, ∆φ ≈ d1/f ≈ 10° should be close to ∆Φ = D′/R′ = 10°−12.8° (Fig. 1e, f), where d1 is the optical fibre core diameter, f, D′, and R′ represent the focal length of the large microlens, the arc distance of adjacent microlenses, and the local radius of curvature of the microlens array, respectively. More generally, the effective acceptance angle is jointly determined by the focal geometry, aperture size, and fibre core diameter. Otherwise, the receiving areas of adjacent fibres would have a large overlap, lowering the angular resolution. For comparison, we also designed setups that included only a pinhole array or an array of curved microlenses with a small diameter, allowing them to function under different conditions. Detailed comparisons of the microlens (large-diameter) array (Table 1), the pinhole-only array, and the microlens (small-diameter) array are provided in Supplementary Information2.

Characteristics of the front-end imaging system of the FACEcam prototype

In the assembly process, the Flanged Plastic Optical Fibres are fabricated (Supplementary Information 3). In the assembly process, 159 flanged plastic optical fibres (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Information 3) are threaded into an ultrahigh precision 3D-printed perforated dome (diameter: 12.6 mm; Supplementary Fig. S1a, microArch® S140, BMF Precision Tech Inc.), with all the flanged ends sitting on the dome’s outer surface (Fig. 2c, d). The opposite, bare ends of the fibres are fed into a perforated planar buncher (Supplementary Fig. S1b), which preserves the corresponding spatial relationship of fibres on the dome surface. Light exiting from the bare fibre ends is projected onto a flat imaging sensor through an imaging lens (Fig. 1f). A microlens array, featuring 159 microlenses aligned to the flanged fibre ends, is mounted onto the dome via four positioning grooves (overall diameter: 15.1 mm; Fig. 2a). Each microlens restricts incident light to the desired acceptance angles of the corresponding fibre. An aperture diaphragm with a pinhole array is sandwiched between the microlens array and the optical fibre array so as to suppress the crosstalk from neighbouring ommatidia (not to restrict acceptance angle, Fig. 2b), and the diameter of the aperture here is similar to the diameter of the microlens. Both the dome and the aperture diaphragm are 3D-printed using black material to absorb stray light, mimicking the crosstalk-prevention function of the first and second pigment layers in NCEs. A yoke incorporating a flexible tube encloses the fibres between the dome and the planar buncher (Fig. 2g). Two tethers attached to the yoke allow for the adjustment of the FACEcam head’s orientation (Fig. 2f, g). All components are hosted by a screwed hollow tube (Supplementary Fig. S1c), including the head (comprised of the microlens array, aperture diaphragm, the dome and the yoke), the flexible tube, the buncher, the imaging lens and the flat imaging sensor (Fig. 2g). This configuration enables light captured from the curved surface to be efficiently transmitted to the flat sensor, while also allowing head flexibility, thus closely replicating the structure and function of the Drosophila NCEs (Supplementary Tables S1, 18–30 s in Supplementary Movie 1).

Operating principles of Flexible Artificial Compound Eye Camera (FACEcam).

The red dotted line indicates the boundary of the optical fibre. f Top view of the FACEcam head. g 3D view of an assembled FACEcam. h Concept of image formation. Using a ‘×‘ line-art pattern as the object (top panel), designated microlenses allow light to pass through (second panel) and activate specific optical fibres (third panel). An imaging lens then projects the output from the bare fibre ends onto a flat imaging sensor chip (fourth panel), where the light is converted into the final digital image (bottom panel).

In the image formation process, the light from the object first passes through the microlenses on the microlens array within a specific acceptance angle, thereby entering the corresponding flanged fibre ends (Fig. 2h). At the other bare fibre ends, the planar images are projected onto the flat imaging sensor chip. Afterward, the final images are obtained for digital image processing. The imaging lens prevents the contact of the bare fibre ends with the vulnerable image chip surface. The low acceptance angle reduces motion blur, while total internal reflection within the fibres preserves angular mapping regardless of bending (Supplementary Information 4).

Experimental results

To highlight the advantages of the three-level synergistic mechanism, the pinhole-only array assembly was utilised in experiments. First, the FACEcam achieves an ultrawide FOV of up to 270° by mechanically bending its head. For experimental validation, laser spots were projected at angular intervals of 22.5° across both the x and y directions, covering angles from 0° to 135° (Fig. 3a, b). In contrast to previous ACEs with fixed heads23, FACEcam can dynamically bend its head by 45° at maximum when the incident angle of the laser approaches the edge of the aperture diaphragm (around 90°), resulting in an effective FOV between −135° and +135°. Across this 270° range, the captured spots exhibit high uniformity in terms of size, brightness, and angular correspondence (see Supplementary Information 5 and Supplementary Movie 2 for details on intrinsic angular acceptance, bending accuracy, and signal variation with angle). This exceptionally wide FOV offers significant advantages for applications such as MR, where enhanced spatial coverage can greatly improve the sense of immersion.

Secondly, FACEcam can perform real-time, direct imaging without distortion and off-axis aberrations. Unlike certain ACEs that rely heavily on additional postprocessing steps 19,20, FACEcam achieves imaging without distortion inherently, just like NCEs (Supplementary Information 6). Further, when the logo of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University is placed in front of the camera, it is captured clearly, with no observable off-axis aberrations. (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. S2). This is because, in a hemispherical, shell-like arrangement of ommatidia, each optical channel primarily operates near its local on-axis condition, thereby largely avoiding off-axis sensitivity issues. Moreover, FACEcam facilitates depth perception. In the experimental setup, a cross-shaped target is positioned at varying distances from the FACEcam head (red lines in Fig. 3d). The point spread parameter σ is obtained by analysing the greyscale intensity profiles along vertical edges (pink lines in Fig. 3d, Supplementary Information 7). This enables the establishment of a linear relationship between σ and the inverse object distance u−1 (Fig. 3e), consistent with theoretical predictions21,31,32. Once the value of σ is derived from a captured image, the corresponding distance u can be estimated directly using the fitted linear relationship. This method allows the determination of object distances from newly acquired images by simply calculating their σ values. Through this capability, FACEcam not only supports real-time object visualisation for MR but also enhances spatial awareness in MR by providing distance estimation.

Thirdly, FACEcam is capable of achieving panoramic imaging over an FOV exceeding 180° via head bending. To demonstrate this, five letters “POLYU” (an abbreviation of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University) span from −126° to 126°. The imaging outcomes (Fig. 3f–h, Supplementary Fig. S3) show that FACEcam obtains high-quality panoramic images without noticeable off-axis aberrations. Unlike other ACEs with fixed structures23, the mechanical flexibility of FACEcam significantly extends its observational range. This enables bringing objects from reality to a virtual environment seamlessly over a wide angle.

Fourthly, FACEcam enables continuous tracking of moving objects under low illumination by actively bending its head to enhance signal acquisition (see Supplementary Information 8 and Supplementary Movie 3, 4 for additional details on the dynamic limitations of head bending). In this demonstration, a cross-shaped target (0.1 lux) moves from −45° to 45°. When the head remains non-bending (five representative positions shown in Fig. 4a), the target appears at different locations in the images due to the variations of angular position, causing weak signals in detection and thus difficulty in discernment (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. s40a and Supplementary Information 9). In contrast, the bending of FACEcam allows the head to keep tracking the moving target, capturing it consistently at the same position in the image frame (Fig. 4c). This tracking mechanism facilitates image overlay at the same pixel region in the image sensor chip and drastically enhances the signal level by integrating signals over time (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. S40b and Supplementary Information 9), resulting a clearer image and a better discernment of the target. Moreover, it also significantly reduces the distance estimation errors. To quantify the enhancement, we evaluated the value of σ under both non-bending and bending conditions. When the head is non-bending, lower signal intensity leads to higher estimation errors (pink regions in Fig. 4e). Conversely, the bending mode shows much lower errors (light-blue regions in Fig. 4e) with the increase of the number of overlaid frames. To comprehensively assess the performance of continuous tracking, two quantitative metrics are employed: Information Entropy and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM). The former objectively measures the randomness and information content of the image (33, “Methods”), and the latter evaluates image fidelity by considering luminance, contrast and structural information in a manner consistent with human perception (Supplementary Information 10). Under non-bending conditions, both metrics remain low (pink regions in Fig. 4f, g), indicating limited information content and poor recognisability. However, as more frames are overlaid during head bending, both entropy and SSIM increase steadily (light-blue regions Fig. 4f, g), demonstrating improved information richness and structural clarity. This continuous tracking capability enables FACEcam to robustly identify and reconstruct objects under low-light conditions, thereby enhancing its utilisation in MR environments.

Fifthly, FACEcam enables the establishment of a seamless link between physical reality and MR. In this demonstration, a female equipped with a white light-emitting diode (LED) is positioned at various angular locations around a male wearing FACEcam, specifically at −45°, 0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°, with 45° intervals (Fig. 5b, g). The white light of the LED serves as a recognition signal within the MR environment. When the head of FACEcam remains non-bending (Fig. 5a), it successfully captures the LED signals from the female at −45°, 0°, 45°, and 90° (Fig. 5c1–4, k upper panel). These images are then directly processed by a convolutional neural network (CNN; Fig. 5d, Supplementary Information 11), which identifies the LED signals and maps the corresponding positions of the female in MR space (Fig. 5e). However, when the female is positioned at 135°, the LED signal cannot be captured due to the limited FOV of the non-bending FACEcam (Fig. 5c5, k upper panel). To lift this limitation, FACEcam can bend its head toward the target, allowing it to detect the signal at 135° (Fig. 5f). As a result, FACEcam can record signals from all five positions (−45° to 135°, Fig. 5h, k lower panel), which are accurately recognised by the CNN (Fig. 5i) and used to reconstruct the virtual representation of the female at each corresponding location in the MR environment (Fig. 5j). This allows FACEcam to continuously track moving objects by bending its head, just like Drosophila does. Furthermore, the response speed of FACEcam can reach up to 7000 fps (Supplementary Information 12), which is ~100 times faster than conventional virtual reality systems (typically 60–180 fps)34, thereby significantly reducing user discomfort and enhancing the overall sense of immersion and realism. This ultra-fast, seamless integration and interaction between physical reality and virtuality demonstrate how FACEcam supports immersive MR experiences, ultimately enabling users to transcend the spatial and temporal limitations of the real world.

More importantly, the beauty of MR lies not only in the virtualisation of physical objects, but also in the real-time interactivity between the real and virtual worlds. In this demonstration, FACEcam is employed to capture variations in dynamic optical signals generated by movements in the physical world, which are then used to trigger corresponding responses in MR. For instance, a female equipped with a white LED is positioned in front of a male wearing FACEcam. The captured light signal is utilised to render the female’s virtual counterpart within the MR environment. To demonstrate interactive control, a male subject wearing a red LED on his hand can wave it in front of FACEcam to simulate an action, such as waving in MR to move a cloud over the female (Supplementary Movie 5). Such capabilities provide a technological foundation for human presence, interaction, and evolution within the metaverse (see Supplementary Information 13 for more details about the CNN-assisted MR).

Discussion

In this work, we innovate a flexible camera that features a three-level synergistic mechanism inspired by Drosophila vision: ommatidial optics, muscular head movement, and neural-inspired processing (see Table 2 for detailed comparisons). Unlike conventional ACEs that focus solely on mimicking ommatidial optics, our FACEcam combines bioinspired optics with active head motion (see Supplementary Information 14 for detailed comparisons). This integration enhances performance by providing an extended field of view, real-time panoramic imaging, and continuous tracking in low-light conditions, thereby improving object visibility, spatial accuracy, and information richness. A detailed discussion about the alignment, calibration, and scalability of the system is provided in Supplementary Information 15. The microlens (large-diameter) array provides a well-balanced performance between angular resolution and sensitivity, while the other two designs (i.e., the pinhole-only array and the microlens (small-diameter) array) are retained only as supplementary references to illustrate how different parameter choices affect the performance. When paired with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), FACEcam facilitates ultrafast mixed reality (MR) interaction, positioning it as a versatile 3D vision platform for intelligent wearable devices and metaverse applications. However, the spatial resolution (i.e., pixel count) remains limited. More details can be found in the Supplementary Information35–42. Future research could consider replacing each optical fibre with a fibre bundle. Since a typical fibre bundle contains around 10,000 individual fibres, this change could increase the pixel count from a few hundred to several million, making it suitable for numerous practical applications, including drones and robots. In addition, future work will explore larger-scale advanced flexible curved photodetector arrays that can be directly integrated with curved pinhole or microlens architectures, further advancing the biomimetic imaging paradigm.

Methods

Information entropy

For a discrete random variable x, the information entropy H(x) is defined as,

where m denotes the number of possible outcomes and pi(x), i = 1,2,3,…,m, represents the probability associated with the i-th outcome. In the context of an image, where x corresponds to a grey level, pi(x) indicates the proportion of pixels with grey level x relative to the total number of pixels. Thus, by analysing the greyscale histogram, pi(x) can be calculated for each grey value, and the entropy of the image can be quantitatively assessed.

In our experiments, five images were acquired under each condition (i.e., at each viewing angle under either a non-bending or bending condition). After subtracting the background, the entropy for each image was calculated. The average entropy and corresponding standard deviations were then computed to characterise the information content under each scenario.
