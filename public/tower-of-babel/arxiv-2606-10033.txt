Uniform Positivity of Lyapunov Exponents for anti Hölder potentials
Nicholas Chiem

Abstract
. We consider Schrödinger operators with dynamically defined potentials that are anti α \alpha -Hölder and generated by hyperbolic dynamics. We prove an anti Lipschitz estimate on functions related to the orbit of e → 1 \vec{e}_{1} under an associated projectivized cocycle. After, we apply the estimate on subshifts of finite type and maps with one expanding direction on 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} where d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 . In particular, the application leads to uniformly positive Lyapunov exponents with sufficiently large coupling constants.



1.
Introduction

We are interested in studying the family of discrete Schrödinger operators with potentials generated by a compact metric space
(\Omega,T,\mu)
, where
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is continuous and invertible and
\mu
is a
T
-invariant measure. The one-dimensional Schrödinger operator is defined as
H_{\lambda,v,\omega}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
where

(1)

[H_{\lambda,v,\omega}\psi]_{n}=\psi_{n-1}+\psi_{n+1}+\lambda v(T^{n}\omega)\psi_{n}.

In (
1
),
\omega\in\Omega
is called the phase,
v:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bounded measurable function called the potential, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is called the coupling constant. If
T
is not invertible we replace
\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathbb{N}
and impose Dirichlet boundary condition:
\psi_{-1}=0
.

Our interest in studying Schrödinger operators stems from a physical phenomenon discovered in 1958 by Phillip W. Anderson, Anderson localization. Localization takes on two forms: spectral and dynamical. We are particularly interested in spectral localization, which is when the spectrum of the operator is pure point with exponentially decaying eigenfunctions. One of the most well known cases is the Anderson model, which is when the potential is sampled by independent identically distributed (iid) random variables, each with a common distribution with support containing at least two distinct points. A special case is when the distribution has exactly two points: the associated operator family is called the Anderson-Bernoulli model. The latter setting is considered the most difficult and was initially proven by Carmona-Klein-Martinelli
[
10
]
using multi-scale analysis. Later, it was also shown using one-dimensional tools by
[
2
,
9
,
26
,
21
,
31
,
35
]
. For more information and results on localization, see
[
27
,
14
,
19
,
22
,
24
,
25
,
23
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
.

To study localization, one explores the behavior of solutions from the eigenvalue equation
H_{\lambda,v,\omega}\psi=E\psi
for energies
E\in\mathbb{R}
. One direction to study the solutions is through Schrödinger cocycles
(T,A^{(E-\lambda v)})
, especially through the Lyapunov exponent. The Lyapunov exponent plays a central role in understanding localization; Kotani theory is a bridge between the two, as it relates the absolutely continuous spectrum to the essential closure of the set of energies where the Lyapunov exponent is zero. It has been observed that when paired with a large deviation estimate, one has a strong indication of localization, see
[
31
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

We wish to point out that the method in the preceding paragraph studies the spectrum through a dynamical lens rather than a spectral lens. The latter has been explored by Damanik-Fillman
[
15
,
16
]
. They show that for the doubling map and hyperbolic toral automorphisms with continuous sampling function, one has a connected essential spectrum. By using Kotani theory, one can return back to Lyapunov exponents and conclude positivity almost everywhere!

For our paper, we are interested in removing the entirety of the absolutely continuous spectrum by proving that the Lyapunov exponent is uniformly positive for all energies. The investigation of positive Lyapunov exponents has a rich history; the most famous settings established were the iid setting by Furstenberg
[
20
]
and the quasi-periodic setting by Sorets-Spencer
[
36
]
by extending methods from Herman
[
28
]
, and Bourgain
[
5
]
for
d
-dimensional tori.

Unfortunately, in alternative settings such as hyperbolic dynamics, the results are less gratifying. Independence plays a central role, thus by reducing to a strongly mixing system, one faces many complications. Our paper will focus on the hyperbolic setting, which contains two regimes: small and large coupling constants. For small coupling constants, Figotin-Pastur
[
33
]
developed methods that provided a necessary tool for Chulaevsky-Spencer
[
12
]
to show positivity away from the center and edges of the spectrum for deterministic potentials with small coupling constants. Building upon this, Bourgain-Schlag
[
8
]
established a large deviation theorem to achieve localization for almost every phase in the same spectral regime as Chulaevsky-Spencer
[
12
]
. For large couplings, Bjerklöv
[
3
]
established positivity for circle endomorphisms and Zhang-Li
[
41
]
extends the result to expanding toral automorphisms. Moreover, J. Bourgain and E. Bourgain
[
6
]
established positivity for any non-negative coupling, as long as one has sufficient hyperbolicity and
C^{1}
non-constant potentials. Results in this regime have also been shown by Sadel-Baldes
[
34
]
, Damanik-Killip
[
18
]
, and Krüger
[
32
]
. Recently, a more complete answer for hyperbolic base dynamics was given from Avila-Damanik-Zhang
[
1
]
. They were able to show that for
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions, the set
\mathcal{Z}=\{E\in\mathbb{R}:L(E)=0\}
is discrete and there exists a constant
\lambda_{0}(v)
so that
\mathcal{Z}
is finite for all
0<\lambda<\lambda_{0}
. Additionally, there is an open dense set
\mathcal{O}^{\alpha}\subset C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{R})
such that one has uniformly positive Lyapunov exponents for all potentials in
\mathcal{O}^{\alpha}
. The technique used by Avila-Damanik-Zhang is the invariance principle, which has also been used to study positivity and continuity of the Lyapunov exponent, see
[
4
,
37
]
. For more general information on results of positive Lyapunov exponents we refer to
[
38
]
.

For our paper, a method of interest was developed by Young
[
40
]
. Young studied cocycles of the form

(2)

A_{\epsilon}(\cdot)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}R_{\phi_{\epsilon}(\cdot)}

where
R
is a rotation matrix that depends on a function
\phi_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Young observed that positivity could be concluded so long as
\phi_{\epsilon}
satisfies three conditions: compactly supported, monotone on the support, and derivative is positive and bounded away from zero. Damanik
[
13
, Problem 6]
observed that it would be possible to show positive Lyapunov exponents for Schrödinger cocycles with the method Young used. At the time, it was unclear how to apply it directly since the method required the cocycle to be of the form (
2
). Zhang showed that Schrödinger cocycles with sufficiently large coupling constants could be studied by defining a new cocycle which has a similar form of (
2
). The difference from (
2
) to Zhang’s setting is that the matrix entries
\lambda
are no longer constants, but functions. However, this can be dangerous as the functions may cancel the expanding effects of the base dynamics. One of Zhang’s key observations was the function attached to the rotation matrix only needed two of the properties from Young’s list: monotonicity and derivative uniformly bounded away from zero. This led to Zhang’s result of positivity for potentials generated by the doubling map
[
43
]
.

Zhang’s method for proving positivity followed a five step process, which is summarized below.

(i)

polar decomposition,

(ii)

control the derivatives of inductively defined functions
\theta_{n}=\phi_{n}+\theta\circ T^{n}
from below,

(iii)

count discontinuities and points that achieve a
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
rotation,

(iv)

control the measure of pre-images of
\delta
-balls around achieved
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
points,

(v)

conclude positivity.

Briefly, step (i) has the purpose to move the Schrödinger cocycle to the form on (
2
). For step (ii), the functions
\phi_{n}
and
\theta\circ T^{n}
tracks the angle of the orbit of
\vec{e}_{1}
after the action of the hyperbolic matrix and rotation, respectively. For concrete definitions of
\phi_{n}
and
\theta\circ T^{n}
we refer to section 2. Next, one counts the
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
rotations, as this is where the expanding effects of the hyperbolic matrix can be canceled out. In (iv), one ensures that even though
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
points arise, the amount of points close by is minimal to ensure expansion. Finally, positivity can be concluded using an argument similar to Young’s method.

This method was proven to be flexible, as the method was used to show positivity for any hyperbolic
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
matrix acting on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
; the adjustment from the
\mathbb{T}
to
\mathbb{T}^{2}
was to replace the uniform lower bound of the derivative with a uniform lower bound on the directional derivative in the unstable direction.

Our paper further develops this method by providing a checklist of conditions to attain a suitable counterpart to (ii). We briefly move away from our original setup to prove a more general result which may be of independent interest. Instead, we let
\Omega
be an ordered set with a self map
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
. There are two key ingredients: monotone potential and
T
satisfying a hyperbolic injective condition. The first is clear, but we wish to emphasize that the potential does not need to be differentiable anywhere. The latter condition is as follows: there exists a sequence of partitions
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
such that for every
n>m
, then
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. This means for every
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, there exists an
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
such that
S\subset S^{\prime}
. Moreover, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
T^{n}|_{S}
injects into
\Omega
for all
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. When this condition is satisfied we say the map
T
satisfies the
hyperbolic injective condition
. We are now ready to state our key lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Omega
be a space with order. Let
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
satisfy the hyperbolic injective condition. If
v:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is monotone and bounded, then there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(v)>0
such that
\theta_{n}
is monotone on every
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
there exists a constant
\eta=\eta(v,K)>0
such that for all
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in S
, one has the inequality

(3)

|\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime},t)-\theta_{n}(\omega,t)|\geq\eta|v(T^{n}\omega^{\prime})-v(T^{n}\omega)|,

for all
t\in K
.

The proof of Lemma
1
requires us to engage in precise estimates on the inductively defined functions. The overarching idea to show monotonicity comes from the geometry of the graph of
\theta_{n}
, particularly the interaction between the sum of
\phi_{n}
and
\theta\circ T^{n}
. Geometrically, for each
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, there is small subset
U\subset S
where
\phi_{n}
is expanding and monotone. On
S\setminus U
,
\phi_{n}
is a contraction and is not necessarily monotone. On the other hand,
\theta\circ T^{n}|_{S}
is monotone increasing on all of
S
. Thus,
\theta_{n}|_{U}
is dominated by
\phi_{n}
and, due to high expansion, controls the monotonicity. On
S\setminus U
, the high contraction of
\phi_{n}
implies that
\phi_{n}
contributes little to the sum, leading
\theta\circ T^{n}
to dominate and monotonicity of
\theta_{n}
on all of
S
.

Even though the geometry is clear, the analysis becomes much more complicated, particularly for the latter half of the lemma. Our functions need not be differentiable, but the estimates from the Mean Value Theorem hold because we obtain a uniform bound to replace the derivative of the function. The essential idea is to extract the uniform bound from
v
having bounded image in
\mathbb{R}
.

Returning to our original setup, it is evident that Lemma
1
provides a sufficient replacement to (ii), but it is not clear how monotonicity and the hyperbolic injective property imply positivity. The practical application of Lemma
1
is when we impose further conditions on
v
, such as a anti Lipschitz condition. A function
v
is anti Lipschitz if there exists a constant
L>0
such that for any
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega
,

|v(\omega)-v(\tilde{\omega})|\geq Ld(\omega,\tilde{\omega})

where
d
is some metric on
\Omega
. In particular, Jitomirskaya and Kachkovskiy studied these potentials and were able to prove localization in the quasi-periodic setting, see
[
29
,
30
]
.

This class of anti Lipschitz potentials does not contain all the possible potentials that can be utilized. Thus, we introduce the class of anti
\alpha
-Hölder potentials. That is,
v:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is anti
\alpha
-Hölder on
\Omega
, or globally anti
\alpha
-Hölder, if for some
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
there exists an
H>0
such that for any
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega
,

(4)

|v(\omega)-v(\tilde{\omega})|\geq Hd(\omega,\tilde{\omega})^{\alpha}.

It is apparent that the new class of potentials contains all anti Lipschitz functions. We note that for some base dynamics
(\Omega,T)
, there may exist an
\alpha(\Omega)>0
such that the set of anti-Hölder functions are empty for all
(0,\alpha]
. Thus, our result automatically holds when considering such
\alpha
. For examples of potentials in the class of anti
\alpha
-Holder, we refer to Application
2
.

Provided a potential in the class of anti
\alpha
-Hölder and depending on the base dynamics, we are able to emulate Zhang’s method to achieve positive Lyapunov exponents. To express the significance of Lemma
1
we explore two applications: subshifts of finite type and
d
-dimensional tori with a map with at least one expanding direction.

For the following application sections, we leave out explicit definitions to introduce the main goals of each section. The definitions will be provided in the corresponding section where the proofs lie; for Application
1
and
2
, see Sections
4.1
and
5
, respectively.

1.1.
Application 1: Subshift of finite type

For this section, let
\Omega
represent a subshift of finite type and
T
represent the left shift map. Let
\mu
be a
T
-invariant probability measure with bounded distortion property and fully supported on
\Omega
. For each
\omega\in\Omega
, define a local unstable leaf
W^{u}_{loc}(\omega)
, which is the set of elements that agree with
\omega
on components
n\leq 0
. For the subshifts in the half-line setting we write
\Omega^{+},T_{+},\mu^{+}
with the same assumptions. For concrete definitions we refer to Section
4.1
.

For our purposes we weaken the assumption of (
4
) to uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder along local unstable sets. That is, for some
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
there exists a uniform constant
H>0
such that for any
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
then for any
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})

|v(\omega)-v(\tilde{\omega})|\geq Hd(\omega,\tilde{\omega})^{\alpha}.

It is apparent that (
4
) implies the above. The converse direction is false in general, as one may not be able to compare elements in different local unstable sets, see Example
6
. This leads us to our main theorem in this section.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the full-line subshift of finite type
(\Omega,T,\mu)
as above. Let
v
be uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, bounded, and monotone with respect to dictionary order all on
W^{u}_{loc}(\omega)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
. Consider the family of Schrödinger operators as in (
1
). Then there exists a
C_{0}=C_{0}(v)>0
and
\kappa=\kappa(\mu)\geq 1
such that for any
\lambda>0
,

L(E;\lambda)>\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-C_{0}\text{ for all }E\in\mathbb{R}.

Before following the framework of (i) - (v), we must reduce the full-line to the half-line. A direct application of Rohklin’s disintegration theorem leads to the half-line case. Hence it suffices to prove Theorem
2
to prove Theorem
1
. See Section
4.3
for details.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the half-line subshift of finite type
(\Omega^{+},T_{+},\mu^{+})
as above. Let
v
be anti
\alpha
-Hölder, continuous, bounded, and monotone with respect to dictionary order on
\Omega^{+}
. Consider the family of Schrödinger operators for the half-line. Then there exists a
C_{1}=C_{1}(v)>0
such that for any
\lambda>0
,

L(E;\lambda)>\log\lambda-C_{1}\text{ for all }E\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that there is a
\kappa^{-1}
constant difference between the theorems. The constant arises as one cannot directly apply Theorem
2
on the disintegrated measure. Indeed, the reduction may lead to measures that lack
T_{+}
-invariance. By paying the price of a constant, one can pass to a
T_{+}
-invariant measure to apply Theorem
2
.

For the half-line, steps (i), (iii), and (v) all follow by the arguments in
[
43
]
. Steps (ii) and (iv) contain essential difficulties. For (ii), there is no differentiable structure present for subshifts of finite type; the estimate in Lemma
1
is utilized as the replacement of (ii). On the other hand, (iv) has two complications: relating
\delta
-balls around
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
points to measurable sets and estimating the measure of the sum of all such measurable sets. The first is primarily handled by the estimate from Lemma
1
. The second is resolved through the bounded distortion property of
\mu
.

We conclude by remarking some well-known dynamical systems that are encapsulated in the result:

(1)

Any subshift of finite type with a measure of maximal entropy.

(2)

Bernoulli-shift on
n
-letters for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
with Bernoulli measure.

(3)

Topological Markov chains with Markov measure.

1.2.
Application 2: One expanding direction on d-dimensional tori

We turn our attention to a more concrete class of systems. It is well known that hyperbolic dynamics can be encoded into shift space, but it is not true that the subshift with dictionary order respects the order of the original dynamics encoded. For example, Arnold’s cat map and local unstable leaves, as defined in
[
11
]
, have a significant ordering difference compared to the encoded local unstable sets. On the other hand, some systems do preserve order, most notably the doubling map on
\mathbb{T}
. Even with order preserved, the set of anti
\alpha
-Hölder potentials on the encoded space can drastically differ from the set on the original space. Lemma
1
enables us to prove positivity without considering the encoded space. In particular, we are able to prove positivity for maps with only one expanding direction!

For any positive integer
d\geq 1
, define the set
G(d,\mathbb{Z})
to be the collection of integer valued
d\times d
matrices with nonzero determinant and at least one real-eigenvalue
|\beta|\geq 2
. For notation, let
u
represent the associated eigenvector to the eigenvalue
\beta
. Given a matrix
M\in G(d,\mathbb{Z})
we let
T_{M}
represent the induced map on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and when the context is clear we write
T
. We remark that Lebesgue measure is
T
-invariant, since
M
has nonzero determinant. Similarly to the previous section we consider potentials that are uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder along local unstable leaves, but we only consider
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
. Briefly, local unstable leaves are line segments contained in the fundamental domain pointed in the direction
u
, see Section
5
for concrete details.

The main theorem of the section is the following.

Theorem 3
.

Consider any positive integer
d\geq 1
and
M\in G(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Consider the measurable system
(\mathbb{T}^{d},T_{M},Leb)
and a potential
v
that is uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder, continuous, bounded, and monotone all along local unstable leaves. For the family of Schrödinger operators defined as in (
1
), there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(v)>0
such that for all
\lambda>0

L(E;\lambda)>\log\lambda-C_{0}

for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
.

To prove Theorem
3
, consider a positive integer
d\geq 2
. The first step is to reduce the problem to
1
-dimension, as steps (i) - (v) necessitate
1
-dimension. Analogous to Arnold’s cat map, we will partition
\mathbb{T}^{d}
into union of line segments. The generalization is to find a set
Z
of dimension
d-1
, contained in the fundamental domain
F
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, so that every
z\in Z
corresponds to a unique line segment
W_{z}
. By Rohklin’s disintegration theorem the problem is reduced to proving positivity on
W_{z}
. With this reduction, the argument on
W_{z}
is the same that one would run for
d=1
. We then apply (i) with a minor difference by considering the convergence with respect to
C^{0}
-topology instead of
C^{1}
. After, we partition
W_{z}
appropriately to apply Lemma
1
to replace (ii). The remaining steps (iii) - (v) follow a similar argument as
[
43
]
. Consequently, Theorem
3
extends results from
[
43
,
11
]
, which we formulate in two corollaries.

Corollary 1
.

Consider the expanding map
(\mathbb{T},x\mapsto kx\mod 1,Leb)
with integer
k\geq 2
. Let
v
be a potential that is anti
\alpha
-Hölder, continuous, bounded, and monotone. For the family of Schrödinger operators defined as in (
1
), replacing
\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(v)>0
such that for all
\lambda>0

L(E;\lambda)>\log\lambda-C_{0}

for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
.

Corollary 2
.

Consider any hyperbolic
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and consider
(\mathbb{T}^{2},T_{M},Leb)
. Let
v
be a potential that is uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder, continuous, bounded, and monotone all along the local unstable leaves. For the family of Schrödinger operators (
1
), there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(v)>0
such that for all
\lambda>0

L(E;\lambda)>\log\lambda-C_{0}

for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
.

The corollaries contain the family of
C^{1}
potentials with derivative bounded away from zero, since such potentials are anti
1
-Hölder. Indeed, for any positive integer
d\geq 1
consider any potential
v
that is
C^{1}
on the fundamental domain of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and is monotone in direction
u
with directional derivative bounded away from zero. That is,
|D_{u}v|>c
for some constant
c
. In either case,
D_{u}v>c
or
D_{u}v<-c
, from the Mean Value Theorem, we obtain

|v(\omega)-v(\tilde{\omega})|\geq c||\omega-\tilde{\omega}||

where
||\cdot||
represents
d
-dimensional Euclidean metric.

On the other hand, recall that the essential assumptions were monotonicity and derivative uniformly bounded away from zero. Our corollaries allows us to relax the latter condition to state derivative positive or negative. One benefit is that our potentials are allowed to admit critical points, so long as we have monotonicity and can verify the anti
\alpha
-Hölder condition. In practice, verifying the anti
\alpha
-Hölder condition can be complicated unless additional assumptions are imposed. This leads to a natural first set of examples which are a class of
C^{r}
potentials.

Before being able to show that a class of
C^{r}
functions are anti
\alpha
-Hölder, we need a preliminary example. In particular, the example shows that piecewise anti Hölder functions, with each piece having possibly differing
\alpha
constants, are globally anti
\alpha
-Hölder. Moreover, if
v
is anti
\alpha
-Hölder, then
v
is anti
\beta
-Hölder where
\beta\in[\alpha,\infty)
. For simplicity, all the examples provided assume monotone increasing, but this is not necessary as there is an analogous adjustment of assumptions for monotone decreasing.

Example 1

(Piecewise anti Hölder functions are globally anti Hölder.)
.

Let
v:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
continuous, monotone increasing, and bounded. Let
a\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[1,\infty)
. Assume that
v|_{[0,a]}
is anti
\alpha_{1}
-Hölder with constant
H_{1}>0
and
v|_{[a,1)}
is anti
\alpha_{2}
-Hölder with constant
H_{2}>0
. Then
v
is anti
\alpha
-Hölder with constant
H=2^{1-\alpha}\cdot\min\{H_{1},H_{2}\}
where
\alpha=\max\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
.

Note that if
x,y\in[0,a)
or
x,y\in[a,1)
then there is nothing to show, since anti
\alpha_{1}
-Hölder and anti
\alpha_{2}
-Hölder imply
\alpha
-Hölder. It remains to consider
y\in[0,a)
and
x\in[a,1)
. Observe,

\displaystyle v(x)-v(y)

\displaystyle=v(x)-v(a)+v(a)-v(y)

\displaystyle\geq H_{1}|x-a|^{\alpha_{2}}+H_{2}|a-y|^{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq\min\{H_{1},H_{2}\}\cdot\Big(|x-a|^{\alpha}+|a-y|^{\alpha}\Big)

Notice that the function
x\mapsto x^{\alpha}
is convex, so by convexity with
t=\frac{1}{2}
and points
(x-a)
and
(a-y),
, we find:

2^{1-\alpha}|x-y|^{\alpha}\ \leq\Big(|x-a|^{\alpha}+|a-y|^{\alpha}\Big)

It follows that

|v(x)-v(y)|\geq H|x-y|^{\alpha}.

Thus,
v
is anti
\alpha
-Hölder.

Example
1
can be generalized to hold for any finite number of pieces. In particular, if
v:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following: there exists
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}=1
and
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}\in[1,\infty)
such that
v|_{[x_{j},x_{j+1}]}
is anti
\alpha_{j}
-Hölder for each
j=0,\dots,n-1
. Then
v
is
\alpha
-Hölder where
\alpha=\max_{j}\alpha_{j}
, which can be shown by using an inductive argument with the method as in Example
1
. Notably, this provides a construction to create new anti
\alpha
-Hölder functions from a finite number of anti
\alpha
-Hölder functions. This idea is utilized to show that a class of
C^{r}
potentials are anti
\alpha
-Hölder.

Example 2

(
C^{r}
potentials)
.

Let
v\in C^{r}([0,1))
for
r\geq 1
, monotone increasing, bounded, and exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
has derivatives vanishing up to orders
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in 2\mathbb{N}
, respectively. Moreover, we assume for each
j=1,\dots,k
, then
v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})>0
.

We claim that for each
x_{j}
, there exists a
\delta_{j}>0
such that
v|_{\overline{B(x_{j},\delta_{j})}}
is anti
r
-Hölder. Letting
M_{j}=\max_{k}|v^{(k)}(x_{j})|
, then it is apparent that

M_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r-n_{j}-1}\Big|\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\Big|\rightarrow 0

when
x\rightarrow 0
. Thus, we may pick
\delta_{j}>0
so that

\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}-M_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r-n_{j}-1}\Big|\frac{(y-x)^{k}}{k!}\Big|>\frac{c}{2(n_{j}+1)!},

for any
y\in\overline{B(x_{j},\delta_{j})}
. By a direct application of Taylor’s theorem and, if necessary, shrinking
\delta_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle|v(y)-v(x_{j})|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}(y-x_{j})^{k}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}(y-x_{j})^{n_{j}+1}+\sum_{k=n_{j}+2}^{r}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}(y-x_{j})^{k}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=|(y-x_{j})|^{n_{j}+1}\Bigg|\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}+\sum_{k=n_{j}+2}^{r}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}(y-x_{j})^{k}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\geq|y-x_{j}|^{n_{j}+1}\Bigg|\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}-M_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r-n_{j}-1}\Big|\frac{(y-x_{j})^{k}}{k!}\Big|\Bigg|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{2(n_{j}+1)!}|y-x_{j}|^{n_{j}+1}>0,

for any
y\in\overline{B(x_{j},\delta_{j})}
. For simplicity, let
x,y\in\overline{B(x_{j},\delta_{j})}
and
y>x
. Using Taylor’s theorem and convexity of
x\mapsto x^{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle v(y)-v(x)

\displaystyle=v(y)-v(x_{j})+v(x_{j})-v(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}\Bigg((y-x_{j})^{r}-(x-x_{j})^{r}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=n_{j}+2}^{r}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}\Bigg((y-x_{j})^{k}-(x-x_{j})^{k}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-n_{j}}\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}(y-x)^{n_{j}+1}+\sum_{k=n_{j}+2}^{r}2^{1-k}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}(y-x)^{k}

\displaystyle=2^{-n_{j}}(y-x)^{n_{j}+1}\Bigg(\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}+\sum_{k=n_{j}+2}^{r}2^{1-k+n_{j}}\frac{v^{(k)}(x_{j})}{k!}(y-x)^{k}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-n_{j}}(y-x)^{n_{j}+1}\Bigg(\frac{v^{(n_{j}+1)}(x_{j})}{(n_{j}+1)!}-M_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r-n_{j}-1}\Big|\frac{(y-x_{j})^{k}}{k!}\Big|\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{2^{n_{j}}(n_{j}+1)!}(y-x)^{n_{j}+1}

The preceding calculation implies that
v|_{\overline{B(x_{j},\delta_{j})}}
is anti
(n_{j}+1)
-Holder with constant
\frac{c}{2^{n_{j}}(n_{j}+1)!}
.

On the other hand, without loss of generality, we may order

x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{k}.

Define
I_{0}=[0,x_{1}-\delta]
,
I_{i}=[x_{i}+\delta,x_{i+1}-\delta]
for
i=1,\dots,k-1
, and
I_{k}=[x_{k}+\delta,1]
. Monotone increasing implies
v^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,1)
. As a consequence, if
x\in I_{j}
for some
j=1,\dots,k
, then
v^{\prime}|_{I_{j}}(x)>c_{j}>0
for some constant
c_{j}
. Similarly, this leads to
v|_{I_{j}}
to be anti
1
-Holder with constant
c_{j}
. Thus, the potential is piecewise anti Holder and by Example
1
, one can conclude that
v
is anti
r
-Hölder.

Unfortunately, the orders of the derivatives that vanish must be even numbers, unless on the boundary. If not, then the function would locally behave like
x^{2}
around the vanishing points, which destroys monotonicity. Indeed, assume that there existed a
v\in C^{2}([0,1))
and a point
x_{0}
not on the boundary such that
v^{(1)}(x_{0})=0
and
v^{(2)}(x_{0})>0
. Consider
y_{0}=x_{0}-\delta
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, which exists since
x_{0}
on not on the boundary. Then by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus we have

-v^{(1)}(y_{0})=\int_{y_{0}}^{x_{0}}v^{(2)}(x)dx>c\delta,

which directly implies that
v^{(1)}(y)<0
. Thus, leading to a contradiction to the assumption that
v
is monotone increasing.

Example 3

(Analytic potentials.)
.

Let
v\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
be strictly increasing, which can be lifted to a real analytic function and then restricted to
[0,1)
, so we denote this as
\tilde{v}\in C^{\omega}([0,1),\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
\tilde{v}
has finitely many critical points and each critical points has finite order. If each of the critical points vanish up to an even order, then with a direct application of the argument in Example
2
one can conclude
\tilde{v}
is anti
\alpha
-Hölder for some
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
.

The examples can be further generalized to higher dimension, as one can replace the derivative with a directional derivative in the unstable direction. To conclude the section, we discuss four more examples of potentials falling under Theorem
3
. The first two are
C^{1}
functions that can be defined in any dimension. The third is uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder along local unstable leaves, but not anti
\alpha
-Hölder. The final example shows that anti
\alpha
-Hölder is a larger class of potentials than anti Lipschitz and provides a simple example of potentials with critical points.

Example 4
.

Consider any positive integer
d\geq 1
,
M\in G(d,\mathbb{Z})
with unstable unit eigenvector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}>0
. Define

\displaystyle v:[0,1)^{d}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle\mapsto\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}x_{i}\Big).

The directional derivative of
v
in direction
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded below by
\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}
. The local unstable leaves would be the the image of the lines
f_{z}(t)=tv+z
restricted to
[0,1)^{d}
where
z\in[0,1)^{d}
, more compactly Image
(f_{z})
. Consider any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x,y\in
Image
(f_{z})
. By the Mean Value Theorem it follows that

|v(x)-v(y)|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}||x-y||.

Hence,
v
is uniformly anti
1
-Hölder along local unstable leaves.

Example 5
.

Consider any positive integer
d\geq 1
,
M\in G(d,\mathbb{Z})
with unstable unit eigenvector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}>0
. Define

\displaystyle v:[0,1)^{d}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto\log(1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})

The directional derivative of
v
in direction
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded below by

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}}.

Similarly to Example
4
, the local unstable leaves would be the the graph of the lines
f_{z}(t)=tv+z
restricted to
[0,1)^{d}
where
z\in[0,1)^{d}
. Consider any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x,y\in
Image
(f_{z})
. By the Mean Value Theorem

|v(x)-v(y)|\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}v_{i}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}}||x-y||.

Hence,
v
is uniformly anti
1
-Hölder along local unstable leaves.

Example 6

(Uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder only on local unstable leaves)
.

For simplicity, consider the matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

acting on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. The local unstable leaves are the graphs of the function
y=z
for a constant
z\in[0,1)
. Define the potential

\displaystyle v:[0,1)^{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(y-\frac{1}{2})^{2}+x.

Now consider any
(x,y),(\tilde{x},y)\in[0,1)^{2}
on the same local unstable leaf. It is immediately clear that

|v(x,y)-v(\tilde{x},y)|=|x-y|=d(x,y).

On the other hand, consider
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4})
and
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4})
, then it follows

\Bigg|v\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4}\Big)-v\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}\Big)\Bigg|=0<\Bigg|\Bigg|\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4}\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}\Big)\Bigg|\Bigg|.

Implying that
v
does not satisfy (
4
).

Example 7

(Anti
\alpha
-Hölder, but not anti Lipschitz)
.

Consider any any positive integer
n\geq 1
and define

\displaystyle v:[0,1)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto x^{n}.

Note that
v
is continuous, bounded, and monotone increasing. To see that
v
is not anti Lipschitz, assume for a contradiction that there exists a constant
L>0
that satisfies the anti Lipschitz condition. The condition implies
L\leq\frac{1}{j^{n-1}}
for all any positive integers
j\geq 1
, which is a contradiction. On the other hand,
v
is anti
n
-Hölder, since for
a,b\in(0,1)
,

a^{n}+b^{n}\leq(a+b)^{n}.

Let
x,y\in(0,1)
where
x>y
. Replacing
a=x-y
and
b=y
, it follows that

(x-y)^{n}\leq x^{n}-y^{n}.

Hence,
v
is anti
n
-Hölder.

1

2.
Preliminaries

Recall we consider a compact metric space
(\Omega,T,\mu)
with a continuous and invertible map
T
and a measure
\mu
that is
T
-ergodic. We are interested in studying Schrödinger operators acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, as in (
1
). In the case that
T
is not invertible, we replace
\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathbb{N}
and impose Dirichlet boundary condition
\psi_{-1}=0
.

We are interested in the behavior of
H_{\lambda,v,\omega}\psi=E\psi
for
E\in\mathbb{R}
. For each energy
E\in\mathbb{R}
, define an associated Schrödinger cocycle
(T,A^{(E-\lambda v)})
. The cocycle map
A^{(E-\lambda v)}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is defined as

(5)

A^{(E-\lambda v)}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}E-\lambda v(\omega)&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The iterates of the cocycle map are written as

A^{(E-\lambda v)}_{n}(\omega)=\begin{cases}A^{(E-\lambda v)}(T^{n-1}\omega)\cdots A^{(E-\lambda v)}(\omega)&n>0\\
I_{2}&n=0\\
A^{(E-\lambda v)}(T^{n}\omega)^{-1}\cdots A^{(E-\lambda v)}(T^{-1}\omega)^{-1}&n<0\end{cases}

and have a direct relation to solutions of the eigenvalue equation, since
\psi
is a solution, not necessarily in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, if and only if

\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{n}\\
\psi_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}=A^{(E-\lambda v)}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}\\
\psi_{-1}\end{pmatrix}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The Lyapunov exponent is defined as

(6)

\displaystyle L(E;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\log||A^{(E-\lambda v)}_{n}(\omega)||d\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\log||A^{(E-\lambda v)}_{n}(\omega)||

\displaystyle\geq 0

where the existence of the limit follows by subadditivity. Moreover, if
\mu
is ergodic, then by Kingman’s Subadditive Ergodic Theorem on each
E\in\mathbb{R}
to obtain

L(E;\lambda)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log||A^{(E-\lambda v)}_{n}(\omega)||\text{ for a.e }\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

For more information on why the Lyapunov exponent is interesting, general theory, and specific classes on Schrödinger operators, we highly recommend, respectively
[
39
,
14
,
17
]
.

Throughout the remainder of the paper let
0<c<1<C
represent universal constants that are small and large respectively. These constants only depend on the bounded, continuous, monotone, and anti
\alpha
-Hölder potential
v:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Without loss of generality, we will always assume that
0\leq v(\omega)\leq 1
for all
\omega\in\Omega
except for the proof of Lemma
1
.

Next, we briefly discuss polar decomposition, as it is an essential starting point, see
[
43
]
for a more detailed account. Let
t=\frac{E}{\lambda}
and
t\in\mathcal{I}:=[-1,2]
. Define functions
r,g:\Omega\times\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle r(\omega,t)

\displaystyle=t-v(\omega),

\displaystyle g(\omega,t)

\displaystyle=r^{2}(\omega)+1.

Since
v
is bounded we have

c<g(\omega,t)<C\text{ for all }\Omega\times\mathcal{I}.

We define a new matrix

(7)

A(\omega,t,\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\sqrt{g(T\omega,t)}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{1}{g(T\omega,t)}}\end{pmatrix}R_{\theta(\omega,t)}

where we let
\tilde{\Lambda}(T\omega)
represent the hyperbolic matrix and

R_{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma\\
\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

We define inductively defined functions from
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that follow the orbit of
\vec{e}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
under the projective action of the the cocycle
A
. Define

\theta(\omega,t)=\cot^{-1}(t-v(\omega))

and let
\theta_{0}=\theta(\omega,t)
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\phi_{n}(\omega,t)=\cot^{-1}(\lambda^{2}g(T^{n}\omega)\cot\theta_{n-1})\text{ and }\theta_{n}(\omega,t)=\phi_{n}(\omega,t)+\theta(T^{n}\omega).

Lastly, we denote the Lyapunov exponent for the cocycle
(T,A(\cdot,t,\lambda))
as
\tilde{L}(t;\lambda)
.

The relationship between the cocycles
A
and
A^{(E-\lambda v)}
is not immediately clear. In the construction of
A
, we found that
A^{(E-\lambda v)}
is a
\log
-integrable
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
conjugacy away from a matrix
\Lambda\cdot O
where
\Lambda
represents a diagonal matrix and
O
is a rotation matrix both depending on
\lambda
. As
\lambda
tends to infinity, the conjugated matrix
\Lambda\cdot O
approaches
A\circ T^{-1}
in the
C^{0}
-topology. By proving that
\tilde{L}(t;\lambda)
is positive, we may pass positivity and the lower estimates to the cocycle
(T,\Lambda\cdot O)
by
T
-invariance and the stability of our argument under
C^{0}
perturbations. Moreover, the Lyapunov exponent is preserved under
\log
-integrable conjugation, so positivity and lower estimates for
(T,\Lambda\cdot O)
imply that the same holds for
(T,A^{(E-\lambda v)})
. In the non-invertible case, one instead shows
A^{(E-\lambda v)}\circ T
is a conjugacy away from
(\Lambda\circ T)\cdot O
and the rest follows an analogous argument. For additional details on polar decomposition we refer to
[
42
]
and
[
43
, Appendix A]
.

3.
Proof of Key Lemma

In this section, we prove Lemma
1
, which will be an essential tool for us to show positivity. The argument is stable in
C^{0}
-perturbations, since the only dependence is on the shape of
\theta
and
g
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Consider any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
, since
K
must be closed and bounded there must exist some connected and compact interval containing
K
. Thus, without loss of generality, assume that
K=[-m_{1},m_{2}]
for some
0\leq m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Define

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\},

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=M+||v||_{\infty},

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(1+G^{2})},

and

\lambda_{0}=\eta^{-1}(5||v||_{\infty}+2M).

Fix any
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. All estimates provided will be independent of
\lambda
and
t\in K
.

Let
n=0
and
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
such that
\omega<\omega^{\prime}
. By monotonicity of
v
and
\cot^{-1}
being a decreasing function, we may conclude

\theta_{0}(\omega)=\cot^{-1}(t-v(\omega))<\cot^{-1}(t-v(\omega^{\prime}))=\theta_{0}(\omega^{\prime}).

Thus,
\theta_{0}
is monotone on
\Omega
.

To show (
3
), consider the function
\cot^{-1}:[-G,G]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. By the Mean Value Theorem, for any
x,y\in[-G,G]
with
x<y
, there is a
z\in(x,y)\subset[-G,G]
such that

(8)

\cot^{-1}(x)-\cot^{-1}(y)=\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}(y-x)\geq\eta(y-x).

Remark that
t-v(\omega)\in[-G,G]
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Thus, we may replace
x,y
with
t-v(\omega^{\prime}),t-v(\omega)
, so

\theta_{0}(\omega^{\prime})-\theta_{0}(\omega)=\cot^{-1}(t-v(\omega^{\prime}))-\cot^{-1}(t-v(\omega))\geq\eta(v(\omega^{\prime})-v(\omega)).

For induction, assume for some positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
that
\theta_{n}
is monotone on any
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and satisfies (
3
). Consider any
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
and let
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in S
such that
\omega<\omega^{\prime}
. By definition of
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
being a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, there exists some
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that
S\subset S^{\prime}
. By inductive hypothesis,
\theta_{n}
must be monotone on
S^{\prime}
and hence
S
too. Monotonicity of
v
,
T^{n+1}|_{S}
injective, and
\cot^{-1}
decreasing, imply that
\theta\circ T^{n+1}|_{S}
is monotone.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})\in\Big(\alpha\pi,(\alpha+1)\pi\Big)
, then
\phi_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})\in\Big(\alpha\pi,(\alpha+1)\pi\Big)
. Similarly, if
\theta_{n}(\omega)\in\Big(k\pi,(k+1)\pi\Big)
for some
k<\alpha
, then
\phi_{n+1}(\omega)<\alpha\pi
. The preceding line implies
\phi_{n+1}(\omega)<\phi_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})
and hence
\theta_{n+1}(\omega)<\theta_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})
. The estimate follows by (
8
).

\displaystyle\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})-\theta_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{0}(T^{n}\omega^{\prime})-\theta_{0}(T^{n}\omega)

\displaystyle\geq\eta(v(T^{n}\omega^{\prime})-v(T^{n}\omega))

It remains to handle when

(9)

\theta_{n}(\omega),\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})\in\Big(\alpha\pi,(\alpha+1)\pi\Big).

If
\phi_{n+1}(\omega)<\phi_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})
, then the argument above completes this case. Thus, we assume

(10)

\phi_{n+1}(\omega)>\phi_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime}).

Observe that (
9
) implies
\cot\theta_{n+1}(\omega)
and
\cot\theta_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})
lie in the same
\pi\mathbb{Z}
interval. The function
\cot^{-1}
is Lipschitz, so

0<|\phi_{n+1}(\omega)-\phi_{n+1}(\omega^{\prime})|\leq\lambda^{2}g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})\cot\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})-\lambda^{2}g(T^{n+1}\omega)\cot\theta_{n}(\omega)

By injectivity, we have
\theta(T^{n+1}S)\subset\theta(\Omega)
and by (
8
) we obtain

\theta(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})-\theta(T^{n+1}\omega)\geq 2\eta\Big(v(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})-v(T^{n+1}\omega)\Big)

for all
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in S
.

On the other hand, assumption (
10
) implies

(11)

g(T^{n+1}\omega)\cot\theta_{n}(\omega)<g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})\cot\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}).

Consider two cases:

(1)

g(T^{n+1}\omega)<g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})

(2)

g(T^{n+1}\omega)>g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})

These cases determine the sign of
\cot\theta_{n}(\omega)
and
\cot\theta_{n}(\omega)
. In case 1,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))-g(T^{n+1}\omega)\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})}_{\geq 1}\Big(\underbrace{\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))-\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))}_{<0}\Big)+\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))\Big(\underbrace{g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})-g(T^{n+1}\omega)}_{>0}\Big)

which implies
\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))>0
and
\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))>0
. In case 2,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>g(T^{n+1}\omega)\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))-g(\omega^{\prime})\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{g(T^{n+1}\omega)}_{\geq 1}\Big(\underbrace{\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))-\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))}_{>0}\Big)+\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime}))\Big(\underbrace{g(T^{n+1}\omega)-g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})}_{>0}\Big)

which implies that
\cot\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})<0
and
\cot(\theta_{n}(\omega))>0
.

Consider the following subcases of the first case:

(a)

g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})\cot\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}

(b)

0<g(T^{n+1}\omega^{\prime})\cot\theta_{n}(\omega^{\prime})<\frac{1}{\lambda}

Subcase a:
Define the function

\displaystyle f(x):(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\cot^{-1}(x)-\frac{1}{x}

and notice that
f
is an increasing function. If
x<y
, one has

(12)

\cot^{-1}(x)-\cot^{-1}(y)<\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{y}.

Replacing the
x,y
in (

) with

4.
Subshifts of Finite Type

4.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{A}:=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
for some any positive integer
\ell\geq 2
. The full shift space is
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and equipped with the left shift map
T
:

[T\omega]_{n}=\omega_{n+1}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We let
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
have a topology generated by the metric

d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\begin{cases}e^{-N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}&\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}\\
0&\omega=\omega^{\prime}\end{cases}

where
N:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
defined as

(14)

N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\omega_{j}=\omega^{\prime}_{j}\text{ for all }|j|<n\}.

Cylinder sets are defined as

[n;i_{0}\dots i_{k-1}]:=\{\omega:\omega_{n+l}=i_{l}\text{ for all }0\leq l\leq k-1\}

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
i_{0},\dots,i_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
. We say cylinder sets of the form above are of length
k
. If
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
we may write
\underline{\omega}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
to represent the first
k
-coordinates, that is,
\underline{\omega}_{n}
is equal to
\omega_{0}\dots\omega_{k-1}
. In summary, cylinder sets beginning at position
n
with a length
k
word can be written as
[n;\underline{i}_{k}]
.

Consider
\Omega\subset\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
a subshift of finite type. That is, let
\mathcal{F}
be a set of finitely many words of length
n
that are forbidden, then
\Omega
is the set of words that do not contain any forbidden words. Precisely,

\Omega:=\{\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{Z},\omega_{j}\dots\omega_{j_{n}-1}\notin\mathcal{F}\}.

Every subshift defined in this way can also be encoded as a topological Markov chain. A topological Markov chain is defined as follows: consider a transition matrix
A
of size
\ell\times\ell
where entries
a_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
for
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
. The associated topological Markov chain associated to
A
is

\Omega_{A}=\{\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}:a_{\omega_{n},\omega_{n+1}}=1\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

It is apparent that
\Omega_{A}
is a subshift of finite type with the family of banned words being
\mathcal{F}=\{ij\in\mathcal{A}^{2}:a_{i,j}=0\}
, which consist only of words of length 2. Without loss of generality, we will always assume that
\mathcal{F}
, corresponding to
\Omega
, contains words of length 2.

Next, define

\displaystyle\Omega^{+}

\displaystyle=\{(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 0}:\omega\in\Omega\},

\displaystyle\Omega^{-}

\displaystyle=\{(\omega_{n})_{n\leq 0}:\omega\in\Omega\},

which represent the set of positive and negative sequences and can be equipped with shift maps
T_{\pm}
. Denote cylinder sets in
\Omega^{\pm}
as
[n;\underline{i}]^{\pm}
, respectively. Let
\pi^{-}
and
\pi^{+}
denote the canonical projections onto
\Omega^{-}
and
\Omega^{+}
, respectively. Also, for each
j\in\mathcal{A}
we define
\Omega_{j}=\Omega\cap[0;j]
and
\Omega^{\pm}_{j}=\Omega^{\pm}\cap[0;j]^{\pm}
.

For each
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
and
\omega^{+}\in\Omega^{+}
we define

\displaystyle W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})=\{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega:\omega^{\prime}_{j}=\omega_{j}^{-},\forall j\leq 0\},

\displaystyle W^{s}_{loc}(\omega^{+})=\{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega:\omega^{\prime}_{j}=\omega_{j}^{+},\forall j\geq 0\},

which are called local unstable and stable sets respectively. Throughout, we will work over the unstable sets, but one can work over stable sets with an appropriate adjustment. We impose dictionary order on
W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
, which means for
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
,

\omega<\omega^{\prime}\iff\omega_{N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}<\omega^{\prime}_{N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}.

Equality holds only when
\omega_{j}=\omega^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. We collect some results that will be useful in the following section.

Lemma 2
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

is monotone on cylinder sets
[0;\underline{i}]\subset W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
where
\underline{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
and
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
and consider any any positive integer

. Cylinders

are sets of the form

where
\omega_{0}=\omega^{-}_{0}
. Let
\omega^{\prime}\in[0;\underline{\omega}_{n}]
. Then note

and without loss of generality, assume that
\omega_{N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}<\omega^{\prime}_{N(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}

Lemma 3

A measure

or

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C\Big(\frac{\delta}{2^{i+1}}\Big)^{-\ln p}\log\Bigg(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big(\frac{\delta}{2^{i+1}}\Big)^{-\ln p}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=-C\delta^{-\ln p}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\ln p}{2^{-(i+1)\ln p}}\log\Bigg(\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{\ln(p)}}\Big(\frac{\delta}{2^{i+1}}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=-C\delta^{-\ln p}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{i+1}{2^{-(i+1)\ln p}}\log\Bigg(\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{-\frac{i+1}{\ln(p)}}\Big(\frac{\delta^{\frac{1}{i+1}}}{2}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq-C\delta^{-\ln p}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{i}{(2^{-\ln p})^{i}}

\displaystyle\geq-C\delta^{-\ln p}.

Consider

J_{0}^{c}=\{\omega^{+}\in\Omega^{+}:||\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})-\frac{\pi}{2}||\geq\delta\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{J_{0}^{c}}\log|\cos\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})|dm(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{J_{0}^{c}}\log|\sin(\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})-\frac{\pi}{2})|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{J_{0}}\log\frac{2}{\pi}|\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})-\frac{\pi}{2}|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{J_{0}}\log(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big(\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}\Big)^{-\ln p})d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle>C\log\delta^{-\ln p}.

Pick
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{1}{m}<\delta^{-\ln p}<\Big(\min\{\frac{\pi}{8},\frac{H}{5^{\alpha}\cdot 40}\}\Big)^{-\ln p}
, which provides

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{+}}\log|\cos\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{J_{0}^{c}}\log|\cos\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\int_{J_{0}}\log|\cos\theta_{k}(\omega^{+})|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle>-\frac{1}{m}C-C\log m

\displaystyle>-C.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{\Omega^{+}}\log|\cos\theta_{j}(\omega^{+})|d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})>-C

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The preceding estimate implies

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega^{+}}\log||A_{n}(\omega^{+})\vec{e}_{1}||d\mu^{+}(\omega^{+})>\log\lambda-C_{0}=\log\lambda-C_{0},

which completes the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

4.3.
Full-Line

To attain a full-line result, we must first reduce the full-line to the half-line. We do so by thinking of the local unstable sets as a map on
\Omega^{-}
. That is,

W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})=\{\omega\in\Omega:\pi^{-}(\omega)=\omega^{-}\},

which provides the following partition

\Omega=\bigsqcup_{\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}}W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-}).

The partition leads to a disintegration of
\mu
along the local unstable sets where existence follows from Rohklin’s disintegration theorem. A disintegration along local unstable sets is a family of probability measures
\{\mu_{\omega^{-}}^{u}\}_{\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}}
where each is supported on
W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
and satisfies

\mu(D)=\int_{\Omega^{-}}\mu_{\omega^{-}}^{u}(D)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-})

for each measurable
D\subset\Omega
. The disintegration implies that given
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
one obtains the following equality:

\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)d\mu(\omega)=\int_{\Omega^{-}}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}f(\omega)d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-}).

In particular, if
f(\omega)=\frac{1}{n}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||
:

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||d\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{-}}\Bigg(\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\frac{1}{n}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)\Bigg)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-}).

For each
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
, define

\displaystyle I_{\omega^{-}}:\Omega^{+}_{\omega^{-}_{0}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})

\displaystyle\omega^{+}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})

which is an isomorphism. From the isomorphism it is apparent that the inner integral from (
26
) is essentially the half-line. At this point, one would like to directly apply the half-line setting, but it is not necessarily true that the disintegrated measures have
T_{+}
-invariance. Thus, the half-line theorem cannot be applied. To resolve the predicament, we show that the disintegrated measures
\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}
are equivalent to a
T_{+}
-invariant measure, in particular
(I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}
. Moreover, the Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym derivative between the two is precisely
\psi
from local product structure of
\mu
.

Lemma 11
.

For each
j\in\mathcal{A}
, if
\mu^{+}_{j}
has bounded distortion property, then
(I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}
has bounded distortion property for all
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}_{j}
.

Proof.

Use the same argument as Lemma
3
and replace
\pi^{+}
,
\mu^{+}
,
\mu
with
I_{\omega^{-}}
,
(I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}
,
\mu^{+}_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

For each
j\in\mathcal{A}
,

d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}=\psi d((I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j})

holds for
\mu^{-}_{j}
almost every
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}_{j}
and
\psi
is the same function from local product structure of
\mu
.

Proof.

Fix any
j\in\mathcal{A}
. Local product structure of
\mu
implies

\mu_{j}(E)=\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\int_{\Omega^{+}_{j}}\psi(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\chi_{E}d\mu^{+}_{j}(\omega^{+})d\mu^{-}_{j}(\omega^{-}),

for any measurable set
E\subset\Omega_{j}
. On the other hand, by Rohklin’s Disintegration Theorem

\mu_{j}(E)=\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-},j}(E)d\mu^{-}_{j}=\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\chi_{E}d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}d\mu^{-}_{j}.

Consider the measure
(I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}
, as in Lemma
11
. It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\int_{\Omega^{+}_{j}}

\displaystyle\psi(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\chi_{E}d\mu^{+}_{j}(\omega^{+})d\mu^{-}_{j}(\omega^{-})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\psi(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\chi_{E}d((I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j})(\omega^{+})d\mu^{-}_{j}(\omega^{-}).

Notice

\nu_{\omega^{-}}:E\mapsto\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\psi(\omega^{-},\cdot)\chi_{E}(\omega^{-},\cdot)d((I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j})(\omega^{+})

is a measure. The two preceding equations leads to

\mu_{j}(E)=\int_{\Omega^{-}_{j}}\nu_{\omega^{-}}(E)d\mu^{-}.

Thus,
\nu_{\omega^{-}}=\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}
for
\mu^{-}_{j}
almost every
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}_{j}
, by uniqueness of Rohklin’s Disintegration Theorem. Hence, the following equality holds

d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}=\psi d((I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}).

∎

Corollary 6
.

Let
v
be uniformly anti
\alpha
-Hölder, continuous, bounded, and monotone with respect to dictionary order all on
W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})
for all
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
. Then there exists a
C_{0}>0
and
\kappa=\kappa(\mu)\geq 1
such that for any
\lambda>0
and any
\omega^{-}\in\Omega^{-}
, we have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\log||A^{E}_{n}(\omega)||d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)>\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-C_{0}

for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By the isomorphism
I_{\omega^{-}}
, Lemma
4
, and Lemma
12
, one has

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\log||A^{E}_{n}(\omega)||d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\psi(\omega)\log||A^{E}_{n}(\omega)||d(I_{\omega^{-}})_{*}\mu^{+}_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq\kappa^{-1}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega^{+}_{\omega^{-}_{0}}}\log||A^{E}_{n}(I_{\omega^{-}}(\omega^{+}))||d\mu^{+}_{j}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle=\kappa^{-1}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega^{+}_{\omega^{-}_{0}}}\log||A^{E}_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})||d\mu^{+}_{j}(\omega^{+})

\displaystyle>\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-\kappa^{-1}C_{1}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-C_{0}

where
C_{0}=\kappa^{-1}C_{1}
.
∎

In Corollary
6
, we consider the
\liminf
because
\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}
does not have
T
-invariance, so the limit may not exist. With Corollary
6
, we can conclude Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Combining (
26
), Fatou Lemma, and Corollary
6
, one can conclude positivity as follows.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||d\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega^{-}}\frac{1}{n}\Bigg(\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)\Bigg)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega^{-}}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\Bigg(\int_{W^{u}_{loc}(\omega^{-})}\log||A_{n}(\omega)||d\mu^{u}_{\omega^{-}}(\omega)\Bigg)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-})

\displaystyle>\int_{\Omega^{-}}\Big(\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-C_{0}\Big)d\mu^{-}(\omega^{-})

\displaystyle=\kappa^{-1}\log\lambda-C_{0}

∎

5.
One Expanding Direction on Tori

In this section, we prove Theorem
3
. In particular, we show that tori of arbitrary integer dimension
d\geq 1
with a map with at least one expanding direction leads to the Schrödinger cocycle admitting positive Lyapunov exponents. We begin with the positive integers
d\geq 2
, as
d=1
will become a consequence of the argument. The first step is to reduce the dimension of the problem to dimension 1. To do so, we construct a set
Z
of dimension
d-1
such that every
z\in Z
admits a unique line segment
W_{z}
. The union of all such line segments is equal to the fundamental domain
F
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We provide a geometric picture for
d=3
.

Figure 1.
Geometric picture representing the local unstable leaves (red,
W_{z}
) and the plane parameterizing the leaves (blue,
Z
) for the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
acting on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Note that the choice of
Z
is not unique.

Begin by considering any positive integer
d\geq 1
and fix any
M\in G(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Let
u
denote an associated unit eigenvector to
\beta
. Define the map

\displaystyle T_{M}:\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto M\omega

which, slightly abusing notation, induces a map
T_{M}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Equip
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mu
. To construct
Z
we introduce the following notation

\sgn(x)=\begin{cases}1&x>0\\
0&x=0\\
-1&x<0\end{cases}

and

I_{\sgn(x)}=\begin{cases}[0,1)&x=0,1\\
(-1,0]&x=-1\end{cases}.

Let the fundamental domain of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
be

F:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}I_{\sgn(u_{i})},

which is chosen for convenience in the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

Consider a positive integer
d\geq 2
and assume
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a unit vector. Then there exists an affine hyperplane
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that when we define

Z=P\cap F,

the following statement holds: For every
\omega\in F
, there exists a unique
z\in Z
such that
\omega\in
Image
(f_{z})\cap F
where

f_{z}(t)=tu+z.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume

u_{i}=\begin{cases}>0&i=1,\dots,k\\
<0&i=k+1,\dots,j\\
=0&i=j+1,\dots,d\end{cases}

for
0\leq k,j\leq d
where
0\leq k+j\leq d
. Note one can permute the
1,\dots,d
subscripts to match the original vector.

Define an affine hyperplane

P:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j}x_{i}=1\}.

Let
Z=P\cap F
. We claim this choice of
Z
suffices. Let
\omega\in F
and define

g(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j}\omega_{i}.

Notice that
g(u)\neq 0
, since if
g(u)=0
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j}\omega_{i}.

The left hand side is strictly positive and the right is strictly negative, which is a contradiction.

Define
t_{0}
as

t_{0}=\frac{g(\omega)-1}{g(u)}

and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with components defined as

z_{i}=\begin{cases}\omega_{i}-t_{0}u_{i}&i=1,\dots,k+j\\
0&i=k+j+1,\dots,d\end{cases}.

Observe

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j}z_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\omega_{i}-tu_{i})-\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j}(\omega_{i}-tu_{i})

\displaystyle=g(\omega)-tg(u)

\displaystyle=g(\omega)-(g(\omega)-1)

\displaystyle=1,

which implies that
z\in P
.

It remains to show that
z\in F
, so consider three cases:

(1)

\omega\in P
,

(2)

\omega
satisfies
g(\omega)>1
,

(3)

\omega
satisfies
g(\omega)<1
.

Case 1 is trivial as
z=\omega
. Let us consider case
2
. Assume for a contradiction that there exists a
j=1,\dots,k
such that
z_{j}<0
. Define
\tilde{t}=\frac{\omega_{j}}{u_{j}}
. Since
z_{j}<0
,

z_{j}=\omega_{j}-t_{0}u_{j}<0\implies\frac{\omega_{j}}{u_{j}}<t_{0}\implies\tilde{t}<t_{0}.

But
\omega_{j}-tu_{j}
is a decreasing function and
w_{j}-\tilde{t}u_{j}=0
, so
\tilde{t}>t_{0}
, which is a contradiction.

The argument for
i=k+1,\dots,k+j
is the same as above and there is nothing to show when
i=k+j+1,\dots,d
. Also, notice if
z\in Z
, then
f_{z}(t_{0})=\omega
and hence
\omega\in
Image
(f_{z})\cap F
. The proof for case 3 has an analogous argument to case 2.
∎

From this point on we fix
Z
to represent the affine hyperplane, as in Lemma
13
. Define for each
z\in Z
a local unstable leaf

W_{z}=\text{Image}(f_{z})\cap F.

It follows that

F=\bigsqcup_{z\in Z}W_{z}

and after taking the quotient

\mathbb{T}^{d}=\bigsqcup_{z\in Z}W_{z}.

By Rohklin’s disintegration theorem, one attains the equality (
26
), where the conditional measures are normalized Lebesgue measure and denoted as
m_{z}
for each
z\in Z
. On this note, it remains to show positive Lyapunov exponents along
W_{z}
with the measure
m_{z}
.

To find a suitable partition, recall that
W_{z}
is isometric to a line segment in the direction of
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which induces order on
W_{z}
. First, define a set that contains the endpoints of
W_{z}
,
\mathcal{D}_{0,z}=\{w_{0},w_{1}\}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we observe that
T^{n}_{M}W_{z}
is of finite length, since each iterate of
T_{M}
increases the length of the line segment by a factor of
\beta
. Consequently, there are a finite number of times the line crosses the fundamental domain after each iteration of
T_{M}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{n,z}
be the collection of all such points in
W_{z}
at step
n
, then define the set

\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{n,z}.

By the argument in
[
11

one finds that
Card(\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime})<\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\beta^{k+j+1}
for some fixed

which is chosen to satisfy
2\sqrt{d}(d+2)<\beta^{k}
. With the induced order, we can label and order all the points in
\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime}
as

w_{n,0}<\dots<w_{n,Card(\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime})-2}.

Define
I_{n,z,j}=[w_{n,j},w_{n,j+1})
for

. Remark that for every

, the points
\omega_{n,0},\omega_{n,Card(\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime})-2}
are the end points of
W_{z}
. For each

, define the partition

\mathcal{P}_{n,z}=\{I_{n,z,j}\}_{j}.

By construction,
\mathcal{P}_{n+1,z}

, since
\mathcal{D}_{n,z}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{D}_{n+1,z}^{\prime}

for some

by construction of

. By applying Lemma
1
on
\mathcal{I}
provides the following corollary.
