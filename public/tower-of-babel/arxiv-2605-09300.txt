Abstract
. Identifying covariates that modify treatment effects is a central problem in causal inference. Yet existing data-adaptive procedures do not provide finite-sample control over the expected number of false discoveries, risking spurious findings that fail to replicate. We introduce causal stability selection , an algorithm that combines cross-fitted estimation of conditional average treatment effects with integrated path stability selection. The method accommodates arbitrary treatment effect estimators and arbitrary base selectors, and produces a selection set with an explicit, non-asymptotic bound on the expected number of false positives. Under standard causal identifying assumptions and regularity conditions on the base selector, we prove that the estimated selection probabilities converge to their oracle counterparts at the rate of the underlying treatment effect estimator. This establishes a direct connection between treatment effect estimation and effect modifier discovery. We illustrate the method on a randomized trial in oncology and on observational data on maternal smoking and infant birthweight.



1.  Introduction

A large proportion of randomized and observational studies report null average treatment effects. For example, 40% of trials registered on
ClinicalTrials.gov
find no significant overall effect
(
14
)
. Yet null averages need not imply null effects: treatment response may vary systematically with pre-treatment covariates, even when the population mean is zero
(
19
)
. The same pattern appears in social policy and public health, where interventions with negligible average impacts often exhibit substantial variation in response
(
37
)
. Discovering which covariates drive this variation—that is, identifying
effect modifiers
—has both practical and scientific value. Practically, effect modifier discovery is at the foundation of treatment targeting for precision interventions
(
24
)
. Scientifically, identifying effect modifiers helps characterize populations and subgroups in which treatments are most and least effective
(
2
)
. Identifying effect modifiers can therefore reveal clinical or policy value in interventions otherwise deemed ineffective.

Formally, effect modifiers are defined in terms of the
conditional average treatment effect
(CATE),

(1.1)

\displaystyle\tau(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}\right],

where
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the pre-treatment covariates and
Y(1)
and
Y(0)
are potential outcomes under treatment and control, respectively
(
30
)
. When
\tau
is non-constant in
\bm{x}
, the treatment effect is said to be
heterogeneous
. Many approaches have been developed to estimate
\tau
; however, a method may estimate
\tau
accurately without revealing which covariates drive heterogeneity. In this work, our objective is effect modifier discovery, that is, to identify the covariates that explain variation in
\tau
(this task is formally defined in Section
2
).

While CATE estimation has received significant attention in recent years, several methods aimed specifically at effect modifier discovery have also been introduced. Many of these focus on constructing variable importance measures to quantify each covariate’s contribution to treatment effect heterogeneity. For example,
21
develop model-free importance measures based on efficient influence functions,
6
derive importance measures from causal forests, and
8
introduce a nonparametric framework for defining effect modifier importance and establish asymptotic properties of their proposed estimators. While these approaches provide valuable tools for quantifying the contribution of each covariate to heterogeneity—and some include inferential procedures that can inform selection—they are not primarily designed to identify a parsimonious set of genuine effect modifiers with error rate guarantees. In a separate line of work more closely aligned with our objective,
41
propose a two-stage procedure that first estimates nuisance functions related to the CATE, then applies the lasso
(
35
)
to select a parsimonious model for effect modification.

Despite these advances, existing methods do not provide finite-sample bounds on the expected number of selected covariates that are spurious effect modifiers. As we illustrate below, the absence of such control can lead to severely inflated false positive rates, undermining the reliability and replicability of downstream analyses and targeted interventions
(
7
;
39
)
. Achieving false discovery control while maintaining power to detect genuine effect modifiers is the central challenge we address.

1.2.  Contributions

We present a formal framework for effect modifier discovery and a method,
Causal Stability Selection
(
CausalStabSel
), for selecting effect modifiers that combines CATE estimation and cross-fitting approaches from modern causal inference
(
18
;
23
)
with stability selection
(
26
;
28
)
.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we develop a unified framework for effect modifier discovery. To our knowledge, this is the first that provides a non-asymptotic, finite-sample upper bound on the expected number of false positives without restrictions on the form of the base selector or the CATE estimator. Second, we prove that the central quantities of
CausalStabSel
, called
selection probabilities
, are asymptotically unbiased for the corresponding oracle probabilities obtained if the true conditional treatment effect were observed. The convergence rate is governed directly by the rate of the underlying CATE estimator, establishing a connection between treatment effect estimation and effect modifier discovery. Third, by aggregating results across repeated subsamples,
CausalStabSel
yields stable discoveries while remaining computationally tractable in high dimensions. Our proposed framework requires standard causal assumptions (SUTVA and strong ignorability) and regularity conditions on the base selector, making it applicable to both randomized trials and observational studies.

2.1.  Notation and causal framework

Uppercase letters denote random variables and lowercase letters represent their realized values. Bold letters represent vectors and matrices. For any positive integer

is the observed outcome. For generic random variables

Assumption 1

(Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA))
.

For all
i\in[n]
, the potential outcome
Y_{i}
satisfies:

(i)

No interference
:
Y_{i}(\mathbf{z})=Y_{i}(z_{i})
for any
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
;

(ii)

Consistency
: If
Z_{i}=z
, then
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(z)
almost surely.

Part (i) states that each unit’s outcome does not depend on treatments assigned to the other units, and hence that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
are well-defined.
Part (ii) ensures each treatment level has a single version, so the observed outcome satisfies
y_{i}=Y_{i}(z_{i})
.
The fundamental problem of causal inference is that only one of
Y_{i}(0)
or
Y_{i}(1)
is observed for each
i

(
22
)
.

Assumption 2

(Strong Ignorability)
.

The treatment assignment satisfies:

(i)

Unconfoundedness
:
(Y(0),Y(1))\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Z\mid\bm{X}
;

(ii)

Overlap/Positivity
:
0<P(Z=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})<1
for all
\bm{x}
in the support of
\bm{X}
.

Strong ignorability ensures that, conditional on covariates, treatment assignment is independent of the potential outcomes (unconfoundedness) and that each unit has a positive probability of receiving either treatment (overlap)
(
29
)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the observed outcome satisfies

(2.1)

\begin{split}Y=Y(0)+Z(Y(1)-Y(0))=\mu(\bm{X})+Z\tau(\bm{X})+\varepsilon,\end{split}

where
\mu(\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid\bm{X}]
is the prognostic function,
\tau(\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\bm{X}]
is the CATE, and
\varepsilon=Y(0)-\mu(\bm{X})+Z(Y(1)-Y(0)-\tau(\bm{X}))
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid\bm{X},Z]=0
.
Under Assumption
2
, the prognostic and CATE functions satisfy

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid\bm{X}]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z=0,\bm{X}],

\displaystyle\tau(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\bm{X}]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z=1,\bm{X}]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z=0,\bm{X}].

2.2.  Treatment effect modifiers

The CATE captures how treatment effects vary with
\bm{X}
, while
\mu(\bm{X})
captures associations between
\bm{X}
and
Y
unrelated to treatment.
Formally, we define prognostic variables and effect modifiers as follows.

Definition 1

(
Prognostic Variables and Effect Modifiers
)
.

A covariate
X_{j}
is:

(i)

A
prognostic variable
if
\mu(\bm{X})\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}
, i.e.,
X_{j}
provides information about the baseline outcome not captured by other covariates;

(ii)

A
treatment effect modifier
, or, more simply, an
effect modifier
, if
\tau(\bm{X})\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}
, i.e.,
X_{j}
provides information about treatment effect heterogeneity not captured by other covariates.

We denote the sets of prognostic variables and effect modifiers by
\mathcal{P}:=\{j:\mu(\bm{X})\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}\}
and
\mathcal{E}:=\{j:\tau(\bm{X})\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}\}
, respectively.

Remark 1

(Interpretation of Definition
1
)
.

Since
\tau(\bm{X})
and
\mu(\bm{X})
are deterministic functions of
\bm{X}
, the conditional independence statements in Definition
1
have a direct functional interpretation. The statement
\tau(\bm{X})\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}
is equivalent to
\tau
not being almost-surely a function of
\bm{X}_{-j}
alone; that is, there exist values
\bm{x}_{-j}
at which
\tau
varies with
x_{j}
. An analogous interpretation applies to
\mu
and prognostic variables.

The conditional independence formulation in Definition
1
extends prior characterizations developed for specific settings (for example, coefficient sparsity in linear models
(
34
)
) to a general, model-agnostic form applicable to arbitrary
\mu
and
\tau
. In the linear setting,
Equation

1.2
, where
\mu(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
and
\tau(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}
, these reduce to
\mathcal{P}=\{j:\beta_{j}\neq 0\}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{j:\gamma_{j}\neq 0\}
. Furthermore, we note that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{E}
are not mutually exclusive; that is, a covariate may be both prognostic and an effect modifier.

The following lemma shows that our definition of effect modifier in
Definition

1
is equivalent to variance-based variable importance measures of heterogeneity
(
21
;
38
;
32
)
.
However, the latter require that
\tau(\bm{X})
has a finite second moment, making our characterization in terms of conditional independence slightly more general.

Lemma 2.1

(Equivalent Characterizations of Effect Modifiers)
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}(\tau(\bm{X})^{2})<\infty
and define
\tau_{j}(\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\bm{X}_{-j}]
. Then
X_{j}
is an effect modifier if and only if
\text{Var}(\tau_{j}(\bm{X}))\neq\text{Var}(\tau(\bm{X}))
.

Proof.

If
X_{j}
is not an effect modifier, that is, if
\tau(\bm{X})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}
, then

\displaystyle\tau(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}]=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\bm{X}_{-j}]=\tau_{j}(\bm{X})

almost surely, where the second equality holds by the tower property of conditional expectation.
Hence
\text{Var}(\tau(\bm{X}))=\text{Var}(\tau_{j}(\bm{X}))
.
Conversely, if
\text{Var}(\tau(\bm{X}))=\text{Var}(\tau_{j}(\bm{X}))
, then

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\tau_{j}(\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\tau(\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}])+\mathbb{E}(\text{Var}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}])

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\tau_{j}(\bm{X}))+\mathbb{E}(\text{Var}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}]).

Hence
\mathbb{E}(\text{Var}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}])=0
, implying that
\text{Var}[\tau(\bm{X})\mid\bm{X}_{-j}]=0
almost surely.
Thus,
\tau(\bm{X})
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\bm{X}_{-j}
, so
\tau(\bm{X})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid\bm{X}_{-j}
.

3.  Causal Stability Selection

Rather than apply a generic variable selection algorithm once to the full dataset,
CausalStabSel
—and stability selection more broadly—aggregates selections across multiple random subsamples of the data, yielding more stable results and finite-sample false discovery control
(
26
)
. However, existing versions of stability selection assume the outcome of interest is directly observed, making them ill-equipped for effect modifier discovery. To overcome this limitation,
CausalStabSel
incorporates CATE estimation into the subsampling scheme, repeatedly using one subset of samples to estimate the CATE and the remaining samples for selection.

The rest of this section is dedicated to describing the
CausalStabSel
algorithm. In
Section

3.1
, we introduce the central quantities underlying
CausalStabSel
,

, which quantify the probability that a covariate is an effect modifier. In
Section

3.2
, we explain how selection probabilities are used to construct the final
CausalStabSel
estimate of the true set of effect modifiers,
\mathcal{E}
, and in
Section

3.3
, we discuss the inputs to
CausalStabSel
.

3.1.  Selection probabilities

Let
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
be any supervised variable selection algorithm parametrized by
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
; that is, given observed covariate–response pairs
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\tau_{i}):i\in A\}
for some index set

,
\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\{(\bm{x}_{i},\tau_{i}):i\in A\})\subseteq[p]
is an estimate of the covariates that directly relate to the response. In our setting, the responses
\tau_{i}=\tau(\bm{x}_{i})
are the unobserved CATE values, and the relevant covariates are the effect modifiers,
\mathcal{E}
. Specific examples of
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
are provided in
Section

For a subset
A\subseteq[n]
of size
m
, the
selection probability

\pi_{j,m}(\lambda)
of variable

is

(3.1)

\displaystyle\pi_{j,m}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(j\in\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A},\tau(\bm{X}_{A}))\right).

We regard these selection probabilities as oracle quantities because they correspond to the idealized setting in which
\tau
is known. Note that under the independent samples assumption,
\pi_{j,m}
depends on

only through the subsample size

CausalStabSel
consists of two key steps: (i) estimating the selection probabilities
\pi_{j,m}(\lambda)
from observed data, and (ii) using the estimated selection probabilities to construct an estimator of
\mathcal{E}
.
Algorithm

1

B
subsampling steps, a random subset
A_{2b-1}\subseteq[n]
of size
m

\hat{\tau}
is trained using the held-out samples
A_{2b-1}^{c}=[n]\setminus A_{2b-1}
. The resulting model,
\hat{\tau}_{A_{2b-1}^{c}}
, is then used to predict CATE values for samples in
A_{2b-1}
and
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
is applied to the pairs
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\hat{\tau}_{A_{2b-1}^{c}}(\bm{x}_{i})):i\in A_{2b-1}\}
. The final estimated selection probability,
\hat{\pi}_{j,m}(\lambda)
for feature

\lambda

is selected across all

(
31
)
.

Using disjoint samples for CATE estimation and selection in
Algorithm

is critical: when the same samples are used for both, the prediction
\hat{\tau}(\bm{x}_{i})
depends on
\bm{x}_{i}
through the training process, inducing an artifactual dependence between

and
\hat{\tau}(\bm{x}_{i})
that the base selector mistakes for genuine effect modification. Cross-fitting breaks this dependence by ensuring

is independent of
\bm{x}_{i}
for

. Furthermore, as shown by the violations of FDR control in
Figure

1

repeated
cross-fitting—that is, training

on samples in

and selecting on samples in
A
across multiple iterations—is this source of bias controlled. We formalize this in
Section

4
.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{SS}}

\displaystyle=\left\{j:\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\hat{\pi}_{j,m}(\lambda)\geq\gamma\right\},

where
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a set of
\lambda
values and
\gamma
is a user-specified threshold. However, this selection criterion is known to produce overly-conservative estimates, due in part to its relatively loose theoretical upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})

(
1
;
28
)
. In
28
, a significantly tighter upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
is obtained via the selection criterion

(3.3)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left\{j:\int_{\Lambda}f\left(\hat{\pi}_{j,m}(\lambda)\right)\,\mu(d\lambda)\geq\gamma\right\},

where
f(x)=(2x-1)^{3}\1(x\geq 1/2)
and
\mu
is an arbitrary probability measure on
\Lambda
whose specification is discussed in
Section

3.3
. This approach, called
integrated path stability selection
(IPSS), achieves this tighter upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
by transforming (via
f
) and integrating (rather than maximizing) the selection probabilities over
\Lambda
, leading to more true positives than other versions of stability selection while maintaining false positive control. For this reason, we use the IPSS criterion (
3.3
) when implementing
CausalStabSel
. In
Section

4.2
, we prove that an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
similar to the original IPSS bound holds for
CausalStabSel
.

Another benefit of IPSS is that the threshold
\gamma
does not need to be specified by the user. Instead, the theoretical upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
is used to assign an
expected false positive
(efp) score,
\mathtt{efp}(j)
, to each variable
j

(
27
)
. Given a user-specified target
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
level
t
, the final selection set
\hat{\mathcal{E}}=\{j:\mathtt{efp}(j)\leq t\}
has at most
t
false positives on average. For FDR control, we select the largest set satisfying
t/|\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\leq\alpha
for a nominal FDR level
\alpha
.

3.3.  Implementation

CausalStabSel
involves several inputs: the variable selection algorithm, or
base selector
,
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
, the CATE estimator
\hat{\tau}
, the subsample size
m
, the parameter set
\Lambda
and probability measure
\mu
on
\Lambda
, and the number of iterations
B
. The CATE estimator is unique to
CausalStabSel
relative to other stability selection methods, and
m
plays a different role here than in standard stability selection: smaller subsamples leave more data for CATE estimation, and we show in Section 4 that this trade-off is asymptotically favorable. Both are further discussed below and analyzed in detail in
Section

4
. The remaining inputs serve identical purposes in
CausalStabSel
and in IPSS. We briefly describe these below, referring the reader to
28
and
27
for details.

CausalStabSel
is compatible with any base selector
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
. Two broad classes are (i) penalized regression and (ii) thresholding variable importance scores. The former consists of regularized parametric models such as the lasso
(
35
)
and adaptive lasso
(
42
)
, in which case
\lambda\geq 0
is the regularization strength. The latter consists of selectors
\hat{S}_{\lambda}=\{j:\Phi_{j}>\lambda\}
, where
\Phi_{j}
measures the importance of variable
j
computed via any algorithm—such as tree-based or deep learning models—and
\lambda
is the importance threshold. In this work, we use lasso-based selection and mean decrease impurity (MDI) from XGBoost
(
11
)
as our base estimators. When combined with IPSS, MDI from XGBoost exhibits strong selection performance and computational efficiency relative to other importance scores across a wide range of settings, most notably when there are nonlinear relationships in the data
(
27
)
. We note that the Lipschitz condition on importance scores introduced in Section
4
(Assumption
3
) is most readily verified for linear scores such as lasso coefficients; for tree-based importance measures including MDI, the condition is taken as a high-level regularity assumption.

Similar to the base selector,
CausalStabSel
is compatible with any CATE estimation method. In this work, we consider three meta-learning approaches: T- and X-learners
(
25
)
, and the DR-learner
(
23
)
. Each combines one or more supervised regression models—its
base learners
—to form a CATE estimate. On the theoretical side, the performance of
CausalStabSel
is governed by the CATE estimation error
\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}}-\tau\|
; when this error vanishes at the parametric rate
n^{-1/2}
, the
CausalStabSel
selection probabilities are asymptotically unbiased (
Corollary

1
), the
CausalStabSel
selection set converges to its oracle counterpart (
Corollary

2
), and the false discovery bound matches that of the oracle setting (
Theorem

4
). The DR-learner attains this rate under standard regularity conditions, while analogous guarantees have not been established for T- and X-learners. On the empirical side, we compare all three meta-learners, each implemented with both ridge regression and gradient boosting as base learners (with lasso as the
CausalStabSel
base selector in the linear setting and XGBoost importance scores in the nonlinear setting).
Figure

S2
reports the results. The three ridge-based implementations produce nearly identical
CausalStabSel
performance: strong in the linear setting, where the model is correctly specified, and poor in the nonlinear setting. The XGBoost-based implementations differ modestly, with the DR-learner achieving better FDR control than the T- and X-learners. All three perform well in the nonlinear setting and moderately worse than the ridge-based estimators in the linear setting, and their computational requirements are similar in practice. Combined with its theoretical properties, this leads us to recommend the DR-learner as the default CATE estimator in
CausalStabSel
.

For IPSS and other forms of stability selection, the subsample size
m
is typically fixed at
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. For
CausalStabSel
, however, there is benefit to choosing smaller values when
n
is large, since the
n-m
held-out samples are used for CATE estimation which, in our experience, tends to benefit more than selection from additional samples. In
Section

4
, we formalize this claim, proving that
CausalStabSel
achieves oracle performance asymptotically as
n,B\to\infty
provided that
m=o(\sqrt{n})
. For smaller sample sizes where the cost of reducing
m
below
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
outweighs the benefit, we recommend
m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

The roles of the remaining inputs,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
B
, are identical to their roles in IPSS. Briefly,
\Lambda=[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]
is discretized to a finite grid with
\lambda_{\max}
chosen large enough that no variables are selected, and the integral in (
3.3
) is approximated by a Riemann sum. For
\mu
, we use the family of probability measures
\mu_{\delta}(d\lambda)\propto\lambda^{-\delta}\,d\lambda
; results are robust to the choice of
\delta

(
28
;
27
)
. Similarly, results vary little with
B
provided
B\geq 50

(
31
;
28
)
. Our default is
B=100
.

4.  Theoretical Results

The central challenge of effect modifier discovery is that
\tau
must be estimated, introducing additional uncertainty that can bias selections and inflate false discoveries. In this section, we characterize the performance of
CausalStabSel
in terms of CATE estimation error, establishing a direct connection between causal variable selection and prediction. We formalize this correspondence in three steps. First, we prove that the selection probabilities estimated via
Algorithm

1
are asymptotically unbiased for their oracle counterparts (
Theorem

1
). Second, we prove that the
CausalStabSel
estimator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}
converges to the oracle selection set under CATE consistency and suitable subsampling conditions (
Theorem

2
). Third, we establish finite-sample false discovery control for
CausalStabSel
(
Theorem

3
). Proofs are deferred to
Appendix

S1
.

4.1.  Oracle selection

Theorems

1
and
2
assume that
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
selects variables by thresholding importance scores (

). Specifically, we consider selectors of the form

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A},f)=\bigl\{j:\Phi_{j}(\bm{X}_{A},f)\geq\lambda\bigr\},

Common examples of importance scores include magnitudes of estimated regression coefficients obtained from fitting generalized linear models, the aforementioned mean decrease impurity (MDI) scores computed from tree-ensemble methods such as random forests or gradient boosting, and even more general scores such as permutation importance and SHAP values

We now establish a finite-sample upper bound on the expected number of false positives,
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})=\mathbb{E}\lvert\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}\rvert
, selected by
CausalStabSel
. Let

(4.7)

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\bigl|\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A},\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}})\bigr|

denote the expected number of variables selected by
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
on samples
\{(\bm{X}_{i},\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}}(\bm{X}_{i})):i\in A\}
, where
\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}}
is trained on samples in
A^{c}
. Similarly, let

be the expected number of variables selected by
\hat{S}_{\lambda}
on samples in
A
, but with the true CATE in place of
\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}}
.

Assumption 5
.

For

, let

be subsets of

of size

(4.8)

\displaystyle\max_{j\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{P}\!\left(j\in\bigcap_{b=1}^{2r}\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A_{b}},\hat{\tau}_{A_{b}^{c}})\right)\mu(d\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}\left(\frac{\hat{q}(\lambda)}{p}\right)^{2r}\mu(d\lambda).

Similarly, we say that
5
b holds for
r
if

(4.9)

\displaystyle\max_{j\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{P}\!\left(j\in\bigcap_{b=1}^{2r}\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A_{b}},\tau)\right)\mu(d\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}\left(\frac{q(\lambda)}{p}\right)^{2r}\mu(d\lambda).

Assumptions

\lambda\in\Lambda

\hat{S}_{\lambda}
selects the same null variable across multiple random subsamples of the data is no larger than the corresponding average probability under uniformly random selection with expected size
\hat{q}(\lambda)
or

, respectively. The only difference between the two is that

, while

.

Remark 2

(Relaxation of the IPSS condition)
.

is stated in terms of integrals over
\Lambda

Theorem 3

(False discovery control)
.

Suppose

. Then

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl|\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}\bigr|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{\hat{q}(\lambda)^{2}}{B^{2}p}+\frac{3(B-1)\hat{q}(\lambda)^{4}}{B^{2}p^{3}}+\frac{(B-1)(B-2)\hat{q}(\lambda)^{6}}{B^{2}p^{5}}\,\mu(d\lambda).

The proof follows the structure of the IPSS
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{FP})
bound with two modifications:
\hat{q}(\lambda)
is defined for the cross-fitted causal selector
\hat{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{A},\hat{\tau}_{A^{c}})
, and the multinomial expansion at the heart of the argument requires only the integrated form of

(

2

\hat{S}_{\lambda}
, since its proof does not invoke

and

Theorem

in place of
\hat{q}(\lambda)
, plus an additional term

Theorem 4

(Oracle false discovery control)
.

If the assumptions of
Theorem

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl|\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}\bigr|\leq\rho+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{q(\lambda)^{2}}{B^{2}p}+\frac{3(B-1)q(\lambda)^{4}}{B^{2}p^{3}}+\frac{(B-1)(B-2)q(\lambda)^{6}}{B^{2}p^{5}}\,\mu(d\lambda),

Remark 3

(FDR control via efp scores)
.

Theorem

We conduct a series of simulation studies to evaluate the performance of

5.1.  Data generation

We generate data from the potential outcomes model (
2.1
) with an additional parameter,
a
, that controls the relative contributions of the prognostic variables and effect modifiers to the outcome; see (
5.1
) below. Covariates

are drawn from a multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and Toeplitz covariance

,
\Sigma_{ij}=\rho^{|i-j|}
, then standardized to unit empirical variance. The sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{E}
are subsets of
[p]
of fixed sizes

and
|\mathcal{E}|

may therefore be nonempty. Treatment assignment is modeled after a randomized control trial,

, in 30 of the 34 experiments. In the other four, there are 5 or 10 confounders drawn uniformly at random from
[p]
, which affect treatment assignment via a logistic propensity score,
\Pr(Z_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=\bigl(1+\exp({-\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}X_{ij}})\bigr)^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{C}\subseteq[p]
indexes the confounders. Confounders may overlap with
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{E}
.

In the linear case, the prognostic function and CATE are given by

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\gamma},

where the nonzero coordinates of
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\gamma}
are indexed by
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{E}
, respectively, and drawn independently and uniformly from
[-1,-0.5]\cup[0.5,1]
. In the nonlinear setting,

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{X})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{8}X_{j}^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(\bm{X})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{8}X_{j}^{2}\right).

and

\displaystyle Y=a\mu(\bm{X})+Z\tau(\bm{X})+\varepsilon,

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}=\text{Var}\bigl(a\mu(\bm{X})+Z\tau(\bm{X})\bigr)\,/\,\sigma^{2},

with the variance computed empirically on each simulated dataset. The parameter

controls the relative magnitude of the prognostic and treatment components: in the RCT case,

and
\text{Var}(Z\tau(\bm{X}))=1/2

We apply

6.1.  PRIME Randomized Trial

PRIME is a multicenter, open-label, randomized phase 3 trial comparing

The analytic sample consists of

The observational analysis uses data from

We run

6.3.  Application remarks

Together, these applications demonstrate
CausalStabSel
on two complementary regimes. PRIME involves a near-null average treatment effect, where heterogeneity is the substantive finding and recovering the canonical KRAS modifier serves as calibration. The birthweight study involves a strongly significant negative ATE, where heterogeneity is detected against a clear treatment effect and the estimate of the ATE itself provides an independent calibration of the pseudo-outcomes. In both cases
CausalStabSel
identifies a parsimonious set of effect modifiers with established literature support, and held-out BLP-on-CATE inference provides corroboration for at least a subset of selections.

7.  Discussion

We introduced
CausalStabSel
, a method for effect modifier discovery that combines cross-fitted CATE estimation with integrated path stability selection. The procedure accommodates arbitrary treatment effect estimators and arbitrary base selectors, and produces a selection set with an explicit, non-asymptotic upper bound on the expected number of false positives. Our theoretical results connect the bias of the estimated selection probabilities directly to the convergence rate of the underlying CATE estimator, establishing a quantitative link between treatment effect estimation and effect modifier discovery. Across a comprehensive simulation grid and two real-world applications spanning a randomized oncology trial and an observational study of maternal smoking,
CausalStabSel
consistently controls false discovery while maintaining competitive power.

Several extensions follow naturally from the framework developed here. The selection probability machinery applies, with appropriate modification, to subgroup discovery, time-varying treatments, selection subspaces and settings with interference, where the relevant heterogeneity object differs from the CATE but the cross-fitted stability selection structure is preserved
(
40
)
. On the inferential side, post-selection inference for the effect modifiers identified by
CausalStabSel
(e.g., building on the held-out BLP-on-CATE strategy used in our applications, but with formal guarantees) would convert discovery into estimation with valid coverage. We leave these directions for future work.
