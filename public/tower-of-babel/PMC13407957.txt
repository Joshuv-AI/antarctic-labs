Carbapenem Resistance at the Crossroads: A New Therapeutic Era for Critical Care
Authors: Jay Prakash
Published: 2026

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a major global health concern. In particular, carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria have become a significant threat in intensive care units (ICUs). Of all these microbes, carbapenem-resistant enterobacterales (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) contribute toward morbidity and mortality, increase hospital stay, and raise the cost of healthcare services. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified these as high-priority microbes that need urgent therapeutic innovation and coordinated global action.1,2

This is most obvious in India, which is considered one of the countries with the largest burden of AMR. Many reasons contribute to the formulation of new drugs or other options or substitutes to treat such multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms that flourish in India. High usage of antibiotics, crowded hospitals, poor preventive practices, and lack of access to rapid diagnostic tests can all be mentioned as key contributors. Recent burden estimates indicate that about 1.27 million people lost their lives due to AMR, while another 5 million died from conditions associated with AMR in 2021.3 The most hazardous situation is observed when it comes to critically ill patients who usually face infections from CRE, CRAB, and CRPA, leading to death rates as high as 40–70%.4

In light of the foregoing facts, the guidelines provided by the Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine (ISCCM) concerning the use of the drug under consideration prove to be relevant and justified.5 In fact, by using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, this guideline try to fill the identified gap that exists in Indian clinical practices, where there are no evidence-based guidelines specific to local conditions and needs.

Indeed, for decades now, polymyxins, especially colistin and polymyxin B, have been considered the drug of choice in the management of carbapenem-resistant infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria. However, the reason behind their popularity lies not in their efficacy and effectiveness but in the lack of better options. The use of polymyxins has become common practice due to the shortage of alternative drugs.6 Yet their clinical limitations are increasingly recognized. Nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, suboptimal pharmacokinetic profiles, poor pulmonary penetration, and concerns regarding heteroresistance have questioned their relevance in current antimicrobial therapy.7

The most significant message that one can draw from the ISCCM recommendations are not just the suggestion that new agents are superior to polymyxins, but rather that there is enough data now that makes the use of polymyxins an impractical option when better alternatives exist.

For patients suffering from CRE infections, all evidence points to novel β-lactam/β-lactamase (BL/BLI) inhibitors, specifically ceftazidime-avibactam.8 Overall, the non-polymyxin therapies had significantly higher clinical cure rates, better microbiological clearance, fewer toxicities, and a trend toward less mortality in all studies examined for this guideline. While the effects did not reach statistical significance for reduced mortality in most of the studies, the trends were consistent across studies.9 The difference here is that recommendations made by GRADE take into account more than just statistical significance in their assessments.

It could also be argued that it is not appropriate to issue strong recommendations based on such a low-certainty body of evidence.10 This misconception arises because of a misunderstanding of GRADE methodology. It is possible for a recommendation to be strong despite a lack of certainty within the evidence base, especially when the balance of harms and benefits clearly favors one treatment over another. In the scenario of CRE infection, BL/BLI drugs demonstrate improved cure rates, lower nephrotoxicity, pharmacokinetic advantages, and biologically plausible advantages over polymyxins. It can be stated that there is sufficient scientific and biological logic for the transition from the use of polymyxins. In other words, this recommendation is based on principles of harm minimization rather than statistical significance.

The findings concerning CRAB are equally intriguing. Traditionally, CRAB is viewed as one of the most challenging microorganisms to deal with, especially because of its unique ability to acquire various resistance factors and survive within the healthcare setting. Treatment alternatives have been rather limited, leading to extensive reliance on colistin-based regimens despite their known toxicities.

The development of sulbactam-durlobactam could mark the biggest achievement in CRAB therapy in decades. Clinical outcomes from the analysis of the guideline suggest increased clinical cure and reduced toxicity with sulbactam compared with colistin. These findings are reinforced by the ATTACK trial, a pivotal study that has improved our understanding and treatment of CRAB infections.11 Moreover, the effectiveness of sulbactam is based on its ability to inhibit the growth of pathogens, such as Acinetobacter bacteria, by interacting with penicillin-binding proteins. Durlobactam enhances this effect by acting as a BLI.12

However, microbiological eradication outcomes occasionally favored colistin in observational studies. Nonetheless, one should exercise caution with these results because a positive effect on bacterial cultures may not mean an improvement in patient outcomes, especially if the negative impact of the medication on patients is greater than the benefits.13 The modern approach to intensive care recognizes that patient-oriented outcomes, such as survival, the need for organ support, length of stay in the hospital, and recovery quality, matter much more.

Recommendations for CRPA highlight another crucial aspect of contemporary antimicrobial stewardship. Although polymyxins did not demonstrate clear inferiority or superiority to alternative agents in terms of efficacy, unlike the findings for CRE and CRAB infections. The newer antimicrobial agents consistently showed a more favorable safety profile. This underscores an emerging principle in modern antimicrobial therapy: Treatment decisions should not be based solely on antibacterial activity. Rather, they should also consider drug-related toxicity, patient-centered outcomes, and the overall risk–benefit balance.14

An important positive aspect of the ISCCM guideline is the clear recognition of the limitations of evidence. Currently, most studies are still observational in nature; the number of participants is often small, and the variety of sources of infection, severity of illness, and treatment regimens can make the findings less reliable. The contrast between randomized clinical trials and observational studies makes the study of antimicrobial effectiveness problematic. The problems of confounding by indication, survival bias, and selection bias may affect the results of observational studies. These biases are particularly relevant in critically ill patients who are prescribed last-resort medications such as colistin.

Beyond the personal choice to use these drugs in treating infections, these recommendations also have significant consequences for antimicrobial stewardship programs. The transition from polymyxins to a BL/BLI regimen plus sulbactam is not merely a substitution of one antibiotic for another, but a paradigm shift in the management of CRAB infections. Rather, it is a part of an ongoing trend toward precise antimicrobial therapy, which is challenging in the absence of appropriate microbiologic diagnostic techniques, appropriate testing for antibacterial drug susceptibility, strong stewardship policies, and, obviously, new drugs.

Nevertheless, there were some other important advances made recently in the domain of AMR epidemiology that emphasized the importance of these recommendations. The AMR collaborators estimate that, under current trajectories, AMR could be associated with nearly 39 million deaths worldwide between 2025 and 2050, with South Asia among the most heavily affected regions.2 Among these fatalities, it is expected that a considerable share will be made up of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria due to their quick spread, lack of treatment options, and ability to cause fatal complications in critically ill and immunocompromised individuals. The alarming trend indicates that AMR is not only a problem associated with infectious diseases but also a very important one in terms of global health security and healthcare sustainability.

The ISCCM recommendations, therefore, arrive at a pivotal moment. In addition to serving as guidelines for bedside antimicrobial selection, the recommendations also signify a significant paradigm shift toward evidence-based, precision medicine. This shift away from polymyxins represents more than the introduction of newer antimicrobial agents. It reflects the recognition that treatment success should be measured not only by bacterial eradication but also by organ protection, reduced toxicity, and improved clinical outcomes.15 Equally important is the message about the proper stewardship of antimicrobials in resource-limited settings. Despite the development of effective drugs like ceftazidime-avibactam and sulbactam-durlobactam, there should not be therapeutic complacency. Instead, the introduction of these new drugs needs to be coupled with strong stewardship, rapid diagnosis, infection prevention, and surveillance of the resistance mechanisms.14 Throughout history, it has been shown again and again that all new antibiotics come with an equally inevitable risk of developing resistance. In other words, the effectiveness of these drugs must be safeguarded, and this can be considered a global responsibility. For this reason, these guidelines not only offer therapeutic guidance but also are an urgent call to address the AMR challenge ahead in India.

However, the economic aspect must not be overlooked either. The cost of novel antibiotics is still significantly higher when compared to polymyxins. However, the cost of drugs is only one element of the total medical cost. From an economic perspective, the higher upfront cost may be offset by reduced nephrotoxicity, shorter ICU stays, lower dialysis requirements, and improved clinical outcomes.

For this reason, the release of such guidelines can be seen only as the beginning, not as an end in itself. There are still several important gaps in our knowledge. It is necessary to carry out more randomized studies to establish the appropriate strategy for managing resistance of various types. The relative efficiency of combining therapies and monotherapy needs to be studied further as well. It may become useful to develop a rapid diagnostic method enabling carbapenemase classification for more personalized treatment.

Overall, it is evident that the developed ISCCM guideline represents a major milestone for the management of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections in Indian ICUs. The proposed recommendations form a reliable framework based on the systematic assessment of the literature and the GRADE approach; it provides a practical framework to help clinicians address one of the most challenging problems in modern critical care. Indeed, the main takeaway from this can be formulated as follows: The era of polymyxins as a routine drug choice will come to an end very soon. While uncertainty dominates here, it is reasonable to assume that the trend favors the safer, more effective, and biologically justified therapy.
