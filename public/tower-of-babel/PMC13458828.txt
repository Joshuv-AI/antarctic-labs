The Konakondla-Telfeian-Shen (KTS) Pyramid: a safe entry zone for ventral thoracic pathologies
Authors: Sanjay Konakondla, Shailen G Sampath, Isaac Lee, Jian Shen, Albert Telfeian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransforaminal (TF) endoscopic access to the lumbar spine has long relied on Kambin’s triangle as a posterolateral safe zone. As endoscopic indications expand, there are fundamental differences in thoracic anatomy that Kambin’s triangle does not account for, including smaller foraminal dimensions, the presence of the spinal cord, cranio-caudal pedicle angulation, and the rib head-costovertebral complex. To date, no formal anatomic framework exists to standardize thoracic TF planning. The objective of this study is to define and describe a three-dimensional thoracic TF working corridor, the Konakondla-Telfeian-Shen (KTS) Thoracic Pyramid, and to propose a standardized anatomic language and preoperative planning framework for endoscopic access to ventral thoracic pathology.MethodsThe thoracic TF corridor is conceptualized as a six-faced three-dimensional volume. Level-dependent anatomic constraints, including the rib head–costovertebral complex, pedicle morphology, spinal cord position, and segmental vascular anatomy, are incorporated. Variations due to degeneration, fusion, prior surgery, and vascular anatomy are discussed within this geometric framework.ResultsThe KTS Pyramid defines a reproducible, level-specific posterolateral access corridor to the ventral thoracic canal. The corridor predictably narrows from T12 to T1 as the rib head and costovertebral joint migrate cranially toward the disc plane and pedicle angulation increases. This framework identifies which anatomic “face” of the pyramid limits access at each thoracic level.ConclusionsThe KTS Pyramid provides a formal geometric and anatomic description of a thoracic TF working corridor. By standardizing thoracic endoscopic planning with level-specific boundaries and terminology, this framework supports safer access to ventral thoracic pathology, shortens the learning curve, and enables consistent communication, training, and future comparative research in thoracic endoscopic spine surgery.Keywords: Endoscopic surgery, Konakondla-Telfeian-Shen Pyramid (KTS Pyramid), thoracic spine, Kambin’s triangle

Highlight box

Key findings

• The Konakondla-Telfeian-Shen (KTS) Thoracic Pyramid defines a reproducible, three-dimensional working corridor for endoscopic transforaminal (TF) access to ventral thoracic pathology. The corridor is bounded by six key anatomic faces: the exiting nerve root/dorsal root ganglion, caudal pedicle, superior articular process, disc/endplate complex, spinal cord/dural sac, and rib head–costovertebral joint. The framework highlights that this corridor predictably narrows from T12 to T1 as the rib head and costovertebral joint migrate closer to the disc plane and thoracic pedicle constraints increase.

What is known and what is new?

• Kambin’s triangle is well established for lumbar TF endoscopic surgery, but thoracic anatomy introduces unique constraints, including the spinal cord, smaller foramina, pleura, segmental vessels, pedicle morphology, and the rib head-costovertebral complex. This manuscript introduces the KTS Pyramid as a thoracic-specific anatomic framework to standardize terminology, preoperative planning, and identification of the limiting structure at each level.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Surgeons should use level-specific computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging assessment to identify the limiting KTS face, determine whether needle-only access, drill-assisted expansion, or an alternate corridor is safest, and consider vascular imaging when indicated. Future cadaveric, morphometric, and clinical studies are needed to validate this framework.

Introduction

Transforaminal (TF) access to the lumbar disc space was formalized with the description of Kambin’s triangle, a posterolateral safe working zone that enabled percutaneous and, later, endoscopic approaches to common lumbar pathologies (1,2). The corridor was originally conceived and popularized by Parviz Kambin and colleagues in the late 1970s–1980s in the context of posterolateral percutaneous lumbar discectomy (1,2). In its classic form, the triangle is bounded by the exiting nerve root as the hypotenuse (superolateral border), the superior endplate of the caudal vertebral body as the base (inferior border), and the superior articular process (SAP) of the inferior vertebra as the height (posteromedial border). Recognizing the SAP as a true border clarifies prior inconsistencies in nomenclature and distinguishes the triangle from enlarged corridors created only after partial facetectomy (1,3). Although the working zone is commonly conceptualized as a three-dimensional (3D) volume, sometimes termed “Kambin’s prism”, the classic definition above anchors planning for instrument passage in the foramen (1,3).

Adoption of the triangle has paralleled the rise of endoscopic spine surgery, where safety depends on respecting its borders to avoid the exiting root and dorsal root ganglion while achieving direct disc access for discectomy and interbody preparation (1,3). Contemporary anatomic and imaging studies underscore that the dimensions of Kambin’s triangle vary across patients and spinal levels and can be altered by degenerative change, congenital morphology, or prior surgery, necessitating standardized preoperative measurement and cautious intraoperative technique (3-5). Advanced planning tools, including artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted 3D reconstructions and nerve segmentation, further refine individualized trajectories and channel sizing before docking and dilation (6,7).

As endoscopic indications expand beyond the lumbar spine, cervical and thoracic applications are increasingly reported. However, simply extrapolating lumbar concepts is insufficient: the thoracic region introduces distinct constraints, smaller foramina, nearby pleura and rib head/costovertebral anatomy, segmental vessels, and the presence of the spinal cord, that redefine the safe area and the size of an adequate working channel. Level-dependent differences across T1–T12 create predictable patterns in corridor dimensions, implying that TF access to ventral thoracic pathology requires its own standardized language, measurements, and decision rules.

Building on the clarified lumbar definition and its imaging-based planning paradigm, the present work aims to propose a standardized preoperative workflow, including imaging landmarks, safety checks, and instrument sizing, to guide access to ventral thoracic pathology. By codifying level-specific corridors and prerequisites, we seek to harmonize discussions among surgeons, improve reproducibility across centers, and provide a practical framework for downstream operative planning.

Methods

This study was designed as an anatomic framework and illustrative case-based description of the KTS Pyramid for endoscopic TF access to ventral thoracic pathology. Level-specific changes in rib head position, costovertebral anatomy, pedicle morphology, transverse process (TP) spacing, and vertebral body size were used to classify the corridor into type 1, type 2, and type 3 KTS Pyramid patterns. Representative clinical cases were selected to demonstrate application of the KTS Pyramid framework across the thoracic spine. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Lenox Hill Hospital (No. Lenox IRB 25-O774) and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Results

Anatomy

Bony anatomy of thoracic spine

In the thoracic spine, the vertebral bodies enlarge caudally (T1→T12). As is characteristic of this region, each vertebral body bears demifacets for the rib head, which together with the intervertebral disc form the costovertebral articulation (8). The typical rib has a head, neck, tubercle, and shaft. The rib head articulates with the demifacets of adjacent vertebral bodies (often spanning the disc), and the tubercle articulates with the ipsilateral TP at the costotransverse joint (8-10). The costotransverse joint allows rotation and glide with respiration and stabilizes the posterior rib arch (9-11). The costovertebral joint, meanwhile, permits small gliding motions that contribute to thoracic stiffness and coupled rib-spine kinematics (8-10). Thoracic facet joints are oriented predominantly in the coronal plane, a configuration that favors axial rotation while limiting flexion–extension; this coronal orientation becomes more pronounced at more cranial thoracic levels (T1 relative to T12). In the thoracic spine, the TP serves as the bony partner of the costotransverse joint and a muscular/ligamentous anchor relevant to working-channel docking (11,12). Finally, the pedicles are notably variable in width and medial angulation in the thoracic spine. The pedicles are most constrained in the mid-thoracic levels, which has implications for portal angulation and bony work (11,12).

Segmental arterial supply: artery of Adamkiewicz

The artery of Adamkiewicz (great anterior radiculomedullary artery) is the dominant feeder to the thoracolumbar anterior spinal axis. It most commonly arises between T8 and L1 with a left-sided predominance, then courses along the vertebral body and enters via an intervertebral foramen to join the anterior spinal artery, making preoperative identification crucial when planning posterolateral corridors (13).

Description of the KTS Pyramid

We define the KTS Pyramid (named after the authors) as the three-dimensional, posterolateral working volume to the ventral thoracic spinal canal (Figure 1). Unlike the lumbar region, this window progressively narrows from T12 to T1 due to changing relationships among the rib head-costovertebral complex, pedicle trajectory and morphology, and canal contents. Made up of six faces, the KTS Pyramid takes into account the various landmarks that must be considered when planning entry into the thoracic foramen. It is made up of a medial, lateral, cranial, caudal, ventral and dorsal face and each face represents key anatomical structures that must be considered (Figure 2).

Cranial & caudal face (faces 1, 2)

Cranially, the exiting thoracic nerve root and dorsal root ganglion form the cranial face of the pyramid and must be respected during initial needle passage, dilation, and cannula rotation to avoid nerve injury. The caudal face is the cranial portion of the ipsilateral caudal pedicle, whose width and cranio-caudal tilt govern how much pedicle resection is necessary to gain safe and effective entry into the ventral thoracic canal.

Dorsal & ventral face (faces 3, 4)

The dorsal roof of the KTS Pyramid is the ventral non-articulating surface of the SAP of the inferior vertebra; this is the true osseous limit of the corridor and key considerations must be taken pre-operatively to account for this boundary. The ventral floor of the KTS Pyramid is the posterolateral disc/endplate complex at the target level. This is made up of the dorsal inferior endplate of the superior vertebral body, the posterolateral annulus, and the dorsal superior endplate of the inferior vertebral body.

Medial and lateral face (faces 5, 6)

Medially the boundary is the spinal cord within the dural sac; any over-medialized docking risks cord compression in a canal that is narrower and less forgiving than in the lumbar region. Laterally, the pyramid is closed by the rib head and costovertebral joint; this complex sits relatively caudal to the disc plane at lower thoracic levels but migrates cranially towards the disc as one ascends the thoracic spine, progressively narrowing the posterolateral window. At lower thoracic levels the rib head and costovertebral joint sit caudal and away from the disc space, permitting a wider TF path. Moving cranially, the rib head and the costovertebral joints migrate closer to the disc level and progressively occludes the corridor (Figure 3).

Smaller volume with higher levels. Four KTS pyramids are portrayed at T11–T12, T8–T9, T5–T6, and T2–T3. As can be seen in this illustration, the KTS pyramid narrows as one goes more cranially as the rib head and the costovertebral joints migrate closer to the disc level and progressively occludes the corridor. Variations

It is important to note that not only does standardizing the approach to ventral thoracic spine pathologies using the KTS Pyramid help with planning for normal anatomy, it provides a framework to better understand variants as well. Anatomical variations should lead to further pre-operative planning and consideration. Important considerations can include a fused or collapsed disc space, a fused costovertebral or costotransverse joint, posterolateral disc osteophyte complexes, calcified lateral disc components, hyperostosis of the ventral or ventrolateral facet joints, artery of Adamkiewicz, and previous thoracic surgeries.

With collapsed disc spaces, or autofused rib articulations or hyperostotic facet joints, the window dimensions can be significantly narrowed by physical bony structures that are not normally present. Autofused rib articulations also means that the rib head is not mobile and some degree of bony resection will be mandatory to safely enter the thoracic foramen of interest. This is especially the case for levels T9 and higher, where the costovertebral joint at surface 6 of the KTS pyramid is directly in the path of foraminal entry. Because the artery of Adamkiewicz (great anterior radiculomedullary artery) most commonly arises between T8–L1 as mentioned, levels in this band that have suspicious findings should be considered for preoperative vascular imaging given the potential for the vessel to further reduce the safe window or alter the working trajectory. Depending on the patient’s surgical history, it is important to review the preoperative images specific to that previous surgery. For instance, if the patient had a previous open thoracotomy that involved the dorsal wall of the thoracic cavity, thoracic structures, including the pleura and lung, can drift and sit more dorsal. At times, these structures can be dorsal enough to obstruct safe entry into the foramen of interest. This is a direct contraindication to any approach or entry into these thoracic levels due to high risk of visceral injury.

Discussion

This work presents a formal geometric description and nomenclature for thoracic TF working volume, the KTS Pyramid, intended to standardize planning for endoscopic access to ventral thoracic pathology. By explicitly defining the corridor’s faces and their level-dependent behavior, we move beyond prior, loosely applied analogies from lumbar surgery or pain-medicine injections that lacked a thoracic-specific geometry and consistent borders.

Our observations reinforce that the corridor predictably narrows from T12 to T1 as the rib head and costovertebral joint migrate toward the disc plane, while pedicle cranio-caudal tilt further limits instrument angulation in the mid-thoracic spine (8,10,12). It is also important to note that entry into the pyramid is also affected by the distance between TPs of each level, which also narrows from T12 to T1. These anatomic trends mean that each thoracic level has a different difficulty profile and often a different amount of bony work required simply to reach the “floor” of the pyramid as we lay out by delineating type 1 through 3 KTS pyramid types (Table 1). Using a level-grouped schema (upper T1–5; mid T5–8; lower T8–12) with clear definitions of when needle-only targeting suffices versus when drilling/reaming is obligatory can make discussions precise and reproducible.

In the thoracic spine, targeting must incorporate the costovertebral/costotransverse morphology. The intimate relationship of the rib head and costovertebral joint to the disc plane, together with the shape and motion of the costotransverse joint, cannot be an afterthought; it is the lateral wall of the pyramid and often the proximate reason a drill-assisted entry is required at upper and mid-thoracic levels (9-11). Embedding these relationships into preoperative measurement [computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) along the intended working axis] will reduce failed docking and unplanned bone work.

The thoracic spine carries unique hazards: spinal cord injury, dural violation with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak (occasionally pleural fistula), epidural hematoma, pleural injury with pneumothorax/hemothorax, and vascular injury to segmental arteries or even the aorta (14-18). Historical pitfalls also include wrong-level surgery, incomplete decompression, intercostal neuralgia, and iatrogenic instability when excessive bone is removed (15,19). Endoscopy appears to reduce some complications relative to open exposures, but it introduces its own failure modes, unrecognized dural/pleural violation and confined-space bleeding, that magnify the consequences of imprecise targeting (14,17). Hence our emphasis on a predefined workflow: level-specific measurements, vascular mapping when indicated, explicit declaration of which face limits access, and a plan for drill-assisted expansion when the surgical window is insufficient.

Pathology-specific considerations when applying the KTS Pyramid

Using the KTS Pyramid as a common geometry helps translate very different thoracic pathologies into the same set of planning questions: which face of the pyramid actually limits access (cord/medial wall, rib head-costovertebral back wall, caudal pedicle, ventral SAP/roof, or the disc “floor”)? Can we reach the floor needle-only? Will drill-assisted expansion be required, or is an alternate corridor safer?

Thoracic disc herniation

Consistency, location, and migration drive feasibility. Soft, paracentral herniations generally suit a posterolateral/TF route, where limited foraminoplasty opens the back wall and allows decompression without cord manipulation. In contrast, calcified or giant discs and central lesions often exceed the surgical window, especially in mid/upper thoracic levels with minimal epidural reserve, so the safest plan may be drill-assisted access or a direct ventral decompression via anterior or retropleural approaches (16,20,21). Contemporary endoscopic series and reviews support that carefully selected cases can achieve favorable outcomes while minimizing soft-tissue trauma, but selection hinges on honest appraisal of the pyramid’s limiting face and the tolerance for cord manipulation (22).

Foraminal/lateral recess stenosis

When stenosis is driven by rib head–costovertebral osteophytes, SAP hypertrophy, or disc-osteophyte complexes, the lateral and dorsal faces are the constraint areas. Planning should include a foraminoplasty as an intentional step. A trans-SAP variant has been described in the lumbar literature, but this specific technique is uncommonly used in the thoracic spine in our practice for the many considerations mentioned in this article (23).

Posterior element hypertrophy

These dorsal pathologies do not target the canal floor so the application for KTS pyramid principles would not be appropriate for these pathologies. Interlaminar endoscopy with over-the-top decompression aligns better with the pathology. CT defines the extent and morphology of ossification (and alerts to dural ossification), while MRI quantifies cord compression and myelopathy. Complication profiles emphasize dural adherence and the need for incremental bone work under continuous visualization (14,20).

Ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament/ventral spondylotic stenosis

By definition, these produce central ventral compression with little epidural reserve; the medial wall (cord) “gives” very little. The ventral floor of the KTS pyramid should be comprehensively enlarged to minimize spinal cord manipulation and provide adequate space to deliver the ventral compressive components (24,25).

Neoplasms

For extradural ventral vertebral-body disease, the KTS corridor is typically adjunctive (biopsy or limited decompression) rather than definitive. Goals (oncologic control, stability, timely adjuvant therapy) often push planning toward retropleural/anterior exposure and staged fixation when the anterior column is compromised. Endoscopy can still add value by lowering morbidity and accelerating recovery (26,27).

Infection

When an abscess tracks to the ventral canal, foramen or ventral in the vertebral bodies or disc spaces, endoscopic debridement/drainage can be effective, but friable tissue and endplate loss shrink the safe window and raise instability risk; it is important in these cases to be explicit about stabilization strategy and pleural avoidance (28).

Trauma

Retropulsed fragments make the medial wall the dominant hazard and unstable injuries usually demand fixation first, with endoscopic decompression reserved for carefully selected, stable patterns requiring focal ventral or dorsal decompression (29).

Across all categories, the discipline is the same: identify the limiting KTS face, decide needle-only vs drill-assisted vs alternate corridor before incision, and pair that decision with targeted imaging (CT/MRI—and vascular mapping when indicated). Done consistently considering the KTS Pyramid turns heterogeneous thoracic disease into a reproducible, anatomy-first conversation that supports safer endoscopic care.

Learning curve and standardization

Thoracic endoscopy should be attempted only after substantial experience with lumbar and cervical endoscopic techniques and a formal proficiency pathway that includes cadaveric rehearsal and mentored cases; despite minimally invasive surgery benefits, the thoracic canal leaves little margin for error (22). For surgeons training or learning approaches for endoscopic thoracic surgery, the KTS Pyramid may provide a common language for mentorship.

Overall, the KTS Pyramid offers a foundational anatomical framework for thoracic endoscopic surgery. By defining corridor faces, highlighting level-dependent narrowing, and mandating vascular and bony constraints be measured in advance, it supports safer execution of these high-stakes operations and creates the substrate for future, pathology-specific refinements (8,12-14,22).

This work has several limitations. First, the KTS Pyramid is a conceptual framework derived from previously described thoracic anatomical relationships rather than an original cadaveric, radiographic, or morphometric analysis, and we did not independently quantify corridor dimensions or level-specific variability. Second, the framework has not yet been validated through cadaveric studies, imaging-based measurements, or clinical outcome data, limiting conclusions regarding its reproducibility, operative feasibility, and impact on surgical outcomes. Finally, while the geometric organization of the KTS Pyramid is novel, the individual anatomical structures that define its boundaries have been previously described in the literature. Future studies should focus on morphometric validation, intraoperative correlation, and clinical application of this framework across thoracic pathologies.

Conclusions

The thoracic foramen is a unique tightly constrained, level-dependent space, shaped by a narrow canal and cord, cranio-caudal pedicle trajectory, and the rib head-costovertebral/costotransverse complex, and it cannot be approached as a simple extrapolation of the lumbar spine. The KTS Pyramid provides an anatomic description that provides a shared language for what limits foraminal access at each thoracic level. Standardizing these windows and terms may shorten the learning curve for surgeons and support reproducible reporting. The proposed framework in this study will require validation and future multicenter studies to determine its utility.
