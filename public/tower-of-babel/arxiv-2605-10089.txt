A molecular perspective on coordination, screening, and emergent length scales in lithium electrolytes
Amaury Coste, Eva Zunzunegui-Bru, Ambroise van Roekeghem, Ioannis Skarmoutsos, Stefano Mossa

Abstract
Lithium electrolytes are commonly described using separate conceptual frameworks for local coordination chemistry, electrostatic screening, and ionic transport. This separation is effective in dilute conditions but breaks down at higher concentration, where coordination, ion pairing, clustering, and collective dynamics become intrinsically coupled. In this Perspective, we develop a unified multiscale framework that links local coordination motifs, mesoscopic ionic organization, and macroscopic transport within a single physical picture. Through representative examples spanning carbonate liquids, polymer electrolytes, concentrated systems, and confinement, we show that increasing concentration drives a systematic evolution from solvent-dominated Li + coordination to ion pairing, clustering, and correlated domains. In this regime, screening and transport are not independent phenomena but arise from the same underlying correlated structures. This perspective implies that rational electrolyte design must simultaneously control short-range coordination, mesoscale organization, and collective electrostatic response.

Introduction

The objective of this Perspective is to develop a unified multiscale framework that connects these levels of description. The conceptual development of this framework is illustrated in Fig.
1
. The central claim is that coordination, screening, and transport should not be treated as separate topics, but as different manifestations of the same underlying hierarchy of correlated structures. Local coordination determines the elementary motifs from which ion pairs, clusters, and larger ionic domains are built. Those larger structures, in turn, control dielectric response, screening, and the effective carriers relevant for charge transport.

The discussion is organized around representative examples, largely drawn from our own work where appropriate. Consequently, it should by no means be regarded as an exhaustive survey of the extensive literature in the field. We also emphasize, however, that the perspective developed here builds upon concepts and insights that are already present in the literature, albeit often in a fragmented or dispersed form. Most of the data and illustrations we consider originate from molecular simulations, which provide a consistent and controlled framework for analysis. This emphasis reflects the fact that experimental probes, while essential, are often not sufficiently selective to unambiguously isolate the most relevant microscopic degrees of freedom and their correlations. By contrast, simulations allow one to resolve shell composition, cluster statistics, and transport correlations at a level of detail that helps disentangle the underlying physical mechanisms, while the resulting physical picture is intended to remain broader than any single methodology.

We begin by examining local coordination in molecular and polymer electrolytes, and then introduce a hierarchy of length scales that links short-range structure to mesoscopic organization and electrostatic screening. We next address how confinement perturbs this hierarchy, and finally show how a unified description of transport and design principles emerges from this perspective. If established, such a framework could guide the rational design of electrolyte materials by explicitly connecting molecular-level structure to macroscopic transport properties. Within this picture, local coordination defines the effective charged entities, their aggregation governs screening, and the resulting correlated structures control transport. In what follows, we use “motif” to denote local coordination environments, “cluster” for mesoscopic ionic aggregates, and “domain” for larger-scale correlated structures at high concentration.

II
Local coordination as the elementary structural problem

II.1
Carbonate solvents and the tetrahedral motif

For conventional carbonate electrolytes, the classical starting point is the interaction of Li
+

At the same time, the tetrahedral motif should not be over-interpreted as a rigid molecular complex. The simulations of
61

+
is embedded in substantial structural heterogeneity extending to distances more than five times larger than the first coordination shell radius. This is already a first sign that the electrolyte cannot be reduced to a collection of isolated clusters. The shell is local, but its composition and dipolar environment are influenced by fluctuations on larger scales.

This point matters because the literature on carbonate solvation has long been marked by apparently conflicting coordination numbers extracted from Nuclear Magnetic Resonance, Raman, mass spectrometry, and simulations

;
53
;
22
;
10
;
73
;
74
. Part of the discrepancy is methodological, but part is physical: the ”coordination number” is not always a sharply defined observable. It depends on concentration, on the operational cutoff used, on whether one counts molecules or donor atoms, and on whether anions participate in the same shell. What survives across methods is the robust importance of a short-range carbonyl-oxygen cage around Li
+
, and the exceptional sensitivity of this cage to composition and concentration.

II.2
Preferential solvation in mixed solvents

Mixed solvents provide a clear demonstration that local coordination is not controlled by polarity alone. If electrostatics were the only criterion, the most polar cyclic carbonates would always dominate the first shell. Yet both simulation and electronic-structure work show a more subtle picture. For EC/DMC and EC/DMC/PC mixtures, DFT calculations on isolated clusters tend to favor more strongly polar components, whereas molecular dynamics in the liquid may predict enhanced DMC participation in the first coordination shell
13
;
11
;
61
;
56
;
10
;
74
. This is not a contradiction; it reflects the fact that local structure in the liquid is shaped by
packing
and by
collective dipolar cancellation
, not only by isolated binding energies.

The simulations of Ref.
61
are especially illuminating on this point. Within a locally tetrahedral shell, the vector sum of the solvent dipoles can be strongly reduced when similar molecules occupy the shell in symmetric arrangements. Under such circumstances, the total shell dipole may actually increase when a less polar linear carbonate contributes, because it disrupts otherwise more complete dipole cancellation. This is a remarkably important lesson for electrolyte design. The question is not simply which solvent binds Li
+
more strongly, but which mixture produces the most favorable balance of local geometry, shell dipole, and bulk transport.

II.3
Anion entry into the first shell

The most direct route by which coordination chemistry becomes a problem of charged-fluid physics is anion participation. In concentrated carbonate electrolytes, anions such as BF

The standard four-solvent tetrahedral picture remains a good reference for dilute conditions, but it ceases to be the correct local pattern under many battery-relevant concentrations. The relevant object becomes a cation–solvent–anion complex.

This result was generalized and refined in the quantum-chemical study of
56

molecular

interatomic
sense even as the molecular interpretation changes. This is an elegant example of how the physically relevant coordination descriptor must be chosen with care.

The transport consequences follow directly, as discussed in more detail below. When an anion occupies the coordination shell, the dressed lithium object is less positively charged and often less mobile under an applied field. This provides a clean microscopic rationale for the conductivity loss at high salt concentration
33
;
56
;
34
;
67
;
16

+
plus a neutral shell, but a partially neutralized correlated complex.

III
Polymer coordination and the extension of the same ideas to solids

Polymer electrolytes are frequently discussed as a separate class of electrolytes, but many of the structural ideas discussed above apply. The same coordination principles hold, but their consequences become inherently dynamical, as ion transport is now coupled to the slow relaxation of the host matrix
6
;
12
;
17
. The major difference is that the donor atoms belong to a deformable, slowly relaxing matrix rather than to a molecular solvent. As a result, coordination and transport become strongly entangled with the internal dynamics of the host.

In poly(ethylene oxide)-based electrolytes, Li
+
is stabilized by ether oxygens, and its migration is tied to polymer segmental motion
6
;
12
. In carbonate-based polymer electrolytes, such as LiPF
6
/polypropylene carbonate, the donor groups are more directly reminiscent of liquid carbonate solvents, but the dynamical landscape is much slower and more heterogeneous. The recent simulations of
17
show that such systems exhibit strong ionic correlations, only a limited fraction of truly free ions, and a large population of negatively charged clusters at the highest salt concentrations. Even when the local Li
+
–carbonate interaction is not very strong on a pairwise basis, the network of ionic and polymeric constraints can suppress the mobility of the cation more than that of the anion at experimentally relevant temperatures.

This, again, reinforces the same message: local coordination is the elementary structural problem, but not the whole story. The polymer matrix changes the statistics of site availability, residence times, and exchange pathways. What appears as a simple coordination shell in a static structural snapshot is, dynamically, a transient and constrained network. That network determines whether Li
+
moves by carrying a coordination shell, by exchanging among neighboring coordination sites, or by participating in the motion of larger ionic aggregates.

Hybrid polymer/ceramic systems offer another instructive example. The recent analysis of SiO
2
nanoparticles in PEO/LiTFSI shows that adding passive nanoparticles can reduce, rather than enhance, ionic conductivity above the melting temperature of the polymer
18
. The reason is not merely geometric obstruction, important as it is, but a reorganization of local coordination and species distributions into distinct regimes separated by a concentration threshold. In other words, filler addition changes the phase space of local coordination environments and, therefore, the statistics of transport pathways. The fact that this can be understood with the same coordination language used in liquids is conceptually important.

Finally, liquid-crystalline solid electrolytes provide an intermediate case in which ion-conducting domains become structurally ordered on nano-scales while still allowing local Li
+
hopping
35
. These systems make explicit what is otherwise hidden in conventional electrolytes: local coordination motifs can self-organize into mesoscopic channels, and transport then depends on the architecture of the coordinated domains as much as on the shell itself.

IV
A hierarchy of length scales

Building on previous work on ion pairing, clustering, and mesoscale ionic organization

Instead, it is useful to introduce a hierarchy of organization levels that bridge local coordination and macroscopic response. A schematic version of this hierarchy is shown in Fig.
1

At the smallest scale, the basic object is a
motif
: the local coordination environment of Li
+
, including solvent molecules and, at higher concentration, anions. This scale is set primarily by short-range chemistry—ionic radius, donor strength, steric shape, and solvent or polymer flexibility. In carbonate liquids it corresponds to the carbonyl-oxygen cage around Li
+
and is typically of order a few Å

;
33
;
56
. In polymers, the same scale is defined by coordinating ether oxygens, nitriles, or other donor groups embedded in a slower and more constrained matrix.

The next level is the
cluster
scale. Here local motifs are no longer independent, but assemble into contact ion pairs, solvent-separated ion pairs, and larger associated ionic aggregates. This is the first scale at which local coordination and concentration meet directly: whether the anion remains outside the first shell or enters it defines a genuine structural crossover. Cluster analysis in battery electrolytes has shown that even chemically simple mixtures can generate rich populations of associated ionic species
64
;
46
;
48
;
47
. In polymer electrolytes these associated structures may become long-lived and dominate the charge balance of the ionic sub-ensemble
17
;
64
;
46
;
48
;
47
.

At still larger distances one reaches
correlated domains
. These need not be permanent phases; they may instead be dynamically evolving regions over which charge correlations, mesoscale order, or partial network connectivity persist. In liquid-crystalline electrolytes, for example, ordered ionic domains and lamellae explicitly introduce this scale
35
. More generally, the correlated domain is the natural level at which one should discuss screening and collective transport, because it is this organization—rather than isolated ions—that determines how charge fluctuations are transmitted and relaxed.

This hierarchical description is useful because it unifies phenomena that are often treated separately. Local coordination determines the elementary charged motifs; those motifs assemble into clusters; and clusters or larger correlated domains control macroscopic observables such as screening lengths, conductivity, transference, and the validity or failure of Nernst–Einstein-like descriptions. In that sense, the hierarchy shown in Fig.

V
Screening: from Debye to correlated ionic domains

V.1
Why Debye–Hückel becomes insufficient

The Debye–Hückel picture remains the proper asymptotic starting point for low ionic strength
42
;
29
. In that limit, ions behave approximately as weakly coupled point charges embedded in a dielectric continuum, and the interaction between charges is screened over the Debye length. But battery electrolytes are usually far from that regime. The local dielectric response is heterogeneous, ion sizes are not negligible, molecular shapes matter, and ion pairing or clustering can be substantial.

Here, we focus specifically on two closely interrelated questions within the modern screening literature. First, how should one describe the crossover from monotonic Debye-like decay to oscillatory strong-coupling behavior

underscreening

V.2
A cluster-based interpretation of screening

The simulation study of Ref.
60
provides a particularly useful framework because it directly links structural, dielectric, and transport data in a single model system over a huge concentration range. Several points from that work are especially important for the perspective developed here.

First, the electrolyte does not simply become “more correlated” in a vague sense as concentration increases. Rather, the ionic organization evolves through identifiable structural regimes, including the growth, aggregation, and eventual
percolation
of ionic clusters
60
;
64
;
46
;
48
;
47
. Second, the correlation length relevant for screening can be interpreted more naturally when one assigns effective charges to correlated ionic domains instead of insisting on bare-ion charges.

Third, the largest deviation between the true conductivity and the Nernst–Einstein estimate occurs where electrostatic interactions are strongest and where the cluster-based description becomes essential. In this sense, the effective charge carrier undergoes a progressive renormalization with increasing concentration: from a solvated ion defined by local coordination, to a partially neutralized ion pair, and ultimately to correlated ionic clusters that act as the relevant objects for both transport and screening
60
;
20
;
21
;
46
;
47
.

This is a conceptually attractive result because it closes the loop with local coordination. If contact ion pairing and shell neutralization change the identity of the moving charge carrier, then they should also change the effective object that participates in screening. The same elementary redefinition of the carrier propagates upward in scale. Viewed in this way, screening is not an abstract continuum property layered on top of chemistry; it is the long-wavelength consequence of the chemistry having already reorganized the charged fluid into dressed, clustered entities.

V.3
Screening as a design parameter

From a practical point of view, the relevance of screening goes beyond surface force balance experiments. It determines the electrostatic coupling between charged species, influences dielectric friction, constrains ionic motion, and affects how electrolytes respond to confinement or interfaces. Because these properties are concentration dependent and structurally renormalized, electrolyte design should include not only first-shell binding arguments but also control over the growth of correlated ionic domains.

This idea is already implicit in several classes of electrolyte engineering. High-concentration formulations deliberately trade off some free-ion mobility for interfacial benefits and altered solvation chemistry
66
;
16
. Single-ion or anchored-anion systems aim to restrict the anion contribution to conductivity and reshape the effective screening environment
50
. Liquid-crystalline electrolytes attempt to impose mesoscale order on ionic pathways
35
. In each case, screening serves as a unifying physical diagnostic of whether the targeted local coordination motifs translate into the intended collective organization.

VI
Electrolytes Under Confinement

Confinement provides a particularly stringent test of the multiscale framework developed above because it introduces an external geometric length that competes directly with intrinsic ionic and correlation lengths. From this perspective, nanoconfinement is not a separate problem from bulk electrolyte physics, but a controlled perturbation of the same hierarchy of coordination, clustering, and screening scales
60
;
18
;
66
. Examples include nanoporous electrodes in supercapacitors, solid electrolyte interphases, and nanoporous separators. The key competition is between the ionic size
\ell_{\rm ion}
, the characteristic correlation length
\xi_{\rm corr}
, associated with the ionic domains and reducing to the Debye length in the dilute limit, and the confinement size
H
, see Fig.
3
.

When
H\gg\xi_{\rm corr}
, confinement has only a weak effect and the electrolyte remains essentially bulk-like. Once
H
becomes comparable to either the ionic size or the correlation length, however, qualitatively new regimes emerge. Local coordination is modified by excluded volume and interfacial fields, cluster statistics can change, and the effective electrostatic response may differ strongly from that of the bulk fluid.

A striking illustration is provided by ionic liquids confined in nanopores
40
. The Molecular Dynamics simulations of
49
have shown that the interplay between ion size, pore width, and intrinsic mesoscale ordering can produce re-entrant phase behavior, including capillary crystallization and ordered quasi-two-dimensional states. The structural state depends sensitively on whether the pore accommodates a frustrated packing of ions, a layered arrangement, or a bulk-like liquid organization.

Confinement can also alter screening in a qualitatively different way. In metallic nanopores, image-charge effects reduce the effective electrostatic interaction between ions and may allow ions of identical charge to occupy the same pore region, generating the so-called
superionic state
discussed in the theoretical and experimental literature

;
39
;
23

The broader lesson is that confinement makes the hierarchy of length scales experimentally unavoidable. Once the external dimension
H
approaches either
\ell_{\rm ion}
or the relevant correlation length, local coordination, mesoscale organization, and screening can no longer be treated independently. Although many examples come from ionic liquids and super-capacitors, the same logic applies to lithium electrolytes near interfaces and in confined morphologies: confinement modifies the very correlated structures that control both transport and screening in the bulk

VII
A unified picture of lithium transport

Within the framework developed above, transport is not an independent phenomenon layered on top of structure and screening. It is the dynamical consequence of the same correlated objects that define local coordination, ion pairing, cluster formation, and screening response. Fig.
4
summarizes three limiting transport mechanisms.

VII.1
Vehicular motion

The first limiting mechanism is vehicular transport: Li
+
moves together with a relatively persistent coordination shell. This picture is most natural in dilute carbonate electrolytes, where the cation behaves as a dressed particle defined by its local solvation environment
61
. It remains useful whenever solvent exchange is slow compared with translational motion, or when the shell identity is sufficiently long-lived that the cation effectively carries its local environment with it.

Vehicular motion is nevertheless not equivalent to ideal independent-ion diffusion. The mobility depends on shell size, solvent viscosity, dielectric friction, and the dipolar structure of the coordination environment. Preferential solvation therefore affects not only thermodynamics but also dynamics: two shells with similar mean coordination number can exhibit different mobility if they differ in symmetry, dipole cancellation, or solvent friction.

VII.2
Structural exchange and hopping

The second limiting mechanism is structural diffusion, in which Li
+
transport proceeds through exchanges between coordination environments rather than by persistent motion of a single intact shell. This mechanism is especially important in polymer electrolytes, where the cation migrates between transient coordination sites defined by polymer segments
6
;
12
;
17
, but analogous exchange processes can also occur in structured liquids and liquid-crystalline systems
35
.

In this regime, transport is controlled by the competition between residence times and exchange rates. If coordination is too strong, Li
+
becomes trapped; if too weak, dissociation and structural connectivity may be lost. Fast transport is therefore often associated with intermediate coordination strength, where local binding remains sufficient to maintain a connected ionic environment while allowing rapid rearrangement between neighboring motifs.

VII.3
Collective cluster motion

The third limiting mechanism is collective transport. In concentrated liquids and polymers, charge may be carried not only by nominally individual ions but by associated ionic entities or correlated domains. This generally reduces the accuracy of independent-particle transport pictures and is one important origin of large deviations from the Nernst–Einstein relation
21
;
20
;
17
;
60
. Depending on cluster charge and lifetime, collective motion may either reduce net conductivity through anticorrelated cation–anion motion or open new transport pathways within connected ionic morphologies.

This mechanism is the natural dynamical counterpart of the cluster-based view of screening developed in the previous section. If correlated domains provide the physically relevant screened objects in static correlations, they should also influence current relaxation, conductivity, and transference. In that sense, Nernst–Einstein failure is not merely a breakdown of a formula; it is a signature that the wrong effective carriers have been chosen.

The same considerations apply to the lithium transference number, which cannot be interpreted as an intrinsic single-ion property in correlated electrolytes. As ion pairing, clustering, and collective dynamics develop, the effective charge carriers become correlated entities rather than individual ions
21
;
20
;
55
. The measured transference number then reflects the net contribution of these collective modes, including anticorrelated cation–anion motion, and may become small or even negative despite significant lithium mobility
55
;
20
. In this framework, the transference number emerges as a direct dynamical probe of the correlated structures that govern both screening and transport.

VII.4
One framework, three weights

Vehicular, hopping, and collective mechanisms are therefore best viewed not as separate theories, but as three concentration-dependent weights in a common transport framework. Their relative importance depends on solvent composition, polymer relaxation, salt identity, and concentration. Dilute liquids are usually dominated by vehicular transport; intermediate concentrations show increasing shell exchange and ion pairing; highly concentrated liquids and many polymer systems require explicit cluster and network language. The central point is that transport evolves continuously with the same correlated structures that also control screening and mesoscale organization.

A key implication of this framework is that transport does not change through discrete mechanisms, but through a continuous redistribution of weights governed by the stability and dynamics of correlated structures. In particular, optimal transport often emerges in regimes where coordination is sufficiently robust to sustain connectivity, yet sufficiently labile to allow rapid reorganization. At higher concentration, where correlations extend over multiple length scales, transport becomes increasingly sensitive to the topology and lifetime of ionic aggregates and domains, and single-particle descriptions lose predictive power. In this regime, quantities such as the lithium transference number and the notion of single-ion transport must be interpreted in terms of these correlated carriers rather than individual ions.

From a design perspective, this implies that improving electrolyte performance requires controlling not only local coordination chemistry, but also the emergent mesoscale organization and collective dynamics it induces. We return to this point in more specific terms in the next section.

VIII
A Multiscale Computational Framework for Correlated Electrolytes

The hierarchy of length scales discussed above provides a unified picture of electrolyte structure. A natural next step is to translate this picture into a predictive computational framework (see Fig.
5
) capable of
propagating information
from the nanoscale, where chemical specificity resides, to the macroscale, where transport and screening are experimentally probed. This is not simply a problem of bridging spatial and temporal scales, but of identifying the relevant degrees of freedom at each level of description and ensuring consistent information transfer across qualitatively different representations.

Electronic-structure level: defining coordination motifs.

Electronic-structure calculations provide the most fundamental description of the electrolyte, by resolving ion–solvent and ion–ion interactions at quantum-mechanical scales

;
11
;
24
. Beyond binding energies, they provide information on polarization response, charge distribution, and the free-energy landscape associated with different coordination environments. Within the present framework, these calculations define the elementary coordination motifs that form the microscopic building blocks of the electrolyte. Importantly, the relevant information extends beyond isolated clusters: local dipoles, anisotropy, and environment response must also be characterized, as they influence assembly at larger scales.

Atomistic simulations: statistical realization of motifs and clusters.

Classical Molecular Dynamics simulations extend this description by sampling the statistical ensemble of coordination environments, cluster populations, and dynamical processes
61
;
60
. This level provides direct access to coordination-number distributions, ion-pairing statistics, cluster lifetimes, and current correlations entering transport coefficients.

At this scale, motifs become dynamically interacting entities that assemble into clusters and, at higher concentration, extended correlated domains. Deviations from Nernst–Einstein behavior and the emergence of collective transport can be directly linked to these correlations. Despite their richness, atomistic simulations remain limited in accessible time and length scales and do not directly provide a reduced description of the effective carriers governing macroscopic behavior.

Coarse-grained representations and emergent carriers.

A central step in the multiscale framework is the definition of coarse-grained variables that capture intermediate- and large-scale behavior. In the present context, these variables naturally correspond to clusters and correlated ionic domains that govern screening and transport. This requires identifying relevant structural objects through cluster analysis or connectivity criteria

;
46
;
48
, and assigning them effective properties such as charge, mobility, and interaction potentials. This mapping is not unique: effective charge depends on internal correlations and polarization, while dynamical properties depend on both internal structure and coupling to the surrounding medium. Preserving the physical content of this mapping is therefore essential.

Machine learning as a unifying layer.

Machine learning (ML) provides a powerful framework for both constructing interatomic models and identifying coarse-grained representations. At the atomistic level, machine-learned interatomic potentials enable simulations that retain near ab-initio accuracy while reaching system sizes and time scales inaccessible to direct electronic-structure methods

. This is particularly important for electrolytes, where polarization and many-body effects are central. More fundamentally, ML offers a systematic route to identifying collective variables governing information transfer across scales. Unsupervised and representation-learning methods can extract low-dimensional descriptors of local environments, cluster configurations, and network connectivity directly from simulation data

;
69
.

A particularly promising direction is the use of graph-based representations, where ions and solvent molecules are treated as nodes connected by interaction-dependent edges. Within this framework, clusters and correlated domains emerge naturally as subgraphs whose properties can be learned and propagated across scales

;
2
;

. Such approaches offer a direct route to linking local coordination patterns with macroscopic observables such as conductivity and screening length.

Interfaces between scales: consistency and information transfer.

A critical aspect of any multiscale framework is the treatment of couplings between different levels of description. These couplings are best viewed as mappings between distinct representations of the same physical system. Three mappings are particularly important. First, electronic-structure calculations provide information on local energetics, polarization, and charge transfer that must be translated into classical force fields or machine-learned interatomic potentials for large-scale simulations
5
;
3
. Second, atomistic simulations must be connected to coarse-grained descriptions in which molecular configurations are projected onto clusters or correlated domains
68
;
69
. Third, coarse-grained models must be linked to continuum descriptions, where transport, screening, and electrochemical response are defined at experimental scales.

Consistency across these mappings requires preserving key observables under coarse-graining, including thermodynamic quantities, structural correlations, and transport coefficients. Reduced models must therefore reproduce both static structure and dynamical observables such as current autocorrelation functions, collective diffusion coefficients, and dielectric relaxation. ML provides a natural framework for enforcing such consistency by learning mappings constrained simultaneously by multiple observables
51
.

From correlated structures to macroscopic response.

Within this framework, information propagates through a sequence of transformations linking microscopic structure to macroscopic response. Local coordination determines elementary charged species, cluster formation modifies their effective charge and mobility, and mesoscale correlations shape collective transport. Quantities such as screening length, conductivity, and transference number therefore emerge from this sequence rather than constituting independent inputs. The correlation length associated with ionic domains provides a natural extension of the Debye length beyond the dilute limit, while deviations from Nernst–Einstein behavior reflect the growing importance of correlated transport.

Several challenges remain in realizing this framework in practice. A first concerns the accurate treatment of polarization and many-body effects across scales, which are essential for quantitative electrolyte modeling
44
. A second is the identification of transferable coarse-grained variables that remain robust across chemistries and thermodynamic conditions
52
. A third is the treatment of rare events and long-time dynamics, which are often central to transport yet difficult to sample directly
65
.

Despite these challenges, the combination of electronic-structure methods, large-scale simulations, and machine learning offers a promising route toward predictive multiscale modeling of electrolytes
14
. By connecting coordination chemistry, mesoscale organization, and collective dynamics within a unified framework, this approach could transform electrolyte design from a largely empirical process into a predictive discipline.

The unified picture developed here has a direct design implication: local coordination cannot be optimized in isolation. A solvent, polymer architecture, or additive is not beneficial simply because it binds Li

Sodium-ion electrolytes provide a particularly important point of comparison. Because Na
+

+

Multivalent electrolytes introduce additional complexity. In magnesium or zinc systems, the stronger Coulomb interactions associated with higher ionic charge can produce more tightly bound solvation shells and stronger ion–anion correlations

. These effects may promote cluster formation and correlated ion motion even at relatively low salt concentrations, potentially leading to significant deviations from dilute-solution transport models. Understanding how coordination chemistry and electrostatic correlations interplay in such systems remains an important challenge for the development of next-generation battery electrolytes.

Finally, the view we have discussed here points to the need for improved coarse-grained descriptors for materials discovery. If the key variables are not merely dielectric constant and donor number, but shell dipole distributions, cluster charge statistics, and correlation lengths, then new descriptor sets will be required for both physics-based screening and data-driven design.

XI
Conclusions

The traditional separation between coordination chemistry, screening theory, and transport theory is increasingly unhelpful for modern lithium electrolytes. These systems are better understood as multiscale charged fluids in which local coordination motifs, ion pairs, clusters, and correlated domains form a connected hierarchy. Within that hierarchy, screening and transport emerge not from isolated bare ions, but from the statistics and dynamics of correlated ionic structures.

The physical picture may be summarized simply. At low concentration, Li
+
is often described reasonably well as a cation dressed by a local solvent shell. With increasing concentration, anion participation modifies that shell and partially neutralizes the dressed ion. The resulting motifs assemble into ion pairs, clusters, and sometimes network-like ionic domains. Those correlated objects, rather than nominally isolated ions, increasingly determine conductivity, transference, dielectric response, and apparent screening lengths.

The practical implication is equally direct. Electrolyte design should target the full hierarchy of structure shown in Fig.
1
, not only the first shell. Improving performance requires joint control of coordination chemistry, the onset of ion pairing, the growth of clusters, and the collective screening landscape that emerges from them. This is a more demanding program than local optimization alone, but it is also a more realistic basis for rational design.

Future work combining molecular simulations, advanced scattering techniques, and theoretical modeling will be essential to quantitatively connect these structural correlations with macroscopic transport properties. Such efforts may ultimately enable the rational design of electrolytes in which coordination environments, screening length scales, and correlated transport pathways are tuned to optimize conductivity and electrochemical stability across different battery chemistries.
