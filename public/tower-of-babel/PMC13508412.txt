In-office balloon dilation for baro-challenge-induced Eustachian tube dysfunction: study protocol for a randomized sham-controlled trial
Authors: Mohammad Aleinati, Jennifer L. Spiegel, Trung N. Le, Justin T. Lui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBalloon dilation of the Eustachian tube (BDET) is a potential treatment for Eustachian tube dysfunction (ETD). However, high-quality evidence for its efficacy in baro-challenge-induced ETD (BCETD) is lacking. Blinded, sham-controlled trials are critical in this context, particularly given the largely subjective nature of this condition. The objective of this study protocol is to test the hypothesis that BDET is superior to a sham procedure in reducing symptoms of BCETD.MethodsThis randomized, sham-controlled, multicenter trial will be conducted at multiple tertiary care centers in Canada. Adults experiencing BCETD symptoms and have failed conservative management will be randomized to undergo BDET or a sham procedure. The BDET group will undergo in-office balloon dilation under local anesthesia, while the sham group will undergo an in-office sham procedure. The primary outcome measure is the change in BCETD symptom severity as measured by the 7-item baro-challenge-induced Eustachian tube dysfunction questionnaire (BCETDQ-7), an adapted version of the 7-Item Eustachian tube dysfunction questionnaire (ETDQ-7), at 6 weeks post-procedure. Secondary outcomes include the ETDQ-7, ability to perform a middle ear pressure equalization maneuver, health-related quality of life (EQ-5D-5L questionnaire), and subjective work and activity impairment. Outcome measures will be assessed at baseline and at 6-, 24-, and 52-weeks post-procedure.DiscussionBDET may offer a minimally invasive therapeutic option for BCETD patients who fail medical management, but the lack of blinded and sham-controlled studies limits the reliability of patient-reported outcomes. This trial will offer the first blinded and sham-controlled study of BDET in the BCETD population. This design aims to reduce bias and improve the reliability of patient-reported outcomes. Upon study completion and data analysis, we intend to publish our findings in a peer-reviewed journal. If BDET is found to be effective, it may offer a novel, minimally invasive treatment option for BCETD, addressing a critical gap in management of this population.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT05719207. Background

The Eustachian tube (ET) is a dynamic canal that connects the nasopharynx to the middle ear space to serve multiple functions including pressure equalization, middle ear ventilation, and mucociliary clearance [1]. ET dysfunction (ETD) is an umbrella term that encompasses a spectrum of conditions of the ET, including obstructive ETD (OETD) and baro-challenge-induced ETD (BCETD). OETD is characterized by transient or chronic failure of ET opening, which can result in aural fullness, otalgia, and hearing loss. When paired with objective findings of abnormal middle ear ventilation, a diagnosis of OETD can be established. Contrastingly, BCETD is characterized by failure to equalize middle ear pressure during changes in ambient pressure. Symptoms include aural fullness, otalgia, and hearing loss with rapid barometric changes [1], such as during flying or diving [2, 3]. In severe cases, middle ear effusion or barotrauma (e.g., tympanic membrane perforation, hemotympanum, inner ear injury) may develop [4–6]. The prevalence of BCETD is not well established, but has previously been reported to affect 13–14% of commercial flight passengers [7]. Considering the approximate 4.4 billion air travelers worldwide in year 2023 alone [8], the global burden of BCETD is likely more substantial than is recognized.

The diagnosis of BCETD is primarily based on patient history, as objective measures such as otoscopy and tympanometry are often normal at normobaric pressures [1]. Although several objective and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been investigated in the context of BCETD, there is no clear consensus on the outcome measure of choice [9]. The 7-Item Eustachian tube dysfunction questionnaire (ETDQ-7) is a PROM developed for OETD [10] and has not been adequately validated in BCETD [11].

Conventional medical treatments including nasal steroids, decongestants, or antihistamines do not provide a sustained benefit in OETD [2, 12–14]. In the context of BCETD, a prophylactic dose of 120 mg of pseudoephedrine has been shown to prevent symptoms in adults, but the risk and side-effect profiles of this medication limit its use [15]. Pressure-relief ear plugs have shown discordant results in prior studies [16, 17]. Pneumatization devices have shown benefit in studies of limited power and quality [7, 18], but such devices may not be practical in all settings (diving, military aviation, etc.). Invasive treatment of BCETD includes tympanostomy tube placement, which does not address the underlying dysfunction of the ET and can also result in complications [19].

Since being first described in 2010 [20], balloon dilation of the Eustachian tube (BDET) has gained international popularity and regulatory agency approval as a therapeutic option in ETD. Although randomized clinical trials have supported the efficacy of BDET in OETD [21–25], this level of evidence in BCETD is lacking. A recent systematic review identified multiple, low-quality studies that support the efficacy of BDET, specifically in the BCETD population [26]. To date, no randomized, sham-controlled trials of BDET in BCETD have been published in the literature. Therefore, we opted to develop and describe a multi-center, randomized, sham-controlled clinical trial protocol examining the efficacy of BDET in BCETD.

Objectives and hypotheses

We hypothesize that BDET is superior to a sham procedure in improving symptoms of BCETD. Our primary objective is to determine if BDET is superior to a sham procedure in improving the primary outcome (BCETD symptom severity), as measured by an adapted version of the ETDQ-7 modified for BCETD, as well as secondary outcomes (ETDQ-7 scores, ability to perform a middle ear pressure equalization maneuver, work/activity impairment, and health-related quality of life) at 6-weeks post-intervention. Our secondary objectives include assessing long-term durability of outcomes at 24- and 52-weeks and re-demonstrating the safety and tolerability of in-office BDET.

Methods

This protocol is reported according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [27]. This study design is a randomized, sham-controlled, multicenter trial with two parallel groups and a primary efficacy endpoint of BCETD symptom severity at 6-week follow-up. The study will compare in-office BDET to an in-office sham procedure. Participants undergoing the sham procedure are explicitly offered the option to cross over to the intervention group at the time of their 6-week follow-up visit. Setting and participants

The study will be conducted at multiple academic, tertiary care centers across Canada. Patients referred to tertiary Otology/Neurotology clinics will be identified based on referral as possible study candidates. Potential participants will be screened against the eligibility criteria during the initial visit. Eligible participants are adults (≥18 years) experiencing BCETD symptoms at least every 4 months for the preceding year, despite a trial of conservative management (e.g., decongestants, pneumatization devices, or pressure-relief ear plugs). Exclusion criteria include signs/symptoms of patulous ETD; contraindication to in-office BDET (e.g., challenging nasal anatomy, or anatomy that would preclude dilation); concurrent need for adjunctive surgical procedures (e.g., septoplasty, tympanostomy tube); presence of tympanic membrane (TM) perforation, grade IV retraction, tympanosclerosis involving >50% of the TM, or tympanostomy tube; fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, active acute otitis media; uncontrolled sinonasal disease, gastroesophageal reflux, symptomatic temporomandibular joint dysfunction/disorder, immunodeficiency, or significant allergies; known internal carotid artery dehiscence of the bony ET; recent head and neck surgery within the preceding 3 months or planned surgery during the study period; history of radiation therapy to the head and neck; history of craniofacial abnormality; prior invasive intervention to the ET; and psychiatric conditions or cognitive impairment that preclude capacity for informed consent.

The trial information will be posted on the searchable research database of the coordinating institution, and will be circulated among local otolaryngologists, audiologists, family physicians, as well as chief medical officers of the two largest national airlines.

Randomization, allocation, and blinding

Participants will be randomized to either BDET or a sham procedure in a 1:1 ratio by the first author using the NIH clinical trial randomization tool before being placed in sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes [28]. Allocation concealment will be maintained by storing the sealed envelopes securely at the coordinating centre prior to commencement of enrolment. Each allocation will be retrieved in sequential order by the medical administrative assistant only immediately prior to the participant entering the procedure room, at the time of their scheduled procedure. The participant identification number and sequential allocation number are recorded in a designated allocation log maintained at the coordinating centre. The first author will not be involved in participant enrolment. Trial participants will remain blinded to their assignment until after the primary endpoint at 6-week post-procedure. Audiologists performing tympanometry/audiometry will also be blinded to group assignment at the primary endpoint. Participant unblinding will occur in the case of a serious adverse event (SAE). Given the nature of the intervention, blinding of the surgeon is not possible. All collected data will be entered into a level 4 electronic database, from which the first author will extract data for analysis while remaining blinded to group assignments.

Interventions

Both BDET and sham procedures will be performed on an outpatient basis in a minor procedure setting of a tertiary care hospital. This protocol has been adapted from previous literature [22, 29–31].

In the experimental group, participants will receive an oral anxiolytic (diazepam 5–10 mg) and the option of an analgesic (acetaminophen 1000 mg and/or ibuprofen 400 mg) 20–30 min prior to the procedure. The dose is adjusted based on age, weight, hepatic function, and renal function. During the procedure, participants are monitored by continuous pulse oximetry and blood pressure measurements every 5–10 min and as needed. Initially, 0.1% xylometazoline spray (4 sprays) followed by topical 4% lidocaine spray (2 sprays) are administered intranasally. Following this, an eye cover is placed to assist with the blinding process. Two cotton pledgets, each soaked in 1 mL of 4% lidocaine, are then placed along the floor of the nose bilaterally for 10 min. Both nasal passages are anesthetized to allow for the use of an angled scope in the contralateral nasal passage, if required. Following the removal of the pledgets, 0.5 mL of anesthetic ointment (e.g., 2.5% lidocaine/2.5% prilocaine, 4% tetracaine) is applied directly to the ET orifice using a curved suction under endoscopic visualization. Additionally, under otomicroscopy, 0.5 mL of anesthetic ointment is also applied to the lateral surface of the tympanic membrane of the ipsilateral ear. The previously placed cotton pledgets soaked in 1 mL of 4% lidocaine solution are replaced bilaterally for another 10 min. The ipsilateral inferior turbinate, septum, and posterior nasopharynx are palpated with a straight Frazier suction to confirm adequate analgesia. If required, 1 to 4 mL of 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine can be infiltrated into the nasal septum or torus tubarius using a 27-gauge spinal needle. Under direct visualization of a 0- or 30-degree rigid endoscope, a Stryker Audion ET dilation system (Stryker ENT, Plymouth, MN, USA) catheter is inserted into the ET orifice. The balloon is then inflated at a rate of approximately 1 ATM per second until the pressure reaches 12 ATM. The balloon is then held in place for 2 min, deflated, then slowly retracted.

In the sham group, several adjustments are made, including administering a smaller dose of oral anxiolytic (diazepam 2 mg) and reducing the optional analgesic doses (acetaminophen 500 mg and/or ibuprofen 200 mg). Moreover, the ET is neither cannulated nor dilated, rather dilation is simulated as the inflation syringe and balloon are disconnected.

Participants may withdraw consent and discontinue participation at any point. During BDET, specific discontinuation criteria include: (1) development of neurological signs or symptoms during dilation, (2) instability in vital signs, (3) severe procedural pain, (4) inability to cannulate the Eustachian tube safely, or (5) other unanticipated complications or findings during the procedure. Any SAEs will be reported to the coordinating centre research ethics board. All participants will continue to be followed, where possible, to minimize missing data.

Outcomes

Assessment of BCETD treatment outcomes is challenged by the lack of clinical consensus on measured outcomes. No previously validated, disease-specific patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) exists for BCETD. The ETDQ-7 was developed and validated specifically for obstructive ETD and its time-constrained framing does not adequately capture the episodic, activity-dependent nature of BCETD symptoms. Therefore, the ETDQ-7 questionnaire was adapted into a 7-item instrument specific to BCETD [the 7-item baro-challenge-induced Eustachian tube dysfunction questionnaire (BCETDQ-7)] (Table 2.). This was achieved by inquiring about symptoms encountered during barometric challenges rather than during the preceding 1-month timeframe. This study protocol is the first to implement this modification and will help establish its usefulness in BCETD. Since the BCETDQ-7 has not been previously validated, we will also administer the ETDQ-7. Additional PROMs will include subjective improvement in symptom frequency and severity (Likert scale), subjective work/activity impairment (5-item Likert scale questionnaire), and health-related quality of life (EQ-5D-5L questionnaire) [32]. PROMs are assessed at baseline and at 6-, 24-, and 52-weeks post-dilation. In terms of objective outcome measures, audiometry and tympanometry will be performed at baseline, as part of participants’ diagnostic workup. Tympanometry will be repeated at the 6-week and 24-week follow-up to ensure no confounding conditions, such as middle ear effusion or OETD, arise during the immediate follow-up period. Audiometry will be repeated at the 24-week follow-up to ensure no potentially confounding changes to participants’ pure tone thresholds during the immediate follow-up period. Results of audiometry will be documented including pure tone averages and air-bone gaps at each of the following frequencies: 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. Tympanometry results will be documented including type (A, As, Ad, B, or C), peak pressure, and compliance. Otomicroscopy will be performed at every encounter, both to assess for the presence or absence of a middle ear effusion or tympanic membrane retraction and to assess the ability to perform a middle ear pressure equalization maneuver. If the participant is unable to perform a modified Valsalva maneuver [33], they will be instructed on the Toynbee [34] or Lowry [35] techniques.

Sample size

A power calculation was not possible as the primary outcome measure (BCETDQ-7) has not been previously validated, with no established or estimated minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Based on prior sample sizes in BCETD studies [26, 36], as well as resource constraints, we opted to use a sample size of n = 24 (12 per arm). Given the absence of a validated MCID for the BCETDQ-7, the results of this trial should be interpreted as preliminary effect size estimates and may inform the design of future adequately powered trials. As the primary endpoint is at the 6-week follow-up, we anticipate a high retention rate, as participants will likely be incentivized by the possibility of crossover to the intervention group. Participants who cross over restart their assessment schedule from the date of the crossover procedure, with follow-up visits at 6, 24, and 52 weeks thereafter. Given the small sham-arm sample size (n = 12) and the anticipated high crossover rate, between-group comparisons at 24 and 52 weeks should be interpreted as descriptive rather than inferential if a substantial proportion of sham participants choose to cross over. All reasonable efforts will be made to promote participant retention and adherence to follow-up, including compensation for personal vehicle parking.

Data collection and management

Upon recruitment, each participant will be assigned a participant identification number. De-identified study data will be password-protected, encrypted, and accessible to the coordinating centre committee members only. Data will be stored for a total of 5 years from the study completion date, per the coordinating centre institutional policy. All study personnel will receive an initial orientation and briefing on the policies/procedures of keeping all information confidential. All study personnel have undergone institutional privacy awareness training and research ethics training.

Statistical analysis

A two-sample t-test will be used for between-group comparisons of continuous variables (BCETDQ-7, ETDQ-7, work/activity impairment questionnaire, EQ-5D-5L) at the primary endpoint (6 weeks). If a continuous variable is not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk Test, p-value < 0.05), a Mann–Whitney U test will be used instead. Longitudinal analysis of continuous variables across the 6-, 24-, and 52-week timepoints will be performed using a linear mixed model (LMM), with fixed effects for study group and baseline measurements. For categorical variables (ability to equalize middle ear pressure, otomicroscopy findings, tympanometry type), between-group differences at the primary endpoint (6 weeks) will be analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Longitudinal analysis of categorical variables will use a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). The use of a LMM or GLMM is justified because it allows for the inclusion of both fixed effects (e.g., treatment group, baseline covariates) and random effects, and is particularly well-suited for longitudinal data with the potential for missing data points [37].

Although an intention-to-treat principle will be used for the primary endpoint of the study (6-week follow-up), participants who do cross over after the primary endpoint will be analyzed in an as-treated manner. For the primary 6-week endpoint, a treatment-policy estimand is applied; all randomized participants are analyzed within their allocated arm irrespective of any subsequent crossover. For secondary outcomes at 24 and 52 weeks, an as-treated estimand is used. The LMM/GLMM will account for any missing data points, after performing a Little’s missing completely at random (MCAR) test [38]. All statistical testing will be conducted using R Statistical Software (v4.3.2; R Core Team 39 [39]. A significance level of α = 0.05 will be used throughout.

To help establish the usefulness of the BCETDQ-7, internal consistency will be assessed using Cronbach’s alpha to determine item coherence (α ≥ 0.7, indicating reliability). Sensitivity to change will be assessed by calculating the standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of BCETDQ-7 score change in the BDET group from baseline to 6-week follow-up, assessing responsiveness to intervention. Although not intended as a validation step, an exploratory Pearson correlation between BCETDQ-7 and ETDQ-7 scores will be conducted to assess their relationship.

Oversight and monitoring

Trial coordination will be the responsibility of the coordinating centre committee composed of the principal investigator, the first author, a research assistant, and a medical administrative assistant. The principal investigator is responsible for overall study governance, regulatory submissions, and sponsor communication. The first author oversees day-to-day trial coordination, data management, and communication with participating centres. The research assistant assists with participant scheduling, data entry, and regulatory documentation. The medical administrative assistant provides logistical and administrative support. At each participating center, a lead investigator will be designated and will be responsible for recruitment and execution of the study protocol. Meetings between the coordinating center committee and lead investigators will be planned at three time points: prior to recruitment, once recruitment has reached 50%, and at the conclusion of the study protocol. Additional meetings will be coordinated on an as-needed basis, for example in the case of any protocol modifications. All data will be collated and analysed by the coordinating centre committee.

A data monitoring committee has not been established since the planned intervention does not cause or prevent significant morbidity or mortality. Participants will be monitored throughout the duration of the study for any procedure-related adverse events. This will take place during each of the 6-, 24-, and 52-week follow-up visits. Any SAEs will prompt a detailed review of the study protocol and the implementation of necessary adjustments or trial termination, if indicated. SAEs will be reported to the local research ethics boards. No formal interim analyses are planned, given the small sample size, short recruitment period, and low-risk nature of the intervention. The trial may be discontinued early for safety reasons at the discretion of the principal investigator in consultation with the sponsor and relevant research ethics board. No formal independent audit of trial conduct is planned. Data completeness, protocol adherence, and recruitment progress will be reviewed by the principal investigator at each scheduled coordinating committee meeting.

Ethical approval and informed consent

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained via the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB22-1601). Participating institutions independently obtained IRB approval from their respective institutions. Written, informed consent for participation in the trial will be obtained by a board-certified otolaryngologist after reviewing the study’s goals, methodology, possible risks and benefits, participant rights, privacy safeguards, and guidelines for granting informed consent. Any amendments to this protocol will be submitted for approval to the relevant IRB prior to implementation. All approved amendments will be communicated to trial investigators and documented in the trial registration record (Table 3). Participants currently enrolled will be informed of any amendments that may affect their continued participation or willingness to remain in the trial. Dissemination plan

The findings of this trial will be disseminated through multiple avenues, including publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentation at national and international conferences focused on Otolaryngology and Audiology. After data analysis is completed, trial results will also be made available to participants on a per-request basis.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design or conduct of this trial. Trial results will be disseminated to participants on a per-request basis.

Discussion

This clinical trial protocol represents the first randomized, blinded, sham-controlled clinical trial assessing the efficacy of BDET in BCETD. Although BCETD reflects a milder degree of dysfunction along the spectrum of ETD, symptoms can be quite limiting for those frequently exposed to varying ambient pressures. BCETD may even pose a safety concern in high-stakes environments such as aviation or diving, where failure to equalize middle ear pressure can be catastrophic. Current non-surgical management options lack high-quality evidence of efficacy, as noted in a recent systematic review by Raymond et al. in 2022 [26]. Tympanostomy tubes, while effective, are also invasive, temporary, and can be disqualifying in some professions [40]. Additionally, they carry risks of complications, including persistent TM perforation, iatrogenic cholesteatoma, tympanosclerosis, chronic inflammation, and conductive hearing loss [41]. Given these limitations, BDET presents a promising minimally invasive alternative that warrants rigorous evaluation.

The primary innovation in this trial protocol is the sham-controlled design, which minimizes potential biases, particularly those related to the placebo effect. Although a sham-controlled trial of BDET in BCETD has not been published to date, Laakso et al. (2024) did publish a small (n = 15), sham-controlled pilot RCT in OETD patients [36]. Interestingly, their pilot study found no significant differences between BDET and a sham procedure in OETD patients, despite both objective and subjective outcome measures [36]. This finding strongly reinforces the necessity of our sham-controlled design in BCETD, where outcomes are almost entirely subjective.

The secondary innovation in this trial is the introduction of the BCETDQ-7, an adaptation of the ETDQ-7 tailored to activity-dependent symptoms triggered by barometric changes. While this is a relatively minor modification, this was necessary given that BCETD symptoms are activity-dependent rather than time-constrained (the ETDQ-7 queries symptoms only in the preceding 1 month). This trial will contribute to the validation of BCETDQ-7, potentially establishing it as a tool for future BCETD research.

We recognize that participant blinding may be challenging, given the procedural differences between the BDET and sham procedure. To minimize this, we will implement several measures to closely simulate the BDET procedure: placing eye covers, administering a subclinical dose of oral anxiolytics, applying nasal decongestants and local anesthetics to both the nasal cavity and the ET orifice, and operation of the balloon inflation handle. We believe that with proper implementation of these measures, the risk of blinding failure will be minimal.

Conclusion

This trial represents the first randomized, sham-controlled study evaluating the efficacy of BDET in BCETD. By addressing a critical gap in the literature, this trial aims to provide high-quality evidence to support or refute the use of BDET as a management option for BCETD. If BDET is found to be efficacious, this would provide a minimally invasive therapeutic option for a population with limited non-invasive treatment options.

Trial status

This trial was first registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov database on January 15, 2023 (NCT05719207). Study enrollment began in June of 2023. The first trial participant was enrolled in October of 2023. The trial protocol was submitted for publication in April of 2025, at which point enrolment was 50% complete.
