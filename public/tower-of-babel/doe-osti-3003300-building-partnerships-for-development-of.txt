1 Overview
Atmospheric dynamics often play a critical role in the sustainability and reliability of diverse forms
of energy production. This is especially true for the growing number of renewable energy de-
ployments that harness aspects of the environment for power production. While the University of
Memphis has a strong research background in energy systems, we have little experience working
with the Earth and Environmental Systems Science Division (EESSD) and their associated User
Facilities. Of particular interest to us is the Atmospheric Science Research and the Atmospheric
Radiation Measurement (ARM) user facility to address surface-boundary layer interactions and
physical phenomena. One of the major challenges for understanding and developing energy sys-
tems and management platforms is accurate modeling/forecasting of atmospheric conditions across
disparate spatial and temporal scales. These conditions are often required to understand the lowest
levels of the atmospheric boundary layer, but are also important to understand higher atmospheric
conditions where aerosols affect cloud development.
 The objective of this work was to develop partnerships with national laboratories for collab-
oration on environmental science and its intersection with sustainable energy systems, as well as
to leverage the ARM user facility data repositories to enhance our research capabilities in energy
systems and their inter-dependence on environmental systems for future engagement with EESSD.
Specifically, we accomplished these objectives by (1) developing collaborations with Oakridge Na-
tional Laboratory ARM Data Science and Integration Group which resulted in student internships,
(2) employed ARM data to develope modeling of the atmospheric boundary layer optical turbu-
lence, and (3) optimally-sized large-scale renewable energy systems and their associated energy
storage systems with ARM repository data.

 The University of Memphis (UM) is a diverse, growing, urban research institution, which re-

3 Project Objectives
Adverse conditions or inadequately-assessed sites pose challenges to the deployment of sustain-
able energy systems. All forms, but especially non-traditional and renewable systems, must be
adequately prepared and assessed for the effects of atmospheric dynamics in order to reduce the
LCOE. Of particular interest to us is the Atmospheric Science Research and the ARM user facility,
especially the new deployment of third ARM Mobility Facility in nearby northern Alabama, to ad-
dress surface-boundary layer interactions and physical phenomena that influence energy production
and costs. The overall objectives of the proposed work are the following:
  1. Developing Partnerships: We will build on initial relationships we have begun with scientists
and researchers at ORNL Environmental Science Division, especially with the ARM Data Science
and Integration Group. We will also look to build relationships with other national laboratories,
including the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), to enhance our collaboration on
environmental science and its intersection with sustainable energy systems;
  2. Leveraging User Facilities: We will seek to enhance our research capabilities in energy sys-
tems by leveraging the ARM user facility data repositories and to understand energy system inter-
dependence on environmental systems for future engagement with EESSD.
4 Accomplishments
1. Established partnerships in ORNL
Collaboration with researchers in the ORNL ARM Data Science and Integration Group involved
training two undergraduate students in ARM measurement data discovery, providing foundational
experience in the management and analysis of extensive atmospheric datasets. Both students sub-
sequently participated in the DOE Science Undergraduate Laboratory Internship (SULI) program
during the summer, contributing to the organization and curation of metadata to enhance ARM
data discoverability. Following this period, research activities continued with a focus on compu-
tational science applications to engineering problems. Building on their summer experience, the
students later joined the ORNL group as interns after graduation, further developing their expertise
in data-intensive atmospheric research and contributing to ongoing ARM initiatives.
Additionally, the PI attended yearly ASR/ARM PI meetings and presented preliminary outcomes
with other researchers.

2. Leveraged ARM measurements in modeling of ABL
Throughout this project, many different type of ARM observations in the ABL were examined.
In particular interest was how to address the effect of turbulence on the ABL with imaging. We
examined how spatial variations in the refractive index structure function coefficient (Cn2 ) within
the atmospheric boundary layer affect imaging and targeting missions conducted parallel or at an
angle to the Earth’s surface. Measuring and modeling Cn2 both vertically and horizontally remains
challenging, so the research leverages an extensive dataset from the ARM Facility at the Southern
Great Plains (SGP) site near Lamont, Oklahoma.

 3

Using three years of data (April 2020–April 2023) encompassing roughly 3,000 radiosonde sound-
ings, high-frequency meteorological tower measurements, and an eddy covariance tower, the study
investigates how atmospheric stability and surface energy fluxes influence Cn2 . Vertical Cn2 profiles
derived from soundings are decomposed into a mean structure, represented by the Hufnagel–Valley
model, and a fluctuation component based on statistical variability. A random forest regression
model is then trained to link the Hufnagel–Valley parameters and surface measurements corre-
sponding to each sounding.
A fourth year of data (April 2023–April 2024) is used for model testing and validation. Results
show that boundary layer height and surface Cn2 are the most influential predictors of vertical struc-
ture. Under unstable conditions, Cn2 is more strongly affected by sensible heat fluxes than under
stable conditions. Overall, the random forest approach enables high-frequency estimation of Cn2
profiles in the atmospheric boundary layer from surface-based measurements, improving temporal
resolution between radiosonde launches.

3. Leveraged ARM measurements in modeling of energy systems

An optimization framework for the reliable and cost-effective sizing of large-scale renewable en-
ergy systems and their associated energy storage systems (ESSs), while accounting for the uncer-
tainties inherent in renewable generation, is developed. Although government policies, incentives,
and carbon reduction goals have significantly lowered the cost of renewable electricity, the vari-
ability and intermittency of wind and solar resources make accurate system design challenging. To
address this, a Monte Carlo–based optimization approach is developed to quantify the effects of
uncertainty in wind speed and solar irradiance on system sizing and performance.
Using 15 years of high-resolution (15-minute interval) atmospheric data from ARM SGP, 1,000 an-
nual scenarios are generated through Monte Carlo sampling. These scenarios are then used within a
least-cost optimization framework to determine optimal system configurations over a 10-year hori-
zon. The results demonstrate that this method prevents oversizing and improves cost efficiency for
both residential and utility-scale renewable systems integrated with battery energy storage systems
(BESS).
While ESSs are essential for mitigating intermittency, demand side management (DSM) can com-
plement them by addressing operational limitations and improving flexibility. DSM, when paired
with ESSs, can reduce the required storage capacity while enhancing overall energy efficiency. The
proposed framework also investigates how the type of renewable source—solar or wind—affects
the performance and economic value of DSM, ESSs, and their combination. A critical comparison
highlights the influence of these sources on energy savings, curtailment, ESS sizing, and storage
costs.
A sensitivity analysis of the proposed model shows that system estimates can vary by a factor of
three depending on the assumptions used, emphasizing the importance of realistic consideration of
key parameters in long-term planning to avoid potential failure or loss. The framework is demon-
strated through a case study at the Natural Energy Laboratory of Hawaii Authority (NELHA), the
world’s largest multizone seawater utility. The optimization model identifies suitable renewable
and energy-saving strategies for this application and is validated against a state-of-the-art energy
