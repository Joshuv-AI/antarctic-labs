Endowing variational phase-field fracture models with custom strength criteria Journal:
Roberto Alessi, Matteo Brunetti, Roshan Udaram Patil, Jacinto Ulloa

Abstract
By now, several approaches have been proposed to endow phase-field fracture models with the ability to describe crack nucleation under multiaxial stress states. These include techniques for splitting the free energy, direct modifications of the phase-field driving or resisting forces that sacrifice the variational structure of the problem, and the introduction of additional internal variables, such as plastic strains or other nonlinear strains. In this paper, we propose a fundamentally different strategy for incorporating arbitrary elastic domains into phase-field fracture models, formulated within the variational framework of generalized standard materials. The proposed approach relies on letting the dissipation potential depend on the current state of the material. In this way, the variational structure of the problem is preserved, while elastic degradation and the strength criterion remain two distinct and independently controllable aspects of the material response. Simple yet representative models are presented and thoroughly discussed to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology. The resulting evolution of the elastic domain is investigated in both strain and stress spaces. Moreover, numerical simulations demonstrate a range of crack nucleation processes under multiaxial loading conditions for various analytical strength surfaces. This work paves the way for future developments and applications in several directions.

Introduction

The phase-field modeling of brittle fracture
[
14
]
is nowadays a consolidated theory within the
realm of fracture mechanics. The defining features of the phase-field approach that
have contributed to its efficacy and popularity are, among others, its underlying variational structure, its ability to capture geometrically complex and a priori undefined crack patterns, and its ease of implementation. Nowadays, innumerable models relying on the phase-field approach have been derived and explored, often coupled with other physical behaviors. Examples include extensions to plasticity
[

,
9
,
45
]
, viscoelasticity
[

,

,

, fatigue in brittle
[

,

,

and ductile

,

]
materials, dynamic crack propagation
[

,

]
, and various multiphysics problems
[

,

,
24
]
, to cite a few.

Despite the widespread adoption and remarkable success of the phase-field approach to fracture, several important issues remain open and deserve further investigation. Among the most relevant is the possibility of arbitrarily prescribing a strength criterion and consistently describing the evolution of the corresponding elastic domain as a function of the phase-field variable. This aspect is crucial for an accurate modeling of crack nucleation under mixed-mode loading conditions, which play a fundamental role in virtually all relevant fracture problems.

Several approaches have been proposed in the literature to address this issue. These can be broadly classified according to the adopted modeling strategy as follows:

(a)

Modification of the free energy through energy splits;

(b)

Direct modification of the PDE system through additional non-variational source terms;

(c)

Coupling with plastic strains or other nonlinear strains.

Approach
(a)
is undoubtedly the most widely adopted one

[
10
,
46
,
27
,
28
,
23
,
52
,
66
]
. Its main advantage is that the problem retains a variational structure. However, this approach generally lacks flexibility in describing arbitrary elastic domains and mixed-mode strength criteria.

An example of approach
(b)
that directly modifies the
resisting force
is found in
[
11
]
, where a mixed-mode cohesive-zone model is obtained through a suitable fracture toughness expression depending on different history variables. On the other hand, examples of approach
(b)
where the
driving force
is modified can be found in
[
44
,
68
,
67
,
33
,
25
,
1
]
. This approach allows for significant modeling versatility
[
33
,
32
]
, particularly for introducing strength criteria. Nevertheless, in all approaches belonging to class
(b)
, the variational structure of the problem is lost, together with the theoretical and computational advantages associated with it. Moreover, a feature shared by approaches
(a)
and
(b)
is that the elastic degradation behavior and the strength criterion are strongly coupled and cannot be tuned independently.

Finally, the flexibility required to describe arbitrary strength criteria is achieved in approach
(c)
by coupling the phase-field variable with plastic strains or other nonlinear strain measures. These formulations remain variationally consistent, introducing yield criteria in the ductile case or strength criteria in the quasi-brittle case via the support function of the convex set defining the elastic domain. The idea was originally developed for ductile fracture
[
5
]
, where degradation is coupled to plastic dissipation within a fully energetic formulation. Micromechanically motivated models have also been proposed within this framework, yielding natural energy splits for quasi-brittle frictional materials
[
64
]
. More recently, plastic strains have been replaced by reversible nonlinear strain measures to describe quasi-brittle fracture with tunable strength criteria
[
65
,
15
]
. In general, the class of models
(c)
is associated with cohesive fracture in the sharp-crack limit
[
21
,
38
]
. Although flexible, these models require additional internal variables that increase the complexity of the formulation and the resulting mechanical response.

The objective of the present work is to propose a new modeling paradigm for incorporating arbitrary strength criteria into standard phase-field models. As a consequence, the resulting framework is also able to describe mixed-mode fracture behavior. This is achieved by letting the dissipation potential depend on the loading direction in the
invariant stress–strain space
. Considering a state-dependent dissipation potential has been the key modeling strategy to endow, for instance, Griffith’s theory and phase-field models with fatigue effects
[
7
,
17
,
63
,
6
]
, or to recover a variational structure in non-associative plasticity
[
26
,
62
]
.
The proposed model is developed within the energetic formulation for generalized standard materials
[
50
]
. Therefore, the variational structure of the problem is retained. Moreover, elastic degradation behavior is kept well distinct from the underlying strength criterion, a feature we deem desirable as these mechanisms characterize different material properties, namely stiffness and strength. Indeed, the free energy, which defines the phase-field driving force, will encode which part of the elastic stiffness is degraded, while the dissipation potential, which defines the phase-field resisting force, will primarily encode the strength criterion and the corresponding elastic domain. The resulting model turns out to be very flexible, easy to calibrate, and of clear mechanical interpretation.

The paper is structured as follows. Sec.
2
discusses the approach for incorporating an arbitrary strength criterion into standard phase-field models within a variational framework. Sec.
3
presents specific material models to assess the flexibility of the modeling approach. These models, each owning different features, are investigated and compared through analytical and numerical analyses in Sec.
4

5

2
The general model

In this section, the general model is presented, and the evolution equations are derived within the energetic formulation framework.
The modeling setting is kept as simple as possible without loss of generality. Possible modeling extensions are addressed throughout the text.

2.1
Fracture energy depending on the load direction in stress/strain space

In order to incorporate a sufficiently general strength criterion into a phase-field fracture model, we let the fracture energy be a material function dependent on suitable variables that characterize the loading state of each material point. In this work, we limit our analysis to strength criteria that depend only on the first and second strain/stress invariants, that is, fully described in the volumetric–deviatoric strain and hydrostatic–deviatoric stress planes,
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}
–
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}
and
\sigma_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{h}}}
–
\sigma_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}
, respectively. Roughly speaking, the strength criterion is identified by the corresponding elastic domain that defines the states for which the mechanical response is purely elastic and no dissipative phenomena take place. A rigorous definition of elastic domain will be provided later.
In such a framework, the implicit loading direction can be completely characterized by a single scalar parameter, such as the loading angle
\vartheta
representing the loading direction in the volumetric–deviatoric strain plane or hydrostatic–deviatoric stress plane. We then let the fracture energy depend on this parameter,

G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}=G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}(\vartheta),

(5)

as depicted in Fig.
1
.
In this schematic illustration, the elastic domain in strain space is linked to the polar plot of
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}(\vartheta)
and evolves as a function of the phase-field variable
\alpha
.

, and associated with the fracture energy function with respect to the loading angle
\vartheta
in the
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}
–
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}

The
\vartheta

[

]
.

2.2
The variational framework

The local material behavior is first characterized within the framework of generalized standard materials (GSM)
[
29
,
54
,
55
]
. Global energetic quantities are then defined and the evolution equations are derived consistently with the energetic formulation
[
50
,
8
]
.

We consider a body occupying the domain
\Omega\subset\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$}^{n}
, where
n
is the spatial dimension. The state at a material point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega
is characterized by the displacement field
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and the irreversible phase-field variable
\alpha({\boldsymbol{x}})\in[0,1]
, with
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
corresponding to sound and fully damaged states, respectively.
Throughout the manuscript,
\alpha
will be referred to interchangeably as
damage
or
phase-field variable
.
Imposed displacements
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
are applied on the Dirichlet part of the boundary
\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}\Omega
, while imposed forces
\bar{f}
are applied on the Neumann part
\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{N}}}\Omega
, such that
\partial\Omega=\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}\Omega\cup\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{N}}}\Omega
and
\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}\Omega\cap\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{N}}}\Omega=\varnothing
. The body may be subjected to volume forces
{\boldsymbol{b}}
. The space of kinematically admissible displacement fields is denoted as
\mathscr{U}\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$}^{d})\;:\;{\boldsymbol{u}}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\text{ on }\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}\Omega\}
, while the corresponding space of admissible displacement variations reads
\tilde{\mathscr{U}}\coloneqq\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$}^{d})\;:\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ on }\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}\Omega\}
. Similarly, the space of admissible damage fields is given by
\mathscr{A}\coloneqq\{\alpha\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$})\;:\;0\leq\alpha\leq 1\}
, while the corresponding space of admissible damage variations reads
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}(\alpha)\coloneqq\{\tilde{\alpha}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$})\;:\;\tilde{\alpha}\geq 0\text{ if $\alpha<1$, $\tilde{\alpha}=0$ \text{ otherwise}}\}
.

2.2.1
The (local) energy densities

The material behavior is fully characterized once the way the material stores and dissipates energy is defined. In this regard, within the GSM framework, this is accomplished by defining two local energetic quantities, the
free energy density
and the
dissipation potential
.

The free energy density, here also called
elastic energy density
, is assumed to be convex with respect to strains and decomposable into a fully
degradable
part and a
residual
part as follows:

\boxed{\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\coloneqq g(\alpha)\,\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})+\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}),}

(6)

with

\alpha\mapsto g(\alpha)
the elastic degradation function fulfilling
Hypothesis 1
in
[
56
]
, namely being continuously differentiable in
\alpha\in[0,1)
and satisfying

g(0)=1,\qquad g(1)=0,\qquad g^{\prime}(\alpha)<0.

(7)

Energy expressions more complex than (
6
) can be considered. For instance, instead of a single degradation function, several functions degrading different parts of the elastic energy density can be used
[
11
,
69
]
.

The conjugate variables dual to the state variables
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
and
\alpha
are respectively given by

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)=g(\alpha)\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})+\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}),\qquad Y\coloneqq-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)=-g^{\prime}(\alpha)\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})

(8)

where
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is the
mechanical stress
and
Y
is the
phase-field/damage driving force
. In the spirit of
[
58
]
, a potential dual to the free energy density, that is, the
complementary energy density
, can be defined as

\psi^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha)\coloneqq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\Sym}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}-\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\right),

from which

{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha)=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\psi^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha).

(9)

The dissipative aspects of the model are encoded in the following state-dependent dissipation potential:

\boxed{\phi(\dot{\alpha},\nabla{\dot{\alpha}};{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha,\nabla{\alpha})\coloneqq\frac{1}{c_{w}}G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\left(\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}\dot{\alpha}+\ell\,\nabla{\alpha}\cdot\!\nabla{\dot{\alpha}}\right),}

(10)

where
\ell
is the internal material length, related to the phase-field localization band width, and
\alpha\mapsto w(\alpha)
is the phase-field dissipation function fulfilling
Hypothesis 1
in
[
56
]
, namely being continuously differentiable
\alpha\in[0,1)
and satisfying

0\leq w(\alpha)\leq 1,\quad w(0)=0,\quad w(1)=1,\quad w^{\prime}(\alpha)\geq 0.

(11)

For rate-independent processes, such as the quasi-static fracture problem considered in this work, the dissipation potential (
10
) coincides with the dissipated power. From a mechanical view point, the
fracture function

({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\mapsto G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)
encodes the load-direction dependence of the fracture energy
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}(\vartheta)
discussed in Sec.
2
, and must be positive to comply with the second law of thermodynamics
[
39
]
.
In standard phase-field models, the fracture function is constant and directly linked to the material fracture toughness; specifically,
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}=G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}
with
c_{w}=8/3
for the
AT1
model and
c_{w}=2
for the
AT2
model
[
61
]
.

It is worth noting that the dissipation potential (
10
) depends on both the strain and phase-field states, and in general cannot be expressed as a time derivative of any primitive function. Therefore, differently from standard phase-field models, the dissipated work considered here is not a state function. State-dependent dissipation potentials have by now proven effective in the modeling of a wide variety of complex phenomena, as discussed in the introduction.

As will be clear in Sec.
2.2.4
, the free energy form (
6
) primarily encodes which part of the elastic energy is degraded, whereas the state-dependent dissipation potential (
10
) tunes the shape of the elastic domain by means of
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)
. In the present model, these two aspects are kept well separated.

2.2.2
The global energy quantities

In order to set up the energetic formulation, global energetic quantities are defined, representing the global energy storage, dissipation, and exchange with the surrounding environment. The
total stored energy
functional reads

\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{q}})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{u}}),\alpha)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

(12)

with
{\boldsymbol{q}}\coloneqq({\boldsymbol{u}},\alpha)
. On the other hand, the energy dissipation between two states
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}
is characterized by the
dissipation distance
functional

\mathrm{D}_{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{q}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi\left(\dot{\alpha}(s),\nabla{\dot{\alpha}}(s);{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(s),\alpha(s),\nabla{\alpha}(s)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\text{with}\quad{\boldsymbol{q}}(s)={\boldsymbol{q}}_{0}+s({\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{q}}_{0}).

(13)

In (
13
), a straight monotonic (radial) path connecting
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}
is assumed, according to a directional local stability condition
[
2
,
17
,
63
]
. The
dissipated work
in a time interval
[0,t]
is given by

\mathcal{D}\left({\boldsymbol{q}};[0,t]\right)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\phi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha,\nabla{\alpha};\dot{\alpha},\nabla{\dot{\alpha}})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(14)

Similarly, we can define an
external work distance

\mathrm{D}_{\mathcal{L}}(t,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})
for the work done by external forces at a time instant
t
, and the
work of external actions

\mathcal{L}\left({\boldsymbol{u}};[0,t]\right)
over the time interval
[0,t]

[
63
]
.

2.2.3
Energetic formulation

According to the energetic formulation for rate-independent systems applied to GSM
[
50
,
49
]
, once the global energetic quantities are defined as in Sec.
2.2.2
, the evolution is determined by
stability
and
energy balance
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{q}}(t))\leq\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{q}}(t)+h\tilde{{\boldsymbol{q}}})+\mathrm{D}_{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{q}}(t)+h\tilde{{\boldsymbol{q}}})-\mathrm{D}_{\mathcal{L}}(t,{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)+h\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}})\quad\forall\tilde{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\in\tilde{\mathscr{U}}\times\tilde{\mathscr{A}},

(ST)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{q}}(t))+\mathcal{D}\left({\boldsymbol{q}};[0,t]\right)=\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{q}}(0))+\mathcal{L}\left({\boldsymbol{u}};[0,t]\right).

(EB)

Energy balance (
EB
) is an expression of the first law of thermodynamics, whereas the stability condition (
ST
) incorporates a state-dependent selection criterion for equilibrium states at a given time. Concerning stability, several notions exists; see
[
49
]
for a detailed overview. Motivated by
[
8
,
17
,
63
]
, we assume the evolution is governed by a local directional stability condition.

In case of smooth evolutions, necessary conditions for the evolution are the
first-order directional stability condition
and the
first-order energy balance condition

[
39
]
. Then, by standard arguments of calculus of variations, and proceeding as in
[
63
, Sec. 2.3.2, local formulation]
, we find that these first-order conditions are equivalent to the equilibrium equation

{}\div{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}+{\boldsymbol{b}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

(15)

with boundary conditions

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}={\boldsymbol{f}}\qquad\text{on $\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{N}}}\Omega$},

(16)

and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)+G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\left(\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}-2\ell\Delta\alpha\right)\geq 0\quad\text{ in $\Omega$},

(17a)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}\geq 0\quad\text{ in $\Omega$},

(17b)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)+G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\left(\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}-2\ell\Delta\alpha\right)\right)\dot{\alpha}=0\quad\text{ in $\Omega$},

(17c)

with boundary conditions

\nabla{\alpha}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}=0\qquad\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.

(18)

Here,
{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the outer unit normal to the boundary. Conditions (
17c
) are referred to as
damage
or
phase-field

criterion
,
irreversibility condition
and
loading-unloading condition
, respectively. We note that a full stability analysis of the model up to higher-order conditions, as performed in
[
57
,
2
,
34
]
, is possible but out of scope in the present work.

2.2.4
The elastic domain

The damage conditions (
17a
) and (
17c
) allow us to introduce the notion of elastic domain in both strain and stress spaces
[
58
,
39
]
. In the present context, in agreement with
[
66
]
, the elastic domain refers to the elastic limit of a material with a homogeneous damage distribution. Specifically, the elastic domain in strain space
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
and the elastic domain in stress space
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha)
read

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\Sym:-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\leq G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}\right\},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\Sym:\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\psi^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha)\leq G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha)\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}\right\}.

(20)

In the last expression, we have used
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha)=G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{c}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\alpha),\alpha)
and the constitutive relation (
9
). The strength surface, representing the boundary of the elastic domain, is then denoted in each space by

\mathcal{S}(\alpha)=\partial\mathcal{R}(\alpha),\qquad\mathcal{S}^{*}(\alpha)=\partial\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha).

(21)

In the following, we consider material models obeying
strain-hardening
and
stress-softening
properties for the elastic domain
[
58
]
, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\alpha)\subset\mathcal{R}(\beta)\quad\forall\alpha<\beta

(strain-hardening)
,

(22a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha)\supset\mathcal{R}^{*}(\beta)\quad\forall\alpha<\beta

(stress-softening)
.

(22b)

The strain-hardening property ensures uniqueness of the homogeneous response with respect to any strain level, whereas the stress-softening property ensures that in a homogeneous response the stress decreases as the damage evolves, allowing for the occurrence of damage localization.

Moreover, we assume that
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha)
are
{\boldsymbol{0}}
-star-convex, that is,
\forall{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
and
\forall\eta\in[0,1]
,
\eta{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
, and similarly for
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\alpha)

[
30
]
. This property ensures that an elastic-damaging material complies with the thermodynamic condition that admissible strain cycles always induce a non-negative interior work
[
40
,
66
]
.
The star-convex property also ensures that the unloaded material state
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
always belongs to the elastic domain, another fundamental thermodynamic requirement.

2.2.5
Numerical solution scheme

In this section, a numerical solution scheme for the evolution problem described in Sec.
2.2.3
is devised. Let us consider
n_{t}+1
discrete time instants
0=t_{0}<\dots<t_{n}<t_{n+1}<\dots<t_{n_{t}}=\bar{t}
, where all state variables are known up to
t_{n}
, and the goal is to find the state at the current time step
t_{n+1}
. For the sake of convenience, we define
\Box_{n}
as a generic quantity
\Box
evaluated at time step
t_{n}
. The same quantity evaluated at
t_{n+1}
will be denoted without subscript, namely
\Box\coloneqq\Box_{n+1}
.

As commonly done for phase-field models, we employ a staggered solution technique based on an algorithmic decoupling of the governing equations. An overview of the solution algorithm is shown in Alg.
1
. In this setting, the equilibrium problem (
15
)–(
16
) is solved for
{\boldsymbol{u}}
at fixed
\alpha
, and the damage problem (
17c
)–(
18
) is solved for
\alpha
at fixed
{\boldsymbol{u}}

Equilibrium problem

The weak form of the equilibrium problem (
15
)–(
16
) automatically descends from the first-order stability condition and reads

\int_{\Omega}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}},\alpha)\cdot\nabla_{s}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{\partial_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{N}}}\Omega}{{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S=0\qquad\forall\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\tilde{\mathscr{U}}.

(23)

This is a standard linear problem and is solved using a preconditioned gradient conjugated solver.

Phase-field problem

According to the first-order stability, the irreversibility, and first-order energy balance conditions (
17c
), the phase-field variable
\alpha
is found by solving the following set-valued PDE in weak form:

\int_{\Omega}\left(-Y({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\alpha)\,\tilde{\alpha}+G_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{f}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{n},\alpha_{n})\left(\dfrac{w^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\ell}\tilde{\alpha}+\ell\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\tilde{\alpha}\right)+\partial\chi_{\text{${\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$}^{+}}$}}(\Delta\alpha)\tilde{\alpha}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\ni{\boldsymbol{0}}\qquad\forall\tilde{\alpha}\in\tilde{\mathscr{A}},

(24)

where the characteristic function
\chi_{\text{${\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$}^{+}}$}}
accounts for damage irreversibility.

Finite element discretization

For all models, the damage field is discretized using standard linear Lagrange elements. On the other hand, the displacement field is discretized using Lagrange elements with either linear or quadratic shape functions, depending on whether the elastic energy is fully or partially degraded. In the latter case, a selective reduced integration of order one is performed for the space integral involving
\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}
. Such a numerical treatment, thoroughly discussed in
[
4
]
, is needed to avoid locking phenomena, which arise in damaged regions where
g(\alpha)\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})
and
\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})
, and consequently their effective elastic moduli, may differ in several orders of magnitude. Locking phenomena, occurring for instance in the common volumetric–deviatoric energy split used to model material non-interpenetration
[
18
]
, may lead to damage localization bands with an excessive thickness, thus overestimating the fracture energy and predicting erroneous displacement fields and crack path geometries.

3
Material models

In this section, we introduce and discuss a variety of material models that differ in elastic degradation and strength criterion. Modeling assumptions common to all discussed models are first presented. Most of these assumptions can be relaxed but are enforced for the sake of simplicity. In particular, they turn out to be sufficiently flexible to describe all key aspects of the present modeling approach.

3.1
Assumptions and features common to all considered models

Elastic behavior

For all considered models, a linear isotropic elastic behavior is assumed at fixed damage. For an undamaged material, we then have

\psi({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},0)=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\,\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}^{2}+\mu\,\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}^{2}

(25)

and

\psi^{*}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},0)=\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{h}}}^{2}}{\kappa}+\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}^{2}}{4\mu},

(26)

where
\kappa
and
\mu>0
are the bulk and shear moduli of the undamaged material, respectively.

Phase-field model

The underlying phase-field fracture model we consider is
AT1

[

7

g(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{2},\qquad w(\alpha)=\alpha.

(27)

Let us also define the damage-dependent compliance modulation function and its derivative as

s(\alpha)=g^{-1}(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{-2},\qquad s^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\dfrac{g^{\prime}(\alpha)}{(g(\alpha))^{2}}=2(1-\alpha)^{-3}.

(28)

Energy split and elastic degradation

Recalling the elastic energy decomposition (
6

25

\begin{cases}\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\,\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}^{2}+\mu\,\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}^{2},\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})=0,\end{cases}

(

\begin{cases}\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{D}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{+}\,(\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}^{+})^{2}+\mu\,\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{d}}}^{2},\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{-}\,(\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}^{-})^{2},\end{cases}

(

where

\begin{cases}\kappa^{+}(\alpha)=g(\alpha)\kappa,\\

By means of the constitutive relation (

Fracture function

Concerning the fracture function, we assume

The Drucker–Prager (DP) criterion is a classical model in geomechanics, widely used to describe pressure-sensitive failure in cohesive-frictional materials such as rocks and concrete, where compressive-shear failure plays a crucial role. Its use has been explored in recent phase-field models
[
64
,
66
,
33
]
. Owing to its dependence on the hydrostatic stress, the DP criterion is suitable for distinguishing tensile and compressive-shear strengths. In
M3
, this criterion is embedded in the proposed framework, assuming full elastic degradation according to (
FED
).

The DP elastic domain is a cone in stress/strain space, identified by two constants
\left\{a,b\right\}
defining the intercepts of the initial strength surface with the axes. Accordingly, during material degradation, these intercepts evolve as
\left\{a\,\epsilon(\alpha),b\,\epsilon(\alpha)\right\}
in strain space and
\left\{a\,\varsigma(\alpha),b\,\varsigma(\alpha)\right\}
in stress space. Fig.
6
illustrates the geometry of the elastic domain and the meaning of the parameters.

3.2.4
Drucker–Prager strength criterion with partial elastic degradation (model
M4
)

Differently from the previous models, we assume for
M4
that no degradation of the compressive bulk modulus can occur, corresponding to (
PED
) to account for non-interpenetration. Nevertheless, we wish to recover the same exact strength criterion of model
M3
, that is, the Drucker–Prager elastic domain (
52
)/(
53
). To this aim, it is sufficient to slightly modify the fracture perturbation function (
51
) as follows:

\tilde{f}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}},\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\left(\dfrac{b^{2}}{a^{2}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}}^{2}+({\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}}^{+})^{2}\right)-\dfrac{2b^{2}}{a}(\sqrt{1-\alpha})\,{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}}+b^{2}-1.

(54)

By straightforward calculations, the resulting elastic domain turns out to be the same as (
52
) and (
53
).

3.2.5
Huber strength criterion with partial elastic degradation and arbitrary tensile-volumetric/deviatoric limits (model
M5
)

Unilateral frictionless contact under compression was first addressed by
[
10
]
within the classical phase-field approach to brittle fracture, exploiting the volumetric–deviatoric decomposition of the elastic energy (corresponding here to (
PED
)). This model has been rigorously proven, in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence, to satisfy the non-interpenetration material condition, that is, to allow only non-negative normal jumps without affecting the tensile and shear behavior in the presence of a crack
[
18
]
. As in the case of full elastic degradation (Sec.
3.2.1
), when combined with a standard phase-field formulation, this energy split does not allow independent control of the shear and tensile strengths.

In the
M5
model, we show one possibility to overcome these limitations in the present framework by combining the flexibility of
M2
, which allows for arbitrary volumetric–deviatoric limits, with the non-interpenetration condition. Specifically, for
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}\geq 0
, we consider the same elliptical domain with prescribed vertices used in
M2
, taking
c(\alpha)=0
without loss of generality. For
\varepsilon_{\text{\scalebox{0.9}{v}}}\leq 0
, instead, the deviatoric limit is kept constant and is therefore unaffected by compression, as in a von Mises yield criterion. Accordingly, we employ two positive parameters
\left\{a,b\right\}
representing the intercepts of the strength surface with the axes in the undamaged material. During material degradation, these intercepts evolve as
\left\{a\,\epsilon(\alpha),b\,\epsilon(\alpha)\right\}
in strain space and
\left\{a\,\varsigma(\alpha),b\,\varsigma(\alpha)\right\}
in stress space. Fig.
8
illustrates the geometry of the elastic domain and the meaning of these two parameters. The resulting model turns out to have the sought flexibility to prescribe the shear and tensile-volumetric strengths independently, while retaining non-interpenetration.

4.2
Multi-axial nucleation tests

We conduct numerical crack nucleation tests for the biaxial disk problem, following the numerical solution scheme of Sec.
4
. In addition to the displacement boundary conditions (
58
), we impose the Dirichlet condition
\alpha=0
on the whole boundary of the disk throughout the loading process, thereby preventing fracture along the boundary.

The domain is discretized with standard triangular finite elements using an unstructured mesh. For models
M1
–
M3
, linear shape functions are considered with average mesh size
h_{1}=\ell/5
. For models
M4
–
M5
, which adopt the
PED
model, quadratic shape functions are adopted for the displacement field with doubled average mesh size, namely
h_{2}=2h_{1}=2\ell/5
, so as to keep the number of degrees of freedom approximately the same. This choice of shape functions is motivated by the fact that, as examined in detail in
[
4
]
, the
PED
model may exhibit volumetric locking problems, causing the damage localization bands to appear with excessive thickness, overestimating the fracture energy, and failing to predict correct crack path geometries. These issues are mitigated by adopting selective reduced integration for the undamaged part of the elastic energy. Further implementation details can be found in
[
4
]
.

The results of the considered models are reported in Fig.

. For loading directions along which the strength surface is not reached, damage evolution is never observed. For each loading direction, we plot the phase-field variable at the time step corresponding to crack nucleation, whenever nucleation occurs. For loading directions that do not trigger nucleation, a zero-damage field is shown for completeness. 5
Conclusions and perspectives

We have proposed a novel modeling strategy to endow variational phase-field fracture models with custom strength criteria. In contrast to prior approaches that either modify the free energy through specific energy splits, directly alter the phase-field evolution equation through non-variational source terms, or introduce additional internal variables such as plastic or nonlinear strains, the key idea here is to introduce a state-dependent dissipation potential that depends on stress/strain invariants, while leaving the free energy responsible for the elastic degradation behavior. Developed within the energetic formulation for generalized standard materials, this construction preserves the variational structure of the problem and clearly separates two distinct material features: stiffness degradation, encoded in the free energy, and strength, encoded in the dissipation potential through a prescribed elastic domain.

The resulting framework allows elastic degradation and strength criteria to be tuned independently. This feature has been illustrated through representative models, including full and partial elastic degradation together with double-ellipse, Drucker–Prager, and Huber-type strength criteria. The homogeneous response analyses and biaxial disk nucleation tests show that the proposed formulation can reproduce different mixed-mode nucleation behaviors while retaining a clear mechanical interpretation and flexible calibration. To capture unstable crack propagation events, future developments will employ time-regularizing strategies, such as inertial terms
[
36
,
16
,
20
]
and/or viscous effects
[
43
,
12
,
53
]
. Moreover, the present framework sets the stage for incorporating more complex material mechanisms into variational phase-field fracture models in a systematic way.
