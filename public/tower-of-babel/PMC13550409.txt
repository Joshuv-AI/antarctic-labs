Real-time yoga posture correction using deep learning for individuals with physical disabilities
Authors: Vansh Aggarwal, Vaibhav Yadav, Saloni Chaudhary, Gaurav Singal, Riti Kushwaha, Lokesh Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapidly increasing global adoption of yogic practice as a comprehensive inclination towards wellness has significantly increased the need for advanced as well as effective methods to recognize yoga postures. This study investigates a solution based on deep learning to deliver real-time yoga pose correction, tailored specifically for individuals with physical disabilities. For many, access to a yoga instructor or personal guide is not feasible, making artificial intelligence a practical alternative for offering pose guidance from home. Existing research on pose estimation in yoga primarily focuses on classifying poses for people without physical impairments. In our novel approach, we use the Thunder variant of the ultra fast MoveNet model, which enables real-time pose estimation, to identify and correct posture errors for individuals with lower-body disabilities. The proposed system applies deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to evaluate user posture through video inputs. This study enhances accessibility in chair yoga practices for people with physical challenges by providing accurate real-time pose corrections using deep learning methods, and by curating a state-of-the-art dataset for chair yoga poses, not worked upon before.

Literature review

Recent research emphasizes the progression of human pose estimation from two dimensional to three dimensional models, advancing from single-person to multiple-person posture analysis. Techniques for evaluating human posture are typically split into two primary groups: top-down and bottom-up approaches. The top-down approach in Kumar et. al. 1 involves detecting key points by first identifying human subjects, then applying a posture analysis algorithm. In contrast, the Bottom-up approach detects key points from multiple potential subjects and associates them with specific body parts using various data association techniques. This study introduces an approach for automatically identifying yoga asanas from both live and pre-recorded videos. The method leverages the OpenPose model to extract key points, followed by a combination of CNN and LSTM models for pose classification. The system achieves remarkable results, with an accuracy of 99.04% on individual frames and 99.38% accuracy after polling 45 consecutive frames. Furthermore, it attains 98.92% accuracy during real-time testing with 12 participants, highlighting its ability to handle diverse individuals and conditions effectively. The system operates seamlessly using input from a standard RGB camera. Potential enhancements include adding more asanas, expanding the dataset to encompass both images and videos, and adapting the system for deployment on portable devices for real-time predictions and self-guided training.

The study by B. Jo and S. Kim compares several pose estimation models, including OpenPose, PoseNet, and MoveNet 2. OpenPose is a pioneering open-source framework, developed by Cao et al. 11, for real-time multiple-person pose estimation. It employs a bottom-up approach to overcome the constraints of the top-down method. The model processes 2D images or videos, producing a confidence map from the input. After detecting an object, it draws a bounding box around it and assigns a probability score to the object. From this, OpenPose computes part affinity fields (PAFs). Finally, bipartite matching is applied using confidence maps and PAFs to produce full-body pose estimates. PoseNet, developed by Google Creative Lab, adopts the Top-down approach and is another open-source model capable of real-time human pose estimation12. Multi-person pose estimation, requires additional parameters: the maximum number of detected poses, a confidence score threshold, and a non-maximum suppression radius. These extra inputs enhance the model’s accuracy in detecting multiple individuals. MoveNet13 and Thunder15 differ in input size and depth multiplier, with Lightning accepting a fixed-size image (192×192) with a depth multiplier of 1.0, while Thunder uses a (256×256) input size and a 1.75 depth multiplier. Among these models, OpenPose provides the most detailed body tracking, whereas MoveNet Lightning excels in speed. OpenPose is capable of estimating poses for multiple individuals simultaneously. PoseNet, on the other hand, achieves the highest accuracy. However, the study’s limitation lies in its comparison of model performance solely based on images, without evaluating results from live camera inputs.Figure 3Comparison of pose estimation models.

The objective of a study by Dill et al.3 is to take an initial step in quantitatively evaluating the performance of MediaPipe Pose, focusing on its accuracy in calculating metrics instrumental to physical therapy while identifying its advantages and limitations. In an experiment, pose data from multiple individuals performing physical exercises were collected using two cameras and a motion capture (MoCap) system. Shoulder and hip widths were derived from MediaPipe data by calculating the Euclidean distance between shoulder and hip joints, respectively. To further enhance accuracy, a logical progression would be to integrate pose data from the two cameras, which could minimize angle dependency and mitigate the effects of self-occlusion.

Sometimes there are difficulties in maintaining accurate yoga postures. The research by Borthakur et. al.4 discusses about creating a system capable of identifying correct yoga poses and delivering real-time feedback. This approach holds promise for further research and demonstrates significant potential for deployment on simple smartphones, offering real-time pose evaluation to users practicing yoga at home with minimal latency.

The research by Haque et. al.5 employs ExNET, a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), to identify human poses from 2D images of human exercises. ExNET is a multilayer convolutional network designed to process grayscale images with an input size of 64 × 64. Their model was tested on a custom dataset comprising 2000 images, categorized into five classes. Several experiments were conducted on the test dataset, achieving a maximum accuracy of 82.68%. The research paper by M. Goni6 concludes that yoga asana is an effective method for treatment, correction and prevention of every common postural deformity. It lists out various asanas that should be performed for specific physical deformities. Asanas for deformities like Bow/Knock Knees, Flat Foot, Scoliosis, Kyphosis and Lordosis are described in this paper. Limitations of this paper are that it follows a generalized approach towards deformities, without considering intensity of a particular deformity for suggesting an asana.

Study by Madhusmita et al.7 examines the n impact of incorporating a Yoga program alongside physiotherapy for individuals with paraplegia. Their findings reveal that Integrated Yoga and Physiotherapy program for just a month is more beneficial in managing paraplegia patients compared to physiotherapy alone.

The study by k. Yadav8 examines the therapeutic effects of Yoga asanas on individuals with spine curvature disorders. The key conclusion of this review indicates that practicing yoga can help reduce pain and enhance functional abilities in patients experiencing spinal discomfort. However, the research relies on observed responses and does not establish a mathematical correlation.

The system introduced by Kale et. al.10, known as e-YogaGuru, provides recommendations for necessary adjustments in postures for each iteration of a vision-based system. These adjustments focus on: (i) the sequence of Yogasana postures, (ii) body angles within the postures, and (iii) the duration for holding the main posture. The system attained a 98.29% accuracy rate in recognizing Yogasanas and a 93.46% accuracy rate in recommending adjustments. Methodology

MoveNet is a model designed to accurately detect human poses in videos with intense motion. Unlike traditional pose estimation models, MoveNet is specifically optimized to handle dynamic and fast-moving scenarios, making it suitable for applications such as sports analysis, dance recognition, and gesture control.

At its core, MoveNet utilizes a deep neural network design as shown in Figure 6 that integrates convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with keypoint estimation modules. The model receives video frames as input and passes them through a series of convolutional layers to derive significant spatial characteristics. These features are then used to predict the locations of key body points. MoveNet leverages temporal information across video frames to improve pose estimation accuracy. By considering the temporal coherence of poses over time, the model can better handle motion dynamics and produce smoother pose trajectories. MoveNet incorporates mechanisms to handle occlusions and partial visibility of body parts.Figure 6MoveNet Architecture.

MoveNet Architecture.

MoveNet marks a major breakthrough in pose estimation technology, delivering cutting-edge performance in accurately identifying human poses, even in videos with rapid or complex movements. Its efficient design, resilience to occlusions, and capability to manage dynamic environments make it an essential asset for numerous computer vision and human-computer interaction tasks. Complementing MoveNet, a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model is employed due to its adaptability, allowing for the addition of more layers and neurons to effectively handle larger datasets. MLPs leverage multiple layers with nonlinear activation functions.

Multilayer Perceptron model

For each class, frames containing yoga poses are extracted from the videos in a sequence. Pose landmarks are detected using the MoveNet Thunder SinglePose model, which outputs 17 keypoints with their respective coordinates (x, y) and confidence score which indicates the confidence of the estimation of keypoint. These frames are normalized and converted to a numpy array. The model takes numpy array as input of size [1, 256, 256, 3] and produces output array of size [1, 1, 17, 3] which represent the 17 keypoints in the body and each keypoint of size 3. Invalid images such as those not in RGB format, are rejected. The minimum landmark confidence threshold of 0.4 was selected empirically based on preliminary experiments. Lower thresholds resulted in noisy keypoints and reduced model accuracy, while higher thresholds significantly reduced the number of usable frames. A threshold of 0.4 provided an optimal trade-off between data quality and quantity, ensuring stable model performance. Frames with pose landmarks of confidence scores below the threshold are discarded, and the remaining landmarks are flattened, and stored in CSV format. Separate CSV files are created for each class, and these files are combined to create the final training and testing CSV files.

Once the preprocessing is done, we move on to the calculation of Pose Embeddings. The pose landmarks are used to calculate pose embeddings, resulting in 34 embeddings per pose. Pose center and size are calculated using TensorFlow operations. The center (0,0) is computed by averaging the coordinates of left and right body parts, while the size is calculated by the gap between hip and shoulder centers or the maximum distance from pose center to each landmark, multiplied by a multiplier (2.5). Landmarks are then normalized to a constant pose size by subtracting the pose center from every landmark and dividing by the pose size. The normalized embeddings are flattened and converted into TensorFlow tensors for further processing.Figure 7Process Workflow.

Process Workflow.

Once we have both the preprocessed data and the pose embeddings tensors in our hands, we finally build our Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model28. As the embedding size is 34, the input layer of the MLP28 model receives inputs of size 34. The model comprises hidden layers with sizes of 128 and 64, respectively, with dropout layers (dropout rate of 0.5) added to prevent overfitting. Posture correction mechanism

Beyond pose classification, the system incorporates a posture correction mechanism that compares detected keypoints with reference pose templates. Deviations in joint positions are analyzed to identify incorrect posture alignment.

Based on these deviations, the system provides corrective feedback in the form of visual cues and textual suggestions. For example, if arm elevation is insufficient, the system prompts the user to raise their arms higher. This feedback mechanism enhances usability by guiding users toward proper posture execution in real-time.

Result analysis

Results for Multi-layer Perceptron model are evaluated on metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and ROC curve. The training procedure was carried out on NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 GPU using tensorflow and NVIDIA CUDA toolkit.

Performance evaluation

Validation accuracy is calculated by splitting the training set into validation with a size of 0.15. Deployment

TensorFlow.js is employed to save the model for deployment on the frontend using React.js. The model is hosted on a free cloud service provided by Vercel Blob. The final step is Real-time Model Deployment. The trained model is deployed for real-time pose classification on a React.js frontend using TensorFlow.js. The React-Webcam library is utilized to capture live video, and the MoveNet Thunder model is applied to extract landmarks. Landmarks with a keypoint score greater than 0.4 are detected in the live video, and the embeddings are passed to the above model for classification. The application records the duration for which poses are held and displays performance metrics such as the best time achieved. To validate the real-time capability of the proposed system, inference speed was evaluated on a system equipped with an NVIDIA GTX 1650 GPU. The model achieved an average processing speed of approximately 18–22 frames per second (FPS), demonstrating its suitability for real-time applications.

The average latency per frame was observed to be under 50 milliseconds, ensuring smooth user interaction during live pose detection. These results confirm that the proposed system meets the requirements for real-time deployment in practical scenarios.

Conclusion and future work

The proposed article provides a model and an application to successfully detect the correctness of 10 classes of chair yoga poses and 1 no pose class. It is the first and only study which provides pose detection for people with physical limitations, specifically chair yoga poses. The article also proposes a model for sequential detection of yoga pose frames throughout the performance of the pose. In the future, step by step instructions for each pose can be added to this model for better assistance to the user.

Future work in this field of study could include expansion of the dataset to increase the efficacy of the model. With higher data frames, other models can be used to increase the accuracy of detection by taking into account more number of data points in the body skeleton. After expert consultations, calibrated classes can be added to cater to the type and intensity of disability. This would ensure that the difficulty of pose correction is managed according to the degree of physical limitation of an individual. Moreover, higher computational power and more pre-processing of the dataset would help in distinguishing poses that are very similar to the no pose class with a better accuracy.
