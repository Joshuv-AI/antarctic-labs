Numerical Considerations for the Construction of Karhunen-Loève Expansions
Cosmin Safta, Habib N. Najm

Abstract
This report examines numerical aspects of constructing Karhunen-Loève expansions (KLEs) for second-order stochastic processes. The KLE relies on the spectral decomposition of the covariance operator via the Fredholm integral equation of the second kind, which is then discretized on a computational grid, leading to an eigendecomposition task. We derive the algebraic equivalence between this Fredholm-based eigensolution and the singular value decomposition of the weight-scaled sample matrix, yielding consistent solutions for both model-based and data-driven KLE construction. Analytical eigensolutions for exponential and squared-exponential covariance kernels serve as reference benchmarks to assess numerical consistency and accuracy in 1D settings. The convergence of SVD-based eigenvalue estimates and of the empirical distributions of the KL coefficients to their theoretical 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathcal{N}(0,1) target are characterized as a function of sample count. Higher-dimensional configurations include a two-dimensional irregular domain discretized by unstructured triangular meshes with two refinement levels, and a three-dimensional toroidal domain whose non-simply-connected topology motivates a comparison between Euclidean and shortest interior path distances between the grid points. The numerical results highlight the interplay between the discretization strategy, quadrature rule, and sample count, and their impact on the KLE results.

Introduction

Random field representations are a foundational tool in computational science and engineering, providing a rigorous mathematical framework for quantifying field uncertainty in physical systems. Applications span a broad range of disciplines, including subsurface flows, material science, and uncertainty quantification (UQ) for high-fidelity simulations. A common challenge in all of these settings is the need to represent, discretize, and sample from infinite-dimensional stochastic processes using a finite number of parameters, while preserving the statistical structure of the underlying field. The Karhunen-Loève expansion (KLE)
[
3
]
addresses this challenge by providing an optimal, mean-square-convergent series spectral representation of a second-order stochastic process in terms of a set of deterministic orthogonal basis functions and uncorrelated scalar random variables.

†
†
footnotetext:
Paper published as Sandia National Laboratories Technical Report SAND2026-18707.

In its continuous form, the KLE is computed via the solution of a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind, whose kernel is the covariance function of the process. The resulting eigen-pairs - eigenvalues and eigenfunctions - provide a hierarchical decomposition of the process variance, with the leading modes capturing the dominant spatial correlations. The KLE is optimal in the mean-square sense, i.e. any truncation to a finite number of terms minimizes the mean-square reconstruction error among all linear orthogonal expansions of the same dimension, which lends itself useful for dimensionality reduction. This property is shared with principal component analysis (PCA) in the data-driven setting
[
5
]
.

The use of KLEs in computational science and UQ gained momentum following the work of Ghanem and Spanos
[
2
]
, who employed it in the stochastic finite element method to propagate input uncertainties through partial differential equation (PDE) models. Their framework, combined with polynomial chaos expansions for the output quantities of interest, established a spectral approach to stochastic computation. Le Maître and Knio
[
4
]
provide a comprehensive overview of spectral methods for UQ that encompasses KLE methods. The accuracy and efficiency of the downstream UQ computation depends on the fidelity of the KLE used to parameterize uncertain input fields.

Numerous studies have examined the numerical aspects of constructing KLEs in practical settings. The discretization of the Fredholm equation on a finite spatial mesh converts the continuous eigenproblem into a discrete one. Betz
et.al.

[
1
]
provide a systematic comparison of discretization strategies - including the Galerkin, collocation, and midpoint methods - and their accuracy as a function of the mesh resolution relative to the correlation length. Schwab and Todor
[
6
]
analyzed the convergence properties of KLE approximations and developed fast multipole-based methods to reduce the computational cost of assembling and solving large covariance eigenproblems. Alternative computational approaches avoid the explicit formation of the covariance matrix by working with an ensemble of field realizations: the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the sample matrix yields the same eigenpairs as the discretized Fredholm equation when quadrature weights are incorporated appropriately. This connection to PCA and the SVD is well-established in the statistical literature
[
5
]
and motivates data-driven KLE constructions in settings where an analytical covariance model is not available. The choice of covariance kernel - exponential, squared-exponential, and others - governs the smoothness of the random field realizations and the rate of eigenvalue decay, which in turn determines how many KLE terms are needed for a given accuracy. Analytical eigensolutions exist only for a limited class of kernels on simple domains, e.g. the exponential kernel on a bounded interval
[
2
]
and the squared-exponential kernel on the real line under a Gaussian spatial measure
[
5
,
7
]
, and these serve as benchmarks for validating numerical implementations.

The accuracy of a numerical KLE depends on the interplay between several modeling choices: the discretization of the computational domain, the correlation length of the covariance kernel relative to the domain size, and - in the data-driven setting - the number of available random field samples. Under-resolved grids fail to capture the oscillatory structure of high-frequency eigenmodes, while an insufficient sample count introduces statistical variability in the estimated eigenvalues and may lead to empirical KLE coefficients that deviate from the expected distribution. These issues become particularly pronounced in multi-dimensional settings and on complex, non-simply-connected domains, where the choice of distance metric used to evaluate the covariance function (Euclidean versus shortest interior path) can affect the eigensolution.

In this technical report we present the theoretical underpinnings of the KLE, derive analytical solutions for the exponential and squared-exponential covariance kernels, and then study the effect of modeling choices highlighted above on the numerical KLE results. The report is organized as follows. Section
2
establishes the mathematical framework, presenting the Karhunen-Loève expansion, the Fredholm integral equation of the second kind, and the relationship between the continuous expansion and its discretized counterparts, including both the assembly-based and SVD-based solution strategies. Section
3
presents a numerical study of KLE construction across a range of mesh discretizations, correlation lengths, and sample sizes. One-, two-, and three-dimensional test cases are considered, including domains with unstructured meshes and a toroidal geometry for which the effect of the choice of distance metric (Euclidean versus shortest interior path) on the eigensolution is examined. Section
4
summarizes this report.

2.1
Definitions

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}})
be a probability space, with
\Omega
being the sample space,
\Sigma
a
\sigma-
algebra over
\Omega
, and
\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
a probability measure assigning probabilities for each event
\omega
in
\Sigma
. We consider a
d-
dimensional space
\mathfrak{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
Y(\bm{x},\omega)
, with
\bm{x}\in\mathfrak{D}
, e.g. spatial and/or temporal coordinates, and
\omega\in\Sigma
a random event, be a stochastic process over the space
\mathfrak{D}\times\Omega
,
Y:\mathfrak{D}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For any fixed
\bm{x}\in\mathfrak{D}
,
Y(\bm{x},\cdot)
is a random variable and for any fixed event
\omega\in\Sigma
,
Y(\cdot,\omega)
is a sample from a stochastic process over
\mathfrak{D}
.

We consider the case with second-order random variables (RVs), i.e. RVs with finite second-order moments, equipped with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
over the event space

\langle Y_{1}(\bm{x},\omega),Y_{2}(\bm{x},\omega)\rangle_{\omega}=\int_{\Omega}Y_{1}(\bm{x},\omega)Y_{2}(\bm{x},\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[Y_{1}Y_{2}].

(1)

Without loss of generality and to simplify notations below, we will assume a zero-mean stochastic process, i.e. one has already subtracted the mean:

Y(\bm{x},\omega)\rightarrow Y(\bm{x},\omega)-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[Y]

where
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[Y]=\int_{\Omega}Y(\bm{x},\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega)
.

Let
C(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})
denote the covariance function of the stochastic process
Y
. By definition, for an
L_{2}
space, the covariance function is symmetric, bounded, and positive definite. Furthermore, by Mercer’s theorem, we have that
C
admits a spectral decomposition in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of its corresponding Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator
T_{C}

(T_{C}f)(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathfrak{D}}C(\bm{x},\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}).

where
\mu_{\mathfrak{D}}
is a Lebesgue measure over
\mathfrak{D}
. Following Ref
[
5
]
, here we consider cases where there is a probability density
\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})
over
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})=\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
.

The eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the integral operator
T_{C}
are the solutions of the Fredholm equation of the second kind

\int_{\mathfrak{D}}C(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})f(\bm{x}_{2})\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x}_{2})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{2}=\lambda f(\bm{x}_{1}).

(2)

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are real, and arranged in decreasing order,
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots
. Further, the sum of their squares is finite

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}<\infty.

and

C(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}f_{k}(\bm{x}_{1})f_{k}(\bm{x}_{2}).

(3)

The eigenfunctions
f
are orthonormal over
\mathfrak{D}
under the measure
\mu_{\mathfrak{D}}
,

\int_{\mathfrak{D}}f_{k}(\bm{x})f_{l}(\bm{x})\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})=\delta_{kl}.

(4)

The Karhunen-Loève expansion of the zero-mean stochastic
Y(\bm{x},\omega)
is given by

Y(\bm{x},\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}f_{k}(\bm{x})\xi_{k}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\bm{x})\zeta_{k}(\omega).

(5)

In the first sum above
\xi_{k}
are uncorrelated RVs with zero-means and unit variances. In the second sum,
\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}
is folded inside the RVs. When using this form,
\zeta_{k}
are uncorrelated RVs with zero means and variances
\lambda_{k}
. These RVs are given by

\zeta_{k}(\omega)=\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\xi_{k}(\omega)=\langle Y(\bm{x},\omega)f_{k}(\bm{x})\rangle_{\bm{x}}.

(6)

2.2
Analytical Solutions of the Fredholm Equation

In this section we present two cases with covariance kernels that exhibit analytical solutions for the Fredholm equation eigenproblem in Eq. (
2
). In the first case, we consider an exponential covariance kernel on a finite 1D domain. In the second case we present results for a squared-exponential covariance kernel on an infinite 1D domain.

2.2.1
Karhunen–Loève Expansion for Exponential Covariance Kernel

Consider the one–dimensional, stationary exponential covariance kernel

C(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp(-|x_{1}-x_{2}|/{\ell_{c}}),

(7)

defined on the finite interval,
x_{1},x_{2}\in[0,L]
. Here,
{\ell_{c}}
is the correlation length of the kernel. We seek eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and orthonormal eigenfunctions
f_{k}(x)
satisfying Eq.
2
with
\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(x)=1/L
. To simplify the analysis, we will work with normalized coordinates

x_{1}\rightarrow x_{1}/L,\,x_{2}\rightarrow x_{2}/L,\,{\ell_{c}}\rightarrow{\ell_{c}}/L

using the normalized coordinates and keeping the same notation for the normalized coordinates

\int_{0}^{1}C(x_{1},x_{2})\,f_{k}(x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}=\lambda_{k}\,f_{k}(x_{1})

This equation admits an analytical solution for the corresponding eigenfunctions and eigenvalues

f_{k}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{2}{\ell_{c}}\omega_{k}}{\sqrt{{\ell_{c}}^{2}\omega_{k}^{2}+2{\ell_{c}}+1}}\left[\cos(\omega_{k}x)+\frac{1}{{\ell_{c}}\omega_{k}}\sin(\omega_{k}x)\right].

and

\lambda_{k}=\frac{2{\ell_{c}}}{{\ell_{c}}^{2}\omega_{k}^{2}+1},

where
\omega_{k}
is the solution of the equation

\tan\omega_{k}=\frac{2{\ell_{c}}\omega_{k}}{{\ell_{c}}^{2}\omega_{k}^{2}-1}.

For more details on the derivation to obtain the above expressions see Ref.
[
2
]
.

For large
k
,
\omega_{k}\approx k\pi
and

\lambda_{k}\approx\frac{2{\ell_{c}}}{(k\pi{\ell_{c}})^{2}+1}\approx\left(\frac{2}{\pi^{2}{\ell_{c}}}\right)\frac{1}{k^{2}}

(8)

and the eigenfunctions approach pure cosines

f_{k}(x)\rightarrow\sqrt{2}\cos(k\pi x).

For short correlation lengths,
\omega_{k}\rightarrow(2k-1)\pi/2
. Figure
1
shows the analytical eigenvalue spectra for several correlation lengths. Also shown, with dashed lines, are the asymptotic results corresponding to a small correlation length (
{\ell_{c}}=0.05
) and a large correlation length (
{\ell_{c}}=0.5
), relative to the domain size,
L=1
. For the small correlation length, the analytical and asymptotic results are close, verifying the estimates above. Similarly, the asymptotic estimates for large
k
are verified through the agreement observed for
{\ell_{c}}=0.5
for
k>30
.

2.2.2
Karhunen–Loève Expansion for Squared-Exponential Covariance Kernel

In this section we explore random fields generated by the covariance kernel

C(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp(-(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}/(2{\ell_{c}}^{2})),

(9)

over a one-dimensional space, with
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
. This case admits analytical results
[
5
,
7
]
for the KL expansion eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and eigenfunctions
f_{k}
, as follows.

We will use the same notation as in Ref.
[
5
]

a=\frac{1}{4\sigma_{x}^{2}},\,\,\,b=\frac{1}{2{\ell_{c}}^{2}},\,\,\,c=\sqrt{a^{2}+2ab}

and

A=a+b+c,\,\,\,B=b/A.

The eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are given by

\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{2a}{A}}B^{k-1},\,\,\,f_{k}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{x}\sqrt{c}}{2^{k-1}(k-1)!}}\exp(-(c-a)x^{2})H_{k-1}(\sqrt{2c}x),\,\,\,k=1,2,\ldots

(10)

where
H_{k}(x)
is the
k
-th order “physicist” Hermite polynomial, orthogonal with respect to
\exp(-x^{2})

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}k!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_{k}(x)H_{l}(x)\exp(-x^{2})dx=\delta_{kl}

(11)

with
H_{0}(x)=1
,
H_{1}(x)=2x
, and

H_{k+1}(x)=2xH_{k}(x)-2kH_{k-1}(x).

For a given choice of
\sigma_{x}
and
{\ell_{c}}
, the expression for the eigenvalues in Eq. (
10

\rho={\ell_{c}}/\sigma_{x}

\lambda_{k}=\frac{2^{k-\frac{1}{2}}\rho}{\left(\rho^{2}+2+\rho\sqrt{\rho^{2}+4}\right)^{k-1/2}}.

(12)

For
\rho={\ell_{c}}/\sigma_{x}=1/4
, the eigenvalues decay about
17\%
per mode and for
\rho=4
the decay rate is about
94\%
per mode, with two modes necessary to capture

of the process variance. For the case with
{\ell_{c}}=\sigma_{x}
, i.e.

60\%
per mode. Figure

2.3
Numerical Approximations via the Fredholm Equation

In practical settings we will consider a discrete mesh
\bm{X}=\{\bm{x}_{j},j=1,\ldots,{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{x}}}\}
, where samples of the stochastic process are available or will be generated. Associated with this grid are quadrature weights
w_{j}
that will be used to solve Eq. (
2
) numerically. In this report we will use trapezoidal quadrature for cases where samples points are chosen at random and Gauss quadrature when one can choose grid points according to specific quadrature rules.

The covariance matrix corresponding to the covariance function evaluated on this grid will be denoted by
K
with
K_{ij}=C(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
. For brevity we will use the notation for
\mathsf{f}_{k}
to denote the evaluation of the eigenfunctions
f_{k}(\bm{x})
over
\bm{X}
, with
f_{k}(\bm{x}_{j})\rightarrow\mathsf{f}_{k,j}
.
The orthonormality of eigenfunctions, via Eq. (
4
), implies that numerically

\int_{\mathfrak{D}}f_{k}(\bm{x})f_{l}(\bm{x})\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{x}}}w_{j}\mathsf{f}_{k,j}\mathsf{f}_{l,j}=\delta_{kl}.

(13)

The Fredholm equation (Eq. (
2
)) is discretized as follows

\int_{\mathfrak{D}}C(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})f(\bm{x}_{2})\mathrm{p}_{\mathfrak{D}}(\bm{x}_{2})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{2}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{x}}}w_{j}\mathsf{K}_{ij}\mathsf{f}_{j}=\lambda\mathsf{f}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{x}}}.

(14)

In matrix notation

\mathsf{K}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{f}=\lambda\mathsf{f}

(15)

where
\mathsf{W}
is a diagonal matrix with the quadrature weights on the diagonal,
\mathsf{W}_{jj}=w_{j}
. In this form, the
\lambda
and
\mathsf{f}
are eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathsf{K}\,\mathsf{W}
. However, unless
\mathsf{W}\propto I
, it is clear that
\mathsf{K}\,\mathsf{W}
is not symmetric. As a result, we do not have the desired symmetric positive definite eigenproblem with the above decomposition in terms of real eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

This can be alleviated by rewriting Eq. (
15
) as follows

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathsf{f}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{K}\mathsf{W}^{1/2})\,(\mathsf{W}^{1/2}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathsf{f}

\displaystyle\underbrace{(\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{K}\mathsf{W}^{1/2})}_{\mathsf{A}}\,\underbrace{(\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{f})}_{\mathsf{h}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\underbrace{\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{f}}_{\mathsf{h}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}\mathsf{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathsf{h}

(16)

The eigenvectors of
\mathsf{A}:=\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{K}\mathsf{W}^{1/2}
are orthonormal

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathsf{h}_{k,j}\mathsf{h}_{l,j}=\delta_{kl},

(17)

and, since
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{f}
, this results in eigenvectors
\mathsf{f}
satisfying Eq. (
13
).

Once eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and eigenvectors
\mathsf{f}_{k}
are available, one can obtain samples of uncorrelated RVs using a discretized form of Eq (
6
)

\xi_{k}^{(p)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}Y_{j}^{(p)}\mathsf{f}_{k,j}\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,\zeta_{k}^{(p)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}Y_{j}^{(p)}\mathsf{f}_{k,j}

(18)

where
Y_{j}^{(p)}
is the
p-
th sample at location
\bm{x}_{j}
of stochastic process
Y
, and
\xi_{k}^{(p)}
is the
p-
th sample of RV
\xi_{k}
.

2.4
Numerical Approximation via Singular Value Decomposition

Let
S
be the matrix constructed with samples of
Y
. Each column
i
of
S
is one sample
Y_{i}
. Each row
j
corresponds to grid point
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathfrak{D}
. Let
m
be the number of grid points and
n
the number of samples. The covariance matrix
K
can be constructed from samples
S
as

\hbox{\hskip 11.07066pt\hskip-3.47223pt\hbox{$\mathsf{K}$}\hskip-3.47223pt\hskip-11.07066pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle m\times m$}}\hskip-11.07066pt\hskip 11.07066pt}=\frac{1}{n-1}\hbox{\hskip 10.20259pt\hskip-2.77779pt\hbox{$\mathsf{S}$}\hskip-2.77779pt\hskip-10.20259pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle m\times n$}}\hskip-10.20259pt\hskip 10.20259pt}\,\hbox{\hskip 10.20259pt\hskip-5.92761pt\hbox{$\mathsf{S}^{T}$}\hskip-5.92761pt\hskip-10.20259pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle n\times m$}}\hskip-10.20259pt\hskip 10.20259pt}

(19)

Please note that in the above equation and those following below we display the size of matrices to highlight the equivalence between the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) algorithm and the discretized Fredholm equation approach described in the previous section. The SVD algorithm avoids the estimation of the covariance matrix, as follows.

Consider the sample matrix
\mathsf{S}
scaled as in
\mathsf{S}/\sqrt{n-1}
. Applying SVD to this matrix gives

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\,\hbox{\hskip 10.20259pt\hskip-2.77779pt\hbox{$\mathsf{S}$}\hskip-2.77779pt\hskip-10.20259pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle m\times n$}}\hskip-10.20259pt\hskip 10.20259pt}=\hbox{\hskip 11.07066pt\hskip-3.43752pt\hbox{$\mathsf{U}$}\hskip-3.43752pt\hskip-11.07066pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle m\times m$}}\hskip-11.07066pt\hskip 11.07066pt}\,\hbox{\hskip 10.20259pt\hskip-3.61111pt\hbox{$\mathsf{\Sigma}$}\hskip-3.61111pt\hskip-10.20259pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle m\times n$}}\hskip-10.20259pt\hskip 10.20259pt}\,\hbox{\hskip 9.33452pt\hskip-6.48317pt\hbox{$\mathsf{V}^{T}$}\hskip-6.48317pt\hskip-9.33452pt\raisebox{-8.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle n\times n$}}\hskip-9.33452pt\hskip 9.33452pt}

(20)

where
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{V}
are unitary matrices and
\mathsf{\Sigma}
is a rectangular matrix, generally non-square, with non-negative singular values
\sigma_{i}
on the diagonal. Let’s follow with some algebraic manipulations.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\mathsf{S}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\mathsf{S}^{T}=\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}\,\underbrace{\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{V}}_{\mathbb{I}}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}^{T}\,\mathsf{U}^{T}

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}\,\underbrace{\mathsf{\Sigma}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}^{T}}_{\mathsf{\Sigma}^{2}}\,U^{T},\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathsf{\Sigma}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}^{2}\,\mathsf{U}^{T}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}\,\mathsf{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}^{2}

(22)

The last expression above is the eigensystem for the covariance matrix
\mathsf{K}
when the quadrature weights are all equal to one, i.e.
W=\mathbb{I}
. For this case the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of the covariance matrix are the squares of singular values
\sigma_{i}
of the scaled sample matrix,
\mathsf{S}/\sqrt{n-1}
. The left singular vectors of
\mathsf{S}/\sqrt{n-1}
, that form the columns of
\mathsf{U}
, are the right eigenvectors of
\mathsf{K}
.

We can adapt the scaling of
\mathsf{S}
to accommodate non-uniform quadrature weights. Starting with the eigensystem in Eq. (
16
),
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{h}=\lambda\mathsf{h}
, we rewrite
\mathsf{A}
as

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\,\mathsf{K}\,\mathsf{W}^{1/2}=\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\,\frac{1}{n-1}\mathsf{S}\mathsf{S}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}^{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{S}\right)\,\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{S}\right)^{T}

(23)

Therefore the eigenvalues of
A
(corresponding to the discretization of the Fredholm equation) are the squares of the singular values of the weighted-scaled sample matrix:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\mathsf{S}

(24)

2.5
Uniform Scaling of Quadrature Weights

Consider a setup with where the covariance matrix
\mathsf{K}
is scaled by a constant factor, e.g.
\mathsf{K}\rightarrow\sigma^{2}\mathsf{K}
, i.e. the stochastic process has variance
\sigma^{2}
. The eigensystem in Eq. (
16
) becomes

\underbrace{(\sigma\mathsf{W}^{1/2})\,\mathsf{K}\,(\sigma\mathsf{W}^{1/2})}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\,\underbrace{\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\,\mathsf{f}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{h}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{\prime}\,\underbrace{\mathsf{W}^{1/2}\,\mathsf{f}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{h}^{\prime}}.

(25)

The scaling of
\mathsf{K}
, e.g. increasing or decreasing the variance of the random process, is equivalent to a uniform scaling of the symmetric matrix
\mathsf{A}
. Given that the eigenvectors are numerically estimated using algorithms that yield orthonormal vectors, this is equivalent to a new set of eigenvalues that are similarly scaled compared to the original system,
\lambda^{\prime}=\sigma^{2}\lambda
, and the same set of orthonormal eigenvectors,
\mathsf{h}^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}
. Thus

\mathsf{f}^{\prime}_{k}=\mathsf{f}_{k}=\mathsf{W}^{-1/2}\mathsf{h}_{k}.

4
Summary

This report examines numerical aspects of constructing Karhunen-Loève expansions (KLEs) for second-order stochastic processes, with emphasis on the interplay between spatial discretization strategy, covariance kernel structure, correlation length, and the number of available random-field samples. Two complementary solution pathways were considered: the direct numerical solution of the discretized Fredholm integral equation of the second kind via a symmetrized eigenproblem and the algebraically equivalent singular value decomposition of the weight-scaled sample matrix.

In one dimension, numerical results for the exponential kernel confirm that a grid of given spacing accurately reproduces the analytical spectrum up to mode
k
provided there are enough samples to capture the mode oscillations; eigenvalues are significantly more sensitive to under-resolution than eigenfunctions. The inter-seed variability of SVD eigenvalue estimates and the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the empirical KLE coefficient distributions from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, both converge at the Monte Carlo rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{spls}}}})
, uniformly across mode indices. For the squared-exponential kernel on
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
, eigenvalues decay geometrically with a per-mode rate determined entirely by the ratio
\rho={\ell_{c}}/\sigma_{x}
between the correlation length and the standard deviation corresponding to the spatial density. Gauss-Hermite quadrature is superior for
\rho\gtrsim 1
due to its extended tail coverage, while Monte Carlo sampling is competitive for
\rho<1
where the dominant eigenfunctions are spatially compact.

On a two-dimensional unstructured triangular mesh, the resolution criterion
{\ell_{c}}>\Delta_{\mathrm{max}}=\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{max}}}
is necessary for converged numerical solutions. The three-dimensional example features a multiply-connected domain, resulting in the relevance of chosen distance measures. The shortest interior path distance kernel is a physically consistent approach to estimate correlations between sample points. Differences between the shortest interior path and Euclidean kernels are negligible for low-order modes but become visible for higher mode indices.
