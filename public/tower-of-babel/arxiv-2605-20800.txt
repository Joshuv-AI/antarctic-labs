Introduction and Main Results

The maximal displacements of spatial branching systems have been investigated by many authors.
For supercritical branching random walk (
m>1

it can be traced back to Hammersley

Since then extensively studies on this topics have appeared in recent years, see for example

For critical or subcritical cases, the system dies out eventually,
so the maximal displacement of the system is finite almost surely.
Thus, it is natural to consider the tail probability of the maximal displacement.

The asymptotic law for the maximal displacement of a critical branching Brownian motion can trace back to Sawyer and Fleischman
[
24
]
.
For the critical branching random walk, see
[
19
,
21
]
and references therein.
In
[
21
]
, Lalley and Shao introduced a discrete Feynman-Kac formula, and proved that under mild moment condition

\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}x^{2}\mathbb{P}(M\geqslant x)=\frac{6\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}

(1.1)

where
\eta^{2}
is the variance of jump distribution and
\sigma^{2}
is the variance of offspring distribution. Moreover, they proved a conditional limit theorem, which shows

\mathcal{L}\left(\left.\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right|Z_{n}>0\right)\Longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(G),

(1.2)

where
G
is some random variable related to a super-Brownian motion.

For subcritical cases, by using the discrete Feynman-Kac formula, in
[
23
]
, Neuman and Zheng obtain the asymptotic law of the maximal displacement. Recently, in
[
12
]
, we reconsidered the subcritical branching random walk, and used the spine decomposition and optional line method refined the results. The results show that

\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}e^{\lambda x}\mathbb{P}(M\geqslant x)\quad\text{exists and belongs in}\quad(0,1].

(1.3)

where
\lambda
is the unique positive solution of the equation
\Lambda(\lambda)+\log m=0
, and
\Lambda(\lambda)
is the log-Laplace exponent of jump distribution, and
m\in(0,1)
is the mean of offspring distribution.

In this article, we are interested in a branching random walk in random environment (BRWre), i.e., a branching random walk in time-inhomogeneous environment, which has been introduced by Biggins and Kyprianou in
[
6
]
.
For supercritical branching random walk in random environment, the results about extreme processes are obtained by Huang and Liu in
[
17

and Mallein and Miłoś in
[

]
.

In recent work
[
13
]
, we investigated a kind of critical branching random walk in random environment (such system will die out eventually). We proved that
conditional on survival event
\{Z_{n}>0\}
,

\mathcal{L}\left(\left.\frac{M_{n}}{n^{\frac{3}{4}}}\right|Z_{n}>0\right)\Longrightarrow\mathcal{L}\left(A_{\Lambda}\right),

(1.4)

where
M_{n}
is the maximal displacement at generation
n
, and

is a random variable determined by some Brownian meander process. And based on above conditional limit theorem, we also proved that there exist some constants

and

such that

0<C_{1}\leqslant\liminf\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}x^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbb{P}(M>x)\leqslant\limsup\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}x^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbb{P}(M>x)\leqslant C_{2}<\infty,

(1.5)

where

is the maximal displacement of the whole system.
Compared with the constant environment case (i.e. the
eqs.

1.1
and

it reveals that, the conditional limit speed for

in random environment (i.e.,

The phenomenon stems from the essential effect induced by the random environment. The random environment allows the branching system, conditional on survival, to have more particles survive, which in turn leads to heavier tail behavior for the maximum position.

In this paper, we consider a kind subcritical branching random walk in random environment, and study the asymptotic behavior of the tail probability for the corresponding maximum displacement.

.
Then there exists some positive constant
C_{1}\in(0,1]
, such that

\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}e^{\lambda_{1}x}\mathbb{P}(M\geqslant x)=C_{1}.

(1.27)

Remark 5
.

The subcritical branching random walk in a constant environment can be regarded as a special case of
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)>0
cases subcritical branching random walk in random environment (i.e. the
eq.

1.25
is always satisfied for
\Lambda_{e}(\lambda)=\lambda\log m
).
From this perspective,
Theorem

2
also indicates the corresponding result for branching random walks in constant environment.

Under
\alpha>1
, if
\varTheta(1)=0
, the random walk
\{R_{x},x\geqslant 0\}
under
Q_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}
is recurrent. For such cases, besides the exponential decay
e^{-\lambda_{1}x}
, there is also an additional polynomial decay.

Theorem 3
.

Under
1
and
2
, assume
\alpha>1
and

\varTheta(1)=0
, which implies that
\Lambda_{e}(1)<0
and

\varTheta(1)=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(1)+\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{1})-\lambda_{1}\Lambda_{s}^{\prime}(\lambda_{1})=0.

(1.28)

where
\lambda_{1}
satisfies
\Lambda_{e}(1)+\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{1})=0
.
In addition, we assume that there exists some
\delta>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left(e^{X_{1}-\Lambda_{e}(1)}(\log_{+}\eta_{1})^{2+\delta}\right)<\infty.

(1.29)

Then there exists some finite and positive constant
C_{2}
, such that

\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{x}e^{\lambda_{1}x}\mathbb{P}(M\geqslant x)=C_{2}.

(1.30)

Remark 6
.

For the
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)=0
cases subcritical branching random walk in random environment, the condition
eq.

1.28
is essential, which implies that the system necessarily admits a nontrivial random environment. Under this condition, besides the exponential decay, for the further polynomial decay, the environment needs to be more favorable for the system to reach larger heights. This is the reason for the emergence of the additional
\sqrt{x}
decay.

Under
\alpha>1
, if
\varTheta(1)>0
, the leading exponential decay of
\mathbb{P}(M\geqslant x)
is not given by
e^{-\lambda_{1}x}
any more.
To obtain the probability estimate for such cases, we should introduce a reference function
\varTheta(\rho)
.

For any
\rho\in(0,\alpha]
, we have
\Lambda_{e}(\rho)\leqslant 0
. Thus, there exists a unique finite and positive number
\lambda_{\rho}
such that
\Lambda_{e}(\rho)+\rho\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})=0
.
The function
\varTheta(\rho)
is defined on
(0,\alpha]
, such that

\varTheta(\rho)=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(\rho)+\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})-\lambda_{\rho}\Lambda_{s}^{\prime}(\lambda_{\rho}),\quad\forall~\rho\in(0,\alpha].

(1.31)

Remark 7
.

About the equation
\Lambda_{e}(\rho)+\rho\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})=0
, it represents the balance between the cost required for a measure transformation on the environment and the cost required for a measure transformation on the jump distribution, thereby enabling the extraction of the dominant order of the tail probability of the maximal displacement. Strictly speaking, for the random variable
R_{x}
(see
eq.

2.30
), we should obtain
P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left[e^{\rho(R_{x}+\lambda_{\rho}x)}\right]=1
, then the leading order of tail probability of
M
is exactly given by
e^{\rho\lambda_{\rho}x}
.

About the function
\varTheta(\rho)
, following lemma provides some necessary properties.

Lemma 1
.

Under
1
and
2
,
the function
\varTheta(\rho)
is finite on
(0,\alpha]
, where
\alpha:=\sup\left\{\rho:\Lambda_{e}(\rho)\leqslant 0\right\}
, and is a strictly increasing function on
(0,\alpha]
. Moreover, we have

\lim\limits_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\varTheta(\rho)<0.

(1.32)

And if
\alpha<\infty
, we have

\varTheta(\alpha)>0,

(1.33)

which leads that in
(0,\alpha]
, the exist a unique zero point of
\varTheta(\rho)
.

Obviously, under
1
and
2
, according to the definition of
\varTheta(\rho)
, it is finite on
(0,\alpha]
.
Calculating the deviation of
\varTheta(\rho)
, we have

\varTheta^{\prime}(\rho)=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)-\lambda_{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\Lambda_{s}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{\rho}).

(1.34)

Under
1
and
2
, according to the convexity of log-Laplace exponent, for any
\rho>0
, we have

\Lambda_{e}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)>0
and
\Lambda_{s}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{\rho})>0
.
Also, for
\rho\in(0,\alpha]
, by the convexity and
\Lambda_{e}(0)=0
, we know that
\frac{\Lambda_{e}(\rho)}{\rho}
is a increasing function about
\rho
. Then, due to the relationship between
\lambda_{\rho}
and
\rho
(i.e. the equation
\Lambda_{e}(\rho)+\rho\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})=0
), for
\rho\in(0,\alpha]
,
\lambda_{\rho}
is a decreasing function about
\rho
. Therefore, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\leqslant 0
.
Combining with
eq.

1.34
, for any
\rho\in(0,\alpha]
, we have

\varTheta^{\prime}(\rho)>0,

(1.35)

which ensures that
\varTheta(\rho)
is strictly increasing on
(0,\alpha]
.

For
\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}
, under
1
, we have

\lim\limits_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\Lambda_{e}(\rho)}{\rho}=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(0)<0

(1.36)

And by the continuous of
\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)
and its inverse function
\kappa(\delta)

\lambda_{0}:=\lim\limits_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\lambda_{\rho}=\kappa(-\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(0))>0

(1.37)

which satisfies
\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{0})=-\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(0)
.
Then, be the continuity of corresponding function, we have

\lim\limits_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\varTheta(\rho)=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(0)+\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{0})-\lambda_{0}\Lambda_{s}^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})=-\lambda_{0}\Lambda_{s}^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})<0.

(1.38)

And if
\alpha<\infty
, under
1
, we have
\Lambda_{e}(\alpha)=0
and
\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
, then by the relationship
\Lambda_{e}(\rho)+\rho\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})=0
, we have
\lambda_{\alpha}=0
, thus

\varTheta(\alpha)=\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(\alpha)>0.

(1.39)

So far,

1
is proved.
∎

Note that, the function
\varTheta(\rho)
doesn’t require

For such cases, the exponential decay of

Theorem 4
.

Under
1
and
2
, assume that
\alpha>1
and

or
\alpha<1

(1.43)

Remark 8
.

For
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)>0
or
\alpha\leqslant 1
cases subcritical branching random walk in random environment, the condition

is essential. Under this condition, for annealed probability estimates, the maximum position can reach the target height by choosing the non-typical environments, whose associated cost is lower. This ultimately leads to a difference in the exponential decay of the annealed probability compared with other cases.

However, owing to the absence of a crucial and sharp upper bound inequality on the harmonic moments (i.e. the conditional expectation of

, which is defined in

2.31

.

Based on

2
,

and

, we classify the subcritical branching random walk in three cases.

Definition 1
.

About the subcritical branching random walk in random environment (BRWRE in short)

,

(1) if
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)<0
, then
(\mathcal{T},V)
is said to belong into Class I subcritical BRWRE.

(2) if
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)=0
, then

is said to belong into Class II subcritical BRWRE.

(3) if
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)>0
or
\alpha\leqslant 1
, then
(\mathcal{T},V)
is said to belong into Class III subcritical BRWRE.

Remark 9
.

If
\alpha>1
, depending on the sign of
\varTheta(1)
, the above classification is complete.
However, it is possible that
\alpha
is less than or equal to 1.
For the branching random walk in random environment falling into this case, it should note that the measure change on environment with exponent parameter
\rho=1
is excessive, which share the same intrinsic structure as the
\alpha>1
and
\varTheta(1)>0
case.
So, we categorized

as the Class III as well. And in the latter part of the paper, Class III admits a relatively unified treatment.
Moreover, the above classification incorporating both
\alpha
and the sign of

is exhaustive under
1
and
2
.

Remark 10
.

If
\Lambda_{e}^{\prime}(1)\leqslant 0
, i.e. the classical strongly or intermediately subcritical BPRE (see
[
18
]
), regardless of the specific structure of the jump distribution, we always have

and

, thus the corresponding subcritical BRWRE belongs into Class I cases.
And if

, i.e. the classical weakly subcritical BPRE, the corresponding structure becomes much more complicated, and all three classes outlined above can occur.

Remark 11
.

The classification comes form the affection of random environment. For the subcritical branching random walk in constant environments, see
[
23
,
12
]
, the tail probability of the maximum position exhibits only exponential decay, whereas for subcritical branching random walk in random environment, its asymptotic behavior is far more intricate. This profoundly reflects the essential impacts induced by random environments.

2
Preliminaries

Given the environment
\xi
, under
P_{\xi}
, the branching system
(\mathcal{T},V)
is a typical inhomogeneous branching random walk. Conditional on the first generation of the branching process, it is not hard to verified that the quenched probability
P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)
satisfies a typical non-linear stochastic difference equation. For differential equations, we can have the Feynman-Kac formula to formally construct the solutions. However, in the discrete case, similar tools may need to be further refined. For example, in
[
21
]
, they introduced a discrete Feynman-Kac formula and investigate the maximal displacement of critical branching random walk, and later in
[
23
]
and so on.
It should be noted that,
for the branching system, the recently developed size-biased measure change method may be a relatively effective approach. Also, inspired by our previous work
[
12
]
about homogeneous subcritical branching random walk, we can use the size-biased measure change and optional line to express the quenched probability
P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)
.

2.1
Some Results About Random walk

Assume that
\{H_{0}:=0,H_{1},\cdots\}
is a random walk with one step distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
(i.e.
\{H_{k}-H_{k-1}:k\geqslant 1\}
are i.i.d random variables with the distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
), and we just use
Q
denote the probability.
Define by
\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\sigma(H_{0},H_{1},\cdots,H_{n})
the natural filtration of the random walk, and set
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}=\sigma\left(\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)
.

For any
\lambda>0
, due to the martingale property of
\{e^{\lambda H_{n}-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)}:n\geqslant 0\}
(i.e the exponent martingale), by Kolmogorov consistency theorem, there exists a probability
Q_{\lambda}
defined on
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
, such that
for any
n\geqslant 1
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{\lambda}}{\mathrm{d}Q}\big|_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}=e^{\lambda H_{n}-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)}.

(2.1)

Under
2
, for
\lambda>0
, under
Q_{\lambda}
,
\{H_{0},H_{1},\cdots\}
is a random walk with positive drift
\Lambda_{s}^{\prime}(\lambda)
.
Thus, for any positive integer
x
, the first hitting time
\tau_{x}:=\inf\{k\geqslant 1:H_{k}=x\}
satisfies

\tau_{x}<\infty,\quad Q_{\lambda}\quad a.s.

And we are interested about the probability
Q_{\lambda}(\tau_{x}=n)
.

Lemma 2
.

Under
2
, for any positive integers
x,n
and
\lambda>0
, We have

Q_{\lambda}(\tau_{x}=n)=Q(\tau_{x}=n)e^{\lambda x-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)}.

In fact,
\{\tau_{x}=n\}\in\sigma(H_{0},H_{1},\cdots,H_{n})
.
According to relationship between
Q_{\lambda}
and
Q
, we have

Q_{\lambda}(\tau_{x}=n)=Q(e^{\lambda H_{n}-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)},\tau_{x}=n).

And under
2
, we know that
H_{\tau_{x}}=x,~Q~a.s.
.
Hence, the lemma is proved.
∎

2.2
Martingale, Optional line and Measure change

In this subsection, by measure change and optional line methods, we can obtain a useful expression about the probability of target event
\{M\geqslant x\}
for time-inhomogeneous branching random walk.

Given an environment
\xi\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbb{N}_{0}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{n}=-\infty
, where
\{S_{n};n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are defined in
eq.

1.9
.
Under the quenched probability
P_{\xi}
,
the branching system
(\mathcal{T},V)
is a time-inhomogeneous branching random walk. For any
\lambda>0
, define
W_{0}:=1
and for any
n\geqslant 1
,

W_{n}(\lambda):=\sum_{\nu\in N_{n}}e^{\lambda V\left(\nu\right)-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{n}}.

(2.2)

It is not hard to verify that under
P_{\xi}
,
\{W_{n}(\lambda),n\geqslant 0\}
is a martingale of the time-inhomogeneous branching system
(\mathcal{T},V)
.

For any positive integer
x>0
, define the optional line
\mathcal{L}_{x}
such that

\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad V(\nu)\geqslant x~\text{and}~V(\nu(k))<x~\text{for any}~k<|\nu|.

(2.3)

Although
\mathcal{L}_{x}
is a function on the nodes, it will be often convenient to identify
\mathcal{L}_{x}
with the set of nodes where the function takes the value
1
, and we also call
\mathcal{L}_{x}
the optional line. Abuse use of notations, we also use
\mathcal{L}_{x}
to represent the set
\{\nu\in\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1\}
.
Intrinsically,
\mathcal{L}_{x}
is the set of vertexes in
\mathcal{T}
which hit
[x,\infty)
for the first time.
Note that the optional line
\mathcal{L}_{x}
is a very simple line
(i.e. for any
\nu
, the value of
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)
is measurable with respect to
\sigma\left(\left\{X(\nu(i)),0\leqslant i\leqslant|\nu|\right\}\right)
, which means that whether
\nu
is on the line or not is determined by looking the trajectory of its ancestries).
Readers can see the conception about optional line in
[
6
]
and references therein.

Next, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}
be the function corresponding to the line
formed by the member of
\mathcal{L}_{x}

in the first
n
generations and the
n
-th generation nodes
with no ancestor in
\mathcal{L}_{x}
, i.e. for any
\nu\in\mathcal{T}
,

\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}(\nu)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\begin{cases}|\nu|\leqslant n\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1;\\
|\nu|=n\quad\text{and}\quad X(\nu(k))\in(-\infty,x),~\forall k\leqslant|\nu|.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Using above optional lines, together with additive martingale (eq. (
2.2
)),
according to Lemma 6.1, 14.1 and 14.3 in
[
6
]
,
for any
\lambda>0
, we define (set
\sum_{\emptyset}:=0
)

W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda):=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)e^{\lambda V(\nu)-\left|\nu\right|\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{|\nu|}},\quad\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)]=1.

(2.5)

W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}}(\lambda):=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{L}^{n}_{x}(\nu)e^{\lambda V(\nu)-\left|\nu\right|\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{|\nu|}},\quad\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}}(\lambda)]=1.

(2.6)

\{W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}}(\lambda)\}_{n\geqslant 0}~~\text{is a martingale and converges in mean to}~~W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda).

(2.7)

According to above relationship, for
P_{\xi}
almost surely, we have

\{M\geqslant x\}=\{\exists\nu\in\mathcal{T}:V(\nu)\geqslant x\}=\{\mathcal{L}_{x}\neq\emptyset\}=\{W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}>0\}.
Therefore, we know

P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)=P_{\xi}(|\mathcal{L}_{x}|>0)=P_{\xi}\left(W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)>0\right),

(2.8)

where
|\mathcal{L}_{x}|
is the size of the set
\{\nu\in\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1\}
.

Due to the non-negative martingale property, we can use the closed martingale
\{W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{n}}(\lambda)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
to construct a new probability
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, such that

\mathrm{d}\tilde{P}_{\xi}=W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)~\mathrm{d}P_{\xi}.

(2.9)

Thus, according to
eqs.

2.8
and
2.9
, we have

P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)=P_{\xi}(W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)>0)=\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)}\right]

(2.10)

And under the probability measure
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
(the well-known size-biased distribution), the branching system behaves as follow:

•

In generation 0, single particle (denoted by
\omega_{0}
) locates at
0
, and it is the initial spine particle.

•

For any
k\geqslant 1
, in generation
k
,
the spine particle survived at generation
k-1
(denoted by
\omega_{k-1}
) reproduces according to
F^{S}_{k}
(the size-biased distribution of
F_{k}
, which is defined in
eq.

1.8
). Then, uniformly choose one child as the next spine particle (denoted by
\omega_{k}
), and this spine particle moves according to
e^{\lambda y-\Lambda_{s}(y)}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
. Except spine particle, other newborn particles are normal, and move according to
\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
.

•

For any
k\geqslant 1
, in generation
k
, except spine particle, normal particles behave as usual (i,e. they reproduce according to
F_{k}
and all children move independently according to jump distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
).

Denote by
\{V(\omega_{0}):=0,V(\omega_{1}),\cdots\}
the trajectory of spine particles. According to above construction, we know that the distribution of
\{V(\omega_{0}):=0,V(\omega_{1}),\cdots\}
under
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
is the same as the distribution of
\left\{H_{0},H_{1},\cdots\right\}
under
Q_{\lambda}
(defined in
Section

2.1
) and is independent with the environment
\xi
.
For any integer
x\geqslant 1
, denote by

\tau_{x}:=\inf\{k\geqslant 0:V(\omega_{k})\geqslant x\}

(2.11)

the first hitting time of the trajectory.
Let
\mathcal{G}:=\sigma\left(\left\{V(\omega_{0}):=0,V(\omega_{1}),\cdots\right\}\right)
by the
\sigma
field generated by the spine’s trajectory.
And under
2
, the random walk with one step distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
doesn’t experience overshoot. Therefore, for any
\nu\in\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1
, we have
V(\nu)=x
.

According to above construction, under the size-biased probability measure
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, we have

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{\tau_{x}}}+\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1}e^{\lambda V(\nu)-|\nu|\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{|\nu|}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda x-\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{\tau_{x}}}+\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1}e^{\lambda x-|\nu|\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{|\nu|}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda x-\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{\tau_{x}}}+\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}e^{\lambda V(\omega_{k})-k\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{k}}\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda x-\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-S_{\tau_{x}}}\left[1+\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}e^{\lambda(V(\omega_{k})-x)-(k-\tau_{x})\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{k}-S_{\tau_{x}})}\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x)\right].

(2.12)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}}:=\{\omega_{k}\}\bigcup\{\nu\in\mathcal{T}:|\nu|>k,~\nu(k)=\omega_{k}~\text{and}~\nu(k+1)\neq\omega_{k+1}\}
is the subtree rooted at
\omega_{k}
and constructed only by the normal particles (i.e. excluded the subtree rooted at
\omega_{k+1}
), and

\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x):=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1}e^{\lambda\left(x-V(\omega_{k})\right)-(|\nu|-k)\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{|\nu|}-S_{k})}.

(2.13)

Remark 12
.

Note that, under
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, for any
\nu\in\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\nu)=1
, either
\nu
is the spine particle
\omega_{\tau_{x}}
, or
\nu
is some normal particle and can trace back to
\omega_{k}
for some

, which implies that
\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}}
. And

2.12
is the realization of such observation.

Lemma 3
.

Given the environment, under
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, conditional on
\mathcal{G}
, for admissible

k
(i.e.
0\leqslant k\leqslant\tau_{x}-1
), the conditional distribution of
\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x)
depends only on the pair
\left(x-V(\omega_{k}),\Gamma_{k}(\xi)\right)
, where
\Gamma_{k}
is defined in
eq.

1.11
. Moreover, for different admissible
k
, the random variables
\{\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x);0\leqslant k\leqslant\tau_{x}-1\}
are conditional independent with each other.

According to the construction of
(\mathcal{T},V)
unde
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, conditional on
\mathcal{G}
, the conditional distribution of the subtree
\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}}
is determined by
\Gamma_{k}(\xi)
. Also, by the space homogeneity and the definition of
\Phi
, the lemma is obvious.
∎

Combining with
eqs.

2.10
and
2.12
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{W_{\mathcal{L}_{x}}(\lambda)}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left[e^{S_{\tau_{x}}+\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-\lambda x}\left(1+\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}e^{\lambda(V(\omega_{k})-x)-(k-\tau_{x})\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{k}-S_{\tau_{x}})}\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x)\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left[e^{R_{x}}B_{x}\right]

(2.14)

where
R_{x}:=S_{\tau_{x}}+\tau_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-\lambda x
and

B_{x}:=\left(1+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\tau_{x}-1}e^{\lambda(V(\omega_{k})-x)-(k-\tau_{x})\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{k}-S_{\tau_{x}})}\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}},V(\omega_{k}),x)\right)^{-1}
. Note that,
B_{x}
is a positive and bounded random variable (in fact,
0<B_{x}\leqslant 1
).

By
eq.

2.14
, we construct the quenched size-biased probability distribution
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, and use some associated random variables such as
R_{x}
and
B_{x}
to reformulate the quantity
P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)
. Similarly to
eq.

1.13
, there exists a probability measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
such that

\iint f(x,\xi)\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\xi)=\iint f(x,\xi)\tilde{P}_{\xi}(\mathrm{d}x)P_{E}(\mathrm{d}\xi),

(2.15)

and abuse use of notations, we also use
P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
to represent
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.

2.3
An Annealed Coupling

Note that, under
P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, besides the two-side environment
\xi
, there is a trajectory of spine particles, denote by
\{V(\omega_{0}),\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})\}
. For fixed
\lambda>0
, in annealed probability
P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, the trajectory and the environment are independent with each other. Furthermore, conditioned on the trajectory and the environment, the full branching system can be generated according to a suitable rule. This insight inspires us to consider constructing an annealed coupling. Also, inspired by our previous work
[
12
]
, we can look the size-biased branching system standing at time
\tau_{x}
and position
x
, and desire to find a suitable coupling.

It is worth mentioning that for homogeneous branching systems, due to homogeneity,
no matter the specific value of
\tau_{x}
, the branching mechanism remains identical when looking forward and backward from time
\tau_{x}
, therefore,
it is not very hard to reconstruct the branching system (see
[
12
]
for more details).
However, for random environment setting, the time inhomogeneity brings an essential change, making it infeasible to construct a coupling directly under the quenched probability measure
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
.
Therefore, we consider constructing a suitable coupling in the annealed sense.

The construction of the annealed coupling is divided into four steps.

•

First, we specify a two-sided random environment. More precisely, we characterize the probabilistic structure of the environment as viewed from time
\tau_{x}
.

•

Second, we determine the trajectory of the spine particles. This part is constructed mainly by the excursion theory.

•

Third, we describe the whole branching system. To do such, we use the language of transfer measure (or can be translated into the language of conditional expectation). Formally, to obtain the whole branching system, conditional on environment and the trajectory of spine particles, we attach some subtrees to the spine.

•

Finally, based on above constructions, we introduce the annealed coupling measure and several
\sigma
fields. After that, we adopt the excursion theory and regard each excursion as a time scale. Then we treat space as a new time index and construct the random walk
\{R_{x},x\geqslant 0\}
. Also, from the whole branching system, we construct a sequence of random variables
\{B_{x},x\geqslant 0\}
. Moreover, we use these random variables to obtain a coupling lemma (i.e.
Lemma

6
), which can be used to express the annealed probability.

Environment:

Under
P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, the random time
\tau_{x}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{G}
(i.e. the
\sigma
field generated only by the trajectory of spine particles) and independent with
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\sigma(\xi)
(i.e. the
\sigma
field generated only by environment
\xi
). Thus, under the annealed probability, if we look the system at time
\tau_{x}
, the shifted environment
T_{\tau_{x}}\xi
has the same distribution as
\xi
. Therefore, the annealed probability distribution of the environment viewed from time
\tau_{x}
is exactly given by
P_{E}
.
The reason for constructing a two-sided environment lies in that the behavior of the subtrees attach to the spine constructed subsequently is indeed affected by the environment before
\tau_{x}
and after
\tau_{x}
.

Therefore, we generate a two side random environment
\xi
according to
P_{E}
, and denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\sigma(\xi)
the
\sigma
field generated by the environment.

The Trajectory of Spine Particles:

Recall that, under
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, the size-biased branching system has a spine. When looking the trajectory of spine at time
\tau_{x}
and position
x
, the trajectory can be reconstructed by excursion theory. Moreover, for different
x
, these trajectories admit a unified treatment in distribution.

Let
\{H_{0},H_{1},\cdots\}
be a random walk with one step distribution
e^{\lambda y-\Lambda_{s}(\lambda y)}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
. Define
\theta:=\inf\{k\geqslant 1:H_{k}=1\}
, and we have

\mathbb{E}[\theta]=\left(\int ye^{\lambda y-\Lambda_{s}(\lambda y)}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{-1}=\Lambda^{\prime}_{s}(\lambda)^{-1}<\infty.

(2.16)

From the excursion
\left(H_{j},0\leqslant j\leqslant\theta\right)
, we denote by

\varepsilon:=\left(H_{\theta-j}-H_{\theta}=H_{\theta-j}-1,0\leqslant j\leqslant\theta\right)

(2.17)

the inverse excursion.

Let
\{\varepsilon_{m}:=(\varepsilon_{m}(j),0\leqslant j\leqslant\theta_{m});m\geqslant 1\}
be a sequence of independent copies of
\varepsilon
. We construct an infinite trajectory
\{Y_{0},Y_{1},\cdots\}
by connecting these excursions one by one. For a strict mathematical description, for any
x\geqslant 1
, we define

T_{x}:=\sum_{j=1}^{x}\theta_{j},

(2.18)

and set
T_{0}:=0
. Then the process

Y_{k}:=-x+\varepsilon_{x+1}(k-T_{x}),\quad\text{for}\quad T_{x}<k\leqslant T_{x+1},

(2.19)

with
Y_{0}:=0
, is the infinite trajectory what we construct.
Above construction can trace back to Tanaka’s construction
[
25
]
.
From the infinity path
\textbf{Y}:=\{Y_{0},Y_{1},\cdots\}
, for any
k\leqslant 0
, we define

\overline{Y}_{k}:=Y_{-k},

then we can obtain a infinity path
\overline{\textbf{Y}}:=\{\cdots,\overline{Y}_{-2},\overline{Y}_{-1},\overline{Y}_{0}=0\}
defined on non-positive time.
Also, according to above construction, for any integer
x\geqslant 0
, we have

T_{x}=\sup\{k\geqslant 0:Y_{k}=-x\}=-\inf\{k\leqslant 0:\overline{Y}_{k}=-x\}.

(2.20)

Abuse use of notations, denote by
\mathcal{G}_{x}:=\sigma\left(\{\varepsilon_{k}:1\leqslant k\leqslant x\}\right)
and
\mathcal{G}:=\sigma\left(\overline{\textbf{Y}}\right)
, then
T_{x}
is
\mathcal{G}_{x}
measurable random variable. Also, denote by
P_{\overline{Y}}
the corresponding probability of
\overline{\textbf{Y}}
.

According to above construction, also see
[
18
,
25
]
, we have

Lemma 4
.

The distribution of
\left(\overline{Y}_{-T_{x}},\overline{Y}_{1-T_{x}},\cdots,\overline{Y}_{0}\right)
under
P_{\overline{Y}}

is the same as the distribution of
(H_{0}-x,H_{1}-x,\cdots,H_{\tau_{x}}-x)
under
Q_{\lambda}
, and the distribution of
\left(V(\omega_{0})-x,V(\omega_{1}),\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-x\right)
under
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
.

By
Lemma

4
, the infinite trajectory
\overline{\textbf{Y}}
constructed herein coincides with the spine trajectory under the original measure, differing only in the observation perspective in time and position.

Attach the subtrees to the spine (in the language of transfer measure):

Given a two-side environment
\xi
and an infinity trajectory
\overline{\textbf{Y}}
(we use
P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}
denote the transfer probability described as follow), for any
k\leqslant-1
, at time
k
, we drive a time-inhomogeneous branching random walk which is rooted at
(k,\overline{Y}_{k})
and denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{k}
. The branching system
\mathcal{T}_{k}
behaves as follow:

•

Initially, at generation
k
, a single particle is located at
\overline{Y}_{k}
.

•

Initial particle dies, and then reproduces several children, whose distribution is the same as
D_{k}-1
, where
D_{k}
is a random variable with distribution given by
F^{S}_{k+1}
(the size-biased distribution of
F_{k+1}
, which is defined in
eq.

1.8
).
After that, every new particle moves according to the jump distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
respectively to its parent.

•

In generation
t
(
t>k
), every particle behaves as usual, i.e. reproduces independently according to
F_{t+1}
, and offspring particles jump independently according to
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
.

From any
k\leqslant-1
, we just attach
\mathcal{T}_{k}
to the infinite path
\overline{Y}
at time
k
. Formally, after that, we connect these subtrees together and form a bigger branching system
\mathcal{T}:=\bigcup\limits_{k=-\infty}^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
For any
\nu\in\mathcal{T}
, denote by
|\nu|
the time where
\nu
survives in,
and for any
s<|\nu|
, denote by
\nu(s)
the only ancestor particle of
\nu
at generation
s
.
Define an optional line
\mathcal{L}_{0}
such that for any
\nu\in\mathcal{T}
,

\mathcal{L}_{0}(\nu)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad V(\nu)=0~\text{and}~V(\nu(s))<0,\forall s<|\nu|.

(2.21)

For any
k\leqslant-1
, with the optional line
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, define

\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y}_{k},0):=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{k},\mathcal{L}_{0}(\nu)=1}e^{-\lambda\overline{Y}_{k}-(|\nu|-k)\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{|\nu|}-S_{k})}.

(2.22)

Lemma 5
.

Given
\xi
and
\overline{Y}
, any
k\leqslant-1
, under
P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}
, the distribution of
\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y},0)
depends only on the pair
(\overline{Y}_{k},\Gamma_{k}(\xi))
, where
\Gamma_{k}
is defined in
eq.

1.11
, and for different
k
, these random variables are independent with each other.
And if the pair
(\overline{Y}_{k},\Gamma_{k}(\xi))
is equal to the pair
(H_{\omega_{k}}-x,\Gamma_{k}(\xi))
in
Lemma

3
, then these two random variables has the same conditional distribution.
Moreover, we have

P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}\left[\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y}_{k},0)\right]=\eta_{k+1}.

(2.23)

According to the construction of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
in this subsection and
\mathcal{T}_{\omega_{k}}
in
eq.

2.13
, the first part of the lemma is obvious.

About
eq.

2.23
, for
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, for any
t\geqslant k+1
, we define

\overline{W}_{k}(t):=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{k},|\nu|=t}e^{\lambda(V(\nu)-\overline{Y}_{k})-(t-k)\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{t}-S_{k})}.

(2.24)

By the branching property,
it is not hard to verified that
\{\overline{W}_{k}(t);t\geqslant k+1\}
is an additive martingale with respectively to the natural filtration generated by the branching system
\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
And according to the branching mechanism of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
at time
k
, we have

P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}[\overline{W}_{k}(k+1)]=\eta_{k+1}.

(2.25)

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is a simple optional line for
\mathcal{T}_{k}
. Therefore,
according to Lemma 6.1, 14.1 and 14.3 in
[
6
]
(i.e. the same arguments appeared in
Section

2.2
),
and we have

\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y}_{k},0)=\overline{W}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{0}(\nu)e^{\lambda(V(\nu)-\overline{Y}_{k})-(|\nu|-k)\Lambda_{s}(\lambda)-(S_{|\nu|}-S_{k})}.

Hence, for
\lambda>0
, we have

\quad P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}[\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y}_{k},0)]=P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}[\overline{W}_{k}(k+1)]=\eta_{k+1}.

So far, the lemma is proved.
∎

Annealed coupling measure, several
\sigma
fields and random variables:

From
P_{E},P_{\overline{Y}}
and
P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}
, there exist a probability measure
\overline{\mathbb{P}}
such that for any positive and finite function

\iiint f(\xi,\overline{Y},u)\overline{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}\xi,\mathrm{d}\overline{Y})=\iiint f(\xi,\overline{Y},u)P_{\xi,\overline{Y}}(\mathrm{d}u)P_{E}(\mathrm{d}\xi)P_{\overline{Y}}(\mathrm{d}\overline{Y}).

And to avoid using too much notation, also, abuse use of notations, we use

.

Under the annealed coupling measure
P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}
,
for any integer

, define

\mathcal{H}_{x}:=\sigma\left(\{\mathcal{G}_{x},\{F_{j}:j>-T_{x}\},\{\mathcal{T}_{j}:j>-T_{x}\}\}\right),\quad\mathcal{H}_{\infty}:=\sigma\{\mathcal{H}_{x}:x\geqslant 0\}.

(2.27)

For any
m\geqslant 1
, denote by

\zeta_{m}:=(F_{1-T_{m}},\cdots,F_{-T_{m-1}},\overline{Y}_{1-T_{m}}+m-1,\cdots,\overline{Y}_{-T_{m-1}}+m-1)

(2.28)

the
m

\overline{Y}
and the environments on this interval.
According to the independence between
\overline{Y}
and
\xi
under product measure

, and by the construction about
\overline{Y}
, it is not hard to find that

\zeta_{m}
are i.i.d random elements. Moreover,
\zeta_{m}
is
\mathcal{H}_{m}
measurable and independent with

.

Following, we construct several random variables.
For any
k\leqslant 0
, we define

(2.29)

And for any

, define

(2.30)

with

.
Note that,

is a function about excursion

. Therefore, under

\{R_{x}:x\geqslant 0\}
is a random walk respectively to

(i.e. for any

is
\mathcal{H}_{x}
measurable, and

\mathcal{H}_{x}
).

Also, under
P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}
, from the whole branching system
\mathcal{T}
, we define

B_{x}:=\left(1+\sum_{k=-T_{x}}^{-1}e^{J_{x}}\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{k},\overline{Y}_{k},0)\right)^{-1},

(2.31)

where

is defined in

2.18

2.20
,

2.22
, and

.

Lemma 6
.

(Coupling Lemma)
For any integer
x\geqslant 1
,
the law of
(B_{x},R_{x})
under
P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
is the same as the law of
(B_{x},R_{x})
under
P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}
.
And in particular, we have

P_{E}\otimes P_{\xi}(M\geqslant x)=P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{R_{x}}B_{x})=P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{R_{x}}B_{x}\right).

(2.32)

By
Lemma

4
, we know that
\left(V(\omega_{0})-x,V(\omega_{1})-x,\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-x\right)
has the same distribution as
(\overline{Y}_{-T_{x}},\overline{Y}_{1-T_{x}},\cdots,\overline{Y}_{0})
. Then for any admissible
k
, and a measurable set
C
of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and some measurable sets
A_{-k},A_{1-k},\cdots,A_{0}
of
\mathbb{R}
,
and by the independence between
\xi
and the trajectory of spine particles
\left(V(\omega_{0}),V(\omega_{1}),\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})\right)
, we have

\displaystyle P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}(T_{k}\xi\in C,V(\omega_{0})-x\in A_{-k},V(\omega_{1})-x\in A_{1-k},\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-x\in A_{0}|\tau_{x}=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{E}(T_{k}\xi\in C)\times P_{E}\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left(V(\omega_{1})-x\in A_{1-k},\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-x\in A_{0}|\tau_{x}=k\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{E}(T_{k}\xi\in C)\times P_{\overline{Y}}\left(\overline{Y}_{-T_{x}}\in A_{-k},\overline{Y}_{1-T_{x}}\in A_{1-k},\cdots,\overline{Y}_{0}\in A_{0}|T_{x}=k\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(T_{k}\xi\in C,\overline{Y}_{-T_{x}}\in A_{-k},\overline{Y}_{1-T_{x}}\in A_{1-k},\cdots,\overline{Y}_{0}\in A_{0}|T_{x}=k\right)

(2.33)

Also, the distribution of
\tau_{x}
under

is equal to the distribution of
T_{x}
under
P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}
.
Therefore, by the law of total probability, we can deduce that
the law of
(T_{\tau_{x}}\xi,V(\omega_{0})-x,V(\omega_{1})-x,\cdots,V(\omega_{\tau_{x}})-x)
under

is equal

under

.
Therefore, by

3
and

, and according to the constructions of

and

Define
\gamma_{x}=\inf\{0\leqslant k\leqslant x,R_{k}=\max\limits_{0\leqslant j\leqslant x}R_{j}\}

Lemma 14
.

For any fixed integer

, we have

\liminf\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}x^{\frac{3}{2}}Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\rho)R_{x}}B_{x},\gamma_{x}=j\right)>0.

(3.53)

Recall the

According to the construction about

Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}[B_{x}|\mathcal{H}_{\infty}]\geqslant\left(1+\sum_{k=-T_{x}}^{-1}e^{J_{k}}\eta_{k+1}\right)^{-1}=:A_{x},

(3.54)

And
R_{x}
is a

(also

(3.55)

Recall
\zeta_{j+1}
(defined in

\displaystyle A_{x}^{-1}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=-T_{x}}^{-1}e^{J_{k}}\eta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=0}^{x-1}e^{\sum\limits_{i=-T_{j}+1}^{0}X_{i}+T_{x}\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{1})+\lambda_{1}\overline{Y}_{-T_{j}}}\left(\sum_{k=-T_{j+1}}^{-T_{j}-1}\eta_{k+1}e^{\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{-T_{x}}X_{i}-(k+T_{j})\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{1})+\lambda(\overline{Y}_{k}+j-1)}\right)

About the upper bound of target probability, we have following lemma. Although it indeed provides control via an upper bound inequality, it is not sharp and thus insufficient to derive the exact polynomial decay rate of the annealed probability.

Lemma 15
.

For any
\delta\in(0,\rho)
, there exists some constant
C_{\delta}<\infty
, such that for any
x\geqslant 1
, we have

Q^{\rho}_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)\leqslant C_{\delta}.

Then by the Hölder inequality, we have

Q^{\rho}_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)\leqslant C_{\delta}^{\frac{1-\rho}{1-\delta}}<\infty.

(3.61)

By
Lemma

13
, we have

\displaystyle Q^{\rho}_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\rho\lambda_{\rho}x}P_{E}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1+\rho-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\rho\lambda_{\rho}x}P\otimes P_{\xi}\left(e^{(1+\rho-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right).

(3.62)

Under
P\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}
,
for
P~a.s.
environment
\xi
,
define
U:=\sup\{S_{n}+n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho});n\geqslant 0\}
.
Note that,
\{S_{n}-n\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho});n\geqslant 0\}
is a random walk with log-Laplace exponent
\overline{\Lambda}(\theta)=\Lambda_{e}(\theta)+\theta\Lambda_{s}(\lambda_{\rho})
, which has a positive zero point at
\theta=\rho
(under the Assumption of
Theorem

4
).
Then,
P
almost surely, we have
U<\infty
.
Moreover, we have

P(U\geqslant y)\sim e^{-\rho y}.

Note that, if
\xi\in\{U<\lambda_{\rho}x\}
, we have
R_{x}\leqslant 0,~\tilde{P}_{\xi}~a.s.
. So, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{(1+\rho-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x})\leqslant\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{R_{x}}B_{x}),

which implies that

P\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{(1+\rho-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x};U<\lambda x)\leqslant P\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{R_{x}}B_{x}).

And if
\xi\in\{U\geqslant\lambda_{\rho}x\}
, and the inequality
R_{x}\leqslant U-\lambda_{1}x
holds. Then we have

\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{(1+\rho-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x})\leqslant e^{(\rho-\delta)(U-\lambda_{1}x)}\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{R_{x}}B_{x})\leqslant e^{\frac{1}{p}(U-\lambda_{1}x)},

which implies that

P\otimes\tilde{P}_{\xi}(e^{(1+\frac{1}{p})R_{x}}B_{x};U\geqslant\lambda x)\leqslant P\left(e^{\frac{1}{p}(U-\lambda_{1}x)};U\geqslant\lambda_{1}x\right)

And according to the tail probability of
U
, it is not hard to verify that as
x\rightarrow\infty
, we have

P\left(e^{(\rho-\delta)(U-\lambda_{1}x)}|U\geqslant\lambda_{1}x\right)\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\rho-\delta)y}e^{-\rho y}\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Combining above relationships, there exists some finite constant
C
, such that

Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)\leqslant Q_{E}^{\rho}\left(e^{(1-\rho)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)+C.

And by Hölder inequality, we have

Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)\geqslant Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\rho)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\rho}}

As long as
\delta\in(0,\rho)
, we know that the inequality
x^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\rho}}\leqslant x+C
implies that there exists some finite constant
C_{\delta}
such that
x\leqslant C_{\delta}
. Therefore, we have

Q_{E}^{\rho}\otimes P_{\overline{Y}}\left(e^{(1-\delta)R_{x}}B_{x}\right)\leqslant C_{\delta}<\infty.

So far, the proof is finished.

Remark 14
.

As mentioned earlier, for Class III subcritical BRWRE, we fail to derive the exact polynomial decay rate due to the absence of crucial upper bound estimates. Specifically, we elaborate on this issue by drawing on relevant results concerning branching processes in random environments (BPRE). In the calculation of the extinction probability for BPRE, a robust upper bound is established such that
P_{\xi}(Z_{n}>0)\leqslant\min\{e^{S_{k}};0\leqslant k\leqslant n\}=e^{L_{n}}
, where
L_{n}:=\min\{S_{k};0\leqslant k\leqslant n\}
is the minimal of the associated random walk
\{S_{n},x\geqslant 0\}
, and this estimate is sharp, which is sufficient to accomplish the required upper bound control, see
[
14
,
18
]
and references therein for detail discussions.

However, for subcritical branching random walk in random environment, borrow some notations in
section

2
, by the fact
\{M\geqslant x\}\subset\{M\geqslant y\}
for any
y\leqslant x
, we can deduce that

P_{\xi}\left(M\geqslant x\right)\leqslant\min\{\tilde{P}_{\xi}\left(e^{R_{y}}\right),0\leqslant y\leqslant x\}.

While, under the measure
\tilde{P}_{\xi}
, we technically introduce additional information about the spine, which prevents the minimum operation in the above formula from being commuted into the expectation without any loss. And this is the intrinsic reason why we can’t obtain the exactly polynomial decay.
