The association of triglyceride-glucose index with acute myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality in patients with coronary artery disease stratified by acute myocardial infarction status: a large-scale retrospective cohort study from South China
Authors: Yanping Wang, Chujuan Zeng, Yong-Liang Huang, Yulin Fang, Ashkan Rahmati, Zekai Zhou, Songming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the association of the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index with the risk of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and all-cause mortality in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), and to inspect whether AMI mediates the relationship between TyG index and mortality.DesignA large-scale, retrospective cohort study.SettingThis single-centre study was conducted at a tertiary academic hospital in South China. The association between the TyG index and AMI was assessed using multivariable logistic regression, with progressive adjustment for demographic and clinical covariates. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the HRs for all-cause mortality associated with TyG index. Restricted cubic splines and mediation analysis were employed to examine non-linear relationships and the mediating role of AMI.ParticipantsA total of 20 125 patients diagnosed with CAD during hospitalisation between January 2020 and February 2025 were initially enrolled. After applying exclusion criteria (insufficient data), 18 245 participants were included in the final analysis.Outcome measuresWe examined the association of the TyG index with the risk of AMI, as well as its association with all-cause mortality across different CAD subgroups.ResultsThe association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality was significantly modified by AMI status (P for interaction <0.05). In the fully adjusted model, no significant association was observed in the overall CAD cohort. However, subgroup analysis revealed divergent patterns: among patients without AMI, a modest protective effect was observed (Q3 vs Q1: HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.94), whereas in those with AMI, the TyG index exhibited a harmful, dose-response relationship with mortality (per-SD increase: HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.38; Q4 vs Q1: HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.25). Additionally, each SD increment in the TyG index was associated with 12% higher odds of AMI (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.19). Mediation analysis further demonstrated that AMI mediated 9.51% of the total effect of TyG index on mortality (p<0.001).ConclusionsAn elevated TyG index independently predicts the risk of AMI in patients with CAD. Its prognostic value for mortality, however, is critically dependent on the presence of AMI: while a higher TyG index is associated with increased mortality in patients with AMI, moderately elevated TyG levels (Q2–Q3) are associated with lower mortality, whereas the highest quartile shows no significant association. Mediation analysis further reveals that AMI significantly mediates the association between TyG index and mortality, highlighting the importance of AMI prevention in mitigating the adverse prognostic impact of insulin resistance in the CAD population. These findings warrant validation in prospective studies.

Background

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain a leading cause of premature mortality and chronic disability worldwide, imposing a substantial economic burden through both direct and indirect costs.1 2 Despite a dramatic decline in ischaemic heart disease mortality in recent years, owing to timely invasive coronary angiography and percutaneous or surgical revascularisation,3 ischaemic heart disease still accounts for the majority of CVD-related health loss.1

Insulin resistance is defined as a decrease in tissue response to insulin stimulation in glycogen synthesis and can induce the development of CVD.4 Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been used since 2008 as a surrogate index then prevalent for insulin resistance.57

The TyG index is associated with established cardiovascular risk factors and can be used as a useful, low-cost predictive marker for coronary artery disease (CAD).8 Elevated TyG levels have also been shown to predict the presence of CAD.9 Among patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the TyG index has been linked to coronary artery calcification, multivessel coronary artery disease10 and increased CAD complexity.11 An elevated TyG index is associated not only with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) but also with poor long-term prognosis in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI).12 13 Although previous valuable studies have indeed established the TyG index as a prognostic marker, they have largely focused on either the general population’s risk of developing CAD or the prognosis specifically in patients presenting with AMI. In certain studies, after adjusting for potential confounders, the TyG index did not remain statistically significantly associated with all-cause or cardiovascular mortality in the broader CAD population.14 15

Overall, the prognostic value of TyG index for mortality in patients with CAD remains inconclusive. Furthermore, no study has simultaneously compared its association with outcomes between patients with and without AMI within the same cohort. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of the TyG index and to examine its differential impact between patients with AMI and the broader CAD population. The distinct focus of our work is on the subgroup of patients with established CAD but without a history of AMI. This is a clinically significant yet less studied population.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational cohort study used data from the cardiovascular department of the First Hospital of Shantou University Medical College, collected between 1 January 2020 and 28 February 2025. The cohort is representative of the regional CAD population, as data were drawn from the primary tertiary referral centre serving a broad catchment area and capturing a wide spectrum of disease severity. Vast majority (>99%) of participants were of Han Chinese ethnicity. At the time of admission to the First Hospital of Shantou University Medical College, all patients (or their legal guardians) provided written informed consent for the use of their anonymised medical data for future research purposes, as per the hospital’s standard procedure. Our study strictly adhered to this protocol. All data used in this analysis were fully de-identified before being processed. The study protocol was also approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (Approval Number: B-2025-012), which confirmed that our use of the data complied with the terms of the initial patient consent and ethical guidelines. Overall, 20 125 participants with a principal diagnosis of CAD during hospitalisation were enrolled. The flowchart of this study is shown in figure 1.

Patient and public involvement

This study is a secondary analysis of cardiovascular specialty data from the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University. Patients or the public were not involved in the study’s design, implementation or interpretation of results. In compliance with ethical requirements, the data have been anonymised, preventing individual identification. Therefore, results will not be directly communicated to participants. The research findings will be disseminated through academic journals and conferences.

Data collection and definitions

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including: (1) demographics: age, sex, smoking status and alcohol consumption; (2) medical history: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and prior diagnoses of myocardial infarction and heart failure; (3) clinical measurements on admission: systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and heart rate; (4) laboratory parameters: triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), serum creatinine (Scr), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), cardiac troponin T (cTnT), serum potassium (K), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), myoglobin (Myo), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NE) and C-reactive protein (CRP); and (5) in-hospital medications: ACE inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), statins, antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers (CCBs). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was also recorded.

In this cohort, we defined CAD as the presence of typical angina symptoms accompanied by coronary angiography demonstrating ≥50% stenosis in at least one major epicardial coronary artery.16 In our study, we included only newly occurred myocardial infarction during the current hospitalisation. AMI comprised ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). The diagnosis of AMI was established according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction (2018), requiring evidence of myocardial injury (cardiac troponin values exceeding the 99th percentile upper reference limit) along with at least one of the following criteria: (1) symptoms of myocardial ischemia; (2) new ischaemic changes in the ECG; (3) development of pathological Q waves; (4) imaging evidence of new loss of vital heart muscle or new regional contraction disorders, the location of which corresponds to the ischaemic aetiology; or (5) detection of a clot in the coronary artery during coronary or sectional examination.17 Management of STEMI and NSTEMI followed contemporary Chinese clinical guidelines.1820

Hypertension was defined as: (1) a documented history of hypertension; (2) at least two separate measurements of systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg on different days during hospitalisation; or (3) current use of antihypertensive medication.

Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), random blood glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) and/or HbA1c ≥6.5% or treatment with hypoglycaemic drugs or insulin.

Heart failure was defined as a clinical syndrome characterised by typical symptoms and signs caused by structural and/or functional cardiac abnormalities, leading to reduced cardiac output and/or elevated intracardiac pressures.21

The TyG index, which served as a substitute for insulin resistance by combining fasting glucose with triglyceride levels, was calculated as follows: TyG index=ln (fasting glucose (mg/dL) × triglycerides (mg/dL)/2).5

Morning fasting venous blood samples were collected to assess liver function, lipids, Scr, CK, CK-MB (creatine kinase-MB), WBC, NE and FBG.

The primary endpoint of this study was all-cause mortality, and the secondary endpoint was incident AMI. Follow-up began at study enrolment (1 January 2020) and continued until the date of death or the end of the study period (28 February 2025), whichever occurred first. Mortality data were ascertained through the electronic medical record system. For patients without in-hospital follow-up records, survival status and date of death were obtained via telephone interview. The median follow-up duration was 217 days.

Management of missing data

Little’s test indicated that data were not missing completely at random (p<0.001). To address missing data, we adopted a two-step approach. For covariates with <25% missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed. For covariates with ≥25% missingness, values were categorised into three groups: missing, ≤ median and >median. Furthermore, we applied the random forest method for multiple imputation to re-verify the key findings, ensuring the robustness of the data. The missing data are shown in online supplemental table S1.

Statistical analysis

Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables (as sample size exceeded 5000). The TyG index was categorised into quartiles based on the distribution in the overall cohort, with cutpoints at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. This categorisation aimed to minimise the influence of outliers and to evaluate potential non-linear associations with clinical outcomes. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarised across TyG quartiles. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (IQR); categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons across quartiles were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards models were adopted to estimate the HRs and 95% CIs for the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality across different cohorts. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, LDL, HDL, SBP, Scr, smoking history and alcohol consumption; Model 3 was further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, use of ACEIs, use of antiplatelets, heart rate and HbA1c. The directed acyclic graph (DAG) of the covariates is shown in online supplemental figure S1. HRs for all-cause mortality associated with each SD increase in the TyG index, as well as across TyG quartiles (Q1–Q4), were estimated using the same three models described above. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots were used to further examine the shape of the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality.

To evaluate the association between TyG index quartiles and incident AMI, we used multivariable logistic regression models to estimate ORs and CIs. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history and alcohol consumption. Model 3 was further adjusted for LDL, HDL, Scr, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, WBC and CRP. ORs for AMI associated with each SD increase in the TyG index, as well as across TyG quartiles (Q1–Q4), were estimated using the same three models. RCS with three knots were used to further examine the dose-response relationship between the TyG index and AMI risk.

Subgroup analyses for AMI risk were stratified by sex, age (≤65 vs >65 years), hypertension and diabetes. For all-cause mortality, heart failure was added as an additional stratifying variable, given its clinical relevance as a common complication of CAD.

To investigate whether AMI mediates the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality, we performed a causal mediation analysis within a counterfactual framework. A series of generalised linear models were fitted: a linear regression model for AMI (mediator) regressed on the TyG index (mediator model), and a probit regression model for mortality (outcome) regressed on both the TyG index and AMI (outcome model). All models were adjusted for predefined confounders, including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, LDL and Scr. Using the mediation package in R, we estimated the average direct effect (ADE) and the average causal mediation effect (ACME). The proportion of the total effect mediated by AMI was calculated as (ACME / total effect) × 100%. Statistical inference was performed using non-parametric bootstrapping with 5000 resamples to generate 95% CIs and robust standard errors. The causal assumptions underlying this analysis are illustrated in the DAG shown in online supplemental figure S1.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics

A total of 18 245 patients with a principal diagnosis of CAD were enrolled in this study, including 2273 cases of STEMI and 1471 cases of NSTEMI. Baseline characteristics stratified by TyG index quartiles are presented in table 1. Across ascending TyG quartiles (Q1 to Q4), there were significant increasing trends in levels of TG, TC, LDL, HbA1c, DBP, SBP, HR, CK, cTnT, ALT, K, Scr, WBC, FBG and CRP, whereas HDL levels and age showed significant decreasing trends (all P for trend <0.05). Patients in higher TyG quartiles were more likely to have a history of smoking, AMI, hypertension and diabetes, and were more frequently prescribed ACEIs, ARBs, CCBs, statins, beta-blockers and antiplatelet agents (all p<0.05). The analysis between TyG and all-cause mortality in different CAD cohorts

We employed a multivariable Cox regression model to examine the CAD population, stratified by AMI status. The CAD cohort was categorised as three distinct groups: all-participants (n=18 245), participants without AMI (n=14 501) and only AMI participants (n=3744). Cox proportional hazards models and RCS were applied to make comparisons among them.

All-participants cohort: In the unadjusted Model 1, TyG demonstrated a protective effect in Q2 and Q3 (table 2). However, these associations became non-significant following further covariate adjustment in subsequent models. The association between TyG index and AMI

Multivariable logistic regression analysis and multivariable RCS were employed to investigate the relationship between the TyG index and AMI. After full adjustment for covariates in Model 3, the TyG index remained significantly associated with AMI in patients with CAD (table 3). In continuous analysis, per SD increase in the TyG index was associated with a 12% higher risk of AMI (OR=1.12; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.19). Quartile-based analysis confirmed this association: compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), patients in Q3 (OR=1.16; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.31) and Q4 (OR=1.24; 95% CI 1.09 to 1.41) had significantly elevated risks (table 3). The RCS analysis revealed that the odds of AMI increased progressively with rising TyG levels, although the relationship was non-linear (figure 3).

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the association between an elevated TyG index and increased mortality in patients with CAD is primarily mediated by its contribution to AMI, a major complication of CAD. Conversely, among CAD patients without AMI, the TyG index exhibited a protective effect against mortality. These results underscore that the occurrence of AMI portends a poor prognosis in the CAD population, and that the prognostic value of the TyG index is critically dependent on AMI status (online supplemental table S3).

Mediation analysis investigating the role of AMI

Causal mediation analysis revealed that AMI significantly mediated the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality. The total effect of TyG on mortality was significant (effect=0.00279, p<0.001), with an ADE of 0.00308 (p<0.001) and an ACME of 0.00032 (p<0.001). Approximately 9.5% of the total effect was mediated through AMI, indicating that AMI partially explains the increased mortality risk associated with higher TyG levels (figure 4).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings (figure 5). The TyG-AMI association was significant across most subgroups, except in patients with diabetes or hypertension (figure 5A). In the overall cohort, TyG was not associated with mortality in most subgroups, with the exception of patients aged <65 years (figure 5B). Among patients without AMI, the protective effect of TyG was observed only in males and in those without diabetes or heart failure (figure 5C). Among patients with AMI, the harmful effect of TyG was evident in most subgroups but was not significant in females or in those with hypertension, diabetes or heart failure (figure 5D). These results support the primary conclusion that the prognostic impact of TyG index is modified by AMI status and clinical characteristics.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we employed multiple imputation using the random forest method to re-analysed the primary outcomes. The results remained consistent with our original analyses: the TyG index remained predictive of all-cause mortality across different CAD subgroups, and its association with AMI was unchanged (online supplemental table S4 and S5). Similarly, the mediation analysis yielded results comparable to those obtained from the primary analysis (online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

This large-scale, single-centre, retrospective cohort study provides novel insights into the complex relationship between the TyG index, AMI and prognosis in patients with CAD. Our main findings are: (1) the prognostic value of the TyG index for all-cause mortality was critically dependent on the presence or absence of AMI; (2) within the overall CAD population, a higher TyG index was significantly and positively associated with an increased risk of AMI, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors; (3) AMI acted as a significant mediator in the pathway linking an elevated TyG index to increased all-cause mortality risk. Collectively, this study is the first one to reveal that a higher TyG index is associated with a greater risk of AMI within the CAD population and establish the mediating role of AMI in the TyG-prognosis relationship.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown TyG index was a prognostic factor in patients with AMI. Importantly, this reinforces the role of the TyG index in predicting MACE, with AMI being a key component of MACE. Our study contributes to this understanding by specifically demonstrating the critical importance of TyG index in prognosticating MACE outcomes among the studied population. Elevated TyG index was indicated to have higher mortality risks in AMI due to the incidence of cardiac shock,22 contributing to a high global mortality.23 A study enrolling 438 patients with NSTE-ACS indicated that TyG index might be an independent predictor of MACE.24 In addition, Zeng D et al found that an increase in the TyG index level was significantly associated with all-cause mortality of patients diagnosed with STEMI after percutaneous coronary intervention.25

The detrimental effect of a low TyG index has been confirmed in numerous studies involving the general CAD population, typically demonstrating a U-shaped relationship. This is consistent with the overall trend observed in our total cohort. After excluding patients with myocardial infarction, the harmful effect of a low TyG index became more pronounced in our study. Specifically, while the left arm of the U-shaped curve for the total population crossed the line of unity (HR=1)—indicating statistically ambiguous risk—this ambiguity was resolved in the non-AMI subgroup, where the lower TyG range showed a clearly significant increase in hazard. Notably, analyses of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data indicated a U-shaped association with all-cause mortality, where both very high and very low TyG levels were associated with increased risk.26 Similarly, another study reported an elevated risk of cardiovascular mortality at the lower end of the TyG distribution.27 These studies support our observation of a potential adverse prognosis associated with a lower TyG index in the stable, non-AMI CAD subgroup. This may not reflect a protective effect of low insulin resistance, but rather signify an underlying ‘metabolic frailty’ or catabolic state. An extremely low TyG index likely indicates concomitant issues such as malnutrition, severe systemic illness or neuroendocrine dysfunction, all of which are strong independent predictors of mortality. Therefore, in stable CAD patients, a very low TyG may serve as a readily available biomarker for identifying a vulnerable subset with compromised metabolic reserve. Another explanation was low TyG levels might trigger counter-regulatory hormones, increase secretion of epinephrine, attributing to vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation through hypoglycaemic conditions, likewise increased CAD risk.28 Therefore, an appropriate level of TyG index is important in maintaining health. Overall, the precise underlying mechanism between TyG index and CAD remains unclear. The association between TyG and CAD differs across studies depending on variations in participant characteristics, the influence of unmeasured confounding factors and differences in follow-up durations. In some studies, the CAD patients may involve patients with AMI; therefore, the positive relationship may be strengthened.

Remarkably, our present study exhibits that the adverse prognostic impact of a high TyG index in patients with CAD is critically dependent on the presence of AMI. While a recent large-scale meta-analysis reported that a high TyG index predicted poor outcomes in CAD,29 the included studies predominantly enrolled patients with ACS. Evidence within the broader CAD population remains relatively limited. Indeed, recent studies conducted within comprehensive CAD cohorts have reported a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and clinical outcomes,26 rather than a consistently detrimental linear association. We therefore propose a dual-pathway model: a high TyG index increases susceptibility to AMI in patients with CAD, and once AMI occurs, these individuals face an elevated risk of mortality. This framework reconciles the seemingly divergent findings across the literature and underscores the importance of considering AMI status when interpreting the prognostic value of TyG. The TyG index may contribute to both AMI susceptibility and post-AMI mortality, a dual mechanism that likely explains its association with adverse prognosis, especially its predictive value for MACE in the broader CAD population. Our findings suggest that the TyG index, a simple and inexpensive calculation from routine laboratory tests, can serve as a practical tool for identifying patients at higher risk for future mortality and cardiovascular events. Clinicians could consider incorporating this index into the risk assessment profile of patients with AMI to flag those who may require more vigilant monitoring. An elevated TyG index can signal underlying insulin resistance and heighten cardiometabolic risk, potentially prompting clinicians to intensify lifestyle counselling, optimise control of both triglycerides and glucose, or consider earlier referral to cardiometabolic specialty care. Future interventional studies are needed to determine whether actively lowering the TyG index translates into a reduction in the observed long-term MACE risk.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-centre, retrospective design with an exclusively Han Chinese cohort may limit generalisability and introduce residual confounding. The TyG index was based on a single fasting measurement, and perfect fasting compliance cannot be assured. Key covariates with substantial missing data were only adjustable as dummy variables. Mortality data from hospital records and telephone follow-up may be incomplete. The COVID-19 pandemic during the study period may have influenced mortality outcomes independently. In mediation analysis, perfect prediction required covariate simplification, potentially introducing bias. Finally, causal inferences regarding the TyG→AMI→mortality pathway are constrained by the observational design. Nevertheless, the large cohort and rigorous analytical approach—including multiple imputation, spline regression and causal mediation analysis—support the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study elucidates a critical pathway: an elevated TyG index increases the risk of AMI within the CAD population, and AMI subsequently mediates the association between high TyG levels and increased all-cause mortality. This underscores the TyG index as a valuable tool for risk stratification and highlights the pivotal role of preventing AMI as a key strategy to mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of insulin resistance in coronary artery disease.
