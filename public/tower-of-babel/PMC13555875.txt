Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in a family with protein S deficiency: genetic analysis and phenotypic characterization
Authors: Fengjiao Wang, Ling You, Xiaoli Chen, Fei Xu, Qiyu Xu, Mingshan Wang, Lihong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProtein S deficiency (PSD) markedly increases cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) risk; although global PROS1 variant data are extensive, Chinese reports remain limited.ObjectiveTo characterize the genetic and clinical phenotypes of a family with hereditary PSD presenting as CVST, and to preliminarily explore underlying molecular pathogenesis.MethodsClinical and coagulation data were collected from the proband and family members. CVST was confirmed by magnetic resonance venography (MRV). The coding region of PROS1 gene was sequenced, and the pathogenicity, evolutionary conservation, and functional impact of identified variant was evaluated using bioinformatics tools and Pymol software. The thrombin generation assay (TGA) was used to preliminarily assess the proband’s thrombin generation capacity.ResultsThe proband exhibited reduced PS activity (PS: A, 59%), along with decreased total and free PS antigen levels. Similar reductions were observed in her father, two sisters, and two nephews. MRV revealed extensive CVST. Genetic sequencing identified a heterozygous missense variant c.886 A > C (p.Lys296Gln) in exon 9 of the PROS1 gene in all affected individuals. The variant was classified as variant of uncertain significance. It resides in highly conserved region, altering local hydrophilicity and hydrogen bond interactions and likely impairing PS function. The TGA results indicated that the proband’s thrombin generation capacity was significantly increased.ConclusionThis study reports a PROS1-related CVST case in which inherited variant acted synergistically with modifiable prothrombotic risk factors. The findings underscore the importance of comprehensive etiological screening in unexplained thrombosis and support familial genetic counseling and surveillance for early prevention.

Introduction

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a distinct cerebrovascular disease accounting for approximately 0.5%-1.0% of all stroke cases [1]. CVST often presents with nonspecific symptoms and carries a generally favorable prognosis; however, severe cases may progress to intracranial hypertension, requiring timely intervention to prevent brain herniation [2–4]. The clinical manifestations of this disease are atypical, leading to delayed diagnosis. Diagnosis primarily relies on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance venography (MRV) [4–6]. The condition is primarily non-infectious in origin, with its key mechanism involving hemodynamic alterations and a hypercoagulable state [7]. Thrombophilia represents a major contributing factor, with geographic differences noted: Factor Ⅴ Leiden variants are common in Western populations, whereas defects in anticoagulant protein genes predominate in Asian populations [8]. Protein S deficiency (PSD) is a thrombophilia disorder characterized by impaired anticoagulant function, predisposing individuals to hypercoagulability and significantly increasing the risk of CVST. Protein S (PS) is a vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein synthesized primarily in the liver that serves as an essential cofactor for activated protein C in the anticoagulation pathway, thereby helping to maintain the coagulation-fibrinolysis balance.

Mature PS has a molecular weight of approximately 75 kDa, a total plasma concentration of approximately 30.9 ± 3.9 mg/L, and circulates in both free (about 40% activity) and C4b-binding protein-bound forms [9]. Clinically, PSD is defined by reduced PS antigen or activity, leading to impaired anticoagulant capacity and elevated thrombotic risk [10]. Its diagnosis relies primarily on detection of free PS (FPS) and PS activity (PS: A), with total PS antigen (TPS: Ag) as an adjunctive marker [11]. Based on these parameters, the disorder is categorized into three phenotypic subtypes: type Ⅰ (reduced TPS, FPS, and PS: A), type Ⅱ (isolated PS: A reduction, typically < 55%), and type Ⅲ (reduced FPS and PS: A, with normal TPS) [12]. Genetically, hereditary PSD is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 20,000 and marked ethnic disparities [13]. The reported prevalence ranges from 0.03% to 0.13% in Caucasian populations to approximately 1.12% in Japan. Variants in the PROS1 gene, which encodes PS constitute the major genetic etiology of PSD. More than 545 variants have been documented, mostly single-base substitutions distributed across its functional domains [14]. However, data on PROS1 variants in Chinese populations remain limited.

In this study, we conducted integrated laboratory tests and genetic sequencing analyses in a hereditary PSD family presenting with CVST, to preliminarily elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms and laboratory phenotypes.

Methods

Family

The proband (Ⅱ8), a 45-year-old female, initially presented with headache. Her symptoms progressively worsened and were accompanied by generalized weakness. On August 17, 2025, cranial MRI at a local hospital revealed abnormal signals within the cerebral venous sinuses, no targeted intervention was administered at that time. She was subsequently transferred to our hospital for further evaluation and management on the following day. After admission, laboratory tests showed elevated D-dimer, decreased hemoglobin (Hb), and increased platelet count, indicating a hypercoagulable state. Cranial MRV clearly demonstrated extensive thrombosis within the intracranial venous system, confirming a diagnosis of CVST. Laboratory tests revealed reduced PS: A, indicating probable PSD. The inpatient treatment regimen primarily consisted of anticoagulation, intracranial pressure reduction, and antiepileptic therapy. Additionally, adjunctive therapy with polysaccharide iron, folic acid, and vitamins was provided to manage concomitant anemia and hyperhomocysteinemia. This study included the proband and available family members, encompassing a total of 15 individuals across three generations (Fig. 1).

Normal control

A total of 100 healthy individuals (54 males and 46 females) admitted during the same period were enrolled as the normal control group, with a mean age of 40 ± 10 years. None had a history of hepatic or renal diseases that might influence coagulation function. Peripheral blood samples were collected from all controls to establish baseline laboratory reference ranges.

Blood samples

Blood samples were collected from the proband and all enrolled family members during the acute phase of the proband’s illness, prior to low-molecular-weight heparin administration. Venous blood was drawn into tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate, with a standardized anticoagulant-to-blood ratio of 1:9 v/v and processed within 2 h after collection. Following centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 min at 22℃, the supernatant plasma was used for thrombin generation assay (TGA) and routine coagulation examination. The cellular sediment was reserved for subsequent genomic DNA extraction.

Laboratory testing

Coagulation parameters, including plasminogen activity (PLG: A), antithrombin activity (AT: A), protein C activity (PC: A), and PS: A, were measured using a STAGO STA-R-Max automated coagulation analyzer (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières sur Seine, France) with manufacturer-matched reagent kits. TPS: Ag and free PS antigen (FPS: Ag) levels were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). All assays were performed in duplicate, in strict adherence with the manufacturer’s protocols and Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.

Medical imaging

Cranial MRV was performed to confirm the diagnosis with comprehensive visualization of the entire cerebral venous system. Serial MRV examinations were obtained at regular intervals after treatment to assess thrombus resolution, venous recanalization, and relevant complications [15].

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted via the phenol-chloroform method. Primers spanning all exons and flanking regions of the PROS1 gene were designed and synthesized by Shanghai Saiheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Polymerase chain reaction products were purified and subsequently subjected to sanger sequencing by the same company. Sequence chromatograms were aligned against the PROS1 reference sequence (NCBI RefSeq: NG_009813.1; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using Chromas v2.23 software. Putative variant was verified by reverse primer re-sequencing. Following variant identification, the corresponding exons of all enrolled family members were sequenced. All experimental procedures were conducted in duplicate in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols and CLSI guidelines.

In silico analysis

The pathogenicity of the identified variant was predicted with two well-established algorithms: Mutation Taster (https://www.mutationtaster.org/) and CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/score). Evolutionary conservation was assessed through multiple sequence alignment of homologous RefSeq sequences with Clustal X 2.1 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/). Structural alterations induced by the variant were modeled and visualized using Pymol (v2.7). Population allele frequencies were obtained from the gnomAD database. In accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) variant classification guidelines [16], the identified genetic variant was assessed for pathogenicity.

TGA

Thrombin generation was detected using a calibrated automatic thrombin generation assay. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the FluoCa kit (produced by Stago, France) and 20 µl PPP reagent were used to trigger coagulation with 5 pmol/L tissue factor. The continuous thrombin generation analysis was conducted to generate a complete thrombin generation curve. Key parametric indices, including peak thrombin (nmol/L) and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP, nmol/L*min), were calculated from the acquired curve.

Results

Laboratory testing

In the acute phase, coagulation analysis revealed a marked reduction in PS: A (59%), as well as reductions in TPS: Ag and FPS: Ag. At the 12-week follow-up during the stable phase, repeated coagulation assays demonstrated that these indicators remained persistently low. Baseline immunological testing yielded positive results for anti-SSA and anti-β2-glycoprotein Ⅰ (anti-β2-GPI), whereas anti-β2-GPI seroconverted to negative at the 12-week reassessment. Therefore, the proband did not meet the diagnostic criteria for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) according to the 2026 Chinese Guidelines for Clinical Application of Antiphospholipid Antibodies. Meanwhile, a negative salivary gland biopsy ruled out other autoimmune diseases, including Sjögren’s syndrome. Additionally, the proband presented with elevated homocysteine (43 µmol/L), decreased hemoglobin (77 g/L), and thrombocytosis (374 × 10⁹/L). Similar reductions in PS: A and antigen levels were identified in the proband’s father, two sisters, and two nephews. No notable abnormalities were found in other coagulation parameters, immunological markers or other hematological parameters among the remaining family members. Imaging findings

Initial cranial MRV obtained on August 17, 2025 demonstrated extensive thrombosis involving the superior sagittal sinus, right transverse sinus, right sigmoid sinus, and right internal jugular vein. Follow-up cranial MRV acquired on October 22, 2025 revealed a marked reduction in thrombus burden, with only minimal residual thrombus within the right sigmoid sinus (Fig. 2).

Genetic analysis

Genetic sequencing of the proband identified a heterozygous PROS1 c.886 A > C variant in exon 9, leading to a p.Lys296Gln substitution. This variant co-segregated with the clinical phenotype within the family: it was detected in the affected father, two sisters, and two nephews, while unaffected members harbored the wild-type allele (Fig. 3). The p.Lys296Gln variant (rs1559932774) is documented in public databases, including gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and dbSNP (https://ncbiinsights.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/). To date, no previous studies have reported this variant in association with hereditary PSD.

In silico analysis

Two in silico prediction tools (Mutation Taster and CADD) classified the p.Lys296Gln variant as likely pathogenic (Table 2). Cross-species sequence alignment indicated complete evolutionary conservation of this residue across six representative species (Fig. 4). Structural modeling revealed that the substitution of lysine with glutamine creates an additional hydrogen bond with Glu298 (Fig. 5). According to the ACMG guidelines, the c.886 A > C (p.Lys296Gln) variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). This classification was supported by compliance with the PP1 and PP3 evidence criteria. Specifically, the identical variant was identified in the proband’s father, two sisters, and two nephews. All family members harboring this variant presented with reduced PS: A levels, indicating consistent familial segregation (PP1). Multiple in silico prediction tools suggested a pathogenic effect, and the affected residue is highly conserved across different species (PP3).

Discussion

Based on imaging results and clinical manifestations, the proband was diagnosed with CVST. No obvious precipitating factors were identified in her medical history, including trauma, prolonged immobilization, long-distance travel, or use of hormonal contraceptives. To explore the underlying etiology, comprehensive laboratory testing was performed. Coagulation assays demonstrated reduced TPS and FPS levels, consistent with a diagnosis of type Ⅰ PSD. Concurrent genetic analysis identified a heterozygous PROS1 missense variant (c.886 A > C, p.Lys296Gln). In accordance with ACMG guidelines, this variant was classified as VUS, and bioinformatical analysis confirmed its localization within a functionally conserved region essential for PS structure and function. PS is a 635-amino-acid single-chain polypeptide stabilized by multiple disulfide bonds, with three core functional domains responsible for calcium binding and anticoagulant cofactor activity [17, 18]. The PROS1 c.886 A > C variant identified in this study is located at the protein carboxyl terminus. The substitution is predicted to moderately reduce local hydrophilicity and neutralize the regional charge. Furthermore, the variant creates an additional hydrogen bond between the altered residue (p.Lys296Gln) and Glu298, which may disrupt PS conformation and consequently impair its in vivo anticoagulant function. TGA revealed elevated peak value and ETP in the proband, indicating enhanced thrombin generation capacity and an increased thrombotic risk. This finding remains exploratory, and further mechanistic validation will be performed in subsequent studies. The proband’s thrombotic event was likely attributed to the combined effect of multiple auxiliary risk factors: positive anti-SSA serostatus, moderate anemia, and hyperhomocysteinemia. This may explain why the proband’s sisters and nephews remain asymptomatic despite carrying the same genetic variant. Anemia promotes hypercoagulability via hypoxic compensation and altered coagulation function [19–22], whereas hyperhomocysteinemia predisposes to thrombosis by inducing endothelial dysfunction [23]. Furthermore, although robust evidence is lacking to confirm that isolated positive anti-SSA serostatus directly increases thrombotic risk, existing studies indicate that individuals with anti-SSA positivity combined with other autoantibodies have a significantly higher risk of venous thrombosis and cerebral infarction [24].

To further evaluate genotype-phenotype correlations within this family, available relatives underwent laboratory assessment. All PROS1 variant carriers presented reduced PS: A, along with decreased FPS: Ag and TPS: Ag levels. Except for the proband’s father, who experienced a cerebral infarction in 2023, no other family members had a history of overt thrombotic events. Accordingly, the father’s cerebral infarction may be attributed to the combined effect of advanced age [25] and underlying PROS1-related PSD. Notably, the proband developed symptomatic thrombosis, whereas her variant-carrying sisters and nephews remained asymptomatic. Clinical and laboratory review confirmed that these relatives had no additional acquired thrombotic risks factors. This clinical discrepancy further underscores the contributory effect of additional comorbid conditions in triggering thrombosis among PROS1 variant carriers.

Consistent with the central role of anticoagulation in CVST management, the proband received standardized inpatient therapy comprising anticoagulation, intracranial pressure reduction, and seizure prophylaxis. Low molecular weight heparin was initiated together with mannitol in the acute phase to alleviate intracranial hypertension. One seizure episode occurred following admission; after which levetiracetam was administered for secondary seizure prevention. Concurrent comorbidities were managed with vitamin C and folic acid to reduce serum homocysteine levels, along with polysaccharide iron complex capsules and mecobalamin to correct anemia. Upon entering the recovery phase, the proband was switched to oral rivaroxaban for long-term anticoagulation. Her clinical condition improved substantially. At admission, she presented with severe neurological disability with a modified Rankin Scale score of 5. Following the above regimen, headache and other symptoms gradually resolved. By clinical stabilization on September 1, 2025, her modified Rankin Scale score had improved to 0, indicating full neurological recovery without residual deficits. Baseline imaging revealed extensive thrombosis involving the superior sagittal, right transverse, right sigmoid sinuses and right internal jugular vein. Follow-up imaging after anticoagulation and etiological treatment demonstrated substantial thrombus regression, with only minimal residual thrombus remaining in the right sigmoid sinus. These findings confirm a favorable therapeutic response and marked clinical improvement of CVST.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified PROS1 variant of uncertain significance (c.886 A > C) in a CVST patient with multiple acquired prothrombotic risk factors, including positive anti-SSA serostatus, anemia and hyperhomocysteinemia. This study highlights the necessity of comprehensive etiological evaluation in patients with unexplained thrombosis, encompassing screening for both hereditary thrombophilia and reversible acquired risk factors. Genetic counseling and targeted screening should be offered to at-risk family members to facilitate early risk stratification and preventive intervention.
