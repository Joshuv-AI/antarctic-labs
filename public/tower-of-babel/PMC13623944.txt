A chromosomal-level genome assembly of Sinodiscula theae-sinensis strain Sthe1
Authors: Xingyun Shi, Zehong Meng, Shuai Li, Xiuxian Shen, Jianfeng Jin, Zhong Li, Yufeng Zhou, Jinfeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sinodiscula theae-sinensis, the type species of Sinodiscula, causes tea anthracnose in China. The lack of a high-quality reference genome has hindered studies on its molecular pathogenicity and evolutionary relationships with closely related species such as S. camellicola. In this study, we present the assembly of a chromosome-level genome for S. theae-sinensis, which was obtained by integrating PacBio, Illumina, and Hi-C sequencing data. The assembled genome size is 41.25 Mb, consisting of 55 scaffolds and 136 contigs, with scaffold and contig N50 values of 4.98 Mb and 4.93 Mb, respectively. A total of 98.5% (40.65 Mb) of the genome has been assigned to 9 chromosomes. BUSCO analysis utilizing the fungi_odb10 dataset (n = 758) revealed a genome assembly completeness of 98.1%, comprising 97.8% single-copy and 0.3% duplicated BUSCOs. We predicted 9,941 protein-coding genes and found repetitive elements that make up 6.52% (2.69 Mb) of the genome. This high-quality reference genome is a valuable resource for studying the pathogenic and evolutionary mechanisms of S. theae-sinensis.

Background & Summary

The tea plant (Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze) is a globally important economic crop, renowned for its rich content of beneficial compounds that can effectively lower the risk of human disease1,2. It is widely cultivated in many countries, with China being a top producer. In southern regions of China, tea plants serve as a crucial cash crop and play a pivotal role3,4. However, its yield is always at risk from many biotic stresses, such as tea anthracnose, which is a devastating foliar disease5,6. It is a serious disease that affects tea plants in Japan and China, and has been reported in Sri Lanka6–8. In Japan, the three main diseases affecting tea plants are known to be tea anthracnose, tea exobasidium blight, and tea white scab8. Anthracnose induces widespread necrosis and premature leaf drop in tea plants, resulting in notable reductions in both yield and quality6. Therefore, accurate identification of pathogens is crucial for formulating effective control strategies.

The causal agent of tea anthracnose has undergone significant taxonomic revision. During the past decades, the genus of Colletotrichum has been considered the major pathogenic fungus9–12. More recently, through phylogenetic investigations, the primary pathogen, which was first described as Gloeosporium theae-sinensis, has been transferred to the newly erected genus Sinodiscula and designated as the type species13. This genus was validly published in 2024, and to date, species such as S. theae-sinensis, S. camellicola, and S. flaviconidiata have been included14. The distribution of various species within this genus demonstrates clear regional distinctions. Among these, S. camellicola has been identified as the predominant fungal species in tea-growing regions of Guizhou, Hubei, and Anhui provinces in China, whereas S. theae-sinensis is the dominant species in tea plantations of Zhejiang and Fujian provinces13–15. This pattern of geographical distribution indicates possible variation in their ecological adaptability or interactions with the surrounding environments, which warrants in-depth investigation at the genomic level.

Although S. theae-sinensis is the type species of Sinodiscula and is dominant in several major tea-growing regions, genomic resources for this species remain limited. A chromosome-level genome assembly has recently become available for the closely related species S. camellicola (accession number GCA_053477585.1), providing an important foundation for comparative analyses within Sinodiscula. However, without a high-quality reference genome for S. theae-sinensis, it remains difficult to robustly compare gene repertoires (e.g., candidate effectors and secondary metabolite clusters) and to dissect the genetic basis underlying differences in pathogenicity and ecological adaptation across Sinodiscula species. To address this gap, we present the first chromosome-scale whole-genome sequence of S. theae-sinensis, generated by integrating data from PacBio HiFi, Illumina, and Hi-C technologies. The assembly includes comprehensive annotations of repetitive elements, non-coding RNAs, and protein-coding genes. This genomic asset will serve as a fundamental reference for future comparative studies investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying virulence, host interaction, and evolutionary dynamics within the Sinodiscula genus.

Methods

Sample collection, identification and sequencing

Tea anthracnose fungus strain Sthe1 used in this study was collected from diseased tea leaves in Tuanshan Village, Duyun City, Qiannan Buyi and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Guizhou Province, China, on April 21, 2021, for subsequent DNA and RNA sequencing. In addition, its morphological characteristics resemble those of S. theae-sinensis13. For further analysis, the Sthe1 strain was cultivated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium at 25 °C for 15 days under a 12-h light/dark cycle. After cultivation, the mycelia were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for ten minutes, followed by storage at −80 °C in the laboratory until sequencing.

Genomic DNA was extracted from mycelium cultured on PDA for 7 days. Total genomic DNA extracted using the MolPure® Fungal DNA Extraction Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used as template for PCR amplification. Four loci were used for Sinodiscula identification, including ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS), the present study uses a combined taxonomic approach based on morphology and DNA sequence analyses of the partial 28S nrDNA (nrLSU), DNA-directed RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2), and translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed in a Bio-Rad PCR Thermal Cycler T100 Gradient (Biorad, USA) under the following conditions, adapted from Guo et al.13. The ITS16, nrLSU17,18, rpb219 and tef120,21 loci were amplified for strain identification. Primer sequences, target loci and references are summarized in Table 1. Each 25 μL reaction contained 9.5 μL of nuclease-free Water, 12.5 μL of 2 × Taq PCR MasterMix II (KT211; TIANGEN Biotech; Beijing; China), 1 μL of extracted genomic DNA and 1 μL (10 μM stock) of the appropriate fungal-specific primers. Reactions were performed with negative controls containing no DNA. The quality of all PCR products was checked by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and then purified and sequenced by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Genomic DNA (gDNA) for Illumina sequencing was extracted from pooled specimens using the Blood & Cell Culture DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA was extracted from approximately 100 mg of frozen mycelia of S. theae-sinensis strain Sthe1 using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions22. RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 5 μg of total RNA using the TruSeq RNA v2 Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. PCR-free short-read libraries were prepared with the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Kit. The Hi-C library preparation involved chromatin crosslinking with formaldehyde (formaldehyde solution, #F809702, Macklin, Shanghai, China), chromatin digestion using the four-cutter restriction enzyme MboI (NEBNext DpnII Restriction Endonuclease, #R0543L, Ipswich, MA, USA), DNA end repair and labeling (NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing Module, #E7546L, Ipswich, MA, USA), proximity ligation, and DNA purification using AMPure XP beads (Pacific Biosciences). The ligated DNA was subsequently sheared, followed by biotin pull-down to enrich Hi-C fragments23. Subsequently, these junctions were enriched and physically sheared to obtain fragments of the target length (350 bp) for downstream sequencing analyses23. These chimeric fragments then served as the basis for constructing paired-end sequencing libraries, which effectively capture the original cross-linked long-range physical interactions. For short-read sequencing, the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform was utilized. In contrast to short-read methods, long-read sequencing relied on 15 kb SMRTbell libraries that were constructed using the PacBio SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0. These libraries were then sequenced in HiFi mode on a PacBio Sequel II system. Berry Genomics (Beijing, China) was responsible for the library preparation and sequencing processes. Genome assembly

Quality control of raw Illumina reads utilizes BBTools v38.8224: clumpify.sh removed duplicates, followed by bbduk.sh for adapter trimming and strict filtering (discard Q < 20 or >5 Ns; trim poly-A/G/C tails >10 bp; correct overlapping pairs). A survey analysis employing GenomeScope v2.025 was performed on the processed sequencing data to assess the genome size and repetitive sequence content of S. theae-sinensis. The genome size was approximately around 47.67 Mb, of which 6.84 Mb (14.37%) consisted of repetitive sequences (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Estimation of the Sinodiscula theae-sinensis genome size using GenomeScope.

Estimation of the Sinodiscula theae-sinensis genome size using GenomeScope.

We assembled an initial genome from PacBio HiFi reads utilizing Hifiasm v0.19.826 with default parameters. Heterozygosity was reduced by purging haplotigs with Purge_Dups v1.2.527 (-s 70 haplotype cutoff). Hi-C reads were quality-checked and then mapped to the assembly through Juicer v1.6.228. The resulting contact maps were scaffolded into chromosome-scale assemblies using 3D-DNA v18092229. The final assembly was subject to manual verification and correction in Juicebox v1.11.028 to address misjoins and orientation errors. Potential contaminants were detected through scanning the assembly against the NCBI nucleotide database utilizing MMseqs. 2 v11.130. Vector contamination was further evaluated using BLASTN (BLAST + v2.11.0)31 against the UniVec database, and sequences exhibiting >90% identity to UniVec entries were designated as contaminants. After removing these contaminants, the final chromosome-level assembly of S. theae-sinensis is 41.25 Mb in length with 55 scaffolds and 136 contigs, which is close to the genome size estimated (47.67 Mb) from the initial k-mer analysis. This high-quality genome assembly also has adequate continuity at the chromosomal level, with N50 values for both scaffolds and contigs reaching 4.98 Mb and 4.93 Mb, respectively (Table 4). In addition, the assembly anchored 98.55% of the genome (40.65 Mb) onto 9 chromosomes (Table 4; Figs. 2, 3). The observed high number of contigs (136), as opposed to the expected nine chromosomes, likely results from the fragmentation of the DNA template. Genome annotation

Repetitive elements were identified through de novo annotation using RepeatModeler v2.0.432, with the -LTRStruct option activated to improve detection of LTR retrotransposons. The resulting repeat library was merged with curated sequences from RepBase-2023090933 and Dfam v3.534 to build a custom database for downstream analyses. Repetitive sequences were then annotated and masked with RepeatMasker v4.1.235, which aligns the genome against this integrated database. The analysis identified 2.69 Mb, accounting for 6.52% of the S. theae-sinensis genome assembly, as repetitive sequences. Using Infernal v1.1.436 with the Rfam v14.1037 database for ncRNA identification and tRNAscan-SE v2.0.938 for tRNA detection, we identified a diverse ncRNA repertoire in S. theae-sinensis. We identified 197 ncRNAs, comprising 84 tRNAs, 73 rRNAs, 23 snRNAs, 3 sRNAs, 2 ribozymes, and other RNA types (Table 5).

The protein-coding genes of S. theae-sinensis were annotated using MAKER v3.01.0339. This annotation process combined homology-based evidence, de novo gene predictions, and transcriptome alignments to produce high-confidence gene models. Additionally, transcriptome reads were aligned to the genome with HISAT2 v2.2.140 and subsequently assembled into transcripts using StringTie v2.1.641. For ab initio gene prediction, we employed BRAKER v2.1.642, which integrates GeneMark-ES/ET/EP v4.68_lic43 and AUGUSTUS v3.4.044. Furthermore, another gene prediction approach was applied with GeMoMa v1.945, which leverages protein homology data from four well annotated Sordariomycetes reference genomes: Podospora pseudopauciseta (GCF_035222475.1), Pyricularia pennisetigena (GCF_004337985.1), Remersonia thermophila (GCF_042764415.1), and Sordaria macrospora (GCF_033870435.1). These species were selected because they provide curated protein sets and high-quality assemblies within Sordariomycetes, which improves homology-based gene model inference. In the annotation of protein-coding genes for S. theae-sinensis, a total of 9,941 were identified (Table 5), with an average gene length measuring 2,307.6 bp. The typical gene structure includes 3.2 exons (average length: 645.6 bp), 2.2 introns (average length: 114.4 bp), and 3.1 coding sequence (CDS) segments (average length: 505.3 bp). Gene prediction quality was evaluated using BUSCO (Fungi_odb10; n = 758), which revealed 98.1% completeness (743 BUSCOs), including 97.8% single-copy (741), 0.3% duplicated (2), 0.4% fragmented (2), and 1.5% missing (13). This high level of completeness confirms the reliability of the annotation.

To annotate the functions of the predicted proteins, homology searches against the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot database were carried out using DIAMOND v2.0.11.146. Furthermore, eggNOG-mapper v2.0.147 and InterProScan 5.53–87.048 were used for the assignment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms, identification of KEGG and Reactome pathways, and annotation of protein domains. The InterProScan analysis uses data from five databases: Pfam49, SMART50, Superfamily51, Gene3D52, and CDD53. The outputs from these resources were combined to generate overall predictions of gene function. In total, 9,514 genes were annotated with UniProt entries, with 6,290 of them being assigned GO terms, 2,723 mapped to KEGG pathways, 2,095 linked to EC numbers, and 8,207 classified into COG (Table 6). Phylogenomic analyses

To determine the taxonomic position of strain Sthe1, a multi-locus sequence analysis was performed using ITS, nrLSU, rpb2 and tef1, following Zhang et al.14. Amplicon-derived data for Sthe1 were aligned alongside homologous loci from representative Sinodiscula species and closely related taxa acquired from GenBank, guided by recent taxonomic revisions. Initial multiple sequence alignments were generated utilizing the MAFFT v.7 web server (http://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/), followed by rigorous visual inspection and manual curation within BioEdit v.7.1.1155. The refined single-gene alignments were subsequently concatenated into a unified multi-locus supermatrix via MEGA v.5.156. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred through parallel Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) frameworks. ML estimation was performed in RAxMLGUI v.1.5b157 under the GTR + GAMMA nucleotide substitution model, with topological robustness evaluated across 1,000 rapid bootstrap replicates. For the BI analysis, the optimal evolutionary model was predetermined utilizing MrModeltest v.2.358 based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Bayesian posterior probabilities were computed in MrBayes v.3.2.659 employing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms. These simulations were executed for 10,000,000 generations with a sampling frequency of 100, discarding the initial 25% of sampled trees as burn-in to ensure chain stationarity. Nakataea oryzae (CBS 243.76) was designated as the outgroup. Data Records

Sequencing data produced in this study is accessible through the following NCBI SRA accession numbers: Hi-C data (SRR35428425)60, transcriptome reads (SRR35428426)61, Illumina short reads (SRR35428427)62, and PacBio HiFi long reads (SRR35428428)63. The finished genome assembly is available under NCBI accession GCA_051027275.164. Genome annotation data, including repeated DNA pieces, gene structure predictions, and functional annotations, have also been deposited on Figshare65.

Technical Validation

To evaluate the quality of the genome assembly, two different methods were employed. First, we estimated assembly completeness with BUSCO v5.0.466 using the Fungi reference set (n = 758). The assembly has high BUSCO completeness (98.1%), including 97.8% of genes present as single copies, 0.3% duplicated, 0.4% fragmented, and only 1.5% missing. Second, we estimated accuracy by read-mapping rates; we mapped PacBio and Illumina reads to the assembly with Minimap2 v2.2367 and SAMtools v1.968, respectively. The assembly exhibited high mapping rates for both PacBio (94.61%) and Illumina (90.93%) reads. Together, these complementary analyses confirm that our genome assembly is of high quality.
