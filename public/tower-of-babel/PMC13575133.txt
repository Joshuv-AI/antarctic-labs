Predictors of visual outcomes and recurrence after pars plana vitrectomy for uveitis: A 3-Year longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Jae Hyup Lee, Eun Kyoung Lee, Chang Ki Yoon, Un Chul Park, Kyu Hyung Park, Kunho Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUveitis is a chronic relapsing inflammatory disease that often requires long-term systemic immunomodulatory (IMD) therapy. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) is increasingly used as an adjunctive treatment for selected uveitis cases; however, robust long-term evidence remains limited. This retrospective cohort study evaluated 3-year outcomes in 159 uveitic eyes undergoing PPV and a reference group of 22 eyes receiving medical therapy alone to assess visual, anatomical, and systemic treatment burden changes. Following PPV, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved significantly and remained stable through 36 months (P < 0.001). Central macular thickness decreased from 326.8 to 288.4 μm (P < 0.001), and the proportion of eyes with vitreous haze < grade 1 increased from 24.5% to 90.0%. Systemic corticosteroid doses and the number of IMD agents were significantly reduced at 36 months (both P < 0.001), whereas these improvements were not observed in the reference group. Multivariable analyses identified worse baseline BCVA and higher preoperative intraocular pressure as predictors of poorer visual outcomes. Higher preoperative corticosteroid dose (> 15 mg/day) was associated with improved visual recovery but increased recurrence risk. Introduction

Uveitis encompasses a heterogenous group of intraocular inflammatory disorders with diverse etiologies, including infectious, autoimmune, and idiopathic causes. These conditions vary widely in anatomical involvement, systemic associations, and disease chronicity, resulting in marked variability in clinical presentation and prognosis1–4. Crucially, this inherent heterogeneity poses a major challenge in uveitis research, as it often limits the ability to derive consistent, generalizable outcomes and to establish standardized management strategies applicable across all disease subtypes1,2,5. In a subset of patients, recurrent or chronic inflammation leads to persistent vitreous opacity, macular edema, or vitreoretinal interface abnormalities resulting in sustained visual impairment that may be refractory to pharmacologic therapy alone.

Pharmacologic management—including corticosteroids, immunomodulatory (IMD) agents, and biologic therapies—remains the mainstay of treatment once infectious etiologies have been excluded1,6. Currently, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) is not regarded as a primary therapeutic modality; consequently, high-level evidence from randomized clinical trials directly evaluating PPV against standard medical therapy is lacking7. Historically, concerns regarding surgically induced inflammation limited the role of PPV to cases with advanced structural complications. However, with the advent of small-gauge vitrectomy systems and refined surgical techniques, PPV has become a safer and less invasive adjunctive intervention. Beyond addressing structural issues, PPV can improve media clarity, relieve vitreoretinal traction, and potentially reduce the intraocular inflammatory burden by removing inflammatory mediators and cellular debris8–12.

Despite these advances, the absence of large-scale, long-term data has hindered efforts to define the optimal indications and timing of PPV in uveitis. Given that the therapeutic impact of PPV is unlikely to be uniform, it is essential to understand how outcomes differ according to anatomical subtype, inflammatory activity, and surgical indication. In this context, well-characterized data from large longitudinal cohorts can provide critical clinical insights that are difficult to obtain through conventional trials.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the 3-year outcomes of PPV in a large, well-characterized cohort of uveitis patients. By systematically analyzing clinically relevant subgroups, we sought to provide a robust assessment of PPV outcomes in real-world practice. Rather than positioning PPV as a replacement for medical therapy, this study seeks to identify the specific clinical contexts in which PPV, as part of a comprehensive perioperative management strategy, is associated with the greatest sustained benefit, thereby providing a practical framework for surgical decision-making in uveitis management.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two tertiary centers in Korea: Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) and Busan Paik Hospital. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of both participating institutions (SNUH IRB no. 2308-129-1459; Busan Paik Hospital IRB no. 2025-11-036), and the study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Owing to the retrospective design of the study and the use of de-identified patient information, the need for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Boards.

We included consecutive patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis between January 2000 and December 2021, all of whom followed for at least 36 months. Exclusion criteria included eyes with (1) Severe visual impairment worse than positive light perception, (2) History of previous vitrectomy, (3) High myopia (− 6.0 diopters or greater spherical equivalent, or 26.5 mm or greater axial length), (4) Presence of any other ocular pathology that could interfere with visual function other than cataract, epiretinal membrane (ERM), or vitreous haze.

This study was designed as a longitudinal cohort analysis of patients with uveitis. To better contextualize these surgical outcomes and provide a perspective on the natural disease trajectory over a 3-year period, a group of uveitis patients managed exclusively with medical therapy was utilized as a clinical contextual reference. This group was not a randomized or balanced control arm, and the small sample size limits direct comparative efficacy analysis.

Surgical procedure

The surgeries were performed by 3 retina specialists following the same procedure. Most patients increased their systemic steroid dosage 1 month prior to surgery. A standard 3-port PPV using a 23 or 25-gauge was carried out under general or retrobulbar anesthesia. In the cases requiring cataract surgery, phacoemulsification and intraocular lens (IOL) implantation preceded the vitrectomy procedure. Peeling of the ERM and/or internal limiting membrane (ILM) was performed selectively in cases with ERM or intractable macular edema (ME). Additional procedures, including intravitreal injections, intraocular tamponades, and endolaser photocoagulation, were performed at the discretion of the physician. In most cases, a local injection of triamcinolone acetonide or dexamethasone implant was administered at the end of surgery.

Ophthalmologic evaluation

Baseline demographics of the patients, etiology and anatomical location of uveitis, indication for PPV, and duration from the onset of uveitis to PPV were recorded. A comprehensive ophthalmologic examination including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), slit lamp biomicroscope, indirect ophthalmoscopy, and spectral domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Cirrus™ HD-OCT; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) measurements were performed at baseline, 1, 6, 12, and 36 months after treatment. The preoperative fluorescein angiography (FAG, Heidelberg Retina Angiograph, Heidelberg, Germany or Optomap panoramic 200 A, Optos plc, Dunfermline, Scotland) images were obtained to assess the activity of inflammation and the anatomic location of uveitis.

Assessment of ocular inflammation

The degree of intraocular inflammation was evaluated by each operating retinal specialist both preoperatively and postoperatively. The assessment was conducted according to the standardization of uveitis nomenclature (SUN) criteria, using slit lamp biomicroscope and indirect ophthalmoscopy2. The absence of active inflammation or complete inflammation control was determined by observing a grade of 0.5 + or fewer cells in the anterior chamber and anterior vitreous with no involvement of the posterior segment2,13. To assess the clarity of view of the posterior segment on funduscopic examination, we used the National Institutes of Health grading system, which defines vitreous haze as a grade of 0 to 4.13 The study also evaluated the number of recurrent uveitis events during each time period. It was considered as a recurrence if any of the following conditions was confirmed when compared with the best-achieved postoperative quiescent state (or the immediately preceding visit), (1) 1 step or more increase in the grade of cells in the anterior chamber; (2) 1 step or more increase in vitreous haze; (3) any increased inflammation that required supplemental therapy(e.g., initiation or dose escalation of topical, periocular, or systemic corticosteroids, or addition of IMD agents)14,15. The types and dosage of systemic IMD agents were reviewed at baseline and follow-up visits. An increase in systemic IMD agent dosage was defined as an increase in the dosage of all systemic IMD agents compared to baseline. Conversely, a decrease in dosage was defined as a reduction in the dosage of all systemic IMD agents. Subjects who did not meet the criteria above were classified in the maintaining group.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome of the present study was to assess changes in BCVA and inflammatory status over a 36 months period. The inflammation status was evaluated based on the recurrence of uveitis, the grade of vitreous haze, changes in central macular thickness (CMT), and the dosage and number of medications. Initial analysis compared BCVA and inflammatory status between the PPV group and the reference group (non-PPV) to establish a baseline for long-term trends.

To further investigate the specific impact of surgical intervention, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed within the surgical cohort. Eyes that underwent PPV were stratified according to three clinically relevant factors: anatomical subtype, primary surgical indication, and preoperative inflammatory activity. First, based on anatomical subtype, eyes were classified into (1) intermediate uveitis group, defined as inflammation primarily involving the vitreous (including eyes with peripheral snowballs or snowbanking), and (2) posterior uveitis group, which comprised eyes with posterior uveitis or panuveitis (inflammation involving the retina, choroid, or diffuse intraocular inflammation (eFigure 1).2 Second, according to the primary surgical indication, eyes were categorized into the ERM/ME group or the Media opacity (vitreous haze/hemorrhage) group. Finally, preoperative inflammatory activity was classified as active (presence of active inflammation) or inactive (absence of active inflammation) at the time of surgery, based on the criteria defined above. All subgroup analyses were prespecified prior to data analysis. However, because the study was not formally powered to detect differences within these specific subsets, the results of all subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory. Furthermore, because all subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature, formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied. Consequently, nominal P-values are reported, and theses specific findings should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

All visual acuity measurements were converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units for statistical analysis. To evaluate longitudinal changes in clinical parameters, within-group differences from baseline were assessed using the paired t test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the independent t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the normality of the distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A total of 25 patients contributed both eyes to the analysis (50 bilateral eyes). To account for within-subject correlation between fellow eyes of the same patient (inter-eye correlation), generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure were used for the primary comparative analyses. With the exception of CMT, complete longitudinal data were available for all included eyes. Missing data for CMT at specific follow-up time points (in 35 eyes) were handled using available-case analysis within the GEE framework. Factors associated with long-term functional and inflammatory outcomes were evaluated using univariable and multivariable regression models based on GEE. Variables with a significance of P value < 0.15 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analysis. Recurrence was modeled as a binary outcome (occurrence within 36 months) for logistic regression, while the recurrence rate was calculated as the number of events per eye divided by the follow-up duration in months. To account for unequal follow-up intensity, the longitudinal recurrence metric was calculated for each interval by dividing the total number of distinct recurrence events by the total person-months of follow-up (events/month). All statistical analyses were performed using Python (Version 3.x; Python Software Foundation) with the statsmodels library (Seabold & Perktold, 2010) and pandas library. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

(1) Visual outcome.

In the PPV cohort, BCVA improved significantly at all postoperative time points compared with the preoperative baseline (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The greatest improvement was observed early after surgery, and these visual gains were maintained throughout follow-up. In the reference cohort, BCVA showed only modest improvement over time.

In subgroup analyses within the PPV cohort, both the combined phacovitrectomy group and the vitrectomy-only group demonstrated significant improvement in BCVA at each follow-up visit compared with baseline (all P < 0.05). Visual outcomes did not differ significantly between the two surgical approaches at any time point (all P > 0.05).

(2) Inflammatory control.

Following PPV, CMT decreased significantly from a mean of 326.8 μm at baseline to 298.9 μm at 12 months (P = 0.014) and further to 288.4 μm at 36 months (P < 0.001). In the reference cohort, CMT remained largely stable with only minor fluctuations during follow-up.

The recurrence rate (defined as the number of recurrences divided by follow-up duration in months) progressively declined after PPV (from 0.09 events/month at 0–1 month to 0.06 events/month at 1–6 months, 0.05 events/month at 6–12 months, and 0.02 events/month at 12–36 months) and reached statistical significance during the 12–36-month interval (P = 0.022). In the reference cohort, recurrence rates tended to increase over time compared with the initial follow-up period (from 0.00 events/month at 0–1 month to 0.06 events/month at 1–6 months, 0.08 events/month at 6–12 months, 0.03 events/month at 12–36 months).

Vitreous haze improved substantially after PPV. The proportion of eyes with vitreous haze < grade 1 increased from 24.5% at baseline to 90.0% at 6 months and remained stable through 36 months. (Fig. 2) The burden of systemic therapy also decreased following surgery. In the PPV cohort, the mean systemic corticosteroid dose was significantly reduced at all follow-up time points compared with baseline (all P < 0.001), and the mean number of systemic IMD agents decreased significantly at 36 months (P < 0.001). In the reference cohort, no significant changes were observed in corticosteroid dose or the number of systemic IMD agents beyond 6 months (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis

(1) Anatomical location.

Among eyes in the PPV cohort, 65 eyes were classified as intermediate uveitis and 94 as posterior uveitis group (eTable 2). Baseline logMAR BCVA was significantly better in the intermediate uveitis group than in the posterior uveitis group (0.84 vs. 1.19, P = 0.019), however, both groups demonstrated significant visual improvement at all follow-up time points compared with baseline (Fig. 4A, B). Baseline CMT was significantly higher in the posterior uveitis group than in the intermediate group (346.0 vs. 300.0 μm, P = 0.023). After surgery, CMT decreased progressively in the posterior uveitis group, and no significant between-group differences were observed from 1 month onward (all P > 0.05 at 1, 6, 12, and 36 months, respectively).

The proportion of eyes undergoing ERM peeling did not differ significantly between groups (47.7% in intermediate vs. 44.7% in posterior uveitis; P = 0.708). Both systemic corticosteroid dose and the number of systemic IMD agents gradually decreased over time, with significant reductions at 36 months compared with baseline in both groups (P < 0.001 and P = 0.003, respectively). No significant between-group differences were observed in systemic corticosteroid dose (P = 0.070) or the number of systemic IMD agents (P = 0.083) over the follow-up period (eFigure 2).

(2) Surgical indication.

Eyes were stratified according to the primary surgical indication. The media opacity group (vitreous haze or hemorrhage) included 65 eyes, and the ERM/ME group included 94 eyes (eTable 2). Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in BCVA from baseline as early as 1 month, which was sustained through 36 months. The magnitude of visual improvement was greater in the media opacity group (Fig. 4). Trends in CMT differed between groups. The ERM/ME group showed a steady decrease in CMT throughout follow-up, whereas the media opacity group exhibited a gradual increase over time (Fig. 4).

Systemic treatment burden decreased in both groups. Systemic corticosteroid dose was significantly reduced at 36 months in both groups, with earlier reductions observed in the ERM/ME group (eFigure 2). The number of systemic IMD agents decreased significantly in the ERM/ME group from 12 months onward (P = 0.031), whereas it remained relatively stable in the media opacity group.

(3) Preoperative inflammatory activity.

Eyes were stratified according to preoperative inflammatory status: 97 eyes without active inflammation (inactive group) and 62 eyes with active intraocular inflammation at the time of surgery (active group). Baseline BCVA was significantly worse in the active group than in the inactive group (1.29 vs. 0.89 logMAR; P = 0.009) (eTable 2). Nevertheless, both groups demonstrated significant improvement in BCVA beginning at 1 month postoperatively, which was sustained throughout follow-up (all P < 0.001). With respect to anatomical outcomes, both groups showed a decreasing trend in CMT, however, a statistically significant reduction at 12 months was observed only in the inactive group (Fig. 4). Systemic corticosteroid dose decreased significantly in both groups by 36 months (both P < 0.001). The number of systemic IMD agents decreased significantly only in the inactive group during follow-up (P < 0.001 at 36 months), despite a lower baseline medication burden compared with the active group, demonstrating that preoperative quiescence facilitates more efficient postoperative tapering. (eFigure 2).

Postoperative complications

The most common postoperative complication following PPV was elevated IOP (41 eyes, 25.8%), followed by ME (28 eyes, 17.6%), secondary ERM formation (16 eyes, 10.1%), VH (2 eyes, 1.3%), rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) (2 eyes, 1.3%), and choroidal detachment (1 eye, 0.6%). Among eyes with elevated IOP, 10 (6.3%) required topical IOP-lowering therapy, whereas IOP remained controlled without treatment in the remaining cases. All cases of RRD were successfully managed with a single additional vitrectomy, with no subsequent recurrence. Other complications were transient and resolved without long-term sequelae.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that PPV is associated with durable functional and inflammatory improvements in uveitis over a long-term period. Importantly, the observed outcomes likely reflect the combined effect of the entire perioperative treatment package—including PPV, systemic corticosteroid intensification, combined cataract surgery, and intraoperative local steroid injections—which may have contributed to early improvements in visual acuity and inflammatory control, rather than the isolated effect of vitrectomy. Across 159 eyes followed for 36 months after vitrectomy, BCVA improved at all postoperative visits and remained stable thereafter, while inflammatory control was reflected by sustained reductions in CMT and a marked increase in the proportion of eyes without vitreous haze. Systemic treatment burden also eased over time, with both dose and number of systemic IMD agents significantly reduced by 36 months. Although improvements in BCVA from baseline were also observed in the reference group and the PPV group showed better visual acuity at all follow up visits, the non-randomized, small sample size of this clinical comparator limits direct causal comparisons. In contrast, inflammatory status and treatment burden showed no significant improvement at any follow-up time point in the reference group, with the recurrence rate even showing a progressive increase by 12 months. These findings suggest that PPV is associated with improvements in visual and anatomical outcomes and may facilitate a steroid-sparing approach in cases where medical therapy alone is insufficient. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of the superior isolated effect of PPV over medical therapy alone, given the small size of the reference group, potential baseline imbalances, the non-randomized study design, and perioperative treatment intensification in the PPV group.

The present findings extend prior small studies suggesting visual and inflammatory benefits following PPV, which were often limited by short follow-up and heterogeneous reporting12,16,17. In contrast, this study applied uniform assessment intervals (1, 6, 12, and 36 months) and evaluated functional, anatomical, and treatment-burden outcomes in parallel, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term role of PPV and perioperative treatment in uveitis management.

Vitrectomy can improve visual and anatomical outcomes in patients with uveitis through several complementary mechanisms, although these effects occur within the context of the overall surgical and perioperative treatment strategy rather than in isolation. Mechanistically, PPV can clear inflammatory cells, cytokines, and immune complexes from the vitreous cavity, which may help reduce antigenic stimulation and chronic inflammation8–12. It also alleviates tractional stress from inflammatory membranes and restores optical transparency and foveal contour18. Moreover, by removing the vitreous scaffold, PPV may facilitate intraocular drug penetration, enhancing the efficacy of adjunctive therapies8,10,12. The comparable long-term visual outcomes observed between PPV alone and combined phacovitrectomy suggest that the principal benefits derive from inflammation control and traction release rather than lens extraction itself12.

Since uveitis encompasses diverse types and etiologies that result in a broad range of clinical manifestations and prognoses1–4,19, this study investigated how surgical outcomes vary across phenotypes through detailed subgroup analyses. These analyses clarified how presentation characteristics shape the postoperative course. By anatomical location, both intermediate and posterior uveitis eyes achieved and maintained significant BCVA improvement at all visits, although posterior cases began with poorer vision and thicker macula. The intergroup CMT difference narrowed after early follow-up, consistent with traction release and improved drug penetration after vitrectomy. Clinically, these findings suggest that PPV may be beneficial in appropriately selected eyes regardless of anatomic subtype when structural complications or persistent vitreous haze threaten vision, provided that perioperative inflammation is adequately controlled.

Surgical indication also carried prognostic significance. Eyes operated for ERM or ME showed a steady decline in CMT throughout follow-up, whereas those treated primarily for media opacity exhibited the opposite trend. Despite these contrasting CMT patterns, both groups demonstrated sustained visual gains. This difference likely reflects the underlying pathophysiology: macula-centered traction in ERM/ME group benefits directly from membrane removal, whereas media opacity group represents diffuse vitreous inflammation that has less immediate effect on foveal thickness. Medication trends also diverged. Although both groups achieved significant corticosteroid reduction by 36 months, ERM/ME group reached earlier tapering and a significant decrease in the number of systemic IMD agents by 12 months. Furthermore, the ERM/ME group showed a lower incidence of uveitis recurrence within 36 months. Collectively, surgical indication functions as a practical surrogate for disease phenotype and an early guide to steroid tapering kinetics.

Preoperative inflammatory activity added another layer of distinction. Even eyes with residual inflammation at surgery achieved early and sustained visual improvement, underscoring that complete quiescence is not always required for benefit once infection is excluded and perioperative IMD is adequately managed. However, eyes without active inflammation at baseline experienced greater reductions in treatment burden—particularly a significant decrease in systemic agents by 36 months—indicating that preoperative quiescence facilitates more efficient postoperative tapering even when long-term functional outcomes are similar.

Regression analyses using GEE models provided additional insight into factors associated with long-term functional outcomes and disease recurrence. With respect to visual outcomes, worse baseline BCVA and higher preoperative IOP were independently associated with worse BCVA at 36 months. The relationship between baseline vision and postoperative outcome likely reflects the cumulative, often irreversible, deleterious effects of chronic inflammation on the retina and optic nerve20,21.

Higher preoperative IOP was independently associated with poorer visual outcomes. In uveitic eyes, elevated IOP often reflects secondary glaucoma mechanisms, including trabeculitis, inflammatory debris within the trabecular meshwork, and synechial angle closure22. Our findings suggest that higher values compared to the typically lower IOP seen in ciliary body hyposecretion may indicate a compromised trabecular outflow facility and a higher cumulative burden of anterior segment inflammation. This chronic inflammatory burden may contribute to subclinical glaucomatous neuroretinal damage or increase the susceptibility of the optic nerve head to perioperative stress, ultimately limiting long-term visual potential23.

In contrast, a higher preoperative systemic corticosteroid dose (> 15 mg/day) was independently associated with better BCVA at 36 months (eTable 4). Although a requirement for higher corticosteroid doses typically reflects more severe inflammatory disease, this association may highlight the importance of aggressive perioperative inflammation control. Uveitic eyes are susceptible to exaggerated postoperative reactive inflammatory response following surgical trauma, which may contribute to fibrin formation, cystoid macular edema, or hypotony24. The use of higher-dose corticosteroids prior to surgery likely represents an intentional strategy to suppress this rebound inflammation and optimize the surgical environment. Thus, aggressive preoperative quiescence appears to protect retinal function and facilitate visual recovery25. However, regarding disease relapse, this same high medication burden was identified as the independent risk factor for recurrence. This suggests a dual implication: while aggressive preoperative suppression may be beneficial for maximizing visual outcomes, the necessity for such high-doses inherently signals a more biologically aggressive and refractory disease phenotype that requires vigilant long-term surveillance5.

The safety profile was favorable and comparable to other contemporary PPV series26–31. The most common postoperative events were elevated IOP, ME, and secondary ERM formation. Most complications were successfully managed, and no severe sight-threatening events were observed. Considering the sustained visual and inflammatory improvements and reduced systemic treatment burden, PPV appears to be a safe and effective adjunctive surgical approach in uveitic eyes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and non-randomized allocation to PPV introduce a significant potential for selection bias. Patients undergoing surgery likely represent a distinct clinical subgroup compared to those managed medically, meaning the observed long-term improvements may partially reflect differences in underlying disease characteristics rather than the independent effect of vitrectomy. Additionally, as a dual-center study, potential center effects related to differences in patient populations and treatment strategies cannot be excluded. Second, it is crucial to recognize that the favorable outcomes observed in the PPV group should not be attributed solely to the removal of the vitreous. Rather, they reflect the effects of a comprehensive surgical and perioperative treatment package. This package typically includes not only the vitrectomy itself but also the administration of perioperative intensive corticosteroids and concurrent procedures such as phacoemulsification. These adjunctive treatments and co-interventions undoubtedly act synergistically with the mechanical clearance of inflammatory mediators. Third, because both eyes from the same patient were included in some cases, statistical adjustments using GEE were applied to account for inter-eye correlation; however, formal sensitivity analyses were not conducted due to the limited sample size and residual confounding related to institutional practice patterns may persist. Furthermore, although outcomes and recurrence were evaluated over 36 months, longer follow-up may be necessary given the chronic, relapsing nature of uveitis. In addition, the long study period (2000–2021) may introduce era effects, particularly with the transition to small-gauge vitrectomy systems and the evolving use of biologic agents. Despite these limitations, a key strength of this study is the large-scale, long-term real-world dataset that reflects diverse clinical contexts encountered in practice.

In conclusion, PPV was longitudinally associated with sustained improvements in visual function, inflammatory control, and reduction of systemic treatment burden across diverse uveitis subtypes. Our results highlight that higher preoperative IOP and worse preoperative BCVA are adverse prognostic factors for vision. Furthermore, aggressive preoperative corticosteroid control appears to optimize visual recovery by suppressing reactive inflammation, although patients requiring such high doses remain at higher risk for recurrence. Collectively, these results support the judicious use of PPV as a safe and valuable component of comprehensive, long-term management strategies for uveitic eyes.
