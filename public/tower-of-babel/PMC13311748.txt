Protein biomarkers for predicting incident carotid plaque: a nested case–control study in the ChinaHEART cohort with transportability assessment in the UK Biobank

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesCarotid plaque is an early manifestation of atherosclerosis and is closely associated with the risk of myocardial ischaemia, ischaemic stroke and other atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVDs). To identify new protein biomarkers associated with carotid plaque, which will enhance early warning of ASCVDsDesignNested case–control study within a prospective cohort, with external assessment of transportability in an independent population-based cohort.Participants and data sourcesThe development cohort is from China, which is a nationwide, community-based cohort and the external validation cohort is from the UK Biobank in the UK. Introduction

Carotid plaque is an early sign of atherosclerosis and is closely associated with the risk of myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke and other atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD).1 2 The prevalence of carotid plaque remains high, with an estimated percentage change of 58.97% globally and 72.19% in China among people aged 30–79 years from 2000 to 2020.3–5 Given the complicated pathogenesis of atherosclerosis,6 there are various biomarkers involved in the pathological processes, such as lipid metabolism disorders, endothelial damage, inflammatory response, wall shear stress and so on.7–9 Identifying new biomarkers associated with carotid plaque is expected to improve the early detection and prediction of ASCVD on top of traditional risk factors.

However, biomarkers discovered in previous studies are mostly based on people who have already undergone a cardiovascular event,10–12 and these biomarkers tend to reflect late-stage atherosclerotic progression and may not be sensitive to the early phase of the disease. This makes it easy to miss the window for early intervention in atherosclerosis. Protein biomarkers can offer a broader and deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underscore cardiovascular disease (CVD), and it is foundational to the development of future therapeutic interventions.13

Moreover, it has not been explored how much incremental value these biomarkers, especially protein biomarkers measured by the highly sensitive and efficiently detected measures, can add to traditional prediction models, and how the effect of prediction varies among different populations. To address these knowledge gaps, we selected a number of biomarkers related to vascular injury, inflammation, angiogenesis and cardiometabolism and measured them using a highly sensitive and efficient assay (Meso Scale Discovery, MSD). We aimed to filter out candidate biomarkers for the incidence of carotid plaque, construct and validate prediction models, and then assess the additional prediction value of these new biomarkers, by leveraging data and blood samples from the ChinaHEART (Health Evaluation And risk Reduction through nationwide Teamwork) cohort, as well as data from the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study consists of two sections. First, we conducted a nested case–control biomarker study based on the ChinaHEART project, a national population cohort, to explore biomarkers with potential value in predicting incident carotid plaque. Second, we used data from the UK Biobank to evaluate the incremental value they provided in ASCVD risk prediction.

The ChinaHEART is a public health programme aiming at screening and managing high-risk individuals with CVD throughout China. Detailed information on the ChinaHEART project has previously been published.14 In brief, from January 2015 to December 2018, residents aged 35–75 years, who had lived in the community for at least 6 months in the past 12 months, were invited and recruited in each study site which was scattered in 31 provinces of China. For this analysis, among participants with plasma samples collected at baseline and carotid ultrasound performed both at baseline and 2-year follow-up (follow-up procedures are detailed in online supplemental appendix 1), those without carotid plaque or thickened carotid intima-media, without a history of CVD (ie, myocardial infarction, coronary artery bypass graft surgery, percutaneous coronary intervention, stroke, valvular heart disease, arrhythmia, heart failure and acute coronary syndrome), cancer and chronic kidney disease (CKD) at baseline were considered potentially eligible. Eventually, participants with incident carotid plaque during the 2-year follow-up were classified into the case group, and the control group was generated by selecting individuals without carotid plaque over the follow-up, who were matched with the cases according to age, sex, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure (SBP), body mass index (BMI), smoking status and a history of diabetes. Detailed procedures of patient selection and match criteria are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental appendix 4.

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort of over 500 000 individuals aged 40–69.15 The UK Biobank data set issued on 3 August 2023 was used for analyses. Proteomics data were available for 53 006 participants. We excluded those with carotid plaque, and a history of CVD, cancer or CKD at baseline, then divided participants into case and control groups based on whether an ASCVD event occurred.

Patient and public involvement

This study was conducted using existing cohort data. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Data collection and variables

In the ChinaHEART project, participants underwent standardised in-person interviews via an electronic data acquisition system equipped with a real-time logical check function conducted by skilled workers. Information on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyles and medical history was gathered during the interviews. Participants were also subjected to laboratory tests and physical examinations under standardised equipment and procedures, obtaining information such as blood pressure, fasting blood glucose and lipid profiles (see more in online supplemental appendix 2).

During the baseline and each follow-up visit, carotid artery ultrasound examinations were performed by trained physicians from local hospitals, and then the ultrasound reports were checked by a specialised doctor according to the guidelines for ultrasound measurements approved by the National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases (NCCD). The carotid intima–media thickness of both the right and left common carotid arteries was measured using ultrasound scanners equipped with a variable frequency (5–12 MHz) linear array transducer. The presence of carotid plaque is defined as an intima-media thickness of ≥1.5 mm at the site, or a localised area of the carotid artery wall that has a thickening of at least 0.5 mm,2 16 or 50% greater than the thickness of the surrounding vessel wall, which is confirmed by the ultrasound image and report (the quality control of carotid ultrasound test in the ChinaHEART is detailed in online supplemental appendix 3).

In the UK Biobank, participants answered self-completed questionnaires and interview complements on sociodemographic, lifestyle and health-related factors and completed a range of physical measures at recruitment. The UK Biobank data were linked to Hospital Episode Statistics data, which used the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and Related Health Problems, 9th Revision (ICD-9) to record diagnosis information, and Office of Population, Censuses and Surveys: Classification of Interventions and Procedures, version 4 (OPCS-4), to code operative procedures. Death registries used include all death records with both primary and secondary causes of death coded in ICD-10. We identified individuals with incident ASCVD including ischaemic heart diseases (ICD-10 codes: I20–I25, ICD-9: 410–413), cerebral infarction, stroke and occlusion and stenosis of precerebral and cerebral arteries (ICD-10: I63–I66, G45, ICD-9: 430–436), atherosclerosis, thrombosis and peripheral vascular disease (ICD-10: I70, I73–I74, ICD-9: 440, 443–444) and related operations (OPCS-4 codes: K40–K50, K75, L50–L54, L58–L60, L63, X09).

Biomarker measurement

In ChinaHEART, blood samples of participants were collected at baseline using EDTA vacuum tubes. After centrifuging at 2100 g for 10 min within a day of being collected, plasma samples were transported on dry ice to the NCCD and stored at −80°C or −180°C for future research purposes. Biomarkers were measured in the plasma via the V-plex assays of the MSD platform (Meso Scale Diagnostics) at the laboratory of the NCCD. Based on previous studies,7–9 we ensured the biomarkers associated with vascular injury, inflammations, angiogenesis and metabolism and filtered out 28 potential biomarkers, including adiponectin, fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (Flt-1), placental growth factor (PlGF), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), VEGF C (VEGF-C), VEGF D (VEGF-D), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), Cystatin C, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), FGF-23, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin 10 (IL-10), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 8 (IL-8), E-Selectin, P-Selectin, intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), intercellular cell adhesion molecule-3 (ICAM-3), thrombomodulin, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), serum amyloid A (SAA), C reactive protein (CRP), endothelial cell-specific tyrosine kinase receptor-2 (Tie-2), C-peptide, insulin, human proinsulin and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1). All values at or below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) or above the upper limit of detection (ULOD) were replaced by the LLOD or ULOD value as suggested by the assay parameters and are shown in the online supplemental appendix (sTable 1).

In the UK Biobank, participants also provided blood samples.17 The biomarker data of UK Biobank were from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP)—a precompetitive biopharmaceutical consortium characterising the plasma proteomic profiles of 54 219 UK Biobank participants. In the study, only proteins that were measured in both ChinaHEART and UK Biobank were used in the models because the coincident biomarkers were different.

Statistical analysis

Before performing the analysis, we conducted a log transformation and normalisation of the biomarker data in the development cohort (online supplemental sFigure 1), and biomarker information in UK Biobank was processed in a standard way by the assay platforms. The means and SDs were computed for continuous variables, and the frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. The differences between groups were tested by the χ² test for categorical variables and the t-test for continuous variables.

To select potentially useful biomarkers and address the collinearity issue of variables, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model was applied. Subsequently, the logistic regression was performed to establish the predictive model using biomarkers identified by the LASSO regression. To examine the robustness of predictors, we also tried other methods to select significant biomarkers, including univariate logistic regression, best subset selection and stepwise regression.

We used bootstrapping for internal validation. Specifically, 1000 bootstrap samples were generated by sampling with replacement from the original dataset. In each resample, the fitted model was applied to estimate model performance and optimism was calculated by comparing performance between bootstrap samples and the original dataset.

The final performance estimates were corrected for optimism based on the bootstrap results. The receiver operator characteristics (ROC) curve was generated and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the discriminative performance of the predictive model. Additionally, the performance of this model was evaluated by the calibration curve, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (HL test) as well as the Brier score.

In the UK Biobank, we employed multiple imputations using the random forest algorithm to fill in missing demographic features and traditional risk factors, conducting five rounds of imputation to ensure a robust estimation of the missing data. To assess potential multicollinearity among predictors, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for variables included in the multivariable models. A VIF greater than 5 was considered indicative of substantial collinearity. To assess the added value of new biomarkers, we used traditional ASCVD predictors only and traditional ASCVD predictors with biomarkers to construct logistic prediction models. We similarly used the same methods and indicators to evaluate the discrimination and calibration of the model, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were also calculated to assess the additional value of the biomarkers. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings to the handling of missing data. A complete-case analysis was performed including only participants without missing values in the variables of interest. We also went for additional values across sex, age, BMI, hypertension status and different incident time (ie, 3 years, 5 years and 10 years) to find out in which populations and stages the biomarkers had better predictive increments.

All analyses were conducted with SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute) and R V.4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p<0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Selection of biomarkers

After a 2-year follow-up in the ChinaHEART cohort, 157 participants with incident carotid plaque were randomised to the case group, and 157 individuals were involved in the control group accordingly. Finally, we included 292 participants without missing protein data in the development cohort, including 145 cases and 147 controls (figure 1). In both case and control groups, the mean age was around 54 years, and approximately 84% were female. Study characteristics are detailed in online supplemental sTable 2. The expression of eight biomarkers was found to be significantly different between the case and control groups, including ICAM-3, adiponectin, proinsulin, IL10, MCP-1, Flt-1, PlGF and Tie-2 (p<0.05 for all) in the development cohort (table 1).

Construction and internal validation of predictive models

After being screened by the LASSO regression, the number of biomarkers was reduced from 28 to 11. We used these 11 biomarkers to construct a logistic model to predict the incidence of carotid plaque. The model we obtained exhibited excellent performance with an AUC of 0.761 (0.706–0.816), and a Brier score of 0.192, and the result of the HL test was not statistically significant (p=0.467), indicating a good model fit. For the results of internal validation, the bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.704 (0.642–0.765) across 1000 replicated samples, which showed a stable performance with the optimism of 0.057 (figure 2). Decision curve analysis showed that the model incorporating biomarkers provided a higher net benefit than the traditional model across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities. However, the magnitude of the improvement was modest, and the net benefit curves were relatively close (online supplemental sFigure 2).

Additionally, the predictive variables selected by the univariate logistic regression, best subset selection and decision tree algorithm were largely consistent with those identified by the LASSO regression and we also calculate their model performance, which underscores the robustness of the results (online supplemental sTable 3).

Additional value of the biomarkers in the UK Biobank

Following the inclusion and exclusion process (figure 1), out of the 30 800 participants in the UK Biobank, 3465 individuals (11.25%) were identified as having developed incident ASCVD, with a median time to incident diagnosis of 7.74 years. Compared with the control group, participants in the case group were older, and more likely to be male, with higher BMI, higher SBP, larger Townsend index, larger smoking rate and more family history (p<0.05, online supplemental sTable 4).

Because of the absence of Tie-2 in the UK Biobank, we added 10 biomarkers externally to the traditional ASCVD prediction model to assess the additional value of the biomarkers. By calculating VIF, no substantial multicollinearity was observed among the predictors, as all VIF values were below 5 (online supplemental sTable 5).

First, we constructed the prediction model based on the traditional predictors, with all the risk factors strongly and independently associated with ASCVD risk in UK Biobank (table 3). Older age, males, other ethnic, family history of ASCVD, higher BMI, blood pressure and a higher ratio of total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol were all associated with higher ASCVD risk (p<0.05, table 3). The AUC of the model is up to 0.700.

Discussion

In this study, we identified new biomarkers using the MSD platform based on the ChinaHEART cohort and developed an exploratory biomarker-based model to evaluate their potential association with early carotid plaque. We further evaluated the transportability of these biomarker associations and their incremental predictive value for ASCVDs in the UK Biobank, a large independent population-based cohort.

Our findings suggest that these biomarkers may provide additional information beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors. However, the observed improvement in discrimination in the UK Biobank was modest, indicating that the incremental predictive value of these biomarkers is limited. These results highlight the potential role of protein biomarkers as complementary tools in risk stratification rather than standalone predictors. We have identified several protein biomarkers that are associated with the formation of carotid plaque, which involve various mechanisms including vascular injury, inflammation and vascular regeneration. These biomarkers predominantly engage in two key pathways during the progression of carotid plaque and ASCVD. The first pathway is the VEGF receptor pathway, which is essential for promoting angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis. Within this family, placental growth factor and VEGF-D play significant roles. PlGF is involved in the repair processes of endothelial and smooth muscle cells, potentially offering a protective effect in the context of carotid plaque and ASCVD.18 Conversely, VEGF-D is critical for the maintenance of vascular homeostasis.19 The second pathway pertains to the angiopoietin (Ang)–Tie system.20 21 Under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions, levels of Ang-2 are elevated,22 which subsequently activates Tie-2, resulting in a marked increase in the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1. Atherosclerosis is characterised by the infiltration of leucocytes into inflamed regions, where monocytes are activated by chemokines such as MCP-1. This activation enables monocytes to adhere to endothelial cell adhesion molecules, including VCAM-1 and intercellular adhesion molecule-1, facilitating their stable attachment to the endothelium and subsequent transmigration into the arterial intima. The movement of monocytes is mediated by selectins, particularly P-selectin.23 Additionally, ICAM-3, another member of the cell adhesion factor family, is upregulated to enhance the inflammatory response of macrophages.24 Furthermore, other biomarkers such as adiponectin and IL-10, while not directly involved in these two pathways, also contribute to carotid plaque progression by promoting lipid reduction and exerting anti-inflammatory effects.25–29

The noteworthy observation is that the coefficients of GDF-15 displayed contrasting signs in the logistic model employed to predict the occurrence of carotid plaque and ASCVD. This observation leads us to hypothesise that GDF-15 may serve divergent functions in the processes of carotid plaque formation and the pathogenesis of ASCVD. As an immunoregulatory cytokine, GDF-15 is posited to play a crucial role in the modulation of both the initiation and maintenance of atherosclerosis.30 Previous studies have identified dual functions of GDF-15, with certain studies reporting cardioprotective effects and inconsistent correlations between GDF-15 levels and subsequent ischaemic events. Notably, GDF-15 is expressed at low levels in healthy human cardiovascular tissues, but its expression increases significantly in response to cardiovascular injuries related to atherosclerosis and other CVDs.31–33 Excessive GDF-15 may increase angiogenesis and inflammation, thereby contributing to the increased vulnerability of atherosclerotic plaque.34 35 Regarding the predictive capacity of individual biomarkers, both GDF-15 and PlGF can achieve large incremental value in ASCVD prediction models. GDF-15 has been used in the prediction of major adverse cardiovascular events and thrombosis,36 37 and PlGF has been validated as a predictor for cardiovascular events in patients with CKD.38 Nevertheless, the application of these biomarkers for predicting incident carotid plaque and ASCVD in asymptomatic populations remains limited.

The biomarkers of atherosclerosis at early stages identified in this study have significant applications. First, due to the similarities in the pathophysiological processes between plaque and ASCVD,39 40 the biomarkers associated with carotid plaque can also be used to predict ASCVD. Integrating these biomarkers into traditional ASCVD prediction models provided modest incremental predictive value. Our study further suggests that the performance of these biomarkers may vary across different demographic groups and timelines of incidence. Specifically, biomarkers exhibited a relatively greater incremental predictive value in older individuals, males, hypertensive populations, obese individuals and those with symptom incidence within 3 years, compared with their counterparts. The addition of these biomarkers to established risk models provided modest improvements in risk stratification beyond traditional risk factors, which is consistent with the incremental gains commonly observed when incorporating novel biomarkers into existing prediction models.41 Furthermore, because the biomarker measurements were obtained prior to the development of carotid plaque or ASCVD events, these findings may provide insights into biological changes associated with the early stages of atherosclerosis. However, given the modest magnitude of improvement and the differences between the derivation and validation cohorts, the potential role of these biomarkers in population screening and clinical risk assessment requires further prospective validation. At present, these biomarkers should be considered as potential complementary tools rather than established screening markers for ASCVD.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, a high proportion of female participants (~84%) was observed in the derivation subset, which may limit generalisability to men. This may partly reflect the application of exclusion criteria and selection processes, which could have resulted in a relatively higher proportion of healthier female participants in the final analytical sample. However, sex-stratified analyses in the UK Biobank showed consistent incremental predictive value of the biomarker panel in both sexes (online supplemental sTable 7), supporting its cross-sex applicability. Nevertheless, further validation in cohorts with a higher proportion of male participants is warranted. Second, despite the relatively modest sample size, the study was conducted using a nested case–control design with an adequate events-per-variable ratio (>10), supporting the stability of the multivariable model. In addition, internal validation based on bootstrap resampling demonstrated consistent discrimination and good calibration, with no evidence of substantial overfitting. Nevertheless, the limited sample size may still constrain statistical power and the robustness of effect estimates. Therefore, further validation in larger and more diverse populations is warranted. Third, the analysis concentrated on 28 protein biomarkers identified through previous research and experience, which may have resulted in the exclusion of other pertinent biomarkers. Furthermore, in the present study, analyses were restricted to observed proteomic data without imputation. As a result, after excluding missing values, the number of cases and controls was no longer strictly balanced. Missingness was mainly attributable to technical factors, such as detection limits, and is therefore unlikely to be strongly outcome-dependent. Nevertheless, this may introduce some degree of selection bias. Fourth, there were inconsistencies between the variables in the ChinaHEART cohort and the UK Biobank, including differences in population characteristics and the biomarkers evaluated, as well as variations in the methodologies used for biomarker data processing. These discrepancies hindered our ability to validate the predictive performance of Tie-2 within the UK Biobank population. The UK Biobank proteomic data may be subject to selection, and the cohort is not fully population-representative. Nevertheless, consistent performance in this independent cohort supports the robustness of our findings. Meanwhile, our data are derived from a prospective cohort, but some variables, including lifestyle factors and medical history, were based on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall bias or misclassification. Although data collection followed standardised procedures, such bias cannot be completely excluded and may have affected the accuracy of some covariates. Lastly, while we assessed the predictive impact of these biomarkers on carotid plaque and ASCVD using population data, further fundamental research is necessary to investigate the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as evidence of modest incremental association and transportability rather than definitive evidence of clinical utility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we explored new protein biomarkers of inflammation, endothelial damage and other related effects that may provide predictive value for carotid plaque formation. Meanwhile, the biomarkers showed modest incremental value for predicting ASCVD risk in an independent cohort. Given the differences in study design, outcomes, and biomarker availability, the current findings support incremental association rather than established clinical utility. And these findings should be interpreted as evidence of transportability and incremental value rather than strict validation. Further studies are warranted to assess their clinical utility and potential role in risk stratification.
