Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among adolescents and youth in the aftermath of the Tigray war, 2024: prevalence and associated factors
Authors: Tesfay Gebreslassie Gebrehiwot, Guesh Teklu Woldemariam, Haileslassie Tesfay Tadese, Haftom Tesfay Gebremedhin, Mulu Ftwi Beraki, Abadi Kidanemariam Berhe, Alem Gebremariam, Yemane Gebremariam Gebre, Embay Amare Alemseged, Yemane Berhane Tesfau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide is a leading cause of death among adolescents and youth globally and represents an important public health concern in low- and middle-income and conflict-affected settings. Prior evidence from Ethiopia showed substantial levels of suicidal behavior among young populations even before the recent conflict. Exposure to war-related trauma, violence, displacement, and disruption of health and social systems may further increase the risk of suicidality among adolescents and youth. However, there is limited evidence regarding suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among adolescents and youth in post-war Tigray, Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among adolescents and youth in the aftermath of the Tigray War.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 599 adolescents and youth aged 15–24 years in post-war Tigray, Ethiopia, of whom 57.4% were female. Data were collected using validated tools assessing suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and exposure to violence. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts.ResultsThe prevalence of suicidal ideation was 19.9% (95% CI: 16.7%, 23.3%), while the prevalence of suicide attempts was 9.7% (95% CI: 7.4%, 12.3%).Depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder were significantly associated with both suicidal ideation and attempts. Sexual abuse and witnessing physical violence increased the odds of suicidal Attempts. However, physical activity was protective against suicidal ideation.ConclusionThe findings suggest that suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts represent important mental health concerns among adolescents and youth in post-war Tigray. Depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, and trauma-related experiences were significantly associated with suicidality in this study. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Nevertheless, the findings underscore the need for strengthened adolescent mental health services and trauma-informed support programs in conflict-affected settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

According to the World Health Organization,Suicide is the second most common cause of death for adolescents and the third most common cause for people aged 15 to 29, making it a critical public health issue worldwide [1]. Suicide causes more fatalities per year than homicide and war combined, making up 1.4% of the world’s disease burden [2]. Suicidal behavior is particularly prevalent and rising among young people, with over 800,000 of them dying by suicide annually, despite the fact that suicide rates climb with age [1, 2]. The problem of mental illness, including suicide, falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. In these settings, a treatment gap exceeds 90%, driven by a critical shortage of resources, infrastructure, and trained professionals [4]. This gap is widened by intense stigma, low mental health literacy, and the competing priority of infectious diseases, leaving young individuals with few avenues for care [5].

A Global School-Based Health Survey (GSHS) across 46 LMICs reported prevalence of 14.5% suicidal ideation and 12.7% for attempts [2]. The problem is even more acute in Africa; a recent meta-analysis focusing on university students reported suicidal ideation at 21.8% and suicide attempts at a startling 17.6% [6] and studies in sub-Saharan Africa report ideation rates ranging from 11.2% to 31.9% [7].

Within Ethiopia, even before the recent conflict, studies indicated a significant public health issue. A national systematic review reported 19.96% [8], pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation among youth. the prevalence of lifetime suicide ideation and attempt among Adolescent High School Students in Dangila Town, Northwest Ethiopia, was 22.5% and 16.2%, respectively, with school absenteeism, poor social support, and being physically hurt identified as associated factors [9]. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of suicide ideation and suicide attempt among high school adolescents in Ethiopia was 16 and 10%, respectively, with factors such as gender, disappointment with school results, family history, alcohol use, social support, history of abuse, anxiety, and depression being significant correlates [10]. Another study among high school students in Woldia town found prevalence of 16.29% for ideation and 12.87% for attempt, linked to poor social support, PTSD, and family history of suicide [11].

This is particularly clear in post-conflict settings, where the risk of suicide is greatly increased by exposure to combat trauma. Research from South Africa and Afghanistan shows that 50–70% of people affected by violence go through at least one traumatic incident [12–14]. This pattern is confirmed by data from Ethiopia’s other war-affected areas.

A study in Dessie Town found a 15.3% prevalence of suicidal behavior, associated with being divorced/widowed, unemployed, depressed, suffering from PTSD, and having inadequate social support [15]. Among war-affected internally displaced people in northwest Ethiopia, prevalence was 22.4% for ideation and 6.7% for attempts, significantly associated with female gender, depression, posttraumatic stress symptoms, and a lack of social support [16]. Similarly, a survey of war survivor women in Northern Ethiopia showed that 4.3% reported suicidal ideation and 1.9% had attempted suicide, with significant associations with being a housewife, married, a farmer, educated, and having experienced violence [17].

The recent two-year war in Tigray (2020–2022) inflicted a devastating trauma upon the population. This was characterized by extreme violence, mass atrocities, systematic sexual violence, and widespread displacement. the conflict was compounded by a government blockade that restricted access to food, medicine, and essential services, precipitating a man-made famine [18]. This context exceeds a humanitarian emergency, representing a shared and insightful psychological injury with particularly devastating consequences for the mental health of the region’s youth [19].

The War in Tigray systematically dismantled the very structures that typically foster resilience. The near-total collapse of the health system, with over 80% of facilities looted or destroyed [20], means there are virtually no services for those in acute psychological distress. The educational system was decimated, shattering academic futures and fostering hopelessness [21]. Social and community networks were fractured by mass displacement, distrust, and pervasive trauma, destroying crucial buffers against suicidality [22]. The resulting economic devastation has increased poverty and feelings of perceived burdensomeness which is a key theoretical component of suicidal desire [23].

Failure to address this crisis could cause severe intergenerational repercussions, including the possibility of losing a generation that could help restore Tigray and the continuation of violent and drug-related cycles. [24, 25]. Therefore, specific, useful evidence is desperately needed. There is lack of published, quantitative studies on adolescents and youth suicidality in the particular setting of post-war Tigray, despite a wealth of international literature on conflict and mental health as well as new data from other conflict affected regions of Ethiopia. This research is designed to fill this ultimate gap by systematically investigating the prevalence and associated factors of suicidal ideation and attempts. The results are necessary for developing focused, evidence-based preventative initiatives and promoting the inclusion of mental health in the humanitarian response [26].

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts and identify associated factors among adolescents and youth in the aftermath of the Tigray War, Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, setting, and period

A school-based cross-sectional study design was used, and data were collected from December 15, 2024, to January 16, 2025, in Adigrat Town. The town is one of the zonal capitals of the Tigray Region, located approximately 120 km north of the regional capital, Mekelle, Ethiopia. The education system in the town engages thousands of students in public and private schools. As of 2013, Adigrat Town had a total of 13 public and 7 private schools across different educational levels [27]. Of these, at the time of the study, four public and one private schools were secondary schools and constituted the sampling frame for the present study. According to the Adigrat Town Education Bureau, a grand total of 10,714 students (4,671 male and 6,043 female) were enrolled in secondary school (grades 9–12) in 2024/2025. Public schools accounted for 8,914 of these students.

Study population

All secondary school students in both public and private schools (N = 10,714) enrolled in the year 2024/2025 in Adigrat Town were the study population. This study included students who had been living in the region before the Pretoria Agreement (a permanent ceasefire) and were attending classes during data collection. Students who had acute medical illness or severe cognitive or psychological conditions at the time of data collection that prevented them from understanding the study procedures or completing the questionnaire were excluded. Eligibility was assessed through an initial screening by trained data collectors, with consultation from school personnel when necessary.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size required for this study was 608, determined by using single population proportion formula that assumes a 15.3% prevalence [15] and sets a 3% margin of error and 10% nonresponse rate. We used a simple random sampling technique to select participants from all five secondary schools in the town (four public and one private school) based on their student identification numbers. We combined student lists from each school and selected participants using computer-generated random numbers created in Microsoft Excel. Finally, the randomly selected participants were guided to designated private classroom areas within the school compound during school hours. The questionnaires were self-administered in small groups after receiving standardized orientation and instructions from trained data collectors and supervisors. Participants completed the questionnaires independently while maintaining sufficient seating distance to ensure privacy and prevent viewing of each other’s responses. Questionnaire completion required approximately 25–30 minutes on average. No financial or material compensation was provided to participants, schools, or parents/guardians.

Data collection technique and instruments

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from standardized and validated instruments previously used in adolescent and youth mental health research. The questionnaire included socio-demographic characteristics, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, substance use, and exposure to violence.

The questionnaire incorporated several validated instruments to measure key variables. Socio demographic characteristics were captured using a standard section. The primary outcomes, suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, were assessed using questions adapted from the World Mental Health Composite International Diagnostic Interview (WMH-CIDI) [28]. Mental health was evaluated using the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale for anxiety [29, 30], the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for depression [31], and a tool adapted from the PCL-5 Life Events Checklist to gauge trauma exposure consistent with DSM-5 Criterion A [32].

Behavioral and psychosocial factors were measured using the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) for substance use [33, 34], the Oslo-3 Social Support Scale (OSS-3) for perceived social support [35], a single-item measure from the WHO/CDC Global School-based Health Survey (GSHS) for bullying experiences [36], and four questions from the ISPCAN Child Abuse Screening Tool Children’s Version for a history of sexual abuse [37]. The internal consistency reliability of the study instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. The reliability coefficients for the scales used in the present study were, The PHQ-9 demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84, the GAD-7 showed 0.89, the PTSD screening tool had 0.86, and the Oslo Social Support Scale showed 0.78.

Operational definition

Suicidal ideation: If the respondent answers the question “Have you seriously thought about taking your own life in the last 12 months?” with the answer “yes,” then it is considered that the respondent had suicidal ideation.

Suicide attempt: If the respondent answers the question “Have you attempted to take your own life in the last 12 months?” with the answer “yes,” then it is considered that the respondent had attempted to take her/his own life.

Current substance use: Substance use was assessed using items adapted from the ASSIST questionnaire. Participants were asked separate questions regarding lifetime and current use, including: ‘Have you ever used [substance] in your lifetime?’ and ‘Have you used [substance] during the past three months?’ for substances such as alcohol, khat, tobacco, and other substances. Current substance use was defined as self-reported use of at least one substance during the previous three months. Classification was based on use within the recall period and did not incorporate duration, quantity, or frequency of use.

Sexual abuse: Sexual abuse was assessed using four items adapted from the ISPCAN Child Abuse Screening Tool–Children’s Version (ICAST-C), including behavior-specific yes/no questions related to experiences of sexual abuse. Participants who reported experiencing any form of sexual abuse on at least one item were classified as having a history of sexual abuse.

Physical abuse: Physical abuse was assessed using behavior-specific yes/no items related to experiences of intentional physical harm. Participants reporting at least one episode of physical abuse were classified as having experienced physical abuse.

Social support: Using the Oslo Social Support Scale, those students who scored < 3–8 on the scale were considered as having low social support, 9–11 were considered as having medium social support, and 12–14 were considered as having strong social support [38].

Anxiety symptoms: Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale. Participants scoring ≥ 10 were classified as having anxiety symptoms [39].

Depression symptoms: were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Participants scoring ≥ 10 were classified as having depressive symptoms [39].

Stress: Students who scored on DASS-21 “≥15” were declared to having stress [39].

Meeting recommended physical activity: Physical activity was assessed using the question: ‘During the past 7 days, on how many days were you physically active for a total of at least 60 minutes per day?’ Participants who reported engaging in at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day on five or more days during the previous week were classified as meeting recommended physical activity levels; otherwise, they were classified as not meeting the recommendation.

Witnessing physical violence: Participants were asked whether they had witnessed acts of physical violence. For analysis purposes, participants who reported witnessing physical violence more than two times were categorized as having experienced repeated exposure to witnessed physical violence, whereas those reporting two or fewer episodes or no exposure were categorized as not having repeated exposure.

Data quality management

Data were collected using a questionnaire in the Tigrigna language. To ensure linguistic and conceptual validity, the original English instrument underwent a rigorous translation process: it was first professionally translated into Tigrigna by bilingual experts and then back-translated into English by an independent translator to verify consistency with the original. The questionnaire’s clarity and appropriateness were further refined through a pretest conducted two weeks prior to the main study among 43 students who were not selected for participation in the main study sample. To maintain high data quality, all supervisors and data collectors completed a comprehensive two-day training program covering the study objectives, participant recruitment procedures, informed consent and assent processes, ethical considerations and confidentiality, standardized questionnaire administration procedures, management of sensitive mental health questions, and data quality assurance methods. During the fieldwork, continuous on-site supervision was implemented, with supervisors performing daily checks of all completed questionnaires for accuracy and completeness.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were meticulously checked, coded, and entered into Epi-Data software (Version 3.1) before being exported to SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Version 25) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were computed to characterize the study population’s socio-demographic profile and other key variables. To identify factors associated with the outcome, bivariate logistic regression was performed for each independent variable. All variables that showed a significant association at p ≤ 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding effects. Statistical significance in the final model was declared at a p-value of less than 0.05, and the results are presented as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). The model’s adequacy was confirmed, with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicating a good fit (p = 0.273), and multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed that all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were below the threshold of 5. The findings are summarized in Text, tables and supplementary figures.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted after Ethical approval was secured from the Adigrat University College of Medicine and Health Sciences Health Research Ethics Review Committee (Reference Number: ADU.CMHS/HRERC/0009/2024) and permission letters were secured from the relevant education offices. Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and assent was obtained from those under 18 years of age in accordance with ethical guidelines. All participants were assured that their involvement was voluntary, would not impact their academic standing, and that the confidentiality of their data would be strictly maintained. A trained and qualified facilitator was available on-site for students to consult with any study-related questions. During the data collection process, privacy was ensured through appropriate seating arrangements and supervision procedures to maintain confidentiality and minimize peer influence during questionnaire completion.

Result

A total of 608 participants aged 15 years and older were sampled for the study. Of these, 599 completed the self-administered questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 98.2%. The participants’ ages ranged from 15 to 24 years, with a mean age of 17.61 (±1.63) years. The study included participants from all five secondary schools in Adigrat Town, comprising four public and one private school. The majority of respondents attended public schools (506, 84.5%). Regarding ethnicity, 574 (98.8%) were Tigrian, and 545 (91.0%) were followers of the Orthodox faith. Approximately two-thirds of the participants (385, 64.3%) reported living with both parents. In terms of grade distribution, more than one-third (233, 38.6%) were enrolled in grade nine, and 179 (29.9%) stated they had more than three close friends. Prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts

The prevalence of suicidal ideation among secondary school students was 19.9% (95% CI: 16.7%, 23.3%), while the prevalence of suicide attempts was 9.7% (95% CI: 7.4%, 12.3%). With respect to lifetime prevalence, 23.9% of students reported having ever experienced suicidal ideation, and 12.2% reported at least one suicide attempt in their lifetime. Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts were reported across sex categories. Among those reporting suicidal ideation, 50 (42%) were male and 69 (58%) were female. Regarding suicide attempts, 28 (48.3%) were male and 30 (51.7%) were female.

Factors associated with suicidal ideation

Variables entered into the multivariable logistic regression model for suicidal ideation included age, current khat use, alcohol use, tobacco use, PTSD symptoms, social support, physical activity, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, sexual abuse, experiencing physical violence, witnessing physical violence, and history of combat exposure. Among these variables, several factors were found to be significantly associated with suicidal ideation at a p-value < 0.05. A history of sexual violence was the strongest predictor, with individuals who had encountered sexual violence being over five times more likely to report suicidal ideation compared to those who had not (AOR = 5.55; 95% CI: 2.80–9.10). The odds of suicidal ideation were also significantly higher among participants with depressive symptoms (AOR = 3.91; 95% CI: 2.27–6.74) and those with anxiety symptoms (AOR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.01–3.11). Discussion

In this study of secondary school adolescents and youth aged 15–24 years in post-war Tigray, the 12-month prevalence of suicidal ideation was 19.9% and suicide attempts was 9.7%.These figures represent a substantial mental health burden and are interpreted below in the context of pre-war data from Ethiopia, regional estimates from Africa, and global findings from other low- and middle-income countries. Prior to the Tigray war (2020–2022), evidence already indicated a significant burden of suicidal behavior among Ethiopian youth. A national systematic review reported a pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation of 19.96% among young populations of high school and university students aged 14–25 years, although most included studies were conducted before the current conflict [8].

The magnitude of suicidal ideation (19.9%) observed in our study is higher than pooled estimates from low- and middle-income countries. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 46 LMICs reported a 12-month prevalence of suicidal ideation of 14.5% among school-going adolescents aged 12–17 years [40]. However, our finding of 19.9% exceeds this global pooled estimate. Likewise, our prevalence is higher than rates reported in individual country studies involving adolescents aged 13–17 years in Nepal (13.9%) [41], school-going adolescents in Tunisia (9.6%) [42], high school students aged 14–18 years in Brazil (7.9%) [43], school-attending adolescents in Tanzania (7%) [44], school adolescents in Nigeria (6.1%) [45], adolescents aged 13–17 years in India (5%) [46] and studies among school-going adolescents in Thailand (8.8%) [47].

A systematic review of suicidal behavior among adolescents aged 11–18 years in sub-Saharan Africa found ideation rates ranging from 11.2% to 31.9%, with our estimate falling in the upper range of this spectrum [7].

Differences may be related to variations in assessment tools, recall periods, socio-cultural contexts, and the unique psychological burden of post-war trauma in Tigray. For instance, the Tunisian study used the SBQ-R tool whereas we used a CIDI-based measure [42]. Studies from Tanzania and Nigeria used single-item questions assessing ideation in shorter timeframes, such as one month, contributing to lower estimates [44, 45]. Countries like India and Thailand also possess stronger mental-health systems, which may facilitate earlier detection and treatment of depression and anxiety—key drivers of suicidality [4].

Conversely, the prevalence we found is lower than several estimates from Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries. A systematic review and meta-analysis of Ethiopian high school adolescents reported a pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation of 16% [10] which is slightly lower than our finding of 19.9%, though our estimate falls within the confidence interval of this national pooled estimate. Individual Ethiopian studies have reported higher rates—Fiche (20.5%) [48], pre-war study (conducted before 2020) in Dangila Town among students aged 14–19 years (22.3%) [9], while one study conducted during the active conflict period in 2022 in Woldia among high school students with a mean age 16.8 years reported a lower rate (16.29%) [11]. When compared with other LMICs, our prevalence is lower than pooled estimates from Latin America; a systematic review of suicidal behavior among Peruvian school-going urban adolescents aged 12–18 years found ideation prevalence of 26.3% [49, 50] and a multi-country analysis of school-based surveys reported rates of 23.2%) in school-aged youth with mean age 15.9 years in Benin [27], study among school-going adolescents with a mean age 15.3 years in Uganda 21.6% [51], study among adolescents aged 12–18 years in Ghanaian 25.1% [52] and 33.6% in Colombian adolescents and young adults aged 14–24 years [53]. A meta-analysis of suicidal ideation among university students across LMICs reported a pooled prevalence of 21.8% [6] which is comparable to our finding of 19.9% despite differences in population age range and educational level. Higher estimates in these settings may relate to higher female participation (meta-analyses consistently show female adolescents have 1.5–2 times higher risk of suicidal ideation than males [54]).

The 9.7% prevalence of suicide attempts in our study is comparable to or lower than pooled estimates from several regions. A global meta-analysis of suicidal behavior among children and adolescents reported a pooled 12-month prevalence of suicide attempts of 12.7% (95% CI: 9.8%–15.6%) across 46 LMICs [40], which is slightly higher than our finding of 9.7%. Our estimate is comparable to individual country studies from Tunisia (7.3%) [42], Nepal (10.3%) [41], Bhutan (11.3%) [55], and South Africa (14.8%) [56].

A systematic review of suicide attempts among Ethiopian high school adolescents reported a pooled prevalence of 10% (95% CI: 7–13%) [10], which closely aligns with our finding of 9.7%. However, our prevalence is higher than Bangladesh (5.9%) [57], China (3.3%) [58], as reported in a large pooled analysis of 90 countries [57]. On the other hand, our suicide attempt prevalence is lower than findings from several African and Latin American settings, including a pre-war study (conducted before 2020) in Dangila (16.2%) [9], in Fitche (12.5%) [48], Benin (28.3%) [59], Liberia (33.7%) [60], Guatemala (16.6%), and Mongolia (32.2%) [61]. This might likely due to both a longer recall period and conflict-related stressors.

Our post-war estimates—19.9% for ideation and 9.7% for attempts—suggest several observations: suicidal ideation in post-war Tigray remains comparable to from other Ethiopian regions, this may reflect underreporting due to heightened stigma in the post-conflict setting or survivorship bias. In addition prevalence of suicide attempts in our study is lower than pre-war estimates from Dangila and Woldia. This pattern may suggest that war-related trauma in the immediate aftermath manifests more strongly as ideation rather than attempts, with attempts potentially increasing over time as chronic stressors (poverty, food insecurity, loss of social support) accumulate.

Factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts

Depression was a major predictor of both suicidal ideation and attempts in our findings. This is consistent with robust evidence from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses. A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies examining suicide risk factors among adolescents found that depression was one of the strongest predictors of subsequent suicidal ideation and attempts, with pooled odds ratios ranging from 3.5 to 6.2 across 15 cohort studies [62]. A separate systematic review focusing on low- and middle-income countries confirmed that depressive symptoms consistently elevated suicide risk across diverse cultural contexts, with particularly strong effects in conflict-affected settings [63]. Individual studies from Vietnam among adolescents aged 12–18 years [64], from England among adolescents aged 16–17 years [65], the United States among adolescents aged 13–18 years [66], from South Korea among adolescents aged 12–17 years [67], from Zambia among school adolescents [68], from Tunisia among school-going adolescents [69], from Nigeria among adolescents [70] and previous Ethiopian youth studies [9, 48, 71] have reported similar associations. Depression increases hopelessness, worthlessness, impaired coping, and cognitive distortions—all well-established psychological mechanisms underlying suicidal behavior [23, 72–74]. Neurobiological studies also indicate reduced serotonin function among depressed individuals as a key biological driver of suicide attempts [74].

Anxiety strongly predicted both suicidal ideation and attempts. Multiple studies—including from Taiwan [75], Vietnam [76], Mozambique [77], Malaysia [78], and cross-country evaluations [79]—have reported similar associations. Anxiety contributes to persistent worry, intrusive thoughts, panic, emotional dysregulation, and comorbidity with depression—all of which heighten suicide risk [80, 81].

PTSD was significantly associated with suicidal ideation, which is consistent with studies from Ukraine among adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 years affected by war [82], Palestine among school-aged children and adolescents [83], Sierra Leone among war-affected youth aged 10–18 years [84], Rwanda among adolescents affected by HIV/AIDS, aged 10–17 years [85] and Ethiopian youth populations (high school students and internally displaced youth) [9, 15, 16, 48]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of PTSD and suicide risk among adolescents and young adults found that individuals with PTSD had approximately three times higher odds of suicidal ideation compared to those without PTSD (pooled OR = 2.98; 95% CI: 2.31–3.84) [86]. PTSD increases emotional dysregulation, intrusive re-experiencing, hyperarousal, and numbing—factors that reduce coping capacity and increase suicidal vulnerability [87, 88]Additionally, PTSD is often comorbid with depression, anxiety, or substance abuse, which further amplifies risk [89]. The unique context of the Tigray war likely intensified these effects, resulting in elevated PTSD-linked suicidality.

A history of sexual abuse significantly increased suicidal ideation. This finding is strongly supported by systematic reviews. A meta-analysis of 42 studies examining childhood sexual abuse and adolescent suicidality found that individuals with a history of sexual abuse had nearly four times higher odds of suicidal ideation and three times higher odds of suicide attempts compared to those without such histories [90]. The effect size in our study exceeds these pooled estimates, which may reflect the combined impact of war-related sexual violence in the Tigray context. Similar findings have been reported in individual studies from Ethiopia high school adolescents aged 14–19 years) [9], Ghana among adolescents aged 12–18 years [52], Benin among school-aged youth aged 11–19 years [27] and Brazil among public high school students aged 14–18 years [43]. Sexual abuse causes chronic shame, trauma, internalized,4distress, and long-term psychological impairment, making it one of the strongest predictors of adolescent suicidality [91].

Witnessing physical violence predicted suicide attempts, consistent a systematic review of violence exposure and suicidal behavior among youth, which reported a pooled odds ratio of 2.34 (95% CI: 1.87–2.93) for suicide attempts among adolescents exposed to community or interpersonal violence [92]. Our finding is also in line with studies from South Africa adolescents aged 13–19 years [77], Uganda school-going adolescents aged 13–19 years [93] and multiple conflict-affected regions. Exposure to interpersonal violence disrupts emotional security and normalizes self-harm, creating pathways toward suicidal behavior [94]. Post-war Tigray adolescents have faced high levels of community violence, contributing to this link.

Physical activity was protective against suicidal ideation (AOR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.35–0.94). This finding is supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 studies examining the association between physical activity and suicidal ideation, which found that physically active individuals had significantly lower odds of suicidal ideation compared to inactive individuals (pooled OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.60–0.79) [95]. The protective effect was stronger among adolescents than adults, and the association remained significant after adjustment for depression and other confounders [95]. A separate systematic review focusing on low- and middle-income countries confirmed that regular physical activity reduces depressive and anxiety symptoms through neurobiological pathways including reduced cortisol, increased endorphin release, and enhanced emotional regulation [96]. Individual studies from China among high school students aged 15–19 years) [97], in Finland among adolescents [95]and among adolescents of South Korea [71] have reported similar protective associations. In conflict-affected communities like post-war Tigray, where physical activity levels are often low (54.4% of our sample did not meet recommended activity levels), promoting structured physical activity may represent a feasible, low-cost intervention to mitigate suicide risk.

Limitations of the study

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, because of the cross-sectional study design, temporal relationships and causality between associated factors and suicidal behaviors cannot be established. Second, the use of self-reported measures for sensitive experiences such as suicidal behavior, abuse, and mental health symptoms may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to underreporting or misclassification. Third, the study was school-based and excluded adolescents not attending school; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all adolescents and youth in the community, particularly displaced or out-of-school populations who may experience different levels of psychological distress. Additionally, data collection occurred in a post-conflict setting where psychological symptoms may have been temporarily elevated because of recent war-related experiences. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out.

Although previously validated instruments were used, the integrated questionnaire was not subjected to comprehensive psychometric validation in this specific post-conflict adolescent population.

Conclusions and recommendation

The prevalence of suicidal ideation (19.9%) and suicide attempts (9.7%) among adolescents and youth in post-war Tigray was substantial and appears higher than estimates reported in some pre-war Ethiopian studies, while remaining within the range reported among adolescents and youth in conflict and post-conflict settings globally and regionally. These findings may reflect the cumulative impact of war-related trauma, educational disruption, displacement, and psychosocial stressors experienced in the aftermath of the Tigray conflict.

Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts represent important mental health concerns among adolescents and youth in post-war Tigray. Depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, and trauma-related experiences were significantly associated with suicidality in this study. Trauma-related experiences such as sexual abuse and witnessing physical violence further increased suicidal behavior, whereas physical activity showed a protective effect. These results highlight the urgent need for comprehensive, trauma-informed, adolescent-focused mental health services in post-conflict areas.

Based on the findings of this study, a multi-sectoral approach is critical to address the high burden of suicidality among youth in post-war Tigray. For the health sector, we recommend the integration of routine adolescent mental health screening for depression, anxiety, and PTSD into school-based and primary healthcare services. This should be coupled with the establishment of trauma-informed counseling programs specifically for adolescents with histories of sexual abuse or violence exposure. Furthermore, training for teachers, school health workers, and community health professionals in the early detection of suicidal behavior and the strengthening of referral pathways to specialized psychiatric care are essential. Within the education sector, it is imperative to implement school-based mental health programs focused on building psychological resilience, encourage regular physical activity through structured and extracurricular programs, and introduce confidential reporting systems for violence and abuse. For community and policy stakeholders, efforts should focus on developing widespread awareness campaigns to stigmatize mental health conditions and promote help-seeking, promoting robust child-protection systems to combat abuse, and ensuring that mental health rehabilitation is a cornerstone of post-conflict reconstruction plans.

Future studies are recommended to further assess construct validity using advanced psychometric technique.
