Uncertainty relations in classical and quantum theories of electromagnetism Journal: New Journal of Physics
Zofia Bialynicka-Birula

Abstract
Sharp uncertainty relations restricting the values of variances in the position space and in the momentum (wavevector) space are derived. They have the same form Δ ​ r ​ Δ ​ k ≥ 5 / 2 \Delta r\Delta k\geq 5/2 in the classical theory of light beams, in the quantum theory of coherent light beams, and in the quantum theory of individual photons.



Keywords
: uncertainty relations, photons, helicity

1
Introduction

In recent advanced experiments in nanophotonics
[
1
,
2
]
the limitations imposed by the uncertainty relations play a significant role. In the present work we clarify the role of the uncertainty relations in these experiments. We prove the uncertainty relation in three distinct cases: in the classical Maxwell theory, in the quantum theory of coherent light beams and in the quantum theory of single photons. Quite unexpectedly, the uncertainty relations are universal. In all three cases they have not only exactly the same form:

\displaystyle\Delta r\Delta k\geq 5/2,

(1)

where
\Delta r
and
\Delta k
are square roots of the variances
\Delta r^{2}
and
\Delta k^{2}
but the functions that saturate the inequality (
1
) are identical. We use the reciprocal space of wavevectors rather than momentum space in order to eliminate the Planck constant from our uncertainty relations. This convention emphasizes the classical–quantum universality of our results. We believe that these findings add a new perspective to the ongoing debate on the quantumness of the Heisenberg uncertainty relations
[
3
,
4
]
.

In earlier work
[
5
]
, we derived the uncertainty relations for photons based on the center of energy operators
\hat{\bm{R}}
. That derivation yielded the lower bound in the uncertainty relation equal to
1+\sqrt{5}/2
. However, the non-commutativity of these operators obscures the physical interpretation. In particular, one may question whether the expectation value

\langle\hat{\bm{R}}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{R}}\rangle
is an appropriate measure of spatial spread. Instead, in the present work we use the energy density to characterize the spatial extent of the field.

The classical-quantum relationship in the uncertainty relations has been analyzed in detail in review article
[
6
]
. In our work we significantly extend this analysis by calculating explicitly not only the lower bound but also all functions which play a role in the uncertainty relations.

The main theoretical tool in our derivations is the Riemann-Silberstein (RS) vector
{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)=\frac{{\bm{D}}({\bm{r}},t)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}}+\rmi\frac{{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}},t)}{\sqrt{2\mu_{0}}}.

(2)

The description in terms of the RS vector is universal and applies to all three cases considered here, although its physical interpretation differs in each case.

2
Uncertainty relation for classical electromagnetic waves

Maxwell equations expressed in terms of the RS vector read,

\displaystyle\rmi\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)=c{\bm{\nabla}}\times{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t).

(3)

The general solution of this equation is given by the Fourier integral

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})

(4)

\displaystyle\times\left[f_{+}({\bm{k}})\rme^{\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\rmi ckt}+f_{-}^{*}({\bm{k}})\rme^{-\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}+\rmi ckt}\right],

where
k=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}}
, the indices
\pm
label waves with positive/negative helicity (left-handed and right-handed circular polarization) and

{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})
is the normalized polarization vector,

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k^{2}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-k_{x}k_{z}+\rmi k_{y}k\\
-k_{y}k_{z}-\rmi k_{x}k\\
k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}\end{array}\right]={\bm{e}}^{*}(-\bm{k}).

We will also need the RS vector in the Fourier representation,

\displaystyle{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k},t)=\left[{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})f_{+}({\bm{k}})\rme^{-\rmi ckt}\!+\!{\bm{e}}^{*}(\bm{k})f_{-}^{*}(-{\bm{k}})\rme^{\rmi ckt}\right].

(9)

The electromagnetic field does not possess a conserved four-vector current. The time-component
\rho({\bm{r}},t)
of such a vector could be used to characterize the distribution in space. This was the basis of the uncertainty relations for relativistic electrons
[
12
]
. A natural substitute for
\rho({\bm{r}},t)
is the field energy density
{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r},t)\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)
whose variance
\Delta r^{2}
we use to measure the spatial spread of the field. We are interested in the minimal possible spatial extent, so we set
t=0
. At other times the extent of the wave packet is larger because the wave packets spread quadratically with time,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\frac{\int\!d^{3}r\,{\bm{r}}^{2}{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})}{\int\!d^{3}r{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})}=2c^{2}.

(10)

The variance in position space is therefore defined as,

\displaystyle\Delta r^{2}=\frac{\int\!d^{3}r\,{\bm{r}}^{2}{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})}{\int\!d^{3}r{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})},

(11)

where all field vectors are taken at
t=0
. By analogy, we define the variance in wavevector space,

\displaystyle\Delta k^{2}=\frac{\int\!d^{3}k\,{\bm{k}}^{2}{\bm{\tilde{}}F}^{*}(\bm{k})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k})}{\int\!d^{3}k{\bm{\tilde{}}F}^{*}(\bm{k})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k})}.

(12)

By the Plancherel theorem, the denominators in (
11
) and (
12
) are equal,

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\int\!d^{3}r{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})=\int\!d^{3}k{\bm{\tilde{}}F}^{*}(\bm{k})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k})

(13)

\displaystyle=\int\!d^{3}k\left(|f_{+}({\bm{k}})|^{2}+|f_{-}({\bm{k}})|^{2}\right).

The equations which determine the lowest bound in the uncertainty relation are obtained by varying the product
\Delta r^{2}\Delta k^{2}
with respect to
f_{\pm}^{*}({\bm{k}})
. To obtain these equations, we express
\Delta r^{2}
in terms of the amplitudes
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
by replacing the multiplication by
\bm{r}
with the derivatives of
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
with respect to
\bm{k}
. One finds,

\displaystyle\Delta r^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\int\!d^{3}k\,f_{+}^{*}({\bm{k}})\left[\frac{1}{k_{\perp}^{2}}f_{+}({\bm{k}})+\frac{2\rmi k_{z}}{kk_{\perp}^{2}}\left(k_{x}\partial_{k_{y}}f_{+}({\bm{k}})-k_{y}\partial_{k_{x}}f_{+}({\bm{k}})\right)-\Delta_{k}f_{+}({\bm{k}}))\right]

\displaystyle+(f_{+}({\bm{k}})\to f_{-}({\bm{k}})),

(14)

where
\Delta_{k}
is the Laplacian in k-space. The expression for
\Delta k^{2}
reads,

\displaystyle\Delta k^{2}=N^{-1}\int\!d^{3}k\,k^{2}\,f_{+}^{*}({\bm{k}})f_{+}({\bm{k}})+f_{+}({\bm{k}})\to f_{-}({\bm{k}}).

(15)

Since the functions
f_{+}({\bm{k}})
and
f_{-}({\bm{k}})
enter
\Delta r^{2}
and
\Delta k^{2}
in exactly the same way, we will first consider
f_{+}({\bm{k}})
and we will drop the subscript
+
. The variational equation then becomes,

\displaystyle N\frac{\delta(\Delta r^{2}\Delta k^{2})}{\delta f^{*}({\bm{k}})}=\Delta k^{2}\left[\frac{1}{k_{\perp}^{2}}f({\bm{k}})+\frac{2\rmi k_{z}}{kk_{\perp}^{2}}\left(k_{x}\partial_{k_{y}}f({\bm{k}})-k_{y}\partial_{k_{x}}f({\bm{k}})\right)-\Delta_{k}f({\bm{k}}))\right]

\displaystyle+\Delta r^{2}k^{2}f({\bm{k}})-2\gamma^{2}f({\bm{k}})=0.

(16)

The substitution
f(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\,g(k)/k
reduces (
2
) to an eigenvalue equation
for a three-dimensional harmonic oscillator,,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\Delta k^{2}\left(-\partial_{k}^{2}-\frac{2}{\kappa}\partial_{\kappa}+\frac{2}{k^{2}}\right)+\Delta r^{2}k^{2}\right]g(k)=\gamma^{2}g(k),

(17)

We have assumed spherical symmetry, since we are interested in the lowest eigenvalue and any angular dependence increases the eigenvalue. By introducing the dimensionless variable
\kappa=k\sqrt{\Delta r/\Delta k}=k\sqrt{\gamma}/\Delta k
, we transform (
17
) to the form,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[-\partial_{\kappa}^{2}-\frac{2}{\kappa}\partial_{\kappa}+\frac{2}{\kappa^{2}}+\kappa^{2}\right]g(\kappa)=\gamma\,g(\kappa).

(18)

This equation has the spectrum of eigenvalues
\gamma_{n}=5/2+2n,\;n=0,1,\dots
with generalized Laguerre polynomials as eigenfunctions,

\displaystyle g_{n}(\kappa)=\kappa\exp(-\kappa^{2}/2)L_{n}^{3/2}(\kappa).

(19)

In the uncertainty relations we take the lowest eigenvalue
\gamma=5/2
which yields (
1
). The corresponding radial function
g(\kappa)
is,

\displaystyle g_{0}(\kappa)=\kappa\exp(-\kappa^{2}/2).

(20)

Since the variational equations for
f_{+}(\bm{k})
and
f_{-}(\bm{k})
are identical, any linear combination of
C_{+}f_{+}(\bm{k})
and
C_{-}f_{-}(\bm{k})
will also saturate the uncertainty relation. The formulas,

\displaystyle f_{+}({\bm{k}})=C_{+}\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\exp\left(-a^{2}k^{2}/2\right),\;f_{-}({\bm{k}})=C_{-}\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\exp\left(-a^{2}k^{2}/2\right),

(21)

with
a=\sqrt{\Delta r/\Delta k}
yield the following RS vector,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)=\!\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})\!\left[\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\,\rme^{-a^{2}k^{2}/2}\left(C_{+}\rme^{\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\rmi ckt}+C_{-}^{*}\rme^{-\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}+\rmi ckt}\right)\right].

(22)

In the next step we replace the components of the wave vector in
{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})
by the corresponding space derivatives and we obtain,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}(\bm{r},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x}\partial_{z}+\rmi\partial_{y}\partial_{t}/c\\
\partial_{y}\partial_{z}-\rmi\partial_{x}\partial_{t}/c\\
-\partial_{x}^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2}\end{array}\right]{\mathcal{F}}(r,t),

where,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(r,t)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{4k\pi^{3/2}}\,\rme^{-a^{2}k^{2}/2}\left[C_{+}\rme^{\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\rmi ckt}+C_{-}^{*}\rme^{-\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}+\rmi ckt}\right].

(27)

This integral can be evaluated in closed form,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\pm}(r,t)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{4k\pi^{3/2}}\,\rme^{-a^{2}k^{2}/2}\rme^{\pm\rmi(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-ckt)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dk\,\sin(kr)\left[\rme^{-a^{2}k^{2}/2}\rme^{\mp\rmi ckt}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2ar}\left[D(l_{+})+D(l_{-})\mp\rmi\sqrt{\pi}/2\left(\rme^{-l_{+}^{2}}-\rme^{-l_{-}^{2}}\right)\right],

(28)

where
D(w)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp(-w^{2}){\rm erfi}(w)/2
is the Dawson function
[
13
]
and
l_{\pm}=(r\pm ct)/(\sqrt{2}\,a)
are the light-cone variables. The complete RS vector in position space is obtained by evaluating the derivatives in (
2
). Although the resulting expressions are lengthy, they involve only the Dawson functions and the exponential functions of the light-cone variables
l_{\pm}
. The electromagnetic field which at
t=0
saturates the uncertainty relations in general has both: the Dawson part and the exponential part. The simplest field which saturates the uncertainty relation is,

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{r})=C\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}y\\
-x\\
0\end{array}\right].

Depending on the value of the constant
C
it could be an electric field when
C
is real, a magnetic field when
C
is imaginary, or a combination of both when
C
is complex. The Fourier transform of
\bm{F}(\bm{r})
is,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{F}}(\bm{k})=\rmi Ca^{5}\exp{\left(-\frac{a^{2}k^{2}}{2}\right)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k_{y}\\
-k_{x}\\
0\end{array}\right].

The expressions (
2
) and (
2
) substituted into (
11
) and (
12
) yield,

\displaystyle\frac{\int\!d^{3}r\,{\bm{r}}^{2}{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})}{\int\!d^{3}r{\bm{F}}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!{\bm{F}}(\bm{r})}=\frac{5a^{2}}{2},\quad\frac{\int\!d^{3}k\,{\bm{k}}^{2}{\bm{\tilde{}}F}^{*}(\bm{k})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k})}{\int\!d^{3}k{\bm{\tilde{}}F}^{*}(\bm{k})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\tilde{}}F}(\bm{k})}=\frac{5}{2a^{2}},

(37)

which confirms our uncertainty relation (
1
).

3
Uncertainty relation for coherent states of the quantized electromagnetic field

Quantization proceeds according to the standard rules. The c-number amplitudes
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
and
f_{\pm}^{*}({\bm{k}})
are replaced by the annihilation and creation operators. The canonical commutation relations between these operators are,

\displaystyle[a_{+}({\bm{k}}),a^{\dagger}_{+}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})]=k\delta^{(3)}({\bm{k}}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime})=[a_{-}({\bm{k}}),a^{\dagger}_{-}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})].

(38)

Actually, we should include a dimensional multiplier
\sqrt{\hbar c}
in these replacements to obtain proper
commutation relations for field operators,

\displaystyle[\hat{B}_{i}({\bm{r}},t),\hat{D}_{j}({\bm{r}}^{\prime},t)]=\rmi\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_{k}\delta^{(3)}({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}).

(39)

However, the factor
\sqrt{\hbar c}
does affect our uncertainty relations because it cancels in the definitions of variances (
11
) and (
12
).

As in the classical theory, the operator of the energy density
\mathcal{\hat{E}}({\bm{r}},t)
plays the central role in the quantized theory,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{E}}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=:\!\hat{\bm{F}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r},t)\!:.

(40)

Double colons denote normal ordering of creation and annihilation operators. We construct a general coherent state vector
|{\rm coh}\rangle
by acting with the unitary Glauber displacement operator
[
14
]
on the vacuum state vector,

\displaystyle|{\rm coh}\rangle=\rme^{a^{\dagger}_{+}+a^{\dagger}_{-}-a_{+}-a_{-}}|0\rangle,

(41)

where
a^{\dagger}_{\pm}
and
a_{\pm}
are creation and annihilation operators with arbitrary forms of the wave functions
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
,

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{\pm}=\!\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{k}f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bm{k}}),\;\,a_{\pm}=\!\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{k}f^{*}_{\pm}({\bm{k}})a_{\pm}({\bm{k}}).

(42)

The definitions of the second moments have the same form (
11
) and (
12
) but the classical RS vectors are replaced now by the expectation values of field operators evaluated in the coherent state,

\displaystyle\Delta r^{2}=\frac{\int\!d^{3}r\langle{{\rm{coh}}|{\bm{r}}^{2}:{\hat{\bm{F}^{\dagger}}}({\bm{r}})\cdot{\hat{\bm{F}}}(\bm{r}):|{\rm{coh}}\rangle}}{{\int\!d^{3}r\langle{\rm{coh}}|:{\hat{\bm{F}^{\dagger}}}(\bm{r})\cdot{\hat{\bm{F}}}(\bm{r}):|{\rm{coh}}\rangle}},

(43)

\displaystyle\Delta k^{2}=\frac{\int\!d^{3}k\langle{{\rm{coh}}|{\bm{k}}^{2}:{\hat{\bm{F}^{\dagger}}}({\bm{k}})\cdot{\hat{\bm{F}}}(\bm{k}):|{\rm{coh}}\rangle}}{{\int\!d^{3}k\langle{\rm{coh}}|:{\hat{\bm{F}^{\dagger}}}(\bm{k})\cdot{\hat{\bm{F}}}(\bm{k}):|{\rm{coh}}\rangle}}.

(44)

Since the evaluation of the expectation value of field operators in coherent states amounts to replacing all creation and annihilation operators by the corresponding wave functions
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
and
f_{\pm}^{*}({\bm{k}})
, the definitions (
43
) and (
44
) are the same as the definitions (
11
) and (
12
) in the classical theory.
Therefore, the uncertainty relations for the coherent states of the quantized electromagnetic field have the same form (
1
) as in the classical theory.

4
Uncertainty relation for individual photons

In our previous publication
[
15
]
we derived the uncertainty relation for all massless particles,

\displaystyle\Delta r\Delta k\geq 1+\sqrt{1/4+2h},

(45)

where
h
is the modulus of helicity. For photons (
h=1
), this relation coincides with (
1
). The derivation of the formula (
45
) was based on the description of the wave functions in terms of relativistic spinors. We have not succeeded there to find an explicit form of the wave functions which saturate the uncertainty relations. The description in terms of the RS vector enabled us in the present work to fill this gap.

There are two wave functions describing photons with positive helicity
\bm{F}_{+}
and negative helicity
\bm{F}_{-}
. Both can be obtained from the RS vector
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Since photons, like all elementary particles, have positive energy, only the positive-frequency part of the RS vector (
4
) represents a physical wave function. This is the wave function of photons with positive helicity,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}_{+}}(\bm{r},t)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\bm{e}}(\bm{k})f_{+}({\bm{k}})\rme^{\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\rmi ckt}.

(46)

The wave function of photons with negative helicity is obtained from the RS vector by complex conjugation of the negative frequency part,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}_{-}}(\bm{r},t)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\bm{e}^{*}}(\bm{k})f_{-}({\bm{k}})\rme^{\rmi\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\rmi ckt}.

(47)

As shown by Wigner
[
16
]
, these two wave functions describe the quantum states of two different particles. The wave functions
{\bm{F}_{+}}(\bm{r},t)
and
{\bm{F}_{-}}(\bm{r},t)
are different geometrical objects. They transform in the same way under rotations,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\prime}={\bm{F}}\cos\varphi+{\bm{n}}\times{\bm{F}}\sin\varphi+{\bm{n}}\left({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{F}}\right)(1-\cos\varphi),

(48)

where
{\bm{n}}
is the rotation axis and
\varphi
is the rotation angle. However, there is a difference in sign in their transformation properties under Lorentz transformations
[
9
]
,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\prime}={\bm{F}}\cosh\psi\mp\rmi{\bm{n}}\times{\bm{F}}\sinh\psi+{\bm{n}}\left({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{F}}\right)(1\!-\!\cosh\psi),

(49)

where
{\bm{n}}={\bm{v}}/|{\bm{v}}|
and
\psi={\rm{arctanh}}(|{\bm{v}}|/c)
.

The helicity of photons differs from the helicity of massive particles. The helicity states for massive particles mix under rotations and Lorentz transformations while for photons they transform separately. Of course, in both cases helicity contributes
\pm\hbar
to the total angular momentum, as discussed in detail in
[
17
,
18
]
.

The uncertainty relation (
1
) in the case of photons follows directly from our results for the classical electromagnetic field. The calculations are the same as those that led to the formula (
2
). However, this time the amplitudes
f_{\pm}({\bm{k}})
are the wave functions of the photon in Fourier space and not the amplitudes of the classical electromagnetic wave. The formulae,

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}_{\pm}(\bm{r},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x}\partial_{z}\pm\rmi\partial_{y}\partial_{t}/c\\
\partial_{y}\partial_{z}\mp\rmi\partial_{x}\partial_{t}/c\\
-\partial_{x}^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2}\end{array}\right]{\mathcal{F}}_{+}(r,t),

describe the photon wave functions of positive and negative helicity. The uncertainty relations are saturated at
t=0
. At this time both functions are equal and they have the following form,

\displaystyle F_{x}=\frac{1}{a^{5}r^{5}}\left[\sqrt{\pi}yr^{5}\rme^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2a^{2}}}-xz\left(\sqrt{2}ar(3a^{2}+r^{2})-2(2a^{4}+(a^{2}+r^{2})^{2})D(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}a})\right)\right],

\displaystyle F_{y}=\frac{1}{a^{5}r^{5}}\left[-\sqrt{\pi}xr^{5}\rme^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2a^{2}}}-yz\left(\sqrt{2}ar(3a^{2}+r^{2})-2(2a^{4}+(a^{2}+r^{2})^{2})D(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}a})\right)\right],

\displaystyle F_{z}=\frac{1}{a^{5}r^{5}}\Bigg[\sqrt{2}ar\left((x^{2}+y^{2})(a^{2}+r^{2})-2a^{2}z^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+2\left((a^{4}+r^{4})(x^{2}+y^{2})-a^{2}z^{2}(a^{2}+r^{2})\right)D(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}a})\Bigg].

(54)

The substitution of these functions into (
11
) results in
\Delta r^{2}=5a^{2}/2
. In turn, the substitution of the Fourier transform
{\tilde{\bm{F}}}(\bm{k},0)
into (
12
) results in
\Delta k^{2}=5/(2a^{2})
. This confirms the uncertainty relation (
1
) for the photon wave functions.

5
Conclusions

The main conclusion of this work is that the uncertainty relations in the theories of electromagnetism
characterize the wave properties, rather than quantum properties. They can be derived without
any reference
to operators in the Hilbert space of quantum states. The same form of these relations for classical electromagnetic waves and for photon wave functions is the best proof of this universality. To underscore this property, we have eliminated completely the Planck constant from uncertainty relations by replacing momentum by the wave vector. Of course, by using the connection between the photon wave vector and the photon momentum
\bm{p}=\hbar\bm{k}
, we may give our uncertainty relation the quantum-mechanical form
\Delta r\Delta p\geq 5/2\hbar
. It is true that the probabilistic nature of wave functions plays the significant role in the physical
interpretation
of the uncertainty relations in quantum theory, but in the mathematical derivation of these relations the apparatus of quantum theory is not needed.
