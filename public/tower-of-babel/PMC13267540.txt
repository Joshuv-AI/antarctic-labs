AngioVac aspiration as a combined diagnostic and therapeutic strategy for an indeterminate right atrial mass in a patient with malignancy
Authors: Ma’in Abumuhfouz, Ahmed Sena, Saeed Suleiman, Mohanned A. El Haija, David G. Sanchez, Steven Lewis, Ahmed Azzam, Talal Asif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Right atrial masses in patients with active malignancy pose a diagnostic challenge, as imaging may not reliably distinguish thrombus from tumor and surgical biopsy may carry prohibitive risk. We report the case of a woman with primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma who was incidentally found to have a right atrial mass during evaluation for bacteremia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested thrombus versus malignant involvement. Given high surgical risk, catheter-directed aspiration using the AngioVac system was performed, resulting in significant debulking and successful tissue retrieval without procedural complications. Histopathologic analysis confirmed fibrin thrombus without evidence of malignancy, allowing definitive diagnosis and guiding anticoagulation-based management. The patient’s bacteremia resolved following central line removal and targeted antibiotic therapy. This case highlights the dual diagnostic and therapeutic utility of the AngioVac system in managing right-sided intracardiac masses when noninvasive imaging is inconclusive.

Introduction

Right atrial masses represent a diagnostic challenge, particularly in patients with active malignancy, where the differential diagnosis includes intracardiac thrombus, tumor extension, primary cardiac tumor, and infective vegetation. Noninvasive imaging modalities such as TTE, TEE, and cardiac MRI can provide important structural and tissue characterization, but these modalities may not always reliably distinguish thrombus from malignant involvement. Surgical resection and biopsy remain the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. However, these procedures may carry substantial risk in patients with significant comorbidities or active malignancy. In selected high-risk patients, catheter-based aspiration systems such as the AngioVac device offer a minimally invasive alternative that may provide both diagnostic tissue and therapeutic debulking of intracardiac masses.

Case report

A 30-year-old woman with a history of primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma was admitted for planned administration of cycle 5 chemotherapy. Three days prior, she had been hospitalized for evaluation of suspected infection. Testing was positive for respiratory rhinovirus, and blood cultures were obtained from both a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) and peripheral sites. She remained clinically stable and was discharged with supportive care.

At the time of her subsequent admission for chemotherapy, blood cultures from the prior hospitalization returned positive for Tsukamurella species. Chemotherapy was deferred, and the PICC line was removed. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics were initiated. Repeat cultures from the removed PICC again isolated the same organism, while blood cultures obtained from the new central line remained negative.

On presentation, the patient was clinically stable and asymptomatic, denying chest pain, palpitations, dizziness, syncope, or other cardiac-related symptoms. She was afebrile with a temperature of 36.8°C, hemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 124/81 mm Hg, and was maintaining oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Physical examination was otherwise unremarkable.

The patient had been diagnosed with primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma 3 months prior and was undergoing treatment with dose-adjusted R-EPOCH and intrathecal methotrexate. Aside from her malignancy, she had no significant past medical history. She denied any personal history of cardiovascular disease and reported no family history of premature coronary artery disease, cardiomyopathy, or sudden cardiac death. Prior electrocardiograms were reportedly normal. A transthoracic echocardiogram performed 3 months earlier at the time of lymphoma diagnosis was unremarkable. A positron emission tomography (PET) scan obtained 1 month prior demonstrated no metabolic uptake within the right atrium, suggesting that the lesion enlarged rapidly in the interim. This temporal discordance is clinically relevant, as it modestly shifts the pretest probability between thrombus and tumor depending on whether this represents interval rapid growth versus a previously occult, metabolically inactive or subcentimeter lesion.

The discovery of a new right atrial mass in the setting of known malignancy raised concern for intracardiac thrombus versus malignant cardiac involvement. Initial laboratory evaluation showed a leukocytosis of 11 ×10³/µL and a hemoglobin level of 9 g/dL, consistent with her baseline. C-reactive protein was elevated at 26 mg/dL, while renal function, liver function tests, and lactate were within normal limits. Baseline cardiac biomarkers were obtained, with an N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide level <35 pg/mL and negative troponin. The presenting electrocardiogram revealed normal sinus rhythm.

As part of the evaluation for bacteremia, A TTE demonstrated a 2.9 × 1.2 cm echo-dense mass adherent to the lateral (free) wall of the right atrium, appearing broad-based with no independent mobility and without evidence of prolapse into the tricuspid valve or extension toward the superior vena cava (SVC). Subsequent TEE confirmed a 2.4 × 1.8 cm sessile, echogenic right atrial mass arising from the atrial free wall, again demonstrating minimal to no mobility and no involvement of the tricuspid valve apparatus or SVC inflow region. The lesion lacked typical echocardiographic features of vegetation, including independent oscillation, valve leaflet attachment, or associated valvular destruction; however, in the setting of bacteremia, infective endocarditis could not be excluded based on imaging alone (Fig. 1). Cardiac MRI, performed using cine steady-state free precession (SSFP), T1- and T2-weighted black-blood sequences, first-pass perfusion imaging, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging (obtained approximately 10-15 minutes after contrast administration), demonstrated a nonenhancing mass attached to the right atrial free wall and measuring 26 × 16 × 9 mm with isointense signal on T2-weighted imaging and mildly hyperintense signal on T1-weighted sequences, without evidence of right atrial wall invasion. These findings collectively raised concern for intracardiac thrombus versus cardiac involvement by lymphoma. Given the patient’s underlying malignancy and overall clinical context, cardiothoracic surgery determined that she was at high risk for open surgical resection. Due to concern for intracardiac thrombus, the patient was initiated on intravenous heparin infusion.

Two days later, catheter-directed aspiration using the AngioVac system was performed via bilateral femoral venous access under general anesthesia. Systemic anticoagulation was achieved with intravenous heparin to maintain an activated clotting time in the therapeutic range throughout the procedure. A suction cannula was advanced into the right atrium and connected to a veno-venous extracorporeal bypass circuit with reinfusion through a separate femoral venous return cannula. Continuous intra-procedural imaging was provided primarily by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) for real-time localization of the mass and cannula positioning, supplemented by fluoroscopic guidance for device navigation. Large-bore venous access sheaths were utilized for both inflow and outflow cannulas, and access sites were closed using percutaneous closure devices at the end of the procedure without immediate vascular complications. Fluoroscopy time was not documented (Fig. 5). Multiple tissue-like fragments were successfully aspirated (Fig. 6), resulting >50% reduction in the mass burden now measuring 11 × 7 × 4 mm. Retrieved specimens were sent for histopathologic analysis. Given the patient’s stable clinical condition, she was discharged home on anticoagulation with apixaban based on high suspicion for intracardiac thrombus. Several days after discharge, histopathologic analysis of the aspirated material revealed fragments of fibrinous thrombus with areas of organization and no evidence of malignancy. There were no associated acute inflammatory infiltrates, bacterial or fungal organisms, or foreign material identified. These findings were not consistent with infective endocarditis or a septic vegetation, and in conjunction with the clinical course and microbiologic data, effectively excluded an infectious etiology, confirming the diagnosis of a right atrial thrombus.

At 2-week outpatient follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic from a cardiac standpoint. Anticoagulation with apixaban was continued for a minimum of 3 months. Chemotherapy was resumed according to the original treatment plan. Repeat echocardiography at approximately 5 months demonstrated complete resolution of the right atrial mass.

Discussion

We report a rare case that highlights the dual role of the AngioVac system in both establishing a definitive diagnosis and providing therapeutic intervention for a right atrial mass ultimately confirmed to be thrombus. The diagnostic challenge posed by right atrial masses and the successful application of catheter-directed aspiration emphasizes clinically relevant considerations in current cardiovascular practice.

Right atrial masses present a significant diagnostic dilemma, as the differential diagnosis includes thrombi, tumors, vegetations, and other pathological entities, each requiring distinct management approaches [1]. Conventional imaging modalities, including transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, and cardiac MRI, provide valuable anatomic and tissue characterization data but may not always definitively distinguish between these entities [2].

Thrombi typically appear hypoechoic and homogeneous on echocardiography, while tumors tend to be larger, more heterogeneous, and demonstrate hyperintensity on T2-weighted sequences and enhancement with contrast [3]. However, atypical presentations can lead to misdiagnosis [2]. The PLACE-T scoring system has emerged as a useful tool for differentiating thrombus from tumor, incorporating patient history, lobulation, attachment site width, clinical context, echogenicity pattern, and tissue characterization [2]. Despite these advances, imaging alone may be insufficient for definitive diagnosis in certain cases, particularly when clinical context is ambiguous or when masses exhibit atypical features [1,4]. This diagnostic uncertainty underscores the value of obtaining tissue for histopathological examination when feasible.

In our patient, multimodality imaging remained indeterminate despite echocardiography and cardiac MRI, as the mass demonstrated overlapping features of both thrombus and possible tumor, without definitive tissue characterization. In addition, the clinical context of concurrent bacteremia further complicated interpretation, raising concern for potential vegetation versus sterile thrombus or neoplasm.

Several management strategies were considered. Continued anticoagulation alone was felt to be suboptimal given the need for diagnostic certainty and the uncertain embolic risk of a potentially organized or nonthrombotic mass. Surgical excision with biopsy was discussed but deferred due to its higher procedural risk in the setting of the patient’s comorbidities and the feasibility of a less invasive diagnostic approach. Catheter-directed thrombolysis was also considered; however, it was not favored given the uncertainty of the diagnosis and concern for bleeding risk in the absence of confirmed thrombus.

The AngioVac system, introduced in 2014, represents a minimally invasive approach to managing intravascular and intracardiac masses [5,6]. The device utilizes a large-bore aspiration cannula connected to an extracorporeal veno-venous bypass circuit, allowing aspiration of pathologic material while filtering and reinfusing blood [6]. In our case, the AngioVac procedure served the critical dual purpose of obtaining tissue for definitive diagnosis while simultaneously providing therapeutic debulking. This approach is particularly valuable when preoperative imaging cannot definitively distinguish between thrombus and tumor, as the retrieved material allows for immediate histopathological analysis [7]. Thus, AngioVac was selected as the optimal strategy to balance procedural risk with the need for diagnostic certainty and immediate therapeutic intervention in a single minimally invasive procedure.

The RAPID (Registry of AngioVac Procedures in Detail) multicenter registry, which included 234 patients, demonstrated that 70%-100% thrombus or mass removal was achieved in 58.5% of patients with right heart masses [8]. For right heart thrombi specifically, successful removal (defined as 70%-100% reduction) was achieved in 59.6% of cases [9]. Procedural success rates in single-center experiences have ranged from 67% to 85% for right atrial masses and thrombi [5,7]. The safety profile of AngioVac procedures is generally favorable, though not without risk. Common complications include bleeding requiring transfusion (17%-45% of cases), intraoperative vasopressor requirement (89%-94%), and postprocedural intensive care unit admission (97%) [5,9].

The current evidence base consists primarily of retrospective case series and registry data, with no randomized controlled trials comparing AngioVac to alternative management strategies. Future research should focus on identifying optimal patient selection criteria, standardizing procedural techniques, and conducting comparative effectiveness studies with longer-term follow-up [6].

Despite these limitations, this case underscores the important dual diagnostic and therapeutic role of the AngioVac system in the management of right atrial masses. The ability to obtain histopathological confirmation of thrombus while simultaneously achieving debulking represents a significant advantage in cases where imaging cannot definitively distinguish between pathologic entities. As experience with this technology expands and outcomes data mature, AngioVac is likely to play an increasingly important role in the management of right-sided cardiac masses, particularly in patients who are poor surgical candidates or require tissue diagnosis to guide therapy.

Conclusion

In this patient with active malignancy and high surgical risk, catheter-directed aspiration using the AngioVac system provided a minimally invasive alternative to open cardiac surgery, highlighting the expanding role of transcatheter interventions. This case report demonstrates the successful use of this approach to debulk a right atrial mass while enabling tissue acquisition for histopathologic diagnosis, thereby guiding subsequent management.
