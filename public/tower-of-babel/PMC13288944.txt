Sex-related and age-related differences in healthcare use before multiple sclerosis symptom onset: a matched cohort study
Authors: Marta Ruiz-Algueró, Feng Zhu, Farahnaz Amini, Yinshan Zhao, Ruth Ann Marrie, Helen Tremlett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreased healthcare use precedes classical multiple sclerosis (MS) symptom onset. Limited evidence exists on sex and age variation. We assessed physician visit patterns pre-MS onset by sex and age.MethodsUsing data from British Columbia, Canada, we compared annual physician visit rates (overall, by reason and specialty) in the 15 years before the neurologist-determined MS symptom onset date (index) and a matched non-MS cohort, stratified by sex and age (<30, 30–49, ≥50).ResultsWe included 2038 MS and 10 182 non-MS persons (74% female). Mean age (years) at index was 37.6 (females) and 38.7 (males). Compared with matched non-MS persons, females with MS showed earlier and more consistent elevations in physician visits (years −14 to −1), while males had sporadic elevations (years −5, –3 and −1). Females also had longer periods of elevated rate ratios (RRs) for ill-defined signs/symptoms (years −15 to −1), mental disorders (years −14 to −1 except year −7) and musculoskeletal conditions (years −6 to −1). Females exhibited sustained elevated visits by specialty, including general practice (all years; RR ≥1.1), psychiatry (years −12 to −1 except −8 to −6; RRs ≥1.6) and ophthalmology (years −9 to −1 except −2; RRs ≥1.4). Compared with matched non-MS counterparts, those aged 30–49 years had sustained higher RRs for psychiatry visits (years −12 to −1 except −8 and −6; RRs ≥1.9) and ophthalmology (years −9 to −1; RRs ≥1.4). Other age groups had fewer elevated RRs preindex. Across comparisons, RRs were of similar magnitude across sex and age groups.ConclusionsSex-specific and age-specific differences in physician visits extended up to 15 years pre-MS onset, suggesting a durable prodromal signature, most evident in females and those aged 30–49 years.Keywords: MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS

Introduction

Healthcare utilisation varies by sex and age.1 Multiple sclerosis (MS) disproportionately affects females and shows differences in disease course and severity.2,6 The prodromal phase precedes classical MS symptom onset, during which nonspecific symptoms and health changes may emerge. In the years preceding a first demyelinating event (recorded in administrative data), females had higher visit rates for musculoskeletal and sensory complaints, whereas males had higher rates of genitourinary and nervous-system-related visits.3 Younger individuals accessed ophthalmologic care more frequently, while older individuals showed increased visits for infections and injuries, patterns also observed after MS diagnosis.3 4 7 8 Physician visit rates were also higher for people with MS (pwMS) versus matched individuals without MS, beginning up to 15 years before the neurologist-determined date of MS symptom onset.9 Yet little is known about how these findings vary by sex and age.3 9 We hypothesised that healthcare use before clinical MS onset differs by these demographics and aimed to characterise such differences to support early detection and risk-stratification efforts.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective matched cohort study in British Columbia (BC), Canada, using linked administrative (health claims) and clinical databases. Administrative databases included physician services (visits) and hospitalisations, with diagnoses coded using the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10) system. Outpatient/community pharmacy dispensations were captured from the provincial prescription database. Population demographics and registry data included sex, birth date, region and area-level socioeconomic status (SES) and coverage in the mandatory provincial health insurance system.10

PwMS were attendees diagnosed at any of four MS clinics using the prevailing criteria, with an MS symptom onset date determined by the neurologist (January 1991–September 2018). If a demyelinating-related administrative claim preceded the clinical onset date (6.4% of pwMS), the earlier date was used (online supplemental tables S1.1–S1.2). Each pwMS was matched on sex, exact birth year and postal code at MS onset with up to five BC residents without MS/demyelinating disease. All individuals maintained BC residency for ≥90% of days each year for at least 5 years preonset and until the first MS clinic visit date. Annual physician visit rates were analysed up to 15 years pre-MS symptom onset by sex and age group (<30, 30–49, ≥50 years), initially as overall (all-cause) visits, then by ICD-9 diagnostic chapter and physician specialty (online supplemental tables S2–S3). We applied negative binomial regression with generalised estimating equations (GEE), or quasi-Poisson models, as appropriate, to compare rates between MS and non-MS cohorts. Models included the logarithm of person-time as an offset and were adjusted (where appropriate) for sex and, at the MS symptom onset date (or equivalent for the matched cohort), age, SES, urban/rural residence and calendar year. For GEE models, an exchangeable working correlation structure accounted for within-person repeated yearly observations. Results were expressed as adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) or unadjusted RRs for quasi-Poisson models (all with 95% CIs). Interaction terms (MS×year) were applied to assess temporal trends, with additional MS×sex× year and MS×age-group×year interactions to evaluate effect modification. Analyses by ICD chapter and physician specialty were exploratory, based on non-overlapping 95% CIs, without formal interaction tests.

Results

We included 2038 pwMS and 10 182 matched individuals; 74% were female. Mean age at MS symptom onset was 1.1 years earlier for females versus males (table 1), with age and sex distributions broadly similar across calendar periods (online supplemental table S4).

Overall physician visit rates

From 14 years pre-MS symptom onset (year –14) through to year −1, females with vs without MS had significantly higher physician rates (aRR range=1.18–1.27). Among males, increased aRRs were also observed with significant differences in years −5 to –3 and −1. Overlapping 95% CIs indicated no clear differences in the magnitude of the aRRs between females and males (figure 1A).

By age group, overall physician visit rates were consistently higher for individuals aged 30–49 years with versus without MS from year –14 through to year –1. In contrast, significantly higher visit rates emerged later (ie, closer to MS onset) for individuals <30 years of age (from year –7) and those ≥50 years (from year –3) (figure 1B). However, aRR magnitudes did not differ substantially across age groups (overlapping 95% CIs).

Physician visits by ICD chapter and specialty

Among females, those with (vs without) MS had significantly higher physician visit rates by ICD chapter for: ill-defined signs/symptoms (years −15 to −1), mental disorders (years −14 to −1 except year −7) and musculoskeletal conditions (years −6 to −1). Among males (with vs without MS), although RRs were generally above 1, significance was reached in fewer years (online supplemental figure S1). Although females exhibited significantly elevated RRs over a longer duration, the magnitude of these RRs was generally similar between sexes with overlapping 95%CIs in observed years (online supplemental table S5e).

For physician visits by specialty, females (with vs without MS) had higher general practitioner (GP) visit rates (RR ≥1.1 for all 15 years preonset), psychiatrists (years −12 to −1 except years −8 to −6, RRs ≥1.6) and ophthalmologists (years −9 to −1 except year −2, RRs ≥1.4). Among males (with vs without MS), RRs for those three specialties were generally >1, but significant in fewer years (online supplemental figure S2). Although females had more years with significantly elevated RRs, the magnitude was similar between the sexes across all three specialties, with overlapping CIs in almost all years.

By ICD chapter, and likewise by specialty, age-related differences were most apparent in the length of the elevated window preonset. Individuals with vs without MS in the two younger age groups (<50 years) had significantly higher physician visits for ill-defined signs/symptoms for most of the observed years. Among those aged 30–49 years, visits were sporadically elevated from year –15 and became consistently elevated from year –10 to –1, while among those <30 years of age, elevations were observed from year –7 to –1. In contrast, among individuals aged ≥50 years, increased visits for ill-defined symptoms were only observed in years −15 to –7 and the final 3 years pre-MS onset (online supplemental figure S3). By specialty, GP visits were significantly higher among those with (vs without) MS aged <50 years for almost all years pre-MS onset (RRs ≥1.1, except year −13 for those <30 years of age), while among those ≥50 years, a significant elevation was observed only at year −1. Compared with matched non-MS counterparts, those aged 30–49 years had longer periods of significantly elevated rates for psychiatrist visits (years −12 to −1, except −8 to −6; RRs ≥1.9) and ophthalmologist visits (years −9 to −1; RRs ≥1.4), whereas the other age groups showed significance at only 1 or 2 years (online supplemental figure S4).

Discussion

In this matched cohort of 2038 pwMS and 10 182 without MS, we observed differences in physician-visit patterns during the 15 years preceding MS onset. Females with versus without MS had higher overall physician visit rates from 14 years pre-MS onset. In contrast, increases among males were shorter in duration. Females with vs without MS also had higher visit rates, over a longer pre-MS onset period for ill-defined signs/symptoms, mental disorders and musculoskeletal conditions and higher rates of visits to GPs, psychiatry and ophthalmology. In contrast, males showed only intermittent increases preonset, particularly in psychiatry-related visits, suggesting a less durable prodromal signature. Across comparisons, RRs were broadly similar between females and males. Physician visit RRs were most durable among 30–49-year-olds (being elevated from year −14 to −1), while elevations were shorter in those <30 years (up to 7 years preonset), and ≥50 years (up to 3 years). These patterns may reflect early manifestations of evolving MS with sex-related and age-related heterogeneity in symptom expression. However, whether some conditions directly trigger MS cannot be ruled out. Recognising these early features may support earlier identification and inform risk-stratified monitoring.

In a Canadian study (whose cohort overlapped with ours), males had higher genitourinary and nervous-system-related visits, females had higher musculoskeletal visits, and younger persons had more ophthalmology and sensory encounters, while older adults showed more injury-related and infection-related hospitalisations.3 However, that study only assessed the 5 years preceding MS onset. A Canada-Sweden study found sustained excess healthcare use for over two decades before a first demyelinating event, with steeper increases in males and older adults, and earlier but smaller rises in younger individuals.8 In our cohort, these demographic patterns appeared earlier and persisted longer before the neurologist-determined MS symptom onset. Females and those aged 30–49 years showed the most sustained increases in ophthalmology and psychiatry visits more than a decade before MS onset, indicating that sex-specific and age-specific heterogeneity extends into the prodromal phase.

Strengths include neurologist-determined clinical MS symptom onset dates, long follow-up and analyses by sex and age. By anchoring our analyses to the neurologist-determined MS symptom onset date rather than diagnosis, we aimed to better capture the early disease course while minimising bias introduced by diagnostic delays and temporal changes in diagnostic criteria and clinical practice. Limitations include inability to capture signs/symptoms not prompting a physician visit, potential residual confounding and limited numbers in some of the yearly age/sex analyses. Findings should be interpreted with due caution.

Overall, the findings highlight a prolonged, demographically patterned MS prodrome, characterised by sustained increases in GP, psychiatry and ophthalmology visits, particularly among females and mid-aged adults, suggesting early, non-specific healthcare contact that could inform future risk identification and monitoring.
