Nonlocal Energy Transfer Mechanism in Three-dimensional Quantum Turbulence
Elliot Bes, Guillaume Balarac, Juan Ignacio Polanco

Abstract
We investigate the kinetic energy cascade in zero-temperature quantum turbulence. Using simple theoretical arguments and unprecedented numerical simulations, we unveil an universal mechanism transferring energy directly from large to very small scales, thus bypassing the Kolmogorov-like local energy cascade and resulting in nonclassical energy spectra. This mechanism rests both on the vast separation of scales typical of superfluid helium-4 flows and on the alignment between quantum vortices and large-scale velocity gradients, in direct analogy with vortex stretching in classical flows.



Introduction

Near absolute zero, helium-4 exists in a superfluid state known as He II
which has remarkable hydrodynamical properties.
In addition to being inviscid, all vorticity of zero-temperature He II is
concentrated in atomically thin vortices with quantized circulation

\kappa\approx 0.997\,10^{-7}\,\text{m}^{2}/\text{s}
known as quantum
vortices
Donnelly 1991
.
Despite these major differences with classical fluids, quantum
turbulence (QT) in He II
Barenghi et al. 2023
is widely thought to present
significant similarities with classical turbulence, especially at large scales where the
collective behavior of quantum vortices becomes dominant
Maurer and Tabeling 1998
;
Baggaley et al. 2012a
;
Müller et al. 2021
;
Bret et al. 2025
;
Polanco et al. 2025
;
Galantucci 2025
.
In typical He II experiments
Maurer and Tabeling 1998
;
Walmsley et al. 2007
;
Paoletti et al. 2008
;
Švančara and La Mantia 2017
;
Tang et al. 2020
;
Salort et al. 2021
;
Bret et al. 2025
, QT
is characterized by a very wide range of scales, ranging from the microscopic vortex
core radius
a_{0}\sim 10^{-10}\,\text{m}
to the integral scale

L_{0}\sim 10^{-2}\text{--}10^{-1}\,\text{m}
associated to the largest turbulent structures.
Between the two, the typical distance between quantum vortices,
\ell\sim 10^{-5}\text{--}10^{-4}\,\text{m}
,
sets the transition between
the
quantum
scales, where the discrete nature of quantum vortices is apparent,
and the
inertial
scales, where the collective motion and the partial
polarization of quantum
vortices
L’vov et al. 2007
;
Roche and Barenghi 2008
;
Baggaley and Barenghi 2011
;
Polanco et al. 2021
can lead to
quasi-classical behavior.

Up to now, the inertial and quantum ranges have been mostly regarded as two
disconnected regions of scale space with their own very different dynamics
Vinen and Niemela 2002
;
L’vov et al. 2007
.
In this Letter we show that this is not truly the case, since, due to the vast
separation between
\ell
and
a_{0}
in He II turbulence, the quantum range
acts as an energy sink which directly receives kinetic energy from the inertial
scales, thus bypassing the locality of the energy cascade on which rests
Kolmogorov’s classical turbulence phenomenology
Frisch 1995
.
A major consequence is that, even at scales significantly larger than
\ell
, the kinetic energy spectrum significantly departs from
the expected quasi-classical prediction
E(k)\sim\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}
, where
k
is the wavenumber
and
\varepsilon
the (constant) energy flux.
Similar
spectral shortcut
mechanisms have been identified in classical turbulent flows
interacting with plant canopies
Finnigan 2000
and rigid slender fibers
Olivieri et al. 2020
;
Cannon et al. 2024
.

Using simple theoretical arguments, we show that in QT such nonlocal energy transfers rest on the preferential
alignment of quantum vortices with large-scale straining motions induced by
the vortex tangle itself.
Such alignment tends to increase the quantum vortex line density
\mathcal{L}\equiv\ell^{-2}

and thus the energy content
E_{\text{small}}\sim\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}\ln(\ell/a_{0})
at quantum scales,
at the expense of large-scale energy.
Note that, unlike energy, vortex length is not a conserved quantity and therefore it is allowed to increase.
This vortex line production mechanism can be directly related to the ubiquitous vortex stretching
phenomenon in classical turbulent
flows
Tennekes and Lumley 1972
;
Ashurst et al. 1987
;
Buaria et al. 2020
, which arises from similar alignments.
The identified mechanism appears to be a generic property of QT as well, as it can be deduced from
past numerical
Nore et al. 1997a
;
Araki et al. 2002
;
Villois et al. 2016
;
Müller and Krstulovic 2020
and
experimental
Stalp and Donnelly 1998
;
Barenghi et al. 2006
;
Walmsley et al. 2007
;
Walmsley et al. 2014
observations of growth of vortex
length at early times in decaying settings.
At later times, as a disordered vortex tangle develops, such vortex growth is likely to be
arrested by the loss of vortex length associated to vortex
reconnections
Leadbeater et al. 2001
;
Villois et al. 2020
.
In particular, in steady-state regimes, the identified line production mechanism
can be seen as a necessary condition for preserving the total vortex line density over
time.

In the following, we describe quantum vortex dynamics at scales much larger than
a_{0}

using the vortex filament model (VFM)
Schwarz 1985
;
Hänninen and Baggaley 2014
.
In the VFM, quantum vortices are described as closed or infinite lines in three-dimensional (3D) space,
parametrized by the vortex positions
\bm{s}(\xi)
where
\xi
is the arc length.
A tangle of quantum vortices induces a velocity field through space given by the Biot–Savart law,

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x},t)=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{C}(t)}\!\frac{(\bm{s}-\bm{x})\times\mathrm{d}\bm{s}}{|\bm{s}-\bm{x}|^{3}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{C}(t)
denotes the instantaneous vortex geometry.
When evaluated on a vortex location
\bm{s}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}(t)
, integration must be omitted over a small distance

|\bm{s}-\bm{s}_{0}|<a_{0}\,e^{\Delta}/2
(we set
\Delta=1/2

Hänninen and Baggaley 2014
)
to avoid the singularity in Eq. (
1
) and to account
for the finite vortex core size
a_{0}

Saffman 1993
.
A well known result of this desingularization procedure
is the localized induction approximation (LIA)
Arms and Hama 1965
,

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{s})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{e^{\Delta}a_{0}}\right),\quad a_{0}\ll\epsilon\ll\frac{1}{|\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}|},

(2)

which expresses the velocity induced by a small local segment
\xi\in[\xi_{0}-\epsilon,\xi_{0}+\epsilon]

on a vortex point
\bm{s}(\xi_{0})
, and primes denote derivatives with respect to
\xi
.
In particular,
\bm{s}^{\prime}
and
\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}
are respectively the local unit tangent and curvature vectors.

Spectral energy fluxes

In the following we consider a statistically isotropic and spatially
homogeneous turbulent quantum vortex tangle in a cubic periodic domain of
volume
V=L_{\text{box}}^{3}
.
The kinetic energy per unit mass associated to the velocity field (
1
) is

E=\langle|\bm{v}_{\text{s}}|^{2}\rangle/2=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{8\pi V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{s}_{1}\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{s}_{2}}{|\bm{s}_{1}-\bm{s}_{2}|}

Saffman 1993
,
where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes a spatial average.
The Biot–Savart law (
1
) is then equivalent to
Sonin 1987

\frac{\delta E}{\delta\bm{s}}=\frac{\kappa}{V}\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}(\bm{s}),

(3)

which is interpreted as a Magnus force (per unit length and mass) acting on each vortex location
\bm{s}
.
From the definition of the functional derivative
\frac{\delta E}{\delta\bm{s}}
, the
energy variation rate due to an arbitrary vortex velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{L}}(\bm{s})
is

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{\bm{v}_{\text{L}}}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\delta E}{\delta\bm{s}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{L}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi
.

By decomposing

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x})
into Fourier modes,
one can readily generalize Eq. (
3
) to

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}_{k}}{\delta\bm{s}}=\frac{\kappa}{V}\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}(\bm{s})
,
where
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}(\bm{x})=\sum_{|\bm{k}|\leq k}\widehat{\bm{v}}_{\text{s}}(\bm{k})\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}

is a low-pass filtered velocity field
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}=\langle|\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}|^{2}\rangle/2
is the cumulative
energy up to wavenumber
k

Frisch 1995
.
Ultimately, this allows us to define the energy flux through wavenumber
k
as
the energy removed from the large scales by the total induced velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}
(
1
),

\Pi_{k}\equiv-\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{\bm{v}_{\text{s}}}\!\!=-\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}_{k}}{\delta\bm{s}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{s}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}\right)\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(4)

Since the superfluid vorticity can be formally expressed as
\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x})=\kappa\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{s})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{s}
,
this is in fact equal to the usual definition

\Pi_{k}=\langle\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\cdot(\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}})\rangle=\langle\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\cdot(\bm{v}_{\text{s}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\text{s}})\rangle

in incompressible Navier–Stokes turbulence
Frisch 1995
.
Note that, as in classical fluids, the flux vanishes as
k\to\infty
.

Nonlocal energy transfer

In 3D classical isotropic turbulence, for
k
in the inertial range, the energy flux
\Pi_{k}

is constant and equal to the energy cascade rate
\varepsilon
.
Moreover, the energy cascade is local in the sense that
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}
mostly
transfers energy to wavenumbers
k^{\prime}\lesssim 2k

Kraichnan 1966
;
Zhou 1993
;
Eyink and Aluie 2009
;
Cardesa et al. 2017
.
In the following we show that this is generally not the case in
zero-temperature QT, as energy is directly transferred from inertial scales
(
k\ll k_{\ell}\equiv 2\pi/\ell
) to quantum scales
k^{\prime}\gg k_{\ell}
.
Here the mean inter-vortex distance is
\ell=\mathcal{L}^{-1/2}
where

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}\xi
is the vortex line density.

We start by generalizing Eq. (
4
) to write the nonlocal energy
transfer from large (
<k
) to small (
>q
) scales as

\Pi_{k}^{>q}=\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{>q})\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\,\mathrm{d}\xi
,
where
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{>q}\equiv\bm{v}_{\text{s}}-\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<q}
is a high-pass filtered (i.e. small-scale) velocity.
Since nonlocal vortex interactions are negligible at scales below
\ell
,
one can safely assume that
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{>k_{\ell}}
is well approximated by the
LIA (
2
) with
\epsilon\sim\ell
.
This ultimately leads to

\Pi_{k}^{>k_{\ell}}\approx\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4\pi}\ln\left(\frac{2\ell}{e^{\Delta}a_{0}}\right)\mathcal{S}_{k},\quad\mathcal{S}_{k}\equiv-\frac{1}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5)

where we have used the geometrical relation
\bm{s}^{\prime}\times(\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{s}^{\prime\prime})=-\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}
.
Equation (
5
) states that nonlocal energy
transfers from inertial to quantum scales are enabled by a possible anti-alignment
between the large-scale velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}
and the local vortex curvature
\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}
,
and are amplified by the scale separation between
\ell
and
a_{0}
.

Importantly, one can interpret
\mathcal{S}_{k}
as the production of vortex line density
\mathcal{L}
by
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}
.
Indeed, using integration by parts,

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\bm{s}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}}{\partial\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\bm{s}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\cdot\bm{s}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(6)

Here
\bm{s}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}}{\partial\xi}
expresses
the local vortex line growth rate (per unit length) due to

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}
.
Remarkably, the last integral is strongly reminiscent of the
enstrophy (
\Omega\equiv\langle|\bm{\omega}|^{2}\rangle/2
) production term from the Navier–Stokes equations, which writes

P_{\Omega}=\langle\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{\omega}\rangle=\langle\omega_{i}S_{ij}\omega_{j}\rangle

where
S_{ij}=(\partial_{i}v_{j}+\partial_{j}v_{i})/2
is the strain-rate tensor
Tennekes and Lumley 1972
.
This term is generally
positive in 3D classical turbulence due to the vortex stretching mechanism
Taylor 1938
;
Tennekes and Lumley 1972
;
Buaria et al. 2020
,
intimately linked to energy cascade dynamics.
In the case of Eq. (
6
),
\mathcal{S}_{k}
corresponds to vortex stretching by the
nonlocal
(or large-scale)
strain rate tensor, whose most extensional eigenvector has
been shown to preferentially align with vorticity in classical turbulence
Hamlington et al. 2008
;
Buaria and Pumir 2021
.
Therefore, it is natural to expect
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to be positive for
k\ll k_{\ell}

if the large scales of QT have any similarities with classical fluid
turbulence.
Paradoxically, such similarities would result in
positive nonlocal energy transfers
\Pi_{k}^{>k_{\ell}}

[Eq. (
5
)], ultimately leading to nonclassical
scaling laws at scales larger than
\ell
.
This scenario is confirmed using numerical simulations below.

Numerical simulations

We perform VFM numerical simulations of statistically stationary zero-temperature QT at high vortex densities with an
unprecedented separation of scales between
L_{0}
and
\ell
.
To circumvent the
O(N^{2})
cost of directly computing far-ranged interactions
between
N
vortex points, we adopt a novel fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based approach
Polanco 2025a
inspired by
Ewald summation methods
Arnold and Holm 2005
;
Arnold et al. 2013
, commonly used in
molecular dynamics to evaluate electrostatic interactions
in periodic systems.
Our method, summarized in the End Matter, enables the
efficient evaluation of Eq. (
1
) in
O(N\log N)
time.
Unlike other fast approaches
Baggaley and Barenghi 2012
;
Yui et al. 2025
, this method naturally accounts for the infinity of periodic
vortex images (instead of effectively introducing a cut-off distance

r_{\text{cut}}\sim L_{\text{box}}

Hänninen and Baggaley 2014
), while also providing highly accurate
Fourier-space velocity fields at no extra cost, facilitating the evaluation of energy spectra and
fluxes (
4
).
The methods are implemented in the open-source GPU-accelerated VortexPasta.jl
solver
Polanco 2025b
.

To eliminate limitations associated to usual time-decaying configurations – such
as statistical convergence issues and the influence of initial conditions – we devise a novel energy injection
scheme to keep the system in a statistically stationary state.
Our approach mirrors standard practice in numerical classical turbulence studies.
Concretely, we introduce an external forcing velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
such that vortices move according to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{s}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{v}_{\text{s}}(\bm{s})+\bm{v}_{\text{f}}(\bm{s}).

(7)

As detailed in the End Matter [Eq. (
9
)],
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
can be defined such that it injects kinetic
energy at the largest scales of the system (wavenumbers
k<k_{\text{f}}
) at rate
\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}
, while leaving
smaller scales unmodified (as verified in Fig.
2
, open circles).
We also ensure that
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}\cdot\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}=0
so that
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
does not directly modify
the total vortex length (and thus the energy at quantum scales).
Note that determining
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
requires knowing the Fourier-filtered velocity
\bm{v}^{<k_{\text{f}}}
at all times, which is
greatly facilitated by our FFT-based numerical method.

Vortex lines are discretized as quintic splines, ensuring an accurate
estimation and interpolation of geometrical quantities such as
\bm{s}
,
\bm{s}^{\prime}
and

\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}
.
All line integrals are evaluated using 3-point Gauss–Legendre quadratures between
neighboring vortex points, and the relative accuracy of Biot–Savart
computations is kept below
10^{-6}

Polanco 2025a
.
As in previous works
Araki et al. 2002
;
Adachi et al. 2010
;
Baggaley et al. 2012b
;
Galantucci et al. 2019
, the distance

\Delta\xi
between vortex points is kept within
[\delta_{\text{res}},2\delta_{\text{res}})

using adaptive line refinement.
The resolution is set to
\delta_{\text{res}}\approx\ell/6
, adjusted according to
the mean inter-vortex distance
\ell
in each simulation.
Following standard practice, filaments are reconnected when their distance is below
\delta_{\text{res}}
, but only if
this leads to loss of vortex length (effectively dissipating energy at quantum
scales) and if the reconnecting segments are not locally parallel
Baggaley 2012a
.
Equation (
7
) is advanced in time using an adaptive 4th-order Runge–Kutta scheme.

We set the nondimensional vortex circulation to
\kappa=1
, the periodic domain size to

L_{\text{box}}=2\pi
and the vortex core radius to
a_{0}=10^{-7}
.
Since
a_{0}\approx 10^{-10}\,\text{m}
in He II, this gives

L_{\text{box}}\approx 2\pi\,\text{mm}
in physical units, which is representative of a
typical measurement section in current experiments.
We also perform simulations with
a_{0}=10^{-4}
to illustrate the effect of reducing the
\ell/a_{0}
ratio.
Forcing is applied up to wavenumber
k_{\text{f}}=2.5
.
A total of 7 runs are performed with different values of
a_{0}
and of the energy
injection rate
\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}
as detailed in the End Matter (Table
1
).
An instantaneous visualization of a typical run is shown in Fig.
1
,
highlighting the formation of highly polarized vortex
bundles, a key feature of quasi-classical QT
L’vov et al. 2007
;
Roche and Barenghi 2008
;
Baggaley et al. 2012b
;
Baggaley 2012b
;
Polanco et al. 2021
.

Numerical results

We start by plotting in Fig.
2
the spectral energy fluxes obtained from one of our runs.
The total energy flux from large to small scales (
\Pi_{k}
, filled circles)
displays a plateau at scales larger than
\ell
with a value close to the energy injection rate
\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}
.
At first sight, this is consistent with the energy cascade picture in classical turbulence
Alexakis and Biferale 2018
.
At smaller scales, the flux starts decreasing very slowly (logarithmically)
with
k
, and is expected to reach zero near the microscopic scale
k_{a}\sim 1/a_{0}

(not visible in the figure).
This drastically differs from the Navier–Stokes case where the energy flux
rapidly decays to zero when the viscous scales are reached.

The flux
\Pi_{k}
provides no indication on the locality of the energy cascade.
With this aim, we consider the local flux
\Pi_{k}^{<2k}

to neighboring scales
k^{\prime}\in[k,2k]
(crosses in Fig.
2
), defined by replacing
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}
with
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<2k}
in
Eq. (
4
).
The local flux clearly decreases beyond the injection scales, thus breaking the
hypothesis of a constant energy flux associated to Kolmogorov’s (K41) scaling laws.
This is also consistent with the behavior of the nonlocal flux to quantum scales

\Pi_{k}^{>k_{\ell}}
(squares), which is positive and increases throughout the inertial range.
The nonlocal flux is in fairly good agreement with our estimation (
5
)
based on the vortex length production term
\mathcal{S}_{k}
(dashed line), providing strong support
to the proposed picture of a nonlocal energy transfer driven by the alignment
between quantum vortices and large-scale velocity gradients.
Our results confirm that a preferential alignment exists at all
scales (up to the largest simulated scale) leading to a continuous
depletion of the local cascade.
More profoundly, they also suggest that this is the main mechanism enabling a
transfer of energy from large to quantum scales, before
it being converted to acoustic energy (phonons) at microscopic scales via vortex reconnections
Nore et al. 1997b
;
Leadbeater et al. 2001
;
Kobayashi and Tsubota 2005

and the Kelvin wave cascade
Kivotides et al. 2001
;
Vinen et al. 2003
;
Kozik and Svistunov 2004
;
L’vov and Nazarenko 2010
;
Krstulovic 2012
;
Baggaley and Laurie 2014
.

The existence of nonlocal energy transfers is expected to lead to the breakdown of
K41 scaling laws in QT.
This is confirmed in Fig.
3
, which shows the kinetic
energy spectra obtained from all numerical runs.
The normalized spectra collapse at quantum scales, meaning that they all follow
the analytical prediction
E(k)=(\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}/4\pi)k^{-1}

associated to an isolated straight vortex for
ka_{0}\ll 1

Araki et al. 2002
;
Polanco 2025a
.
Note that a production of vortex lines directly increases the energy content
throughout the quantum scales, shifting the spectrum upwards.
At inertial scales (
k\ell\ll 1
) the spectra become much steeper, indicating the emergence of
collective dynamics and energy-containing vortex bundles generally
associated to a partial polarization of the tangle.
While it is possible to fit power law scalings within this region, these are
substantially steeper than Kolmogorov’s
k^{-5/3}
law, suggesting a higher degree of polarization
than that traditionally associated to quasi-classical QT
L’vov et al. 2007
;
Polanco et al. 2021
.
As expected from Eq. (
5
), the deviations are
further amplified as the ratio
\ell/a_{0}
is increased
from
\sim 10^{3}
(blue curves) to
\sim 10^{6}
(red curves).

Despite these major deviations, one can attempt to reconcile the observed energy
spectra with K41 theory by considering a nonconstant local energy flux
which, for simplicity, behaves as
\varepsilon_{\text{local}}(k)\sim k^{-\alpha}
.
Then, dimensional analysis leads to
E(k)\sim\varepsilon_{\text{local}}(k)^{2/3}k^{-5/3}\sim k^{-(5+2\alpha)/3}
.
Taking
\Pi_{k}^{<2k}
(inset of Fig.
3
) as a proxy for
\varepsilon_{\text{local}}(k)
, we find

\alpha\approx 1.25
and
0.8
for the two considered values of
\ell/a_{0}

over about half a decade in
k
.
These values exactly correspond to the
E(k)\sim k^{-2.5}
and
k^{-2.2}

scalings fitted in the main panel of Fig.
3
.
This gives strong support to the idea that, similarly to classical turbulence, inertial range scalings are
independent of the specific energy injection mechanisms and of quantum scale
dynamics, despite the lack of a constant local energy cascade.

Discussion

We have identified a novel mechanism transferring energy from inertial to quantum scales in zero-temperature QT.
Besides significantly altering QT scaling laws at scales above
\ell
, this
mechanism seems to predominantly explain how energy arrives to quantum scales
before being ultimately dissipated by phonon emission.
The crucial realization enabling these transfers is that, while individual
quantum vortices cannot be stretched in the classical
sense
Alamri et al. 2008
;
Barenghi et al. 2023
, nothing disallows them from being
elongated by large-scale velocity gradients, as vortex length is not a
conserved quantity.
Importantly, this mechanism transfers energy from large scales to
all
quantum scales at
once, which highlights the relevance of the scale separation between
\ell

and
a_{0}
in this process.

One major question is whether nonlocal transfers vanish at larger scales as the
extent of the inertial range (or the Reynolds number) is increased.
Our simulations suggest that this is indeed the case, as the local energy flux
(inset of Fig.
3
) appears to saturate to
a constant value of the order of the energy injection rate
\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}
–
as expected based on classical turbulence phenomenology.
Physically, this may be explained by an increasing decorrelation between the
orientations of individual quantum vortices and of the resulting coarse-grained
vorticity at large scales.
The alignment between velocity gradients and vorticity at different scales will
be the subject of a future investigation.

Being based on an alignment between large-scale velocity gradients and quantum vortices, we
expect the identified mechanism to stay valid in finite-temperature QT driven by
classical means (e.g. mechanical forcing), where vortices coexist and interact
with a viscous normal fluid via a mutual friction force.
These two components are expected to be tightly coupled at large scales, roughly exhibiting one-fluid behavior.
The identified nonlocal transfers can be expected to promote their decoupling
and thus the mutual friction between the two components, potentially leading to temperature-dependent
energy spectra as the quantum scale is approached.

While our work is motivated by isotropic turbulence in He II, the identified mechanism
may also be relevant in rotating settings or in other superfluids where the
ratio
\ell/a_{0}
also spans several orders of magnitude in realistic settings.
This can be the case in
3
He-B, where
a_{0}\sim 10^{-8}\,\text{m}
while
values
\ell\sim 10^{-4}\,\text{m}
can be experimentally achieved
Bradley et al. 2006
.
The separation of scales can be even more dramatic in the core of neutron stars
Lattimer and Prakash 2004
, where

a_{0}\sim 10^{-14}\,\text{m}

Seveso et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2025
while the inter-vortex distance associated to the star rotation alone can be estimated as

\ell\sim 10^{-5}\,\text{m}

Graber et al. 2017
.

Definition of forcing velocity

In analogy with Eq. (
4
), one can express the cumulative energy injection rate up to scale

k
due to an external velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
as

\mathcal{F}_{k}\equiv\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{\bm{v}_{\text{f}}}=\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k}\right)\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{f}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(8)

Clearly, choosing
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}^{0}=\alpha\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k_{\text{f}}}
(with
\alpha>0
a nondimensional forcing coefficient)
ensures energy injection up to scale
k=k_{\text{f}}
.
However, to avoid the unwanted injection of energy at quantum scales [see
Eq. (
5
)], we also require
\bm{v}_{\text{f}}
to be
orthogonal to
\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}
.
This leads to

\bm{v}_{\text{f}}(\bm{s})=\bm{v}_{\text{f}}^{0}-\frac{\bm{v}_{\text{f}}^{0}\cdot\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}}{|\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}=\alpha\frac{\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{<k_{\text{f}}}}{|\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\bm{s}^{\prime}\times\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}.

(9)

From Eq. (
8
), the large-scale energy injection rate associated to this choice is then

\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{k_{\text{f}}}=\alpha\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\left|\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\bm{v}^{<k_{\text{f}}}\right|^{2}}{|\bm{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(10)

In practice, we dynamically tune the value of
\alpha
to achieve a constant
energy injection rate
\varepsilon_{\text{inj}}
, similarly in spirit to commonly used forcing schemes in
classical turbulence
Lamorgese et al. 2005
.

Fast evaluation of Biot–Savart integrals

In the following, we summarize the FFT-based approach used to efficiently evaluate
Biot–Savart integrals in periodic domains.
For details the reader is referred to Ref.
Polanco 2025a
.
Similarly to standard Ewald summation methods
Arnold and Holm 2005
, the main idea is to
split the self-induced velocity field (
1
) as

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x})=\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(n)}}(\bm{x})+\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{x})
, where
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(n)}}
and
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}

respectively account for the influence of vortex elements located near and far from the
evaluation point
\bm{x}
.
To obtain expressions for these two components, we first introduce the vector
potential
\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x})
such that
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}
.
It is related to the vorticity by Poisson’s equation
\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{v}_{\text{s}}=-\nabla^{2}\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}
,
whose solution can be written as the convolution
\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}=G\ast\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}

where
G(\bm{r})=1/(4\pi r)
is the Green’s function associated to the 3D Poisson’s equation.
In the context of the VFM, this becomes

\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}(\bm{x})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{s}}{|\bm{s}-\bm{x}|}.

(11)

It can be readily shown that
\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}
leads to the Biot–Savart law (
1
).
As a side note, knowing
\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}
at vortex locations
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{C}
conveniently
allows to evaluate the total kinetic energy per unit mass as
E=\frac{\kappa}{2V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\bm{\psi}_{\text{s}}(\bm{s})\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{s}
.

The main idea of standard Ewald methods is to split the Green’s function
G(\bm{r})
as

G(\bm{r})=\frac{\erfc(\alpha r)}{4\pi r}+\frac{\erf(\alpha r)}{4\pi r}=G^{\text{(n)}}(\bm{r})+G^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{r}),

(12)

where
\erf(x)
is the error function and
\erfc(x)=1-\erf(x)
.
Here
\alpha
is a tunable splitting parameter (an inverse length scale) setting the
transition between near- and far-ranged interactions.
Taking the gradient of Eq. (
12
), a similar splitting can then be obtained for the Biot–Savart kernel,

\bm{\nabla}G(\bm{r})=-\bm{r}/(4\pi r^{3})=\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}(\bm{r})+\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{r})
,
with

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=-\left[\erfc(\alpha r)+\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-(\alpha r)^{2}}\right]\frac{\bm{r}}{4\pi r^{3}},

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=-\left[\erf(\alpha r)-\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-(\alpha r)^{2}}\right]\frac{\bm{r}}{4\pi r^{3}},

(14)

allowing to define

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(n)}}=\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}\ast\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}
and
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}=\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}\ast\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}
.
With the above choice of splitting, it can be shown that
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}
is nothing else than the velocity field induced by a Gaussian-filtered vorticity field

\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{x})=(\varphi_{\alpha}\ast\bm{\omega}_{\text{s}})(\bm{x})

with
\varphi_{\alpha}(\bm{r})=(\alpha/\sqrt{\pi})^{3}e^{-(\alpha r)^{2}}
.

The first crucial point here is that, unlike the full Biot–Savart kernel,
\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}(\bm{r})
decays
very quickly with
r
, which means that one only needs to consider nearby pairs
within a radius
r_{\text{cut}}=\beta/\alpha
to obtain an arbitrarily accurate estimation of

\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(n)}}
.
Here
\beta
is a nondimensional parameter controlling truncation
errors
Polanco 2025a
, which we set to
\beta=3.5
for
10^{-6}

relative accuracy.
Secondly,
\bm{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{r})
is nonsingular and well-behaved near
r=0
, unlike
\bm{\nabla}G(\bm{r})
.
In periodic domains, this allows to represent
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}
as a truncated Fourier
series up to wavenumber
k_{\text{max}}=2\beta\alpha

Polanco 2025a
.
Then, the Fourier coefficients of
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}
are simply

\widehat{\bm{v}}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{k})=\frac{i\bm{k}\times\widehat{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}(\bm{k})}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}=\frac{i\bm{k}\times\widehat{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}(\bm{k})}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}\,e^{-k^{2}/4\alpha^{2}},

(15)

where
\widehat{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}(\bm{k})=\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{s}

is a Fourier coefficient of the superfluid vorticity field.

In practice, to obtain
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}
, we start by approximating the
\widehat{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}(\bm{k})
line integral using 3-point
Gauss–Legendre quadratures on each discrete vortex segment, requiring
3N

spline interpolations of
\bm{s}(\xi)
and
\bm{s}^{\prime}(\xi)
.
This results in a Fourier sum over
3N
nonequispaced points, which is then
efficiently evaluated using the type-1 nonuniform FFT (NUFFT)
algorithm
Dutt and Rokhlin 1993
;
Greengard and Lee 2004
.
This step yields
\widehat{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}(\bm{k})
coefficients on a uniform 3D Fourier grid
truncated at
\pm k_{\text{max}}
in each direction.
Then, once Eq. (
15
) has been evaluated, we
interpolate
\bm{v}_{\text{s}}^{\text{(f)}}
at vortex locations in physical space using the type-2 NUFFT.
Overall, by properly choosing the splitting parameter
\alpha
, this method
leads to
O(N\log N)
complexity, enabling the fast and arbitrarily accurate
evaluation of Biot–Savart interactions in dense vortex systems.
