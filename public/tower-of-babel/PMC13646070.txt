Alzheimer's detection using quanvolutional neural networks with federated training

Prakhar Mishra, Subitha D., Pramukh Rajendra Jain, Arogya Sharma, Kavitha J. C.

Abstract

Alzheimer's disease detection from MRI data is challenging because of subtle anatomical patterns, high-dimensional image data, and privacy constraints associated with centralized medical-data training. We propose a hybrid quantum–classical federated learning framework using a frozen four-qubit quanvolutional front-end. The 128 × 128 grayscale MRI images are divided into 2 × 2 patches, and quantum features are pre-extracted using a fixed circuit implemented in PennyLane. The resulting features are classified using trainable classical dense layers implemented in TensorFlow/Keras. Federated training is performed across five non-IID clients using FedAvg and FedNova over ten communication rounds, with dynamic local epochs of 5–30 and batch size 32. Expectation-Value Local Differential Privacy (EV-LDP) is also evaluated. The proposed FedNova-DQ framework achieved a final global accuracy of 0.923, compared with 0.921 using FedAvg. The centralized QCNN achieved 0.891 accuracy compared with 0.872 for the classical CNN baseline. Under EV-LDP with ε = 2.0, the model maintained 90.85% accuracy. At K = 200 clients, FedNova achieved 90.15% accuracy compared with 82.15% for FedAvg. The frozen quantum front-end eliminates quantum-parameter communication and decouples quantum feature extraction from federated communication rounds while maintaining strong classification performance under heterogeneous client distributions.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder that leads to a progressive loss of memory, reasoning, and behavioral skills in patients. Early diagnosis of AD based on medical imaging techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is crucial, as early diagnosis could slow the disease's progression and benefit patients. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans provide high-resolution structural information about brain tissue, but it remains challenging to extract meaningful diagnostic features from MRI scans because of their complexity and high dimensionality.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been extensively applied to medical image analysis; however, their application is limited by the difficulty CNNs have in capturing subtle, non-linear relationships in MRI data. Moreover, deep learning models are usually trained centrally, which raises privacy concerns because medical data from different hospitals are sensitive and cannot be shared due to regulatory requirements. These issues have led to the need for decentralized, privacy-preserving training techniques that can preserve diagnostic performance while protecting patient data.

This paper presents a Quanvolutional Neural Network (QCNN) integrated with Federated Learning (FL) for distributed, privacy-aware Alzheimer's detection. In the hybrid model, classical convolutional filters on 2 × 2 patches are replaced by a fixed four-qubit circuit that maps each patch into quantum measurement features. The quantum feature maps are pre-computed once per image and then used as input to classical dense layers implemented in TensorFlow and Keras. Only the classical parameters are updated during the training and federated aggregation. Unlike existing Quantum Federated Learning (QFL) approaches that jointly optimize quantum circuit parameters across clients, the proposed framework employs a fixed four-qubit quanvolutional layer as a universal quantum feature extractor. The quanvolutional circuit is designed offline and remains unchanged throughout the training. This completely eliminates quantum parameter transmission (0 quantum parameters exchanged) and decouples quantum circuit simulation latency from federated communication rounds, removing the burden of repeated quantum optimization across distributed sites.

The training framework uses five clients, each holding a private, non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) subset of the data. Clients train locally and transmit only classical parameter updates to a central server (raw MRI images are never shared). The aggregation strategies FedAvg and FedNova-DQ are compared across the same non-IID client partitions. Note that the primary comparison () uses different local epoch schedules for the two strategies—FedAvg with a fixed E = 25 per round and FedNova-DQ with dynamic Ek∈[5, 30]—so it measures the combined effect of step normalization and dynamic epoch scheduling. A controlled comparison with both strategies using an identical fixed epoch budget (E = 10) is reported in the scalability experiment (). FedNova normalizes client updates by the amount of local optimization performed; when local dataset sizes differ, this adjusts each client's contribution according to the number of local steps.

Motivation

Alzheimer's MRI classification is dominated by subtle, low-contrast structure in the early-stage classes (Very Mild vs. Non Demented) that classical convolutional filters can under-represent. Alzheimer's disease diagnosis in the early stage is highly challenging due to the structural abnormalities of human brain and distributed across multiple anatomical regions. Hence it is very difficult to learn this pattern from direct MRI images using conventional CNNs and this may happen at the expense of huge volume of data for effective feature extraction. A quanvolutional circuit embeds each 2 × 2 patch into a Hilbert space via parameterized rotations and entanglement, providing a fixed, non-learnable feature transform whose expressivity is independent of the classical model capacity. Crucially, because these quantum features are pre-computed once and then frozen, the (relatively expensive) quantum evaluation is decoupled from the federated training loop: no quantum parameters are exchanged or updated across clients. The expected benefit is therefore a modest but reusable improvement in class separability contributed by the quantum embedding, rather than a replacement for classical networks. These quantum embeddings help to improve the discrimination of subtle neuroanatomical variations while maintaining a compact model with relatively few trainable parameters. As a result of this, QCNNs provide an attractive feature extraction framework for Alzheimer's disease classification, particularly in privacy-preserving federated learning environments where medical data are decentralized and limited.

Why a frozen quantum front-end rather than a trainable one?

Prior federated quanvolutional architectures () jointly optimize variational quantum circuit (VQC) parameters across clients during every communication round. While this produces expressive quantum models, it introduces three practical burdens that become acute in real-world medical federations: (a) quantum circuit simulation or execution must be repeated at every local training step, dominating wall-clock cost; (b) quantum gate parameters must be serialized, transmitted, and aggregated alongside classical weights, increasing the communication payload and introducing a new surface for gradient-leakage attacks; and (c) variational circuits are susceptible to barren plateaus at moderate depth, making distributed optimization across heterogeneous non-IID clients unreliable. A frozen front-end eliminates all three burdens simultaneously: quantum features are extracted once offline, only classical dense-layer weights enter the federated loop, and the bounded expectation values (〈Z〉∈[−1, 1]) provide a natural anchor for the Expectation-Value Local Differential Privacy (EV-LDP) mechanism introduced in this work. The scientific question addressed here is therefore not “can quantum circuits be federated?”—which already answered affirmatively—but rather “is a frozen quantum embedding sufficient to improve federated medical-image classification while removing the quantum communication and optimization overhead?”

Literature review

Quantum machine learning (QML) has become one of the effective methods for exploiting the characteristics of quantum computation to improve classical learning models. The original idea of Quanvolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs) was introduced by , who showed that quantum circuits could perform the same role as standard convolutional filters, producing high-dimensional quantum feature maps for image classification. further developed this concept by showing that a quantum-enriched feature space can offer computational benefits for some learning tasks, opening the way for hybrid quantum-classical models. Later work investigated variants of quantum convolutional neural networks, including , quantum convolutional architectures for high-energy physics data (), and resource-efficient implementations optimized for use on small quantum devices (). Together, these studies indicate that quantum feature extraction has the potential to provide richer data representations than purely classical convolutional networks.

Another area of development has been the enhancement of QCNN performance in domain-specific applications. showed that pretrained QCNNs can be applied to medical image detection tasks, demonstrating relevance to healthcare diagnostics. Other researchers (; ; ) investigated optimization methods, parameter allocation, and depth–width trade-offs to maximize the expressiveness of QCNNs without sacrificing computational efficiency. The hybrid quantum-classical approach has since been reinforced by frameworks and benchmarks such as , as well as by numerous surveys (; ). Nevertheless, the majority of current QCNN studies assume centralized training, which restricts their use in privacy-sensitive practice areas such as medicine.

Federated learning (FL) is one possible solution to this issue, as it allows distributed model training without data centralization. Federated optimization was first proposed by and , who introduced communication-efficient methods such as FedAvg. deployed secure aggregation methods so that model updates could be exchanged safely between clients. Since then, FL has become popular in healthcare (; ), where it enables institutions to jointly learn models while maintaining patient privacy. and have highlighted the problem of non-IID (non-independent, identically distributed) data, which can significantly worsen model performance when the distribution of client data is highly heterogeneous.

To address these issues, algorithms such as FedNova () have been proposed to mitigate client drift and improve convergence under heterogeneous data conditions. Recent studies (; ; ; ) have explored differential privacy, personalization, and large-scale deployment of FL systems across diverse domains, including medical imaging and edge computing. Closely related work has also begun to combine quantum models with federated training directly. introduced a federated quanvolutional neural network that trains a hybrid quantum–classical model across distributed sites on medical imaging data without exchanging raw samples. proposed the first federated training scheme for hybrid quantum–classical models, demonstrating comparable accuracy with reduced distributed training cost. Subsequent studies extended this direction with tensor-network-based federated quantum learning for healthcare () and with communication- efficient quantum federated learning optimizations for multi-center healthcare data .

Positioning relative to large-scale QFL studies

The field of quantum federated learning has rapidly expanded in scope and client scale since 2023. demonstrated up to K = 50 client nodes using trainable variational quantum circuits (VQCs). Subsequent communication-efficient QFL work by extended this to multi-center deployments with compression mechanisms but still operates under the trainable-VQC paradigm where quantum parameters must be communicated every round. More broadly, classical federated learning studies (; ; ; ) routinely evaluate K≥100 clients and even cross-device deployments with thousands of participants, establishing scalability benchmarks that the QFL community is only beginning to approach. The present work does not claim to match those classical-FL scales on quantum hardware; instead, it demonstrates empirically across K∈{5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200} that a frozen quantum front-end—which decouples quantum circuit evaluation from the federated loop—degrades in accuracy far more gracefully than a trainable-VQC approach as the client population grows, maintaining 90.15% accuracy even at K = 200 where FedAvg degrades to 82.15%. This positions the frozen paradigm as a practically viable stepping stone toward larger quantum federated deployments: because no quantum parameters enter the communication protocol, the per-round overhead scales only with the classical head size (~69.7 MB), a property that becomes increasingly important as the community moves toward K≥100 quantum clients.

Despite these advances, the integration of quantum feature extraction within a federated learning framework for Alzheimer's MRI classification remains comparatively underexplored. The existing quantum federated learning studies treat the quantum and federated components in a largely monolithic manner and are evaluated on generic benchmarks, leaving a gap in hybrid systems that combine a frozen quanvolutional front-end, explicit non-IID Alzheimer's partitioning, and a direct FedAvg/FedNova comparison—which is the specific contribution of this study.

This work addresses that gap by developing a unified framework that integrates QCNNs with the FedNova algorithm to enable decentralized, privacy-preserving Alzheimer's detection from MRI images. To clearly elucidate the technical, architectural, and experimental advancements over prior quantum federated learning literature—specifically the baseline federated quanvolutional framework of — presents a direct itemized comparison.

As detailed in , while established the foundational feasibility of collaborative quantum learning using trainable quanvolutional filters, their framework requires continuous optimization and transmission of quantum parameters across federated rounds. In contrast, our proposed paradigm freezes the quanvolutional front-end as a universal feature extractor. This completely eliminates quantum parameter transmission, reduces communication payload to classical dense head weights, and enables the introduction of EV-LDP on bounded expectation values.

Dataset description and pre-processing

Experiments utilize the widely studied four-class Alzheimer's MRI benchmark dataset from Kaggle (, originally compiled by ), comprising two-dimensional (2D) axial brain MRI slices labeled as Mild Demented, Moderate Demented, Non Demented, and Very Mild Demented. The raw collection contains 6,400 images with severe natural class imbalance: Non Demented 3,200, Very Mild Demented 2,240, Mild Demented 896, and Moderate Demented 64 (a 50:1 ratio between Non Demented and Moderate Demented).

In computational Alzheimer's MRI studies on 2D slice benchmarks, evaluating diagnostic sensitivity under extreme natural imbalance (where only 64 raw Moderate slices exist) leads to severe evaluation instability and minority-class gradient starvation. To enable robust multi-class evaluation and balanced sensitivity analysis across all dementia stages, standard class-balancing data augmentation (random rotations ±15°, horizontal reflections, subtle spatial shifts, and intensity scaling) was applied to the minority classes, establishing a standardized balanced evaluation benchmark of 3,675 total slices. This benchmark was partitioned using a stratified 80% training (Ntrain = 2, 940) and 20% testing (Ntest = 735) split. details the raw dataset distribution alongside the exact per-class breakdown of the 3,675-slice evaluation benchmark.

Data heterogeneity across client nodes was generated using a Dirichlet distribution Dir(α) with concentration parameter α = 0.5 on class labels, creating realistic label imbalance across institutions (illustrated in for K = 5). Client 5 was assigned a balanced class mixture to serve as an institutional reference node (this balanced assignment applies only to the K = 5 partition; for K>5, all clients receive Dirichlet-sampled allocations with no explicitly balanced node). The images are pre-extracted axial slices rather than full clinical volumetric series; they are widely used in computational Alzheimer's classification studies but should not be interpreted as raw multi-site clinical volumes with scanner metadata, and they are used here under the dataset's public Kaggle license for non-commercial research.

Images were preprocessed with OpenCV and NumPy. Each slice was resized to 128 × 128 pixels, converted to grayscale, and scaled to the [0, 1] intensity range. For evaluation of centralized CNN/QCNN models, the data were evaluated on the 80/20 train/test split. For federated experiments, the training portion was partitioned across simulated institutional clients (K∈{5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200}) under non-IID label skew, using the partitioning scheme (Dir(α = 0.5); Client 5 balanced at K = 5 only) described above.

Quantum feature extraction

The quanvolutional front-end replaces a shallow classical convolution on 2 × 2 patches with a four-qubit quantum circuit—a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC)—simulated in PennyLane (default.qubit, expectation-value mode). Each 128 × 128 grayscale image is partitioned into non-overlapping 2 × 2 patches, yielding P = 64 × 64 = 4096 patches per image. Each patch is assigned to four wires and processed by the circuit in .

Encoding and gates: Pixel intensities in [0, 1] are linearly mapped to rotation angles on single-qubit RX, RY, and RZ gates. The circuit also includes fixed-angle rotations (for example, multiples of π/2, π/3, π/4, and π/6 as drawn in ) and a ladder of controlled-NOT (CNOT) gates that entangle neighboring wires. After the unitary, the Pauli-Z expectation value on each qubit is evaluated, producing a four-dimensional feature vector per patch. In this encoding, the four pixels of a 2 × 2 patch are mapped one-to-one onto the four wires (one pixel intensity per qubit), so the circuit is a local, patch-level embedding rather than a global image encoder; the “high-dimensional” benefit refers to the per-patch Hilbert-space representation, not to a whole-image quantum state. A single layer of RX/RY/RZ rotations followed by the CNOT ladder constitutes the full circuit (no repeated ansatz layers), and because angles are fixed it has zero trainable quantum parameters. Concatenating all patches yields a feature vector of length P×4 = 16, 384, which is the input to the classical network.

Frozen (non-trained) quantum front-end: In this study the quantum circuit is used as a fixed feature transform: angles tied to pixel values and the fixed circuit angles are not optimized with gradient descent, and quantum parameters are not included in federated updates. Features for each image are pre-extracted once offline and then reused during classical training. Consequently, only classical dense-layer weights are trainable. Expectation values are computed analytically in the simulator (not from finite-shot sampling), so measurement shot noise is not used as a regularizer.

The fixed non-pixel rotation angles shown in (such as multiples of π/2, π/3, π/4, and π/6, and the constant terms drawn in the diagram) are hyperparameters of the feature transform, chosen a priori and held constant; they are not data-dependent and are not trained. Because features are pre-extracted once and then frozen, the wall-clock cost of this quantum evaluation is a one-time offline expense and is therefore excluded from the per-round communication/latency profiling reported in .

Classical convolutional neural network (CNN) baseline

For a controlled comparison with the quantum front-end, a classical CNN baseline uses a single trainable 2 × 2 convolutional layer (four output channels) in place of the quantum patch encoder, followed by the same dense head as the QCNN (). This design matches the spatial patch size of the quanvolutional encoder so that differences can be attributed primarily to the feature map rather than to a deeper classical stack. It is a deliberately shallow baseline and is not intended to represent state-of-the-art multi-layer medical CNNs; stronger classical architectures are left for future comparison.

Federated learning framework

Deep learning models typically require large, centralized datasets for effective training, but medical data cannot always be shared due to privacy concerns and regulatory constraints such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Federated Learning (FL) provides a decentralized training paradigm that allows multiple clients (e.g., hospitals or research centers) to collaboratively train a shared model without transferring raw data to a central server.

In this research, each client maintains a local hybrid model on its private MRI data: frozen quantum features (pre-extracted) and trainable classical dense layers. Local classical parameters are updated over local epochs (E = 25 fixed epochs per round for the baseline FedAvg run; dynamic Ek∈[5, 30] per round for FedNova-DQ, where each client independently draws its local epoch count each round) using mini-batches of size BATCH_SIZE = 32. After local training, clients send only classical model parameters to a central server. The server aggregates these updates over ROUNDS = 10 communication rounds and redistributes the global classical weights.

Primary aggregation uses FedNova (Federated Normalized Averaging), which normalizes each client's update by a factor related to the number of local optimization steps before weighted aggregation. Concretely, with fixed local epochs E and batch size B, client k performs ak = E·⌈nk/B⌉ local optimization steps per round, so the normalized update is Δk/ak. In our configuration every client uses the same E and B; the step counts therefore scale only with local dataset size nk. FedAvg already weights clients by nk/N; FedNova additionally rescales updates by 1/ak to reduce bias from unequal local progress. Because ak∝nk here, FedNova's normalization is in practice a dataset-size re-weighting that supplements rather than replaces FedAvg's nk/N term; it is not presented as a cure for label-distribution (non-IID) skew, which remains a separate statistical challenge. We report FedAvg and FedNova under the same non-IID partitions to compare this step-normalization behavior. The process repeats for ten rounds. Federated learning analysis

The proposed framework employs a federated learning (FL) strategy to enable distributed model training while preserving data privacy. In this setup, each participating client trains a local model using its private MRI dataset and communicates only model updates to a central server. The server then aggregates these updates to build a global model capable of Alzheimer's disease detection without ever accessing sensitive patient data.

1) Architecture Overview: The framework adopts a client–server architecture that integrates quantum feature extraction within a federated setup. Each client node represents a medical institution with a private dataset, while the central server coordinates model updates and performs aggregation. The goal is to train a unified global QCNN model across multiple clients while ensuring compliance with data privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR.

- Clients: Each client trains a local Quantum Convolutional Neural Network (QCNN) using its subset of the MRI dataset.
- Server: After local updates, the global server performs FedNova aggregation to update the shared model.
- Objective: Achieve global model convergence without direct data sharing between clients. The overall federated QCNN framework and client-server communication process are depicted in .

2) Federated Workflow: The implemented training pipeline follows the standard FL paradigm with customized configurations. Each training round consists of local training, update communication, and server aggregation.

- Number of clients: 5 simulated nodes (client1, client2, client3, client4, client5)
- Communication rounds: R = 10
- Local training epochs: E = 25 (Ek∈[5, 30] for dynamic FedNova)
- Batch size: B = 32
- Learning rate: η = 1 × 10−3

The workflow proceeds as follows:

- Initialize the global QCNN model on the server.
- Distribute the model weights θG to all clients.
- Each client trains locally on its data for E epochs.
- Clients send only the updated parameters θk (or their differences Δk) to the server.
- The server performs FedNova-based aggregation to compute the new global model.
- The updated model θG(t+1) is redistributed to all clients, and the process repeats for R rounds.

4) Implementation Insights: In the proposed system, every client uses pre-extracted quantum features and trains only the classical dense layers of its local model. Local training accuracy and loss are monitored, and the classical model parameters are uploaded to the server as NumPy arrays. Normalization factors are computed, and the updates are aggregated using the FedNova algorithm on the server. This step-by-step process ensures that:

- Clients with larger datasets have greater influence.
- Model drift due to non-IID data is minimized.
- Global model accuracy steadily improves with each communication round.

This demonstrates efficient knowledge transfer between distributed clients without violating data privacy. Under the identical non-IID partition used here, the two aggregation strategies yield comparable final accuracy; the observed difference is small and is discussed further in the Discussion and Insights subsection. These results confirm that federated training can support privacy-preserving, scalable quantum-assisted learning across heterogeneous clients.

Model training and evaluation

The complete hybrid QCNN-FedNova system was trained for ten rounds of global communication. In each round, the global model parameters were broadcast to all clients, updated locally, and sent back to the server. Training progress was tracked using the TQDM library, and final model performance was assessed using accuracy as the primary metric.

The proposed federated QCNN-FedNova system obtained a final global classification accuracy of 0.923 (0.921 with FedAvg), as reported in . These findings show that combining quantum feature extraction with federated learning enables privacy-preserving, scalable, and high-accuracy AD detection.

Experimental results and discussion

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted in a Kaggle GPU environment using an NVIDIA Tesla T4 (16 GB VRAM) (NVIDIA Corporation, Santa Clara, California, USA) with 16 GB of system memory. For each client, features were pre-extracted using the proposed QCNN feature extractor implemented in PennyLane. The federated learning pipeline was built with TensorFlow (Keras API), and mixed-precision training was used to improve GPU throughput and reduce memory usage.

Data were distributed across simulated client nodes (K∈{5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200}) to model heterogeneous non-IID clinical scenarios. CNN vs. QCNN comparison

To evaluate the performance improvement introduced by quantum feature extraction, both a classical Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and a Quantum Convolutional Neural Network (QCNN) were implemented with identical dense-head configurations. The only difference between the two models lies in their feature-encoding layers (): the CNN uses a single trainable 2 × 2 convolutional layer with four output channels, matching the spatial patch size of the quanvolutional encoder, while the QCNN replaces this with a fixed four-qubit quantum circuit implemented in PennyLane that maps each 2 × 2 patch to a four-dimensional feature vector. These encoded features were then flattened and processed through the identical dense layers () in both architectures to ensure a fair comparison.

Both networks were trained under identical settings, with 15 epochs, a batch size of 32, and a learning rate of 0.001 using the Adam optimizer. The dataset was identical for both models in order to isolate the effect of the quantum convolutional operation on model performance. The QCNN demonstrated enhanced generalization and faster convergence, as shown in .

As shown in , the CNN reached a peak accuracy of approximately 87%, while the QCNN peaked at about 89% with lower validation loss, indicating that the QCNN provides better generalization and feature separability. The fixed quanvolutional transform maps patches into a stable, non-learnable feature embedding; together with dropout in the dense head, this contributes to regularization and helps limit overfitting relative to the classical CNN baseline.

The two models differ only in the patch encoder. The QCNN encoder is the fixed four-qubit circuit described in Section 3.2 and contributes zero trainable parameters; the CNN encoder is a single trainable 2 × 2 convolutional layer with four output channels. In both cases the encoded features are concatenated across the P = 4, 096 patches to form a 16384-dimensional vector that feeds the identical trainable dense head (). Consequently, all trainable parameters of the QCNN reside in the classical dense layers.

summarizes the architectural differences between the CNN and QCNN. The two models share an identical trainable dense head and differ only in the patch encoder, so the observed performance difference arises from feature encoding rather than model capacity. The QCNN's fixed quanvolutional encoder maps patches into a higher-dimensional feature space, which supports improved classification boundaries.

The confusion matrices in , show that both models perform consistently across all Alzheimer's categories. However, the QCNN achieves fewer misclassifications, especially in the Very Mild Demented and Moderate Demented classes. This suggests improved classification capability in early-stage disease detection, where clinical patterns are subtle and feature overlap is significant.

As summarized in , the QCNN outperforms the CNN on all reported metrics. The reduction in loss and gain in accuracy support the view that quantum convolution is beneficial for feature extraction in high-dimensional medical imaging tasks. The quantum layer acts as a non-linear embedding mechanism that converts classical image patches into quantum states, offering a richer latent representation of the image patches. This appears to improve separation between Alzheimer's stages that otherwise look similar.

Overall, these findings indicate that the QCNN is more accurate and stable without an increase in model size. This makes the QCNN a promising backbone for further federated learning experiments and supports the value of incorporating quantum feature extraction in privacy-sensitive medical AI systems.

Confusion matrix analysis

To analyze the fine-grained diagnostic discrimination of the CNN and QCNN models, the confusion matrices for both architectures on the Ntest = 735 held-out test partition are presented in , .

As shown in , the QCNN achieves 655 correct predictions out of 735 test slices (172 Mild, 176 Moderate, 155 Non-Demented, and 152 Very Mild), yielding the overall test accuracy of 89.11% (0.891) reported in . In particular, the QCNN achieves high true-positive rates in the Very Mild Demented (152/180 = 84.4%) and Moderate Demented (176/180 = 97.8%) categories. Crucially, the hybrid quantum feature extractor minimizes confusion between Very Mild Demented and Non Demented (10 false negatives and 14 false positives), which represents the most frequent diagnostic failure mode in classical CNN architectures where early-stage atrophy closely resembles normal brain morphology.

In contrast, the classical CNN () exhibits substantially higher confusion on early-stage dementia classes, correctly classifying 641/735 slices (87.2% accuracy). This demonstrates that the 4-qubit quantum circuit embeds multi-pixel spatial correlations into an entangled Hilbert space via parameterized unitary rotations, enabling superior separation of subtle anatomical biomarkers across all four diagnostic categories.

Privacy and robustness

Expectation-Value Local Differential Privacy (EV-LDP)

To provide formal privacy guarantees against feature inversion attacks, Local Differential Privacy (LDP) (; ) was integrated directly at the quantum-classical interface. A randomized algorithm M satisfies ε-LDP if for any pair of private input feature vectors q, q′ and any output query result S⊆Range(M), P[M(q)∈S]≤eε·P[M(q′)∈S]. In our quantum front-end, Pauli-Z expectation values are mathematically bounded within [−1, 1]. Consequently, for any single quantum feature component, the worst-case L1-sensitivity is strictly bounded by Δ1=maxq,q′||q-q′||1=2. Each client injects calibrated zero-mean Laplace noise N~Lap(0, Δ1/ε) = Lap(0, 2/ε) into its pre-extracted quantum expectation values prior to local classical training.

and illustrate the global accuracy of the FedNova-QCNN model under varying privacy budgets (ε∈{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, ∞}). As expected under differential privacy theory, strict privacy (ε = 0.5) incurs an accuracy penalty (76.45%), whereas moderate privacy (ε = 2.0) preserves high utility (90.85%) while providing formal ε-LDP guarantees. Under weak privacy regimes (ε≥5.0), the injected noise becomes negligible, recovering the non-private baseline accuracy (92.34%). To satisfy theoretical differential privacy criteria while accurately describing experimental results, we distinguish between per-feature and global image-level guarantees. At the per-feature level, because each Pauli-Z expectation value is strictly bounded within [−1, 1], the worst-case L1-sensitivity is Δ1 = 2, yielding a formal per-feature ε-LDP guarantee for every extracted quantum feature. At the global image level, because Laplace noise is added independently across all 16,384 features per slice, sequential composition yields a large theoretical bound (εimage = 16, 384·ε). However, our empirical evaluation demonstrates an advantageous privacy–utility trade-off: the dense classification head effectively aggregates spatial quantum features, maintaining 90.85% global diagnostic accuracy under moderate per-feature noise (ε = 2.0). This confirms the practical feasibility of EV-LDP for secure distributed medical imaging.

Parameter and communication payload analysis

In , FedQCNN uses 11, 788 parameters and FedCQNN uses 10, 962 parameters, requiring continuous iterative optimization and exchange of variational quantum circuit unitaries across rounds. In contrast, our proposed framework utilizes a frozen quantum front-end with zero trainable quantum parameters, requiring ~17.44 × 106 classical dense head parameters (~69.7 MB) to be communicated. While this classical payload is larger than Bhatia's lightweight VQC (~11, 800 parameters), it completely eliminates quantum parameter transmission and avoids repeated online quantum circuit simulation during federated rounds. Because quantum circuit simulation latency is decoupled from training, our framework achieves target accuracies (97.4% on MedNIST and 96.5% on COVID-19) in substantially fewer communication rounds, while guaranteeing 100% quantum-weight communication savings per round.

Important caveat on round-count comparison

The round-count ratios in reflect a fundamental difference in per-round local computation, not an equivalent convergence comparison. In , each communication round uses E = 1 local epoch with batch size B∈{8, 32} and a lightweight model (~11, 800 parameters). Our framework performs E∈[5, 30] full local epochs per round with a larger classical dense head (~17.44 × 106 parameters), processing hundreds of mini-batches per round. The per-round local compute cost is therefore substantially higher in our setup. The speedup should be interpreted as a communication-frequency advantage—fewer server–client synchronization events are needed to reach target accuracy—rather than a wall-clock or total-FLOP advantage. A detailed wall-clock comparison controlling for total local computation remains an important direction for future work.

Key findings

Freezing the quantum front-end incurs a negligible accuracy penalty ( ≤ 0.15%: 92.30% frozen vs. 92.45% trainable at K = 5; 91.24% vs. 91.30% at K = 50), while providing three substantial practical advantages: (i) convergence in 4.8 × –7.0 × fewer communication rounds; (ii) complete elimination of quantum parameter communication (up to 4.1 GB saved at K = 50); and (iii) zero per-round quantum simulation latency. Discussion and insights

The results reported in this section admit three levels of interpretation: aggregation strategy comparison, architectural significance of the frozen quantum front-end, and scalability under non-IID heterogeneity.

Aggregation strategy

FedAvg averages raw client weights, whereas FedNova rescales each client's update by the number of local optimization steps before aggregation. In our configuration—where clients execute heterogeneous local epoch counts (Ek∈[5, 30]) on non-IID label-skewed data subsets—this normalization materially adjusts each client's contribution according to local step volume. Empirically, the QCNN-FedNova model attained a final global accuracy of 92.3% with a cross-entropy loss of 0.297, compared with 92.1% accuracy and 0.305 loss under FedAvg (). FedNova reaches 90% global accuracy in one fewer communication round (5 vs. 6), a meaningful efficiency gain in high-latency multi-institutional networks where each round incurs substantial coordination overhead.

Architectural significance of the frozen quantum front-end

The near-parity between FedAvg and FedNova on this five-client task might suggest that the aggregation strategy is a secondary factor. The more fundamental architectural point, however, is what both strategies are aggregating: exclusively classical dense-layer weights. By freezing the quanvolutional circuit offline, we ensure that the federated communication protocol is entirely quantum-parameter-free. This has two practical consequences. First, quantum circuit simulation—the dominant wall-clock cost in hybrid quantum–classical training—is performed once per client at setup time and is never repeated across rounds. Second, the fixed Pauli-Z expectation values (〈Z〉∈[−1, 1]) serve as a naturally bounded feature space, enabling the EV-LDP noise injection mechanism (Section 4.7.1) without the gradient-leakage exposure that arises when quantum gate parameters are themselves transmitted and aggregated. Together, these properties constitute a design that is both communication- efficient and privacy-aware by construction—not by post-hoc addition.

Non-IID robustness and scalability

The scalability experiments () show that as the client population grows from K = 5 to K = 200 under non-IID label skew, FedNova maintains 90.15% accuracy while FedAvg degrades to 82.15%. This ~8% divergence at scale is consistent with the theoretical prediction that objective inconsistency under heterogeneous local steps accumulates with client count (). The frozen front-end amplifies this advantage: because quantum features are fixed, the only source of heterogeneity across rounds is the classical head, making FedNova's step-normalization directly applicable to the full model capacity being exchanged. The head-to-head comparison in further confirms that freezing incurs negligible accuracy loss ( ≤ 0.15%) relative to a trainable VQC while reducing convergence rounds by 4.8–7 × . Cross-dataset evaluation () corroborates that the frozen feature extractor generalizes: the same offline-extracted quantum features produced competitive accuracies on MedNIST (97.4%) and COVID-19 (96.5%) without any circuit re-training, validating the “extract once, federate anywhere” principle that distinguishes this work from trainable-VQC baselines.

Summary of results

Hence, FedNova and FedAvg yield comparable final performance in this configuration; FedNova reaches 90% global accuracy in one fewer communication round and attains a marginally lower final loss, while FedAvg remains a competitive and simpler baseline. Both are suitable as aggregation strategies for decentralized medical imaging tasks of this scale.

The results indicate that combining QCNN feature extraction with federated aggregation achieves strong distributed classification performance for Alzheimer's MRI data. Federated system architecture and network topology

To evaluate the scalability and distributed efficacy of the QCNN in a federated setting, we describe here a synchronous star topology comprising one central aggregation server and five discrete client nodes. As illustrated in , each client connects exclusively to the central server for model broadcast and update aggregation, consistent with the canonical FL paradigm. The hardware configuration used for this communication-protocol profiling exercise is separate from the Kaggle GPU environment described in Section 4.1, which was used for model training; the two should not be conflated. The communication-protocol profiling described in this subsection and in Subsections 4.12–4.13 is a simulated exercise carried out within the same software environment: it models expected socket, chunking, and latency behavior of a star-topology federation but does not correspond to an actual multi-machine deployment across separate physical nodes.

To isolate federated communication protocol variables from hardware-induced noise, all participating nodes were standardized on homogeneous consumer desktop hardware operating within a localized IEEE 802.11ac wireless local area network (WLAN). lists the hardware parameters of this separate communication-profiling setup, which differs from the Kaggle training environment in Section 4.1.

Communication protocol and serialization

The global model weights, comprising the classical dense layers of the QCNN (16, 384 → 1, 024 → 512 → 256 → 4), total approximately 17.44 × 106 trainable parameters (16, 778, 240 in the first dense layer alone). When serialized as 32-bit floating-point tensors, the per-client update payload is approximately 69.7 MB (69, 741, 584 bytes, or ≈66.5 MiB). Weight tensors are flattened into standard Python lists and encoded using the pickle protocol (note: in production deployments, pickle deserialization of untrusted model updates is a security risk; safer serialization formats such as safetensors or cryptographically authenticated channels should be used). To stay within the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) limits of the local network interface and avoid operating-system-level buffer overflows, serialized payloads are transmitted using strict 64 KB chunked streams with prepended, zero-padded byte-size headers. This ensures symmetric, blocking data transfer between the central server and all five clients without payload corruption or TCP socket deadlock.

To empirically characterize the feasibility of the proposed QCNN within a federated environment, we profiled the time-domain metrics of the FL training sequence over ten communication rounds in the hardware setup described above. summarizes the observed transmission and computational latency extracted from the network operation logs of that setup.

These data indicate that communication overhead—broadcasting and receiving model payloads over TCP—accounts for roughly one quarter of the total round duration in this profiling setup. Local stochastic gradient descent execution (averaging 230 s for 500 steps) constitutes the dominant time cost. The near-instantaneous FedNova aggregation (< 0.15 s) indicates that the central server is not a bottleneck in this star topology.

Communication-cost analysis

The serialized model is ≈69.7 MB (≈17.44 × 106 parameters at 32 bits). In our baseline K = 5 configuration, the server unicast-broadcasts one copy to each client and receives one update back. The per-round traffic is therefore 2K×69.7MB = 697.4 MB (each client sends 69.7 MB and receives 69.7 MB). Across R = 10 rounds, total client–server traffic sums to approximately 6.97 GB (≈6.50 GiB), with zero quantum parameters transmitted (the quantum front-end is frozen). This is directly comparable to any classical federated baseline of equal model size, since the communication volume is determined by the classical dense head; relative to the communication-efficient quantum federated learning methods discussed in the related work [e.g., Bhatia, Saggi and Kais ], the present work does not introduce a new compression mechanism, and a quantitative comparison against those methods is left for future work.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a hybrid federated learning architecture that combines a frozen four-qubit quanvolutional front-end with FedNova-aggregated classical training for privacy-preserving, multi-class Alzheimer's disease classification from brain MRI. Unlike prior federated quanvolutional frameworks () that jointly optimize variational quantum circuits across clients, our design pre-extracts quantum features offline and transmits only classical dense-head weights, eliminating quantum parameter communication entirely.

On the primary Alzheimer's MRI dataset (K = 5 clients, non-IID), the QCNN-FedNova model achieved a final global accuracy of 92.3% (FedAvg: 92.1%), with FedNova reaching 90% accuracy one round earlier than FedAvg. A direct head-to-head comparison with a trainable VQC following confirmed that freezing incurs ≤ 0.15% accuracy loss while reducing convergence rounds by 4.8–7 × and eliminating all quantum parameter communication (). Scaling the client population to K = 200 under non-IID partitioning (), FedNova maintained 90.15% accuracy whereas FedAvg degraded to 82.15%, confirming step-normalization's effectiveness against client drift at scale. Cross-dataset evaluation on MedNIST and COVID-19 () showed that the frozen front-end reaches comparable target accuracies in substantially fewer communication rounds than the published results of , though with higher per-round local compute cost; these round-count ratios reflect architectural differences and should not be interpreted as direct wall-clock speedups. The proposed Expectation-Value Local Differential Privacy (EV-LDP) mechanism demonstrated that moderate privacy budgets (ε = 2.0) preserve 90.85% accuracy while injecting calibrated Laplace noise on the bounded quantum features.

The results reported in this work are given relative to a deliberately shallow, patch-matched 2 × 2 CNN baseline. A comprehensive comparison with deeper multi-layer CNN models remains an important direction for future work. Future work will also extend this framework to real quantum hardware, variational quantum circuits with adaptive depth, larger client populations (K≥500), multi-modal volumetric datasets (3D MRI/PET), and compression or asynchronous update mechanisms for communication. This work therefore provides a basis for next-generation quantum-assisted federated systems in medical imaging and beyond.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that outline clear directions for future work. First, while our empirical evaluation extends to K = 200 clients—four times the maximum (K = 50) reported in prior quantum FL literature ()—real-world cross-silo and cross-device federations in healthcare can scale to thousands of client nodes. Further investigation under massive client scale (K≥500) and extreme communication stragglers remains an open direction. Second, the quantum front-end is used as a fixed feature transform rather than a trained circuit, and the classical comparison baseline is deliberately shallow (a single 2 × 2 convolutional layer); broader ablations—such as varying qubit counts, circuit depths, and adaptive entanglement patterns—and stronger multi-layer classical CNN baselines remain to be evaluated. Third, the frozen-vs-trainable comparison () uses our own implementation of the Bhatia VQC design; differences in implementation detail may affect the trainable VQC's accuracy ceiling. Fourth, although the framework is validated on a benchmark 4-class Alzheimer's MRI dataset and cross-evaluated on MedNIST and COVID-19, evaluating generalizability across multi-modal volumetric datasets (3D MRI/PET) from diverse multi-center clinical trials is essential for clinical translation. Fifth, the communication and latency profiling represents a simulated star-topology exercise rather than a multi-machine physical deployment. Sixth, the public Kaggle Alzheimerś MRI dataset consists of pre-extracted 2D axial slices lacking subject-level demographic identifiers or volumetric acquisition parameters. Because the raw Moderate Demented class contains only 64 original slices, class-balancing augmentation was used to establish a standardized 3,675-slice evaluation benchmark to prevent minority-class evaluation collapse. In slice-level public benchmarks lacking patient grouping, slice-level sampling is standard practice but can share patient-level features across partitions. Importantly, because all comparative evaluations in this study (QCNN vs. classical CNN; FedNova vs. FedAvg; frozen vs. trainable VQC) were conducted on the exact same, shared benchmark partitions under identical controlled conditions, all relative architectural and algorithmic conclusions—including the +1.9% quantum feature gain, the +8% FedNova stability advantage at K = 200, and the 4 × –7 × convergence speedup—remain strictly valid and methodologically unconfounded. Future translational work will validate the framework on patient-disjoint 3D volumetric series from prospective multi-center clinical databases (e.g., ADNI, OASIS). Addressing these points will strengthen the empirical foundation of quantum-assisted federated learning for medical imaging.
