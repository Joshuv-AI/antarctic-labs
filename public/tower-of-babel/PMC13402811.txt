Stabilizing–sensing synergistic geogrid for high-speed railways
Authors: Haoran Fu, Haoyu Zhang, Liuyang Xiao, Wenhao Zhan, Jianqun Jiang, Yunmin Chen, Xuecheng Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-speed railway systems demand robust substructure performance and real-time monitoring to ensure operational safety and efficiency. To meet these challenges, we introduce the stabilizing–sensing synergistic geogrid (SMART geogrid), which employs geogrids as soil-stabilizing materials and integrates Fiber Bragg Grating sensors within the geogrid to detect soil variations. Extensive theoretical, numerical, and experimental studies have been conducted on the coupling characteristics between the optical fiber, adhesive, and geogrid in the SMART geogrid. These studies indicate that the SMART geogrid demonstrates superior reliability and testing accuracy. Pull-out and compression tests validate its real-time monitoring capabilities for shear resistance and compressive strain of soil, as well as its ability to enhance soil bearing capacity. The application in full-scale railway tests, enduring 2 million cycles of loading, verifies the SMART geogrid’s effectiveness in full life cycle monitoring. Moreover, a Foreign Object Intrusion Detection System built upon the SMART geogrid, combined with machine learning algorithms, achieves a position prediction R² of 0.9999 and 100% classification accuracy, highlighting its potential for intelligent trackbed sensing in high-speed railway applications.

Introduction

Railways are vital infrastructure that connect cities and boost economic growth1,2. The railway substructure, comprising the trackbed and the subgrade, bears the gravity of the tracks and the dynamic load of trains, ensuring safe and efficient train operations3–5. High-speed railways, in particular, are subjected to significantly greater dynamic loads than conventional railway systems, intensifying stress on the substructure6,7. Increased train speeds exacerbate lateral ballast movement, leading to trackbed fluidization and accumulative trackbed settlement, which jeopardize operational safety8,9. Traditional maintenance methods, such as ballast replacement and tamping10,11, are labor-intensive, equipment-dependent, and costly12. Although geogrid stabilization has demonstrated effectiveness in controlling trackbed settlement13–15, current research is limited to laboratory studies, with minimal validation from field applications. As a result, railway infrastructure maintenance incurs substantial costs, with global expenditures reaching approximately USD 75.59 billion in 2024 and projected to rise to USD 95.56 billion by 2033, indicating a sustained financial burden with a compound annual growth rate of 2.6%16. Additionally, the high-frequency service and harsh environmental conditions of high-speed railways result in short maintenance cycles17–19. Consequently, trackbed issues often remain undetected until safety is compromised, triggering emergency repairs that disrupt operations and risk substantial economic and human losses. Therefore, improving the stabilization and real-time monitoring capabilities of railway roadbeds during operation is essential for disaster prevention and cost-effective maintenance.

Current railway substructure deformation monitoring technologies are divided into non-contact and contact methods, depending on their interaction with the soil20. Non-contact methods include remote sensing technologies like the global navigation satellite system (GNSS), Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), and interferometric synthetic aperture radar (INSAR)21–25. Although INSAR and similar techniques can detect large-scale surface-level deformations of the railway trackbed, they do not provide insights into subsurface deformations. These methods are also affected by environmental conditions, lack real-time all-weather measurement capabilities, and require improvements in accuracy. In contrast, contact methods, such as inclinometers, settlement gauge, and strain gauges based on resistance or vibrating strings, enable the measurement of subsurface soil deformation but are prone to electromagnetic interference, rust, corrosion, and possess limited durability26,27. These sensors are typically point-specific, making the monitoring of extensive areas of railway substructure soils prohibitively expensive in terms of human and material resources, thus limiting their suitability for large-scale, long-distance, and deep soil monitoring28. Additionally, conventional geotechnical sensors are often bulky and may exhibit stiffness mismatches with the surrounding soil or ballast, affecting measurement accuracy29.

The development of optical fiber sensors marks a significant advancement in this field30–32. These sensors are known for their compact size, strong anti-interference capabilities, and high measurement accuracy. The application of geogrids in traditional geotechnical engineering is well-established33–35. However, their integrated use for stabilization-monitoring within soil represents a functional leap from passive stabilization materials to active sensing elements. Consequently, optical fiber sensors have become a pivotal technology in the development of geogrids integrated with sensing capabilities for infrastructure monitoring. Prior research has explored integrating various optical fiber sensors with geogrids-particularly in slope36,37 and ground deformation monitoring-primarily through surface attachment. These geogrids aim to provide distributed monitoring of deformation within soil structures.

However, despite their advantages, optical fiber sensors remain susceptible to damage under the complex conditions of geotechnical environments and harsh weather. This vulnerability highlights the need for careful installation and protective measures to ensure their longevity and effectiveness. To address this, researchers have developed protection methods like cable encapsulation38 and 3D printing encapsulation39 to shield optical fibers from environmental damage. However, these protective measures can significantly increase the volume of the buried sensors, creating a separation layer between the roadbed being measured and the sensor’s actual measurement point, which can compromise measurement accuracy40,41.

To overcome these challenges, we propose the stabilizing–sensing synergistic geogrid (SMART geogrid). This work focuses on the development and experimental validation of an embedded integration process that couples optical fibers with geogrids. The system employs additive manufacturing, high-precision grooving, and light-curing techniques to embed optical fiber sensors within the geogrid ribs, forming an integrated sensing network for high-speed railway trackbeds. Compared with surface-mounted configurations, this embedded design places the optical fibers inside the geogrid ribs, improving mechanical protection during installation and long-term service. This configuration also improves strain coordination and transfer between the optical fiber and the geogrid, thereby supporting real-time monitoring of the railway substructure. Additionally, the application of geogrids restricts the movement of discrete particles within the substructure, thereby preventing soil fluidization and reducing cumulative settlement. The results compare SMART geogrids with traditional geogrids using surface-mounted optical fibers. Through cyclic loading tests up to 100,000 cycles, the embedded sensing configuration showed lower wavelength drift and more stable signal response. Pull-out tests reveal the interfacial friction properties between the geogrid and the soil, while compression tests provide insights into real-time monitoring of soil strain under various conditions and the reinforcing effects of SMART geogrids. A full-scale railway test further monitors trackbed responses under extensive cyclic loads. In addition, a Foreign Object Intrusion Detection System developed based on the SMART geogrid and machine learning algorithms achieves an R² of 0.9999 for object position prediction and 100% accuracy in area classification. The SMART geogrid integrates trackbed stabilization with distributed sensing, providing a basis for railway infrastructure monitoring and risk mitigation.

Results

Stabilizing–sensing synergy

Just as human biological systems protect vital organs while sensing external changes to trigger appropriate responses, our stabilizing–sensing synergy technique employs geogrids for soil stabilization and integrates an optical fiber sensing network within the geogrid (Fig. 1a). This configuration combines soil stabilization by geogrids with an embedded optical fiber sensing network within the geogrid. It improves trackbed mechanical performance while enabling real-time condition monitoring and supporting targeted maintenance. SMART geogrid design and fabrication

Figure 1c depicts the detailed fabrication process of the SMART geogrid. Initially, the geogrid with grooves is created either through Stereo Lithography Apparatus (SLA) 3D printing using Union Black, a photosensitive resin, or via high-precision grooving on commercial geogrid. In this study, the geogrid ribs are 6 mm wide and 2.5 mm thick, featuring grooves sized at 1 mm×1 mm. Optical fibers with fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors are then positioned within these grooves, and both ends of the fibers are pre-tensioned to ensure proper alignment and tension. The grooves are subsequently filled with ultraviolet (UV) adhesive to securely encapsulate the optical fibers, which is then cured under ultraviolet light to solidify the adhesive. This process not only secures the fibers within the geogrid but also leverages the corrosion resistance and mechanical properties of the geogrid to protect the embedded optical fibers from external damage and interference. Figure 1d provides a visual demonstration of the SMART geogrid and its components, showcasing the integration of technology and materials.

The SMART geogrid achieves a unity transfer coefficient more rapidly along its length, demonstrating a higher average strain transfer coefficient than the surface-mounted setup. The embedded optical fiber’s average strain transfer coefficient (0.93842) exceeds that of the surface-mounted fiber (0.89496), suggesting that the embedded configuration enhances accurate strain sensing by closely integrating the fiber within the geogrid. This integration reduces strain discrepancies compared to the surface-mounted fibers, which are more exposed and have weaker coupling with the geogrid, resulting in a reduced strain transfer effect. Under long-term cyclic loading, although adhesive degradation may affect both configurations, the embedded design maintains cooperative deformation and a more uniform cross-sectional strain response, leading to more reliable strain measurements.

Consequently, shorter lengths, such as 20 mm or 10 mm, are often preferred. As the adhesive length decreases, the strain transfer coefficient near the edges of the geogrid diminishes. This demonstrates that variations in adhesive length critically affect the strain transfer coefficient in the surface-mounted method. In contrast, the SMART geogrid, with its pre-fabricated grooves for positioning optical fibers, effectively addresses this challenge. It reveals that the transfer coefficient increases with the rising modulus of the adhesives.

Transfer coefficient for SMART geogrid in bending

When buried in the soil, the geogrid not only undergoes tensile deformation but also experiences out-of-plane bending due to vertical traffic loading and out-plane bending from soil particle extrusion within the mesh48. Both were modeled as a single rib (50 mm in length) subjected to a bending angle of 2.86°, with all other geometric and material parameters consistent with the tensile model. As shown in Fig. 2c, the surface-mounted configuration exhibits a higher transfer coefficient (1.63) because the optical fiber is located far from the neutral axis and experiences amplified deformation during bending. In contrast, the optical fiber embedded in the SMART geogrid is positioned closer to the neutral axis, where bending-induced deformation is smaller, resulting in a lower transfer coefficient (0.68). Therefore, embedded configuration prevents premature fiber fracture and enabling monitoring over a longer portion of the geogrid’s service life. Supplementary Fig. 5 displays the out-plane bending transfer coefficient of the SMART geogrid when encapsulated with different adhesives. The uniform segments of the transfer coefficient curves for these three adhesives are very close.

The extrusion of soil particles into geogrid apertures induces both mesh expansion and in-plane rib bending. To investigate this interaction mechanism, two complementary numerical biaxal geogrid models were developed (Fig. 2d): a single-particle extrusion model and a full-mesh particle extrusion model (for modeling details, please refer to Supplementary Note 3). Both models simulate particle-mesh interactions, with the critical study region (highlighted by a red frame) focusing on the deformation characteristics of representative geogrid ribs and optical fibers.

Supplementary Fig. 6 presents the geogrid deformation under single-particle extrusion, along with strain evolution at two critical points as a function of extrusion displacement. It is observed that when the extrusion displacement approaches ~1.5 mm, the strain at Point 1 reaches the 3D print geogrid’s fracture threshold. In Fig. 2e, the deformed optical fiber is displayed, and the strain responses at corresponding locations are analyzed with respect to extrusion displacement. Additionally, using mechanical parameters reported in the literature49, a single-particle extrusion test was performed on the polypropylene (PP) geogrid, with results presented in Supplementary Fig. 7. The results indicate that within the particle-extrusion-dominated deformation range relevant to ballast interaction, the geogrid reaches its failure threshold before the embedded optical fiber attains its fracture strain (6.1%50). Figure 2f further depicts the strain distribution at locations susceptible to optical fiber fracture, offering insights into potential failure zones.

Supplementary Fig. 8 illustrates the strain along the optical fiber and the SMART geogrid rib under the full-mesh particle extrusion model, as well as the transfer coefficient between the two under mesh expansion. In the middle of the geogrid rib, due to soil particle extrusion, both the rib and optical fiber exhibit significant strain, with their trends being similar. The transfer coefficient curve shape is analogous to that seen in tension, increasing from both ends towards the middle, where it approaches 1.

Figure 2g compares the transfer coefficients of geogrids with surface-mounted and embedded optical fibers. Since the optical fiber and adhesive in surface-mounted configurations extend beyond the section, the surface-mounted fiber cannot deform in coordination with the geogrid rib. Consequently, the strain in the squeezed part (the middle position of the geogrid) cannot be accurately measured. Therefore, SMART geogrids have a significant advantage in accurately capturing strain under such conditions. Additionally, the UV adhesive exhibits a transfer coefficient comparable to that of epoxy resin.

Durability of SMART geogrid under cyclic loading test

The encapsulation of FBG sensors can compromise the strength of the geogrid, potentially reducing the durability of SMART geogrids under repeated high-speed train operations in railway engineering. Consequently, it is crucial to select adhesives that not only optimize the transfer coefficient but also enhance the long-term monitoring stability of FBG sensors under cyclic loads. To this end, we conducted cyclic loading tests on SMART geogrids and surface-mounted optical fiber geogrids for up to 100,000 cycles. The schematic in Fig. 3a depicts the configuration for applying bending load, employing a geogrid rib model with a length of 100 mm. The test procedure followed the methodology detailed in the Methods. Supplementary Fig. 9(b) shows the results for surface-mounted optical fiber geogrids using epoxy resin and UV adhesive. Notably, the geogrid encapsulated with epoxy resin failed after approximately 50,000 cycles, while the UV adhesive-encapsulated geogrid sustained 100,000 cycles without failure. Moreover, both geogrids exhibited significant wavelength fluctuations under cyclic loading due to the considerable strain on surface-mounted optical fibers during bending deformation, leading to misalignment between the fibers and the adhesive as well as cracking of the adhesive. Figures S9(c) demonstrates the wavelength variations in embedded optical fibers encapsulated with epoxy resin, UV adhesive, and AB adhesive. The wavelength drift for all samples showed an increasing trend with the number of cycles, particularly pronounced within the first 20,000 cycles as shown in Fig. 3a. After 20,000 cycles, the wavelength variation of embedded fibers encapsulated with epoxy resin and UV adhesive stabilized. This comparative analysis indicates that embedded optical fibers offer superior stability compared to surface-mounted ones. After 100,000 loading cycles, the wavelength drift in the surface-mounted fibers exceeded that of the embedded fibers by a factor of 13. The greater wavelength drift in surface-mounted fibers is attributed to their distance from the neutral axis of the geogrid and the lower mechanical properties of the encapsulation materials, which are more susceptible to cracking damage. This damage compromises the integration of the geogrid and the optical fiber. In contrast, in the SMART geogrid, the optical fiber is embedded within the geogrid’s interior and tightly integrated with it. The geogrid absorbs most external forces and distributes them evenly to the embedded optical fiber, thus providing protection and enhancing the overall durability of the system. Supplementary Fig. 10 illustrates the strain distribution across the optical fiber cross-section under compressive loading for the two installation configurations, based on a single 200 mm geogrid rib. The model is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 10(a). In this model, the surrounding soil is represented as an equivalent continuum to provide macroscopic confinement and compressive boundary conditions, rather than to resolve discrete ballast particle interactions. To validate this assumption, experimental compression tests with identical geometry were conducted, and the results show good agreement with the numerical predictions (Supplementary Fig. 11). When subjected to the same external load (50 kPa), the surface-mounted optical fiber (Supplementary Fig. 10(b)) exhibits a larger strain than the embedded fiber (Supplementary Fig. 10(c)). This difference arises from their distinct mechanical constraints: the surface-mounted fiber is primarily protected by the adhesive layer, which has a much lower elastic modulus than the geogrid and therefore allows greater local deformation. In contrast, the embedded optical fiber is mechanically constrained by both the adhesive and the surrounding geogrid material, whose higher stiffness effectively suppresses fiber deformation, resulting in a smaller measured strain. Besides of the larger strain, surface-mounted FBG sensors, exposed to external environmental conditions, may undergo tension, compression, or torsion, leading to wavelength drifts that can impact the accuracy and stability of the sensors. Figure S10(c) and S10(d) (e) shows that the strain variation under identical loading conditions is very similar for optical fibers encapsulated by UV adhesive and epoxy resin.

In Fig. 3a, among the encapsulation materials tested, UV adhesive and epoxy resin exhibited similar wavelength drifts, with UV adhesive showing slightly less drift. Moreover, comparative analysis of these findings underscores UV adhesive as the most suitable encapsulation material.

Beyond advantages in durability, strain homogeneity, and transfer coefficient, UV adhesive also excels in the fabrication process. It can completely fill the narrow grooves of the SMART geogrid. In contrast, due to the higher viscosity of epoxy and AB adhesives, filling such narrow gaps often results in the formation of bubbles that impair the bonding between the optical fiber and the geogrid, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 12. Additionally, UV adhesive cures within seconds under ultraviolet light exposure, whereas epoxy resin typically requires hours or days, making UV adhesive more practical for rapid applications.

Calibration of FBG sensors under diverse temperature conditions demonstrated a strong linear relationship between wavelength and temperature, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, with a calibration coefficient of 0.084. Similarly, axial force calibration of the SMART geogrid (Fig. 3b) demonstrated a highly linear and repeatable response (R2 > 0.99), with a calibration coefficient of 30.27, indicating stable proportional sensitivity to axial force. A uniaxial tensile test conducted on a single rib of the SMART geogrid indicates that the maximum reliable strain measurement reaches ~1.17% (Supplementary Fig. 14).

This wavelength stability is essential for long-term monitoring, as it preserves a consistent measurement baseline and prevents non-physical signal drift under cyclic loading, thereby ensuring the reliability and repeatability of strain data over extended service periods. The inset in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 15 illustrate the schematic of a bearing plate compression test configuration and the layout of FBG sensor integration within the SMART geogrid, using a triaxial geogrid. Figure 3c shows the strain response at measurement point 1 under increasing compressive loads from 0 to 400 kPa, while the strain responses at other measurement points are presented in Supplementary Fig. 16. Under constant loading conditions, most FBG sensors exhibited stable strain signals, indicating the overall sensing reliability of the SMART geogrid. A gradual decrease in strain was observed for FBG sensor 6, which is likely associated with local bonding degradation or slight interfacial sliding between the sensor and the geogrid under sustained load. This localized behavior does not affect the global strain monitoring results but underscores the importance of robust sensor-geogrid bonding for long-term measurement stability. Supplementary Fig. 17 presents the strain versus applied normal stress for all FBG sensors. The curves exhibit clear nonlinearity, reflecting the nonlinear constitutive behavior of the granular materials. Variations between sensors arise from the heterogeneous distribution of force chains and from the inherent spatial non‑uniformity of the stress field due to boundary conditions and particle packing.

To assess the potential impact of grooving on the mechanical performance of geogrid ribs, tensile tests were conducted on individual ribs. Although the introduction of grooves locally reduced the cross-sectional area by approximately 6.67%, the ultimate tensile strength decreased by only 6.98% (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating a limited effect on load-bearing capacity. In contrast, the grooving design substantially improves the mechanical protection and long-term stability of the embedded optical fiber. Comparative fatigue and durability tests show that, relative to surface-mounted configurations, the grooved design markedly reduces fiber damage and signal degradation under cyclic loading. Overall, the grooving strategy enables reliable sensor integration with only a minor and controllable reduction in mechanical performance, representing a reasonable engineering trade-off.

Strain monitoring in compressive test

To assess the stabilization effect of SMART geogrids and their deformation during soil compression, a series of tests were conducted on various geogrid types under strip footing loading. These included configurations without geogrids, as well as biaxial, triaxial, and double-layer triaxial geogrids.

Figures 3d and S22 depict the detailed specifics of the strain monitoring experiment setup, while Supplementary Fig. 23 presents the two types of geogrids used in the compression tests: biaxial and triaxial geogrids. Drawing from prior research on model dimension optimization55,56, the compression model was established with dimensions of 300 mm × 300 mm × 280 mm, accompanied by a foot strip width of 30 mm. With width-to-footing and height-to-footing ratios of 10 and 9.33, respectively, both meeting or exceeding commonly accepted thresholds of 10 and 6 for minimizing boundary effects57,58, the influence of boundary constraints in the present model test is considered negligible. The experimental setup and procedure followed the compressive test section in the Methods.

Figure 4e shows the pressure-settlement curves for the various foundations. Initially, all foundations exhibited an increase in bearing capacity with displacement until reaching a peak, followed by a decline due to soil shear failure and plastic deformation, which reduced strength and stiffness. The stabilized foundations exhibited significantly higher stiffness and maximum bearing capacity compared to the original foundation. Under the same applied pressure, the triaxial geogrid exhibited a smaller measured settlement than the biaxial geogrid in this model test. To enable a controlled comparison focusing on geometric effects, both geogrids were fabricated from the same material using identical 3D printing processes, and their total volumes were kept comparable (60,160.65 mm3 for the triaxial and 56,118.98 mm3 for the biaxial geogrid, with a difference of ~7%). Adding a second geogrid layer further increased the foundation’s ability to withstand higher pressures, confirming experimental reliability aligned with the literature59.

Supplementary Fig. 24(a) depicts the variation in geogrid strain during the loading process in a single-layer geogrid-stabilized foundation. As the soil settles, the strain in the SMART geogrid initially changes minimally but follows the trend of the pressure-settlement curves in Fig. 4e, increasing before decreasing. In the double-layer geogrid scenario, Supplementary Fig. 24(b) exhibits a similar trend, with the strain in the upper layer initially increasing before stabilizing.

Due to the fact that the FBG sensor test results are symmetrically distributed, an interpolation method is used to generate the strain contour map for the entire plane, as shown for the triaxial geogrid in Fig. 4f and g. The strain contour maps are generated by interpolating discrete strain measurements obtained from FBG sensors embedded along the geogrid ribs. The actual sensor locations are explicitly indicated in the figures, while the remaining regions are filled via interpolation to visualize the overall strain distribution. Both figures demonstrate the distributed measurement capability of SMART geogrids, presenting the 2D strain distribution at maximum displacement for both single-layer and double-layer geogrid-stabilized foundations. The results reveal compressive strain beneath the strip footing and tensile strain in the regions outside the footing. This is attributed to the bending deformation of the geogrid rib (Supplementary Fig. 25) and the offset of the FBG from the geogrid’s neutral plane, while other locations show tensile strain due to the surrounding soil’s constraint. The Y-shaped strain pattern observed across the geogrid plane is attributed to boundary effects. While strong shear confinement localizes deformation in the central region, the reduced confinement at the edges induces lateral stress diffusion, resulting in the observed divergence.

Notably, the strain in the upper geogrid layer of the double-layer configuration is significantly greater than in the single-layer system, as confirmed by numerical simulations conducted under plane strain conditions. The simulations, which modeled the soil-geogrid composite under fixed boundaries, support the observed strain amplification in the upper layer of the reinforced system.

Figure 4h presents numerical results of strain distribution along the geogrid from the center to the end under a 2 mm strip footing displacement. The experimental results show good agreement with the numerical results, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, validating the reliability of the model. In most locations, both tensile and compressive strains in the upper layer of the double-layer geogrid exceed those in the single-layer. This is due to the lower geogrid constraining the deformation of the upper layer and the surrounding soil, enhancing the interaction between the upper geogrid and the soil, thereby increasing the strain transfer effect between soil and geogrid. Supplementary Fig. 27 additionally compares three scenarios: unreinforced soil, single-layer geogrid stabilization, and double-layer geogrid stabilization. The inclusion of geogrids significantly alters the stress-strain distribution within the soil. Specifically, the presence of a lower geogrid constrains deformation of the overlying soil and upper geogrid, leading to stress redistribution and an overall improvement in stabilization performance.

Figure 5d and Supplementary Fig. 37 reveal the strain monitoring results from each measurement point in SMART geogrids 1 and 2 after enduring 2 million axle load cycles. SMART geogrid 1, at 17t and 100 km/h, displayed minimal strain, while higher axle loads and speeds (17t at 360 km/h and 25t at 100 km/h) significantly increased the cumulative deformation of the subgrade. Notably, positions 1 and 3 on the FBG sensors exhibited substantial cumulative strains, identifying potential failure points under high-speed rail operations. This significant strain in SMART geogrid 1 reflects strong and effective interlock at the ballast–subballast interface, where the geogrid is mobilized to restrain shear stresses and lateral deformation induced by stiffness contrast under cyclic loading. In contrast, SMART geogrid 2, embedded within uniformly confined ballast, experiences lower strain due to greater lateral restraint.

The lower strain observed in Group 2 compared with Group 3 is attributed to deformation compatibility beneath the sleeper. Group 2 undergoes largely compatible deformation of ballast, geogrid, and sub-ballast under strong confinement, whereas Group 3, located near the track edge, experiences greater differential deformation and lateral strain demand, resulting in higher tensile strain in the geogrid.

As shown in the figure, the cumulative strain in the geogrid located in the inter-sleeper zones (measurement point groups 1–3) is significantly higher than that beneath the sleepers (groups 6–8), while the strain in the zones outside the sleeper ends (groups 4,5,9,10) is generally the lowest. This difference primarily stems from the deformation compatibility at different locations: beneath the sleepers, the ballast and the geogrid undergo relatively synchronous compressive settlement under train loading, which restrains tensile deformation of the geogrid. In contrast, the inter-sleeper zones lack sufficient underlying support, making them more prone to induce tensile deformation in the geogrid and thus resulting in greater cumulative strain. Outside the sleeper ends, the area is farther from the main loading zone; consequently, the strain level is correspondingly lower.

The distributed strain captured by the SMART geogrid provides significant engineering value by establishing a direct link between its internal mechanical response and the macroscopic performance of the foundation. From a mechanistic perspective, tensile strain in the geogrid ribs is primarily induced by lateral extrusion of ballast particles and vertical displacement of the soil. Consequently, areas exhibiting high cumulative strain rates physically correspond to zones of lost lateral confinement or intensified local settlement54. Furthermore, the differential strain accumulation rate between adjacent ribs serves as a key early-warning indicator. Pronounced strain gradients highlighted by this metric reveal severe uneven deformation or stress concentration, enabling a shift from passive structural health monitoring to proactive condition-based maintenance. This facilitates the precise identification and remediation of internal weak zones before they evolve into differential settlement or operational risks.

Foreign object intrusion detection system based on SMART geogrid

Given the potential threat posed by foreign objects—such as fallen rocks and construction debris—to the safety of road and railway infrastructure, particularly in mountainous regions, we developed a Foreign Object Intrusion Detection System (FIDS) based on the SMART geogrid. This system employs machine learning algorithms to identify both the position and the occupied area of foreign objects, including rocks and construction or demolition waste.

The experimental device and algorithm are outlined in the Methods and Supplementary note 5. The FIDS configuration is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 38, featuring a total of 36 FBG sensors embedded within the biaxial SMART geogrid, which is buried 55 mm below the soil surface. Fine sand was used in the impact localization experiments to ensure controllable and repeatable mechanical signals during the initial training of the machine learning model. The number of trials for position identification (173 trials) and occupied-area identification (657 trials) was determined by balancing experimental cost and data acquisition efficiency. Position identification required repeated impact tests at different spatial locations and therefore relied on a smaller number of raw experiments, complemented by data augmentation. In contrast, occupied-area identification could be efficiently conducted at a fixed location, allowing a larger set of raw data to be collected without augmentation. Detailed descriptions of feature extraction, model architecture, training strategy, and data augmentation are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Each release was documented photographically (Supplementary Fig. 40), and time-series data from the 36 sensors were used to extract 14 key features per sensor. In addition to position prediction, determining the area occupied by foreign objects is crucial for assessing their engineering impact. As further supported by Supplementary Fig. 54, spatial distribution also plays a critical role—overly concentrated layouts degrade performance, while evenly distributed configurations significantly enhance system effectiveness.

From an engineering perspective, the proposed FIDS is not intended to replace optical monitoring systems, but to serve as a complementary sensing approach. By relying on distributed mechanical sensing, it enables all-weather operation independent of lighting conditions and provides quantitative physical responses to intrusion events. Owing to the absence of visual information and the sensor-density-dependent spatial resolution, the system is best suited for optically constrained or high-robustness-required sections, where it can act as a triggering layer to enhance the reliability of integrated monitoring systems.

Discussion

This study presents a stabilizing-sensing synergistic geogrid (SMART geogrid) that integrates trackbed stabilization with distributed FBG sensing for long-term substructure monitoring. Quantitatively, compared with conventional surface-mounted configurations, the SMART geogrid achieves a higher strain transfer efficiency (0.93842 vs. 0.89496), an ~13-fold reduction in wavelength drift after 100,000 cyclic loading cycles, and nearly 50% lower measured strain under identical external loading conditions, indicating improved strain transmission and signal stability. These advantages are further supported by pull-out and compression tests and by full-scale experiments involving more than 2 million axle load cycles, demonstrating stable sensing under realistic service scenarios. In addition, the FIDS achieves a position prediction accuracy of R2 = 0.9999 and 100% classification accuracy in controlled tests, highlighting the potential of the SMART geogrid for spatially intelligent sensing.

Beyond demonstrating sensing capability, the distributed rib-strain data provide engineering-relevant insight into localized deformation and soil-geogrid interaction. In particular, differential strain accumulation between adjacent ribs is physically linked to local deflection and can serve as an early indicator of non-uniform settlement, providing a basis for transitioning from passive monitoring to condition-based maintenance.

The transition from shallow laboratory conditions to deep field burial introduces signal attenuation challenges for FIDS. Nevertheless, this does not significantly compromise identification accuracy. In full-scale tests, SMART geogrids buried under ballast successfully captured dynamic strain from train loads, demonstrating stress-wave transmission through granular force chains. Although burial depth attenuates signals, impact events such as falling rocks generate high-energy signals that maintain a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio. Moreover, the machine learning model employs multi-domain feature extraction-including robust frequency-domain features like PSD peaks and FFT distributions, rather than relying solely on depth-sensitive amplitude. This allows the system to retain high distinguishability even with amplitude reduction. Combined with stronger impact energy and the potential for transfer-learning-based field adaptation, FIDS remains effective for monitoring in deep ballast environments. In future field deployments, a transfer learning strategy can be employed to fine-tune the model according to specific trackbed depths and environmental conditions.

Compared with conventional geogrids employing surface-mounted optical fibers, the embedded-fiber configuration requires additional manufacturing steps, particularly the grooving process, which increases fabrication complexity and initial cost. However, scaling analysis indicates that under automated laser-grooving conditions, the incremental cost can be controlled at approximately ¥1.60 per meter, accounting for about 3.2% of the raw geogrid material cost. More importantly, the embedded design enhances long-term fiber survivability and sensing reliability under cyclic loading, which is expected to offset the modest increase in initial cost from a life-cycle perspective. Nevertheless, long-term durability under extreme environmental conditions and extended service periods still warrants validation through prolonged field deployment. Overall, the SMART geogrid offers a robust and scalable route toward integrated stabilization and intelligent monitoring of geotechnical infrastructure.

Fabrication of the SMART geogrid

A geogrid with grooves was constructed using 3D modeling and subsequently printed with Union Black, a photosensitive resin, using Stereo Lithography Apparatus (SLA) 3D printing (Liantai Lite600, Shanghai Union AM Technology Co., Ltd). The properties of the Union Black material are detailed in Table S2. The specific dimensions of the biaxial and triaxial geogrids used in this study are presented in Table S3. For the full-scale model test, a triaxial geogrid made from polypropylene, measuring 5.5 meters in both length and width, was produced by Tensar Co. Ltd. The detailed grooving procedure is provided in Supplementary Note 4, and their mechanical properties are summarized in Table S4. The Union Black photopolymer exhibits tensile strength and elastic modulus comparable to those of polypropylene within the small-strain range (<1.5%) relevant to the model tests, enabling reliable simulation of geogrid stabilization behavior. Optical fiber

In this study, FBG sensors manufactured by NanZee Sensing are utilized. The sensors employ a single-mode optical fiber with a grating length of 10 mm. The coating material for these fibers is acrylic ester.

Optical fibers embedded with FBG sensors are carefully placed into the prepared grooves, with both ends applied with a pre-defined tension to ensure precise alignment and uniform strain distribution. The grooves are then filled with an ultraviolet (UV) adhesive (TY-2900, Zhongshan Xingxu Electrical Appliances Co., Ltd.) to fully encapsulate the fibers. The adhesive is subsequently cured under 395 nm UV light (ZGD-GHD, Zhongshan Xingxu Electrical Appliances Co., Ltd.) for 40 s to achieve full solidification.

Signal acquisition and data processing

The FBG sensors used in this study do not require additional signal conditioning. Wavelength shifts were directly acquired using an FBG interrogator (NZS-FBG-A06(32H)). The calibrated relationships between wavelength shift and the corresponding physical quantities (e.g., strain or axial force) were then applied to convert the raw wavelength data into engineering measurements.

Finite element analysis

In contrast, a simplified “without-nodes” geometry was adopted exclusively in the finite element model to improve mesh quality and numerical convergence, in which the thickened nodal regions at rib intersections were not explicitly modeled. The commercial finite element software Abaqus2022 is employed to establish the simulation model of SMART geogrids for analyzing tensile and bending deformation, mesh expansion, reliability, and soil compression deformation. The optical fibers used in the simulations have a radius of 0.0625 mm. Mechanical calibration of SMART geogrid

To determine the relationship between the wavelength and the axial force of the geogrid, the single rib of the SMART geogrid was calibrated using a Zwick tensile testing machine(Z5.0TN), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 56(a). The total length of the single rib measured 367.4 mm, with each triangular plate at both ends being 65 mm long. The calibration process involved seven stages of loading and unloading the geogrid, both with and without nodes, in five repeated cycles, with each loading increment set at 30 N.

The comparative analysis between geogrids with and without nodes was conducted to verify whether the geometric simplification adopted in the numerical model affects the predicted mechanical response. The calibration results for the SMART geogrid without nodes are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 56(b). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 56(c), although all physical geogrids used in the experiments were of the noded type, the experimental results indicate that the mechanical behavior of the ribs is similar regardless of the presence of nodes. Therefore, the corresponding simplification in the FEA model is considered reasonable.

Cyclic loading test

Three-point bending tests were performed on the SMART geogrid ribs using a Zwick Z5.0TN tensile testing machine. Each specimen was prepared from a single rib of the SMART geogrid and was simply supported over a span of 100 mm. A cyclic load was applied at the mid-span via a loading nose, with a frequency of 2 Hz and an amplitude of 6 mm. The loading profile and test setup are schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9(a). Throughout the test, displacement and applied force were recorded by the machine’s internal sensors, while wavelength data along the rib were obtained from the embedded optical fiber sensors.

Temperature sensitivity test

The SMART geogrid was placed in a constant temperature tank, and the thermostat was adjusted until the desired temperature was reached and stabilized. At this point, the wavelength of the FBG sensor was observed. Following this procedure, the temperature in the tank was then incrementally increased from 0 °C to 45 °C in 5 °C intervals, with the wavelength of the FBG sensor being recorded after stabilization at each temperature step.

Pull-out test

The pull-out tests were performed using a geosynthetic testing apparatus (Tianjin Meitesi Testing Machine Factory) designed for evaluating the frictional interaction between geosynthetic materials and soils. The instrument is equipped with a high-precision servo motor to control pull-out rates accurately, and a servo-controlled normal loading system to apply precise vertical pressure. The pull-out apparatus consists of a split steel test box and a rigid clamping system. The box comprises an upper and a lower chamber, each with inner dimensions of 300 mm (length) × 300 mm (width) × 150 mm (height). The geogrid specimen is firmly secured at the loaded end by a set of metal clamping plates using a bolted connection. The system can apply a maximum force of 30kN (accuracy: ±0.3%) and control horizontal displacement up to 50 mm (precision: ±0.3%).

As illustrated in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 57, the procedure for the pull-out test involved layering and compacting the soil in the lower part of the pull-out model box to slightly exceed the height of the box. The SMART geogrid was then inserted through the pull-out opening, centered over the soil surface, with a section of the rib mesh extending outside the box side wall and secured to a horizontal fixture, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 58. Additional soil is then added in layers to the upper part of the box, and compacted to achieve ~95% compaction. In each test, after applying the specific load to the plate, the SMART geogrid was pulled out at a controlled rate of 1 mm/min, while the axial force on the geogrid was measured using FBG sensors.

Compressive test

The testing apparatus consisted primarily of a model container and a loading system. The model container was designed as a cube with internal dimensions of 300 mm in length, 300 mm in width, and 300 mm in height. The loading system included a Zwick Z250SE universal testing machine and a steel plate simulating a strip footing. The steel plate measured 298 mm in length, 30 mm in width, and 20 mm in height. To minimize boundary interaction between the footing and the inner walls of the container, a gap of approximately 1 mm was maintained on each side between both ends of the plate and the container walls, thereby reducing the influence of lateral constraints on the test outcomes.

The initial depth of the geogrid was consistently set at 15 mm, except in non-geogrid scenarios, and the spacing between layers in double-layer triaxial setups was also maintained at 15 mm. Furthermore, the length of the geogrid perpendicular to the strip footing was standardized at 240 mm. Considering the symmetry of the model, five FBGs were encapsulated in the geogrid.

Uniformly graded sand was compacted layer by layer within the model box to a relative density of 95%, representative of typical foundation density. For stabilized foundations, the soil was leveled up to the geogrid layer before placing a layer of SMART geogrid and continuing soil addition up to 280 mm. The completed model box was then positioned on the testing machine platform, and the strip footing was centered over the soil foundation and loaded at a rate of 2 mm/min.

For the test shown in Fig. S11, the same apparatus and container described above were used. A square aluminum loading plate with a side length of 200 mm was employed. The normal pressure was increased to 50 kPa and then held steady, at which point the wavelength shift of the FBG sensor was recorded.

Data extraction of the FIDS

The extracted features are derived from the wavelength–time data directly collected by the FBG demodulator. Based on this dataset, a series of time-domain and frequency-domain features were computed, including basic statistical metrics—mean, standard deviation (std), maximum, minimum, and median—as well as skewness and kurtosis to characterize the distribution shape. Additional features comprise first-order differences (diff1_mean and diff1_std), autocorrelation, moving averages (moving_avg_mean and moving_avg_std), the peak of the Fourier transform (fft_peak), and the peak of the power spectral density (psd_peak). Collectively, these features capture the central tendency, fluctuation amplitude, distribution characteristics, temporal trends, and frequency-domain properties of the data.

Device of the FIDS

The experiments utilized a three-axis moving slide rail system to simulate the dropping of foreign objects at various positions, achieving a displacement accuracy of 0.1 mm. A cylinder gripper was employed to precisely grasp and release the foreign objects. All tests were conducted within a 400 mm × 400 mm model box. The burial depth of the SMART geogrid was ~55 mm, about 15% of the model height.

The algorithm architecture of FIDS position detection

The core architecture of the proposed model comprises three key components: a feature extraction network, a dual-branch prediction module, and a multi-objective optimization strategy. Given the limited availability of experimental training data, data augmentation techniques were employed to improve the model’s generalization capability. These techniques included the addition of Gaussian noise, random scaling, and random translation, expanding the dataset to 692 samples. Feature importance was assessed based on model-assigned weights, and the top 50 ranked features were selected for a second round of data augmentation and model training. This feature selection strategy effectively eliminates redundant inputs, enhances training efficiency, improves predictive performance, and mitigates the risk of overfitting.

Soil parameters

In this study, standard quartz sand was used. The particle size distribution of the sands was analyzed using a CamsizerX2 instrument, which employs dynamic image analysis. The resulting gradation curves are presented in Supplementary Fig. 59. The sand used for the pull-out and compression tests (Supplementary Fig. 59(a)) had a uniformity coefficient (Cᵤ) of 1.43 and a curvature coefficient (Cc) of 1.27, indicating a uniformly graded material. The sand employed in the FIDS tests (Supplementary Fig. 59(b)) was similarly well-graded, with Cᵤ = 1.66 and Cc = 1.13. Laboratory tests indicate a unit weight (γ) of 16.8 kN/m³ and an internal friction angle (φ) of 31.6° for the sand.

Full-scale testing apparatus

A full-scale testing apparatus was designed and constructed at Zhejiang University, China, to realistically simulate the in-service conditions of ballasted railway tracks. The setup comprises four primary subsystems: a sequential loading system, a control system, a monitoring system, and a physical model. The model container measures 15.0 m in length and 5.5 m in width. Since railway sleepers are discrete elements that transmit train loads to the underlying structure, the moving train load can be simulated as a series of discrete dynamic loads applied at sleeper positions. These loads are identical in magnitude but exhibit phase differences due to the fixed geometric relationship between the sleepers and the moving train wheels, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 28.The sequential loading system consists of eight actuators arranged in series over the sleepers. By introducing a constant phase shift between adjacent actuators, the system effectively reproduces the passage of a real train. Trial tests have demonstrated that the apparatus can simulate axle loads up to 25 t and train speeds up to 360 km/h.

Full-scale testing materials and model preparation

The full-scale physical model was constructed in a 15.0 m × 5.5 m container to simulate a ballasted railway track substructure. The model consisted of four compacted layers from bottom to top: a silt subsoil (ubiquitous in southeast China), a gravel-soil subgrade, a graded-gravel subballast, and a crushed-tuff ballast layer. Their classification according to the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS, ASTM D2487) is provided in Table S10. The grain size distribution for each layer is presented in Supplementary Fig. 60, and the particle parameters for the subsoil, subgrade, subballast, and ballast are presented in Table S8. The friction angle of the ballast particles is about 40°. Each layer was compacted in 15-20 cm lifts using a mechanical tamper. Compaction quality was verified through cone penetration tests (tip resistance >1.5 MPa for subsoil) and by measuring the layer density, modulus K30 (for subgrade and subballast), and sleeper supporting stiffness (for ballast). All measured parameters met the specifications of high-speed railway design codes (Table S9). Two SMART geogrids were placed at the interface between the subballast and ballast layers and within the ballast layer, respectively, each covering an area of 5.5 m × 5.5 m. Material quantities used in the model included 186 t of silt, 440 t of granular soil, 74 t of graded gravel, and 34 t of fresh ballast. The geogrid aperture-to-particle size ratio was approximately 1.56, which lies within the commonly reported optimal interlock range (1.2–1.6)61–64. In contrast, finer particles were intentionally used in small-scale tests to avoid boundary effects and to isolate sensing and strain-transfer mechanisms.
