Project Title: Holographic Quantum Simulation of Strongly Correlated Electron Systems
 Award Number: DE-SC0022102
 Recipient Organization: The University of Texas at Austin
 Principal Investigator: Prof. Shyam Shankar (shyam.shankar@utexas.edu)
                        Project Period: 09/01/2021 – 08/31/2025
 Submission Date: Dec 1, 2025

Project Overview and Major Goals
The project aimed to demonstrate a new holographic quantum simulation approach and co‐
designed quantum hardware to tackle three specific problems that fall within the broad
umbrella of unraveling the physics of strongly correlated electron systems (SCES). These tasks
were: (1) holographic preparation of ground‐ and thermal‐ states of correlated magnetic and
electronic systems including quasi‐2d frustrated‐spin, Fermi‐Hubbard, and fractional quantum
Hall (FQH) systems, (2) holographic‐simulation of long‐time out‐of‐equilibrium dynamics and (3)
holographic analogs of embedding methods such as dynamical mean‐ field theory (DMFT) and
density‐matrix embedding theory (DMET) to solve systems with complex structure or long‐range
interactions. These tasks are prototypes for the kinds of material simulation problems of
interest to BES, such as the simulation of multiferroic materials, perovskite photovoltaics and
high‐temperature superconductors, that tax the capabilities of the most powerful classical
supercomputers.

Scientific and Technical Accomplishments

Theory and algorithm development:
The research activity in this grant developed two new, qubit-efficient quantum algorithms to
simulate highly-entangled quantum states relevant to the simulation of electronic and magnetic
materials. Publications: [1,9] respectively introduced algorithms to recursively approximate the
ground-state of 1d spin-chains [1] near a phase transition and 2d electronic state relevant to 2d
materials such as graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides [9]. By exploiting a recursive,
scale-invariant structure underlying these states, the algorithms require circuit depth scaling
only logarithmically in the number of spins/orbitals being simulated – an exponential
improvement on previously known methods in the fermion example. This improvment is crucial
to reduce the impact of errors and enable near-term noisy and memory-limited quantum
processors to simulate such materials. While these methods enable simulation of effectively
zero temperature (near-ground-state) physics, many practical materials modeling tasks require
understanding the thermal behavior at non-zero temperatures. To this end, in [3] we developed
a qubit-efficient holographic simulation method to simulate thermal states of quantum spin
chains using holographic quantum tensor network methods.

In addition to this practical quantum algorithms development, the theoretical research funded
by this grant exploited insights from tensor networks, statistical mechanics, and topological
phases to contributed to the fundamental understanding of the complexity of classical materials
simulations to better delineate the frontier for quantum advantage over classical methods [8]
and to understand effects of quantum measurements on entangled many-body states [6,7]. It
also led to novel and resource-efficient error mitigation [5,7] and quantum error correction [2]
techniques that improve the resilience of quantum computations against noise and errors.

Co-design of algorithms with hardware:

In a publication titled “Sequential quantum simulation of spin chains with a single circuit QED
device” [4], we proposed and numerically validated a sequential quantum simulation algorithm
on circuit Quantum-Electrodynamics (cQED) architecture consisting of one transmon qubit plus
a long-lived cavity (harmonic oscillator), to build matrix-product states of spin chains using the
cavity as a quantum memory and the transmon for sequential operations. We demonstrated
that analog (pulse-level) control prepares target states significantly faster than gate-based
methods, enhancing robustness to decoherence and enabling variational ground-state
preparation under realistic noise.

Experimental implementation on circuit Quantum-Electrodynamics (cQED) hardware:

In addition to the above publications, a cQED processor was built to experimentally implement
sequential quantum simulation of spin-chains. Analog (pulse-level) control was found to be
difficult to tune up in practice despite multiple attempts. This is ultimately attributed to the
complexity of accurately characterizing the system Hamiltonian. Previous approaches performed
only analog control of the cavity Hilbert space, up to Fock-level of about 6, whereas sequential
quantum simulation requires unitary control of the joint qubit-cavity Hilbert space up to Fock-
level of 10. To address this issue, digital gate-based methods are being attempted as they
promise to be easier to debug. The work on this approach is ongoing as of the completion of this
report.

Experimental implementations on trapped-ion hardware:

To complement the ongoing development of the cQED hardware, multiple of the new
algorithms and theory predictions were experimentally implemented on trapped-ion quantum
processors built by industry collaborator Quantinuum. Notably, in [1], our team and
collaborators at UC Berkeley experimentally demonstrated holographic circuits for simulating
phase transitions in spin-chain models of quantum magnets, achieving quantitatively accurate
simulation of spin chains with up to 32 spins – at the time, a record -- using only 10 physical
trapped ion qubits.

References [5,7] utilized mid-circuit measurements and qubit reuse (MCMR) techniques and
classical tensor network algorithm advances developed in this grant to explore non-equilibrium
phase transitions in many-body quantum spin systems subjected to programmable dissipation
and feedback. By exploiting MCMR and holographic simulation ideas we were able to extend the
size of the quantum simulations beyond the limitations of conventional simulation methods,
simulating non-equilibrium phase transitions in systems with up to 72 spins using only 20
physical qubits, and performing the first ever truly-scalable observation of a measurement
induced phase transition. We further developed new error mitigation techniques based on
processing the measurement record using classical tensor network algorithms, which

significantly suppressed the effect of noise. These results extend the reach of qubit-limited and
noisy quantum hardware to conduct simulations in large quantum many-body systems.

Training and Professional Development
The project supported two postdoctoral fellows, multiple graduate students, and several
undergraduates across UT Austin and UBC. Trainees gained expertise in nanofabrication,
cryogenic measurement of superconducting quantum circuits, HPC/GPU simulation, algorithm
design, and working on commercial trapped-ion qubit platforms at Quantinuum.

Impact
Principal Discipline(s): Predicting the ground‐state, low‐energy excitations, and dynamical
response of correlated electronic and magnetic systems are central tasks for computational
materials science. Unfortunately, exact classical numerical computation is infeasible even for
simplified models, and approximation methods do not produce accurate results for many
problems. Quantum computers promise to be able to perform accurate materials simulation at
significantly reduced computational cost. However, state‐of‐the‐art quantum simulation
algorithms for implementing these tasks will require thousands of qubits with high‐fidelity
control to outperform classical computers, which is out of reach for near‐term quantum
hardware. In this project, we developed “holographic” quantum simulation algorithms for
solving various problems related to understanding the physics of strongly correlated electron
systems. The problems we attempted to tackle are prototypes for the kinds of material
simulation problems, such as the simulation of multiferroic materials, perovskite photovoltaics
and high‐temperature superconductors, that tax the capabilities of the most powerful classical
supercomputers. The impact of our work will be to substantially reduce the size and fidelity
requirements for quantum simulation of realistic materials and devices, bringing practical
quantum material simulations closer to the capabilities of near‐term hardware.

Other Disciplines: The algorithmic and hardware capabilities developed here are expected to
have applications beyond materials simulation. The holographic simulation framework is a
fundamental paradigm shift from conventional models of quantum computing based on unitary
circuits followed by a single round of global measurements. Mid‐circuit measurements allow for
qubit re‐ use that enables holographic algorithms to simulate much larger systems than can
directly fit in quantum memory. Further, it endows holographic algorithms with a degree of
noise‐resilience that is absent in conventional models. These capabilities would all be broadly
useful for a variety of quantum computing applications beyond material simulation. These
include adjacent application such as simulating molecules and chemical reactions, or
pharmaceutical compounds, but also further‐flung application areas such as quantum machine
learning where tensor networks have already been identified as promising data structures.

Workforce: Training a quantum‐educated workforce has been identified as a key challenge for
continuing the success of commercial quantum computing technology and cementing the
United States’ leadership in this promising emerging technology. This project has provided
training opportunities for 2 postdoctoral fellows, 6 graduate students and 4 undergraduate
students enabling them to become experts in state‐of‐the art quantum computing hardware,
architecture, algorithms, and applications. Further, the collaboration with industry partner
Quantinuum has provided opportunities for these student researchers to work directly on
commercial quantum computing technology.
