Artificial intelligence-based fluid quantification predicts clinical outcomes in diabetic macular oedema eyes treated with intravitreal dexamethasone implants: DIADEMA project
Authors: Francesc Franquesa-Garcia, Barbara Romero-Nuñez, Ruben Martin-Pinardel, Sandro De Zanet, Julio José González-López, Jose Juan Escobar-Barranco, María P Ruiz-Del-Tiempo, Anna Boixadera, Alex Fonollosa, Alba Gómez-Benlloch, Daniel Velazquez-Villoria, José Ignacio Fernández-Vigo, Gonzaga Garay-Aramburu, Alba Parrado-Carrillo, Luis Castillon, Sandra Gómez Sánchez, Francisco Javier Lavid, Carolina Arruabarrena, Marc Figueras-Roca, Carolina Bernal-Morales, Carlos Ciller, Javier Zarranz-Ventura, on behalf of DIADEMA investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo explore associations between artificial intelligence (AI)-based baseline optical coherence tomography (OCT) fluid compartment quantifications and 12-month visual outcomes in diabetic macular oedema (DME) eyes treated with the intravitreal dexamethasone implant.MethodsThis was a multicentre, real-world, national DME database and associated OCT dataset study. Demographics, visual acuity (VA), treatments and visit data were collected using a validated web-based tool (Fight Retinal Blindness). Fluid compartment quantifications, including intraretinal fluid (IRF) and subretinal fluid (SRF), were measured in nanolitres (nL) using a validated AI tool (Discovery). Univariate and multivariate regression mixed models evaluated associations between anatomical variables and VA outcomes.ResultsA total of 101 treatment-naïve DME eyes were grouped into quartiles according to their fluid volume for each fluid compartment (Q1: lowest volume, Q4: highest volume). Baseline IRF was associated with greater VA gains at month 12 (+6.34 letters, p=0.07) but poorer final VA (−8.95, p=0.07), while SRF was associated with worse final VA at 12 months (−12.5, p=0.01). At month 3, IRF was associated with a VA decrease at 12 months (−13.7, p=0.02) and lower final VA (−29.8, p<0.001). At month 12, IRF was associated with lower final VA (−11.6, p=0.03). Quantitatively, a reduction of 100 nL of IRF at 3 months was associated with a +1.54 letters gain (p=0.03) in the multivariate analysis.ConclusionThis real-world, multicentre study describes objective baseline fluid volumes that predict visual outcomes at 12 months in routine clinical care. Accurate quantification of baseline fluid volumes may play a predictive role for final visual outcomes.Keywords: Imaging, Macula, Retina, Subretinal Fluid, Prognosis

Introduction

Diabetic macular oedema (DME) represents one of the main causes of vision impairment in patients with diabetes mellitus.1 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is the main tool for diagnosis and management of DME, and central retinal thickness (CRT) is the main anatomical outcome measure for DME evaluation in clinical trials, an endpoint accepted by regulatory agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency.2–4 Nevertheless, in this particular field, there is still a lack of an objective relationship between anatomical and visual outcomes in DME. Visual acuity (VA) has shown poor correlation with CRT in several studies, suggesting that it may be an insufficient parameter to explain functional outcomes in DME.5 For this reason, an increased interest in the qualitative analysis of OCT images in DME has arisen in recent years. Novel imaging features such as the presence of intraretinal fluid (IRF) and subretinal fluid (SRF), integrity of the external limiting membrane (ELM) and the ellipsoid zone (EZ), as well as the number of hyper-reflective foci (HRF) have gained relevance in recent publications, particularly as potential OCT biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis of DME.6 However, the qualitative interpretation of OCT images depends on the physician’s criteria, which can increase interobserver variability and inconsistencies in interpretations.

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in OCT image processing, mainly based on deep learning, has enabled the objective evaluation of OCT images and accurate measurements of some of these parameters.7 8 The automatic quantification of fluid compartment volumes and its correlation with CRT has been studied in conditions such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or retinal vein occlusion.9 Additionally, associations between fluid volume and visual outcomes in patients with AMD have been evaluated, both at baseline and during the maintenance phase.10 11 In DME, the association between fluid quantifications and clinical data appears particularly relevant, as the spectrum of DME presentations appears more diverse.4 In this sense, the quantification of central macular fluid volume (CMFV) has shown greater association with VA than CRT in cases of DME prior to anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatment.12 Furthermore, pilot studies have been conducted to predict the response to anti-VEGF drugs in patients with DME based on this fluid quantification.13 Interestingly, the management of DME encompasses other treatment options beyond anti-VEGF drugs, such steroids as the dexamethasone (DEX) implant. According to currently available clinical guidelines,14–16 DEX implants are used in previously treated eyes and suitable treatment-naïve candidates. For these reasons, the evolution of fluid response to steroid treatments remains a poorly explored area that appears to be of great interest in a multiple treatment options scenario.

Electronic medical record systems have emerged as ideal tools for the structured collection of clinical data, particularly in a multicentre setting. The Fight Retinal Blindness (FRB) project is a global initiative based on a validated online tool for clinical data collection. This tool facilitates the creation of large, high-quality structured datasets on the management of treated patients, allowing for clinical outcomes audit of intravitreal therapies at both international17 and national levels.18–20 In DME, this tool has been proven effective in the assessment of treatment response to anti-VEGF drugs and steroids.21 22

The aim of this real-world observational DIAbetic macular edema DExamethasone iMAge (DIADEMA) study is to investigate in a cohort of treatment-naïve DME eyes treated with DEX implants the relationship between baseline anatomical biomarkers, such as fluid compartments, and clinical and functional outcomes at 1 year. All anatomical data (IRF and SRF) were obtained from baseline OCT images processed with a validated AI-based solution (RetinAI Discovery; Ikerian, Bern, Switzerland and RetinAI US, Boston, USA), and clinical data were recorded using the DME module of the FRB registry nationwide.

Methods

Study design, ethics and selection criteria

This was a multicentre, retrospective study using a national DME database with associated OCT data collection. Treatment-naïve DME eyes which started intravitreal therapy with DEX implant were included. Treatment selection was mandated at each centre by treating physicians as per local management guidelines. Eyes were required to have at least 12 months of follow-up and OCT scans available at baseline, 3 months and 12 months. Bilateral eyes were included in the analysis. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and anatomical data: data management and assessments

Management of clinical and anatomical data involved combining data from each participating centre. Pseudonymisation was performed locally at each centre by using a consecutive coding method, and a pseudonymised copy was provided to the analysis team within agreed timelines and merged into a centralised database for subsequent analysis in the coordinating centre. Clinical data were collected using the FRB registry tool and included age, gender, ethnicity and best corrected VA expressed in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) letters according to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study. All assessments and treatment decisions were made by local physicians. Anatomical data were obtained from macular cube OCT scans. Scans from three OCT devices were included: SD-OCT Cirrus (Carl Zeiss Meditec), SD-OCT Spectralis (Heidelberg Engineering) and SS-OCT Triton (Topcon). OCT scan protocols other than macular cube scans (such as raster scans, radial scans, etc) or smaller scan areas (<6×6 mm) were not considered. No exclusion criteria were applied based on quantitative image quality parameters, such as signal intensity index.

AI-based OCT analysis

The OCT cube scans were processed in the coordinating centre using a Commission Européenne (CE)-marked AI-based software solution (Discovery, Ikerian).8 Fluid quantification metrics were obtained for IRF and SRF compartments. Different macular locations were evaluated, such as the fovea (central 1 mm area), parafovea (central 3 mm area) and perifovea (central 6 mm area). Additionally, automated thickness and volume maps were generated for accurate quantification of the fluids.

Timepoint definitions and matching windows for clinical and OCT data

Clinical data and OCT scans were collected during routine clinical care and were not necessarily performed on the same date due to variability in service delivery among centres (eg, one-step vs two-step clinics, treatment-only visits, differences in clinical referral pathways, etc). To harmonise data collection, specific time windows (±1 month) were applied: at baseline, 3 months and 12 months.

Fluid volume and frequency of injection subgroup analysis

Fluid volumes, measured in nanolitres (nL), were assessed at baseline and at 3 and 12 months, respectively. Based on the distribution of these measurements and for the purpose of subgroup analysis, eyes were grouped by quartiles (‘Q4’ for the highest volume quartile, top 25% of eyes, and ‘Q1’ for the lowest volume, bottom 25% of eyes). Comparisons were performed between Q4 and the combination of the three other quartiles (Q3+Q2+Q1). This classification was applied to all fluid compartments (IRF and SRF) and evaluated regions (fovea, parafovea and perifovea).

Statistical methods

Descriptive data analysis included counts, percentages, means with SD, medians and values of the first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3). For comparisons, t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used when appropriate. Χ2 tests were used for categorical variables. Univariate and multivariate generalised estimating equations (GEE) and linear mixed models were used to estimate the association between fluid and VA results, accounting for intereye correlation in the analysis. Analysis was performed in R V.4.0.3 (cran.r-project.org; http://cran.r-project.org) using nlme (3.1.153) and geepack (1.3.2) packages for linear mixed models and GEE, respectively.

Results

Demographics and characteristics of study eyes

A total of 101 naïve eyes from 83 patients with DME treated with DEX implants fulfilled all the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. Demographics are represented in table 1. The mean (SD) age of the patients was 67.5 (12.2) years, with a mean (SD) VA at baseline, 3 months and 12 months of 58.4 (15.8), 63.1 (16.5) and 56.7 (20.3) letters, respectively. Mean VA change from baseline to 3 months was +2.9 (10.9) letters and at 12 months was −1.7 (18.3) letters, and treated eyes received an average of 1.8 DEX implants during the follow-up. Two implants were needed in 33% of eyes, and 23% required three or more injections. The mean number of visits per eye was 7.3, of which 5.5 were non-treatment related.

At baseline, SRF was significantly associated with worse final VA in the univariate model (−12.5 letters, p=0.01), a trend that was also observed for IRF (IRF: −8.95, p=0.07). Regarding the VA change, no statistically significant associations were found for any of the fluid compartments, and only a trend was observed for IRF volume in the multivariate analysis (+9.84, p=0.07).

At month 3, IRF and SRF volumes were associated with a statistically significant worse final VA (−29.8, p<0.001; −17.9, p=0.02) in the univariate analysis, an association that was maintained for IRF in the multivariate analysis (−26.4, p=0.004). With regard to VA change, IRF volume was associated with a decrease in VA in both the univariate (−13.7, p=0.02) and multivariate analyses (−14, p=0.04).

At 12 months, IRF volume showed a strong association with worse final VA in both univariate (−11.6, p=0.03) and multivariate analyses (−12.5, p=0.03). No fluid compartment showed significant associations with VA change at 12 months.

At 3 months, a 100 nL reduction in IRF volume was consistently shown to be associated with VA gain in all three models (univariate +1.25, p=0.03; multivariate +1.51, p=0.02; adjusted multivariate +1.54, p=0.03). At 12 months, none of the fluid compartments showed significant associations.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the use of AI-based software in a real-world setting is possible to investigate potential associations between fluid volume quantifications and visual outcomes in suitable naïve patients with DME treated with the DEX implant at 1-year follow-up. The results reported provide valuable insights into the efficacy of treatment with the DEX implant and the fluid dynamics in the different compartments, highlighting the potential of AI to support clinical decisions and individualise patient management.

In DME, CRT has historically been the main biomarker driving clinical decisions,23 often influenced by other factors such as age, gender, axial length and, more importantly, presence of retinal atrophy.24 Nevertheless, the correlation between baseline CRT and long-term visual outcomes in DME has been controversial, with some studies showing CRT as a predictor of VA25 not confirmed by other authors.26 For this reason, in recent years, a growing interest has been directed to investigate additional OCT parameters which could help the clinical decision-making. Biomarkers such as IRF, SRF, ELM and/or EZ disruption and HRF have been associated with visual prognosis in patients with DME.6 27–31 Since most of these findings depend on qualitative assessments, they are therefore exposed to subjectivity, challenging their use in routine clinical care. To overcome this limitation, the advent of machine learning-based tools capable of analysing these biomarkers in a quantitative fashion has generated enormous interest in the retina community. Fluid quantification is one of the most investigated fields, with several commercially available validated software able to reliably quantify fluid volumes in the different compartments of the retina in conditions such as AMD,17 highlighting the potential of AI to obtain objective measurements in OCT images.

Validation studies of AI algorithms to quantify fluids in DME have appeared,32 with some reports suggesting that the total CMFV could be a better predictor of VA than CST at 1 month but showing only a moderate correlation with VA13 regardless of its distribution in the different retinal layers. Since the presence of SRF and IRF may have different pathogenetic characteristics and could therefore represent different phenotypes of DME, further investigations with longer follow-up are required.

Our study took into consideration the different retinal compartments and evaluated the relationship between IRF and SRF volumes with functional results. In our cohort, DEX implant treatment achieved a notable reduction of IRF and SRF, especially at 3 months, after the peak of maximum drug release (8 weeks). Persistence of IRF could lead to structural damage at the level of bipolar cells and Müller fibres, negatively contributing in the long term, which may suggest that early interventions may be beneficial in these patients.33 In our study, there was no statistically significant association between IRF volume at baseline and final VA. However, at month 3, IRF volume was shown to be a predictor of poor long-term VA, as unresolved IRF volume at this timepoint may have a negative impact on final visual outcomes. This is one of the main findings of the study, as it reflects that IRF resolution at 3 months predicts the final VA achieved by these patients at 12 months. Similarly, IRF volume at month 12 also correlated with poor visual outcome. These results were supported by the volumetric analysis of the effect of 100 nL IRF reduction, which showed that at month 3, this reduction was associated with an objective improvement in final VA (+1.5 letters), indicating that early IRF clearance at 3 months could have a beneficial impact on the final visual outcomes. However, the initial advantages of IRF reduction seemed to weaken over time with relapses, suggesting that the best long-term VA outcomes are observed with early and sustained IRF control.

In our series, baseline SRF volume was not associated with visual gain at 12 months but showed an existing association with worse final VA level. We do believe that this could be explained by case selection, as the quartile with the highest SRF volume may correspond also to the most severe cases, therefore with the lowest visual potential. Previous studies have described the qualitative analysis of SRF as a predictor of visual gains,33 34 but without volumetric associations. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the absolute values of SRF were much lower than those of IRF (fovea 10.4 nL vs 69.7 nL, parafovea 37.4 nL vs 259 nL, perifovea 66.8 nL vs 424.4 nL), which could explain the differences observed between compartments. For this reason, a reduction of 100 nL of SRF in the overall cohort at 3 months revealed a trend for VA loss, possibly related to thinner retinas as a surrogate indirect marker of diffuse atrophy.

This study has certain strengths and limitations. As in other previous national multicentre studies, the use of the FRB platform ensured high-quality data collection.19 As a real-world study, treatment indication was mandated by local treatment protocols without prespecified patient profiles, as a product of routine clinical care. This is particularly important in naïve DME eyes treated with DEX implants, as according to currently available clinical guidelines,14–16 this is considered in suitable patients, described as pseudophakic eyes, phakic eyes with impending cataract surgery, patients with previous major cardiovascular events or those unwilling to maintain frequent injections. Even with this common framework, the adoption of this therapy varies internationally, and the results described in this series may not apply to other countries with different treatment algorithms, limiting the external validity of the results reported. An additional consideration is that the 12-month timepoint may refer to a combination of stable DME eyes and those with relapses that require further injections just at the moment of the visit, a pattern often seen in DEX studies which may limit the visual outcomes reported at this timepoint. The use of a validated AI-based software for objective fluid quantifications introduced several methodological considerations. Notably, fluid measurements were accepted as ground truth without independent verification by a reading centre, which may influence the reliability of the reference standard. However, the software’s compatibility with OCT cube data from three distinct imaging devices enhances the generalisability of the findings. Although this multidevice approach introduces variability into the image dataset, it simultaneously broadens the applicability of the results across diverse clinical settings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates existing associations between AI-based objective fluid quantifications and 1-year visual outcomes in patients with DME treated with the DEX implant in a real-world setting. This research contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of AI software in the management of DME, highlighting the potential of this technology to enhance treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes in routine clinical care.
