Tissue-engineered approach using autologous fibroblast-seeded volume-stable collagen matrix for managing gingival recession- A randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Devapriyan Sankar, Deepavalli Arumuganainar, Shivakumar Baskaran, Sivaram Gopalakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGingival recession is a common deformity, and tissue engineering, using active regeneration, is emerging as a modern approach for management. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of autologous gingival fibroblasts seeded onto a volume-stable collagen matrix (VSCM) in treating gingival recession.Materials and methodsA split-mouth randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted on 20 sites in 10 patients presenting with Miller Class I and II gingival recession. Sites were allocated to receive either coronally advanced flap (CAF) with volume-stable collagen matrix (VSCM) (control) or CAF with fibroblast-seeded volume-stable collagen matrix (FVSCM) (test). Autologous gingival fibroblasts were cultured and seeded onto VSCM before transplantation. Plaque index (PI), probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), recession depth (RD), recession width (RW), and keratinized tissue width (KTW) were recorded at baseline, 1, 3, and 6 months. Secondary outcomes included Early Healing Score (EHS), Root Coverage Esthetic Score (RCES), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (V23) and p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvements in clinical parameters over time. The test group showed significantly greater CAL gain and reduction in RD and RW at all follow-up intervals, with sustained outcomes at 6 months. PI and PD remained comparable between groups. Although EHS, RCES, and KTW showed favourable trends in the test group, differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionAutologous fibroblast-seeded collagen membranes show promise as a biologically driven alternative for managing gingival recession. Further multicentric trials are warranted for long-term validation.

Introduction

The displacement of the gingival margin from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) exposing the tooth root surface is designated as gingival recession (GR). Evidence from epidemiological data suggests that GR is a common periodontal disease affecting the adult population. The initiation and progression of GR has been associated to diversified aetiological factors including plaque-mediated periodontal destruction; iatrogenic factors, namely, endodontic or prosthetic restorations, encroaching the biological width; aberrant anatomical factors, such as inadequate width of keratinized gingiva, high frenal attachment, thin cortical plates; traumatic factors, such as tongue piercings and injudicious toothbrushing habits 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

A plethora of mucogingival surgical procedures have been implemented to manage GR.6 Among them, coronally advanced flap (CAF) combined with connective tissue graft (CTG) has been considered the gold standard addressing a multitude of indications.7 In addition, other surgical techniques, including pedicle grafts, free gingival grafts, allografts, xenografts, and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) techniques, have been commonly utilised in practice 8, 9, 10. Techniques involving GTR have benefited greatly from their exclusive new attachment formation. Other benefits included a lack of requirement for second surgical site preparation and thus no donor site morbidity, and less chair-side time apart from the unlimited availability for use in multiple GR sites. Studies have also reported the formation and insertion of new connective tissue fibres into the new cementum, along with evidence of alveolar bone regeneration. Other histological studies have demonstrated less recurrence of GR with GTR techniques compared to the CAF. In addition, several alternative biomaterials have been introduced to achieve root coverage with reliable results using minimally invasive methods. These include acellular dermal matrices (ADM), xenogeneic collagen matrices, enamel matrix derivatives (EMD), and platelet aggregates.11,12 A recent study has demonstrated that EMD alone can efficiently promote new cementum formation and functionally adapted connective tissue fibers when applied to dehiscence defects in female beagle dogs.13

In addition to the aforementioned surgical techniques, tissue engineering has evolved as a viable therapeutic approach that applies principles of engineering in the controlled restoration of lost tissues by providing an unlimited supply of a triad of cells, growth factors, and signalling molecules.14 The common application involves the ‘cell expansion’ technique, which is carried out in a controlled environment and subsequently transplanted into the defect area. Previous studies have utilised this technique, focusing primarily on fibroblasts as they are the primary connective tissue, producing biomimetic factors and collagen, significant for tissue regeneration.15,16 A suitable scaffold is utilised for transplanting the fibroblasts to the defect site. These include ADM, collagen, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, etc 15, 16, 17 However, previous studies have suggested collagen membrane to be more efficacious when compared to other scaffolds.18 Moreover, autologous gingival fibroblasts seeded volume-stable collagen matrix has been suggested as a viable alternative in managing gingival recession. As most of the available collagen-based membranes lack volume stability, a new volume-stable, porous collagen matrix (VSCM) was introduced for periodontal soft tissue restorations techniques. Studies have demonstrated that the VSCM possessed high biocompatibility and exhibited favourable outcomes in the augmentation procedures of periodontal soft tissue.19,20

The paradigm shift in periodontal regeneration and the evolving tissue engineering conceptions have emphasised the possibility of using these tissue-engineered constructs for treating gingival recessions. Hence, the current study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of autologous gingival fibroblasts-seeded volume stable collagen matrix on human subjects presenting with Miller's class I/II gingival recession.

Materials and methods

Study design and protocol

A split-mouth design was employed for this randomized controlled clinical trial, enabling intra-patient comparison and minimizing variability due to individual differences. Twenty sites from 10 patients (10 males, with a mean age of 30.40 ± 5.315 years) were recruited from the Department of Periodontics, Ragas Dental College and Hospital, Chennai, India. Patients were assigned to either of the treatment groups with a computer-generated randomization sequence. The allocation sequence was concealed, and assignments were revealed only after patient enrolment and baseline measurements were completed. The investigators were calibrated to standardize procedures, and participant blinding was maintained to reduce bias. Patients were included with: presence of Miller's Class I or II bilateral gingival recession affecting the anterior teeth or premolars, no radiographic evidence of interdental bone loss, identifiable CEJ, modified plaque and bleeding scores below 20%, age between 20 and 40 years, and no history of periodontal surgery in the past 12 months. Exclusion criteria included patients on medications that could interfere with surgical or healing outcomes, pregnant or lactating women, smokers, individuals with acute gingival lesions, allergies to surgical materials or medications, non-carious cervical lesions greater than 2 mm, or palatal fibrosis. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ragas Dental College and Hospital, Ref no: EC/NEW/INST/2023/4006. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was also registered under the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI), with reference number: CTRI/2024/07/071448. The two study groups included were: Group I, which consisted of a CAF combined with a volume-stable collagen matrix (VSCM), Group II: CAF combined with autologous gingival fibroblast (AGF)-seeded volume-stable collagen matrix (FVSCM), (Geistlich Fibro-Gide®).

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using a standard formula, incorporating key statistical parameters. The standard deviation (σ) was set at 1.5, the effect size at 2, the tolerated margin of error (Z1-/α/2) at 1.96, and the power (Z1-β) at 0.84. An allocation ratio of 1:1 was applied, ensuring equal participants in both groups. This calculation resulted in a minimum sample size of 8.82, which was rounded up to 9 participants per group. To ensure adequacy, 10 participants were recruited for each group, meeting the requirements for statistical reliability.

Sample preparation and culture: in-vitro stage

After administering a local anesthetic, a palatal trap door was carefully opened to expose the underlying tissue. A connective tissue biopsy measuring ≅ 2 × 2 mm in length and breadth, with 1 mm thickness was harvested and placed in sterile 15 ml transport tubes containing Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with Penicillin, Streptomycin, and Amphotericin B to prevent microbial contamination. Once the biopsy was obtained, the trap door was repositioned and secured. Sterile gauze was applied with slight pressure to the harvested site to control bleeding. The area was sutured using 3-0 silk sutures. The tubes were then immediately transported for further processing in the cell culture laboratory.

On the day of arrival, the sample was rinsed thoroughly in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) and then transferred into a sterile six-well plate. The connective tissue samples were then sectioned into tiny fragments and placed in the growth medium containing DMEM supplemented with Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), Penicillin, Streptomycin, and Amphotericin B, in a six-well plate. The medium was added until the wells were one-fourth filled, ensuring adequate immersion of the tissue fragments for optimal growth. The six-well plate was placed in an incubator maintained at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide to create a conducive environment for tissue culture and cellular growth.

Monitoring and media replacement

The confluency of cells at the bottom of the six-well plates was monitored using a light and digital microscope every third day. The growth media were replaced at the same interval to maintain optimal conditions for cell growth. Once the cells reached 70%–80% confluency on the bottom surface of the wells, they were rinsed four to five times with PBS to remove the residual growth media. Trypsin-Phosphate-Versene-Glucose (TPVG) solution was then added to the wells to facilitate cell detachment. The six-well plate was placed in the incubator, and cell detachment was monitored every 5 min using a light and digital microscope. As the cells detached, they appeared round under the microscope. Once all cells had separated, the detached cells, along with the TPVG solution, were transferred to a 15 ml centrifuge tube for centrifugation at 3000 RPM for 3 min to separate the cells from the solution. The cells were gently resuspended using a pipette and transferred into a 25 cm2 cell culture flask containing fresh growth media with the same components as used previously. The flask was then placed in the incubator to continue the cell culture process under optimal conditions.

Seeding and preparation of the collagen matrix

At passage 3, the cells were seeded onto the volume-stable collagen matrix and incubated for 2 h to allow the gingival fibroblasts to adhere to the collagen matrix. The attachment of cells to the matrix was examined under a light and digital microscope to confirm adherence. Once proper attachment was observed, fresh growth media were introduced, and the matrix-cell construct was cultured for two days to promote further proliferation and stability. The cell-seeded matrix was washed several times with PBS to remove any residual media. It was then placed in a sterile 25 cm2 cell culture flask containing PBS, ready for safe transport to the designated center for surgical intervention. Pre-operative and post-operative clinical measurement

Clinical measurements

Clinical parameters were recorded at baseline (0 days), pre-surgical, and at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-operatively. The parameters assessed included PD, CAL, RD, RW, and KTW. The UNC 15 periodontal probe was used to measure the parameters to the nearest millimeter. Standardized photographs of the recession defect were taken at baseline, and at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-operatively. These photographs, alongside the millimeter markings on the UNC 15 probe, were used to calculate the recession area at each time point accurately.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by the same surgeon to ensure consistency across both groups. The selection of the flap design followed the criteria for the success of the Coronally Advanced Flap as described by Maynard et al., in 1977.21 According to this criterion, the tissue margins of adjacent teeth should be within 0.5–1 mm from the Cemento-Enamel Junction (CEJ).

Surgical protocol

Before the procedure, strict antisepsis was performed using betadine and 0.2% chlorhexidine (CHX). All surgeries were carried out under local anesthesia. The CAF was executed following the protocol by Bernimoulin et al. (1975).22 Paramedian incisions bordering the papillae adjacent to the recession area were made and connected with a reverse bevel incision along the gingival margin. Two vertical releasing incisions were made at the mesial and distal ends of the horizontal incision, extending into the alveolar mucosa. A full-thickness flap was elevated up to the level of the recession defect, followed by the creation of a partial-thickness flap to facilitate easy mobilization. The exposed root surface was then thoroughly root planed to ensure a clean and smooth surface. For groups I and II, the VSCM and FVSCM were trimmed and shaped to fit the exposed root surfaces, respectively. The flaps were coronally advanced to cover the collagen matrix completely and were secured using 4-0 vicryl sutures. Follow-up and maintenance protocol

On the seventh day post-surgery, participants returned for follow-up. The periodontal dressing (Coe-Pak) was carefully removed, and the surgical site was irrigated. Pain experienced by the patients was evaluated using the Early Healing Score and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). All participants were enrolled in a periodontal maintenance program, which included follow-up visits at the end of the first four weeks, as well as at the third and sixth months, to monitor healing and ensure the long-term success of the procedure.

The Early Healing Score (EHS), as described by Marini et al.,23 was assessed during the 1-week postoperative period for the three parameters, namely: Clinical Signs of Re-epithelization (CSR), Clinical Signs of Hemostasis (CSH), and Clinical Signs of Inflammation (CSI). The score was calculated as the summation of the points assigned to the three parameters, with a score of 10 indicating ideal wound healing and 0 representing the worst possible outcome. In addition, the Root Coverage Esthetic Score (RCES) by Cairo et al., evaluated five variables, namely, gingival margin, marginal tissue contour, soft tissue texture, mucogingival junction alignment, and gingival colour, at 6 months postoperatively.24 The score was similarly calculated as the summation of the points assigned to the five variables, with a score of 10 indicating ideal wound healing and 0 representing the worst possible outcome.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median with interquartile range (IQR). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which revealed that the data were not normally distributed. Hence, non-parametric statistical methods were employed for analysis.

Given the split-mouth study design, intergroup comparisons between test and control sites at each time point were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, accounting for the paired nature of observations.

Intragroup comparisons across different time intervals (baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months) were analyzed using the Friedman test for repeated measures. When the Friedman test showed statistical significance, post hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Effect size for intergroup comparisons was calculated using the rank-based measure (r), derived from the Z value of the Wilcoxon test (r = Z/√N). Effect size for intragroup comparisons was assessed using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) obtained from the Friedman test, indicating the magnitude of change over time. The categorical variable, keratinized tissue width (KTW), was expressed as frequency and percentage. Intergroup comparisons for KTW were performed using the Chi-square test. However, when the expected cell count was less than 5, Fisher's exact test was applied as appropriate. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect size analysis using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) demonstrated weak to moderate, non-significant changes in plaque index over time in both control (W = 0.255) and test groups (W = 0.122). In contrast, probing depth showed a strong effect size (W ≈ 0.80), indicating substantial reduction over time in both groups.

Clinical attachment level exhibited strong to very strong effect sizes (W = 0.791–0.900), reflecting marked improvement across follow-up periods. Recession depth demonstrated a perfect effect size (W = 1.000), indicating complete and consistent reduction across all subjects. Similarly, recession width showed very strong effect sizes (W = 0.909), confirming substantial and sustained improvement over time in both groups.

Overall, these findings indicate that the treatment resulted in clinically meaningful and consistent improvements in periodontal parameters, with the greatest magnitude of change observed in recession-related outcomes.

Discussion

Gingival recession is a frequently encountered mucogingival deformity, which poses both functional and esthetic concerns to the patients and challenges in management to the clinicians. While the conventional surgical approaches, such as connective tissue grafts and CAF, have shown predictable treatment outcomes in terms of root coverage, the recent advancements in regenerative biology have promoted the integration of tissue engineering modalities that may aid in overcoming the inherent shortcomings of autologous graft harvesting. In this study, we elucidated the clinical effectiveness of FVSCM compared to VSCM, when used with CAF. The results demonstrated statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in both the primary and the secondary outcome measures in the FVSCM group.

The PI values remained consistent among both groups throughout the several follow-up periods, showing no significant differences between them. This indicated that the incorporation of seeded fibroblast collagen matrix did not show any influence on plaque accumulation or bacterial colonisation on the treated sites. This finding aligns well with the previous study by Jhaveri et al., who also reported no difference in the level of biofilm accumulation when comparing the traditional connective tissue grafts with autologous fibroblasts seeded dermal matrix. Although probing depth (PD) reduction was significant within groups, intergroup differences were not statistically significant. This is in agreement with previous studies, where PD changes are generally minimal in recession defects due to the absence of deep periodontal pockets. Similarly, systematic evidence suggests that while collagen matrices significantly improve recession coverage and soft tissue thickness, differences in probing depth between treatment modalities are often negligible.15

In addition, the enhanced CAL gain in the FVSCM group could be attributed to the autologous fibroblasts’ regenerative capacity, which secretes several growth factors, such as PDGF and FGF, that will eventually enhance the proliferation of fibroblasts, followed by deposition of extracellular matrix, aiding in the integration of periodontium. A previous study by Koseoglu et al., showed a similar gain in CAL when treated with an AGF-seeded collagen membrane than the collagen membrane alone.18 In addition, a study by Sanchez et al., utilised a collagen scaffold incorporated with gingival-derived mesenchymal stem cells, which demonstrated a significant CAL gain in animal model experiments.25 Moreover, our previous report has suggested a substantial gain in the width of the keratinized gingiva, which was maintained throughout the 45 months of post-operative period, aligning with the current study results.26

With respect to the recession depth, the test group persistently demonstrated a greater reduction than the control group at all the follow-up intervals. The incorporation of AGF would have likely enhanced the organization of an integrated connective tissue network over the denuded root surface, bolstering the previously demonstrated histological evidence of fibroblast-induced tissue healing. This finding is also corroborated by the report of Milinkovic et al., who showed an effective recession coverage using collagen constructs seeded with AGF.14 In addition, the recession width also showed a significant reduction in the test group, demonstrating complete resolution at the sixth month post-operative period. This reflects on the volumetric stability and the remodelling ability of the matrix scaffold. The persistent maintenance of the volume rendered by VSCM attributes to this outcome, and the AGF probably enhanced the remodelling of the grafted site. These results were in alignment with those of Koseoglu et al., who also reported a similar reduction in recession depth and width in AGF-treated sites.18 In addition, Ardakani et al., have reported that human gingival fibroblasts showed better survival and adhesion on natural collagen membranes derived from porcine or human sources compared to synthetic cross-linked collagen membranes.27 The phase contrast microscopic images confirmed the successful institution of the primary human gingival fibroblast cultures, showcasing their characteristic spindle-shaped appearance and spreading on the collagen matrix. This in vitro evidence bolsters the clinical improvements observed in the current study. It also precisely demonstrates the proliferation, the viability, and the integration potential of the seeded cells, which are directly attributed to the enhanced tissue regeneration obtained in the current study.

The keratinized tissue is a significant clinical parameter that influences the long-term stability of root coverage outcomes. The progressive increase in keratinized tissue width (KTW) observed in the test group, although not statistically significant, indicates a favourable biological trend. Increased gingival thickness and keratinized tissue have been associated with improved long-term stability of root coverage procedures. Recent studies have similarly reported gains in gingival thickness and keratinized tissue when collagen matrices are used, particularly when combined with biologically active agents.16,18,25,26 The gain achieved in the current study could be attributed to the initial immune-inflammatory responses and the scaffold-associated volumetric augmentation, following the surgical intervention. In addition, the presence of an AGF-seeded collagen membrane would have contributed to the formation of granulation tissue and matrix deposition, enhancing the thickness of the keratinized tissue.

With respect to secondary outcomes, the test group demonstrated higher Early Healing Scores (EHS) and Root Coverage Esthetic Scores (RCES), although these differences did not reach statistical significance. The improved esthetic trend observed in the FVSCM group may be explained by enhanced tissue quality and integration associated with fibroblast activity. This enhanced healing response could also be attributed to the angiogenic and anti-inflammatory potential of cytokines such as IL-10 and VEGF, which are fibroblast derivatives. This may, in turn, facilitate neovascularisation and re-epithelialization. The clinical reliability of EHS in apprehending the early tissue healing responses was demonstrated previously by Marini et al., in which they substantiated the use of biomimetic scaffolds for enhancing favourable healing kinetics.23 The enhanced RCES in the test group, could have stemmed from the orchestrated gingival tissue regeneration mediated by the fibroblasts. They are also known to regulate the collagen fibres' alignment and influence the dermal architecture by upregulating matrix deposition, including collagen, proteoglycans, hyaluronic acid, elastin, and laminin, which are necessary for achieving harmonious aesthetics.14,28

By and large, results of the current randomised controlled clinical trial provide an affirmation of the regenerative potential of autologous gingival fibroblasts seeded on collagen membrane as a viable alternative to conventional bio-scaffolds for managing gingival recession. The enhanced clinical attachment gain, favourable tissue healing kinetics, and improved aesthetics exhibited by the test group underscore the biological potential of integrating the autologous cellular components into biomimetic constructs. The study's findings emphasise the constructive synergy of cell-based therapy and scaffold designing, rendering a forward-looking strategy in mucogingival surgical procedures.

Limitations and future directions

The study, however, has a few limitations; the small sample size and short follow-up may limit the actual interpretation of the outcomes. Studies should be conducted to address these limitations by undertaking multicentric designs and extended period observation, along with histological evaluation to validate the long-term efficacy and translational feasibility of such cell-based constructs in periodontal regeneration.

Conclusion

The promising results demonstrated in this current study underscore the potential of autologous fibroblast-seeded collagen membrane as a biologically driven viable alternative for managing gingival recession. However, the clinical application of tissue engineering concepts to periodontics remains in the translational inceptive phase, further necessitating larger multicentric randomised trials with extended follow-up to validate the long-term predictability and clinical stability of such constructs.
