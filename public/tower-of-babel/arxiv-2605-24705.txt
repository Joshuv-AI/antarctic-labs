Spectral Obstructions to Contracting Transport Maps on Curved Spaces
Shrey Aryan

Abstract
. Caffarelli’s contraction theorem states that the Brenier optimal transport map from the standard Gaussian measure to a more log-concave probability measure is 1 1 -Lipschitz. Owing to its many applications in analysis, probability, and geometry, the problem of extending this theorem to curved spaces has appeared repeatedly in the literature, going back to Villani [ 18 ] . More recently, Milman [ 15 ] formulated precise conjectures in this direction. In this work, we construct counterexamples to these conjectures.

1.
Introduction

In
[
2
]
, Caffarelli proved the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(cf. Theorem 11
[
2
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
, and let
\gamma_{d}
denote the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\gamma_{d}(\mathrm{d}x)
and
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-W(x)}\gamma_{d}(\mathrm{d}x)
are two probability measures where
W
is a convex function, then there exists a convex function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
T=\nabla\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz, and transports
\mu
to
\nu
, i.e.
T_{\#}\mu=\nu.

The Gaussian space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})
can be regarded as a weighted
manifold with synthetic dimension
\infty
and constant Bakry–Emery
curvature
1
. In particular, it satisfies the curvature-dimension
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
condition (cf. Theorem 0.12 in
[
13
]
) and therefore one can view Theorem
1.1
as a statement that asks for contracting volume-preserving maps from the Gaussian space to a possibly more curved space (cf. p. 514 in
[
18
]
). In
[
15
]
, Milman formulated precise conjectures to generalize this observation on finite and infinite dimensional spaces:

Conjecture 1

(cf. Conjecture
3

[
15
]
)
.

For any
(\mathbb{S}^{d},g,\operatorname{vol}_{g})
satisfying
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq\rho g
with
\rho>0
, we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g,\operatorname{vol}_{g})\geq\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}},\operatorname{vol}_{g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}})\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

where
g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}
denotes the canonical metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
rescaled to have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}}=\rho g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}.

Conjecture 2

(cf. Conjecture
4

[
15
]
)
.

For any
(\mathbb{S}^{d},g,\operatorname{vol}_{g})
satisfying
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq\rho g
with
\rho>0
, there exists a map

(1.3)

\displaystyle T:(\mathbb{S}^{d},g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}},\operatorname{vol}_{g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}})\to(\mathbb{S}^{d},g,\operatorname{vol}_{g}),

pushing forward
\operatorname{vol}_{g^{\rho}_{\mathrm{can}}}
onto
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
up to a finite constant and contracting the corresponding metrics.

Conjecture 3

(cf. Conjecture
1^{*}

[
15
]
)
.

For any
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)
satisfying
\operatorname{CD}(\rho,\infty)
with
\rho>0
, we have

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)\geq\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}_{\rho})\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

where
\gamma_{\rho}^{d}
denotes the centered Gaussian probability measure

(1.5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rho}^{d}(\mathrm{d}x):=c_{\rho}^{d}e^{-\rho|x|^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
c_{\rho}^{d}>0
is a normalization constant.

Conjecture 4

(cf. Conjecture
2^{*}

[
15
]
)
.

For any
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)
satisfying
\operatorname{CD}(\rho,\infty)
with
\rho>0
, there exists a map

(1.6)

\displaystyle T:(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}_{\rho})\to(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu),

pushing forward
\gamma^{d}_{\rho}
onto
\mu
up to a finite constant and contracting the corresponding metrics. Here
\gamma_{\rho}^{d}
denotes the centered Gaussian probability measure

(1.7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rho}^{d}(\mathrm{d}x):=c_{\rho}^{d}e^{-\rho|x|^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
c_{\rho}^{d}>0
is a normalization constant.

The main result of this work shows that all the above conjectures are false in general. In particular,

Theorem 1
.

Let
d\geq 4
. Then there exists a smooth Riemannian metric
g
on

\mathbb{S}^{d}
, a number
\rho>0
, and an integer
K\geq 1
such that

(1.8)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq\rho g

but

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lambda_{K}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g)<\lambda_{K}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g_{\mathrm{can}}^{\rho}).

The above result implies that Conjecture 3 in
[
15
]
is false in general. Next, in the
\operatorname{CD}(\rho,\infty)
setting, we show

Theorem 2
.

For every
d\geq 4
, there is a smooth complete weighted manifold
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)
satisfying
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
such that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)<2=\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}).

Thus, Conjecture
1^{*}
in
[
15
]
is false in general. Combining Theorems
1
and
2
with Theorem
2.1
, we also deduce that Conjectures
2^{*}
and 4 are false in general; see Corollaries
3.2
and
4.2
.

Although the conjectures above are false in general, several positive
generalizations of Caffarelli’s theorem are known. These include results on
infinite-dimensional spaces
[
7
,
14
]
,
results for
1/d
-concave densities
[
3
]
, and recent
results in curved settings

[
6
,
12
,
9
]
. Kim and
Milman
[
11
]
used the heat flow to construct
Lipschitz transport maps and gave an alternative proof of Caffarelli’s theorem.
Entropic optimal transport has also led to new proofs and extensions

[
4
,
5
]
.

Besides the Caffarelli contraction theorem, our interest in contracting
transport maps on curved spaces was also motivated by a conjecture of Yau, which states that if

\Sigma^{2}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
is a closed embedded minimal hypersurface,
then its first nonzero Laplace–Beltrami eigenvalue is
2
(cf.
[
20
]
). Using the convention
0=\lambda_{1}(\Sigma)\leq\lambda_{2}(\Sigma)\leq\cdots,
one can show that this is equivalent to
\lambda_{2}(\Sigma)\geq 2
since the coordinate functions restricted to
\Sigma
are eigenfunctions with eigenvalue
2
.
For
n=2
, a contracting measure-preserving map

T:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\Sigma
would imply, by Theorem
2.1
, that

(1.11)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Sigma)\geq\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

which in particular would imply the desired estimate when
k=2.
However, this stronger conclusion is false in general. For instance, for the Clifford
torus

(1.12)

\displaystyle\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\subset\mathbb{S}^{3},

we have (cf. Remark 2
\mathrm{(ii)}
in
[
16
]
)

(1.13)

\displaystyle\lambda_{5}(\Sigma)=2<6=\lambda_{5}(\mathbb{S}^{2}).

The fact that a product structure as above can give rise to high multiplicity of small eigenvalues is the key observation behind the construction of the counterexamples in this work.

2.
Preliminaries

A weighted manifold is a triple
(M^{d},g,\mu)
, where
(M,g)
is a complete
smooth Riemannian manifold,

\mathrm{d}\mu=e^{-W}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g},

and
W
is smooth. The weighted Laplacian is defined as:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g,\mu}f=\Delta_{g}f-\langle\nabla_{g}W,\nabla_{g}f\rangle_{g}.

We use the nonnegative operator
-\Delta_{g,\mu}
and write its variational eigenvalues in non-decreasing order as

0=\lambda_{1}(M,g,\mu)\leq\lambda_{2}(M,g,\mu)\leq\cdots,

including multiplicity. The Bakry–Emery curvature tensor is

\operatorname{Ric}_{g,\mu}:=\operatorname{Ric}_{g}+\nabla_{g}^{2}W.

From Theorem 0.12
[
13
]
, a weighted manifold
(M^{d},g,\mu)
is said to satisfy curvature-dimension condition
\operatorname{CD}(\rho,\infty)
for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g}+\nabla_{g}^{2}W\geq\rho g.

Let
\gamma^{d}(\mathrm{d}x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x
denote the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the weighted Laplacian being the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator whose first few eigenvalues satisfy (cf. Section 2.1 in
[
15
]
)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})=0,\qquad\lambda_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})=\cdots=\lambda_{d+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})=1,\qquad\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})=2.

Note that
(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})
is
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty).
Next, we recall the following theorem due to Milman
[
15
]
that relates spectral comparison between weighted manifolds to the Lipschitz transport maps.

Theorem 2.1

(Contraction Principle)
.

Let

T:(M_{1},g_{1},\mu_{1})\to(M_{2},g_{2},\mu_{2})

denote an
L
-Lipschitz map between two complete weighted manifolds
pushing forward
\mu_{1}
onto
\mu_{2}
up to a finite constant. Then

\lambda_{k}(M_{2},g_{2},\mu_{2})\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\lambda_{k}(M_{1},g_{1},\mu_{1})\qquad\forall k\geq 1.

In particular, a
1
-Lipschitz transport map from a model space to a target space implies that the spectrum of the target space dominates that of the source.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

Let
d\geq 4
. We denote the round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}=\{(z,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:|z|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1\}
and will write
z=x_{1}+\mathrm{i}x_{2}
. Consider the map

(3.1)

\displaystyle F:(0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\to\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle F(t,\theta,\omega)=(e^{i\theta}\sin t,(\cos t)\omega).

Then
F
maps into the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and is a diffeomorphism onto the open set
U:=\{(z,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:z\neq 0,\ y\neq 0\}.
Let
g_{0}
denote the induced Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, then in local coordinates on
U
, we can write the metric
g_{0}
using the pull-back metric
F^{*}g_{0}
as follows:

(3.3)

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}+|\mathrm{d}y|^{2}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=|e^{i\theta}(\cos t)\mathrm{d}t+ie^{i\theta}(\sin t)\mathrm{d}\theta|^{2}+|-(\sin t)\omega\mathrm{d}t+(\cos t)\mathrm{d}\omega|^{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}t\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sin^{2}t\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}t\mathrm{d}t^{2}+(\cos^{2}t)g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sin^{2}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\cos^{2}t\,g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}},

where in the above expression we used the orthogonality of
e^{i\theta}
and
ie^{i\theta}
with respect to the real Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the fact
|\omega|^{2}=1
which after differentiating implies that
\langle\omega,\mathrm{d}\omega\rangle=0
.

The idea is to perturb this metric slightly in the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-direction. As
expected, this lowers the Ricci curvature lower bound from
d-1
to some value
\rho
, but it will also drop the eigenvalues of the perturbed manifold below that of the sphere with the round metric and
\operatorname{Ric}=\rho g.
Note that,
g_{0}=g_{\mathrm{can}}
where
g_{\mathrm{can}}
denotes the canonical round metric on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and
\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{0}}=(d-1)g_{0}.

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
1
, we record a
useful lemma (cf. Lemma 2.3 in
[
10
]
) which we will use to
compute the Ricci curvature of warped product metrics.

Lemma 3.1

(Ricci tensor of a multiple warped product)
.

Let
(B,g_{B})
and
(F_{i},g_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq m
, be Riemannian
manifolds, and let
b_{i}:B\to(0,\infty)
be smooth positive functions. Let
M=B\times_{b_{1}}F_{1}\times\cdots\times_{b_{m}}F_{m}
be the multiply warped product with metric

g=g_{B}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}g_{i}.

Write
s_{i}=\dim F_{i}.
Let
X,Y
be vector fields tangent to the base
B
, and let
V_{i},W_{i}

be vector fields tangent to the fiber
F_{i}
, all lifted to
M
. Then the
Ricci tensor of
g
is given as follows:

(1)

\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Ric}_{B}(X,Y)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{s_{i}}{b_{i}}\operatorname{Hess}_{B}b_{i}(X,Y).

(2)

\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(X,V_{i})=0.

(3)

if

i\neq j
, then

\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(V_{i},V_{j})=0.

(4)

for each
1\leq i\leq m
we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(V_{i},W_{i})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ric}_{F_{i}}(V_{i},W_{i})

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad-g(V_{i},W_{i})\left[\frac{\Delta_{B}b_{i}}{b_{i}}+(s_{i}-1)\frac{|\nabla_{B}b_{i}|^{2}}{b_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{m}s_{k}\frac{\langle\nabla_{B}b_{i},\nabla_{B}b_{k}\rangle_{B}}{b_{i}b_{k}}\right].

Here all gradients, Hessians, Laplacians, and inner products appearing on
the right-hand side are computed with respect to the base metric
g_{B}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
d\geq 4
. Our proof proceeds in three main steps.

\mathrm{(i)}

Construction of the metric

For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, define a metric on the coordinate chart
U
as

(3.9)

g_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sin^{2}t\bigl(1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t\bigr)\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\cos^{2}t\,g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}.

This is a smooth, well-defined metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
as can be seen by rewriting

(3.10)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=g_{0}+\varepsilon|z|^{2}\alpha\otimes\alpha,

where
\alpha=x_{1}\,dx_{2}-x_{2}\,dx_{1}=\sin^{2}t\mathrm{d}\theta
and we used the fact that
|z|^{2}=\sin^{2}t.

\mathrm{(ii)}

Ricci curvature lower bound

We now estimate its Ricci tensor. Set

(3.11)

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(t):=(\sin t)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t},\quad c(t):=\cos t

Then, working in a local orthonormal frame with basis vectors
e_{t}=\partial_{t}
,
e_{\theta}=\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}(t)}\partial_{\theta}
and
e_{j}=\frac{1}{\cos t}v_{j}
where
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d-2}
is a local
g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}
-orthonormal frame on the
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
-factor, we can denote

(3.12)

\displaystyle R_{t}=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(e_{t},e_{t}),\quad R_{\theta}=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(e_{\theta},e_{\theta}),\quad R_{S}=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(e_{j},e_{j})\text{ for }1\leq j\leq d-2.

Note that the last term is independent of any
1\leq j\leq d-2
since the Ricci curvature of
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
is a constant multiple of the
g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}.
Using Lemma
3.1
we can compute each term as follows. First,

(3.13)

\displaystyle-\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}=1-\varepsilon\left(\frac{\sin^{2}t(10-12\sin^{2}t)+2\varepsilon\sin^{6}t(3-4\sin^{2}t)}{(1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t)^{2}}\right),

and

(3.14)

\displaystyle\tan t\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}=1+\frac{2\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}{1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}.

We shall also use the elementary estimate

(3.15)

\displaystyle f(q):=\frac{q(10-12q)+2\varepsilon q^{3}(3-4q)}{(1+\varepsilon q^{2})^{2}}\leq\frac{25}{12}

for
q\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{25}{12}(1+\varepsilon q^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle-\left(q(10-12q)+2\varepsilon q^{3}(3-4q)\right)

(3.16)

\displaystyle=12\left(q-\frac{5}{12}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon q^{2}\left(8q^{2}-6q+\frac{25}{6}\right)+\frac{25}{12}\varepsilon^{2}q^{4}\geq 0,

because
8q^{2}-6q+\frac{25}{6}>0
for all
q\in[0,1]
.
Thus

(3.17)

\displaystyle R_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ric}_{B}(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{s_{i}}{b_{i}}\operatorname{Hess}_{B}b_{i}(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})

(3.19)

\displaystyle=0-\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{(d-2)c^{\prime\prime}}{c}

(3.20)

\displaystyle=1-\varepsilon f(\sin^{2}t)+(d-2)

(3.21)

\displaystyle\geq(d-1)-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12},

where
B=(0,\pi/2)
,
s_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{S}^{1}=1
,
s_{2}=\dim\mathbb{S}^{d-2}=(d-2)
,
b_{1}=a_{\varepsilon}
, and
b_{2}=c
. Similarly, applying Lemma
3.1
to the

\mathbb{S}^{1}
-factor with
B=(0,\pi/2)
,
F_{1}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
F_{2}=\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
,

b_{1}=a_{\varepsilon}
,
b_{2}=c
,
s_{1}=\dim\mathbb{S}^{1}=1
, and

s_{2}=\dim\mathbb{S}^{d-2}=d-2
and using (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle R_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(e_{\theta},e_{\theta})=\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\left[\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{\theta})-g_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{\theta})\left(\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}+(d-2)\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}c}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}-(d-2)\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}+(d-2)\tan t\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=(d-2)+\frac{2(d-2)\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}{1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}+1-\varepsilon f(\sin^{2}t)

(3.22)

\displaystyle\geq d-1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12}.

Lastly, applying Lemma

3.1
to the
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
-factor gives

\displaystyle R_{S}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(e_{j},e_{j})=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(v_{j},v_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left[\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}(v_{j},v_{j})-g_{\varepsilon}(v_{j},v_{j})\left(\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c}+(d-3)\frac{(c^{\prime})^{2}}{c^{2}}+\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}c}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d-3}{c^{2}}-\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c}-(d-3)\frac{(c^{\prime})^{2}}{c^{2}}-\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}c}

\displaystyle=d-2+\tan t\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{a_{\varepsilon}}

(3.23)

\displaystyle=d-2+1+\frac{2\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}{1+\varepsilon\sin^{4}t}\geq d-1.

Combining the above lower bounds on
R_{t}
,
R_{\theta}
and
R_{S}
implies that

(3.24)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\geq\left(d-1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12}\right)g_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{on }U.

By continuity the above inequality holds on all of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, i.e.

(3.25)

\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\geq\left(d-1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12}\right)g_{\varepsilon}.

Set
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=d-1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12}.
For
d\geq 2
,
\rho_{\varepsilon}>0
for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.

\mathrm{(iii)}

Construction of test functions

We finally show the desired spectral inequality. To achieve this goal, we will
construct suitable test functions. Consider the Rayleigh quotient,

(3.26)

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla f|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}.\end{split}

The simplest test functions to try would be to take functions of the form
|z|^{k}
, however to take advantage of the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
factor in the metric, we will consider for
k\geq 1
, the function

(3.27)

\displaystyle f_{k}=\operatorname{Re}(z^{k})=q^{k/2}\cos(k\theta),\text{ where }q=\sin^{2}t.

The
\cos(k\theta)
term in
f_{k}
will turn out to be advantageous. Observe that

(3.28)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f_{k}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}q^{k}\cos^{2}(k\theta)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}q^{k}\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}

where we used the fact that
\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}},
and the fact that
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2}(k\theta)=\pi=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta
. Then denote
Q_{j}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}q^{j}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}.
Using
\sqrt{1+x}\geq 1+\frac{x}{2}-\frac{x^{2}}{8}
and the above expression, we get

(3.30)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f_{k}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{Q_{k}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon Q_{k+2}}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}Q_{k+4}}{16}.

Next, we estimate the numerator. Since,
q=\sin^{2}t
we have

\partial_{t}f_{k}=kq^{(k-1)/2}\sqrt{1-q}\,\cos(k\theta),\qquad\partial_{\theta}f_{k}=-kq^{k/2}\sin(k\theta),

we have

|\nabla f_{k}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}=k^{2}q^{k-1}(1-q)\cos^{2}(k\theta)+\frac{k^{2}q^{k-1}}{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\sin^{2}(k\theta).

Thus,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla f_{k}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}k^{2}q^{k-1}(1-q)\cos^{2}(k\theta)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}

(3.32)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\frac{k^{2}q^{k-1}}{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\sin^{2}(k\theta)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}

(3.33)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}q^{k-1}(1-q)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}+\frac{k^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\frac{q^{k-1}}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k^{2}}{2}\left(2Q_{k-1}-Q_{k}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}Q_{k+2}+\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{8}Q_{k+3}\right)

(3.35)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}k(k+d-1)Q_{k}-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}Q_{k+2}+\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}}{16}Q_{k+3}

where we used the elementary inequality for
q\in[0,1]
,

(3.36)

\displaystyle(1-q)\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon q^{2}}}\leq 2-q-\frac{\varepsilon q^{3}}{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}q^{4}}{8}

and the fact that the sum of the squares of the first two coordinate functions on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
namely
q=|z|^{2}
, follows a beta distribution
\operatorname{Beta}(1,\frac{d-1}{2})
(cf. Corollary 1.1
[
8
]
). This implies, in particular, that (cf. Equation (2.2) in
[
17
]
)

(3.37)

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{k-1}}=\frac{2k}{2k+d-1},\quad 2Q_{k-1}-Q_{k}=\left(\frac{k+d-1}{k}\right)Q_{k}.

Define

(3.38)

\displaystyle\beta_{d,k}:=\frac{k^{2}+k(k+d-1)}{2k(k+d-1)}\frac{Q_{k+2}}{Q_{k}}.

Since

(3.39)

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{k+2}}{Q_{k}}=\frac{2(k+1)}{2(k+1)+d-1}\cdot\frac{2(k+2)}{2(k+2)+d-1}\to 1,\text{ as }k\to\infty

we see that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\beta_{d,k}=1
. Therefore, since
d\geq 4
, we may choose
k\geq k_{0}
for some
k_{0}\gg 1
such that

(3.40)

\displaystyle\eta_{d,k}:=\beta_{d,k}-\frac{25}{12(d-1)}>0.

For some fixed
k\geq k_{0}
, define

(3.41)

\displaystyle\Phi_{d,k}(\varepsilon):=\frac{\frac{1}{2}k(k+d-1)Q_{k}-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}Q_{k+2}+\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}}{16}Q_{k+3}}{\frac{Q_{k}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon Q_{k+2}}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}Q_{k+4}}{16}}.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the denominator is positive and
(
3.28
) and (
3.31
) imply

(3.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f_{k})\leq\Phi_{d,k}(\varepsilon).

Moreover,
\Phi_{d,k}
is smooth near
\varepsilon=0
and

(3.43)

\displaystyle\Phi_{d,k}(0)

\displaystyle=k(k+d-1),

(3.44)

\displaystyle\Phi_{d,k}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k^{2}+k(k+d-1)}{2}\frac{Q_{k+2}}{Q_{k}}=-k(k+d-1)\beta_{d,k}.

Thus there is a constant
C_{d,k}<\infty
such that, for all sufficiently small

\varepsilon>0
,

(3.45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f_{k})\leq k(k+d-1)(1-\beta_{d,k}\varepsilon)+C_{d,k}\varepsilon^{2}.

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
further so that

(3.46)

\displaystyle C_{d,k}\varepsilon^{2}<\frac{1}{2}k(k+d-1)\eta_{d,k}\varepsilon,

we get

(3.47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f_{k})

\displaystyle<k(k+d-1)\left(1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12(d-1)}\right)

(3.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{d-1}k(k+d-1),

where we recall that
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=d-1-\frac{25\varepsilon}{12}.

Define the following real vector spaces

(3.49)

\displaystyle E_{k-1}:=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{k-1}H_{\ell},\quad F_{k}:=E_{k-1}\oplus\operatorname{span}\{f_{k}\}

where

is the space of spherical harmonics of degree

. Note that

is orthogonal to
H_{\ell}
for any

since, after complexifying

can be expressed as a linear combination of

for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

and some constants

as follows:

(3.50)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}h_{\ell}f_{k}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha+\beta+|\gamma|=\ell}c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}\mathrm{Re}(z^{k})\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

(3.51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha+\beta+|\gamma|=\ell}\frac{c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}(z^{k}+\bar{z}^{k})\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

(3.52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha+\beta+|\gamma|=\ell}c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha+k}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha}\bar{z}^{\beta+k}y^{\gamma}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\right].

Both of the above integrals vanish. This can be seen by making the change of variables

(z,y)\mapsto(e^{i\tau}z,y)
which preserves the volume form
\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

(3.53)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha+k}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=e^{i(\alpha+k-\beta)\tau}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha+k}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}.

Since
\alpha+\beta+|\gamma|=\ell\leq k-1,
we get
\alpha+k-\beta\neq 0.
Choosing

\tau

e^{i(\alpha+k-\beta)\tau}\neq 1

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha+k}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}=0.

Similarly,

(3.54)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}z^{\alpha}\bar{z}^{\beta+k}y^{\gamma}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, for any
0\leq\ell\leq k-1
, we have

(3.55)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}h_{\ell}f_{k}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}=0.

We now claim that

(3.56)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\neq 0,f\in F_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)<\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{d-1}k(k+d-1).

To this end, take any

and write

, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
h_{k-1}\in E_{k-1}
. Then

(3.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(h_{k-1}+af_{k})

(3.58)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla h_{k-1}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+a^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla f_{k}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|h_{k-1}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+a^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f_{k}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}

(3.59)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(h_{k-1}),\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f_{k})\},

where we used the fact that for any
h_{k-1}\in E_{k-1}
,

(3.60)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\langle\nabla h_{k-1},\nabla f_{k}\rangle_{g_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(\partial_{t}h_{k-1})(\partial_{t}f_{k})\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

(3.61)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon q^{2})}(\partial_{\theta}h_{k-1})(\partial_{\theta}f_{k})+\frac{1}{\cos^{2}t}\langle\nabla_{\omega}h_{k-1},\nabla_{\omega}f_{k}\rangle\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}

(3.62)

\displaystyle=0.

Indeed,
f_{k}
is independent of
\omega
, so the last term vanishes. For a monomial

z^{\alpha}\bar{z}^{\beta}y^{\gamma}
of degree
\ell\leq k-1
, the
\partial_{t}
- and

\partial_{\theta}
-terms above are finite sums of terms with
\theta
-weights

e^{i(\alpha-\beta+k)\theta}
or
e^{i(\alpha-\beta-k)\theta}
, multiplied by
functions of
t
and
\omega
only. Since
\alpha+\beta+|\gamma|=\ell\leq k-1
, both

\alpha-\beta+k
and
\alpha-\beta-k
are nonzero. Their
\theta
-integrals vanish,
and the claim follows by linearity. We already have an upper bound for
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f_{k})
so we focus on
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(h_{k-1}).
To this end, first observe that for the Rayleigh quotient for the canonical round metric on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we have

(3.63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}(h)\leq(k-1)(k+d-2)

for any
h\in E_{k-1}
since

(3.64)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{g_{0}}h_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+d-1)h_{\ell}

for any
h_{\ell}\in H_{\ell}
and
0\leq\ell\leq k-1.
Furthermore, for any function
h\in E_{k-1}
and for sufficiently small

we have

(3.65)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla h|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}h^{2}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}}}

(3.66)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|\nabla h|_{g_{0}}^{2}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}h^{2}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{0}}}

(3.67)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h)

(3.68)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)(k-1)(k+d-2)

(3.69)

\displaystyle<\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{d-1}k(k+d-1),

where the last inequality follows by choosing
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
where
\varepsilon_{0}:=\frac{2k+d-2}{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)(k+d-2)+\frac{25}{12(d-1)}k(k+d-1)}
. Therefore, by (
3.47
) and (
3.65
) we have

(3.70)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\neq 0,f\in F_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)<\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{d-1}k(k+d-1).

Define
K:=\operatorname{dim}E_{k-1}+1
, and
g^{\rho_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{can}}:=\frac{d-1}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}g_{0}.
Then,

(3.71)

\displaystyle\lambda_{K}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g^{\rho_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{can}})=\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{d-1}k(k+d-1).

However, by the min–max characterization of eigenvalues we have

(3.72)

\displaystyle\lambda_{K}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g_{\varepsilon})<\lambda_{K}(\mathbb{S}^{d},g^{\rho_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{can}}),

which completes the proof.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
2

The idea behind Theorem
2
is to deform the Euclidean
metric in polar coordinates. Recall that the standard metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

is

\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

so the spherical slices
\{r=\mathrm{constant}\}
have radius
r
and keep
expanding as
r
increases. In our construction, we instead choose a
rotationally symmetric metric for which these slices expand in the usual way
near the origin, so that the metric closes smoothly, but then their radius
becomes constant and equal to
\sqrt{d-2}
. Thus the end of the manifold is
cylindrical, with cross-section
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d-2})
. This cylinder is the
source of the spectral obstruction: its cross-section has first nonzero
eigenvalue

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d-1}{d-2}<2,

attained by the
d
coordinate functions restricted to

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d-2})
, while the cylindrical direction produces one
additional low-energy radial mode. Finally, we put a quadratic potential in the
cylindrical direction, so that the end carries a Gaussian weight. The weight makes the total measure of the space finite and provides enough
curvature for the curvature–dimension condition

\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
. Thus, the constructed weighted
manifold satisfies the curvature-dimension lower bound, but its cylindrical end
produces
d+1
nonzero test functions with Rayleigh quotient below the
Gaussian threshold
2
, forcing

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)<2=\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}).

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
2
, we record a
technical smoothing lemma used to construct the torpedo function (cf.
[
1
]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\delta>0
,
B=\frac{\pi\delta}{2}
, and
0<\varepsilon<B/2
. There exists a smooth function

\theta_{\varepsilon}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\pi/2]
such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\delta}\qquad\text{when }0\leq r\leq B-\varepsilon,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\qquad\text{when }r\geq B+\varepsilon,

with
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0
,
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\leq\delta^{-1}
, and
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
.

Proof.

Define

\psi(s)=\begin{cases}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{1-4s^{2}}\right),&|s|<\dfrac{1}{2},\\[6.0pt]
0,&|s|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Then
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((-1,1))
,
\psi\geq 0
,
\psi
is even, and

\psi
is not identically zero. Set

D:=\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u>0

and define

(4.5)

\displaystyle\eta(s)=\frac{1}{D}\int_{s}^{1}\psi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Since
\psi\geq 0
, we have
0\leq\eta\leq 1
, and

\eta^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\psi(s)}{D}\leq 0.

Moreover, since
\psi
is supported in
[-1/2,1/2]
, we have

\eta(s)=1\quad\text{for }s\leq-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\eta(s)=0\quad\text{for }s\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus
\eta
is constant near
s=-1
and near
s=1
. Finally, since

\psi
is even, we have

\eta(-s)=1-\eta(s).

Therefore,

\int_{-1}^{1}\eta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl(\eta(s)+\eta(-s)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s=1.

Define

(4.6)

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{r}{\delta},&0\leq r\leq B-\varepsilon,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{B-\varepsilon}{\delta}+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{(r-B)/\varepsilon}\eta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,&B-\varepsilon\leq r\leq B+\varepsilon,\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{\pi}{2},&r\geq B+\varepsilon.\end{cases}

When
B-\varepsilon\leq r\leq B+\varepsilon
, we have

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\delta}\eta\!\left(\frac{r-B}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\eta^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{r-B}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq 0.

Since
0\leq\eta\leq 1
, this gives

0\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\leq\delta^{-1}.

The same inequalities are immediate on the two outer regions, where

\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)=r/\delta
and
\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)=\pi/2
, respectively. Thus

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0
,
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\leq\delta^{-1}
, and

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
on all of
[0,\infty)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
d\geq 4
and let
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. The argument proceeds in a similar style as in the proof of Theorem
1
.

\mathrm{(i)}

Construction of the metric

Set

(4.7)

\displaystyle\delta=\sqrt{d-2},\qquad B=\frac{\pi\delta}{2}.

Following the notation of
[
1
, Definition 2.4]
, we use a
torpedo function
\rho_{\delta}
. Since we require more precise estimates on

\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, we define it explicitly. Let

\theta_{\varepsilon}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\pi/2]
be the function from Lemma

4.1
, and define

(4.8)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)=\delta\sin\theta_{\varepsilon}(r).

Then

(4.9)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\delta\sin(r/\delta)\qquad\text{when }0\leq r\leq B-\varepsilon,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\delta\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{when }r\geq B+\varepsilon.

Moreover, since

(4.11)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}=\delta\cos\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}-\delta\sin\theta_{\varepsilon}\,(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2},

and
0\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}\leq\pi/2
, we have
\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
. Thus

\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}
is a torpedo function in the sense of

[
1
, Definition 2.4]
, except that we keep track of the
transition scale
\varepsilon
.
We now define the metric directly on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,
write

(4.12)

\displaystyle x=r\omega,\qquad r=|x|,\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Define

(4.13)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

where
g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is the canonical round metric on the unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. As
r\to 0
we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)=\delta\sin(r/\delta)=r-\frac{r^{3}}{6\delta^{2}}+O(r^{5}),

the metric smoothly extends across the origin as a rotationally symmetric metric
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover,

(4.15)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\delta^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\qquad\text{when }r\geq B+\varepsilon.

This also shows that
g_{\varepsilon}
is cylindrical at infinity and hence complete.

\mathrm{(ii)}

Construction of the measure

Next, we will define a suitable weight which will be quadratic on the cylindrical end. To this end, choose a smooth function
q_{\varepsilon}:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such
that

(4.16)

\displaystyle q_{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{on }[0,B-2\varepsilon],\qquad q_{\varepsilon}=1\quad\text{on }[B-\varepsilon,\infty).

Then define
V_{\varepsilon}
as

(4.17)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}(r-u)q_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,\qquad V_{\varepsilon}\geq 0,\qquad V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0.

Set

(4.18)

\displaystyle\nu_{\varepsilon}=e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}},\qquad Z_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon},\qquad\mu_{\varepsilon}=Z_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\nu_{\varepsilon}.

Since
V_{\varepsilon}
is bounded from below by a quadratic function in
r
for

r\geq B-\varepsilon
, the normalizing constant
Z_{\varepsilon}<\infty
.

\mathrm{(iii)}

Verification of the
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
condition

We now show that
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
is
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
. Let

\partial_{r}
denote the unit vector in the radial direction and let

X\perp\partial_{r}
be a
g_{\varepsilon}
-unit vector. Since the potential

V_{\varepsilon}=V_{\varepsilon}(r)
is radial, the Bakry–Emery tensor

(4.19)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}:=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{2}V_{\varepsilon}

is given by (cf. proof of Proposition 2 in
[
19
]
)

(4.20)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{r},\partial_{r})

\displaystyle=-(d-1)\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}+V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime},

(4.21)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{r},X)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(X,X)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}+(d-2)\frac{(1-(\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2})}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}+\frac{V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}.

Since

(4.23)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0,\qquad\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0,

and since

(4.24)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\delta\cos\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime},

we have from Lemma
4.1

(4.25)

\displaystyle(\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}\leq\cos^{2}\theta_{\varepsilon}=1-\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

Since
\delta^{2}=d-2
, we get

(4.26)

\displaystyle(d-2)\bigl(1-(\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}\bigr)\geq\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}.

Since also
-\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
, we get

(4.27)

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}+(d-2)\frac{1-(\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-2)(1-(\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2})-\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}\geq 1.

Moreover,

(4.28)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}\geq 0,

because
V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0
. Thus

(4.29)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(X,X)\geq 1.

It remains to check the radial direction. If

, then

(4.30)

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)=\delta\sin(r/\delta),\qquad\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}.

Hence

(4.31)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{r},\partial_{r})

\displaystyle=-(d-1)\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}+V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\geq\frac{d-1}{\delta^{2}}=\frac{d-1}{d-2}>1.

If
r\geq B-\varepsilon
, then since

(4.32)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}q_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\implies V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)=q_{\varepsilon}(r),

and the identity
q_{\varepsilon}=1
on
[B-\varepsilon,\infty)
gives
V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)=1
. Combining this fact with

\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
, we get

(4.33)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{r},\partial_{r})=-(d-1)\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}}+1\geq 1.

Thus, on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,

(4.34)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{2}V_{\varepsilon}\geq g_{\varepsilon},

which by continuity extends to
r=0.
Writing
W_{\varepsilon}=V_{\varepsilon}+\log Z_{\varepsilon}
, we have

(4.35)

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}=e^{-W_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g_{\varepsilon}},\qquad\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{2}W_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{2}V_{\varepsilon}\geq g_{\varepsilon}.

Hence
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
satisfies
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
.

\mathrm{(iv)}

Construction of test functions

We finally show the desired spectral inequality. To achieve this goal we will
construct suitable test functions. Since the normalization constants cancel in the
Rayleigh quotient, it suffices to use
\nu_{\varepsilon}
instead of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
. Thus,
for any nonzero test function
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})
, set

(4.36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}}.

We would like to compare
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
with a simpler model Rayleigh quotient. To
this end define

(4.37)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(r)=\begin{cases}\delta\sin(r/\delta),&0\leq r\leq B,\\
\delta,&r\geq B,\end{cases}\qquad V_{0}(r)=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq r\leq B,\\
\frac{(r-B)^{2}}{2},&r\geq B.\end{cases}

Let

(4.38)

\displaystyle\nu_{0}=e^{-V_{0}}\rho_{0}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}.

By construction,
\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}=\rho_{0}
on

[0,B-\varepsilon]\cup[B+\varepsilon,\infty)
, while
V_{\varepsilon}=V_{0}
on

[0,B-2\varepsilon]
. Next, observe that since
q_{\varepsilon}(u)=0
on

[0,B-2\varepsilon]
and
0\leq q_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
, we have

(4.39)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}(B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B-2\varepsilon}^{B}(B-u)q_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\leq 2\varepsilon^{2},

(4.40)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B-2\varepsilon}^{B}q_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\leq 2\varepsilon.

Furthermore, since
V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)=1
for all
r\geq B
, we get

(4.41)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)-V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}^{r}V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=r-B,

(4.42)

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}(r)-V_{\varepsilon}(B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}^{r}V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(B)(r-B)+\frac{1}{2}(r-B)^{2}.

Therefore, using the fact
V_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2}(r-B)^{2}
for
r\geq B
, we get
for
\varepsilon\leq 1
,

(4.43)

\displaystyle 0\leq V_{\varepsilon}(r)-V_{0}(r)\leq 2\varepsilon(1+r-B).

Thus, using the elementary inequality
1-e^{-x}\leq x
for all
x\geq 0
, we
get for
r\geq B
,

(4.44)

\displaystyle\left|e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}-e^{-V_{0}(r)}\right|\leq e^{-V_{0}(r)}|1-e^{-(V_{\varepsilon}(r)-V_{0}(r))}|\leq 2\varepsilon(1+r-B)e^{-(r-B)^{2}/2}.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
be the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
restricted to the
unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. In particular, they satisfy

(4.45)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}=(d-1)x_{i},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=0,

and

(4.46)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}x_{j}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=0\qquad(i\neq j).

Set

(4.47)

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)}{\delta},\qquad U_{\varepsilon,i}(r,\omega)=a_{\varepsilon}(r)x_{i}(\omega).

Then
|a_{\varepsilon}|\leq 1
,
|a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{\delta}
, and
U_{\varepsilon,i}

are smooth as
r\to 0
. For
r>0
,

(4.48)

\displaystyle|\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}|^{2}=(a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}|^{2}.

Define

(4.49)

\displaystyle a_{0}(r)=\frac{\rho_{0}(r)}{\delta},\qquad U_{0,i}(r,\omega)=a_{0}(r)x_{i}(\omega),

and

(4.50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{U}^{0}=\int U_{0,i}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{U}^{0}=\int|\nabla U_{0,i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0},

where
a_{0}^{\prime}
is understood in the weak sense in
\mathcal{N}_{U}^{0}
. Denote
I_{\varepsilon}:=[B-2\varepsilon,B+\varepsilon]
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\int U_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{D}_{U}^{0}\right|+\left|\int|\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{N}_{U}^{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|a_{\varepsilon}^{2}x_{i}^{2}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{d-1}-a_{0}^{2}x_{i}^{2}e^{-V_{0}}\rho_{0}^{d-1}\right|\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\left((a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}|^{2}\right)e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{d-1}-\left((a_{0}^{\prime})^{2}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}|^{2}\right)e^{-V_{0}}\rho_{0}^{d-1}\right|\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}|I_{\varepsilon}|+C_{d}\int_{B}^{\infty}\left|e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}-e^{-V_{0}(r)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\varepsilon+C_{d}\varepsilon\int_{B}^{\infty}(1+r-B)e^{-(r-B)^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}r

(4.51)

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\varepsilon,

with constants
C_{d}>0
depending on the dimension
d\geq 4
and possibly different on each line. In the above display, we used that
x_{i}
,
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}
, and the
quantities

|a_{\varepsilon}|\leq 1,\qquad|a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{\delta},\qquad 0\leq\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}\leq\delta,\qquad e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\leq 1

are uniformly bounded on
I_{\varepsilon}
. Define
\delta_{U}:=2\mathcal{D}_{U}^{0}-\mathcal{N}_{U}^{0}
, then

(4.52)

\displaystyle\omega_{d-1}:=|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|,\qquad I_{m}:=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{m}t\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad J_{0}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Using

(4.53)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=\frac{(d-1)\omega_{d-1}}{d},

and
\delta^{2}=d-2
, we compute

(4.54)

\displaystyle\delta_{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{d}\left[2\delta^{d}I_{d+1}+2\delta^{d-1}J_{0}-\delta^{d-2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\bigl(\cos^{2}t+d-1\bigr)\sin^{d-1}t\,\mathrm{d}t-(d-1)\delta^{d-3}J_{0}\right]

(4.55)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{d}\left[\delta^{d-2}\bigl((2d-3)I_{d+1}-dI_{d-1}\bigr)+\delta^{d-3}(d-3)J_{0}\right]

(4.56)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{d}\left[\delta^{d-2}\frac{d(d-4)}{d+1}I_{d-1}+\delta^{d-3}(d-3)J_{0}\right]>0,

where in the last line we used the recurrence
I_{d+1}=\frac{d}{d+1}I_{d-1}
and
d\geq 4.
Next, we choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that

3C_{d}\varepsilon<\delta_{U}
. Then, using (
4
), we get

(4.57)

\displaystyle\int|\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathcal{N}_{U}^{0}+C_{d}\varepsilon=2\mathcal{D}_{U}^{0}-\delta_{U}+C_{d}\varepsilon<2(\mathcal{D}_{U}^{0}-C_{d}\varepsilon)\leq 2\int U_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}.

Hence,

(4.58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon,i})<2\qquad(i=1,\dots,d).

We next define another test function coming from the radial direction along the cylindrical end. Denote

(4.59)

\displaystyle b_{A}(r)=\begin{cases}\cos(r/\delta),&0\leq r\leq B,\\
-A(r-B),&r\geq B,\end{cases}

where
A>0
is chosen so that
\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}=0
. This can be done since

(4.60)

\displaystyle\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{B}\cos(r/\delta)\bigl(\delta\sin(r/\delta)\bigr)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r-A\omega_{d-1}\delta^{d-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau e^{-\tau^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.61)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{B}\cos(r/\delta)\bigl(\delta\sin(r/\delta)\bigr)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r-A\omega_{d-1}\delta^{d-1}.

The first term is strictly positive, since
\cos(r/\delta)>0
and

\sin(r/\delta)>0
for
0<r<B
. Hence there is a unique choice

(4.62)

\displaystyle A=\frac{\int_{0}^{B}\cos(r/\delta)\bigl(\delta\sin(r/\delta)\bigr)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}{\delta^{d-1}}>0

for which
\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}=0
. Our test function will be

(4.63)

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}:=b_{A}-c_{\varepsilon},

where we choose
c_{\varepsilon}:=Z_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}
so that
\int B_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}=0
. Although
b_{A}
is generally not
C^{1}
at
r=B
, it is continuous and piecewise smooth. Hence its weak derivative is the piecewise derivative

(4.64)

\displaystyle b_{A}^{\prime}(r)=\begin{cases}-\delta^{-1}\sin(r/\delta),&0<r<B,\\
-A,&r>B.\end{cases}

The function
b_{A}
has at most linear growth and
b_{A}^{\prime}
is bounded. Since

\nu_{\varepsilon}
has finite mass and has a Gaussian tail on the cylindrical end,

b_{A},B_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})
. Thus
B_{\varepsilon}
is an admissible test function, and all appearances of
B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=b_{A}^{\prime}
below are understood in the weak sense. We first note that

c_{\varepsilon}=O_{d}(\varepsilon)
. This is because
\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}=0
,
(
4.44
), and

\displaystyle\left|\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}\right|+\left|Z_{\varepsilon}-\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\right|=\left|\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\right|+\left|\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+|b_{A}(r)|)\left|e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}-e^{-V_{0}(r)}\rho_{0}(r)^{d-1}\right|\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}1\,\mathrm{d}r+C_{d}\int_{B+\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1+r-B)\delta^{d-1}\left|e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}-e^{-V_{0}(r)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}r

(4.65)

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\varepsilon+C_{d}\varepsilon\int_{B+\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1+r-B)^{2}e^{-(r-B)^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}r\leq C_{d}\varepsilon.

Moreover, choosing
\varepsilon\leq B/4
, we have that
B/2\leq B-2\varepsilon
and therefore
V_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0
on
[0,B/2]
which implies

(4.66)

\displaystyle Z_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{B/2}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r=\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{B/2}\bigl(\delta\sin(r/\delta)\bigr)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r=c_{d}>0.

Thus,

(4.67)

\displaystyle|c_{\varepsilon}|=\left|\frac{\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}}{Z_{\varepsilon}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{d}\varepsilon}{c_{d}}\leq C_{d}\varepsilon.

Denote

(4.68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}=\int B_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}=\int|B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}.

Since
B_{\varepsilon}=b_{A}-c_{\varepsilon}
,
\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}=0
, and

B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=b_{A}^{\prime}
, we have

(4.69)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}=\int b_{A}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}+c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0},\quad\mathcal{N}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}=\int|b_{A}^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}.

We next claim that

\delta_{b}:=2\int b_{A}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}-\int|b_{A}^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}>0.

First, the condition
\int b_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}=0
gives the explicit value

A=\frac{\int_{0}^{B}\cos(r/\delta)(\delta\sin(r/\delta))^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}{\delta^{d-1}}=\frac{\delta}{d}.

Also, by integration by parts,

(4.70)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{2}e^{-\tau^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=J_{0}.

Now using the same notation as in (
4.52
) we get

(4.71)

\displaystyle\delta_{b}

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-1}\left[2\delta^{d}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2}t\,\sin^{d-1}t\,\mathrm{d}t+2\delta^{d-1}A^{2}J_{0}-\delta^{d-2}I_{d+1}-\delta^{d-1}A^{2}J_{0}\right]

(4.72)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-1}\left[\delta^{d-2}\left(2(d-2)(I_{d-1}-I_{d+1})-I_{d+1}\right)+\delta^{d-1}A^{2}J_{0}\right]

(4.73)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-1}\left[\delta^{d-2}\frac{(d-4)}{d+1}I_{d-1}+\frac{\delta^{d+1}}{d^{2}}J_{0}\right]>0.

Here we used
I_{d+1}=\frac{d}{d+1}I_{d-1}
,
\delta^{2}=d-2
,
A=\delta/d
, and
d\geq 4
. Then

(4.74)

\displaystyle\delta_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}

\displaystyle:=2\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}-\mathcal{N}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}=\delta_{b}+2c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\geq\delta_{b}>0.

We next compare the
\nu_{\varepsilon}
-mass and Dirichlet energy of
B_{\varepsilon}
to these limiting
quantities. Since
|c_{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{d}\varepsilon
, on the cylindrical end

(4.75)

\displaystyle|B_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+|B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|^{2}\leq C_{d}(1+(r-B)^{2}).

Thus, recalling that
I_{\varepsilon}=[B-2\varepsilon,B+\varepsilon]
and using (
4.44
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\int B_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}\right|+\left|\int|B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{N}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}1\,\mathrm{d}r+C_{d}\int_{B}^{\infty}\bigl(1+(r-B)^{2}\bigr)\left|e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}-e^{-V_{0}(r)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}r

(4.76)

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\varepsilon+C_{d}\varepsilon\int_{B}^{\infty}\bigl(1+(r-B)^{2}\bigr)(1+r-B)e^{-(r-B)^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}r\leq C_{d}\varepsilon.

Moreover,

(4.77)

\displaystyle\int b_{A}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{d-1}\delta^{d}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2}t\,\sin^{d-1}t\,\mathrm{d}t=:m_{d}>0.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that
C_{d}\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}
, which is possible since
\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}\geq\int b^{2}_{A}\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\geq m_{d}>0
, and

3C_{d}\varepsilon<\delta_{b}
. Then, using (
4
) and

\delta_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}\geq\delta_{b}
, we obtain

(4.78)

\displaystyle\int|\nabla B_{\varepsilon}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int|B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathcal{N}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}+C_{d}\varepsilon

(4.79)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}-\delta_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}+C_{d}\varepsilon<2(\mathcal{D}_{B_{\varepsilon}}^{0}-C_{d}\varepsilon)

(4.80)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int B_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}.

Hence

(4.81)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon})<2.

Let

(4.82)

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{span}\{1,B_{\varepsilon},U_{\varepsilon,1},\dots,U_{\varepsilon,d}\}.

We show that the functions spanning
F_{\varepsilon}
are pairwise
L^{2}(\nu_{\varepsilon})
-orthogonal. Since

(4.83)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}=e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

we have

(4.84)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.85)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{\varepsilon,i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}a_{\varepsilon}(r)e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)=0,

(4.86)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B_{\varepsilon}U_{\varepsilon,i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{\varepsilon}(r)a_{\varepsilon}(r)e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)=0,

(4.87)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{\varepsilon,i}U_{\varepsilon,j}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}a_{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}x_{j}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)=0\quad(i\neq j).

Thus the functions in the spanning set are
L^{2}(\nu_{\varepsilon})
-orthogonal. In
particular they are linearly independent, and hence
\dim F_{\varepsilon}=d+2
. We next show orthogonality with respect to the Dirichlet energy inner product. To this end, we first compute
the gradients,

(4.88)

\displaystyle\nabla B_{\varepsilon}=B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\partial_{r},\qquad\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i}=a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}x_{i}\partial_{r}+\frac{a_{\varepsilon}}{\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{2}}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}.

As a result, we get

(4.89)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla B_{\varepsilon},\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i}\rangle_{g_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)e^{-V_{\varepsilon}(r)}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)=0,

for all
1\leq i\leq d
. Moreover,

(4.90)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla U_{\varepsilon,i},\nabla U_{\varepsilon,j}\rangle_{g_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}(a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}x_{j}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)

(4.91)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}a_{\varepsilon}^{2}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{d-3}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i},\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{j}\rangle\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\right)

(4.92)

\displaystyle=0,

for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
i\neq j
. Here the last equality follows because

(4.93)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}x_{j}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=0\quad(i\neq j),

and, using
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{j}=(d-1)x_{j}
,

(4.94)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i},\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{j}\rangle\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x_{i}x_{j}\,\,\mathrm{dvol}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}=0.

Thus the spanning functions are pairwise orthogonal both in
L^{2}(\nu_{\varepsilon})

and with respect to the Dirichlet energy inner product. Then, for every nonzero
u\in F_{\varepsilon}
,

(4.95)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq\max\{\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon,1}),\dots,\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon,d})\}<2.

By the min-max characterization of the variational eigenvalues,

(4.96)

\displaystyle\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})\leq\sup_{0\neq u\in F_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(u)<2.

Recall that (
2.3
) implies that

\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})=2
. Thus

(4.97)

\displaystyle\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})<\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}),

and the theorem follows.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)
be the weighted manifold constructed in Theorem

2
, which satisfies
\operatorname{CD}(1,\infty)
. Then there does not exist a
1
-Lipschitz map

T:(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d})\to(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)

pushing forward
\gamma^{d}
onto
\mu
up to a finite constant.

Proof.

If such a map existed, then Theorem
2.1
would imply that for all
k\geq 1
we have

(4.98)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g,\mu)\geq\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},|\cdot|,\gamma^{d}).

This contradicts (
1.10
).
