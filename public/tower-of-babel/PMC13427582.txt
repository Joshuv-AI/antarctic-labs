An experimentally validated structure-based computational framework for humanisation of anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies
Authors: Xuehua Yang, Xuemeng Dong, Jiahan Lu, Xiaojing Chi, Xiuying Liu, Huarui Duan, Peixiang Gao, Jing Xue, Wei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe re-emergence of orthopoxviruses, most notably mpox virus (MPXV), poses a growing global public health threat. Well-characterised murine anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies are clinically limited by anti-mouse antibody responses, while traditional sequence-based humanisation often impairs antigen-binding activity.MethodsWe developed an experimentally validated structure-guided computational humanisation framework prioritising 3D architectural congruence over sequence identity, integrating Foldseek-based structural alignment and interface-residue constraints. We applied this framework to humanise two murine anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies (7D11, A27D7), with comprehensive in vitro and in vivo validation.FindingsStructural superimposition confirmed high conformational conservation between the humanised variants (POX1.1 and POX2.1) and their parental mAbs, with root mean square deviation (RMSD) values below 0.6 Å for all variable domains. Both humanised variants retained full epitope specificity with natural humanness profiles. POX1.1 showed enhanced neutralisation potency against vaccinia virus (VACV) and MPXV, compared with the parental 7D11. POX2.1 preserved the broad cross-reactive binding and the extracellular enveloped virion neutralising activity of the parental A27D7. In the lethal VACV mouse model, both monotherapies conferred significant prophylactic and therapeutic protection, reducing pulmonary viral loads and improving survival. The dual-targeting combination of POX1.1 and POX2.1 achieved markedly improved in vivo efficacy compared with individual antibodies, delivering 100% survival even when administered 2 days post-challenge. In the MPXV CAST/EiJ mouse model, the combination significantly reduced splenomegaly and MPXV DNA loads in plasma, spleen and lung tissues, effectively suppressing systemic viral dissemination.InterpretationThese findings establish that the structure-centric workflow enables efficient humanisation of well-characterised murine anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies, providing a validated framework to support the development of countermeasures for orthopoxvirus pandemic.FundingThis work was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China, the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences Innovation Fund for Medical Sciences, the Scientific Research Innovation Capability Support Project for Young Faculty and the National Science and Technology Major Project.Keywords: Orthopoxvirus, Mpox virus, Antibody humanisation, Structure-guided design, Therapeutic antibodies

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Orthopoxviruses including mpox virus (MPXV) have re-emerged as major public health threats. While numerous murine monoclonal antibodies with potent anti-orthopoxvirus activity have been characterised, their translation is hindered by immunogenicity risks. Conventional antibody humanisation relies on sequence-driven CDR grafting, which often compromises binding affinity, structural stability, and antiviral function. Validated structure-guided strategies that preserve paratope geometry and biological activity for anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies remain underdeveloped.

Added value of this study

We establish an experimentally validated, structure-based computational framework that prioritises three-dimensional architectural congruence over sequence identity. Using this pipeline, we humanised two well-defined murine antibodies while preserving epitope specificity and antiviral activity. We provide comprehensive structural, biophysical, and in vivo evidence supporting the fidelity and performance of the humanised variants and their dual-targeting combination.

Implications of all the available evidence

This work establishes a reproducible structure-guided paradigm for antibody humanisation that can be broadly applied to existing murine antibodies. It strengthens the mechanistic understanding of framework selection and paratope preservation, supporting rational design of antibody-based countermeasures against emerging and re-emerging viral pathogens.

Introduction

The emerging and re-emerging viral pathogens represent a profound challenge to global public health, disrupting international travel, trade, and the stability of healthcare systems.1,2 Among these threats, the genus Orthopoxvirus (OPXV) of the family Poxviridae has recently regained prominence as a critical zoonotic concern.3,4 While the 20th century was defined by the successful eradication of smallpox, caused by the variola virus (VARV), the 21st century has witnessed the unexpected resurgence of other OPXVs, most notably the mpox virus (MPXV).5 Since May 2022, an outbreak of mpox has spread with unprecedented speed across more than 110 countries. By the end of 2025, cumulative reports indicated over 115,000 laboratory-confirmed cases and approximately 350 deaths worldwide.6 This surge, characterised by sustained human-to-human transmission through close contact, prompted the World Health Organisation (WHO) to declare it a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), a status that underscored the vulnerability of the modern global population.7

The genus Orthopoxvirus includes several species capable of infecting humans, including VARV, MPXV, cowpox virus (CPXV), and vaccinia virus (VACV).8,9 Historically, VARV was one of the most devastating human pathogens, with mortality rates reaching 30%.10 Following the official declaration of smallpox eradication in 1980, routine vaccination with VACV-based vaccines ceased globally around 1981.11 Consequently, a significant and increasing proportion of the world’s population, now estimated at over 70%, lacks pre-existing immunity to OPXVs.12,13 This “immunity gap” creates fertile ground for the spillover of zoonotic poxviruses. In individuals with compromised immune systems, such as those with untreated HIV-1 co-infection, OPXV infections can manifest as severe, disseminated disease characterised by prolonged viral shedding and high mortality rates.14,15 Despite the availability of the TPOXX (tecovirimat) antiviral, its efficacy across different MPXV clades (Clade I and Clade II) remains a subject of ongoing clinical debate, and the emergence of drug-resistant strains highlights an urgent need for diversified therapeutic interventions.16, 17, 18

Neutralising antibodies (nAbs) have emerged as an indispensable class of therapeutics for emerging infectious diseases, particularly those caused by high-consequence pathogens like Ebola virus and SARS-CoV-2.19 In the context of poxviruses, natural infection or vaccination typically confers long-term protection. However, the endogenous antibody response in primary infections often lags behind the rapid viral replication cycle, failing to prevent severe pathology in vulnerable hosts.20 Passive immunisation with exogenous nAbs can provide immediate protection, neutralising the virus before it disseminates and providing a crucial window for the host’s cellular immunity to mount a response.21,22 Recent efforts have successfully identified several potent nAbs from the sera of individuals recovering from mpox and people who received mpox vaccination.23, 24, 25, 26 These antibodies target key viral surface proteins, such as those involved in the intracellular mature virion (IMV, MV) and extracellular enveloped virion (EEV, EV) stages of the poxvirus life cycle.

The long history of poxvirus research has yielded a wealth of murine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with well-defined inhibitory mechanisms and structural epitopes.27, 28, 29 For instance, the murine mAb 7D11, which targets the L1R protein (essential for IMV entry), is considered a gold-standard neutralising agent due to its high potency and broad reactivity.30 Similarly, antibodies targeting the A33R protein have shown significant efficacy in preventing the long-range dissemination of EEVs.31 However, the utility of these murine antibodies is severely limited by their potential to induce human anti-mouse antibody (HAMA) responses, which can lead to rapid systemic clearance or even anaphylaxis.32,33

In the current era of structural biology and machine learning, a fundamental question arises: when faced with a re-emerging threat, must we always undergo the labour-intensive process of de novo antibody discovery? Or can we leverage the existing repertoire of high-performing murine antibodies through advanced computational humanisation? Traditional humanisation, primarily via complementarity determining region (CDR) grafting onto homologous human germline frameworks (FRs), is often a “trial-and-error” process.34 This method frequently leads to a loss of affinity or structural stability because it fails to account for the subtle second–shell interactions and framework residues that support the CDR loops.35 While several algorithmic tools like BioPhi and Hu-mAb have been developed to automatically achieve humanisation, many remain focussed on sequence-level identity and lack rigorous experimental validation against complex viral targets.36,37

In this study, we propose a rapid computational humanisation strategy that shifts the paradigm from sequence-based grafting to structural-alignment-driven framework optimisation. By utilising a structure-based search for human frameworks that best match the spatial configuration of the murine prototype, we ensure the preservation of the essential paratope geometry. We further integrated an “interaction-site retention” strategy, guided by manual inspection of the antigen–antibody interface, to maintain critical interaction contacts. Applying this workflow, we humanised two potent murine antibodies targeting the L1R and A35R proteins, respectively. Our results demonstrate that this strategy successfully yielded humanised variant. We provide comprehensive characterisation including affinity kinetics, broad-spectrum neutralisation assays, and in vivo efficacy in lethal challenge animal models. This study establishes an accessible, reproducible workflow for the humanisation of well-characterised murine anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies, providing a structurally guided framework for the development of preclinical anti-poxvirus therapeutic candidates.

Methods

Cell lines and viruses

Cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). BHK-21 (ATCC, CCL-10) and Vero E6 cells (ATCC, CRL-1586) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The African green monkey kidney cell line BS-C-1 (ATCC, CCL-26) was cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS and 50 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Expi293F cells (A145271, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 8% CO2 and orbital shaking. All cell lines used in this study were routinely tested for mycoplasma and found to be mycoplasma-free.

Vaccinia virus (VACV) strain Western Reserve (WR; ATCC VR-119) was propagated in HeLa cells (ATCC, CCL-2) and purified via sucrose gradient centrifugation. Viral titres were determined by plaque assay on BS-C-1 monolayers. All experiments involving live VACV were performed under Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) conditions. MPXV-B.1-China-C-Tan-CQ01 were isolated from first imported mpox case of mainland China in 2022. Viruses were cultivated in Vero cells and titrated via plaque assay as described previously.38 All infectious MPXV work was conducted in a specialised BSL-3 facility (Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, CAMS & PUMC, Beijing, China) by trained personnel following strict institutional safety protocols.

Computational pipeline for antibody humanisation

The humanisation of candidate antibodies was performed using a structure-based CDR grafting strategy. Crystal structures of the L1R/7D11 (PDB ID: 2I9L) and A33R/A27D7 (PDB ID: 4M1G) complexes were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Structural models were pre-processed to remove constant regions and water molecules, retaining only the Fv regions and their respective antigens.

Defining critical interface residues via PDBePISA analysis

The epitope–paratope interface was analysed using the PDBePISA server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/pistart.html). Residues were defined as critical “immutable constraints” if they formed hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, hydrophobic interactions, or distance within 4 Å with the antigen at the interface. These residues were strictly retained during the humanisation process.

CDR boundary delineation

CDR boundaries were precisely delineated using the IMGT numbering scheme, ensuring universal compatibility with antibody sequence and structure annotation standards.

Selection of human Fv by foldseek structural alignment

Structural homology searches of murine VH and VL Fv domains were performed using the Foldseek server (https://search.foldseek.com/search) in 3Di/AA mode, against the PDB100 database of human antibody structures. Search iterations were disabled, and candidate human framework templates were prioritised by TM-score (≥0.8 threshold, indicating high global fold similarity) and root mean square deviation (RMSD), with a maximum acceptable RMSD cutoff of 1.0 Å for the Fv domain. We prioritised candidate frameworks where both light and heavy chains could be aligned; if no such frameworks were available, we prioritised either the light or heavy chain based on their importance in antigen interaction. We usually selected 3 candidate acceptor frameworks, and experimental validation typically yielded 1–2 functional variants.

CDR grafting

Structural alignments between donor murine Fv and selected acceptor human Fv were further validated via manual visual inspection using PyMOL. The identified human frameworks served as acceptors for CDR grafting, ensuring that all critical paratope residues identified by PDBePISA were strictly preserved. At least three distinct human acceptor frameworks were selected for each antibody to optimise the probability of maintaining or enhancing parental affinity. To validate the structural conservation of our final humanised variants, we generated structural models of the POX1.1 and POX2.1 Fv domains using AlphaFold3. Superimposition with the parental murine structures was performed with Pymol.

Humanness scoring

All structurally shortlisted templates underwent systematic evaluation of humanness profiles (via AbNatiV. https://www-cohsoftware.ch.cam.ac.uk/index.php/abnativ) and sequence homology to the human germline antibody gene repertoire. Humanness profiles of the humanised variants were assessed using the AbNatiV server, with default parameters for framework region humanness scoring relative to the natural human antibody repertoire.

Back mutation for framework somatic hypermutation (SHM)

We sometimes encounter situations where the candidate antibody framework region undergoes SHM. After we have conducted a comprehensive assessment of the antibody's affinity and biological activity, we will perform a back mutation on those SHM residues in the framework regions to ensure they are consistent with the germline genes. Usually, the back mutation does not affect the binding activity of the antibody, and sometimes it even improves it.

Production and purification of antibodies

The variable region sequences of the humanised antibodies were codon-optimised for mammalian expression and cloned into a series of pCAGGS-based plasmids. These vectors were designed for the expression of Fc-fusion proteins under the control of the hEF1α-HTLV promoter, featuring an N-terminal interleukin-2 signal peptide (IL-2 ss) and a C-terminal Fc region from either human or mouse IgG. Recombinant antibodies were expressed in Expi293F cells via transient transfection using polyethyleneimine (PEI, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Cell supernatants were harvested five days post-transfection and clarified by centrifugation. Antibodies were captured using Protein A MagBeads (GenScript, Nanjing, China), washed with PBS, and eluted with 0.1 M Glycine-HCl (pH 3.5). The eluates were immediately neutralised with 1.0 M Tris-HCl (pH 9.0) and buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.0). Protein purity and identity were confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Western Blot analysis following standard protocols.

Affinity and binding kinetics measurements

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) was performed using a Biacore T200 (GE HealthCare, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Recombinant poxvirus membrane proteins were immobilised onto CM5 sensor chips via standard amine coupling. Briefly, the surfaces of the sample and reference channels were activated with a 1:1 mixture of 0.1 M N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) and 0.4 M 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. After protein immobilisation, residual active groups were blocked with 1.0 M ethanolamine (pH 8.0). The running buffer consisted of PBS-P (PBS, pH 7.0, 0.05% surfactant P20). Serial dilutions of antibodies were injected as analytes over the chip surface. The sensor surface was regenerated with Glycine-HCl (pH 2.5) after each association/dissociation cycle. Binding kinetics were analysed using BIAevaluation software, fitting the data to a 1:1 binding model.

Competitive binding assays

For competitive binding assays, purified L1R and A33R proteins (20 μg/mL in 10 mM Acetate buffer, pH 5.5 or 4.0) were immobilised onto AR2G biosensors. Sensors were pre-activated in water for 10 min and then activated with an EDC/NHS mixture (1:1). Proteins were immobilised until a response threshold of at least 2.0 nm was reached, followed by blocking with 1.0 M ethanolamine. Purified 7D11, POX1.1, A27D7, POX2.1, and an isotype control IgG (IgG-iso) were diluted in PBS-P. Sensors were first saturated with high concentrations of POX1.1 or POX2.1 for 200 s. Without allowing for dissociation, the sensors were immediately moved into wells containing 7D11 or A27D7. Control groups involved pre-saturation with IgG-iso. Data were collected using Octet Data Acquisition software and processed to generate binding curves.

Neutralisation of intracellular mature virus (IMV)

The neutralising activity of POX1.1 against VACV or MPXV was assessed using a Plaque Reduction Neutralisation Test (PRNT). BS-C-1 cells (for VACV) or Vero E6 cells (for MPXV) were seeded in 24-well plates to reach confluence. Antibodies were serially diluted in PBS and mixed with 50–100 PFU of the virus. Mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Cell monolayers were then infected with the antibody-virus mixture for 2 h at 37 °C. After infection, the inoculum was replaced with a semi-solid overlay containing Earle’s MEM, 2.5% FBS, and 1.5% methylcellulose. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and plaques were counted.

Inhibition of extracellular enveloped virus (EEV)

For POX2.1, the neutralisation of the EV form of VACV was evaluated. To specifically target EV, 50 μg/mL of POX1.1 (an IMV-neutralising antibody) was added to MEM containing 2.5% FBS (MEM2.5-POX1.1) to block potential IMV particles in the EV preparation. For the plaque diameter assay, BS-C-1 cells were infected with the EV suspension for 2 h. After infection, semi-solid medium containing 50 μg/mL of the test antibody with or without 10% rabbit complement (C′; Cedarlane) was added. Plaque diameters were measured 48 h post-infection.

For the PRNT of EV, the EV suspension (in MEM2.5-POX1.1) was pre-incubated with 10% C' or PBS for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with 50 μg/mL of the test antibody for an additional 1 h. BS-C-1 monolayers were then infected with this mixture for 2 h, followed by the addition of semi-solid overlay and plaque quantification 48 h later.

In vivo protection in a prophylactic model

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8–10 per group). On Day −1, mice received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of individual antibodies (5 mg/kg), a 1:1 cocktail of POX1.1 and POX2.1 (5 mg/kg in total), or a control antibody. On Day 0, mice were anesthetised with isoflurane and intranasally challenged with 0.5 × 105 PFU of VACV-WR in a 10 μL volume. Body weight and clinical signs were monitored daily for 14 days. Mice were humanely euthanised if weight loss exceeded 20% or if they were unable to feed. On Day 5 post-infection, a subset of mice (n = 4–5) was euthanised; lungs were harvested, with one lobe fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathology and the other reserved for viral titre determination.

In vivo protection in a therapeutic model

Mice were infected intranasally on Day 0 as described above. At 24 h or 48 h post-infection, antibodies (POX1.1, POX2.1, or control) were administered i.p., at a dose of 20 mg/kg. An additional group received the 1:1 antibody cocktail (20 mg/kg total). Survival, body weight, and Day 5 lung pathology/titres were assessed using the same criteria as the prophylactic model.

VACV titre determination in lung

Lung tissues were weighed and homogenised in PBS to a 10% (w/v) suspension using a tissue homogeniser. After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min, the supernatant was serially diluted. 100 μL of each dilution was added to BS-C-1 cell monolayers in 24-well plates. After 2 h of infection (with gentle rocking every 30 min), the inoculum was replaced with 400 μL of methylcellulose-containing medium. After 48 h, cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and plaques were counted to calculate the virus titre (PFU per gram of tissue).

Antiviral activity against MPXV in CAST/EiJ mice

The efficacy of POX1.1 against MPXV was evaluated in CAST/EiJ mice (n = 5 per group). In an ABSL-3 laboratory, mice were injected i.p., with 10 mg/kg of POX1.1, 7D11, or PBS (200 μL volume). Thirty minutes post-administration, mice were challenged i.p., with 1 × 106 PFU of MPXV. Mice were monitored for 4 days for weight loss and clinical signs. On Day 4, mice were euthanised for collection of blood, spleen, and lung samples. The spleen-to-body weight ratio was calculated. Viral loads (gene copy numbers) in tissues were quantified via qRT-PCR targeting MPXV-specific genes.

Ethics

The experimental protocol for mice infection of VACV has been approved by the Beijing Laboratory Animal Research Centre (BLARC-SSYY-DW/013-JL/001) to ensure compliance with ethical guidelines and the humane care and use of laboratory animals. The experimental protocol for MPXV infection in CAST/EiJ mice has been approved by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, CAMS & PUMC, Beijing, China (XJ24002) to ensure compliance with ethical standards and the principles of humane care and use of laboratory animals.

TCID50 of MPXV in mouse tissues

For the measurement of viral titres in tissues of infected CAST/EiJ mice, TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose) assays were performed. After euthanasia, spleen and lung samples were collected, weighted, placed into pre-labelled cryovials, and stored at −80 °C. Prior to testing in the viral titre assays, tissue samples were thawed and placed in DMEM medium and were homogenised twice with 3-mm stainless steel beads for 20 s at 75 HZ/s using tissue homogeniser (LUKA, Guangzhou, China). Homogenates were spun down to remove debris, and supernatants were tested in the viral titre assays. Four-fold serial dilutions of tissue homogenates were made in DMEM medium and added to 90–100% confluent Vero E6 cells in triplicate wells. The plates for the samples were incubated at 37 °C for three days. After three days, wells were visually inspected for cytopathic effect (CPE). Non-infected wells were identified by a clear confluent cell layer. Infected cells demonstrated cell rounding. TCID50 values per gram of tissue were calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

Histopathology

The lung or spleen from mice were collected from each animal at the time of sacrifice at the end of the study period. These tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and sectioned at 5 μm. Slides were baked for 30–60 min at 55 °C then deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a serious of graded ethanol to distilled water, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Images were taken with a 3D Histech (Hungary) Pannoramic MIDI microscope and viewed through CaseViewer 2.4. All pathological evaluations were performed by a pathologist with 10 years of histology experience and then confirmed by another pathologist.

Statistics

All data were statistically analysed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Differences among animal groups were evaluated using ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test for multiple comparisons. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Specific statistical tests used for each experiment are detailed in the corresponding Fig. legends.

Results

A structure-guided computational workflow for rapid antibody humanisation

To address the key limitations of traditional sequence-based antibody humanisation for anti-orthopoxvirus antibodies, we developed a structure-guided computational workflow that prioritises 3D architectural congruence over primary sequence identity. We first validated the shortcomings of conventional humanisation approaches using the well-characterised anti-L1R murine mAb 7D11 as a prototype. Both traditional CDR grafting onto sequence-homologous human germline scaffolds, and machine learning-driven sequence-based humanisation methods yielded antibodies with predicted high humanness, but complete loss of VACV neutralising activity (Supplementary Fig. S1). Recognising that paratope maintenance is contingent upon the precise spatial arrangement of the FRs, we hypothesised that an ideal humanised antibody must maximise preservation of the atomic coordinates of the original CDRs while substituting the FRs with the most structurally congruent human counterparts (Fig. 1A). Our optimised workflow begins with the acquisition of high-resolution atomic coordinates from antigen–antibody complex structures (e.g., from the PDB). For candidates lacking experimental structures, we recommend AlphaFold3 to generate high-confidence structural models. CDR boundaries were precisely delineated using the IMGT numbering scheme to ensure universal compatibility. A pivotal step in our pipeline involves the use of PDBePISA to identify specific interacting residues at the epitope–paratope interface. These residues are designated as “immutable constraints” that must be retained or minimally perturbed during the humanisation process. Subsequently, rather than searching for human sequences with high identity, we utilised the Foldseek tool to perform a global structural alignment of the murine VH and VL Fv domains against a comprehensive database of human antibody structures.39 This approach allows for the identification of human acceptor FRs that share the highest fold similarity, and thus the highest likelihood of maintaining the original CDR topology, regardless of primary sequence divergence. Finally, the murine CDRs were grafted onto these structurally selected human scaffolds (Fig. 1A).

Applying this structure-guided strategy to 7D11 and A27D7 resulted in the humanised candidates POX1.1 and POX2.1, respectively. To validate the structural conservation of our final humanised variants, we generated structural models of the POX1.1 and POX2.1 Fv domains using AlphaFold3 and performed structural superimposition. All structural alignments and RMSD calculations were performed over the backbone heavy atoms (N, Cα, C, O) of the entire Fv domain, including both framework regions and CDRs. POX1.1 demonstrated remarkable conformational identity with 7D11, yielding RMSD of 0.41 Å for the VH and 0.36 Å for the VL. Similarly, POX2.1 exhibited high structural similarity to A27D7, with VH and VL RMSDs of 0.60 Å and 0.53 Å, respectively (Fig. 1B). This level of structural conservation suggests that the global fold of the murine progenitor was successfully recapitulated in a human context.

Further analysis of the germline characteristics of the humanised variants revealed a significant departure from the murine prototypes, indicating that our workflow prioritises structural fold over lineage identity (Fig. 1C). To quantify the degree of humanisation, we employed AbNatiV, a deep learning tool for assessing antibody humanness. The results demonstrated that while the murine prototypes scored poorly, POX1.1 and POX2.1 achieved humanness profiles nearly indistinguishable from those of natural human antibodies across all framework regions, retaining only the minimal murine residues necessary for antigen contact in the CDRs (Fig. 1D and E). Humanness prediction provides an initial assessment of potential immunogenicity risk, but formal in vivo immunogenicity studies are required to further validate the clinical translational potential of these candidates. The sequences for all antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Collectively, these structural and computational analyses validate our workflow as a highly user-friendly tool for transitioning murine antibodies into humanised formats while ensuring preservation of their spatial architecture.

Characterisation of the L1R-Targeting humanised antibody POX1.1

To determine whether the structural fidelity of POX1.1 translated into functional equivalence, we conducted a systematic evaluation of its binding and neutralisation properties against the corresponding antigens. L1R is a highly conserved, essential protein on the membrane of the IMV particle and is a primary target for neutralising antibodies. Based on our recent structural classifications,40 L1R-specific antibodies can be categorised into three classes. The prototype 7D11 is a quintessential Class I antibody, targeting a highly sensitive neutralisation site situated on the “distal” side of the viral surface, with a footprint centred on the Asp35 residue (Fig. 2A). We first examined the conservation of the L1R protein across the orthopoxvirus genus. Analysis of the ∼180-residue extracellular domains from VARV, MPXV, VACV, and ectromelia virus (ECTV) revealed that L1R is exceptionally conserved, with only six amino acid variations across the selected viruses. Crucially, the residues involved in the 7D11 interface were found to be strictly conserved, with the exception of ECTV, which harbours an Asp35 mutation that may render it resistant to this class of antibodies (Fig. 2B).

Interestingly, when expressed in an Expi293F suspension culture system, POX1.1 showed comparable thermal stability with 7D11, withstanding temperatures up to 65 °C without aggregation; the onset of protein polymerisation occurred at 75 °C (Supplementary Fig. S2). To confirm that POX1.1 occupied the same spatial niche as its prototype, we performed biolayer interferometry (BLI)–based competitive binding assays. The results showed that POX1.1 and 7D11 exhibited mutually exclusive binding to the L1R antigen, confirming they share a common epitope (Fig. 2C). The fidelity of the epitope was further confirmed using site-directed mutagenesis. Previous studies identified Asp35 as a linchpin for 7D11 binding. We generated a D35N mutant and assessed antibody binding via BLI. While both 7D11 and POX1.1 bound with high efficiency to the wild-type L1R, both antibodies completely lost binding affinity toward the D35N mutant (Fig. 2D–G). These findings provide evidence that the humanisation process did not perturb the fine specificity of the antibody–antigen interface.

We then compared the binding kinetics and neutralisation activity of the two antibodies. Intriguingly, POX1.1 demonstrated enhanced affinity compared to the murine 7D11. As shown in Fig. 2H–L, the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of POX1.1 for VACV L1R improved by 2.17-fold. The affinity for MPXV M1R increased by 3.47-fold, while for VARV and CPXV L1R, the affinity improved by 3.06-fold. We hypothesise that structure-guided selection of the human FRs may optimise the conformational dynamics of the CDR loops, potentially reducing the entropic penalty upon antigen binding. Further structural and biophysical studies are required to formally validate this mechanistic hypothesis. The improved affinity of POX1.1 was directly reflected in its enhanced antiviral activity. We conducted plaque reduction neutralisation tests (PRNT) using both VACV and MPXV IMV particles. POX1.1 consistently outperformed 7D11 across all metrics: the IC50 and IC90 values for POX1.1 represented a 2.6- to 15.2-fold improvement in neutralisation potency compared to the murine prototype (Fig. 2M−O). POX1.1 represents a potential therapeutic candidate that not only maintains the epitope specificity of 7D11 but also exhibits enhanced developability, affinity, and neutralising breadth.

Characterisation of the A33R-Targeting humanised antibody POX2.1

A33R is an essential component of the EEV membrane and is vital for the efficient cell-to-cell spread of orthopoxviruses. Unlike the monomeric L1R, A33R forms a homodimer. Structural analysis of the A27D7/A33R complex shows that the antibody inserts its VH and VL domains into a groove formed by the A33R dimer interface (Fig. 3A). Although A33R exhibits higher sequence variability than L1R across the orthopoxvirus genus, the core residues forming the A27D7 binding pocket remain relatively conserved (Fig. 3B). Notably, MPXV and ECTV exhibit mutations at positions 117 and 118, with ECTV harbouring an additional mutation at residue 173 (Fig. 3B).

Given the recent global prevalence and public health threat posed by MPXV, we further analysed the potential risk of viral escape for the two antigens targeted by two antibodies. We aligned the protein sequences of M1R and A35R from clinically relevant MPXV isolates belonging to Clade Ia, Clade IIa, and Clade IIb (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). The M1R protein is extremely conserved, with 100% sequence identity across all examined clinical isolates (Supplementary Fig. S3). A35R sequence polymorphisms were observed primarily at three positions in the middle region (residues 65, 67, and 87), whereas the C-terminal region (residues 110–180) recognised by A27D7 is completely conserved among all circulating strains (Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings indicate that the epitope regions targeted by both 7D11/POX1.1 and A27D7/POX2.1 are highly conserved across globally circulating MPXV strains.

Validation via BLI competition assays confirmed that POX2.1 and A27D7 occupy an identical epitope on the VACV A33R dimer (Fig. 3C). To pinpoint the critical residues, we generated an A33R mutant (D115A/Y116A) located at the centre of the binding interface. Both antibodies failed to bind this mutant while retaining high affinity for the wild-type protein, verifying that POX2.1 utilises the same key contact residues as the murine prototype (Fig. 3D–G). In breadth-of-binding assays, POX2.1 demonstrated broad reactivity against A33R orthologs from VACV, MPXV, VARV, and CPXV, with KD values in the subnanomolar range, nearly identical to those of A27D7 (Fig. 3H and I). Interestingly, the Q117K and L118S mutations present in the MPXV A35R did not appreciably diminish the binding affinity of POX2.1 and A27D7. Given the importance of MPXV, we independently verified the identity and integrity of our MPXV A35R recombinant protein using an MPXV-specific detection mAb (Supplementary Fig. S5). The two antibodies exhibited nearly identical thermal stability, remaining uniformly dispersed at temperatures of 65 °C or below. Protein polymerisation was only observed upon heating to 75 °C (Supplementary Fig. S6). The consistent performance of POX2.1 across divergent orthologs underscores the robustness of the structure-guided humanisation approach.

We subsequently evaluated the inhibitory activity of POX2.1 and A27D7 against VACV infection. Neutralisation of the EEV form is technically challenging due to the inherent instability of the outer membrane and the presence of contaminating IMV particles in virus preparations. To determine EEV-specific effects, we conducted all experiments in the presence of saturating concentrations of the L1R-neutralising antibody 7D11. In virus spread assays, POX2.1 significantly restricted the size of VACV plaques to a degree comparable to A27D7, effectively blocking the dissemination of the virus from infected to neighbouring cells (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, we assessed EEV neutralisation in the presence and absence of rabbit complement. POX2.1 demonstrated a slight but statistically significant enhancement in neutralisation potency compared to A27D7, achieving up to 50% inhibition of plaque formation (Fig. 3K). Taken together, these results confirm that POX2.1 is a functionally potent humanised antibody that successfully targets the EEV form with high specificity and broad-spectrum efficacy.

In vivo prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy of POX1.1 and POX2.1 in mice

The translational potential of POX1.1 and POX2.1 was evaluated using a lethal VACV challenge model in BALB/c mice. Neutralising antibodies can exert protection through both prophylactic (pre-exposure) and therapeutic (post-exposure) mechanisms. We first investigated the prophylactic efficacy by administering a single 5 mg/kg dose of the respective antibodies intraperitoneally (i.p.) on day −1, followed by an intranasal (i.n.) challenge with a 0.5 × 105 PFU of VACV on day 0 (Fig. 4A). Mice treated with POX1.1, POX2.1, or A27D7 displayed robust protection, characterised by minimal weight loss (5–10%) and a rapid return to normal growth kinetics by day 7. In contrast, mice treated with the murine 7D11 antibody showed slightly more pronounced weight loss (∼15%) before recovery, while the negative control group (irrelevant IgG) suffered catastrophic weight loss (>30%) (Fig. 4B). Survival rates were 100% for all antibody-treated groups, whereas the control group reached 0% survival by day 8 (Fig. 4C). Quantitation of viral loads in the lungs at day 5 post-infection revealed that POX1.1 and POX2.1 treatment resulted in the lowest viral titres, significantly lower than those observed in the murine antibody groups, indicating superior viral clearance in the respiratory tract (Fig. 4D).

We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of these antibodies, which is more representative of clinical intervention. Mice were challenged on day 0 and received a single dose of antibody on either day 1 or day 2 post-infection (Fig. 4E). Overall, the therapeutic protection was less effective than the prophylactic results, reflecting the rapid replication kinetics of the poxviruses. However, POX2.1 administered on day 1 (POX2.1-D1) provided remarkable protection, with mice showing almost no weight loss (Fig. 4F). Survival analysis showed that POX2.1-D1 and POX1.1-D1 achieved 80% survival, while administration on day 2 (D2) resulted in 60% survival (Fig. 4G). Viral load analysis in the lungs corroborated these findings, with POX2.1 consistently outperforming POX1.1 in reducing the pulmonary viral burden (Fig. 4H). To visualise the extent of protection, we performed histopathological analysis of lung tissues. Control mice exhibited severe pneumonia with massive inflammatory cell infiltration into the alveoli. In contrast, mice treated with POX1.1 or POX2.1 showed significantly preserved lung architecture, with the POX2.1-D1 group appearing nearly identical to the uninfected naïve controls (Fig. 4I). These in vivo findings demonstrate that POX1.1 and POX2.1 are highly effective as both prophylactic and therapeutic agents, with POX2.1 exhibiting particularly potent activity in halting systemic viral progression.

Enhanced protective efficacy of a dual-targeting humanised antibody cocktail

The OPXVs were characterised by the distinct IMV and EEV forms, suggesting that a monotherapy targeting only one particle type may allow for viral escape or suboptimal inhibition. We hypothesised that a “cocktail” combining POX1.1 (targeting the IMV form) and POX2.1 (targeting the EEV form) would provide enhanced protection by simultaneously blocking viral entry and cell-to-cell spread. We evaluated this cocktail in the same lethal VACV mouse model, examining both prophylactic (Fig. 5A–D) and therapeutic (Fig. 5E–H) regimens. In the prophylactic setting, the POX-mix cocktail provided essentially perfect protection. Mice receiving the cocktail exhibited minimal weight loss, 100% survival, and lung viral titres that were at the limit of detection, comparable to those of naïve mice (Fig. 5B–D). The superior combined efficacy was even more evident in the therapeutic model. Administration of the cocktail on day 1 post-infection (POX-mix-D1) resulted in 100% survival and no clinical symptoms. Remarkably, even when the cocktail was administered as late as day 2 (POX-mix-D2), when single-antibody treatments showed only partial survival, the cocktail group achieved 100% survival with only transient, minor weight loss (∼10%) followed by rapid recovery (Fig. 5F–H).

Detailed histopathological examination of the lung tissues at day 7 post-infection underscored the efficacy of the dual-targeting strategy. While single-antibody treatments left residual inflammatory markers, the POX-mix–treated lungs showed clear alveolar spaces and a lack of leukocytic infiltration, matching the status of uninfected animals (Fig. 5I). In summary, the POX-mix cocktail represents a highly potent and enhanced therapeutic intervention that provides near-complete protection against lethal VACV challenge, outperforming individual humanised components and establishing a new benchmark for poxvirus countermeasures.

Protection of POX-mix against MPXV infection in vivo

Building on the robust efficacy of the two humanised antibodies against VACV, we further evaluated their protective potential against MPXV in vivo. Since most immunocompetent mouse strains are resistant to MPXV infection, we employed the CAST/EiJ strain, a nonlethal model that supports detectable MPXV replication and allows for assessment of systemic viral dissemination, but does not enable evaluation of protection against lethal MPXV disease. We therefore assessed protective efficacy using spleen-to-body weight ratio (splenomegaly), plasma viral DNA load, and tissue viral DNA titres as readouts of systemic infection (Fig. 6A).

POX-mix exhibited significant protective efficacy in vivo. Mice treated with the antibody cocktail showed a marked reduction in splenomegaly compared to the control group, regardless of whether treatment was administered before or after viral challenge (Fig. 6B). Moreover, MPXV DNA loads in plasma and tissues (spleen and lung) were substantially lower in the POX-mix–treated groups, particularly when antibodies were given prior to infection (Fig. 6C–E). When we measured MPXV viral titres in the spleen and lungs of CAST/EiJ mice, we found that viral titres in all three POX-mix antibody treatment groups were near the limit of detection, whereas those in the control antibody group were 4–5 orders of magnitude higher (Fig. 6F and G). Furthermore, we performed histological studies on the spleens of MPXV-infected CAST/EiJ mice. Histological analysis revealed that MPXV infection in the control group induced pronounced splenic pathological changes, characterised by white pulp hyperplasia with prominent germinal centres, along with increased extramedullary haematopoiesis and macrophage infiltration in the red pulp. In contrast, all three POX-mix treatment groups exhibited much milder histopathological manifestations (Fig. 6H and I). These findings confirm that the humanisation strategy successfully preserved the functional integrity of the original murine antibodies, demonstrating in vivo activity against MPXV dissemination in CAST/EiJ model.

Discussion

The recent global resurgence of mpox and the persistent threat of other orthopoxviruses have exposed the fragility of our current therapeutic arsenal.41 While tecovirimat remains the primary antiviral option, concerns regarding drug resistance and clade-specific efficacy necessitate the development of diversified interventions.42 Neutralising antibodies represent a proven therapeutic modality; however, the timeline for de novo discovery and the technical hurdles of traditional humanisation often delay the deployment of safe, high-potency candidates during an outbreak. In this study, we addressed this challenge by developing a structure-guided computational humanisation strategy that successfully transformed established murine anti-poxvirus antibodies into superior humanised variants, POX1.1 and POX2.1.

The core innovation of this workflow lies in the shift from sequence-based identity to structural-congruence-driven framework selection. Traditional CDR grafting relies on sequence homology to select human germline frameworks, a method that often fails to preserve the 3D conformation of the paratope, as sequence identity does not guarantee structural congruence. Existing computational humanisation tools, such as BioPhi and Hu-mAb, are predominantly optimised for sequence-level humanness prediction and germline matching, with limited integration of antigen–antibody interface constraints and scarce experimental validation in complex viral target systems. In contrast, our workflow prioritises global structural alignment of the Fv domain to select human frameworks that best recapitulate the native CDR topology, combined with immutable interface constraints to retain critical antigen-contacting residues. This structure-first approach directly addresses the key limitation of sequence-based methods, as demonstrated by the loss of neutralising activity in antibodies generated via traditional CDR grafting and machine learning-driven sequence-based humanisation for 7D11.

Our strategy leverages Foldseek to perform a global structural alignment of the murine Fv domains against the vast repository of human antibody structures. By prioritising the TM-score (global fold similarity) and minimising RMSD, we identify human scaffolds that naturally support the specific spatial topology of the murine paratope. This is exemplified by POX1.1, which not only retained the specificity of 7D11 but achieved a 2- to 15-fold improvement in neutralisation potency. We hypothesise that this enhancement stems from the optimised flexibility and entropic profile provided by the structurally matched human framework, which may allow for a more favourable fit upon antigen encounter.

Another pivotal advantage is the integration of “immutable constraints” identified via PISA analysis. By mapping the precise epitope–paratope interface at an atomic level, we ensured that the humanisation process did not interfere with critical interaction contacts. This was validated by competitive binding and site-directed mutagenesis assays, where POX1.1 and POX2.1 strictly maintained the fine specificity of their murine ancestors. The methodology established here provides a validated framework for the rapid humanisation of existing murine antibodies against orthopoxviruses, a genus with a large repertoire of well-characterised murine mAbs with limited clinical translation. For other viral pathogens or therapeutic areas, further validation of this workflow is required in future studies.

Notably, the current workflow is optimised for antibodies where the majority of the paratope resides within the CDRs, as is the case for the two mAbs characterised in this study. For antibodies where the paratope extends into the framework regions, a modified workflow would be required. For example, critical framework paratope residues from the murine antibody could be retained in the human scaffold, or targeted backmutation could be performed to preserve binding activity, while maintaining the overall human framework to minimise immunogenicity risk.

Several fully human or humanised antibodies against orthopoxviruses have recently been developed.26,43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48 As summarised in Supplementary Table S2, POX1.1 shows high-affinity binding to M1R with a KD of 1.90 × 10−10 M, comparable or superior to reported M1R-targeting antibodies such as A138 and hmMM1-40. POX1.1 exhibits potent neutralising activity, with IC50 values of 0.006 μg/mL against VACV and 0.273 μg/mL against MPXV, which are more favourable than those of hmMM1-40. Structurally, POX1.1 targets the top region of M1R, with an epitope largely overlapping with hmMM1-40 but distinct from A129, which binds the side of M1R. POX2.1 binds A35R with KD values of 3.51 × 10−10 M for MPXV and 2.40 × 10−11 M for VACV, exceeding the affinity of several convalescent-derived A35R antibodies. In contrast to EV35-2 and mAb975, which recognise only one subunit of the A35R dimer, POX2.1 interacts with both subunits, supporting its distinctive binding mode and robust neutralisation against EEV forms. In vivo, both POX1.1 and POX2.1 provide 100% prophylactic protection at 5 mg/kg in the lethal VACV model, comparable to clinically relevant antibodies such as A138 and EV35-6. In therapeutic settings, single doses of POX1.1 or POX2.1 achieve 80% survival when administered at 1 day post-infection and 60% at 2 days, similar to A138 and B026. Notably, the POX1.1/POX2.1 cocktail markedly improves therapeutic efficacy, conferring 100% survival even when given at 2 days post-infection.

Beyond infectious diseases, this strategy is highly relevant for chronic conditions, including oncology and autoimmune disorders. In these fields, many therapeutic targets are highly conserved self-antigens. Generating high-affinity human antibodies against such targets is often technically daunting due to immune tolerance mechanisms in human-transgenic mice or phage display libraries. Murine immunisation, followed by our structure-guided humanisation, provides an alternative route to access high-affinity paratopes that are otherwise difficult to obtain. The preservation of the atomic coordinates of the CDRs ensures that the therapeutic window and specificity of the original lead are maintained, reducing the risk of off-target effects in complex chronic disease environments.

A key finding of this study is the superior efficacy of the dual-targeting “POX-mix” cocktail. These findings indicate that the epitope regions targeted by both POX1.1 and POX2.1 are highly conserved across globally circulating MPXV strains, suggesting that naturally occurring escape mutations capable of evading neutralisation by these antibodies are unlikely to emerge. Furthermore, because POX1.1 targets the IMV-associated M1R protein while POX2.1 targets the EEV-associated A35R protein, this dual-targeting combination provides an additional critical barrier against viral escape. For the virus to escape neutralisation by the cocktail, it would need to simultaneously acquire escape mutations in two structurally and functionally distinct antigens that are both essential for viral infectivity and spread. The probability of such concurrent mutations arising naturally is extremely low, thereby improving the robustness and resistance barrier of this antibody cocktail against viral escape.

Despite the promising neutralising and protective profiles observed in our preclinical analyses, this work has several notable limitations that warrant consideration for future translational development. Our in vivo efficacy data for MPXV were generated in a nonlethal CAST/EiJ mouse model, with readouts limited to viral DNA load and splenomegaly. These data support the biological activity of the antibody cocktail against MPXV, but do not constitute a formal demonstration of therapeutic efficacy against lethal MPXV disease. Further evaluation in more stringent MPXV models, such as nonhuman primates, is required to fully define the therapeutic window of these candidates. Furthermore, while our computational strategy is highly effective, the “interaction-site retention” currently involves manual inspection of the interface. Future iterations of the workflow could integrate deep learning-based interface prediction to fully automate the identification of immutable paratope residues. Certainly, pharmacokinetic profiling, in vivo immunogenicity assessment, and comprehensive developability characterisation will be essential to advance these preclinical candidates towards clinical evaluation.
