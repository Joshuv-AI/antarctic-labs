Lecture Note for Bounded Controls in Continuous-Time and Control of Several Variables
Louis Shuo Wang

Abstract
In this note, we develop the first-order theory of optimal control problems with box constraints on the control. We emphasize the precise modification of Pontryagin’s maximum principle when the admissible control set is compact, the projection/clamping formula for scalar quadratic Hamiltonians, the distinction between intrinsic projection inside the optimality system and post hoc truncation of an unconstrained solution , and the corresponding forward-backward sweep implementation. The presentation is pitched at senior PhD students who are already comfortable with variational arguments, adjoint systems, and basic nonlinear analysis. These notes are mainly based on the book “optimal control applied to biological models” of Suzanne Lenhart and John T. Workman.



1
Part I

1.1
Motivation and problem class

In many scientifically relevant control models, the control cannot range freely over all of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Physical, technological, pharmacological, ecological, or regulatory considerations impose pointwise bounds:

u(t)\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\qquad U=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{m},b_{m}].

Examples include:

(i)

dosage or treatment intensity:
0\leq u_{i}(t)\leq u_{i}^{\max}
,

(ii)

harvesting effort or extraction rate:
0\leq u(t)\leq\bar{u}
,

(iii)

actuator saturation in engineering:
\|u(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{u}
.

A prototypical Bolza problem is

\max_{u(\cdot)}\;J(u)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt+\phi(x(t_{1}))

(1)

subject to

\dot{x}(t)=g(t,x(t),u(t)),\qquad x(t_{0})=x_{0},\qquad u(t)\in U\text{ a.e. on }[t_{0},t_{1}].

(2)

Throughout,
x:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state and
u:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control.

The key message is simple but fundamental:

For constrained controls, one does not merely solve the unconstrained stationarity equation and then clip the answer afterward. The control constraint must be enforced
inside
the optimality system.

1.2
Hamiltonian formalism with box constraints

Definition 1

(Hamiltonian)
.

For (
1
)–(
2
), define the Hamiltonian

H(t,x,u,\lambda):=f(t,x,u)+\lambda^{\top}g(t,x,u),

where
\lambda(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the adjoint (costate).

The unconstrained first-order condition often takes the form

H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})=0.

With box constraints, this is generally false on intervals where the optimal control lies on the boundary of the admissible set.

1.2.1
Constrained Pontryagin principle

For a maximization problem, Pontryagin’s principle states that the optimal control must maximize the Hamiltonian
pointwise over the admissible control set
:

u^{*}(t)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v\in U}H(t,x^{*}(t),v,\lambda^{*}(t))\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

(3)

The state and adjoint equations remain

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=H_{\lambda}(t,x^{*}(t),u^{*}(t),\lambda^{*}(t)),

(4)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=-H_{x}(t,x^{*}(t),u^{*}(t),\lambda^{*}(t)),

(5)

with transversality

\lambda^{*}(t_{1})=\nabla\phi(x^{*}(t_{1}))

when the terminal state is free and the final time is fixed.

Remark 1
.

For minimization problems, the same equations (
4
)–(
5
) hold, but (
3
) is replaced by pointwise minimization:

u^{*}(t)\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{v\in U}H(t,x^{*}(t),v,\lambda^{*}(t)).

1.3
Scalar box constraint: complementary sign conditions

Assume a scalar control
u\in[a,b]
and
H
is differentiable in
u
.
Then the constrained maximum condition is equivalent to the familiar sign logic:

\begin{cases}H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})<0\;\Rightarrow\;u^{*}(t)=a,\\[2.5pt]
H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})=0\;\Rightarrow\;a\leq u^{*}(t)\leq b,\\[2.5pt]
H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})>0\;\Rightarrow\;u^{*}(t)=b.\end{cases}

(6)

Equivalently,

\begin{cases}u^{*}(t)=a\;\Rightarrow\;H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})\leq 0,\\[2.5pt]
a<u^{*}(t)<b\;\Rightarrow\;H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})=0,\\[2.5pt]
u^{*}(t)=b\;\Rightarrow\;H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})\geq 0.\end{cases}

(7)

For minimization, the inequalities reverse:

\begin{cases}H_{u}>0\;\Rightarrow\;u^{*}=a,\\
H_{u}=0\;\Rightarrow\;a\leq u^{*}\leq b,\\
H_{u}<0\;\Rightarrow\;u^{*}=b.\end{cases}

(8)

1.3.1
Variational derivation in one paragraph

Let
u^{*}
be optimal and let
u_{\varepsilon}=u^{*}+\varepsilon h
be an admissible one-sided perturbation with
\varepsilon>0
small. The first variation satisfies

0\geq\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(u_{\varepsilon})\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0^{+}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})\,h(t)\,dt.

At a time
s
where
u^{*}(s)<b
, one may choose a nonnegative bump
h
concentrated near
s
. Therefore
H_{u}(s)\leq 0
. Similarly, if
u^{*}(s)>a
, one may choose a nonpositive bump, yielding
H_{u}(s)\geq 0
. Combined, these imply (
7
). This is the one-dimensional box-constrained version of the Hamiltonian maximization condition.

1.4
Projection formula for quadratic Hamiltonians

A large class of biological, economic, and engineering models leads to Hamiltonians that are strictly concave or convex in
u
. For a scalar maximization problem, suppose

H(t,x,u,\lambda)=\Psi(t,x,\lambda)+\beta(t,x,\lambda)\,u-\frac{\rho}{2}u^{2},\qquad\rho>0.

Then the unconstrained maximizer is

\tilde{u}(t)=\frac{\beta(t,x,\lambda)}{\rho}.

Imposing
u\in[a,b]
gives the exact constrained maximizer

u^{*}(t)=\operatorname{clamp}_{[a,b]}\!\left(\tilde{u}(t)\right)=\min\{b,\max\{a,\tilde{u}(t)\}\}.

(9)

Proposition 1

(Projection formula)
.

If
H(t,x,\cdot,\lambda)
is strictly concave and quadratic in
u
, then (
9
) is the unique maximizer of
H
over
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Since
H
is strictly concave in
u
, any local maximizer is the unique global maximizer. The unconstrained critical point
\tilde{u}
is unique. If
\tilde{u}\in[a,b]
, it is feasible and optimal. If
\tilde{u}<a
, strict concavity implies
H
is increasing up to
\tilde{u}
and decreasing thereafter, so the constrained maximizer is the left endpoint
a
. The case
\tilde{u}>b
is analogous.
∎

Remark 2
.

The “projection” in (
9
) is not a heuristic numerical correction. It is the exact pointwise optimizer of the Hamiltonian over the admissible interval.

1.5
Bang-bang structure and switching functions

When the Hamiltonian is affine in the control,

H(t,x,u,\lambda)=\Psi(t,x,\lambda)+\sigma(t)\,u,\qquad u\in[a,b],

the pointwise maximization problem is trivial:

u^{*}(t)=\begin{cases}b,&\sigma(t)>0,\\
[a,b],&\sigma(t)=0,\\
a,&\sigma(t)<0.\end{cases}

(10)

The scalar function
\sigma(t):=H_{u}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda^{*})
is called the
switching function
. If its zeros are isolated, the optimal control is bang-bang: it sits on one bound and switches to the other at isolated times.

Remark 3

(Singular arcs)
.

If
\sigma(t)\equiv 0
on a nontrivial interval, first-order PMP does not determine
u^{*}
there. One must differentiate the switching function until the control enters, or invoke higher-order optimality conditions such as Legendre–Clebsch type criteria.

1.6
Worked example: linear state dynamics, quadratic running cost

Consider the maximization problem

\max_{0\leq u\leq 2}\;J(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\big(\alpha x(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}ru(t)^{2}\big)\,dt

(11)

subject to

\dot{x}(t)=ax(t)+bu(t),\qquad x(0)=x_{0},\qquad\alpha,r>0.

Hamiltonian

H(x,u,\lambda)=\alpha x-\frac{1}{2}ru^{2}+\lambda(ax+bu).

Adjoint equation

\dot{\lambda}(t)=-H_{x}=-(\alpha+a\lambda(t)),\qquad\lambda(T)=0.

Thus

\lambda(t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{a}\Big(e^{a(T-t)}-1\Big),&a\neq 0,\\[7.5pt]
\alpha(T-t),&a=0.\end{cases}

Control characterization

H_{u}=-ru+b\lambda.

The unconstrained maximizer is

\tilde{u}(t)=\frac{b}{r}\lambda(t).

Hence the constrained optimizer is

u^{*}(t)=\min\left\{2,\max\left\{0,\frac{b}{r}\lambda(t)\right\}\right\}.

Detailed Interpretation of the Control Structure

The optimal constrained control
u^{*}(t)
is strictly determined by the exact projection of the unconstrained ideal control
\tilde{u}(t)=\frac{b}{r}\lambda(t)
onto the admissible interval
[0,2]
. Because the adjoint variable carries a terminal condition
\lambda(T)=0
and is typically strictly positive and monotonically decaying for
t<T
, the control trajectory naturally partitions into three distinct phases. This yields the canonical
bound–interior–bound
structure:

(i)

Initial Upper Bound (Saturation):
Early in the control horizon (
t\ll T
), the future marginal value of the state is high, making
\lambda(t)
sufficiently large such that
\tilde{u}(t)>2
. The physical constraints of the system become active, and the optimal action is to saturate the input:
u^{*}(t)=2
.

(ii)

Interior Arc (Unconstrained Operation):
As time progresses toward the terminal horizon, the value of the adjoint
\lambda(t)
diminishes. Once the ideal control drops into the feasible region (
0<\tilde{u}(t)<2
), the bounds become inactive. The optimal control strictly follows the continuous, unconstrained feedback law:
u^{*}(t)=\tilde{u}(t)
.

(iii)

Terminal Lower Bound (Decay to Zero):
Approaching the terminal time
T
, the residual value of the state drops to zero (
\lambda(T)=0
). Consequently, the ideal control
\tilde{u}(t)
decays entirely. The optimal control smoothly tracks this decay, hitting the lower bound
0
at exactly
t=T
(or earlier, if
\lambda(t)
were to become non-positive).

1.7
Why post hoc truncation is generally wrong

A frequent mistake in applied work is:

(i)

solve the unconstrained optimality system,

(ii)

obtain
\hat{u}
that violates the bounds,

(iii)

define
u^{\text{clip}}(t)=\operatorname{clamp}_{[a,b]}(\hat{u}(t))
afterward.

This is generally
not
the optimal constrained control.

Remark 4

(Structural reason)
.

The state and adjoint satisfy

\dot{x}=g(t,x,u),\qquad\dot{\lambda}=-H_{x}(t,x,u,\lambda),

so changing the control changes both
x
and
\lambda
, which in turn changes the control law. The constrained problem has a different coupled boundary-value structure from the unconstrained problem. Projection must therefore occur
within each iterate of the solve
, not after the fact.

A useful slogan

Project the control law, not the finished trajectory.

1.8
Forward-backward sweep with box constraints

The forward-backward sweep method (FBSM) is a standard indirect numerical scheme for solving PMP systems.

Let
u^{(k)}
be the current control iterate.

(i)

Forward state solve:

solve

\dot{x}^{(k)}(t)=g\big(t,x^{(k)}(t),u^{(k)}(t)\big),\qquad x^{(k)}(t_{0})=x_{0}.

(ii)

Backward adjoint solve:

solve

\dot{\lambda}^{(k)}(t)=-H_{x}\big(t,x^{(k)}(t),u^{(k)}(t),\lambda^{(k)}(t)\big),\qquad\lambda^{(k)}(t_{1})=\nabla\phi(x^{(k)}(t_{1})).

(iii)

Control update:

compute the pointwise optimizer

\tilde{u}^{(k+1)}(t)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v\in U}H\big(t,x^{(k)}(t),v,\lambda^{(k)}(t)\big),

and then often damp it:

u^{(k+1)}(t)=\theta\,\tilde{u}^{(k+1)}(t)+(1-\theta)u^{(k)}(t),\qquad 0<\theta\leq 1.

If the unconstrained scalar update is
\tilde{u}=\Gamma(t,x,\lambda)
and the admissible interval is
[a,b]
, then the bounded update is

\tilde{u}^{(k+1)}(t)=\min\{b,\max\{a,\Gamma(t,x^{(k)},\lambda^{(k)})\}\}.

This is the exact mathematical content of the constrained maximization step.

Remark 5
.

In practice, damping is often essential for stability, especially for stiff dynamics, long horizons, or highly nonlinear state-costate coupling.

1.10
A compact theorem statement

Theorem 1

(PMP with box-constrained scalar control)
.

Consider the maximization problem

\max_{u(\cdot)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt+\phi(x(t_{1}))

subject to

\dot{x}(t)=g(t,x(t),u(t)),\qquad x(t_{0})=x_{0},\qquad a\leq u(t)\leq b.

Assume regularity sufficient for Pontryagin’s principle. If
(x^{*},u^{*})
is optimal, then there exists an absolutely continuous adjoint
\lambda
such that

\dot{x}^{*}=H_{\lambda}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda),\qquad\dot{\lambda}=-H_{x}(t,x^{*},u^{*},\lambda),\qquad\lambda(t_{1})=\nabla\phi(x^{*}(t_{1})),

and

u^{*}(t)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v\in[a,b]}H(t,x^{*}(t),v,\lambda(t))\quad\text{for a.e. }t.

If
H
is differentiable with respect to
u
, then for a.e.
t
,

u^{*}(t)=a\Rightarrow H_{u}\leq 0,\qquad a<u^{*}(t)<b\Rightarrow H_{u}=0,\qquad u^{*}(t)=b\Rightarrow H_{u}\geq 0.

2
Part II

2.1
Motivation and Scope

Many modern control problems are intrinsically multi-dimensional. In mathematical biology one tracks several interacting populations; in epidemiology one may control vaccination, treatment, and isolation simultaneously; in engineering one often regulates a vector-valued state with multiple actuators. The scalar one-state/one-control formalism is therefore only a warm-up. The correct framework is vector-valued state dynamics together with vector-valued controls, and the natural analytical object is the Hamiltonian associated with the control system.

In Part II we develop four themes:

(i)

Pontryagin necessary conditions for problems with multiple states and controls.

(ii)

The finite-horizon continuous-time linear quadratic regulator (LQR) and the Riccati differential equation.

(iii)

Reduction of higher-order differential equations to first-order systems.

(iv)

Isoperimetric constraints and their conversion into endpoint constraints via an auxiliary state.

The presentation assumes familiarity with ordinary differential equations, functional analysis at the level of weak compactness heuristics, and basic optimal control.

2.2
General Multi-State, Multi-Control Optimal Control Problems

Let the state be
\bm{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the control be
\bm{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Consider the finite-horizon problem

\max_{\bm{u}(\cdot)}\left\{J(\bm{u}):=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t))\,dt+\phi(\bm{x}(t_{1}))\right\}

(12)

subject to

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{g}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t)),\qquad\bm{x}(t_{0})=\bm{x}_{0}.

(13)

Here

f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\bm{g}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},

and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are assumed continuously differentiable unless stated otherwise.

2.2.1
Hamiltonian Formalism

Introduce the adjoint (costate) vector
\bm{\lambda}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Hamiltonian is

H(t,\bm{x},\bm{u},\bm{\lambda})=f(t,\bm{x},\bm{u})+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\bm{g}(t,\bm{x},\bm{u}).

(14)

This compactly encodes both the dynamics and the objective.

2.2.2
Pontryagin Necessary Conditions

Theorem 2

(Pontryagin principle, smooth finite-horizon form)
.

Suppose
\bm{u}^{*}
is an optimal control with corresponding state
\bm{x}^{*}
. Then there exists an absolutely continuous adjoint
\bm{\lambda}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, for almost every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{H}{\lam}=\bm{g}(t,\bm{x}^{*}(t),\bm{u}^{*}(t)),

(15)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\lambda}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{\x},

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\bm{u}\in U}H(t,\bm{x}^{*}(t),\bm{u},\bm{\lambda}(t)),

(17)

with terminal condition

\bm{\lambda}(t_{1})=\nabla\phi(\bm{x}^{*}(t_{1})),

(18)

provided the terminal state is free and
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the admissible control set.

Remark 6
.

For minimization problems, one can either minimize the Hamiltonian directly or maximize the negative Hamiltonian. Both conventions are used in the literature. What matters is consistency.

2.2.3
Componentwise Form

If
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{\top}
,
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})^{\top}
, and
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})^{\top}
, then

H(t,\bm{x},\bm{u},\bm{\lambda})=f(t,\bm{x},\bm{u})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,g_{i}(t,\bm{x},\bm{u}),

and the necessary conditions become

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{H}{\lambda_i}=g_{i}(t,\bm{x},\bm{u}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(19)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x_j},\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(20)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{H}{u_k}\quad\text{if }u_{k}\text{ is unconstrained and the optimum is interior}.

(21)

2.2.4
Control Bounds

If a scalar control component satisfies

a_{k}\leq u_{k}(t)\leq b_{k},

then the stationarity condition is replaced by the projection rule

u_{k}^{*}(t)=\begin{cases}a_{k},&\text{if }\displaystyle\partialderivative{H}{u_k}<0,\\[4.30554pt]
\text{interior value solving }\displaystyle\partialderivative{H}{u_k}=0,&\text{if admissible},\\[4.30554pt]
b_{k},&\text{if }\displaystyle\partialderivative{H}{u_k}>0.\end{cases}

This is the smooth analog of a bang-bang/boundary-interior structure.

2.2.5
Endpoint Variants

The transversality conditions depend on which endpoint values are fixed or free:

(i)

If
x_{i}(t_{1})
is free and
\phi
is present, then
\lambda_{i}(t_{1})=\partial\phi/\partial x_{i}
.

(ii)

If
x_{i}(t_{1})
is fixed, then no transversality condition is imposed on
\lambda_{i}(t_{1})
.

(iii)

If there is no terminal payoff, then
\phi\equiv 0
and free terminal states imply
\lambda_{i}(t_{1})=0
.

2.3
A Worked Multi-State Example

Consider the minimization problem

\min_{u(\cdot)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(x_{2}(t)+u(t)^{2}\right)\,dt

(22)

subject to

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=x_{2}(t),\qquad x_{1}(0)=0,\quad x_{1}(1)=1,

(23)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=u(t),\qquad x_{2}(0)=0.

(24)

2.3.1
Hamiltonian and Adjoint System

For minimization, use

H=x_{2}+u^{2}+\lambda_{1}x_{2}+\lambda_{2}u.

The adjoint equations are

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x_1}=0,

(25)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x_2}=-(1+\lambda_{1}).

(26)

Hence

\lambda_{1}(t)\equiv C,\qquad\lambda_{2}(t)=-(C+1)(t-1),

where the terminal condition
\lambda_{2}(1)=0
was used.

The stationarity condition gives

0=\partialderivative{H}{u}=2u+\lambda_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u^{*}(t)=-\frac{\lambda_{2}(t)}{2}=\frac{C+1}{2}(t-1).

2.3.2
State Recovery

Then

\dot{x}_{2}(t)=\frac{C+1}{2}(t-1)

implies

x_{2}(t)=\frac{C+1}{2}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-t\right),

using
x_{2}(0)=0
. Next,

\dot{x}_{1}=x_{2}

gives

x_{1}(t)=\frac{C+1}{2}\left(\frac{t^{3}}{6}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right),

using
x_{1}(0)=0
. Imposing
x_{1}(1)=1
yields
C=-7
, and therefore

\displaystyle u^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=3-3t,

(27)

\displaystyle x_{2}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=3t-\frac{3}{2}t^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle x_{1}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}t^{2}-\frac{1}{2}t^{3}.

(29)

Remark 7
.

The essential point is structural: with
n
states one introduces
n
adjoints. The dimension of the two-point boundary value problem doubles relative to the state dimension.

2.4
State Elimination and Problem Reduction

In multi-state models it is often possible to remove superfluous state variables before writing the adjoint system.

2.4.1
Structural Elimination

Suppose the problem is

\max_{u(\cdot)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t,x_{1},x_{2},u)\,dt

subject to

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=g_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2},u),

(30)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=g_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2},u),

(31)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{3}

\displaystyle=g_{3}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},u),

(32)

with given initial data. If neither the payoff nor the first two state equations depend on
x_{3}
, then the pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
with control
u
forms a closed optimal control problem. One solves for
(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},u^{*})
first and then recovers
x_{3}^{*}
from its forward ODE.

2.4.2
Elimination via Explicit Integration

A second mechanism occurs when one state can be solved explicitly and substituted into the objective.

Example 1

(Bioreactor-style reduction)
.

Suppose

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{G\,u(t)}{H+u(t)}x(t)-Dx(t)^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-Kx(t)z(t),

(34)

with objective

\min_{u(\cdot)}\left\{\ln z(T)+\int_{0}^{T}A\,u(t)\,dt\right\}.

The second state solves explicitly as

z(t)=z_{0}\exp\!\left(-K\int_{0}^{t}x(s)\,ds\right),

hence

\ln z(T)=\ln z_{0}-K\int_{0}^{T}x(s)\,ds.

Since
\ln z_{0}
is constant, the problem is equivalent to

\max_{u(\cdot)}\int_{0}^{T}\left(Kx(t)-Au(t)\right)\,dt

subject only to the scalar state equation for
x
. This removes the state
z
and also removes one adjoint variable from the necessary conditions.

2.5
Finite-Horizon Continuous-Time LQR

We now specialize to the linear quadratic regulator, one of the few classes for which a nearly closed-form structural solution exists.

2.5.1
Problem Statement

Let

\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t),\qquad x(0)=x_{0},

(35)

where

x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and
A(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.

The finite-horizon quadratic objective is

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}x(T)^{\top}Mx(T)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(x(t)^{\top}Q(t)x(t)+u(t)^{\top}R(t)u(t)\right)\,dt,

(36)

where

M=M^{\top}\succeq 0,\qquad Q(t)=Q(t)^{\top}\succeq 0,\qquad R(t)=R(t)^{\top}\succ 0.

The positive definiteness of
R(t)
ensures invertibility.

2.5.2
Hamiltonian Derivation

The Hamiltonian is

H=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Qx+\frac{1}{2}u^{\top}Ru+\lambda^{\top}(Ax+Bu).

The stationarity condition is

0=\partialderivative{H}{u}=Ru+B^{\top}\lambda,

hence

u^{*}(t)=-R(t)^{-1}B(t)^{\top}\lambda(t).

(37)

The adjoint equation is

\dot{\lambda}(t)=-Q(t)x(t)-A(t)^{\top}\lambda(t),\qquad\lambda(T)=Mx(T).

(38)

2.5.3
Riccati Ansatz

Assume

\lambda(t)=S(t)x(t),

(39)

where
S(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is to be determined. Differentiating and substituting the state and adjoint equations yields the Riccati differential equation

-\dot{S}(t)=A(t)^{\top}S(t)+S(t)A(t)-S(t)B(t)R(t)^{-1}B(t)^{\top}S(t)+Q(t),

(40)

with terminal condition

S(T)=M.

(41)

Theorem 3

(Finite-horizon LQR feedback law)
.

If
S(t)
solves the Riccati equation above, then the optimal control is

u^{*}(t)=-R(t)^{-1}B(t)^{\top}S(t)x(t).

(42)

Thus the optimal control is a linear state feedback, with gain matrix

K(t)=R(t)^{-1}B(t)^{\top}S(t).

The Riccati equation trades a forward-backward Hamiltonian system for a backward matrix ODE. After solving
S(t)
backward from
T
to
0
, one integrates the closed-loop state equation

\dot{x}(t)=\left(A(t)-B(t)R(t)^{-1}B(t)^{\top}S(t)\right)x(t).

2.5.4
Scalar Example

Consider

\min_{u(\cdot)}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(x(t)^{2}+u(t)^{2}\right)\,dt

subject to

\dot{x}(t)=u(t),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}.

Here
A=0
,
B=1
,
Q=1
,
R=1
, and
M=0
, so the Riccati equation becomes

-\dot{S}=1-S^{2},\qquad S(T)=0.

This scalar ODE integrates to

S(t)=\frac{1-e^{2(t-T)}}{1+e^{2(t-T)}}.

The optimal feedback law is

u^{*}(t)=-S(t)x(t),

and therefore the optimal state solves

\dot{x}(t)=-S(t)x(t).

A direct integration yields

x^{*}(t)=x_{0}\,\frac{e^{t}+e^{2T-t}}{1+e^{2T}},\qquad u^{*}(t)=x_{0}\,\frac{e^{t}-e^{2T-t}}{1+e^{2T}}.

2.6
Higher-Order Differential Equations as Systems

Pontryagin’s principle is formulated for first-order systems. Higher-order controlled differential equations must therefore be lifted to state-space form.

2.6.1
General Reduction

Suppose the problem is

\max_{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f\bigl(t,x,\dot{x},\dots,x^{(n)},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\bigr)\,dt

subject to

x^{(n+1)}(t)=g\bigl(t,x,\dot{x},\dots,x^{(n)},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\bigr),

with initial conditions

x(t_{0})=\alpha_{1},\quad\dot{x}(t_{0})=\alpha_{2},\quad\dots,\quad x^{(n)}(t_{0})=\alpha_{n+1}.

Define

x_{1}=x,\qquad x_{2}=\dot{x},\qquad\dots,\qquad x_{n+1}=x^{(n)}.

Then the system becomes

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{2},

(43)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{3},

(44)

(45)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n+1},

(46)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=g(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}),

(47)

with the original objective now rewritten in terms of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})
.

2.6.2
Second-Order Example

Consider

\min_{u(\cdot)}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(u(t)^{2}-x(t)^{2}\right)\,dt

subject to

x^{\prime\prime}(t)=u(t),\qquad x(0)=1,\quad x^{\prime}(0)=1.

Set

x_{1}=x,\qquad x_{2}=x^{\prime}.

Then

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{2},\qquad x_{1}(0)=1,

(48)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=u,\qquad x_{2}(0)=1.

(49)

The Hamiltonian is

H=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{1}x_{2}+\lambda_{2}u.

The first-order conditions are

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{H}{u}=u+\lambda_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u^{*}=-\lambda_{2},

(50)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x_1}=x_{1},

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x_2}=-\lambda_{1},

(52)

with terminal conditions
\lambda_{1}(\pi)=\lambda_{2}(\pi)=0
if the terminal state is free and there is no terminal payoff.

This example is a useful template: every higher-order optimal control problem can be converted to a first-order Hamiltonian system of larger dimension.

2.7
Isoperimetric Constraints

Integral constraints on the trajectory or the control are common in applications: fixed total fuel, fixed cumulative dosage, fixed resource expenditure, or a prescribed total harvested biomass.

2.7.1
General Form

Consider

\max_{u(\cdot)}\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt+\phi(x(t_{1}))\right\}

(53)

subject to

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=g(t,x(t),u(t)),\qquad x(t_{0})=x_{0},

(54)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}h(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt

\displaystyle=B,

(55)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq u(t)\leq b.

(56)

2.7.2
Auxiliary-State Conversion

Define the additional state

z(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}h(s,x(s),u(s))\,ds.

Then

\dot{z}(t)=h(t,x(t),u(t)),\qquad z(t_{0})=0,\qquad z(t_{1})=B.

Thus the isoperimetric constraint is converted into an endpoint constraint, and the problem becomes a standard multi-state problem.

Remark 8
.

This conversion is conceptually simple but extremely important. It is the standard way to bring integral equality constraints into the scope of Pontryagin’s principle without introducing an external Lagrange multiplier at the functional level.

2.7.3
Example

Consider

\min_{u(\cdot)}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u(t)^{2}\,dt

subject to

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=u(t),\qquad x(0)=0,\quad x(1)=1,

(57)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=2.

(58)

Introduce

\dot{z}(t)=x(t),\qquad z(0)=0,\quad z(1)=2.

The Hamiltonian becomes

H=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\lambda_{1}u+\lambda_{2}x.

Adjoint equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{x}=-\lambda_{2},

(59)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\partialderivative{H}{z}=0.

(60)

Hence
\lambda_{2}=C
and

\lambda_{1}(t)=k-Ct.

Stationarity gives

0=\partialderivative{H}{u}=u+\lambda_{1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u^{*}(t)=Ct-k.

Integrating twice,

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2}t^{2}-kt,

(61)

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{6}t^{3}-\frac{k}{2}t^{2}.

(62)

Using
x(1)=1
and
z(1)=2
, one finds

C=-18,\qquad k=-10.

Therefore

\displaystyle u^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=10-18t,

(63)

\displaystyle x^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=10t-9t^{2}.

(64)

2.8
Advanced remarks for PhD readers

2.8.1
On Existence Versus Necessary Conditions

Pontryagin’s principle provides necessary conditions, not existence. Existence typically requires separate arguments involving:

(i)

compactness of admissible controls,

(ii)

convexity/coercivity of the running cost,

(iii)

growth bounds for the dynamics,

(iv)

lower semicontinuity of the objective.

In nonlinear biological and engineering models, failure of convexity can create multiple extremals, singular arcs, or nonexistence in the classical sense.

2.8.2
Second-order issues

When the Hamiltonian is affine in the control and switching functions have high-order zeros, first-order conditions may be inconclusive. Singular arc analysis, generalized Legendre–Clebsch conditions, and second variation arguments become necessary.

2.8.3
Relationship to HJB

The Hamiltonian maximization condition in PMP matches the optimization appearing in the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation:

-V_{t}(t,x)=\max_{u\in U}\big\{f(t,x,u)+\nabla_{x}V(t,x)^{\top}g(t,x,u)\big\}.

Formally, the adjoint plays the role
\lambda(t)=\nabla_{x}V(t,x^{*}(t))
along an optimal trajectory when the value function is sufficiently smooth.

2.8.4
Singular Controls

When the Hamiltonian is affine in a control component,

H(\cdot)=\cdots+\psi(t)u,

the stationarity condition does not determine
u
in the interior. One must then differentiate the switching function
\psi
until the control appears explicitly. Singular arc analysis lies beyond the scope of these notes, but students should recognize the warning sign: a control enters linearly and boundedly, yet the switching function vanishes on an interval.

2.8.5
Numerics

General nonlinear multi-state optimal control problems are typically solved numerically by one of three paradigms:

(a)

Indirect methods:
solve the state-adjoint boundary value problem.

(b)

Direct methods:
discretize the control/state and solve a nonlinear program.

(c)

Dynamic programming / HJB methods:
feasible only in low dimension because of the curse of dimensionality.

LQR is exceptional because the Riccati equation yields an analytically structured feedback solution.

2.8.6
Take-home principles

(i)

The state and adjoint equations keep their Hamiltonian form under box constraints.

(ii)

The control law changes from
stationarity
to
pointwise optimization over the admissible set
.

(iii)

For scalar controls, sign conditions on
H_{u}
determine whether the optimizer lies at the lower bound, upper bound, or in the interior.

(iv)

For quadratic Hamiltonians, the optimal constrained control is the projection of the unconstrained optimizer onto the admissible interval.

(v)

Bang-bang structure emerges when the Hamiltonian is affine in the control.

(vi)

One must enforce bounds during the solve; clipping an unconstrained solution afterward is generally wrong.

(vii)

In indirect numerics, bounded controls are implemented by a projected control update inside each forward-backward sweep iteration.
