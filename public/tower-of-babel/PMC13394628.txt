Microbiome–Driven Carcinogenesis and Circulating Microbial Signals in Genitourinary Cancers
Authors: Atsunari Kawashima, Kentaro Jingushi, Takuro Saito, Toshihiro Uemura, Akinaru Yamamoto, Nesrine Sassi, Hiromu Horitani, Shunsuke Inoguchi, Yuki Horibe, Yu Ishizuya, Takuji Hayashi, Yoshiyuki Yamamoto, Norio Nonomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe microbiome is increasingly recognized as a regulator of carcinogenesis, tumor immunity, and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), but its role in genitourinary cancers remains less clearly defined than in melanoma or colorectal cancer. In this review, we summarize evidence that microbiome‐related pathways influence urothelial carcinoma (UC) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) across the continuum from tumor initiation to immunotherapy outcome. We highlight recent preclinical studies showing that gut microbial environments can causally modify urothelial carcinogenesis through carcinogen metabolism, inflammatory priming, and diet‐microbiome interactions in N‐butyl‐N‐(4‐hydroxybutyl) nitrosamine‐based mouse models, including a newly established upper tract urothelial carcinoma mouse model, as well as emerging clinical data indicating that circulating extracellular vesicle (EV)‐associated bacterial DNA is associated with tumor immune phenotypes and ICI outcomes in UC and RCC. Because blood is a low‐biomass matrix, we use the term “circulating microbial signals” rather than “blood microbiome” and emphasize the need for contamination‐aware analysis, careful EV characterization, and external validation. Current evidence supports a framework in which microbiome‐related signals connect intestinal ecology, systemic immune tone, tumor immune contexture, and treatment response, while indicating that circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA remains an exploratory biomarker platform.Keywords: extracellular vesicles, immunotherapy, liquid biopsy, microbiome, urothelial carcinoma

Introduction

The human body contains diverse microbial communities across multiple anatomical sites, with the gastrointestinal tract serving as the best‐characterized reservoir of host–microbe interactions that influence metabolism, immunity, and inflammatory tone [1, 2]. In oncology, the microbiome is now recognized as a regulator of carcinogenesis, tumor progression, and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. However, most mechanistic and translational evidence has been generated in melanoma, colorectal cancer, and lung cancer, whereas the role of microbiome‐related signals in genitourinary malignancies remains less clearly defined [3, 4, 5, 6].

This gap is clinically important for two reasons. First, urothelial carcinoma (UC) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) are immunologically heterogeneous diseases in which conventional tumor‐intrinsic biomarkers only partially explain outcome after ICI therapy. Second, stool profiling is useful for characterizing intestinal ecology but may not fully capture systemic host‐microbe interactions or the immune state of the tumor microenvironment. These limitations have stimulated interest in blood‐based microbial readouts.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are attractive in this setting because both host cells and microorganisms release membrane‐bound particles that can carry nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and metabolites in a relatively protected form in the circulation [7, 8, 9, 10]. At the same time, blood is a low‐biomass matrix. Microbial DNA detected in blood‐derived fractions should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly when contamination and EV heterogeneity are not rigorously addressed. For this reason, we use the term “circulating microbial signals” rather than “blood microbiome,” which may imply the existence of a stable ecologic community in blood [11].

In this review, we summarize evidence that microbiome‐related pathways influence genitourinary cancers across the continuum from carcinogenesis to immunotherapy outcome (Table 1). We focus on two areas in which the field has recently advanced: causal insights from BBN‐based models of urothelial carcinogenesis and emerging clinical studies suggesting that circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA reflects tumor immune contexture and may help stratify ICI response in UC and RCC.

Microbiome Pathways Relevant to Carcinogenesis and Immunotherapy

The microbiome can shape cancer biology through several overlapping mechanisms, including microbial genotoxicity, chronic inflammatory activation, immunometabolic signaling, and microbiota‐dependent modification of xenobiotics or carcinogens [6, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In colorectal cancer, a strong example of direct microbial mutagenesis is colibactin produced by pks‐positive Escherichia coli . Exposure of human intestinal organoids to pks‐positive E. coli induces DNA damage and generates a mutational signature that is detectable in human colorectal cancers, including as an early clonal event in some tumors [13, 14].

These colorectal cancer data should not be interpreted as direct evidence that identical genotoxic or invasion mechanisms operate in UC or RCC; rather, they provide paradigms that can now be tested in genitourinary settings.

Microbiome‐related effects also extend beyond mutation to local immune organization. In colorectal cancer, tumor invasion by intestinal bacteria, particularly Fusobacterium nucleatum , has been associated with accumulation of FOXP3‐low CD45RA‐negative non‐suppressive CD4‐positive T cells rather than expansion of FOXP3‐high effector Treg cells. These FOXP3‐low non‐Treg cells secrete inflammatory cytokines and are associated with better prognosis, whereas tumors dominated by suppression‐competent FOXP3‐high Treg cells show poorer prognosis. By contrast, enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis promotes intestinal inflammation and malignancy through exosome‐related regulation of host miRNA pathways [12, 15]. These studies illustrate that microbial products can remodel local immune organization in context‐dependent ways, ranging from inflammatory antitumor T‐cell states to inflammation‐associated epithelial damage and tumor promotion.

The same conceptual framework is relevant to immunotherapy. Landmark studies in melanoma showed that baseline gut microbiome composition differs between responders and nonresponders to anti‐PD‐1 therapy and that antibiotic exposure is associated with impaired efficacy [3, 4, 5]. Subsequent mechanistic studies demonstrated that defined commensal consortia can enhance antitumor immunity, that microbiome‐derived inosine can augment checkpoint blockade, and that the microbiota can strengthen systemic antitumor responses through dendritic‐cell migration and related immune circuits [16, 17, 18]. Additional pathways include tryptophan‐derived aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling, butyrate/niacin‐GPR109A signaling, bacterial outer membrane vesicles, STING‐type I interferon‐dependent myeloid reprogramming, Enterococcus peptidoglycan sensing, and bacterial or bacteriophage antigen cross‐reactivity with tumor‐directed T cells [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Early interventional trials further demonstrated that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can restore anti‐PD‐1 sensitivity in a subset of patients with refractory melanoma [26, 27].

In genitourinary cancers, the evidence base is smaller but increasingly persuasive. In metastatic UC, antibiotic exposure around the start of atezolizumab was associated with inferior survival, supporting the view that disruption of the gut microbiome may reduce ICI efficacy in at least some patients [28]. In RCC, baseline gut microbial composition has been linked to primary resistance, and prospective studies have suggested that microbiome‐directed intervention may modify clinical outcome [29, 30, 31]. In a randomized phase I study, the addition of the live bacterial product CBM588 to nivolumab plus ipilimumab improved progression‐free survival (PFS) and objective response rate; however, a later study with cabozantinib plus nivolumab suggested that the clinical effect is context dependent [30, 31]. Taken together, these data argue that the microbiome is not merely a bystander but part of the biologic system that influences response to immunotherapy.

Most recently, FMT‐based trials have extended this interventional evidence in metastatic RCC. The Phase I PERFORM trial showed that encapsulated healthy‐donor FMT combined with first‐line ICI‐based therapy was feasible and had no serious FMT‐related toxicity [32]. The randomized phase IIa TACITO trial tested donor FMT from complete ICI responders versus placebo FMT in patients receiving pembrolizumab plus axitinib; although the primary 12‐month PFS endpoint was not met, median PFS favored donor FMT [33]. These data support microbiome modulation as a plausible therapeutic strategy while emphasizing that donor selection, strain‐level engraftment, and concomitant systemic therapy may determine clinical benefit.

A recurring lesson from these studies is that the microbiome‐immunotherapy relationship is highly context dependent. Taxonomic associations differ across cohorts, antibiotic effects depend on timing and treatment setting, and functional properties may be more reproducible than any single named organism. For UC and RCC, the key translational question may therefore be less about identifying one universally favorable taxon and more about determining which microbial functions or circulating signatures mark an immune‐permissive or immune‐restrictive host state.

Causal Evidence From Experimental Urothelial Carcinogenesis

Experimental mouse models have been central to the study of urothelial carcinogenesis. The BBN‐induced bladder cancer model is especially valuable because it recapitulates stepwise disease progression and several molecular features of human urothelial cancer [34, 35]. Historically, however, most BBN‐based systems have modeled bladder tumors rather than upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC), and they have been used primarily as carcinogen exposure models rather than as platforms for dissecting host‐environment interactions.

That perspective has changed with the recognition that gut microbes can directly regulate urothelial carcinogen metabolism (Figure 1). Roje and colleagues showed that intestinal microbiota metabolize BBN into active intermediates in the lower gut and thereby influence distal urothelial tumor formation. Depletion of the gut microbiota reduced production of N‐butyl‐N‐(3‐carboxypropyl) nitrosamine (BCPN), an active metabolite of BBN, and markedly suppressed tumor formation, providing direct causal evidence that intestinal microbial metabolism can determine exposure of urothelial tissues to carcinogenic products [36]. This finding shifts the BBN model from a simple carcinogen system to a tool for mechanistic microbiome research.

A BBN‐based mouse model suggests that host background, sex, and diet‐microbiome interactions influence susceptibility to upper tract urothelial carcinoma. BALB/c female mice show susceptibility, whereas other strain/sex combinations show limited or no tumor development under carcinogen exposure. Dietary modulation is associated with changes in intestinal microbiota, inflammatory activation in upper urinary tract tissues, and tumor suppression.

A second important advance is the establishment of a UTUC‐prone BBN model in BALB/c female mice [37]. In this system, BALB/c females showed striking susceptibility to invasive upper tract tumors, whereas other strains and sexes exhibited different patterns of disease development. Transcriptomic analyses supported molecular similarity between the mouse tumors and human UTUC, making the model relevant not only biologically but also translationally [37]. The model therefore offers a practical platform for studying how host background and intestinal environmental factors modify UTUC risk. It is also conceptually important because it shows that microbiome‐linked cancer susceptibility is unlikely to be uniform across strain, sex, diet, or tissue context.

A notable feature of this model is that inflammatory activation precedes overt tumor formation. Dysplastic epithelial change, increased proliferative activity, macrophage infiltration, and upregulation of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐alpha, IL‐1beta, and IL‐6 were detectable at early stages, before macroscopic tumors emerged [37, 38]. These observations are consistent with the broader concept that chronic inflammatory priming can drive epithelial carcinogenesis and with the idea that gut‐derived microbial signals shape systemic inflammatory tone [2, 39].

The most compelling evidence for a diet‐microbiome‐carcinogenesis axis comes from dietary intervention in the same UTUC‐prone model. BALB/c female mice displayed a distinct intestinal microbial profile, including reduced abundance of Parabacteroides distasonis , together with a metabolic environment associated with alanine [37, 40]. When mice were maintained on an alanine‐free diet, Parabacteroides abundance was restored, inflammatory cytokine expression was suppressed, and tumor formation was prevented despite continued BBN exposure [37]. This intervention provides unusually strong causal support for the proposition that diet can reshape the microbiome, reprogram inflammatory signaling, and alter carcinogenic outcome in the urinary tract.

Other BBN‐based studies point in the same direction. Sulforaphane attenuated bladder pathology while normalizing intestinal flora and gut barrier function, pentadecanoic acid reduced early pathologic progression while altering gut microbiome features, and MyD88 deficiency suppressed bladder carcinogenesis with accompanying changes in microbial trajectories [41, 42, 43]. Although these studies differ in design and mechanistic depth, they converge on a common message: microbiome‐linked environmental and immune pathways can influence urothelial tumor development.

The field has therefore moved beyond simple association. At present, at least three microbiome‐related processes appear mechanistically relevant to urothelial carcinogenesis: microbiota‐dependent carcinogen metabolism, inflammatory priming before overt tumor formation, and diet‐microbiome interactions that modify host susceptibility. What remains unresolved is which taxa or metabolites are the key functional effectors, how local and systemic signaling should be integrated, and how these findings can be translated to human disease.

Circulating Microbial Signals and EV‐Associated Bacterial DNA

If microbiome‐related effects on genitourinary cancers are systemic as well as local, then blood‐based microbial readouts may provide information complementary to stool profiling rather than replacing it. Stool analysis remains the most direct approach for characterizing intestinal ecology, whereas circulating microbial signals may capture host‐microbe interactions influenced by intestinal permeability, microbial translocation, vesicle release, host clearance, urinary tract microbial ecosystems, and tumor‐associated inflammation [1, 2, 44, 45]. Therefore, paired assessment of stool, blood, urine, tumor tissue, and immune phenotypes is likely to be more informative than any single biospecimen.

Several studies have identified bacterial DNA or intracellular bacteria in tumor tissues across multiple cancer types, suggesting that microbial signals can be present within tumor microenvironments [46, 47, 48]. Recent work has also shown that intratumoral microbiota can influence spatial and cellular heterogeneity within tumors and that tumor‐resident intracellular microbiota can promote metastatic colonization in breast cancer [46, 47]. However, tumor and blood specimens are typically low‐biomass samples, making them highly vulnerable to contamination, database artifacts, and batch effects [49, 50, 51]. A large population study found no evidence for a common core blood microbiome in healthy individuals after contamination‐aware filtering, which supports the view that blood‐derived microbial DNA is better interpreted as sparse or transient circulating signal rather than proof of stable blood colonization [11].

EV‐enriched fractions are biologically plausible carriers of such signals. Both host cells and bacteria release vesicles that can transport DNA, RNA, proteins, lipids, and other molecules in protected form [7, 8, 9, 10]. Bacterial vesicles can directly engage host immune pathways, and microbiota‐derived EVs may represent one mechanism by which local microbial ecosystems communicate with distant tissues. Nevertheless, EV preparations are heterogeneous. Depending on the isolation method, they may contain mixed vesicle populations and nonvesicular particles, so any claim about function or cellular origin must be made cautiously unless orthogonal characterization is available [8, 10, 52, 53].

Within this framework, two lines of clinical observation in genitourinary oncology are noteworthy (Figure 2). In UC, a Firmicutes‐derived DNA signal detected in serum EV‐enriched fractions was associated with immune‐low tumors and poorer pembrolizumab outcome [54]. In RCC, a Bacteroidia‐derived DNA signal detected in serum EV‐enriched fractions was associated with Treg‐like CD4‐positive tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes and shorter nivolumab PFS, and a related RCC study suggested that serum EV‐associated bacterial information can reflect adherent perinephric fat inflammation [55, 56]. A parallel gastric cancer study further supports the broader feasibility of this EV‐based circulating biomarker platform [57].

At the same time, detection of bacterial DNA in an EV‐enriched fraction does not establish whether the source is intestinal, urinary, intratumoral, or procedural. Nor does it prove that the material is truly vesicle enclosed rather than co‐isolated with vesicles. These uncertainties argue against overinterpretation and reinforce the need for a contamination‐aware, technically conservative framework.

For translation, methodological rigor is essential. Studies of circulating microbial DNA should incorporate extraction blanks, PCR blanks, batch tracking, contamination‐aware computational analysis, and orthogonal validation whenever possible [11, 49, 50, 51, 58]. Because the readout is obtained from EV‐enriched fractions, investigators should also follow current EV reporting guidance, including MISEV2018 and MISEV2023, and avoid over‐attributing function or source when the preparation has not been deeply characterized [52, 53]. At present, EV‐associated bacterial DNA is best viewed as a promising but early biomarker class rather than as a validated standalone assay.

Clinical Implications of Circulating Microbial Signals in UC and RCC

Clinical response to ICIs is shaped by the immune contexture of the tumor microenvironment. Tumor‐intrinsic markers such as PD‐L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and related gene‐expression features can be informative, but they do not fully explain the heterogeneity of clinical benefit observed across patients [59, 60]. This has increased interest in systemic determinants of tumor immunity, including microbiome‐related signals.

In our previous studies, multiparametric flow cytometric analysis of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes identified clinically relevant immune phenotypes in both UC and RCC [61, 62] (Figures 2 and 3). In UC, tumors could be broadly separated into immune‐high and immune‐low states based on T‐cell infiltration and checkpoint‐related activation patterns [61]. In RCC, more dysfunctional immune states were associated with aggressive disease, and a Treg‐like CD4‐positive population characterized by a PD‐1‐low TIM‐3‐positive phenotype showed transcriptional features including FOXP3, IL2RA, and CCR8 [62, 63]. These observations provided a framework for testing whether blood‐based microbial signals mirror the immune landscape within the tumor.

Proposed framework linking tumor immune contexture, circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA, and clinical outcome of immune checkpoint inhibitor‐based therapy in urothelial carcinoma and renal cell carcinoma. Tumor immune phenotypes, defined by flow cytometric analysis of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes from surgical specimens, are shown together with corresponding circulating microbial signals detected in serum/plasma EV‐associated bacterial DNA. In urothelial carcinoma, Firmicutes‐derived DNA signals detected in serum EV‐enriched fractions are associated with an immune‐low phenotype and less favorable outcome with pembrolizumab. In renal cell carcinoma, higher Bacteroidia‐derived DNA signals detected in serum EV‐enriched fractions are associated with a more immunoregulatory microenvironment enriched for Treg‐like CD4‐positive T‐cell populations and with less favorable outcome with nivolumab. These findings support blood EV‐associated bacterial DNA as a candidate orthogonal biomarker reflecting host–microbe–immune interactions in genitourinary cancers.

In UC, a higher Firmicutes‐derived DNA signal in serum EV‐enriched fractions, rather than evidence of circulating intact bacteria, was associated with an immune‐low phenotype marked by reduced CD3‐positive T‐cell infiltration and lower activation of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes [54]. Importantly, patients with lower Firmicutes‐derived DNA signal had a better response to pembrolizumab and longer PFS (Figure 3). In RCC, a higher Bacteroidia‐derived DNA signal in serum EV‐enriched fractions correlated with the Treg‐like CD4‐positive population described above and was associated with shorter PFS after nivolumab treatment [55]. Although the taxa involved differ between the two diseases, the broader signal is similar: circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA appears to track tumor immune state and clinical outcome.

These biomarkers should not be viewed as replacements for tumor genomics or tissue‐based immune profiling. Their potential value is orthogonal. Whereas PD‐L1, tumor mutational burden, or gene‐expression signatures describe properties of the tumor and its immediate microenvironment, circulating microbial signals may report aspects of host‐microbe‐immune interaction that are not captured by tumor‐intrinsic assays alone. This may be especially relevant in genitourinary cancers, where discordance between apparent immune infiltration and actual ICI benefit remains a practical problem in clinic. Even if future validation studies revise the exact taxa involved, the convergence of blood‐based microbial signals, tumor immune phenotypes, and ICI outcome in both UC and RCC is strong enough to justify continued translational study.

Current Limitations and Future Directions

The current literature also has clear limitations. Most clinical studies of circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA in UC and RCC have been based on small single‐center cohorts, including several from our group, and should therefore be regarded as hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Taxonomic profiling by 16S rRNA sequencing has limited resolution, contamination cannot be excluded completely in low‐biomass samples, and the biologic relationship between gut ecology, blood EV‐associated DNA, and tumor immune contexture remains unresolved. Blood‐derived microbial signals may reflect intestinal ecology, urinary tract microbiota, intratumoral bacteria, or host barrier dysfunction, and these possibilities are not mutually exclusive. In addition, differences in EV isolation and sequencing workflows make cross‐study comparison difficult.

The next step is not simply to collect more sequencing data. What is needed is prospective multicenter validation combined with harmonized low‐biomass controls, careful EV characterization, and paired analysis of stool, blood, tumor tissue, and immune phenotypes. Functional studies should then test whether the taxa or vesicle‐associated signals identified in blood are mechanistically involved in immune regulation or merely correlated with it.

In summary, recent work supports a model in which microbiome‐related pathways influence genitourinary cancers from carcinogenesis to immunotherapy response (Figure 4, Graphical abstract). BBN‐based systems now provide causal support for diet‐microbiome and carcinogen‐metabolism effects in urothelial cancer, while early clinical studies suggest that circulating EV‐associated bacterial DNA may serve as an accessible readout of tumor immune contexture in UC and RCC. The concept is promising, but rigorous validation is still required before circulating microbial signals can be adopted as clinically robust biomarkers.
