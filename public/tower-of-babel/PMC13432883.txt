A novel Acinetobacter phage reveals altered virulence traits in phage-resistant strains
Authors: Jingchen Hao, Jun Xie, Kangrong Ma, Xinrong Li, Xutao Chen, Guangyu Bao, Jian Hu, Guocai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhage therapy represents a promising alternative for combating bacterial infections. This study employed an A. baumannii isolate harboring the I-F CRISPR-Cas system as a host to isolate phage and evaluate its biological characteristics. Phage-resistant mutants were screened using a double-layer agar plate assay, and the underlying molecular mechanisms were identified through whole-genome sequencing, followed by validation via gene knockout. Transcriptome sequencing was subsequently applied to alterations in the global regulatory networks of these mutants. Our results demonstrate the successful isolation of a novel myovirus, stable at 40–50°C, which was successfully isolated and found to utilize the capsule as its adsorption receptor. Whole-genomic analysis confirmed its distinction from currently published phages. Investigation into the primary resistance mechanism revealed that the capsule loss, due to an insertional mutation in the UDP-glucose 4-epimerase encoding gene galE. This conclusion was further validated through targeted gene knockout of galE. This defect concurrently attenuated bacterial virulence, as demonstrated by significantly reduced lethality in the Galleria mellonella infection model and enhanced susceptibility to serum killing, while concurrently enhancing the capacity for biofilm formation. Transcriptomic profiling indicated that the ΔgalE significantly upregulated multiple biofilm-associated genes and remodeled the transcriptomic-wide regulatory. Furthermore, the combination of carbenicillin or ceftazidime with the phage exhibited a synergistic effect in vitro, effectively inhibiting biofilm formation and suppressing the emergence of phage resistance. Overall, this work characterizes a novel phage and delineates the host’s biological network changes triggered by phage resistance, offering valuable insights for developing phage-based antimicrobial strategies.KEYWORDS: Acinetobacter baumannii, phage resistance, virulence, biofilm, capsule

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is a prevalent opportunistic pathogen responsible for severe nosocomial infections. In recent years, the rapid development of antimicrobial resistance in this bacterium has posed a serious threat to global public health. Among diverse resistance mechanisms, OXA‑23 family class D β‑lactamases play a major role in conferring carbapenem resistance to Carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii [1]. The genes encoding these carbapenemases can be rapidly disseminated among bacterial populations through mobile genetic elements, including insertion sequences and transposons [2]. A. baumannii can form biofilms on both biotic and abiotic surfaces, which facilitates its colonization and infection, enhances its ability to withstand environmental stress, and ultimately limits the efficacy of anti-infective therapy [3,4]. In A. baumannii, biofilm formation is coordinately regulated by several two-component systems (TCSs), including PmrAB, BaeSR, and BfmSR [5–7]. Among these, BfmSR is also associated with the expression of important structural components, including outer membrane proteins, lipopolysaccharides, fimbriae, and the polysaccharide capsule [8,9]. These findings indicate the existence of a sophisticated regulatory network governing biofilm formation in A. baumannii. Functionally, bacterial biofilms represent a crucial adaptive strategy, not only enhancing tolerance to diverse environmental stresses but also facilitating the horizontal gene transfer of antimicrobial resistance determinants, thereby compounding challenges in infection control [10,11]. Given that biofilm formation constitutes a major virulence determinant in A. baumannii, targeted disruption of biofilms has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic strategy for combating its infections.

Given the limited therapeutic options for multidrug-resistant bacterial infections, there is an urgent need to develop novel treatment strategies to combat the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance. Phage therapy has consequently emerged as a promising alternative therapeutic approach [12]. Phages can overcome common antimicrobial resistance mechanisms and specifically eliminate target bacteria, showing great application prospects. However, the rapid emergence of phage-resistance mutations is one of the major drawbacks of phage therapy. Furthermore, these phage-resistant mutants usually exhibit the recovery of antibiotic sensitivity and a reduction in virulence [13]. In A. baumannii, the capsule serves as the primary receptor for phage adsorption. Consequently, phage resistance in this pathogen frequently arises from mutations disrupting the capsule synthesis gene cluster, leading to a loss of this critical surface polysaccharide [14]. The UDP-glucose 4-epimerase encoded by galE is thought to be involved in surface polysaccharide synthesis and bacterial virulence [15,16]. A key therapeutic objective is to develop strategies that steer the evolution of phage resistance toward clinically advantageous outcomes, such as attenuated virulence or restored antibiotic susceptibility [13]. The CRISPR-CAS system is also commonly found in bacteria to mediate immune defense against bacteriophage [17]. Guided by crRNA, specific Cas proteins achieve programmable cleavage of invading exogenous DNA [18]. In response, bacteriophages can encode anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins to effectively infect bacteria possessing an intact CRISPR-Cas system [19]. Furthermore, studies have indicated an inverse correlation between the presence of CRISPR-Cas systems and antibiotic resistance [20,21]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that functional CRISPR-Cas systems can acquire spacers targeting mobile genetic elements, such as plasmids, thereby preventing the horizontal acquisition of exogenous antibiotic resistance genes. While previous studies have predominantly characterized phages isolated from CRISPR-Cas-negative hosts, this study employed an A. baumannii host harboring a complete type I-F CRISPR-Cas system for phage isolation. This approach allowed us to investigate the phage–host interaction within a functional bacterial immune context. Critically, we then used the newly isolated phage to screen for spontaneous resistant mutants and identified that disruption of the galE gene mediates phage resistance. To further understand the broader consequences of acquiring galE-mediated resistance, we utilized transcriptomic profiling to elucidate global changes in regulatory networks. Finally, we evaluated the combined efficacy of antibiotics and the isolated phage in suppressing both biofilm formation and the evolution of phage resistance.

Method

Bacterial collection and phage isolation

A previous study provides a partial characterization of this bacterial isolate [22]. The strain used here, A. baumannii AB43, was originally isolated from the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University. It carries a complete I-F CRISPR-Cas system and has been preserved in our laboratory collection.

Environmental sewage samples were collected and processed by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then filtered and transferred into sterile conical flasks. Subsequently, sterile LB broth and a logarithmic-phase bacterial culture were added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 37°C with shaking. The resulting culture was centrifuged again at 8,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered to obtain a crude phage lysate. Phage titers were quantified using a double-layer agar plaque assay. A single, well-isolated plaque was picked and subjected to three rounds of enrichment and purification to obtain a purified phage stock, which was used in all subsequent experiments.

Evaluation of phage biological characteristics

Optimal multiplicity of infection

Bacterial cultures were mixed with phage suspensions at multiplicity of infection (MOI) ratios of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001, respectively. The mixtures were co-incubated for 4 h at 37°C with shaking. Subsequently, the number of infectious phage particles in each sample was quantified using the standard double-layer agar assay to determine the phage titer (PFU/mL).

One-step growth curve of phage

The bacterial suspension was mixed with phages at the optimal MOI and incubated to allow adsorption. The mixture was centrifuged to remove the supernatant containing unabsorbed phage particles. The resulting bacterial pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of fresh, sterile LB broth and incubated at 37°C with shaking to allow for phage proliferation. To monitor the replication kinetics, 200 μL aliquots were withdrawn at designated time points post-infection, and the phage titer in each sample was immediately determined using the double-layer agar.

Phage thermal stability

To evaluate the thermal stability of the phage, purified phage stock (initial titer approximately 1 × 108 PFU/mL) was aliquoted and incubated at 40°C, 50°C, 60°C, and 70°C for 1 h, respectively. After the heat treatment, the samples were immediately cooled on ice to halt any further thermal effects. The surviving infectious phage particles in each sample were then quantified using the double-layer agar plaque assay to determine the relative titer compared to an untreated control.

Identification of phage adsorption receptors

Fresh overnight bacterial cultures were harvested, and the bacterial suspension was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard. A 1 mL aliquot of this suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in a sodium periodate (Sangon, China) solution prepared in acetic acid. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 25°C for 1 h. After incubation, the cells were collected by centrifugation and washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resulting pellet was finally resuspended in 1 mL of PBS. Phages were then added to the bacterial suspension and co-cultured at 37°C. Following a 15-min adsorption period, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The phage titer in the supernatant was determined using the double-layer agar plaque assay.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

The antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates was determined and interpreted by the broth microdilution method in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [23]. Firstly, a series of two-fold antibiotic dilutions was prepared in 96-well microtiter plates using cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth as the medium. Each well was inoculated with a standardized bacterial suspension equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard. Following incubation at 37°C for 16–20 h, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined for each antibiotic, including β-lactams, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones. The MIC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Quality control was ensured by concurrently testing the reference strain Escherichia coli ATCC 25922.

Biofilm formation ability and serum resistance test

Biofilm formation was assessed using a 96-well plate model. To evaluate the impact of phage and/or antibiotics on biofilm formation, a 2 µL aliquot of the bacterial culture was diluted 1:100 in sterile LB broth containing the indicated concentrations of phage or antibiotics, and 200 µL of this dilution was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, the planktonic cells were removed, and the adhered biofilms were gently washed three times with PBS. The biofilms were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) for 15 min. Following staining, the wells were rinsed three times with PBS to remove unbound dye and air-dried. The bound crystal violet was subsequently solubilized with 95% ethanol. Finally, the optical density (OD) of the solution in each well was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm.

The serum resistance of bacterial isolates was assessed using a standardized bactericidal assay [24]. Firstly, bacterial cultures were grown overnight in LB plate at 37°C. The cells were then harvested and adjusted to a density of approximately 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL (equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard) using sterile normal saline. This bacterial suspension was further diluted 1:100 in sterile normal saline. A 25 μL aliquot of the diluted suspension was mixed with 75 μL of pooled healthy human serum. The mixture was incubated at 37°C with constant shaking, and 10 μL aliquots were collected at 0-, 1-, 2-, and 3-h post‑incubation were serially diluted and spot‑plated onto LB agar plates to determine viable counts.

Construction of a gene deletion mutant and complementation

The gene knockout strains were constructed using a homologous recombination-based method [25]. Firstly, the upstream and downstream homology arms flanking the target gene were amplified by PCR. A fusion fragment was then generated by splicing these arms to a kanamycin resistance cassette (pKD4) via overlap extension PCR. The resulting fragment was electroporated into electrocompetent A. baumannii AB43 cells containing the pAT04 plasmid (Tetracycline resistance). Transformed cells were selected on LB plates containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL), and subsequent excision of the KanR cassette was mediated by the pAT03 plasmid (Carbenicillin resistance). The pAT04 and pAT03 plasmids were eliminated through passage on antibiotic-free LB agar. Mutants were confirmed by colony PCR and sequencing. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

A DNA fragment encompassing the full-length target gene along with its upstream region was amplified and cloned into the E. coli and Acinetobacter shuttle vector pWH1266 [26]. After propagation in E. coli DH5α, the plasmid was electroporated into the knockout mutant competent cells, and transformants were selected on LB agar plate containing carbenicillin (50 µg/mL).

Galleria mellonella infection model

A 10 μL aliquot of bacterial suspension prepared in sterile normal saline was injected into healthy G. mellonella larvae via the posterior proleg using a sterile syringe. Sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) was administered equivalently as a negative control. Inoculated larvae were maintained at 37°C, and survival was recorded at 12-h intervals. Each experimental group consisted of ten larvae, with three independent biological replicates. Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (v9.2.0), and statistical significance was determined by the log-rank test.

Whole genome sequence and analysis

Genomic DNA was fragmented and processed through end repair, A-tailing, and Illumina adapter ligation to construct sequencing libraries, which were then quality-controlled using Qubit and Bioanalyzer before Illumina sequencing. Raw sequencing data underwent stringent filtering to remove low-quality reads, adapter contamination, and potential host DNA. De novo assembly was performed using multiple approaches: SOAP de novo, SPAdes, and ABySS, with results integrated using CISA software, followed by gap filling. The complete phage genome was assembled after subtraction of the host genome sequence. General characteristics (e.g. genomic features, lifestyle, taxonomy) of phages were analyzed using the PhageScope database [27]. Sequence alignment was conducted using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and SnapGene software. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were calculated with OAT software, which was also employed for phylogenetic tree construction. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) platform (https://itol.embl.de/). The CRISPR-Cas systems and their corresponding spacer sequences were analyzed using the CRISPRone online service (https://omics.informatics.indiana.edu/CRISPRone/index.php) [28].

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA from prokaryotic samples was quality-controlled using Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, followed by ribosomal RNA depletion and ethanol precipitation. The RNA was fragmented and converted to cDNA using random hexamer primers, with strand specificity maintained through dUTP incorporation during second-strand synthesis. Libraries were prepared through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, and PCR amplification, then quantified using Qubit and Bioanalyzer before Illumina sequencing by synthesis. Raw sequencing data were processed with fastp to remove adapters and low-quality reads, followed by alignment to reference genomes using Bowtie2. Transcriptome features, including novel genes, operons, TSS/TTS, and antisense transcripts, were identified using Rockhopper, while UTRs were analyzed with RBSfinder and TransTermHP. Small RNAs were predicted and characterized using RNAfold and IntaRNA. Gene expression was quantified as FPKM using HTSeq, with differential expression analysis performed using DESeq2 (for samples with replicates). Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes was analyzed through GO terms (GOseq) and KEGG pathways (KOBAS), with additional pathway-level insights gained through Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using predefined gene sets from GO [29] and KEGG databases [30].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Congo red staining

TEM observation of phage morphology was performed at the Testing Center of Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China). Phages were negatively stained with 0.2% phosphotungstic acid before observation and subsequently observed using transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi TEM system, Japan).

Bacterial colonies grown overnight on blood agar plates were selected for staining. A small amount of each colony was emulsified in a drop of Congo red dye solution (Macklin, China) pre-placed on a glass slide to create a smear. After air-drying, the smear was decolorized using dilute hydrochloric acid, followed by counterstaining with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, China) for 30 s. The slide was then gently rinsed under running water, air-dried thoroughly, and examined under an oil immersion objective using an upright microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Extraction and visualization of exopolysaccharides

Exopolysaccharides (EPS) were extracted from A. baumannii isolates using a bacterial exopolysaccharide assay kit (Solarbio, China). Bacterial strains were cultured to the logarithmic growth phase. Subsequently, reagent A was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions to resuspend the cells, followed by centrifugation to collect the bacterial pellet. Reagent B was then added to resuspend the pellet, and the mixture was incubated at 80°C for 6 h. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant containing the extracted EPS was collected.

The EPS samples were separated by SDS‑PAGE using a 6% gel at 150 V for 75 min. The gel was then washed three times at 50°C with a fixation buffer consisting of 25% ethanol and 10% acetic acid. Subsequently, the gel was stained with 0.1% Alcian blue (Macklin, China) at 50°C for 15 min, followed by destaining overnight at room temperature in the same fixation buffer (25% ethanol/10% acetic acid) [31].

Bacterial cells were fixed in glutaraldehyde at 4°C, washed three times with PBS, and post‑fixed with 0.1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. After PBS rinsing, samples were dehydrated through an ethanol and acetone series, embedded, and polymerized at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut, mounted on copper grids, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 10 min in the dark, followed by lead citrate for 10 min under CO2‑free conditions. After washing, the grids were air‑dried overnight and observed under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi TEM system, Japan).

Growth kinetics experiment of bacteria/phage co-culture

After mixing phages with bacteria at MOI, they were resuspended in 6 mL of LB broth. Cultures were incubated at 37°C with shaking to select phage-resistant strains. Moreover, we tested the effect of the addition of specific antibiotics on bacterial-phage growth kinetics using an Eppendorf BioPhotometer (Hamburg, Germany) to measure the optical density.

Bacteriophage-insensitive mutation frequency

The frequency of emergence of phage‑insensitive mutants was determined following a method described in a previous study [32]. Bacterial strains were cultured to the logarithmic growth phase and then diluted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension and 100 µL of the phage suspension were mixed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The mixture was then mixed with semi‑solid agar and poured onto LB solid agar plates. After 24 h of incubation, the number of surviving bacterial colonies was counted. The frequency of emergence of phage‑insensitive mutants was calculated as the ratio of the number of viable bacteria under a high MOI to that of the control group (in which bacteria were treated only with SM buffer).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 software. Phage titers and serum-resistant bacterial counts were log10-transformed for graphical presentation. One‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons was employed to assess the differences among groups, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of bacteriophages

Following enrichment and purification, we successfully isolated a novel bacteriophage, designated AbYft_4, from A. baumannii. In a double-layer agar plaque assay, AbYft_4 formed small plaques after 20 h of incubation (Figure 1(a)). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that AbYft_4 exhibits a morphology consistent with the Myoviridae family, characterized by a contractile tail (Figure 1(b)). A one-step growth curve analysis indicated that the phage achieved its maximum burst size at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 (Figure 1(c)). AbYft_4 demonstrated high thermal stability at 40°C and 50°C. However, its activity declined moderately at 60°C and was completely inactivated following incubation at 70°C for 1 h (Figure 1(d)). The one‑step growth curve demonstrated that the phage titer was relatively stable from 30 to 90 min, gradually rose from 90 to 210 min, sharply increased between 210 and 240 min, and subsequently plateaued after 240 min (Figure 1(e)). A significant increase in free phage particles was observed after sodium periodate treatment of host cells, suggesting that a surface polysaccharide structure serves as the receptor for phage adsorption (Figure 1(f)).

Whole-genome sequencing revealed that AbYft_4 possesses a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 95,374 bp with a GC content of 33.6%. Taxonomic annotation confirmed its taxonomic classification within the viral class Caudoviricetes. Bioinformatic screening detected no homologs of known virulence or antibiotic resistance genes in the AbYft_4 genome. The lifestyle of phage AbYft_4 was determined using the PhageScope database, which predicted it to be virulent. A BLASTn comparison against the NCBI database revealed that the genome of AbYft_4 shared 58% coverage and 95.45% similarity with Acinetobacter phage BS46 (GenBank: MN276049.1), and 58% coverage and 92.95% similarity with Acinetobacter phage Phab24 (GenBank: MZ477002.1). The average nucleotide identity (ANI) between AbYft_4 and phages Phab24 and BS46 was 91.38% and 90.95%, respectively. Furthermore, ANI values with Acinetobacter phages Bestia (GenBank: MW316733.1) and Herod (GenBank: MW316732.1) were 88.49% and 90.95%, respectively (Figure S1). Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that AbYft_4 forms a distinct clade, indicating significant genetic divergence from other reported Acinetobacter phages (Figure 1(g)).

Selection of phage-resistance mutation

To investigate the mechanism of resistance to the novel phage in A. baumannii, phage-resistant mutants were isolated using double-layer agar plates with this phage. We first assessed the emergence frequency of bacteriophage-insensitive mutants (BIMs) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. For strain AB43, the BIM frequency was determined to be 3.61 × 10−3 ±1.13 × 10−3.

Subsequently, a single phage‑resistant isolate derived from AB43 (designated PR AB43) was selected for further study. Whole-genome sequencing of these mutants revealed a premature termination mutation in the galE gene, which encodes UDP-glucose 4-epimerase (Figure 2(a)). The galE gene is situated within the capsular biosynthesis locus, flanked by an ISAba26 insertion sequence and the pgi gene (Figure 2(b)). Further sequence analysis confirmed that the galE coding sequence was disrupted by an IS3 family transposase (Figure 2(b)). No mutations were identified in the CRISPR-Cas system or its associated genetic loci in the resistant strain PR AB43. Sequence alignment revealed that the cas and csy genes remained unaltered compared to the wild type strain. Moreover, PR AB43 encoded the same number of spacers as the original strain, and the spacer sequences were identical between the two strains. To determine whether the acquisition of phage resistance was associated with CRISPR-Cas-mediated immunity, we performed sequence alignments comparing these spacer sequences against the genome of the phage used in this study. No homology was detected, indicating that the resistant strain lacks a spacer specifically targeting this phage.

Alterations in physiological characteristics mediated by galE disruption

To functionally validate the role of galE in phage resistance, we constructed a galE deletion mutant (AB43ΔgalE) in the AB43 background, based on the precise location where the galE gene was disrupted by an insertion sequence in the spontaneous phage‑resistant isolate PR AB43 (Figure 2(a)). The ΔgalE mutant proved resistant to phage-mediated lysis, confirming the necessity of galE for phage infection (Figure 2(c)). Congo red staining showed that the surface of the ΔgalE mutant exhibited structural defects compared to the wild-type strain (Figure 2(d)). Phage-resistant AB43 (PR AB43) and ΔgalE mutants showed significantly enhanced biofilm formation compared with the wild-type strain AB43 (Figure 2(e)). Accordingly, the survival rates of PR AB43 and the ΔgalE mutant in serum were significantly reduced compared to the wild-type strain (Figure 2(f)). While the wild-type AB43 strain exhibited only a slight reduction in viable counts after 3 h of serum exposure, both the phage-resistant (PR AB43) and ΔgalE mutants were rapidly cleared, with no viable bacteria detected at early time points (Figure 2(f)). To further evaluate the effect of galE disruption on exopolysaccharide production, Alcian blue staining was carried out. A polysaccharide band was observed near the 180 kDa position of the protein marker. The intensity of this band was markedly reduced in both strain PR AB43 and the ΔgalE mutant compared to AB43 (Figure 3(a)). Observation by transmission electron microscopy demonstrated that the wild‑type AB43 strain possessed an evident and intact capsular layer surrounding the cell surface (Figure 3(b)). However, both the phage‑resistant isolate PR AB43 and the ΔgalE mutant displayed significantly thinner and atypical capsular structures compared to the wild‑type strain (Figure 3(c,d)).

In the Galleria mellonella infection model, both the PR AB43 and ΔgalE mutant strains exhibited significantly attenuated virulence compared to the wild-type AB43 strain, at final inoculum concentrations of 1 × 106 CFU/larva. Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in mortality was observed between the PR AB43 and galE mutant groups at either concentration (Figure 4).

Transcriptional profile changes mediated by galE deficiency

Transcriptomic profiling was employed to delineate the global regulatory changes resulting from galE disruption. RNA‑seq analysis identified 514 significantly up‑regulated and 469 down‑regulated genes in the ΔgalE mutant compared to the wild‑type strain, based on the criteria of |log2FoldChange| >1 and padj < 0.05 (Figure S2(a)).

Among these, several key regulatory and structural genes, including bfmR (Log2FC: 1.23) and baeS (Log2FC: 1.11), were significantly upregulated (padj < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant upregulation of two‑component systems (Figure S2(b), padj < 0.05). In contrast, multiple core metabolic pathways were markedly downregulated, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation; fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism; pyruvate metabolism; glycolysis/gluconeogenesis; arginine and proline metabolism; and carbon metabolism (Figure S2(c), padj < 0.05). Specifically, the arginine metabolism genes (argF, Log2FC: −1.87; astA, Log2FC: −1.77; astB, Log2FC: −2.60; astD, Log2FC: −2.32; astE, Log2FC = −1.85) and the lipid A biosynthesis related gene lpxA (Log2FC: −1.81) were significantly downregulated (padj < 0.05). Furthermore, a marked downregulation was observed in multiple Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) transporter genes, including K90J37_RS01115 (Log2FC: −3.24) and K90J37_RS00200 (Log2FC: −2.14) (Table 1). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed a significant enrichment of downregulated genes in the oxidation-reduction process (GO:0055114, padj < 0.05), while upregulated genes were predominantly associated with nucleic acid metabolic processes and nucleobase-containing compound metabolic process (Table S2). Critically, the ΔgalE mutant exhibited a pronounced upregulation of biofilm formation genes (pgaA, Log2C: 1.66; pgaB, Log2FC: 1.34; pgaC, Log2FC: 1.10), pilus assembly 5 genes (papC, Log2FC: 13.60; papD, Log2FC: 12.79; pilM, Log2FC: 1.64), and efflux pump genes (adeK, Log2FC: 1.90; adeI, Log2FC: 2.34; adeJ, Log2FC: 2.56; adeL, Log2FC: 1.61) (Table 1). Notably, the expression levels of the multidrug efflux transcriptional repressor adeN (Log2FC: −3.70), the cell division protein gene ftsA (Log2FC: −1.05), and the penicillin‑binding protein gene mrcB (Log2FC: −1.09) were significantly downregulated (Table 1). Additionally, the type I-F CRISPR-associated gene cas1f (Log2FC: 1.42) and the heat shock survival gene clpK (Log2FC: 18.70) were overexpressed following galE disruption (Table 1). Interactions between bacteria and antibiotics/phage

Given the rapid emergence of phage resistance and the significantly enhanced biofilm formation capacity of phage‑resistant strains, we subsequently explored the inhibitory effect of phage‑antibiotic combinations on phage-resistance development and bacterial biofilm formation. We selected representatives from several major antibiotic classes to screen for those that exhibit synergistic activity with the phage. The classes included β‑lactams (carbenicillin, ceftazidime, and meropenem), a fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin), a tetracycline (tetracycline), a rifamycin (rifampicin), and the polymyxins (colistin and polymyxin B). The screening aimed to find combinations capable of suppressing phage resistance and inhibiting bacterial biofilm. Broth microdilution susceptibility testing revealed that AB43 was susceptible to ceftazidime, meropenem, tetracycline, levofloxacin, and polymyxin B (Table 2). We first assessed the ability of phage AbYft_4 to restore antibiotic susceptibility in A. baumannii AB43. At a final phage concentration of 106 PFU/mL, the MICs of ceftazidime, carbenicillin, and rifampicin against AB43 were each reduced by 8-fold (Table 2). Additionally, the MICs of meropenem and levofloxacin decreased by 2-fold and 4-fold, respectively. However, the susceptibility of the strain to tetracycline remained largely unaltered upon phage addition.

Finally, to build upon the observed phage- mediated increase in susceptibility to carbenicillin and ceftazidime (Table 2), we selected these two clinically relevant anti‑Gram‑negative antibiotics to investigate whether they could influence the evolution of phage resistance. As phage-sensitive bacteria were progressively lysed, phage-resistant mutants were concomitantly enriched over the course of incubation. Growth kinetics assays revealed that increasing concentrations of carbenicillin progressively altered both bacterial and phage population dynamics. A carbenicillin concentration of 3.2 µg/mL, which alone did not inhibit bacterial growth, significantly curtailed the emergence of phage-resistant mutants (Figure 5(b)). A similar suppressive effect on phage-resistance evolution was observed with ceftazidime at 1 µg/mL (Figure 5(c)). Spot plate counting confirmed that the concomitant application of carbenicillin or ceftazidime with phage markedly reduced the frequency of phage-resistant mutants (Figure 5(d)).

Discussion

In this study, we isolated a novel bacteriophage (AbYft_4) using an A. baumannii strain harboring a complete type I-F CRISPR-Cas system as the host. Genomic alignment confirmed that AbYft_4 represents a new viral strain (Figure 1), thereby expanding the known diversity of Acinetobacter phages and providing a new candidate for phage therapy. The CRISPR-Cas system confers immunity by utilizing spacer sequences that guide the specific recognition and cleavage of foreign nucleic acids [33]. The successful propagation of AbYft_4 in AB43 suggests that the host’s CRISPR-Cas system lacks a spacer sequence specifically targeting this phage. Furthermore, we observed a significant upregulation of the CRISPR-associated gene cas1 in phage-resistant mutants, implying a potential role of the CRISPR-Cas system in the bacterial adaptive response. The regulatory link between the galE deletion and this CRISPR-Cas activation warrants further investigation. Anti-CRISPR proteins, which inhibit CRISPR-Cas function to facilitate phage infection, have been identified in numerous bacteriophages [19]. However, research on Acr proteins in the context of A. baumannii phages remains limited, and the existence of a functional Acr in AbYft_4 requires experimental validation. Previous studies have shown that bacteria harboring the CRISPR-Cas system can acquire new spacers upon phage challenge, enabling cleavage of the phage genome [17,34,35]. However, this phenomenon was not observed in our study. Consistent with previous findings [14], our study confirmed that phage-resistant mutants exhibited attenuated virulence, a phenotype primarily attributable to defects in capsular synthesis (Figure 3). The capsule is a critical virulence determinant in A. baumannii, instrumental in evading host immune phagocytosis and adapting to environmental stress [36]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting capsular synthesis have emerged as promising avenues for antibacterial development [37].

The Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) transporters facilitate the transmembrane movement of diverse substrates, including sugars, ions, carbohydrates, lipids, amino acids, peptides, and nucleosides [38,39]. Given that capsule biosynthesis consumes substantial quantities of various carbohydrates and their precursors [40], our transcriptomic data suggest a global metabolic adaptation that involves the cell likely downregulates the import of these metabolic precursors as an energy-saving strategy when the energetically costly process of capsule synthesis is disrupted (Figure S2). Furthermore, our analysis implicated several key regulatory systems. Specifically, the two-component systems-related factors BfmR and BaeS, which are characterized in A. baumannii for their roles in modulating virulence and antimicrobial resistance , were significantly altered. This finding is consistent with a prior report that demonstrated bfmRS and baeSR mutations affect biofilm formation and virulence by regulating the paa and csu operons [6], underscoring their critical function in the bacterial adaptive response. The regulatory influence of BaeSR extends to the efflux systems AdeIJK and MacAB-TolC [41]. Similarly, the two-component system BfmRS is a key modulator of biofilm formation, primarily through its direct regulation of the csu pilus gene cluster [42]. This is corroborated by the observation that a bfmS knockout mutation significantly impairs biofilm formation in A. baumannii [7]. Beyond pilus‑mediated attachment, the synthesis of the poly‑1,6‑N‑acetylglucosamine exopolysaccharide (a critical biofilm matrix component) is governed by the pgaABCD operon [43]. Our transcriptomic data indicate that the phage-resistant mutation concurrently alters the expression of both the csu and pgaABCD operons, suggesting a coordinated transcriptional reprogramming of biofilm formation pathways. A previous study proposed that enhanced biofilm formation in capsule-deficient strains may be attributed to increased cell surface hydrophobicity [44]. Our findings build upon this model by demonstrating that the robust biofilm phenotype in our phage-resistant, capsule-deficient mutant is likely driven by the concerted upregulation of multiple biofilm-associated genes. This comprehensive transcriptional reprogramming suggests a multi-faceted adaptive strategy, as biofilm formation is a well-established mechanism bacteria employ to survive under hostile conditions [45]. We therefore speculated that the enhanced biofilm formation serves as a compensatory stress response in these phage-resistant mutants, offsetting the vulnerability caused by the loss of the capsular barrier. Biofilm formation serves as a key determinant of persistent bacterial infections [46]. One study in K. pneumoniae has also shown that capsule‑deficient isolates are associated with enhanced biofilm formation, thereby increasing the risk of persistent infection [47]. Our findings also suggest that in A. baumannii, acquisition of phage resistance may contribute to an increased risk of refractory infections, potentially by enhancing biofilm formation. A limitation of this study is the absence of a galE complemented strain. We made multiple unsuccessful attempts to complement the galE mutation due to technical challenges. Notably, issues in genetic complementation have also been encountered for wzc, another capsule synthesis-related gene in A. baumannii [9]. Thus, further studies are required to address this issue.

Combining antibiotics with phages represents a key strategy for enhancing phage therapy efficacy. This approach is particularly relevant given that biofilms function as a major defense mechanism against phage predation. Compounding this challenge, it has been roport that phages themselves can upregulate various bacterial factors that promote biofilm development [48]. Furthermore, sub-inhibitory concentrations of ceftazidime have been reported to enhance biofilm formation in A. baumannii [49]. Therefore, the synergistic combination of phages and antibiotics offers a promising approach to simultaneously suppress biofilm formation and limit the emergence of phage resistance. Although no significant reduction in AB43 biofilm was observed when phages or antibiotics were used alone, a synergistic effect was observed when phage was used in combination with sub-MIC carbenicillin, rifampin, and ceftazidime (Figure 5). This highlights that a phage-antibiotic combination can overcome the biofilm-promoting effects of individual agents on A. baumannii.

The spontaneous resistance frequency of our phage was 3.61 × 10−3. For comparison, another previously characterized Acinetobacter phage exhibited a higher frequency of approximately 1.7 × 10−2 [50]. However, our value remained substantially above the 1.53 × 10−6 frequency reported for some other Acinetobacter phages, indicating that our phage is still prone to rapid resistance development [51]. The combination of sub-MIC ceftazidime and carbenicillin-phage has also shown application potential in inhibiting the development of phage resistance (Figure 5). This reduction can be explained by our results that the presence of the phage significantly restored the susceptibility of A. baumannii AB43 to ceftazidime and carbenicillin (Table 2). We propose that the phage enhances the bactericidal activity of these β-lactam antibiotics, which are commonly used clinically to treat Gram-negative bacterial infections. It should be noted that rifampicin is not a conventional antibiotic for treating Gram‑negative bacterial infections. Consequently, in the combination treatment, the antibiotics effectively eliminate both sensitive bacteria and any phage-resistant mutants that arise, thereby suppressing the emergence of phage resistance. These results also provide a basis for the development of combined antibiotic and phage therapy.

Conclusions

Overall, this study reports the isolation and characterization of a novel bacteriophage from an A. baumannii host carrying an I-F CRISPR-Cas system. We demonstrated that capsule deficiency serves as the primary mechanism of phage resistance, albeit at the cost of attenuated bacterial virulence. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that this capsule deficiency triggers extensive remodeling of global regulatory networks, encompassing biofilm formation and energy metabolism, which reflects a bacterial adaptive evolutionary strategy. Furthermore, the combination of this phage with conventional antibiotics effectively suppressed biofilm development and curtailed the evolution of phage resistance in vitro, highlighting a promising synergistic approach to combat multidrug-resistant infections.
