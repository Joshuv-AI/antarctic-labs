Chagas disease, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, affects an estimated 300,000 individuals in the United States and represents a critical example of how childhood infections determine lifetime cardiovascular risk.1 The disease is typically acquired during childhood, but chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy evolves over several decades after infection in 30% to 40% of infected individuals. This temporal disconnect between pediatric infection and adult cardiomyopathy has profound implications for screening, treatment, and prevention strategies.1,2Take-Home Messages•Pediatric Chagas disease represents a critical window for intervention—early benznidazole treatment can prevent the 30% to 40% lifetime risk of chronic cardiomyopathy by eliminating persistent parasitemia before irreversible myocardial fibrosis develops.•Clinicians in nonendemic regions should maintain high suspicion for Chagas disease in children with unexplained myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, or arrhythmias who have epidemiologic risk factors, as speckle-tracking echocardiography and CMR can detect subclinical disease and guide risk stratification.

The acute phase is typically a nonspecific febrile illness lasting several weeks, with diagnosis commonly missed because 70% of cases remain asymptomatic. Although uncommon (<10%), patients can progress directly from acute Chagas disease to the chronic cardiac form.1,2 More commonly, chronic Chagas heart disease develops after several decades of the indeterminate form.3, 4, 5

The critical mechanistic insight is that early antiparasitic treatment interrupts this progression. A longitudinal study of 234 treated children followed for >6 years demonstrated only 4.7% with electrocardiographic alterations and only 1.7% with probable Chagas-related cardiac changes, supporting the protective effect of early treatment.6 Drivers of progression include persistence of high parasitemia and tissue parasitism, a proinflammatory immunologic profile with sustained oxidative stress, and microvascular disturbances.2,4,6

Comprehensive echocardiographic modalities—particularly speckle-tracking echocardiography and 3-dimensional imaging—enable detection of subclinical myocardial dysfunction that predicts progression from the indeterminate to cardiac form. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) with late gadolinium enhancement identifies myocardial fibrosis associated with arrhythmic risk and adverse outcomes.7,8 These imaging techniques provide a mechanistic window into disease progression, revealing the substrate of persistent inflammation and fibrosis that characterizes the transition from childhood infection to adult cardiomyopathy.7,8

Case Presentations

Case 1: acute myocarditis with complete recovery—interrupting the pathophysiological cascade

A previously healthy girl experienced cardiopulmonary arrest during routine surgery, requiring epinephrine resuscitation. Initial echocardiography revealed mildly depressed left ventricular (LV) systolic function with global hypokinesis but no segmental wall motion abnormalities or pericardial effusion (Video 1). She received milrinone and intravenous immunoglobulin. Karius cell-free DNA testing and peripheral blood smear confirmed T cruzi infection. Benznidazole treatment was initiated immediately, representing the critical intervention to prevent lifetime cardiomyopathy risk.

At 1-month follow-up, she was clinically well with normalized LV systolic function. CMR showed complete resolution of myocardial inflammation without late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 1), confirming that early treatment prevented the persistent inflammation and fibrosis that drive progression to chronic cardiomyopathy.7 This case demonstrates the mechanistic principle that early benznidazole treatment interrupts the pathophysiological cascade—eliminating persistent parasitemia and preventing the chronic inflammatory state that would otherwise evolve into cardiomyopathy decades later.2,6Figure 1Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Short-Axis T2-Weighted Sequence Demonstrating Resolution of Myocardial Edema With No Evidence of Late Gadolinium Enhancement or Fibrosis

Case 2: fulminant myocarditis—possible direct progression to chronic cardiac form

A 9-month-old boy presented with fulminant myocarditis/pericarditis, acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, and biventricular dysfunction (Video 2). Peripheral blood smear confirmed T cruzi infection. Despite intravenous immunoglobulin, corticosteroids, benznidazole, and amiodarone, he developed frequent ventricular tachycardia and junctional rhythm with relative bradycardia.

On hospital day 7, echocardiography revealed severely depressed LV function (ejection fraction: 15%-20%), LV apical thrombus (Figure 2, Video 3), and moderate right ventricular dysfunction. Speckle-tracking demonstrated severely reduced global longitudinal strain with regional heterogeneity, particularly in the apical and inferolateral segments (Figure 3). This case may represent the rare (<10%) direct progression from acute Chagas disease to the chronic cardiac form, bypassing the typical decades-long indeterminate phase. The severity of myocardial inflammation and injury suggests high parasite burden and/or virulent strain—mechanistic factors associated with worse outcomes and accelerated disease progression.2,7 The severely reduced global longitudinal strain and presence of LV apical thrombus at such a young age indicate that the pathophysiological substrate for chronic cardiomyopathy—myocardial fibrosis, regional wall motion abnormalities, and arrhythmogenic foci—has already been established in infancy.2,9

Case 3: chronic cardiomyopathy with fibrosis—the typical adult presentation

A 13-month-old boy with Chagas disease presented with moderately depressed LV systolic function, ventricular ectopy, and history of LV thrombus. He had been diagnosed with Chagas disease several months prior and was treated with benznidazole.

The correlation between late gadolinium enhancement distribution and regional strain abnormalities illustrates a fundamental principle: myocardial fibrosis detected by CMR identifies the anatomic substrate for both mechanical dysfunction (detected by strain imaging) and electrical instability (manifesting as ventricular ectopy). This mechanistic insight explains why patients with extensive fibrosis on magnetic resonance imaging have increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, even when global ejection fraction is preserved.1,2

He was managed with guideline-directed medical therapy including enalapril and metoprolol for heart failure. Anticoagulation with enoxaparin was continued given the history of LV thrombus. He continued benznidazole for Chagas disease treatment. His clinical status improved over time, with resolution of heart failure symptoms. This case demonstrates that even with established myocardial fibrosis—the irreversible consequence of chronic parasitemia and inflammation—appropriate medical management can stabilize clinical status and improve functional capacity.

Discussion

Mechanistic connection between childhood infection and lifetime cardiomyopathy risk

These 3 cases illustrate how persistence of high parasitemia and tissue parasitism, coupled with a proinflammatory immunologic profile and sustained oxidative stress, drive progressive myocardial damage that evolves over decades.3,4 Case 1 demonstrates the critical window for intervention. Early benznidazole treatment eliminated parasitemia and prevented the establishment of chronic inflammation, as evidenced by complete resolution of myocardial inflammation on CMR without late gadolinium enhancement. This case exemplifies the protective effect of early treatment: in a longitudinal study of 234 treated children followed for >6 years, only 4.7% showed electrocardiographic alterations and only 1.7% developed probable Chagas-related cardiac changes, supporting the protective effect of early antiparasitic therapy on the development of cardiologic lesions.5

Case 2 illustrates the rare but devastating direct progression from acute to chronic cardiac form. Although uncommon (<10%), patients can progress directly from acute Chagas disease to the chronic cardiac form, bypassing the typical decades-long indeterminate phase. The severity of myocardial inflammation and injury—manifesting as severely reduced global longitudinal strain, biventricular dysfunction, and LV apical thrombus in a 9-month-old infant—suggests that the pathophysiological substrate for chronic cardiomyopathy was established during the acute phase.3 This accelerated disease trajectory likely reflects high parasite burden, virulent strain, or host genetic factors that determine susceptibility to severe cardiac involvement.3,7

Case 3 represents the typical adult presentation: chronic cardiomyopathy with established myocardial fibrosis decades after childhood infection. The late gadolinium enhancement in the inferior wall and apex reflects the coalescence of focal areas of myocardial inflammation, necrosis, and fibrosis—the pathologic hallmark of chronic Chagas heart disease that develops progressively over years to decades after initial infection. The correlation between fibrosis distribution on magnetic resonance imaging and regional strain abnormalities demonstrates how advanced imaging reveals the mechanistic substrate for both mechanical dysfunction and electrical instability.1,6,8,9

Comprehensive imaging reveals the pathophysiological substrate

Speckle-tracking echocardiography and CMR provide mechanistic insights by detecting the structural and functional consequences of persistent parasitemia and chronic inflammation. Global longitudinal strain detects subclinical myocardial dysfunction before changes in ejection fraction, particularly in patients with the indeterminate form when abnormalities are more subtle. Regional strain is of particular interest in Chagas disease given the frequent segmental myocardial involvement in the apical, inferior, and inferolateral walls.1,3,6

CMR with late gadolinium enhancement identifies myocardial fibrosis—the irreversible consequence of chronic inflammation and myocytolysis—that defines the substrate for ventricular arrhythmias and progressive dysfunction. Malignant ventricular arrhythmias in Chagas disease are almost invariably related to regional scarring and fibrosis, resulting in regional wall motion abnormalities. The presence and extent of myocardial fibrosis correlate with NYHA functional class and likelihood of ventricular arrhythmias, particularly when a transmural pattern extends beyond ≥2 contiguous segments.1,10

Importantly, myocardial fibrosis detected by CMR is an independent predictor of cardiovascular death and sustained ventricular tachycardia, providing incremental prognostic value beyond LV ejection fraction. In a prospective study of chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy, the presence of any scar was independently associated with the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death and sustained ventricular tachycardia in multivariable analysis. This finding demonstrates that myocardial fibrosis is a fundamental lesion in Chagas cardiomyopathy and likely provides the re-entry pathway mostly responsible for sudden cardiac death and sustained ventricular arrhythmias.2

Clinical implications

These cases underscore critical gaps in awareness of Chagas disease among health care providers in nonendemic regions. Among Latin America–born patients with newly diagnosed nonischemic cardiomyopathy in the United States, 13% to 19% have Chagas disease as the underlying etiology. The prognosis of Chagas cardiomyopathy is poorer than other forms of dilated cardiomyopathy of similar severity, with 50% mortality within 4 years in adults with heart failure. Health care providers should maintain high suspicion for Chagas disease in children with unexplained myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, or cardiac arrest, particularly those with epidemiologic risk factors such as birth in or travel to endemic regions.3,8

Based on American Heart Association and American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, comprehensive cardiac imaging should include the following: 1) baseline electrocardiogram and 2-dimensional echocardiography with Doppler for all seropositive children; 2) speckle-tracking for global longitudinal strain measurement and 3-dimensional imaging when available; 3) CMR for tissue characterization and risk stratification, especially in patients with abnormal electrocardiographic or echocardiographic findings; and 4) serial monitoring guided by clinical status.1,3

Conclusions

These 3 pediatric cases demonstrate the mechanistic connection between childhood Chagas disease and lifetime cardiomyopathy risk, illustrating how persistence of high parasitemia and tissue parasitism, coupled with chronic inflammation, drive progressive myocardial damage that evolves over decades.3,4 Speckle-tracking and 3-dimensional imaging combined with CMR are essential for comprehensive diagnosis, risk stratification, and management of pediatric Chagas cardiomyopathy.

Case 1 demonstrates that early benznidazole treatment interrupts the pathophysiological cascade, eliminating persistent parasitemia and preventing the chronic inflammatory state that would otherwise evolve into cardiomyopathy decades later. The absence of late gadolinium enhancement on CMR confirms that early treatment prevented the irreversible myocardial fibrosis that defines chronic Chagas heart disease.

Case 2 illustrates the rare but devastating direct progression from acute to chronic cardiac form, with severely reduced global longitudinal strain and LV apical thrombus in a 9-month-old infant indicating that the pathophysiological substrate for chronic cardiomyopathy was established during the acute phase. This accelerated disease trajectory likely reflects high parasite burden, virulent strain, or host genetic factors that determine susceptibility to severe cardiac involvement.1,6

Case 3 represents the typical adult presentation: chronic cardiomyopathy with established myocardial fibrosis decades after childhood infection. The late gadolinium enhancement in the inferior wall and apex reflects the coalescence of focal areas of myocardial inflammation, necrosis, and fibrosis—the pathologic hallmark of chronic Chagas heart disease. The correlation between fibrosis distribution on magnetic resonance imaging and regional strain abnormalities demonstrates how advanced imaging reveals the mechanistic substrate for both mechanical dysfunction and electrical instability.2,3

These cases underscore the importance of clinical awareness in nonendemic settings, where 13% to 19% of Latin America–born patients with newly diagnosed nonischemic cardiomyopathy have Chagas disease as the underlying etiology, but the disease burden remains underrecognized. The prognosis of Chagas cardiomyopathy is poorer than other forms of dilated cardiomyopathy of similar severity, emphasizing the critical importance of early recognition and treatment.1,4

The integration of multimodality imaging—conventional echocardiography, speckle-tracking strain analysis, and CMR—provides comprehensive assessment enabling early detection, accurate risk stratification, and informed clinical decision-making in pediatric Chagas disease, ultimately preventing the progression from childhood infection to adult cardiomyopathy. Speckle-tracking echocardiography detects subclinical myocardial dysfunction before changes in ejection fraction, particularly in patients with the indeterminate form when abnormalities are more subtle. CMR with late gadolinium enhancement identifies myocardial fibrosis that correlates with arrhythmic risk and adverse outcomes, with the presence of any scar independently predicting cardiovascular death and sustained ventricular tachycardia.2,3,5
