Increased rates of surgical resection of early-stage small cell lung cancer in a single-institution cohort
Authors: Sora Ely, Justin M Bader, Brooks Van Udelsman, Emily June Zolfaghari, Anne C Chiang, William de Santis, Shawn Reginauld, Andrew P Dhanasopon, Robert Homer, Guoping Cai, Gavitt Alida Woodard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing chest imaging utilization, including for screening, identifies more non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) at earlier stages. We suspected that a parallel increase in early detection and resection of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) was also occurring. We therefore reviewed our institutional experience to explore trends in surgically resected SCLC frequency and outcomes.MethodsA single-institution, retrospective review was performed of all SCLC cases resected between 2000–2022. We evaluated demographics, oncologic characteristics, overall survival (OS), and disease-free survival (DFS).ResultsAmong 30 patients with resected SCLC, the majority underwent lobectomy (73%) and only 37% were known to have SCLC preoperatively. The frequency of resections increased over time with almost half (47%) occurring in the last four study years (2019–2022). This increase in SCLC resections coincided with increasing lung cancer screening (LCS) volume, with no change in thoracic surgical case volume. The 2019–2022 cohort was predominantly clinical stage I (86%) compared to 56% pre-2019 (I vs. II–IV, P=0.08), and also trended toward more screen-detected (43% vs. 13%, P=0.08) patients. Subgroup analysis showed encouraging OS outcomes following surgical resection for clinical and pathological stage I patients. There was a trend toward better OS among screen-detected patients (73% vs. 34%, log-rank P=0.07) that was significant in DFS (75% vs. 26%, P=0.02).ConclusionsThe number of surgically resected SCLC patients at our institution has increased in recent years, as has the proportion of screen-detected and clinical stage I cases, alongside rising LCS volume. This may be an early signal of a broader trend toward increasing diagnosis of limited stage SCLC eligible for resection.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), surgical resection, limited/early stage, long-term survival, lung cancer screening (LCS)

Highlight box

Key findings

• The frequency of surgically resected small cell lung cancer (SCLC) at a major academic center increased from fewer than 1 to 3.5 cases per year, in parallel to rising institutional lung cancer screening (LCS) volume.

• The 2019–2022 cohort showed a trend toward a higher proportion of clinical stage I and screen-detected cases compared with pre-2019 cases.

• Screen-detected patients had significantly better disease-free survival, and pathologic stage I patients achieved 82% 5-year overall survival.

What is known and what is new?

• National database analyses have identified increasing rates of surgical resection of SCLC and a modest stage shift toward earlier-stage disease coinciding with the adoption of LCS.

• This single-institution 20-year series documents an increase in resection frequency and a trend toward earlier clinical stage and screen-detected cases in recent years. Most patients (63%) carried a presumptive non-small cell lung cancer diagnosis at the time of surgery, underscoring a persistent diagnostic challenge even in the era of expanded early detection.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians across specialties should anticipate the possibility of increasing early-stage, “surprise” SCLC discovered on final pathology in screen-detected or incidentally identified pulmonary nodules.

• Validation in larger, population-level datasets is needed to confirm these trends and inform evidence-based management strategies for early-stage SCLC in the era of expanding early detection.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is known for its aggressive biology and poor survival (1). Most patients are diagnosed with advanced disease and surgical resection is reserved for highly selected cases of local disease (1). Increased use of chest computed tomography (CT) scans has resulted in detection of lung cancer at earlier stages (2), and within screening programs, a majority of patients may be diagnosed at stage I (3). These advances in early detection have focused on non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but there may be a concomitant benefit in SCLC. Within the National Cancer Database (NCDB), the percentage of early-stage SCLC patients undergoing surgical resection has been rising (4), although data about incident stage is more limited. Since the initial recommendation by the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) for lung cancer screening (LCS) in December 2013, and with continually increasing CT utilization, we suspected that SCLC would also be detected at earlier stages and more frequently resected over time, sometimes as presumptively diagnosed NSCLC. To investigate this, we conducted a detailed review of all surgically resected SCLC cases at our institution. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0460/rc) (5).

Methods

A retrospective review was performed of all SCLC surgical resections between 2000–2022 at a single, quaternary care, academic institution. Patients were identified through CoPath (Clinisys, Tucson, AZ, USA), a comprehensive pathology database with complete institutional data. Additional manual chart review was performed to collect demographic, oncologic, operative, treatment, and outcomes data. Screen-detected was defined as patients diagnosed through LCS with an LDCT. Date of last review for recurrence and mortality was May 30, 2025. All pathology cases were manually reviewed by an expert thoracic pathologist to confirm diagnostic accuracy and to compare with preoperative biopsy slides where available. Tumors were staged according to criteria outlined by American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition (6).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Yale School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (No. 2000032034), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed with independent t-test, chi-square test, or Wilcoxon rank sum test for univariate outcomes (differences in historical cohort characteristics) and by Kaplan-Meier method for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Computations were carried out using R software, version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 30 patients underwent surgical resection of SCLC between 2000–2022. The study population was notably 90% white, and 100% were current or former smokers (Table 1). Complete follow-up, defined as documented clinical contact within 1 year of last chart review or a recorded death date, was available for 28 patients (93%). Median follow-up available was 34 months. Consistent with the aggressive biology of SCLC, follow-up was primarily limited by death: among patients who died, median follow-up was 24 months, compared to 48 months among still-living patients. The frequency of SCLC surgical resection was low overall (30 patients over 23 years, or 1.3 cases/year). Almost half of all resections during the 23-year period occurred within the last 4 years of the study period (2019–2022, n=14, 3.5 cases/year). The increased frequency of resected SCLC cases coincided with increasing low-dose CT (LDCT) volume in our LCS program, with no change in thoracic surgery lung cancer and total case volumes during the same timeframe (Figure 1).

Based on this observed prominent increase, we compared the characteristics and outcomes of SCLC cases resected 2000–2018 (“pre-2019”, n=16) to those resected 2019–2022 (n=14). This division into historical cohorts was based purely on the visually estimated turning point, as the sample size precluded formal trend analyses for a statistically-determined cutoff. When stratified into these historical cohorts, there were no differences with respect to age, sex, or race/ethnicity (Table 1). Patients in the 2019–2022 cohort were mostly current smokers at time of diagnosis (79%), whereas almost all in the pre-2019 cohort had quit before diagnosis (94%, P<0.001). Data was available for preoperative invasive workup in all patients (100%), for preoperative imaging workup in all but 1 patient (97%), for neoadjuvant treatment in all but 2 patients (93%), and for adjuvant treatment in all but 3 patients (90%); all patients missing data were in the pre-2019 group. There were no differences in preoperative imaging workup, including utilization of positron emission tomography (PET). Invasive mediastinal staging, which was largely via endobronchial ultrasound, was less common in the 2019–2022 cohort (P=0.02), likely reflecting notable stage differences. The 2019–2022 cohort was predominantly clinical stage I (86%) compared to only about half (56%) pre-2019 (clinical stage I vs. II–IV, P=0.08). A similar trend toward more screen-detected cases was observed (43% vs. 13%, P=0.08). All screen-detected cases (n=8) were clinical stage I, of which 38% (n=3) were upstaged at surgery to pathologic stage II. Over half, 57%, of the 2019–2022 cohort were also pathologic stage I, compared to only 19% pre-2019 (pathologic stage I vs. II–IV, P=0.03). Overall follow-up duration was similar between the cohorts, 34 and 36 months in the pre-2019 and 2019–2022 groups respectively (P=0.38). However, the immaturity of survival data in the 2019–2022 cohort was evident in the available follow-up among still-living patients, 104 and 46 months in pre-2019 and 2019–2022 (P=0.04). Among 2019–2022 patients, 5-year follow-up had accrued for only 2 patients, plus 5 had died within the follow-up period; 5-year follow-up or death data was available in all 16 pre-2019 patients.

A preoperative diagnostic biopsy was performed in a majority (57%, n=17) of cases, but only 65% (n=11) of patients who underwent biopsy were diagnosed definitively with SCLC prior to surgery. Of those who were not, one biopsy was non-diagnostic, one (performed at an outside hospital) did not sample the primary tumor or the lymph node station later found to be positive, one was false negative, and the remaining biopsies were classified as either carcinoma or poorly differentiated NSCLC. Although 88% (15/17) of patients who underwent biopsy were correctly diagnosed with malignancy, diagnostic accuracy specific to SCLC was low and did not change meaningfully over time (30% to 43%, P=0.61). The majority of biopsies were obtained by Interventional Radiology (IR; 76%, n=13) and the rest by Interventional Pulmonology (IP). Among IR biopsies, 77% (n=10) were diagnostic of SCLC; among IP, 25% (n=1). Overall less biopsies were performed in the 2019–2022 era, 50% (n=7) compared to 63% (n=10), but this was not statistically significant. All patients without a biopsy carried a presumptive diagnosis of NSCLC at the time of surgery. In total, only 37% (n=11) of the entire surgical cohort were known to have SCLC prior to resection.

There were no differences in resection extent between cohorts, with the majority of patients undergoing lobectomy (Table 2). The median total nodal stations examined was 4, and again this did not differ between groups. Most patients did not receive neoadjuvant treatment, with only 2 patients from the pre-2019 era undergoing preoperative chemoradiotherapy and none from 2019–2022. Most patients (93%) received chemotherapy as their first-line adjuvant systemic treatment, including one who received chemoimmunotherapy. The only significant difference in adjuvant therapy was the lower use of prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) in the 2019–2022 group.

For Kaplan-Meier curves, 5-year outcomes are shown to maintain consistency in the data representation across subgroup comparisons; median OS and DFS for the whole cohort and the same subgroups are presented in Table 3. Given the small sample size, all subgroup analyses were exploratory and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Among clinical stage I and pathologic stage I patients (Figure 2A,2B), 5-year OS was 58% and 82%, respectively (log-rank P=0.007 and 0.02). A similar pattern was observed in DFS but did not reach statistical significance (log-rank P=0.08 and 0.054). In additional exploratory subgroup analyses, pre-2019 5-year OS was 31% compared to 64% for 2019–2022, with similar patterns in 5-year DFS (25% compared to 57%). Median OS and DFS in the pre-2019 cohort were 34 months and 22 months, and were not reached in the 2019–2022 cohort. However, these differences in OS and DFS by historical cohort were not significant (Figure 3A). Screen-detected cases (Figure 3B) trended toward better OS (log-rank P=0.07) and had significantly better DFS (log-rank P=0.02). Median OS and DFS for screen-detected patients was 221 months, compared to only 32 and 14 months respectively in those who were not. When examining patient and tumor factors, only sex was associated with survival. Female patients (Figure 4A) trended toward better OS and had significantly better DFS (log-rank P=0.07 and 0.03). OS was slightly better for patients in whom the SCLC diagnosis was known preoperatively versus discovered on surgical pathology (50% vs. 37%; Figure 4B) and among patients with pure small cell versus mixed histology (58% vs. 13%; Figure 4C), but these differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this single-institution, retrospective review of all surgically resected SCLC cases from over two decades, we identified an increase in the frequency of SCLC cases undergoing surgical resection in recent years (2019–2022). Cases from this period were more likely to be early-stage at the time of diagnosis (86% clinical stage I and 14% clinical stage II) and trended toward an increased proportion of screen-detected cases. This shift coincided with increasing institutional LCS volume. We also observed numerically superior survival among this recent cohort, particularly among screen-detected and stage I cases; however, with small numbers and immature survival data, these differences were not statistically significant.

These local findings echo a national trend of progressively increasing utilization of surgery for T1–2N0 SCLC from 2004–2007 to 2008–2010 and to 2011–2013 in the NCDB, an overall rise from 6% to 24% (4). Importantly, a more recent analysis of the NCDB found not only a continued increase in surgical resection from 2010 to 2017 but also a small but statistically significant stage shift (7). Their analysis detected a general shift toward lower stages but the absolute increase in stage I was only 0.9% (4.3% to 5.2%). These findings were supported and extended by a 2010–2020 NCDB analysis that identified an inflection point at 2014 (the first year of USPSTF-recommended LCS) in annual rates of clinical stage I diagnosis among lung cancer screening-age-eligible (55–80 years old) SCLC patients, whereas clinical stage I rates were flat among age-ineligible (45–54 years old) patients (8).

Although LCS has a proven mortality benefit for NSCLC, it has historically not been expected to impact SCLC due to its rapidly progressive behavior and tendency to present as interval lung cancer. In the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST), 80% of the screen-detected or interval SCLC presented as stage III–IV (9). However, given that many of our cases were presumptively diagnosed as early-stage NSCLC, NSCLC early detection programs may be contributing to increased resection of some proportion of early-stage SCLC. This possibility is compatible with the higher proportion of screen-detected and earlier-stage cases observed in recent years within our population. Selection bias, with resection reserved only for early-stage patients, likely explains the difference in screen-detected SCLC stage in NLST as compared to our exclusively surgical cohort. Nevertheless, 3-year lung cancer-specific survival among early-stage SCLC patients in NLST was 83%, similar to 3-year OS among our screen-detected patients (who were all clinical stage I) of 88% (9). So, while LCS will not catch most SCLC cases at early-stage, it may identify some cases, who go on to experience excellent survival.

Additionally, early detection is no longer limited to LCS, for which uptake has been notoriously poor. The steadily increasing utilization of chest CT, agnostic of screening, has been shown at the population-level to be associated with early stage shift in lung cancer (2). In conjunction, the proliferation of incidental lung nodule programs as a complement to screening have the potential to capture a much larger population, including those who may be eligible for LCS but are not participating. These lung nodule programs can outpace LCS program early-stage lung cancer diagnosis due to the sheer volume of incidental nodules identified, and have even been shown to be associated with early-stage proportions similar to that in LCS and survival superior to cases diagnosed outside of either program (10). State-level US data from 2010–2019 show a rising proportion of lung cancers diagnosed at localized stage, suggesting that early detection efforts beyond LCS may be contributing to a national stage shift at the population level (11). It is possible that these non-screening early detection efforts could be further contributing to incidental early detection of SCLC.

Strengths of this study include near-complete case ascertainment of all resected SCLC over more than 20 years at a major academic center, with granular clinical data and correlation with institutional LDCT activity. Limitations include the retrospective design and immature survival data in the more recent cohort. Additionally, the single institution design limits generalizability. The small sample size was not sufficient to perform well-powered subgroup, adjusted, temporal trend, or correlative (with LDCT volume) analyses. However, it is important to note that SCLC, particularly at the resectable stage, is a very rare entity. Our 30 cases from a single institution are not insignificant given that two recent national database studies from the same time period reported <200 cases. A study of resected SCLC in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database 2000–2020 revealed only 185 eligible cases (12), and a study in the Canadian Small Cell Lung Cancer Database (CASCADE) 2001–2022 only 108 surgical cases (13). We therefore believe these findings are still valuable, particularly when considered in the broader context.

Our observed population changes and the favorable outcomes observed among screen-detected and stage I patients must be cautiously interpreted in the context of potential biases intrinsic to retrospective study, small samples, and early detection. Selection bias is an inherent feature of any surgically resected cohort, as patients were by definition deemed fit for surgery with resectable disease. The resultant overrepresentation of earlier-stage and possibly less aggressive disease is evident in the discordance of our outcomes with that of unselected SCLC populations such as in the NLST (9). Additionally, survival analyses in screen-detected population are susceptible to both length and lead-time bias. Lead-time bias artificially extends the survival time when earlier diagnosis advances the initial timepoint without actually altering the disease course or timing of mortality. However, SCLC is so aggressive that the preclinical detectable phase is short (on the order of months), and one methodologic study showed that lead-time bias plays a limited role in SCLC. Yang et al. found that the age difference between early- and late-stage at diagnosis was minimal and that the loss-of-life expectancy saved and raw life expectancy gained were nearly identical (1.5 versus 1.7 years) in SCLC, indicating that stage-shift-based corrections for lead-time bias are less applicable in SCLC than NSCLC (14). Length bias is essentially another form of selection bias due to the predilection of screening programs to detect tumors with longer preclinical phases and therefore more favorable behavior. This is likely similarly constrained by the uniformly aggressive biology of SCLC. Selection bias is unavoidable and inextricable in a retrospective resection-only cohort and is the most important bias impacting the interpretation of these results. On the other hand, we are not arguing that all early-stage SCLC patients should be resected, and so perhaps some element of selection bias is reflective of what can be expected with real-world outcomes of incidentally detected, early-stage SCLC. These considerations reinforce that the observed patterns and outcomes, particularly in the screen-detected subgroup, should be treated as hypothesis-generating. It was not feasible in this study to examine the denominator of all SCLC including un-resected cases, but this would be an important next step, ideally in a large, national cancer database.

Conclusions

In this retrospective, single-institution surgical SCLC population, we observed an increase in the frequency of SCLC resections performed in recent years, coinciding with rising LDCT volume. This shift was associated with a trend toward more screen-detected and more clinical stage I among more recent (2019–2022) SCLC cases. Taken together with promising national database trends in SCLC stage and resection rates, we believe our findings are an early signal that early-stage SCLC resection may become more common with the continued growth of early detection programs. Validation in larger and particularly population-level samples will be needed.
