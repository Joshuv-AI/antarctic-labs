Proteinuria selectivity index as a predictor of remission and rituximab efficacy in primary membranous nephropathy: a single-center retrospective analysis
Authors: Hui Li, Ya Zhang, Xiaomin Ding, Chong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe protein selectivity index (PSI) has been proposed as a prognostic marker in nephrotic syndrome, but its prognostic value in primary membranous nephropathy (PMN) has not been well established in the modern era of advanced diagnostics and therapeutics. This study assessed the predictive value of PSI for proteinuria remission and response to rituximab therapy.MethodsA total of 73 patients with PMN treated between September 2021 and September 2024 were included in this single-center retrospective study, all with at least six months of follow-up. PSI was calculated from serum and urinary immunoglobulin G and transferrin.ResultsThe optimal PSI cutoff for predicting complete remission was 0.303. Low-PSI (<0.303) patients had higher complete remission rates than high-PSI (≥0.303) patients (59.65% vs. 12.50%, p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed in eGFR changes or CKD progression. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significantly higher cumulative incidence of complete remission in the low-PSI group (p = 0.016). Cox regression confirmed that patients with low PSI were more likely to achieve complete remission (HR = 4.88, 95% CI: 1.17–20.36, p = 0.030). In the rituximab subgroup, a cutoff of 0.313 identified better responders (51.35% vs. 0%, p = 0.003). The albumin-based alternative index correlated strongly with traditional PSI (r = 0.839, p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn conclusion, PSI predicts proteinuria remission and treatment response in PMN. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate its clinical utility.Keywords: Protein Selectivity Index, primary membranous nephropathy, proteinuria, rituximab

Primary membranous nephropathy (PMN) is a kidney-specific autoimmune disease caused by autoantibodies targeting endogenous antigens [1]. It is characterized by subepithelial immune complex deposition, leading to thickening of the glomerular basement membrane [2]. The typical clinical manifestation is nephrotic-range proteinuria, but its course is highly heterogeneous. Therapeutic responses vary widely among patients, with some achieving complete remission while others relapse or progress to chronic kidney failure. Therefore, the identification of early biomarkers that predict therapeutic response and disease prognosis is critical for the implementation of personalized therapeutic strategies.

Recent clinical guidelines recommend incorporating the protein selectivity index (PSI) into risk stratification for patients with PMN. A PSI >0.2 may be considered an adjunctive risk marker in the presence of persistent nephrotic-range proteinuria (>3.5 g/day) and an inadequate response to conservative therapy [3]. PSI is defined as the ratio of the clearance of high-molecular-weight proteins, such as immunoglobulin G (IgG), to low-molecular-weight proteins, such as, transferrin, serving as a marker of alterations in glomerular macromolecular permeability [4]. Impairment of the glomerular filtration barrier diminishes its selectivity, resulting in increased macromolecular leakage, elevated PSI, and the emergence of nonselective proteinuria. A PSI below 0.1 is generally considered indicative of highly selective proteinuria, characterized by the free passage of small proteins and minimal leakage of large proteins, suggesting mild glomerular structural damage [4]. PSI values of 0.1–0.2 correspond to moderately selective proteinuria, while values exceeding 0.2 denote nonselective proteinuria, commonly accompanied by significant glomerular injury [4].

The PSI was initially proposed to predict glucocorticoid responsiveness in adults with nephrotic syndrome [5]. However, follow-up studies evaluating its clinical utility have been relatively scarce, possibly because simpler and more readily available biomarkers, such as urinary protein quantification and serum creatinine. PSI is derived from protein clearance rather than total urine volume, thereby minimizing errors resulting from incomplete urine collection. Moreover, in terms of evaluating glomerular permeability, PSI has its advantages compared to renal biopsy, as renal biopsy assesses only a limited number of glomeruli. Previous studies have shown that patients with lower PSI at the time of biopsy are more likely to achieve proteinuria remission and have higher renal survival rates without doubling of serum creatinine [6]. In addition, small-sample studies have reported an association between highly selective proteinuria and remission in patients with membranous nephropathy [7]. With the evolution of treatment strategies, B-cell-targeted therapy such as rituximab has become a first-line option for PMN. Nevertheless, reliable biomarkers to predict therapeutic response remain lacking [8,9]. Allinovi reported that PSI may help identify patients who are more likely to benefit from rituximab in adults with minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [10].

This study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of PSI for proteinuria remission in PMN, determine its optimal cutoff, and assess its ability to predict response to rituximab therapy, thereby providing a basis for individualized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-center, retrospective cohort study included patients aged over 18 years who were diagnosed with PMN between September 2021 and September 2024 at the Department of Nephrology, Xin Hua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. PMN diagnosis was established according to one of the following criteria: (1) renal biopsy confirmation, or (2) clinical diagnosis with compatible symptoms and serum anti-phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antibodies over 14 RU/mL, excluding secondary causes. All enrolled patients had active disease, defined as a 24-h urinary protein excretion exceeding 1 g [11]. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absence of baseline PSI data, and (2) follow-up duration of less than six months at our center. During the follow-up, patients underwent blood and urine analyses at intervals of one to three months. Follow-up was censored upon initiation of kidney replacement therapy, death, referral to another institution, or the study cutoff date (July 31, 2025), whichever occurred first. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xin Hua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. XHEC-C-2023-086-1). Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All identifiable patient data were anonymized before analysis.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using PASS version 15 software to provide a reference for study scale. Owing to the lack of contemporary data in PMN, a prior study addressing a similar research question was used as a reference [7]. Based on this study, assuming a remission rate of 53% in the low-PSI group, an odds ratio (OR) of 4.5, a two-sided α of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the estimated minimum sample size was approximately 46 patients. This estimation was intended as an exploratory, reference-based assessment rather than a definitive a priori power calculation.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. These included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and malignancy), and treatment regimens. Laboratory assessments included both serum and urinary parameters. Serum parameters included albumin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, transferrin, IgG, complement 3 (C3) and C4, uric acid, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), hemoglobin, leukocyte count, and platelet count. Urinary parameters included total protein, albumin, creatinine, β2-microglobulin, transferrin, and IgG. The PSI was calculated using the formula PSI = (urinary IgG × serum transferrin)/(serum IgG × urinary transferrin), as previously described by Cameron and Blandford. As a surrogate marker for validation, the ratio of the urinary-to-serum IgG ratio to the urinary-to-serum albumin ratio was calculated [12]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was derived using the CKD-EPI equation. Histopathological evaluation was performed on renal biopsy specimens. Two renal pathologists independently assessed the samples using standard histologic and immunofluorescence techniques, as described in previous publications [13,14]. Ten consecutive cortical microscopic fields from each biopsy specimen were examined. Tubular atrophy was scored as 0 when absent or involving less than 5% of the cortical area, 1 when involving less than 25%, 2 when involving 25%-50%, and 3 when involving more than 50%. Similarly, interstitial fibrosis and/or inflammatory infiltration were graded according to the extent of involvement as follows: 0 (<5%), 1 (<25%), 2 (25%–50%), and 3 (>50%). The scores of tubular and interstitial damage were summed to generate a composite tubulointerstitial damage (TID) score. The percentage of glomerulosclerosis was obtained by dividing the number of sclerosed glomeruli by the total number of glomeruli and multiplying by 100. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was overall remission, defined as achievement of either complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR). CR was defined as disappearance of clinical symptoms, 24-h urinary protein <0.3 g, serum albumin >35 g/L, and normal serum creatinine. PR was defined as disappearance of symptoms, 24-h urinary protein <3.5 g with ≥50% reduction from baseline, serum albumin >30 g/L, and <20% increase in serum creatinine from baseline. All other patients were classified as no-responders. Secondary endpoints included time to overall remission, absolute and relative changes in eGFR, and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), defined as eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or initiation of dialysis.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0) and R (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Group comparisons used Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated and the optimal cutoff determined by the Youden index. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and intergroup differences were compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, a PSI value >0.20 may support the initiation of immunosuppressive treatment, including rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors combined with rituximab, or cyclophosphamide plus corticosteroids. Based on this threshold, patients in the rituximab-treated subgroup were classified into the selective proteinuria group (PSI ≤ 0.20) and the nonselective proteinuria group (PSI > 0.20). The correlation between PSI and its albumin-based surrogate index was assessed by the Pearson correlation coefficient, and agreement was further evaluated with Bland–Altman plots and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between September 2021 and September 2024, 196 hospitalized patients with PMN were screened at our center. Of these, 80 patients had complete baseline data, including PSI. After excluding seven patients with less than six months of follow-up, 73 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Based on the reference-based sample size estimation, the final sample size exceeded the estimated study scale proposed at the design stage. Baseline characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 66 years, and 78.08% were male. Hypertension and diabetes were present in 72.60% and 13.70% of patients, respectively. PLA2R antibody-associated PMN accounted for 86.30% of patients, with a median baseline serum PLA2R antibody level of 81.70 (23.86–207.52) RU/mL. The median baseline serum creatinine was 76.00 (62.50–107.50) μmol/L, and the median 24-h urinary protein excretion was 5224.75 (3497.70–7539.39) mg. The cohort comprised newly diagnosed patients (75.3%) and patients hospitalized for relapse or persistent disease. The median disease duration was 5.93 (1.30–16.62) months. Immunosuppressive therapy was administered in 90.41% of patients, including cyclophosphamide, rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors, and corticosteroids. The median follow-up duration was 16.23 (12.87–25.80) months. During follow-up, 58 patients (79.45%) achieved overall remission, 35 (47.95%) achieved complete remission, and 10 (17.24%) relapsed after initial remission. At the end of follow-up, 33 patients (45.21%) maintained complete proteinuria remission, whereas two patients (2.74%) experienced a doubling of serum creatinine from baseline.

PSI cutoff

The predictive ability of PSI for outcomes in PMN was assessed using ROC curve analysis (Figure 2(A and B)). Lower PSI values were associated with higher remission rates. For predicting complete remission, the AUC was 0.670 (95% CI: 0.546–0.795), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.303 based on the Youden index (sensitivity 0.972, specificity 0.378). For predicting overall remission, the AUC was 0.622 (95% CI: 0.453–0.790), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.202 (sensitivity 0.733, specificity 0.517). These findings suggest that PSI showed better predictive performance for complete remission than for overall remission.

Time-dependent ROC curves were further plotted to assess the predictive value of PSI over follow-up (Figure 2(C)). The AUCs for predicting complete remission at 9, 12, 18, 24, and 30 months were 0.565 (95% CI: 0.372–0.749), 0.663 (95% CI: 0.503–0.787), 0.717 (95% CI: 0.559–0.856), 0.684 (95% CI: 0.482–0.865), and 0.750 (95% CI: 0.517–0.929), respectively (Figure 2(D)). Overall, PSI demonstrated higher predictive ability for complete remission during the 12–30 months follow-up period.

Predictive value of PSI for proteinuria remission

Patients were stratified into low-PSI (PSI ≤ 0.303, n = 57) and high-PSI (PSI > 0.303, n = 16) groups according to the optimal cutoff value for predicting complete remission. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The high-PSI group had significantly higher serum creatinine [103.50 (78.25–20.75) μmol/L vs. 72.00 (61.00–99.50) μmol/L, p = 0.006] and lower eGFR [61.45 (45.12–89.97) vs. 84.18 (58.81–98.40) mL/min/1.73 m2, p = 0.025]. Hemoglobin levels were lower in the high-PSI group but were not statistically significant (p = 0.073). No significant differences were observed in other laboratory parameters, immunosuppressive regimens, or renal pathological features (all p > 0.05). As shown in Table 2, median follow-up was 17.73 months for the low-PSI group and 14.53 months for the high-PSI group (p = 0.196). At the end of follow-up, 34 patients (59.65%) in the low-PSI group achieved complete remission, compared to two patients (12.50%) in the high-PSI group (p = 0.001). Change in eGFR from baseline to last follow-up did not differ significantly (p = 0.238) and the proportion of patients progressing to CKD stages 3–5 was similar (28.07% vs. 31.25%, p = 0.766). Only one patient in the low-PSI group required dialysis therapy. Relapse after remission occurred in nine patients in the low-PSI group and one in the high-PSI group (p = 0.675). No deaths occurred in either group.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significantly higher cumulative incidence of complete remission in the low-PSI (log-rank p = 0.016; Figure 3). Cox proportional hazards analysis further indicated low-PSI patients had a significantly higher likelihood of achieving complete proteinuria remission (HR = 4.88, 95% CI: 1.17–20.36, p = 0.030). In univariate logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Table 1), PSI was significantly inversely associated with complete remission (OR = 0.001, 95% CI: 0.00–0.19, p = 0.012), and patients in the low-PSI group were more likely to achieve complete remission (OR = 10.35, 95% CI: 2.15–49.90, p = 0.004).

PSI and tubulointerstitial lesions

A semiquantitative evaluation of histologic lesions was conducted in 47 patients (64.38%) who underwent both renal biopsy and PSI assessment, including 36 in the low-PSI group and 11 in the high-PSI group (Table 1). In the high-PSI group, 36.36% of patients had interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy (IFTA) ≥25%, compared with only 8.33% in the low-PSI group, showing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.042). The median TID scores were 0.00 (0.00–2.00) in the low-PSI group and 2.00 (1.00–4.00) in the high-PSI group, with a significant difference (p = 0.019). No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of global glomerulosclerosis (<10% vs. ≥10%) or in the presence of arteriolar hyalinosis between the two groups (p = 0.091 and p = 0.428, respectively). However, the proportion of global glomerulosclerosis differed significantly between the two groups [4.55 (0.00–10.97)% vs. 10.00 (5.88–15.38)%, p = 0.023].

Predictive value of PSI for rituximab response

A total of 47 patients with PMN who received rituximab were included in this subgroup analysis, and their baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 3. Among them, 20 patients (42.6%) had selective proteinuria (PSI ≤ 0.20) and 27 had nonselective proteinuria (PSI > 0.20). At the end of follow-up, complete remission was achieved in 11 patients (55.0%) in the selective group and 8 patients (29.63%) in the nonselective group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.080).

The predictive ability of PSI for rituximab response was further evaluated through ROC curve analysis (Figure 4). The AUC was 0.692 (95% CI: 0.542–0.841). Alternative selectivity index based on albumin and IgG

Because transferrin is not routinely measured during PMN follow-up, we evaluated the feasibility of using albumin as a surrogate marker for transferrin in calculating the selectivity index. Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between the alternative index and the conventional PSI (r = 0.839, p < 0.001; Figure 5(A)). ICC analysis indicated good agreement between the two indices, with a single-measure ICC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.59–0.89, p < 0.001). Bland-Altman analysis showed a mean difference of −0.03 (95% CI: −0.04–−0.02), with the alternative index being slightly higher than the conventional PSI (p < 0.001). The 95% limits of agreement ranged from −0.142 to 0.080, and most data points fell within these limits, indicating good overall concordance. No proportional bias or trend was observed in the plot (Figure 5(B)). The AUC of the alternative index for predicting complete proteinuria remission in PMN patients was 0.639 (95% CI: 0.510–0.768). Based on the maximum Youden index, the optimal cutoff value was 0.212, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.528, a specificity of 0.757, and a Youden index of 0.285 (Figure 5(C)).

Discussion

Proteinuria selectivity reflects the degree of glomerular permeability impairment and serves as a key indicator of glomerular filtration barrier injury. Within the framework of contemporary PLA2R-based disease classification and current therapeutic strategies, such as anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, we assessed the prognostic relevance of the PSI in PMN, including its association with response to rituximab therapy. Our findings suggest that PSI has potential clinical value in predicting disease remission in PMN.

Although the KDIGO guidelines acknowledge PSI as a reference parameter for risk stratification in PMN, its prognostic significance has not been comprehensively established. Early evidence indicates that PSI may predict treatment in nephrotic syndrome. Laurent reported that lower PSI values were associated with a more favorable response to glucocorticoid therapy in patients with minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, or IgM nephropathy [15]. Similarly, Mallick found that among 12 patients with MN who achieved remission, nine had low PSI, whereas only eight of 28 patients with persistent proteinuria exhibited low PSI [7]. Moreover, Bazzi demonstrated a significant association between proteinuria selectivity and the severity of tubulointerstitial injury. A PSI value below 0.1 indicates highly selective proteinuria, reflecting minimal tubulointerstitial damage and a higher likelihood of achieving clinical remission [14]. However, most earlier studies were constrained by small sample sizes and conducted several decades ago. Therefore, their findings may not accurately reflect the current clinical spectrum of PMN, particularly in the era following the discovery of PLA2R antibodies and the widespread adoption of biologic agents. In our cohort, the mean PSI among patients with PMN was 0.22, consistent with previously reported values of 0.20 (interquartile range, 0.14–0.30) [16]. The optimal PSI cutoff for predicting complete remission in PMN was 0.303, with an AUC of 0.647, indicating moderate predictive performance. Importantly, even when proteinuria levels are below the nephrotic threshold of 3.5 g/day, patients with PSI > 0.303 may still require closer monitoring and more active therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, PSI demonstrated predictive potential for achieving complete remission following rituximab therapy, aligning with prior observations in adults with minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, where highly selective proteinuria was associated with favorable treatment response [10]. These results suggest that PSI may serve as an adjunctive tool for individualized management in PMN. Although its independent predictive value remains limited, combining PSI with other clinical indicators could further enhance its utility, warranting further investigation in future studies. Nevertheless, based on the current evidence, PSI alone is unlikely to be sufficient to directly guide specific treatment selection.

We speculate that the prognostic relevance of PSI may be related to the tubular toxicity of high-molecular-weight proteins [14]. The leakage of high-molecular-weight proteins can directly damage renal tubular epithelial cells, leading to interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Conversely, chronic pathological lesions such as glomerulosclerosis and fibrous crescents can severely disrupt the glomerular filtration barrier, increasing the leakage of large proteins, including IgG, and transforming proteinuria from selective to nonselective, potentially creating a mutually reinforcing cycle. Accordingly, as observed in our study, patients with nonselective proteinuria are more likely to exhibit significant tubulointerstitial damage and consequently have a poorer prognosis. Previous research has suggested that urinary loss of rituximab may be closely associated with treatment failure and reported a significant positive correlation between urinary rituximab levels and the proteinuria selectivity index (r = 0.61; p = 0.001), along with increased urinary rituximab loss in patients with PSI ≥ 0.30 [17]. A higher PSI value generally reflects increased urinary excretion of high-molecular-weight proteins, particularly IgG and therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. Based on this mechanism, we speculate that patients with higher PSI values are more likely to experience substantial urinary loss of rituximab, leading to reduced effective drug exposure, attenuation of its immunosuppressive effects, and ultimately a lower likelihood of achieving renal remission.

We further observed that an alternative index based on the IgG/albumin clearance ratio performs comparably to the conventional IgG/transferrin-based PSI in predicting prognosis in PMN. Historically, proteinuria selectivity was first assessed using the IgG/albumin clearance ratio. However, due to the limited accuracy of albumin assays at the time, transferrin gradually became the reference protein. In 1966, Cameron proposed calculating PSI using the IgG/transferrin ratio. With recent advancements in albumin assay technology, measurement accuracy has markedly improved, making the IgG/albumin ratio a clinically feasible alternative. Moreover, albumin is routinely monitored during PMN follow-up, allowing dynamic monitoring of PSI changes. From a molecular perspective, albumin (66 kDa) is smaller than transferrin (80 kDa), potentially providing a more precise reflection of selective IgG excretion in proteinuria and thus a theoretical advantage [18]. Previous small-scale studies have also indicated that PMN patients who achieve complete remission exhibit significantly lower IgG/albumin-based PSI values compared with those who do not [12].

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size and relatively short follow-up, and the validity of the identified PSI cutoff should be confirmed in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts. Second, no adjustment for potential key confounders, such as baseline proteinuria, anti-PLA2R antibody levels, and IFTA, was performed, which may have affected the robustness of the observed associations. Third, the cohort included both newly diagnosed and relapsed patients, all of whom were hospitalized, potentially representing a more severe subset and introducing selection bias. Fourth, the limited availability of transferrin or IgG assays in some non-specialized institutions may constrain the broader implementation of PSI. Fifth, PSI was measured only once, and its stability and reproducibility across different disease states have not been fully validated. In addition, as a real-world retrospective study, heterogeneity in immunosuppressive regimens and the lack of full standardization of concomitant supportive therapies may have influenced the assessment of treatment response. Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to clarify the prognostic utility of PSI across diverse treatment regimens and its clinical significance in individualized PMN management.
