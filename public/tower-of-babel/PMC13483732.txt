Effect of music intervention on anxiety and pain after anatomical endoscopic enucleation of the prostate: a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Irfan Safak Barlas, Mehmet Yilmaz, Halil Cagri Aybal, Mehmet Duvarci, Selcuk Guven, Lutfi Tunc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis prospective randomized study aimed to assess the effect of self-selected music, delivered via loudspeaker, on early postoperative anxiety and pain in patients who underwent anatomical endoscopic enucleation of the prostate (AEEP) under spinal anesthesia without sedation.MethodsAdult male patients scheduled for AEEP were randomly allocated to either a music intervention group (self-preferred music) or a control group (no music). Baseline characteristics, preoperative urethral catheter status, International Prostate Symptom Scores (IPSS), operative duration, and pre- and postoperative anxiety scores (State Anxiety Inventory, SAI) were recorded. Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was used to evaluate postoperative pain two hours after surgery.ResultsA total of 135 patients were analyzed (music group, n= 67; control group, n= 68). Baseline characteristics and operative times were comparable between groups (p>0.05). The music group had significantly lower postoperative SAI (32.0 ± 7.32 vs. 38.38 ± 11.28; p = 0.001) and VAS scores (1 [1-2] vs. 2 [1-3]; p = 0.001). The reduction in postoperative SAI compared to preoperative value was also greater in the music group (14 [10.00-21.00] vs. 7 [1.00-11.75]; p = 0.001). Music type had no significant impact on outcomes (p>0.05). In multivariate analysis, music intervention and preoperative catheterization were independent predictors of postoperative anxiety. ConclusionsSelf-selected music intervention significantly reduced early postoperative anxiety and pain following AEEP under spinal anesthesia. Given its simplicity and non-invasive nature, music may serve as an effective adjunct for enhancing patient comfort, regardless of music genre.Clinical trial numberNCT07255118Keywords: AEEP, Music type, Postoperative comfort, State Anxiety Inventory, Visual Analog Scale

Background

Significant progress in the surgical treatment of lower urinary tract symptoms caused by prostatic enlargement has resulted in the adoption of minimally invasive procedures that prioritize anatomical precision and energy-based technologies alongside traditional methods [1]. Among these, laser enucleation of the prostate represents a major advancement. Following the introduction of holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP), additional techniques such as thulium fiber laser enucleation of the prostate (ThuFLEP) have been developed [2]. These laser-based procedures are performed as part of anatomical endoscopic enucleation of the prostate (AEEP), which allows complete removal of obstructive tissue and has facilitated the widespread adoption of energy-based enucleation therapies [3].

Benign prostatic obstruction (BPO), which commonly results from progressive prostate growth, is frequently encountered in older men and represents a major cause of urinary complaints [4]. With advancing age, the occurrence of comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular and pulmonary disease increases, thereby elevating perioperative risk [5, 6]. In this clinical context, spinal anesthesia offers more stable hemodynamic control compared to general anesthesia and is associated with reduced postoperative pain, lower opioid use, decreased healthcare costs, and fewer complications [7–9]. It is therefore considered a safe and effective method for patients undergoing AEEP of the prostate for BPO [9].

Remaining awake and aware during surgical procedures has become increasingly prevalent. With advancements in surgical and anesthetic techniques, procedures are now frequently performed under local or regional anesthesia [10]. Even when performed without general anesthesia, surgical treatment planned for BPO patients may still induce psychological impacts such as fear, stress, and anxiety. In addition to surgical procedures performed under regional anesthesia [7, 8], various invasive procedures such as biopsies performed under local anesthesia have utilized different methods to reduce anxiety and pain [11–13]. These methods include hypnotherapy [14], cognitive behavioral techniques [15] and virtual reality technology [16]. Among these approaches, music intervention during invasive procedures under local anesthesia has become a frequently employed method due to its demonstrated ability to reduce anxiety and pain [11–13]. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the role of music intervention on early postoperative anxiety and pain in patients undergoing the AEEP procedure under spinal anesthesia.

Methods

Study design and participants

The present prospective study was conducted at the Urology Clinic of Acibadem Ankara Hospital between September 2023 and January 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Acibadem University (decision number: 2023-10/425, dated August 1, 2023). Following the provision of a detailed Participant Information Form, written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting and the trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT07255118.

Patients who consented to participate in the study were randomly allocated to either the music intervention group or the control group using a block randomization method based on a pre-generated random number table. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent researcher not involved in patient recruitment or outcome assessment. The envelopes were thoroughly mixed, and one envelope was selected by each participant immediately before surgery and opened together with a member of the clinical team, ensuring that neither the patient nor the investigators had prior knowledge of group assignment. The integrity of the envelopes was checked prior to use under the supervision of the research team. The processes of randomization sequence generation, envelope preparation, data entry and outcome assessment were carried out by different members of the research team and no outcome assessor was involved. To ensure blinding during data analysis, group allocation was masked using color-coded labels instead of explicit group names. Music subgroups were coded in the same manner, and although no music subtype existed for the control group, entries were assigned analogous color codes within the same variable to prevent identification. The coding scheme was disclosed to the analyst only after completion of the analysis.

In the control group (n = 68), no music was applied during the procedure. In the music intervention group (n = 67), patients listened to their preferred music delivered via loudspeaker (JBL Flip 6, Harman International Industries, Stamford, CT, USA). Patient music preferences were assessed after randomisation and prior to the procedure, and the selected playlists were prepared accordingly. The volume of the music was standardized across all cases to ensure that it did not interfere with communication between the surgical team and the patient. Although no objective sound level measurements in decibels were performed, the volume was set at approximately 25–30% of the device’s maximum output at the start of each procedure and maintained at this level throughout, ensuring that verbal communication remained clearly audible. The intervention was maintained throughout the patient’s stay in the operating room, from entry until discharge, thereby encompassing both spinal anesthesia administration and the surgical procedure.

Patients in the music intervention group were invited to indicate their preferred music type. Based on these preferences, standardized playlists were created using an online music platform (Spotify, Spotify AB, Stockholm, Sweden), consisting of songs within the same type. To ensure consistency across participants, playlists, regardless of type, were restricted to low-to-moderate tempo tracks with a predominantly calming and emotionally neutral or positive tone, avoiding highly stimulating or dynamic compositions. The playlists were predefined and not altered during the procedure, and were played continuously throughout the operation, with total duration adjusted to match the surgical time. No music was provided to patients in the control group throughout the procedure.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) male patients aged ≥ 40 years; (ii) patients who underwent surgery under regional anesthesia without sedation; (iii) patients who did not benefit from medical treatment for BPO; (iv) preoperative International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) > 19; (v) preoperative maximum urine flow rate (Qmax) < 15 mL/sec; and (vi) post-voiding residual volume (PVR) > 50 mL. The exclusion criteria included: (i) patients with urethral stricture; (ii) patients with bladder stone; (iii) patients who underwent urological treatments other than laser enucleation of the prostate; (iv) patients with urinary tract infections and/or hematuria; (v) patients with urinary system malignancy; (vi) patients with prostate cancer (vii) patients with a history of previous prostate surgery; (viii) patients with neurogenic bladder; (ix) patients with hearing impairment; and (x) patients with psychiatric disorders or those using medications prescribed by a psychiatrist.

AEEP procedure

All procedures were performed under regional anesthesia, with all patients receiving the same spinal anesthetic agents following an identical protocol administered by the same anesthesia team. Moreover, each patient was operated on by the same surgeon (LT) whom they had personally chosen for their operation. All operations were performed using a 26 Fr continuous-flow resectoscope with a laser bridge (Karl Storz Endoscopy, CA, USA) in combination with a Thulium Fiber laser (Fiber Dust®; Quanta System) at a wavelength of 1940 nm and a maximum power output of 60 W, delivered through a 550 μm optical fiber (Optical Fiber; Quanta System). The Omega Sign Technique [17] was employed during the procedure.

Postoperatively, all patients were catheterized with a 22 Fr urethral Foley catheter. All patients had a urethral catheter in place during the postoperative questionnaires. No routine postoperative analgesia was administered. Instead, a postoperative analgesic protocol was applied, with same nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory treatment administered on demand to patients who required analgesia based on patient-reported pain intensity.

Questionnaires

The State Anxiety Inventory (SAI) questionnaire, developed by Spielberger, was used to measure patients’ anxiety levels [18]. For anxiety assessment, patients were administered the 20-question form in their hospital rooms both before the operation and two hours after returning to their rooms post-operatively. For each question, patients selected one of the following responses: “Not at all,” “Somewhat,” “Moderately so,” or “Very much so.” Each response was scored from 1 to 4 according to their response. The total score obtained ranged from 20 to 80 points, with higher scores reflecting greater anxiety levels. In addition to the post-operative SAI assessment, patients were asked to rate their pain intensity two hours after surgery with the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), a 0–10 numerical scale, where 0 corresponded to “no pain” and 10 to “the worst possible pain.

The two-hour postoperative time point was selected based on clinical experience, as the effects of regional anesthesia typically begin to diminish within this period and postoperative pain generally becomes clinically apparent, allowing a more accurate assessment of analgesic requirement. Furthermore, by this time, patients are typically more settled, have overcome the immediate perioperative stress, and are better able to comprehend and reliably respond to assessment tools.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses used SPSS software version 22.0 and R version 4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org). All statistical analyses were prespecified prior to data collection. The Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were used for evaluated the normality of continuous variables. We reported continuous variables with normal distribution as mean ± standard deviation, and those with non-normal distribution as median (interquartile range-IQR). Group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test or independent samples t-test. The Pearson chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were utilized to compare categorical variables. Bonferroni correction was applied to all pairwise comparisons to account for multiple testing, and adjusted p values are reported. A Spearman or Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine potential correlations. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to determine effective factors for post-operative SAI. The distribution of the dependent variable (post-operative SAI) was assessed for normality, and a logarithmic transformation was applied as the data were not normally distributed. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with all values indicating no significant multicollinearity. The criterion for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Sample size

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4). Assuming a two-sided independent samples t-test, a power of 0.80, an alpha level of 0.05 and a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), the required sample size was calculated to be 128 participants in total (64 per group). The final sample size of the study (67 vs. 68) was sufficient to achieve the desired statistical power with approximately a 5% recruitment buffer to account for potential dropouts or data loss.

Results

In the present study, 189 consecutive patients diagnosed with BPO were assessed for eligibility, of whom 135 were included in the final analysis. The enrolled patients and their group allocation are illustrated in Fig. 1, which presents the CONSORT 2025 flow diagram [19]. A total of 54 patients were excluded for the following reasons: prostate cancer (n = 4), history of urethral stricture (n = 5), presence of bladder stones requiring cystolithotripsy (n = 7), planned additional intraoperative procedures (n = 20), preoperative sedation (n = 11), and planned surgery under general anesthesia (n = 7). None of the patients excluded after allocation.

Flow Diagram

In the overall cohort, the median age was 66 [61–71] years, and their mean BMI was 27.4 ± 3.8 kg/m². Among them, 53 patients (39.2%) were actively employed, and 99 patients (73.3%) had received a high school or higher level of education. Alcohol consumption was reported in 34 patients (25.1%), and 43 patients (31.8%) were current smokers. The cohort included 40 patients (29.6%) with a history of cardiovascular disease, 76 patients (56.2%) with hypertension, and 21 patients (15.5%) with diabetes mellitus. In addition, 33 patients (24.4%) underwent pre-operative urethral catheterization due to acute urinary retention. The median operative time was 74 [56–103] minutes.

In the overall cohort, pre-operatively, the median IPSS score was 32 [29–35] and the median SAI score was 45 [36–54]. Post-operatively, the median VAS score was 2 [1–3], and the median SAI score was 34 [28–41], reflecting a median decrease of 10 [5–15] points compared to pre-operative levels.

Music was provided during the procedure in 67 patients, while 68 patients underwent the procedure without music intervention. Among the patients in the music intervention group, music type was determined based on patient preference: 40 (59.7%) selected popular music, 19 (28.4%) selected regional folk music, and 8 (11.9%) selected classical music. In addition, post-operative VAS scores were significantly lower in the music intervention group (1 [1–2] vs. 2 [1–3], respectively; p = 0.001) (Fig. 2c).

Within the postoperative period first two hours, 5 patients (7.4%) in the music intervention group and 12 patients (17.6%) in the control group experienced pain requiring analgesic treatment. All cases were effectively managed with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Analgesic administration was performed after the two-hour VAS assessment in patients who reported pain at that time point. No patients reported severe pain requiring earlier intervention prior to the two-hour assessment.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the effects of music intervention on early postoperative anxiety and pain levels in patients undergoing AEEP under spinal anesthesia. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of music intervention specifically in patients undergoing AEEP. Patients were allowed to select their own music, and based on their choices, we categorized the music into three groups: popular, regional folk, and classical. Our analysis showed that all music types provided similar levels of benefit, indicating that the therapeutic effect was consistent across types when the music was self-selected.

An increasing number of studies have indicated the positive impact of music intervention in reducing anxiety during urological procedures [11–13]. Similarly, in a study conducted by the anesthesiology department the intraoperative sedative effect of music in patients undergoing transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) under spinal anesthesia [20]. The analysis was limited to intraoperative anxiety and vital signs; postoperative outcomes and pain were not assessed. These findings support the anxiolytic effect of music observed in various surgical contexts.

There are also studies reporting that music interventions do not significantly reduce procedure-related anxiety levels during invasive procedures. Notably, a study involving patients undergoing transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided biopsy while listening to classic rock music did not observe a statistically significant difference in anxiety between music and control groups [21]. As the authors noted, this might be explained by variations in music delivery, volume, or type. Classic rock may not have been sufficiently calming for all patients, and inappropriate or excessively loud selections could have diminished the effectiveness of the intervention.

The use of noise-canceling headphones for delivering music during TRUS-guided biopsy was examined in a previous study, in which patients listened to classical music through these headphones and reported lower anxiety levels; however, the difference was not statistically significant [22]. This may reflect individual variability in music preferences, and the use of isolating headphones may have interfered with communication between patients and medical staff, thereby potentially limiting the anxiolytic effect of the music. In contrast to this approach, we chose to deliver ambient music via loudspeakers in the operating room to avoid excessive patient isolation and to maintain effective communication throughout the procedure. Although background conversations and other operating room sounds could still be perceived by the patients and might have been a source of discomfort, we considered that isolating the patients completely would have been more detrimental, as it could compromise their sense of connection with the surgical team and increase anxiety. On the other hand, to minimize potential contamination effects of ambient music related to noise or staff interaction, the music volume was kept at a moderate level that would not disturb either the patient or the surgical team. In our study, no adverse effects related to the chosen music volume or disruption of communication were observed. In line with these considerations, Hole et al. [23] also emphasized that each method of music delivery has its own advantages and disadvantages, yet music can reduce anxiety after surgical procedures regardless of the method of delivery.

In addition to its anxiety-reducing effects, music intervention has also been shown to alleviate pain across various procedures [12, 13]. Similar pain-reducing effects have been reported in various urological interventions in several studies [24], further supporting our findings. Notably, a study comparing patients who received music through headphones, those exposed to noise-canceling headphones alone, and a control group reported that although the reduction in anxiety did not reach statistical significance, post-procedural pain was higher in the control group than both intervention groups [22]. In addition to the effect of music, these findings suggest that patient isolation provided by noise-canceling headphones may contribute to pain reduction, even in the absence of a measurable anxiolytic effect. Conversely, the study involving patients undergoing TRUS-guided biopsy while listening to classic rock music found no significant differences in post-procedural pain between the music and control groups, consistent with their findings on anxiety [21]. This may suggest that both the type of music and the method of its delivery can influence not only anxiolytic but also analgesic outcomes. In another study conducted during TRUS-guided biopsy, the use of regional folk music significantly reduced anxiety levels, although it did not result in a meaningful difference in pain scores [25]. This finding may further support the notion that using pre-selected music, rather than patient-preferred selections, could limit the analgesic benefits of music intervention. In contrast, this study allowed patients to choose their own music, which may have enhanced both the anxiolytic and analgesic effects observed in the postoperative period.

In this study, regression analysis revealed that patients who required preoperative catheterization due to acute urinary retention exhibited higher levels of postoperative anxiety. Similarly, Fowler S et al. [26], in their study of 27 long-term catheter users, reported that catheterization was a significant source of anxiety. Factors contributing to this included the need to adjust to catheter use, difficulties managing the catheter away from home, nighttime discomfort, cosmetic concerns, social limitations, dependence on others for assistance, unpredictability of catheter-related issues, and the overall impact on quality of life. These findings align with our observations. In addition to catheterization status, we also examined other demographic parameters such as educational level and employment status. Consistent with previous studies evaluating the effects of music intervention during thyroid [11] and breast [12] biopsy procedures, it was found that these demographic characteristics were not significantly linked to postoperative anxiety or pain in this study. The findings emphasize the relevance of considering music intervention status in the assessment of postoperative anxiety risk and the optimization of supportive strategies.

Taken together, the greater reduction in SAI scores in the intervention group, along with the lower postoperative pain scores observed in our study, supports a beneficial effect of the music intervention. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies in similar procedural or postoperative settings. The sample size in our study is comparable to and slightly larger than those of the studies we discussed, which may have enabled a more stable estimation of the effect and slightly strengthened the analysis. Accordingly, this may have contributed to a more reliable assessment of the observed effect size.

Although AEEP is a recognized standard technique for the surgical management of BPO, its application remains relatively new compared to other standard modalities. One of the main strengths of this study is its contribution to the literature on the use of AEEP under spinal anesthesia. Another strength of this study is the extensive institutional experience with AEEP performed under spinal anesthesia, which enabled us to enroll a sufficient number of patients to evaluate postoperative pain and comfort in this specific clinical setting. Furthermore, unlike most previous studies that applied one type of music to all patients, this study allowed patients to select their own preferred music and, distinct from other studies employing self-selected music, compared outcomes across different music types.

This study has several limitations to be mentioned. Firstly, due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of patients and surgeons was not feasible, as participants were aware of the study procedures prior to surgery. Although efforts were made to minimize bias, the unblinded nature of the study may have introduced expectancy and reporting bias, particularly given the inability to use entirely objective outcome measures. Therefore, the potential contribution of placebo or contextual effects cannot be fully excluded, and this limits the extent to which the observed reductions in anxiety and pain can be attributed solely to the music intervention. Secondly, the educational level of the study population was relatively high, which may restrict the generalization of our findings to populations with different sociodemographic backgrounds. Thirdly, the short follow-up period limited the evaluation of potential longer-term effects; therefore, the findings cannot be extrapolated to longer-term pain and anxiety outcomes. Lastly, although the single-center and single-surgeon design ensured a standardized procedure across all patients, it may limit the external applicability and generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of music intervention on postoperative anxiety and pain in patients undergoing AEEP under spinal anesthesia. Our findings suggest that music significantly reduces both anxiety and pain in the early postoperative period and that self-selected music is beneficial regardless of its type. Given its simplicity and patient-centered nature, music intervention may be considered a valuable adjunct for enhancing patient comfort during laser enucleation procedures performed under regional anesthesia. If supported by further studies, these findings may contribute to the broader integration of music interventions into perioperative care.
