Abstract

This report is in support of the Innovative and Novel Computational Impact on Theory and Experiment (INCITE)
program sponsored by the U.S. Department of Energy (USDOE). With INCITE-level resources, one project, ti-
tled BubblyFlow, was granted computational resources for the 2025 calendar year on the Polaris supercomputer
at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF). The project aims to conduct simulations to understand
the fundamental characteristics of turbulent bubbly flow phenomena in nature. Staff at the ALCF and Argonne’s
Computational Science division, along with collaborators at the City College of New York and University of Illinois
at Chicago, helped a summer student to assess the accuracy and performance of two high performance computing
(HPC) codes. Both codes, ImExLBM and FluTAS, are fundamentally different in their mathematical and numerical
modeling. However, both may be used to solve the same physical problem. The collaboration sought to better
understand the differences between both codes in terms of accuracy and efficiency. This would ultimately help the
BubblyFlow project better utilize resources and establish a knowledge-base of code capabilities in future simulation
campaigns. We compare ImExLBM and FluTAS, two high-performance multiphase computational fluid dynamics
(CFD) solvers, in terms of physical fidelity, time-to-solution, and parallel efficiency. We validate ImExLBM (Implicit-
Explicit Lattice Boltzmann Method) against a canonical benchmark and assess it’s performance relative to FluTAS
(Fluid Transport Accelerated Solver), a well-established open-source CFD code.

1 1. Introduction

2 Multiphase flow, the motion of immiscible fluid phases such as liquid–liquid or gas–liquid mixtures, occurs across
3 many fields: industrial applications including emulsification, froth flotation, and oil production [1, 2, 3]; energy
4 systems featuring boiling and phase change in nuclear reactors [4]; and biomedical contexts such as targeted drug
5    delivery [5, 6].
6 Accurately simulating multiphase flows is challenging due to the inherently multiscale nature of these systems and
7 the need to capture complex interfacial dynamics such as droplet deformation, breakup, and coalescence. Numerical
8 methods for resolving fluid–fluid interfaces must conserve mass, momentum, and kinetic energy at the discrete
9 level, accommodate large contrasts in material properties, and robustly handle topological changes such as merging
10 and breakup [7]. Another issue in multiphase simulations is that the interface thickness is typically many orders
11 of magnitude smaller than the overall simulation domain. A variety of interface-resolved approaches have been
12 developed to address these challenges, including Front-Tracking, Volume-of-Fluid (VOF), Level Set, and Phase
13 Field methods. Each offers distinct advantages depending on the target physics and computational framework. For
14 instance, the Phase Field method introduces a diffuse interface characterized by a finite thickness parameter, whereas
15 Front-Tracking, VOF, and Level Set methods represent the interface sharply. This diversity allows researchers to
16 select the most suitable approach for their specific multiphase flow applications [8].
17 In addition to careful choice of the numerical methods discussed above, a modern CFD method must also be designed
18 with computational efficiency in mind. In particular, how straightforward its adaptation to heterogeneous parallel
19 architectures is, specifically GPU architectures. It is for this reason that we compare the two codes, FluTAS (VOF,
20 sharp interface) and ImExLBM (LBM, phase-field), on the same 3D canonical test case, the single rising bubble. [9].
21 This case compares two fluids characterized by density and viscosity ratios of 10, Reynolds number of 35 and Bond
22 number of 10. We sought to validate if the ImExLBM code developed at City College of NY would show benefits in
23 performance or accuracy of data, specifically in parallel efficiency and mass conservation.

 77 The DBE models the evolution of particle distribution functions (PDFs) fα (x, t), which represent the probability
 78 of finding a particle at location x, time t, and moving in lattice direction α. This kinetic formulation captures
 79 hydrodynamic behavior through statistical averaging of particle collisions and streaming. [16]
 80 By performing a Chapman-Enskog multiscale expansion of the DBE [17], one recovers the macroscopic continuity
 81 and momentum equations in the low Mach number limit. In this sense, the LBM is not simply a discretization of
 82 the Navier-Stokes equations, but an alternate, kinetic route that recovers them asymptotically.
 83 The LBM discretizes both velocity space and physical space: the velocity space is represented using a finite set
 84 of discrete directions — in this case, a D3Q27 lattice — while the physical space is discretized using a uniform
 85 Cartesian grid. Time is advanced in fixed steps, and the update of the PDFs follows a two-stage process of collision
 86 and streaming.

132 We conclude this section with some context. Due to some of its desirable features, the Lattice Boltzmann Method
133 (LBM) is employed as an alternative to traditional, direct Navier-Stokes solvers. One major drive behind the use of
134 LBM is ease of parallelization, but another unique feature increases its popularity: it solves a simplified Boltzmann
135 equation that is essentially a set of 1st order hyperbolic PDEs for which many excellent discretization schemes are
136 available. This effectively allows scientists and developers to avoid solving a Poisson equation for the pressure.
137 ImExLBM is a 2D & 3D code capable of solving these equations on heterogeneous CPU-GPU platforms using the
138 Message Passing Interface (MPI) + Kokkos parallelization framework.

139   2.3. FluTAS
140 The Fluid Accelerated Transport Solver, or FluTAS, code is a scalable GPU code for multiphase flows. It is well-
141 tested and previously benchmarked for the same test case reviewed here. This code, unlike LBM, directly solves
142 the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for two-fluid systems using a direct FFT-based Poisson solver for the
143 pressure equation. The interface between the two fluids is represented with a Volume of Fluid (VoF) method, in
144 contrast to ImExLBM’s phase-field diffuse interface method. The code is written in modern Fortran and parallelized
145 with OpenACC directives in the GPU implementation, which is the subject of this review. We test it against our
146 code with both a 1D and 2D domain decomposition to determine which has the best performance in this problem.
147 For more information on the formulation of FluTAS, the authors of this review direct you to [8].

209 We evaluated the parallel performance of ImExLBM and FluTAS on a 128×128×256 domain, as well as ImExLBM

211 ranks per node and each MPI rank is bound to one (1) GPU on the node. Total solver time was recorded for each
212 run. All simulations were performed on the Polaris machine at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility, where
213 each node consists of four A100 NVIDIA GPUs and one AMD EPYC ”Milan” processor.
214 Parallel efficiency measures the ratio of ideal speedup as compared to actual speedup when scaling to multiple MPI

246   4.2. GPU-Aware MPI
247 In the simulation code, the particle distribution functions (PDFs) and macroscopic fields are stored in separate
248 Kokkos views to optimize both computation and communication. The quantity buffer pack f is defined as a four-
249 dimensional view, Kokkos::View<double****, Space>, which stores the PDFs for each discrete velocity direction
250 at every lattice node. This layout provides efficient access during the collision and streaming steps of the LBM
251 algorithm. Similarly, buffer pack u is defined as a three-dimensional view, Kokkos::View<double***, Space>,
252 which stores macroscopic quantities such as velocity and density.
253 Kokkos permits flexibility in selecting the memory space in which these views are allocated, and this choice has
254 important performance implications. When the views are allocated in CudaHostPinnedSpace, the data reside in
255 page-locked host memory. Such allocations are advantageous for communication, as pinned memory enables efficient
256 data transfer between host and device and can be directly accessed by MPI during halo exchanges. Host access to
257 this memory is fast, while device access still requires transfer across the PCIe bus; however, pinned memory reduces
258 this overhead compared to standard pageable memory.
259 In contrast, allocating the views in CudaSpace places the data directly in device memory. This maximizes performance
260 for GPU kernels, as the collision and streaming operations can be executed without additional memory movement.
261 To enable direct device-to-device communication in practice, additional configuration was required. During compi-
262 lation, the Cray MPI GPU transport library was explicitly linked by appending
263 -L/opt/cray/pe/mpich/8.1.28/gtl/lib -lmpi_gtl_cuda
264 to the executable link line in the Makefile. Furthermore, at runtime the environment variable
265 export MPICH_GPU_SUPPORT_ENABLED=1
266 was set to activate GPU-aware communication within the Cray MPICH implementation. CudaHostPinnedSpace was
267 used in all previous simulations, allowing for quick communication. In order to investigate the effect of GPU-bound
268 memory arrays, we migrated all halo exchange buffers to CudaSpace and tested strong scaling. The simulation in
269 question is the same as in section 3.2, with domain size [128x256x128] and run to nondimensional time T /t0 = 1.

 19

 Figure 11: Time to solution plot for ImExLBM with GPU-Aware MPI with domain size [128x256x128] (left) compared to non-GPU
 aware MPI time to solution of ImExLBM on the same domain (right).

270 On a single rank, GPU-aware MPI yielded a performance improvement, reducing time-to-solution from 217 s to 152 s.
271 This result is expected, as no inter-rank communication occurs and all data remain resident on the GPU, eliminating
272 host-device transfer overhead. With this result, it is shown that on 1 rank ImExLBM executes significantly faster
273   than FluTAS (152s vs. 202s).
274 However, for multi-rank executions, GPU-aware MPI did not immediately provide a strong-scaling advantage. At
275 two ranks, the GPU-aware configuration performed significantly worse (184 s) than the host-pinned configuration
276 (104 s). This behavior suggests that for message sizes typical of halo exchanges at 2 ranks counts, communication
277 latency and GPU synchronization overhead dominate execution time, overwhelming any benefits of direct device-
278 memory communication. Indeed, it can be seen that the streaming operation, which is where the halo exchanges
279 occur, is impacted most significantly by this. We acknowledge that further investigation is required as to why there
280 is a performance degradation from 1 to 2 ranks.
281 As the number of ranks increased, the performance gap steadily narrowed. By 16 ranks, both approaches achieved
282 nearly identical runtimes, and at 64 and 128 ranks the difference was reduced to approximately 5–7%. This conver-
283 gence suggests that GPU-aware MPI becomes competitive only once communication volume is sufficiently large for
284 bandwidth advantages to offset latency costs.
285 Overall, these results demonstrate that while GPU-aware MPI improves single-rank performance, it does not sig-
286 nificantly enhance strong scaling for this LBM workload. For moderate rank counts, host-pinned communication
287 remains more efficient due to lower latency and better overlap of computation and communication. One potential
288 path of investigation is to understand the difference in network traffic between the two implementations. At the
289 moment, our results suggest host buffers benefit from improved bandwidth over device buffers. Of course, further
290 testing is required and will be part of future work.

291 5. Conclusion

292 We presented detailed results of simulation, scaling, profiling and timing results for two CFD codes, ImExLBM and
293 FluTAS, applied to the canonical 3D Rising Bubble test case at density and viscosity ratio of 10. Strong scaling was
294 demonstrated on up to np = 128 A100 NVIDIA GPUs on ALCF’s Polaris HPC. ImExLBM showed increased parallel
295 performance over FluTAS, with above 80% parallel efficiency up to 16 ranks in the smaller domain case (128x256x128)
296 and above 90% parallel efficiency up to 16 ranks in the larger domain case (256x512x256), as compared to only 18%
297 from FluTAS over the same range.
298 While both codes simulate the 3D Rising Bubble benchmark problem, the codes solve fundamentally different equa-
299 tions, use different discretizations, solvers and GPU-abstraction layers. Despite these differences, the simulation
300 results were showed to be in strong agreement and the scaling results showed that ImExLBM offered significant
301 improvements in terms of performance and solution speed. In addition to offering improved performance, this work

 20

302 also demonstrates the desirable mass conservation properties of the conservative phase-field LBM scheme with the
303 continuous surface stress.

304   5.1. Future Work
305 In future work, we would seek to explore higher density and viscosity ratio regimes, which are more challenging
306 numerically and are representative of industrially relevant flows, such as liquid-metal and molten-salt systems. We
307 would also apply this to thermally coupled multiphase problems, as one might see in nuclear or battery storage
308 applications. We would also perform other benchmark problems in multiphase flow, such as droplet collision, bubble
309 coalescence, and Rayleigh–Taylor or Kelvin–Helmholtz instability cases, to further validate solver accuracy and
310 stability. On the performance side, we would optimize data movement and memory access for even better GPU
311 performance. We would investigate whether GPU-Aware MPI shows further improvements at higher rank counts
312 and with larger domain sizes. We would use profiling tools like NVIDIA’s Nsight. We would also look to run this
313 code on other systems, such as ALCF’s new Aurora computer at Argonne National Lab.
