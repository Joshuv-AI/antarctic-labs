Expanding Upon Ambulatory Blood Pressure Variability Reproducibility: Methodological and Clinical Perspectives
Authors: Aarav Vasanthan, Ava Suko, Aayush Visaria
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

It is with delight that we read the work of Lykka et al. titled “Reproducibility of Ambulatory Blood Pressure Variability in Untreated Hypertensive Patients” [1], which provided valuable insight into the reproducibility of both daytime and nighttime changes to systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as pulse pressure indices. However, we would like to raise some specific concerns regarding the study's findings on the reproducibility of the nighttime Blood Pressure Variability (BPV) index and the statistical models, both of which may be crucial to assessing reproducibility.

The authors noted that all nighttime BPV indices showed poorer reproducibility than those during the day. One notable consideration is that, with the 24 h blood pressure monitor inflating and deflating every 15 min, subjects' sleep quality and sleep cycles may be disrupted, thereby affecting nocturnal BPV [2]. Additionally, a meaningful consideration when measuring the reproducibility of ambulatory BPV is that individual patients' sleep reports may introduce variability into nocturnal BPV indices due to differences in sleep duration and quality. It would be worthwhile to examine the subjects using sleep trackers that monitor sleep quality and duration, especially the daytime and nighttime indices. Doing so would provide insight into whether the poor reproducibility of nocturnal BPV is due to physiological variation, to artificial fluctuations caused by sleep disruption, or to potential self‐report biases.

While the ICC values largely indicate moderately reproducible findings for daytime indexes, Cohen's Kappa, on average, yielded poor to fair agreement. The study's choice of a 75th‐percentile cutoff could create a threshold effect. Additionally, the use of Kappa has limitations, including the paradox of maintaining lower scores even when other metrics indicate moderate to good agreement, as well as its absoluteness in categorizing data [3]. While the use of this percentile is appropriate for this study, in clinical cases, the diagnostic instability may lead to errors for individual patients.

The authors' decision to exclude hypertensive patients receiving antihypertensives was an effective way to control confounding effects and assess reproducibility. However, these findings raise questions about the effects of various antihypertensives on patient BPV. In clinical settings, physicians would benefit from understanding the varying degrees of antihypertensive efficacy in reducing BPV, which is important for patient health. Class‐specific differences have been observed in the literature, with Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs) and non‐loop diuretics being most effective at lowering BPV [4]. Accounting for differences across drug classes is important because most patients who visit clinics are on hypertension medications [5]. Future studies on measuring ambulatory BPV in subjects on medication could point towards more informed treatment decision‐making.

Overall, the authors found strong evidence for moderate reproducibility in most daytime ambulatory BPV indexes, but poorer reproducibility across all nighttime indexes. Furthermore, future studies may introduce conditions for measuring ambulatory BPV in medicated patients, thereby providing further insights into the efficacy of drugs in controlling BPV.
