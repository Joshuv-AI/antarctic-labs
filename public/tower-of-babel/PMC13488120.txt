Socioeconomic inequalities in online fresh food delivery use and associations with the neighborhood food environment
Authors: Bochu Liu, Chuhao Liu, Thomas Burgoine, Guangping Chen, Yue Wang, Lan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOnline delivery services may reduce physical barriers to acquiring fresh foods, but evidence on their socioeconomic patterning and associations with the neighborhood food environment remains limited. We examined socioeconomic inequalities in online fresh food ordering and its associations with the physical food environment.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study linking citywide transaction records for online green grocery, supermarket, and fruit delivery orders to neighborhood sociodemographic characteristics across fine-grained grids (n = 11,581) in Shanghai, China. We assessed socioeconomic inequalities in online fresh food delivery use by population composition (shares of female, older, young, low-income, and car-free residents) using concentration curves. We then estimated associations of online fresh food order counts with population composition and the physical food environment (counts of supermarkets, grocery stores, fruit stores, and fast-food outlets within a 1 km street-network buffer) using Bayesian spatial zero-inflated Poisson regression models with a residential population offset, which account for spatial autocorrelation, excessive zeros, and overdispersion in grid-level order counts.ResultsConcentration curves ranked by the proportion of low-income residents fell below the line of equality, indicating that online fresh food orders were disproportionately concentrated in more economically advantaged neighborhoods. In Bayesian spatial regression models, grid-level order rates were lower in neighborhoods with higher shares of older, young, and low-income residents, but higher in areas with a greater proportion of car-free residents. Greater densities of nearby supermarkets and grocery stores were associated with lower online order rates across all order types. In the zero-inflation component of the models, a higher number of grocery stores was associated with a lower probability of zero green grocery orders, and greater supermarket access was associated with a lower probability of zero supermarket orders.ConclusionsOnline fresh food delivery is used disproportionately in socioeconomically advantaged neighborhoods, highlighting persistent inequalities in access to healthy foods. Yet, higher use in areas with a higher share of car-free residents and in places with fewer nearby retailers suggests that delivery services can partially offset mobility constraints and limited physical food options, offering a potential avenue for improving fresh food access in underserved communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28121-6.Keywords: Fruit and vegetable consumption, Online food delivery, Socioeconomic inequality, Neighborhood food environment, Food access, Food consumption, Urban China

Background

Healthy diet is crucial for maintaining healthy body weight and reducing risk of diet-related non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. In urban settings, physical access to retailers selling fresh produce enables consumption of fresh produce to facilitate healthy diets [3, 4]. However, barriers such as transportation and time constraints, and the lack of preferred stores nearby impede access to fresh foods such as fruits and vegetables [5–8]. These barriers particularly affect socioeconomically disadvantaged communities, who are living in “food deserts” devoid of supermarkets and grocery stores [9, 10].

Online fresh food delivery offers an opportunity to mitigate these physical barriers, as service providers rather than consumers are responsible for packing and transporting fresh foods [11]. Fresh food orders are commonly fulfilled via rapid grocery delivery services (RGDS) such as immediate or same day delivery and delivery within the hour scheduled. Globally, online grocery shopping has become an important means for acquiring fresh foods. Between 2017 and 2023, global online grocery sales surged from 110 billion to 390 billion USD [12]. In China alone, users purchasing fresh food online increased from 323 million in 2019 to 530 million in 2023, generating transactions worth 642.69 billion RMB (89.21 billion USD) [13]. Online fresh food delivery offers both “click and collect” and delivery-to-door formats [14], and the latter is the dominant model across platforms operating in China. Considering the convenient delivery model and its expanding user base, online grocery shopping is emerging as a potential health-promoting behavior, reducing physical access constraints and potentially resulting in healthier food choices [15–18].

Despite its improvement on food accessibility and potential health benefits, it has been understudied who orders fresh foods online and whether fresh food orders are equally distributed across socioeconomic groups. This question is worth investigating as differential abilities to access healthy foods can result in dietary and health inequalities. However, past research has primarily documented socioeconomic disparities in ordering ready-to-eat food, with limited attention on fruits and vegetables. For instance, analyses of data from the International Food Policy Study have indicated that younger adults and individuals with higher educational attainment were more likely to order restaurant meals online [14, 19]. Recent work has begun to explore socioeconomic disparities in online grocery usage but have yielded inconsistent findings. Studies show that online grocery shoppers in the U.S. were generally younger, more educated, ineligible for the Supplementary Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and had higher income [20]. Similarly, UK-based research noted higher online grocery delivery service utilization among high-income households, suggesting that grocery purchasing may predominantly depend on financial resources [21]. In contrast, another U.S. study identified no relationship between income and the adoption of online grocery shopping, instead observing that higher-income individuals were more likely to revert to physical stores after the pandemic [22]. Discrepancies also exist regarding vehicle availability. While a U.S. study reported lower propensity for online grocery among households without cars [23], UK-based evidence highlighted the absence of private vehicles as a driver for adoption of grocery delivery service use [24]. On top of the inconsistency in findings, the existing body of research is predominantly situated within Western contexts, leaving critical gaps in understanding disparities and drivers of online grocery usage in middle-income countries such as China.

The physical food environment may also have an impact online fresh food ordering. However, most research on online fresh food usage lacks a spatial lens, leaving open questions about how usage varies across neighborhoods and the extent to which spatial factors, such as physical food stores and population or built environment density, explain that variation. One exception is Wang et al. [22] who found that higher grocery store density reduced intentions to continue using online grocery delivery after initial adoption during the Covid-19 pandemic, but that study did not examine actual ordering frequency or other store types. Building on evidence linking frequent takeaway ordering to the surrounding physical food retailers [25, 26], future work should explicitly examine how brick-and-mortar retailers such as supermarkets, fruit and vegetable stores, and fast food restaurants relate to online fresh food ordering. Identifying which neighborhood conditions are associated with higher or lower use can inform targeted interventions to promote equitable access to healthy food.

To addressing these gaps, we leverage citywide transaction data from Shanghai, China, linking fine-grained records of online fresh food orders to detailed neighborhood characteristics. This design enables us to investigate two central questions: (1) What are the sociodemographic patterns in the use of online fresh food delivery services and (2) Is online fresh food ordering associated with characteristics of the physical food environment? By adopting a spatial perspective, this study will shed light on how digital food purchasing intersects with socio-spatial contexts, information essential for urban health management and planning.

Data and methods

Study setting and data sources

Shanghai is the largest city in China with over 24 million residents. This city has a large and growing market of online food delivery. By 2024, nearly 3 million meal-delivery orders occurred per day in Shanghai [27]. Fresh food delivery services have likewise grown, with major operators reporting double-digit increase in transaction values (i.e., gross merchandise volume) in 2024 [28]. Our study area comprises the territory enclosed by Shanghai’s suburban ring expressway, spanning 3073 km2 and containing 21 million usual residents as of the 2020 census1 [29].

We obtained anonymized, aggregated order count (i.e., frequency) from online stores specializing in fresh foods (i.e., vegetables, fruits, and other perishable foods) from Ele.me, a leading rapid food delivery platform in Shanghai and one of the two dominant players in China’s market. The distribution of fresh food retailers on this platform has been documented in a previous study [30]. Among stores registered on this platform, over 4251 supermarkets, 3037 grocery stores (including wet markets), and 2305 fruit stores can accept orders from the study area. The data provider granted us access to a sample from one typical, non-holiday week (i.e., seven consecutive days) in June 2024. To comply with data-protection regulations while preserving socio-spatial representativeness, they supplied at least 30% (the exact percentages were undisclosed) of orders from supermarkets, grocery stores, and fruit stores, respectively, all of which were anonymized and aggregated. The data provider aggregated online fresh food order frequency to 500 × 500 m grids, a resolution that complies with privacy protection constraints while retaining intra-urban heterogeneity. This scale lies within common practice in food delivery analyses, which range from 100 m to 1 km grids [25, 26, 31].

To characterize local population profiles, we obtained total population and sociodemographic attributes for usual residents from another major Internet service provider’s location-based services data product (not from Ele.me), which have been used in recent studies conducted in Shanghai [25, 32]. The original 100 × 100 m sociodemographic data were aggregated to the 500 × 500 m grid cells described above to maintain consistency. All data used in this study were anonymized and aggregated before analysis, with no personally identifiable information included. We also obtained spatial data of road network and brick-and-mortar retailers from Gaode Map, a leading open map service provider in China.

Count of online fresh food orders

The dependent variables are weekly counts of online fresh food delivery orders. Orders are categorized into green grocery, supermarket, and fruit orders based on store type. Neighborhood grocery stores and vegetable markets (i.e., wet markets) that specialize in fresh produce and other perishables are important sources of fresh food in urban China, owing to consumers’ perceptions of freshness, convenience, and cultural shopping norms [33, 34]. Vegetable markets can register under Ele.me’s sunshine vegetable market program, which enables ordering through the platform [35]. FreshHippo stores and Dingdong community front warehouses—two brands specializing in selling fresh food online—are also listed as grocery stores. Orders from the above-mentioned store types are recorded under the green grocery category. We defined supermarket orders as those delivered from supermarkets registered to accept orders online. Despite carrying a broader range of goods, supermarkets are important sources of healthy foods [36], and access to supermarkets has been linked to diet quality in different populations [37]. Using nationally representative panel data from China, researchers show that improved supermarket access leads to higher dietary diversity and nutrient intake [38]. We note, however, that some supermarket orders include items other than fresh foods. Fruit orders refer to orders placed with fruit stores and shops selling ready-to-eat fruit servings.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Key explanatory sociodemographic variables include the proportion of female residents, the share of older adults, the percentage of low-income and carless residents (i.e., residents not owning any private vehicles). Older adults are defined as residents aged 65 years and above, whereas young adults are those aged between 18 and 34 years. Low-income share refers to residents earning under 4000 RMB (USD 558) per month, approximately 1.5 times of Shanghai’s minimum monthly salary [39]. Car availability is quantified as proportion of residents not owning private automobiles. These sociodemographic variables are selected to capture potential disparities in usage of online fresh food delivery services associated with sex, age, income and mobility.

Physical food environment and location indicators

We calculated the number of brick-and-mortar fast food outlets, supermarkets, grocery stores, and fruit stores within a 1 km street network distance of each grid centroid, a commonly used distance threshold that represent food retailers reachable within a 15-minute walk [40, 41]. Because outlets selling quick, prepared meals in Chinese cities often provide dine-in options [42], we identified fast food outlets based on their provision of quick-service meals, including both chain and independent outlets offering Western (e.g., burger, pizza) and Chinese cuisines (e.g., braised chicken rice). Grocery stores encompassed neighborhood grocery stores and vegetable markets. Location was represented by three zones, including central city, inner suburbs, and outer suburbs (See Additional file 1). Built environment density was proxied by total road length within a 1 km street network buffer from the grid centroid.

Statistical analysis

Concentration curve and index

We first assessed overall inequality in use with Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient. We calculated order frequency per resident for each grid cell and applied population weights, so the curves and statistics reflect inequality among residents, rather than across unevenly populated areas. A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of orders against the cumulative share of residents, ranked from the lowest to the highest order frequency per resident. The farther the curve falls below the diagonal equality line, the more concentrated the orders are among a smaller share of residents.

To examine disparities by socioeconomic attributes, we then used concentration curves and concentration index (CI) [43]. Grids were ranked by a socioeconomic variable (e.g., the proportion of low-income residents), placing more disadvantaged populations first. The concentration curve plots the cumulative share of orders against the cumulative share of the population, with population weighting. Curves below the 45° equality line indicate concentration among the advantaged, while coincidence with the equality line indicates no socioeconomic inequality. The CI defined as one minus twice the area under the concentration curve equals zero under equality and is positive when use of online delivery is pro-advantaged. Using evenly spaced quantiles and adjusting for multiple comparisons, we administered dominance tests to determine whether the equality line statistically significantly dominates a concentration curve or vice versa.

Spatial zero-inflated Poisson model

We specified a spatial zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) model to examine how population socioeconomic characteristics and neighborhood food environment variables are associated with online fresh food order counts. Conventional models such as the spatial error model or the zero-inflated generalized linear model cannot simultaneously account for spatial autocorrelation, zero inflation, and overdispersion. To address these issues, we employed a Bayesian spatial ZIP model with an observation-level random effect.

This model adopts an intrinsic conditional autoregressive (ICAR) structure within a Bayesian framework to capture area-level random effects [44]. Over half of the 11,581 grid cells in the analysis reported zero orders—5,531 for green grocery, 4,447 for supermarket, and 4,840 for fruit—indicating substantial zero inflation. The newly introduced ZIP likelihood in Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) allows up to ten fixed-effect covariates in the zero-inflation component [45]. In our specification, we included sociodemographic and locational covariates (e.g., percentage of older residents) in this component. An observation-level random intercept was added to absorb extra-Poisson noise (overdispersion) in the counts [46].

The logarithm of the total number of residents in each grid was included as an offset term, with its coefficient fixed at one, so that order counts were modeled relative to population size and model coefficients represented associations with ordering rates [47]. The spatial ZIP model was implemented using the INLA package in R [48]. As a sensitivity analysis, we fitted zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regressions, which accommodate structural zeros arising from a binary process alongside sampling zeros from the count process.

Results

Inequality patterns of online fresh food delivery use

Spatial distributions of online fresh food orders

High values cluster in the central city and in suburban centers, with consistent hotspots in central-western Changning, Lujiazui (Pudong New Area), the highly developed areas around the Hongqiao transportation hub, and the Shanghai Jiao Tong University area in southern Minhang. Low-count grids are typically sparsely populated and less accessible by transport. Examples include industrial zones opposite Gongqing Forest Park and the manufacturing area in Minhang. Grids with no orders are largely peri-urban agricultural areas, especially along the southern fringe near the express ring road. Compared with supermarkets and fruit stores, green grocery hotspots appear less contiguous and more closely aligned with the residential distribution (Fig. 2).

Each curve depicts the cumulative share of online orders (y-axis) against the cumulative share of usual residents (x-axis). Regarding the proportion of older adults, the concentration curves lie above the equality line for green grocery (CI=-0.074, p < 0.05) and fruit (CI=-0.108, p < 0.05) orders (Fig. 4A). The supermarket curve shows a notably larger pro-old pattern (CI=-0.226, p < 0.05), indicating a higher share of orders from grids with higher proportions of older adults. In contrast, when ranked by the proportion of young adults, supermarket orders show a pro-advantaged distribution of supermarket orders, with the curve falling well below the equality line (CI = 0.160, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

When ranking by the proportion of low-income residents, the concentration curves for green grocery, supermarket and fruit orders consistently lie below the equality line (CI = 0.176, 0.222, and 0.218, respectively; all p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). This indicates that online fresh food orders were disproportionately concentrated in grids with lower shares of low-income residents, and therefore underrepresented among economically disadvantaged residents. For illustration, the first quintile (20%) of residents in low-income grids (i.e., the poorest quintile) accounts for 10.4% green grocery, 6.2% supermarket, and 7.6% fruit orders online.

Patterns differ across order categories with respect to car availability. The green grocery curve is close to the equality line (CI = 0.015), whereas supermarket (CI=-0.039, p < 0.05) and fruit (CI=-0.131, p < 0.05) orders are concentrated in grids with higher shares of carless residents, as shown by curves above the equality line (Fig. 4D).

Associations between sociodemographic characteristics and online fresh food orders

Bayesian spatial ZIP models show significant associations between sociodemographic composition and online fresh food orders (Table 1). Because the count models included the number of residents as an offset, these estimates reflect differences in orders relative to the residential population. Using online grocery orders as an example, higher percentages of female residents, older adults, young adults, and low-income residents were each associated with fewer orders. A ten-percent increase in the female share was associated with 40.0% (95% CrI: 37.0%, 42.9%) lower grocery order rate. Ten-percent increases in the shares of older adults and young adults corresponded to 25.0% (95% CrI:12.5%, 35.7%) and 18.3% (95% CrI: 14.9%, 21.6%) lower grocery order rates, respectively. With ten-percent increase in the share of low-income residents, online grocery order rate was expected to decrease by 24.6% (95% CrI: 22.0%, 27.1%).

Findings for supermarket and fruit orders were broadly consistent with those for grocery orders. Order rates generally declined as the proportions of female, older adult, young adult, and low-income residents increased. The main exceptions were the null association between the proportion of older adults and supermarket order rate and the null association between the proportion of young adults and fruit order rate.

In contrast, the proportion of carless residents was positively associated with online order rates. For grocery orders, a ten-percent increase in the carless population share was associated with a 15.4% (95% CrI: 9.7%, 21.5%) increase in the expected order rate. Similar relationships were observed for supermarket and fruit orders, corresponding to increases in order rates of 14.0% (95% CrI: 9.3%, 18.9%) and 20.5% (95% CrI: 15.1%, 26.2%), respectively.

In the zero-inflation component, areas with larger shares of carless residents tended to have lower odds of structural zeros, suggesting higher odds that any orders occurred. However, this association was only marginally significant. For example, the odds ratio for grocery orders was 0.956 (95% CrI: 0.902, 1.013).

Associations between neighborhood food environment and online fresh food orders

Higher counts of nearby supermarkets were significantly associated with lower grocery and fruit order rates, with IRRs of 0.986 (95% CrI: 0.977, 0.995) and 0.982 (95% CrI: 0.972, 0.991), respectively. To put it in context, one additional physical supermarket within 1 km was associated with estimated decreases of 1.4% (95% CrI: 0.5%, 2.3%) in grocery order rate and 1.8% (95% CrI: 0.9%, 2.8%) in fruit order rate. Likewise, greater numbers of nearby grocery stores were consistently associated with lower order rates, including grocery orders (IRR = 0.993, 95% CrI: 0.988, 0.997), supermarket orders (IRR = 0.992, 95% CrI: 0.987, 0.997), and fruit orders (IRR = 0.988, 95% CrI: 0.983, 0.993). These results indicate that areas with more physically accessible grocery retailers had reduced fresh food order rates on average. Additionally, the count of fruit stores was not associated with order rates.

In the zero-inflation component, a higher number of nearby physical stores was possibly associated with a lower probability of observing zero online orders. For example, one additional supermarket within 1 km was associated with an estimated 34.2% reduction in the odds of no online supermarket orders, although the credible interval included zero (95% CrI: -3.8%, 58.6%). Similarly, the odds ratios for having no orders were below one for grocery (0.704, 95% CrI: 0.437, 1.107) and fruit (0.725, 95% CrI: 0.455, 1.141) deliveries, suggesting a tendency for areas with more corresponding physical retailers to have any online fresh food ordering activity.

As a sensitivity analysis, non-spatial ZINB regressions yielded broadly similar findings to the spatial ZIP models, although effect sizes and statistical significance varied (See Additional File 2).

Discussion

In our study, both the concentration curve analyses and the spatial models reveal generally pro-advantage distributions, indicating that online fresh food use is concentrated in neighborhoods with larger shares of middle-aged, higher-income, and car-free residents. We also find that greater densities of nearby fresh food retailers temper online ordering frequency while reducing the prevalence of no-order areas. We next compare these findings with prior literature and discuss their implications for urban policy.

Comparison with existing literature and explanations

Our findings show that higher shares of both older and young adults are negatively associated with usage of online fresh food delivery services. Previous studies have reported that the odds of online food delivery use decline with age, reflecting the age gradient of takeaway and restaurant delivery orders [19]. Similar to this observation, older adults are underrepresented among online grocery users [20, 49, 50]. Our finding adds to the evidence base that, in addition to lower uptake among older populations, areas with higher proportion of older adults had relatively fewer fresh food delivery orders. Possible explanations include perceived difficulty in use mobile applications and concerns about produce quality, along with a preference to see and touch perishable items [49, 51]. Aligned with this interpretation, prior work has suggested that consumers with a strong need for touch may discourage online purchases of perishables [52].

Distinct from the higher engagement with online food purchasing broadly, particularly restaurants and convenience outlets [14, 19], our lower per capita ordering among young adults likely reflects the intersection of food type and life-course context. Our outcome captures fresh grocery and fruit delivery rather than prepared food or broad food delivery that masks differences between outlet types. Young adults, despite being early adopters of delivery services [14, 53], are composed of full-time college students and early employees who often consume prepared foods (e.g., from canteens and takeaway outlets) [54, 55]. This can yield fewer fresh grocery orders despite high restaurant/convenience delivery among the same age range.

Middle-aged adults, by contrast, may be the core users of online grocery delivery services to fulfill their needs at this life stage. Millennials parents are reported to be more time-constrained than earlier cohorts, in part because dual-earner households are more common [14]. When juggling about long work, commuting, caregiving, and other household responsibilities, middle-aged adults may find options that save time (i.e., online food delivery) more appealing to them. Since higher family meal frequency is associated with higher-quality dietary intake [56], parents may have elevated motivation to provide shared family meals (from raw ingredients) for children’s dietary health. This interpretation aligns with evidence that living with school-aged children increases the odds of using online takeaway outlets [19] and grocery retailers [14]. Together with previous research [14, 20], our findings demonstrate a “convex” age pattern curve with middle-aged yielding more fresh food orders and declined use by young and older adults.

Turning to economic differences, our analysis indicates that neighborhoods with a higher share of low-income residents have fewer online fresh food delivery orders. A ten-percentage-point rise in low-income share is associated with a 24.6% drop in green grocery orders. This finding accords with evidence from the UK and US that higher-income households are more likely to use online grocery delivery [20, 21] and with studies linking income adequacy to greater online purchasing across multiple retailer types [14]. Our finding extends this literature beyond adoption to disparities in use frequency. Such disparities may be explained by delivery fees, service charges, and minimum spend requirements that disproportionally deter budget-constrained shoppers [49]. Previous research also documented concerns about higher online prices for fresh foods [57, 58] and limits on using food subsidies such as SNAP benefits through online channels [15].

Car availability also appears to influence online fresh food delivery patterns. Our model indicates that areas with a higher share of car-free residents were more likely to exhibit higher order frequency. Prior study reported that carless households are more inclined to shop grocery online [24]. Car-free households may find delivery especially convenient and time-saving for bulky, perishable food and may come to rely on it for regular purchases. Meanwhile, our positive association with more order volume contrasts with Dias et al. [23], who reported lower online grocery engagement among zero-car households a US sample. Differences in demographics, geographic contexts, and outcome quantifications likely explain this discrepancy (we model number of orders, whereas Dias et al. [23] used number of days with ≥ 1 delivery).

Finally, our analysis shows a dampening effect of nearby brick-and-mortar grocers on online fresh food ordering. A greater number of supermarkets and grocery stores within 1 km was associated with fewer online grocery, supermarket, and fruit orders, holding other factors constant. Fast food outlets and fruit stores exhibited no detectable association with order frequency except for a positive relationship between fast food outlets and online supermarket orders. Prior evidence on this relationship has been limited. One US study reported that higher physical accessibility to grocery stores decreases respondents’ intentions to continue using online grocery delivery [22], which our results corroborate at the population level. Building on studies on takeaway and restaurant deliveries [25, 26, 59], our study demonstrates how the physical food environment is linked to fresh food delivery use, contributing category-specific evidence on fresh foods that distinguishes green grocery, supermarket and fruit orders.

Policy implications

The observed socioeconomic inequalities highlight the need for policies that expand equitable access to online fresh food delivery. The U.S. SNAP Online Purchasing Pilot (OPP) demonstrated that lifting payment barriers can substantially increase uptake with online benefit redemption increased 67-fold during Covid-19 [60]. For Shanghai, authorities could make consumption vouchers redeemable for online purchases from local vegetable markets and partner with delivery platforms to subsidize (or waive) delivery fees for economically disadvantaged households. In the meantime, because many low-income residents will continue to rely on in-person shopping, maintaining and revitalizing neighborhood vegetable markets is essential to prevent widening dietary disparities.

Even in a transit-rich city like Shanghai, delivery services appear to provide a viable alternative channel for car-free residents to acquire fresh produce. Given evidence of substitution between online and in-person grocery shopping [23, 61, 62], elevated order frequency in car-less areas implies that a substantial share of fresh food demand can be met online, potentially reducing store visits and mitigating transport-related barriers (e.g., carrying heavy items). This shift may enhance equitable access to healthy, perishable foods while also reducing food-related vehicle miles travelled. From a policy and practice perspective, delivery platforms could prioritize service coverage in carless clusters by integrating neighborhood produce markets into their platforms and piloting reduced order minimums and fee-waiver promotions targeted to these areas.

We find more online fresh food orders in neighborhoods that had fewer brick-and-mortar supermarkets and grocery stores. For example, in the inner suburbs, the predicted count of online grocery orders decreases by 7.9% as the number of nearby grocery stores rises from the first to the fourth quintile (results not shown). This suggests that online delivery could function as a substitute in places with poorer physical access, helping residents acquire fresh foods despite limited in-person options. This evidence is associative rather than causal, yet it points to the potential of online services to mitigate shortfalls in the local retail environment.

In tandem with the negative association with order frequency, the negative association between nearby physical food stores and the probability of zero orders suggests a dual-mechanism hypothesis. On the one hand, local physical access appears to help neighborhoods cross the adoption threshold, reducing the likelihood of no use. On the other hand, it also substitutes away from higher online order counts when walkable shopping options are available. Within the Online-to-Offline (O2O) ecosystem, brick-and-mortar fresh food retailers function as fulfillment nodes [63], so a richer local supply base reduces the probability of being zero-order areas by ensuring service presence. Second, at short, walkable distances, these retailers provide convenient in-person alternatives, thereby curbing frequent online purchasing. A recent study indicates that each additional commercial establishment within a 0.5-mile radius increased the likelihood of walking by 23% for groceries and 17% for prepared meals [64].

The coexistence of supportive and competitive relationships argues against designing 15/20-minute neighborhoods that rely solely on online delivery. Instead, an integrated system linking physical retailers and delivery services is needed. Platform data and logistics can extend the reach of neighborhood produce markets [65], thereby expanding residents’ food choices. This improves access in areas with limited walkable options and promotes in-person shopping where local stores are plentiful.

Limitations and future work

Using large-scale delivery records aggregated to urban grids, our study provides, for the first time, a citywide spatial analysis of socioeconomic patterns in the use of online fresh food delivery and its associations with the physical food environment. This study showcases methodological strength in both modeling and equality-focused analyses. The Bayesian spatial ZIP regression models applied in this study effectively address spatial autocorrelation, zero-inflation, and overdispersion embedded within the grid-level order counts data. The use of concentration curves and index offers a richer analysis of sociodemographic inequalities than Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients alone.

This study is not without limitations. Grid-level analyses may obscure within neighborhood heterogeneity and introduce the risk of ecological fallacy. Future research at the individual level could link online ordering records with household surveys to account for shopping preferences, time constraints, and intra-household dynamics when examining inequality patterns. Although delivery platform users are disproportionately young compared with the general population [66], the concentration curve results do not show higher ordering in neighborhoods with larger shares of young adults. This suggests that the observed inequalities are not simply a reflection of age-based platform selection. Nevertheless, because the age, income, and car ownership profiles of platform users may differ from those of the general population, the inequality results should be interpreted as evidence of platform-mediated ordering behavior rather than fresh food purchasing among the total population. In accordance with data use regulations, we analyzed weekly order counts aggregated to grid cells. The short observation window and the lack of order data from other food delivery platforms may limit representativeness. Future research could improve this assessment by incorporating multi-platform order data for a longer period of time. To provide a more comprehensive understanding of online fresh food delivery use, more studies are needed to test whether the patterns observed here generalize to other platforms and urban regions in China and internationally.

Conclusions

Using citywide, fine-grained data on online fresh food delivery orders in Shanghai, this cross-sectional study shows that the use of online fresh food delivery was socioeconomically patterned and closely related to the neighborhood food environment. Online orders for green grocery, supermarket, and fruit categories were disproportionately concentrated in more socioeconomically advantaged neighborhoods. Neighborhoods with higher shares of older adults, young adults, and low-income residents tended to have lower online ordering rates, whereas areas with a higher proportion of car-free residents had higher ordering rates. Greater densities of brick-and-mortar supermarkets and grocery stores were associated with lower ordering rates, but also with a reduced probability of having no online orders. Together, these findings suggest that online delivery may partially compensate for mobility constraints and limited nearby physical food retail options, particularly among areas with more car-free residents. At the same time, the associations with local food retailers indicate that digital food consumption remains shaped by the physical food environment. Policymakers should consider how the digital transformation of food retail can be leveraged to improve equitable access to fresh foods, especially in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities underserved by physical food retailers.
