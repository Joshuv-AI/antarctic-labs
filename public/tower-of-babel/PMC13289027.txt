Impact of ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) pollution on disease burden in BRICS from 1990 to 2023: evidence from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023
Authors: Shangkun Si, Shizhe Deng, Yueyang Zhang, Xingxin Wang, Gonglei Yue, Yiyang Sun, Hai Lu, Junyang Li, Feng Tao, Mengxuan Lin, Zhexu Dong, Bifang Zhuo, Li Li, Bo Pang, Xinyao Jin, Zhihong Meng, Jiangwei Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmbient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) pollution (APMP) is a leading global mortality risk factor. Its long-term trends, drivers and future burden trajectories in emerging economies remain inadequately understood. This study provides the first systematic assessment of spatiotemporal patterns, key drivers and projections to 2050 of APMP-attributable health loss in BRICS countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa) from 1990 to 2023, benchmarked against global and Group of Seven (G7) trends.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease 2023 data, this study analysed APMP-attributable deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) via absolute numbers and age-standardised rates. The multidimensional framework included joinpoint regression, age-period-cohort (APC) modelling, Das Gupta decomposition, disease spectrum ranking and Bayesian-APC projection.ResultsIn 2023, APMP caused 2.955 million deaths in BRICS (59% of the global total). Age-standardised mortality and DALY rates remained higher than global averages and increased since 1990, contrasting with sustained declines in the G7. Substantial heterogeneity existed: India’s burden was heaviest and growing, China’s remained high but stabilised, while Brazil and Russia achieved marked reductions. APC modelling revealed pronounced mortality increases in older ages (especially 95+ years in China and India). Decomposition identified population ageing as the key driver in BRICS, especially China; population growth and deteriorating epidemiological conditions under high exposure drove increases in India and South Africa. Leading APMP-attributable burdens in BRICS were ischaemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and intracerebral haemorrhage, unlike in the G7 where Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias ranked first. Projections indicate the BRICS burden will remain above the global average by 2050, with China and India continuing to bear severe burdens.ConclusionBRICS is the epicentre of global APMP-attributable burden. The widening gap with the G7 underscores divergent environmental health governance paradigms. Findings call for differentiated strategies within BRICS and enhanced transnational collaboration to curb APMP burden rises and bridge global environmental health equity gaps.Keywords: Global Health, Environmental health, Epidemiology, Public Health, Health policies and all other topics

Introduction

Ambient air pollution has emerged as the second leading mortality risk factor worldwide (surpassed only by hypertension). The unavoidable nature of exposure, broad scope of impact and complex pathogenic mechanisms pose systemic threats and challenges to public health that cannot be ignored in both developing and developed countries.1 Among air pollutants, particulate matter pollution (PM), particularly fine particles with an aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5), imposes significantly greater health risks than gaseous pollutants such as ozone and nitrogen dioxide.2 Over 90% of the global population is exposed to environments exceeding the WHO recommended guideline for PM2.5 concentrations (10 µg/m³).3 Based on source differences, PM2.5 can be classified into ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) pollution (APMP) and household air pollution (HAP).4 APMP sources comprise human activities such as industrial emissions, vehicular exhaust and construction, as well as natural sources like soil dust and sea salt, along with biogenic materials including pollen and fungal spores.5 Following respiratory deposition, APMP can translocate across the alveolar-capillary barrier, triggering pathological responses including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction.6 7 It is closely linked to various non-communicable diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes and cancer.8

While the global disease burden attributable to PM2.5 and its HAP subtype shows a declining trend, the APMP burden is rising alarmingly. According to the State of Global Air 2024 report, of the 8.1 million global deaths attributable to air pollution in 2021, APMP accounted for 58%, followed by HAP at 38%. Concurrently, APMP rose from 12th to sixth leading level 4 risk factor for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) between 1990 and 2023, highlighting the growing prominence of this key PM subtype as a public health challenge.4

Globalisation and industrialisation have accelerated the spread of APMP, a trend that is particularly pronounced in developing countries.9 As representative emerging economies, Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa (BRICS) are undergoing multifaceted transitions marked by rapid population growth, economic expansion and accelerated urbanisation. However, these countries face increasingly severe APMP governance challenges owing to lagging regulatory standards, weak enforcement mechanisms and insufficient environmental monitoring capacity.10 Furthermore, significant disparities in exposure levels and population susceptibility within the BRICS exacerbate the inequitable distribution of the associated health burdens.

Current research on APMP disease burden predominantly focuses on global or super-regional scales, leaving a notable gap in systematic assessments targeting emerging economies such as BRICS. Existing studies often suffer from multiple limitations: they emphasise overall trends while neglecting the decomposition of driving mechanisms, lack an analysis of age-period-cohort effects and fail to adopt a long-term predictive perspective. By leveraging data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 Study, the current study presents the first comprehensive, multidimensional integration: quantifying the spatiotemporal evolution of APMP-attributable deaths and DALYs in BRICS from 1990 to 2023; integrating multi-perspective attribution through age stratification, sex differences, demographic transition, period-cohort effects and disease spectrum analysis; constructing a predictive framework to model burden trajectories over the next 30 years; and introducing a reference system for cross-development model comparison, benchmarking the findings against both global average and trends in G7 (the UK, France, the USA, Germany, Italy, Japan and Canada). This research not only fills the critical gap in granular environmental health assessment for the BRICS bloc but also establishes an evidence base to foster cross-regional environmental governance collaboration, optimise healthcare resource allocation and inform targeted public health interventions. Ultimately, it aims to reduce the health loss and socioeconomic costs attributable to the APMP and advance the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals.

Methodology

Overview

Based on GBD 2023 data, this study conducted a multidimensional quantitative assessment of the disease burden attributable to APMP in BRICS from 1990 to 2023. The primary metrics employed were absolute counts and age-standardised rates (per 100 000 population) along with their uncertainty intervals for deaths and DALYs. Furthermore, age as an explanatory variable (from <5 to 95+ years, in 5-year intervals) was used in specific analytical frameworks (such as the age-period-cohort modelling). Relevant analyses were also stratified by sex (male/female).11 A comprehensive analytical framework was constructed by integrating the following approaches: joinpoint regression to identify temporal trend inflection points, an APC model to dissect driving mechanisms, Das Gupta decomposition analysis to quantify the contribution of key factors, a heatmap analysis to visualise shifts in APMP-related diseases and predictive modelling of future trends. Burden estimates for BRICS were systematically compared with global and G7 nation benchmarks to elucidate the differences.

Data sources

Data were sourced from the publicly accessible GBD 2023 database, curated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, University of Washington, and retrieved from the Global Health Data Exchange platform (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/).12 The database provides geographically comprehensive coverage of 204 countries and territories, 21 regions and seven super regions. It encompasses health metrics for 375 diseases/injuries, 288 causes of death and 88 risk factors including mortality, incidence, prevalence, years of life lost and years lived with disability, stratified by sex, age and sociodemographic indices. Data integration employs a multi-source validation framework: foundational data include censuses, civil registration systems, household surveys, etc; health system data are derived from disease registries and health service utilisation records; environmental exposure data originate from ground monitoring stations and satellite remote sensing; and supplementary data include disease surveillance reports and systematic literature reviews. The methodology of the GBD study has been detailed in relevant literature.4

Definitions

APMP was selected as the primary risk factor. APMP is defined as the population-weighted annual average mass concentration of fine particulate matter (particles with an aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 μm) per cubic metre (µg/m³) in the ambient air.4 Throughout this manuscript, APMP is synonymous with ambient PM2.5. The GBD study used the Data Integration Model for Air Quality, which integrates diverse environmental data sources, including satellite observations of atmospheric aerosols, simulations from chemical transport models, ground-based measurements, land-use information and population estimates. This integration generates high-resolution (0.1°×0.1°) gridded estimates of PM2.5 concentrations, ensuring high precision and extensive coverage of the data.13

The core outcome metrics were deaths and DALYs. Deaths represent the absolute number of fatalities occurring within a specified timeframe and directly reflect the immediate impact on population health. DALYs provide a composite measure of health loss, combining the burden of premature mortality and non-fatal health outcomes, including disability. One DALY is equivalent to the loss of 1 year of full health.14

Metrics were quantified as absolute numbers and rates. Absolute numbers represented GBD raw estimates, whereas rates were expressed per 100 000 population. To eliminate the influence of varying age structures, the rates were calculated as age-standardised rates. This method applies a weighted average based on the official GBD world standard population age distribution to enhance comparability across different spatiotemporal scales. All original estimates reported by the GBD were accompanied by 95% UIs. These UIs were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 draws by aggregating and weighting the procedure from the posterior distribution of each estimate, comprehensively integrating heterogeneity from multiple sources, including parameter estimation, data quality, temporal variability and model uncertainty.15

To enable comparisons at the aggregate level (global, BRICS and G7), the data for individual BRICS and G7 member countries were summed (for absolute counts) or combined using population-weighted averages (for rates).

Estimated annual percentage change (EAPC)

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) is a statistical metric commonly used in epidemiological and public health research to quantify the average annual change in health indicators (such as incidence or mortality rates) over a specified period. EAPC helps researchers understand the direction and speed of changes in disease trends, making it an important tool for analysing time series data. We calculated the EAPCs in the age-standardised rates and amount of APMP. These EAPCs described trends within a specified time interval and were calculated with the following regression model: Y = α + βX+e, where Y represents ln(age-standardised rate or number), X is the calendar year and e is the error term. Thus, β represents the positive or negative age-standardised rate trend. The natural logarithm of an age-standardised rate or number is assumed to be linear over time; thus, EAPC=100 × [exp(β) –1]. The 95% CI of an EAPC was obtained from the linear model. If the lower limit of the 95% CI for EAPC was positive, its corresponding age-standardised rate or number had an increasing trend; conversely, if the upper limit of the 95% CI for EAPC was negative, its corresponding age-standardised rate or number had a decreasing trend. The trend is considered stable if the 95% CI crosses zero. To assess the impact of the inherent uncertainty in the GBD point estimates on overarching temporal trend findings, we conducted a Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis (K=5000). For complete EAPC Monte Carlo simulation data, see Supplementary Appendix.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Trend analysis was performed using Joinpoint software (V. 5.1). This method segments long-term temporal trends into contiguous linear segments and identifies statistically significant inflection points (joint points). Operating under a Poisson distribution assumption, the software uses techniques including Bonferroni correction, adjustment for multiple comparisons, permutation tests and grid search algorithms to determine the optimal number and location of joinpoints, which are validated using the Bayesian Information Criterion.16 For each identified segment, the software calculated the annual per cent change, representing the local trend within that segment, and its 95% CI. The average annual percent change (AAPC), summarising the overall trend across the entire study period by averaging the segment-specific annual percent changes (weighted by segment length), and its 95% CI were also computed. A decreasing trend for a segment (APC) or overall period (AAPC) was indicated when the lower limit of the 95% CI was below zero. Conversely, if the lower limit of the 95% was above zero, it showed an increasing trend.17

Age-period-cohort modelling

An age-period-cohort (APC) model was used to disentangle and quantify the independent effects of age, period and birth cohort on APMP-attributable mortality rates. Age effects reflect biological ageing processes and changes in social roles over an individual’s life course. Period effects capture external factors that change over time, such as variations in environmental exposure, implementations of public health policies and advancements in medical technologies. Cohort effects represent differences in risk across birth cohorts attributable to shared early life exposures and experiences. The model quantifies trends through parameters like net drift (overall annual percentage change), local drift (age-specific annual percentage change), longitudinal age curves (adjusted age-specific rates), period rate ratios (relative risk for each period compared with a reference period) and cohort rate ratios (relative risk for each birth cohort compared with a reference cohort). Data were organised into consecutive 5-year age groups and 5-year calendar periods, encompassing 20 age groups (<5 to 95+ years), 6 periods (1994–1999 to 2019–2023) and 25 overlapping birth cohorts (1899–1903 to 2019–2023). The period 2004–2008 was selected as the reference for period effects, and the 1959–1963 birth cohort was selected as the reference for cohort effects. Analysis was conducted using the National Cancer Institute online APC analysis tool (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/). This tool, based on a Poisson distribution and Wald χ² tests, resolves the inherent linear dependency between age, period and cohort using the intrinsic estimator algorithm.17

Decomposition analysis

The Das Gupta decomposition method was employed to mathematically decompose the changes in DALYs over time into the contributions of three core driving factors: changes in population age structure (a_i,y*), changes in total population size (p_y*) and changes in epidemiology (mortality rates, e_i,y). Specifically, the contribution of population age structure was calculated by comparing the proportional distribution of the population across age groups between years, holding age-specific rates constant. The contribution of the population size was assessed by the change in the total population, holding the age structure and rates constant. The contribution of epidemiological change is measured by the change in age-specific mortality rates, holding the population size and structure constant. This method isolates and quantifies the independent contribution of each factor by constructing counterfactual scenarios in which other factors are fixed at baseline levels, while sequentially varying the target factor.18

Assessment of cause-specific burden

We analysed the changes in the ranking of diseases contributing to APMP-attributable deaths and DALYs from 1990 to 2023. The GBD database hierarchically classifies diseases and injuries ranging from broad categories down to specific disease entities. Our analysis focused on the most granular, terminal disease entities within each hierarchical level of the classification system. For example, within the category of cardiovascular diseases under the broad group of non-communicable diseases, ischaemic heart disease (IHD) was included as a terminal cause, whereas stroke was further disaggregated into three pathological subtypes (ischaemic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage) for independent assessment.11 This granular approach provides crucial quantitative evidence for the identification of key disease targets for APMP-related prevention and control.

Projection

We projected the evolution of the APMP-attributable disease burden from 2022 to 2050 using a Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model within the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) framework.19 The BAPC model leverages historical GBD data and United Nations population projections (https://population.un.org). It assumes continuity in the effects of age, period and birth cohort across adjacent time points within the target population. Second-order random walk priors were applied to smooth the temporal trends in age-specific rates. The INLA framework provides a computationally efficient approximation of marginal posterior distributions. While MCMC methods offer superior accuracy by converging to the exact target distribution, INLA was selected as a pragmatic alternative to handle the large-scale computational demands of multi-country projections.20 This approach generates high-precision long-term projections of the APMP burden, providing quantitative evidence to inform prospective public health strategies.

Statistical software and code

All statistical analyses and data visualisations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data management, result processing and figure generation were conducted in the R for Windows (V. 4.2.1) environment using the relevant R packages. The complete R code and the list of package dependencies used for the analyses have been made publicly available. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

APMP-attributable deaths and DALYs burden

In 2023, APMP (PM2.5) caused a total of 2955.03[2285.37-3723.57] thousand deaths in BRICS, marking a significant 194% increase from 1004.87[565.72 to 1585.72] thousand deaths in 1990 (EAPC=3.42[3.27 to 3.56]). This accounted for 59% of the global death toll (4930.27[3880.74 to 6126.95] thousand), establishing BRICS as the core region for global APMP-attributable mortality growth. Concurrently, the ASMR in BRICS exhibited an upward trajectory, rising from 65.29[35.29 to 106.37] per 100 000 in 1990 to 79.96[62.26 to 101.19] per 100 000 in 2023, significantly exceeding the global rate of 56.15[44.3 to 69.9] per 100 000. In stark contrast, both the absolute number of deaths and ASMR in the G7 declined consistently, with the 2023 G7 ASMR reaching only 13.05[7.11 to 22.13] per 100 000—far below global and BRICS levels. Notable disparities existed among BRICS members. In 2023, India recorded the highest ASMR at 89.68[70.14 to 113.95] per 100 000 (EAPC=2.24[2.01 to 2.46]), followed by China at 82.90[65.14 to 103.28] per 100 000 (EAPC=0.8[0.44 to 1.18]). South Africa’s ASMR was 57.56[44.99 to 73.92] per 100 000 (EAPC=−0.06[-0.41 to 0.29]), representing the median level. Russia demonstrated a dramatic decline, falling from the highest BRICS ASMR of 154.43[82.68 to 224.46] per 100 000 in 1990 to 37.16[24.29 to 52.21] per 100 000 in 2023 (EAPC=−4.6[-5.06 to -4.14]). Brazil’s ASMR declined steadily to 30.73[21.01 to 42.85] per 100 000 (EAPC=−1.94[-2.06 to -1.83]), the lowest within BRICS though still higher than the G7 average.

Regarding DALYs, while the age-standardised DALY rate (ASDR) declined globally and in the G7, BRICS exhibited a contrasting upward trend. In 2023, the BRICS ASDR was 1826.52[1454.06 to 2260.85] per 100 000, higher than the global rate of 1446.02[1192.91 to 1729.57] per 100 000. The G7 ASDR was markedly lower at 306.67[183.98 to 459.05] per 100 000. India’s ASDR showed a significant increase (EAPC=1.09[0.85 to 1.33]), reaching 2262.68[1816.46 to 2779.26] per 100 000 in 2023—the highest among BRICS—followed by South Africa (1660.51[1319.1 to 2085.09] per 100 000). China’s ASDR stabilised (EAPC=−0.03[-0.31 to 0.25]). Brazil and Russia continued their declining trends (EAPC=−2.17[-2.28 to -2.05] and −4.67[-5.15 to -4.19], respectively), with Brazil recording the lowest 2023 ASDR at 780.32[582.07 to 997.05] per 100 000.

APMP burden trends: joinpoint analysis

Joinpoint regression identified significant trend inflection points. For BRICS overall, notable turning points in ASMR and ASDR occurred around 2017 and 2020: a period of slow increase from 1990 to 2016 was followed by a rapid decline during 2017–2020 and a subsequent rebound post-2020. However, across the entire study period (1990–2023), BRICS exhibited an overall increasing trend (AAPCASMR=0.64[0.43 to 0.85]; AAPCASDR=0.07[-0.12 to 0.26]). Globally, a minor uptick was observed from 2020 to 2023 (APCASMR=3.0[2.10 to 3.91]; APCASDR=2.53[1.79 to 3.27]), but the overall trend remained downward (AAPCASMR=−0.72[-0.90 to -0.54]; AAPCASDR=−0.80[-0.96 to -0.64]). The G7 demonstrated a consistent and significant decline (AAPCASMR=−4.23[-4.58 to -3.88]; AAPCASDR=−3.77[-4.18 to -3.36]).

Among BRICS members, China and India experienced long-term growth in ASMR and ASDR. China showed an inflection point around 2014, with a sharp decline after 2017 (APCASMR=−9.54[-11.06 to -8.00]; APCASDR=−8.86[-10.63 to -7.05]) and a rebound in 2020. India saw a brief, slight dip during 2017–2020 before resuming an upward trajectory. South Africa’s ASMR generally increased until 2009 (with a brief dip from 1998 to 2004), then declined after 2010. Brazil’s trend mirrored the G7 with a steady decline. Russia’s rates peaked during 1990–1994 and subsequently declined persistently. Notably, all regions except Russia exhibited a rebound trend after 2020.

Sex-specific burden differences

From 1990 to 2023, the absolute number of deaths and DALYs was generally higher among males than females. Globally, and in BRICS overall, Brazil, India and China, male deaths exceeded female deaths. Conversely, in the G7 and Russia, female deaths were higher than male deaths throughout the period. Notably, Brazil (2022–2023) and South Africa (post-2009, except 2021) showed instances where female deaths surpassed male deaths. Regarding DALYs, male DALYs were generally higher than female DALYs in the G7 and Russia (with parity in the G7 post-2015) and in South Africa throughout the 34-year period. Across the study period, male ASMR and ASDR were universally higher than female rates.

APC analysis of mortality

The APC model indicated negative net drift rates globally, in the G7, for BRICS overall, and for all BRICS members except India, suggesting an overall decline in mortality. Within BRICS, local drift values were negative for the 0–29 age groups (with the steepest decline in the <5 age group) but turned positive for most groups above age 30. The fastest mortality increases were observed among males aged 95+ and females aged 90–94. Brazil and Russia showed negative local drift across all age groups, indicating declining mortality in every stratum. The steepest declines were in the <5 age group for females in both countries and the 15–19 age group for Russian males. China and India had net drift rates of −0.53[-0.84 to -0.22] and 1.91[1.74 to 2.09], respectively. Both countries shared a pattern of increasing local drift with age: the lowest (negative) drift was in the <5 age group (fastest decline), while the peak (positive) drift was in the 95+ age group (fastest increase). Key differences were that local drift was negative for Chinese males under 25 years and aged 60–79 years and for Chinese females under 70 years, whereas in India, it was negative for those under 20 years (under 25 years for males). South Africa exhibited divergent sex patterns: males had a negative net drift with negative local drift across all ages (steepest decline in the 25–29 age group), while females had a positive net drift, with positive local drift in the 15–19 and 30–89 age groups (peak increase in the 45–49 age group).

Regarding the age effect (longitudinal age curve), mortality exhibited a general upward trend globally, in the G7, BRICS overall, and individual members. Mortality was relatively high in children under 5 years, increased substantially after age 50 and surged dramatically after age 65. With the exception of Russian males (peak at 90–94 age group), mortality peaked in the 95+ age group for all other regions and sexes. The mortality rate in the 95+ age group was significantly higher in China and India than in other regions.

For period effects (using 2004–2008 as reference, RR=1), the mortality risk trend in BRICS overall was not pronounced, with the 2019–2023 period risk lower than the reference. Global, G7, Russian, and Brazilian trends showed declining period effects, with faster declines in the G7 and Russia. India exhibited a steady upward trend. China’s risk increased initially and then declined, peaking around the reference period. South Africa’s risk declined from 1994 to 1998 to 2004–2008, rose to a peak in 2009–2013 and then declined again, with the 2019–2023 risk below the reference.

For cohort effects (using the 1959–1963 birth cohort as reference, RR=1), the overall mortality risk in BRICS increased from the initial 1899–1903 cohort, experienced a slight dip around the 1929–1933 cohort, remained relatively stable thereafter (with little change relative to the reference), and began a sustained and marked decline from the 1994–1998 cohort, reaching the lowest point in the 2019–2023 cohort. Globally, and in the G7, Russia, Brazil and South Africa, a downward trend was observed from an overall period perspective. In particular, the G7 and Russia showed a sharp, steady decline, with the risk for the initial 1899–1903 cohort being orders of magnitude higher than that for the 2019–2023 cohort, following an exponential decrease, yet the declines globally and in Brazil were more linear. South African females experienced two minor rebounds in risk for the 1969–1989 and 1999–2004 cohorts, peaking in the 1979–1984 cohort (RR>1). China’s cohort risk showed an initial increase, with two peaks above the reference level (1934–1939 and 1979–1984 cohorts), followed by a decline (which lagged in males), resulting in the 2019–2023 cohort risk falling below the reference. India’s initial cohort risk was only one-eighth of the reference, rose steadily to a peak in the 1984–1989 cohort, and then declined, with the 2019–2023 cohort risk slightly below the reference.

Decomposition analysis of APMP trends

Decomposition analysis revealed that population ageing was the primary driver of DALY increases in BRICS, followed by population growth. Epidemiological changes contributed to DALY driven decreases in BRICS and globally. In the G7, epidemiological improvement was the main factor reducing DALYs, while population ageing was the key factor increasing them.

Patterns varied among BRICS members. Ageing and population growth all acted as positive drivers (increasing DALYs) in China, India and South Africa. Ageing was the dominant driver in China, whereas population change was primary in India and South Africa. In Brazil and Russia, epidemiological improvement served as a driver (reducing DALYs). Regarding sex differences, epidemiological changes represent an improvement factor for Chinese males (reducing DALYs), whereas the opposite holds true for females.

Cause-specific burden attributable to APMP

In 2023, the top three causes of global APMP-attributable ASMR were IHD, COPD and intracerebral haemorrhage (IH), with ischaemic stroke falling to fourth place. Within BRICS, the top three causes remained IHD, COPD and IH, constituting the major contributors to the burden. The G7 disease profile shifted, with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias (ADOD) rising to the top, followed by IHD and tracheal, bronchus and lung cancer. Among BRICS members in 2023, IHD and COPD ranked first and second in both China and India; IH was third in China, while ischaemic stroke was third in India. In Brazil and South Africa, IHD ranked first; ADOD and lower respiratory infections (LRI) were second and third in Brazil, while LRI and diabetes mellitus type 2 (T2DM) held these positions in South Africa. In Russia, the top three were IHD, ADOD and LRI.

Regarding ASDR in 2023, the top two causes in BRICS and globally mirrored the ASMR rankings (IHD and COPD). IH remained the third-ranked cause in BRICS, while LRI ranked third globally. In the G7, the top three causes were ADOD, IHD and T2DM. Among BRICS members, IHD was the leading cause everywhere except South Africa. In China, the second and third causes were IH and COPD, respectively, while in India they were COPD and LRI. In South Africa, LRI ranked first for ASDR, followed by T2DM and IHD. In Brazil, ADOD and T2DM were second and third, while in Russia, ischaemic stroke and ADOD held these positions.

Overall, IHD was the primary contributor to the APMP-attributable burden globally and within BRICS, while the G7 must remain vigilant regarding ADOD and South Africa needs to control the rising trend of LRI.

Projection of APMP-attributable deaths and DALYs to 2050

Projections integrating United Nations population data with the BAPC model indicate that APMP-attributable deaths and DALYs are projected to continue rising globally and in BRICS (except Russia), while declining in the G7 and Russia. Meanwhile, Brazil is projected to see declining mortality but rising DALYs. Among BRICS members in 2050, China is projected to bear the heaviest burden (ASMR=894.67[SD = 1955.86] per 100 000; ASDR=14 698.37[SD = 28,719.59] per 100 000), followed by India (ASMR=379.53[SD = 553.33] per 100 000; ASDR=9303.98[SD = 11,265.69] per 100 000). Russia is projected to have the lowest burden (ASMR=2.19[SD = 7.64] per 100 000; ASDR=72.31[SD = 220.35] per 100 000). Brazil and South Africa are projected to rank at the median level within BRICS. Notably, the projected ASMR and ASDR for BRICS in 2050 remain above global levels, while the G7 is projected to remain far below both.

Discussion

Global and regional disparities: the environmental health governance gap and divergent APMP burden trajectories

This study reveals persistent and widening regional disparities in the APMP (PM2.5) health burden. In 2023, BRICS accounted for 59% of global APMP-attributable deaths. The absolute figure of 2.955 million deaths, representing a 194% increase since 1990, coupled with ASMR and ASDR rates, which are exceeding and diverging upwards from the global average, solidifies BRICS’s status as the ‘epicentre’ of the global APMP burden. This situation stands in stark contrast at the opposite end of ‘a century-long spectrum’ from that of the G7, which demonstrated sustained and profound improvement, with its 2023 ASMR plummeting to an exceptionally low level of 13.05 per 100 000 and its ASDR (306.67 per 100 000) less than one-sixth of the BRICS rate, highlighting the efficacy of its environmental health governance.

This divergence is essentially a direct manifestation of differing environmental health governance paradigms and development priorities. The BRICS predicament is rooted in the structural contradictions inherent in its ‘growth-first’ logic: rapid industrialisation, urbanisation and fossil fuel dependence, compounded by lagging environmental standards and limited public health system capacity, collectively foster a vicious cycle of ‘development-pollution-health loss’ that escalates the APMP burden.10 21 Notably, Joinpoint analysis revealed critical inflection points in the BRICS burden trend around 2017 and 2020. This pattern suggests a possible lag of several years in the observable impact of stringent environmental policies, exemplified by China’s Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan. However, the subsequent widespread rebound trend serves as a warning that the foundation for structural emission reductions remains precarious.

In contrast, the G7 have adopted a ‘health-endogenous governance’ model that internalises public health within environmental-economic policy, relying on stringent regulations (eg, the US Clean Air Act, the EU PM2.5 limit of 25 µg/m³),22 23 market incentives (eg, the European Union Emissions Trading System), disruptive clean technologies (eg, advanced dust removal, denitrification/desulphurisation and particulate filtration)24 and a committed transition to cleaner energy,25 supported by real-time monitoring networks.26 This has decoupled growth from pollution.27 The G7 disease profile has now shifted to Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, signalling a new phase of long-term chronic effects. For BRICS, still grappling with cardiovascular diseases and COPD, the G7 offers a blueprint for resilient governance. It demonstrates that economic growth and environmental protection are not zero-sum but can be synergistic. BRICS must systematically adopt this ‘prevention-monitoring-intervention’ framework to mitigate the long-term threat posed by APMP.

Brazil: a bioeconomy beacon with lingering concerns and equity challenges

Brazil presents an alternative pathway for green development, leveraging its natural endowment and bioenergy policies. Its APMP-attributable burden has consistently declined to the BRICS low, approaching G7 levels and positioning as a BRICS benchmark for green development. This achievement stems from its ecological assets and science-based policies: the Amazon’s carbon sink,28 the Forest Code,29 the hydropower-dominated energy and the RenovaBio biofuel policy30 alongside ‘low-emission agriculture subsidies’,31 systematically reducing industrial and agricultural particulate emissions.32 Concurrently, Brazil’s Environmental Crimes Law imposes substantial fines for pollution.33 Decomposition analysis confirms that epidemiological improvement was the main factor, reflecting the role of its high-coverage Unified Health System in enhancing overall population health resilience.34 However, regional inequalities persist: PM2.5 levels in Amazon mining zones exceed limits threefold,35 and COPD prevalence is higher among Indigenous populations.36 The APC model shows abnormal risk in the 1899–1903 birth cohort (corresponding to the rubber boom era), reflecting historical exploitation.

Brazil’s governance faces severe tests of equity and sustainability. Traffic pollution from rapid urbanisation is eroding air quality.37 Rainforest protection faces regression risks with illegal development. Increased Amazon fires have led to a rebound in localised exposure risk.38 39 To consolidate gains, it should advance an inclusive green transition. It could adopt G7 smart city solutions (eg, bicycle prioritisation)40 and ecological compensation mechanisms41 while strengthening international supply chain oversight to curb illegal deforestation. Introducing an ‘Environmental Health Justice’ framework (eg, the US Environmental Justice Screening Tool)42 is essential to ensure equitable distribution of environmental benefits and health protection, especially for marginalised communities.

Russia: a post-industrial transformation paradigm and legacy challenges

Russia stands out as the most successful case of environmental health transition within BRICS. Its burden experienced a sharp decline, nearing G7 levels, with the scale and pace of reduction leading other BRICS members. Key drivers include post-Soviet industrial restructuring,43 National Air Quality Standards (PM2.5 limit: 25 µg/m³),44 the Environmental Protection Law,45 the Ecological Security Doctrine policy,46 coal-to-gas conversion,47 vast forest carbon sinks (20% of the global total) and a satellite-based monitoring network.48 The APC model confirms this transition, demonstrating significant intergenerational policy dividends. Nevertheless, legacy pollution in Ural and Siberia disproportionately affects female mortality. Additionally, the rising share of Alzheimer’s disease and diabetes signals emerging ageing-related non-communicable disease challenges, similar to the G7.49

Russia’s ‘success’ is thus marked by its transition economy characteristics and historical baggage. Future governance should shift from ‘emission reduction’ to ‘health protection’. It could draw on G7 models including transboundary governance (eg, the Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution monitoring network and Arctic cooperation frameworks)50 51 and establishing brownfield remediation funds (modelled on the US Superfund)52 for industrial wastelands legacies.

India: the health quandary of pollution crisis amidst demographic dividend

India epitomises the systemic governance deficits driving a sustained rise in APMP burden, whose burden ranks as the highest among BRICS in 2023. Its predicament is the concentrated manifestation of multiple challenges: the world’s fastest-growing population, heavily coal-dependent energy,53 agricultural burning,54 rapid motorisation,55 and lagging, poorly enforced regulatory standards,56 engulfing cities like Delhi in perennial ‘toxic haze’ (with PM2.5 concentrations consistently exceeding 300 µg/m³—10 times the WHO guideline).57 The joinpoint curve shows a brief dip during 2017–2020, indicating that current policies, exemplified by the National Clean Air Programme, lack sufficient potency. The APC model reveals extreme elderly vulnerability,58 and decomposition analysis confirms a vicious cycle of high exposure and weak health protection.59

A comprehensive governance approach is required to break the path dependency of ‘pollute first, clean up later’. India’s future strategies should draw from G7 policy toolbox: accelerate clean energy transition via fiscal incentives (eg, the US Inflation Reduction Act,60 UK clean stove programmes61); establish a pollution health cost accounting system (eg, France’s Air Santé surveillance)62 and pollution taxes (eg, the French carbon tax)63 64; strengthen interstate environmental enforcement (modelled on the US EPA)65; and bolster primary healthcare (eg, Japan’s community centres or the UK’s GP system)66 for vulnerable populations.

China: transition pains and the ageing tsunami amidst rapid development

As the ‘world’s factory’, China bears a severe APMP disease burden. Projections indicate it will climb to the top within BRICS by 2050—a paradox of marginal current improvement vs a persistent future warning.67 As a global manufacturing and energy consumption hub, China’s coal-dominant structure68 and heavy industry create composite exposure, with the North China Plain facing PM2.5 levels triple the WHO guideline.69 Stringent policies (eg, Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan70 and Blue Sky Defense War71,73) drove post-2015 improvements, but diminishing returns and post-2020 rebound suggest administration-led, end-of-pipe governance challenges (eg, pollution stock inherited from earlier extensive development models).74 The APC model shows rapid mortality decline in children under 5, but increasing risk above age 30, with peak cohort effects for those born in the 1980s–1990s—a ‘health debt’ from rapid industrialisation during the early reform era.75 Decomposition analysis further confirms that population ageing has replaced mere pollution exposure as the primary driver of burden increase.76

In the context of ‘ageing before becoming affluent’, China’s dual challenge is to address historical pollution health debt while protecting an ageing population. Drawing on G7 experience, future strategies should include regional coordination (eg, Japan’s regional joint prevention), energy transition under the ‘Dual Carbon’ goals,77 health impact assessment78 (eg, Canada’s Health Risk Assessment),79 and targeted interventions for vulnerable groups (eg, community air purification stations).

South Africa: health equity challenges in a resource-dependent context

South Africa has a moderate aggregate burden with high internal inequality. The APC model reveals a reversed sex-specific risk pattern: female mortality is rising (fastest at ages 45–49), likely due to women’s high exposure to fuel collection and cooking, synergising with high HIV prevalence.80 81 The disease profile shows that lower respiratory infections rank first in burden, reflecting a vicious cycle of ‘exposure-immune vulnerability-morbidity’. Cohort effects peaking for males born 1952–1956 (mining boom) and females born 1972–1976 (accelerated urbanisation) reflect the intergenerational transmission of historical trauma.

South Africa’s dilemma reflects the ‘resource curse’: coal dependence,82 83 energy poverty and historical inequalities (eg, apartheid) create compound vulnerability—a governance challenge beyond emission reduction.84 Targeted, integrated strategies similar to those the G7 employs for environmental justice issues are needed. Future strategies should adopt a health equity framework (eg, US Environmental Justice Screening Tool),85 establish an EU-style Just Transition Fund for coal-dependent communities,86 87 and create a high pollution zone health impact fund similar to the Green Climate Fund.88

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, our reliance on the GBD 2023 database introduces inherent estimation uncertainties. Historical estimates in transitioning economies are frequently affected by cause-of-death misclassification and under-registration. While the GBD employs rigorous harmonisation algorithms (eg, Garbage Code Redistribution), this post-hoc statistical modelling introduces uncertainty. Furthermore, because our secondary analytical models used these GBD point estimates as deterministic inputs, they inevitably inherit and propagate this foundational ‘level-one uncertainty’. While the substantial magnitude of the long-term macro-trends guarantees directional robustness, the absolute estimates and minor statistical fluctuations should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Second, the ‘epidemiological changes’ component in our decomposition analysis inherently functions as a composite metric, bundling variations in APMP exposure levels with background healthcare improvements. Consequently, we cannot mathematically isolate the precise proportional contributions of environmental emission reductions versus medical advancements. Future research using risk-deleted decomposition methods is warranted to disentangle these policy-specific drivers.

Third, our projections using the BAPC model are extrapolated based on historical epidemiological momentum and demographic transitions. While this provides a rigorous ‘business-as-usual’ baseline, it does not account for potential trajectory shifts driven by future socioeconomic policies or technological breakthroughs. Future studies should integrate Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) to evaluate varied mitigation scenarios.

Finally, while APMP constitutes the dominant air pollution threat in BRICS countries, this study did not include gaseous pollutants such as ozone and NO2. Future comprehensive assessments should integrate multiple pollutants to fully capture the holistic health burden of ambient air pollution in emerging economies.

Conclusion

BRICS has become the ‘epicentre’ of the global APMP (PM2.5) health burden. Its escalating trajectory and the G7’s steady improvement stand as the opposite poles of a ‘century-long spectrum’. This marked divergence concretises a clash of development paradigms: the G7, through a ‘health-endogenous’ governance system, has decoupled pollution from health burden via regulation, technology and energy transition. In contrast, BRICS remains trapped in a ‘growth-first’ logic, facing composite exposure from industrialisation, rapid urbanisation and fossil fuel dependence. Lagging environmental regulation and fragile public health systems jointly elevate preventable health loss.

Breaking this impasse requires differentiated yet synergistic pathways. China and India must move beyond end-of-pipe governance, driving the greening of energy structures and industrial upgrading, while integrating Health Impact Assessment into policymaking to address long-term health risks from ageing and cumulative historical exposure. Brazil and Russia should consolidate their green transition and structural emission reduction gains, shifting governance focus towards regional equity and legacy pollution remediation, serving as transition references for other emerging economies. South Africa needs to dismantle the health inequities entrenched by the ‘resource curse', implementing targeted interventions to protect energy-poor and high-disease-burden populations, achieving deep integration of environmental governance and social fairness.

At the global level, developed nations must fulfil commitments on climate finance and technology transfer to support BRICS’ clean energy transition. BRICS, in turn, should construct an Environmental Health Cooperation Alliance to foster data sharing, technical collaboration, and mutual learning of governance experiences. Only through scientific planning, political resolve and transnational cooperation can we curb the relentless rise of the APMP burden, bridge the global environmental health equity gap, advance towards the goal of a ‘Healthy Planet’, and share a sustainable, healthy future.
