1 Overview
This report is an addendum to a publication series that focuses on approximately 100
different local geographies, or “clusters”1 . This addendum expands the report series2
to include large multifamily building characteristics as well as energy and emission data
for each local geography. The intention of this addendum is to help policymakers at the
city, county, and state levels better understand building energy use and emissions in
large multifamily buildings. Specifically, this report breaks down the energy consumption
and emissions for the building stock in Indianapolis-St Louis-Springfield-Peoria counties
by building type, building size, end use, energy consumption, emissions, and building
segment.

The source of the data in this report is the public release of ResStock Dataset 2024.1
Documentation 2024. The data and estimated values communicated in this report
represent the housing stock in approximately 2018; they are not present-day val-
ues.

Electricity emission totals in the figures below use eGRID 2021 subregion emissions
factors, which are calculated from emissions associated with electricity generation in
the year 2021 in each of the 26 subregions covering the United States. Electricity emis-
sions factors (i.e., pounds of CO2 e per kWh of electricity) are decreasing year-over-year
as new low-emissions generation comes online, and projections show this trend con-
tinuing in the future (Cambium 2023 Scenario Descriptions and Documentation 2023).
Estimates of future electricity emissions reductions using historical electricity emissions
factors will likely overstate realized emission reductions as a result.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) provides residential building energy
data by state (Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) Survey (U.S. Energy
Information Administration (EIA)) 2020). Similar commercial building energy data are
provided by the EIA for the nine U.S. census divisions, each of which covers multiple
states (Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS) 2018). However,
city, county, and state policymakers often need finer resolution data to understand the
building stock and guide decision-making. This report presents large multifamily resi-
dential building energy data for the counties shown in Figure 2. The energy data in this
report are broken out by building size, end use, vintage, and segment (Figure 1).

1.3 What Building Stock Segments Are Covered?
This report covers large, multifamily residential buildings greater than 10,000 square
feet. The large multifamily building stock can be divided into six segments that have
similar potential approaches for retrofit solutions across jurisdictional boundaries. These
six segments are primarily based on building height, heating system type, and the pres-
ence of ductwork for heating or cooling systems.

Most habitable residential buildings have heat, air conditioning, lighting, appliance ser-
vices, and hot water, which use various fuel types (see Appendix B for fuel type defi-
nitions). Historically, many buildings have been categorized by their vintage, but end
use equipment, such as HVAC, lighting, water heating, plug loads, and appliances (see
Appendix B for a description and example of each end use) can have similar profiles
across many home types, and therefore similar retrofit opportunities. Therefore, it is
helpful to identify building similarities, or “segments”, that cross the lines of traditional
building types for energy consumption and emission analysis.

1.6 How Were the Geographic Clusters Developed?
Clusters in this report align with the Understanding Commercial Buildings in the U.S.,
n.d., and were formed as groups of counties, dependent on building type, age, and cli-
mate. Adjacent counties with similar commercial densities, types, and age distributions
form a cluster. Clusters form regional groups if they belong to the same 2006 ASHRAE
climate zone. See Horsey, Rozenfeld, and Bergmann 2023 for more detail on the clus-
tering method.

1.7 What Portions of the Housing Stock Are Covered?
This report focuses on large residential buildings over 10,000 square feet. Data on
energy use in the residential sector are typically collected at the housing unit level, so
we approximate the 10,000 square feet cut off by scaling up the unit size by the num-
ber of units and adding 25% of the building floor area assumed for common area space
(e.g., laundry rooms, hallways, lobbies). For example, a building with eight 1,000 square
foot dwelling units would qualify because 1,000 ft2 x 8 x 1.25 = 10,000 ft2 . We only in-
clude buildings with 5 or more housing units in the building. Estimates of energy and
emissions by total building size and segment are provided throughout the report to un-
derstand the energy and emissions impacts of focusing retrofits on different portions of
the housing stock. The large multifamily buildings >10,000 square feet considered in
this analysis represent 4% of the annual residential energy use in your area, while mul-
tifamily buildings <10,000 square feet use 2% of the building site energy consumption.
Additionally, these housing types emit 5% and 3%, respectively, of the total residential
sector emissions, using 2021 emissions factors (Figure 2). This report excludes build-
ings classified as “group quarters” such as prisons and university dormitories (RECS:
Housing Unit (EIA) 2024), residential care facilities such as nursing homes and assisted
living quarters (CBECS: Lodging 2024), as well as non-conventional housing such as
boats. In the rest of this analysis, only multifamily buildings over 10,000 square feet are
presented.

3 Results Summary
In this section, we provide a summary of energy use in large multifamily buildings and
potential retrofit solutions. In this report series, “end use” is defined as the equipment
that directly consumes energy in a building such as HVAC, lighting, water heating, ap-
pliances (e.g., cooking, refrigeration or clothes washing/drying), or other miscellaneous
loads (e.g., electronics, well pumps). The relative importance of each of these varies
by region, but HVAC is the largest consumer of residential energy in most areas of the
United States.

3.1 Site Energy and Emissions by End Use
Figure 4 summarizes the site energy and associated emissions by fuel type3 and end
use for large multifamily buildings in your area4 (see Appendix B for descriptions and
examples of fuel types and end uses). In this figure we see that 71% of your large mul-
tifamily building stock’s site energy consumption is Electricity, with 51% of the Electricity
consumption from HVAC, 14% from Water Heating, 9% from Lighting, 11% from Appli-
ances, and the remaining 16% from Miscellaneous Loads. In addition, 87% of your large
multifamily building stock’s emissions are from Electricity, with 51% of the Electricity
consumption from HVAC, 14% from Water Heating, 9% from Lighting, 11% from Appli-
ances, and the remaining 16% from Miscellaneous Loads. Of the emissions attributable
to on-site combustion, 65% come from HVAC, 29% from Water Heating, 4% from Appli-
ances, and 2% from Miscellaneous Loads.

In the residential sector, energy use and emissions depend on multiple factors. One
factor is the number of housing units, as most appliance counts are fixed per unit (e.g.,
typically one refrigerator per apartment), although usage scales with the number of oc-
cupants. Another factor is the total floor area as heating, cooling, and lighting scale with
home size and ultimately the size of the walls, windows, ceilings, floors, exposed to out-
door conditions.

In your area, multifamily buildings above 25,000 square feet comprise 21% of buildings
and 52% of the large multifamily housing units, but 52% of the floor area, 49% of large
multifamily site energy consumption, and 49% of large multifamily greenhouse gas emis-
sions.

Energy and emissions from HVAC can be reduced either by installing higher efficiency
heating and cooling equipment or by reducing the need for heating and cooling by im-
proving the insulation and air-sealing of the envelope. Heat pump technologies are es-
pecially promising as higher efficiency equipment because they move heat between
locations as opposed to producing it and are applicable for most residential applications
nationwide. The envelope can be air-sealed by reducing holes in the building where
air might leak between the indoors and outdoors. In buildings with older windows, win-
dow replacement with a newer model can serve dual purposes of improving the insulat-
ing properties of the window while also reducing air leakage around the window frame.
Strategies such as placing exterior continuous insulation are highly effective for reduc-
ing heat transfer between the interior and exterior, but this is not always an option given
the desired look of the building, distance to property lines, or historic preservation stan-
dards. Insulation can also be added inside existing walls (depending on the material) or
to the interior of the wall if a slight loss of interior floor area is acceptable.

5.4 Lighting

 For this report, lighting includes interior fixtures used within housing
 units. Exterior and common area lighting is excluded.

In your area, lighting accounts for 6% of the energy used and 7% of the emissions for
large multifamily buildings. This number is an underestimate of the contribution of light-
ing to emissions because the dataset underlying these energy and emissions numbers
does not include common spaces of multifamily buildings (e.g., lobbies, laundry rooms,
hallways). Common spaces could be up to 25% of the floor area of a building, and most
of these spaces would have lighting. Lighting fixtures in multifamily buildings are typi-
cally a mix of incandescent, LED, and Compact Fluorescent Lamp (CFL) bulbs. Incan-
descent bulbs use about 7 times as much energy as an LED bulb to produce an equiva-
lent amount of light, and CFLs use around twice as much energy as an LED to produce
the equivalent amount of light. LEDs make up only around half the installed bulbs in the
residential sector, so lighting emissions could be rapidly reduced by encouraging further
adoption of LEDs. Lighting energy and emissions by vintage are enumerated in Table 22
and Table 23 of Appendix C.

5.5 Miscellaneous Loads
 Miscellaneous loads include small things plugged into the wall (e.g.,
 cell phones, toasters, computers, alarm clocks) as well as less common
 energy uses that might not be present in every home (e.g., well pumps).

In your area, Miscellaneous Loads are 12% of site energy consumption and 14% of
emissions. Within Miscellaneous Loads, 96% are electric, primarily plug loads, and 4%
come from fossil fuel. Because miscellaneous loads are predominately small, distributed
items, they can be difficult to reduce. Potential solutions for miscellaneous loads in-
clude smart plugs, plug controls, and occupant feedback and awareness of the impact
of these plugged in devices on energy consumption and resulting emissions. Miscella-
neous Loads energy and emissions by vintage are enumerated in Table 24 and Table 25
of Appendix C.

7 Conclusions and Outlook
In conclusion, 71% of your large multifamily stock’s site energy consumption is from
Electricity, with 51% of the Electricity consumption coming from HVAC. Low-Rise, Cen-
tral Heating buildings use the most energy of the identified large multifamily segments,
particularly in buildings constructed after 2019. A potentially effective efficiency solu-
tion you can implement for your housing stock is replacing the existing central heating
system with a high-efficiency air source or geothermal heat pump while improving the
air-sealing and insulation of the building to reduce the heating needs and increase occu-
pant comfort.

In addition, 87% of your large multifamily stock’s emissions are from Electricity, with 51%
of the Electricity emissions coming from HVAC.

Water Heating is 14% of your large multifamily stock’s emissions, of which 77% is from
Electricity and 21% is from Natural Gas. In-unit solutions include heat pump water
heaters if there is sufficient space in each apartment to support. Water and space heat-
ing technologies could also potentially be jointly replaced with heat pump equipment that
produces hot water for both space heating and water heating, particularly in the 45% of
the stock that has shared space conditioning.

Potentially effective efficiency retrofits for Appliances, Lighting, and Miscellaneous
Loads are listed below. Please refer to the Advanced Energy Design Guide for Multi-
family Buildings 2022 for additional strategies.

 • Replacing appliances with energy efficient models (ENERGY STAR® ), especially
 equipment that has reached end-of-life.

 • Replacing gas-powered equipment, such as HVAC, water heaters, ranges, and
 clothes dryers, with electric models.

 • Installing control technologies, such as smart outlets or advanced power strips, to
 automatically turn off plug loads when not in use. Educate occupants to ensure
 they know how to interact with the technologies.

 • Enabling low-power or sleep settings on equipment, especially computers and
 printers.

 • Replacing all existing lighting with LED bulbs.
