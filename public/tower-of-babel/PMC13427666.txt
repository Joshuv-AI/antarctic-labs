Semantic knowledge improves molecular machine learning for chemical toxicity prediction
Authors: Giuseppe Albi, Arianna Dagliati, Riccardo Bellazzi, Joseph D Romano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryQuantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) modeling predicts chemical activity from structural and physico-chemical molecular properties. Beyond molecular structure, a growing body of semantic knowledge—often represented as knowledge graphs, in which biomedical entities are linked by interpretable relations reflecting scientific evidence—can inform computational toxicology. Here, we assess adding semantic knowledge to graph neural network (GNN)-based QSAR modeling to improve assay- and endpoint-level toxicity prediction, formulated as binary classification of active/inactive compounds. Both approaches we investigate use GNNs to learn molecular representations and augment them with semantic knowledge. Across publicly available in silico toxicity datasets, including Tox21 assays and external endpoints, semantic models improve discrimination while maintaining calibration comparable to structure-only QSAR models. Model explanations show that semantic knowledge helps the GNN encoder focus on substructures with known toxicity effects. Integrating mechanistic semantic context with molecular structure offers a route to more accurate and interpretable toxicity models that support chemical safety assessment.Keywords: cheminformatics, machine learning, graph machine learning, semantics, explainable AI, toxicology, visualization, knowledge graphs

Introduction

Assessing the toxicologic effects of chemical substances using in vitro or in vivo experiments could be expensive and time consuming when considering screening a vast number of chemicals of toxicological concern.1 For this reason, the field of computational toxicology has emerged, offering approaches to evaluate chemical toxicology in silico.2 The scientific community has made numerous contributions advancing computational toxicology, including releasing and maintaining publicly available datasets of in vivo and in vitro,3,4 creating informatics knowledge bases (KBs) containing the scientific evidence related to known toxicity outcomes, such as the comparative toxicogenomics database (CTD)5 and adverse outcome pathway database (AOP-DB),6 and supporting AI research by harmonizing and combining the previously mentioned resources via suitable frameworks such as ROBOKOP7 and ComptoxAI,8 among others.

Quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) is one of the most used method to evaluate toxicity of chemicals in silico, and nowadays generally comprise all forms of predictive modeling in which quantitative descriptors of molecules’ structures are used as input to train a computational model in predicting endpoints of interest.9 The descriptors that are typically used include physico-chemical properties, such as molecular weight, structural descriptors, such as number of rings, molecular fingerprints10,11,12 (i.e., bitstrings that indicate the presence of particular molecular substructures or fragments), or high-dimensional vector representations, called embeddings, obtained using deep learning (DL)13 or similar techniques. While traditional machine learning (ML) has dominated QSAR modeling in the past decades (e.g., using random forest (RF),14 k-nearest neighbors,15 gradient boosting,16 and deep neural networks17,18), recently the focus has shifted to graph representation learning (GRL).19 The reasons are essentially 2-fold: GRL models are able to operate directly on graph structures that are a natural and interpretable way to represent molecules, and GRL is suitable for leveraging the multimodal information that resides in biomedical KBs and datasets.20

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are one of the most used GRL paradigms in computational chemistry.21 GNNs are employed to learn atom, bonds and molecular graph embeddings, but differently from earlier node/graph embeddings methods,22,23,24 GNN can combine graph structural information (network topology) as well as quantitative features annotated onto the nodes and edges in the graph. Many works have been published to-date using GNNs in QSAR toxicity predictions, with recent advances including the use of 2D molecular representations,25,26 the combination of molecular structure with molecular fingerprints via feature concatenation,27 the refinement of molecular message passing through multi-view node features and adjacency-matrix preprocessing to account for indirect edge interactions,28 and the use of more sophisticated architectures that incorporate 3D molecular geometry29 or hybrid graph-transformer modules,30 among others. While most of the research including toxicity predictions adopts exclusively data-driven methods, recent works proposed knowledge-enriched frameworks to improve the pretraining of the molecular representations. For example, Fang et al.31 create a knowledge graph (KG) by using the period table and functional group knowledge that is used to learn element embedding to augment the molecular graph, while Li et al.32 use quantifiable information such as molecular descriptors and fingerprints as external knowledge to characterizes the molecular graph and as additional learning signal during the pretraining. Differently, other studies enrich the molecules representation by incorporating images containing phenotypical information of cells treated with the molecules of interest.33,34 However, these methods neglect the wealth of external semantic knowledge resources that have the potential to improve prediction performance.35 Although a recent KG-integrated toxicity prediction model proposes to combine molecular fingerprints with ComptoxAI-derived KG features,36 the role of downstream semantic graph propagation for updating pretrained molecular representations across multiple assay- and endpoint-level toxicity tasks remains underexplored.

GNNs are generally considered black-box models, meaning that it is not straightforward to interpret why they make specific predictions, unless explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods are added.37 Explainability is a fundamental step when building a pipeline for predicting molecule properties,38 and is generally assessed by using well-known XAI methods,39 such as shapley additive explanations (SHAP).40 Although XAI methods focused on toxicity predictions have been proposed in literature,41 little effort has been devoted to the quantitative explainability of GNN models for toxicity prediction.42 Furthermore, most of the graph-XAI methods proposed in literature leverage the gradient computations to infer the most informative chemical substructures pertaining to the task of interest.43 This objective is achieved by feeding the GNN model with a masked input graph that makes this method not suitable for specific GNN model architecture that comprise for example node and edge projection to high-dimensional embeddings.

Considering recent advances in publicly available computational toxicology knowledge resources, the aim of this study is to investigate the effect of adding structured semantic data to QSAR modeling for assay- and endpoint-level toxicity prediction. In this study, toxicity prediction is formulated as a set of binary classification tasks, where each task corresponds to a specific assay readout or toxicity-related endpoint. The objective is to improve the predictive performance obtained with well-known and primarily data-driven QSAR predictive models, by including additional information regarding the chemicals samples in input and their relationships with other biomedical entities. We leverage the ComptoxAI KG to first build a dataset of 2D molecular structures from the chemicals entities, and pretrain a GNN molecular encoder to learn molecule embeddings, and second, to extract a heterogeneous graph structure made of chemicals, assay, genes, and chemicals lists nodes connected with semantic relations. From the extracted data, we create a classification pipeline consisting in traditional ML models and fine-tuned GNN molecular encoder, which receive in input molecular fingerprint and 2D molecular graph, respectively, and we train them to predict binary active/inactive labels for individual Tox21 assays and external toxicity-related endpoints. We explore the role of adding semantics to toxicity prediction through two parallel approaches: (1) by separately learning molecular embeddings which are then static in the larger classification model made of a GNN trained on the semantic network (called semantic encoder); and (2) by unifying the GNN molecular encoder and the GNN semantic encoder training, thus dynamically updating molecule embeddings during a single training process. We compare the predictive performance of these strategies on different toxicity datasets and show that adding semantic knowledge into toxicity prediction models improves both discrimination and calibration. We conclude by quantitatively evaluating the explanations of the predictive model by exploring the GNN molecular encoders output from which we obtain the predicted importance of the nodes that is then compared with ground-truth node attribution linked to toxicity structural alerts. We show that the addition of the semantic network improves the attribution metrics and can better highlights molecule substructures that influence toxicity outcomes for which we report some examples.

Results

Framework overview

To support the addition of semantic data into our QSAR models, we first extracted and preprocessed relevant semantic concepts and relations from the ComptoxAI graph database.8 The graph database is comprised of entities (nodes) of different types (such as chemicals, genes, and diseases) connected via edges that indicate semantic relations. We extracted chemical entities, genes, chemicals lists and concepts pertaining to 37 toxicity assays originally sourced from the Tox21 database3 (Table S1), along with relations including whether chemicals do or do not activate each assay (the number of chemicals with indications for each assay range from 5,388 to 7,748 and the percentage of positive sample range from 1.58% to 24.84%) (see experimental procedures). Starting from the canonical simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) strings,44 that are a typical way to define molecule structure with an ASCII string, we preprocessed each chemical using RDKit45 to convert them into 2D graphs, where each node is represented as a 9-dimensional array of atom features and edges are represented as a 3-dimensional array of bond features (Figure 1A; Table S2; Table S3). Then, we pretrain GNN molecular encoders (Figure S1A) to learn graph embeddings from the 2D molecular structure of the chemicals, using two of the most used self-supervised pretraining frameworks: molecular contrastive learning of representations via GNNs (MolCLR)46 that use contrastive learning to learn a robust representation of molecules after corrupting their graph structure, and HiMol47 that builds a hierarchy in the 2D graph of a molecule by leveraging the BRICS algorithm to identify molecule’s fragment, and adopting predictive task to reconstruct graph structure and attributes to guide the optimization of the embedding learning (experimental procedures; Figure 1B). To verify the embeddings capture meaningful information about chemical function, we project embeddings into two dimensions using t-SNE48 and color the chemicals involved in the 37 Tox21 tasks with chemical and physical properties (Figure 2A; Figure S2).

We evaluated two approaches to inject semantic data into our predictive toxicology QSAR model. We refer to the first as semantic GNN, where we create a semantic graph from the entities and relations extracted from ComptoxAI (Figure 2B), initialize the chemical nodes as the static embeddings extracted from the pretrained GNN molecular encoder, and train a GNN that update the nodes representations with the semantic data (experimental procedures; Figure 1C; Figure S1C). Therefore, the GNN molecular encoder computes chemical embeddings statically, without be influenced by the training loss. In the second strategy called SemMol, we create a hierarchical graph data structure in which chemicals reside in the molecule space and semantic entities reside in the ComptoxAI semantic space (Figure 2B). Then, we use hierarchical message passing49 to first update the chemical representations with the pretrained GNN molecular encoder, followed by compute the chemical embeddings from the molecule embedding flowing through hyperedges that connect each atom to the molecule to which it belongs, and finally we update these embeddings with the GNN semantic module before using a prediction head to obtain toxicity predictions (experimental procedures; Figure 1C; Figure S1D).

We trained a single model for each Tox21 dataset and for three other publicly available toxicity datasets, which differ in number and percentage of positive samples, and comprising one assay investigating cardiotoxicity,50 four neurotoxicity-related assays,51 and five assay assessing the metabolic activities of different cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes52 (Table S1). Specifically, we trained each model for five different random runs and compared the average model performance with baseline ML models that use MACCS 166 fingerprints11 as chemical features, consisting of a bitstring in which the values 1 and 0 indicate the presence/absence of a specific molecular structural component, respectively, and with the fine-tuned GNN molecular encoders (Figure S1B), i.e., by directly using the chemical embedding in the toxicity prediction downstream task (experimental procedures; Figure 1C). We perform hyperparameter tuning for the baseline models, for the GNN molecular encoders, and for the semantic GNN via grid search using values proposed in previous works35,46,47 (Table S4). We compared the trained predictive models with both discrimination and calibration metrics (Figures 2C–2E; Figure S3). In addition, we perform a comparative analysis of the chemical entities connectivity on the most imbalance Tox21 assays (Table S5). Finally, similarly to Rao et al.,42 we quantitatively assess models predictions explanations by computing for chemicals with positive activity the atom importance, comparing it with the ground-truth nodes attributions. From these, we compute attribution metrics that we compare between the fine-tuned GNN molecular encoders and the ones extracted from the trained SemMol models. The explanations are obtained after training and evaluating the proposed toxicity prediction pipeline on an additional datasets addressing hepatotoxicity,53 belonging to the MolRep benchmark,54 and specifically by applying a layer-saliency method that is suitable for the kind of GNN encoder layers adopted (Table 3; Figure 3).

Including semantic knowledge improves toxicity prediction vs. baseline models

As shown in Figure 2C, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was significantly greater (p value <0.001 in all pairwise comparisons) when semantic data are included (AUROC = 0.894 ± 0.053 for the semantic GNN; AUROC = 0.89 ± 0.054 for the SemMol, versus the RF (AUROC = 0.845 ± 0.065), XGB (AUROC = 0.855 ± 0.061) and fine-tuned MolCLR encoder (AUROC = 0.849 ± 0.061) baseline models. In addition, the models using semantics showed a lower variance in AUROC scores when compared to the baseline models, suggesting that the semantic strategy is more robust to class imbalance across the assays included in the study. Similar considerations can be performed by considering the results obtained with the HiMol molecular encoder, as shown in Figure 2D, where both the strategies that use semantic (AUROC = 0.881 ± 0.013 for the semantic GNN; AUROC = 0.886 ± 0.015 for the SemMol) leads to higher discrimination performances with significative (p value <0.001) statistical significance when compared to the baseline models (AUROC = 0.855 ± 0.018 for RF, AUROC = 0.855 ± 0.019 for XGB and AUROC = 0.853 ± 0.014 for the fine-tuned HiMol encoder.

When stratifying on individual assays, in 33 assays either semantic GNN or SemMol, independently from the molecular encoder adopted and from the addition or not of chemicals lists, attain the highest AUROC, (see Figure 2E). Therefore, adding semantic data to QSAR modeling improves toxicity predictions both overall and for individual toxicity assays. In only 4 Tox21 assays, the baseline ML models outperform both the semantic GNN and SemMol models. We note that there is not a significant difference in AUROC between the semantic GNN with SemMol models.

Conversely, when considering model discrimination via area under the precision recall curve (AUPRC) and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), the semantic GNN yields lower performance when compared with baseline ML models (Figures S3A and S3B; Figures S3D and S3E). Specifically, the Semantic GNN models yield an AUPRC of 0.443 ± 0.171 and of 0.393 ± 0.042 for MolCLR and HiMol, respectively, which is significantly less than the AUPRC for both RF (0.479 ± 0.154; p value <0.001), and XGB (0.490 ± 0.155; p value <0.001), and have MCC values of 0.382 ± 0.124 and of 0.351 ± 0.022 for MolCLR and HiMol, respectively, which is also significantly different from that of the RF (0.431 ± 0.123; p value <0.001), and XGB (0.405 ± 0.124; p value = 0.001) models. However, the SemMol models achieve similar performances to the baseline ML models in terms of AUPRC (0.465 ± 0.166 for MolCLR and 0.461 ± 0.039 for HiMol), and MCC (0.409 ± 0.133 for MolCLR and 0.408 ± 0.025 for HiMol), which is only significantly different from the RF model when compared to HiMol (p value = 0.019 for MCC and p value = 0.021 for AUPRC). Interestingly, SemMol show significantly higher AUPRC and MCC versus the fine-tuned molecular encoders only (for MolCLR AUPRC = 0.437 ± 0.163, MCC = 0.375 ± 0.121 and for HiMol AUPRC = 0.435 ± 0.046, MCC = 0.367 ± 0.022) and semantic GNN. These results suggest that ad-hoc strategies adding semantic data to QSAR models can yield comparable performance when compared to well-established ML models and overcome the limitations encountered by simpler GNN models.

To further investigate why models incorporating semantic knowledge yield lower AUPRC and MCC than traditional ML models for some highly imbalanced Tox21 assays, we performed an additional analysis of the semantic graph connectivity of active and inactive compounds (Table S5). For each assay, we compare the number of semantic edges associated with positive and negative chemicals, considering total semantic connections. Across all Tox21 assays, active compounds show lower median total semantic connectivity than inactive compounds in 36 out of 37 assays. This result is particularly evident in the 12 most imbalanced assays, with less than 5% active compounds, where active chemicals show lower median total semantic connectivity in 11 out of 12 assays. These results suggest that, in highly imbalanced assays, the minority positive class is not only underrepresented in the labels but also less represented in the semantic graph. Therefore, semantic message passing may provide richer contextual information for the majority inactive class than for the rare active class, which can partly explain why semantic models improve AUROC but do not always improve AUPRC or MCC.

Similar conclusions can be made for calibration performance, where SemMol yields a significantly lower brier score (0.099 ± 0.04 when using MolCLR and 0.082 ± 0.018 when using HiMol) versus semantic GNN (0.127 ± 0.034 when using MolCLR and 0.145 ± 0.009 when using HiMol) with p value <0.001, and significantly lower brier score when compared to the fine-tuned molecular encoder (0.107 ± 0.039 for MolCLR and 0.11 ± 0.011 for HiMol) with p value = 0.02 for MolCLR and p value <0.001 for HiMol (Figures S3C and S3F).

In addition, we report from both Figures 2C and 2D that the models that include a wider semantic space with the inclusion of the chemicals lists nodes do not report statistically significative differences when compared to the semantic GNN settings (AUROC = 0.896 ± 0.009 for the semantic (+CL) GNN and AUROC = 0.893 ± 0.016 for the SemMol(+CL) that use MolCLR, AUROC = 0.882 ± 0.013 for the semantic (+CL) GNN and AUROC = 0.885 ± 0.015 for the SemMol(+CL) that use HiMol), and specifically for the case when MolCLR is used in SemMol and when compared to semantic (+CL) GNN (this latter shows higher AUROC mean values with p = 0.046). Similar considerations can be performed by observing the other metrics in Figure S3: both the semantic (+CL) GNN (when adopting MolCLR it achieves an AUPRC of 0.462 ± 0.046, an MCC of 0.384 ± 0.017 and a Brier score of 0.125 ± 0.007; when adopting HiMol it achieves an AUPRC of 0.393 ± 0.042, an MCC of 0.354 ± 0.022 and a Brier score of 0.145 ± 0.009), and the SemMol(+CL) (when adopting MolCLR this model achieves an AUPRC of 0.462 ± 0.046, an MCC of 0.384 ± 0.017 and a Brier score of 0.125 ± 0.007; when adopting HiMol it achieves an AUPRC of 0.393 ± 0.042, an MCC of 0.354 ± 0.022 and a Brier score of 0.145 ± 0.009) do not achieve a significative increase in discrimination and calibration performances. However, adding chemicals lists as semantic nodes still improves the performance when compared to both baseline models and to the base version of semantic GNN and SemMol.

We also evaluated an expanded semantic network including pathway entities connected to gene nodes. However, this larger semantic space did not improve model performance. This suggests that increasing the size of the semantic network is not necessarily beneficial when the added information is more distant from the chemical nodes being classified. In particular, pathway information can influence chemical representations only indirectly through chemical-gene-pathway routes, which may dilute endpoint-specific signal and introduce additional noise during message passing.

Validation of the pipeline on external datasets

The classification results measured with the mean AUROC are reported for each dataset and for each task in Table 1. We observe that when evaluating the proposed pipeline on the cardiotoxicity datasets (comprising n = 7,104 chemicals with a percentage of positive samples of 11.5%), the SemMol model with HiMol molecular encoder achieves the highest values, with significative differences when compared to semantic GNN (p-value = 0.042), followed by semantic (+CL) GNN. However, when considering the MolCLR molecular encoder the SemMol model achieve significative higher values when compared to SemMol (+CL) and semantic (+CL) GNN (p value <0.001 for both), semantic GNN (p value = 0.0071). Considering different classification metrics (Figure S4) we observe more differences respect to the ones concerning AUROC: when adopting HiMol the SemMol (+CL) model (AUPRC of 0.682 ± 0.079, MCC of 0.563 ± 0.039, and Brier score of 0.078 ± 0.01) do not improve significatively vs. SemMol (AUPRC of 0.661 ± 0.038, MCC of 0.545 ± 0.05, and Brier score of 0.083 ± 0.011); however, it shows significant higher value when compared to semantic (+CL) GNN (p value = 0.018 for AUPRC, p value = 0.028 for MCC and p value = 0.048 for Brier score), semantic GNN (p value = 0.028 for AUPRC and p value = 0.02 for MCC) and molecular encoder (p value = 0.033 for AUPRC and p-value = 0.048 for Brier score). For MolCLR the SemMol model (AUPRC of 0.661 ± 0.038, MCC of 0.545 ± 0.05, and Brier score of 0.083 ± 0.011) significantly outperforms semantic GNN (p value <0.01, p value = 0.03 and p value = 0.027 for AUPRC, MCC and Brier score, respectively); however, we observe significant performance drop when adding chemicals lists to these model. The results obtained on the cardiotoxicity datasets suggests that our pipeline can be successfully adopted in the case of new Tox21 assays.

Discussion

In this work, we show that the addition of semantic data in QSAR modeling can improve assay- and endpoint-level toxicity-related predictions compared to traditional ML algorithms using well-known structural descriptors. This has been acknowledged previously,35 but in this work we leverage GNNs to both learn a continuous vector representation of the chemicals of interest from their 2D graph, and to update this representation according to semantic relations mined from ComptoxAI. Since the aforementioned prior work shows that starting with MACCS keys and updating the chemical representation with semantic knowledge improves predictive performance, we focus on the application of GNNs, which is a natural evolution considering the simple conversion of molecules to graphs, and that graph databases have become one of the preferred ways to represent complex biomedical knowledge, thanks to their ability to represent multimodal entities as nodes that are connected by semantic relations.20 As future work, we plan to incorporate additional structural descriptors in the baseline models, and overcome the potential pitfall of the 2D molecule representation with the 3D molecule representation.55

In this direction, recent advances in representation learning suggest that further improvements may come from richer strategies for initializing and refining chemical representations. For instance, multi-view contrastive learning has been used to jointly exploit molecular sequence information from SMILES and network-derived representations, supporting the idea that structural and relational views can be integrated to obtain more discriminative chemical embeddings.56 Similarly, large language model (LLM)-based approaches have been proposed to extract molecular-level semantic information from biomedical knowledge graphs and transform this information into text-derived embeddings, which can then be refined through GNN message passing.57 Although these approaches have been applied to drug-drug interaction prediction rather than toxicity prediction, they support the idea that integrating molecular graph encoders, semantic graph propagation, and advanced representation-learning strategies, such as contrastive learning or LLM-derived semantic embeddings, can improve molecular representations and, consequently, prediction performance in downstream tasks.

As part of this study, we investigated two ways to inject semantic data into predictive models, evaluating their performance on assay- and endpoint-level binary classification tasks using data sourced from the Tox21 database and three additional toxicity-related datasets dealing with cardiotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and CYP450 activity. We observed that updating pretrained chemical representations with message passing over the created semantic graph improves discrimination performance, regardless of the size and the percentage of positive samples for each dataset. Indeed, one general obstacle for the field of computational toxicology is the low prevalence of positive examples58; however, compared to previous work, we do not face this problem with over- or under-sampling techniques,59 since we are interested in assessing the predictive performance by using the original data. In addition, we evaluate the prediction performance on a larger number of Tox21 assays, compared to most works that rely only on the Tox21 challenge.17,18,60 In this context, a recent closely related study integrated molecular fingerprints with KG features derived from ComptoxAI for toxicity prediction, further supporting the relevance of semantic knowledge in this setting.36 Our work is complementary but differs by explicitly comparing two downstream semantic-injection strategies, namely static semantic propagation and end-to-end semantic-molecular learning, across a broader set of assay- and endpoint-level toxicity tasks. In addition, we evaluate not only discrimination performance, but also calibration, class-specific semantic graph coverage, and the effect of semantic network expansion. Thus, our study helps characterize when semantic knowledge improves QSAR prediction and when its benefit may be limited by graph coverage, endpoint imbalance, or weakly relevant semantic expansion.

The lower AUPRC and MCC observed for some semantic-knowledge models on highly imbalanced Tox21 assays may therefore be explained not only by label imbalance, but also by imbalance in semantic graph coverage. Our connectivity analysis shows that active compounds are typically less connected than inactive compounds in the semantic network, particularly in the most imbalanced assays. Since AUPRC and MCC are especially sensitive to the correct identification of rare positive samples, limited semantic coverage of active compounds may reduce the ability of semantic message passing to prioritize and correctly classify the minority class. In this setting, semantic propagation can still improve the global ranking of compounds, as reflected by AUROC, while being less effective at enriching rare positives at the top of the prediction list or producing optimal threshold-based classifications. Traditional ML models based on fixed molecular descriptors or fingerprints may therefore remain more stable for some extremely imbalanced endpoints, because they do not depend on the availability and distribution of external semantic graph information.

The relative performance of the two semantic strategies may also depend on the nature of the endpoint. In particular, semantic GNN outperformed SemMol for the NT and NA neurotoxicity datasets. This may be explained by the fact that NT represents a broad mammalian neurotoxicity classification task compiled from heterogeneous evidence sources, rather than a single homogeneous in vitro assay.61,62 NA, although assay-defined, is based on microelectrode array-derived NA and therefore captures functional perturbations of neuronal network behavior, which may arise from multiple neurophysiological mechanisms.51,63 In these settings, preserving pretrained molecular representations and updating them through semantic graph message passing, as done in semantic GNN, may provide a more robust inductive bias than fully adapting the molecular encoder through endpoint-specific classification loss, as done in SemMol.

We observed that, for specific datasets, the use of a larger semantic network including chemical-list nodes can improve calibration performance. In addition, when the semantic update is computed in series with the chemical encoding, calibration improves compared with predictive models using the single GNN modules. This can be explained by considering that semantic data may provide additional contextual information that partially alleviates the difficulty of training GNN predictive models when label data and positive samples are limited. However, our results also show that simply increasing the size of the semantic network does not necessarily improve performance. In particular, adding pathway entities did not lead to performance gains. A possible explanation is that chemical-list nodes are directly connected to chemical entities, whereas pathway nodes are connected mainly through gene entities and can influence chemical representations only through longer chemical-gene-pathway routes. Therefore, pathway-level information may introduce broad biological context that is less directly aligned with the assay-level prediction target, increasing the risk of noisy message passing or over-smoothing. This suggests that the usefulness of semantic expansion depends not only on graph size, but also on the relevance and proximity of the added entities to the chemicals being classified. A further limitation of this work is related to the chemicals’ mapping to ComptoxAI, which is linked to the amount of semantic information available in the knowledge resource considered. This bottleneck can be only partially overcome by increasing the amount of semantic information, since for unknown molecules there may be no available knowledge in the literature. For that reason, in future work we aim to investigate the use of different semantic networks and endpoint-specific semantic subgraph selection strategies to support QSAR modeling for toxicity prediction.

Recent graph-based representation-learning approaches provide additional directions for extending our framework. In particular, hierarchical KG reasoning methods have shown that mapping specific relations to higher-level semantic concepts can improve generalization over heterogeneous relational structures.64 In the context of toxicity prediction, a similar strategy could be used to organize semantic relations mined from ComptoxAI into broader biological or toxicological categories, allowing molecular embeddings learned from chemical graphs to be updated at multiple levels of semantic abstraction. In parallel, decoupled KG embedding approaches suggest that different semantic spaces within a KG can be modeled separately and then integrated through relation-aware aggregation, which could help reduce noise when chemical nodes are connected to heterogeneous biomedical entities.65 Moreover, recent multimodal and dual-graph learning methods, although developed in recommendation settings, further support the use of attention, graph reconstruction, and self-supervised or contrastive objectives to denoise graph structures and align complementary views.66,67 Overall, these studies suggest that future QSAR models could benefit from hierarchical and multi-view GNN architectures that combine molecular graph embeddings with structured semantic knowledge.

Lastly, we used a layer-based nodes attribution method to assess the toxicology predictions’ explanations for active drugs for the hepatotoxicity dataset. Our results highlight that the addition of semantic information improves the identification of the most important nodes that belong to specific substructures defined as ground-truth and potentially linked to liver injury.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, the gains in predictive performance from incorporating semantic knowledge are, overall, modest: while the semantic models attain significantly higher AUROC than the structure-based baselines, the absolute improvement is small, and for some highly imbalanced assays the baseline models remain competitive or superior on AUPRC and MCC. This is by design rather than oversight, as the primary objective of this work was to improve the interpretability and biological grounding of toxicity predictions through semantic context, rather than to maximize raw predictive accuracy; nonetheless, the marginal accuracy gains should be weighed against the added model complexity. Second, the benefit of semantic knowledge is bounded by the coverage of the underlying knowledge resource: chemicals must be mapped to ComptoxAI to receive semantic context, and active compounds tend to be less connected in the semantic graph than inactive ones, which limits the usefulness of message passing for the rare positive class and cannot be fully resolved simply by expanding the network. Third, our experiments rely on a 2D molecular representation and a fixed set of structural descriptors in the baselines, and therefore do not exploit 3D conformational information that may carry additional toxicologically relevant signal. Finally, we did not observe consistent gains from enlarging the semantic network with more distantly connected entities (such as pathway nodes), suggesting that the relevance and proximity of the added knowledge—rather than its volume—governs whether semantic expansion helps. Natural directions for future work include addressing these limitations through richer semantic networks, endpoint-specific subgraph selection, and 3D molecular representations.

Method details

Extracting and preprocessing data from ComptoxAI

ComptoxAI is a data infrastructure proposed for computational toxicology, with main component a graph database, resulted from the harmonization of dataset and knowledge resource used in computational toxicology research. We use the ComptoxAI Python package (version 1.2.0) to query the ComptoxAI graph – which contains at the time of writing 1,346,793 nodes and 10,619,461 edges – to mine information from chemical, assay, gene and chemicals lists entities and the semantic relations between them.

Chemicals: ComptoxAI stores mainly chemical nodes (n = 1,116,847), comprising both approved drug and chemical substances, belonging to different source such as PubChem,71 DSSTox,72 DrugBank,73 CTD.5 Most of the chemicals are small molecules and have a molecular weight <500 Da. We retrieve node name, canonical SMILES string and MACCS fingerprint attributes from all the ComptoxAI chemicals entities, by keeping the ones with available an SMILES string as attribute, leading to a set of 1,078,384 chemical substances.

Assays: The toxicological assays available in ComptoxAI derive from the Tox21 dataset,3 which includes high-throughput in vitro screening results designed to assess chemical activity across toxicity-related biological pathways and endpoints. ComptoxAI includes n = 68 Tox21 assay nodes, each representing a specific assay readout and comprising a set of chemicals annotated as active or inactive for that assay.

Genes: The gene entities included in the ComptoxAI come from NCBI genes74 and are n = 193,313. Each gene node could be connected to one or more chemical nodes, depending on whether its expression is regulated by certain chemicals or if some chemical substances bind to it, with evidence extracted from AOP-DB6 and Hetionet.75 In addition, Hetionet is also used to derive information about interactions between genes, i.e., edges between gene nodes. Lastly, gene entities are connected to Tox21 assay nodes, indicating the toxicology target.

Chemicals Lists: The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has consolidated lists of chemical substances for different purposes such as regulation, risk assessment and reporting. These lists are part of the ComptoxAI set of semantic entities by grouping the chemical nodes in n = 300 categories, such as per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and pesticides.

GNN message passing framework

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are an architecture of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) that model information contained in graphs – a data structure in which entities or nodes are connected via edges. We use a message passing strategy76 to describe the computation performed by a GNN layer. Given a graph G = (V, E), in which V is the set of NV nodes (each characterized by a feature vector x), and E is the set of edges (each characterized by a feature vector e), a general GNN layer computes the hidden representation of a node v as follows:

where xvis the feature vector of node v, xu is the feature vector of node u that belongs to Nv, the set of node neighbors of node v, evu the feature vector of the edge between v and u, ϕ is the message function, ⊕ is a general aggregation operator and γ is the update function. Specifically, a GNN layer 1.) computes the messages between the node i and its node neighbors, based on their node feature vectors, the edge feature vector, and a possible transformation given by the differentiable function ϕ; 2.) aggregates the messages from all the nodes u∈Nv by using a permutation invariant function ⊕; and 3.) updates the node features of the node v by applying a transformation over the aggregated messages and its features.

Pretraining GNN on ComptoxAI chemicals

Starting from the set of the extracted ComptoxAI chemicals, we create a dataset to pretrain a GNN model and learn molecule embeddings. For each chemical, we obtain a 2D structure representation (see Table S2; Table S3 for the complete list of 9 atoms and 3 edge properties, respectively) computed using the RDKit Python package from the chemicals’ canonical SMILES representation. Each resulting chemical is represented in the dataset as an undirected graph Gci=(A,B), characterized by A the set of the NA nodes (atoms), B the set of edges (bonds), XA∈RNA×9 the node feature matrix and XB∈RNB×3 the edge feature matrix. We pretrain GNN molecular encoders by considering two of the most used frameworks, that are MolCLR and HiMol.

MolCLR. Based on SimCLR,77 the framework uses self-supervised learning to train a GNN encoder with the normalized temperature-scaled contrastive loss (NT-Xent) used to maximize the agreement between augmented versions of a training sample (defined as positive pairs) encoded with a GNN model. Specifically, given a training example Gc from a minibatch of N samples, MolCLR first creates two augmented version Gi and Gj by perturbing Gc via node masking or subgraph deletion. Second, a GNN encoderis applied to update node and edge embeddings by obtaining graph embedding hi and hj via pooling node features, and then a projection head modeled as a Multilayer perceptron (MLP) obtains the latent representation zi and zj. Finally, the NT-Xent loss is applied to each pair of positive samples (Gi, Gj):

HiMol. This framework introduces hierarchy in the molecular structure, by first using fragments, i.e., subparts of the molecule, to create a new graph at the motif-levels, in which nodes represent a molecular fragment and edges the spatial connection between them, and second, by connecting each of the new nodes to a supernode that is representative of the overall molecule at the graph-level. We use the original code released by the authors – implemented using the PyTorch Geometric (PyG) package68 – to implement both the pretraining framework. These implementations use a Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN)78 encoder – one of the most widely used GNN encoders for molecules – with a modification that allows edge embeddings.79 Our GNN layer uses an MLP as an update function and sums neighbor features by applying an aggregator operator to edge embeddings, followed by a batch normalization layer. We used ReLU as an activation function for all GNN and linear layers. As a graph augmentation strategy for MolCLR, we adopt atom masking and bond deletion, meaning that for each molecule 25% of the atoms are hidden by replacing their features with special value token (mask) and 25% of the bonds are deleted from the molecule graph. The models’ block diagrams are shown in Figure S1A. We split the pretraining chemical dataset into training (95%) and validation (5%) sets, and train each model for a maximum of 100 epochs – we introduce early stopping for HiMol to ensure a comparable training time with MolCLR-using the Adam optimizer80 and cosine annealing learning rate scheduler81 for MolCLR while a fixed learning rate for HiMol, and evaluate the model by calculating validation loss at the end of each epoch.

We qualitatively evaluate the learned chemicals representations by first selecting all chemicals involved in the 37 Tox21 tasks, then querying ComptoxAI and PubChem to retrieve their chemical and physical properties. Finally, we project their embeddings into two dimensions using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)48 and color these projections according to values of the selected properties.

where ci is a chemical substance, traditionally described by molecular structure descriptors, j denotes a specific assay or endpoint, fj is the computational model trained for that assay or endpoint, ε is the prediction error made by the model, and yˆi,j is the predicted activity label for chemical ci in task j. Note that for the external datasets, we consider chemicals that are successfully mapped to chemical nodes in ComptoxAI through a valid PubChem Compound IDentification (CID).

Tox21: Considering the initial set of Tox21 assays from ComptoxAI, we consider only the ones in which the number of active chemicals is at least the 1% of the sum between active and inactive chemicals, leading to a subset of n = 37 assays with different target such as nuclear receptors and cytotoxicity.

Cardiotoxicity: This dataset belongs to the family of the Tox21 assays; however, it is not present in ComptoxAI at the moment of the extraction. Specifically, this assay targets the drug-induced blockade of the human ether-à-go-go related gene (hERG) channel, considered one of the main cause of cardiotoxicity in postmarketing surveillance.50

Neurotoxicity: We select four different chemical-induced neurotoxicity datasets released in NeuTox 2.0.51 The first assay considers blood-brain-barrier (BBB) permeability, that is the ability of a compound to cross into the brain tissue. The second assay investigates the cytotoxicity of neuroblastoma cells, specifically SH-SY5Y cells, and is indicated as neuronal cytotoxicity (NC). The third assay studies neuronal activity (NA) by using neuronal networks cultivated on microelectrode arrays. The last assay investigates mammalian neurotoxicity (NT) through the exposure to potential neurotoxicants related to neurological disorders.

CYP450: This is a class of enzymes that potentially affect drug toxicity by either reducing exposure to the parent molecule or by converting the drug into a toxic entity. We select five assays that measure the activity of different CYP450 enzymes that act with a specific drug-metabolizing activity, namely CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6 and CYP3A4.52

Each dataset is therefore treated as an independent binary classification task, with samples {ci, yi},where ci is a chemical substance and yi is the corresponding active/inactive label for the considered assay or endpoint. Each dataset is split in training/validation/test with a proportion of 80/10/10. For each model we perform parameters learning (training) on the training set, hyperparameters tuning on the validation set, by choosing the hyperparameters’ values that maximize the AUROC, and model evaluation to assess model generalizability on the independent test set, by considering both discrimination performance (with AUROC, AUPR and MCC metrics) and calibration (with Brier score). We repeat the splitting procedure five times with a random initialization to compute average performance metrics, comparing them between models by averaging the mean values obtain for each assay and applying paired t test, and finally correcting for the number of pairwise comparison between the classifier with the Holm-Sidak correction. We use traditional machine learning models and finetune the MolCLR and HiMol encoder as computational model for toxicity predictions.

Machine learning: We adopt as ML baseline two of the most used ML model, i.e., Random Forest (RF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and use as molecular structural descriptors the MACCS 166 fingerprint.11 For each assay we tune the model hyperparameters (reported in Table S4) by creating different models’ configuration with grid search, by using the hyperparameters’ values used in Romano et al.35 We use the scikit-learn Python package69 to implement the ML models, by weighting the samples’ label according to its support.

Fine-tuning molecular GNN encoder: Like in the original papers, we finetune the GNN models pretrained with MolCLR and HiMol frameworks. A randomly initialised prediction head with decreasing hidden size replaces the MLP used during pretraining, and binary classification task is performed by considering a graph classification approach. Given a set of attributed graphs {(G1, y1), (G2, y2), …, (Gn, yn)}, with Y = {y1, y2, …, yn} the set of labels, graph classification is defined as learning a function f:→Y, with the input space of graphs. This is achieved by aggregating the nodes features to a graph-representation, specifically we use mean pooling with MolCLR, i.e., we compute the graph embedding by averaging node features, while we use directly the supernode’s representation with HiMol. We perform models’ hyperparameters tuning by considering the same hyperparameters’ values used in the original encoder papers, reported in Table S4. We report the models block diagram in Figure S1B. The models are trained with Cross entropy loss, weighted by class support to account for the datasets class imbalance and optimized with Adam optimizer.

Augmenting QSAR toxicity predictions with semantic knowledge

We create a directed heterogeneous graph Gs = (C, T, K, CL, Rs), to ‘inject’ the toxicity prediction task with semantic information. Specifically, we first define the set of chemical nodes C as all chemical substances with known activity values in at least one of the selected toxicologic assay of interest. Second, we gather the set T of assay nodes which are connected with at least one chemical node in C and the assay of interest. Third, we obtain the set CL of chemicals lists for each of the chemical node. Finally, we define the set of gene nodes K as the ComptoxAI-derived gene entities that are linked to the chemical nodes in C. The set of semantic relations Rs = {r1,...,rm} is defined as one of the following: edges between elements of T and C, which define whether the chemical is active or inactive with respect to the assay of interest; edges between elements of C and K, which define either whether a chemical can alter gene expression or that the chemical directly binds to the gene’s protein product; edges between two elements of K, indicating gene-gene interactions; edges between CL and C that express if a chemicals lists include chemicals; and edges between elements of T and K, indicating the key gene involved in a toxic endpoint. The corresponding graph schema is shown in Figure 1B.

Similar to Romano et al.,35 we modelized the QSAR predictions with semantic knowledge as a node classification task applied over the chemical nodes, defined as learning a function f:Xc∈RNc×d→Y, with Xc a matrix in which reach row is a chemical and each column is a numerical vector with dimension d, but differently from the previous work we use molecule 2D graph embedding instead of MACCS fingerprint. We extract the molecule embedding from the GNN pretrained molecular encoder and use these as the initial chemical node representations. Differently, we assign a uniformly sample scalar value to the assay, gene and chemicals lists nodes’ features. To avoid information leakage, when considering a specific toxicologic assay as target label, we delete this node and all the edges connecting this node from the semantic graph. We choose Graph Attention Layer (GAT)82 as the GNN layer for the semantic model. Unified training of the molecular encoder and semantic modules

In the Semantic GNN model, the molecules embeddings are directly used for the downstream prediction task and are updated in a static manner with the semantic knowledge incorporated in Equation 4. Differently, we create a hierarchical version of the semantic toxicity predictive model, in which the molecule embeddings are updated dynamically during the training, meaning that graph embedding of the molecules are not extracted from the molecular encoder but instead this former is trained in combination with the semantic GNN. To implement this strategy, we expand the heterogeneous graph by representing each chemical node in C with its 2D graph structure. Specifically, the graph Gs is expanded to include the set A, composed by the atoms that appears in all the molecules considered atom features matrix XA ∈ RNx9,the set RB made by all the edges (bonds) between the atoms and edges features matrix ε ∈ RNx3. By doing this, the resulting graph is GS=(A,C,T,K,CL,RS,B,RH) became a directed hierarchical graph, in which the chemicals, represented by the graph molecules, lay in the molecule space, while the semantic entities lay in the semantic space. These spaces are connected through the set of hyperedges belonging to RH, which connect each atom in A to the respective node chemical in C, making each node chemical a hypernode. The corresponding graph schema is shown in Figure 1B.

Giving this new heterogeneous graph, we combine the GNN molecular encoder and the Semantic GNN, and train the resulting model for toxicity predictions, referring to it as SemMol The unified model is inspired by the work of Zhong et al.,49 in which authors propose a hierarchical formulation of the message passing framework. Giving a graph in which different hierarchical level can be defined, this framework comprises within-level, bottom-up and top-down message propagation, indicating the exchanging of information between node within the same level of a graph, from a node that belong to a lower level to a node that belong to a higher level, and from a node that belongs to a higher level to a node that belongs to a lower level, respectively. Since the hierarchical graph is composed of only two levels, we adopt within-level and bottom-up level message propagation. For the semantic graph, we initialize the chemical hypernodes features with zero vectors, and gene, assay and chemicals lists features with scalar values as used in the previous approach. Specifically, we first apply within-atom message passing M1 (reported in Equation 8 and representing the modified GIN layer), to update each atom ai representation to hai′ by using the pretrained molecular module. Second, we use bottom-up propagation M2 (Equation 9) to compute for each chemical hypernode cn its representation hcn′, by averaging the atom features for each atom ak ∈ cn. Note that through M2 the message flows from atoms in the molecule space (lower level of the hierarchy) to chemical hypernodes in the semantic space (higher level of the hierarchy) and not vice versa. Lastly, we apply within-semantic message passing M3 (reported in Equation 10 and representing the update rule for the GAT layer) to update for each node q in the semantic graph its representation to hq′, by using the semantic GNN. We do not perform hyperparameter tuning but instead empirically use the same values resulting from the semantic module tuning and train the unified model as in the static approach.

Obtaining quantitative toxicity predictions on the hepatotoxicity dataset

The GNN molecular encoders adopted in this study are based on a GIN layer that considers nodes and edges embeddings starting from nodes and edges attributes in input, respectively, and for that reason the mask strategy adopted by some graph-XAI methods over nodes and/or edges will cause the block of the gradient with respect to the model inputs. For that reason, we adopt LayerGradientXActivation,83 that is a layer-attribution method from the Captum Python library,70 which provide algorithms for PyTorch-based models interpretability, that computes element-wise product of the layer activations and the gradient of the output with respect to that layer. Specifically, considering hi,j(L) that is the hidden representation for the nodes i and feature j, obtained after the forward step of the GNN molecular encoder at layer L of the overall model that outputs logits z, the selected XAI method computes the per-feature attribution matrix S ∈ RNx9 by computing the mean over the j feature dimension for each of the node i of the following score:

Gradient-based attribution methods can be used either to explain the model decision for a specific input, by computing attributions with respect to the predicted class, or to investigate the molecular features associated with a target class, by computing attributions with respect to the true label. In this work, we use this approach to evaluate whether the model identifies molecular substructures associated with hepatotoxic activity. Specifically, we focus on a hepatotoxicity dataset comprising chemicals potentially associated with liver injury and annotated with one or more toxicologically relevant substructures.53 Given a finetuned GNN molecular encoder and a trained SemMol model, we apply LayerGradientXActivation to the active chemicals and compute node-importance scores with respect to the positive/active class. This procedure is repeated for each of the five random training runs. We then average the attribution scores across runs, binarize the scores by selecting the top 25% and top 50% most important nodes, and compare the predicted node importance with the ground-truth node attribution provided by the MolRep benchmark.54 Similar to Rao et al.,42 we compute for each molecule attribution metrics such as AUROC, precision and recall, and then average them on the entire set of active chemicals to compute mean and standard deviation metrics. We compare the finetuned GNN molecular encoder, the SemMol and SemMol(+CL) models, for both MolCLR and HiMol.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative analyses were performed in Python. Each toxicologic assay or endpoint was treated as an independent binary (active/inactive) classification task. For every task, the dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets (80/10/10), and the splitting procedure was repeated five times with random initialization; unless otherwise stated, reported values are the mean ± standard deviation across these five runs, so the unit for all performance statistics is n = 5 independent runs. The number of chemical compounds per assay (n = number of unique compounds) is reported in the Results and in Tables 1, 2, and 3, together with the corresponding class balance.

Model generalizability was assessed on the held-out test set using discrimination metrics —the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) —and a calibration metric, the Brier score. To compare models, the mean metric values were averaged across assays and compared using a paired t test, with the Holm–Šidák correction applied to account for the number of pairwise comparisons between classifiers. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05; exact p-values for individual comparisons are reported in the Results and in the relevant figure legends (Figure 2E; Figures S5 and S6) and Tables 1, 2, and 3. Throughout, the center is reported as the mean and dispersion as the standard deviation.

For the hepatotoxicity explainability analysis, node-importance scores were computed with LayerGradientXActivation for each of the five random training runs and averaged across runs. Per-molecule attribution AUROC, precision, and recall were then computed against the ground-truth substructure annotations and averaged over all active chemicals; results are reported as mean ± standard deviation in Table 3.

Software: baseline machine-learning models (Random Forest and eXtreme Gradient Boosting) were implemented with scikit-learn; the graph neural network models were implemented in PyTorch; and model explanations were computed with the Captum implementation of LayerGradientXActivation. Package versions and hyperparameter settings are provided in the key resources table and Table S4.

Sex and gender: This study uses publicly available chemical bioactivity datasets (Tox21, NeuTox 2.0, cytochrome P450, and hepatotoxicity) in which the experimental units are chemical compounds rather than organisms or human participants. Sex and gender are therefore not applicable to these data and were not analyzed. We note as a limitation that assay- and compound-level data do not capture organism- or sex-specific differences in toxicity.
