Carina™ modular robotic-assisted left lower lobectomy after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy for stage IIIA squamous cell lung cancer: a case report
Authors: Hao Chen, Hancheng Yin, Bin Zheng, Chi Xu, Chun Chen, Guobing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has become a standard approach for locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), enhancing resectability. However, it also increases surgical complexity because of treatment-induced fibrosis—a challenge well addressed by robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS). This report demonstrates the successful application of a modular robotic platform for post-neoadjuvant left lower lobectomy in a patient with stage IIIA squamous NSCLC, highlighting its precision and feasibility in complex oncologic resections.Case DescriptionA 51-year-old man was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma of the left lower lobe (cT2aN2M0) and achieved a partial response following pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy. After completing neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, robot-assisted left lower lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection was performed using the Carina™ Modular Robotic System (Ronovo Surgical, Shanghai, China) because of anticipated pleural adhesions and the complex anatomical location of enlarged lymph nodes. The robotic platform demonstrated excellent outcomes: 20 mL blood loss, 5-minute docking time, and 141-minute console time. Intraoperatively, severe pleural adhesions were encountered but successfully managed with robotic assistance, enabling complete dissection and safe tumor resection. The patient recovered rapidly, with chest tube removal by postoperative day 5 and discharge on day 7. Pathology revealed a major pathological response (MPR) with negative bronchial margins.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the technical feasibility of the Carina™ system for complex post-neoadjuvant lobectomy in a patient with stage IIIA squamous NSCLC. Acceptable short-term perioperative outcomes were observed despite pleural adhesions and nodal complexity. Further experience and larger studies are needed to evaluate the potential role of this platform in thoracic surgery.

Introduction

Lung cancer currently has the highest mortality rate among all cancers, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for the vast majority of cases (1). In recent years, neoadjuvant therapy, particularly chemoimmunotherapy, has become an essential treatment approach for advanced NSCLC, promoting tumor downstaging and enhancing surgical resectability (2,3). However, treatment-induced tissue fibrosis and vascular fragility require surgical precision—an area in which robotic technology excels. Robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) is gaining increasing global attention in thoracic surgery because of its precision, flexibility, and shorter hospital stays compared with conventional thoracoscopic surgery (4). Recently, a new robotic platform Carina™ (Ronovo Surgical, Shanghai, China) featuring independent modular arms and enhanced space efficiency has been successfully implemented in urology, gynecology, and general surgery (5-7). However, research on the use of modular robots for lung resections remains limited. We herein describe a case of stage IIIA left lung squamous cell carcinoma with bulky mediastinal lymph node metastasis in a man, treated with left lower lobectomy by Carina™ following neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://acr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/acr-2026-0009/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and the accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

A 51-year-old man with a 30-year smoking history was incidentally found to have a left lower lobe pulmonary mass during a routine examination. At presentation, the patient had no obvious respiratory symptoms and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. Pre-treatment pulmonary function testing showed a forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) of 3.17 L, FEV1% predicted of 83.77%, diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) of 7.01, and maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV) of 106.58. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a 3.2 cm × 2.2 cm lesion in the left lower lobe, accompanied by multiple enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes, the largest measuring 1.9 cm in short-axis diameter. Positron emission tomography-CT demonstrated intense metabolic activity in the left lower lobe lesion [maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax): 8.3] and pathological nodal uptake at stations 4R, 4L, 5, and 7. The most prominent was a 4.1 cm × 2.7 cm station 7 lymph node (SUVmax: 11.2), which had close anatomical relationships with the left main bronchus, pulmonary vein, and esophagus. Physical examination revealed normal breath sounds bilaterally with no signs of respiratory distress or lymphadenopathy. Diagnostic confirmation was obtained through endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration of the station 7 lymph node. Pathological examination revealed invasive squamous cell carcinoma, with strong positivity for p40 and p63 and negativity for thyroid transcription factor-1, Napsin A, and other adenocarcinoma markers. As a result, the diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma was confirmed, and the case was staged as clinical T2aN2M0 (IIIA) according to the 8th edition Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) classification (8). During preoperative evaluation, the patient developed a Streptococcus constellatus mediastinal abscess, presenting with fever and leukocytosis. Contrast-enhanced CT showed characteristic empyema. The condition was successfully managed with targeted antibiotic therapy, and the empyema was resolved through ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis and drainage tube placement. A decision was then made to proceed with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, consisting of two cycles of pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks) and one cycle of pembrolizumab combined with paclitaxel (243 mg) and carboplatin (area under the curve =5, 660 mg). The last neoadjuvant treatment was pembrolizumab on June 9, 2024, and surgery was performed on July 9, 2024, 30 days later. No treatment-related adverse events were observed during neoadjuvant therapy. Restaging after completion of neoadjuvant treatment showed a reduction in the primary lesion to 2.2 cm × 1.9 cm, assessed as a partial response according to RECIST 1.1 criteria (9), with decreased metabolic activity (SUVmax: 3.6). Although persistent nodal enlargement was noted at station 7 (1.7 cm × 2.1 cm), the marked reduction in fluorodeoxyglucose avidity (decrease in SUVmax from 11.2 to 3.6) indicated a favorable treatment response (significant radiological response). Following successful neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the patient underwent robot-assisted left lower lobectomy with mediastinal lymph node dissection, following a thorough assessment of cardiopulmonary reserve and an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification of II for general anesthesia. The decision to proceed with a minimally invasive robotic technique was based on the post-neoadjuvant fibrosis and the need for precise dissection near critical vascular structures. The patient was positioned in a full right lateral decubitus position (90° tilt) following induction of general anesthesia (Figure 3). The procedure was performed using the Carina™ Modular Robotic System (Ronovo Surgical) (Figure 4) with a standard four-port configuration (Figure 5). Upon entering the thoracic cavity, adhesions were observed in the left pleural space (Figure 6); no visible nodules were present on the pleural surfaces. Electrocautery was used to lyse the adhesions, followed by division of the inferior pulmonary ligament. Dissection continued along the oblique fissure, where the basal segmental artery and superior segmental artery of the left lower lobe were carefully isolated and sequentially divided using two firings of a linear stapler (Figure 7). The left lower lobe bronchus was then exposed, and its accompanying bronchial arteries were divided with an ultrasonic scalpel. After proximally clamping the bronchus, ventilation was briefly resumed to confirm adequate inflation of the left upper lobe. The bronchus was then transected with a linear stapler. Subsequently, the inferior pulmonary vein was isolated and divided with another stapler firing. The anterior aspect of the oblique fissure was completed using three additional stapler applications, and the specimen was retrieved via a utility incision. The margin of the left lower lobar bronchus was sent for frozen section, which confirmed negativity for malignancy. A systematic lymph node dissection was then performed. After meticulous hemostasis, the left upper lobe was inflated under direct vision, confirming both adequate expansion and an airtight bronchial stump. Minor air leaks from the lesion were oversewn with 3-0 polypropylene (Prolene) sutures. After confirming no active bleeding and completing the instrument count, two 28-Fr chest tubes were inserted—one through the camera port (6th intercostal space, midaxillary line) and another through the assistant port (6th intercostal space, anterior axillary line)—both connected to underwater seal drainage. An additional 19-Fr Blake drain (Able tube) was positioned at the 7th intercostal space along the left scapular line for localized drainage. The wounds were closed in layers and dressed sterilely. The total estimated blood loss was 20 mL. The docking time and console time were 5 and 141 minutes, respectively. The primary surgeon, who was trained and experienced in using the da Vinci surgical system, reported a moderate intraoperative cognitive workload (NASA Task Load Index, NASA-TLX: 40/100), with favorable ratings for instrument performance (good) and visual clarity (fairly clear). The NASA-TLX assessment and the qualitative ratings of instrument performance and visual clarity were recorded by departmental staff within 1 hour after surgery as exploratory postoperative observations rather than prospectively predefined study endpoints. The three-dimensional visualization system induced no appreciable ergonomic strain during the 141-minute console time. There were no unplanned conversions or device deficiencies, confirming the system’s reliability for complex oncologic resections.

The postoperative course was notable for rapid recovery. The Able tube was removed on postoperative day 3, followed by removal of the closed drainage system on postoperative day 5. The patient was discharged on day 7 with excellent pain control (numeric rating scale score: 2/10). The resected lesion measured 2.5 cm × 1.2 cm × 1.0 cm. Postoperative pathology revealed residual non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma in the left lower lobe specimen. Approximately 3% of the tumor bed consisted of residual viable tumor, consistent with major pathological response (MPR). Resection margins at both the bronchus and vessel were negative. Lymphatic metastasis was identified at station 7 (1/1), whereas no metastases were observed at stations 4 (0/2), 5 (0/1), 6 (0/3), 10 (0/1), 11 (0/1), 12 (0/2), or 13 (0/1). The final pathological stage was ypT1cN2M0, stage IIIA. Biomarker analysis showed high programmed death-ligand 1 expression (tumor proportion score: 90%) and a proliferative index (Ki-67) of 40%, with positivity for p40 and p63. The patient-initiated adjuvant therapy 1 month postoperatively with one cycle of pembrolizumab plus paclitaxel and carboplatin, followed by three cycles of concurrent chemoradiotherapy combining pembrolizumab-paclitaxel-carboplatin immunochemotherapy with intensity-modulated radiotherapy. Two additional cycles of chemotherapy (paclitaxel and carboplatin) were administered post-radiation. The patient reported full adherence to postoperative adjuvant therapy, with no interruptions or dose reductions. A 9-month surveillance CT demonstrated expected post-lobectomy anatomical changes in the left lung without evidence of recurrence or metastasis. To date, the patient has exhibited no air leak or dyspnea. The overall timeline of diagnosis, neoadjuvant treatment, surgery, postoperative adjuvant therapy, and follow-up is summarized in Figure 8.

Discussion

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide (10). NSCLC, which accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, is the most common type. Accurate staging of NSCLC carries significant clinical implications because it guides the selection of optimal treatment strategies. Stage III disease, in particular, presents a complex clinical scenario characterized by considerable heterogeneity in tumor size, anatomical location, and patterns of nodal involvement (8,11). Historically, stage III NSCLC has been associated with a significantly poorer prognosis than earlier stages. Studies have revealed 5-year survival rates of 60% for stage IIA and 53% for stage IIB, but only 36%, 26%, and 13% for stages IIIA, IIIB, and IIIC, respectively (12). This survival gradient underscores the anatomical and biological complexity of locally advanced disease.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy can reduce tumor size, thereby improving surgical resectability. Studies have shown that neoadjuvant chemotherapy offers a modest 5% improvement in 5-year recurrence-free survival compared with surgery alone, making it a preferable option for patients with poor prognosis and advanced tumors (13). In recent years, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has undergone a transformative shift in the treatment of locally advanced NSCLC, targeting both tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment (14). The KEYNOTE-671 trial (15) demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy significantly enhances pathological response (MPR: 30.2% vs. 11.0%) and event-free survival (hazard ratio: 0.58). Similarly, the CheckMate-816 trial (16) reported a pathological complete response rate of 24.0% with nivolumab plus chemotherapy, compared with 2.2% with chemotherapy alone, with a substantial increase in MPR (36.9% vs. 8.9%). In the present case, the patient achieved a 40.6% reduction in tumor size on CT (from 3.2 cm × 2.2 cm to 2.2 cm × 1.9 cm), indicating a partial response according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria (9), along with a 56.6% decline in SUVmax (from 8.3 to 3.6) following pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy. Postoperative pathology revealed an MPR with only 3% residual tumor, which was consistent with the pathological response observed in prior neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy studies.

While neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy demonstrates clinical benefits for NSCLC, concerns have been raised regarding potential surgical difficulty and complications. Recent studies have reported a higher incidence of prolonged air leaks in patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, along with increased postoperative complications and extended hospital stays compared with both the neoadjuvant chemotherapy group and the group without preoperative treatment (17). Additionally, one analysis found that although the positive lymph node ratio was lower in the neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy group, these patients exhibited more severe damage to vascular structures. Observed changes included greater destruction of elastic fibers in blood vessels, vascular wall degeneration, fibrinoid necrosis and fibrosis, as well as increased pulmonary interstitial exudation (18). Collectively, these findings indicate that while neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy is therapeutically beneficial, it introduces significant technical challenges for subsequent anatomical lung resections and systematic nodal dissection.

Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) has been increasingly adopted in NSCLC because of its high-definition three-dimensional visualization and improved instrument articulation in confined thoracic spaces. In a landmark retrospective analysis by Cerfolio et al., stage-specific 5-year survival rates following robotic lobectomy improved to 70% for stage IIB, 62% for IIIA, and 31% for IIIB disease (19). Additional studies highlight advantages in operative time and reduced conversion rates due to fibrosis or difficult exposure (20). In the present case, the surgical difficulty was increased by preoperative empyema, treatment-related fibrosis, pleural adhesions, and a bulky station 7 lymph node closely adjacent to the left main bronchus, pulmonary vein, and esophagus. These features made dissection planes less distinct and increased the need for careful exposure and precise manipulation around critical structures. In this setting, the robotic approach was considered technically suitable for performing a controlled dissection.

The development of robotic technology has led to the increasing adoption of RATS for lung cancer operations (21). While the da Vinci surgical system remains the mainstream robotic platform, its integrated arm design presents several limitations, including challenges in preoperative setup and an increased risk of arm collisions during complex maneuvers. Additionally, the system’s large footprint and the high costs associated with robotic procedures have driven efforts to improve robotic surgical system design (22,23). Emerging modular robotic systems offer distinct advantages that warrant attention. The Carina™ modular platform differs from integrated robotic systems in that its arm carts are independently positioned, which may offer greater flexibility in operating room setup and instrument arrangement. In this case, these modular features were considered potentially helpful in accommodating the thoracic workspace and maintaining access for the assistant during dissection. Flexible port placement also allowed a setup familiar to surgeons experienced in thoracoscopic and robotic procedures.

Currently, research on modular robotic systems in thoracic surgery remains limited. Fra et al. investigated the application of the Versius modular robotic system in thoracic procedures (24), demonstrating its clinical feasibility and safety. Although this platform has been used in gynecology (25), no prior thoracic oncology cases using the Carina™ system have been reported. The observations from this report should therefore be limited to technical feasibility and short-term perioperative outcome. This single case does not allow conclusions regarding superiority over other robotic platforms, preservation of pulmonary function, or broader clinical effectiveness. We also observed areas for further refinement, including cart placement, docking and undocking workflow, and the absence of haptic feedback. Further clinical experience and larger studies are required to better define the role of this platform in thoracic surgery.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the technical feasibility of the Carina™ modular robotic system for lobectomy after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in a patient with stage IIIA squamous NSCLC. In this case, acceptable short-term perioperative outcomes were achieved despite pleural adhesions and nodal complexity. Although comparative advantages and broader clinical utility cannot be established from a single case, this report supports the technical feasibility of the platform in a complex post-neoadjuvant thoracic procedure. Further clinical experience and larger studies are needed to better define the role of this platform in thoracic surgery.
