Broadening the genetic horizon of rice: comparative insights from gamma radiation and EMS-induced mutagenesis
Authors: Muhammad Rashid, Adeel Khan, Munirah Abdullah Al–Dosary, Fatimah MS Alshudukhi, Saud Alamri, Manzer H Siddiqui, Kamran Ashraf, Qamar uz Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic uniformity in elite Basmati rice cultivars limits yield improvement and adaptation to changing agro-climatic conditions. Induced mutagenesis using physical and chemical agents provides an effective strategy to generate novel heritable variation. This study the efficiency of gamma irradiation (137Cs) and ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) in inducing genetic variability and improving yield-related traits in rice.ResultsSeeds of Super Basmati and Noor Basmati were treated with gamma rays (150–300 Gy) and EMS (0.25–1.25%) and evaluated across M₁–M₄ generations. Both mutagens caused dose-dependent reductions in germination, seedling growth, and panicle fertility, with Noor Basmati exhibiting higher sensitivity. Root length was reduced by 45.17% at 200 Gy and 52.96% at 0.50% EMS in Noor Basmati. Estimated LD₅₀ values for panicle fertility were 267 Gy (Noor) and 274 Gy (Super) under gamma irradiation, and 0.68% and 0.70% under EMS, respectively. Large-scale selection from M₂ resulted in 4,157 promising mutants, which were progressively narrowed to 753 stable M₄ progenies. Mean grain yield per plant increased by 23.63% in M₄ compared with M₃, with the highest gain (32.5 g plant⁻¹) observed in Noor Basmati treated with 0.25% EMS. 1000-grain weight and tillers per plant increased by 27.19% and 10.38%, respectively. High heritability (>90%) with substantial genetic advance was recorded for tillers per plant and 1000-grain weight. Multivariate analyses identified these traits as the primary contributors to yield enhancement, and Cluster analysis grouped M₄ mutants into three clusters, with 406 progenies as high-yielding.ConclusionsGamma irradiation and EMS effectively broadened the genetic base of Basmati rice and enabled significant, stable yield improvement through targeted selection. The identified high-yielding mutant lines represent valuable genetic resources for rice improvement and future functional and genomic studies.Keywords: Rice (Oryza sativa L.), Mutation breeding, LD₅₀, Genetic variability, Grain yield, Multivariate analysis

Background

Rice (Oryza sativa L.), which remains the backbone of agricultural production around the globe, has always been intricately involved with the survival of humankind. But the continuously shifting demands created by climatic change, novel diseases, and the required adoption of sustainable agricultural practices call for innovations in crop improvement research on a continuous basis [1, 2].

In the context, the use of mutagenesis by gamma rays and ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) has proved to be an effective technique, giving scope to develop modifications in the genetic makeup of the rice crop and improve its adaptability [3–5]. These mutagens has been an effective and greatly valuable technique in the field of genetics and plant breeding, and has opened ways for the introduction of genetic variability and improvements in crops [6, 7].

Gamma rays are high intensity electromagnetic radiation released by radioactive materials during radioactive decay processes. They are endowed with the capability to penetrate deeply into living organisms, hence being useful mutagenic agents [8]. The ionizing properties of gamma rays enable them to interact with molecules, thus altering their structure and leading to genetic mutations [9]. When exposed to gamma rays, molecules undergo a series of changes, including substitution, insertion, deletion, and chromosomal translocations. Genes within the molecules undergo mutations, thus leading to genetic diversity created by variations in the nucleotide sequence The random nature of mutations induced by gamma rays leads to the generation of a diverse range of genetic mutations in a crop plant, such as rice [10].

EMS is a chemical mutagen and an alkylating agent. Unlike gamma rays, it directly changes the DNA composition, hence causing mutations. The main effect of EMS is point mutation, which results in nucleotide substitution, as its alkylation of guanine residues [11]. The process of alkylation in this case involves the transfer of an ethyl group to the O6 position of guanine, resulting in the mispairing of bases during replication of DNA, hence point mutation, which results in base substitutions, particularly G/C to A/T transitions, due to the effect of EMS [12]. EMS primarily induces transition point mutations (purine-to-purine or pyrimidine-to-pyrimidine, especially GC→AT), influencing recently identified rice genetic loci responsible for EMS tolerance and mutagenesis efficiency, thus explaining differential mutation rates across strains [13].

Mutagenesis remains a vital tool for improving rice agronomic architecture, offering a pathway to modify traits like plant height, tillering, and grain quality without altering the genetic background of elite varieties [14]. Studies using gamma irradiation have successfully induced semi-dwarfism and early maturity in traditional landraces, facilitating the development of high-yielding, lodge-resistant cultivars [15]. Comparatively, ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) has been instrumental in generating high-density mutant libraries, such as the Samba Mahsuri resource, which produced significant variations in grain length, panicle architecture, and flag leaf angle [16]. These physical and chemical agents allow for the selection of mutants with enhanced photosynthetic efficiency and optimized harvest indices, which are essential for increasing global rice productivity [17].

In this study, the researchers seeks to embark on an interdisciplinary approach that combines gamma rays and EMS mutagens in order to unleash the hidden genetic diversity in rice germplasm [18]. One of the key aspects of this research is the estimation of heritability, which plays an important role in understanding the extent to which genetic factors contribute to variability in a particular trait [19]. The outcome of this research regarding heritability estimates will act as a beacon to guide breeders in selecting suitable mutants for breeding. In addition to that, this study seeks to explore the estimation of genetic advance due to desirable characters [20]. The information will play an important role in understanding the possible gain in yield that will result from the introduction of genetic variations through mutagens to facilitate the breeding of rice for cultivation.

It also casts a wider spectrum to include the analysis of genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) and phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV). These factors will help attain a deeper conception of genetic variation and the role of environmental factors, thus creating a holistic outlook of induced mutations under gamma rays and EMS, in terms of their real possibilities, as seen in 1953 by Burton and DeVane [21]. Inextricably connected with these is the search for correlations between different traits of agronomic significance. Correlation analysis functions as a compass which helps in navigating, between correlated variables, in making a decision on improving multiple traits jointly, in creating a productive synthesis of correlated factors, thereby providing a guide to breeders on breeding mutants, in accordance with Dewey and Lu [22]. In addition, it also includes cluster analysis, which is a technique aimed at making a breedable categorization of mutants on the basis of genetic similarity. Cluster analysis, in accordance with Mahalanobis [23], helps in making breedable selections, which will help in navigating between different genetic environments resulting from gamma rays and EMS mutagens.

Mean performance evaluation then culminates this study, allowing for a holistic perspective on the total agronomic effectiveness of the induced mutants. This method also represents a workable measure for adaptability and effectiveness of the developed rice types [24]. Including a novel component in this investigation is the inclusion of a concern for parabolic distribution. Parabolic distributions of M3-M4 traits provided a comprehensive view of selection-driven shifts toward superior ranges in key agronomic parameters [4].

This study brings into focus the integration of various factors in genetics and agronomy to identify a whole range of factors for developing better varieties of the rice crop. By applying the use of gamma-ray and EMS mutagens alongwith using heritability, genetic advance, genotypic and phenotypic coefficient of variation, mean performance, parabolic distribution function, correlation analysis on M3& M4 stages of mutation, the canvas will help to generate climate resilient rice varieties that can ensure a better future for food security.

Materials and methods

In the project study, dry dormant seeds of commercial rice cultivars, Super Basmati and Noor Basmati, containing approximately 13% moisture content, were subjected to physical and chemical mutagenesis following standard mutation breeding protocols [5]. The seeds were irradiated using gamma rays emitted from a Caesium-137 (137Cs) source. The irradiation doses ranged from 150 Gy to 300 Gy with 50 Gy incremental increases, as commonly adopted in rice mutation breeding to induce optimal genetic variability while minimizing lethality [10].

For chemical mutagenesis, ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) treatment was performed following established seed-soaking and exposure protocols with minor modifications [25]. Seeds of both varieties were retained in 500 mL beakers and soaked in distilled water at room temperature for 20 h, maintaining a water level approximately 5 cm above the seed surface. After decanting the soaking water, seeds were treated with 50 mL EMS solution at concentrations of 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, and 1.25% (v/v). The EMS treatments were conducted for 12 h at room temperature to induce point mutations, primarily base substitutions [26].

Following treatment, EMS solutions were carefully decanted, and the seeds were thoroughly washed with ultrapure water (100 mL, five times for four minutes each), followed by washing with distilled water (200 mL, four times for fifteen minutes each). Subsequently, the treated seeds were rinsed under running tap water for four hours to eliminate residual mutagen and reduce physiological damage prior to planting [5].

After exposure to gamma radiation and EMS, twenty seeds per treatment along with untreated controls were placed on filter paper in Petri plates using 5 mL distilled water. The Petri plates were incubated at 25 °C for five days, after which seed germination percentage was recorded. Seedling traits, including shoot length and root length, were determined using the sandwich blotter technique, which is widely used for early seedling vigor assessment in mutagenized populations [26]. For each dose and variety, twenty seeds were planted in three replications along with untreated controls under two factorial completely randomized design [27]. Seeds were positioned vertically between two moist blotting papers supported by PVC racks placed in plastic trays containing sufficient distilled water to maintain moisture. Shoot and root lengths were measured after two weeks of growth.

For raising the M₁ generation, approximately 2,000 seeds from each mutagenic treatment, along with untreated controls were sown in nursery beds. Thirty-day-old seedlings were transplanted in the field using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications, maintaining a plant to row 10 cm aparat and row to row 30 cm apart. At maturity, panicle fertility percentage was recorded from ten randomly selected guarded plants per treatment per replication. The collected data were analyzed using a two-factor RCBD with Statistix 8 software, following standard biometrical approaches for mutation breeding experiments [28].

Sensitivity to mutagens was assessed by estimating the lethal dose for 50% of plants (LD₅₀) in the M₁ generation by relating panicle fertility percentage to mutagen dose using appropriate dose–response models. LD₅₀ estimation is considered a reliable indicator for selecting effective mutagen doses in rice improvement programs [10, 26].

Large M₂ populations were grown dose-wise, and selection was carried out on a single-plant basis, which is the most efficient strategy for identifying desirable mutants in self-pollinated crops such as rice [29]. Parental checks were planted after every tenth row to facilitate frequent comparisons during selection. Progeny testing was conducted to confirm the heritability of selected traits in the M₂ generation. Re-selection in the M₃ generation was performed to stabilize promising mutant progenies, while selected lines were further evaluated in the M₄ generation for yield performance [5, 25].

Data on agronomic traits including plant height (cm), number of tillers per plant, panicle length (cm), number of seeds per panicle, 1000-grain weight (g), panicle fertility %, and grain yield per plant (g). Data were subjected to statistical analysis using R software (version 4.3.2) comprising analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Linear regression model was used to estimate LD50 for various attributes in M1 generation. Genetic parameters including genotypic (GCV) and phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV), were estimated to quantify the extent of genetic variability within the population. Broad-sense heritability (H2b.s) and genetic advance (GA) as a percentage of the mean were calculated to evaluate the efficiency of selection for the traits under study. Multivariate analysis were employed to further assess genetic divergence. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to identify the primary traits contributing to total phenotypic variance. Interrelationship between traits was determined through the estimation of genotypic (rg) and phenotypic (rp) correlation coefficients. Finally genetic distanc were evaluated using Mahalanobis D2 statistics and hierarchical cluster analysis [28].

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) showed various impacts of mutagen dose (Factor A), genotype (Factor B), and their interaction (A×B) on 137Cs and EMS mutagenesis for various traits of rice. At 137Cs mutagenesis, the mean sum of square for all traits was genotypes, specifically for panicle fertility with a value of 712.85, followed by shoot length with 192.02 and then germination with 195.25, with moderate contribution from mutagen dose, primarily shoot length with 139.54 and then germination with 120.0. The presence of A×B interactions showed variability among genotypes in reaction to 137Cs mutagenesis, thus providing scope for selections. Variations driven by genotypes due to gamma ray mutagenesis have also been documented among rice and several cereals, wherein mutation depends intensely on the genetic background [4, 8].

Conversely, EMS treatment showed a remarkable trend whereingenotype remained the predominant source of variation for vegetative parameters like germination (507.0), root length (47.59), and shoot length (49.23), panicle fertility was dominantly influenced by mutagen dose (24,843), surpassing the effects brought about by genotype (1,914) and interactions (1,055).

It appears that EMS preferentially induces a very strong, negative effect on reproduction compared to 137Cs, which caused more moderate and balanced effects. As previously shown, the high mutagenic activity of EMS can be attributed to its property of inducing point mutations through alkylation, often interfering with gametogenesis and fertility [32, 33]. Notably, the effects of interactions were significant under both mutagens emphasizes the presence of genotypic variability with regard to tolerance, which can be harnessed in mutation breeding and result in the recognition of desirable genotypes with superior tolerance. The results obtained here reinforce that while gamma rays may confer more overall and more stable mutational effects, EMS prefers more precise effects on specific traits, specifically on reproduction, and as such should be properly optimized within mutation breeding protocols [18].Based on the results presented in Figs. 2 and 3 and a clear and dose-dependent reduction in seed germination percentage was observed with increasing levels of 137Cs gamma irradiation and EMS treatments. This decline was more pronounced in the Noor Basmati cultivar compared with Super Basmati, indicating a higher sensitivity of Noor Basmati to both physical and chemical mutagens. Similar reductions in germination with increasing mutagen doses have previously been reported in rice, confirming the inhibitory effects of mutagens on early seed viability [34, 35].

Root length and shoot length are widely recognized as reliable indicators for evaluating the biological effects of mutagens in the M₁ generation [30]. In the present study, a substantial reduction in root length was observed in Noor Basmati, amounting to 45.17% at 200 Gy gamma irradiation and 52.96% at 0.50% EM.

In contrast, Super Basmati exhibited comparatively lower reductions under the same treatments (Figs. 2 and 3). Similarly, shoot length reduction was more severe (37.20%) in Noor Basmati at 0.50% EMS, whereas at 200 Gy gamma irradiation, Super Basmati showed a greater reduction in shoot length. The maximum decrease in shoot length was recorded at 1.0% EMS in Noor Basmati, further highlighting its higher sensitivity. These findings indicate that Super Basmati possesses relatively greater resistance to both gamma irradiation and EMS than Noor Basmati.

Overall, both root and shoot lengths were significantly reduced following mutagen treatments compared with untreated controls, which is consistent with earlier findings in rice and other crops [36]. Additionally, previous studies have reported a nearly linear relationship between increasing doses of physical and chemical mutagens and the reduction in shoot length, supporting the dose-dependent effects observed in the present investigation [33].At maturity, panicle fertility percentage was recorded and showed a consistent decline with increasing doses of both gamma rays and EMS. The reduction in panicle fertility followed an almost linear trend in both varieties, in agreement with earlier reports in rice mutation studies [37]. The estimated LD₅₀ values for panicle fertility under gamma irradiation were 267 Gy for Noor Basmati and 274 Gy for Super Basmati, whereas LD₅₀ values for EMS were 0.68% and 0.70% for Noor Basmati and Super Basmati, respectively (Figs. 2 and 3). These results clearly demonstrate that Noor Basmati is slightly more sensitive to both gamma rays and EMS than Super Basmati in terms of reproductive fitness.

Variation in LD₅₀ values among different genotypes of the same species is a common phenomenon in mutation breeding and is influenced by genetic background, physiological condition, seed maturity, and moisture content at the time of mutagen exposure [38]. The increased panicle sterility observed following mutagen treatments has been attributed primarily to chromosomal aberrations and meiotic irregularities induced by physical and chemical mutagens [39]. In the present study, panicle sterility increased progressively with higher doses of both gamma rays and EMS, confirming the dose-dependent nature of mutagen-induced damage.

These findings are in close agreement with earlier reports indicating that gamma irradiation induces high levels of sterility in M₁ rice plants [38]. The induction of more than 60% sterility at 300 Gy gamma irradiation and over 80% fertility reduction at 1.0% EMS, relative to the untreated control, highlights the higher mutagenic potency of EMS compared with gamma irradiation in Basmati rice. While EMS proved highly effective in generating variability, its greater biological damage at higher concentrations underscores the importance of dose optimization in mutation breeding programs.

Regression analysis

Regression analysis demonstrated that seed germination percentage, root length, and shoot length declined progressively with increasing doses of both gamma rays (137Cs) and EMS. These dose–response relationships were best explained by second-order polynomial regression models, with high coefficients of determination (R² = 0.91–0.96), indicating strong predictive accuracy. The suitability of polynomial models for describing mutagen-induced reductions in seedling growth traits has also been reported in EMS-treated rice, where such models were found effective for predicting growth suppression across increasing mutagen concentrations [33].

In contrast, panicle fertility exhibited a sharp, nonlinear decline with increasing mutagen dose, which was more accurately described by exponential decay models (R² = 0.95–0.97) as shown in Fig. 4. Exponential reduction in fertility under gamma irradiation has been widely documented in cereal crops. Recent studies by Gowthami [34] reported that gamma-ray-induced sterility in rice follows a strong negative exponential curve, making exponential regression particularly suitable for estimating lethal dose (LD₅₀) values. Similarly, Ramesh [39] demonstrated that EMS-induced fertility loss in rice is best represented by nonlinear regression models, with exponential functions providing greater accuracy than linear or polynomial fits.

The estimated LD₅₀ values revealed that Noor Basmati (267 Gy for gamma rays and 0.68% EMS) was slightly more sensitive to mutagen treatments compared with Super Basmati (274 Gy and 0.70% EMS, respectively) as shown in Fig. 4. These findings are in close agreement with recent global reports, which recommend gamma irradiation doses in the range of 250–300 Gy [34] and EMS concentrations of 0.5–0.8% [33, 39] as optimal for inducing genetic variability in rice while maintaining manageable levels of biological damage. The consistency of these results further validates the reliability of regression-based approaches for mutagen dose optimization in rice mutation breeding programs.Adopting a dual-modeling approach using second-order polynomial regressions to quantify gradual declines in seedling growth (germination, root length, shoot length) and exponential decay models to capture abrupt fertility loss provides a robust, biologically meaningful framework for dose response analysis in mutation breeding. This combined strategy improves the precision of LD₅₀ estimation and supports objective mutagen-dose selection for breeding programs, and has been advocated in recent mutation-breeding literature as a best-practice analytical pipeline for optimizing mutagen treatments [29, 40].

Overall mean performance of M3 and M4 populations

To analyze the overall mean performance comparison, parabolic distribution was conducted for each studied trait in the M₃ and M₄ populations as shown in Fig. 5. Selection of rice mutants in the M₄ population revealed a slight increase in plant height (0.92%) when compared to the M₃ population. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of mutants in both the M₃ (37.0%) and M₄ (41.9%) populations were found to have heights within the range of 116.1–130.8 cm, indicating a consistent distribution of plant stature across generations [28, 41].

Parabolic distribution of M3 and M4 populations indicating the percentage of the population falling within specific ranges due to directional selection. These traits include: A Plant height (cm), B Tillers per plant, C Panicle length (cm), D Panicle fertility%, E Number of seeds per panicle, F 1000-Grain Weight (gm), G Grain yield per plant (gm)

Tillers per plant increased by 10.38% in the M₄ population compared to M₃, with the majority of mutants in M₃ (43.1%) ranging from 14.0 to 20.5 tillers and in M₄ (36.7%) ranging from 20.5 to 27.0 tillers. This shift reflects effective selection pressure for high tiller number, which is a known contributor to yield potential in rice [42].

In terms of panicle length, the mean performance of rice mutants showed a negligible increase (0.32%) in the M₄ population compared to M₃. However, the majority of mutants were in the 26.3–28.6 cm range in M₃ (44.2%) and shifted to 28.6–30.9 cm in M₄ (49.0%). Variation in panicle length contributes to changes in grain number per panicle and overall yield, a key focus in agronomic character studies [41].

Panicle fertility% increased by 3.72% in the M₄ population compared to M₃, though most mutants remained within the same general fertility ranges across populations (M₃: 80–90% at 74.7%, M₄: 48.3%). Seed set and fertility directly impact grain yield and are traits often targeted during selection in breeding programs [43].

The number of seeds per panicle displayed a 3.1% increase in M₄ relative to M₃, partly attributed to selecting mutants with higher seed counts. The majority of mutants were in the 119–151.5 range in both populations, but the M₄population had ~ 2–3% more individuals in the higher (151.5–216.5) range due to removal of lower grain-bearing plants [28].Due to the rejection of low-grain plants, the M₄ population showed a substantial 27.19% increase in 1000-grain weight compared to M₃. The maximum grain weight ranged from 15.92 to 23.24 g in M₃ and shifted to 23.24–30.56 g in M₄. Thousand-grain weight is highly heritable and can be effectively improved through selection practices [42].

The M₄ population exhibited a significant 23.63% increase in grain yield per plant compared to M₃. In M₃, the highest grain yield ranges were 22.9–28.2%, while in M₄ the highest categories (28.2–33.5%) comprised 49.5% of mutants. This enhancement is consistent with studies showing that direct selection for yield and its attributing components (tillers, grain weight, fertility) in mutant and breeding populations can substantially elevate grain yield in advanced generations [41–43].

Genotypic and phenotypic coefficient of variation

Genetic diversity is vital for breeders aiming to enhance crop productivity. Induced mutations create genetic variation within existing germplasm, facilitating breeding efforts [44]. Phenotypic variance, driven by both genetics and the environment, impacts the evaluation of quantitative traits essential for yield improvement. Improvement in desired quantitative attributes depends on the occurrence of genetic variability in the population [45]. Consequently, genetic variability, play pivotal role in trait improvement and selection. The present study demonstrated significant differences among M₃ and M₄ progenies subjected to varying doses of mutagens, i.e., ¹³⁷Cs and EMS. For EMS treatment, Super Basmati at 0.50% EMS had GCV (5.55%) and PCV (5.56%) in M₃ and GCV (7.80%) and PCV (8.64%) in M₄. Regarding grain yield per plant, 200 Gy radiation dose was selected for Super Basmati due to GCV (46.8%) and PCV (60.1%) in M₃ and GCV (43.9%) and PCV (53.0%) in M₄, while Noor Basmati at 300 Gy had GCV (20.6%) and PCV (26.8%) in M₃ and GCV (22.4%) and PCV (26.5%) in M₄. EMS treatment at 1.0% EMS in Super Basmati had GCV (21.0%) and PCV (28.7%) in M₃ and GCV (25.2%) and PCV (26.0%) in M₄. Noor Basmati at 0.50% EMS showed GCV (19.0%) and PCV (28.2%) in M₃ and GCV (16.4%) and PCV (19.9%) in M₄. Although PCV values were higher than GCV, the differences were small, indicating minimal environmental influence. High GCV and PCV values indicate substantial variation among progenies, providing opportunities for efficient selection due to higher genetic diversity [49, 50].

Heritability and genetic advance across traits and mutagen doses

The scatter plots of heritability (h²) versus genetic advance (GA) across M₃–M₄ mutant populations (Fig. 7) illustrate the genetic variability induced by gamma rays and EMS treatments in rice. The relationship between h² and GA is a fundamental indicator of the genetic architecture underlying traits and the efficacy of selection. In general, traits exhibiting high h² coupled with high GA suggest predominance of additive gene action, implying that direct selection will be effective in advancing these traits in subsequent generations. Conversely, traits with high h² but low GA indicate non-additive gene effects, while low h² and GA highlight strong environmental influence and limited scope for selection [51]. Regarding Plant height both varieties displayed moderate-to-high h² (52–97%), reflecting the genetic contribution to height variability. However, the corresponding GA was modest (11–27), indicating that expected genetic gain is limited despite heritability. This trend suggests that while plant height is genetically controlled, selection will result in gradual improvements rather than rapid shifts. Similar results were reported in rice mutant studies where mutagenesis created useful variation for semi-dwarf ideotypes but gains per cycle were modest [25].

Scatter plot depicting heritability and genetic advance across different traits and mutagen doses

Tillers per plant demonstrated the most favorable genetic profile, with both Super and Noor showing high heritability (> 90%). Super, however, exhibited consistently higher GA values (up to 29) compared to Noor. This combinationreflects strong additive genetic variance, confirming that tillering is a highly heritable and improvable yield component. These findings corroborate earlier mutation breeding studies, where tiller number was identified as a reliable selection target [52].

For panicle length, heritability estimates were generally high (70–97%), but GA remained low (~ 10–13). This suggests that although panicle length is heritable, it is under non-additive gene control, leading to limited expected selection response. Such patterns are consistent with reports by earlier researchers [53], who found that panicle traits in rice mutants often show restricted additive variance.

Fertility percentage recorded very high h² (> 95%) but very low GA (2–6). This combination highlights that while fertility is genetically stable, improvement potential is limited. Physiological constraints and environmental buffering are likely involved, reducing the efficiency of direct selection. Similar outcomes were observed by researchers [54], who noted that fertility traits tend to be influenced by dominance and epistatic interactions.

Regarding 1000- grain weight, Noor consistently exhibited heritability (46–98%) and moderate-to-high GA (15–28), whereas Super showed more variability in h² (42–98%). Nevertheless, both varieties demonstrated clear scope for additive selection gains in TGW. This confirms TGW as one of the most stable and reliable selection traits for rice improvement under induced mutagenesis [25].

In grain yield per plant the scatter patterns revealed moderate-to-high h² with corresponding GA values. Yield being a polygenic trait, this outcome was expected. Importantly, the plots suggest that yield improvement will be most efficient when indirect selection targets yield components such as tillers per plant and 1000-grain weight. This aligns with FAO/IAEA (2024) recommendations on using component traits as proxies for complex yield selection.

Regarding varietal comparison, between Super and Noor showed clear differences. Super generally produced higher GA values, reflecting greater mutagen-induced variability and stronger response to selection. In contrast, Noor maintained extremely stable heritability across traits and doses, but with lower GA, suggesting slower progress under direct selection. This highlights the influence of genotype × mutagen interactions, as also noted by Singh in 2022 [55].

The integrated results identify tillers per plant and 1000-grain weight as the most promising selection targets due to their combination of high h² and GA. Plant height and grain yield per plant offer moderate improvement potential, while panicle length and fertility percentage will require alternative approaches such as recurrent selection or hybridization. The findings underscore the role of induced mutagenesis in generating useful additive variance in rice, particularly in the M₃–M₄ generations where selection pressure becomes most effective (FAO/IAEA, 2024) [56].

Discussion

This discussion synthesizes the study’s key findings on genetic variability, yield improvements, and selection efficiency, contextualizing them within existing mutation breeding program in rice.Regarding mutagen effects; both gamma rays (150–300 Gy) and EMS (0.25–1.25%) induced dose-dependent reductions in germination, seedling vigor, and panicle fertility, consistent with earlier reports in rice and other cereals [27, 30, 34].Noor Basmati showedgreater sensitivity (LD50: 267 Gy gamma, 0.68% EMS) than Super Basmati (274 Gy, 0.70% EMS), confirming genotype-dependent variation in mutagen tolerance [20, 38]. EMS exerted stronger effects on reproductive traits might be due to point mutations disrupting gametogenesis, while gamma rays produced broader chromosomal changes, aligning with prior reports on alkylating agents versus ionizing radiation in rice [33]. Optimal doses around 250–300 Gy gamma and 0.5–0.8% EMS balance variability induction with manageable sterility [5, 34], confirming regression models (polynomial for growth, exponential for fertility) as reliable for LD50 estimation [33]. Selection across M₁-M₄ generations resulted in 753 stable mutant progenies, with increase in mean grain yield per plant in M₄ relative to M₃. Comparable yield improvements have been reported in advanced rice mutant populations following directional selection [41]. In M4 mean grain yield per plant rising 23.63% (highest: 32.5 g/plant from 0.25% EMS in Noor Basmati), driven by 27.19% gains in 1000-grain weight and 10.38% in tillers per plant. These traits are widely recognized as major contributor to rice yield [52, 59]. High GCV/PCV in these traits under specific doses (e.g., 46.8% GCV yield at 200 Gy Super Basmati) enabled effective single-plant selection [21, 56], as minimal GCV-PCV gaps indicate low environmental influence. The observed rightward shift in parabolic distributions in M₄ populations further reflects successful directional selection and trait stabilization, consistent with reports on advanced mutant generations [4]. In case of genetic parameters; tillers per plant and 1000-grain weight exhibited high heritability (> 90%) with substantial genetic advance (up to 29%), signaling additive gene action ideal for direct selection [19, 60]. In contrast panicle fertility showed high h² but low GA due to non-additive effects and limited selection efficiency [56]. Super Basmati consistently expressed higher genetic advance across traits, underscoring genotype × mutagen interactions previously documented in cereals [8, 61]. These parameters validate mutagenesis for broadening Basmati’s narrow genetic base, supporting FAO/IAEA strategies for component-based yield enhancement. Regarding multivariate insights; PCA revealed PC1-PC3 explaining 50.5% of total variation and highest contributing attributes were tillers per plant, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield. Positive correlations between grain yield and these traits (r = 0.42–0.50) validate indirect selection strategies, while negative association between tillers per plant and seed per panicle suggest physiological trade-offs among yield components [22, 62]. Cluster analysis separated 406 high-yield M4 progenies (Cluster III) from low-yield (Cluster II) based on Mahalanobis D² [23], providing a rational basis for advancing elite lines to multi-location evaluation under Pakistan’s diverse agro-climatic conditions [63].Overall, the results confirm the effectiveness of mutation breeding in broadening the narrow genetic base of Basmati rice and align with FAO/IAEA strategies advocating component-based selection for sustainable yield improvement [2].

Conclusion

Gamma irradiation and EMS mutagenesis effectively broadened the genetic base of elite Basmati rice cultivars Super Basmati and Noor Basmati, generating substantial heritable variation for yield enhancement. Targeted selection across M1-M4 generations yielded 753 stable progenies with 23.63% higher mean grain yield per plant, primarily driven by improvements in tillers per plant (10.38%) and 1000-grain weight (27.19%), traits exhibiting high heritability (> 90%) and genetic advance. Optimal doses (250–300 Gy gamma; 0.5–0.8% EMS) balanced variability induction with biological damage, as confirmed by LD50 estimates and dose-response models, while Noor Basmati showed slightly higher sensitivity. Multivariate analyses pinpointed tillers and grain weight as primary yield contributors, with cluster III containing 406 high-yielding lines suitable for further breeding and disease screening.These mutants address genetic uniformity limiting Basmati adaptation to climate challenges in Pakistan, aligning with national priorities for sustainable rice production and SDGs on food security. The stable lines offer valuable germplasm for functional genomics, hybrid development, and multi-location trials to accelerate varietal release.
