Schwann cell derived extracellular vesicles are multifunctional nanotherapeutic mediators for diabetic oral mucosal wound healing
Authors: Zihang Xu, Haowen Qi, Xinyu Zhang, Fang Qu, Yaqin Wu, Chun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchwann cell derived extracellular vesicles (SC-EVs) have emerged as a specialized extracellular vesicle subtype with intrinsic neuroregenerative, neuroimmune, and neurovascular regulatory capacities, making them particularly relevant for diabetic oral mucosal wound healing. Unlike more widely studied extracellular vesicles derived from mesenchymal stem cells, SC-EVs originate from the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system and therefore retain biological programs directly related to axonal repair, neurotrophic support, and nerve-associated tissue regeneration. Diabetic oral mucosal wounds are characterized by persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, oxidative stress, extracellular matrix dysregulation, and neuropathy-associated defects in epithelial repair. SC-EVs contain a distinctive cargo of proteins, RNAs, and lipids, including neurotrophic factors such as nerve growth factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor, regulatory microRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-146a, and antioxidant molecules that collectively modulate these pathological processes. This review first summarizes the pathophysiological features of diabetic oral mucosal wounds, with emphasis on neurovascular and neuroimmune disruption. It then discusses the biogenesis, molecular composition, and functional mechanisms of SC-EVs, highlighting their roles in inflammatory recalibration, angiogenesis, neuroregeneration, and redox homeostasis. A dedicated discussion of SC-EV-specific cargo composition is included to clarify why SC-EVs represent a particularly suitable extracellular vesicle source for neuropathy-associated oral wound repair. Finally, we evaluate current progress in scalable production, targeted engineering, and biomaterial-assisted delivery, while outlining remaining limitations related to standardization, potency assessment, long-term safety, and clinical translation. By integrating glial biology with extracellular vesicle nanomedicine, this review provides a focused framework for developing SC-EV-based therapeutics for diabetic oral mucosal lesions.Keywords: Schwann cell, Extracellular vesicles, Nanotherapeutics, Diabetic oral mucosal wound healing, Neuroregeneration, Neurovascular coupling, Immunomodulation, Angiogenesis

Introduction

Schwann cell derived extracellular vesicles (SC-EVs) have emerged as a promising yet still underexplored nano-therapeutic platform for regenerative dentistry, particularly in neuropathy-associated tissue repair. Wound repair in the oral mucosa proceeds through a tightly orchestrated sequence of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling, with each phase depending on coordinated crosstalk among platelets, immune cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, epithelial progenitors, and peripheral nerve fibers [1–3]. In healthy individuals, the oral environment supports efficient repair through dense vascularization, rapid epithelial turnover, salivary growth factors, and a specialized mucosal immune milieu that clears pathogens while limiting excessive inflammation [4]. In diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia disrupts this balance at multiple levels [5]. Elevated glucose alters platelet function and coagulation dynamics, delays clot stabilization, promotes endothelial oxidative stress, and perturbs inflammatory resolution, leading to delayed re-epithelialization and impaired tissue restoration [6–8].

Healing efficiency also varies across anatomical sites within the oral cavity, partly reflecting differences in vascularity and innervation. Tongue wounds, which possess dense sensory innervation and abundant vascular supply, generally re-epithelialize rapidly with limited scarring, whereas palatal, gingival, and buccal mucosal injuries may heal more slowly [5, 9]. This regional variation underscores the regulatory role of the peripheral nervous system in local tissue repair. Diabetes-associated neural dysfunction may therefore impair re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and immune resolution [10, 11]. Importantly, peripheral nerves do not merely provide sensation; they release neuropeptides and trophic mediators that influence endothelial cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells. Accordingly, a therapeutic strategy that restores neurovascular and neuroimmune communication may be especially relevant for diabetic oral mucosal wounds.

The rationale for focusing on SC-EVs rather than extracellular vesicles from non-neural sources is rooted in Schwann cell biology. Schwann cells are the principal glia of the peripheral nervous system and are essential for axonal support, myelination, neurotrophic factor release, debris clearance, and nerve-guided tissue repair [12–14]. In addition to direct cell–cell interactions, Schwann cells secrete extracellular vesicles, nanoscale lipid bilayer particles that transport proteins, RNAs, lipids, and metabolites to neighboring cells. Because SC-EVs reflect the identity and repair programs of their parent cells, they exhibit a distinctive cargo composition that may include neurotrophic proteins such as nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), regulatory microRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-146a, and antioxidant or stress-response molecules that together temper excessive inflammation, support angiogenic signaling, and promote axonal regeneration [15, 16]. Compared with cell-based Schwann cell therapy, SC-EVs offer practical advantages including lower tumorigenic risk, easier storage, reduced immunogenicity, and compatibility with biomaterial-based delivery platforms [17, 18].

This review first summarizes the principal pathophysiological alterations underlying diabetic oral mucosal wounds, with particular attention to neuropathy-associated neurovascular and neuroimmune dysfunction. It then focuses on SC-EVs by covering their biogenesis, cargo composition, isolation, and disease-related impairment; delineating their mechanistic roles in immunomodulation, neovascularization, neuroregeneration, and redox homeostasis; and assessing engineering strategies that enhance their targeting and delivery. By explicitly distinguishing SC-EVs from more broadly studied mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs, this review aims to clarify the biological motivation and translational value of SC-EV-based nanotherapeutics in diabetic oral mucosal repair.

Pathophysiology of diabetic oral mucosal wound healing

The healing trajectory of oral mucosal wounds in diabetes is shaped by both systemic metabolic disturbances and profound alterations within the local microenvironment [19–21]. Unlike acute wounds in non‑diabetic individuals, diabetic oral lesions often become trapped in a state of unresolved inflammation, insufficient vascular and neural repair, and dysregulated matrix remodeling [22, 23]. Understanding these interrelated derangements is essential for designing therapies—such as SC-EV-based nano-platforms, that can concurrently overcome the multiple barriers to effective repair.

Clinical and tissue-level features

Oral mucosal wounds represent a unique subtype of chronic wounds, influenced by both systemic metabolic abnormalities and local microenvironmental disruption [24, 25]. These lesions differ markedly from cutaneous wounds in histological structure, healing kinetics, microbial exposure, and immune landscape [26]. In diabetes, persistent hyperglycemia impairs wound healing by inducing oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, immune imbalance, and peripheral neuropathy [27]. Collectively, these insults compromise nearly all aspects of oral wound biology. A comprehensive understanding of these diabetic-specific pathophysiological changes is essential not only for elucidating the delayed healing phenotype but also for informing the rational design of targeted interventions, such as SC-EVs, which may address the multifactorial impairments within the diabetic microenvironment.

Under physiological conditions, the oral mucosa exhibits rapid healing, supported by rich vascularization, abundant salivary growth factors, and a distinct mucosal immune profile that favors regeneration over fibrosis [28, 29]. However, under diabetic conditions, persistent hyperglycemia promotes advanced glycation of proteins, endothelial injury, and chronic oxidative damage [30–32]. These systemic insults are compounded by local factors such as reduced saliva secretion, microbial dysbiosis, and basement membrane disruption that collectively create a hostile environment for tissue healing. Diabetic oral wounds exhibit hallmarks of delayed re-epithelialization, impaired angiogenesis, excessive inflammation, and deficient neurovascular regeneration [33]. Additionally, the interactions between oral epithelial cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts become dysregulated, weakening the critical cellular crosstalk necessary for coordinated tissue regeneration [34].

The healing process is further complicated by compromised barrier integrity and reduced basal cell proliferation in diabetic oral mucosa, both of which are closely associated with aberrant expression of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and adhesion molecules [19, 35]. Collectively, these pathophysiological traits distinguish diabetic oral wounds from both normal oral injuries and diabetic skin wounds, underscoring their classification as a distinct clinical entity and therapeutic challenge. Recognizing this complexity highlights the need for multidimensional regenerative strategies, such as SC-EV-based nano-therapies, that can concurrently modulate immune imbalance, stimulate angiogenesis, support neural repair, and restore redox homeostasis. Phase-specific molecular and cellular disruptions

In diabetes, each phase of the four‑stage wound‑healing cascade hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling, is disrupted by hyperglycemia‑induced molecular derangements and cellular dysfunctions [36]. Platelet glycation and altered coagulation dynamics prolong the hemostatic phase, delaying the formation of a stable provisional matrix [37]. The inflammatory phase becomes pathologically persistent, characterized by excessive accumulation of neutrophils and pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes that secrete high levels of TNF-α, IL-1 β, and IL-6, thereby maintaining NF-kappaB activation. Meanwhile, ROS overproduction and impaired antioxidant defenses impede the transition toward inflammatory resolution and tissue repair [38, 39]. Because macrophage activation exists along a continuous and context-dependent spectrum, this review uses functional descriptions such as pro-inflammatory, inflammatory-resolving, and pro-repair macrophage phenotypes whenever possible.

During the proliferation phase, endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) fail to mobilize effectively or establish functional neovascular networks due to advanced glycation end product-receptor for advanced glycation end product (AGE-RAGE) signaling, reduced VEGF/Ang‑1 levels, and impaired Tie2/eNOS/AKT/ERK phosphorylation [40–42]. Concurrently, abarrent matrix metalloproteinase-2/9 (MMP‑2/9) activity degrades collagen and laminin, while aberrant lumican overexpression hinders basal cell adhesion. In parallel, fibroblast dysfunction and matrix fragmentation further compromise granulation tissue formation and structural integrity [43–45]. Finally, the remodeling phase remains incomplete, as scar‑free regeneration gives way to fibrotic matrix deposition, persistent ECM degradation, and inadequate poor reinnervation, setting stage for non‑healing, recurrent lesions [46, 47]. As summarized in Table 1, diabetes disrupts the sequential wound-healing phases in oral mucosa through a concerted array of impairments. This multiphasic and multifactorial dysfunction presents a fundamental challenge to conventional therapies, necessitating nano‑enabled strategies capable of multi‑targeted intervention at the molecular and cellular scale.

Diabetic neuropathy and its impact on oral mucosal repair

Sensory innervation plays a central role in coordinating wound repair through neuropeptide‑mediated vasodilation, immune modulation, and angiogenic guidance [48, 49]. In diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia induces Schwann cell (SC) dysfunction, axonal degeneration, and reduced synthesis of neurotrophic factors [50, 51]. The consequent loss of substance P and calcitonin gene‑related peptide (CGRP) release diminishes microvascular perfusion and impairs EPC recruitment [52]. Elevated pigment epithelium–derived factor (PEDF) suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signaling, further impairing EPC function [53], while downregulation of the SEMA3C/NRP2/Hedgehog axis and FOXM1-driven SEMA3C expression disrupts pro-repair macrophage activation states and vessel formation [54]. Microcirculatory dysregulation leads to tissue hypoxia, sustained hypoxia-inducible factor-1 α (HIF-1 α) activation, and perpetuation of pro-inflammatory pathways, including PKC/HMGB1/RAGE/NF-kappaB, reinforcing a chronic non-healing state [55]. Mitochondrial Na + overload in endothelial cells exacerbates ROS generation and limits cell migration, while SIRT3 deficiency amplifies oxidative damage and necroptotic signaling [56]. Experimental agents such as baicalin and monensin A have shown that restoring neurovascular signaling axes can partially rescue diabetic wound closure, emphasizing the therapeutic value of interventions that reestablish neurovascular-immune communication [57, 58]. Applications of SC‑EVs in diabetic oral mucosal repair

SC-EVs represent a convergence of glial biology and nanomedicine, offering a cell-free therapeutic modality that captures key regenerative and immunomodulatory programs of Schwann cells. In diabetic oral mucosal wounds, chronic hyperglycemia, persistent inflammation, defective angiogenesis, oxidative stress, and neuropathy interact to stall healing. SC-EVs are particularly attractive in this setting because they are derived from cells that naturally coordinate peripheral nerve repair and neurovascular communication. This section discusses seven thematic areas that delineate the mechanistic basis, translational strategies, and emerging challenges of SC-EV-based interventions for diabetic oral lesions.

Regenerative properties of SCs

SCs display remarkable plasticity upon peripheral nerve injury, abandoning their mature myelinating phenotype in favor of a repair-competent state driven by extensive transcriptional reprogramming [59, 60]. During this process, key regulators such as c‑Jun and Sox2 become upregulated, whereas myelin‑specific proteins including myelin basic protein (MBP), protein zero (P0) and myelin‑associated glycoprotein (MAG) are downregulated [61–63]. This phenotypic switch enables SCs to re‑enter the cell cycle and secrete a host of neurotrophic factors, notably NGF, BDNF and glial cell line‑derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF)—that bind Trk receptors on injured axons to drive sprouting, survival and guidance [64–66]. Concurrently, SCs remodel the ECM environment by producing laminin isoforms and collagen IV, while balancing MMP‑2 and MMP‑9 activity through tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMP1 and TIMP2) [67, 68]. These secreted proteins create a permissive scaffold that supports regenerating axons and associated endothelial sprouts. Moreover, SCs engage in gap-junction-mediated calcium signaling with adjacent fibroblasts and endothelial cells, synchronizing cellular responses across the injury niche and amplifying repair cues [69–71]. Collectively, these attributes establish SCs as master regulators of coordinated neural and vascular repair, a multifaceted program that can be partially transferred through the nanoscale cargo of SC-EVs.

Direct participation of SCs in wound healing

Upon cutaneous or mucosal injury, SCs rapidly dedifferentiate and migrate into the wound bed in response to local growth factors such as platelet‑derived growth factor (PDGF‑BB) and epidermal growth factor [72]. In their reparative state, SCs secrete transforming growth factor‑β3 (TGF‑β3) which engages a Smad2/3–mediated signaling cascade distinct from classical TGF‑β1/2 pathways, promoting controlled fibroblast migration while limiting expression of contractile markers such as α‑smooth muscle actin and thereby reducing fibrosis [73]. SCs also release VEGF and fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), which stimulate neovascularization and ensure adequate perfusion to the proliferating epithelial front [74, 75]. Concurrently, SC-derived MMP-9 clears obstructive ECM fragments to facilitate epithelial migration [76]. Immune modulation is another important function of SCs: chemokines such as CCL2 recruit monocytes, and paracrine interleukin-10 supports inflammatory resolution and pro-repair macrophage programs, accelerating debris clearance and limiting excessive inflammation [77]. In diabetes, hyperglycemia undermines these functions by suppressing SC dedifferentiation, diminishing neurotrophic and chemotactic factor secretion, and maintaining a pro-inflammatory milieu [78, 79]. This diabetes-induced dysfunction of endogenous SCs creates a therapeutic gap that may be partly filled by exogenous SC-EVs, which deliver bioavailable reparative signals without requiring viable cell engraftment.

Exosome biogenesis and functions in repair

Figure 3 schematically illustrates the biogenesis of SC-EVs, highlighting ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent cargo sorting, tetraspanin-enriched microdomains, and lipid-mediated pathways. Exosomes, a subset of extracellular vesicles measuring approximately 30–150 nm, originate from inward budding of endosomal membranes to form multivesicular bodies (MVBs) [80, 81]. Their cargo sorting involves ESCRT-dependent mechanisms, in which TSG101 and Alix scaffold ubiquitinated protein sequestration, as well as ESCRT-independent mechanisms mediated by tetraspanin microdomains and ceramide generation via neutral sphingomyelinase [82, 83]. MVB trafficking toward the plasma membrane is regulated by Rab GTPases, notably Rab27a/b, Rab11, and Rab35, while SNARE complexes facilitate membrane fusion upon calcium influx through mechanosensitive Piezo1 and purinergic P2X7 receptors [84]. The resulting vesicles possess lipid bilayers enriched in phosphatidylserine, cholesterol, and sphingomyelin and encapsulate proteins, messenger RNAs, microRNAs, and long non-coding RNAs. In wound healing, extracellular vesicles function as versatile intercellular communicators by delivering regulatory RNAs to immune cells, transferring pro-angiogenic factors to endothelial cells, modulating fibroblast behavior through Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β/Smad signaling, and providing neurotrophic cues that enhance axonal sprouting and survival [85–88]. Although mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs have dominated regenerative research, SC-EVs uniquely integrate neurotrophic, angiogenic, and immunomodulatory functions, making them especially relevant for diabetic neuropathic lesions [89].

Neural and vascular repair mediated by SC‑EVs in diabetes

In diabetic oral mucosal wounds, persistent hyperglycemia, oxidative stress and neuropathy collectively impair both nerve and vessel regeneration. SC‑EVs counteract these deficits through a multimodal payload. Neurotrophic proteins such as NGF, BDNF, and GDNF activate TrkA/B/C receptors on neurons and remnant SCs, triggering downstream cascades including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK and PLCγ that enhance cytoskeletal dynamics, growth cone motility, and neuronal survival [90–92]. EV-borne microRNAs, including miR-21, miR-23a, and miR-132, may further facilitate axonal regrowth by modulating PTEN/AKT, GSK3β, and Rac1/Cdc42-related signaling [93, 94]. Meanwhile, pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF, FGF2, and PDGF-BB in SC-EVs can stimulate endothelial proliferation and vessel sprouting [95]. Cargo microRNAs such as miR-126 may relieve SPRED1-mediated inhibition of Ras/ERK, while miR-210 can support endothelial adaptation under hypoxia. Surface phosphatidylserine enhances endothelial uptake via TIM4-related recognition mechanisms, amplifying pro-angiogenic pathways [96, 97]. Preclinical models suggest that SC-EV or SC-exosome-based interventions can promote nerve fiber growth, vascular remodeling, and functional recovery in diabetic or nerve injury settings [98–101]. These findings support SC-EVs as nanoscale delivery systems that co-administer synergistic cues for neurovascular coupling, effectively targeting a core defect in diabetic oral mucosal repair. Classification, biogenesis regulation and diabetic Impairment of SC‑EVs

SC-EVs encompass exosomes (30–150 nm), microvesicles (100–1 000 nm) and apoptotic bodies (1–5 µm), distinguished by origin, size range, and surface markers [100, 101]. Upon injury, mechanical cues activate Piezo1 and P2X7 channels, inducing Ca2+ influx that stimulates ERK1/2 and Rab27a to direct MVB trafficking along RhoA/ROCK-dependent actin tracks. Concurrent activation of calcineurin/NFAT and CaMKII‑YAP axes may upregulate ESCRT components such as Alix and TSG101 and mechanosensitive regulators such as CTGF, thereby boosting vesicle formation. Under diabetic conditions, elevated ROS and reduced SIRT3 activity compromise mitochondrial metabolism, which is essential for energy-dependent EV biogenesis, while AGE-RAGE signaling may disrupt Rab27a expression and ESCRT integrity [102]. Consequently, SC-EV yield may be reduced and cargo composition may be shifted toward a less reparative profile. Emerging interventions include mechanical preconditioning, metabolic rescue with β-ketoglutarate or NAD+ precursors, and genetic or pharmacological enhancement of EV biogenesis pathways to restore vesicle production under hyperglycemic stress.

Molecular composition of SC‑EVs

Proteomic analyses reveal that SC‑EVs carry neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF, GDNF), myelin-associated proteins (MBP, MAG, P0), angiogenic mediators (VEGF, Ang-1, FGF2, PDGF), and ECM modulators (MMP-2, MMP-9, TIMP1, fibronectin) [103]. Recent human SC-EV omics characterization further supports the presence of a complex molecular cargo, including proteins, lipids, and a broad miRNA repertoire relevant to axon growth, neuroprotection, myelination, angiogenesis, neuroinflammation, VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, and PI3K-Akt signaling [104]. Nucleic acid cargo is particularly important because SC-EV-associated miRNAs can regulate repair pathways in recipient cells. For example, SC-derived exosomal miR-21 has been implicated in high-glucose regulation of neurite outgrowth and AKT-related signaling [94], while miR-146a is broadly associated with suppression of excessive NF-kappaB activity [105]. Additional miRNAs such as miR-126, miR-132, and miR-210 may support endothelial activation, neurite extension, and hypoxic adaptation through SPRED1/Ras-ERK, Rac1/Cdc42, and HIF-related mechanisms [106, 107]. The lipidome of SC-EVs, enriched in cholesterol, sphingomyelin, and phosphatidylserine, contributes to membrane stability, cellular uptake, and receptor-specific recognition [104, 107]. This integrated protein-RNA-lipid cargo distinguishes SC-EVs from less neuronally specialized EV sources and provides a mechanistic basis for their coordinated effects on neural, vascular, and immune repair (Table 3).

Translational strategies and clinical considerations

Clinical translation of SC‑EVs requires scalable production and rigorous quality control, and a clearer understanding of upstream mechanisms that regulate EV release and cargo selection. Tangential-flow filtration combined with size-exclusion chromatography under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions can yield high-purity vesicles with reduced protein aggregates [108]. Lyophilization in the presence of cryoprotectants such as trehalose or mannitol may preserve EV integrity for long-term storage and off-the-shelf use [109]. However, manufacturing quality should not be assessed solely by particle number or size distribution. Standardized release criteria should include identity markers, sterility, endotoxin level, residual protein contamination, cargo consistency, and potency assays that reflect neurotrophic, angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant functions. Upstream regulation also requires attention. Hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and AGE-RAGE signaling may alter SC phenotype, Rab-mediated vesicle trafficking, ESCRT-related sorting, and miRNA loading. Therefore, donor cell state, culture conditions, oxygen tension, passage number, and metabolic preconditioning should be reported and standardized. SC-EV engineering for targeted delivery may involve conjugating integrin-binding RGD peptides or tissue-homing ligands to surface proteins, while embedding SC-EVs in hyaluronic acid or collagen hydrogels can ensure controlled release and moisture retention [110]. Injectable thermosensitive polymers such as Pluronic F127 may enable minimally invasive oral delivery [111]. Preclinical safety assessments should examine local irritation, ectopic tissue growth, immune activation, biodistribution, and long-term neurovascular effects [112]. To address diabetic barriers, combination therapies integrating SC-EVs with AGE inhibitors, antioxidant preconditioning, or SIRT3 activation may further improve efficacy. Early clinical trials would benefit from rigorous randomization and objective endpoints such as wound closure rate, epithelial thickness, sensory recovery, vascular density, nerve fiber density, and recurrence rate.

Surface functionalization of SC‑EVs

Native EVs rely on intrinsic membrane lipids and proteins for cellular uptake, yet rational surface modification can dramatically improve targeting toward diabetic wounds and enhance retention within the oral mucosa [99]. Two principal approaches dominate: (1) chemical conjugation of targeting ligands and (2) genetic or metabolic engineering of parent SCs to display affinity peptides on EV membranes.

Chemical conjugation typically employs established “click” chemistry—such as azide–alkyne cycloaddition—to attach small peptides or antibody fragments that recognize injury‑specific markers. For example, RGD motifs bind integrin α_vβ_3, which is upregulated on activated endothelial cells and fibroblasts within wound beds [113]. Likewise, vascular cell adhesion molecule‑1 (VCAM‑1)–targeting peptides enhance EV docking to inflamed endothelium. Optimization of linker length and density is crucial to maintain membrane fluidity or vesicle stability [114].

Alternatively, parent SCs can be genetically modified via lentiviral transduction or CRISPR‑mediated knock‑in to express fusion proteins combining an EV‑enriched transmembrane domain (e.g., Lamp2b) with targeting peptides. Metabolic labeling with azide‑bearing lipids offers a non‑genetic route, enabling subsequent click conjugation of targeting moieties. Both strategies have demonstrated Two‑ to fourfold increases in EV accumulation at target tissues in preclinical models, with minimal off‑target binding and no detectable alterations of SC‑EV cargo composition [115].These surface engineering techniques exemplify the precise controllability afforded by nano‑carrier platforms, allowing the biological tropism of SC‑EVs to be rationally enhanced for specific therapeutic applications in the complex oral wound environment.

Hydrogel and scaffold‑based carriers

Within the dynamic oral environment exposed to saliva flow, mastication, and pH fluctuations, free EVs may undergo rapid clearance. Encapsulating SC-EVs within biomaterial matrices provides structural support, moisture retention, and controlled release. Hydrogels are particularly advantageous because their viscoelasticity can be tuned to match mucosal tissue while delivering vesicles over days to weeks [116, 117]. Administration modalities and dosing regimens

Optimizing the route and schedule of SC‑EV administration is critical to balance therapeutic efficacy and patient comfort. Three main modalities are under investigation: topical application, local injection and integration into dental appliances.

Topical formulations such as gels or films incorporating SC‑EV-loaded hydrogels enable non‑invasive, repeatable dosing [120]. These formulations can be applied after dental procedures or trauma, typically once or twice daily, to maintain therapeutic EV concentrations at the wound interface. Pilot studies report that daily topical application of 1 × 1010 EVs per cm2 accelerates closure by over 30% in diabetic mucosal models, without mucosal irritation or taste disturbances [118].

Local injections, either submucosal or intratissue, deliver concentrated EVs directly into the lesion bed, bypassing clearance. In preclinical rodent studies, a single injection of 5 × 1010 EVs in 50 µL enhanced re‑epithelialization and sensory nerve recovery over two weeks. Fractionated dosing (e.g., 2 injections of 2.5 × 1010 EVs one week apart) can further optimize regenerative outcomes while reducing total EV demand [119].

Finally, incorporation of SC‑EV-loaded carriers into dental appliances or intraoral stents provides sustained release during overnight wear. These devices maintain intimate contact with the wound, combining mechanical protection with continuous therapeutic delivery. Albeit in an early stage,it may be useful for patients undergoing extensive oral surgery or experiencing recurrent mucosal lesions.

Taken together, these engineering and delivery strategies—surface functionalization for enhanced targeting, hydrogel or scaffold carriers for controlled release (Table 4), and flexible administration modalities—constitute a cohesive toolkit to translate SC‑EV therapy into clinical application in diabetic oral mucosal repair.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although SC-EVs show substantial promise, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most available evidence derives from preclinical studies in peripheral nerve injury, diabetic neuropathy, or non-oral wound models, and direct studies in diabetic oral mucosal wounds remain limited. Second, the field still lacks standardized protocols for SC source selection, culture conditions, EV isolation, storage, dosing, and potency testing. Third, diabetic conditions may alter both endogenous SC function and SC-EV cargo, making it necessary to define how hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators influence EV biogenesis and therapeutic quality. Fourth, long-term biodistribution, repeated-dose safety, immunogenicity, and potential off-target effects require systematic evaluation before clinical translation. Future studies should combine omics-based cargo profiling, mechanistic validation in oral mucosal models, standardized functional assays, and biomaterial-guided delivery systems. In addition, comparative studies between SC-EVs and MSC-EVs are needed to determine whether the neuroregenerative advantages of SC-EVs translate into superior outcomes in neuropathy-associated diabetic oral wounds.

Conclusions

Diabetic oral mucosal wounds represent a uniquely challenging regenerative context in which systemic hyperglycemia, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuropathy converge to derail the normally rapid and scar-limited healing of healthy mucosa. SC-EVs emerge as a versatile, cell-free nano-therapeutic platform that encapsulates key regenerative programs of Schwann cells in an off-the-shelf form. Through detailed exploration of SC plasticity, this review has shown how repair-phenotype SCs downregulate myelin markers and secrete neurotrophic factors, cytokines, and ECM components that orchestrate axonal regrowth, angiogenesis, immune regulation, and matrix remodeling. The nanoscale vesicles they produce inherit important aspects of this multifunctional activity. Their biogenesis and release through ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent pathways, mechanosensitive triggers, and Rab-mediated trafficking may be impaired under hyperglycemia through mitochondrial dysfunction and AGE-RAGE signaling.

Mechanistically, SC‑EV cargo, including neurotrophic proteins, pro‑angiogenic factors, immunomodulatory miRNAs, antioxidant enzymes, and specialized lipids, acts on multiple cellular targets: neurons regain growth potential, endothelial cells resume neovascularization, fibroblasts balance matrix deposition, and macrophages shift from persistent inflammatory activation toward inflammatory-resolving and reparative programs. This coordinated, multi-targeted action is a hallmark of advanced nanotherapeutics and explains why SC-EVs are especially relevant for neuropathy-associated diabetic wounds. Translational strategies such as scalable GMP-grade purification, targeted surface engineering, hydrogel-based delivery, metabolic preconditioning of donor SCs, and standardized potency assays are beginning to address key bottlenecks in scalability, stability, and in vivo efficacy.

Taken together, the convergence of glial biology, extracellular vesicle engineering, and regenerative dentistry provides a promising pathway to restore coordinated mucosal healing in diabetes. More broadly, the development of SC-EV-based interventions exemplifies the potential of nanotechnology to generate intelligent, cell-free, and multifunctional therapeutics for oral regenerative medicine.
