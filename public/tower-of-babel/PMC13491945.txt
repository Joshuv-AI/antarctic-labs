Intranasal sedation with diazepam–butorphanol in yellow-legged gulls (Larus michahellis): a preliminary clinical evaluation
Authors: Can Nacar, Kamil Serdar Inal, Birsen Deniz Ersoy, Elif Bagatir Kurban, Hatice Ozlem Nisbet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYellow-legged gulls (Larus michahellis) are frequently presented to wildlife clinics, often requiring safe and effective sedation and analgesia for diagnostic procedures. Minimising stress and restraint during handling are critical for both animal welfare and staff safety. To address this need, this study evaluates the first intranasal protocol in gulls, aiming to facilitate routine examination and diagnostic imaging without physical restraint. A total of 16 gulls were included in this prospective, preliminary clinical evaluation. A combination of diazepam (8 mg/kg) and butorphanol (1 mg/kg) was administered intranasally in equal volumes into both nares. Physiological parameters were monitored, and reflexes were assessed to determine sedation depth. Radiographic imaging was performed during deep sedation. Sedation and recovery times were recorded.ResultsAdequate sedation was achieved in all individuals. Deep sedation, occurred at a median of 10.50 (8.18–15.88) minutes in immature and 13.03 (7.32–16.58) minutes in adults (p = 0.798). Recovery and perching were observed at a median 110.50 (83.00–128.00) minutes and 109.00 (88.00–139.00) minutes, respectively (p = 0.798). Radiographic evaluations were completed without restraint or radiation exposure to staff, yielding diagnostic-quality images. Significant differences in physiological parameters were noted between restraint and sedation periods.ConclusionsThis protocol was effective and well-tolerated in yellow-legged gulls. It offers a practical, stress-reducing, and analgesic alternative to physical restraint for routine diagnostic procedures, significantly improving both animal welfare and clinical safety in wildlife practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05626-1.Keywords: Analgesia, Butorphanol, Diazepam, Intranasal sedation, Larus michahellis

Background

Many species of wild birds are frequently brought to animal hospitals or wildlife rehabilitation centres throughout the year due to various traumatic or non-traumatic causes, such as animal attacks, gunshot wounds, infection and weakness [1]. Since many of these presenting injuries are inherently painful, effective pain management is highly warranted. Physical examination, collection of laboratory samples for diagnosis, use of various diagnostic imaging methods and treatment procedures often require birds to be manually restrained. However, this can pose a significant risk, especially for wild species that are highly sensitive to stress, and can further lower their pain threshold during manipulation. The use of appropriate sedative-analgesic agents can help prevent negative situations, such as stress, anxiety and self-injury due to flailing, while also providing essential procedural analgesia [2, 3]. For sedation in birds, it has been reported that various agents can be used alone or in combination via oral, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intravenous or intranasal (IN) routes [4]. In recent years, IN administration has become increasingly preferred due to advantages, such as rapid absorption and bioavailability, fewer side effects on biochemical parameters, painlessness and ease of application. In addition, the weakness of the pectoral muscle group in birds increases the potential for iatrogenic injuries from IM injections, making the IN route more advantageous [2, 5]. Despite the advantages of this route, no intranasal sedation protocol has been reported for Larus michahellis. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the clinical efficacy and tolerability of an IN diazepam-butorphanol combination —selected specifically for its dual sedative and analgesic properties— in Yellow-legged gulls. The protocol’s effectiveness was investigated by evaluating sedation depth and performing radiographic imaging during deep sedation to facilitate stress-free clinical assessment.

Methods

Ethical approval

All procedures conducted in this study were approved by the Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Experiments and Local Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2023-93). Since the subjects were wild animals, official research permission was also granted by the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks (Ref. No: E-21264211-288.04-13608654, Date: 15.03.2024). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with The ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for animal research.

Study design/animals

The study material comprised sex undetermined, Yellow-legged gulls (n = 16), divided into two groups: immature (n = 8) and adults (n = 8). These birds were brought to the Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Hospital at different times by local citizens or officials from the Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks, having been found in the wild with complaints of inability to fly. Since the study groups were defined by biological age characteristics (immature vs. adult), random assignment to groups was not applicable. However, to ensure unbiased data collection, physiological measurements and sedation scoring were performed using standardized protocols for all individuals. Given the high admission rate of this species to our clinic located in a coastal region (Samsun), case enrollment continued until a target of 8 individuals per group (n = 16 total) was reached. This sample size was determined based on comparable avian sedation studies [1, 2, 6–11] and in accordance with the 3Rs principle (Reduction), aiming to use the minimum number of animals necessary to detect clinical efficacy in a preliminary trial. Although these individuals appeared alert and responsive, they exhibited aggressive defensive behaviours (e.g., biting, struggling), rendering manual restraint difficult and stressful for both the animals and the personnel. Consequently, sedation was clinically indicated to facilitate a comprehensive physical examination (particularly detailed palpation of the extremities) and necessary radiographic imaging for orthopaedic assessment (e.g., fractures, dislocations), thereby minimizing handling stress and ensuring the safety of the procedure. Specimens were categorized into two distinct age groups, adult (≥ 4 years) and immature (1–3 years), based on established morphological criteria regarding plumage and soft part coloration [12]. Upon admission, all birds underwent initial triage. Critically ill individuals presenting with signs of dehydration (> 5%), severe emaciation (cachexia), physiological exhaustion, or an agonal state were excluded from the study. Furthermore, birds requiring immediate emergency stabilization, fluid resuscitation, or shock therapy, as well as those deemed hemodynamically unstable or otherwise unfit for sedation, were not enrolled. Only individuals determined to be systemically healthy and stable (ASA physical status I or II), yet unable to fly due to localized traumatic causes (e.g., soft tissue injuries or stable orthopaedic conditions), were included. The dehydration levels of gulls were determined based on the table used by Heatley et al. in 2001 [13]. The protocol included the following routine steps: measurement of body weight, measurement of respiratory rate (fR, breaths/min), heart rate (HR, beats/min) and body temperature before induction, noting the duration of reflex loss and deep sedation onset, measurement of respiratory rate, heart rate and body temperature during deep sedation, radiographic imaging and recording the onset of consciousness return and recovery times. Significant observations were noted at the minute they were detected. After a 4-hour fasting period, each gull underwent the sedation protocol. Respiratory rates were measured three times throughout the process. The first two measurements were taken before induction: the first by counting chest movements during inspection with the bird unrestrained and the second with the bird restrained and placed in sternal recumbency. The third measurement was taken during deep sedation, while the bird was in sternal recumbency. Heart rate and body temperature were measured before and at the onset of deep sedation, with the heart rate assessed using a stethoscope and the body temperature measured using a thermometer placed in the cloaca. It should be noted that heart rate and body temperature measurements were not feasible during the baseline resting period. Obtaining these parameters requires physical contact (e.g., stethoscope placement or cloacal thermometry), which would necessitate manual restraint and induce stress, thereby invalidating the ‘resting’ baseline state. Conversely, respiratory rates were recorded via visual observation of thoracic excursions to avoid any contact-induced stress. For sedation, a combination of diazepam (8 mg/kg) and butorphanol (1 mg/kg) was mixed using a single syringe. Since no specific intranasal dose has been established for Yellow-legged gulls, the selected doses were extrapolated from previously reported protocols that have shown effective and well-tolerated sedation across various avian orders, including Passeriformes, Columbiformes, Psittaciformes, Galliformes, and Struthioniformes. Specifically, in these avian groups, intranasal diazepam doses up to 13–16 mg/kg have been documented as safe [4, 5, 14, 15]. The needle guide of the 22–24 gauge IV catheter was removed, and IN applications were performed slowly (over approximately 15–20 s) in equal volume into both nostrils (Fig. 1).

To prevent aspiration and minimise reflex-associated head movements, the beak was gently stabilised, and the head was elevated and maintained in a fixed position throughout the administration. After induction, times for incoordination [see Additional file 1], loss of palpebral/pedal/cloacal/wing withdrawal reflexes [see Additional files 2, 3, and 4], transition to sternal recumbency, muscle relaxation, consciousness return and complete recovery (perching–standing) were recorded. Incoordination was defined as instability of head–neck position (Fig. 2).

Head and neck position following the induction

Deep sedation onset was defined as muscle relaxation, based on the absence or significant reduction of pedal/cloacal and/or wing withdrawal reflexes [16]. During this stage, light manipulation was performed for the palpebral reflex, the pedal reflex was assessed using metal tweezers with minimal harm, and metatarsophalangeal joint flexion–extension movements were applied. The cloaca was stimulated with a thermometer. The duration of the sternal recumbency was noted as the time the gulls freely remained in this position. Sedation depth was evaluated using a cumulative “Total Sedation Score (TSS)” system. This tailored scoring system was adapted and modified for the present study based on qualitative sedation assessment criteria previously established for avian species [6, 7]. The TSS was based on seven clinical criteria: response to auditory stimulus, postural tone, palpebral reflex, pedal reflex, righting reflex, cloacal reflex, and muscle relaxation (Appendix 1). Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 (normal/awake) to 3 (absent/deep sedation), yielding a maximum possible score of 21. To standardize the auditory stimulus, a sharp hand clap was delivered approximately 30 cm behind the bird’s head, outside its visual field, to assess the acoustic startle response. Reflexes were tested every minute following drug administration. Sedation quality was classified based on the TSS as follows: 0–6: Awake / Mild Sedation, 7–13: Moderate Sedation, 14–20: Deep Sedation (Suitable for radiographic positioning), 21: Full Anaesthesia / Complete Relaxation. To ensure objectivity and minimize observer bias, the entire sedation and recovery process was recorded on video for each bird. These video recordings were subsequently evaluated retrospectively by three independent, blinded PhD specialists in veterinary surgery. The sedation scores and reflex disappearance times reported in this study represent the average of these three independent assessments. To evaluate another clinical effect of the combination, the birds were positioned ventrodorsally on the radiographic table, with their wings and tarsometatarsal region secured with a band (Fig. 3), and radiographs were taken (75 kV/1.2 mA) [see Additional file 4]. After radiographic imaging, the time of consciousness return was recorded as the duration from the moment of intranasal drug administration until one or more pedal/cloacal/wing withdrawal reflexes were observed. Time measurements were performed using a handheld digital stopwatch.

Wings and tarsometatarsal region secured with a band. In this way no staff members were exposed to X-rays

Times for induction and all other reflexes were recorded starting immediately after drug administration. Palpebral, pedal, cloacal, and wing withdrawal reflexes were assessed periodically throughout the study by the same trained clinician to ensure consistency and minimise observer bias. Return of consciousness was defined as the reappearance of one or more reflexes, and full recovery was recorded when birds were able to stand and walk unassisted. Following the completion of diagnostic imaging and physical examination, minor therapeutic interventions required for initial stabilization were performed while the birds remained under deep sedation. These procedures included wound cleaning, antisepsis, and external coaptation (e.g., figure-of-eight bandage) for stable fractures. During these interventions, birds were monitored for physiological and behavioral indicators of pain or distress (e.g., vocalization, tachycardia, or withdrawal movements) to evaluate the antinociceptive effect of the protocol. All interventions were successfully completed without any such reactions, confirming the procedure was pain-free. Subsequently, the Yellow-legged gulls were returned to the hospitalisation unit for monitoring. The time of complete recovery (perching–standing) was recorded when the birds started to stand on their feet and walk under control (Fig. 4).

The time of complete recovery (Perching-standing). The birds started to stand on their feet (A, B)

After 24 h, the birds were released from the cage into a large room in which they were rehabilitated for five days. Birds showing no issues with general behaviour and oral water/food consumption, and a complete return of their flying capability were released back into the wild along the coast.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software package (IBM SPSS Statistics for MAC, IBM Corp.). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Due to the relatively small sample size (n = 8 per group) and the non-normal distribution observed in several physiological and reflex parameters, non-parametric tests were utilized for all analyses. Consequently, descriptive statistics for body weight, onset of incoordination, reflex durations (palpebral, pedal, cloacal, wing withdrawal), muscle relaxation, transition to sternal recumbency, and recovery times were expressed as median (minimum – maximum). To evaluate the statistical differences between immature and adult groups for these parameters, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed. The Friedman test was used to analyze changes in respiratory rate measurements across three time points (resting, handling, and deep sedation). Pairwise comparisons for respiratory rate, as well as the analysis of heart rate and body temperature measurements taken at two different time points (handling and deep sedation), were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the ethical and logistical constraints inherent to wildlife research, a formal a priori power analysis was not performed due to the exploratory nature of this preliminary clinical evaluation. Instead, the sample size was determined by real-world clinical case availability and guided by sample sizes reported in comparable avian sedation studies, in accordance with the 3Rs principle (Reduction).

Results

This study presents the clinical use and sedative effect of the IN diazepam-butorphanol combination in adult and immature Yellow-legged gulls. The body weights of the Yellow-legged gulls were recorded as 657.50 (600–700) g for immatures and 750.00 (700–900) g for adults (Median, Min-Max). A statistically significant difference was found between the body weights of the two age groups (p < 0.05). Based on the body weights of the gulls, the administered total volume ranged from 0.35 to 0.65 mL per nostril. This volume was well-tolerated and did not exceed the anatomical limits of the nasal cavity, as no drug reflux was observed. Furthermore, drug expulsion due to sneezing reflex prior to mucosal absorption was not observed in any of the gulls during the 15–20 s instillation period. The protocol provided effective sedation for all birds included in the study. Radiographic evaluations performed during deep sedation yielded images of diagnostic quality without motion artifacts in all individuals. Since no statistically significant differences were detected between immature and adult groups in terms of basal heart rate, respiratory rate, and body temperature (p > 0.05), physiological data were pooled for analysis (n = 16). No animals, experimental units, or data points were excluded from the analysis. However, when compared according to the measurement time (rest, handling, deep sedation), statistically significant differences were observed in respiratory rates, heart rates, and body temperatures (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Overall, no side effects or complications were observed during sedation, and no issues were encountered during the recovery period. Following the completion of the study and a subsequent rehabilitation period, the birds were released back into the wild only after a thorough veterinary assessment confirmed their full clinical recovery, including the complete restoration of their flying capability and normal behavior.

Observer assessments and induction times were compared between immature and adult Yellow-legged gulls to evaluate age-related responses (Tables 2 and 3). Overall, the age of the birds did not result in statistically significant differences in either the timing of reflex losses or the objective sedation scores (p > 0.05). However, clinical scoring indicated that adult gulls tended to have slightly prolonged induction phases. Specifically, the loss of sternal recumbency and palpebral reflex took clinically longer in adults, displaying moderate effect sizes (r = 0.42 and r = 0.41, respectively), despite not reaching statistical significance. Similarly, observer scores for the palpebral reflex were marginally higher in adults, exhibiting a moderate effect size (r = 0.32).

The Total Sedation Score (TSS) indicated effective sedation and muscle relaxation in all individuals, with a maximum possible score of 21. In terms of TSS and depth of muscle relaxation, both age groups exhibited highly comparable responses with small to negligible effect sizes (Total Score r = 0.28; Muscle Relaxation r = 0.11), suggesting that the chosen protocol provides a consistent and profound sedative profile independent of the animal’s age.

Discussion

The effects of manual restraint and captivity on avian heart rate are highly species-dependent; while handling stress significantly increases heart rate in many species [17–20], it has been reported to cause no change or even a decrease in others, potentially depending on the degree of physical struggling during restraint [6, 21]. Because physiological responses to stress vary so greatly, species-specific clinical evaluations are crucial [8, 9]. Furthermore, restraint typically induces hyperthermia due to physical exertion, though stress itself can drive temperature increases even in controlled environments [9, 22]. In our study, Yellow-legged gulls sedated with IN diazepam-butorphanol exhibited lower heart rates and body temperatures compared to their pre-induction restrained states. This reduction is likely attributable to the protocol mitigating handling stress, alongside hypothermia caused by vasodilation and reduced muscle tone. Additionally, the significant increase in respiratory rate observed during initial handling underscores the sympathetic activation caused by restraint [5, 7]. Consequently, this sedation protocol offers a practical, welfare-improving alternative to manual restraint for clinical interventions.

A common challenge and recognized side effect of IN drug administration is the sneezing reflex, which can cause the sedative agent to be expelled before absorption by the nasal mucosa, resulting in an inadequate dosage [5, 7]. While the occurrence of this reflex varies widely among avian species [7, 10, 23–28], it was successfully prevented in our study. To mitigate this risk, the gulls’ beaks were gently held and tilted upwards against gravity during and immediately after the 15–20 s instillation period. This ensured unimpeded airflow, enhanced the drug’s contact with the nasal mucosa, and prevented expulsion through reflex-associated head movements.

Furthermore, the IN route offers significant clinical advantages, including ease of access, non-invasiveness, and a minimal need for specialized anatomical experience or additional personnel for restraint. The rapid onset of effect and high bioavailability of this route are primarily attributed to the nasal–oral–pharyngeal transition and the extensive vascularization of the nasal submucosa. This dense vascular network allows for rapid absorption into the systemic circulation and effective crossing of the blood–brain barrier [2, 6, 7, 15, 27]. Due to these favorable pharmacokinetic properties, IN administration has been established as a safe, practical, and frequently used method in routine veterinary applications, central nervous system disorders, and even human paediatric emergencies [27, 29, 30].

The utilized protocol achieved an appropriate depth of sedation (median 10.50–13.03 min) without requiring redosing. While parameters like sternal recumbency and cloacal reflexes were profoundly suppressed, auditory responses and muscle relaxation showed partial retention. This highlights a crucial clinical distinction: the protocol induces procedural sedation rather than general anaesthesia. Therefore, maintaining a quiet environment and handling the birds gently remains essential to ensure procedural stability.

In wild animals, any form of restraint or captivity can trigger stress factors, adversely affecting biochemical processes and potentially causing harm to animals or personnel [31]. In such cases, various chemical agents, such as benzodiazepines, are utilized to facilitate effective diagnostic and minor therapeutic procedures while ensuring patient and staff safety [15]. Benzodiazepines, known for their anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, and amnesic effects, are reported to be well-tolerated in birds for both premedication and sedation [3, 11]. In our study, diazepam was included in the combination due to these highly beneficial properties. Previous literature supports the clinical utility of combining benzodiazepines with butorphanol; for instance, in various parrot species, this combination significantly reduced intubation times and excitation during procedures, despite a non-significant increase in recovery times [14]. Similarly, intramuscular use of a related combination in Rock pigeons yielded comparable incoordination and sternal recumbency times, demonstrating that differences in species, dosage, and administration route critically affect clinical outcomes [32].

A key consideration for the IN route is the limited volume of the avian nasal cavity, which can restrict drug administration and lead to potential loss of drug efficacy due to expulsion or oropharyngeal transition. For example, a study in chickens demonstrated that IN butorphanol administration resulted in lower plasma concentrations compared to the intravenous route due to these anatomical constraints; thus, while typical doses are 1–2 mg/kg, higher IN doses (e.g., 3 mg/kg in cockatiels) have been reported to be necessary for efficacy [33]. In our study, the requirement for a relatively higher diazepam dose (8 mg/kg) administered intranasally may be attributed to a combination of potential species-specific resistance in Yellow-legged gulls and these inherent limitations in intranasal bioavailability compared to systemic routes. Following sedation, an increase in appetite and feeding desire was observed in the majority of the gulls; this hyperphagia is consistent with well-documented benzodiazepine effects in both human and veterinary medicine [34]. Furthermore, aquatic birds such as gulls possess specialized supraorbital salt glands with ducts opening into the nasal mucosa, which play a role in the immune system and protect the sensitive mucosa from the irritative effects of saline secretions [35]. Due to the lack of species-specific pharmacokinetic data and the presence of these unique anatomical features, we extrapolated our doses based on effective intranasal ranges reported in other avian species, ensuring safe and effective sedation despite exceeding typical intramuscular levels [4, 36].

Crucially, the sedation protocol allowed for proper positioning on the radiographic table using securing bands. This eliminated the need for manual restraint, completely preventing staff exposure to X-rays—a significant occupational safety improvement [37].

This study has several limitations inherent to its preliminary clinical nature. First, the lack of a separate control group and the non-randomized design limit the ability to draw definitive comparative conclusions. As a preliminary evaluation, the primary aim was to assess the clinical feasibility and efficacy of the protocol in a clinical setting rather than to compare it with established standards. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 16) may limit the external validity of the findings and their applicability to other wild bird species. Furthermore, due to this limited sample size (n = 8 per group), the statistical power to detect subtle physiological or sedative differences between age groups was limited. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant differences should be interpreted with caution, as a Type II error (failing to detect a difference that actually exists) cannot be excluded. Third, supplemental oxygen was not provided, and instrumental monitoring (e.g., pulse oximetry, blood gas analysis) was not employed, relying instead on close clinical monitoring of physiological parameters. While this represents a limitation, this approach aligns with the concept of ‘procedural sedation’ described by Doss and Mans [4, 5]. As they note, unlike general anaesthesia, procedural sedation aims to provide chemical restraint while preserving spontaneous ventilation and protective airway reflexes. Consistent with this concept, the protocol’s capacity to facilitate delicate diagnostic procedures was further demonstrated in a separate study, where it successfully enabled detailed ocular ultrasonography and tonometry in a different cohort of gulls [38]. In the present study, sedation via intranasal route was specifically utilized to facilitate stress-free handling for non-invasive or minor procedures (e.g., physical examination, diagnostic imaging, venous catheter placement, blood sampling, and bandage application) which rely on the maintenance of physiological respiration rather than deep suppression, as also reported in some previous studies [39]. Finally, only clinically stable gulls with mild dehydration were included; thus, the effects and safety of this protocol in critically ill or physiologically compromised individuals remain undetermined. Additionally, all procedures were carried out under controlled clinical conditions, and outcomes may differ in field settings where environmental variables are more pronounced.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the intranasal administration of the diazepam–butorphanol combination offers a practical, well-tolerated, and effective alternative to physical restraint for Yellow-legged gulls. By successfully minimizing stress and allowing for safe handling without overt respiratory compromise, it significantly enhances both animal welfare and occupational safety during clinical procedures. Moving forward, this protocol may be widely adopted in wildlife clinics and rehabilitation centers to facilitate stress-free diagnostic imaging and minor therapeutic interventions in gulls. Future studies should aim to expand upon these preliminary findings by incorporating larger sample sizes, formal evaluation of analgesic efficacy, and application to other wild avian species.
