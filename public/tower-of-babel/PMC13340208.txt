Introduction

Hematopoietic organs refer to those tissues in which new blood cells are formed, and where pluripotent hematopoietic stem cells are located. In mammals the stem cells of all blood cell types are present in the bone marrow. Multipotential hematopoietic stem cells give rise to both the lymphoid and myeloid (including the erythroid) lineages of blood cells. In addition to the bone marrow, lymphoid organs such as the lymph nodes, thymus, or spleen, are part of the hematopoietic system, as they are involved in the differentiation and maturation of lymphocytes1. Based on functional criteria, two categories of lymphoid organs can be distinguished, primary and secondary2. In primary lymphoid organs such as the thymus, the lymphocytes are formed and undergo maturation. Secondary lymphoid organs act as filters of blood and lymph fluid and they are sites of lymphocyte activation. In mammals, secondary lymphoid organs include lymph nodes, tonsils, spleen, Peyer’s patches, and mucosa- associated lymphoid tissue. Morphologically, lymphoid tissues are typically composed of a reticular framework that supports migratory and non-migratory cell populations, including lymphocytes. These lymphoid organs may be discrete entities, like the thymus, or they can be embedded within other hematopoietic tissues such as the spleen.

Teleost fishes lack bone marrow, lymph nodes or Peyer’s patches. The hematopoietic and lymphoid organs of fish include spleen, thymus, anterior kidney (syn. pronephros/head kidney), posterior kidney (syn. mesonephros/trunk kidney/excretory kidney), and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT)3,4,5,6,7,8,9. They function in erythropoiesis, lymphopoiesis, and myelopoiesis (lymphomyeloid tissues). The relative importance of these organs for the various tasks varies between fish species. The interstitium of the kidney and the stroma of the spleen are usually considered to be primary sites of hematopoiesis. In most fish species studied to date, the anterior kidney is the main site of B cell formation, while the thymus is a primary lymphoid organ which is essential for development of T cells from early thymocyte progenitors to functionally competent T cells which emigrate into the periphery. The spleen and MALT are secondary lymphoid organs. The fish kidney (anterior and posterior) functions both as primary and secondary lymphoid tissue. Fishes possess diffuse (MALT) which are located in their body surfaces: gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALT), skin-associated lymphoid tissues (SALT), gill- associated lymphoid tissues (GIALT) which is also known as interbranchial lymphoid tissue, and the recently detected nasopharynx-associated lymphoid tissues (NALT)9,10,11,12,13. Recent evidence suggests the existence of further MALT in the buccal cavity, pharynx, eye, swimbladder, gallbladder and stomach9, 14. The MALT contains T and B cells, with phenotypes different from their systemic counterparts. Lastly, the newly discovered salmonid bursa is a mucosal bursa with similarities to the avian bursa of Fabricius9. The presence of a lymphatic vessel system in fish is controversial15 and is discussed in more detail in the cardiovascular chapter.

The sites of hematopoiesis change during the ontogenetic development of vertebrates. In mammals and birds the first hematopoietic stem cells are derived from extraembryonic ventral mesodermal tissue. These so-called blood islands give rise to erythrocytes and some myeloid lineages. Later, hematopoietic stem cells develop from the dorsal mesoderm. From there, the stem cells migrate to the fetal liver where they proliferate and ultimately seed the bone marrow. In teleost fish, at least in the well-studied zebrafish (Danio rerio), the first hematopoietic stem cells appear – in contrast to mammals and birds – intra-embryonically in a ventral mesoderm-derived tissue, the “intermediate cell mass”16,17,18. The definitive hematopoietic stem cells emerge from the dorsal aorta. Blood cells begin to circulate throughout the zebrafish embryo from 24 hour post-fertilization (hpf) onwards. From 72 hpf onwards, lymphopoiesis is initiated in the thymus, and at 96 hpf, the pronephros contains hematopoietic cells, mainly developing erythrocytes and granulocytes. The spleen seems to differentiate late during fish ontogeny2, 18, 19.

In general hematologic cell types in fish are morphologically and functionally comparable to those of mammals. Cell types typically observed in intravascular or extravascular locations in H&E histologic sections can include erythrocytes, thrombocytes, neutrophils, eosinophilic granular cells (EGCs), lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages (including histiocytic, epithelioid, and giant cell forms). Other cell types (e.g., basophils), have been reported in certain fish species, but are challenging to discern specifically in histologic sections. Likewise, it is often difficult to distinguish fish plasma cells from lymphocytes in tissue sections, and piscine platelets often appear similar to erythrocytes (both cell types are nucleated in fish). It is commonly believed that EGCs are functionally analogous to mammalian mast cells, as opposed to mammalian eosinophils, and any apparent relationship between increased EGCs and parasitic infection seems inconsistent at best. Similar to mammalian mast cells, low numbers of EGCs can be found constitutively in certain anatomical locations (e.g., ventral to the brain in certain species). Unlike the situation in mammals, fish neutrophils represent only a small percentage of circulating leukocytes (approximately 5% in some reports), although underestimation is possible because some piscine neutrophils are non-segmented.

Pigmented macrophage aggregates (PMA; melanomacrophage centers) are focal clusters of macrophages. PMA can be organized as discrete, encapsulated foci, or they can occur as solitary cells or loose cellular aggregates. As the name suggests, they almost invariably contain cytoplasmic pigments such as melanin, lipofuscin, ceroid, and/or hemosiderin20,21,22,23. Consequently, PMA colors can be black, brown, gold, red, or some combination of these, depending on the precise blend of pigments. As opposed to granulomas, which are comprised of epithelioid cells and are always a response to either a pathogen, foreign material, or excess endogenous material (e.g., ectopic mineral or lipid liberated from degenerating adipocytes), PMA are resident structures that are found “normally” in a variety of tissues. Each fish species typically has a characteristic anatomical distribution of PMA. Among teleosts used as laboratory subjects, the spleen and kidney are typical locations, while the livers of certain fish species (e.g., salmonids, sturgeon) may also contain abundant PMA. Other common to occasional sites include the pancreas (mesenteric and intrahepatic), gonads, heart, and gill arches. PMA are chronic repositories for cell breakdown products, and therefore the size and number of PMA typically change with size, health status, or age of the fish. Because increases in PMA size/number have been associated with certain toxicologic exposures, their relative abundance may be measured as a marker of toxicity (e.g., by semiquantitative scoring or by using quantitative approaches such as image analysis or stereology); however if this is done, great care must be taken to control for potential group-wise differences of PMA related to sex and age, so that those factors do not confound the results. The abundance of PMA has been found to change with stress situations, for instance, pollution exposure or pathogen infection23,24,25,26. Functions that have been ascribed to PMA are removal of debris, deposition of iron or resistant intracellular bacteria, and sequestration of products of cellular degradation and toxic materials including catabolic breakdown products and free radicals22. It is thought that PMA may also participate in immune responses, with antigen-presenting functions analogous to the germinal centers of mammalian lymphoid follicles.

Rodlet cells are not necessarily hematogenous in nature but are discussed herein because of the possible role that they may play in inflammatory responses. In fact, the precise origin and function of these enigmatic cells have not been thoroughly established. Initially mistaken for unicellular parasites, the current consensus is that they are of host origin. Although some scientists consider these cells to represent a component of the innate immune system, anti-pathogen activity has not been convincingly established, and these cells are frequently present, often in large numbers, in entirely quiescent (i.e., non-inflamed) tissues. Most frequently associated with endothelial or epithelial basement membranes, rodlet cells have been observed in a broad range of tissue types (including hematologic tissues) among a wide variety of fish species27, 28. In H&E sections, such cells are egg-shaped with a dense basilar nucleus, relatively thick and slightly refractile eosinophilic plasmalemma, and eosinophilic cytoplasmic spicules (“rodlets”) oriented centrally along the long axis of the cell. The presence of rodlet cells can be further highlighted by PAS-staining. Because their numbers appear comparatively amplified in some inflammatory responses, increased rodlet cells may be recorded as a diagnosis, although such increases have generally not been observed as toxicologic effects.

Hematopoietic tissues of fish may contain ectopic thyroid follicles29. Unless the ectopic follicles are demonstrating evidence of hyperplastic proliferation, this diagnosis is not considered to be a pathological finding.

Necropsy and Trimming

For necropsy, the fish is usually positioned in the right lateral recumbency and the body cavity is opened. Among the visceral organs, the spleen is visible as distinct, deeply red organ, and can be easily removed. The trunk kidney is visible after removing the swimbladder, directly ventral to the spinal cord.

To dissect the kidney of a larger fish, an incision has to be made through the peritoneal membrane overlying the kidney. Then, a cut is made dorsally down to the vertebral column, followed by longitudinal cuts along both sides of the kidney. Finally, the kidney tissue is gently lifted from the spinal column and transected at its caudal end30, 31. For small fish like zebrafish, dissection of the kidney without causing lesions is difficult and therefore it is preferable to fix it in-situ. The head kidney is located at the cranial end of the trunk kidney; the border between head and trunk kidney may be indistinct or macroscopically visible as a small incision31. The head kidney extends anteroventrally towards the heart.

Also for the thymus, dissection without causing structural damage is difficult, and thus fixation in-situ and examination on transverse sections at the level of the branchial cavity may be preferable. An exception are transgenic fish lines with fluorescent thymus marker genes. For instance, the lck:GFP zebrafish line expresses a green fluorescent reporter protein linked to the lck gene32; in this case, the thymus can be dissected under a fluorescent dissection microscope. For the trunk kidney it is important to section at multiple sites since the tissue can show cranio-caudal gradients of histopathological features33.

Inflammation in Fish

Inflammation is a protective response of the organism to a variety of injuries (pathogens, toxic substances, particles, trauma, etc.). The inflammatory reaction involves the vasculature, an array of inflammatory cells, and chemical mediators. On the basis of the duration of the inflammatory response and the histological appearance, acute and chronic forms of inflammation can be distinguished.

Acute inflammation is characterized by the exudation of fluid and proteins from the blood stream into the extravascular space and surrounding tissue (edema). Acute inflammation lasts for a few days and is then resolved as the injurious agent is eliminated. Histologically, the exudate appears as eosinophilic staining. Inflammatory cells emigrate from the blood vessels to the site of injury. While in mammals neutrophils are mainly involved in this phase, the participation of neutrophils in acute inflammation in fish is less prominent, reflecting their low numbers in the peripheral circulation34. The neutrophils are rapidly replaced by other inflammatory cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes.

Chronic inflammation develops if the injurious stimulus persists. It is characterized by infiltration with mononuclear cells, mainly macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells, and can involve tissue destruction, extravasation of red blood cells, fibrosis, and proliferation of blood vessels. In contrast to acute inflammation which is exudative, chronic inflammation is proliferative.

The commonly observed form of chronic inflammation in fish is granulomatous inflammation. Other forms like suppurative inflammation are extremely rare, and abscesses do not typically occur in fish. The term granulomatous inflammation encompasses a spectrum of findings. Typically, it is characterized by a mixture of macrophages, lymphocytes, fibroblasts and sometimes neutrophils which are oriented around the site of injury. Multinucleated giant cells can be found in sites with chronic inflammation as well as in granulomas. These are nodular structures composed of a concentric layer of epithelioid macrophages surrounded by layers of lymphocytes and sometimes fibroblasts. The center of the granulomas may be necrotic/caseous. A classic example of chronic inflammation with granulomas is provided by mycobacterial infections of fish35. Morphology, Physiology, Nomenclature, Diagnostic Criteria and Differential Diagnoses

1. Spleen

The morphology of the teleostean spleen has been described in a number of studies and histology textbooks3, 4, 6, 9, 36,37,38,39,40. The spleen is a dark red organ located in the lower posterior peritoneal cavity. It is covered by a thin connective tissue capsule. As in other vertebrates, the parenchyma of the teleostean spleen is composed of red pulp, white pulp, blood vessels and ellipsoids (thick-walled capillaries with periarteriolar macrophage sheaths [PAMS]) (Figs. 5.1 and 5.2Fig. 5.1.Channel catfish. Spleen. Normal. H&E. Image courtesy of Wes Baumgartner.Fig. 5.2.Rainbow trout fry. Spleen. Normal. H&E. Image courtesy of Jan Spitsbergen.). In most teleosts, the majority of the splenic parenchyma is occupied by red pulp, while white pulp is usually poorly developed. The red pulp contains an extensive reticular fiber network which supports the sinusoids and holds diverse cell populations including erythroblasts, immature and mature erythrocytes, thrombocytes, but also macrophages and lymphocytes. The white pulp is often present as layers of lymphoid tissue that surround arterial vessels; usually no defined border between T and B cell exists41. The presence of dendritic cells has been described for the fish spleen42. The distinction between red and white pulp in most teleost spleens is not as clear as in other vertebrates, as the two components are intermingled, and scattered lymphocytes occur throughout the splenic parenchyma. In contrast to the spleen of mammals, where the spleen possesses germinal centers that function in antibody affinity maturation, germinal centers are lacking in the fish spleen. However, more recent research provides evidence that fishes possess the ability for antibody affinity maturation, and it has been suggested that the PMA (see above) are evolutionary precursors or primitive analogues of germinal centers20, 43. PMA are typically present in the teleostean spleen.

Physiologically, the spleen is an erythropoietic, blood filtering and secondary lymphoid organ2, 3, 5, 41, 46. Since teleosts lack lymph nodes, the spleen is one of the major filtering organs, removing foreign agents, antigens and blood cells. As in mammals, the fish spleen is also a blood storing organ. The red pulp of the spleen is tasked with erythrocyte and thrombocyte production, blood filtration, and removal of erythrocytes. It retains substantial numbers of mature red blood cells that can be released into circulation during stress or physical activity. The relative importance of spleen and head kidney as sites of erythropoiesis vary between fish species. The white pulp is the spleen’s lymphoid component; it contains T cells, B cells and antigen- presenting cells. In addition to erythropoiesis, the spleen also plays a role in lymphopoiesis which takes place in the white pulp. The ellipsoids are believed to involve capture of foreign materials such as bacteria and various antigens (Fig. 5.3). Intravascularly injected materials (ink, trypan blue) are taken up by ellipsoids.

Spleen – Congenital Lesions

Congenital lesions of the fish spleen are not typically observed in toxicological studies.

Spleen – Non-Proliferative

In teleost fish, spleen pathologic findings have been reported for numerous infectious diseases. Enlargement of the spleen (splenomegaly) is frequently seen after systemic pathogen infection. The spleen is also commonly involved in chronic granulomatous inflammations associated with bacterial and parasitic diseases, for instance mycobacteriosis, blood fluke (Sanguinicola inermis) infection, and piscirickettsiosis. Viral diseases can induce extensive necrosis due to viruses trapped in the splenic parenchyma. Spleen hemosiderosis can occur as a consequence of pathogen-induced hemolytic anemia. Also conditions like stress can result in changes of the spleen morphology, for instance enhanced degradation of red cells, loss of blast cells, or appearance of hypertrophic macrophage-like cells47. Ceroid, a pigment which is sudanophilic (in paraffin sections), acid-fast, basophilic, isotropic, iron negative, Schiff-positive, and highly resistant to solution in water and alcohol, has been repeatedly reported to occur in the spleen of fish, but it is unclear whether it is related to specific pathological conditions48. The spleen, as a heavily vascularized tissue, can be affected by circulatory disturbances, for instance, passive congestion of the spleen due to tumor formation in other tissues has been observed to cause splenic spongiosis (Spitsbergen, personal communication).

With respect to spleen pathological changes induced by exposure of fish to toxicants, mainly three types of histological lesions have been described: (i) an increase of number and/or size of pigmented macrophage aggregates (PMA), (ii) increased hemosiderin, being indicative of enhanced erythrophagia, and (iii) lymphoid depletion44, 49,50,51,52,53,54,55,56. While certain lesions may arise from chemical actions directly in the spleen, alterations such as hemosiderosis may arise as indirect consequence of toxicant effects on the overall blood system. Interestingly, a number of studies that reported toxicant-induced histopathological effects in organs like liver or gills did not report concomitant lesions in the spleen57,58,59,60. Whether this represents a difference of toxicant vulnerability between hematopoietic and other organs, or is due, for instance, to toxicokinetic factors remains to be elucidated.

Pathogenesis

Toxicant-induced necrosis in the spleen of fish has been described for the white pulp, in the reticuloendothelial cells or with no further specification, just as “splenic necrosis”50, 56. Bacterial infections have been reported to cause ellipsoid necrosis, as the splenic ellipsoids appear to be a primary area of bacterial sequestration70, 71. Fibrinoid necrosis of ellipsoids has been described for systemic iridoviral disease of fish; this probably represents a pressure necrosis caused by enlarged virus-infected cells in a sub-endothelial location72

Diagnostic Features

• Can be focal, multifocal or diffuse

• Can affect cells of the red pulp, the white pulp, and/or the ellipsoids

• Pyknotic debris and loss of cell integrity; cellular shrinkage or hyper-eosinophilia affected cells

Differential Diagnoses

• Autolysis

• Inflammatory foci in the absence of necrotic cells

Comments

Toxicant-induced induction of splenic necrosis has been reported for fishes exposed to pesticides or gossypol as well as for fish from polluted environments or exposed to chemical spills50, 51, 54, 56, 73,74,75. Necrotic areas in the spleen may be an artifact that results from inappropriate tissue dissection.

Other Terms

• Granuloma

Pathogenesis

Granulomatous inflammation in the spleen can be induced by infectious agents such as Mycobacteria spp. It also has also described in the spleens of fish from contaminated environments51

Diagnostic Features

• Can be focal or multifocal

• Hemorrhage or extravasation of erythrocytes may be associated

• Suppurative inflammation is rarely observed in fishes

Granulomatous inflammation is the most commonly observed form of chronic inflammation in fish due to preferential reliance of fishes on cell-mediated immunity compared with mammals. In toxicopathological studies involving the fish spleen, authors often refer only to “inflammation” without further specifying the nature of the inflammatory response but inclusion of the modifier of “granulomatous” in fish is considered both helpful and appropriate. Other Terms

• Melanomacrophage centers

Modifiers

• increased

• decreased

Pathogenesis

Changes in size and frequency of the PMA can occur physiologically, for instance in association with aging, but they can also occur in relation to pollutant exposure or other forms of stress25, 26, 51, 75, 76. Aggregates have been suggested as biomarkers for the measurement of effects of environmental exposure to pollutants20, 21, 23

Diagnostic Features

• Collections of pigmented macrophages containing yellow, brown or black amorphous pigments

• Cell content may stain positively for Periodic acid-Schiff, Schmorls, and Ziehl-Neelsen stains

• Pathogenic microbes are generally not present, but they may be associated with inflammatory reactions and can coexist with granulomas in some cases

• Accumulations of macrophages may consolidate into aggregates, commonly

Differential Diagnosis

• Intracellular pigment deposition in the constituent cells of the spleen

• Inflammation, granulomatous

Comments

This is clearly the most frequently reported splenic lesion in toxicopathological studies with fish. The material in the cells is often iron-containing and is variously composed of hemosiderin, melanin, ceroid and/or lipofuscin, any combination or amount of which may be present77, 78.

In the vast majority of publications, it has been observed that PMA increased after toxicant exposure of fish, however, some studies report a toxicant-associated decrease of the numbers and/or size of PMA24, 74.

A number of studies describe increased deposition of pigments such as hemosiderin and/or melanin in the spleen of toxicant-exposed fish, but do not specify whether this is due to an increase of PMA or if this represents an extracellular deposition or intracellular accumulation in the other cell types of the spleen53, 55; thus it remains unclear whether the enhanced pigment deposition is a lesion on its own or associated with other diagnoses like increased PMA or increased erythrocyte degradation. Also, it should be noted that tissues like spleen may contain a baseline degree of pigment, and a thorough comparison with control specimens is necessary to decide if PMA and/or pigment deposition are indeed increased. Also, age-matched control fish are needed for any field epidemiology study because generally PMA increase with age and/or size in most fish species.

Other Terms

Neoplastic proliferation of lymphocytes or lymphocyte precursors in or from the spleen or thymus

• Monomorphic population of neoplastic small to medium-size lymphocytes

• Inflammation, mononuclear cell

Lymphomas are a group of tumors that develop from neoplastic T or B cell lymphocytes. Most hematopoietic neoplasms in fish appear to be lymphomas80. Hemangioma is derived from endothelial cells. Hemangiomas are relatively common spontaneous neoplasms in untreated zebrafish over 1.5 year of age and neoplasms occur more commonly in carcinogen studies in this species (Spitsbergen, unpublished).

Toxicant-related splenic hemangiomas have not been reported for fish. They may represent spontaneously occurring lesions.

2. Thymus

The morphology of the teleostean thymus has been described in a number of reviews and histology textbooks4, 6, 9, 36, 37, 39, 40, 82,83,84,85,86. The fish thymus is a paired organ in the dorsal part of the branchial cavity, which is in the pharyngeal region. Ontogenetically this location is similar to the location in other vertebrates. In histological sections, the thymus appears as an aggregate of small lymphocytes with intermingled supporting cells. The organ is covered by a thin epithelial layer which separates the thymocytes from the water environment. The epithelium extends trabeculae into the parenchyma of the thymus and this creates a three-dimensional network that contains the thymocytes. Histologically, an outer layer or cortex and an inner layer or medulla can be distinguished (Fig. 5.10Fig. 5.10.Rainbow trout. Thymus. Normal. The medulla and cortex are clearly visible. H&E. Image courtesy of Jan Spitsbergen. and 5.11Fig. 5.11.Zebrafish. Thymus. Normal. Demarcation between the medulla and cortex in this species is often indistinct. H&E. Image courtesy of Helmut Segner.). Although this separation is well expressed in some species like seabass (Dicentrachus labrax), it is hardly visible in other fish species like rainbow trout85,86,87,88. The cortex usually contains a greater density of thymocytes than the medulla and relatively few thymic epithelial cells89, 90. The medulla is less densely populated with thymocytes but the thymic epithelial cells are more prominent and it is better vascularized than the cortex. The cortical thymocytes are positive for recombination activating gene 1 (rag-1), while the medulla cells are negative. In addition to immune and thymic epithelial cells, neuroendocrine cells are present in the fish thymus. Furthermore, in a number (but not in all) of teleostean species, the thymus contains so-called Hassall bodies (corpuscles) which are spherical or oval eosinophilic bodies present in the medulla which increase in size and number with age85, 86. These corpuscles are composed of squamous epithelial reticular cells arranged in a concentric way around a central mass of degenerating cells. The Hassal bodies vary markedly in size. The presence of Hassall bodies is not a pathological finding, however, there are reports that the number of bodies is increased in fish from polluted sites49.

Physiologically, the thymus is the primary lymphoid organ of teleosts2, 83. The thymus provides the necessary microenvironment for T cell selection and maturation. T cell development in the thymus starts with immigration of T cell progenitors, which in fish are believed to originate from the head kidney. Afterwards, the T cells undergo a similar selection process as described for mammals. T cell lymphopoiesis in the mammalian thymus comprises: (i) the entry of T cell progenitors (double negative, DN) into the thymus along the corticomedullary border (BCM), (ii) formation of CD4+/CD8+ double positive (DP) thymocytes at the subcapsular cortex, (iii) the positive selection of DP thymocytes in the cortex from where they migrate across the BCM into the medulla, where (iv) they interact with multiple antigen-presenting cells and can undergo negative selection to single positive cells (SP). Eventually, (v) the mature T cells are exported from the thymus into the periphery. Principally, these processes described for the mammalian thymus appear to be present in the fish thymus as well.

The thymus of mammals undergoes an age-related involution (synonym: atrophy, regression) which involves a reduction of both lymphocytes and epithelial cells in both cortex and medulla. In advanced cases of involution, adipose tissue can infiltrate the thymus. In addition to the age-related involution, which is irreversible, the thymus can experience a transient, reversible regression during period of stress and increased energy demands, for instance, during pregnancy. Both the age-related involution and the transient regression are physiological but not pathological processes.

In fish, it is controversially discussed if an age-related involution exists; there seems to exist variation among fish species90,91,92,93,94. There exist reports of a seasonal or reproductive cycle-associated reversible size reduction of the fish thymus95, 96; it is, however, not clear if this corresponds to the transient regression of the mammalian thymus. Also, it is not clear from the available literature if the seasonal regression of thymus size is accompanied by lymphoid depletion. Most reports on life stage or physiological status-related changes of the fish thymus provide information on thymus size but not on possible alterations of the cellular composition of the thymus.

Lymphoid depletion is a finding reported for the thymus of various fish species exposed to toxic compounds61, 97, 98. 3. Renal hematopoietic tissue

The morphology of the teleostean kidney has been described in a number of studies and histology textbooks4, 6, 9, 36, 37, 39, 40, 112. The kidney is a paired organ which is often centrally fused. It is composed of the anterior kidney (cranial or head kidney) and the posterior kidney (posterior or excretory kidney). “Renal hematopoietic tissue” refers to the erythropoietic, myelopoietic, and lymphopoietic parts of both the head kidney and the posterior kidney. Physiologically, the teleost kidneys support renal functions, endocrine functions as well as multilineage hemato-, myelo and lymphopoiesisc113-116. The kidneys produce erythrocytes, thrombocytes, myeloid cells and B-lymphocytes. The head kidney serves as both primary and secondary lymphoid organ.

The head (syn. anterior) kidney, which corresponds to the pronephros, comprises a reticuloendothelial stroma investing undifferentiated stem cells, blast cells, immature and mature stages of red and white blood cells, as well as a great amount of melanin-containing pigment granules (Fig. 5.15Fig. 5.15.Zebrafish. Kidney (anterior). Hematopoietic tissue. Normal. H&E. In some fish species, the anterior kidney contains renal excretory structures in addition to hematopoietic and interrenal/chromaffin tissues, while in many other species the head kidney lacks excretory kidney tissue. H&E. Image courtesy of Jan Spitsbergen.). Frequently, PMA are present in the head kidney parenchyma. While the head kidney of fish can contain excretory tubules, in many species they are absent. In addition to hematopoietic and excretory components, the head kidney contains endocrine cells: interrenal cells which produce cortisol and chromaffin cells which produce (nor)adrenalin. They are located around the cardinal veins in the head kidney. For some fish species, such as goldfish (Carassius auratus), the presence of ectopic thyroid follicles has been reported29.

The reticuloendothelial tissue of the head kidney resembles to some extent the bone marrow of mammals. It forms an extensive system of sinusoids and dispersed populations of erythroid and lymphoid cells at various maturation stages. Smith et al.113 describe the histology of the pronephros of the carp as resembling the subcortical region of mammalian lymph nodes, while Zapata114 emphasizes the similarity between teleostean pronephros and mammalian red bone marrow. Kondera115 analysed the quantitative composition of the various blood cells in the head kidney of European chub (Squalius cephalus) and found that it contained approximately 10% blast cells, 13% erythroid cells, 13% thrombocytes, 20% myeloid cells, and 44% lymphoid cells. Many of the renal lymphoid cells belong to the B cell lineage33, 112.

With its extensive sinusoid system equipped with macrophages, the head kidney functions in filtering pathogens and particles from the blood. For instance, when radiolabeled bacteria were injected into rainbow trout, more than 70% of the radioactivity was trapped in the head kidney46.

The posterior kidney (syn. excretory kidney, caudal kidney) which corresponds to the mesonephros, is located retroperitoneally between swim bladder and vertebral column. The organ is composed of glomeruli, renal tubules and hematopoietic tissue which forms a support matrix for the excretory structures. The hematopoietic part of the posterior kidney is often referred to as “interstitial tissue”. It contains erythroid cells and myeloid cells as well as lymphocytes (Fig. 5.16Fig. 5.16.Rainbow trout. Kidney (posterior). Hematopoietic tissue. Normal. Hematopoietic tissue is intermingled with excretory tissue. H&E. Image courtesy of Jan Spitsbergen.). The cells are randomly distributed, with no discernible specialized histological architecture. Frequently, the interstitium contains PMA. In addition to the hematopoietic and excretory parts, the posterior kidney also contains endocrine components, the corpuscles of Stannius. The transition between anterior and posterior kidney may be gradual or demarcated.

Rainbow trout. Kidney (posterior). Hematopoietic tissue. Normal. Hematopoietic tissue is intermingled with excretory tissue. H&E. Image courtesy of Jan Spitsbergen.

Lesions of the renal hematopoietic can be difficult to interpret. For instance, the quantity of hematopoietic tissue in the posterior kidney varies between species, making it difficult to assess cell proliferation or cell loss. The frequency of pigmented macrophage aggregates can vary with species and age; therefore age and sex-matched controls are critical for accurate evaluation and interpretation. Congestion may look like hemorrhage, requiring close examination6. Since the tissues are soft and not always easy to dissect, artifacts may be introduced during dissection. Also, particularly for the posterior kidney, pathological studies often focused on the excretory constituents and neglect the hematopoietic parts117, 118.

A frequently reported histopathological response of the hematopoietic part of the posterior kidney to toxicant exposure is an inflammatory reaction (previously called nephritis)59, 60, 119, 120. In the head kidney a decline of lymphopoiesis and/or depletion of lymphoid cells is among the more frequently observed histopathological responses to toxicant exposure121,122,123. The highly vascularized kidneys are target for numerous pathogens and parasites. Pathogen-induced lesions of the kidneys are often predominantly expressed in the hematopoietic parts of the tissues, for instance, in Bacterial Kidney Disease (BKD) and Proliferative Kidney Diseases (PKD)124, 125.

Mineralization (nephrocalcinosis) can be observed in the fish posterior kidney in a number of conditions, however, usually the mineralization occurs in the excretory part of the kidney. The formation of mineral deposits seems to be related to water quality and imbalances in ion- and osmoregulation while there exists no clear evidence for a toxicological etiology.

Other Terms

• Lymphomyeloid cells decreased, Depletion

Pathogenesis

Depletion of hematopoietic cells, including lymphoid cells, from the kidneys can be associated with lesions of the excretory components of the kidney, but they also can be induced by toxic and pathogenic agents. Also for stressed fish, a loss of hematopoietic cells has been reported, accompanied by an invasion of macrophages47.

Diagnostic Features

• Reduced numbers of lymphocyte or myeloid cells or their precurors in the interstitial anterior kidney

Comments

The interstitial tissue contains a mixture of different cell types, including blast cells, thrombocytes and lymphocytes. In fish histopathological studies the majority that report a hematopoietic decrease do not specify which cell types decrease but generally refer to a depletion of the interstitial cells. Standard paraffin sections, in fact, may not provide the resolution to identify which specific cells types are affected, with the identification of erythroid precursors being particularly challenging in fish. Depending on the species of fish, most erythroid precursors are sequestered in the spleen (e.g. catfish), while in others, including zebrafish, they may be present in the anterior kidney.

Toxicants like endosulfan, TCDD, uranium, arsenic or several pesticides were reported to cause a depletion of the hematopoietic and lymphoid cells of the trunk kidney of fish50, 61, 66, 68, 126. Profound depletion of lymphoid and/or myeloid precursors (aplastic anemia) can result in adipose tissue metaplasia of the anterior kidney, which may be followed by regenerative nodules of hematopoietic cells (Spitsbergen, personal communication).

Spitsbergen et al.61 pointed to a possible artifact from tissue preparation: When performing two experiments on TCCD treatment of yellow perch, they observed lymphoid depletion when head kidneys were sectioned in situ while they did not observe lymphoid depletion when head kidneys were dissected out. The authors hypothesized that, because of the loose stromal framework of the head kidney, the tissue may have collapsed during dissection, obscuring possible alterations in cellularity. Based on the tiered terminology used in the rodent INHAND hematolymphoid module, this finding may also be termed Cellularity, decreased, hematopoietic cells or Atrophy.

• Nephritis, interstitial

Pathogenesis

The induction of granulomatous inflammation in the renal hematopoietic tissues of fish has been reported from toxic exposure studies as well as from pathogen infection studies

Diagnostic Features

• A mixture of macrophages, lymphocytes, and sometimes neutrophils

• Oriented in and around the site of injury

• Granulomas may also be observed

Differential Diagnosis

• Hematopoietic cell hyperplasia – a mixture of cell types with a lack of necrosis or granuloma formation

Comments

The anterior kidney is one of the first lines of defense of the piscine immune response to systemic exposure, after the gills, so it is particularly susceptible to infection and to inflammatory responses. Granulomatous inflammation in the renal interstitial tissue is one of the most commonly observed forms of chronic inflammation in fish. Controlled laboratory studies testing single toxic compounds described the presence of granulomatous inflammation in the renal hematopoietic tissue. For instance, Schwaiger et al.59 observed in the kidney of diclofenac-exposed rainbow trout a “distinct proliferation of interstitial cells in the sense of an interstitial nephritis.” The lesion has been also reported from fish collected in contaminated environments51, 75, 127. Pathogenic infections such as Proliferative Kidney Disease or bacterial infections are also known to cause renal granulomatous inflammation71, 125, 129, 130.

Comments

This appears to be a rare response of the renal interstitial tissue to environmental stressors or toxicants. Erythrophagocytosis can be increased in hemorrhage or treatment-induced anemia, but it may be more prominent in the spleen than in the renal interstitial tissue.

Other Terms

• Lymphocyte proliferation; Lymphomyeloid hyperplasia

Pathogenesis

An enlargement of the kidneys due to hematopoietic cell hyperplasia can occur as result of immune stimulation, secondary to vaccination, or infectious disease. In such cases the proportion of large blast cells tends to increase relative to smaller monocytes. A toxicant induced etiology of lymphoid hyperplasia in the renal hematopoietic tissue of fish has been reported in rare cases. Hematopoietic (or lymphomyeloid) cell hyperplasia appears to be more common in cases of infectious disease and vaccination (Hedrick et al.135, Baumgartner, personal communication).

Diagnostic Features

• An increase in the number of lymphocytes and myeloid cells (and precursors)

• Tissue architecture may be maintained; in the trunk kidney the ratio between hematopoietic and excretory tissue is changed and renal tubules may be dispersed, compressed or even lost

Differential Diagnoses

• Lymphoma – a monomorphic population of lymphocytes that replace normal cells and distorts architecture

• Inflammation, granulomatous (renal hematopoietic tissue) - increased macrophages with irregular distribution, granulomas, and/or necrosis

Comments

Based on the tiered terminology used in the rodent INHAND hematolymphoid module, this finding may also be termed Cellularity, increased, hematopoietic cells. In the case of tributyltin-exposed guppies, hyperplasia was characterized by an increase of the renal area occupied by hematopoietic tissue98. This tissue formed solid sheets in which one cell type predominated while the pre-existing hematopoietic tissue became inconspicuous. The predominating cells were large, with faintly eosinophilic cytoplasm and a large hyperchromatic nucleus of irregular shape, which the authors interpreted as precursors of peritoneal macrophages.

Hyperplasia was reported in guppies, Poecilia reticulata, exposed to tributyltin compounds or to methyl mercury98, 136. Taheri et al.69 found a “leukocyte hyperplasia” in the head kidney of fish collected from contaminated field sites.

Singh et al.131 reported an increase of hypertrophic cells in the interstitial parts of the head kidney of catfish, Clarias gariepinus, chronically exposed to fluoride, but it was not reported if this was a response of the interstitial lymphocytes.

Descriptions of neoplastic changes of the renal interstitial tissue of fish are rare. Hematopoietic neoplasm in the kidneys have been found in the course of studies on spontaneous and induced neoplasms in zebrafish81.

Other Terms

• None

Pathogenesis

In mammals, leukemia is a neoplastic change of white blood cell formation in the bone marrow. It can affect different blood cell types, and results in poorly developed white blood cells. Since in fish the renal interstitial tissue is often considered to be the bone marrow analogue, leukemia may develop in this tissue. The literature provides two reports on renal leukemia with a probably infectious etiology. Kent et al.134 described a severe case of leukemia in the kidney of farmed chinook salmon. A virus etiology was suspected but not proven. Hedrick et al.135 found a leukemic condition in chinook salmon infected with an intranuclear microsporidian pathogen.

Toxicant-induced leukemia has not been reported to date for fish renal tissues.

Diagnostic Features

• Hyperplasia of the hematopoietic compartment

• Large irregular cells of any potential hematopoietic lineage

• Infiltrative process

• Locally invasive

Comments

The kidneys display prominent hyperplasia of cells of the hematopoietic tissue.

In the case reported by Kent et al.134, the plasmablasts contained large, often deeply clefted or lobulated nuclei and prominent nucleoli. They had a moderate amount of finely granular, eosinophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm. Many of these cells were mitotically active.

Immunohistochemical and ultrastructural observations indicated that the proliferating cells had similarities to plasma cells. Aggressive invasion and proliferation of the plasmacytoid cells in essentially every tissue and the relative immaturity of the cells were considered more consistent with neoplasm rather than a reactive plasmacytosis.

In the case reported by Hedrick et al.135, there was a hyperplasia of the renal hematopoietic tissue due to the proliferation of microsporidian-infected cells. The infected cells were pleomorphic, with a large irregular nucleus, thin basophilic cytoplasm, and an increased nuclear to cytoplasm ratio. The nuclei contained circular to rod-shaped inclusions. The infected cells had morphological properties of lymphocyte precursors. The migration of the infected cells to other tissues and the adherence to vascular endothelia are features of a neoplastic rather than a proliferative response, but there was no evidence of tumor-like growth. The overall histological appearance of the microsporidian infection was similar to that one reported by Kent et al.134.
