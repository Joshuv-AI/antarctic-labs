Natural killer cell-derived extracellular vesicles reprogram cellular human immunity to enhance tumor cytotoxicity
Authors: Frederic St-Denis-Bissonnette, Karan Mediratta, Nicholas Ho, Melanie Kirkby, Andrew Stalker, Gauri Muradia, Amit Shrestha, Michele Ardolino, Seung-Hwan Lee, Dylan Burger, Arvind Mer, Lisheng Wang, Jessie R Lavoie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural killer (NK) cells are part of the body’s first line of defense that rapidly destroy stressed, infected, or cancerous cells. These immune cells release nanosized biological packages called extracellular vesicles (EVs), which transfer molecular signals between cells and influence immune function. Although NK cell-derived EVs (NK92-EVs) have shown the ability to directly kill cancer cells, how they shape the broader human immune response has remained unclear. Here, NK92-EVs were shown to reprogram cellular human immunity to enhance tumor cytotoxicity using single-cell transcriptomics and functional cytotoxicity assays. When human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy and cancer patients (who exhibit systemic dysregulation) were exposed to NK92-EVs, widespread shifts in gene activity occurred across key immune populations, notably CD8+ T cells and NK cells. These changes enhanced the cells’ ability to recognize and eliminate tumor targets and were consistent across all donors. Functional depletion and enrichment experiments, together with transcriptomic profiling, provide direct evidence that NK92-EV-mediated immune reprogramming enhances NK cell-driven, MHC-I-independent tumor cytotoxicity, but not for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. This work advances understanding of immune communication and highlights NK92-EVs as promising, cell-free candidates for the next generation of cancer immunotherapies that unite potency with clinical scalability.Keywords: cellular reprogramming, EVs, extracellular vesicles or exosomes, immunomodulation, immunotherapy, natural killer cells, NK cells, peripheral mononuclear blood cells, PBMCs, single-cell RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq, triple-negative breast cancer, TNBC

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are essential effectors of innate immunity, capable of eliminating virus-infected and malignant cells without prior sensitization.1,2 NK cells play critical immunoregulatory roles not only through direct cell-cell interactions and secretion of cytotoxic granules and cytokines but also via extracellular vesicles (EVs).3,4 These NK cell-derived EVs (NK-EVs) are membrane-bound nanoscale vesicles with potent anti-tumor activity.5,6,7 They are capable of inducing apoptosis in cancer cells while sparing healthy tissues.8,9,10,11 Compared with cell-based therapies, NK-EVs are less susceptible to tumor resistance mechanisms, can be manufactured at scales, and are amenable to off-the-shelf use, highlighting their potential as promising candidates for solid tumor immunotherapy.12,13

In addition to their cytotoxic cargo (e.g., perforin, granzymes, etc.), NK-EVs carry an immunomodulatory payload capable of shaping immune responses.14,15 For instance, prior studies have shown that NK-EVs can activate T cells and reprogram myeloid cells toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes in a monocyte-dependent manner.16,17 These findings suggest the potential of NK-EVs to bridge innate and adaptive immunity. However, most of these studies rely on EVs derived from primary NK cells, which limits yield, reproducibility, and clinical scalability.4 Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet utilized peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) derived from cancer patients to assess the potential of NK-EVs to rescue their function. Broader immunomodulatory effects and their underlying molecular events also remain underexplored.

To this end, NK92-EVs were produced through a clinically relevant, scalable biomanufacturing workflow using the NK92-MI cell line.4 The NK-92 cell line was established from malignant non-Hodgkin lymphoma and has demonstrated clinical safety in oncology studies without inducing cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, or graft-versus-host disease.18,19 The NK-92MI variant was genetically modified to constitutively produce IL-2, enabling cytokine-independent growth through an autocrine IL-2 signaling loop that sustains proliferation and effector function, representing a canonical example of autocrine regulation in lymphocyte biology.20,21,22

The NK92-EV immunomodulatory effects were evaluated on PBMCs from both healthy donors and patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Effector immune cell populations driving cytotoxic reprogramming were delineated using flow cytometry, live-cell killing assays (with depletion and enrichment of certain immune cell subsets), and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) transcriptomics. By integrating these approaches, this study uncovers novel insights into coordinated lymphoid-myeloid engagement and consistent cytotoxic reprogramming, providing a strong rationale for NK92-EVs as a next-generation cancer immunotherapeutic.

Results

NK92-EVs show broad PBMC association

NK92-EVs were produced using a scalable biomanufacturing workflow process utilizing the NK92-MI cells and were comprehensively characterized for product identity and safety in accordance with MISEV guidelines,5 building on our previously established characterization.4,15

To evaluate the association of NK92-EVs with healthy human PBMCs (n = 3), NK92-EVs were fluorescently labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dye and incubated with PBMCs under various conditions (Figure 1A). Quantitative fluorescence measurements confirmed a significant increase in CFSE signal in NK92-EV-treated samples relative to all controls (p < 0.0001), validating effective and specific dye incorporation (Figure 1B). Residual unbound dye was efficiently removed using the cleaning column, as reflected by the minimal CFSE signal observed in the CFSE-PBS control. Furthermore, DiR (a lipophilic tracer) staining of NK92-EVs was performed to evaluate staining efficiency, with representative visual confirmation shown in Figure S1. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) confirmed that labeling did not alter NK92-EV size distribution or concentration (Figure 1C).

Following co-incubation of PBMCs with CFSE-labelled NK92-EVs over 72 h, high-dimensional flow cytometry (11 colors), combined with fast interpolation-based t-SNE (fitSNE) and FlowSOM clustering enabled robust classification of uptake or association across immune cell subsets (Figure 1D; Figure S2), including B cells (5.18% ± 0.58%), CD4+ T cells (55.86% ± 2.38%), CD8+ T cells (28.83% ± 0.58%), double-negative (DN) T cells (2.89% ± 0.22%), NKT cells (0.05% ± 0.01%), dendritic cells (0.21% ± 0.3%), cytotoxic NK cells (6.05% ± 0.97%), immunoregulatory NK cells (0.48% ± 0.05%), and monocyte-T cell conjugates (CD14+ monocyte associated with CD4+ or CD8+ T cells; 2.38% ± 0.03%), collectively representing ≥99% of PBMCs (no treatment with NK92-EVs). Fewer than 1% of events could not be assigned to known populations. These distributions, including a CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio of ∼1.9, are consistent with expected values for healthy human PBMCs.23 A negligible CFSE signal was observed in PBS and CFSE-PBS controls, whereas strong CFSE positivity was detected across multiple immune subsets in the NK92-EV-treated conditions (Figure 1D). Representative results from one donor are shown; donor-specific patterns are provided in the supplemental data (Figures S3–S5).

Quantitative assessment of PBMCs treated with CFSE-labelled NK92-EVs revealed no significant differences in subset distribution (Figure 1E). However, the CFSE mean fluorescence intensity (normalized to CFSE-stained PBS control) showed pronounced enrichment of NK92-EVs in monocyte-CD4+ T cell conjugates (CD14+ monocytes associated with CD4+ T cells; ∼73K RFU) and dendritic cells (∼20K RFU), corresponding to ∼20-fold and ∼6-fold higher levels, respectively, compared with the average PBMC subsets exposed to CFSE-labelled NK92-EVs (∼4K RFU) (Figure 1F). By contrast, B cells, NK cells, DN T cells, CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells exhibited minimal NK92-EV association (mean RFU = 3.0K). Despite these differences in density, the proportion of CFSE-positive cells was elevated across all subsets (Figure 1G). CFSE co-localization with the major immune cell populations was confirmed using conventional manual flow cytometry gating (Figure S6). Collectively, these results suggest that NK92-EVs interact broadly with diverse immune populations but preferentially associate with myeloid lineages, particularly monocytes and dendritic cells.

NK92-EVs boost PBMC cytotoxicity function

Building on the finding that NK92-EVs associate with specific immune cell subsets, functional reprogramming was evaluated following NK92-EV stimulation of PBMCs. PBMCs derived from healthy donors (n = 6) and TNBC patients (n = 3) were stimulated with NK92-EVs for 72 h and subsequently used in a live-cell imaging assay against human MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells and MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Unstimulated (MOCK) and NK92-EV-stimulated PBMCs (NK92-EV) were co-cultured with RFP-tagged breast cancer cells, and tumor cell confluence/viability (RFP+ cells in the red channel) and apoptosis (Annexin-V staining in the green channel) were monitored over 96 h (Figure 2A). Importantly, NK92-EV treatment did not affect PBMC viability, as assessed by acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) cell counting across healthy and TNBC donors (n = 6) prior to co-culture (Figure 2B), confirming that NK92-EVs do not compromise survival of normal immune cells.4 Orthogonally, resazurin viability-based, and caspase-3/7 activation assays showed no significant loss of PBMC viability or induction of caspase-3/7 activity following NK92-EV treatment (Figure S7).24 Notably, the 4 × 105 EVs to target cell condition correspond to the highest ratio of EV:PBMC used across this study, including in the scRNA-seq and live-cell imaging cytotoxicity experiments.

NK92-EV-stimulated PBMCs induced a robust reduction in the growth of both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cell lines compared with MOCK PBMCs (Figures 2C and 2D). Moreover, when normalized to RFP+ cell count, Annexin-V expression was significantly higher in breast cancer cells co-cultured with NK92-EV-stimulated PBMCs than in MOCK PBMCs, in both healthy and TNBC donor groups (Figures 2E and 2F). Donor-specific data are provided in the supplemental figures (Figures S8 and S9). Together, these findings suggest that NK92-EV stimulation reprograms PBMCs to enhance their anti-cancer activities.

NK92-EVs reprogram immune transcriptome

Given the enhanced anti-cancer activity observed in NK92-EV-treated PBMCs at the cellular level, PBMCs from healthy donors and TNBC patients were treated with NK92-EV (72 h) and processed for scRNA-seq to characterize the transcriptomic changes (Figure 3A).25,26 Untreated PBMCs served as controls to establish a baseline profile. Sample processing, including fixation and recovery, was successful (Figure S10), and quality control metrics confirmed high data quality, with appropriate feature-count relationships and mitochondrial gene percentages across all samples (Figure S11).

The scRNA-seq profiling of ∼90K high-quality PBMCs from both donor groups (n = 6, biological replicates) revealed four major immune cell subtypes (Figure 3B). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization identified distinct populations, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells. Similarly, TooManyCells hierarchical clustering resolved four distinct transcriptomic-cellular branches (Figures 3C; Table 1).27 Branch 1 comprises CD8+ effector/memory T cells and NK cells, representing the principal cytotoxic effector compartment. This clustering pattern highlights the shared transcriptional programs between these cytotoxic populations.28 Branches 2 and 3 consisted exclusively of CD4+ T cells, with branch 2 enriched for a stem-like population and branch 3 representing more differentiated subsets. Branch 4 encompassed immunoregulatory cells such as B cells, IL-7R-memory CD4+ T cells, and regulatory CD4+ T cells. Cell type classification was validated by canonical marker gene expression (Figure 3D).

Immune cell composition was not significantly altered (α = 0.05) following NK92-EV treatment (Figures 3E and 3F; Figure S12), and the percentage of different cells was as follows: B cells (healthy donor: 5.37% ± 2.88% vs. TNBC donor: 6.56% ± 5.21%), CD4+ T cells (healthy donor: 68.65% ± 5.11% vs. TNBC donor: 67.01% ± 7.14%), CD8+ T cells (healthy donor: 18.12% ± 5.68% vs. TNBC donor: 23.78% ± 3.27%), and NK cells (healthy donor: 7.86% ± 1.65% vs. TNBC donor: 2.66% ± 1.26%). The reduced NK cell representation in TNBC donor PBMCs is consistent with systemic immune suppression in cancer patients.29,30 Conversely, it is interesting to note that B cell and NK cell levels increased following NK92-EV treatment, independent of the disease status.

Conversely, at the transcriptomic level, NK92-EV treatment induced diverse responses across immune cell subsets, with the most pronounced transcriptional changes observed in cytotoxic populations, such as those constituting branch 1 (Figure 3G; Figure S13). In CD8+ T cells from TNBC donors, NK92-EVs markedly upregulated the granzyme gene family (GZMA, GZMB, GZMK, and GZMH), along with PRF1, FASLG, and IFNG. Noticeably, PRF1 upregulation indicates enhanced target cell membrane disruption capacity,31 RAB27A upregulation suggests improved cytotoxic granule delivery efficiency,32,33 granzyme upregulation indicates improved cytotoxic payload,34,35 and FASLG upregulation indicates activation of alternative Fas-mediated death pathways.36 Likewise, IFNG upregulation, on the other hand, suggests enhanced anti-tumor immunity and immune coordination capabilities.37,38 Concurrently, CX3CR1, a chemokine receptor that marks circulating, vascular-patrolling terminal effector cells, was downregulated.39,40

NK cells exhibited similar upregulations of primary cytotoxic genes, including GZMA, GZMB, PRF1, FASLG, and IFNG, again accompanied by CX3CR1 downregulation. The observed upregulation of cytotoxic pathways aligns with granzyme-perforin-mediated killing and Fas-FasL-induced apoptosis, indicating an enhanced killing potential.41,42 The downregulation of CX3CR1 in both populations is consistent with a phenotypic shift away from the short-lived, terminally differentiated circulating effector state.43,44 This transition toward a more durable memory-effector program may support sustained anti-tumor surveillance. CX3CR1 is most highly expressed on peripheral effector memory T cells and vascular-patrolling NK cells, and it is inversely correlated with the tissue-homing and memory precursor potential.39,40 Furthermore, NK cells exhibited significant downregulation of CD160, a marker often associated with activation or exhaustion,45 whereas CD8+ T cells showed no significant changes, suggesting population-specific responses (Figure S13). In NK cells, CD160 functions as an activating co-receptor that enhances cytotoxicity and IFN-γ production through engagement with HLA-C molecules.46 However, CD160 can also denote highly activated NK cells approaching functional exhaustion. Thus, its downregulation in functionally competent NK cells may reflect a shift away from CD160-dependent activation toward alternative cytotoxic mechanisms.47

Concurrent LAG3 upregulation was observed across multiple populations, including CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and memory CD4+ T cells (Figure 3G). This pattern likely reflects activation-induced checkpoint expression rather than exhaustion, given the concurrent enhancement of effector functions.48,49 Notably, effector CD4+ T cells showed marked GZMB upregulation alongside significantly decreased IL2 expression, suggesting a shift toward cytotoxic functions rather than helper functions (Figure S13). Expression of key immunomodulatory cytokines, such as IL10 and TGFB1, remained unchanged across populations (Figure S13), suggesting that NK92-EVs specifically potentiate cytotoxic pathways rather than broadly modulating immune regulatory networks.50 Collectively, the transcriptomic analysis suggests that NK92-EVs engage the lymphoid and myeloid arms of the immune system, aligning with previous reports implicating monocyte-T cell crosstalk and APC licensing by NK-EVs.14,16,17 NK92-EV treatment remodels the transcriptomic landscape of cytotoxic immune cells identified in branch 1 (CD8+ T cells and NK cells). These findings indicate NK92-EVs as potent enhancers of cytotoxic effector activity, particularly within immune compartments critical for tumor elimination.51

Branch 1 cells modulate reprogramming: Functional assessment of depleted/enriched subtypes

To dissect the contribution of specific immune subsets to NK92-EV-mediated immunomodulation, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, or CD56+ NK cells were selectively depleted from healthy donor PBMCs, using immunomagnetic separation (Figure 4A, i). Flow cytometry confirmed the efficient removal of CD4+ (94.45% ± 5.47%), CD8+ (94.82% ± 3.04%), and CD56+ populations (88.63% ± 1.24%) (Figures 4A, ii–iv). Representative pseudocolor flow cytometry plots further demonstrate the efficiency of lineage depletion for CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD56+ NK cells (Figure S14). Using a similar strategy, each population was selectively enriched from healthy donor PBMCs (Figure 5A, ii–iv). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed high enrichment purity for CD4+ T cells (97.95% ± 0.39%), CD8+ T cells (94.47% ± 1.27%), and CD56+ NK cells (94.55% ± 3.35%). Representative plots further the validate enrichment efficiency (Figures S15).

Following depletion and enrichment, PBMCs were stimulated with NK92-EVs for 72 h, and their cytotoxicity was assessed by live-cell imaging over 96 h (Figures 4 and 5). As previously demonstrated, the NK92-EV boosted the cytotoxicity activity of MOCK (whole) PBMCs (without depletion) against cancer models (Figures 4Bi and 4Ci). Interestingly, NK92-EV-stimulated PBMCs depleted of CD4+ cells retained a cytotoxicity profile comparable to MOCK PBMCs (whole population), though slightly altered, in contrast to unstimulated controls (Figures 4Bii and 4Cii). By comparison, NK92-EV-stimulated PBMCs depleted of CD8+ (Figures 4Biii and 4Ciii) or CD56+ (Figures 4Biv and 4Civ) subsets exhibited profiles similar to unstimulated controls, with the enhanced cytotoxic response abolished.

As anticipated, enriched CD4+ cells (Figures 5Bii and 5Cii) and CD8+ cells (Figures 5Biii and 5Ciii) stimulated with NK92-EVs yielded a cytotoxicity profile comparable to that of (non-depleted, whole population) MOCK PBMCs. This observation is consistent with the requirement for antigen-presenting cell-mediated priming and CD4+ T cell help for effective cytotoxic function in non-primed T cells. In contrast, enriched CD56+ cells (Figures 5Biv and 5Civ) stimulated with NK92-EVs exhibited improved cytotoxicity profiles compared with whole MOCK PBMCs. Donor-specific depletion trends for the depletion/enrichment conditions are provided in the supplemental data (Figures S16–S23). Endpoint imaging of tumor cell density and early apoptosis corroborated these observations across all depletion conditions (Figure S24) and enrichment conditions (Figure S25).

Lastly, CD33+ depletion was also performed using the same magnetic negative selection workflow validated for the other lineage depletions. Although direct confirmation of myeloid removal by flow cytometry was not feasible due to the unavailability of a suitable detection antibody (Figure S26A), the functional readout provides indirect evidence that depletion was successful. Specifically, the cytotoxicity profile of CD33+-depleted PBMCs was similar to that of CD8+-depleted or CD56+-depleted PBMCs (Figures S26B and S26C). Had myeloid cell removal failed, the expected cytotoxicity profile would have resembled the whole-PBMC condition.

As additional controls, baseline readings for the cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MCF7, including RFP-only and Annexin-only readings, are presented in Figure S27. Dual-depletion and dual-enrichment conditions were not feasible due to insufficient cell recovery.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that NK92-EVs, produced using a clinically relevant biomanufacturing workflow, selectively associate with all major immune subtypes (i.e., T cells, NK cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells) and significantly reprogram the transcriptomes of specialized subsets, thereby enhancing cancer-directed cytotoxicity. Notably, cytotoxic subtypes exhibited pronounced upregulation of cytotoxicity-associated genes and downregulation of exhaustion-associated genes. Live-cell imaging confirmed enhanced killing capacity of PBMCs against tumor targets, supporting the potential of NK-EVs as novel cancer immunotherapeutics capable of priming PBMCs, including those derived from TNBC patients.

While the exact immunomodulatory cascade of NK-EV stimulation upon PBMCs, potentially involving a complex series of interactions between cell populations, remains unresolved, this study strongly implicates CD56+ NK cells as the central effector population. CD8+ T cells are transcriptionally reprogrammed but require accessory cells for cytotoxic execution, and CD33+ myeloid cells likely contribute a supporting layer. Conversely, CD4+ T cells appear to play a negligible role.

These findings are consistent with those in a model in which NK92-EVs preferentially engage recipient NK cells within the PBMC compartment, driving transcriptional reprogramming toward a cytotoxic effector state. This interpretation is supported by three converging lines of evidence. First, CD56+ NK cell enrichment alone recapitulated the full cytotoxic phenotype observed in whole PBMCs, demonstrating that NK cells are sufficient effectors of NK-EV-driven reprogramming. Second, CD56+ depletion abolished the enhanced tumor-killing response, demonstrating that NK cells are necessary effectors. Third, the transcriptomic signature induced in recipient NK cells, marked by coordinated upregulation of GZMA, GZMB, PRF1, FASLG, and IFNG, mirrors the effector program characteristic of the NK92-MI producer cells.4,15 Together, these observations support a signaling model, in which NK-EVs propagate cytotoxic programming from the producer to recipient NK cells. This sufficiency, however, is context dependent. In CD56+ enriched conditions, NK cells are present at a substantially higher concentration than in whole or depleted PBMCs and are released from inhibitory cell-cell interactions, conditions that favor autonomous effector function. Within physiological cellular mixtures, NK cells likely depend on supportive signals from CD8+ T cells and myeloid partners to sustain cytotoxic activity, reconciling their sufficiency in enrichment with the loss of response upon CD8+ or CD33+ depletion.

Conversely, CD33+ myeloid cells appear to contribute to, but are not strictly required for, the core NK-EV-driven response.15 The preferential association of NK92-EVs with monocytes and dendritic cells likely provides a secondary signaling layer that enhances NK cell activation through costimulatory ligand expression or cytokine and chemokine secretion.17,52,53 By flow cytometry, NK92-EVs induce CD4+/CD8+-monocyte conjugates, suggesting EV-driven cell-cell bridging and immune coordination. This supports a model in which NK-EVs could promote immune-synapse formation and functional coordination between immune subsets.54 These findings align with those of Dosil et al., who reported that NK-EVs stimulate IL-2 production, suppress Treg-associated IL-10 secretion, and enhance monocyte-to-dendritic cell differentiation, driving Th1-type polarization.16 Furthermore, cytokine upregulation is consistent with previous findings, where NK-EVs stimulating NK cells cause increased production of IFN-γ and TNF-α.55

In contrast to the depletion results, the modest and non-significant cytotoxic effects observed in enriched CD4+ and CD8+ T cells population are consistent with the established immune mechanisms: NK cells mediate MHC-I-independent tumor cell killing, whereas CD4+ and CD8+ T cells require antigen-specific priming and coordinated support. In this experimental context, where T cells were assessed without CD4+ T cell help or antigen-presenting cells, the comparatively modest cytotoxicity relative to depletion conditions is mechanistically expected. Notably, this reconciles the apparent tension between the CD33+ depletion phenotype and the CD56+ enrichment phenotype—myeloid cells potentiate but do not gatekeep NK-EV activity. In contrast, CD8+ T cells are reprogrammed but appear to play a permissive or supportive role, rather than a central effector role, like the NK cell population. This dissociation warrants dedicated mechanistic investigation in future work.

Conversely, the cargo driving this reprogramming remains an open question. NK92-EVs carry a complex molecular payload, primarily composed of RNA species and proteins, each of which could contribute to mechanisms.4,14,15,56 Direct delivery of cytotoxic cargo, cytokine-driven activation, and small RNA-mediated transcriptional reprogramming represent non-mutually exclusive possibilities, supported by our prior proteomic characterization,4,15 and consistent with reports of NK-EV-mediated T cell modulation.16 Future studies using producer cell line knockouts, receptor blockade, and cargo-specific rescue experiments are required to dissect the relative contribution of each mechanism. Similarly, the durability of NK92-EV-induced reprogramming, whether transient or persistent beyond the 72-h stimulation window, remains an important open question for translational development.

If these findings translate clinically, the translational implications might be substantial. Cancer patients frequently present with dysregulated immune cell compartments, characterized by functional exhaustion, reduced cytotoxicity, altered receptor expression, and metabolic impairment.29,30 Conventional NK cell-directed therapies often rely on ex vivo expansion or adoptive transfer, which are logistically complex, donor dependent, and subjected to the same exhaustion mechanisms following infusion.2,57 By contrast, NK-EVs offer a fundamentally different therapeutic paradigm: rather than replacing dysfunctional NK cells, they may reprogram the patients’ endogenous immune cells. The consistency of the cytotoxic response across donors (n = 9 across the study) provides preliminary support for this concept, suggesting that NK92-EVs can overcome baseline immune dysfunction and reinitiate anti-tumor activity in otherwise immunosuppressed immune cells. Future studies should extend these findings into relevant preclinical models to evaluate translational potential. Humanized mouse models will be particularly important for assessing tumor cytotoxicity and systemic immune modulation. Such models will enable comprehensive evaluation of biodistribution, persistence, safety, and the impact on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes within a physiologically relevant microenvironment.58 If validated, this reprogramming strategy could represent a scalable, off-the-shelf approach to restoring endogenous anti-tumor immunity in patients where cell-based approaches are impractical or have failed.

In conclusion, this is the first study to integrate scRNA-seq with functional live-cell imaging to demonstrate the immunomodulatory effects of clinical-grade NK92-EVs in both healthy and cancer-derived immune systems and to dissect a plausible cellular cascade underlying NK-EV-mediated immune reprogramming. As a scalable platform, NK-EVs hold promise for expanding the therapeutic arsenal against aggressive and treatment-refractory cancers.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture parameters

NK92-MI cells (purchased from ATCC; CRL-2408) and PBMCs were cultured in a serum-free, xeno-free, and feeder-free medium, ImmunoCult-XF T cell Expansion Medium (StemCell Technologies, cat#10981), and maintained at 3 × 105 to 8 × 105 cells/mL. PBMCs were also cultured for 2 days with 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI-FBS, Gibco, cat# 12484-028), without exogenous cytokines or supplementation. Healthy PBMCs (3 donors) and TNBC-derived PBMCs (3 donors) were purchased from StemCell Technologies. Donor characteristics are summarized in Table S1. The human TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 (purchased from ATCC; HTB-26) was cultured in DMEM (Wisent, cat#319-015-CL) supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent, cat#080450). The human breast cancer cell line MCF7 (purchased from ATCC; HTB-22) was cultured in EMEM (Wisent, cat#320-005-CL) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco, cat#11360070), 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, cat#11140050), 10 μg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#91077C), and 1.5 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, S5761). Ethics approval for experiments involving human-derived PBMC samples was obtained from the Health Canada-Public Health Agency of Canada Research Ethics Board under protocol number REB#2022-028H. Cell counts were performed on the Cellometer Auto 2000 Viability Counter (Nexcelom BioScience LLC), using the ViaStain AO/PI Staining Solution (ESBE Scientific, cat#CS2-0106) with the immune cell AO/PI program (channel 1: 470/535 nm for 0.5 s of exposure; channel 2: 540/605 nm for 3 s of exposure).

NK-EV biomanufacturing from NK92-MI cells

NK-EVs were manufactured using a clinically relevant and scalable biomanufacturing workflow that adheres to good manufacturing practices and utilizes NK92-MI cells (IL-2 self-sufficient). Our previous publication provides comprehensive details about the biomanufacturing process and the NK-EV product-related characteristics.4 Briefly, the biomanufacturing of NK92-MI cells and NK-EVs was performed using a closed-loop hollow fiber bioreactor to generate large quantities of clinical-grade NK-EVs. NK92-MI cells are expanded in the cartridge and harvested at the end of the production cycle. Modification in this study is as follows: NK92-MI cells were cultured in GMP-certified ImmunoCult-XF medium (StemCell Technologies, cat#100-0956) with EV-rich conditioned medium (CM) collection performed twice daily (stored at −80°C, daily until further processing).

NK-EV purification by fast protein liquid chromatography using size-exclusion chromatography and ultrafiltration

NK-EV purification was performed as previously described, with slight modifications.4 Thawed NK-EV-rich CM was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min before being loaded into a 60 mL syringe (BD Biosciences, cat#309653) for size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)-based EV purification. SEC was performed on the AKTA Fast Protein Liquid Chromatograph (GE Lifesciences, cat#29022094), using the HiScale chromatography column 10/40 (Cytiva, cat#29360550) packed with CaptoCore 700 resin (Cytiva, cat#17548102) for a total bed height of 20 cm. The column/resin is stored in 20% ethanol (Commercial Alcohols, cat#P006EAAN). Clean-in-place and priming procedures were performed using the following filtered solutions: filtration was performed using the 0.1 μm vacuum filtration unit Filtropur V50 (Sarstedt, cat#83.3941.002): cleaning solution (0.5M NaOH [Supelco, cat#SX0607N-6] and 30% isopropyl alcohol [EMD, cat#PX1834-1] in water), water, and DPBS−/− (Fisher, cat#BP399-1) according to manufacturer protocol. The ultraviolet absorbance was zeroed after the reading stabilized while running filtered DPBS−/− through the instrument. The NK-EV-rich CM was loaded onto the column at a flow velocity of 150 cm/h (flow rate of 2.0 mL/min). Sample fractionation was initiated once the ultraviolet absorbance level reached 230 mAU and stopped when the absorbance level dropped below 1,600 mAU. To ensure sterility, the fractionated sample was combined before filtration at 0.22 μm with a pre-wet (filtered DPBS−/−) Acrodisc Syringe Filter (Pall Corporation, cat#PN4612). Then, the solution was concentrated using a pre-equilibrated (sterilized with 90% ethanol and rinsed twice with filtered DPBS−/−) 10 kDa Centricon Plus-70 Centrifugal Filter - regenerated cellulose membrane (Millipore Sigma, cat#UFC701008) at 4,000 × g for 25 min; the concentration factor was 40–45× pre-concentration volume. The final retentate was collected by inverting the filter into the collection cup and spinning the assembly at 2,000 × g for 2 min. NK-EV products were stored at 4°C, and their use was limited to 7 days post-isolation for the duration of the study.

NTA

NTA analysis was performed as previously described, with slight modifications.4 Purified EV samples were analyzed by NTA using the NanoSight NS300 (Malvern, UK), equipped with a 488 nm laser and an sCMOS camera. For capture settings, the camera level is 14, and the detection threshold is 15 for analysis, resulting in approximately 30–70 particles per frame. Two dilutions were run on the NTA, one ranging from 30 to 50 particles per frame and the other from 50 to 70 particles per frame. After stabilizing it at room temperature, the sample was thoroughly vortexed before preparing an appropriate EV dilution (using filtered DPBS−/−) to obtain a final volume of 1 mL in a 1 mL syringe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#MB9204560TF-LAB) for loading onto the instrument. Roughly half of the syringe content was pushed manually through the instrument until the camera observed stable particles per frame. The syringe was then mounted on the syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus, cat#984730) used for acquisition capture in flow mode using the following NTA script (v.3.4 software): 23°C, one capture of 1 m at a flow speed of 30. After the script was completed, the flow rate was increased to 1,000 for ∼10 s before returning to 30. This sequence was repeated until five captures were taken per sample. This acquisition method allows for the sampling and analysis of the sample through the syringe, ensuring an accurate representation of the entire sample. The five captures of each dilution were averaged separately by the NTA v.3.4 software (Malvern Instruments). To ensure maximal reproducibility between experimental replicates, the EV concentration was calculated for each preparation by averaging the dilution-corrected concentration of both dilutions run on NTA.

Flow cytometric assessment of NK-EV association with PBMCs

CFSE staining of NK-EVs, as previously done59

NK-EVs were incubated with approximately 300 μM of CFSE (Life Technologies, cat# C34554) in 1.5 mL Protein LoBind tubes (Eppendorf, cat# 0030.108.116) for 60 min with gentle mixing (450 RPM on an orbital shaker) at room temperature. A staining control using the same EV preparations was labeled using DiR’; DiIC18(7) (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3',3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide; Invitrogen, cat#D12731), a lipophilic membrane tracer. The same volume of D-PBS combined with an equal amount of CFSE was incubated as a negative control for the assay. Zeba spin desalting columns (7 kDa MWCO, 2 mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#89890) were used to separate stained EVs from unbound CFSE. Briefly, columns were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with three D-PBS washes. Then, CFSE-stained NK-EVs and PBS control were loaded into the columns and spun at 1500 × g for 2 min. Recovered products were processed for NTA to determine particle concentrations. Likewise, fluorescence (Ex/Em = 494/521 ± 10 nm) was measured using a Microplate Reader (BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader; cat#SH1M2G-SN; Gen5 software v.3.14) with a 96-well Flat Clear Bottom Black Polystyrene TC-treated Microplate (Costar, cat#3603) in duplicate.

PBMC treatment with CFSE-stained NK-EVs

A few days before the assay, healthy donor PBMC samples were cultured (see “cell culture parameters”). Using a U-bottom 96-well plate, 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded per well using a pipette repeater. The cells were maintained in ImmunoCult-XF T cell Expansion Medium, with the final well volume normalized to 200 μL and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. The treatment conditions include untreated cells, 1 × 1011 EVs/mL CFSE-stained NK-EVs treated cells, and an equal volume of CFSE-stained PBS treated cells. Notably, the dosing volume was limited to 25% of the total assay volume.

Flow cytometry processing and acquisition

After 4, 24, 48, and 72 h of incubation, PBMCs were collected and processed for flow cytometry. The cells were transferred into a V-bottom plate (Costar, cat#249662) and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. After washing the cells with flow buffer (PBS + 2% FBS), they were stained in 100 μL of titrated antibodies solution containing CD3-AF700 (BD Biosciences, cat#557917), CD4-APC (BD Biosciences, cat#555349), CD8-BV421 (BD Biosciences, cat#562428), CD14-BV510 (BD Biosciences, cat#740163), CD16-BV711 (BioLegend, cat#302044), CD19-BUV737 (BD Biosciences, cat#612756), CD45-APC-R700 (BD Biosciences, cat#566041), CD56-BV605 (BioLegend, cat#362538), and HLA-DR-PE (BD Biosciences, cat#555812), with added 10% BV stain buffer plus (BD Biosciences, cat#566385) on a temperature control orbital shaker set at 4°C and 350 RPM for 30 min, protected from light. The cells were washed twice with flow buffer and resuspended in acquisition buffer (1.5 μM propidium iodide; Life Technologies, cat# P3566). The samples were transferred into 5 mL FACS tubes and analyzed by flow cytometry using the BD FACSAria III Fusion flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) equipped with a 70-μm nozzle (BD Biosciences; P/N 647339) and the BD FACSDiva Software (v.9.0.2). A minimum of 100,000 events per sample were collected at the single-cell live gate.

Flow cytometry data analysis

The gating strategy is detailed in Figure S2. Using FlowJo (v.11.0.1; FlowJo LLC, USA) and R Project software (v.4.4.3), raw data were cleaned with the FlowAI plugin (v.2.3.2). “GoodEvents” were processed to remove obvious debris, doublets, and dead cells. The clean files, containing all the events with embedded compensation parameters, were then exported. FlowAI enhances data quality by detecting and removing outliers and anomalous events based on flow rate, signal stability, and dynamic range. For normalization purposes, the total number of events was set to 100,000 single viable cells per file using the DownSample plugin (v.3.3.1). All concatenated treatment group files within a single donor were combined into a single meta-file, representing all possible events across all groups. This meta-file was used to perform fitSNE and FlowSOM analyses; plugins from the FlowJo-PluginPlay-Bundle-v.1.1.0_Windows10_R-v.4.2.2. Each treatment group was subsequently identified in the meta-file using the “sample ID” function. Algorithm information is as follows: fitSNE is an unsupervised non-linear dimensionality reduction algorithm for flow cytometry data that improves convergence properties and handles challenging datasets better than t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE) analysis. Here, fitSNE analysis was performed with 5,000 iterations and 200 perplexity on compensated fluorescence parameters to provide the most accurate visual representation of the PBMC subsets across samples. Comparatively, FlowSOM is a state-of-the-art SOM for analyzing flow cytometry data, providing a clear overview of marker behavior across cells and detecting otherwise missed subsets. FlowSOM analysis was performed against 12 meta-clusters (Table S2; analytical matrix), using default settings on compensated fluorescence parameters. Subset identification was manually performed based on antigen expression and cross-referenced against the FlowSOM output. FlowSOM clusters comprising <0.1% of total events were excluded to reduce noise. Populations <1% were interpreted with caution due to potential misclassification from low event counts and statistical strength. Limited marker resolution constrained detailed characterization of myeloid and T cell subsets. Each file was individually evaluated at each step through the analysis to ensure the absence of artifacts that could interfere with downstream analysis. This processing scheme enables unbiased cross-sample comparison within a single donor. Cross-donor comparison is not possible due to inherent biological variation in marker-level expression across donors, rendering the fitSNE analysis problematic.

In vitro direct cytotoxicity potency testing of NK-EVs against PBMCs

Direct cytotoxicity of NK92-EVs against PBMCs was performed using our published potency assay.24 PBMCs (5,000 per well) were seeded in 96-well plates in ImmunoCult T cell media, without FBS or any other supplements. Effector EVs were added at varying concentrations (2 × 104, 2 × 105, and 4 × 105 EVs: target cells), with untreated cells and Triton-X-treated cells as negative and positive controls, respectively. After 4 h, PrestoBlue HS Cell Viability Reagent Assay (Invitrogen, cat#P50200) and Cell Event Caspase 3/7 Green Detection Reagent (Invitrogen, cat#C10723) were added in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendation, and fluorescence was measured after 90 min by using a plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader; cat#SH1M2G-SN; Gen5 software v.3.14). The results were averaged and corrected for background.

PBMC subtype depletion/enrichment

PBMC depletion/enrichment processing steps

For depletion experiments, using the EasySep Human Positive Selection Kit from StemCell Technologies, the PBMCs from healthy donors were depleted of CD4 (cat#17852), CD8 (cat#17853), CD33 (cat#17876), CD56 (cat#17855), or CD8/CD56 combo group (processed concurrently and not sequentially) expressing cells, using the EasySep magnet (StemCell Technologies, cat#18000) and separation buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher, cat#R1021). For enrichment experiments, using the EasySep Human Negative Selection Kit from StemCell Technologies, healthy PBMCs were enriched for CD4 (StemCell Technologies, cat#17952), CD8 (StemCell Technologies, cat#17953), or CD56 (StemCell Technologies, cat#17955) expressing cells, using the EasySep magnet and separation buffer, as detailed above. The cells were used for flow cytometry to confirm the depletion/enrichment and for the investigation of the immunomodulatory capacity of NK-EVs using a live-cell imaging cytotoxicity assay.

Flow cytometry processing and acquisition

Depletions/enrichment was verified using flow cytometry. The cells were transferred into a V-bottom plate and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. After washing the cells with flow buffer, they were stained in 100 μL of titrated antibodies solution containing CD3-AF700 (BD Biosciences, cat#557917), CD4-BV421 (BioLegend, cat#344632), CD8-PE-Cy7 (BD Biosciences, cat#560917), CD14-BV510 (BD Biosciences, cat#740163), CD16-BV711 (BioLegend, cat#302044), CD19-BUV737 (BD Biosciences, cat#612756), CD56-BV605 (BioLegend, cat#362538), and HLA-DR-PE (BD Biosciences, cat#555812) with added 10% BV stain buffer plus on a temperature control orbital shaker set at 4°C and 350 RPM for 30 min, protected from light. The cells were washed twice with flow buffer and resuspended in acquisition buffer (1.5 μM propidium iodide). The samples were transferred into 5-mL FACS tubes and analyzed by flow cytometry using the BD FACSAria III Fusion flow cytometer equipped with a 70-μm nozzle and the BD FACSDiva Software. A minimum of 50,000 events per sample were collected at the single-cell live gate.

Investigation of the immunomodulatory capacity of NK-EVs using a live-cell imaging cytotoxicity assay

PBMC treatment with NK-EVs

Healthy and diseased donor PBMC samples were cultured (see “cell culture parameters”). Using a U-bottom 96-well plate, 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded per well by using a pipette repeater, in a final well volume normalized to 200 μL. The samples include untreated cells and 5 × 1011 EVs/mL NK-EVs. The cells were maintained in ImmunoCult-XF T cell expansion medium and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 72 h. Modification for depletion/enrichment, using healthy PBMC donor samples, due to limited sample amount was done was follows: 1.0 × 105 cells of interest (enrichment experiment) or 2.0 × 105 cells of interest (depletion experiment) were seeded per well, using a pipette repeater, in a final well volume normalized to either 100 μL (enrichment) or 200 μL (depletion). Dosing conditions were identical to the above.

Live-cell imaging cytotoxicity assay

MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cell lines were tagged with RFP by lentiviral transduction of the Incucyte Nuclight Red (Sartorius, cat#4625), followed by puromycin (Tocris Bioscience, cat#4089) selection. RFP-tagged cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) and treated with 5 μg/mL mitomycin C (Cayman Chemical, cat#11435) for 1 h at 60%–70% confluence and cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (Gibco, cat#A1896701). Of note, treatment with mitomycin C was not performed in cell-depleted assays. Unstimulated (MOCK) or NK-EV-stimulated (NK-EV) healthy or diseased PBMCs were washed twice with pre-warmed media before seeding them with cancer cells at an effector:target ratio of 2:1. Healthy donor PBMC subtype depletions/enrichments were also tested for the live-cell monitoring assay. NK-EV-stimulated PBMCs were stimulated for 72 h before the live-cell imaging cytotoxicity assay. Importantly, PBMC were washed thrice with pre-warmed ImmunoCult-XF T cell expansion medium to remove NK92-EVs from the solution. Green Annexin V dye for apoptosis (Sartorius, cat#4642) was added to all cells, and then the cells were placed in the Sartorius Incucyte S3, maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for live-cell imaging. Phase-contrast, green-, and red-channel images were captured every 4 h for 96 h. Target breast cancer cell confluence was quantified as the total red fluorescence intensity (RCU × μm2/image) and normalized to cell density at 0 h. Annexin V+ early apoptotic cell count was normalized by RFP+ breast cancer cell count. All analysis was completed on the Sartorius Incucyte 2023A Rev2 software.

scRNA-seq assessment of NK-EV-stimulated PBMCs

PBMC treatment with NK-EVs

Healthy and diseased PBMC donor samples were cultured according to the method given in “cell culture parameters.” Treatment groups are as follows: group 1: EV-treated healthy PBMCs (3 donors); group 2: untreated healthy PBMCs (3 donors); group 3: EV-treated diseased PBMCs (3 donors); and group 4: untreated diseased PBMCs (3 donors). Using a U-bottom 96-well plate, 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded per well by using a pipette repeater. The cells were maintained in ImmunoCult-XF T cell expansion medium, with the final well volume normalized to 200 μL, and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Treatment groups include untreated cells or 5 × 1011 EVs/mL NK-EVs treated cells. Notably, the dosing volume was limited to 55% of the total assay volume.

scRNA-seq (sample collection, fixation, and permeabilization)

Healthy and diseased PBMCs were stimulated for 72 h and then processed for scRNA-seq using the 16-plex Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Prep Kit (10× Genomics, cat#1000414) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, selected treatment groups of PBMCs from the previous section were collected into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. After washing the cells with fresh media, the samples were resuspended in 1 mL for cell counting. After washing the cells with ice-cold PBS, the samples were resuspended in 1 mL of fixation buffer. After 22 h of incubation at 4°C, the cells were spun and resuspended in ice-cold quenching buffers. The cells were counted once more. To maximize sample integrity, enhancer buffer and 10% glycerol were added. Fixed and permeabilized samples were stored at −80°C. Once all samples were acquired and ready for library construction and probe hybridization, they were processed using the V2 barcoding chemistry of 10× Genomics by the StemCore Laboratories at the Ottawa Hospital Research Institute (OHRI, Canada). scRNA-seq was performed on 4 pooled libraries (4 × 4), using the NovaSeq X platform. Libraries were pooled into four sequencing runs, generating between 0.65 and 1.07 billion reads per pool. The total number of cells recovered across all samples was 143,476, with a median recovery of 9,909 cells per sample (targeting 10,000 cells per sample). Reads per cell ranged from 4,238 to 42,454, with a mean of approximately 19,129 reads per cell. scRNA-seq yielded a median of 2,469 genes per cell and 4,198 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per cell, reflecting robust transcriptome capture and library complexity across all samples. Confidently mapped reads were consistently high (≥95%). No significant quality concerns were observed, and all libraries were deemed suitable for downstream analysis. scRNA-seq analysis was performed as follows: the cells were filtered to retain those with 500-4,500 detected genes, 500–14,000 total UMI counts, and <10% mitochondrial gene content. Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder. After quality control, 88,213 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Gene expression data were normalized using SCTransform v.2 with mitochondrial gene percentage regression. Principal-component analysis was performed, with optimal dimensionality determined using an elbow method. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony integration by donor ID. Hierarchical clustering was performed using TooManyCells, a spectral clustering algorithm that constructs a tree-based visualization of cell relationships through recursive bipartitioning on SCTransform-normalized expression data. UMAP dimensionality reduction was applied for two-dimensional visualization. Cell type identification was performed through a multi-level hierarchical approach using output from hierarchical clustering. Manual curation was performed using established canonical marker genes to validate and refine automated annotations. Four major immune cell populations were identified and characterized by their distinctive expression signatures: B cells (MS4A1, CD19, PAX5, ad IGHD), CD4+ T cells (CD4, CCR4, and FOXP3), CD8+ T cells (CD8A, CCR5, and GZMH), and NK cells (NCAM1, KLRD1, and PRF1). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using a stratified paired design with DESeq2 on pseudobulk aggregated data. For each cell type, separate analyses were performed for TNBC and healthy donor populations, followed by meta-analysis to identify genes with (1) TNBC-specific responses, (2) healthy donor-specific responses, or (3) consistent responses across both populations. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Genes were ranked by log fold change and p value for functional enrichment analysis. Pathway analysis was performed using MSigDB C2 collections (size of gene sets: within 20–400 genes). p values were adjusted for multiple testing, using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Pathways were considered significantly enriched at an FDR of 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). The data were normalized to the control group for relative comparison, as indicated in each figure legend. The number of experimental and technical replicates used is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., LaJolla, CA, USA), where a p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and significance differences are marked with single (p < 0.05), double (p < 0.01), triple (p < 0.001), or quadruple (p < 0.0001) asterisks. Two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc tests (Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparisons) was used, as indicated in the figure legend.
