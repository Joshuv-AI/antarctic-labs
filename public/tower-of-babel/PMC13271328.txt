Current practices, opportunities to improve, and behavioral targets related to HPV vaccination in community pharmacies: A qualitative study of pharmacy staff and parents
Authors: Jessie Schwartz, Katherine Meehan, Eliza B Cohn, Jennifer L Bacci, William A Calo, Linda K Ko, Parth D Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) causes 39,300 cancers annually in the United States, yet adolescent vaccination coverage remains suboptimal. Community pharmacies are highly accessible and authorized to vaccinate adolescents but remain underutilized for HPV vaccination. This study examined how current pharmacy vaccination practices align with parent expectations and identified modifiable behavioral and implementation targets to improve uptake. We conducted a qualitative study using key informant interviews with pharmacy staff (n = 11) and parents of adolescents ages 9–17 (n = 13) between February – March 2021. Interview guides were informed by the 5As Behavioral Counseling Framework and Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). Data were analyzed using framework-guided rapid content analysis to identify themes related the vaccination workflow stages. Analyses identified 31 current practices and 35 opportunities for improvement across the 5As, as well as 20 behavioral constructs spanning 12 domains. Key gaps identified by pharmacy staff included inconsistent adolescent vaccine assessment, limited proactive recommendations, lack of standardized communication, insufficient documentation and follow-up for hesitancy, and fragmented reminder and scheduling systems. Parents valued convenience and clear, concise recommendations, while staff highlighted workflow and system constraints. Opportunities centered on standardizing assessment and communication, expanding technician roles, implementing proactive prompts and reminders, improving care coordination with primary care providers, and strengthening follow-up processes. Pharmacy-based HPV vaccination can be strengthened through targeted behavioral and workflow changes across the vaccination continuum. These findings provide a theory-informed, practice-ready framework to guide intervention design and evaluation aimed at increasing HPV vaccine uptake and series completion in community pharmacy settings.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus vaccine, HPV vaccine, HPV vaccination, pharmacy, community pharmacy, pharmacy-based vaccination, adolescent vaccines, vaccine communication, vaccine delivery

Introduction

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) causes 39,300 new cancer cases annually in the United States (US),1 over 90% of which could be prevented through routine vaccination.2 Despite national recommendations since 2006, HPV vaccine coverage remains suboptimal: only 63% of adolescents were up-to-date in 2024 and rates have stagnated.3,4 Pharmacies are promising settings to increase HPV vaccination rates. With over 95% of the U.S. population living within 10 miles of a pharmacy in 2021,5 along with most US states and jurisdictions allowing pharmacists to administer HPV vaccines to preteens and teens,6 pharmacies are well-positioned to expand access and opportunities for vaccination. The President’s Cancer Panel7 and National Vaccine Advisory Committee8 have recommended using pharmacies as part of the “medical neighborhood” to improve vaccine uptake. Additionally, pharmacists are the most prevalent healthcare providers in rural and health professional shortage areas,9 where HPV vaccination disparities are greatest.

Prior research supports the feasibility and acceptability of pharmacist-administered HPV vaccine. National surveys found that 79% of physicians and 81% of parents would support pharmacy-based HPV vaccination.10 However, adolescent HPV vaccination represents a relatively small portion of vaccines administered in pharmacies in the US.6 Wider uptake of pharmacy-based HPV vaccination continues to face barriers, including low parental awareness of pharmacy-based vaccinations11 and inconsistent engagement by pharmacy staff.12 Structural and psychosocial barriers further impede HPV vaccination in pharmacies. Structural challenges include inconsistent insurance coverage, pharmacy staffing constraints, and difficulty tracking multi-dose series completion.13 Psychosocial barriers include parental concerns, especially about vaccine benefits and safety, and misinformation.14,15 Many of these barriers could be mitigated through improving vaccine communication and pharmacy staff engagement with patients.

To date, limited research has examined how pharmacy staff communicate with parents about HPV vaccination within the pharmacy workflow. The purpose of this study was to qualitatively evaluate: (1) how well current vaccination practices in pharmacies align with parents’ expectations for getting their children HPV and other adolescent vaccines; (2) identify opportunities to improve how community pharmacies deliver vaccinations; and (3) determine behavioral and implementation targets for interventions designed to improve adolescent HPV vaccine uptake in pharmacies.

Materials & methods

Study design

This study was a qualitative analysis of data collected through key informant interviews conducted between February and March 2021. The Institutional Review Board at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center approved this study (IRO# 10600). We used The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)16 to prepare this manuscript (see Online Appendix 1).

Participants and procedures

Pharmacy staff

Pharmacy staff were recruited from a regional chain pharmacy located in western Washington State using convenience sampling. A study information e-mail was sent out by the pharmacy’s Director of Clinical Programs inquiring about pharmacy staff’s interest in participating in the key informant interviews. Pharmacy staff were eligible to participate if they were either pharmacists or pharmacy technicians primarily practicing in a community pharmacy and routinely providing vaccines to children ages 9–17. Interested pharmacy staff contacted the study team, who further explained the study, obtained contact information for participants, and scheduled interviews. Verbal consent was obtained prior to the interview. Interviews were conducted by telephone and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant video conferencing platform, based on the participant’s preference. The interviews lasted between 38 and 68 minutes. At the end of the interview, pharmacy staff completed a demographic survey. In total, 22 pharmacy staff contacted the study team, of whom seven pharmacists and four pharmacy technicians provided verbal consent and completed interviews (n = 11). Pharmacy the 11 pharmacy staff that did not complete interviews were unavailable during the study period.

Parents

Parents of children ages 9–17 were recruited using convenience and snowball sampling approaches. We advertised the study through our research institution’s Facebook page, using targeted promotional posts in Washington State. Interested parents who clicked on the study advertisement were redirected to a study information webpage, where they could share their contact information if they were interested in participating. The study staff then reached out to parents, provided additional study information, obtained verbal consent among those who wished to participate, and scheduled interviews conducted by phone or a HIPAA-compliant video conferencing platform. At the end of the interview, parents completed a demographic survey with assistance from the interviewer. Parents who completed interviews were asked to share the study with their peers and direct them to the study team to assess eligibility for interviews. In total, 18 parents contacted the study team, of whom 13 provided verbal consent and completed interviews. Participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Measures: Semi-structured interview guides

The 5As Behavioral Counseling Framework and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) were selected a priori to inform the development of the interview guides. The 5As (Assess, Advise, Agree, Assist, Arrange) Behavioral Counseling Framework is a behavioral counseling approach endorsed by the US Preventive Services Taskforce and has been effectively used in preventive medicine interventions like smoking cessation and physical activity programs.17 The framework provides an easy rubric for clinicians to evaluate and engage patients’ readiness and intentions for behavior change. The 5As approximate typical vaccine delivery steps in pharmacies and, thus, provides a pragmatic structure to inform the topics to be covered during the semi-structured interview. We defined and operationalized each of the 5As in Table 2. The TDF provided a theoretical foundation for our inquiry into barriers and facilitators of HPV vaccination in pharmacies, as it is a comprehensive framework of behavioral and implementation determinants that impact how well individuals like a pharmacist can adopt and integrate an evidence-based practice like HPV vaccination.18 The TDF constructs included in the guide were informed by our previous research on HPV vaccination in pharmacies10,19,20 and our implementation research in pharmacies.21–23 Both the pharmacy staff and parent interview guides were structured similarly for thematic triangulation, and are available as Online Appendix 2. We pre-tested the questions from the interview guide with a convenience sample of parents (n = 3) and pharmacy staff (n = 2) to ensure questions were understood as intended.

Data analysis

Key informant interviews were analyzed using a framework-guided rapid content analysis approach.24 Briefly, two research members (JB, KM) summarized the verbatim interview transcripts using a structured template based on the 5As Behavioral Counseling Framework to generate synopses and identify exemplar quotes about HPV and other adolescent vaccination experiences and practices in the pharmacy. Two separate research members (EC, JS) completed secondary validations of summaries, and all authors weighed in on the summaries to establish consensus about thematic coding and interpretation. The summaries were then consolidated into matrices using Microsoft Excel to identify themes, sub-themes, and exemplar quotes for each communication component of the 5As Framework. Using these matrices, authors (JB, KM, JS, EC) mapped the themes and exemplar quotes to behavioral and implementation domains and constructs defined in the TDF to identify salient behavioral determinants and targets for intervention development. These matrices were iteratively reviewed by all authors to establish consensus in interpretation. Data saturation was achieved through iterative, concurrent data collection and analysis, in which no new themes or insights emerged across interviews with both pharmacy staff and parents. The consistency and redundancy of findings across participant groups indicated that sufficient depth and breadth of perspectives were captured.25

All authors reviewed the themes and sub-themes, exemplar quotes, and mapped TDF domains and constructs as a group to separate qualitative findings as they pertain to current vaccination practices and opportunities for improvement. For current vaccination practices, we assessed how parents’ expectations and vaccination experiences align with what pharmacy staff shared as their current adolescent vaccination practices at their pharmacies. For opportunities for improvement, we assessed the gaps pharmacy staff and parents identified in the way vaccines are delivered. We then collated and compared current practices and opportunities for improvement to specify behavioral and implementation targets as defined by the TDF.

Results

Overall thematic findings

Table 2 summarizes current pharmacy practices and opportunities to strengthen HPV vaccination delivery, with exemplar quotations provided in Electronic Supplemental Tables 1 and 2. Across the 5As, we identified 31 distinct current practices organized under 13 themes and 35 opportunities for improvement spanning 15 themes. We also delineated 20 measurable behavioral constructs mapped to 12 behavioral domains for pharmacy staff and parents, which can be used to operationalize targets and evaluate intervention effects in pharmacy settings, shown in Figure 1, with expanded construct definitions and operationalizations available in Electronic Supplemental Tables 3 and 4.

Assess

Current practices

Pharmacy staff indicated that their pharmacies did not have a standardized, adolescent-focused process for assessing HPV vaccination needs. Assessment varied by location and shift, competed with other workflow demands, and was often oriented toward adult vaccines with established demand. For adolescents, HPV needs were usually identified opportunistically at multiple engagement points (e.g., drop-off, pick-up, counseling, or phone encounters), most often at the pharmacy counter rather than in a private room, and conversations were frequently brief and parent-initiated (1.A). Pharmacy technicians were central to day-to-day operations but were not consistently expected or trained to conduct adolescent vaccine screening, initiate HPV conversations, or provide structured warm handoffs to pharmacists (1.B).

Forecasting tools were widely available and used to verify prior doses, confirm vaccine schedule intervals, and avoid unnecessary vaccination, but HPV vaccine forecasting for adolescents was less systematic than for adult vaccines (1.C). HPV vaccine eligibility checks were often triggered only by parent inquiry or by another vaccine or service, so HPV vaccine recommendations were frequently anchored to other needs rather than explicitly initiated by pharmacy staff (1.C, 1.D). Staff described assuming that primary care clinics were primarily responsible for adolescent vaccines and sometimes hesitated to “overstep,” which could limit proactive offers of HPV vaccination and opportunities to frame pharmacy vaccination as filling gaps in clinic-based care (1.E). Few passive cues, such as paperwork prompts or signage, reliably promoted HPV vaccination needs among parents/patients, since existing forms and posted materials tended to highlight other routinely offered vaccines like influenza or shingles (1.F). Parents also reported that current processes did not consistently offer digital pre-screening options, and paper forms at the counter were not always available or emphasized (1.G).

Opportunities for improvement

First, staff suggested standardizing vaccine assessment across engagement points by explicitly recognizing the counter, counseling areas, and phone encounters as opportunities to identify HPV eligibility and by encouraging brief check-ins about vaccine status rather than waiting for parent questions or vaccination requests (1.A). Second, clarifying expectations and providing brief, skills-based training for technicians and interns were viewed as critical for technician-initiated screening, documentation, and warm handoffs to pharmacists with clear prompts about eligibility and parent vaccine concerns or questions (1.B). Third, pharmacies could prioritize forecasting for preteens and teens alongside adult vaccines and configure automated, age-based prompts tied to date of birth, birthdays, back-to-school periods, and flu-shot encounters; forecast outputs could be printed or displayed as neutral prompts to open HPV discussions and support tracking of eligibility and completion (1.C). Standardizing early recommendation initiation, such as using a simple scripted opener at intake or during counseling, could further reduce missed opportunities and avoid waiting until prescription payment or pick-up to raise HPV vaccination (1.D).

Participants emphasized reframing pharmacy HPV vaccination as complementing, rather than competing with, clinic care by explicitly inviting families to complete HPV vaccination at the pharmacy when clinic-based doses are delayed or harder to access and, when needed, sending brief notifications to primary care providers (PCPs) about vaccine discussions or administered doses (1.E). They also recommended integrating HPV vaccination prompts into standard intake and consent forms, adding short age-specific screening questions, and co-locating HPV vaccine signage with other high-visibility vaccine messaging in the pharmacy to normalize assessment and cue discussions (1.F). Finally, staff and parents endorsed offering multiple flexible screening modalities, including brief online pre-screen tools linked to eligibility outputs and scheduling options alongside paper forms at the counter, so parents can consider vaccination before arriving at the pharmacy (1.G).

Behavioral and implementation targets

Proposed targets for the Assess step map to goal priority, memory and attention, knowledge, social and professional roles, and environmental context and resources (Figure 1). Key indicators include the proportion of adolescent encounters with completed forecasting, age-based prompts and printed forecasts used to initiate discussions, technician screening and warm handoffs, HPV prompts on intake forms and signage, use of private or semi-private spaces when requested, and uptake of online or paper screening tools (Electronic Supplemental Table 3 and 4).

Advise

Current practices

Pharmacy staff employed several strategies to advise parents about HPV vaccination. They typically began by reviewing the child’s vaccination status and then gauged parent knowledge through brief questions, allowing counseling to be tailored to awareness level and informational needs (2.A). Staff consistently referred to trusted sources such as Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines to ensure accuracy and emphasize alignment with national recommendations (2.B). Counseling underscored vaccine benefits, cancer prevention messages, and age-specific recommendations and often reinforced advice from primary care providers to support informed, voluntary vaccination decisions among parents/patients.

Opportunities for improvement

Participants highlighted the value of concise, inclusive phrasing tailored to parents’ knowledge and readiness, as well as brief answers to common questions about safety, timing, and sexual activity (2.C). Packaging core advising points (e.g., guideline-referenced recommendations, parent-friendly benefit statements, and explicit age-based eligibility) into a short, memorable scripts was viewed as a practical way to standardize HPV recommendations (2.A, 2.B). Parents and staff also recommended supplementing verbal counseling with a one-page handout, on paper or digitally, that includes links to trusted/verifiable information sources, a brief side-effects explanation, and simple “next steps” to support information-seeking and decision-making after the visit (2.D).

Behavioral and implementation targets

For the Advise step, targets focus on consistent delivery of a brief, guideline-anchored script and provision of supporting materials (Figure 1). Monitored behaviors include use of the standardized script covering recommendation, benefits, and eligibility, distribution of a one-page handout with trusted links and side-effects information, and staff ability to address common questions using concise, respectful language documented in simple checklist fields (Electronic Supplemental Table 3 and 4).

Agree

Current practices

Pharmacy staff described using motivational interviewing techniques to facilitate parental agreement with HPV vaccination (3.A). Staff reported listening to concerns, exploring reasons for hesitancy, and offering neutral, factual responses tailored to the specific questions parents raised, with the intent of preserving rapport and supporting autonomous, informed decision-making (3.A). Convenience emerged as a central facilitator: staff highlighted convenient location, hours, quick form completion, short wait times, and same-day vaccine availability as features that help translate interest into acceptance when cost barriers are minimal (3.B). Parents echoed that pharmacy convenience often made same-day vaccination feasible.

However, pharmacies reported limited processes to revisit decisions when vaccination was deferred or declined. Reasons for delay or refusal were rarely documented in a structured way, and there were few mechanisms to “close the loop” or reengage parents at later encounters (3.C). Communication of vaccine hesitancy to the child’s PCP was not routine and occurred mainly when parents explicitly requested sharing information, which limited opportunities for team-based problem solving (3.D). No standardized practice was in place to normalize giving parents more time to decide or to schedule check-backs after an initial discussion, so follow-up depended heavily on parents returning with new questions (3.E).

Opportunities for improvement

Pharmacy staff suggested documenting concern type (e.g., safety, timing, eligibility, or cost) and encounter outcome (administered, deferred, or declined) in a structured field to guide future counseling and enable staff to plan specific times to revisit the decision, such as at the next refill or annual visit (3.A, 3.C). A team-based communication model in which pharmacy staff send a concise note to the PCP when parents express concerns or repeatedly defer vaccination could align messaging and encourage coordinated responses (3.D). To maintain convenience while reducing cost barriers, participants proposed leveraging allowable incentives and enrolling as a Vaccines for Children (VFC) provider for eligible families (3.B). The VFC is a federally funded program to provide the recommended vaccines to eligible children at no cost. Finally, staff recommended normalizing that some parents need additional time, explicitly offering the option to “decide later,” and scheduling brief check-backs via text, phone, or in-person visits to re-open discussions without pressuring families (3.E).

Behavioral and implementation targets

Agree targets map to social and professional role, reinforcement, environmental context and resources, and decision processes (Figure 1). Indicators include coded documentation of concern type and outcome, scheduling and completion of check-backs, frequency of PCP notifications about hesitancy, availability of cost-support options such as Vaccines for Children, explicit offers of time to decide, and time from initial discussion to eventual acceptance among initially hesitant families (Electronic Supplemental Table 3 and 4).

Assist

Current practices

Pharmacy staff described a consistent workflow for vaccine administration. Trained technicians and interns typically supported data entry and vaccine preparation, while pharmacists verified the vaccine product and dose before administration and provided the Vaccine Information Statement (VIS) to address immediate questions (4.A). Vaccinations were generally administered in private rooms, which staff viewed as promoting comfort and confidentiality for children and parents (4.B). During administration, staff reported explaining each step in age-appropriate language, using brief distraction strategies, and sometimes offering small incentives (such as stickers) to reduce distress (4.C). Staff also noted readiness to monitor and respond to rare adverse events, such as syncope, using established protocols and basic medical supplies. Parents preferred concise verbal explanations for expected minor post-injection effects, while some wanted written guidance for uncommon but serious side effects (4.D).

Opportunities for improvement

Participants suggested refinements to make assistance more predictable and family-centered. Routinizing an adolescent-focused “what to expect” script, paired with a plain-language take-home sheet, could standardize counseling across staff and vaccination encounters and better accommodate varying parent preferences for information depth (4.A, 4.D). Reducing the time between consent completion and injection through advance preparation and rapid staff cueing was emphasized as central to lowering anticipatory anxiety (4.C). Staff also recommended modest environmental and behavioral changes, such as making vaccination rooms more child-friendly, offering choices about arm or timing (e.g., counting down before injection), and engaging directly with children to support comfort during vaccination (4.C). Where feasible, using the same private space for pre-vaccination HPV counseling as for administration could facilitate sensitive discussions and reduce counter-based deferral (4.B).

Behavioral and implementation targets

Assist targets emphasize environmental resources, skills, and attention processes (Figure 1). Indicators include measuring the wait time from consent to injection, the proportion of counseling and administration occurring in private or semi-private rooms, documented use of a standardized “what to expect” script and take-home information, routine use of at least one age-appropriate comfort strategy, readiness to manage minor adverse events, and parent-reported clarity and satisfaction with counseling and comfort measures (Electronic Supplemental Table 3 and 4).

Arrange

Current practices

Pharmacies reported routine electronic reporting of administered doses to the state immunization data registry, Immunization Information System (IIS), through pharmacy management systems that record vaccine, date, and dose and transmit records automatically, reducing manual entry and facilitating retrieval (5.A). Parents preferred these electronic records over stand-alone paper cards (5.A). Communication with primary care providers occurred most often by fax and typically only when parents requested it (5.A). Reminder and scheduling processes for subsequent HPV doses were inconsistent. Staff frequently asked parents to set reminders on their own phones or calendars, and appointment systems often had short scheduling horizons, limiting pre-booking of later doses and making series completion highly dependent on parent initiation (5.B).

Opportunities for improvement

Participants identified options to make follow-up more systematic and less reliant on parent memory. Pharmacies could implement automated reminders for subsequent doses using multiple channels (e.g., text, e-mail, or automated calls) aligned with parent preferences and integrated into dispensing or scheduling platforms (5.B). Staff-facing prompts or dashboards that flag adolescents due or overdue for HPV vaccination could support proactive outreach during routine encounters, such as prescription pick-up or medication counseling (5.B). Extending the scheduling horizon would allow staff to create the next HPV appointment at the first dose visit and provide families with a confirmed follow-up time that can later be modified (5.B). Participants also recommended routinely notifying PCPs after each administered dose, with parent consent, to close information loops and support coordinated counseling across settings (5.A).

Behavioral and implementation targets

Arrange targets map to behavioral regulation, memory and decision processes, and environmental context (Figure 1). Indicators include the proportion of recipients receiving system-generated reminders and staff acting on due or overdue lists, verified IIS upload and routine PCP notification, the ability to pre-schedule subsequent HPV doses at the first visit, and on-time series completion within recommended intervals (Electronic Supplemental Table 3 and 4).

Discussion

Guided by the 5As Behavioral Counseling Framework, this study addressed three primary aims: (1) assessing how current pharmacy-based HPV vaccination practices align with parents’ expectations; (2) identifying opportunities to improve vaccine delivery in community pharmacies; and (3) specifying behavioral and implementation targets to inform intervention development. Overall, findings revealed substantial misalignment between existing pharmacy workflows and parent expectations, particularly in early vaccine assessment, proactive recommendation, and follow-up processes. Vaccination practices were often opportunistic, reactive, and inconsistently delivered, whereas parents expressed a preference for clear, concise recommendations, proactive engagement, and streamlined, convenient care. Across the 5As, we identified 31 current practices and 35 opportunities for improvement, alongside 20 measurable behavioral constructs spanning 12 domains. Key gaps included limited standardization of adolescent vaccine assessment, insufficient use of structured communication strategies, lack of systematic documentation and re-engagement for hesitant parents, and fragmented reminder and scheduling systems. These findings highlight specific, modifiable points within pharmacy workflows where targeted behavioral and implementation strategies can better align service delivery with parent needs and refine interventions aimed at increasing HPV vaccine uptake and timely series completion.

Analyses using a framework-guided rapid content approach revealed participants’ emphasis on concise, guideline-linked recommendations and counseling as well as reliable approaches to HPV vaccine series initiation and follow-through. These findings align with evidence that reminder/recall for providers26 and parents27 and proactive scheduling28,29 improve vaccinations rates, including HPV series completion.30–32 Our behavioral and implementation targets for automated, multichannel reminders and follow-up appointment creation after series initiation are consistent with those reported and measured in previous trials and systematic evidence reviews in adolescent populations28,29,31,33,34 and with a recent large-scale field work on reminders in pharmacy contexts.35 Pharmacy staff reported use of brief motivational interviewing (MI) and neutral education discussed in the Agree step is concordant with literature suggesting MI can reduce vaccine hesitancy and support vaccine acceptance, and with reviews indicating that both motivational counseling and focused education can improve uptake when delivered systematically.36–39 The operational changes proposed for the Assist step (e.g., standardize talking points, dose prepping, use of private room during vaccination) are consistent with cognitive load theory, which predicts that people will have better comprehension and decision quality when extraneous load (e.g., noise, distractions, time pressure) is reduced and information is chunked.40–43 Finally, the business-process changes highlighted in the Assess and Arrange steps (e.g., technician-initiated screening, IIS integration, VFC participation, routine PCP communication/collaboration) reflect and extend prior pharmacy literature demonstrating that pharmacists improve access and can positively influence vaccine uptake when workflows and policy supports are in place.44–50 Together, these findings integrate prevailing behavior change mechanisms, like cueing, goal prioritization, role clarity, environmental restructuring, and reinforcement, with pragmatic pharmacy operations to specify how, and at which step, behavior change can occur and be measured for HPV vaccination quality improvement.

Based on the qualitative themes and our mapped behavioral and implementation targets, pharmacies can make a handful of straightforward changes that collectively support pharmacy staff and parent behaviors that move families from interest to on-time HPV vaccine series initiation and completion. First, pharmacy staff should assess patient vaccination needs earlier during workflow encounters and more consistently: turn on age-based prompts within pharmacy management systems for adolescents (including back-to-school and flu-shot visits), add HPV vaccination items to intake vaccine questionnaires and consent forms, empower pharmacy technicians to screen patients, use vaccine forecasts as neutral conversation starters with parents, and offer a private or semi-private space to have vaccine discussions when parents prefer it. Next, pharmacists should advise parents and patients with consistent and unambiguous messaging every time: use a short, inclusive script that links the recommendation to guidelines, states the vaccine benefit in plain language, and clarifies age-specific eligibility; be ready with brief answers to common questions; and give an accessible educational handout with trusted links and a short side-effects explanation. When it comes to getting parents to agree with vaccinating their children, make the decision easy to revisit: record the reason for deferral or decline, schedule a specific check-back, share a concise note with the child’s primary care clinician (with consent) to align messages, and reduce cost barriers by participating in VFC where feasible. When pharmacy staff are assisting with vaccination, reduce anxiety and keep the visit moving: use a brief script with parents and patients to help explain what to expect, minimize time from consent to injection, hold counseling in a private space when desired, and pair verbal reassurance about minor side effects with a brief take-home sheet for rare events. Finally, pharmacy staff should arrange reliable follow-through after a vaccination occurred: create a schedule for subsequent HPV doses before patients leave, send reminders through multiple channels, and report vaccinations to the IIS and the child’s PCP. Each of these steps can be tracked with concrete behavioral and implementation indicators, making the behavioral changes measurable, auditable, and ready for continuous quality improvement.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, a framework-guided rapid analysis explicitly anchored to the 5As positioned us to move from thematic description to behavioral specification, yielding a set of targets that are directly implementable in pharmacy vaccination workflows. Second, inclusion of parents and pharmacy staff provided triangulation across patient-facing experiences and behind-the-counter workflows, increasing ecological validity for both communication and operational processes. Third, we link qualitative findings to measurable indicators (e.g., adolescent forecasting rates, inclusive advising script use, concern documentation and check-backs, wait time, private/semi-private counseling, automated reminders, early appointment creation, series completion), supporting pragmatic evaluation in future behavioral intervention and implementation studies. Important limitations also warrant consideration. The sample of pharmacy staff participants was drawn from a single regional pharmacy chain in one US state, which may limit generalizability to other pharmacies and policy environments. However, many of the principles and vaccination practice improvements we recommend based on findings are increasingly being adopted across a range of pharmacy settings,51–53 which enhances the generalizability of our findings. Pharmacy management systems, IIS connectivity, and scope of pharmacy practice – including adolescent vaccination authority54 and VFC participation47—vary across states and organizations, potentially affecting feasibility and impact. Our qualitative study aimed to specify where to intervene and what behavioral and implementation outcomes to measure; as such, future work should test the validity and usefulness of these targets prospectively in multi-site, mixed-methods studies, to track HPV vaccine uptake, equitable access to pharmacy-based services, and sustained use.

Conclusion

This study clarifies how pharmacy-based adolescent HPV vaccination currently unfolds and where modifiable gaps persist between pharmacy workflows and parents’ expectations. By moving beyond process description to specify concrete behavioral and implementation targets associated with vaccine eligibility assessment, provider recommendation, parent/patient consenting, vaccine administration, and follow-through, our findings provide a theory-informed, practice-ready blueprint for future intervention design and evaluation in community pharmacies. The convergence of staff and parent perspectives strengthens confidence in the relevance of these behavioral and implementation targets that can be embedded in routine operations and tracked with auditable indicators. Future multi-site, mixed-methods trials should prospectively test and refine these targets to determine their impact on HPV vaccine uptake, on-time series completion, and sustained integration of adolescent vaccination services in pharmacies.
