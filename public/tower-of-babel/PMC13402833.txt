Decadal-scale pollen records link land use and plant diversity change across European lowlands over seven millennia
Authors: Fabian Rey, Willy Tinner, Lucia Wick, Erika Gobet, Eric Allan, Sönke Szidat, Urs Leuzinger, Oliver Heiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the long-term drivers of biodiversity change requires datasets that expand the timescale of ecological observations. Using lake sediments as natural archives, we reconstruct plant diversity change at high temporal resolution across the Swiss Plateau, a key region for European land use history. Contiguous pollen records, from three sites, spanning 7.6 millennia, were constrained by 227 radiocarbon dates and partly annually laminated sequences, yielding decadal-scale chronologies. We examine relationships between palynological richness (PRI), a proxy for plant diversity, and reconstructed land use probability (LUP) across six cultural–historic epochs. Vegetation histories are similar across sites: while beech forests persist throughout, other deciduous trees decline and grassland taxa expand from the Neolithic onwards. Plant diversity responds strongly to land use, increasing with openings, cultivation and fire, and declining during crises such as the Migration Period and the Black Death. Medieval and early modern landscapes support the highest richness, while diversity has declined in recent decades, likely due to industrialised agriculture. Our findings show that humans have been major drivers of plant diversity change in European lowlands for more than seven millennia and that sustainable land use legacies from the past may hold the key to reversing today’s biodiversity losses.Subject terms: Archaeology, Palaeoclimate, Agroecology, Palaeoecology

Introduction

Human-driven global change is reshaping ecosystems worldwide1–3. Rising temperatures and intensifying land use are altering species distributions, restructuring communities and driving widespread biodiversity loss4–6. Yet, present-day dynamics are not only shaped by current pressures. Past climate variability and, above all, millennia of human land use have been key drivers of biodiversity change, influencing how ecosystems developed and how plant communities were structured through time7–9. Understanding these long-term drivers is essential for explaining present biodiversity patterns and for predicting future trajectories. Such perspectives require palaeoecological approaches that extend far beyond the timescales of current ecological records, since biodiversity survey data do not extend further back than a few decades10–14.

Established and emerging palaeoecological proxies such as pollen, plant macrofossils and ancient DNA provide critical insights into the mechanisms that generate and maintain biodiversity across millennia15–17. In early Holocene Europe, vegetation change was governed largely by natural processes, including climate variability and biotic interactions18,19. With the introduction of agriculture around 7–8 millennia ago, however, human activities became increasingly dominant in shaping vegetation20,21. Forest clearance, selective exploitation of trees, crop and fruit tree cultivation, and the spread of agroforestry fundamentally restructured plant communities22,23. Pre-industrial land use not only transformed European plant communities but also affected biodiversity by increasing disturbance (e.g. browsing, grazing, cutting and burning), ultimately leading to more open landscapes and habitats with novel species assemblages16,24,25.

Despite the recognised importance of land use in shaping European ecosystems, few studies have quantified its long-term influence on plant diversity17,26. Existing reconstructions typically focus on total richness and are often restricted in either temporal or spatial resolution. Diversity estimates are usually based on multi-centennial resolution, limiting their ecological relevance and preventing robust analyses of high-resolution, decadal-scale change across multiple sites. High temporal resolution is critical to understand the causes of past diversity and compositional change27.

Here, we present a study that infers plant diversity changes based on sedimentary pollen at decadal resolution over the last 7.5 millennia across the Swiss Plateau, a key region for land use reconstructions in Central Europe. We analyse 2043 pollen samples from three lakes spanning a transect of ca. 120 km (Fig. 1), anchored by robust and comparable chronologies based on 227 radiocarbon dates. This high temporal resolution allows us to detect diversity dynamics at time scales relevant for understanding ecological processes.

Our approach goes beyond estimating total pollen richness. By partitioning diversity measures into trees, shrubs and herbs, we provide a long-term comparative assessment of how different functional groups contributed to plant diversity, linking changes directly to forests, shrub and grasslands. Furthermore, by exploiting the chronological precision of our records, we examine the relationship between land use probability (LUP), disturbance and diversity across six major cultural–historical epochs: the Neolithic, Bronze Age, Iron Age, Roman period, Middle Ages and Modern Era (Supplementary Table 1).

By integrating high-resolution palaeoecological data with rigorous analytical approaches, this study demonstrates how human activity has shaped biodiversity in European ecosystems over millennia. Our findings not only reveal the long-term origins of present-day plant diversity but also highlight the importance of historical legacies in shaping future biodiversity trajectories under ongoing global change.

Results

Sediment core lithologies and chronologies

The records from the three study lakes span 7.3 thousand calibrated 14C years BP (cal ka BP) at Moossee, 6.5 cal ka BP at Burgäschisee and 7.6 cal ka BP at Hüttwilersee. The three sites rank among the best-dated lake records in Europe (Supplementary Tables 2–5). Only a few radiocarbon outliers were identified (5 of 232 samples; 2.2%), providing chronologies with decadal-scale precision. All sequences consist of organic lake sediments, with partly varved (i.e. annually laminated) sections at Moossee and Burgäschisee28,29. Together, these precise chronologies enable robust regional comparison of vegetation and plant diversity dynamics.

Vegetation, land use and fire history

Across the ca. 120 km on the Swiss Plateau, vegetation histories show regional coherence over the past 7.5 millennia (Figs. 2–8 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Beech (Fagus sylvatica) forests dominated throughout, underscoring their resilience to both climatic variability and millennia of human disturbance. However, other deciduous taxa (Acer, Fraxinus excelsior, Tilia and Ulmus) steadily declined from the Neolithic to the Bronze Age (ca. 6.8–3.4 cal ka BP), with a parallel decrease in the contribution of tree pollen types to the total pollen richness, possibly pointing to long-term overexploitation of disturbance-sensitive late-successional tree species29,30. Open-land habitats expanded in phases, often linked to fire disturbance peaks marked by microscopic charcoal influx. Distinct increases in burning occurred during the Neolithic (around 5.7 cal ka BP), the Roman Period (ca. 1.8 cal ka BP) and throughout the Middle Ages (1.5–0.5 cal ka BP), coinciding with expansions of herbaceous taxa, cultural indicator pollen31 (CI; Σ Cerealia-type, Plantago lanceolata), and light-demanding shrubs (Corylus avellana). These patterns indicate that burning, cultivation and grazing by large herbivores (for which we used coprophilous Sporormiella-type fungal spores as a proxy) shaped landscape openness and were key drivers of diversity gains (Figs. 2–8 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3).

The pollen richness (PRI) signal closely tracked land use across all sites. Specifically, total PRI increased with cultural indicators and land-use probability (LUP), a relationship confirmed as significant by linear mixed effects modelling (LME) with a temporal autocorrelation structure (Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating that human activities promoted higher richness by creating or maintaining habitat mosaics. The LME also identified interactions between land use and epochs, suggesting that the effects of land use on PRI varied across the different cultural periods covered by our record (Supplementary Table 6). The Neolithic diversity maximum (5.7 cal ka BP) was influenced by very local and transient herb expansions associated with pile-dwelling settlements along the lake shores32,33. Disturbance often triggered pulses of Corylus avellana and Betula (pioneer taxa), followed by successional recovery to mixed beech forest. The last such large-scale succession cycle occurred during the Migration Period (around 1.5 cal ka BP). Afforestation phases during periods of agricultural crises are associated with lower palynological richness, primarily reflected in the reduced abundance of herbaceous taxa typical of open-land habitats (e.g. Cerealia-type, Plantago lanceolata, Achillea-type, Papaver rhoeas-type, Rumex acetosa-type, Trifolium pratense-type, Trifolium repens-type, and others; see Figs. 6–8 and Supplementary Data 1–3). These changes may primarily reflect decreased landscape openness and habitat heterogeneity (i.e. reduced beta diversity across the landscape) rather than absolute species loss.

Several pronounced total plant diversity minima punctuate the records. During the Migration Period (ca. 1.5 cal ka BP), PRI dropped to levels comparable to the Neolithic, coinciding with markedly reduced LUP. A second major decline occurred during the Medieval Economic Crisis (ca. 0.6 cal ka BP), when the combined effects of the Black Death, warfare, societal unrest, as well as reduced land use promoted forest regrowth and consequently lowered plant diversity. A third drop followed World War II, reflecting agricultural intensification and homogenisation of landscapes (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Diversity levels at present are comparable to those during the Black Death.

Long-term trajectories also highlight shifts in vegetation composition. From the Roman Period onward, fruit and nut trees (Castanea sativa, Juglans regia, Maloideae and Prunus) together with Vitis gained in importance, enhancing the arboreal contribution to total pollen richness (Fig. 2a). Quercus was an essential element of Neolithic oak–beech forests and was favoured by humans from at least the Iron Age onward, probably linked to acorn-feeding practices34. This inference is supported by influx peaks of Sporormiella-type fungal spores as a grazing proxy. From the Middle Ages, however, landscapes remained permanently open, with no subsequent large-scale natural forest recovery. This persistent openness and the lack of closed forests may explain the weaker relationship between LUP and PRI during the Modern Era (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Other distinct farming impacts are evident. Hemp retting is detectable mainly in the Middle Ages and Modern Era (Cannabis sativa peaks in the records; Figs. 6–8). Neophytes (Zea mays, Platanus) spread during the last 200 years, marking the onset of modern floristic novelty. In contrast, recent decades are characterised by declines in herb and shrub diversity.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed palaeoecological reconstruction of vegetation and plant diversity dynamics for Europe covering the past 7.5 millennia. By analysing more than 2000 pollen samples from three lakes with robust chronologies, underpinned by over 220 radiocarbon dates and partly varved (i.e. annually laminated) sediments, we are able to provide decadal-scale resolution of diversity change, which is a scale relevant for understanding ecological processes. This level of precision contrasts sharply with most Holocene studies that typically resolve centennial to millennial trends16,24,35–37. Besides their significance for ecological assessments, such high temporal resolution and precision are critical for meaningful comparisons with archaeological data and historical records32,33,38–40. Taken together, the combination of high-resolution pollen data, functional-group separation and statistical analyses allows us to disentangle the long-term relationships between land use, climate and plant diversity.

A central outcome is the tight coupling between pollen richness (PRI), as a proxy for overall plant diversity16,24, and LUP from the Neolithic until the early Modern Era (Figs. 3 and 5). This link is consistently supported by patterns in crop and weed pollen, together with fruit and nut tree taxa, all indicative of anthropogenic activity (Figs. 2–8 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Surprisingly, human impact (LUP) triggered strong increases in plant diversity already during the Neolithic, the earliest phase of crop cultivation and animal husbandry. In later epochs, however, the relationship became less steep and slightly saturated (see flatter slopes of LOESS smoothers in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). This pattern likely reflects the very short land use gradient during the Neolithic, when overall levels of human activity were still low, and small increases in land use had disproportionately large ecological effects. Importantly, PRI trends diverge from those expected under climate forcing alone. Although summer temperatures declined gradually after the mid-Holocene29,41 (Fig. 2h), diversity increased, which contrasts with the expected pre-agricultural trend of higher richness during warmer periods16. From the onset of farming, land use rather than climate became the dominant driver of diversity change. This finding corroborates the idea that human activity, even at low population densities42,43 (Fig. 2i), quickly overtook climate and other natural drivers (e.g. disturbance regimes, species interactions, soil development) as the main determinant of plant community composition and biodiversity in European landscapes. Our results differ from recent findings in European mountain ranges near the timberline17,25, where pastoral activities, specifically herbivory by large mammals, were the main drivers of biodiversity increase over the past millennia. In contrast, our records from the fertile lowlands indicate that arable farming and fire disturbance similarly promoted biodiversity (Fig. 5). These dynamics coincided with sustained demographic growth at European and global scales42,43 (Fig. 2i), reinforcing the link between population growth, land-use intensity and enhanced biodiversity.

The role of individual taxa highlights the complexity of millennial-scale human–diversity interactions. Quercus, for instance, was a natural co-dominant tree in oak–beech forests and later became favoured by forest management from the Iron Age through the Middle Ages, but this relationship collapsed during the Modern Era (Fig. 5b). Archaeological and palaeoecological evidence suggests that oak was encouraged for its acorns, while later pre-industrial silvicultural strategies reinforced its presence in managed landscapes29,34. Whether such practices enhanced or reduced diversity depended on the group of plants: oak promotion created light-demanding habitats that supported herbs and shrubs but also contributed to the decline of competing late-successional hardwood trees, such as Tilia and Ulmus. Our results thus support the view that human forest use was never neutral. It selectively favoured certain taxa, reshaping diversity trajectories.

At finer centennial temporal scales, our data show that the initial Neolithic increase in PRI was localised and primarily driven by herb diversity. This likely reflects the establishment of local lake shore settlements44–47, which created small but ecologically diverse open habitats around villages and along lake margins. Such diversity gains were therefore spatially restricted and transient, underscoring the importance of settlement context in interpreting early agricultural impacts. Later epochs, particularly the Roman Period and Middle Ages, show much broader and regionally coherent increases in richness, linked to large-scale clearance, crop cultivation and the spread of open land (Figs. 2, 6–8 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Unlike earlier epochs, during which more intensive land use promoted higher plant diversity, an apparent upper boundary of diversity values was reached in the post-medieval period. Beyond this threshold, further increases in land use intensity did not result in higher PRI (Fig. 3b). This diversity plateau likely reflects the fact that traditional land use mosaics had already maximised landscape heterogeneity and the diversity of herbaceous taxa, so that subsequent intensification yielded diminishing returns.

On decadal scales, our data reveal that diversity losses were often rapid and closely tied to socio-economic crises. Between 1.7 and 1.5 cal ka BP, for instance, sustained PRI declines of ca. 1.25% per decade cumulated in a 25% loss over two centuries (Fig. 2a). Plant diversity dropped to levels comparable to those of the Neolithic 5–6 millennia earlier, reflecting widespread land abandonment, forest regrowth, and the temporary collapse of cultural landscapes during the Dark Ages48 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). When land use and forest management recovered after the Migration Period, diversity rebounded at similar rates (ca. 1.25% per decade), highlighting the reversibility of such declines under pre-industrial systems. The Black Death pandemic and the associated Medieval Economic Crisis provide a second, highly instructive example49. Agricultural contraction led to afforestation and sharp decreases in diversity, pushing PRI between 0.6 and 0.4 cal ka BP to levels very similar to those observed today. This decline coincided with a marked reduction in European and global population size42,43,49 (Fig. 2i), underlining the tight connection between demographic contraction, diminished land use and biodiversity loss in pre-industrial societies. In both cases, recovery of land use coincided with renewed increases in diversity.

The Medieval period emerges as one of the most diverse epochs in our records, reflecting the peak development of mixed-use agrarian systems, diversified cropping, fruit tree cultivation and widespread use of fire23,50. Importantly, even modern pre-industrial landscapes of the 18th and 19th centuries maintained very high diversity, suggesting that the cultural landscapes of early modern Europe sustained species richness at levels unrivalled before or since51,52. However, during the past 80 years, this beneficial relationship between land use and plant diversity has been reversed.

The industrialisation of agriculture initiated a fundamentally new epoch for plant diversity. Fossil fuels allowed mechanisation, chemical fertilisers, large-scale drainage and field enlargement, replacing heterogeneous land use mosaics with simplified, homogenised systems53,54. The result of this epochal change was severe biodiversity loss2,55–57. Processes such as the conversion of diverse meadows into species-poor grasslands, overfertilization, use of herbicides, removal of hedgerows, and abandonment of traditional standard fruit tree cultivation may have all contributed to plant diversity decline58,59. The magnitude of the recent loss is at least comparable to that following the Black Death pandemic, with rates of change similar to those observed during the Dark Ages (ca. 1.35% loss per decade; Fig. 2a), underlining its severity in a historical context. However, unlike earlier crises, today’s declines have occurred despite unprecedented global population growth42,43 (Fig. 2i), reflecting a qualitative shift under industrialised farming: more people and intensified land use no longer foster greater diversity but instead drive homogenisation and decline.

Our findings underscore how humans shape landscape-scale plant diversity over the long-term. Far from being a purely modern phenomenon, humans have influenced species richness for at least seven millennia. Crucially, our data also demonstrate that diversity losses are not irreversible. Both after the Migration Period and following the Black Death pandemic, declines in PRI were followed by rapid recoveries on decadal scales once land use re-established. This resilience suggests that modern plant diversity loss could likewise be reversed, provided that land use is restructured toward heterogeneity and low-intensity management. Historical knowledge of pre-industrial practices, such as crop diversification, maintenance of orchards and hedgerows, small-scale farming, as well as integration of woodland use, remains accessible. Recent initiatives in organic farming, permaculture and agroecology echo these traditions and may provide pathways to restore biodiversity in agricultural landscapes60,61.

The broader implications of this work extend beyond the Swiss Plateau and Central Europe. By achieving decadal-scale resolution over multiple sites, we demonstrate that farming has been the principal driver of vegetation change and biodiversity at continental scales. Its beneficial effect on biodiversity persisted through millennia to come to an end only during the past eight decades. Conversely, agricultural and economic crises were associated with decadal-scale decreases of plant diversity. The tight link between PRI and LUP highlights the necessity of integrating palaeoecological perspectives into conservation planning. Modern biodiversity policies often rely on short-term baselines10,11, yet our data show that plant richness in European lowlands evolved gradually and step-wise through prehistorical and historical times, reflecting the economic efforts of hundreds of generations. This may imply that millennial-long pre-industrial strategies to minimise harvest losses through diversification strategies in crops and land use procedures sustainably promoted biodiversity in Europe62. Effective conservation strategies must therefore account for long-term human legacies rather than aiming to re-establish a reconstructed, supposed or sometimes even imagined ‘natural’ baseline. Although such restoration is relevant in specific contexts, such as National Parks, where natural processes are protected in restricted areas, our results suggest that sustainable farming and agroforestry have the potential to strongly foster and maintain biodiversity at larger scales.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that land use has shaped plant diversity in European lowlands for over seven millennia, often enhancing richness through the creation of heterogeneous landscapes. The decadal-scale resolution of our records reveals both the resilience and vulnerability of diversity to human activity. While industrialised agriculture has recently undermined the positive relationships between land use and biodiversity, historical precedents disclosed in this study suggest that alternative land use systems can restore diversity. The close linkage between the climate and biodiversity crises and the fact that the global population is higher than ever before underscores the urgency of rethinking land use.

Methods

Study sites

The three study lakes lie in the Swiss lowlands north of the Alps in southwestern Central Europe (Fig. 1a, b). Moossee and Burgäschisee are in the western Swiss Plateau, Hüttwilersee in the east, spanning ca. 120 km between the westernmost and easternmost sites. The lakes are similar in altitude, size, depth and catchment characteristics and are set in gently rolling terrain with no major inflows. Moossee (521 m a.s.l.; 47°1′17.0′′ N, 7°29′1.7′′ E) lies near Bern, comprises two basins and has a maximum water depth of 22 m and a surface area of 0.31 km². Burgäschisee (465 m a.s.l.; 47°10′8.5′′ N, 7°40′5.9′′ E), ca. 25 km northeast of Moossee, reaches 31 m water depth and covers 0.21 km². Hüttwilersee (435 m a.s.l.; 47°36′33.2′′ N, 8°50′36.7′′ E) lies ca. 100 km northeast of Burgäschisee near Lake Constance and has a maximum depth of 15.6 m and a surface area of 0.34 km². All lakes are currently meso- to eutrophic with anoxic bottom waters63,64.

The regional climate is temperate, with most precipitation accumulating from May to September. The western sites (Moossee, Burgäschisee) average ca. 9.3 °C and 1025 mm annual precipitation, whereas around Hüttwilersee it is slightly warmer and drier65 (ca. 9.9 °C, 960 mm).

The vegetation is currently similar at all sites with mixed Fagus sylvatica forests on surrounding hills, scattered Alnus glutinosa and Betula trees along shores and in wet areas, as well as intensive agriculture (crops, meadows) on adjacent lowlands (Fig. 1c–e). The vegetation history of the study region is representative for Central and Western Europe66 with at least a 7000-year history of agriculture, evidenced by local Neolithic pile dwellings, which form part of the UNESCO World Heritage 'Prehistoric pile dwellings around the Alps'67.

Corings, chronologies

At Moossee (in spring 2014), three parallel sediment cores (total length 7 m each) were retrieved from the deep basin using an UWITEC piston corer (9 cm diameter, 200 cm sections). At Burgäschisee (in spring 2014), two parallel 7 m cores were taken with the same method. At Hüttwilersee (in autumn 2019), four parallel cores (max. length 13 m) were recovered from the deep basin. The studied interval spans the Neolithic to present (last 6.5 cal ka BP [Burgäschisee] to 7.6 cal ka BP [Hüttwilersee]) and includes annually laminated (varved) sections at Moossee and Burgäschisee28,29. The coring campaigns were conducted with the necessary permits from the Canton of Bern (Moossee), the Cantons of Bern and Solothurn (Burgäschisee), the Canton of Thurgau and the heirs of André Bridel (Hüttwilersee).

The chronologies are based on extensive radiocarbon dating (Moossee: 52 samples, Burgäschisee: 83 [1 outlier excluded, 2 for comparison only], Hüttwilersee: 92 [4 outliers excluded, 6 for comparison only]), calibrated with IntCal2068, supplemented by 137Cs dating of surface sediments and Bayesian age-depth modelling (Figs. 9, 10, Supplementary Tables 2–5 and Supplementary Data 4, 5). Only terrestrial, short-lived plant macrofossils were dated to minimise reservoir or inbuilt age effects69,70. Radiocarbon analyses using accelerator mass spectrometry and measurements of 137Cs activity with a Broad Energy Germanium detector (Canberra GmbH, Rüsselsheim, Germany) were both carried out at the Laboratory for the Analysis of Radiocarbon (LARA), University of Bern.

The Moossee chronology29,69 was re-calibrated with IntCal20 and updated with 137Cs dates. It uses OxCal V-sequence71 for the varved Neolithic to Iron Age sections (Supplementary Data 5) and Bacon72 (priors: mean accumulation rate = 10 years/cm, accumulation shape = 1.5) for other remaining intervals. The Burgäschisee model69,70 applies OxCal P-sequence73,74 (parameter k = 1), V-sequence for varved Bronze Age to Medieval sections (Supplementary Data 5) and Bacon (priors: mean accumulation rate = 10 years/cm, accumulation shape = 1.5) elsewhere. The Hüttwilersee model75 uses Bacon (priors: mean accumulation rate = 10 years/cm, accumulation shape = 1.5) throughout. All three chronologies show narrow uncertainty ranges, typically within a few decades for most of the record.

Pollen, non-pollen palynomorphs and charcoal analyses

Sampling at each site was designed to optimise temporal resolution: for Moossee and Burgäschisee, where varve counts were used when available, this resulted in 815 and 723 samples respectively (ca. 9 years per sample). For Hüttwilersee, contiguous 2 cm slices were taken for the Neolithic and Bronze Age (ca. 18.5 years per sample), followed by 1 cm subsamples for the remaining period (ca. 9 years per sample), totaling 505 samples. Standard sample volume was 1 cm³ across all sites. Samples were processed following established protocols76, involving HCl, KOH, HF, acetolysis treatments, sieving (500 μm mesh) and mounting in glycerine. Lycopodium spore tablets (University of Lund batch no. 1031 with 20,848 ± 3457 spores per tablet for Moossee, University of Lund batch no. 177745 with 18,584 ± 1853 spores per tablet for Burgäschisee and University of Lund batch no. 140119321 with 19,855 ± 521 spores per tablet for Hüttwilersee) were added prior to chemical processing for concentration estimates77. Pollen identification was based on keys76,78 and photo atlases79, and was achieved with ×400 magnification under a compound microscope, targeting at least 500 terrestrial pollen grains per sample. Pollen preservation was excellent, and all analyses were conducted by a single experienced analyst to ensure consistent taxonomic resolution across sites. The results are expressed as percentages of the terrestrial pollen sum, excluding aquatic plants, Cannabis sativa (due to hemp retting practices), fern and fungal spores, as well as other microfossils such as algal cells (Figs. 6–8). For Fig. 2c–g, the three separate datasets were combined and smoothed with a LOESS function (0.05 smoothing factor, 95% confidence intervals). While long-distance pollen transport (up to 50 km) can influence assemblages, the small lake sizes (0.21–0.34 km²) restrict the expected main pollen source area to a few hundred metres to a few kilometres80,81.

Microscopic charcoal particles (>10 to <500 μm), representing regional fire activity, were counted on pollen slides using standard methods82,83. Charcoal influx (particles cm−2 yr−1) is presented in Figs. 6–8 and combined across the three datasets (Fig. 2f) using the same LOESS smoothing approach as for pollen data. Pollen, non-pollen palynomorph and charcoal diagrams were prepared using Tilia version 3.0.1 and finalised in CorelDraw.

Numerical analyses

Palynological richness (PRI) was estimated by rarefaction analysis in PAST 5.2.184, and is widely used as a proxy for plant diversity in palaeoecology16,24,85–91. Calculations were based on a minimum terrestrial pollen sum of 500 grains16,86, enabling inter-site comparison and dataset combination (Figs. 2a and 6–8). The combined PRI record was smoothed with a LOESS function (smoothing factor 0.05, 95% confidence intervals), selected to capture decadal- to centennial-scale variability. Because no single pollen type exceeded 40% abundance in our samples (Figs. 6–8), the assemblages were relatively even and not dominated by individual taxa. This means that the likelihood of encountering various taxa remains consistently high, minimising evenness effects32,33. Therefore, no additional detrending of PRI was applied. PRI was partitioned into trees, shrubs and herbs by rescaling sample proportions according to the taxa presence found in the original counts (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 1–3).

To reconstruct past land use, we applied the LUP index92, which integrates cultural indicator pollen types weighted by their anthropogenic indicator values (AIV). Cultural indicators include both primary indicators of cultivation (Cerealia-type, Secale, Zea mays, Cannabis sativa, Fagopyrum, Vicia faba-type, Linum usitatissimum-type, Castanea sativa, Juglans regia and Olea europaea) and secondary indicators (weeds) associated with human disturbance (Plantago lanceolata, Mercurialis annua and Urtica dioica). Herbaceous and arboreal taxa are combined within the same index. The LUP represents the probability of land use at a given time, but does not strictly measure the intensity of management. Since all sites are located in the Swiss lowlands north of the Alps, AIVs for the colline–montane belt were applied. For each site, LUP values were calculated as the sum of indicator pollen percentages multiplied by their corresponding AIVs (Figs. 6–8), and subsequently combined and smoothed with a LOESS function (smoothing factor 0.05, 95% confidence intervals, calculated in PAST 5.2.184; Fig. 2b)

The relationship between LUP and total palynological richness (PRI) was explored and visualised for the overall dataset and individual time intervals using LOESS smoothers (span = 1, degree = 1; calculated using the function loess in R version 4.593; Fig. 3) and generalised additive models (GAMs, calculated in Canoco 594; Supplementary Fig. 2). A high smoothing span was selected to capture the overall trends. Both approaches yielded comparable patterns of changing relationship strength between land use and plant diversity across time. Additional LOESS and GAM analyses examined PRI in relation to cultural indicator pollen percentages31 (CI; Cerealia-type, Plantago lanceolata) and to fruit and nut tree pollen (Castanea sativa, Juglans regia, Maloideae and Prunus) plus Vitis (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3), representing key proxies of land use through arable farming and fruit cultivation. For epoch-specific analyses, the dataset was subdivided into six cultural–historical periods (Neolithic, Bronze Age, Iron Age, Roman Period, Middle Ages, Modern Era; Supplementary Table 1), and separate LOESS smoothers and GAMs between LUP and PRI were calculated for each time interval.

Linear mixed effects modelling (LME) with temporal autocorrelation structure was used to test the statistical significance of the LUP–PRI relationship (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Code 1). We also tested for interactions between LUP and the six epochs covered by our record to assess whether the relationship between LUP and PRI changed across the time intervals. LMEs were fitted with the lme function in the R (version 4.593) package nlme. PRI was log-transformed to improve the normality of the residuals. The model included LUP, the epochs and their interaction as fixed effects. We tested several different autocorrelation structures for the residuals; in each case, autocorrelation was estimated for each lake separately. We tested autoregressive processes of orders 1 and 2, AR(1) and AR(2) and a continuous autoregressive process, CAR(1). The AR (2) process yielded the best model (lowest AIC), with small (<0.2) and mostly non-significant autocorrelation in the residuals (Supplementary Fig. 4). We also attempted to fit a continuous moving average process, ARMA(1,1), which further reduced residual autocorrelation, however, parameter estimates were close to the non-stationary boundary (φ ≈ 1, θ ≈ −1), indicating potential overfitting. Nevertheless, fixed effect parameters were very similar in the AR(2) and ARMA(1,1) models.

To explore environmental gradients in the pollen data (all 2043 samples), ordination analyses were performed in Canoco 593. A detrended correspondence analysis95 (DCA) with square-root transformation, detrending by segments and no down-weighting of rare taxa was first used to test whether unimodal or linear species responses were appropriate. As the length of the first DCA axis was short (1.79 SD units), principal component analysis (PCA) was selected for subsequent analyses. Microscopic charcoal influx (fire proxy), Sporormiella-type influx (grazing proxy), chironomid-inferred summer temperature for the region29,41, total pollen richness (PRI), LUP, as well as pollen percentages of trees and cultural indicators31 (CI; Cerealia-type, Plantago lanceolata) were included as passive environmental variables in the main PCA (Fig. 5a). This allowed comparison of individual taxa (Corylus avellana typically spreading with disturbance, Betula characteristic for early succession, Fagus sylvatica indicative for late succession, Quercus for late succession and/or land use) with different vegetation groups (e.g. trees, cultural indicators), climate and ecological factors (e.g. fire activity, land use). To assess possible changes in these relationships across the examined time window, separate ordinations were conducted for six cultural–historical epochs (Supplementary Table 1). In all cases, the first DCA axis length was short (ranging from 1.00 to 1.55 SD units), confirming PCA as the appropriate ordination method. The same taxa and environmental variables were included in these epoch-specific analyses (Fig. 5b).
