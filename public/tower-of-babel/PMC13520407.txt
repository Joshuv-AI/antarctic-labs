Investigation of the effect of flipped learning on neuroanatomy education in dentistry faculty students
Authors: Nevnihal Akbaytürk, Tayfun Aygün
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFlipped learning is a learning model in which students are active and effective. This study is a quasi-experimental study conducted using a pretest-posttest-follow-up test design with experimental and control groups to evaluate the effect of flipped learning on the education of second-year dental students regarding the facial nerve in neuroanatomy.MethodsThe study was conducted with 59 students in the experimental (n = 28) and control (n = 31) groups in the 2nd year of Giresun University Faculty of Dentistry. Data were collected using the “Student Demographic Characteristics Form”, “Facial Nerve Anatomy Knowledge Test”, and “Flipped Learning Feedback Form”. Students in the experimental group were trained using the flipped learning method prepared by the researchers. Students in the control group were trained using the traditional education method. Permission and ethical approval were obtained from the institution where the study was conducted (19.03.2025, no:2025/47).ResultsA statistically significant difference was found between the pretest, posttest, and follow-up test scores on the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test for students in the experimental group (p = 0.000). A statistically significant difference was also found between the pretest, posttest, and follow-up test scores for students in the control group (p = 0.022). However, no statistically significant difference was found between the experimental and control groups across all three tests (p > 0.05). Students in the experimental group rated the benefits of the flipped learning method as beneficial, with a median value of 4 (3–4).ConclusionIn this study, both flipped learning and the traditional teaching method significantly improved students’ knowledge of facial nerve anatomy. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups. As a student-centered approach, flipped learning can serve as a complementary method to traditional anatomy education.Keywords: Anatomy, Dental education, Dental students, Flipped learning

Introduction

Anatomy forms the foundation of medicine and health sciences. It is often perceived by students as a difficult and complex subject [1]. Neuroanatomy, with its scope and complexity, is one of the most challenging components of the anatomy curriculum [2]. While traditional anatomy education is supported by numerous teaching methods, advancements in technology have led to the increasing use of effective, engaging, and innovative alternative methods that motivate students and enable their active participation in the learning process.

The flipped classroom is an innovative learning model and an alternative method. Flipped learning aims to make the time spent in the classroom efficient and effective by focusing on individual learning [3, 4]. It differs from the traditional face-to-face learning method in that students learn their lessons whenever and wherever they want before coming to school [5]. Being student-centered and supporting active learning in the learning process, this model has played an important role in integrating traditional education with technology-based methods [6]. Learning is transforming from an instructor-centered model to a student-centered approach that includes individual or team-based collaborative learning [7].

Flipped learning is increasingly being used as an effective teaching and learning method, and research on it is growing. It is emphasized that flipped learning helps with lasting learning and improves students’ academic performance and overall success [6]. The “flipped classroom” teaching model is becoming increasingly common in medical education [8]. In the literature, the flipped classroom model is used in many different fields, from nursing [9–11] to dentistry [7, 12]. There are many studies on flipped learning in anatomy education. Studies show that the flipped classroom method offers advantages in effectively understanding and recalling conceptual and factual anatomical content [13], that it is effective in learning neuroanatomy compared to traditional classrooms but has some practical challenges [2], that it is a more effective strategy compared to traditional classroom methods [1], that it is advantageous for students [14], that it has a positive effect on improving performance in anatomy practical exams [15], and that it increases the motivation of dental students [16]. Overall, the flipped classroom is perceived positively and shows advantages such as increasing self-confidence, academic achievement, participation, learning activities, interaction, satisfaction, and overall performance in anatomy courses [17].

Traditional education is an instructor-centered method where the lesson is delivered in the classroom by the instructor. Students passively listen to the lesson, and in this process, they progress through the first two levels of Bloom’s taxonomy: remember and understand. Afterward, they independently perform the applications related to the higher levels. In a flipped classroom, students complete the first two levels independently at home. The applications related to the higher levels are then performed in the classroom through activities with the instructor’s guidance [18].

The primary area of work for dentists is the head and neck region, innervated by cranial nerves. Neuroanatomy training is of great importance to the anatomy curriculum for dental students [19]. Understanding the anatomy and functions of cranial nerves is essential for establishing an anatomical foundation in clinical practice. However, due to the perception of the subject’s complexity, students often struggle to comprehensively understand fundamental concepts using only textbooks and models [20].

The facial nerve (VIIth cranial nerve) is of critical importance to patients in clinical practice and surgical procedures due to the characteristics of its fibers, its branches, its topographic anatomy, its innervation, and its clinical significance. It is essential to enhance learning methods so that students can effectively and efficiently apply this knowledge in clinical diagnosis and treatment processes. In this context, the student-centered nature of the flipped learning approach can contribute to better understanding of topics in anatomy education, reinforcement of knowledge, and improvement of students’ academic performance. The literature contains numerous studies on the use of the flipped learning model in various areas of anatomy education. However, it is noteworthy that studies focusing on the facial nerve among dental students are limited. This necessitates a focus on methods that support the more effective and efficient learning of the facial nerve, which holds a significant place in clinical practice. Recently emerging innovative approaches in anatomy education, such as narrative-based teaching and multimedia-supported instruction, have also had positive effects on student participation and conceptual learning [21–23].

This study aims to evaluate the effect of the flipped learning model on the anatomy education of the facial nerve among second-year dentistry students.

Methods

Study design

This study was a quasi-experimental design with an experimental and control group using a pretest, posttest, and follow-up test design to evaluate the effects of flipped learning on anatomy education of second-year dentistry students.

Study Setting

This research was conducted as part of the anatomy course offered to second-year students at Giresun University Faculty of Dentistry during the spring semester of the 2024–2025 academic year. The anatomy course consists of two hours of theoretical instruction and two hours of laboratory instruction. Theoretical courses are taught using PowerPoint presentations and question-and-answer methods, while laboratory courses are taught by the instructor using anatomical models.

Participants

The study population consisted of 63 s-year students at Giresun University Faculty of Dentistry during the 2024–2025 academic year. In the selection of second-year dentistry students, the fact that the students had not yet completed the cranial nerves section of their neuroanatomy curriculum was taken into account; this provided a suitable sample for implementing pre-class activities related to the facial nerve within the framework of the flipped learning model. Four students who were repeating the course were excluded from the class.

Power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. According to the results of a study conducted by Jha et al., a minimum sample size of 27 participants per group was calculated (effect size: 1.00, power 0.95, α = 0.05) [14]. Therefore, the study was completed with 59 students (experimental group: 28, control group: 31). As a result of post hoc power analysis, power was found to be 0.95.

Second-year students at Giresun University Faculty of Dentistry who volunteered to participate in the study and had access to a smartphone/computer and internet access were included in the study. Upper-year students, students taking the course who did not have a smartphone/computer, and did not volunteer to participate in the study were excluded.

In this study, second-year students of the Faculty of Dentistry were randomly assigned to experimental and control groups. The assignments were determined by drawing lots based on the last digit of their student number. As a result of the draw, students with an odd-numbered student number were assigned to the experimental group, and those with an even-numbered student number were assigned to the control group. Since student numbers are assigned administratively and are not related to academic achievement, it is not considered likely that this method of group assignment based on odd-even numbering would create systematic academic bias.

Data collection tools

Research data were collected using the “Student Demographic Characteristics Form,” the " Facial Nerve Anatomy Knowledge Test,” and the “Flipped Learning Feedback Form.”

Student demographic characteristics form

The form consists of four questions regarding student demographics (age, gender, difficulty level of the Anatomy course, and resources and tools used while studying the Anatomy course). To help students express their opinions more clearly, they were asked to provide a code instead of their names. Students responded to the difficulty level of the anatomy course on a scale from 1 (very easy) to 5 (very difficult). Students were asked to select one or more resources and tools they used while studying anatomy from the following options: anatomy atlas, anatomy book, anatomy models, mobile anatomy app, video content platforms, and online platforms.

Facial nerve anatomy knowledge test

The facial nerve theoretical course topic and the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test questions were prepared by researchers in line with the literature [24, 25]. The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test, designed to assess the knowledge levels of students in the Faculty of Dentistry regarding anatomy, consists of 20 multiple-choice questions, each with five options. The anatomy knowledge tests were evaluated by three anatomy experts and one measurement and evaluation expert. The experts evaluated all questions.

Following expert opinions, a preliminary application of the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test was conducted with second-year dentistry students (n = 61). The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test is a multiple-choice test, with 1 point awarded for each correct answer, for a maximum score of 20. The test consists of 20 multiple-choice questions. After the pilot study, the item difficulty and item discrimination indices of the test were calculated based on the responses received. As a result of the analysis, the KR20 value of the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test was determined to be 0.95, and two questions were removed. After removing 2 questions from the knowledge test, the KR20 value was found to be 0.96. The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test consists of 18 multiple-choice questions. Students answered the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test online via a Google Forms. The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test was administered in a pre-test, post-test, and follow-up test format.

Flipped learning feedback form

Students evaluated the benefits of flipped learning by selecting one of the following options: 1 (not beneficial) to 5 (very beneficial). They completed the feedback form online via a Google Forms.

Materials used in the flipped learning model

Six instructional materials were prepared by the researchers for the students in the experimental group to use before and during the lesson, in accordance with the flipped learning model.

As part of the pre-lesson applications, video materials included a 13-minute 55-second video on facial nerve explanation and a 6-minute 41-second video on anatomy models. Students were also given a puzzle consisting of 13 questions, worksheets with 7 open-ended questions, and 3 visual questions. During the lesson, question-and-answer and discussion methods were used. To reinforce learning, digital tools (Kahoot, Google Forms) were prepared by the researchers. A test consisting of 10 visual open-ended questions was sent to students online via a link during the lesson using Google Forms. A test consisting of 15 questions in total was prepared on the Kahoot platform. This test consisted of 5 true/false questions, 6 visual multiple-choice questions, and 4 theoretical multiple-choice questions. These questions were sent to students online via a link during the lesson.

Implementation of the study

Participants’ consent to participate in the study was obtained after reading the “İnformed Voluntary Consent Form” on the first page of the Google Forms and checked the “I want to participate in the study” box. Students who agreed to participate in the study were administered the “Student Demographic Characteristics Form” and the " Facial Nerve Anatomy Knowledge Test” online via Google Forms (pre-test). Students were divided into experimental and control groups based on whether the last digit of their school number was even or odd.

The students in the experimental group were taught using a flipped learning model by the researchers. A researcher created a whatsapp group for the students participating in the study. Links to the digital materials (lecture videos, anatomy model videos, puzzles, and worksheets) that students would study before class were sent via WhatsApp 10 days before the class. Students accessed the links on their mobile phones to watch the lecture video and anatomy model video as many times as they wanted, and solved the puzzles and worksheet questions. Their answers were sent to the researchers. The class then moved on to the “facial nerve” lesson, which is included in the anatomy curriculum. In this class, students summarized the information they learned from the videos and addressed any points they didn’t understand using prepared digital resources (Kahoot and Google Forms) and question-and-answer and discussion methods (Table 1) (Fig. 1). Students in the experimental group were interviewed, and they were given consent not to share information with their peers.

Flipped learning procedures among dentistry faculty students

All students in the control group were taught using traditional instructional methods. No resources were provided for preparation beforehand. The control group received instruction using PowerPoint presentations, question-and-answer, and discussion methods.

The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test and the Feedback Form for the experimental group were administered online via Google Forms (post-test) to the experimental and control groups. The facial nerve anatomy knowledge test was administered again one month later (follow-up test). The materials used in the flipped education model were shared and discussed with the control group and all students in the class at the end of the study.

Statistical evaluation

The data obtained from the research were evaluated using the SPSS 23.0 package program. Descriptive statistics of the evaluation results are given as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean, standard deviation, and median (Q1-Q3) for numerical variables. The conformity of the groups to a normal distribution was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Due to the lack of a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U test was used in two-group comparisons. Dependent repeated-measures were tested using the Friedman analysis in three groups (pre-post follow-up test). The Wilcoxon test was used to determine the difference between dependent groups. The Chi-Square Test was used for categorical data. Data were evaluated at a significance level of P < 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Karadeniz Technical University Health Sciences Scientific Research Ethics Committee, number 2025/47, dated March 19, 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional permission for the study was obtained from the Giresun University Faculty of Dentistry. Participants’ consent to participate in the study was obtained after reading the “İnformed Voluntary Consent Form” on the first page of the Google Forms and checked the “I want to participate in the study” box. All participants were informed about the study and were informed that they were free to participate or not, that their data would be kept confidential, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. They were also informed that the materials used in the study would be shared with the control group students at the end of the study.

The median pre-test score for the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test in the control group was 7 [4–9], the median post-test score was 11 [7–14], and the follow-up test score was 10 [3–16], indicating a statistically significant difference (p = 0.022, p < 0.05). This significance was found between the pre-test and post-test scores, and between the pre-test and follow-up test scores. Pre-test-post-test: Z=-3.642, p = 0.000; Pre-test-follow-up test: Z=-2.368 p = 0.018) (p < 0.05) (Table 5). Although the post-test and follow-up test scores for facial nerve anatomy knowledge were numerically higher in the experimental group, no statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in terms of pre-test, post-test, and follow-up test scores (p > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

The flipped classroom model involves students independently acquiring knowledge before face-to-face classroom sessions, where they participate in active discussions and problem-solving. It is an effective way to develop active learning and critical thinking skills among students. Advantages of the flipped learning approach include improved student preparation, active participation, and critical thinking skills. Disadvantages include technical issues such as internet connectivity and lack of planning and motivation [26]. Flipped learning has been defined as one of the methods being investigated for clinical dentistry education [27]. The effectiveness of the flipped learning model in anatomy education was investigated by focusing on the anatomy of the facial nerve, which plays an important role in the education and clinical practice of dental students.

In our study, no statistically significant difference was found between the resources used and not used by students in the experimental and control groups while studying anatomy. It was found that students in the experimental and control groups frequently used anatomy books and video based platforms when studying anatomy (Table 3) (Fig. 2). It is similar to some studies in the literature. Doğan et al. found that students mostly used lecture notes when studying the theoretical part of anatomy and mostly used the textbook when studying the practical part [28]. In their study, Xiao and Adnan noted that both first-year (75%) and third-year (88%) students strongly agreed that digital tools are highly valuable and interactive for studying anatomy [29]. In the study by Yılmaz et al., it was noted that while the traditional method is the most appropriate approach for teaching anatomy, the best alternative is the use of 3D applications [22]. These findings indicate that educational materials used in anatomy instruction vary according to students’ individual learning styles and that the impact of digital materials has increased with advancements in technology.

Anatomy is often perceived as a difficult subject for students to grasp due to its extensive content. Undergraduate students are taught anatomy using a combination of theoretical and practical methods. Traditional education involves a teacher-centered approach and teaching with a passive, one-way transfer of knowledge from teachers to students. In anatomy education, more emphasis needs to be placed on better understanding and learning the subject [7, 30]. In our study, students in the experimental and control groups rated the difficulty level of the anatomy course on a scale from 1 (very easy) to 5 (very difficult). The median value was found to be 4 in both groups (Table 4). It has been observed that most students perceive anatomy as difficult. Effective, visually supported active teaching methods can be recommended to change this perception of difficulty. Dentists must have accurate anatomical knowledge to practice safely in the clinic. For comprehensive learning, students must be able to safely apply the anatomical knowledge they acquire in practice. Flipped learning is an innovative approach to understanding and learning anatomy concepts. Feedback from students using flipped learning model is crucial in evaluating its effectiveness. Evaluating student feedback and contributing to the educational process will contribute to the quality of education. In the study, when the flipped learning benefit scale of students in the experimental group was evaluated, the median value was 4 (Table 4). The findings indicate that the majority of students viewed the flipped learning method as beneficial. This result is consistent with the findings of other studies in the literature [12, 13, 30, 31]. These results indicate that the flipped learning model can effectively increase learning efficiency in challenging subjects such as anatomy. It was determined that there was a statistically significant difference between the pre-test, post-test, and follow-up test of the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test of the students in the experimental group (Table 5). The pre-test score of the experimental group was 6.14 ± 3.55, while that of the control group was 7.48 ± 3.98, indicating that the pre-test score of the experimental group was lower than that of the control group. However, when the post-test scores were evaluated, it was found that the experimental group had a post-test score of 12.14 ± 3.49 and the control group had a post-test score of 10.54 ± 4.31, indicating that the experimental group had a higher post-test score. The experimental group’s post-test score was found to be higher than the pre-test score and higher than the control group’s post-test and follow-up test scores. This increase indicates that student participation improved test scores and had an impact on learning progress. It supports the idea that the flipped learning model is an effective method. Similar to the study’s findings, there are studies in the literature that support this finding, showing a significant increase in average scores using flipped learning compared to the traditional method. Hanafy et al. found that mean exam scores were significantly higher for pre-test flipped learning and post-test flipped learning compared to pre-test and post-test traditional exams [2]. In their study, Shahid et al. found that the mean post-test score of the experimental group receiving education in a flipped classroom and the control group receiving education in a traditional classroom increased significantly compared to the pre-test score, and the post-test score of the experimental group was significantly higher than the control group [1]. In the Joseph study, they found that students in the flipped classroom performed significantly better on the posttest compared to the pretest [31]. In Xiao’s study, students who were continuously exposed to the flipped classroom teaching model reported significantly higher scores than the control group regarding the effect of the flipped classroom model on learning difficult anatomy concepts [32]. Nanjundaiah et al.’s study found that the average value of academic test scores was higher after flipped classroom sessions compared to traditional teaching sessions [33]. This suggests that flipped learning supports students’ learning and helps them prepare.

In the experimental group, the difference between the pretest and posttest and the pretest and follow-up test was found to be significant. The difference between the posttest and follow-up test was found to be non-significant. The mean score of the experimental group in the follow-up test after 1 month was found to be higher than that of the control group receiving traditional education (Table 5). Similar to our study result, Kasat et al. found that long-term evaluation scores after 2 months for the flipped module were higher than for the traditional modules, although not statistically significant [13]. The increase in scores on the follow-up test shows that students remember the information they learned using the flipped learning method and that it helps them retain it, demonstrating that learning is permanent. Lecture-based medical education, where the instructor is at the center, is expected to face disadvantages such as one-way transmission of information, stagnation and fatigue, a long time spent teaching the course, and rapid forgetting of content [17]. Sadeghi et al. reported in their study that approximately 80% of the content taught using the lecture method was forgotten within the first eight weeks [34]. Active learning, on the other hand, offers more lasting learning by placing the student at the center. This is more effective than the teacher-centered, passive lecture approach [35].

The study found a statistically significant difference between the pre-test, post-test, and follow-up test scores of students in the control group who received traditional education on the facial nerve anatomy knowledge test (Table 5). This significance indicates that traditional education is effective due to students’ participation in theoretical lessons. Similar to this study, Jumaa et al. reported a significant difference between pre-test and post-test scores in the study on anatomy practice in traditional learning [35]. Traditional education involves face-to-face interaction and communication between students and the instructor during theoretical lessons. Face-to-face interaction provides a supportive learning environment with a positive effect. This significance is thought to be due to the students’ study time and different learning style characteristics.

In the control group that received traditional training, a significant difference was found between the pre-test and post-test, and between the pre-test and follow-up tests. No significant difference was found between the post-test and follow-up tests (Table 5). No statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in terms of pre-test, post-test, and follow-up test scores for facial nerve anatomy knowledge (p > 0.05) (Table 5).

Conclusion

In this study, both flipped learning and traditional teaching methods significantly increased students’ knowledge of facial nerve anatomy. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups. The findings indicate that flipped learning, rather than establishing a significant advantage over traditional teaching methods, supported the acquisition of facial nerve anatomy knowledge at a similar level. Nevertheless, flipped learning, with its student-centered structure, can contribute as an applicable auxiliary method in teaching facial nerve anatomy.

Limitations

The study was conducted with second-year dental students at a single university. The sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the research is a single-center study with a short follow-up period. The study’s focus on only one topic also narrows its scope. The fact that assessments were conducted online should also be considered. Although students were divided into groups, they were not prevented from accessing and acquiring information from various anatomy resources. All these limitations suggest that the study should be supported by larger sample sizes and longer-term follow-ups.
