The impact of knee positioning on joint space width change: Data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative
Authors: J.T. Crawmer, R. Tang, A.E. Nelson, T. Neogi, Y.M. Golightly, X. Sun, E. Bedrick, C.K. Kwoh, J. Duryea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveQuantify the longitudinal effect of knee positioning on radiographic joint space width (JSW) in knee OA.DesignThis longitudinal cohort study analyzed 1612 knees (806 participants) from the Osteoarthritis Initiative with baseline Kellgren-Lawrence grade = 0 and 12-month follow-up. Images obtained via a standardized fixed-flexion protocol measured minimum JSW (mJSW), fixed-location JSW (fJSW), and a variable related to knee positioning (Xrim). Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models, adjusted for age, gender, race, and BMI, examined associations between changes in ΔfJSW or ΔmJSW and ΔXrim. A secondary case-control analysis of 69 participants KLG 1–3 over 4 years examined the effect of poor but consistent Xrim values on JSW responsiveness, using standardized response means (SRM).ResultsGEE models showed a significant positive association between ΔXrim and both ΔfJSW (95% CI: 0.07–0.15) and ΔmJSW (95% CI: 0.05–0.13). The association between ΔXrim and ΔfJSW was significantly stronger in females, with a significant ΔXrim × gender interaction term (95% CI: 0.03–0.13), with no significant gender difference identified for the association between ΔXrim and ΔmJSW. With ΔfJSW as the outcome, estimated slope for ΔXrim was 0.19 (95% CI, 0.15–0.22) in females and 0.11 (95% CI, 0.07–0.15) in males. No effect modification by age, race or BMI identified. Secondary analysis revealed fJSW responsiveness was unaffected by poor but consistent positioning (cases: -0.80, controls: −0.76), while mJSW was (cases: −0.81, controls: −0.58).ConclusionInconsistent knee positioning affects JSW measurement. Poor but consistent positioning has a minimal effect on fJSW responsiveness but a larger impact on mJSW.

Introduction

Radiography remains a standard imaging modality for tracking disease progression in large cohort studies of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) and clinical trials due to its cost-effectiveness and widespread availability. Although MRI is frequently used in clinical trials [1] for its superior visualization of soft tissue, including cartilage thickness and volume, radiographic joint space width (JSW) remains a robust structural biomarker. In fact, JSW has proven comparable ability to track OA progression with MRI [2] [3]. Unfortunately, JSW can be affected by variations in knee positioning which can systematically shift and bias the measurement of change [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. It can also be affected by factors other than cartilage thickness, such as meniscal position and ligamentous integrity, which on their own can impact the progression of OA [11].

Studies have investigated the influence of medial tibial plateau (MTP) alignment on JSW measurements and consistently show that changes in JSW are highly sensitive to variations in inter-margin distance (IMD), which is defined as the distance between the projected anterior and posterior margins of the medial tibial plateau [[4], [5], [6], [7],12]. Even minor deviations in IMD between time points (i.e., >1 mm) can result in JSW changes that equal or exceed the mean annual rate of true joint space narrowing (JSN) in OA patients, leading to false impressions of OA progression or improvement [6]. For example, a 1 mm change in IMD was associated with a 0.10 mm variation in JSW over 12 months, which is similar in magnitude to the expected OA-caused JSN over that time [6]. Therefore, knee positioning is considered satisfactory in most studies if there is < 1 mm between the anterior and posterior margins of the medial tibial plateau [4,6,7,12].

Botha-Scheepers et al. used a fixed-flexion (FF) protocol and found that 44% of radiographs demonstrated adequate positioning, indicating improvement in knee joint positioning with newer imaging protocols [4]. They also concluded that poor MTP alignment in serial radiographs diminishes the ability to detect meaningful structural progression and may obscure associations with clinical risk factors [4]. Additionally, work using data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) confirmed that knees with an IMD ≤1.7 mm at both time points exhibited significantly greater JSN than poorly positioned pairs (0.15 mm vs. 0.08 mm, p = 0.012) [5].

Numerous radiographic imaging protocols have been developed to improve the reproducibility of knee positioning and minimize variability in JSW measurements. Standardized approaches include the FF view, fluoroscopically guided semi-flexed views, and views based on anatomical landmarks such as the MTP alignment. In the FF protocol, which is commonly used in large cohort studies like the OAI, patients stand with their knee flexed at a certain degree using a positioning frame (e.g., Synaflexer) to optimize consistent tibial rotation and beam orientation [13]. This method is practical, repeatable, and optimal for large epidemiologic and clinical studies, although it is susceptible to knee positioning variation. By contrast, the fluoroscopically guided semi-flexed view allows for real-time adjustment of beam angle and knee positioning to ensure optimum positioning before each radiographic acquisition. While more consistent, the fluoroscopy-guided semi-flexed view is more time-intensive and costly, making it less realistic for large cohort and multi-center studies [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. The MTP protocol focuses specifically on achieving visual alignment of the MTP with the X-ray beam before acquisition, but it too is susceptible to minor variations [17].

Previous research has identified the sensitivity of JSW to tibial plateau alignment, yet no study has systematically quantified this relationship across a large-scale longitudinal cohort. To address this gap, in a primary analysis, this study aimed to determine how variations in knee positioning quantitatively affect both minimum (mJSW) and fixed-location (fJSW) measurements in the OAI cohort in knees from participants who did not progress over time. We also assessed whether demographic factors, such as age, race, gender, or BMI, influence the relationship between positioning and apparent structural change. Further, we sought to test the previous assertion that “… change in JSW related to the poor quality of alignment that is common with the FF view jeopardizes accurate evaluation …” [18]. Therefore, a secondary aim for this study was to evaluate whether poor, but consistent positioning on longitudinal assessments has a detrimental effect on the responsiveness of JSW measurements to elicit whether such participants should be excluded from longitudinal KOA studies using the FF method.

Methods

Participant selection

Our study used images and data from the OAI, a large National Institutes of Health and industry-sponsored multicenter, observational, longitudinal cohort study of OA. The OAI study included nearly 4800 participants aged 45–79 years enrolled from 4 clinical sites: University of Maryland School of Medicine and Johns Hopkins University (Baltimore, MD), Ohio State University (Columbus, OH), University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA), and Memorial Hospital of Rhode Island (Pawtucket, RI). Study oversight was performed by the OAI Coordinating Center at the University of California, San Francisco. Approval was obtained from the institutional review boards at each site.

For the primary analysis investigating the quantitative effect of knee positioning on minimum JSW (mJSW) and fJSW, we selected all OAI participants who had a Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) score of 0 for both the right and left knees at the baseline and 12-month time points, and for whom JSW and knee positioning data were available. We chose participants with consistent bilateral KL of 0 across both time points based on the assumption that observed changes in JSW would be due to inconsistencies in image acquisition alone rather than OA progression. These selection criteria yielded 806 subjects, and we analyzed both knees for each participant. Furthermore, 97.3% of the knees were still KL = 0 at 48 months, offering further evidence that the knees in our sample were not progressing over the observed time frame.

For the secondary analysis looking at the responsiveness of JSW measurements, we selected knees from the OAI with KL scores of 1, 2, or 3 at BL, and JSW measurements available at both BL and 48 months. The 48-month follow-up period was chosen to provide a sufficient window for detectable longitudinal JSW change while maintaining an adequate sample size by minimizing the impact of participant dropout.

Participant demographics were derived from the OAI survey, which asked individuals to answer, “What is your gender, male or female?” Although we acknowledge that gender and biological sex are distinct constructs, we report gender rather than sex differences to maintain consistency with OAI terminology.

Image acquisition and measurements

Weight-bearing, bilateral, standing PA knee radiographs were acquired according to an FF protocol using the Synaflexor positioning frame [13]. Our study used measurements of mJSW, fJSW, and knee positioning (Xrim). The fJSW method uses a Cartesian coordinate system linked to anatomical landmarks [19] and, for our study, fJSW was measured at x = 0.25, where x is a location at approximately 25% of the femur width from the medial side of the femur. This has been shown to be the optimal location to measure fJSW for medial compartment KOA [2]. A positive ΔfJSW or ΔmJSW is defined as an increase in measured JSW from baseline to subsequent radiograph.

Xrim was defined as the distance between the delineated tibial plateau and the furthest projected tibial rim (either posterior or anterior) (Fig. 1). A positive ΔXrim is defined as an increase in the Xrim value from baseline to subsequent radiograph. This differs somewhat from the IMD, although both measurements reflect knee positioning. The imaging measurements were drawn from the OAI database of released JSW readings and supplemented with additional readings by our team (JC and JD). In all cases, the readers were blinded to patient, time point, and all clinical characteristics.

Secondary analysis: effect of xrim on JSW responsiveness

For this knee-level analysis, we identified 70 cases where Xrim > 6.5 mm at both time points (poorly positioned), but the difference in the BL and 48-month Xrim values was less than 2.0 mm (consistently positioned) – i.e., consistent poor positioning. 6.5 m was chosen to agree with the definition of a poorly positioned knee radiograph according to an OAI document which states, “… changes in this measurement of more than ± 2 mm between visits may indicate that knee position has varied significantly between the visits, and a value of this variable larger than 6.5 mm may indicate that a single visit has a poorly positioned knee” [20]. We then searched for a set of controls matched by KL grade, gender, age (Δage <4 years), and BMI (ΔBMI <4 kg/m2), with JSW measurements available at both time points. The consistently positioned controls were also selected to have a difference in the BL and 48-month Xrim values of less than 2.0 mm, but with a value of Xrim < 3.0 mm at both time points (well-positioned). While our definition of consistent positioning is broader than the 2.0 mm threshold utilized in the OAI, we selected this cutoff because at 2.0 mm, the number of matched controls was greatly reduced. Given these constraints, we were able to identify at most 69 matched controls; the one case that was not matched was excluded from the analysis. To account for variations due to random selection, we performed 100 iterations of control selection and report the average and standard deviation of the standardized response means values for all 100 selections. Separate analyses were performed for mJSW and fJSW, and for both genders.

Statistical analysis

We examined the associations between ΔfJSW or ΔmJSW and ΔXrim using GEE models with a Gaussian family to account for the correlation between the two knees within each participant. The models included ΔXrim from baseline to 12 months as the primary predictor and were adjusted for baseline age, gender, race, and BMI. Two-way interaction terms between ΔXrim and each covariate (age, gender, race, and BMI) were added to the main-effects model using forward selection at a significance level of 0.05 to assess potential effect modification. We also conducted a reader reliability (JC and JD) study of a single knee from 50 randomly selected OAI participants at the baseline and 12-month visits and used the root mean square standard deviation (RMSSD) to quantify precision.

Results

For the primary analysis, the sample included 806 participants, was 42.1% male, 14.1% non-white, with a mean age of 59.1 years and a mean BMI of 26.9 kg/m2. GEE analyses of the full sample showed that ΔXrim had a significant positive association with ΔfJSW (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.12–0.17) and ΔmJSW (β = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.08–0.14). These findings indicate that an increase in Xrim from baseline to 12 months is associated with an increase in measured fJSW and mJSW, or pseudowidening of the joint space. Meanwhile, a significant ΔXrim × gender interaction was observed for ΔfJSW (β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.03–0.13), indicating a stronger association in females than in males, whereas no significant gender differences were observed for ΔmJSW. In the GEE model with ΔfJSW as the outcome, the estimated slope for ΔXrim was 0.19 (95% CI, 0.15–0.22) in females and 0.11 (95% CI, 0.07–0.15) in males. No evidence of effect modification by age, race, or BMI was observed for either outcome. Discussion

Our results show that changes in knee positioning affect the measurement of radiographic fJSW and mJSW and that poor but consistent positioning reduces responsiveness for mJSW but not fJSW. We found a systematic positive correlation between ΔXrim and ΔfJSW/ΔmJSW, indicating that if the tibial margins are more misaligned at a subsequent time point, the apparent JSW increases. Future studies should aim to create a correction factor that can adjust for these observed changes.

Interestingly, larger slopes and stronger correlations between ΔXrim and ΔfJSW are observed in females, which may mean that fJSW measurement is more heavily affected by variations in knee positioning for female populations. The data suggest a similar gender difference for mJSW, although this association did not reach statistical significance. Our data also show that the slopes are slightly lower for mJSW than for fJSW. Our results confirm generally what has been reported in the literature, but our study offers substantial additional details.

A slope of nearly 0.2, as seen for female fJSW, suggests that a 1 mm change in Xrim results in a JSW shift of greater than 0.2 mm, which is quite substantial compared to typical annual OA-related loss. To contextualize these findings, the typical annual rate of medial JSW loss in knees with established OA is approximately 0.13 mm [21]. Even in knees demonstrating measurable structural progression, the rate of narrowing is often less than 0.25 mm per year [22]. With that in mind, if a patient experiences both biological JSN and a simultaneous increase in Xrim at a subsequent time point due to positioning error, the measured JSW may appear stable or even improved.

A critical distinction exists between individual measurement precision and population-level validity. Optimal positioning is important for ensuring that an individual's JSW change reflects true KOA progression rather than positioning artifacts, but random misalignment in large cohorts can behave as non-biased noise that averages out around the true mean of progression. However, systematic inconsistencies in imaging between baseline and follow-up radiographs could bias JSW measurements and obscure or exaggerate true disease progression. For instance, a new technologist at the follow-up time point who consistently positions patients such that Xrim is lower may introduce a false JSW progression in the data.

For large-scale cohort assessments, rapid quantitative tools are essential to evaluate mJSW and fJSW. Our approach allows for the systematic isolation of positioning-induced JSW changes from true OA progression. This analysis provides a foundation for developing a correction scheme to deal with poorly aligned images, a process simplified by our finding that these errors are independent of age and BMI. Further research is now required to refine the gender-specific differences and minimize measurement bias in longitudinal studies.

Measuring structural progression over smaller intervals may show smaller disease progression, but more frequent imaging may serve as a quality control mechanism to distinguish positioning-induced outliers from true disease progression. Implementing more frequent imaging enhances the reliability of longitudinal JSW estimates by ensuring that observed changes reflect biological progression rather than technical measurement error.

The secondary analysis yielded some interesting results. First, the knees with poor but consistent positioning were overwhelmingly (72%) male, even though the pool of participants from which we drew for this analysis was only 40% male, implying that there is a gender difference in the prevalence of naturally occurring mis-positioned knees. This could be due to differences in muscle mass, thigh circumference, or general knee size that may result in constraints more common in males when positioning in the Synaflexor device. Our data show that the detrimental effect on mJSW due to poor positioning is mitigated by using fJSW instead, where poor positioning does not appear to degrade responsiveness substantially. These results suggest an advantage of fJSW over mJSW for longitudinal studies of KOA using the FF protocol.

One of the key findings from both the primary and secondary analyses is the gender differences observed in the sensitivity of JSW measurements to variability in radiographic positioning. Specifically, our results demonstrate that for females, fJSW measurements are more susceptible to variations in Xrim, with the effect almost twice that of males (slope of 0.19 for females vs. slope of 0.11 for males). Several anatomical factors may contribute to this disparity. Females typically have smaller tibial plateaus and a steeper posterior tibial slope than men [23,24]. Furthermore, differences in condylar shape could affect females more than males, exacerbating the alignment of the femur and tibia during imaging [25,26]. Trials may require larger sample sizes of females to overcome this noise to avoid underestimating the effect of potential treatments in female populations. If a correction factor is developed, it should be gender-specific to account for this difference.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not have access to radiographs acquired at the same time point after repositioning. Instead, by utilizing patients who had bilateral KL = 0 at both time points, we assumed that our study design would provide an equivalent outcome and that no OA-related change occurred in this group used for the primary analysis. The plots (Fig. 2) showing the linear dependence of Xrim to both mJSW and fJSW had intercepts near zero, indicating minimal OA progression had occurred and that our selection criteria adequately addressed this limitation. A future prospective study could verify our findings by acquiring multiple radiographs of the same patient on the same day with variations in the knee positioning. Selecting subjects with bilateral KL = 0 could introduce a selection bias toward healthier subjects and limit the generalizability of our findings. Similarly, since we did not include actual KOA progressors in the primary analysis due to our study design, it is possible that the relationships we observed between JSW and Xrim could differ in diseased knees. A future study could investigate the relationship between JSW and Xrim in more diseased knees.

Another limitation of our study is that it utilized a FF protocol, so our findings will not apply to other imaging protocols. For logistical reasons, we considered Xrim < 3.0 mm at both time points to be well-positioned in the secondary analysis. This criterion is less strict than recommendations in the OAI documents [27], which defines a difference of >2.0 mm as poor. Our secondary analysis was limited by a small sample size and a significant gender and ethnicity imbalance. The ethnicity imbalance was unavoidable due to the demographics of the OAI database, while the gender imbalance may reflect a biological vulnerability to positioning errors in male knees. Nevertheless, both restrict the robustness of our conclusions across broader populations. Finally, we acknowledge a conceptual distinction between gender and biological sex. We utilized the OAI ‘gender’ variable, but our discussion of femoral and pelvic anatomy pertains specifically to biological sex-based differences.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that radiographic positioning variability has the potential to bias JSW measurements, with female cohorts showing nearly double the sensitivity to these errors, likely due to sexually dimorphic knee anatomy. Additionally, our secondary analysis indicates that fJSW may continue to serve as a longitudinal biomarker, potentially maintaining responsiveness despite suboptimal positioning, provided the positioning remains consistent over time. Consequently, implementing gender-specific correction factors or prioritizing fJSW over mJSW may improve the accuracy of disease-modifying OA drug trials by reducing measurement noise and the risk of misclassification of structural progression.
