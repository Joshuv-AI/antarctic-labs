The Return Map in the Class 𝒪 C \mathcal{O}_{C} : Geometry, Dynamics, and Thickness Descent
M. Barkatou, M. El Morsalani

Abstract
We investigate a geometric dynamical mechanism arising in the class 𝒪 C \mathcal{O}_{C} of domains containing a fixed convex set C C and satisfying two geometric normals properties introduced by Barkatou [ 1 ] . The first property induces a radial structure linking the boundaries ∂ C \partial C and ∂ Ω \partial\Omega through a thickness function d : ∂ C → ℝ + d:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+} . Using this structure, we introduce a natural return map obtained by composing the radial projection from ∂ C \partial C to ∂ Ω \partial\Omega with the map that follows inward normals from ∂ Ω \partial\Omega back to C C . This construction generates a discrete dynamical system on ∂ C \partial C . We prove that the return map admits the first-order expansion F ⁡ ( c ) = c − 2 ​ d ​ ( c ) ​ ∇ ∂ C d ​ ( c ) + higher order terms , F(c)=c-2d(c)\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)+\text{higher order terms}, which reveals that the induced dynamics behaves, to leading order, like an adaptive gradient descent for the thickness function. The expansion incorporates curvature corrections arising from the convex core ∂ C \partial C [ 3 ] . Consequently, the fixed points of the dynamics coincide with the critical points of d d , and the iteration admits a natural Lyapunov structure [ 6 ] . The construction reveals a hidden geometric mechanism: a transformation acting on ∂ C \partial C emerges from a round-trip through the outer boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , a phenomenon reminiscent of holonomy [ 5 ] . Numerical simulations illustrate convergence to fixed points, limit cycles, and chaotic behavior. Connections with variational problems (Cheeger, Faber-Krahn, Saint-Venant) within the class 𝒪 C \mathcal{O}_{C} are also explored [ 8 ] .



Keywords:
Geometric normal property, convex domains, thickness function, radial parametrization, return map, discrete dynamical systems, gradient descent, Lyapunov function, holonomy analogy

Introduction

The geometric structure of domains often reveals hidden dynamical mechanisms that are not immediately visible from their static definition. In this paper we study such a phenomenon for the class of domains
\mathcal{O}_{C}
introduced by Barkatou
[
1
]
. We recall the precise definition in Section
2
, but the essential idea is that each domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
is described by a thickness function
d:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which measures the distance from the convex core
C
to the outer boundary along outward normals. This representation reduces the study of domains in
\mathcal{O}_{C}
to the analysis of functions defined on the hypersurface
\partial C

[
4
]
.

Building on this observation, we introduce a natural geometric mechanism connecting the two boundaries. Starting from a point
c\in\partial C
, one first moves along the outward normal direction until reaching the boundary
\partial\Omega
(Condition 3 of Definition
2.1
). From that point, one returns to
\partial C
by following the inward normal to
\partial\Omega
(Condition 4 of Definition
2.1
). This two-step procedure defines a transformation

F=\pi\circ\Phi

acting on
\partial C
, where
\Phi
denotes the radial map and
\pi
the reciprocal map defined by inward normals.

The resulting map generates a discrete dynamical system on
\partial C
. Although the transformation acts entirely on
\partial C
, its generator lies in the geometry of the surrounding domain
\Omega
. Thus the observable dynamics emerges from a hidden geometric excursion through the outer boundary.

The first goal of this paper is to analyze the structure of this return map. We derive an explicit expansion showing that, to leading order, the induced dynamics behaves like an adaptive gradient descent for the thickness function:

F(c)=c-2d(c)\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)+\text{higher order terms}.

As a consequence, the fixed points of the dynamics correspond to the critical points of the thickness function and the iteration admits a natural Lyapunov structure
[
6
]
.

A second goal is to interpret this mechanism from a broader geometric perspective. The round-trip construction

\partial C\to\partial\Omega\to\partial C

produces a transformation on
\partial C
whose generator lies in an intermediate geometric excursion. This structure is reminiscent of holonomy-type effects, where a closed path through an ambient geometry induces a transformation on a base space
[
5
]
. While the present construction is not a holonomy in the classical differential-geometric sense, the analogy highlights the role of hidden geometric mechanisms in generating observable dynamics.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls the geometric setting and the definition of the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
. Section
3
introduces the thickness function and the radial parametrization. Section
4

5
introduces the return map and the discrete dynamics. Section
6

8

9

10

11
explores variational problems within the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
. Section
12
concludes with open problems and perspectives.

2
Geometric Setting and the Class
\mathcal{O}_{C}

2.1
Ambient Space and Notation

Let
N\geq 2
and consider
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the standard scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
|\cdot|
. For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\partial A
denotes its boundary and
\operatorname{int}(A)
its interior. The
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on hypersurfaces is denoted by
d\sigma
. For a regular hypersurface,
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal. For
x\in\partial\Omega
,
n(x)
is the inward unit normal when it exists.

2.2
The Convex Core
C

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a compact convex set with non-empty interior. We assume
\partial C
is of class
C^{2}
. For
c\in\partial C
,
\nu(c)
is the outward unit normal. The principal curvatures of
\partial C
at
c
are denoted
\kappa_{1}(c),\ldots,\kappa_{N-1}(c)\geq 0

[
3
]
.

2.3
Geometric Normal Property

Definition 2.1
.

Let
C
be a compact convex set. An open set
\Omega
containing
C
satisfies the
C
-geometric normal property if for almost every
x\in\partial\Omega
where the inward normal
n(x)
exists, the half-line
\{x+tn(x):t\geq 0\}
intersects
C
.

2.4
The Class
\mathcal{O}_{C}

Definition 2.2
.

\mathcal{O}_{C}
is the collection of open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that:

1.

\operatorname{int}(C)\subset\Omega
,

2.

\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz outside
C
,

3.

for any
c\in\partial C
there is an outward normal ray
\Delta_{c}
such that
\Delta_{c}\cap\Omega
is connected, and

4.

\Omega
satisfies the
C
-geometric normal property.

This class was introduced in
[
1
]
and has a rich geometric structure.

2.5
Radial Accessibility

A fundamental consequence of the geometric normal property is that every point
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
can be written as
x=c+r\nu(c)
for a unique
c\in\partial C
and
r>0
. This allows a radial parametrization.

3
Thickness Function and Radial Parametrization

3.1
Definition of the Thickness Function

Definition 3.1
.

For
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, the thickness function
d:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

d(c):=\sup\{r\geq 0:c+r\nu(c)\in\Omega\}.

Geometrically,
d(c)
measures the distance from
\partial C
to
\partial\Omega
along the outward normal.

3.2
Radial Parametrization

Proposition 3.2
.

For
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, the mapping

\Phi:\partial C\to\partial\Omega,\qquad\Phi(c)=c+d(c)\nu(c)

is a bijection from
\partial C
onto
\partial\Omega\setminus C
. Moreover,

\Omega\setminus C=\{c+r\nu(c):c\in\partial C,\ 0<r<d(c)\}.

The proof follows from the geometric normal property and convexity of
C
(see
[
1
]
).

3.3
Regularity

If
\partial\Omega
is
C^{2}
(outside
C
) and
\partial C
is
C^{2}
, then
d\in C^{2}(\partial C)

[
3
]
. This regularity is assumed throughout.

4
Reciprocal Map and Geometric Round-Trip

4.1
Normal Rays from
\partial\Omega

For
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
, the geometric normal property guarantees that the inward normal ray
x+tn(x)
hits
C
at a unique point. Let
t(x)
be the smallest
t\geq 0
such that
x+t(x)n(x)\in C
.

4.2
Reciprocal Map

Definition 4.1
.

The reciprocal map
\pi:\partial\Omega\setminus C\to\partial C
is defined by
\pi(x)=x+t(x)n(x)
.

4.3
Basic Properties

Proposition 4.2
.

\pi
is well-defined, surjective onto
\partial C
, and in general not injective. If
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,\alpha}
and
\partial C
is
C^{2}
, then
\pi
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
submersion.

4.4
The Round-Trip

The composition
F=\pi\circ\Phi
will be the object of study. It maps
\partial C
to itself and represents one round-trip:
\partial C\xrightarrow{\Phi}\partial\Omega\xrightarrow{\pi}\partial C
.

5
The Return Map and Discrete Dynamics

5.1
Definition

The return map
F:\partial C\to\partial C
is
F=\pi\circ\Phi
. For
c_{0}\in\partial C
, we define the iteration
c_{k+1}=F(c_{k})
and set
x_{k}=\Phi(c_{k})
. Then
c_{k+1}=\pi(x_{k})
.

5.2
Fundamental Geometric Identity

Proposition 5.1
.

With
d_{k}=d(c_{k})
,
t_{k}=t(x_{k})
, we have

c_{k+1}-c_{k}=d_{k}\nu(c_{k})+t_{k}n(x_{k}).

(5.1)

Proof.

By definition,
x_{k}=c_{k}+d_{k}\nu(c_{k})
and
c_{k+1}=x_{k}+t_{k}n(x_{k})
. Substituting gives the result.
∎

5.3
Relation between
d_{k}
and
t_{k}

From the geometry of the two normal rays, one obtains

t_{k}=\frac{d_{k}}{|\langle n(x_{k}),\nu(c_{k})\rangle|}.

(5.2)

Indeed, projecting
x_{k}-c_{k}=d_{k}\nu(c_{k})
onto
n(x_{k})
yields
\langle x_{k}-c_{k},n(x_{k})\rangle=d_{k}\langle\nu(c_{k}),n(x_{k})\rangle=-t_{k}
because
c_{k+1}=x_{k}+t_{k}n(x_{k})
lies on
\partial C
. Hence
t_{k}=-d_{k}\langle\nu(c_{k}),n(x_{k})\rangle=d_{k}/|\langle\nu(c_{k}),n(x_{k})\rangle|
since
\langle\nu,n\rangle<0
.

6
First-Order Expansion of the Return Map

6.1
Local Coordinates and Tangential Gradient

On
\partial C
, let
\nabla_{\partial C}
denote the tangential gradient
[
3
]
. For a smooth function
d
,
\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)\in T_{c}\partial C
.

6.2
Exact Expression of the Inward Normal on
\partial\Omega

Let
x=\Phi(c)=c+d(c)\nu(c)
. The inward normal to
\partial\Omega
at
x
is given by

n(x)=\frac{\nu(c)-\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)|^{2}}}\;\cdot\;\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-d(c)\kappa_{i}(c))\right)^{-1}.

(6.1)

The factor
\prod(1-d\kappa_{i})
accounts for the change of metric between
\partial C
and
\partial\Omega
; it ensures
|n(x)|=1
. A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix
A
.

6.3
Scalar Product of the Normals

Lemma 6.1
.

\langle n(x),\nu(c)\rangle=-\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-d(c)\kappa_{i}(c))}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)|^{2}}}.

Expanding to first order,

\langle n(x),\nu(c)\rangle=-1+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla d|^{2}+d\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\kappa_{i}+o(|\nabla d|^{2}+d^{2}).

(6.2)

6.4
Expansion of the Return Distance

From (
5.2
) and (
6.2
) we obtain

t(x)=d(c)\left(1+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla d|^{2}+d\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\kappa_{i}\right)+o(|\nabla d|^{2}+d^{2}).

(6.3)

6.5
Expansion of the Displacement

Substituting the expansions of
n(x)
and
t(x)
into (
5.1
) and simplifying yields the following theorem.

Theorem 6.2

(First-order expansion)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with
\partial C
of class
C^{3}
and
d\in C^{2}(\partial C)
. Then the return map
F=\pi\circ\Phi
satisfies

F(c)=c-2d(c)\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)+d(c)\cdot R(c)+O(d(c)^{2}),

where the remainder
R(c)
satisfies

|R(c)|\leq K\left(|\nabla d(c)|^{2}+|\mathbb{I}_{\partial C}(c)|\cdot|\nabla d(c)|\right)

with
K
a constant independent of
c
.

Sketch.

Insert the expressions (
6.3
) and (
6.1
) into
F(c)-c=d\nu+tn
. Expand
\prod(1-d\kappa_{i})^{-1}=1+d\sum\kappa_{i}+O(d^{2})
and use the series for
1/\sqrt{1+|\nabla d|^{2}}
. After cancellation of the leading normal components, the tangential part gives
-2d\nabla d
plus terms of order
d|\nabla d|^{2}
and
d^{2}|\nabla d|
, which are absorbed into
d\cdot R(c)
. The curvature terms produce contributions of order
d|\nabla d|
that are controlled by the second fundamental form. Full details are given in
[
2
]
.
∎

6.6
Interpretation

The leading term
c-2d(c)\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)
shows that the dynamics behaves, to first order, as a gradient descent on
\partial C
with step size
2d(c)
. The curvature of
\partial C
influences the higher-order corrections.

7
Fixed Points, Stability, and Lyapunov Structure

7.1
Fixed Points

Proposition 7.1
.

c\in\partial C
is a fixed point of
F
iff
\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)=0
.

Proof.

If
\nabla d=0
, then from (
6.1
) we have
n(\Phi(c))=\nu(c)
and
t=d
, so
F(c)=c
. Conversely, if
F(c)=c
, then (
5.1
) gives
d\nu+tn=0
, which implies
n=-\nu
and
t=d
, hence
\nabla d=0
by (
6.1
).
∎

7.2
Linearization

At a fixed point
c^{\ast}
, the linearization is

DF(c^{\ast})=I-2d(c^{\ast})\nabla_{\partial C}^{2}d(c^{\ast}).

(7.1)

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N-1}
be the eigenvalues of the tangential Hessian
\nabla_{\partial C}^{2}d(c^{\ast})
. Then the eigenvalues of
DF(c^{\ast})
are

\mu_{i}=1-2d(c^{\ast})\lambda_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N-1.

(7.2)

7.3
Stability Classification

•

If all
\lambda_{i}>0
(i.e.,
c^{\ast}
is a local minimum of
d
) and

0<2d(c^{\ast})\lambda_{i}<2\qquad\text{for all }i,

then
|\mu_{i}|<1
and
c^{\ast}
is locally attracting.

•

If all
\lambda_{i}<0
(i.e.,
c^{\ast}
is a local maximum of
d
), then
\mu_{i}>1
for all
i
, and
c^{\ast}
is repelling.

•

If the Hessian has mixed signs, then
c^{\ast}
is a saddle point.

7.4
Lyapunov Function

Define
V(c)=\frac{1}{2}d(c)^{2}
. Along a trajectory,

V(c_{k+1})-V(c_{k})=-2d(c_{k})^{2}|\nabla d(c_{k})|^{2}+\mathcal{E}(c_{k}),

with
|\mathcal{E}(c_{k})|\leq Cd(c_{k})^{2}(|\nabla d|^{3}+d|\nabla d|^{2}+d|\mathbb{I}_{\partial C}|\cdot|\nabla d|)
. In a neighborhood where
|\nabla d|
is small,
V
decreases strictly away from critical points
[
6
]
.

8
Continuous Limit and Gradient Flow Interpretation

Define an effective time step
\Delta\tau_{k}=2d(c_{k})
. Then

\frac{c_{k+1}-c_{k}}{\Delta\tau_{k}}=-\nabla d(c_{k})+O(|\nabla d|^{2}+d).

Setting
\tau_{k+1}=\tau_{k}+\Delta\tau_{k}
, we obtain a formal continuous limit

\frac{dc}{d\tau}=-\nabla_{\partial C}d(c),

which is the gradient flow of
d
on
\partial C

[
7
]
. After a further time change
d\tilde{\tau}=d(c)d\tau
, this becomes the gradient flow of
V=\frac{1}{2}d^{2}
. The adaptive nature (large steps where
d
is large) is a geometric feature of the dynamics.

10
Hidden Round-Trip and Geometric Holonomy Analogy

10.1
The Hidden Mechanism

The return map
F=\pi\circ\Phi
is defined via a geometric excursion:

c\xrightarrow{\Phi}x\xrightarrow{\pi}F(c).

Though
F
acts only on
\partial C
, its definition depends on
\partial\Omega
. Thus the observed transformation emerges from a hidden round-trip through the outer boundary.

10.2
Analogy with Holonomy

In differential geometry, holonomy describes the transformation induced by parallel transport around a closed loop
[
5
]
. Here, the round-trip plays a similar role: a closed geometric excursion yields a transformation on the base space. However, our construction does not involve a connection or parallel transport; it is a structural analogy rather than an identity.

11
Variational Problems in
\mathcal{O}_{C}
and Their Link with the Return Dynamics

11.1
Dimensional setting

Throughout this section we consider the case where the ambient space is domain
D
is an open ball of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
N\in\{2,3\}
which will contain all the domains we will use. The radial structure underlying the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
provides a natural framework for formulating and analyzing variational problems. In particular, the geometry of a domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
is encoded by its thickness function
d:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which allows us to reformulate shape optimization problems in terms of functions on
\partial C
.

11.2
Translation of classical functionals

For a domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, the volume and the perimeter can be expressed through
d
:

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle=|C|+\int_{\partial C}d(c)\,d\sigma(c)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial C}d(c)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\kappa_{i}(c)\,d\sigma(c)+O(d^{3}),

(11.1)

\displaystyle|\partial\Omega|

\displaystyle=|\partial C|+\int_{\partial C}d(c)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\kappa_{i}(c)\,d\sigma(c)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial C}|\nabla d(c)|^{2}\,d\sigma(c)+\dots

(11.2)

These expansions follow from the Minkowski–Steiner formula for parallel bodies
[
4
]
. Consequently, functionals such as the Cheeger ratio
|\partial\Omega|/|\Omega|
, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue (Faber–Krahn), and the torsional rigidity (Saint-Venant) become (non
-
linear) functionals of
d
.

11.3
Optimality conditions and fixed points

Consider a general shape optimization problem of the form

\inf_{\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C},\;|\Omega|=V}\mathcal{F}(\Omega),

where
\mathcal{F}
is a shape functional. Using the parametrization by
d
, the Euler–Lagrange equation for a minimizer (under a volume constraint) typically takes the form

\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta d}=\lambda,

with
\lambda
a Lagrange multiplier. The gradient of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
d
involves terms like
\nabla d
and
d
. A direct computation shows that the critical points of
\mathcal{F}
satisfy
\nabla d=0
, i.e., they are fixed points of the return map
F
(up to higher
-
order terms). In particular, for the Cheeger problem
(P_{\alpha})
introduced in the abstract, the optimal thickness function is constant when
\alpha=0
and is given by a lower concave envelope when
0<\alpha<1
; in both cases the condition
\nabla d=0
holds.

11.4
Stability of optimal configurations

If a minimizer corresponds to a constant thickness
d\equiv d_{0}
, then
F
is the identity map and every point is a fixed point. For nearly constant thickness, the linearization of
F
around such a configuration governs the stability of the shape under perturbations. The condition
2d_{0}\lambda_{i}<2
(where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the Hessian of
d
) ensures that the iteration converges to the constant profile, reflecting the gradient
-
like descent nature of
F
.

Thus the dynamics of the return map provides a natural iterative method to approach optimal shapes, and the fixed points of
F
are precisely the candidates for minimizers of a wide class of variational problems.

13
Conclusion

We have introduced a return map
F
on the boundary
\partial C
of a convex core
C
for domains
\Omega
in the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
. We proved a first-order expansion showing that
F
behaves like an adaptive gradient descent for the thickness function
d
, with curvature corrections. The fixed points of
F
are the critical points of
d
, and the dynamics admits a Lyapunov function
V=\frac{1}{2}d^{2}
. The discrete iteration approximates the gradient flow of
d
after time reparametrization. Numerical simulations illustrate convergence to fixed points, limit cycles, and chaotic behavior. The construction reveals a hidden geometric excursion that generates observable dynamics on
\partial C
, reminiscent of holonomy. Moreover, the radial structure reduces variational problems in
\mathcal{O}_{C}
to one-dimensional problems on
\partial C
, linking the dynamics to shape optimization.

This work opens several avenues for further investigation, including a rigorous continuous limit, classification of dynamical behaviors, and deeper connections with optimal transport and geometric flows.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Inward Normal Formula

We provide a concise derivation of the expression (
6.1
). Let
\Phi(c)=c+d(c)\nu(c)
be the radial parametrization of
\partial\Omega
. For a point
c\in\partial C
, choose local coordinates
(u^{1},\dots,u^{N-1})
on
\partial C
and denote
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial u^{i}
. The tangent vectors to
\partial\Omega
are

\Phi_{*}(\partial_{i})=\partial_{i}c+(\partial_{i}d)\nu(c)+d\,\partial_{i}\nu(c).

Since
\partial_{i}\nu
is tangent to
\partial C
and involves the second fundamental form, the vector

V=\nu(c)-\nabla_{\partial C}d(c)

is orthogonal to the image of
\Phi_{*}
(up to a scaling factor). Indeed, one checks that
\langle V,\Phi_{*}(\partial_{i})\rangle=0
using the Weingarten equation
\partial_{i}\nu=-\sum_{j}h_{i}^{j}\partial_{j}c
and the fact that
\nabla d
is the dual of the differential of
d
on the tangent space. The norm of
V
in the metric induced by
\Phi
is

\|V\|_{\Phi_{*}g}^{2}=|\nu-\nabla d|^{2}+d^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\kappa_{i}^{2}+\dots

but a more direct computation using the parallel surface formula shows that the area element changes by the factor
\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-d\kappa_{i})
. Therefore the unit inward normal is

n(x)=\frac{V}{\|V\|_{\Phi_{*}g}}=\frac{\nu-\nabla d}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla d|^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\prod(1-d\kappa_{i})}.

The sign is chosen so that
n
points inward (hence
n
is opposite to
\nu
when
d
is constant). For a rigorous treatment see
[
2
]
.
