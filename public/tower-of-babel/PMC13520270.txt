Plasma TIM-1 concentration: correlation with cytokines in pulmonary tuberculosis patients and development of a predictive model
Authors: Wolazihan Madeniyati, Chunyu Wang, Reyila Tuerhong, Dilixiati Tuerdimaimaiti, Gulisitan Aili, Ajiguli Kulaixi, Remilanmu Nizhamuding, Baihetinisha Tuerdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the dynamic expression of T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain molecule-1 (TIM-1) in pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients at various stages, and examine possible relationships with IFN-γ and IL-17, to elucidate the potential roles in PTB pathogenesis. In addition, we aimed to assess the feasibility of using TIM-1 combined with IFN-γ and IL-17 as a biomarker for PTB diagnosis.MethodsPlasma samples were collected from 60 PTB patients, 50 latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) patients, and 50 healthy controls at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between February and November 2023. The concentrations of TIM- 1, IFN-γ, and IL-17 in plasma were quantified using ELISA. Correlation and Logistic regression analyses were conducted, followed by the construction of a predictive model.ResultsSignificantly higher plasma concentrations of TIM-1 and IL-17 and reduced levels of IFN-γ were observed in PTB patients compared to LTBI subjects and healthy controls. A positive correlation was found between TIM-1 and IL-17 levels in PTB patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that TIM-1 exhibited moderate diagnostic performance, with a cutoff value of 22.3 pg·ml− 1, whereas the reduction in IFN-γ level was not statistically significant. The constructed predictive model had a C-index of 78.8% and an area under the ROC curve of 0.79.ConclusionsPlasma TIM-1 concentration was significantly elevated in PTB patients and non-significantly raised in LTBI suggesting possible roles in promoting inflammation and modulating Th17 responses, potentially influencing mycobacterial clearance and perhaps disease progression. This highlights the potential importance of TIM-1 in tuberculous inflammatory processes, immune regulation, and its utility in early diagnostics, suggesting it may be a novel therapeutic target in PTB. These findings are preliminary and require further validation in larger, multicenter studies.

Introduction

PTB is caused by inhaled Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacilli (Mtb) invading the lungs and it remains a serious public health problem [1]. According to global statistics in 2022, the number of newly diagnosed tuberculosis patients exceeded 10.6 million, with 1.3 million deaths, emphasizing the importance of eradicating tuberculosis worldwide [2]. The number of new cases in China was 748,000, ranking second only to India; emphasizing the enormous pressure on TB prevention and control. Furthermore, about 350 million people in China are infected with Mtb. Latent infection is potentially serious; 5%-10% may develop active tuberculosis at some time in their lives [3]. Active tuberculosis is the most important form of tuberculosis, however, current diagnostic and treatment methods are relatively unsophisticated and inadequate, leading to problems with high rates of drug resistance, persistent morbidity and mortality, especially among people with reduced immune function [4]. The increase in multidrug-resistant (MDR)-TB and extensively drug-resistant (XDR-TB), emphasizes the reduced efficacy of traditional first-line drugs, increasing the difficulty of treatment and the urgent need to develop new vaccines and drugs to treat drug-resistant Mtb [5].

Recent studies have emphasized the importance of the Th1/Th2 lymphocyte balance in effectively combating Mtb infection. When Th1 reaction is inhibited and Th2 reaction is too strong, the body’s ability to remove tuberculosis is reduced, and the risk of infection and disease deterioration is increased [6]. TIM-1, is a transmembrane protein which is a key immunoregulatory molecule, playing a role in Th cell differentiation and regulation of immune response. It was discovered on the surface of African green monkey kidney cells and confirmed as human hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 1 (HAVCR-1) or kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) on human kidney cells [7]. TIM-1 is specifically expressed in mature Th2 cells, and stimulates the secretion of Th2 cytokines and promotes the humoral immune response [8, 9]. TIM-1 plays a key role in many RNA virus infections, affecting immune response and cell survival by regulating phagocytosis and T cell activation [10, 11]. A stimulatory TIM-1 monoclonal antibody was shown to enhance the T cell immune response, while a blocking antibody inhibited the immune response by B cell regulation [12]. TIM-1 polymorphism is closely related to some immune-related diseases, and its expression in peripheral blood is related to the prognosis of acute respiratory distress syndrome and immune response in tumors [13, 14]. In relation to tuberculosis [15] TIM-1 promotes Th2 cell proliferation and inhibits Th1 immune responses, especially IFN-γ production, by enhancing production of the Th2-related cytokine, IL-10, favouring Mtb infection [16, 17]. In patients with active PTB, TIM-1 and TIM-3 mRNA expression are markedly increased, particularly in severe, fatal disease [18], accompanied by CD4+ (helper)/CD8+ (cytotoxic) T cell imbalance. This supports an important role of TIM-1 in driving T cell depletion and affecting the overall immune status of patients with PTB [19].

Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine, mainly derived from CD4+T cells, which plays a key role in tuberculosis resistance. IFN-γ binds to surface receptors stimulating macrophages to synthesize and release antibacterial substances such as nitric oxide (NO) and hydrogen peroxide. It also up-regulates the expression of antibacterial proteins, thus enhancing the ability of macrophages to kill and engulf Mtb [20]. As the ‘signature’ cytokine for Th1 immune response, IFN-γ promotes the differentiation and proliferation of Th1 cells, and cooperates with cytotoxic lymphocytes and natural killer cells to inhibit the diffusion of Mtb. Previous studies have shown that the ratio of IFN-γ and CD4+/CD8+ may increase in patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis, reflecting the body’s ability to try to control infection [21]. All kinds of immune responses, such as the production of IFN-γ, are essentially stimulated by pathogens, so theoretically, the stronger the infection activity, the higher the corresponding immune response intensity [22]. The IFN-γ release assay (IGRA) is a blood test used clinically, as an alternative to the tuberculin skin tests (TST, PPD or Mantoux) to demonstrate previous contact with Mtb and latent tuberculosis.

Th17 cells and their cytokines such as IL-17 play a key role in the immune protection and pathologic processes of tuberculosis. IL-17enhances the adaptive immune response on exposure to Mtb and other pathogens [23]. IL-17 A gene knockout mice failed to develop mature granulomas and impaired protection against virulent Mtb [24]. Plasma IL-17 levels were significantly increased in patients with active PTB, and decreased substantially with treatment and sputum conversion [25]. IL-17 is involved in the activation of immune response, the recruitment of neutrophils and early granuloma formation, but excessive activation can lead to excessive inflammatory reaction and tissue damage [26]. IL-17 has complex interactions with other cytokines such as IFN-γ, in Mtb infection: IFN-γ may reduce IL-17 production by inhibiting Th17 differentiation [27], while IL-17 may inhibit the production of IFN-γ, via inflammatory mediator release and altered transcription factor activity [28].

The aim of this study was to study the expression characteristics of TIM-1 in PTB patients, and to explore the correlation between TIM-1 and key cytokines IFN-γ and IL-17 for the first time, so as to reveal the specific mechanism of TIM-1 in regulating the dynamic balance of Th cell subsets, affecting immune effects and promoting or inhibiting the progress of tuberculosis, and provide a certain research basis for immune targeted therapy.

Materials and methods

Research object

From February 2023 to November 2023, 60 PTB patients, 50 LTBI patients and 50 healthy controls (HC) were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. All cases diagnosed as PTB were confirmed strictly in accordance with 288–2017 Diagnostic Guidelines for Tuberculosis [12], with bacteriological and/or pathological evidence of disease. Diagnostic criteria for LTBI included: Mtb-infected people, without immune deficiency, who were positive with a PPD ≥5 mm reaction or a positive γ -interferon release test (TSPOT.TB), without any related symptoms or signs, with no positive imaging. PPD and TSPOT.TB results were negative in the HC group. All the participants, or legal guardians, gave written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University.

The HC group (n = 50) were recruited from healthy individuals at the physical examination center of the same hospital during the same period, during the same period; all had negative chest radiologic signs and negative IGRA tests results. Strict matching for age, sex, and clinical characteristics, ensured comparability with the PTB and LTBI groups, reducing confounding interference with the study results.

Specimen collection

After clinical diagnosis (before treatment), the PTB and LTBI patients had 5-10 ml of fasting, peripheral venous blood taken into EDTA, with centrifugation, within 30 min, at 4℃ and 3000 rpm for 10 min, careful collection of the supernatant into a 2 ml Eppendorf (EP) tube, with annotation and storage at -80℃ for later use. Repeated freezing and thawing of the samples was prohibited. Samples with coagulation, hemolysis or hyperlipidemia were excluded. Fasting peripheral venous blood from HC was obtained during the study period, and centrifuged and frozen in the same way.

Instruments, equipment and testing steps

Human TIM-1/KIM-1 ELISA kits, with article number ELH-TIM-1 and batch number 0915230050, were purchased from Ray Biotechnology Company, USA. The Optical density (OD) values of TIM-1, IFN-γ and IL-17 were measured by ELISA, and their concentrations were obtained from a standard curve. Experimental work was conducted under the guidance of professional technicians from the State Key Laboratory of Causes and Prevention of High Incidence in Central Asia, jointly established by Xinjiang Medical University, in strict accordance with the kit instructions.

Statistical methods

SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analysis of data. Measurements with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean and standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\chi}\pm{s}$$\end{document}), with Students t test used for comparison between two groups, and single factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) used between multiple groups. Non-normally distributed data were described by the median and interquartile range, with the Mann-Whitney test used between for comparison of two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test used to compare multiple groups. Descriptive data were summarized. Possible correlations between indicators were explored using Spearman correlation analysis. Logistic regression analysis was carried out for statistically significant indicators and odds ratios (OR) were calculated. R language software was used to establish Receiver Operator Curves (ROC) for a prediction of diagnosis model (P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant). In addition, the model was illustrated by constructing a Nomogram.

Discussion

This study found that the plasma concentration of TIM-1 in PTB patients was significantly increased compared to the HC group, but though raised compared to the latent tuberculosis group, the trend did not achieve statistical significance. This may indicate that TIM-1 alters in different stages of tuberculosis.

This study found that serum IFN-γ concentrations in patients with active PTB were significantly lower than those in healthy controls. Possible reasons include the following: First, chronic MTB infection may lead to T cell exhaustion. Persistent antigen stimulation can impair CD4 + T cell function, manifested as reduced secretion of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, a phenomenon previously reported in active tuberculosis [29]. Second, the body may activate immunoregulatory mechanisms to prevent excessive inflammatory damage, such as upregulating regulatory T cells or secreting inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), thereby systemically suppressing Th1-type immune responses and leading to reduced peripheral blood IFN-γ levels. Additionally, IFN-γ production may be regulated by the dynamic equilibrium of other Th cell subsets (e.g., Th2 or Th17). More importantly, peripheral blood cytokine levels do not fully reflect the immune status at the local lesion site—IFN-γ may be highly enriched and function at the site of pulmonary infection, only to be diluted or cleared in peripheral blood, resulting in detected suboptimal concentrations. Therefore, although IFN-γ is a core factor in anti-tuberculosis immunity, its low levels in peripheral blood may reflect systemic immunosuppression or T cell functional exhaustion rather than an absence of immune response. However, this study revealed no significant difference in IFN-γ levels compared to the LTBI group, consistent with previous studies, reporting that IFN-γ level or frequency of specific IFN-γ producing cells could not distinguish LTBI from active tuberculosis [22]. It remains possible that chronic infection and persistent antigen stimulation might have triggered regulatory feedback exhaustion of T cell function, and IFN-γ production. We found no obvious correlation between plasma TIM-1 and IFN-γ, suggesting that TIM-1 may play a role, unrelated to IFN-γ, in tuberculous immunopathologic processes.

In this study we found a negative correlation between plasma IL-17 and IFN-γ, suggesting a dynamic balance of the two T cell subsets. TIM-1 and IL-17 concentrations were both significantly elevated in active PTB, were significantly positively correlated, and were associated with reduced plasma IFN-γ. It follows that TIM-1 may enhance IL-17 expression and amplify inflammation in PTB. Additionally, TIM-1 alters Th17/Treg balance by inhibiting the generation and function of Treg cells, stimulating Th17 cell response and further promoting inflammation [28]. These results support a significant role for TIM-1 in regulation of the immune response and PTB progression, emphasizing the importance of exploring the mechanism of TIM-1 in the development of new therapeutic strategies for Mtb infection.

It is unclear whether the up-regulated TIM-1 expression in active PTB directly leads to altered immune response, through imbalance of Th1/Th2 or Th17/Treg cells. The effects mediated by key cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-17, require further experimental verification. Additional research is needed to investigate serial changes in TIM-1 at different stages and in different clinical phenotypes of PTB to reveal the precise roles of TIM-1 in order to provide a firm theoretical basis for developing new therapeutic strategies involving TIM-1 signaling pathway or binding.

We showed using logistic regression that TIM-1 and IL-17 were independent indicators predicting the diagnosis of PTB, suggesting their potential as possible biomarkers for early identification and disease assessment. We successfully constructed a nomogram prediction model based on plasma TIM-1, IFN-γ, and IL-17 levels. This model demonstrated moderate predictive performance with an AUC value of 0.79. These findings are preliminary and require further validation before clinical application. The ROC curve description now emphasizes the AUC of 0.79 as indicating moderate rather than high accuracy. RCS analysis confirmed that the relationship between each biomarker and disease risk conformed to the linear assumption, supporting the inclusion of linear terms in the model. This outcome not only enhances the model’s statistical validity and robustness but also ensures clinical interpretability and simplicity. Formal study is needed to establish if this is helpful clinically, for early screening of PTB and individualized risk assessment, to guide treatment strategy. Further research is required to explore the interaction of TIM-1, IFN-γ and IL-17 in PTB with the aim of improving clinical management.

This study provides initial evidence; however, due to the limited sample size and cross-sectional design, the results should be interpreted as preliminary, and further research is needed to confirm these observations. Although this study revealed changes in TIM-1, IL-17, and IFN-γ concentrations in the plasma of PTB and LTBI patients, several important limitations remain. First, the limited sample size may affect the stability and statistical power of the biomarker diagnostic model, restricting the generalizability of the study conclusions. Second, the cross-sectional study design provides only static data at a single time point, failing to track the dynamic trajectories of these markers during anti-tuberculosis treatment. This makes it difficult to elucidate their causal relationship with disease progression and outcomes. Third, the predictive model lacks external validation, and its generalizability requires confirmation in future multicenter, larger-scale independent cohort studies. Future research urgently requires multicenter, large-sample longitudinal studies incorporating samples from diverse regions and populations. Systematic sampling at pre-treatment, key treatment milestones, and follow-up periods should be conducted to map the dynamic profiles of biomarkers like TIM-1. This holds significant value for elucidating their mechanisms in tuberculosis immunopathology, evaluating treatment efficacy, and determining prognosis.
