Excited Pfaffians: Generalized Neural Wave Functions Across Structure and State
Nicholas Gao, Till Grutschus, Frank Noé, Stephan Günnemann

Abstract
Neural-network wave functions in Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) have achieved great success in accurately representing both ground and excited states. However, achieving sufficient numerical accuracy in state overlaps requires increasing the number of Monte Carlo samples, and consequently the computational cost, with the number of states. We present a nearly constant sample-size approach, Multi-State Importance Sampling (MSIS), that leverages samples from all states to estimate pairwise overlap. To efficiently evaluate all states for all samples, we introduce Excited Pfaffians . Inspired by Hartree-Fock, this architecture represents many states within a single neural network. Excited Pfaffians also serve as generalized wave functions, allowing a single model to represent multi-state potential energy surfaces. On the carbon dimer, we match the 𝒪 ⁡ ( N s 4 ) \mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{4}) -scaling natural excited states while training > 200 × >200\times faster and modeling 50% more states. Our favorable scaling enables us to be the first to use neural networks to find all distinct energy levels of the beryllium atom. Finally, we demonstrate that a single wave function can represent excited states across various molecules.

Introduction

Figure 1
:
Computational scaling with the number of states. While previous works scale superlinearly, our method exhibits near-constant scaling
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{0.14})
.

Recently, the
in-silico
design of materials and molecules has attracted significant interest for accelerating discovery
(
62
)
.
Central to assessing a molecule’s properties is the electronic Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}{\Psi}_{s}=E_{s}{\Psi}_{s}

(1)

where
{\hat{H}}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\to\mathcal{L}^{2}
is the Hamiltonian operator encoding the system’s total energy and
{\Psi}_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}
is the wave function describing the behavior of the system’s
{N_{\mathrm{e}}}
electrons
(
51
)
.
The eigenvalues
E_{0}\leq E_{1}\leq\ldots
are the system’s energy levels.
While the ground state
{\Psi}_{0}
, associated with
E_{0}
, represents the most stable electronic configuration, excited states
{\Psi}_{1},{\Psi}_{2},\ldots
govern photochemical processes, spectroscopic properties, and reaction pathways.
Accurate computation of excited states is essential for applications ranging from drug design to photovoltaics.
Unfortunately, analytical solutions to the Schrödinger equation exist only for the simplest systems.

Neural-network wave functions within the variational Monte Carlo (VMC) framework have achieved unprecedented accuracy for both ground and excited states
(
24
;
42
;
23
;
43
)
.
However, a single calculation may take days on contemporary hardware, and the standard approach of performing independent calculations for each system and state multiplies this cost drastically.
For ground states, recent work has amortized training costs across molecular structures by learning generalized wave functions
(
49
;
17
;
13
)
.
No such scheme exists for excited states, where current methods scale superlinear to quartic with the number of states.
This unfavorable scaling has two roots: (1) estimating pairwise overlaps
\langle{\Psi}_{s}|{\Psi}_{t}\rangle
requires more samples with more states, and (2) representing many states demands either separate wave functions or prohibitively large networks.

In this work, we address both limitations.
To tackle the sampling problem, we introduce
Multi-State Importance Sampling
(MSIS), which pools samples from all states to estimate pairwise overlaps, reducing the average variance of the estimator from
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}/{N_{\mathrm{b}}})
to
\mathcal{O}(1/{N_{\mathrm{b}}})
for a fixed batch size of
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
.
To tackle the representation problem, we introduce
Excited Pfaffians
, a neural wave-function architecture inspired by Hartree-Fock theory that shares most parameters across states, differing only in a lightweight state-specific selector.
Together, these contributions enable near-constant computational scaling with the number of states (
fig.

1
).
Moreover, Excited Pfaffians integrate seamlessly into the generalized wave function framework
(
15
)
, yielding the first method capable of jointly generalizing across molecular structures, chemical compositions, and electronic states within a single neural network.

In our experiments, we find MSIS critical for stable convergence.
Without it, overlap estimates exhibit high variance, causing erratic energy optimization.
This enables us to match the accuracy of NES
(
42
)
with 40
\times
fewer samples and
200\times
less compute while modeling 12 instead of 8 states on the carbon dimer potential energy surface (PES).
Our favorable scaling enables us to be the first to compute all excited states of the beryllium atom with neural networks.
For the first time, we demonstrate that a single model may approximate excited states across molecular compounds.
Lastly, we find that Excited Pfaffian improves ground-state performance at state crossings compared to ground-state-only methods.

Background

We seek the
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
lowest-energy solutions of the electronic Schrödinger equation,
eq.

1
, with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i,m}\frac{{Z}_{m}}{|\bm{r}_{i}-{\bm{R}}_{m}|}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|},

(2)

comprising kinetic energy, electron-nuclear attraction, and electron-electron repulsion for
{N_{\mathrm{e}}}={N_{\uparrow}}+{N_{\downarrow}}
electrons (spin-up and spin-down) and nuclei at positions
{\bm{R}}_{m}
with charges
{Z}_{m}
.
As electrons are fermions, the wave function must be antisymmetric under permutation of same-spin electrons.
Most methods exploit the variational principle: for any unnormalized trial wave function
{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle E[{\psi}]=\frac{\int{\psi}({\mathbf{r}}){\hat{H}}{\psi}({\mathbf{r}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}}{\int{\psi}^{2}({\mathbf{r}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}}\geq E_{0},

(3)

so minimizing
E[{\psi}_{\theta}]
over parameters
\theta
yields an upper bound on the ground-state energy.
We denote unnormalized wave functions as
{\psi}
and normalized ones as
{\Psi}={\psi}/{\mathcal{Z}}
with
{\mathcal{Z}}=\|{\psi}\|
.
Excited states are obtained by minimizing energy subject to orthogonality to all lower states.

Hartree-Fock (HF)
is a mean-field approximation, which restricts
{\psi}
to a single
Slater determinant
of one-electron functions
\{{\phi}_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{p=1}^{N_{\mathrm{o}}}
, called
molecular orbitals (MO)
.
While
{N_{\mathrm{o}}}
molecular orbitals are optimized,
{N_{\mathrm{e}}}
of these are selected for the final wave function

\displaystyle{\psi}_{\mathrm{HF}}({\mathbf{r}})=\det\left[\Phi_{\text{HF}}({\mathbf{r}})\Pi_{0}\right],

(4)

where
\Phi_{\text{HF}}({\mathbf{r}})_{ip}={\phi}_{p}(\bm{r}_{i})
is the orbital matrix function and
\Pi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
is an
orbital selector
.
In the case of energy-sorted MOs,
\Pi_{0}
corresponds to an identity matrix followed by a zero matrix.
The orbitals
{\phi}_{p}
are defined as a linear combination of basis functions
\{{\xi}_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{\mu=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}}
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{HF}}({\mathbf{r}})=\Xi({\mathbf{r}})C

(5)

where
\Xi({\mathbf{r}})_{i\mu}={\xi}_{\mu}(\bm{r}_{i})
.
The determinant in
eq.

4
ensures the wave function obeys the fermionic antisymmetry.
The coefficients
C\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{o}}}}
are chosen to minimize the variational energy

E[{\psi}_{\mathrm{HF}}]
under the constraints

\innerproduct{\orb_\porb}{\orb_\qorb}=\delta_{pq}

(
45
)
.
Importantly, a set of orthogonal wave functions
{\psi}_{\text{HF},s}
may be constructed by replacing
\Pi_{0}
with
\Pi_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
such that
\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T\Orbsel_\tstate)=\delta_{st}
for all
s,t
, see
appendix

A

(
54
)
.

Variational Monte Carlo (VMC)
recasts
eq.

3
as

\displaystyle E[{\psi}_{\theta}]=\frac{\int{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}){\hat{H}}{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}}{\int{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{\theta}}\big[E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})\big],

(6)

where
E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{({\hat{H}}{\psi}_{\theta})({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})}
is the local energy and
\rho_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})\propto{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})^{2}
is sampled via Markov chain Monte Carlo
(
5
)
.
Since minimization is done via gradient descent, one can optimize any differentiable unnormalized wave function with VMC.
See
appendix

B
for details.

Excited states in VMC
can be obtained via penalty-based methods
(
41
;
12
)
that minimize the energy of wave functions
{\Psi}_{\theta_{0}},\ldots,{\Psi}_{\theta_{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1}}
subject to
\langle{\Psi}_{s}|{\Psi}_{t}\rangle=\delta_{st}
through the objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1}E[{\Psi}_{\theta_{s}}]+\sum_{t\neq s}\omega_{st}\langle{\Psi}_{\theta_{s}}|{\Psi}_{\theta_{t}}\rangle,

(7)

\displaystyle\omega_{st}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}>E_{t}-E_{s}&\text{if }E[{\Psi}_{\theta_{s}}]<E[{\Psi}_{\theta_{t}}],\\
=0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(8)

The triangular shape of
\omega
ensures convergence to pure eigenstates rather than linear combinations that would share the same loss
(
59
)
.
Since the lower bound requires true state energies,
\omega_{st}
is typically overestimated to avoid state collapse.
See
appendix

C
for an estimator of
\omega_{st}
.

The overlap can be written as an expectation in terms of

\displaystyle\begin{split}O_{st}=\innerproduct{\Wf_\sstate}{\Wf_\tstate}&=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{s}}\left[\frac{{\Psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\Psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}\right]\\
&=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{s}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}\right]\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{t}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}})}\right]}.\end{split}

(9)

The reformulation in the second line cancels the normalizers
{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}/{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}
.
Notably, for a fixed total batch size of
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
, the variance of this estimator is
\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}(1-O_{st}^{2})}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}
, see
appendix

D
.
Furthermore, the random variable
\frac{{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}
is
unbounded
and diverges for
{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})\to 0
, leading to rare but extreme outliers.

Neural wave functions
parameterize the trial wave function in VMC using a neural network.
Typically, a permutation equivariant neural network represents
many-electron
orbitals
{\phi}_{p}^{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}
that are combined in a linear combination of Slater determinants to construct an antisymmetric many-electron wave function
(
23
)
.
Alternatives to the Slater determinant are antisymmetric geminal powers
(
33
)
or Pfaffians
(
28
;
17
)
.

Generalized wave functions
approximate the eigenstates of multiple Hamiltonians that differ in nuclear positions and charges, i.e., for a set of systems
\mathcal{M}=\{({\mathbf{R}}_{m},{\mathbf{Z}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
, a generalized wave function
\Psi
minimizes
\sum_{m=1}^{M}E[\Psi_{\theta({\mathbf{R}}_{m},{\mathbf{Z}}_{m})}]

(
14
)
.

This is commonly accomplished by having the wave functions’ parameters
\theta
be a function of the nuclear positions
{\mathbf{R}}
and charges
{\mathbf{Z}}
, typically through a meta-network that produces system-specific parameters
(
15
;
49
)
.
This enables amortized training where the cost of adding new systems grows sublinearly, as the meta-network learns transferable representations across chemical space
(
13
)
.

Related Work

Neural wave functions
achieved high accuracy for single structures
(
4
;
23
;
43
;
60
;
61
;
33
;
28
)
, with further improvements in accuracy
(
20
;

)
and efficiency
(
29
;
52
;

)
, but separate
training per geometry is prohibitively expensive for PES.
Generalized wave functions address this for multi-structure
(
50
;
14
)

and multi-molecule computations, using hand-crafted selectors
(

;

)
, or HF
orbitals
(

)
.
The Neural Pfaffian
(
17
)
replaces the Slater determinant
\det(\Orbmat)
with a Pfaffian
{\mathrm{Pf}}(\Phi A\Phi^{T})

(
1
;
2
)
, where

is skew-symmetric and
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{o}}}}
need not be square, enabling a fully-learnable generalization of Slater-type wave functions.
Here, we extend the Neural Pfaffian to efficiently represent excited states.

Excited states
are one of the big challenges in quantum chemistry
(
35
;
40
)
.
In VMC, excited states can be obtained through variance minimization
(
39
;
63
;
44
)
, by definition
(
10
;
9
)
, through symmetry
(
8
)
, or via overlap penalties
(
41
)
, which were the first method applied to neural wave functions
(
12
)
.
While most require
a priori
knowledge about the target state, overlap penalties are flexible but require careful tuning
(
59
)
.
Alternatively, natural excited states (NES)
(
42
)
avoids explicit orthogonalization but scales
O({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{4})
.
When spin states are known, spin penalties may be used to target specific states
(
55
;
31
)
.
Recent work extends penalty-based methods to PES
(
47
)
.
We extend the method further to generalization across molecules and improve scaling in
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
.

Monte Carlo estimation
quality depends critically on the sampling distribution
(
40
)
.
While sampling from alternative distributions has reduced variance for energy gradients
(

;
38
;

)
, overlap estimation remains problematic.
Its sensitivity to sample size

, and the standard remedy is scaling samples with

(

55

.

4
Method

We train a neural network
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{n}}}\times 3}\times\mathbb{N}_{+}^{N_{\mathrm{n}}}\times\{1,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\}\to\Omega
that maps nuclear positions
{\bm{R}}
, charges
{\mathbf{Z}}
, and target state
s
to parameters
\theta({\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}},s)
for a wave function Ansatz
{\Psi}_{\theta({\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}},s)}
approximating one of the
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
lowest eigenfunctions of
{\hat{H}}_{{\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}}}
.
While previous works
(
14
;
15
;
49
)
demonstrated cost amortization for generalization over
{\bm{R}}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
, we demonstrate amortization for excited states.

For favorable scaling, we keep the total number of samples
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
drawn from all wave functions
\{{\Psi}_{\theta({\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}},s)}\}_{{\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}},s}
constant, so samples per wave function decrease with more structures and states.
While this works for intra-wave function losses like energy
(
13
)
, previous works
(
12
)
and our experiments (
section

5
) show that naively applying it to the overlap degrades estimates significantly.
We tackle this challenge by introducing
Multi-State Importance Sampling
(MSIS), which pools samples from all states of a structure to estimate overlaps.

However, the overlap requires evaluating all
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
wave functions per structure
({\bm{R}},{\mathbf{Z}})
.
To accelerate this, we introduce the
Excited Pfaffian
architecture, inspired by Hartree-Fock theory, that maximizes parameter reuse across states.
We additionally introduce a spin-state snapping loss to prevent convergence to linear combinations of states with different spin, and a pretraining scheme to ensure state continuity across geometric perturbations.
We conclude by describing how the architecture generalizes across molecules.

Multi-State Importance Sampling (MSIS).

.

The variance of the single-state importance sampling estimator in
eq.

9
scales linearly in the number of states
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
.
Previous works scaled the total number of walkers
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\propto{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
to keep the variance constant, thereby increasing compute time.

Instead of increasing
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
, we pool samples from all states
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}=\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\rho_{s}
and rewrite the inner product as

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\Wf_\sstate}{\Wf_\tstate}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}\left[\frac{{\Psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}}){\Psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}({\mathbf{r}})}\right].

(10)

As depicted in
fig.

2
, this improves coverage yielding three crucial benefits.
(1) The random variable
\frac{{\Psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}}){\Psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}({\mathbf{r}})}
is bounded to
\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
, eliminating the heavy-tailed outliers near wave function nodes.
(2) The average overlap variance reduces from
\mathcal{O}(\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}})
to
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}})
, removing the need to scale batch size with the number of states.
(3) For states with vanishing
Bhattacharyya coefficient

\langle|{\Psi}_{s}|\,|{\Psi}_{t}|\rangle\to 0
, i.e., low unsigned overlap, the variance vanishes, whereas the single-state estimator retains unit variance.
We refer to
appendix

D
for proofs and
appendix

E
for gradients.

However, since
{\psi}_{s}
are unnormalized, we need to estimate the normalizer ratios
r_{s}=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
to evaluate
\frac{{\Psi}_{t}({\mathbf{r}}){\Psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})}{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}({\mathbf{r}})}
.
We accomplish this via multi-distribution bridge sampling
(
37
)
.
In short,
{\bm{r}}
can be estimated as the fixed point of the following system of equations:

\displaystyle B^{({\bm{r}})}_{st}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{t\neq s}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[w^{({\bm{r}})}_{s}]&\text{, if }s=t,\\
-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[w^{({\bm{r}})}_{t}]&\text{, else,}\end{cases}

(11)

\displaystyle b^{({\bm{r}})}_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}[w^{({\bm{r}})}_{s}].

(12)

where
q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{({\bm{r}})}=\sum_{s}r_{s}q_{s}
,
w^{({\bm{r}})}_{s}=\frac{q_{s}}{q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{({\bm{r}})}}
.
We initialize
{\bm{r}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{1}
and iterate
\hat{{\bm{r}}}^{(t+1)}=({\bm{B}}^{(t)})^{-1}{\bm{b}}^{(t)}
until reaching the fixed point.
For further details, we refer the reader to
appendix

F
.
As the overlap, with or without MSIS, requires evaluating all wave functions for all samples, MSIS comes at no meaningful runtime cost (
section

F.1
).

Excited Pfaffian.

We seek to parametrize many
orthogonal
wave functions that generalize to arbitrary molecules.
We start by following the Neural Pfaffian architecture
(
17
)
that defines the wave function as a linear combination of Pfaffian wave functions

\displaystyle{\psi}({\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{k}}}{\mathrm{Pf}}\left(\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{k}A_{k}\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{k}^{T}\right)

(13)

where the elements of the orbital matrices
\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{kip}
are linear readouts of the electron’s embeddings
{\bm{h}}({\mathbf{r}})_{i}
and exponentially decaying envelope functions
\chi_{kp}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{kip}

\displaystyle={\bm{h}}({\mathbf{r}})_{i}^{T}{\bm{w}}_{kp}\cdot\chi_{kp}(\bm{r}_{i}).

(14)

The electron embeddings are obtained through permutation equivariant architectures like graph neural networks
(
23
;
15
)
, transformers
(
58
)
, or deep sets
(
43
)
.
The skew-symmetric antisymmetrizer
A_{k}
, the weights
{\bm{w}}_{kj}
and the envelopes
\chi_{kj}
are constructed on a per-nuclei basis and concatenated such that their number grows with the system size, ensuring that
{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\geq{N_{\mathrm{e}}}
.

A trivial extension to many states is to expand
\Phi_{skp}
and consequently
{\bm{w}}_{skp},\chi_{skp}
with an additional dimension running over the number of states
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
, yielding
{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{k=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{k}}}}{\mathrm{Pf}}\left(\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{sk}A_{sk}\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{sk}^{T}\right)

(
47
;
42
)
.
While simple, this definition incurs a high memory usage when applying approximate second-order methods due to the large number of parameters
(
21
)
.
For instance, for typical choices of hyperparameters (
table

2
), the Jacobian of the linear projection alone may require 1TB of VRAM, see
appendix

G
.

Fortunately, as we outlined in
section

2
, wave functions do not need to differ in many parameters to be orthogonal.
In HF, orthogonal wave functions can be constructed by simply changing the orbital selector
\Pi_{s}
.
By applying the identity
{\mathrm{Pf}}(BAB^{T})=\det(B){\mathrm{Pf}}(A)
, one can see that we may represent these within the Neural Pfaffian framework

\displaystyle{\psi}_{\text{HF,s}}=\det\left(\Phi_{\text{HF}}\Pi_{s}\right)\propto{\mathrm{Pf}}(\Phi_{\text{HF}}\Pi_{s}A\Pi_{s}^{T}\Phi_{\text{HF}}^{T})

(15)

for any non-singular skew-symmetric
A\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
.

Thus, we generalize Hartree-Fock and Neural Pfaffian with our set of orthogonal wave functions

\displaystyle{\psi}_{s}({\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{k=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{k}}}}{\mathrm{Pf}}\left(\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{k}A_{sk}\Phi({\mathbf{r}})_{k}^{T}\right)

(16)

which only differ in their orbital selection
A_{sk}
.
We drop the spin-shared orbitals
(
16
)
and use separate parameters per spin for the orbitals to allow for non-symmetric excitations.

fig.

3
depicts our architecture.
As states only differ in
A_{sk}
, most of the forward pass is shared, enabling the efficient evaluation of all states.

Spin-state snapping.

The Hamiltonian commutes with the total spin operator
\hat{S}^{2}
, thus, eigenstates of
{\hat{H}}
are also eigenstates of
\hat{S}^{2}
with the eigenvalues
S(S+1)
for integer or half-integer
S

(
54
)
.
During initial experimentation, we encountered linear combinations of singlet and triplet states.
While directly fitting the target eigenvalue is possible and resolves this
(
31
)
, the spin states of the
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
lowest states are generally unknown.

To resolve this without knowing the spin states
a priori
, we snap wave functions dynamically to their closest eigenvalue
S^{*}=\argmin_{S\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{2}}|S(S+1)-\langle{\Psi}|\hat{S}^{2}|{\Psi}\rangle|
of
\hat{S}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{snap}}=\lambda\left(\langle{\Psi}|\hat{S}^{2}|{\Psi}\rangle-S^{*}(S^{*}+1)\right)^{2}.

(17)

Note that, naively computing
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{snap}}
requires differentiating the wave function twice
(
55
)
.
Instead, we use the spin-raising operator
\hat{S}_{+}
following
31
, exploiting the identity

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi}|\hat{S}^{2}|{\Psi}\rangle=\frac{({N_{\uparrow}}-{N_{\downarrow}})({N_{\uparrow}}-{N_{\downarrow}}+2)}{4}+\langle\hat{S}_{+}{\Psi}|\hat{S}_{+}{\psi}\rangle.

(18)

Following
31
, gradients of
\langle\hat{S}_{+}{\Psi}|\hat{S}_{+}{\psi}\rangle
can be computed efficiently without second-order derivatives.
To avoid strong biases to states similar to the pretraining targets, we initialize
\lambda
as zero and ramp it up after 80k steps.

Pretraining Excited Pfaffians.

Neural-network VMC typically uses HF solutions as supervised pretraining targets, as these already cover most of the total energy
(
24
)
.
For single-structure excited states, one typically chooses HF excited determinants
(
42
;
55
)
.
Excited-state PESs introduce a novel challenge: matching excited determinants across structures to obtain smooth, orthogonal targets.
We achieve this by organizing structures into a DAG and propagating canonical orbitals via basis projection, see
appendix

H
.

When matching these targets
\{\Xi,C,\Pi_{s}\}_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
to our wave function, we need to account for disagreement in orbital ordering
(
15
)
, which we discuss in the following for a single structure and determinant.
Instead of
17
’s alternating optimization loops, we propose a closed-form solution to pretraining generalized Pfaffian wave functions.
For the orbitals
\Phi_{\text{HF}}=\Xi C
, we minimize

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MO}}=\|\Phi R-\Phi_{\text{HF}}\|_{2}^{2}

(19)

where
R\in O({N_{\mathrm{o}}})
aligns the orbitals.
This is an orthogonal Procrustes problem with solution
R^{*}=UV
from the SVD
U\Sigma V^{T}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\Phi_{\text{HF}}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})\Phi({\mathbf{r}}_{n})
.
When rotating the orbitals, the orbital selector transforms to
R^{*T}\Pi_{s}
, represented through
A_{s}
in our Excited Pfaffians.
We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\|R^{T}\Pi_{s}\tilde{A}_{\text{HF,s}}\Pi_{s}^{T}R-A_{s}\|_{2}^{2}

(20)

where
\tilde{A}_{\text{HF,s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
is an orthogonal skew-symmetric matrix representing the order ambiguity of selected orbitals.
This Procrustes problem has minimizer
\tilde{A}^{*}_{\text{HF,s}}=\tilde{U}_{s}\tilde{V}_{s}
from the SVD
\tilde{U}_{s}\tilde{\Sigma_{s}}\tilde{V}_{s}=\Pi_{s}R^{T}A_{s}R\Pi_{s}^{T}
.
We differentiate through
R^{*}
but not
\tilde{A}^{*}_{\text{HF},s}
, as the repeated singular values (0 or 1) have no well-defined SVD gradient.

Generalizing over systems.

Following the Neural Pfaffian framework
(
17
)
, we generalize across molecules by conditioning a subset of wave function parameters on the nuclear configuration
({\mathbf{R}},{\mathbf{Z}})
.
A graph neural network operating on the nuclei predicts structure-dependent parameters: nuclear embeddings, envelope decays and combinations, orbital projection weights
{\bm{w}}_{kp}
, and the antisymmetrizer
A_{sk}
.
Most parameters of the electron embedding network remain shared across all systems.
To ensure the number of orbitals scales with system size, we predict
{N_{\mathrm{orb}/\mathrm{nuc}}}
orbitals per nucleus and concatenate them, yielding
{N_{\mathrm{o}}}={N_{\mathrm{n}}}\cdot{N_{\mathrm{orb}/\mathrm{nuc}}}
.
The antisymmetrizer
A_{sk}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{o}}}}
is constructed from
{N_{\mathrm{n}}}^{2}
blocks
A_{sk}^{(mn)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{orb}/\mathrm{nuc}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{orb}/\mathrm{nuc}}}}
, one for each pair of nuclei.

Limitations.

While we successfully reconstruct many-state PES, our pretraining scheme does not guarantee state continuity.
Depending on the training dynamics, states may still exhibit rapidly changing electronic characteristics due to small geometric perturbations, or be biased toward higher-energy states that are structurally more similar to the pretraining target.
Like previous works
(
19
;
13
)
, accuracy may degrade for distinct, uncorrelated compounds in multi-structure training, see
section

5
.
Finally, while the spin-state snapping loss resolves many linear combinations, snapping to the wrong spin state early in training may prevent convergence to lower ones.

6
Discussion

We addressed the superlinear time-scaling of excited states in neural-network variational Monte Carlo via two main contributions.
Multi-State Importance Sampling reduces the variances of overlap estimates by pooling samples across states, and the Excited Pfaffian architecture efficiently encodes many states in a single model.
Jointly, these result in sublinear, near-constant, time scaling in the number of excited states.
Moreover, we made independently valuable contributions to neural wave functions.
Spin-state snapping provides the benefits of a spin penalty without requiring prior knowledge of the correct spin sectors, and our improved pretraining procedure eases the initialization of generalized wave functions.

Taken together, these contributions enabled us to match the accuracy of up to 200
\times
more expensive methods.
We computed many-state potential energy surfaces with high accuracy at a fraction of the cost, and were the first to identify all excitations of the beryllium atom using neural networks in VMC.
We also demonstrated the first neural wave function that generalizes across molecules for excited states.
However, while strong synergies exist in modeling energy surfaces, like previous works
(
19
;
17
)
, lossless generalization across chemical compounds remains an open challenge.

Nonetheless, the advances made may enable new applications of neural-network VMC in photochemical simulations
(
56
)
or in the modeling of absorption spectra
(
32
)
.
Beyond direct applications, generalized excited-state wave functions enable the generation of accurate reference data for training excited-state machine-learning force fields
(
26
)
, or density functionals
(
7
;
34
)
.

Impact statement

Our work reduces the computational cost of simulating excited states. We do not foresee negative societal consequences beyond those common to computational chemistry.

Appendix A
Hartree-Fock excited-state construction

In Hartree-Fock theory, the ground state occupies the
{N_{\mathrm{e}}}
lowest-energy orbitals, selected by
\Pi_{0}=[I_{{N_{\mathrm{e}}}},\mathbf{0}]^{T}\in\{0,1\}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
.
Excited states are constructed by replacing occupied orbitals with virtual (unoccupied) orbitals.
For a single excitation from orbital
p
to orbital
a>{N_{\mathrm{e}}}
, the selector
\Pi_{p\to a}
is obtained by swapping the
p
th and
a
th rows of
\Pi_{0}
.

More generally, let
\Pi_{s},\Pi_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
be two orbital selectors where each column contains exactly one 1. The resulting wave functions are orthogonal if and only if

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi}_{\text{HF},s}|{\Psi}_{\text{HF},t}\rangle=\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbsel_\tstate)=\delta_{st}.

(21)

This follows from the fact that for Slater determinants
{\Psi}_{s}=\det(\Orbmat\Orbsel_\sstate)
and
{\Psi}_{t}=\det(\Orbmat\Orbsel_\tstate)
with orthonormal orbitals
\Phi^{T}\Phi=I
:

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi}_{s}|{\Psi}_{t}\rangle=\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbmat^T \Orbmat\Orbsel_\tstate)=\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbsel_\tstate).

(22)

When
\Pi_{s}
and
\Pi_{t}
select disjoint sets of orbitals in at least one position,
\Pi_{s}^{T}\Pi_{t}
has a zero row, yielding
\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbsel_\tstate)=0
.
When they select identical orbitals,
\Pi_{s}^{T}\Pi_{t}
is a permutation matrix with
\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbsel_\tstate)=\pm 1
.

More generally, this result extends to real-valued selectors
\Pi_{s},\Pi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
: the condition
\det(\Orbsel_\sstate^T \Orbsel_\tstate)=\delta_{st}
remains necessary and sufficient for orthogonality of the resulting wave functions.

Appendix B
Variational Monte Carlo

Variational Monte Carlo optimizes a parametrized wave function
{\psi}_{\theta}
by minimizing the energy expectation

\displaystyle E[{\psi}_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{\theta}}\left[E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})\right],

(23)

where
\rho_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})^{2}}{\int{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}
and
E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{({\hat{H}}{\psi}_{\theta})({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})}
.

Sampling.

Samples from
\rho_{\theta}
are obtained via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), typically using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.
Starting from a configuration
{\mathbf{r}}
, a proposal
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
is drawn from a proposal distribution
q({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{r}})
and accepted with probability

\displaystyle\begin{split}A({\mathbf{r}}\to{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\min\left(1,\frac{\rho_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})q({\mathbf{r}}|{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}{\rho_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})q({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{r}})}\right)=\min\left(1,\frac{{\Psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})^{2}q({\mathbf{r}}|{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}{{\Psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})^{2}q({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{r}})}\right).\end{split}

(24)

For symmetric proposals
q({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{r}})=q({\mathbf{r}}|{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, this simplifies to accepting based on the ratio
{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})^{2}/{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})^{2}
.

Energy gradients.

The gradient of the energy with respect to parameters
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}E[{\psi}_{\theta}]=2\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{r}}\sim\rho_{\theta}}\left[\left(E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})-E[{\psi}_{\theta}]\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log|{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})|\right],

(25)

where the baseline
E[{\psi}_{\theta}]
reduces variance without introducing bias.
This expression is estimated using
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
samples
\{{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}
from the MCMC chain:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}E[{\psi}_{\theta}]\approx\frac{2}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\left(E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})-\bar{E}\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log|{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})|,

(26)

where
\bar{E}=\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\sum_{i}E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})
.

Local energy computation.

The local energy requires evaluating
{\hat{H}}{\psi}_{\theta}/{\psi}_{\theta}
. For the electronic Hamiltonian, this involves:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbf{r}})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{\nabla_{i}^{2}{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}})}+V({\mathbf{r}}),

(27)

where
V({\mathbf{r}})
contains the Coulomb potential terms. The kinetic term is computed as

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{i}^{2}{\psi}_{\theta}}{{\psi}_{\theta}}=\nabla_{i}^{2}\log|{\psi}_{\theta}|+\left(\nabla_{i}\log|{\psi}_{\theta}|\right)^{2}.

(28)

Appendix C
Overlap penalty scale estimation

As shown by
41
and refined by
59
, the penalty coefficients must satisfy

\displaystyle\omega_{st}>|E_{t}-E_{s}|

(29)

to avoid collapse of the optimized states onto each other. While any value satisfying this constraint yields the correct global minimum, values closer to the bound can reduce training noise.

Following
55
, we employ automatic scaling of
\omega_{st}
based on running estimates of the energies:

\displaystyle\omega_{st}=\tilde{\omega}\cdot\max\left(|\bar{E}_{s}-\bar{E}_{t}|,\sigma(E_{s}),\epsilon\right),

(30)

where
\tilde{\omega}>1
is a shared hyperparameter,
\bar{E}_{s}
denotes the exponentially weighted mean of the local energies for state
s
, and
\sigma(E_{s})
is the standard deviation. The first term ensures the constraint is satisfied while adapting to the system-specific energy gaps. The second term prevents state collapse during early training when energy estimates are unreliable. The floor
\epsilon=10^{-3}\penalty\ E_{h}
provides numerical stability.

This parametrization significantly reduces system-dependence compared to manually tuning individual
\omega_{st}
values.
Like
55
, we use
\tilde{\omega}=4
for all systems in this work.
For PES calculations,
47
propose dynamical state reordering during training to maintain state continuity across geometries.
We adopt the same approach.
States are reordered by energy at each optimization step, ensuring that the penalty coefficients
\omega_{st}
consistently penalize high-energy states rather than states with fixed indices.

Appendix D
Variance of the overlap estimator

We analyze Monte Carlo estimators for the normalized overlap integral
O_{st}=\langle{\Psi}_{s}|{\Psi}_{t}\rangle
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}=\sqrt{\int{\psi}_{s}(x)^{2}\mathrm{d}x}
is the normalization constant,
{\Psi}_{s}(x)=\frac{{\psi}_{s}(x)}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}}
is the normalized wave function and
\rho_{s}(x)={\Psi}_{s}(x)^{2}
is the corresponding probability density. Estimator 2 requires the normalizing constant ratios
r_{s}={\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}/{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{2}
; these are estimated via bridge sampling as described in
F
. The variance analysis below is conditional on these ratios.

D.1
Estimator 1: Single-state distribution.

Sampling from
\rho_{s}
alone yields

\displaystyle O_{st}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{t}(x)}{{\psi}_{s}(x)}\right]\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}}.

(31)

The integrand
{\psi}_{t}(x)/{\psi}_{s}(x)
is
unbounded
: wherever
{\psi}_{s}(x)\to 0
while
{\psi}_{t}(x)\neq 0
, the ratio diverges. For orthogonal states, this occurs in extended regions where the nodal structures differ, leading to high-variance estimates.

Denote the random variable
g(x)={\psi}_{t}(x)/{\psi}_{s}(x)
. The second moment is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[g^{2}]=\int\frac{{\psi}_{s}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\psi}_{t}^{2}}{{\psi}_{s}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\int\frac{{\psi}_{t}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}},

(32)

giving variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{s}}[g]=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}-\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}O_{st}^{2}=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}\left(1-O_{st}^{2}\right).

(33)

Although this expression appears bounded, the random variable
g(x)={\psi}_{t}(x)/{\psi}_{s}(x)
itself is
unbounded
: it diverges wherever
{\psi}_{s}(x)\to 0
while
{\psi}_{t}(x)\neq 0
. The finite variance arises only because such regions have measure zero under
\rho_{s}
. In practice, finite-sample estimates suffer from rare but extreme values when samples land near nodes of
{\psi}_{s}
, causing heavy tails and unreliable convergence.

A further complication is that this estimator requires knowledge of the normalization ratio
\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}}
. This can be circumvented by sampling from both
\rho_{s}
and
\rho_{t}
and using the geometric mean. Define
A=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[g]
and
B=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[g^{-1}]
. Then
AB=O_{st}^{2}
, so
|O_{st}|=\sqrt{AB}
with the normalization ratios canceling. Using the delta method with
\frac{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}
samples from each distribution, the per-pair variance is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{st}]\approx\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}(1-O_{st}^{2})}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}.

(34)

This depends only on
O_{st}
, not the normalization ratio. However, the underlying random variables remain unbounded, and the delta method requires
|O_{st}|
bounded away from zero, so this approximation may be unreliable for nearly orthogonal states.

With sample reuse across all
\binom{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
pairs, the total variance is

\displaystyle\sum_{s<t}\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{st}]\approx\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\sum_{s<t}(1-O_{st}^{2}).

(35)

For orthogonal states where
O_{st}\approx 0
, this becomes
\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1)}{4{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}
, scaling as
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{3}}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\bigr)
.

D.2
Estimator 2:
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
-state mixture.

When optimizing
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
states simultaneously, we estimate all
\binom{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
pairwise overlaps from a single
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
-component mixture
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}(x)=\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}\sum_{s=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{\Psi}_{s}(x)^{2}
. The pairwise overlap becomes

\displaystyle O_{st}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}(x)],\quad\text{where}\quad f_{st}(x)=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot{\Psi}_{s}(x){\Psi}_{t}(x)}{\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2}}.

(36)

By AM-GM,
\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}\geq{\Psi}_{s}^{2}+{\Psi}_{t}^{2}\geq 2|{\Psi}_{s}{\Psi}_{t}|
, so
|f_{st}(x)|\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
is
bounded
.

Per-pair variance bound.

The second moment of
f_{st}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}^{2}]=\int\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}\sum_{u}{\Psi}_{u}^{2}\cdot\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}{\Psi}_{s}^{2}{\Psi}_{t}^{2}}{(\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2})^{2}}\mathrm{d}x={N_{\mathrm{s}}}\int\frac{{\Psi}_{s}^{2}{\Psi}_{t}^{2}}{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

(37)

Using the AM-GM bound
\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}\geq 2|{\Psi}_{s}||{\Psi}_{t}|
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}^{2}]\leq{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\int\frac{|{\Psi}_{s}|^{2}|{\Psi}_{t}|^{2}}{2|{\Psi}_{s}||{\Psi}_{t}|}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}\int|{\Psi}_{s}||{\Psi}_{t}|\mathrm{d}x=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}\operatorname{BC}_{st},

(38)

where
\operatorname{BC}_{st}=\langle|{\Psi}_{s}|||{\Psi}_{t}|\rangle
is the Bhattacharyya coefficient (overlap of unsigned wave functions).
The per-pair variance with
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
samples is therefore bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{st}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[f_{st}^{2}]-O_{st}^{2}}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\operatorname{BC}_{st}-2O_{st}^{2}}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}.

(39)

For orthogonal states (
O_{st}=0
), this simplifies to
\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{st}]\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\operatorname{BC}_{st}}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}
.
States with low coefficients (spatially disjoint) have lower variance, states with high coefficients contribute more.
Since
O_{st}\leq\operatorname{BC}_{st}\leq 1
, the per-pair bound scales as
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}/{N_{\mathrm{b}}})
.
However, analyzing all pairs jointly yields a tighter result.

Joint variance bound.

The sum of second moments is

\displaystyle\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}^{2}]={N_{\mathrm{s}}}\sum_{s<t}\int\frac{{\Psi}_{s}^{2}{\Psi}_{t}^{2}}{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

(40)

Using
(\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2})^{2}=\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{4}+2\sum_{s<t}{\Psi}_{s}^{2}{\Psi}_{t}^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}\int\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{4}}{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}-P),

(41)

where
P:=\int\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{4}}{\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x
.
P
satisfies
1\leq P\leq{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
: the lower bound follows from Cauchy-Schwarz (
\sum_{s^{\prime}}({\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2})^{2}\cdot{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\geq(\sum_{s^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2})^{2}
applied pointwise), and the upper bound from
{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{4}\leq{\Psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}\sum_{t^{\prime}}{\Psi}_{t^{\prime}}^{2}
.
Dividing by
\binom{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
pairs gives the average second moment:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}}\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}[f_{st}^{2}]=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}-P}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1}\leq 1.

(42)

With
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}
total samples (
\frac{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}
per state), the sum of Monte Carlo variances satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{s<t}\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{st}]=\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\sum_{s<t}(\mathbb{E}[f_{st}^{2}]-O_{st}^{2})}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}-P)}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}-\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\sum_{s<t}O_{st}^{2}}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\leq\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1)}{2{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}.

(43)

For orthogonal states, this scales as
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}}\bigr)
, a factor of
\frac{{N_{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}
improvement over Estimator 1.

Effect of estimated normalization ratios.

The analysis above conditions on the true ratios
r_{s}=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{2}}
. In practice, these are estimated via bridge sampling (
appendix

F
), introducing additional variance.
For two states, bridge sampling quality degrades when the unsigned overlap
\operatorname{BC}_{st}
decreases, as the distributions have less overlapping support.
However, with many states, a state only needs spatial overlap with
any
of the others, not all.
Even if
\operatorname{BC}_{st}
is small, intermediate states can bridge the gap.
The overlap estimator
f_{st}
depends on
r_{s}
only through the mixture weights in the denominator
\sum_{s^{\prime}}r_{s^{\prime}}{\psi}_{s^{\prime}}^{2}
, where errors in individual
r_{s}
are averaged over
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
terms.
The variance bounds in
eq.

43
therefore hold up to
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{b}}}})
corrections from ratio estimation.

Appendix E
Gradients of the overlap estimator

The gradient of the pairwise overlap with respect to the network parameters
\theta
can be written as
(
12
)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}O_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\left(\frac{{\psi}_{j}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{j}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})}\right]\right)\nabla_{\theta}\ln{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})\right]\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{j}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{j}({\mathbf{r}})}\right].

(44)

This expression requires accurate estimates of the overlap ratios
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}[\frac{{\psi}_{j}}{{\psi}_{i}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{j}}[\frac{{\psi}_{i}}{{\psi}_{j}}]
.
When estimated from single-state samples, these ratios exhibit high variance early in training, leading to noisy gradients that destabilize optimization (see
section

5
).
We address this by computing the expectation over the same pooled distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}
used for the overlap itself, replacing the single-state overlap estimates with our MSIS estimator from
eq.

10
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}O_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\left(\frac{{\psi}_{j}({\mathbf{r}})}{{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})}-\hat{O}_{ij}\right)\nabla_{\theta}\ln{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})\right]\hat{O}_{ji},

(45)

where
\hat{O}_{ij}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}\left[\frac{{\psi}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}){\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{r}})}{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}({\mathbf{r}})}\right]
is the MSIS overlap estimator.
We also experimented with using
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}
for the outer expectation, but found it made little difference in practice and was difficult to integrate with second-order preconditioners like SPRING
(
21
)
.

Appendix F
Normalizing constant ratio estimation

The importance weights
w_{s}=\frac{q_{s}}{\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}}
in the MSIS estimator require the ratios
r_{s}=\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}^{2}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{2}}
for
s\in\{2,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\}
. We estimate these via the iterative bridge sampling algorithm of
37
.

Bridge sampling.

Given unnormalized densities
q_{s}={\psi}_{s}^{2}
and
q_{t}={\psi}_{t}^{2}
with unknown normalizing constants, bridge sampling estimates their ratio by introducing a
bridge function

\alpha({\mathbf{r}})
that connects expectations under both distributions. The key identity is

\displaystyle r_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[\alpha\,q_{t}]=r_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[\alpha\,q_{s}],

(46)

which holds for any
\alpha>0
and relates the ratio
\frac{r_{s}}{r_{t}}
to computable expectations. The optimal bridge function that minimizes estimator variance is
\alpha^{*}\propto\frac{1}{r_{s}q_{s}+r_{t}q_{t}}
, which places more weight where both densities have support.

Multi-state estimation.

For
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
states, we use a shared bridge function
\alpha=\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(r)}}
with
q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(r)}=\sum_{s}r_{s}q_{s}
. Defining responsibilities
w_{s}=\frac{q_{s}}{q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(r)}}
, the identity becomes
r_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[w_{t}]=r_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[w_{s}]
. Using all
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}-1)
pairwise identities with
r_{1}=1
yields a linear system
{\bm{B}}{\bm{r}}={\bm{b}}
for
{\bm{r}}=(r_{2},\ldots,r_{N_{\mathrm{s}}})^{T}
:

\displaystyle B_{st}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{t\neq s}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[w_{s}]&\text{, if }s=t,\\
-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}}[w_{t}]&\text{, else,}\end{cases}

(47)

\displaystyle b_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}[w_{s}].

(48)

Since
w_{s}
depends on the unknown
{\bm{r}}
through
q_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(r)}
, we iterate: initialize
\hat{r}_{s}^{(0)}=1
, then solve
\hat{{\bm{r}}}^{(t+1)}=({\bm{B}}^{(t)})^{-1}{\bm{b}}^{(t)}
. We run 10 iterations per optimization step and clip updates to a factor of 2 for stability.

Appendix G
Memory usage of separate orbitals per state

A naive extension to multiple states expands the orbital matrices with a state dimension, i.e.,
\Phi_{skip}
, requiring separate orbital weights
{\bm{w}}_{skp}
and envelopes
\chi_{skp}
for each state
(
47
;
42
)
.
While conceptually simple, this approach leads to prohibitive memory requirements when combined with approximate second-order optimizers such as SPRING
(
21
)
, which require storing the Jacobian of the network outputs with respect to parameters.

For typical hyperparameters, batch size
{N_{\mathrm{b}}}=4096
,
{N_{\mathrm{orb}/\mathrm{nuc}}}=8
orbitals per nucleus, embedding dimension
{N_{\mathrm{f}}}=256
,
{N_{\mathrm{k}}}=16
determinants,
{N_{\mathrm{s}}}=32
states, and meta-network embedding dimension of 64, the Jacobian of the linear layer mapping nuclear embeddings to orbital projections alone requires

\displaystyle 4096\times 8\times 256\times 16\times 32\times 64\times 4\,\text{bytes}\approx 1\,\text{TB}

(49)

of memory, far exceeding current GPU capacities.

Previous works have addressed this limitation differently.

47
compute and precondition gradients for each state separately, then average the updates.
However, generalized wave function methods have demonstrated that jointly preconditioning the total loss yields better optimization dynamics
(
14
;
49
)
.
In
42
, this issue does not arise because they use fewer orbitals, treat orbitals as direct parameters, and do not employ a meta-network for generalization.

Our Excited Pfaffian architecture avoids this memory bottleneck entirely by sharing orbitals across states and varying only the lightweight antisymmetrizer
A_{sk}
, whose parameter count is independent of the embedding dimension.

Appendix H
Pretraining target construction

Pretraining neural-network wave functions to Hartree-Fock solutions reduces the number of costly VMC optimization steps
(
24
)
, as HF captures approximately 99% of the total energy
(
54
)
.
However, constructing consistent pretraining targets for multi-structure excited states poses unique challenges: HF molecular orbital coefficients are only defined up to unitary transformations, and different structures yield independently computed solutions with arbitrary relative phases and orderings.
We address this by (1) organizing structures into a directed acyclic graph, (2) selecting consistent excitations at the root, and (3) propagating canonical orbitals between structures via basis projection and overlap maximization.

Structure graph construction.

To ensure smooth pretraining targets across structures, we must establish a canonical correspondence between molecular orbitals at different geometries.
Naively running independent HF calculations at each structure yields orbitals with arbitrary phases and orderings, causing discontinuous pretraining targets that hinder generalization.

We observe that geometrically similar structures have similar electronic structure, so their HF orbitals can be aligned with small error.
This motivates organizing structures into a graph where edges connect similar geometries.
Specifically, we compute pairwise RMSD between all structures sharing the same nuclei
{\mathbf{Z}}
and electronic configuration
({N_{\uparrow}},{N_{\downarrow}})
, and construct a minimum spanning tree (MST) over this complete graph.
The MST minimizes the total edge weight, which corresponds to minimizing the cumulative geometric dissimilarity along propagation paths.

The MST defines connectivity but not direction.
To obtain a rooted tree suitable for sequential propagation, we select the root as the node minimizing the tree’s diameter (maximum distance to any leaf).
This choice minimizes the worst-case propagation depth, reducing error accumulation for the most distant structures.
The rooted tree naturally defines a DAG via parent-child relationships.

Traversing this DAG in topological order yields a sequence where each non-root structure
i
has a unique predecessor
p(i)
whose orbitals have already been determined.
This reduces the global
N
-structure matching problem to
N-1
independent pairwise matchings, each between geometrically adjacent structures where alignment is most reliable.

Excitation selection.

Following
42
, we enumerate excited states via orbital selectors
\Pi_{s}\in\{0,1\}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{e}}}}
that specify which orbitals to occupy for each state
s
.
To maintain consistency across structures, we determine the excitation pattern (i.e., which occupied orbitals are replaced by which virtual orbitals) at the root structure and apply the same
\Pi_{s}
to all related structures.
This ensures that corresponding excited states across different geometries represent the same electronic transition, enabling smooth interpolation of potential energy surfaces.
However, this requires that molecular orbitals maintain consistent ordering and character across structures, which we address in the following paragraphs.

Orbital propagation.

We perform a single Hartree-Fock computation at the root structure, obtaining basis functions
\Xi_{\text{root}}
and molecular orbital coefficients
C_{\text{root}}
.
For each non-root structure
i
with predecessor
p(i)
, we propagate the orbitals by projecting
C_{p(i)}
onto structure
i
’s basis
\Xi_{i}
.
The projection must preserve orthonormality of the molecular orbitals.
Given the overlap matrix
\tilde{O}_{i}
of structure
i
’s basis functions, where
(\tilde{O}_{i})_{\mu\nu}=\langle{\xi}_{\mu}|{\xi}_{\nu}\rangle
, we compute:

\displaystyle C_{i}=C_{p(i)}\left(C_{p(i)}^{T}\tilde{O}_{i}C_{p(i)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(50)

This symmetric orthogonalization ensures
C_{i}^{T}\tilde{O}_{i}C_{i}=I
, preserving orthonormality while staying maximally close to the predecessor’s orbitals in a least-squares sense.

Block-wise overlap maximization.

To ensure consistent orbital ordering, we seek an orthogonal transformation
Q_{i}\in O({N_{\mathrm{o}}})
that minimizes the distance between the molecular orbitals of structure
i
and its predecessor
p(i)
:

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{*}=\arg\min_{Q_{i}\in O({N_{\mathrm{o}}})}\left\|\Xi_{i}C_{i}Q_{i}-\Xi_{p(i)}C_{p(i)}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(51)

Expanding the Frobenius norm and using the orthonormality of molecular orbitals, this simplifies to maximizing
\tr(\Talign_i^T \MOovlp_i)
where
\bar{O}_{i}=C_{i}^{T}\langle\Xi_{i}|\Xi_{p(i)}\rangle C_{p(i)}
is the inter-structure molecular orbital overlap matrix.
This is an orthogonal Procrustes problem with closed-form solution
Q_{i}^{*}=\hat{U}_{i}\hat{V}_{i}^{T}
, where
\hat{U}_{i}\hat{\Sigma}_{i}\hat{V}_{i}^{T}=\bar{O}_{i}
is the singular value decomposition.
We then update
C_{i}\leftarrow C_{i}Q_{i}^{*}
to obtain the aligned orbitals.

However, applying this global alignment over all orbitals can produce spurious matchings.
Core orbitals have small spatial extent and may have higher overlap with spatially extended higher-energy orbitals from nearby atoms than with their true counterparts.
To address this, we partition orbitals into groups based on their Hartree-Fock orbital energies
\epsilon_{k}

(
54
)
and solve independent Procrustes problems within each group.
This block-wise approach is physically motivated: orbitals with degenerate or near-degenerate energies are not uniquely defined and may mix arbitrarily, so matching within energy shells respects this gauge freedom.

We cluster orbitals using kernel density estimation on the orbital energies.
Given energies
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}}
, we estimate the density using a Gaussian kernel
\hat{f}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{{N_{\mathrm{o}}}h\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k}\exp\left(-\frac{(\epsilon-\epsilon_{k})^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right)

with bandwidth
h
.
Local maxima of
\hat{f}
define cluster centroids, and each orbital is assigned to its nearest centroid.
This approach has a single hyperparameter (the bandwidth
h
) and naturally adapts to the energy level structure of each system.

Appendix L
Compute time estimates

Comparing our method to NES based solely on the total number of training samples does not paint the full picture, as it ignores differences in
N_{s}
scaling.
In NES, each sample from the total wave function contains
N_{s}\times N_{e}
electrons, which we have accounted for in the sample counts given in the main body.
Here, we estimate the computational speedup of Excited Pfaffian over NES
(
42
)
under conservative assumptions that favor the baseline.
NES theoretically scales as
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{4})
per sample.
Empirical measurements report
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{3.07})
scaling for the Psiformer architecture with folx and
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2.5})
without folx
(
42
;
18
)
.
We conservatively assume
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2})
scaling for NES.
We further assume that the NES implementation with KFAC
(
36
)
achieves a
2\times
per-step speedup over our SPRING-based implementation, accounting for differences in codebase optimization.

Carbon dimer PES.

For the C
2
potential energy surface, NES requires training one model per structure (10 structures) with 8 states each.
Our method trains a single model across all structures with 12 states.
Accounting for our empirical
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{0.14})
scaling, our computation is 41% more expensive than a ground state only computation.
Under the
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2})
assumption, the relative compute cost of NES is
\frac{8^{2}\times 10}{2*1.41}\approx 226
times ours.
Applying this scaling to 12 states for a direct comparison yields
\frac{12^{2}\times 10}{2*1.41}\approx 511
times higher cost.

Beryllium excited states.

For the 33-state beryllium computation, NES would require
\frac{33^{2}}{2}\approx 728
times more compute than a ground state calculation under the conservative
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2})
assumption.
However, as the number of states grows, we expect the scaling to approach the theoretical scaling of
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{4})
.
Assuming
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{3})
, the estimate increases
\frac{33^{3}}{2}\approx 24000
.
Given our measures
\mathcal{O}({N_{\mathrm{s}}}^{0.14})
scaling for up to 33 states, we estimate an increase of
63\%
, yielding a final conservative speedup of
\frac{728}{1.63}\approx 444
.
