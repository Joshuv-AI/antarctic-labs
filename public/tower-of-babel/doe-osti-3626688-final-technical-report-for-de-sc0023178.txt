Final Report 1

ABSTRACT

Low-dimensional quantum materials, such as graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides,
show promise as a platform for advances in computing, communication, and sensing thanks to
their unique electronic and optical propeties. When low-dimensional quantum materials are
biased or illuminated, their response is drastically different from that of atomically similar three-
dimensional bulk systems. Describing the electronic and optical response of quantum materials
means accurately describing the dynamics of electrons that are driven far from equilibrium,
which is a regime where microscopic phenomena arising from low dimensionality get amplified
or become interrelated thanks to external excitations. This requires a time-dependent far-from-
equilibrium theoretical framework—both mathematical and numerical—that self-consistently
couples the electron system of interest, the driving system (electromagnetic fields), emergent
quasiparticle systems (e.g., plasmons and excitons), and various relaxation channels (e.g.,
phonons). In this project, the PI and team worked on the development and deployment of a suite
of such theoretical and computational techniques and successfully applied them to describe the
complex physics of electron systems in low-dimensional quantum materials.

ACTIVITIES

The work conducted during the course of the project can be grouped into three thrusts:
 1. Nonlinear optical properties of two-dimensional quantum materials
 2. Terahertz (THz) frequency properties of two-dimensional quantum materials
 3. Novel mathematical and numerical methods for describing light–matter interaction in
 two-dimensional quantum materials

1. Nonlinear optical properties of two-dimensional quantum materials

Motivation: Nonlinear optics offers a promising platform for controling and manipulating light
at the nanoscale for nanophotonic applications. It relies on matter-mediated photon-photon
interactions, which are intrinsically very weak. Therefore, realizing nonlinear nanophotonics
requires new materials and structures that will enhance nonlinear optical effects.
 Graphene, the first two-dimensional quantum material, and its nanostructures strongly
interact with light and are a fitting class of materials for nonlinear optics applications. A proven
way to enhance light–matter interaction and nonlinear response is by integration of graphene
with systems that support photonic resonances. An alternative is through plasmonic field
enhancement combined with quantum confinement, which is attractive because of electrical
tunability of the carrier density (and thus plasmonic resonances) by a back gate. For example,
plasmon-enhanced Kerr nonlinearity of small graphene nanoislands with features below 10 nm
could be as large as 10−11 m2/ V2, almost four orders of magnitude greater than in graphene
sheets. However, the synthesis of sub-10-nm nanoislands with precise geometry, size, and edge
termination, which is required for the control of their plasmonic response, is very challenging
and makes these systems difficult to envision in integrated nanophotonics applications. In

contrast, the synthesis of graphene sheets and integration of them into nanophotonics and
nanoelectronics are mature, well-developed processes. However, launching propagating
plasmonic waves in graphene is challenging in practice because the wave vectors of graphene
plasmons are much larger than the wave vector of the free-space electromagnetic waves with the
same frequency. Owing to the wavevector mismatch, graphene plasmons cannot be simply
excited by free-space light. Fortunately, one can launch propagating plasmonic waves in
graphene sheets and ribbons patterned with periodically distributed antidots (holes). The
periodicity aids with momentum conservation, which enables plasmon excitation. Successful
plasmon excitation in these structures has been experimentally demonstrated.

Technical approaches: We used the density-matrix formalism for electron quantum transport to
calculate the optical response, including nonlinear response, for graphene nanomeshes (GNMs),
which are graphene sheets patterned with antidots.

Key findings: We found that GNMs have large plasmon-enhanced nonlinear optical response.
GNMs can be designed to behave as quasi-one-dimensional plasmonic crystals in which
plasmons with large propagation lengths are efficiently excited. The associated third-order Kerr
and third-harmonic-generation susceptibility can be as high as 10−7 and 10−9 m2/V2, respectively,
over the mid-to-near-infrared frequency range. Furthermore, carrier-density tuning in GNMs can
flip the propagation direction of plasmonic waves and enables bidirectional switching of optical
signals, which makes GNMs a promising core material for nonlinear optics applications.
 In addition to modulation applications, we find that GNMs offer impressive capabilities
for switching applications. By fine-tuning the Fermi energy, which is readily achieved by a back
gate, we found we could control the strength of the GNM plasmon and nonlinear optical
responses, as well as influence the direction of propagation of the plasmonic wave. The latter
capability enables bidirectional switching of optical signals by small changes in the Fermi level.
Fermi level change by about 5 meV, corresponding to carrier-density separation of about 3x 1012
cm-2, is sufficient to completely resolve forward and backward-propagating modes.

Thrust 2. Terahertz (THz) frequency properties of two-dimensional quantum materials

Motivation: Transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are widely studied two-dimensional
quantum materials. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) is a common example of this group of
materials, showing promise for thermal, mechanical, and electronic applications. The bandgap of
MoS2 changes from indirect to direct bandgap as the material goes from bulk to single layer,
which makes monolayer MoS2 a candidate for a wide range of optoelectronic applications.
However, the transfer process from TMD nanolayers onto the substrate impacts its optical and
electrical properties.
 The complex conductivity σ(ω), where ω is the angular frequency, is a response quantity
that characterizes electrical transport under low-amplitude ac electrical biasing or excitation by

Final Report 3

electromagnetic waves. The terahertz (THz) frequency range is interesting for various device
applications, but also from the standpoint of fundamental science, because the scattering rates of
electrons in many bulk semiconductors and two-dimensional materials fall in this range. There
have been experimental measurements of the low-terahertz conductivity of monolayer MoS2.
However, theoretical work on the electronic and optical properties of TMDs in general and MoS2
in particular in this frequency range is scarce. A key challenge is the need to treat electronic
transport and electrodynamics on an equal footing because of the similarity between the
excitation frequencies and typical scattering rates, which makes the commonly employed
quasistatic approximation for electrodynamics insufficient.

Technical approaches: We calculated the static and frequency-dependent electrical conductivity
for MoS2 in the terahertz range by employing EMC–FDTD, a multiphysics simulation tool
developed by the PI’s group. EMC–FDTD incorporates an ensemble Monte Carlo (EMC) solver
for electron transport coupled with a finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) solver for full-wave
electrodynamics.

Key findings: In MoS2, electron dynamics is governed by the interactions with intrinsic phonons,
charged impurities, and substrate phonons. In the static case, the conductivity is nonlinear in
electron density owing to dynamical screening, which FDTD captures. In the case of excitation
by an electromagnetic wave, we calculate the complex conductivity as a function of frequency
and investigate the role of electron density and substrate choice (SiO2 or hexagonal boron nitride,
hBN) in frequency-dependent electronic transport. Owing to the dominance of surface-optical-
phonon scattering, MoS2 on SiO2 has the lowest static conductivity, but also the weakest overall
frequency dependence of the conductivity. In fact, at high THz frequencies, the conductivity of
MoS2 on SiO2 exceeds that of either suspended or hBN-supported MoS2. We extract the density-
and-substrate-dependent fitting for Drude-model fits to the conductivity versus frequency curves
obtained from microscopic simulation, which may benefit further experimental efforts in the
application of two-dimensional materials for terahertz-based devices and integrated circuits,
sensors, and detectors.

Thrust 3. Novel mathematical and numerical methods for describing light–matter
interaction in two-dimensional quantum materials

Motivation: The control of light-matter interaction at the nanoscale is a grand challenge that cuts
across modern photonics, plasmonics, and optoelectronics. The development of simulation tools
for predictive modeling of nanostructured light emitters and absorbers is critical to successfully
tackling this grand challenge. Existing state-of-the-art tools are not suitable for the following
reasons: a) Phenomenological models of materials, commonly employed in the full-wave
solutions to Maxwell’s equations, are fit to experimental data for bulk materials and fall short
when attempting to analyze nanostructures. b) Nonlinearities in optical response can arise solely

Final Report 4

from nanostructuring, and require time-dependent quantum electronic transport techniques to
capture. c) Emergent materials with exotic and tunable physical properties are increasingly used,
but their properties are very sensitive to the substrate, impurities, and edge termination, and
require an accurate description of interband and intraband processes, as well as dissipation. d)
Microscopic modeling of electronic transport usually employs simplified electrodynamics, and
realistic models that couple full electronic transport with full-wave electrodynamics are needed
for accurate photonics simulations.
 When it comes to coupling of electrodynamics with quantum transport, time-dependent
density functional theory captures optical response within atomic-scale quantum transport, but is
computationally intensive and limited to small structures. The problem of coupling quantum
transport with electrodynamics on the spatial scales of real, experimentally relevant devices
remains an open area of inquiry. Prior work included solving second-order equations for the
magnetic vector potential A and electric scalar potential ϕ in the Lorenz gauge and coupled those
with a Schrödinger solver. However, even setting aside the fact that solving the Schrödinger
equation is a single-particle problem and not a full quantum-transport problem requiring account
of nonequilibrium kinetics, the second-order equations for the potentials, where the vector
potential is sourced by the current and the potential by the charge density, while theoretically
pleasing, are in fact numerically suboptimal because of the large memory requirements
associates with all the field storage needed to update the second-order equations and the absence
of absorbing boundary conditions whose performance is nowhere near par with standard
computational electrodynamics.

Technical approaches and key findings: We developed, implemented, and tested a novel self-
consistent field–potential (FiPo) FDTD algorithm using a first-order time derivative A–ϕ
formulation in conjunction with the traditional E–H Maxwell’s equations. The first-order
formulation retains the key benefits of standard FDTD while enabling the calculation of
potentials needed for integration with various quantum-transport frameworks. By coupling the
FiPo FDTD solver with a quantum transport solver, FiPo FDTD enables the calculation of
potentials for the quantum transport solver, and the current and charge densities can be fed back
into FiPo FDTD as sources.
 Additionally, we have formulated and implemented a complex-frequency shifted form of
the convolutional perfectly matched layer, which we will simply refer to CPML here. In terms of
speed, accuracy, and the quality of absorbing boundary conditions, FiPo FDTD (in its Basic and
Hybrid forms) is on par with the state-of-the-art of traditional FDTD, which is the mainstay of
computational electromagnetics. This computational advance is particularly useful for
calculating the potentials from known sources that are used to drive quantum systems some
distance apart (e.g., radiation problems) and where retardation effects must be carefully
accounted for.
