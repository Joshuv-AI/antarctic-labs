Probability characteristics and extreme value analysis of fluctuating wind pressure on heliostat arrays
Authors: Haiyin Luo, Terigen Bao, Shi Zuo, Xuewen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heliostats, with their large-area reflective surfaces, are inherently sensitive to wind loads. This study investigates wind load sensitivity of heliostats in open terrains through wind tunnel simulations encompassing 130 operational conditions, systematically analyzing probability characteristics and extreme value prediction of surface fluctuating wind pressures. The analytical framework establishes diagnostic criteria for pressure distribution patterns, and subsequently validated through the analysis of probability density histograms and pressure time-history traces. To address prevalent non-Gaussian characteristics, Block Maxima (BM), Peak Over Threshold (POT), and Bayesian methodologies were implemented for extreme pressure estimation. Rigorous threshold optimization and parametric refinement demonstrated these methods’ superior predictive capability over conventional peak factor approaches. Results indicate Gaussian zones predominantly occupied central regions below 60°elevation, while peripheral areas exhibited non-Gaussian behavior due to vortex shedding. For extreme value prediction under non-Gaussian conditions, comparative analysis demonstrated that the Bayesian method achieved a narrower 95% confidence interval width of 18.86 compared to the baseline method, indicating substantially improved estimation precision with reduced uncertainty. Furthermore, chi-square goodness-of-fit tests confirmed the conformity between the empirical distribution of observed wind pressure data and the Bayesian-derived parametric model, with most interval-specific components below 2.0 and central probability regions consistently below 1.0. The proposed risk-operational identification mechanism based on azimuth-elevation combinations and Bayesian extreme value paradigm provides a theoretical basis for wind-resistant heliostat design.

Introduction

Research background

Solar heliostat arrays are typically deployed in open terrains characterized by flat topography, regions frequently subjected to high-intensity wind events. Due to their structural configuration, these systems exhibit limited wind resistance, rendering them susceptible to structural damage under severe wind loading conditions. More critically, operational functionality necessitates precise optical alignment to direct reflected solar radiation onto thermal receivers, mandating strict control over mirror surface deformations within rigorously defined tolerance thresholds. Given these operational imperatives, research efforts prioritize optimizing wind-resistant designs by focusing on two critical mechanical parameters: peak stress concentrations within support columns and extreme wind pressure distributions across mirror surfaces. Accurate quantification and effective management of these mechanical indices constitute crucial determinants for ensuring both operational stability and structural integrity of heliostat systems.

In structural wind engineering, fluctuating wind pressures are commonly assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution. However, research has revealed that wind pressures on specific regions of heliostat mirror surfaces exhibit non-Gaussian probabilistic characteristics. Relying on Gaussian models to describe this distribution introduces significant errors into wind load calculations, thereby compromising the accuracy of wind resistance assessments for these critical solar tracking structures. Understanding the wind load characteristics and underlying mechanisms governing photovoltaic (PV) and concentrated solar power (CSP) systems like heliostats is paramount for evaluating their operational performance and structural stability. While knowledge specific to wind loads on photovoltaic panels is not directly applicable to heliostats, the underlying research methodologies possess considerable universality. Key examples include wind tunnel testing techniques, statistical frameworks employed in extreme value analysis, and qualitative understandings of array interference effects, all of which can be critically adapted for application in heliostat studies. Wind tunnel testing remains an essential methodology for investigating structural wind pressures.

Wind load mechanisms, prediction methods, and experimental techniques

The investigation of wind load characteristics and their underlying mechanisms constitutes the foundation for wind-resistant design of heliostats. Wu’s wind tunnel experiments investigated wind load coefficients on heliostat panels within solar power tower systems, identifying that actual flow characteristics significantly influence the base overturning moment, which notably decreases with increasing heliostat elevation1. Zou et al.2 explored the probabilistic characteristics, particularly the extreme values, of the base shear force coefficient for pedestal mounts supporting planar roof-mounted trough concentrators, establishing that the Sadek-Simiu method yielded the most accurate predictions. Merarda et al.3 analyzed the impact of mirror shape on heliostat aerodynamic loads, determining that circular mirrors offer aerodynamic advantages under turbulent flow conditions. Jafari et al.4 investigated the influence of atmospheric turbulence on the dynamic wind loads experienced by heliostats near ground level, finding that the dominant frequencies of turbulent wind loads are sensitive to the heliostat’s height and size. Matthew Maran’s study on polyhedral heliostats highlighted that gaps between facet mirrors increase wind loads, particularly as the facet width-to-depth (aspect) ratio increases; additionally, changing the gap ratio induces shifts in the center of pressure5. Based on wind tunnel tests of rectangular heliostat fields at a 40°elevation angle, Yang et al.6 revealed that aerodynamic loads follow a distinct edge-amplified pattern, with interior heliostats experiencing notably uniform worst-case forces. They proposed optimizing the array layout at a fixed field density and employing wind fences, which are particularly beneficial for heliostats in the edge regions. Sahar et al.7, through detailed wind tunnel experiments, revealed the local wind pressure distribution characteristics on a heliostat facet, demonstrating that local force coefficients under specific elevation-azimuth angle combinations can significantly exceed known global coefficients, and establishing a nonlinear relationship between wind load coefficients and turbulence intensity at low turbulence levels.

The analysis of non-Gaussian characteristics drives the application of advanced extreme value prediction methodologies, and the resulting precise predictions ensure the safety and reliability of wind-resistant design for heliostats. Emes’ study reveals that while wind tunnel simulations agree with field measurements in mean load coefficients versus elevation, significant discrepancies exist in fluctuating load distributions. Field data exhibit stronger non-Gaussian characteristics (higher skewness), attributed to anisotropic turbulence in low-altitude natural wind and its deviation from standard models. This divergence directly impacts extreme load estimation, potentially leading to underestimated lift or overestimated drag if relying solely on wind tunnel data8. Emes et al.9 compared various statistical methods for predicting peak wind loads on rectangular heliostat models under high-frequency wind load fluctuations. Crucially, their findings demonstrated that statistical prediction methods relying solely on longitudinal gust wind speed magnitude tend to underestimate the tilt-moment load coefficients, particularly under stow position conditions and large azimuthal moment scenarios. Consequently, they concluded that accurate prediction of peak hinge moments and azimuthal moments necessitates consideration of both the frequency and amplitude characteristics of the vertical wind speed component. As established, the non-Gaussian characteristics of wind pressure significantly influence the extreme wind loads experienced by heliostat structures. Emphasizing this critical aspect, Song et al.10 investigated these non-Gaussian properties and their impact on extreme wind pressures for long-span structure roofs, concluding that accounting for non-Gaussian is essential for accurate extreme wind load assessment. Similarly, Yang’s research examined the influence of turbulence integral scale on both the non-Gaussian of surface pressures and extreme wind loads, revealing a notable distribution pattern: pressures on the windward face generally conformed to Gaussian behavior, whereas those on the side and leeward faces exhibited pronounced non-Gaussian characteristics11. Focusing specifically on heliostats, Xiong et al.12 analyzed the probabilistic characteristics and peak factors associated with the base shear force on heliostat pedestals. Employing correlation methods to systematically evaluate Gaussian conformity across all operational conditions, they identified the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) method as most suitable for predicting the peak base shear. Accurate prediction of peak wind loads is a prerequisite for ensuring the structural integrity and optical precision of heliostats. However, conventional extreme value prediction methods exhibit significant limitations when applied to the complex geometric attitudes of heliostats. Studies have confirmed that statistical models based solely on the magnitude of longitudinal gust wind speed significantly underestimate the load coefficients for the azimuth moment when predicting heliostats in stow position or under specific yaw angles13. The accuracy of peak load prediction is also highly dependent on understanding the microscale physical properties of the Atmospheric Boundary Layer (ABL). Above heliostat fields located in low-roughness desert or flat terrain, the relationship between the gust factor and turbulence intensity deviates from traditional semi-empirical formulas established for urban or suburban terrains14.

The stow position of a heliostat also affects its wind resistance. In the stow position, where the heliostat is typically oriented horizontally, its mechanical response is highly sensitive to geometric parameters. Research indicates that reducing the mirror chord length and adjusting the elevation axis height can significantly decrease the peak lift and hinge moment. Notably, when the elevation axis height is less than or equal to half the chord length, the fluctuating wind pressure on the structure is effectively suppressed15. However, traditional quasi-static models demonstrate significant limitations in predicting extreme loads for heliostats in the stow position. While the equivalent static wind load method offers valuable reference under certain conditions, the complexity of atmospheric boundary layer turbulence leads to markedly non-Gaussian characteristics in the fluctuating surface pressures on heliostats. Experimental analysis reveals that under critical conditions, the peak loads acting on these drive systems can far exceed conventional predictions16. By implementing real-time monitoring of wind speed and direction across the field, a zoned management strategy for heliostats becomes feasible. For specific wind direction angles, the operational wind loads on certain heliostats may be less than 50% of the stowage load threshold. Allowing these heliostats to continue operating under such conditions can significantly enhance the annual energy yield17.

Aerodynamic interference in arrays and stow position effects

Beyond single-unit behavior, research on heliostat wind resistance frequently extends to the critical considerations of array interference effects and aerodynamic interactions. In a related study, Emes et al.18 further explored the impact of atmospheric surface layer turbulence properties on the peak wind load coefficients for heliostats in a parked configuration, providing insights to optimize design wind loads, structural sizing, and associated costs. Additionally, Emes investigated the effects of turbulence within the atmospheric boundary layer on the peak wind loads of both tandem-arranged and isolated heliostats, correlating these loads with key heliostat scaling parameters15. Furthermore, in complex concentrating solar-thermal systems, aerodynamic interference among different components can significantly alter the local flow-field structure. For instance, within a system comprising both heliostats and a central receiver, their mutual interaction induces a notable increase in local turbulence intensity, consequently elevating the peak wind loads. Statistical analysis based on extensive operational conditions indicates that a safety factor ranging from 1.1 to 1.2 should be incorporated into structural optimization design to compensate for this interference effect19. This phenomenon becomes particularly intricate in array layouts, where the packing density of heliostat clusters and the implementation of windbreaks profoundly modify the internal flow-field profiles and pressure evolution patterns within the array20. Building on the theme of configuration effects, Jeremy et al.21 investigated the aerodynamic loading of heliostats arranged in tandem configurations to quantify the significance of shielding by upstream units. Their results indicated a substantial reduction of up to 60% in the peak drag force on downstream heliostats, achieved at a 90°elevation angle and a separation distance of two chord lengths. Empirical studies indicate that reducing the gust cycle from the conventionally recommended 3 s to 1 s allows for a more sensitive capture of the instantaneous impact of high-frequency gusts on the structure. Under low surface roughness conditions, this adjustment to the gust cycle can increase the predicted gust factor by approximately 2.4%, with a more pronounced increase observed in high-roughness terrain. At the array level, the distribution of extreme wind loads exhibits strong spatial non-uniformity. High-fidelity Large Eddy Simulation (LES) of complex arrays containing hundreds of mirrors reveals that extreme wind pressure concentrations are not uniformly distributed but are concentrated on the windward front edge and the lateral flanks of the array22.

Heliostat wind resistance research often relies on experimental methods, numerical simulations, or hybrid approaches combining both. Du et al.23 conducted pressure measurement tests on large-span flexible PV support structures, systematically analyzing the distribution patterns of mean and fluctuating wind pressure coefficients across PV panel surfaces under various combinations of wind azimuth angles and panel tilt angles. Niu et al.24 employed an innovative Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) approach to analyze the time-history of wind loads on multi-row PV arrays subjected to both mean and fluctuating wind components, comparing results with steady-state flow fields to elucidate the specific influence of turbulence on PV panel force characteristics. Using a Fluid–Structure Interaction (FSI) method, Zuo developed a simulation model for a dish-type solar concentrator system, enabling the investigation of posture and wind speed effects on wind-induced vibration responses, which led to improvements in its vibration-resistant design25. Through wind tunnel testing, Li investigated the spatio-temporal distribution of wind pressures on heliostats and associated interference effects, mapping the mean pressure distribution and quantifying interference effects under different operational conditions, ultimately identifying the specific conditions and locations corresponding to maximum and minimum pressure measurements26. Complementing the focus on load prediction, Bao et al.27 utilized field testing combined with finite element analysis to investigate the vibration characteristics of tracking PV support systems, successfully validating the accuracy and applicability of the proposed analytical model for predicting their structural response.

Through a comprehensive meta-analysis of 26 existing studies, this research identifies five critical gaps in the current understanding of wind loads on heliostat arrays. These include the lack of systematic analysis regarding non-Gaussian properties across various operational configurations (azimuth and elevation), the inadequacy of conventional Gaussian-based methods in capturing "heavy-tailed" distributions, the limited understanding of spatial pressure distribution on the mirror surface, the poorly documented combined effects of dynamic adjustments and array interference, and the insufficient coverage of operational scenarios in previous experiments.

To bridge these gaps, this study establishes four core objectives with corresponding implementation steps. First, to expand the experimental database, a comprehensive wind tunnel test encompassing 130 distinct operational conditions (13 azimuths × 10 elevations) is conducted. Second, to characterize non-Gaussian behavior, a probabilistic framework based on higher-order moments (specifically skewness and kurtosis) is implemented to identify critical loading zones. Third, regarding extreme value prediction, three statistical methods are rigorously compared and validated. Finally, for spatial risk assessment, detailed pressure contour maps are generated to pinpoint high-risk regions such as edges and corners, thereby providing a holistic solution to enhance the reliability of heliostat array design.

Testing and experiments

This investigation comprehensively characterizes the fluctuating wind pressure properties and associated extremes for heliostat structures. Field measurements were systematically conducted at) a representative, topographically flat and open site in Northwest China (36° 32′ 18″ N, 99° 25′ 33″ E), characterized by predominant natural grassland coverage interspersed with exposed wind-formed dunes. The dataset captures key measurement points under diverse operational conditions, detailing both the power spectral density distributions of fluctuating wind pressures and their extreme value distributions. The in-situ wind measurement system consists of cup anemometers and low-inertia wind vanes mounted on a 10-m tall mast. The sensors are installed at seven different heights with a vertical spacing of 1.4 m to measure wind speed and direction simultaneously across multiple levels. A CYD9100 portable 16-channel data acquisition unit (Chaoyu Inc.) was employed for continuous and synchronous signal recording, with a sampling frequency set at 32 Hz. This field measurement infrastructure facilitated the acquisition of the requisite wind field characteristics, enabling rigorous analysis of the site-specific atmospheric conditions (Figs. 1 and 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10).

Experimental investigations for this study were conducted within the HD-3 closed-circuit atmospheric boundary layer wind tunnel located at Hunan University (Figs. 6 and 8). This facility features a primary test section measuring 14 m in length with a rectangular cross-section of 3.5 m by 3.0 m. At its center, a rotating turntable system with a diameter of 1.8 m provides comprehensive simulation capabilities, enabling investigation across a full 360-degree range of wind azimuth angles and wind speeds continuously adjustable between 0 and 25 m/s. Precise replication of the atmospheric boundary layer conditions pertinent to the target site was achieved through the deployment of an active flow control array. To simulate the atmospheric boundary layer wind profile of the target site, a combination of triangular-section spires and cubical roughness elements was installed upstream of the wind tunnel test section entrance. Multiple spires were arranged transversely at equal intervals, with their heights calculated to generate the required boundary layer thickness and velocity gradient. Downstream of the spires, several rows of small cubical roughness elements were arranged in a regular pattern on the floor. Their dimensions and spacing were calibrated according to the aerodynamic roughness length of the target terrain (Fig. 9). A sampling frequency of 312.5 Hz was employed, with 10,000 wind pressure data points recorded for each measurement location, resulting in a sampling duration of 32 s. The wind speed in this test is approximately 7 m/s. Calculations show that the Reynolds number for this wind tunnel test is on the order of 9.3 × 104 to 1.1 × 105.

Figures 3, 4 and 5 present the simulated wind field characteristics, including the wind speed profile, turbulence intensity profile, and wind speed power spectrum, at the center of the wind tunnel turntable, based on measured data from a site in northwestern China28. The turbulence intensity profile is compared with profiles specified in the Chinese (GB5009), Japanese (AIJ-2004), and British standards (ESDU 85020), as well as with wind tunnel test data and on-site measurements. The wind speed power spectrum is compared with theoretical models, namely the Von Karman, Kaimal, and Davenport spectra, along with wind tunnel experimental data and on-site measured spectra. Preliminary tests measuring velocity and turbulence intensity profiles at multiple upstream spanwise locations confirmed the horizontal homogeneity of the flow within the test section. The spires and roughness elements were placed sufficiently upstream to ensure the flow reached a fully developed equilibrium state before encountering the model. Consequently, the inflow characterization, validated both along the centerline and in the spanwise direction, adequately represents the approaching flow conditions acting on the heliostat.

A rigid heliostat model (Fig. 7), served as the test subject. The scaled model accurately replicates key structural components, including the pedestal support assembly, central rotating shaft, supporting space frame structure, and a monolithic mirrored panel surface. This integrated reflector surface, measuring 232 mm × 200 mm, was manufactured using a 5 mm thick glass panel. It is mechanically connected to the rotating shaft via hollow steel support pillars, each measuring 10 mm in diameter, 2 mm in wall thickness, and 112 mm in height.

The study strictly adhered to a 1:50 geometric scaling ratio, accurately replicating the dimensions of key components including the support structure, rotation axis, and mirror panel. This rigorous geometric similarity provides a fundamental basis for fixing the flow separation points. By maintaining an appropriate wind speed ratio that corresponds to the scaling relationship between the wind tunnel inflow velocity and the prototype wind speed, the similarity of dynamic characteristics such as vortex shedding frequency was ensured, enabling the correct simulation of unsteady flow phenomena. Although the geometric scaling introduces Reynolds number discrepancies that may affect the similarity of fine-scale turbulence and boundary layer development, for bluff-body structures like heliostats, the flow separation points are primarily determined by geometry. Once beyond a critical Reynolds number, the sensitivity of the overall aerodynamic forces to Reynolds number variations decreases significantly. Based on the assessment that the relatively small mirror area minimizes the aerodynamic impact of potential gaps, combined with insights from the literature indicating limited flow-solid interaction effects at this scale, the reflective surface was implemented as a single, continuous plate to preclude complex fluid–structure coupling phenomena29.

The wind velocity and pressure measurement systems in the HD-3 wind tunnel are described as follows: A TFI Cobra Probe was utilized to capture 3D wind velocity, pitch and yaw angles, turbulence intensity, and static pressure. The probe operates within a velocity range of 2–100 m/s with an accuracy of ± 0.5 m/s. The angular measurement range covers a ± 45° cone, with a precision of ± 1.0° for both pitch and yaw. The sampling frequency was set to 500 Hz. The pressure system comprises hardware and software components. The hardware integrates a nitrogen supply, a computer, and a DSM3400 pressure scanner. The scanner consists of eight modules with 64 channels each, enabling the simultaneous acquisition of wind pressure data from 512 points. Data acquisition and processing were managed via dedicated software. The sampling frequency for the pressure system was 312.5 Hz, Sampling duration for each operating condition is 32.768 s (Fig. 10).

The experimental design commenced with a systematic selection of relevant control parameters and their respective discrete levels. This selection methodology was informed by comprehensive analysis of 2023 meteorological records from the local monitoring station, revealing that heliostat operational parameters typically operated within defined ranges: azimuth angles spanning 0° ~ 180°and elevation angles varying between 0°and 90°. Based on this climatological foundation, azimuth angle and elevation angle were consequently identified as the primary experimental variables. Each parameter was discretized at increments aligned with representative operational intervals, resulting in the definition of 130 distinct experimental conditions (13 azimuth levels × 10 elevation levels) for subsequent investigation (Table 3). The selection of azimuth and elevation angles was directly derived from the historical operational data of the target power plant. A uniform discretization at fixed intervals across their full physical ranges was employed to construct a set of static test conditions that covers typical operating scenarios. This design ensures engineering representativeness and enables the systematic revelation of wind pressure characteristics as a function of angular variation.

To characterize spatio-temporal pressure distributions across operational scenarios, surface pressure measurements were systematically acquired. Subsequent analysis implemented a probabilistic framework grounded in higher-order moment theory, establishing a robust distribution identification protocol. Gaussian pressure fields were sufficiently delineated through their first two statistical moments (mean and variance), whereas non-Gaussian behavior necessitated incorporation of third-order (skewness) and fourth-order (kurtosis) moment descriptors. These higher-order metrics jointly quantify probability distribution profile asymmetries and extreme-value tail weight characteristics. The wind pressure distribution on the heliostat facet is non-uniform. To investigate its spatial patterns, several representative operational conditions were selected for analysis. These conditions are denoted in the format: Azimuth -Elevation.

As illustrated in Fig. 12, the four figures illustrate the distributions of the mean pressure coefficient on a heliostat mirror surface under different elevation angles. For the condition 0–90 (mirror perpendicular to the ground), the pressure distribution exhibits a distinct stagnation characteristic, with a high-pressure core (Cp≈1.5) formed in the central region due to flow stagnation, which gradually decreases towards the edges. The pattern is symmetric about the vertical centerline, and a low-pressure band (Cp≈1.2) appears near the bottom due to flow acceleration. In the condition 0–60, the overall pressure coefficient increases, and the distribution shows a layered pattern along the height: the lower region forms a low-pressure zone (Cp≈1.2) due to the flow climbing along the mirror surface, while the upper region features a high-pressure band (Cp≈1.9) influenced by backflow or reattachment, exhibiting significant symmetry in the width direction. The pressure distribution for the condition 0–30 is similar to the condition 0–60, with high pressure (Cp≈1.6) in the upper region resulting from flow impingement and low-pressure vortex patches (Cp≈0.6) in the lower region due to flow separation/reattachment, revealing asymmetric vortex structures in the width direction. In the 0–0 (mirror parallel to the ground), the polarity of the pressure distribution reverses. The upper region forms a positive pressure zone (Cp≈0.2) under the influence of a separation bubble, while the lower region is a negative pressure zone (Cp≈− 0.6), reflecting a switch between flow separation and reattachment on the mirror surface.

Existing literature indicates that as the elevation angle decreases from 90°, the magnitude of the mean pressure coefficient decreases, and the pressure distribution on the heliostat mirror exhibits distinct vertical stratification, characterized by a high-pressure zone in the upper region and a low-pressure zone in the lower region29. At intermediate elevation angles, flow separation occurs at the upstream edge of the panel, followed by reattachment downstream, leading to the formation of asymmetric vortex structures, this results in a widthwise asymmetry in the pressure distribution30. Overall, the elevation angle significantly modulates the spatial distribution of the wind pressure by altering the interaction between the incoming flow and the mirror surface.

For the 0–90, the RMS distribution exhibits a distinct asymmetry, with a core of high fluctuation located slightly above the center (Cp,rms≈0.49), indicating intense turbulent pulsations generated as the flow detours upwards after stagnating at the mirror’s center. In the condition 0–60, the intensity of pressure fluctuations increases significantly and displays a stratified pattern along the height: the upper region forms a band of high-intensity fluctuations (Cp,rms≈0.64), while the lower region shows weaker fluctuations, reflecting the combined influence of positive pressure shear and backflow effects experienced by the airflow during its upward climb. Literature indicates that as the elevation angle increases, the flow separation point moves upstream. The consequent enhancement in the instability of the separated shear layer leads to a significant increase in the intensity of the fluctuating wind pressure, resulting in the formation of a high-fluctuation energy band in the upper region of the panel31. Furthermore, in parallel flow, the instability of the leading-edge separated shear layer causes periodic Kármán vortex shedding. Correspondingly, high-frequency, intense fluctuating pressure signals are measured on the upper surface of the panel, while the lower surface maintains a lower turbulence intensity32. Overall, as the elevation angle decreases, the interaction between the airflow and the mirror surface transitions from stagnation and detour to attached shear flow, ultimately leading to a shift in the fluctuating energy from localized concentration to periodic shedding.

Within the building wind engineering community, the pioneering work of Kumar and Stathopoulos on low-rise building roofs first demonstrated that wind pressures in flow separation zones exhibit strongly non-Gaussian behavior characterized by negative skewness (Csk < − 1.0) and excess kurtosis (Cku > 5.0), whereas pressures on windward surfaces remain approximately Gaussian with |Csk|< 0.5 and Cku ≈ 3.0–3.533. This spatial heterogeneity in distribution type was subsequently confirmed by Huang et al. for tall buildings, who reported that Csk values ranged from − 1.8 to 0.3 and Cku from 3.0 to 9.5 across different facade zones, with non-Gaussian characteristics intensifying near edges and corners where flow separation dominates34.

In the solar energy domain specifically, Gong et al.29,30 conducted the first systematic investigation of non-Gaussian wind pressure characteristics on heliostat mirror surfaces through wind tunnel experiments. Their measurements revealed that Csk values on heliostat surfaces typically ranged from − 1.5 to 1.0, while Cku values spanned approximately 2.5 to 8.0, with the most pronounced non-Gaussian behavior occurring at high elevation angles (60°–90°) where vortex shedding from mirror edges generates strongly intermittent pressure fluctuations. Based on these comprehensive datasets, Gong established explicit Gaussian conformity criteria for mirror-type structures: pressure data may be classified as Gaussian when − 0.5 < Csk < 0.5 and Cku ≤ 4.0 simultaneously, with violations of either condition indicating non-Gaussian behavior. More recently, Emes et al.18,35 investigated the turbulence-dependence of heliostat pressure statistics and reported that increasing freestream turbulence intensity amplifies both |Csk| and Cku, particularly on the leeward surface and near mirror edges, suggesting that Gaussian conformity probability is inherently configuration- and environment-dependent. Pfahl et al.36 further demonstrated that aspect ratio variations modulate the spatial extent of non-Gaussian pressure zones on tracker-type structures.

Figure 14 presents the experimental results of this study, in which the skewness and kurtosis coefficients of the measurement points under various conditions were calculated. The figure annotates the Gaussian and non-Gaussian regions based on criteria defined in previous literature. Figure 14 illustrates that at elevation angles below 60°, a subset of measurement locations exhibit pressure distributions conforming to the defined Gaussian criterion. However, as elevation increases to 60°and beyond, the spatial representation of Gaussian-conforming points diminishes substantially, with most sensor positions transgressing the established statistical boundaries. Under these higher inclination conditions, the entire mirror surface manifests pronounced non-Gaussian characteristics. This transition likely stems from aerodynamic interactions occurring when the mirror approaches vertical alignment relative to the ground plane: flow separation at the peripheral regions induces secondary attachment vortices, subsequently expanding the negative pressure zone and amplifying absolute skewness magnitude. Consequently, rigorous validation of the adopted Gaussian distribution thresholds requires complementary analysis to confirm their applicability to the pressure distribution patterns observed across the instrumented mirror surface in this investigation.

Calculation of extreme wind pressure on mirrors

Extreme value distribution algorithm

Conventional approaches for estimating extreme wind pressures on heliostat surfaces have predominantly relied on the peak factor method. However, since the pressure distributions across most regions of the mirror under all operational conditions exhibit pronounced non-Gaussian characteristics, the fundamental assumptions underlying the peak factor technique are violated. Consequently, applying this method introduces substantial inaccuracies in extreme pressure estimations37. To address this limitation, the present study employs the Block Maxima method, the Peak Over Threshold (POT) approach, and a Bayesian framework for extreme value calculation. By conducting a rigorous comparative assessment of the advantages and limitations inherent in these three statistical techniques, this work aims to identify the most robust and suitable approach for quantifying extreme wind pressures on heliostat surfaces.

Block Maxima approach

Block Maxima approach

Block Maxima approach

The Block Maxima approach represents a foundational methodology within extreme value theory. This technique involves partitioning datasets into discrete temporal or spatial intervals and subsequently isolating the peak value within each segment as representative extreme observations. According to extreme value theory principles, these extracted maxima asymptotically converge to the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution38. In wind engineering applications, the parameter set comprising point estimate (Estimate), lower confidence bound (CI-Low), upper confidence bound (CI-High), and interval width (CI-Width) collectively provides comprehensive characterization of extreme value predictions. The Estimate parameter represents the quantile-based point estimate (e.g., 0.995 quantile pressure value), establishing fundamental design benchmarks. The CI-Low and CI-High values demarcate the confidence interval boundaries, providing critical reference limits for assessing extreme loading scenarios under worst-case conditions. The CI-Width metric quantifies estimation uncertainty magnitude, wherein broader intervals necessitate more conservative design approaches.

Based on the comprehensive data presented in Table 10, which details point and interval estimates from Block Maxima, POT, and Bayesian methods across conditions 0–0 to 0–90, key patterns emerge for structural wind resistance design. The point estimates across all methods show a general increasing trend with elevation angle (from ~ 30 Pa at 0–0 to over 100 Pa at 0–90), reflecting the expected growth of wind load as the heliostat facet becomes more exposed. The POT method consistently produces the widest confidence intervals across most conditions, especially at higher elevations (e.g., 69.767 Pa at 0–90), indicating high estimation uncertainty that could lead to overly conservative design. In contrast, the Bayesian method demonstrates superior statistical efficiency by yielding the narrowest confidence intervals for nearly every condition (e.g., 18.86 Pa at 0–90), signifying more precise and reliable extreme value predictions with reduced uncertainty. The Block Maxima method typically provides intermediate CI widths. This pattern suggests that while all three methods converge on similar central estimates for extreme pressure, the Bayesian framework offers a more confident and less uncertain prediction. The widening of CIs, particularly for POT, at higher angles is likely linked to increased flow complexity and non-Gaussian pressure fluctuations as the facet becomes upright, making extreme value extrapolation more challenging. Therefore, for engineering design prioritizing both accuracy and controlled conservatism, the Bayesian method is recommended due to its balanced output of robust point estimates with quantifiably lower uncertainty. Its application, mindful of prior distribution sensitivity, provides a rational basis for transitioning from deterministic to reliability-based wind load specifications.

The transient peaks of non-Gaussian wind pressure can introduce high-frequency disturbances, compromising the precision (required to be ≤ 2.5 mrad) of the heliostat’s dual-axis tracking system. The resulting high-frequency micro-vibrations of the mirror may lead to optical misalignment and reduce concentrating efficiency. Concurrently, vortex shedding associated with non-Gaussian regions poses a risk of flow-induced resonance. This risk is particularly pronounced when the dominant frequency of wind pressure couples with the structure’s natural frequency, amplifying the dynamic response and threatening operational stability. The heavy-tailed distribution of non-Gaussian wind pressure implies more frequent occurrences of high-amplitude cyclic loads, which can accelerate fatigue damage in support structures (e.g., struts and frames) and increase the risk of crack initiation. Furthermore, conventional Gaussian models systematically underestimate extreme wind pressure by approximately 20–30%. Designs based on such models may therefore expose the heliostat to heightened risks of buckling or connection joint failure under extreme wind events (e.g., the 100-year return period wind pressure).

Conclusion

This study systematically investigates the probability characteristics and extreme value prediction of fluctuating wind pressure on heliostat surfaces through wind tunnel tests simulating 130 operational conditions with a 1:50 scaled model.
