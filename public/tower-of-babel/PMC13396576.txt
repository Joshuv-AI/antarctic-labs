Clinical management and outcomes of relapsed NSCLC after chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation: real-world evidence from Germany
Authors: Georg Evers, Franziska Glanemann, Maximilian Webendörfer, Marcel Wiesweg, Dennis Görlich, Daniel C Christoph, Petra Hoffknecht, Heiner Zimmermann, Fabian Acker, Martin Sebastian, Andrea Kerkhoff, Michael Mohr, Georg Lenz, Anna-Carina Hund, Tobias R Overbeck, Nikolaj Frost, Martin Faehling, Wolfgang M Brückl, Cornelia Kropf-Sanchen, Wolfgang Schütte, Frank Griesinger, Lea E Reitnauer, Annalen Bleckmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Despite recent advances in the treatment of locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), most patients relapse after definitive chemoradiotherapy followed by durvalumab consolidation. Due to limited evidence on post-relapse management, no standard treatment approach has been established for this patient population.Objectives:The objective of this study was to evaluate real-world treatment strategies and clinical outcomes in patients with NSCLC who experienced disease recurrence after durvalumab consolidation.Design:This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted across 12 German lung cancer centers.Methods:A total of 145 patients with recurrent NSCLC following chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation were analyzed. Clinical, pathological, and treatment-related data were collected, including smoking status, histology, molecular testing, therapeutic sequences, and survival outcomes. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were estimated and compared across subgroups according to relapse characteristics and post-relapse treatment strategies. Multivariate analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors.Results:The median OS from initial diagnosis was 27.1 months (95% confidence interval (CI): 22.5–31.8). After relapse, median OS was 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.9–14.4), and median PFS was 7.1 months (95% CI: 5.7–8.4). Approximately 70% of patients received systemic therapy after relapse, including platinum-based chemotherapy (18.6%), single-agent chemotherapy (18.6%), immune checkpoint inhibitors with or without chemotherapy (28.3%), and targeted therapy (4.8%). Patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies showed a numerical improvement in OS compared with chemotherapy alone, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Multivariate analysis identified first-line post-relapse treatment, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, and histologic subtype as independent prognostic factors for OS.Conclusion:Patients with relapsed NSCLC after durvalumab consolidation have a poor prognosis and remain a therapeutically underserved population. Clinical outcomes appear to be influenced by performance status, histology, and the choice of post-relapse therapy. Prospective clinical trials are urgently needed to define optimal treatment strategies for these patients.Keywords: definitive chemoradiotherapy, durvalumab, NSCLC, real-world data, relapse

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for more than 1.8 million deaths and approximately 2 million new cases annually. 1 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant subtype, with most patients being diagnosed at an advanced stage. Approximately one third present with stage III disease, which is often characterized by mediastinal lymph node involvement. 2

For patients with non-resectable stage III NSCLC who are ineligible for surgery or perioperative systemic therapy, definitive chemoradiotherapy has long been the standard curative approach. However, long-term survival remains poor due to high relapse rates. Attempts to improve outcomes through treatment intensification strategies, such as induction 3 or consolidation chemotherapy, 4 have not shown a survival benefit in clinical trials.

A significant shift occurred with the PACIFIC trial, which demonstrated for the first time a survival benefit from adding immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) as consolidation therapy. This phase III trial evaluated the anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody durvalumab versus placebo following chemoradiotherapy and established a new standard of care for patients with non-resectable, locally advanced NSCLC. 5 Recent long-term follow-up data confirmed a sustained survival benefit, with 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates of 33.1% and 42.9%, respectively, in the durvalumab group, compared to 19.0% and 33.4% in the placebo groups. 6

More recently, additional consolidation strategies have been explored to further improve outcomes in selected patient populations. In patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC harboring epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, the phase III LAURA trial demonstrated a significant PFS benefit with osimertinib following chemoradiotherapy. 7 In parallel, ongoing trials such as PACIFIC-9 are evaluating combination immunotherapy strategies to enhance the efficacy of durvalumab consolidation. 8 Despite these advances, the majority of patients treated with durvalumab experience a relapse within 2 years of diagnosis, and their prognosis remains limited. 6 Most relapses involve distant metastases, with isolated locoregional recurrences and combined relapse patterns being less frequent. 9 Locoregional recurrence is most commonly located in the radiotherapy field, and curative retreatment is rarely feasible. 9 While local therapies may offer survival benefits in selected patients with locoregional or oligometastatic disease, 10 systematic data on outcomes after relapse following chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation are lacking.

In the PACIFIC trial, patients who experienced a relapse received various systemic treatments, including ICI rechallenge or cytotoxic chemotherapy, while only about 20% underwent further radiotherapy. 6 In clinical practice, these patients are usually managed in accordance with stage IV treatment guidelines, although there are no dedicated data on the efficacy of first-line regimens in the post-durvalumab setting. 11

Targeted therapies are preferred for patients with driver mutations. For others, ICI with or without chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment. However, pivotal trials for stage IV NSCLC excluded patients who had previously received chemoradiotherapy and ICI, leaving a gap in the evidence base for this specific population.

Given that relapse occurred despite prior checkpoint inhibition, the benefit of retreatment with ICI is uncertain. Similarly, it is unclear whether these patients would benefit from platinum-based chemotherapy following prior exposure. Furthermore, data on prognostic factors influencing survival in this unique setting are scarce.

In this multicenter retrospective study, we analyzed real-world data from patients with NSCLC who relapsed after definitive chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation. Our aim was to evaluate survival outcomes and identify clinical and therapeutic factors that influence prognosis, particularly the impact of various first-line treatment strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter, retrospective cohort study included patients treated at 12 German lung cancer centers between April 2018 and November 2023.

Eligible patients were identified retrospectively from institutional electronic medical records and oncology databases at participating centers. Consecutive patients with stage III NSCLC who experienced disease recurrence during or after durvalumab consolidation following definitive chemoradiotherapy were identified and reported by participating centers as part of a multicenter retrospective real-world cohort.

The study specifically focused on patients with initially stage III disease who experienced relapse after chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation. As such, data collection was limited to patients with documented recurrence, and no systematic denominator data were collected for the overall population of patients receiving durvalumab during the study period. Accordingly, the study was not designed to assess the incidence of relapse in the overall treated population.

Patient data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records, while radiological data were obtained from the hospitals’ picture archiving and communication systems. All clinical and treatment data were verified through chart review.

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Number 2020-964-b-S, Ethics Committee of the Medical Association of Westphalia-Lippe and the University of Münster) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Participating centers were: Department of Medicine A, Hematology, Oncology and Pneumology, University Hospital Muenster; Department of Medical Oncology, University Hospital Essen Westdeutsches Tumorzentrum; Department of Medical Oncology, Evangelische Kliniken Essen-Mitte; Department of Thoracic Oncology, Franziskus-Hospital Harderberg—Niels-Stensen-Kliniken GmbH, Georgsmarienhuette; Department of Hematology and Oncology, Pius-Hospital Oldenburg; Goethe University Frankfurt, University Hospital, Department of Medicine 2, Hematology and Oncology; Department of Hematology and Medical Oncology, Universitätsmedizin Goettingen; Department of Infectious Diseases and Pneumology, Universitätsklinik Charité—Campus Virchow Klinikum, Berlin; Department of Cardiology, Angiology and Pneumology, Klinikum Esslingen; Department of Respiratory Medicine, Allergology and Sleep Medicine, Paracelsus Medical University, General Hospital Nuremberg; Pulmonology Department, University Hospital Ulm; Department of Internal Medicine II, Hospital Martha-Maria Halle-Dölau.

Study objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate OS and PFS in patients with NSCLC who experienced disease relapse after definitive chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation. These outcomes were compared with results from previously published clinical trials and real-world datasets.

A secondary objective was to identify prognostic factors associated with OS and PFS by analyzing subgroups defined by clinical and pathological characteristics, as well as first-line treatment strategies after relapse. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of outcome.

Patient selection and data collection

The analysis included 145 patients with relapsed NSCLC after chemoradiotherapy and at least one cycle of durvalumab.

Inclusion criteria were:

(iv) treatment with definitive chemoradiotherapy (with or without induction chemotherapy)

(v) disease recurrence during or after durvalumab consolidation.

Patients were retrospectively identified from institutional databases at participating centers. Centers were instructed to report all consecutive patients with stage III NSCLC who had received definitive chemoradiotherapy followed by durvalumab consolidation and subsequently developed disease recurrence during the study period, reflecting a multicenter real-world registry approach.

This strategy was intended to ensure comprehensive case capture and to minimize potential selection bias. Reported cases were subsequently reviewed for data completeness and consistency. Patients were excluded only in cases of clear ineligibility or insufficient data, including absence of documented disease recurrence, stage IV disease at initial diagnosis, lack of definitive chemoradiotherapy followed by durvalumab consolidation or duplicate records. Exclusion reasons were systematically documented.

The following clinical, pathological, and treatment-related variables were collected: age, sex, initial UICC TNM stage (8th edition), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at durvalumab initiation and at relapse, histological subtype (squamous, non-squamous, or other), PD-L1 expression (tumor proportion score, TPS), smoking status (never, former, or current), use of induction chemotherapy, best response to chemoradiotherapy according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1), 12 interval from chemoradiotherapy to durvalumab initiation, durvalumab dosing regimen, type of first-line therapy after relapse, best response to relapse therapy, number of therapy lines administered, molecular profiling status, time to relapse from durvalumab start, relapse pattern (intrathoracic only vs extrathoracic), durvalumab completion status, reasons for treatment discontinuation (progression, immune-related adverse events, other), cause of death, and vital status at last follow-up.

Molecular profiling data, including oncogenic driver alterations and selected co-mutations (e.g., TP53, STK11, KEAP1), were collected from available molecular pathology reports. Due to incomplete availability and heterogeneity of molecular testing across centers, co-mutations were not included in efficacy or relapse analyses.

Response assessment and follow-up

Tumor response was assessed by local investigators using contrast-enhanced computed tomography and classified according to the RECIST v1.112 as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). OS was defined as the time from initial NSCLC diagnosis to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to documented disease progression or death. Post-relapse OS and PFS were defined from the start of first-line treatment after recurrence to death or progression, respectively.

Given the retrospective real-world design of the study, imaging intervals were determined according to institutional clinical practice and were not standardized.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test, and continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U test. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust significance levels in multiple testing.

OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable Cox regression with forward and backward selection was performed to identify independent prognostic factors.

A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Missing data were not imputed, and analyses were performed based on available cases.

The median age at diagnosis was 64 years (95% CI: 62–65), with a balanced distribution between patients younger and older than 65 years. Male patients comprised approximately two-thirds of the cohort. At diagnosis, 40% were active smokers, 50% were former smokers, and 5.5% had never smoked. The mean smoking history was 39.3 pack-years (95% CI: 35.2–43.5).

Approximately 50% of patients had squamous cell carcinoma, while 40% had adenocarcinoma. The remaining 9.7% (n = 14) were categorized as “other” histologies, as they could not be clearly classified histopathologically as belonging to either the adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma groups. This group was therefore analyzed separately to account for potential biological differences. The population within this category primarily consisted of patients diagnosed with adenosquamous carcinomas. Consistent with EMA approval criteria for durvalumab, the majority of patients had PD-L1 expression ⩾1% (TPS); 40% had a TPS ⩾50%, and PD-L1 status was unknown in ~10% of patients. A small number of patients (n = 5) with PD-L1 expression <1% (TPS) received durvalumab through expanded access programs.

At initial diagnosis, FDG-PET/CT was performed in 131/145 patients (90.3%). Over 90% of the patients were diagnosed with UICC stage III disease (8th edition): 25.5% were stage IIIA, 47.6% stage IIIB, and 19.3% stage IIIC. Five patients (3.4%) were stage IIB but were treated with definitive chemoradiotherapy due to being ineligible for surgery. Staging information was lacking for six patients, although locally advanced disease was assumed.

Induction chemotherapy was administered to 53.1% of patients, most commonly comprising two cycles of platinum-based doublets. These induction regimens were predominantly cisplatin-based, while carboplatin was used in a subset of patients. Paclitaxel-based combinations (including nab-paclitaxel) were the most frequently used partners, followed by pemetrexed, vinorelbine, and etoposide.

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy consisted of platinum-based doublet chemotherapy administered with thoracic radiotherapy. Cisplatin was the predominant platinum agent, most commonly combined with vinorelbine or etoposide, whereas carboplatin was primarily combined with paclitaxel-based regimens. Overall, vinorelbine represented the most frequently used combination partner during concurrent treatment, followed by paclitaxel-based regimens and, less commonly, etoposide or pemetrexed.

Radiotherapy doses ranged from 54 to 66 Gy in 71% of patients. Durvalumab was initiated after a median of 30 days (95% CI: 25–34) following chemoradiotherapy. Best response prior to consolidation was a PR in 71.0% of patients, SD in 18.6%, and a CR in 1.4%.

Approximately 20% of the patients completed 1 year of durvalumab treatment, while 80% discontinued early, primarily due to disease progression (72.9%) or adverse events (19.5%). Of all patients for whom the site of recurrence was documented (n = 139), 42.5% exhibited an intrathoracic recurrence alone, while 57.5% also demonstrated an extrathoracic recurrence at the time of recurrence. Detailed information on imaging at relapse is provided in Table S1.

ECOG performance status at relapse was 0 in 20.7% of patients, 1 in 42.1%, 2 in 20.0%, and 3–4 in 8.9%.

Overall, molecular testing was performed in 91 patients (62.8%), while 49 patients (33.8%) had no documented molecular analysis, and in 5 patients (3.4%), the testing status was unknown. Molecular testing was more frequently performed in patients with adenocarcinoma (80.7%) compared to squamous histology (40.7%).

Information on the timing of molecular testing was available for 87 patients. Among these, 24 patients (27.6%) underwent molecular testing at the time of initial diagnosis (defined as within 90 days), whereas the majority (n = 63, 72.4%) received molecular testing only later during the disease course. The median time from initial diagnosis to molecular testing was 356 days (95% CI: 284.0–400.6).

Among patients who underwent molecular testing, the presence of a driver mutation was significantly associated with earlier relapse (p = 0.042; Table S2). Additionally, Table S3 shows the distribution of driver and co-mutations by histological subtype.

Patients who relapsed within 12 months were not more likely to have PD-L1-negative tumors, and no statistically significant differences were observed for time of relapse (<12 vs ⩾12 months) stratified by PD-L1 expression (⩾50% vs <50% TPS; Table S2).

Treatment after relapse

At relapse, treatment decisions were made in a multidisciplinary setting for the vast majority of patients. First-line therapy was initiated in 82.1% of the patients, while 12.4% received best supportive care (BSC) alone (Table2). Second-, third-, and fourth-line treatments were administered to 48.7%, 37.9%, and 22.7% of patients, respectively. Among treated patients, first-line therapy was started after a median of 31 days post-relapse (95% CI: 25–40). The type of first-line therapy varied: ICI alone (9.7%), ICI + chemotherapy (13.8%), platinum-doublet chemotherapy (18.6%), single-agent chemotherapy (18.6%), targeted therapy (4.8%), local therapy only (11.7%), and combined local/systemic therapy (5.5%). The therapy type was unknown in 7.6%. Details on the specific chemotherapy combinations used after relapse, including platinum-based doublets and accompanying agents, are provided in Table S4.

Local therapies included radiotherapy and/or surgery. Radiotherapy was the predominant local treatment (56%), while surgical approaches were used for a select group of patients (28%). Sixteen percent of patients underwent surgery in combination with radiotherapy; all of these patients underwent cerebral metastasectomy following cerebral radiation.

Of those receiving local approaches, 16% had intrathoracic-only disease, while 84% had extrathoracic involvement. Notably, around 50% of patients who received first-line therapy did not receive subsequent treatment. Second-line therapy was initiated after a median of 5.3 months (95% CI: 4.2–6.8), followed by third-line therapy at 5.6 months (95% CI: 3.6–7.9). A substantial proportion of patients did not receive subsequent therapy after each treatment line, including 51.3% after first-line, 62.1% after second-line, and 77.3% after third-line therapy (Figure 1).

Survival outcomes

At the follow-up at a median of 23.5 months after diagnosis (95% CI: 20.8–28.5), 40 patients were still alive. Most deaths were cancer-related (78.1%), with 12.6% due to non-cancer causes.

Median PFS and OS from diagnosis were 12.2 months (95% CI: 10.6–13.7) and 27.1 months (95% CI: 22.5–31.8), respectively (Figure 2(a) and (b)). Post-relapse median PFS was 7.1 months (95% CI: 5.7–8.4) and OS was 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.9–14.4; Figure 2(c) and (d)). The 1-, 2-, 3-, and 4-year post-relapse OS rates were 50.1%, 27.3%, 16.3%, and 14.0%, respectively.

In a subsequent subgroup analysis, patients receiving chemo-immunotherapy (n = 20) demonstrated significantly improved PFS and OS compared with those treated with chemotherapy alone (n = 54; PFS: p = 0.008; OS: p = 0.045). However, within the ICI-treated cohort, no significant survival difference was observed between chemo-immunotherapy and ICI monotherapy (PFS: p = 0.649; OS: p = 0.411). Likewise, no significant difference in OS or PFS was detected between patients receiving platinum-doublet versus single-agent chemotherapy (PFS: p = 0.819; OS: p = 0.141).

Patients with intrathoracic-only recurrence had a longer median OS (15.7 months, 95% CI: 9.5–22.0) compared to those with other patterns of recurrence (11.7 months, 95% CI: 9.4–14.0), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.223; HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.88–2.69; Figure 3(d)).

No significant differences in OS were observed with regard to PD-L1 TPS, sex, or age. Similarly, baseline stage and smoking status showed no impact on post-relapse OS.

Independent prognostic factors

Multivariable Cox regression analysis identified several independent predictors of OS (Table 4). A lower ECOG performance status at relapse was significantly associated with improved survival (p = 0.05). First-line treatment with ICI (±chemotherapy) was also independently associated with longer OS (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Despite advances in the treatment of locally advanced NSCLC, most patients still experience disease progression after definitive chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation. 6 Recurrence in this setting is associated with a poor prognosis, and there is currently no established standard of care for post-relapse treatment. Current guidelines offer various options, including systemic therapy, surgery, and radiotherapy, but survival data in this specific population remain limited. 11

To our knowledge, this is the largest retrospective analysis of patients with NSCLC relapsing after chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation. We systematically evaluated post-relapse treatment strategies and prognostic factors for OS. Chemotherapy (both platinum-based and single-agent, 18.6% each) was the most common systemic treatment, followed by ICI (28.3%) and targeted therapies (4.8%). Some patients only received local therapies (9.7%) or BSC (12.4%).

Patients treated with ICI-based or targeted therapies showed improved OS compared to those receiving chemotherapy alone. This may reflect biological differences in the post-durvalumab setting: ICI rechallenge after a treatment-free interval may restore anti-tumor immunity, whereas early relapse likely represents primary immune resistance driven by tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental mechanisms, including STK11/KEAP1 alterations and T-cell exclusion.13–17 In contrast, targeted therapies act independently of prior PD-L1 blockade by inhibiting dominant oncogenic drivers.18,19 Our findings, therefore, align with existing biological and clinical evidence suggesting that response to subsequent therapies after prior checkpoint inhibition is heterogeneous and depends on the underlying mechanisms of primary versus acquired resistance.20–23 From a biological perspective, chemo-immunotherapy may modulate the tumor microenvironment and enhance immune responsiveness. 24 However, within the immunotherapy-treated cohort, no significant survival difference was observed between chemo-immunotherapy and ICI monotherapy. Interpretation is limited by the small subgroup size and potential baseline imbalances, particularly performance status and timing of relapse. Prospective studies are required to better define the role of chemotherapy in ICI-based treatment after relapse following chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab consolidation.

Multivariate analysis identified first-line post-relapse treatment, ECOG performance status, and histology as independent prognostic factors. In contrast to findings from the PACIFIC trial, variables such as age, sex, and baseline performance status were not significantly associated with survival in our cohort.

The median OS and PFS observed in our study were comparable to the outcomes reported for metastatic NSCLC prior to the introduction of immunotherapy, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.25,26 Retrospective data from patients treated with pembrolizumab ± chemotherapy after durvalumab showed comparable survival, although the addition of chemotherapy did not significantly improve outcomes. 27 Our findings are largely consistent with a recently published Italian multicenter study, which also analyzed real-world outcomes in patients progressing after durvalumab consolidation. 28 In that cohort, platinum-based chemotherapy represented the predominant subsequent treatment (43%), followed by non-platinum regimens and targeted therapy. Patients who received immunotherapy rechallenge or chemo-immunotherapy achieved the longest median post-relapse PFS and OS, whereas those treated with chemotherapy alone had poorer outcomes. Similar to our observations, the duration of prior durvalumab treatment and the time interval to progression influenced the choice of post-relapse therapy and prognosis. Together, these data support the concept that a selected subgroup of patients, particularly those with late relapse after completing durvalumab, may derive benefit from ICI re-exposure. Prospective validation of this strategy is warranted.

Resistance to checkpoint inhibition may contribute to relapse during or shortly after durvalumab therapy, possibly due to T-cell exhaustion. 29 Previous studies suggest that longer treatment-free intervals may restore sensitivity to ICI. 30 In our cohort, later relapse (>12 months after durvalumab initiation) was associated with a greater benefit from ICI-based treatment. While clinically meaningful, these findings remain exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited size of the subgroup. In contrast, Hasegawa et al. reported no significant PFS difference for ICI versus chemotherapy in late relapses, emphasizing the need for prospective validation. 31

In line with data from PACIFIC and other real-world studies, chemotherapy remained the most frequently used post-relapse treatment.6,31 Interestingly, we observed no OS benefit for platinum-doublet compared to single-agent chemotherapy. This contrasts with earlier findings from the pre-durvalumab era, when platinum rechallenge was beneficial if relapse had occurred more than 6 months after completing treatment. 32 More recent data suggest that the timing of relapse after durvalumab treatment might influence the response to further systemic therapy. 31 In our cohort, however, no significant PFS difference was observed between platinum-based chemotherapy, platinum plus ICI, or single-agent regimens. The role of platinum-based retreatment in this context remains uncertain.

Consistent with previous reports, most patients in our study presented with extrathoracic relapse. 9 This contrasts with the PACIFIC trial, in which progression was predominantly intrathoracic. 33 Emerging retrospective data suggest that patients with low-volume or oligometastatic relapse may benefit from local therapies. 34 Although randomized evidence in the specific post-durvalumab setting remains limited, current clinical guidelines recommend consideration of local ablative treatment in selected patients with oligometastatic disease when technically feasible and clinically appropriate. 35 These recommendations are primarily based on prospective phase II data and expert consensus, as confirmatory evidence from randomized phase III trials is still lacking.36–38

Novel strategies are being explored to further improve outcomes in unresectable stage III NSCLC. In molecularly selected populations, the phase III LAURA trial demonstrated a significant PFS benefit with consolidation osimertinib following chemoradiotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutant disease. 7

In unselected populations, several phase III trials are currently investigating treatment intensification strategies, including durvalumab-based combinations (PACIFIC-8, PACIFIC-9), dual immune checkpoint blockade (SKYSCRAPER-03), and pembrolizumab-based regimens with or without PARP inhibition (KEYLYNK-012).8,39–41

Together, these studies reflect ongoing efforts to improve systemic disease control and reduce relapse risk.

Molecular testing practices in our cohort reflected contemporaneous guideline recommendations and real-world feasibility. During the study period, routine comprehensive molecular profiling at initial diagnosis and systematic re-biopsy at relapse were not consistently implemented, particularly in patients with squamous histology. Consequently, molecular data were predominantly available in adenocarcinoma. The relatively small number of patients eligible for targeted therapies likely reflects both the limited prevalence of actionable alterations and the high proportion of squamous cell carcinoma. This is clinically relevant, as patients harboring oncogenic driver alterations may derive less benefit from durvalumab consolidation.42–44

While comprehensive molecular profiling is now standard in advanced NSCLC and increasingly integrated into earlier disease stages,35,45,46 longitudinal molecular testing at both baseline and relapse is not routinely performed. Current guidelines recommend repeat molecular testing primarily in selected clinical contexts, particularly in patients with actionable driver alterations, where identification of resistance mechanisms may inform subsequent treatment decisions.35,45–47 In patients without known driver mutations, rebiopsy is not routinely performed and is generally considered on an individual basis.

Consistent with this, molecular testing in our cohort was predominantly performed later during the disease course rather than at initial diagnosis (median 356 days), reflecting both the clinical trajectory after chemoradiotherapy and durvalumab and historical practice patterns in this setting. The favorable outcomes observed with targeted therapies are biologically plausible, as these agents act on dominant oncogenic drivers and may provide clinical benefit largely independent of prior PD-(L)1 blockade. In line with current evidence indicating improved outcomes in patients receiving targeted therapies, 7 our data also suggest a trend toward better survival in this subgroup; however, this finding did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of patients receiving targeted therapy in our cohort.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the retrospective multicenter design resulted in a heterogeneous patient population and non-standardized treatment approaches. In addition, the sample size necessitated grouping of treatment categories for statistical analyses.

Molecular data were not available for all patients. This reflects temporal changes in clinical practice, with increasing implementation of comprehensive molecular profiling during the study period, rather than unsystematic data collection. While molecular alterations and selected co-mutations (e.g., TP53, STK11, KEAP1) were available in a subset of patients, analyses of their impact on outcomes were limited by small subgroup sizes and incomplete data. In addition, longitudinal molecular profiling at diagnosis and relapse was not uniformly available.

Clinical variables such as tumor burden and local therapies were not consistently documented across all centers.

Recurrence patterns were categorized pragmatically as intrathoracic or extrathoracic, reflecting routine clinical documentation rather than standardized assessment of metastatic burden, which precluded more granular analyses.

Prospective studies with standardized data collection and serial molecular assessment are warranted to further refine these findings.

Conclusion

Patients suffering from relapse following durvalumab consolidation represent a clinically diverse group with a poor prognosis. Our data reveal wide variability in post-relapse treatment strategies, emphasizing the absence of standardized care in this context. Performance status, time to relapse, and initial post-relapse therapy were found to influence outcomes. ICI-based therapy may be particularly beneficial in cases of relapse. Prospective clinical trials are urgently needed to define optimal systemic and local treatment strategies, integrate molecular diagnostics, and ultimately personalize treatment for this patient group.
