Abstract
Some firms export their own products directly, others rely on intermediary firms to export on their behalf, and still others both export their own products and intermediate exports for other producers. To explain this heterogeneity, we develop a model in which firms differ along two dimensions: manufacturing capability and commercial capability. Manufacturing capability lowers the marginal cost of producing a variety, whereas commercial capability lowers the variable cost of reaching foreign customers. Different combinations of these capabilities generate the different types of firms observed in export markets: direct exporters, indirect exporters, pure intermediaries, and hybrid firms. The model predicts that commercially capable intermediaries are matched with more manufacturing-capable producers, and that more commercially capable intermediaries export a broader set of varieties. We provide suggestive evidence for these predictions using Spanish firm-level export data.

1
Introduction

How firms export is a central question in international trade. The empirical literature documents that some producers export directly to foreign markets, while others rely on intermediary firms to export on their behalf
(
Bernard et al., 2010
;
Crozet et al., 2013
;
Bernard et al., 2015
;
Blum et al., 2018
;
Akerman, 2018
)
. Other firms both export their own products directly and act as intermediaries for other producers
(
Abreha et al., 2013
;
Lo Turco and Maggioni, 2013
;
Di Nino, 2015
;
Bernard et al., 2019
;
Arnarson, 2020
;
Erbahar and Rebeyrol, 2023
;
Endoh et al., 2025
)
. Taken together, these patterns reveal four types of firms involved in export activity: direct exporters, indirect exporters, intermediaries, and hybrid firms that combine direct exporting with export intermediation.

This paper develops a simple model that endogenously generates these four types of exporters. Firms differ along two dimensions: manufacturing capability, denoted by
\varphi
, and commercial capability, denoted by
\gamma
. Commercial capability should be understood as foreign-market commercial capability: the ability to identify foreign customers, access distribution channels, negotiate with buyers, and convert commercial opportunities into export sales. Manufacturing capability captures the technical and organizational ability to transform inputs into a final good, while commercial capability captures the ability to commercialize that good abroad. The final sale of a variety in a foreign market requires both production and commercial activities.

The key assumption is that manufacturing capability affects the marginal cost of production, whereas commercial capability affects the variable cost of exporting. A firm with high manufacturing capability can produce at low marginal cost. A firm with high commercial capability can reach foreign customers at low variable cost. Firms with sufficiently strong combinations of both capabilities export their own products directly. Firms with high manufacturing capability but weak commercial capability may instead export indirectly through an intermediary. Firms with high commercial capability but weak manufacturing capability specialize as intermediaries. Finally, firms with high manufacturing and commercial capabilities become hybrid firms: they export their own products directly and also intermediate exports for other producers.

The model treats export intermediation as an exclusive-contract assignment market. Each variety can be intermediated by at most one intermediary in a destination. Intermediaries provide a homogeneous export service, but differ in commercial capability and therefore in their variable cost of reaching foreign buyers. In addition, each intermediary pays a fixed export cost to operate in a foreign market. This fixed cost is paid once for the whole portfolio of varieties exported by the intermediary, not separately for each variety. The model also preserves a per-agreement fixed cost, which is incurred when an intermediary establishes a relationship with a producer. Finally, intermediaries face convex scope costs that increase with the number of varieties handled. These assumptions prevent a winner-take-all outcome and generate a well-defined assignment of varieties across intermediaries.

The model yields two main predictions. First, intermediaries differ in commercial capability and, as a result, in the margins they charge. More capable intermediaries charge lower margins, which gives them a competitive advantage in attracting indirect exporters. The most capable intermediary will therefore be matched with the indirect exporter with the highest manufacturing capability, since that variety can be sold at a lower price and will generate higher export sales. The model therefore predicts positive assortative matching between intermediary commercial capability and producer manufacturing capability. Second, more commercially capable intermediaries export a broader portfolio of varieties.

We assess these predictions using Spanish firm-level export data. Since we do not observe the producers matched with each intermediary, we follow the logic of
Crozet et al., 2013
and use export unit values as an indirect test. In the model, high-commercial-capability intermediaries charge lower export prices because they face lower variable export costs and because they are matched with lower-cost producers. Therefore, within a product–destination pair, export unit values should be negatively related to the intermediary’s commercial capability. We proxy commercial capability with labor productivity and find evidence consistent with this prediction. We also find that more productive intermediaries export a larger number of products.

This paper contributes to three strands of literature. First, it contributes to the literature on export modes and trade intermediaries. Previous work has explained why some manufacturers export directly whereas others rely on intermediaries
(
Ahn et al., 2011
;
Felbermayr and Jung, 2011
;
Crozet et al., 2013
;
Fujii et al., 2017
)
, how trade technologies differ between intermediaries and manufacturers
(
Akerman, 2018
)
, and why some exporters carry along products manufactured by other firms
(
Bernard et al., 2019
;
Eckel and Riezman, 2020
)
. We contribute by developing a parsimonious framework in which two-dimensional firm heterogeneity and capability-based assignment generate direct exporters, indirect exporters, pure intermediaries, and hybrid firms within a unified model.

Second, the paper contributes to the literature on assortative matching in international trade.
Benguria, 2021
document positive assortative matching in productivity between Colombian exporters and U.S. importers, and
Sugita et al., 2023
find positive assortative matching in capability between Mexican exporters and U.S. importers in textiles and apparel. We complement this evidence by studying matching between intermediaries and producers among firms that export indirectly.

Third, the paper contributes to the literature on multi-product exporters.
Bernard et al., 2007
;
Bernard et al., 2011
show that more productive firms export more products, and
Bernard et al., 2019
document a positive relationship between manufacturers’ productivity and the number of products exported through carry-along trade. We extend this logic to specialized intermediaries and show that intermediaries’ productivity is positively correlated with the number of products they export.

2
An open-economy model of direct exporters, intermediaries, and hybrid firms

We develop a partial-equilibrium model in which firms choose how to serve a foreign market, taking aggregate variables such as wages, expenditure, and the foreign-market price index as given. The model builds on the heterogeneous-firm framework with export costs developed by
Melitz, 2003
, incorporates key features of export-intermediation models
(
Ahn et al., 2011
;
Akerman, 2018
)
, and draws on the two-source firm-level heterogeneity framework of
Hallak and Sivadasan, 2013
. The novelty is to distinguish manufacturing capability from commercial capability and to model export intermediation as an exclusive-contract, capability-based assignment market with portfolio-level fixed export costs and convex scope costs.

Our model generates the four types of exporters observed in practice: direct exporters, indirect exporters, intermediary exporters, and hybrid firms. In a closed-economy setting, the model would generate four analogous categories, defined with respect to the domestic market: direct domestic sellers, indirect domestic sellers, domestic intermediaries, and hybrid firms that both sell their own products and intermediate products manufactured by others. The model for the closed-economy scenario is developed in Appendix
A
.

2.1
Preferences and demand in the foreign market

Consumers in the foreign country have access to a set
\Omega_{x}
of varieties of a final good. Preferences are represented by a constant elasticity of substitution (CES) utility function:

U_{x}=\left[\int_{\omega\in\Omega_{x}}q_{x}(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}},

(1)

where
q_{x}(\omega)
is consumption of variety
\omega
in the foreign market and
\sigma>1
is the elasticity of substitution across varieties.

The foreign-market price index is

P_{x}=\left[\int_{\omega\in\Omega_{x}}p_{x}(\omega)^{1-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}},

(2)

where
p_{x}(\omega)
is the consumer price of variety
\omega
in the foreign market. Foreign expenditure on the final good is denoted by
R_{x}
. Demand for variety
\omega
is therefore

q_{x}(\omega)=R_{x}P_{x}^{\sigma-1}p_{x}(\omega)^{-\sigma},

(3)

and revenue is

r_{x}(\omega)=R_{x}\left(\frac{p_{x}(\omega)}{P_{x}}\right)^{1-\sigma}.

(4)

2.2
Capabilities and direct exporting

Firms are heterogeneous along two dimensions. The first is manufacturing capability, denoted
\varphi
. The second is commercial capability, denoted
\gamma
. Throughout the model, commercial capability refers to foreign-market commercial capability: the ability to identify foreign customers, access distribution channels, negotiate with buyers, and convert commercial opportunities into export sales. Capabilities are drawn from a joint distribution
v(\varphi,\gamma)
with support
[0,\bar{\varphi}]\times[0,\bar{\gamma}]
.

Manufacturing capability determines the marginal cost of production. Labor is the only factor of production and the wage is normalized to one. A firm with manufacturing capability
\varphi_{i}
produces one unit of its variety at marginal cost

c_{i}=\frac{1}{\varphi_{i}}.

(5)

Commercial capability determines the variable cost of serving the foreign market. If firm
i
exports its own variety directly, its variable export cost is

t_{ix}=\tau\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}\right),\qquad\tau>1.

(6)

The parameter
\tau
captures the standard iceberg or distance-related component of exporting. The term
1+1/\gamma_{i}
captures the commercial resources required to find buyers, establish commercial relationships, and sell in the foreign market. A larger
\gamma_{i}
lowers this variable export cost. The multiplicative structure between
\tau
and
\left(1+1/\gamma_{i}\right)
captures the idea that the commercial resources required to establish and maintain foreign-market relationships are greater in more distant destinations.

Our specification is consistent with models in which variable trade costs are not only destination-specific but also firm-specific
(
Lawless and Whelan, 2014
;
Alessandria et al., 2021
)
. For example,
Lawless and Whelan, 2014
allow variable trade costs to contain market-specific, firm-specific, and firm–market idiosyncratic components. In our framework,
\tau
captures the destination-level component of exporting costs, while
(1+1/\gamma_{i})
captures the firm-specific component associated with foreign-market commercial capability.

Direct exporting therefore involves marginal cost
t_{ix}/\varphi_{i}
. Under CES demand, the profit-maximizing export price is a constant markup over marginal cost:

p_{ix}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}\frac{t_{ix}}{\varphi_{i}}.

(7)

A firm that exports directly also pays a fixed export cost
f_{x}
. Direct-export profits are

\pi_{ix}^{D}=\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\frac{\varphi_{i}P_{x}}{t_{ix}}\right]^{\sigma-1}-f_{x}.

(8)

Direct exporting is profitable whenever
\pi_{ix}^{D}\geq 0
.

Equation (
8
) shows that direct-export profits increase with both manufacturing capability and commercial capability. A higher
\varphi_{i}
reduces the production cost of the variety. A higher
\gamma_{i}
reduces the variable cost of serving foreign customers.

The zero-profit locus for direct exporting is obtained by setting
\pi_{ix}^{D}=0
. Using (
6
), the locus can be written as

1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}=K_{x}\varphi_{i},

(9)

where

K_{x}\equiv\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\frac{P_{x}}{\tau}\left(\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma f_{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}.

(10)

Solving for
\gamma_{i}
yields

\gamma_{x}^{D}(\varphi_{i})=\frac{1}{K_{x}\varphi_{i}-1},\qquad\varphi_{i}>\frac{1}{K_{x}}.

(11)

As shown in Figure
1
, the direct-export cutoff is downward-sloping in
(\varphi,\gamma)
space: a firm with higher manufacturing capability can profitably export directly with lower commercial capability, and a firm with higher commercial capability can profitably export directly with lower manufacturing capability.

\varphi
measures manufacturing capability,
\gamma
measures commercial capability, and
\pi_{x}^{D}
denotes profits from direct exporting.

2.3
Indirect exporting through an intermediary

A manufacturer that does not export directly may instead export indirectly through an intermediary. This option is especially relevant for manufacturers with strong manufacturing capability but weak commercial capability. The key difference between direct and indirect exporting is that, in the latter case, the manufacturer produces the variety but does not perform foreign-market commercial activities. Those activities are performed by the intermediary.

Consider manufacturer
i
with manufacturing capability
\varphi_{i}
and intermediary
I
with commercial capability
\gamma_{I}
. If manufacturer
i
sells through intermediary
I
, the manufacturer charges a wholesale price
p_{ix}^{w}
. Since the manufacturer does not perform foreign-market commercial activities, its marginal cost is simply
1/\varphi_{i}
. As shown in Appendix
B
, the optimal wholesale price is

p_{ix}^{w}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}\frac{1}{\varphi_{i}}.

(12)

Intermediary
I
purchases the variety at price
p_{ix}^{w}
and performs the commercial activities required to sell it abroad. Its variable export cost is

t_{Ix}=\tau\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{I}}\right).

(13)

Given CES demand, intermediary
I
charges a constant markup over the acquisition cost inclusive of commercial costs. The intermediary-specific markup is

\alpha_{I}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}t_{Ix},

(14)

so the consumer price of variety
i
exported through intermediary
I
is

p_{Ix}(i)=\alpha_{I}p_{ix}^{w}.

(15)

Appendix
C
derives (
14
). Since
\gamma_{I}
lowers
t_{Ix}
, commercially capable intermediaries charge lower prices for any given wholesale price.

The manufacturer must pay a fixed cost
f_{i}
to establish an intermediation agreement. The manufacturer’s profits from exporting indirectly through intermediary
I
are

\pi_{ix}^{M}(\varphi_{i};I)=\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\alpha_{I}^{-\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\varphi_{i}P_{x}\right]^{\sigma-1}-f_{i}.

(16)

This expression does not depend on the manufacturer’s own commercial capability because all foreign-market commercial activities are performed by the intermediary.

For a given intermediary
I
, the cutoff manufacturing capability that makes indirect exporting profitable is

\varphi_{ix,I}^{*}=\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma-1)P_{x}}\left(\frac{\sigma f_{i}}{R_{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}\alpha_{I}^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}.

(17)

Since
\alpha_{I}
is decreasing in
\gamma_{I}
, a more commercially capable intermediary allows producers with lower manufacturing capability to export indirectly.

A producer chooses indirect exporting over direct exporting whenever indirect-export profits exceed direct-export profits:

\pi_{ix}^{M}(\varphi_{i};I)\geq\pi_{ix}^{D}(\varphi_{i},\gamma_{i}).

(18)

Using (
8
) and (
16
), this condition can be written as

\displaystyle f_{x}-f_{i}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\frac{\varphi_{i}P_{x}}{t_{ix}}\right]^{\sigma-1}-\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\alpha_{I}^{-\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\varphi_{i}P_{x}\right]^{\sigma-1}.

(19)

The left-hand side captures the fixed-cost saving from exporting indirectly rather than directly. The right-hand side captures the reduction in operating profits associated with relying on the intermediary’s double-markup pricing structure. Indirect exporting is especially attractive for producers with high manufacturing capability but low commercial capability, because these firms can produce efficiently but face high variable costs when selling abroad on their own.

2.4
The intermediary sector

Firms operating as intermediaries provide a homogeneous commercial service. Conditional on having access to a variety, they market and distribute it to final consumers in the foreign market. Intermediaries differ only in commercial capability. We assume exclusivity at the destination–variety level: each variety can be exported by at most one intermediary in a given destination. Competition across intermediaries therefore takes the form of competition for contracts with producers, not simultaneous retailing of the same variety.

The profit that intermediary
I
obtains from exporting variety
\omega
before the portfolio-level fixed export cost and before scope costs is derived in Appendix
D
. It is given by

m_{Ix}(\omega)=\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\alpha_{I}\sigma}{P_{x}(\sigma-1)\varphi(\omega)}\right)^{1-\sigma}.

(20)

Each intermediation relationship also entails a fixed agreement cost, which, for simplicity, we assume is equal to the cost paid by the indirect exporter,
f_{i}
. Hence the net contribution of variety
\omega
to intermediary
I
before scope costs is

\pi_{Ix}(\omega)=m_{Ix}(\omega)-f_{i}.

(21)

The intermediary pays a fixed export cost
F_{X}
to operate in the foreign market. A key assumption is that this fixed export cost is paid once for the entire portfolio of varieties exported by the intermediary. It is not paid separately for each variety. Thus,
F_{X}
affects entry into export intermediation but does not directly affect the marginal decision to add one more variety.

Let
n_{Ix}\geq 0
denote the number of varieties allocated to intermediary
I
in the foreign market, and let
\Omega_{Ix}
denote the set of varieties assigned to it. To discipline product scope and rule out a winner-take-all allocation, we introduce a convex scope cost, as in
Akerman, 2018
:

\Psi_{x}(n_{Ix})=\frac{\kappa_{x}}{1+\eta_{x}}n_{Ix}^{1+\eta_{x}},\qquad\kappa_{x}>0,\ \eta_{x}>0.

(22)

This cost captures the managerial, coordination, warehousing, and organizational costs of handling a broader export portfolio.

Total export profits of intermediary
I
are

\Pi_{Ix}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{Ix}}\left[m_{Ix}(\omega)-f_{i}\right]-F_{X}-\Psi_{x}(n_{Ix}).

(23)

An intermediary is active in export intermediation if
\Pi_{Ix}\geq 0
for its optimal portfolio. Because
F_{X}
is paid once for the whole portfolio, it shifts the participation condition but does not enter the marginal condition determining the optimal number of varieties conditional on entry.

Ordering varieties assigned to intermediary
I
from highest to lowest manufacturing capability, the optimal product scope satisfies

m_{Ix}\big(\omega(n_{Ix})\big)-f_{i}=\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(n_{Ix})=\kappa_{x}n_{Ix}^{\eta_{x}}.

(24)

Equation (
24
) states that an intermediary expands its portfolio until the net contribution of the marginal variety equals the marginal scope cost.

Competition across intermediaries determines the allocation of varieties. Since contracts are exclusive, a variety is assigned to the intermediary that yields the highest contribution net of the marginal scope cost. For adjacent intermediaries
I
and
J
ordered by commercial capability, the cutoff variety
\bar{\omega}
separating their portfolios satisfies

m_{Ix}(\bar{\omega})-f_{i}-\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(n_{Ix})=m_{Jx}(\bar{\omega})-f_{i}-\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(n_{Jx}).

(25)

Because the per-agreement cost
f_{i}
is common across intermediaries, it cancels out in the assignment condition:

m_{Ix}(\bar{\omega})-\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(n_{Ix})=m_{Jx}(\bar{\omega})-\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(n_{Jx}).

(26)

Because intermediary profits are increasing in commercial capability, the model also implies an entry cutoff for export intermediation. For a given set of potential varieties, there exists a cutoff commercial capability
\gamma_{Ix}^{*}
such that intermediary
I
enters the export-intermediation sector if and only if
\gamma_{I}\geq\gamma_{Ix}^{*}
. This cutoff is implicitly defined by the zero-profit condition

\max_{\Omega_{Ix}}\left\{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{Ix}}\left[m_{Ix}(\omega)-f_{i}\right]-F_{X}-\Psi_{x}(n_{Ix})\right\}=0.

(27)

Unlike the direct-export cutoff, the intermediary cutoff depends on the portfolio of varieties the intermediary can attract. More commercially capable intermediaries obtain higher operating profits from each variety and can therefore cover the portfolio-level fixed export cost with a broader and more profitable set of varieties.

2.5
Four types of exporters

The model generates four types of exporters.

First,
direct exporters
are firms that export their own variety directly. These firms have sufficiently high manufacturing and commercial capabilities to cover the fixed export cost and profitably serve foreign customers without relying on an intermediary. Formally, they satisfy
\pi_{ix}^{D}\geq 0
and direct exporting is weakly preferred to indirect exporting.

Second,
indirect exporters
are producers that export through an intermediary. These firms have sufficiently high manufacturing capability to generate positive surplus abroad, but their own commercial capability is not high enough to make direct exporting the most profitable option. They export indirectly if there exists an intermediary
I
such that
\pi_{ix}^{M}(\varphi_{i};I)\geq 0
and indirect exporting is preferred to direct exporting.

Third,
pure intermediaries
are firms that do not export their own variety directly but profitably export varieties produced by other firms. They have sufficiently high commercial capability to operate as intermediaries and cover the portfolio-level export fixed cost
F_{X}
, the per-agreement costs
f_{i}
, and the convex scope costs of managing a set of varieties.

Fourth,
hybrid firms
both export their own variety directly and intermediate exports for other producers. These firms combine high manufacturing capability with high commercial capability. They can profitably sell their own variety abroad and, at the same time, use their commercial capability to export additional varieties sourced from other producers.

positions the four categories of exporters in the manufacturing–commercial capability space. The figure also includes
\Pi_{Hx}
, which denotes the foreign-market profits of a hybrid firm from selling its own variety directly and intermediating varieties produced by other manufacturers.

2.6
Predictions

The model delivers two testable predictions.

Prediction 1.
Intermediaries and producers sort positively by capability. More commercially capable intermediaries are matched with more manufacturing-capable producers.

The intuition is that intermediaries differ in their commercial capability, which translates into different margins. More capable intermediaries can charge lower margins and thus become more attractive partners for indirect exporters. As a result, the most capable intermediary is matched with the indirect exporter with the highest manufacturing capability, whose variety can be sold at a lower price and generate larger export sales.

Consider two intermediaries,
I
and
J
, with
\gamma_{I}>\gamma_{J}
. Then

t_{Ix}<t_{Jx}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{I}<\alpha_{J}.

(28)

From Appendix
D
, operating profits from variety
\omega
are

m_{Kx}(\omega)=\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\alpha_{K}\sigma}{P_{x}(\sigma-1)\varphi(\omega)}\right)^{1-\sigma},\qquad K\in\{I,J\}.

(29)

This can be written as

m_{Kx}(\omega)=B\alpha_{K}^{1-\sigma}\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-1},

(30)

where

B\equiv\frac{R_{x}}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\sigma}{P_{x}(\sigma-1)}\right)^{1-\sigma}>0.

(31)

Because
\sigma>1
and
\alpha_{I}<\alpha_{J}
, we have

m_{Ix}(\omega)>m_{Jx}(\omega)\qquad\forall\omega.

(32)

Moreover, the profit advantage of intermediary
I
over intermediary
J
is

m_{Ix}(\omega)-m_{Jx}(\omega)=B\left(\alpha_{I}^{1-\sigma}-\alpha_{J}^{1-\sigma}\right)\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-1}.

(33)

Differentiating with respect to
\varphi
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\left[m_{Ix}(\omega)-m_{Jx}(\omega)\right]=B(\sigma-1)\left(\alpha_{I}^{1-\sigma}-\alpha_{J}^{1-\sigma}\right)\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-2}>0.

(34)

Thus, the more commercially capable intermediary has a larger profit advantage for varieties produced by more manufacturing-capable producers.

A variety
\omega
is assigned to intermediary
I
rather than
J
whenever

m_{Ix}(\omega)-f_{i}-\Delta\Psi_{I}(n_{I}+1)\geq m_{Jx}(\omega)-f_{i}-\Delta\Psi_{J}(n_{J}+1),

(35)

where
\Delta\Psi_{K}(n_{K}+1)
is the incremental scope cost of adding one more variety to intermediary
K
’s portfolio. The common per-agreement fixed cost cancels out:

m_{Ix}(\omega)-\Delta\Psi_{I}(n_{I}+1)\geq m_{Jx}(\omega)-\Delta\Psi_{J}(n_{J}+1).

(36)

Because the left-hand-side advantage of intermediary
I
is increasing in
\varphi(\omega)
, there exists a cutoff manufacturing capability above which varieties are assigned to the more commercially capable intermediary. Repeating this logic across adjacent intermediaries ordered by commercial capability yields a monotone assignment: more commercially capable intermediaries are matched with more manufacturing-capable producers. This establishes positive assortative matching.

Prediction 2.
More commercially capable intermediaries export a broader set of varieties. Lower variable export costs raise the net contribution of each potential variety and make it profitable to expand the portfolio until the marginal variety again equals the marginal scope cost.

The optimal scope of intermediary
I
satisfies

m_{Ix}\big(\varphi(n_{I})\big)-f_{i}=\kappa_{x}n_{I}^{\eta_{x}},

(37)

where
\varphi(n)
is the manufacturing capability of the marginal variety when the intermediary exports
n
varieties. Adding varieties worsens the marginal sourced variety, so

\varphi^{\prime}(n)<0.

(38)

Using (
30
), the scope condition can be written as

B\alpha_{I}^{1-\sigma}\varphi(n_{I})^{\sigma-1}-f_{i}-\kappa_{x}n_{I}^{\eta_{x}}=0.

(39)

Define

F(n,\alpha_{I})=B\alpha_{I}^{1-\sigma}\varphi(n)^{\sigma-1}-f_{i}-\kappa_{x}n^{\eta_{x}}.

(40)

The optimal scope solves
F(n_{I},\alpha_{I})=0
. Holding
n
fixed,

F_{\alpha}=B(1-\sigma)\alpha_{I}^{-\sigma}\varphi(n)^{\sigma-1}<0.

(41)

Moreover,

F_{n}=B\alpha_{I}^{1-\sigma}(\sigma-1)\varphi(n)^{\sigma-2}\varphi^{\prime}(n)-\kappa_{x}\eta_{x}n^{\eta_{x}-1}<0.

(42)

By the Implicit Function Theorem,

\frac{dn_{I}}{d\alpha_{I}}=-\frac{F_{\alpha}}{F_{n}}<0.

(43)

Since
\alpha_{I}
is decreasing in commercial capability
\gamma_{I}
, it follows that

\frac{dn_{I}}{d\gamma_{I}}>0.

(44)

Thus, more commercially capable intermediaries export a broader set of varieties.

These predictions guide the empirical analysis in the next section.

3
Consistency between the model predictions and empirical evidence

This section tests the model’s novel implications using Spanish firm-level export data. The first prediction is positive assortative matching between intermediary commercial capability and producer manufacturing capability. We do not observe the specific producers matched with each intermediary. However, following the logic of
Crozet et al., 2013
, we use export unit values as an indirect test. In the model, a more commercially capable intermediary charges a lower export price because it faces a lower variable export cost. In addition, because of positive assortative matching, it is assigned varieties produced by more manufacturing-capable producers, which have lower wholesale prices. Both mechanisms imply that, within a product–destination pair, export unit values should be negatively related to intermediary commercial capability.

This empirical test should therefore be interpreted as evidence consistent with the model’s mechanism rather than as a pure test of matching alone. A negative relationship between intermediary capability and unit values may reflect both the direct effect of commercial capability on variable export costs and the indirect effect operating through matching with more manufacturing-capable producers.

We estimate the following regression:

\ln uv_{ijkt}=\beta\ln\text{Labor productivity}_{i,t-1}+\delta_{i}+\delta_{jkt}+\varepsilon_{ijkt},

(45)

where
uv_{ijkt}
denotes the unit value, defined as export value divided by export quantity in kilograms, for product
k
shipped by intermediary
i
to destination
j
in year
t
. The term
\delta_{i}
is a firm fixed effect that absorbs all time-invariant firm characteristics that may affect export unit values. The term
\delta_{jkt}
denotes destination
\times
product
\times

\varepsilon_{ijkt}
is an idiosyncratic error term. We lag labor productivity by one year to mitigate simultaneity between pricing outcomes and measured productivity.

Our identification strategy exploits within-intermediary variation in labor productivity and tests whether increases in productivity are associated with a lower export unit value, conditional on destination–product–year characteristics.

Data on the universe of Spanish firms’ export transactions in goods were obtained from the Customs and Excise Department of the Spanish Tax Agency (AEAT). The dataset reports a firm identifier, the export destination, the product’s eight-digit Combined Nomenclature (CN8) code, the monthly value of each shipment, and the corresponding quantities. We use data for the period 2011–2023 and aggregate transactions to the annual level for each firm–destination–product triad. Because Customs records do not include firms’ economic activity, we merge the Customs data with Bureau van Dijk’s SABI database to recover sectoral information and construct measures of labor productivity, following the correspondence procedure in
de Lucio et al., 2018
. We restrict the sample to firms with at least 10 employees.

Our dataset does not allow us to identify hybrid firms. We therefore restrict the sample of intermediary exporters to firms whose 2-digit NACE Rev. 2 code is 46 or 47, corresponding to wholesale and retail activities, excluding sales of motor vehicles and motorcycles. We treat these firms as the empirical counterpart of the pure intermediary group described above.

Unit value data are prone to outliers, which can bias the empirical analysis. Because outliers are more likely to arise in small transactions, before aggregation we drop shipments with an export value below 1,500 euros. We also winsorize unit values at the 5th and 95th percentiles within each firm–destination–product triad. Finally, we restrict the analysis to the top 50 destinations of Spanish exports over 2011–2023; these destinations account for 94% of total Spanish export value during the period. Our final sample comprises 7,307 exporting intermediary firms.

4
Conclusion

There are four types of exporters: manufacturers that sell their own products directly in foreign markets, manufacturers that export indirectly through intermediaries, specialized intermediaries that export goods produced by other firms, and hybrid firms that export their own products directly while also intermediating exports for other producers.

This paper develops a partial-equilibrium open-economy model in which firms are heterogeneous along two dimensions: manufacturing capability and commercial capability. Manufacturing capability lowers the marginal cost of production, whereas commercial capability lowers the variable cost of serving foreign customers. Combined with fixed export costs, per-agreement intermediation costs, portfolio-level fixed export costs for intermediaries, and convex scope costs, these two capabilities endogenously generate direct exporters, indirect exporters, pure intermediaries, and hybrid firms.

A central feature of the model is that export intermediaries pay a fixed export cost only once for the whole portfolio of varieties they export. This assumption makes the intermediary’s export fixed cost an entry cost rather than a per-variety cost. Product-level allocation is instead governed by variable export costs, per-agreement costs, and convex scope costs. Because commercial capability reduces variable export costs, more commercially capable intermediaries have a comparative advantage in handling varieties produced by more manufacturing-capable producers. The model therefore predicts positive assortative matching between intermediaries and producers, as well as a positive relationship between intermediary commercial capability and product scope.

Using Spanish firm-level export data, we provide suggestive evidence consistent with these predictions. More productive intermediaries charge lower unit values within product–destination pairs and export a broader set of products. These results are consistent with a model in which commercial capability shapes how firms export and how intermediaries are matched with producers in foreign markets.

Appendix A
A closed-economy version of the model

This appendix develops the closed-economy counterpart of the model presented in Section
2
. The purpose is to show that the logic of the model does not depend on the existence of international trade. The same two-dimensional heterogeneity that generates direct exporters, indirect exporters, intermediary exporters, and hybrid firms in an open economy also generates four analogous types of firms in a closed economy: direct domestic sellers, indirect domestic sellers, domestic intermediaries, and domestic hybrid firms.

The key difference with respect to the open-economy model is that firms no longer need to cross a national border. Therefore, there is no iceberg trade cost
\tau
. However, selling a variety in the domestic market still requires commercial activities: identifying customers, accessing distribution channels, negotiating with buyers, and converting potential demand into sales. In this appendix, commercial capability refers to domestic-market commercial capability and is denoted by
\gamma_{i}^{d}
. This capability may be correlated with, but need not be identical to, foreign-market commercial capability.

A.1  Preferences and domestic demand

Consumers have access to a set
\Omega_{d}
of varieties of a final good. Preferences are represented by a CES utility function:

U_{d}=\left[\int_{\omega\in\Omega_{d}}q_{d}(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}},

(A.1)

where
q_{d}(\omega)
is domestic consumption of variety
\omega
and
\sigma>1
is the elasticity of substitution across varieties.

The domestic price index is

P_{d}=\left[\int_{\omega\in\Omega_{d}}p_{d}(\omega)^{1-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}},

(A.2)

where
p_{d}(\omega)
is the consumer price of variety
\omega
in the domestic market. Domestic expenditure on the final good is denoted by
R_{d}
. Demand for variety
\omega
is therefore

q_{d}(\omega)=R_{d}P_{d}^{\sigma-1}p_{d}(\omega)^{-\sigma},

(A.3)

and revenue is

r_{d}(\omega)=R_{d}\left(\frac{p_{d}(\omega)}{P_{d}}\right)^{1-\sigma}.

(A.4)

A.2  Capabilities and direct domestic selling

Firms differ along two dimensions: manufacturing capability,
\varphi_{i}
, and domestic-market commercial capability,
\gamma_{i}^{d}
. Manufacturing capability determines the marginal cost of production. Labor is the only factor of production and the wage is normalized to one. A firm with manufacturing capability
\varphi_{i}
produces one unit of its variety at marginal cost

c_{i}=\frac{1}{\varphi_{i}}.

(A.5)

Commercial capability determines the variable cost of reaching domestic customers. If firm
i
sells its own variety directly in the domestic market, its domestic commercial cost is

t_{id}=1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}^{d}}.

(A.6)

This expression is the closed-economy analogue of the open-economy cost
\tau(1+1/\gamma_{i})
. The absence of
\tau
reflects the fact that there is no international trade cost. The term
1+1/\gamma_{i}^{d}
captures the resources required to commercialize the variety in the domestic market.

Direct domestic selling therefore involves marginal cost
t_{id}/\varphi_{i}
. Under CES demand, the profit-maximizing domestic price is

p_{id}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}\frac{t_{id}}{\varphi_{i}}.

(A.7)

A firm that sells its own product directly in the domestic market pays a fixed commercialization cost
f_{d}
. Its profits from direct domestic selling are

\pi_{i}^{D,d}=\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\frac{\varphi_{i}P_{d}}{t_{id}}\right]^{\sigma-1}-f_{d}.

(A.8)

Direct domestic selling is profitable whenever
\pi_{i}^{D,d}\geq 0
.

The zero-profit locus for direct domestic selling is obtained by setting
\pi_{i}^{D,d}=0
. Using (
A.6
), the locus can be written as

1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}^{d}}=K_{d}\varphi_{i},

(A.9)

where

K_{d}\equiv\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}P_{d}\left(\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma f_{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}.

(A.10)

Solving for
\gamma_{i}^{d}
yields

\gamma_{d}^{D}(\varphi_{i})=\frac{1}{K_{d}\varphi_{i}-1},\qquad\varphi_{i}>\frac{1}{K_{d}}.

(A.11)

As in the open-economy model, the cutoff is downward-sloping in
(\varphi,\gamma^{d})
space. A firm with higher manufacturing capability can profitably sell directly in the domestic market with lower commercial capability, while a firm with higher domestic commercial capability can do so with lower manufacturing capability.

A.3  Indirect domestic selling through an intermediary

A manufacturer can also sell its product in the domestic market through an intermediary. In that case, the manufacturer produces the variety, while the intermediary performs the domestic commercial activities required to reach final customers.

Consider manufacturer
i
with manufacturing capability
\varphi_{i}
and domestic intermediary
I
with commercial capability
\gamma_{I}^{d}
. If manufacturer
i
sells through intermediary
I
, the manufacturer charges a wholesale price
p_{i}^{w}
. Since the manufacturer does not perform domestic commercial activities, its marginal cost is simply
1/\varphi_{i}
. The manufacturer’s optimal wholesale price is

p_{i}^{w}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}\frac{1}{\varphi_{i}}.

(A.12)

Intermediary
I
purchases the variety at price
p_{i}^{w}
and performs the commercial activities required to sell it in the domestic market. Its variable domestic commercial cost is

t_{Id}=1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{I}^{d}}.

(A.13)

Given CES demand, the intermediary charges a constant markup over the acquisition cost inclusive of domestic commercial costs. The intermediary-specific markup is

\alpha_{I}^{d}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}t_{Id},

(A.14)

so the consumer price of variety
i
when sold through intermediary
I
is

p_{Id}(i)=\alpha_{I}^{d}p_{i}^{w}.

(A.15)

Since
\gamma_{I}^{d}
lowers
t_{Id}
, more commercially capable domestic intermediaries charge lower prices for any given wholesale price.

The manufacturer must pay a fixed cost
f_{a,d}
to establish an intermediation agreement. The manufacturer’s profits from selling indirectly through intermediary
I
are

\pi_{i}^{M,d}(\varphi_{i};I)=\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{-\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\varphi_{i}P_{d}\right]^{\sigma-1}-f_{a,d}.

(A.16)

This expression does not depend on the manufacturer’s own domestic commercial capability because all commercial activities are performed by the intermediary.

For a given intermediary
I
, the cutoff manufacturing capability that makes indirect domestic selling profitable is

\varphi_{id,I}^{*}=\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma-1)P_{d}}\left(\frac{\sigma f_{a,d}}{R_{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}.

(A.17)

Since
\alpha_{I}^{d}
is decreasing in
\gamma_{I}^{d}
, more commercially capable domestic intermediaries allow producers with lower manufacturing capability to sell indirectly in the domestic market.

A producer chooses indirect domestic selling over direct domestic selling whenever

\pi_{i}^{M,d}(\varphi_{i};I)\geq\pi_{i}^{D,d}(\varphi_{i},\gamma_{i}^{d}).

(A.18)

Using (
A.8
) and (
A.16
), this condition can be written as

\displaystyle f_{d}-f_{a,d}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\frac{\varphi_{i}P_{d}}{t_{id}}\right]^{\sigma-1}-\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{-\sigma}\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\varphi_{i}P_{d}\right]^{\sigma-1}.

(A.19)

The left-hand side captures the fixed-cost saving from using an intermediary rather than selling directly. The right-hand side captures the reduction in operating profits associated with the intermediary’s double-markup pricing structure. Indirect domestic selling is especially attractive for producers with high manufacturing capability but low domestic commercial capability.

A.4  The domestic intermediary sector

Domestic intermediaries provide a homogeneous commercial service. Conditional on having access to a variety, they market and distribute it to final consumers in the domestic market. Intermediaries differ only in domestic commercial capability. As in the open-economy model, we assume exclusivity at the variety level: each variety can be sold by at most one domestic intermediary. Competition across intermediaries therefore takes the form of competition for contracts with producers, not simultaneous retailing of the same variety.

The operating profit that intermediary
I
obtains from selling variety
\omega
before portfolio-level fixed costs and scope costs is

m_{Id}(\omega)=\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\alpha_{I}^{d}\sigma}{P_{d}(\sigma-1)\varphi(\omega)}\right)^{1-\sigma}.

(A.20)

Each intermediation relationship also entails a fixed agreement cost
f_{a,d}
. Hence the net contribution of variety
\omega
to intermediary
I
before scope costs is

\pi_{Id}(\omega)=m_{Id}(\omega)-f_{a,d}.

(A.21)

The domestic intermediary pays a fixed cost
F_{d}
to operate in the domestic market. This fixed cost is paid once for the entire portfolio of varieties handled by the intermediary. It is not paid separately for each variety. Thus,
F_{d}
affects entry into domestic intermediation but does not directly affect the marginal decision to add one more variety.

Let
n_{Id}\geq 0
denote the number of varieties allocated to intermediary
I
in the domestic market, and let
\Omega_{Id}
denote the set of varieties assigned to it. As in the open-economy model, we introduce a convex scope cost:

\Psi_{d}(n_{Id})=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{1+\eta_{d}}n_{Id}^{1+\eta_{d}},\qquad\kappa_{d}>0,\ \eta_{d}>0.

(A.22)

This cost captures the managerial, coordination, warehousing, and organizational costs of handling a broader domestic portfolio.

Total profits of domestic intermediary
I
are

\Pi_{Id}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{Id}}\left[m_{Id}(\omega)-f_{a,d}\right]-F_{d}-\Psi_{d}(n_{Id}).

(A.23)

A firm is active as a domestic intermediary if
\Pi_{Id}\geq 0
for its optimal portfolio.

Ordering varieties assigned to intermediary
I
from highest to lowest manufacturing capability, the optimal product scope satisfies

m_{Id}\big(\omega(n_{Id})\big)-f_{a,d}=\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(n_{Id})=\kappa_{d}n_{Id}^{\eta_{d}}.

(A.24)

Equation (
A.24
) states that a domestic intermediary expands its portfolio until the net contribution of the marginal variety equals the marginal scope cost.

Competition across intermediaries determines the allocation of varieties. For adjacent intermediaries
I
and
J
ordered by domestic commercial capability, the cutoff variety
\bar{\omega}
separating their portfolios satisfies

m_{Id}(\bar{\omega})-f_{a,d}-\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(n_{Id})=m_{Jd}(\bar{\omega})-f_{a,d}-\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(n_{Jd}).

(A.25)

Since the per-agreement cost
f_{a,d}
is common across intermediaries, it cancels out:

m_{Id}(\bar{\omega})-\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(n_{Id})=m_{Jd}(\bar{\omega})-\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(n_{Jd}).

(A.26)

The assignment logic is the same as in the open-economy model. Consider two domestic intermediaries,
I
and
J
, with
\gamma_{I}^{d}>\gamma_{J}^{d}
. Then
t_{Id}<t_{Jd}
and
\alpha_{I}^{d}<\alpha_{J}^{d}
. Equation (
A.20
) can be written as

m_{Kd}(\omega)=B_{d}(\alpha_{K}^{d})^{1-\sigma}\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-1},\qquad K\in\{I,J\},

(A.27)

where

B_{d}\equiv\frac{R_{d}}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\sigma}{P_{d}(\sigma-1)}\right)^{1-\sigma}>0.

(A.28)

Because
\sigma>1
and
\alpha_{I}^{d}<\alpha_{J}^{d}
, intermediary
I
obtains higher operating profits from any given variety than intermediary
J
. Moreover, the profit advantage of intermediary
I
over intermediary
J
is

m_{Id}(\omega)-m_{Jd}(\omega)=B_{d}\left[(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{1-\sigma}-(\alpha_{J}^{d})^{1-\sigma}\right]\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-1}.

(A.29)

Differentiating with respect to
\varphi
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\left[m_{Id}(\omega)-m_{Jd}(\omega)\right]=B_{d}(\sigma-1)\left[(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{1-\sigma}-(\alpha_{J}^{d})^{1-\sigma}\right]\varphi(\omega)^{\sigma-2}>0.

(A.30)

Thus, the profit advantage of the more commercially capable domestic intermediary is larger for varieties produced by more manufacturing-capable producers. This single-crossing property generates positive assortative matching between domestic commercial capability and manufacturing capability.

The model also implies that more commercially capable domestic intermediaries handle more varieties. To see this, write the scope condition as

B_{d}(\alpha_{I}^{d})^{1-\sigma}\varphi(n_{Id})^{\sigma-1}-f_{a,d}-\kappa_{d}n_{Id}^{\eta_{d}}=0,

(A.31)

where
\varphi(n)
denotes the manufacturing capability of the marginal variety when the intermediary handles
n
varieties. Since adding varieties lowers the manufacturing capability of the marginal sourced variety,
\varphi^{\prime}(n)<0
. A fall in
\alpha_{I}^{d}
, induced by a higher
\gamma_{I}^{d}
, raises the contribution of every potential variety. The intermediary therefore expands its portfolio until the marginal variety again satisfies (
A.24
). Hence,

\frac{dn_{Id}}{d\gamma_{I}^{d}}>0.

(A.32)

A.5  Firm types in the closed economy

The closed-economy version of the model generates four types of firms.

First,
direct domestic sellers
are firms that sell their own variety directly in the domestic market. These firms have sufficiently high manufacturing and domestic commercial capabilities to cover the fixed cost of domestic commercialization and profitably reach consumers without relying on an intermediary. Formally, they satisfy
\pi_{i}^{D,d}\geq 0
and direct domestic selling is weakly preferred to indirect domestic selling.

Second,
indirect domestic sellers
are producers that sell their variety in the domestic market through an intermediary. These firms have sufficiently high manufacturing capability to generate positive surplus, but their domestic commercial capability is not high enough to make direct domestic selling the most profitable option. They sell indirectly if there exists an intermediary
I
such that
\pi_{i}^{M,d}(\varphi_{i};I)\geq 0
and indirect domestic selling is preferred to direct domestic selling.

Third,
domestic intermediaries
are firms that do not sell their own manufactured variety directly but profitably commercialize varieties produced by other firms. They have sufficiently high domestic commercial capability to cover the portfolio-level fixed cost
F_{d}
, the per-agreement costs
f_{a,d}
, and the convex scope costs of managing a set of varieties.

Fourth,
domestic hybrid firms
both sell their own variety directly and intermediate varieties produced by other firms. These firms combine high manufacturing capability with high domestic commercial capability. They can profitably sell their own variety in the domestic market and, at the same time, use their commercial capability to commercialize additional varieties sourced from other producers.

Therefore, the four-way taxonomy generated by the open-economy model is not specific to export markets. It is a more general implication of two-dimensional firm heterogeneity. Manufacturing capability determines how efficiently a firm can produce a variety, while commercial capability determines how efficiently it can reach customers. Depending on the combination of these capabilities and on the relevant fixed and scope costs, firms choose whether to sell their own product directly, rely on an intermediary, specialize in intermediation, or combine own-product selling with intermediation.
