Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Developing sustainable solutions for single-use packaging is an important objective to combat
the environmental crisis of plastics pollution. Most embodiments of cellulose-based packaging
materials, including CellophaneTM, are completely biodegradable in both terrestrial and marine
environments. However, petroleum-derived alternatives offer some performance advantages for
metrics such as moisture barriers and mechanical properties.

 1

This project leverages molecular dynamics simulation to investigate how molecular
modifications to cellulose-based polymer assemblies impact their material properties. An
important performance criterion for the modified materials was to retain biodegradability; thus,
modifications by naturally occurring, biodegradable additives were the focus of this study.

Specifically, we developed models with xylan and lignin of varying monomeric compositions
into the cellulose matrix. The mechanical properties were investigated by performing stress-
strain simulations, and the water barrier and hydrophobicity were investigated by simulating the
water contact angle.

Our findings indicate that the incorporation of xylan into the cellulose matrix tends to increase
the mechanical properties with an optimal loading of ~27 wt%. We also predict that orienting the
nanoscale directionality of the xylan chains such that they are perpendicular to the cellulose
fibrils will dramatically increase mechanical strength. In contrast, the incorporation of lignin
tends to weaken the composite at all loadings investigated.

Simulations of water contact angle predicted that coating polymers on the surface of the cellulose
assembly creates a more hydrophobic surface than incorporating them throughout the matrix. Of
the coatings investigated, lignin resulted in the most hydrophobic surface, followed by pectin and
keratin, which both imparted modest increases in hydrophobicity.

Future experimental work done by Futamura will focus on designing material prototypes to
capitalize on the predictions of performance enhancement obtained from molecular modeling.
While substantial progress was made by the simulations performed in this project, there still
exists a vast parameter space that we were unable to investigate, including branching, functional
group decoration, and degree of polymerization of polymer additives. However, the methods
developed in this initial investigation will facilitate more rapid evaluation of the impact of
molecular characteristics on the performance of biopolymer composite materials and thereby
accelerate future materials discovery efforts in this area.

Introduction

The world is in an existential situation relative to climate destabilization and land/marine
accumulation of single-use packaging. Single-use packaging is predominantly made from mixed
materials that are made from fossil-derived plastics, which have no end-of-life other than landfill
or, if collected, could be potentially subsidized for repurposing or chemical recycling. Another
alternative is to leverage nature’s stored energy and sequestered carbon found in plant biomass.
Utilization of intact biopolymers for packaging applications is inherently energy and carbon-
efficient because the energy of production is contributed by sunlight and the carbon comes
directly from CO₂. These elegant, naturally mass-produced materials can be derivatized into new
materials that are exceptionally well-suited for single-use packaging. However, accessing these
materials to meet constantly evolving societal needs requires rapid innovation in materials design
and manufacturing technologies that must be accelerated by high-performance computing.

 3

To feed a population that is approaching 9 billion people while avoiding catastrophic levels of
plastic pollution, it is imperative to develop packaging materials that are 100% compatible with
the circular economy. Materials with photosynthetic beginning-of-life and an earth-digestible
end-of-life minimize energy inputs throughout the lifecycle because they leverage naturally
occurring materials synthesis processes (solar-energy-driven CO₂ sequestration) and do not
require energy-intensive collection, sorting, and chemical recycling or repurposing to complete
the lifecycle. One category of materials is made from dissolving-grade cellulose pulp called
viscose that can be then cast into packaging films. This technology was originally called
CellophaneTM. Cellophane was the first transparent packaging film used in the world after its
invention by DuPont at the turn of the 20th century.

There is a rapidly heating “sustainability” froth amongst brands and retailers. Increasing equally
at an alarming rate is the collective demand for earth-digestible/compostable single-use
packaging options. These factors have created a manufacturing opportunity that will be
transformational in both economic and environmental impact. In fact, the global market of
biodegradable plastics is showing a compound annual growth rate of 11.5% and will reach
approximately $3.5 billion by 2022.1 Broadening the application space of cellophane-based
packaging can only be achieved by the development of increased properties such as tensile
strength, impact resistance, tear resistance, and puncture resistance while maintaining its
advantage of being industrial and home compostable as well as soil, anaerobic, and marine-
degradable.

Advances in molecular modeling of carbohydrate materials over the last decade will be
leveraged to accelerate modernization of cellulose-based material solutions to meet application
demands. By modeling the structure-property relationships of accessible cellulose polymer
structures, we will identify new materials to serve the heightened market demand more
effectively than complicated and time-consuming trial-and-error. The figures of merit, such as
bulk mechanical properties, thermal properties, and barrier properties, are emergent properties
that arise from mesoscale (or larger) assemblies of many polymer chains. Simulating molecular
systems of this size is only achievable with high-performance computing resources. The future of
compostable, single use, flexible packaging critically depends on the ability to develop higher-
performing materials.

In this project, we systematically investigated new synthetically accessible cellulose derivatives
to identify production targets with performance advantages. Molecular models of cellulose
derivatives were constructed based on input from Futamura and existing characterization data
from the literature. Initially, validation of the force field was performed by computing the
viscosity of cellulose and viscose solutions over a range of degree of polymerization (DP) and
comparing them to experimental measurements. Following validation, a variety of biopolymer
composites containing cellulose, xylan, and lignin at different compositions were constructed.
The ultimate tensile strength and elastic modulus of each system were determined using MD
simulations of mechanical deformation. Next, the impact of additives and surface coatings on the
hydrophobicity of the film was investigated by simulating the water contact angle.

Mechanical Deformation Simulations

Two DP 40 cellulose-II fibrils roughly 50 x 50 x 100 Å were built in CHARMM and modified by adding
differing amounts of either xylan or lignin polymers to analyze the effect of xylan or lignin modification
on mechanical failure and modulus. A total of fifteen systems were built: One system consisting of two
cellulose-II fibrils without any modifications; four systems with varying amounts of xylan placed
randomly in a parallel orientation to the axial direction of the cellulose fibrils referred to as x4, x8, x16,
and x32; four systems with varying amounts of xylan chains placed randomly perpendicular to the
orientation of the axial direction of the cellulose fibrils referred to as xp4, xp8, xp12, and xp16; and six
systems with varying amounts of lignin chains placed randomly parallel to the orientation of the
cellulose-II fibrils, referred to as l6, l12, l24, and l48. The lignin in each system was built with an S:G
ratio of 13:7, and an additional two systems with 24 lignin chains and S:G ratios of 0:1 and 9:1 were built
to examine the effect of S:G ratio on the fracture behavior. Each system is shown in figures 3 and 4.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed on each system in CHARMM to examine fracture
behavior by restraining the C1 atoms on one of the two cellulose-II fibrils with a 5 kcal/(mol*Å2) force
constant harmonic restraint and applying a force on the C1 atoms in the other crystal in the x-direction
away from the restrained crystal. The lignin or xylan in the system was unrestrained, and no explicit force
was applied to them in the simulations. The simulations were run using periodic boundary conditions
with a gap in the x-direction to allow the cellulose-II fibrils to separate an ample distance.

The force at failure was estimated for each of the fifteen systems by running a binary search algorithm
written in Python consisting of short CHARMM fracture simulations with different forces applied to the
unrestrained cellulose-II crystal. At the end of the short dynamics, the algorithm determined if the data of
the distance between the two cellulose-II fibrils was indicative of fracture behavior modeled by a second-
order polynomial function or non-fracture behavior modeled by a square root function. The algorithm
assigned a penalty value for each model, and the force was either increased or decreased depending on the
penalty value. The minimum force required to elicit a fracture response from each system was recorded as
the force at failure.

After the force at failure for each of the fifteen systems was ascertained, that number was divided by ten
to get the force increment, and mechanical stress simulations as described above were run on each
system. The simulations were run with an applied force of zero to the force at failure, incrementing the
force by the force increment for a total of eleven simulations for each of the fifteen systems. Molecular
dynamics simulations were run at each applied force until the separation distance between the cellulose-II
fibrils remained constant, which occurred within 1 ns. The applied force and separation distance were
averaged over the last 0.1 ns for each simulation run and used to calculate the stress and strain according
to Equations 3 and 4, respectively. The stress and strain for each system were plotted, and the slope of the
linear region was calculated, giving the modulus for each system.

Water Contact Angle Simulations

Water contact angle (WCA) simulations provide an estimate for hydrophobicity, a key surface
performance metric for plastics and especially plastic films. The crystal structure of cellulose II was
presented by Kolpak and Blackwell (Macromolecules 1976) and further refined by Langan et al.
(Biomacromolecules 2001). The cellulose II unit cell from Langan et al. is shown in Figure 5A. It has
been well-established by multiple studies that the predominant exposed crystal faces of cellulose II are the
110, 1-10, and 020 (e.g. Yamane et al. Polymer J. 2006). For clarity, we note that the 020 face is
indistinguishable from the 010 (all these faces are annotated in Figure 5A).

  Figure 7. A) the unit cell and major crystallographic faces of cellulose II. B) An example of the
 starting configuration for water contact angle simulations. Shown in “sticks” representation is a
 cellulose II crystal with water droplet (blue spheres) on top of the 110 face.
We prepared surfaces in silico for each exposed face and computed the WCA via MD
simulations. The starting configuration (Figure 1B) has a contact angle of approximately 90° and
generally relaxes to the equilibrium WCA after ~15 ns. Experimentally, this number has been
reported to range from 12° (Yamane et al. Polymer J 2006) to 60° (Faria-Tischer et al. Mat Sci
Eng C 2015). It is generally regarded as a highly hydrophilic film (Yamane et al. Polymer J
2006, Trentin et al. Biomacromolecules 2021), suggesting a low contact angle, but this can be
influenced by the roughness of the surface, among other factors. In addition, cellulose II surfaces
have been estimated to be ~50-60% crystalline (Aulin et al. Langmuir 2009).

Each surface we build has these common characteristics: DP = 20, 4-6 layers “deep” (~20-30 Å),
and 300-400 Å wide. An “infinite” half cylinder of water is placed on top of the surface (axis
going in and out of the page in Figure 5B). In each case, 30 ns of MD simulation is sufficient to
estimate the equilibrium water contact angle. The results for the three pristine major crystal faces
of cellulose II are shown in Figure 6.

Discussion

Mechanical Property Modification

The lack of clear correlation to the number of polymer chains added in the systems with lignin or
xylan placed parallel to the axial direction of the cellulose-II fibrils is likely because the chains
are randomly placed, with greater or fewer chains between the cellulose-II fibrils irrespective of
the total number of chains added. The strongest interaction in the system is between the two
cellulose-II fibrils, so systems with more xylan or lignin in between the cellulose fibrils may
require a lower force for fracture. Likewise, systems with less xylan or lignin in between the
cellulose fibrils may require a higher force for fracture. This likely explains the high force at
fracture for the system with 6 lignin chains added (Lignin x6 on the graph). This behavior was
addressed in the perpendicular xylan systems by placing 8 xylan chains in between the cellulose-
II fibrils for each system, and then an additional 4, 8, 12, or 16 chains were placed randomly.
The trends with increasing xylan chains are much clearer for the perpendicular xylan systems,
suggesting that the location of the modification within the cellulose-II matrix has a large impact
on the mechanical behavior of the assembly.

 13

Comparing the perpendicular xylan x4 and perpendicular xylan x16 systems suggests that
increasing the number of perpendicular xylan chains increases the modulus; however, the values
in between do not follow this trend. This may be due to the 8 chains of xylan that lie between the
cellulose fibrils in all the perpendicular xylan systems, perhaps dominating the effect on the
modulus in these systems. These results suggest that not only the type of polymer but also the
orientation and location of polymers added to the cellulose-II matrix have a large effect on the
mechanical properties of the composite material.

In general, the results may be interpreted as follows:

 • In general, disruption of cellulose-cellulose interactions weakens the assembly.
 • Addition of xylan can improve mechanical strength until ~30 wt %, after which
 additional hemicellulose begins to weaken the assembly.
 • Xylan applied perpendicular to the axis of the cellulose fibrils is particularly effective.
 • Lignin weakens the tensile strength but may act as a plasticizer to enhance resilience to
 compression and bending.

Surface Wettability

From Figure 8, each crystal face of cellulose II is seen to display relatively low hydrophobicity,
though none exhibit complete wetting. In a recent computational study of various crystal forms
of cellulose, these three faces were all determined to have WCA < 5°, which was determined to
be the limit of sensitivity for the method (Trentin et al. Biomacromolecules 2021). We suspect a
key difference may be in the choice of water model; whereas Trentin et al. utilize the TIP3P
water model, we use SPC/E, which has been shown to more closely match the experimental
water/air surface tension (Chen and Smith J. Chem. Phys. 2007), which is a key parameter in
determining the WCA.

Cellulose-lignin composites have recently been shown experimentally to increase the
hydrophobicity of cellulose I (Wang et al. Small 2021). Here, we examine the effect of coating
the cellulose II surface with lignin. As shown in Figure 9, a “pure” lignin surface (in which water
molecules do not “see” the cellulose surface, but only lignin) increases the hydrophobicity of the
cellulose II surface, as do lower loadings of lignin, to a lesser extent.

Implementation

The workflow was divided into three tasks with the following descriptions and milestones:

Task 1: Computational Method Verification for Cellophane

This task will provide the initial verification that the MD simulations predict accurate properties
for base material, cellophane. Specifically, models of molecular assemblies for cellophane will
be constructed based on information from Futamura and additional details from the literature as
needed. Material properties will be computed and compared to experimental measurements. If
property predictions differ from experimental values by greater than 15%, simulation methods,
including run time, system size, and macromolecular architecture, will be modified until
satisfactory values are obtained.

Task 1 Milestone: Validation of methods by prediction of materials properties for cellophane
within 15% of experimental measurements. This milestone was completed by verifying the
predictions of viscosity against experimental measurements.

Task 2: Property Prediction for Candidate Materials

This task will construct in silico models of molecular assemblies of novel cellulose-derived
polymers. At least 10 different polymers will be investigated based on input from Futamura
regarding which modifications are synthetically achievable and likely to increase performance
metrics. Simulations will be performed to calculate the thermal, mechanical, and barrier
properties of each novel polymer. Guidance regarding which molecular modifications are
synthetically achievable and economically feasible will be provided to NREL from Futamura.
Predictions of material performance metrics will be provided by NREL to Futamura.

Task 2 Milestones: Predict materials properties of at least 10 novel modified cellophane
polymers.

This milestone was completed by investigating the mechanical properties of 14 novel polymer
systems and 4 variations of novel surface coatings.

Task 3: Material Candidate Evaluation and Down-Selection

This task will evaluate the required supply chain and potential value proposition of the top
performing material candidates as predicted by Task 2. The analysis will be primarily conducted
by Futamura, but NREL will assist as needed. The top-performing material candidates will be
predicted by NREL and provided to Futamura. Futamura will use the results of the evaluation to
determine which material candidate(s) will move forward for commercial production.

Task 3 Milestone: Downselect to the top 3 material candidates based on supply chain and TEA.
We were not able to complete the TEA analysis required for this milestone within the lifetime of
the project due to bandwidth constraints; however, the top-performing formulations were
delivered to Futamura, and they will be evaluated in the coming months.

Issues or Challenges

A major source of uncertainty was the lack of nanostructural characterization of cellophane
films. Native cellulose (in plant cell walls, cotton fibers, etc.) exists in bundles ranging from
several dozen to hundreds of chains; however, the extent of bundling in regenerated cellulose
films is largely unknown. To confront this issue, NREL performed SEM and AFM imaging of
cellophane films provided by Futamura. An exemplary SEM image collected at NREL is shown
in Figure 9. These preliminary characterizations revealed nanoscale agglomerates of cellulose
that appeared to form a layered structure, with each layer consisting of agglomerates of
nanoscale bundles. The observations led us to construct models consisting of cellulose bundles
roughly 5 nm in diameter such that the inter-bundle adhesive properties could be examined.

Impact

Company Impact:

The continued development of both the fundamental understanding of our existing process and
products and indeed new processes and products is core to Futamura’s R&D strategy and forms
part of the business’s strategic 5-year plan. We actively seek to engage with external partners
who can help deliver against this core program. The project with NREL, in our view, has been
both valuable and successful in several ways.

1) Being the first (we hope of many) interactions with the team at NREL, we have
established a solid working relationship, allowing all parties to gain a greater understanding of
the capabilities and knowledge base of each other. While measurable outcomes of a project are
primary, it is also of value that this has been a collaborative project that has been enjoyable to
work on.

 16

2) When considering the first target of our collaborative projects, namely “increased
fundamental understanding,” I believe this project has demonstratively succeeded. Working
together, the NREL team has successfully built a model of how the cellulose structure is formed
within our film casting process and appears to be able to accurately predict the behavior of our
films. Understanding this process provides a platform for modifying the said process.
Additionally, the NREL team has successfully produced some of the best SEM microscopy
images of the film structure we have ever seen. One interesting facet of this has been the
discovery that the different films we produce have a range of structures, whereas we have always
believed that one simple structure existed across the film range.

3) When considering the second target of our collaborative projects, namely “developing
new processes and products,” there is also demonstrative success. The NREL team, using their
modeling techniques developed in the first part of the project, was able to begin to model a range
of proposed modifications to the filmic structures, mainly focusing on different additives. One
key outcome of this was some interesting films modeled using the widely available Xylan as an
additive. The key learning gained from this work was the understanding that the conformation of
the xylan in the cellulose network was key to deriving these significant differences in mechanical
properties in the final film. Whilst this has not yet been converted into a workable real-world
process, this work provides a potential springboard for the future development of films with
improved mechanical properties. To date, the use of cellulose films is limited to certain markets
due to the limit of their mechanical properties. However, the use of such a compostable product
would be desirable outside of this limited space. For example, one core product range that may
be accessible would-be ready meal tray lidding. Currently, due to the easy tearing nature of
cellulose film and the need for high-speed roll-to-roll processing in this sector, cellulose film is
not used in this application. However, this sector has been identified around the world as a
suitable space for compostable filmic solutions, as plastic films used here cannot be easily
recycled due to food contamination. The improvements shown in this modeling work suggest
this may be a potential solution to that problem if the structure can be replicated in the real
world.

The NREL team has performed some high-level estimates of potential energy savings impact
from the development of biodegradable films displacing non-biodegradable alternatives in the
circular economy by comparing the energy requirements between two end-of-life scenarios: 1) A
generic plastic film is recycled at a material recovery facility (MRF), and 2) an identical quantity
of biodegradable film that is industrially composted. This comparison was chosen to isolate the
impact from an important attribute of regenerated cellulose films—namely, that it has an end-of-
life composting (or, more generally, biodegradation) option that is not available to conventional
plastic films. We identified literature estimates of energy requirements (or offsets, in the case of

The difference between these two values is equal to about 2.9 MJ / kg, which is the estimated

National and International Impact:

During this project, Futamura faced a significant issue in the UK market. The British government
were in the process of introducing a “UK Plastics Tax,” with a 30% levy placed on all plastic
packaging that could not be recycled. However, sadly our compostable, cellulose, and clearly not
plastic films was included in the list of taxable products. Futamura was able to lobby the
government to address this situation. The NREL team was able to produce a detailed and
compelling report in a very short space of time as to why our films are not considered plastic nor
modified cellulose. This report played a vital role in what concluded as a successful challenge to
the UK government’s position, and finally cellulose films were removed from the taxable list.
This help was very much appreciated across the business.

Future Work

NREL intends to publish the simulation methods and findings, while Futamura plans to fabricate
several of the Xylan-additive films to verify trends predicted by the simulations. Also, although
not an intended outcome of this project, the NREL’s imaging capabilities proved to be of

Laboratory Principal Investigator (PI) Experience Feedback

(1) Why were you interested in working with the HPC4EI Program?

This opportunity was a perfect fit for our team to apply our unique capabilities in biopolymer
simulation to provide direct assistance to an industry partner.

(2) Was your overall experience as part of the HPC4EnergyInnovation Program positive,
neutral, or negative? Why?

Extremely positive. It was very rewarding to see how our somewhat fundamental insights
provided by molecular modeling were used by Futamura to understand previously confusing
experimental results and provide new insights to product design. It was also fortuitous that we
were able to help their company avoid the UK plastics tax as described in the international
impact section.
