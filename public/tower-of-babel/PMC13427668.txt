Dip-pen nanolithography-fabricated Bi and Hg meta-chemical surface sensors for ultra-sensitive detection of heavy metals in water
Authors: Zorik Shamish, Pankaj Kumar, Haya Kornweitz, Moshe Zohar, Dror Shamir, Raz Jelinek, Ariela Burg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe toxicity of heavy metals in water sources requires the use of sensitive devices capable of detecting levels in the parts-per-billion (ppb) range. In this study, meta-chemical surface (MCS-Bi and MCS-Hg) sensors were developed using dip-pen nanolithography (DPN), which enhanced the detection of Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II) at ppb levels, due to DPN’s unique ability to control the surface-to-volume (S/V) ratio of patterned nanoclusters. The density functional theory results indicate that the binding strengths between the heavy metals and the active species (Hg and Bi) are comparable. The difference in the S/V ratio affects the detection limit (0.26 ± 0.04 and 0.52 ± 0.02 ppb for MCS-Bi and MCS-Hg, respectively). This finding highlights the relevance of MCS sensors to the environmental field, their enhanced detection capabilities for toxic materials, and the reduced use of hazardous materials like mercury. The results provide a concrete methodology for reducing the toxicity of analytical devices without compromising sensitivity.

Introduction

Access to clean and fresh water is a fundamental requirement for human well being. However, due to rapid industrialization, groundwater can become contaminated with heavy metals and non-biodegradable waste, which, upon prolonged exposure, can lead to behavioral disorders and neurodevelopmental issues in humans. At present, the excessively high levels of heavy metals, particularly Pb(II) and Cd(II), in fresh water pose a significant concern. For instance, according to the World Health Organization (WHO),1 the permissible limits of Pb(II) and Cd(II) in drinking water are 10 and 3 ppb, respectively. Standard detection techniques such as benchtop inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and ion chromatography (IC) offer high accuracy and sensitivity in the parts per million–parts per billion (ppm-ppb) range. Although recent engineering breakthroughs have introduced portable sampling devices and field-deployable configurations coupled with ICP-MS,2,3,4 these downscaled systems still face severe structural constraints. They remain tethered to laboratory-grade requirements, including high operational power, continuous carrier gas consumption (argon), complex sample treatment, and substantial capital costs.5 Consequently, electrochemical mini-sensors offer a compelling, cost-effective alternative for true decentralized, autonomous, and real-time point-of-need water quality monitoring without necessitating specialized personnel. In this context, electrochemical methods6,7 and miniature sensors7,8 are critical for heavy metal detection, providing on-site and user-friendly capabilities with high precision and accuracy.9,10 These electrochemical methods include ion-selective electrodes (ISEs),11 linear sweep voltammetry (LSV),12 cyclic voltammetry (CV),12 and anodic-, cathodic- or adsorptive-stripping voltammetry (ASV, CSV, and AdSV, respectively),6,13 with pulse-based methods such as square-wave anodic-stripping voltammetry (SWASV) considered among the most sensitive ones.6,14

High selectivity and efficient electron transfer are essential characteristics for developing effective materials for electrochemical heavy metal sensing. Over recent decades, researchers have developed various electrochemical sensors, employing metallic mercury (Hg)15 and bismuth (Bi) electrodes16 for this purpose. These materials are known for their tendency to deposit on electrodes and readily amalgamate with heavy metal ions under negative potentials, particularly when ASV is applied.17 During this process, oxidation reactions occur, producing currents that correspond to specific heavy metal ions at distinct anodic potentials. However, the extensive use of Hg in such methods contradicts current trends in heavy metal analysis, which emphasize the adoption of environmentally benign solid-state materials. To mitigate these concerns, thin-film Hg electrodes have been developed.15 These electrodes minimize Hg usage, enhance ease of handling, and preserve key advantages of conventional Hg electrodes, such as a broad cathodic potential window. In this technique, Hg is electrodeposited from a low-concentration solution onto a solid electrode that serves as a mechanical support and facilitates easier manipulation. As previously mentioned, concerns over the toxicity of Hg have led to the emergence of Bi as a more environmentally favorable alternative for heavy metal sensor development, owing to its significantly lower toxicity.18,19 The advantages of using Bi for heavy metal detection include its partial insensitivity to dissolved oxygen in adsorptive stripping analysis, its functionality in highly alkaline environments, and its ability to provide better separation between peaks during stripping analysis.20,21

Another critical parameter for fabricating highly efficient electrochemical sensors is maximizing the surface-to-volume (S/V) ratio of the electrocatalyst, which directly dictates the density and accessibility of electroactive sites.17 Within this framework, advanced nanomaterials such as reduced graphene oxide (rGO) have been extensively explored as benchmarks in the literature, demonstrating that exceptionally high surface areas can drive down detection limits in heavy metal-sensing applications.22,23 However, translating these interfacial advantages into highly reproducible, uniform sensing platforms requires precise control over the material’s spatial distribution on the substrate. To fully leverage these electrochemical advantages in field-deployable scenarios, precise control over the electrode’s surface architecture is paramount, a requirement that has historically driven the adoption of lithographic microfabrication techniques.

Electrosensing materials can be patterned directly using lithographic and microfabrication processes, paving the way for high-performance, miniaturized devices for heavy metal detection. Over the past three decades, microfabricated electrochemical platforms, ranging from cleanroom-photolithographed microelectrode arrays to disposable screen-printed sensors, have matured significantly into highly robust and precise analytical tools.6,7,14 However, utilizing resist-free, maskless scanning probe nanolithography, such as dip-pen nanolithography (DPN), to precisely engineer the spatial geometry and localized chemical environment of active species for toxic cation sensing represents a more recent paradigm shift. While traditional cleanroom lithography relies heavily on multi-step polymer resists and complex etching procedures, DPN streamlines fabrication by enabling direct, single-step ink deposition. As an alternative, maskless techniques offer more streamlined approaches to both patterning and sensing.

In this context, DPN represents a maskless and revolutionary advancement of nanoscale patterning technology, pioneered by Professor Chad A. Mirkin in the early 2000s at Northwestern University.24 DPN allows the precise deposition of materials with sub-100-nanometer resolution and has found applications across nanoelectronics, biotechnology, and surface science.25,26,27 Fundamentally, DPN employs an atomic force microscope (AFM) tip as a molecular ink pen, enabling the patterning of a wide array of materials, including organic molecules, biomolecules, nanoparticles, and polymers.28,29 The deposition process is based on the controlled diffusion of molecules from the AFM tip to the substrate. By accurately controlling the movement of the AFM tip across the substrate surface, intricate patterns and structures can be generated, highlighting the key advantages of DPN and underscoring its versatility. Despite its many strengths, DPN faces limitations in scalability.30 The main factor for the low production rate is the relatively small number of cantilevers on a single DPN probe that, currently, is 12–48. However, large-scale probes have been developed to address this challenge, including systems with up to 55,000 cantilevers, which enable high-throughput and faster DPN-based lithography.31,32 While the current prototyping process using a 12-tip array requires ∼50 min per sensor, future upscaling using high-density arrays is expected to reduce fabrication time to the minute-scale and significantly decrease costs. In addition, achieving high-throughput patterning with 55,000 tips using DPN requires careful optimization of the experimental conditions and meticulous selection of materials. It is anticipated that this limitation can be mitigated as further research progresses. Consequently, the integration of high-density arrays offers a highly favorable qualitative cost-benefit transition: the upfront operational parameters required for ink formulation and tip calibration are balanced by a multi-order-of-magnitude reduction in marginal fabrication time per sensor unit. By shifting from serial prototyping to mass-parallelized fabrication, this architectural upscaling effectively addresses the throughput bottleneck historically associated with scanning probe setups, thereby driving down unit costs and establishing a commercially viable pathway for batch-scale manufacturing of meta-chemical surface (MCS) devices. By overcoming these manufacturing barriers, it becomes possible to design precisely ordered, high-density surface features that function under an entirely new electrochemical operating paradigm—the MCS.

The patterned clusters generated via DPN constitute MCSs, which are conceptually analogous to metasurfaces commonly employed in photonics.33,34 While optical metasurfaces modulate electromagnetic waves using sub-wavelength elements, an MCS modulates electrochemical mass transport and charge-transfer dynamics through an engineered, periodic arrangement of localized nanoclusters. An MCS typically comprises periodically arranged clusters of materials such as metals, semiconductors, or biomolecules, relying on architectural design principles that align with broader advances in the controlled fabrication and phase stabilization of nanoscale metallic solid solutions on substrates.35 Deliberate microspatial patterning on these surfaces establishes discrete diffusion zones, altering effective boundary-layer conditions and modifying the S/V ratio relative to continuous thin films. This structural modification introduces synergistic enhancements, such as accelerated mass flux and optimized active-site accessibility, yielding electrochemical responses and analytical sensitivities that are entirely distinct from their corresponding bulk forms.36,37

In one of our recent studies, Nitrilotris(methylene)triphosphonic acid (NTPH)-based ink was patterned onto a Pt surface, resulting in an electrode capable of detecting ultra-low concentrations (∼0.49 ppb) of Pb(II).38 In a more recent investigation, black phosphorus ink was patterned with varying dwell times for the detection of antimony, achieving detection in the range of 14–24 ppb.39 In another study, diaminonaphthalene (DAN) and dipenicillamine (DPA) were patterned onto a gold (Au) surface, yielding Pb(II) detection limits of 0.40 and 0.30 ppb, respectively. Glutathione40 ink was patterned on an Au surface to enable simultaneous detection of Pb(II) and Hg(II), with limits of detection (LoDs) of 0.84 and 0.99 ppb, respectively.36 In a separate study, DPN was used to pattern glucose oxidase onto an Au surface, producing highly efficient electrodes with enhanced glucose sensitivity.37 An earlier investigation involved the controlled patterning of Ni(OH)2 clusters onto an indium tin oxide electrode surface, which effectively modulated the water oxidation reaction current and overpotential.41 Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of DPN as a versatile and essential lithographic technique.

Accordingly, in the present work, DPN was employed for the first time to fabricate MCSs from Bi and Hg inks on Au surfaces, and these were utilized for heavy metal sensing. The patterned MCSs consume lower amounts of Hg and Bi; however, when used for multiple cation detection, they exhibit enhanced sensing capabilities compared with their bulk forms. The overarching importance of this study lies in providing the first direct, systematic comparison between Bi- and Hg-based MCSs engineered via maskless nanolithography. While Hg has traditionally served as the gold standard for heavy metal sensing due to its exceptional amalgamation properties, its severe toxicity poses an environmental paradox. Bi has emerged as a green alternative, yet a rigorous nanoscale comparison of its true geometric and electronic trade-offs has been completely absent in the literature. By utilizing DPN to strictly control the S/V ratio, this work bridges the gap between fundamental surface morphology and macro-scale analytical performance. This provides an engineering blueprint for designing eco-friendly, ultra-sensitive electrosensing platforms that safeguard global water resources without compromising analytical sensitivity.

A distinctive feature of MCS sensors is the formation of nanoclusters through DPN-based nanoscale patterning. These clusters possess a high S/V ratio, enabling extensive interaction between the analyte solution and the active species within the nanoclusters, thereby enhancing sensor sensitivity.38,39,40,41 Control experiments confirmed that only the sensor patterned with Hg or Bi, the active components of the ink, was capable of detecting cations in solution, in contrast to the AuAuAu electrode or poly-methyl methacrylate (PMMA)-based sensor lacking Hg or Bi (Figure S2). Our previous work shows that PMMA limits the diffusion of large molecules from solution to the patterned clusters.37 However, another previous work shows that it enhances the binding strength between cations and the ligands in the patterned clusters.40

The LoD values obtained with the MCS-Bi sensor were generally lower than those obtained with the MCS-Hg sensor. For example, the LoD values for lead, as detected by MCS-Bi and MCS-Hg electrodes, are 0.26 ± 0.04 and 0.52 ± 0.02 ppb, respectively, as shown in Figures 4H and 3H.

This result is notable due to the known toxicity of Hg, suggesting that MCS-Bi sensors offer a viable and safer alternative to MCS-Hg sensors. Both electrodes demonstrated LoD values comparable to those reported in the literature (Table S1), as well as to those of other DPN fabricated electrodes reported by our group.36,38,40,47 In order to explain the difference between the sensors, two main factors that could affect the sensitivity were studied: the nanoclusters’ S/V ratio36,39,40,48 and the binding strength36,40,48,49 between the active species (Bi and Hg) and the heavy metals.

Sensing performance in commercial mineral and tap water

Simultaneous detection of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cu(II) with high sensitivity is highly important, as it has been reported in the literature that cadmium and lead can interfere with each other’s detection, and copper may interfere with the detection of both.50,51,52,53 Consequently, the sensor’s performance in matrices containing multiple cations may influence the sensor’s analytical sensitivity. Additionally, it is important to test the MCS electrodes in real water, as their features differ from those made with ultrapure water. Real water samples exhibit diverse physicochemical characteristics, including pH, ionic composition, and alkalinity, which can affect SWASV measurements.52,54 Therefore, calibration curves (Figures 5B–5D) were constructed using a commercial mineral water matrix (Note S3). Their composition is constant and most closely resembles that of tap water (their mineral composition is presented in Note S4), making them suitable for investigating sensor sensitivity and evaluating the applicability of the MCS sensors for tap water analysis. To quantitatively evaluate the real-world matrix effect, the analytical sensitivities (slopes) obtained during simultaneous multi-cation sensing in mineral water were compared directly against the corresponding multi-metal baselines in deionized water. For the MCS-Hg platform, a quantified signal suppression of 62.0%, 73.8%, and 78.7% was observed for Cu(II), Cd(II), and Pb(II), respectively. Notably, the environmentally benign MCS-Bi platform demonstrated a remarkably uniform response under matrix stress, with a tightly bound signal suppression measured at 78.8% for Cu(II), 71.0% for Cd(II), and 71.9% for Pb(II) (representing a predictable ∼3.4- to 4.7-fold decrease in baseline sensitivity). This attenuation reflects the competitive screening effects of background divalent ions (Ca2+ and Mg2+) coupled with intense mutual cation competition at the DPN-fabricated active sites. Remarkably, despite this suppression, both surface architectures maintained highly robust linear correlations (R2 of up to 0.98 in mineral water), confirming the practical capacity of the patterned metasurfaces to resolve trace heavy metal contamination under real-world conditions reliably.

The LoD values determined for Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II) in mineral water (Figure 5E) were higher than those obtained in the ultrapure water medium (Figures 3H and 4H), indicating a decrease in analytical sensitivity under more complex aqueous conditions. This behavior likely arises from competitive interactions or matrix effects introduced by the dissolved mineral components, which can attenuate the electrochemical response.52,54,55 Despite this interference, the LoDs remain within the ppb range, demonstrating the sensor’s adequate performance in realistic water matrices.

Tap water samples were measured after spiking with 0.5–1 ppb of Pb(II). The lead concentration in the tap water samples (their mineral composition is presented in Note S4) was calculated after MCS-Bi electrode detection, using the calibration curve based on mineral water medium from Figure 5D. The lead concentrations measured were in good agreement with the ICP-MS measurements. The recoveries were higher than 80%, highlighting the MCS sensor usability for ppb measurement (Table S2).

Beyond the isolated dimensions of individual nanoclusters, their spatial distribution and micro-spatial density across the MSCs play a critical role in governing the overall electrochemical performance. In our DPN architecture, the pitch and array spacing between consecutive nanoclusters were systematically optimized and maintained constant for both MCS platforms. This deliberate spacing ensures that the localized hemispherical diffusion zones of neighboring active sites do not significantly overlap during the analytical deposition step. By preventing diffusion field overlap and subsequent mass-transport shielding, each cluster operates as an independent micro-interface, fully exploiting its high S/V ratio to maximize the toxic cation flux and enhance sensitivity into the low-ppb range.

The Bi nanoclusters exhibited a larger radius than the Hg nanoclusters (Table 1). Consequently, the exposed Au surface area in the MCS-Bi was smaller than that of MCS-Hg, despite having the same number of nanoclusters on each. The increased exposure to the Au surface could enhance sensitivity,39 in this case, favoring the MCS-Hg sensor. Additionally, the electrical conductivity of Hg (10,438 S cm−1) is higher than that of Bi (8,333 S cm−1).60,61

We hypothesize that these counteracting physical and electronic properties establish a self-limiting trade-off: the higher S/V ratio of the MCS-Bi clusters promotes rapid interfacial mass transport, whereas the superior intrinsic conductivity and greater Au exposure of the MCS-Hg platform accelerate local charge-transfer kinetics. Consequently, these competing geometric and electronic effects balance each other out, providing a consistent mechanistic explanation for the unexpectedly narrow gap observed between their ultimate LoDs, despite the lower atomic loading of Bi.

This interplay between the patterned nanoclusters and the underlying substrate highlights a standard micro-array electrochemical behavior, rather than an internal inconsistency. The pristine, unblocked Au surface functions as a highly conductive macro-contact that optimizes overall electron transport across the electrode, while the DPN-patterned clusters serve as discrete, high-surface-area centers for heavy metal accumulation. In the case of MCS-Hg, the taller, narrower cluster morphology exposes a larger fraction of the highly conductive Au substrate, facilitating secondary electron transfer. Conversely, the flatter and wider MCS-Bi clusters cover more of the Au surface but compensate for this baseline shielding by exposing a significantly larger active chemical architecture per unit volume. This integrated model reconciles both substrate-mediated electronics and cluster-mediated geometry into a cohesive framework.

The viscosity of the inks was measured as a function of shear rate (Table S3). The shear rate relevant to the present fabrication procedure is approximately 8 s−1.62 Viscosity measurements in the range of 6.31–10 s−1 revealed that the Hg-based ink exhibited a higher average viscosity than the Bi-based ink (6.77 mPa s and 2.70 mPa s, respectively). As expected based on the literature, higher ink viscosity results in the formation of smaller-diameter features.62,63 However, it also leads to the formation of taller clusters due to the stretching of the liquid bridge before rupture,64 which allows for greater ink transfer during the DPN process. These combined effects help explain the higher S/V ratio observed for the MCS-Bi electrodes relative to that for MCS-Hg (Table 1). Specifically, the lower viscosity of the Bi-based ink (2.70 mPa s) promotes lateral spreading, creating wider, flatter nanoclusters that maximize the S/V ratio but simultaneously shield a larger portion of the underlying Au substrate. Conversely, the higher viscosity of the Hg-based ink (6.77 mPa s) leads to the formation of taller, narrower clusters. While this results in a lower S/V ratio, it preserves a larger fraction of the pristine, unblocked Au surface, which functions as a highly conductive macro-contact for optimized electron transport. These geometric and electronic factors are inherently coupled and “locked together” by the fluid dynamics of the DPN process, forming a unified morphological-electronic framework. Consequently, rather than implying a causal separation, these attributes must be interpreted as a correlative package where the S/V ratio, substrate exposure, and localized electron transport synergistically govern the ultimate electrochemical sensing performance. It is important to note that for each MCS, the ink mixtures were prepared using equal masses of Hg and Bi precursors. As a result, the active material loading of MCS patterned with Bi ink was ∼1.87 times lower than that of MCS patterned with Hg ink, which strengthens the results, demonstrating that the MCS-Bi electrode exhibits higher sensitivity, even though it contains a smaller amount of active material (Note S2).

Reproducibility and stability of MCS sensors

Reproducibility and stability are fundamental criteria for assessing sensor performance. To evaluate the reproducibility of the MCS sensors, three electrodes (n = 3) each of MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi were fabricated under identical conditions and stored for a week up to a month; SWASV measurements were conducted in a solution containing 1 ppb of Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II), each with three consecutive scans, and the resulting voltammograms were compared. All three electrodes exhibited stripping peaks at nearly identical potentials with closely matching oxidation currents, demonstrating good reproducibility (Figures S3A–S3D) for MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi, respectively. The SD calculated from the measurements was used for error bars in the graphs presented in the article.

The stability of the MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi sensors was examined by conducting SWASV measurements with the same sensor repeatedly, in a solution containing 1 ppb of Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II). The overlaid voltammograms (Figures S3E–S3H) for MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi, respectively, indicated that the sensors maintained efficient detection of Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II) for at least 2 days, without appreciable loss in oxidation current or noticeable shift in oxidation potential, thereby confirming its robust stability.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging conducted prior to and following the electrochemical measurements (Figure S4) confirmed the presence of uniformly distributed nanoclusters, in agreement with AFM observations, and further indicated that the nanoclusters remained intact and interconnected throughout the measurement process. These results explain the stability performance of the electrodes over time (see Figures S3A, S3B, S3C, and S3D for MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi, respectively).

Density functional theory calculations

The binding strength between heavy metals and the active species within the nanoclusters also influences sensor sensitivity.38,39,40 Therefore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted (STAR Methods). The detection of metal ions involves their reduction to the zero-valent state and subsequent co-precipitation with the detecting metal, as described in Reactions (1) and (3). To account for the varying sensitivities of the ions, the calculations focused on the ability of metal ions to adsorb onto the solid electrode in conjunction with the detecting species (Hg or Bi). The adsorption energies revealed that Pb exhibited the lowest adsorption energies (−1.11 and −1.93 eV, for Hg and Bi, respectively), demonstrating the strongest binding with both Hg and Bi and corresponding to the highest detection sensitivity. Conversely, Cu displayed the highest adsorption energies (0.48 and 0.78 eV for Hg and Bi, respectively), correlating with the lowest sensitivity (Table 2). For Cd and Pb, significantly lower adsorption energies were obtained for Bi than for Hg, in line with the specific selectivity trends observed in the experimental multi-cation SWASV results.

DPN is a complex process in which amalgamation/alloying, nanocluster morphology, and the electrochemical interface all play a role. The study of the whole process is beyond the scope of this study. Although a very simple model was used for the DFT calculations and the full process, including the electrochemical steps, was not considered, the trend in the co-adsorption of hazardous ions with the detecting materials matches that in detection sensitivity, as initially assumed. Given the simplicity of the model, only qualitative trends were expected. Despite the limitations of the model, DFT calculations are used in the present study to support the observed sensitivity trends, as previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between binding energies and sensitivity.36,39,40,47

In conclusion, MCSs were patterned using DPN and employed in the present study as sensors for heavy metal detection. This patterning approach successfully upgraded commercial electrodes for detecting heavy metals, from ppm levels to ppb levels. The calculated adsorption energy trends closely matched the trends observed in LoD; specifically, lower adsorption energies corresponded to lower LoD values.

Although the S/V ratio positively influences sensitivity, its effect was found to be less pronounced than anticipated due to the influence of the exposed Au surface on overall conductivity. However, the S/V ratio is a parameter that can be precisely controlled through the DPN process, thereby allowing direct control over sensor sensitivity. This unique capability offers a considerable advantage in the design of high-sensitivity heavy metal sensors.

The MCS-Bi sensor exhibited greater sensitivity than the MCS-Hg sensor, allowing highly sensitive sensing of tap water as well, and both demonstrated LoD values comparable to those reported in the literature (see Table S1). These findings highlight key environmental benefits, indicating that Bi-based sensors can serve as viable and less toxic alternatives to Hg-based sensors. Furthermore, the portability of these sensors offers the potential for real-time monitoring and alerts in response to concentration deviations, capabilities not feasible with conventional, non-portable techniques such as ICP-MS.

Limitations of the study

The ink used to pattern MCS-Hg contains mercury; therefore, this MCS cannot be used to detect mercury in water samples. However, the MCS-Bi sensor exhibited greater sensitivity than the MCS-Hg sensor; therefore, it may serve as an alternative.

The patterning process yields only specific conical shapes of the clusters with round or elliptical bases, and it is not possible to pattern nanoclusters with other shapes.

A very simple model was used for the DFT calculations, and the full process, including the electrochemical steps, was not considered. Given the model’s simplicity, only qualitative interpretation is supported. Additionally, while real-world matrix robustness was successfully validated using a multi-cation mineral water proxy, a fully exhaustive, systematic evaluation of individual binary and ternary ion cross-interference permutations was not executed, which remains a scope for future analytical benchmarking.

Method details

Materials

All materials were purchased and used as received.

MCS fabrication

The MCS electrodes were fabricated using the NLP 2000 DPN platform from NanoInk Inc. (Illinois, USA). The system operates based on AFM, enabling highly precise control over the size and shape of the patterned nanostructures. For nanocluster patterning, a specialized M-type cantilever equipped with 12 tips spaced 66 μm apart was used (Figure S5). Prior to patterning, the cantilevers and ink wells were treated in a plasma cleaner (MTI corporation, medium chamber, USA) using oxygen plasma for 30 min to render the cantilevers hydrophilic and facilitate ink transport. Electrode surfaces were cleaned before patterning using Kimtech Science Kimwipes and flushed with dry nitrogen.

Prior to patterning, the ink was mixed on a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 15 min, followed by vigorous vortexing. The prepared ink was then micropipetted into an inkwell reservoir, where the cantilever tips were loaded with ink in preparation for patterning. Before patterning, excess ink from the tips was blotted onto an inactive area on the electrode to maintain uniform patterning conditions. Ink deposition was achieved by forming a liquid meniscus at the tip ends, enabling controlled material transfer. The structure and uniformity of the patterned features were governed by adjusting the dwell time, defined as the duration for which the cantilever tips remain in contact with the substrate. Prior to ink loading, a calibration procedure was performed to ensure uniform force application across all 12 tips during both the inking and patterning stages (Figure S5). After calibration, three reference points were established around the patterning plane to define its geometry. Once the patterning plane was set, the cantilevers were immersed in the inkwell to begin the patterning process. Subsequently, batches of 3 electrodes were patterned at a time, to maintain constant working conditions.

The patterning pitch, the distance between adjacent nanoclusters, was set to 6.6 μm, and the dwell time was set to 1 s using the NLP control software. Upon completion of the patterning process, the electrodes were cured under a 365 nm UV lamp for approximately 15 min to promote cross-linking of PMMA, a key component of the ink.65 Throughout the patterning procedure, environmental conditions were maintained at a constant temperature of 20 ± 1°C and relative humidity of (40 ± 2) %. Additional information appears in Note S1 in the supporting information.

This method was employed for patterning both metal-based inks (MCS-Hg and MCS-Bi) and the non-metal control ink (MCS-control), as summarized in Table 1.

Preparation of coated electrodes

Uniform coatings were applied using the NLP 2000 platform to fabricate MCS-Hg-coated and MCS-Bi-coated electrodes (Table 1). The same metal-based inks used for patterning the MCS electrodes were employed for the uniform coatings. The NLP 2000 system successfully deposited a consistent layer with an average thickness of approximately 95 nm. Environmental conditions during coating were maintained at ∼20°C and ∼40% relative humidity. Following deposition, the coated electrodes were cured under a 365 nm UV lamp for approximately 15 min to facilitate cross-linking of the PMMA component. The average coating thickness was measured using an EasyScan 2 Flex AFM.

MCS physical characterization

The surfaces of all electrodes were scanned with AFM after patterning for comparison between the resulting electrodes. Surface analysis of the MCSs was performed using AFM in tapping mode with an EasyScan 2 Flex instrument from NanoSurf (Liestal, Switzerland). Morphological characterization by Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) was conducted before and after electrochemical measurements using a VEGA 4 LMS Desktop SEM from Tescan (Brno, Czech Republic). Only some of the electrodes were scanned with SEM because the measurement was destructive to the electrode surface.

Electrochemical characterization

Electrochemical measurements were performed using a portable, USB-powered potentiostat (EmStat4, PalmSens BV, the Netherlands), operated through PSTrace 5.9 software. Square-wave anodic-stripping voltammetry (SWASV) was employed to evaluate the heavy metal sensing performance (SWASV is a common method used in sensors and heavy metal detection).16,52,66 The MCS electrodes were interfaced with the potentiostat using a MicruX Drop Cell Connector (MicruX Technologies, Spain). Electrochemical measurements using the commercial ItalSens SPE were conducted in 20 mM HCl, following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Figures 1 and S6).42 In contrast, based on MicruX recommendations, HCl-containing solutions were avoided when gold electrodes were used. Therefore, all electrochemical measurements involving MCS electrodes were performed in a sodium acetate buffer solution at pH 4.5. For SWASV with MCS electrodes, the following optimized parameters were used (Figures S7 and S8): accumulation for 30 s at −1.3 V, followed by detection under the following SWASV conditions, step potential: 5 mV, amplitude: 100 mV, and frequency: 25 Hz. The electrochemical experiments were conducted at least 3 times for each measurement. The SD of n = 3 measurements was calculated and used for drawing error bars.

Ink preparation and characterization

Ink mixtures for patterning and nanocoating were prepared using mercury(II) acetate or bismuth(III) nitrate with PMMA and acetonitrile (ACN), yielding the MCS-Hg, MCS-Hg-coated, MCS-Bi, and MCS-Bi-coated variants, respectively (see Table 1). In brief, 10 mg of the respective metal salt was added in a 1:1 mixture of 500 μL ACN and 500 μL PMMA, followed by 20 min of thorough mixing. In this formulation, ACN functioned as a diluting agent. PMMA has been previously employed in various patterning applications due to its favorable adhesion to metallic substrates and its light-induced polymerization behavior.67,68 To prevent premature curing, the prepared inks were stored in a cold, dark environment.

Ink viscosity was measured using an AR 2000 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA), with all measurements conducted at 20°C (Table S3; Scheme S1). A control ink used for MCS-control electrodes was prepared using only ACN and PMMA, without any Hg or Bi salts, following the same procedure.

Quantification and statistical analysis

PSTrace 5.9 software was used to operate the potentiostat and collect electrochemical experimental data.

The electrochemical experiments were conducted at least 3 times for each measurement. The SD of n = 3 measurements was calculated and used for drawing error bars.

OriginPro 8.5 software was used to compile figures and Microsoft Excel to compile and analyze experimental data.

LoD calculation

The LoD was calculated using the formula: LoD = 3.3syx/m, where syx is the standard error of the linear regression intercept, and m is the slope of the calibration curve. All calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel.

MATLAB analysis

Nanocluster parameters for each MCS surface were quantified using a custom MATLAB script. The analysis was based on data matrices obtained from the EasyScan 2 Flex AFM. The MATLAB code placed two cursors at the center of each nanocluster, one along the x axis and one along the y axis, to determine the elliptical base dimensions. The semi-major and semi-minor axes were extracted, and the corresponding base diameters were defined as a and b (twice the respective semi-axes), as listed in Table 1. Additionally, the z axis cross-sectional profile was analyzed to measure the height of each nanocluster at its center. The custom MATLAB code then automatically calculated key geometric and physical parameters, including nanocluster volume, nanocluster surface area, and S/V ratio (Table 1). It is important to note that while base dimensions were approximated for characterization, the volume and surface area were calculated by numerically integrating the actual AFM topography data, ensuring accurate estimation for irregular shapes.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

Solid-state calculations were conducted using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)69 to perform non-spin-polarized, first-principles calculations within the framework of DFT. The projector-augmented wave method was employed in conjunction with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional,70 which is based on the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). To account for long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions, the DFT-D2 dispersion correction by Grimme was applied.71 Solvent effects were incorporated using the implicit self-consistent electrolyte solvation model, VASPsol.72 All calculations were carried out with a plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV. For bulk calculations, the Brillouin zone was sampled using a 6 × 6×6 Monkhorst–Pack (MP) k-point mesh, whereas surface calculations employed a 6 × 6×1 k-point mesh. As the primary objective of this study was to investigate trends in adsorption energies, only electronic energies were considered in the adsorption energy calculations. The model system consisted of the adsorption of one M atom, M = Cd, Cu, Pb, on a six-layer Au(111) surface on which two X atoms are adsorbed, X = Bi or Hg, with a vacuum layer of 16 Å, to eliminate interlayer interactions. Adsorption energies were calculated using Equation 5:(Equation 5)Eads(MX2/Au)=EMX2/Au−(EX2/Au+EM)

EMX2/Au is the electronic energy of an Au surface on which two X atoms and one M atom are adsorbed, EX2/Au is the electronic energy of an Au surface on which two X atoms are adsorbed, and EM = Ebulk/n, Ebulk = electronic energy of the bulk unit cell of M, and n = number of M atoms in the bulk unit cell. All bulk structures were obtained from ICSD73: Bi, Au, Cd, Cu, Pb. The VESTA code74 is used to illustrate the stable structure of all structures.
