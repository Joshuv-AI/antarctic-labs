 Abstract
 This report presents a framework for transmission line (T-L) sensor design in Fre-
 quency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar applications, with particular em-
 phasis on modeling collision damage effects. Classical electromagnetic (EM) propa-
 gation theory is combined with impact mechanics to develop a model for analyzing
 transmission line behavior as a sensor under high-velocity impact conditions. The
 framework includes detailed mathematical derivations, spatial and temporal damage
 evolution models, and practical implementation considerations.

1 Introduction
We present two interconnected aspects of transmission line sensor design: the fundamen-
tal electromagnetic modeling and its application within a collision damage framework [1]-
[15],[37]-[44]. These components together provide a pragmatic approach to investigate trans-
mission line behavior in harsh environments when coupled to a Frequency Modulated Con-
tinuous Wave (FMCW) radar processor [16]-[24].
 A transmission line (T-L) is a cable developed to conduct electromagnetic (EM) waves in
a constrained medium. These wave-based propagating lines are specially constructed using
impedance matching techniques to minimize reflections and power losses. Higher transmitted
frequencies lead directly to shorter wavelengths requiring the necessity of a transmission line.
For our problem, we employ the transmission line as a sensor that may be damaged or broken
on impact with the purpose of determining a precise length (distance) measurement [1], [2],
[15].
 This integration of T-L and collision damage modeling is crucial when these sensors
are deployed in harsh environments where high-velocity impacts occur especially in ballistic
testing scenarios [45]-[47]. Here we develop the underlying mathematics employed in de-
veloping a simulation used to analyze the feasibility of this novel approach to measuring
impact displacements. In Sec. II we briefly develop the underlying transmission line theory
to motivate its application as a high-impact sensor [2], [15]. The subsequent discussion of
damage modeling follows coupled to this sensor motivates the displacement issues. In Sec. 4,
the basics of the FMCW radar processor are briefly discussed to show the relationship of the
T-L sensor displacement measurement using the processor to provide an accurate estimate

of distance (displacement) on the vehicle [4], [19]. Finally, a discussion of the high-impact
collision problem synthesis and measurement are developed in Sec. V where the physical
T-L parameters coupled with the FMCW processor yield a test bed for performance analysis
[45], [49], . Appendices discussing the software numerics and MATLAB implementation are
included along with chirp design features.

2.5 Transmission Line Numerical Solutions
With this in mind, it is important to understand the various methods and underlying as-
sumptions for each of these T-L representation especially relative to our damage detec-
tion/estimation problem. Thus, transmission line equations can be expressed in multiple
mathematical forms, each with specific assumptions, validity domains, and solution tech-
niques [8], [13]. Understanding these different forms is crucial for selecting the appropriate
analysis method for specific transmission line problems, particularly in the context of FMCW
radar applications with collision damage.

 The application different solution techniques are appropriate for different equation forms
(above) and analysis requirements [25], [30]: For transient analysis the Method of Charac-

 For steady-state analysis Phasor Analysis provides an efficient aingle-frequency sinusoidal

techniques [28], [29] .
 However, damaged transmission lines present another dimension to the simulation prob-
lem especially when the T-L is employed as a sensor. In this case, as in our problem,
the sensor will be damaged on high-speed impact altering its internal (RLCG) parameters
negating some of the more classical solution approaches. The FDTD method is suitable for
modeling transmission lines with collision damage because it can handle [25], [34], [32]: (1)
lossy lines with R, L, G, C 6= 0; spatially varying parameters: R(z), L(z), G(z), C(z) as well
as time-varying parameters for damage evolution: R(z, t), L(z, t), G(z, t), C(z, t).
 For FMCW radar applications with collision damage modeling: each of the modeling
techniques offer advantages over others. For instance, (1) use FDTD for transient damage
evolution, since it handles the full lossy case with time-varying parameters [35], [36] as
well as damage modeling as time-varying T-L parameters R(z, t), L(z, t), G(z, t), C(z, t); use
phasor analysis for steady-state FMCW chirp response at each frequency; and use Fourier
decomposition to bridge between time and frequency domains.

 Summarizing, this FDTD implementation provides: (1) an accurate transmission line
simulation with controlled numerical dispersion; (2) flexible damage modeling at user-specified
breakpoints; (3) FMCW-based damage localization with mm-scale resolution (to follow); (4)

validation metrics for quality assurance. Thus, the combination of adaptive meshing, CFL-
compliant time stepping, and sophisticated spectral estimation enables a precise characteri-
zation of transmission line behavior under various damage conditions.
 Next, we investigate the processing of this data using a Frequency-Modulated Continuous
Wave (FMCW) radar processor. First, we start with the basic FMCW theory, coupled it
to the T-L sensor and apply to a simple damage model followed by a complete detailed
projectile collision damage representation.

4 Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave Processor
A Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) processor when coupled with an cable
or transmission line sensor embedded in a hypervelocity vehicle under ballistic test provides
an extremely accurate displacement measurement system. The underlying basis or purpose
of this system is to measure the displacement damage and event time, when the vehicle
impacts a thick concrete wall damaging or severing the sensor [68], [19]. Here, the FM
excitation signal is a high frequency chirp linearly swept over a wide bandwidth (BW) at a
high rate providing the basis of the displacement measurement [5], [6].
 The overall FMCW processor is shown in Fig. 4. The novel chirp signal (hardware
developed) is created to satisfy the high frequency BW constraints. It represents the ref-
erence signal exciting the embedded T-L sensor. Here the signal is initially delayed by the
line propagation delay and mixed (modulated or multiplied) with the reference chirp. The
resulting mixed signal is then filtered to remove the high-frequency chirp in order to extract
the intermediate or beat frequencies resulting from the vehicle/wall collision characterizing
the damage zone displacements along the T-L sensor.
 In the simplest scenario, the T-L sensor is severed providing an open circuit reflection
response [1]-[15]. The resulting beat frequencies are estimated using peak detection of the
power spectrum. The known FMCW chirp rate is then used to accurately determine the T-L
displacements (damage zones) from the time delays associated with each of the extracted beat

[25]-[34].

7 Summary and Conclusions
This report integrates electromagnetic wave propagation theory with mechanical impact
physics to create a model for transmission line sensors subject to high speed projectile col-
lision damage.
 The mathematical framework discussed combines:

 • Classical transmission line theory - telegrapher’s equations, characteristic parameters,
 and wave propagation

 • Impact mechanics - momentum and energy transfer, orientation effects, and damage
 extent

 • Material response models - parameter modifications for different damage types

 • Spatial and temporal damage evolution - Gaussian distributions and smooth transitions

 • Numerical implementation - FDTD with variable parameters and stability considera-
 tions

 The model captures the complex physics of high-velocity impact on transmission lines,
accounting for:

 • Impact angle effects through ηimpact = cos(θ)

 • Damage type variations (open circuit, short circuit, impedance transition)

 • Velocity-dependent severity through Mach number scaling

 • Spatial damage distribution with Gaussian profiles

 • Temporal damage evolution with smooth transitions

 • Electromagnetic wave interactions with damaged regions

 Appendices are also developed to detail aspects and snipits of the MATLAB FMCW
TLine FDTD implementation and numerical aspects, transmission line sensor (cable) design,
and the MUSIC spectral estimator.
 This approach encompasses designing and analyzing transmission line sensors in chal-
lenging environments where collision damage is a concern.
