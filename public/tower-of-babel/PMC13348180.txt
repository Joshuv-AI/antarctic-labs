Coexistence of Metal and Dielectric Resonance Modes in a Single Nanostructure of a Hyperbolic Material
Authors: Yaolong Li, Xu Shi, Yuxin Zhang, Yen-En Liu, Lin Qiao, Hong Yang, Shufeng Wang, Guowei Lyu, Yasutaka Matsuo, Xiaoyong Hu, Qihuang Gong, Hiroaki Misawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperbolic materials that exhibit metal and dielectric responses to orthogonal polarizations offer possibilities for controlling nanophotonic modes beyond those of conventional isotropic materials. Here, we demonstrate the coexistence of metal and dielectric resonance modes in single MoOCl2 nanostructures, a hyperbolic plasmon material with metal-dielectric duality. The two modes are nonhybrid and are controlled independently by the two orthogonal in-plane polarizations, without mode mixing or crosstalk. According to mode analysis, localized plasmon resonance is excited along the metal permittivity axis of MoOCl2, while the dielectric magnetic dipole mode is excited along the orthogonal dielectric axis. Compared to the plasmonic mode, the dielectric mode has a much higher Q factor and a different hotspot distribution. The dielectric mode also exhibits much stronger photoemission enhancement due to the different hotspot distribution along the z-axis, as measured by photoemission electron microscopy. By adjusting the structural parameters, the two modes can be tuned to overlap in spectra while maintaining the polarization control. The coexistence of these modes and their polarization dependence in single nanostructures provide a fundamental strategy for nanophotonic design, particularly for engineering polarizations, enhancing nonlinear processes, and achieving multifunctional metasurfaces.

Introduction

Metal and dielectric resonance modes are two kinds of essential modes in nanophotonics, which are also widely applied to other research fields. Metal resonance modes are known for localized plasmon resonances supported by noble metals and semimetals, possessing the advantages of strong near-field enhancement and small mode volume. − Dielectric resonance modes are more diverse and include Mie modes, bound states in the continuum, and other dielectric cavities. − Compared to metal modes, dielectric modes can achieve a much higher Q factor and a generally larger mode volume, although atomic-scale field localizations have been reported in special dielectric cavities. − To combine the advantages of metal and dielectric modes, hybrid structures have been widely used, in which metal-dielectric hybrid modes are generally supported. − However, such hybrid structures often require complicated designs, and the modes are mixed, which limits their potential for fabrication and mode control. It remains challenging to achieve independent metal and dielectric modes in a single nanostructure and, further, to achieve independent mode control and excitation.

The emergence of two-dimensional materials with strong anisotropy has opened avenues for the design of nanoresonance modes. Natural hyperbolic materials, which feature the permittivity components of opposite signs, have drawn significant attention due to their distinct optical field confinement properties. − More recently, the range of hyperbolic materials has expanded from phonon polaritons in the mid-infrared to plasmon polaritons in the near-infrared and visible light spectrum. Among them, an excellent hyperbolic plasmon material, MoOCl2, was recently discovered, − which belongs to the oxychloride family of van der Waals materials MOX2 (M = Nb, Ta, V, Mo, Ru; X = Cl, Br, I). − MoOCl2 exhibits both pristine metal and dielectric permittivity over a broad band. Along the Mo–O chain axis, the material is purely metal (ε x < 0), while in the orthogonal directions, it remains a wideband dielectric (ε y , ε z > 0) across the visible to near-infrared range. ,, This metal-dielectric duality distinguishes MoOCl2 from the conventional isotropic noble metals and dielectric materials, thereby enabling approaches to nanophotonic design. By using MoOCl2, it is expected to achieve the coexistence of localized plasmonic and dielectric modes in a single nanostructure, beyond the scope of hybrid structures and modes based on traditional materials.

Here, we experimentally demonstrate the coexistence of metal and dielectric resonance modes in single MoOCl2 nanostructures. These two modes are nonhybrid and are controlled independently by the two orthogonal in-plane polarizations, without mode mixing or crosstalk. According to mode analysis, the localized plasmon resonance mode is excited at x-polarization (along the metal permittivity axis of MoOCl2), while the dielectric magnetic dipole mode is excited at y-polarization (along the orthogonal dielectric axis). Compared to the plasmonic mode, the dielectric mode has a much higher Q factor and a different hotspot distribution. The dielectric mode also exhibits much stronger photoemission enhancement due to different hotspot distributions along the z-axis, as measured by photoemission electron microscopy (PEEM). By adjusting the structural parameters, the two modes can be tuned to overlap in spectra, while maintaining the polarization control. Notably, a gold film is used as a mirror beneath the MoOCl2 structures to better confine the dielectric mode without a negative effect on the plasmonic mode. The coexistence of these modes and their polarization dependence in single nanostructures provides a fundamental strategy for nanophotonic design, particularly for engineering polarizations, enhancing nonlinear processes, and achieving multifunctional metasurfaces. Our work introduces a nanophotonic solution based on the unique properties of van der Waals materials, inspiring the further development of nanophotonics using two-dimensional materials.

In practical photonic and spectroscopic implementations, it is highly desirable to access multiple electromagnetic functionalities using a single nano-object under a fixed excitation wavelength, because wavelength scanning or realignment often introduces systematic drifts and complicates device integration. The present MoOCl2 nanoantenna provides two nonhybrid resonance channels that can be independently addressed by orthogonal in-plane polarizations: a low-Q plasmonic mode for broadband field enhancement and a high-Q dielectric magnetic dipole mode for spectrally selective response. Importantly, these modes remain polarization-orthogonal even when they are spectrally overlapped by geometric tuning, enabling polarization switching at the same wavelength without mode mixing or crosstalk. Furthermore, the distinct hotspot locations of the two modes (top surface vs Au/MoOCl2 interface) offer polarization-controlled spatial addressing, which can be exploited for layer-selective excitation/readout or multifunctional designs in which different functionalities are intentionally assigned to different regions of the same nanostructure.

Results and Discussion

Structure and Mode Analysis

MoOCl2 is a layered van der Waals crystal with a monoclinic structure (space group C2/m), , which exhibits metal-dielectric duality. − This enables achievements that were previously challenging with conventional materials. As illustrated in Figure a, the designed structure is simple and straightforward, consisting of periodic MoOCl2 elliptical disks on an Au/SiO2 substrate. The structural parameters, namely, the elliptical disk radius (R x , R y ), height (H), and squared period (P), are defined in Figure a. In this case, a 100 nm gold film is used as a mirror beneath the MoOCl2 structure. This approach is intended to enhance the confinement of the dielectric mode and overcome the relatively low refractive index of MoOCl2 along the z-axis. Otherwise, the dielectric modes cannot be supported by MoOCl2 nanostructures directly on glass substrates (without an Au film), as demonstrated in our previous work. It should be noted that the gold film has been demonstrated to have no negative effect on the plasmonic mode of MoOCl2.

Figure b shows the representative spectra of circular disks (Rx = Ry =100 nm, H = 100 nm, and P = 600 nm) under x- and y-polarizations, respectively. Two distinct modes are observed: a broad resonance under x-polarization and a sharp resonance under y-polarization. According to the mode analysis, the mode under x-polarization is attributed to a localized plasmonic mode, while the mode under y-polarization is attributed to a dielectric resonance mode. For x-polarization, Figure c illustrates the mode profile of |E x | in the x–z plane, revealing strong hotspots at the upper surface edge of the disk. Conversely, Figure d shows the y-polarization mode profile of |E y | in the y–z plane, which presents a volume distribution in the disk, consistent with a dielectric Mie resonance mode. (Mode profiles for |E| and |E z | and other components are presented in Figures S1 and S2.) Using multipole expansion, the plasmonic mode is composed of both electric and magnetic dipole terms. In contrast, the dielectric mode is principally a magnetic dipole resonance (Figure e,f, see also Figure S3 for multipole expansion at 45°-polarization). The mode expansions are consistent with the corresponding electric field distributions. For example, the closed vector loops observed in Figure d indicate the presence of circulating displacement currents associated with magnetic-type resonances. Therefore, these two modes coexist in a single MoOCl2 nanostructure, exhibiting different mode types, Q factors, and hotspot distributions. It should be noted that these two modes are independent and exhibit polarization-selective excitation, which is unavailable in conventional materials.

By tuning the structural parameters, the two modes shift differently due to their distinct mode properties. As shown in Figure a, under x-polarization, the resonance peak blue shifts as disk height (H) and elliptical disk radius (R y ) increase, which is consistent with plasmonic properties. Conversely, the resonance peak red shifts under y-polarization due to an increase in structure volume, which is consistent with the dielectric mode properties. Meanwhile, the Q factor of the dielectric mode generally increases significantly with a reduction in volume size (maximum ∼70) (Figure b). In contrast, the Q factor of the plasmonic mode remains very low (3–5) due to its strong radiative decay. Therefore, the Q factor of the dielectric mode can be 1 order of magnitude larger than the plasmonic mode. Additionally, tuning the geometric parameters allows the two modes to overlap in spectra while maintaining polarization selectivity. Therefore, it is feasible to achieve the simultaneous excitation and polarization control of the two modes under a light beam. Note that the unshifted mode around 600 nm is attributed to the grating mode and is not relevant to this study. In addition, the results of the mode shift with the elliptical disk radius (R x ) are presented in Figure S4. Considering that the metasurface period is set at 600 nm, for larger R y values, particularly >150 nm, the interdisk coupling will have a considerable contribution to the spectra, resulting in the emergence of new peaks. In addition, the unshifted grating-mode peak at shorter wavelength is also influenced by larger disk, as demonstrated by its gradual broadening and slight redshift at larger R y . Moreover, complex high-order modes should emerge from short wavelength as the disk size further increases. In this study, we focus on the fundamental mode to simplify the problem, thus designing the disk size within a suitable range.

Sample Fabrication and Spectral Characterization

To experimentally realize the coexisting modes, we fabricated high-quality MoOCl2 nanodisk metasurfaces using electron-beam lithography (EBL) and reactive ion etching (RIE). Figure a presents the schematic of the fabrication workflow. It is noteworthy that a 2.5 nm (25 cycles) Al2O3 layer was pregrown via atomic layer deposition (ALD) on the 100 nm Au film to enhance the adhesion between MoOCl2 and gold. Otherwise, the MoOCl2 nanostructures would easily be washed away if directly in contact with the gold film. This phenomenon may be attributed to the highly lubricious MoOCl2/Au interface and should be investigated via force experiments elsewhere. The presence of a thin Al2O3 layer had no notable impact on either the metal or dielectric modes.

Representative spectral characterizations are shown in Figure . For circular disks, the resonance wavelengths at y-polarization are remarkably shorter than those at x-polarization (Figure a). For example, the resonance peaks of array No. 16 of the sample in Figure at y- and x-polarizations are at 630 and 800 nm, respectively. For elliptical disks, the resonance wavelengths at y-polarization exhibit large red shifts compared to circular disks (e.g., ∼100 nm shift from array No. 16 to No. 7), while exhibiting minimal shifts at x-polarization (Figure b). It should be noted that, in Figure , from (a) to (b), the spectra with the same color are in fact designed with the same R x . However, due to the fabrication inaccuracies, the actual R x value is slightly different. For example, for No. 15 and No. 6, the R x values are 82 and 86 nm, respectively, which are very close. Therefore, the large redshift of y-polarized mode is reasonably attributed to the contribution of R y . In addition, the periods for circular and elliptical disks are different: 500 and 600 nm, respectively. The period also slightly shifts the resonance wavelength of the two resonance modes, as shown by the simulations in Figure S5. For reference, we have added simulated spectra in Figure S6 with the same parameters as in Figures a and b. The simulated spectra generally align with the experimental ones. The red shift of wavelength with the elliptical disk R y is consistent with the FDTD simulations presented in Figure . As expected, the line widths of the dielectric modes are dramatically narrower than those of the plasmonic modes. By tuning the structural parameters (e.g., reducing the disk height), the optimal Q factor of the dielectric mode can reach 45.3 in experiments, which is 5.7 times larger than that of the plasmonic mode (Figure c). The much higher Q factor of the dielectric mode makes it more advantageous for some applications, such as enhancing nonlinear processes and sensing. More importantly, the two modes can be tuned to overlap in spectra by increasing the disk height and the R y /R x ratio (Figure d). Meanwhile, the two modes maintain mode independence and polarization selectivity (see also Figure S7 for mode independence at spectral overlap in FDTD simulations and Figure S8 for polarization-dependent spectra. Under spectral overlap, we quantify “no mixing” as no deviation between the spectra of 45°-polarization and the linear superposition of x- and y-polarizations). Therefore, it is possible to excite both modes simultaneously at the same wavelength and control them with polarizations. In addition, nanoblocks can support similar modes as disks, as shown in Figure S9. Furthermore, mode control via geometric shape is a potential future area of investigation.

Near-Field Enhancement and Mode Dynamics

Plasmonic modes are expected to support near-field enhancement, and so are dielectric resonance modes. To characterize the near-field properties of the two modes, we performed PEEM measurements, including near-field intensity spectra and mode dynamics. This method has been used previously to study various near-field modes supported by metals and dielectrics. ,,,,− For the near-field intensity spectra, a laser beam with tunable wavelengths was incident at the normal angle, and the polarization was controlled using a λ/2 waveplate. Near-field imaging in the near-infrared using PEEM relies on a multiphoton photoemission process to overcome the material’s work function. , The nonlinear photoemission intensity follows PE ∝ IN , where P E is the photoemission intensity, I is the local light intensity, and N is the nonlinear order. Here, N = 3.6–4.2, as measured for different samples (see Figure S10). As shown in Figure a, the resonance peaks of the near-field intensity spectra are generally consistent with the far-field reflectance spectra. The photoemission intensity of the dielectric mode is ∼300 times stronger than that of the plasmonic mode. Since the simulated near-field intensities do not differ greatly (Figure S11), the difference in photoemission intensity can be understood by the distinct hotspot distribution. As shown in Figure b, the hotspots of the dielectric mode are at the lower part of MoOCl2 disk (the Au/MoOCl2 interface). In contrast, the hotspots of plasmonic mode are at the upper part of MoOCl2 disk (the top edge). The photoemission efficiency of Au is much greater than that of MoOCl2, resulting in a significant difference in photoemission intensity. Thus, the difference in photoemission intensity between the two modes indicates their difference in hotspot distribution. In terms of applications, the different hotspot distributions at the upper and lower parts of the MoOCl2 nanostructure offer an additional degree of freedom for controlling the nanoscale light field.

The mode dynamics were measured using time-resolved PEEM (TR-PEEM), , as shown in Figure c. A 7.0 fs laser source with a broad wavelength range of 670–950 nm was used to build the interferometric pump–probe beamline. The pump and probe pulses were generated by a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. Using TR-PEEM allows one to observe the time-domain field evolution and obtain the dephasing time. The dephasing time is the decay time of the electric amplitude (|E|) of the mode and equals twice the energy lifetime (|E|2 or intensity I). Therefore, time-domain dephasing is correlated with the Q factor obtained in the frequency domain. The mode dephasing times were obtained by fitting the interferometric curves. The dephasing times for the x- and y-polarizations were fitted to be 4.5 and 10 fs, respectively. Note that a surface cleaning process involving moderate Ar etching was performed prior to the PEEM measurements. This process reduced the Q-factor of the two modes by introducing extra loss, but the reduction was still acceptable.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our experiments demonstrate that the metal-dielectric duality of MoOCl2 enables the coexistence of metal and dielectric resonance modes in a single nanostructure, beyond the scope of conventional hybrid metal-dielectric systems. The two modes are nonhybrid and are controlled independently by two orthogonal in-plane polarizations, without mode mixing or crosstalk. By tuning the structural parameters, we achieved an optimal Q factor of 45.3 for the dielectric mode in our experiments, which is 5.7 times larger than that of the plasmonic mode. PEEM measurements further reveal that the dielectric mode exhibits much stronger photoemission enhancement than the plasmonic mode because of the different hotspot distributions of the two modes. Importantly, even under spectral overlap, the two resonances remain polarization-orthogonal and independently addressable, enabling polarization switching at a fixed wavelength without crosstalk. The distinct hotspot distributions (top surface vs Au/MoOCl2 interface) provide an additional degree of freedom that can be leveraged for layer-selective excitation/readout and multifunctional nanophotonic designs.

Methods

Sample Fabrication and Characterizations

The MoOCl2 flakes were mechanically exfoliated on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) sheets from bulk crystals (Nanjing MKNANO Tech. Co., Ltd. (www.mukenano.com)), and the thicknesses of the MoOCl2 flakes were estimated by the reflected color under optical microscopy before transfer. The selected MoOCl2 flakes were transferred onto a substrate using the all-dry transfer method under optical microscopy. Each substrate was premarked with gold markers for positioning.

Silica substrates with gold markers were used to sputter 100 nm Au, with a 2 nm Ti adhesion layer (MPS-4000C1/HC1, ULVAC). Then, 2.5 nm Al2O3 layers were grown on the substrates using atomic layer deposition (ALD) at a rate of 1 nm/10 cycles (R-200 advanced, Picosun). Next, the MoOCl2 flakes were transferred onto the Al2O3/Au/SiO2 substrates.

The nanofabrication workflow mainly includes electron-beam lithography (EBL) and reactive ion etching (RIE), with an EB resist as a mask. Before EBL, an EB resist (ZEP520A: ZEP-A = 1:1, Zeon Chemicals) was spin-coated onto the substrate with MoOCl2 flakes at 300 rpm for 3 s and 3000 rpm for 60 s. Next, the substrate was prebaked at 180 °C for 2 min. The EBL system (ELS-F130HM, Elionix) operated at 130 kV with an electron current of 100 pA. After EBL, the sample was developed with ZED-N50 for 60 s and ZED-B for 10 s. Then, RIE (RIE-101iPH, Samco Inc.) was used to etch the MoOCl2 with SF6 gas. After RIE, the EB resist was cleanly removed using the liftoff (DMAC) process at 70 °C for 20–30 min, without ultrasonication. Finally, the sample was cleaned with acetone and methanol.

After preparation, the samples were characterized by using optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (SU8230, Hitachi High-Technologies), and a thickness measuring instrument (ET200, Kosaka Laboratory, Ltd.).

Spectral Measurements

The resonance modes of MoOCl2 metasurfaces were characterized using reflectance spectra, with a home-built optical microscopy system equipped with a photonic multichannel analyzer (PMA C7473, Hamamatsu Photonics). A linear polarizer was used to control the polarization states. A silver mirror was used as the reference, and calibration was performed using the reflectance of silicon to obtain the actual spectra.

PEEM Measurements

The PEEM measurements were performed on a high-resolution low-energy electron microscopy (LEEM)/PEEM system (ACSPELEEM III, Elmitec GmbH). To obtain near-field intensity spectra, a commercial Ti:sapphire femtosecond laser (Mai Tai HP, Spectra-Physics, pulse duration: 80–100 fs) operating at 690–1040 nm (2.9 W @ 820 nm) with an 80 MHz repetition rate was used to provide a tunable laser for PEEM measurements. To obtain the mode dynamics, another laser source (Rainbow, Femtolaser) with a pulse duration of approximately 7.0 fs and a spectrum covering 670–950 nm was used for TR-PEEM measurements. Two chirped mirror pairs and one wedge pair were used for dispersion compensation. A Mach–Zehnder interferometer was used to generate pump and probe pulses with a power ratio of 1:1. The time delay was controlled in 0.35 fs steps, corresponding to 2π/8 phase delay for the carrier wavelength of 830 nm. Both laser beams were focused on the sample surface at normal incidence with a spot diameter of approximately 100 μm. The polarization of the laser was controlled by a λ/2 waveplate. A moderate Ar etching was performed prior to the PEEM measurements by RIE-101iPH, Samco Inc. (0.5 Pa, 50 W, 10 s). This step is intended to remove minimal organic residues from the surface, because PEEM is an inherently surface-sensitive technique.

Simulations

The resonance modes of MoOCl2 metasurfaces were simulated using the finite-difference time-domain method (FDTD, Ansys Lumerical), using the measured permittivity (ε x , ε y ) and theoretical permittivity (ε z ). The optical properties of Au were obtained from Johnson and Christy, and the refractive index of the glass substrate was set to 1.5. The excitation light was set to normal incidence with the required polarization states. The FDTD simulations well reproduced the experimental results.
