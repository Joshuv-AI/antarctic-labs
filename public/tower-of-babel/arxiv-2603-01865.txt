CyclicJudge: Mitigating Judge Bias Efficiently in LLM-based Evaluation
Ziyi Zhu, Olivier Tieleman, Alexey Bukhtiyarov, Jinghong Chen

Abstract
LLM-as-judge evaluation has become standard practice for open-ended model assessment; however, judges exhibit systematic biases that cannot be eliminated by increasing the number of scenarios or generations. These biases are often similar in magnitude to the model differences that benchmarks are designed to detect, resulting in unreliable rankings when single-judge evaluations are used. This work introduces a variance decomposition that partitions benchmark score variance into scenario, generation, judge, and residual components. Based on this analysis, CyclicJudge , a round-robin assignment of judges to scenarios, is demonstrated to be the optimal strategy for a fixed judge-call budget. It eliminates bias precisely while requiring each judge only once per cycle, matching the cost of single-judge evaluation. Empirical results on MT-Bench and MindEval validate the effectiveness of CyclicJudge as predicted, across both general-purpose and domain-specific evaluation settings.



1
Introduction

LLM-as-judge evaluation has become the de facto standard for open-ended model assessment
(
27
)
. A growing body of work indicates that LLM judges are systematically biased: some models are consistently lenient, others strict, with additional position, verbosity, and self-preference effects
(
27
;
21
;
23
)
. A standard Analysis of Variance (ANOVA;
6
) on our data confirms that judge main effect is highly significant for all evaluated models (§
4.2
).

Crucially, systematic bias does not average away. Adding more test scenarios or regenerating responses reduces random noise but leaves judge bias intact. The only way to eliminate judge bias is to use multiple judges whose biases cancel. Yet running every judge on every item multiplies evaluation cost by the panel size and sacrifices generation diversity at a fixed budget.

We show that
CyclicJudge
, a round-robin assignment of judges to items, resolves this tension. To establish this formally, we develop a variance decomposition rooted in generalizability theory
(
2
;
20
)
that separates benchmark score variance into scenario, generation, judge, and residual components. Our contributions are:

1.

A mixed-effects model that decomposes benchmark variance into random noise and systematic judge bias, showing they require fundamentally different remedies (§
3.1
–
3.2
).

2.

A proof that round-robin cycling achieves lower variance than alternative strategies: it eliminates bias while maximizing generation diversity at any budget (§
3.3
).

3.

Empirical results on MT-Bench and MindEval validating the effectiveness of CyclicJudge as predicted, across both general-purpose and domain-specific evaluation settings (§
4
).

3
Methodology

3.1
Model Specification

Consider evaluating model
\theta
on
n
scenarios from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of
N
total scenarios, with
m
independent generations per scenario, each scored by
K
judges drawn from a pool of
K_{\text{tot}}
. The same
K
judges score every scenario–generation cell (a crossed design). We model each score as:

X_{ij\ell}=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\ell}

(1)

where
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
indexes scenarios,
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
indexes generations within scenario
i
, and
\ell
indexes the
K
selected judges. Each term has a distinct source:

•

\mu_{\theta}
: true ability of model
\theta
(the estimand).

•

\alpha_{i}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}(0,\,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2})
: scenario effect—how much individual scenario difficulty varies for model
\theta
.
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}
absorbs the scenario–model interaction; a dataset whose scenarios all test the same aspects of model
\theta
equally has
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}=0
.

•

\beta_{ij}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}(0,\,\sigma_{\beta}^{2})
: generation effect—variability from stochastic decoding.
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}
is model-specific; a deterministic model has
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}=0
.

•

\gamma_{\ell}
: judge bias—a fixed constant for each judge, with centering constraint
\sum_{\ell=1}^{K_{\text{tot}}}\gamma_{\ell}=0
and population dispersion
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}=K_{\text{tot}}^{-1}\sum_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}^{2}
. It is fixed rather than random because judge tendencies are persistent properties documented in prior work
(
23
;
9
)
.

•

\varepsilon_{ij\ell}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}(0,\,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
: residual—captures judge-level noise and all interaction terms; we discuss these modeling choices in Appendix
I
.

The random effects
(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon)
are mutually independent with finite second moments; no distributional assumptions (e.g. Gaussianity) are needed. The
n
scenarios and
K
judges are each drawn by simple random sampling without replacement, independently of each other and of all random effects.

3.2
Variance Decomposition

The benchmark score is the grand mean
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{nmK}\sum_{i,j,\ell}X_{ij\ell}
, which decomposes as
\bar{X}=\mu_{\theta}+\bar{\alpha}+\bar{\beta}+\bar{\gamma}+\bar{\varepsilon}
, where each bar denotes a hierarchical sample mean.

Proposition 1

(Variance decomposition)
.

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X})=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}{nm}+\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nmK}}_{\text{random noise}}+\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}}{K}\cdot\frac{K_{\text{tot}}-K}{K_{\text{tot}}-1}}_{V_{\gamma}\text{: judge bias}}

(2)

Each noise term shrinks with more data at or above its hierarchy level. The bias term
V_{\gamma}
responds only to
K
and vanishes exactly at
K=K_{\text{tot}}
. The proof uses the law of total variance, conditioning on the judge selection, combined with a finite population correction for sampling
K
from
K_{\text{tot}}
centered bias values (Appendix
C
).

3.3
Allocation Strategies

Given a per-scenario budget of
B
judge calls, the design question is how to allocate judges across generations. The scenario term
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}/n
is fixed once scenarios are chosen; we compare three strategies for the remaining variance.

Strategy A: All judges per generation.

Use all
K_{\text{tot}}
judges for each of
m=B/K_{\text{tot}}
generations. Judge bias cancels exactly (
\bar{\gamma}=0
):

V_{A}(B)=\frac{K_{\text{tot}}\,\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(3)

Strategy B: Random single judge per generation.

Use
m=B
generations, each scored by one randomly sampled judge. The random bias acts as additional noise:

V_{B}(B)=\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(4)

Strategy C: CyclicJudge (round-robin).

Use
m=B
generations (
B
divisible by
K_{\text{tot}}
), assigning judge
j\bmod K_{\text{tot}}
to generation
j
. Bias cancels exactly while generation count matches Strategy B:

V_{C}(B)=\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(5)

With one generation per scenario (
m=1
), the same variance and bias cancellation are achieved by cycling judges across scenarios instead: assign judge
i\bmod K_{\text{tot}}
to scenario
i
(
n
divisible by
K_{\text{tot}}
).

Advantage of CyclicJudge.

Direct comparison (Appendix
E
) shows
V_{C}\leq\min(V_{A},V_{B})
for all
B
, with gaps of
O(1/B)
, so CyclicJudge’s advantage is largest at small budgets of judge calls, where most benchmarks operate. The ranking of A vs. B depends on whether
(K_{\text{tot}}{-}1)\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\gtrless\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
: all judges beat random only when judge bias is large relative to generation variance.

Experiments

4.1
Domain and Data

We validate on two benchmarks that span different evaluation regimes.

MT-Bench

(
27
)
is a general-purpose multi-turn conversational benchmark with 80 two-turn questions spanning 8 categories, scored on a 1–10 scale per turn (averaged).

MindEval

(
16
)
is a domain-specific benchmark for multi-turn mental health support with 50 scenarios and composite scores on a 1–5 scale covering empathy, safety, and clinical appropriateness. Each scenario simulates a full 10-turn therapeutic session with a synthetic patient.

For both benchmarks, we evaluate five models—Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct
(
25
)
, Llama 3.3 70B Instruct
(
7
)
, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4.6—which also serve as judges in a symmetric design. MT-Bench uses
m{=}10
generations per scenario (4,000 scores per model); MindEval uses
m{=}5
(1,250 scores per model). Variance components are estimated via crossed ANOVA (Appendix
D
).

5
Conclusion

We develop a variance decomposition for LLM-as-judge evaluation that separates systematic judge bias from random noise and use it to derive CyclicJudge, a round-robin judge assignment strategy. Our analysis and experiments on both a general-purpose benchmark (MT-Bench) and a domain-specific clinical benchmark (MindEval) yield three main findings: (1) Systematic judge bias dominates benchmark score variance at default operating points. (2) Random noise components decrease with model capability and multi-turn evaluation, but judge bias does not. (3) CyclicJudge achieves the lowest variance among all allocation strategies at every budget level, regardless of the model’s variance profile, by eliminating bias exactly while maximizing generation diversity. The method requires no model-specific tuning and maintains the same per-item cost as single-judge evaluation, providing practitioners with a cost-neutral drop-in replacement for more reliable LLM evaluation.

Limitations

Linear model approximation.

Our variance decomposition assumes a linear random effects model, which treats scores as unbounded and continuous. LLM-as-judge scores are in fact bounded ordinal variables, so the linear model can produce boundary artifacts and heteroscedastic residuals near the scale endpoints. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), such as a cumulative-link mixed model, would properly account for the ordinal, bounded nature of the responses. However, GLMMs do not yield the closed-form variance decomposition that our allocation analysis relies on. We therefore retain the linear approximation, noting that it remains reasonable when the scale range is large relative to the observed score dispersion, as in our benchmarks.

Judge pool size.

Our judge pool of
K_{\text{tot}}=5
limits exploration of larger panels. While the theoretical results generalize to arbitrary
K
, empirical validation with larger and more diverse judge pools would strengthen the practical recommendations.

Scenario exchangeability.

The “maximize scenarios” recommendation assumes scenarios are interchangeable draws from the dataset; in practice, some scenarios may be more informative than others, and item-selection methods
(
12
)
could alter the tradeoff.

Balanced design assumption.

The allocation analysis assumes a balanced design in which every scenario receives the same number of judge calls. Extensions to adaptive or sequential designs remain future work.

Uniform cost assumption.

Our budget analysis treats all judge calls as equal cost; in practice, judges vary widely in price and latency. A cost-weighted allocation that accounts for per-model pricing and inference speed would be needed for real deployment decisions.

Appendix C
Proof of Proposition
1

We prove the variance decomposition in three steps: (1) grand mean decomposition, (2) law of total variance, and (3) finite population correction for judge bias.

Step 1: Grand mean decomposition.

Starting from
X_{ij\ell}=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\ell}
, we average hierarchically.

Average over
K
judges within generation
(i,j)
.
The terms
\mu_{\theta}
,
\alpha_{i}
, and
\beta_{ij}
are constant across judges:

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{ij\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell\in S_{J}}X_{ij\ell}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{ij}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell\in S_{J}}\gamma_{\ell}}_{\bar{\gamma}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell\in S_{J}}\varepsilon_{ij\ell}}_{\bar{\varepsilon}_{ij}}

(6)

The judge bias mean
\bar{\gamma}
is the same for every
(i,j)
cell because the same
S_{J}
evaluates all cells (crossed design).

Average over
m
generations within scenario
i
.

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{i\cdot\cdot}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{ij}}_{\bar{\beta}_{i}}+\bar{\gamma}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{mK}\sum_{j,\ell}\varepsilon_{ij\ell}}_{\bar{\varepsilon}_{i\cdot}}

(7)

Average over
n
scenarios.

\displaystyle\bar{X}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}+\bar{\gamma}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}}_{\bar{\alpha}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}\beta_{ij}}_{\bar{\beta}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{nmK}\sum_{i,j,\ell}\varepsilon_{ij\ell}}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}

(8)

Step 2: Law of total variance.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the judge selection. By the law of total variance:

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X})=\operatorname{E}\bigl[\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S})\bigr]+\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S}]\bigr)

(9)

Conditioning on
\mathcal{S}
:

Term (I): Conditional variance.
Given
\mathcal{S}
, the bias
\bar{\gamma}
is a constant. The remaining randomness comes from
\bar{\alpha}
,
\bar{\beta}
,
\bar{\varepsilon}
, which are means of mutually independent zero-mean random variables:

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S})=\frac{\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}{nm}+\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nmK}

(10)

This does not depend on
\mathcal{S}
, so
\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S})]
equals the same expression.

Term (II): Variance of conditional mean.
Since
\operatorname{E}[\alpha_{i}]=\operatorname{E}[\beta_{ij}]=\operatorname{E}[\varepsilon_{ij\ell}]=0
:

\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S}]=\mu_{\theta}+\bar{\gamma}\implies\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}\mid\mathcal{S}]\bigr)=\operatorname{Var}(\bar{\gamma})

(11)

Step 3: Finite population correction.

\bar{\gamma}
is a sample mean of
K
values drawn without replacement from the centered population
\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K_{\text{tot}}}\}
.

Lemma 2

(Finite population correction)
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{P}
satisfy
\sum_{i}a_{i}=0
with
\sigma_{a}^{2}=P^{-1}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}
. If
S
is a simple random sample of size
k
drawn without replacement:

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{a}^{2}}{k}\cdot\frac{P-k}{P-1}

(12)

Proof.

Define indicator variables
Z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for
i=1,\ldots,P
, where
Z_{i}=1
if and only if element
i
is selected in the sample
S
. The sample sum is
T=\sum_{i=1}^{P}Z_{i}a_{i}
, and the sample mean is
\bar{a}_{S}=T/k
.

Under simple random sampling without replacement (SRSWOR) of size
k
from
P
elements, each element is selected with probability
\Pr(Z_{i}=1)=k/P
, so:

\operatorname{Var}(Z_{i})=\frac{k}{P}\!\left(1-\frac{k}{P}\right)=\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}}

(13)

For
i\neq j
, the joint selection probability is
\Pr(Z_{i}{=}1,\,Z_{j}{=}1)=\frac{k(k-1)}{P(P-1)}
, giving:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{i},Z_{j})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}[Z_{i}Z_{j}]-\operatorname{E}[Z_{i}]\operatorname{E}[Z_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{k(k{-}1)}{P(P{-}1)}-\frac{k^{2}}{P^{2}}=-\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}(P{-}1)}

(14)

Since
T=\sum_{i}Z_{i}a_{i}
and the
a_{i}
are constants, expanding the variance gives:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{P}a_{i}^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}(Z_{i})+2\!\sum_{i<j}a_{i}a_{j}\,\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{i},Z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}-\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}(P{-}1)}\cdot 2\!\sum_{i<j}a_{i}a_{j}

(15)

We now simplify each factor. By definition,
\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}=P\sigma_{a}^{2}
. For the cross-term, the centering constraint
\sum_{i}a_{i}=0
implies:

\begin{split}0=\Bigl(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Bigr)^{\!2}=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}+2\!\sum_{i<j}a_{i}a_{j}\\
\implies 2\!\sum_{i<j}a_{i}a_{j}=-P\sigma_{a}^{2}\end{split}

(16)

Substituting both identities into (
15
) (the two negatives in the second term yield a positive contribution):

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}}\cdot P\sigma_{a}^{2}+\frac{k(P{-}k)}{P^{2}(P{-}1)}\cdot P\sigma_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{k(P{-}k)\,\sigma_{a}^{2}}{P{-}1}

(17)

Finally, dividing by
k^{2}
:

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{a}_{S})=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(T)}{k^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{a}^{2}}{k}\cdot\frac{P{-}k}{P{-}1}\qed

(18)

The FPC factor equals 1 when
k=1
and 0 when
k=P
(biases cancel exactly). Applying to judge biases (
a_{\ell}=\gamma_{\ell}
,
P=K_{\text{tot}}
,
k=K
):

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{\gamma})=\frac{\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}}{K}\cdot\frac{K_{\text{tot}}-K}{K_{\text{tot}}-1}

(19)

Combining Terms (I) and (II) completes the proof.

Appendix D
Estimation of Variance Components

Because judges are crossed (not nested), we use a crossed ANOVA.

Residual noise.

The residual mean square estimates
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MS}_{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i,j,\ell}(X_{ij\ell}-\bar{X}_{ij\cdot}-\bar{X}_{\cdot\cdot\ell}+\bar{X}_{\cdots})^{2}}{(nm-1)(K-1)}

(20)

Generation variance.

The between-generation mean square:

\mathrm{MS}_{G}=\frac{K}{n(m-1)}\sum_{i,j}(\bar{X}_{ij\cdot}-\bar{X}_{i\cdot\cdot})^{2}

(21)

with
\operatorname{E}[\mathrm{MS}_{G}]=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+K\sigma_{\beta}^{2}
. Judge bias drops out because the same judges evaluate every generation within a scenario.

Scenario variance.

The between-scenario mean square:

\mathrm{MS}_{S}=\frac{mK}{n-1}\sum_{i}(\bar{X}_{i\cdot\cdot}-\bar{X}_{\cdots})^{2}

(22)

with
\operatorname{E}[\mathrm{MS}_{S}]=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+K\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+mK\,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}
.

Method-of-moments estimators.

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{MS}_{G}-\mathrm{MS}_{W})/K

(23)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{MS}_{S}-\mathrm{MS}_{G})/(mK)

(24)

Judge bias.

We estimate
\hat{\gamma}_{\ell}=\bar{X}_{\cdot\cdot\ell}-\bar{X}_{\cdots}
and compute:

\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}=\frac{1}{K_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{\ell}\hat{\gamma}_{\ell}^{2}-\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nm}\cdot\frac{K_{\text{tot}}-1}{K_{\text{tot}}}

(25)

Appendix E
Allocation Strategy Derivations

We derive the benchmark score variance for each strategy. The
n
scenarios are fixed, so
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}/n
contributes a constant offset and is excluded.

Strategy A: All
K_{\text{tot}}
judges per generation.

Budget
B=mK_{\text{tot}}
, so
m=B/K_{\text{tot}}
. Per-scenario mean:

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\beta_{ij}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{K_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}}_{=\,0}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{mK_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{j,\ell}\varepsilon_{ij\ell}

(26)

Judge bias cancels exactly. The benchmark variance from generation and residual noise:

V_{A}=\frac{1}{n}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}{m}+\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{mK_{\text{tot}}}\right)=\frac{K_{\text{tot}}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(27)

Strategy B: Random single judge per generation.

Budget
B=m
, each generation scored by
\ell_{j}\sim\text{Uniform}(\{1,\ldots,K_{\text{tot}}\})
. Per-scenario mean:

\bar{X}_{i}=\mu_{\theta}+\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\bigl(\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{\ell_{j}}+\varepsilon_{ij\ell_{j}}\bigr)

(28)

Each term in the sum is independent with variance
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
:

V_{B}=\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(29)

Strategy C: Round-robin cycling.

Budget
B=m
(multiple of
K_{\text{tot}}
), generation
j
scored by judge
j\bmod K_{\text{tot}}
. Each judge appears
m/K_{\text{tot}}
times, so bias cancels:

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\gamma_{j\bmod K_{\text{tot}}}=\frac{1}{K_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K_{\text{tot}}}\gamma_{\ell}=0

(30)

The residuals for different
j
are independent with variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
:

V_{C}=\frac{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{nB}

(31)

Pairwise comparisons.

Direct subtraction yields:

\displaystyle V_{A}-V_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{(K_{\text{tot}}-1)\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}{nB}\geq 0

(32)

\displaystyle V_{B}-V_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}}{nB}\geq 0

(33)

\displaystyle V_{A}-V_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{(K_{\text{tot}}-1)\sigma_{\beta}^{2}-\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}}{nB}

(34)

Appendix F
Scenario Coverage

The strategies above fix
n
and optimize the per-scenario budget. A complementary question is how to split a total generation budget
B_{\text{gen}}=nm
between scenarios and generations per scenario. Under cycling, substituting
n=B_{\text{gen}}/m
into Eq.
5
gives:

V_{C}(B_{\text{gen}},m)=\frac{m\,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}+\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{B_{\text{gen}}}

(35)

This is linear in
m
with positive slope
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}/B_{\text{gen}}
, so variance is minimized at
m=1
, maximizing the number of distinct scenarios up to the dataset size
N
. The same monotonicity holds for Strategies A and B. Once the scenario pool is exhausted (
n=N
), excess budget should go to additional generations.

Appendix G
Exact Bootstrap Predictions

The allocation formulas in §
3.3
assume infinite-population generation draws. When validating via bootstrap subsampling from a finite pool of
m_{\max}
observed generations, we compute exact predictions using the empirical pool variances. For all three strategies, the subsampling observations within each scenario are i.i.d. draws from the relevant pool, so
V(B)=C/(nB)
where
C
is the pool variance.

Strategy A.

Each draw is a generation-level mean
\bar{X}_{ig\cdot}
, with
m=B/K_{\text{tot}}
draws per scenario. The pool variance is:

C_{A}=K_{\text{tot}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{m_{\max}}\sum_{g}(\bar{X}_{ig\cdot}-\bar{X}_{i\cdot\cdot})^{2}

(36)

Strategy B.

Each draw is a single cell
X_{ig\ell}
with generation and judge chosen uniformly at random, with
m=B
draws per scenario:

C_{B}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{m_{\max}K_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{g,\ell}(X_{ig\ell}-\bar{X}_{i\cdot\cdot})^{2}

(37)

Strategy C.

Each judge
\ell
gets
B/K_{\text{tot}}
independent draws from its column; the per-scenario mean averages these
K_{\text{tot}}
independent sub-means. The relevant pool variance is the within-column generation variance:

C_{C}=\frac{1}{nK_{\text{tot}}}\sum_{i,\ell}\frac{1}{m_{\max}}\sum_{g}(X_{ig\ell}-\bar{X}_{i\cdot\ell})^{2}

(38)

These empirical constants converge to the population formulas (Eqs.
3
–
5
) as
m_{\max}\to\infty
, but for finite
m_{\max}
they account for the bias in plug-in variance estimates (the
\nicefrac{{(m_{\max}{-}1)}}{{m_{\max}}}
correction) and the fact that the crossed ANOVA residual uses global judge centering while
C_{C}
uses within-scenario-judge centering.

Appendix I
Omitted Interactions

A full factorial design with three facets (scenario, generation, judge) has
2^{3}=8
terms: a grand mean, three main effects, three two-way interactions, and one three-way interaction. Our model (Eq.
1
) retains the grand mean and three main effects, absorbing all interaction terms into the residual
\varepsilon_{ij\ell}
. We enumerate each omitted interaction below.

Scenario–generation.

Because generations are nested within scenarios rather than crossed with them, there is no separable scenario–generation interaction: any such effect is already confounded with the generation main effect
\beta_{ij}
, which captures all within-scenario generation variability by construction.

Scenario–judge.

This interaction captures the extent to which a judge’s bias varies across scenarios (e.g., a judge being lenient on reasoning tasks but strict on creative ones). It is in principle estimable, but explicitly modeling it would add
n(K_{\text{tot}}{-}1)
parameters and complicate the closed-form variance decomposition without changing the allocation analysis: the interaction enters only through the per-cell residual variance, which our empirical predictions (§
4
) already capture without parametric assumptions on its structure.

Generation–judge.

This interaction reflects judge-specific reactions to particular generation artifacts (e.g., one judge penalizing verbose outputs more than another). Since generations are independent stochastic draws, this term is exchangeable across
j
and acts as additional i.i.d. noise on each cell, making it naturally absorbed into
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
.
