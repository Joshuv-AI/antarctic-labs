Evaluation of microtensile bond strength to dentin and adhesive layer thickness of a new generation two-bottle universal adhesive
Authors: Ahmet Özlü, Gülşah Tonga, Hüseyin Hatırlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the immediate and aged microtensile bond strengths (μTBS) and pre-aging adhesive layer thicknesses of G2-Bond Universal, a novel two-bottle universal adhesive, with those of the gold-standard two-step self-etch adhesive (Clearfil SE Bond) and one-step universal adhesive (G-Premio Bond).Materials and MethodsThirty extracted human molars were randomly assigned to three adhesive systems (n=10 teeth per group). After standardized dentin surface preparation, adhesives were applied according to manufacturers' protocols, followed by incremental composite build-up. Specimens were sectioned to obtain rectangular prism-shaped sticks (1 mm² cross-sectional area), which were allocated to immediate and 6-month water aging subgroups (n=90each). μTBS was measured using a universal testing machine. Adhesive layer thickness was assessed on 90 pre-aging sticks via scanning electron microscopy, with measurements performed using ImageJ software. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA for μTBS and one-way ANOVA for adhesive thickness, followed by Tukey's HSD.ResultsAging showed no statistically significant effect on μTBS (p>0.05). Clearfil SE Bond exhibited the highest µTBS values, significantly outperforming G-Premio Bond (p<0.05), while no statistically significant difference was observed compared with G2-Bond Universal (p>0.05). G2-Bond demonstrated the thickest adhesive layer.ConclusionThe thicker adhesive layer of G2-Bond may enhance its mechanical resilience and clinical durability under functional stresses.

Introduction

Recently, universal adhesives have been developed to simplify clinical procedures by incorporating functional acidic monomers into single-step self-etch systems. These materials offer convenient handling and multiple application options (etch-and-rinse/self-etch), enable bonding to various substrates, exhibit compatibility with resin cements, and reduce application time (1, 2). However, integrating chemically distinct components of universal adhesives into a single bottle has been associated with drawbacks such as reduced shelf life, increased water sorption and nanoleakage, and limited bond durability (1).

During the adhesive procedure, maintaining slight dentin moisture is crucial to promote monomer diffusion. For this reason, 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), which acts as a wetting agent, is added to the content of universal adhesives as a monofunctional monomer. HEMA, a small molecule with a low molecular weight, is a good diffusion mediator. Thanks to its high hydrophilicity, this monomer acts as a solvent for other monomers. Additionally, HEMA helps prevent phase separation in one-step adhesive systems where hydrophobic and hydrophilic components coexist (2). Despite these benefits, the hydrophilic nature of HEMA also facilitates water uptake from dentin and the oral environment, leading to hydrolytic degradation at the adhesive interface and, consequently, a reduction in long-term bond strength (3, 4).

The thickness and quality of the adhesive layer are additional factors influencing the long-term bonding performance of universal adhesives (5, 6). During polymerization, oxygen inhibits free-radical propagation, forming a soft oxygen-inhibition layer on the adhesive surface (7, 8). This layer may facilitate water absorption from the underlying dentin and promote hydrolytic degradation (9, 10). Conversely, a thicker adhesive layer can improve bond performance by mitigating polymerization shrinkage stress (11, 12). Typically, universal adhesives form a layer less than 10 µm thick; however, recent studies have reported thicknesses ranging from 10 to 17 µm among different products (13, 14).

Several investigations have demonstrated that the bond strength of single-bottle universal adhesives can be improved by applying an additional coat of adhesive resin or by placing a layer of flowable composite material (15, 16). Instead of these supplementary applications, newly developed two-step (two-bottle) universal adhesives can be used to achieve the desired adhesive layer thickness. One such example currently available is G2-Bond Universal (GC, Tokyo, Japan), which is designed to provide an optimal bond to both dental hard tissues and composite resin through a gradual transition from hydrophilic to hydrophobic properties. This HEMA-free universal adhesive incorporates functional monomers (4-MET, MDP, MDTP) that contribute to its broad bonding capability. According to the manufacturer, this composition reduces water sorption within the adhesive layer while improving bond strength. The manufacturer also reports an adhesive layer thickness of approximately 35 µm.

Beyond chemical composition, the thickness of the adhesive layer has been suggested to play a critical role in stress distribution and long-term bond durability. A relatively thicker adhesive layer may function as a stress-absorbing or stress-buffering layer, reducing polymerization shrinkage stress and improving interfacial integrity under functional loading (17, 18). Previous studies have demonstrated that increasing the adhesive layer thickness, either through multiple adhesive applications or the use of two-step adhesive systems, can enhance bond stability and reduce interfacial degradation over time (19, 20).

Only a limited number of publications have examined the adhesive layer dimensions and bonding capabilities of G2-Bond Universal (21, 22, 23). Tsujimoto et al. (23) reported that, following thermal cycling, G2-Bond Universal demonstrated higher dentin bonding capacity than the other adhesives tested. Tichy et al. (24) observed no statistically significant difference in the bonding effectiveness of G2-Bond Universal after prolonged water storage. In addition, the same study found that the material exhibited markedly lower water uptake than Clearfil SE Bond (Kuraray Noritake Inc., Okayama, Japan).

Accordingly, the present study aimed to assess and compare the immediate and aged microtensile bond strength as well as the pre-aging adhesive layer thickness of G2-Bond Universal with those of a universal adhesive and a two-step self-etch adhesive. The null hypotheses were that (1) adhesive type and aging would have no effect on microtensile bond strength, and (2) adhesive type would have no effect on adhesive layer thickness.

Materials and Methods

Sample size determination

The required sample size was estimated using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). Assuming an effect size of f = 0.3, with a statistical power (1−β) of 95% and a confidence level (1−α) of 95%. The analysis indicated that a total of 30 teeth were required, with 10 teeth randomly assigned to each experimental group (25).

Sample preparation

Human mandibular molars extracted within the previous six months due to orthodontic or periodontal indications were collected for use in this in vitro research. Teeth exhibiting caries, restorations, or structural defects were excluded, and only sound specimens were retained. After thoroughly removing any residual soft and hard tissues, the teeth were immersed in distilled water containing 1 mg/L thymol and stored at 37 °C until testing. Each specimen was then embedded in acrylic resin blocks, ensuring the tooth surface was positioned approximately 3–4 mm below the cemento-enamel junction.

The occlusal one-third of each crown was cut parallel to the occlusal surface with a precision sectioning device (Micracut 152, Metkon, Turkey) to create flat dentin areas. To ensure a uniform smear layer, the dentin was polished for 60 seconds under continuous water irrigation using 180-grit silicon carbide abrasive paper on a polishing apparatus. Afterward, each specimen was examined under a stereomicroscope (Stemi C-2000; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and any sample showing pulp exposure was eliminated.

Subsequently, the teeth were randomly divided into three experimental groups (n = 10) according to the adhesive system used: two-step self-etch adhesive: Clearfil SE Bond (Kuraray Noritake, Okayama, Japan), universal adhesives: G2-Bond Universal and G-Premio Bond (GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

All adhesives were applied in self-etch mode following the manufacturer's guidelines. A matrix band (SuperMat Universal Dental Matrix, Kerr, Switzerland) was positioned, and a microfilled composite resin (Solare-X; GC, Tokyo, Japan) was layered in two increments of 2 mm each, reaching a total height of 4 mm. Every layer was light polymerized with an LED curing unit (Valo; Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) with a light intensity of 1000 mW/cm². The completed restorations were immersed in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 hours before sectioning. Comprehensive details regarding the materials and procedures are summarized in Table 1

Microtensile bonding strength test

A total of nine stick specimens (approximately 1 mm² cross-sectional area) were obtained from each tooth using a precision cutting device under continuous water irrigation. From each tooth, six sticks were allocated for the microtensile bond strength (μTBS) evaluation, while the remaining three were designated for measuring the adhesive layer thickness. In total, 180 sticks were prepared for μTBS analysis. The cross-sectional area of each composite resin–dentin specimen was determined with a digital caliper.

The specimens were separated into two storage subgroups: one tested after 24 h of water storage (immediate subgroup, n = 90) and the other after 6 months of storage (aged subgroup, n = 90). These sticks were equally and randomly allocated, with three sticks assigned to the immediate testing group and three sticks assigned to the aged group, ensuring that both time points were represented within each tooth. To inhibit bacterial growth, the storage solution consisted of 0.5% Chloramine-T, which was replaced every two weeks.

Each stick was fixed to the testing jig with cyanoacrylate adhesive and tested using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until failure. Bond strength (MPa) was calculated by dividing the load at failure (N) by the measured bonding area (mm²). Testing was performed within 5 minutes of removing each stick from the water.

Evaluation of adhesive layer thickness

Thirty sticks from each adhesive group (total n = 90) were examined under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at 10 kV. The upper surfaces were polished under water using sequential silicon carbide papers (600, 800, and 1000 grit), followed by diamond pastes (6 μm, 3 μm, and 1 μm). After ultrasonic cleaning, specimens were treated with 5% hydrochloric acid (30 s) and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (5 min), rinsed with distilled water, sputter-coated with platinum–palladium, and observed at magnifications above ×2000.

Adhesive layer thickness was measured in micrometers using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA) at three locations: left lateral, central, and right lateral regions. The lateral measurement points were positioned 500 μm from the specimen edges. Three readings were taken at each location, and the average value was recorded for analysis (25). (Figure 1) A schematic overview of the experimental design is presented in Figure 2.

Evaluation of fracture type

After μTBS testing, both ends of each fractured specimen were examined under a stereomicroscope (Stemi C-2000; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at ×10 magnification. Fractures were categorized as adhesive, cohesive within dentin, cohesive within composite, or mixed. (26) Additionally, two representative specimens per group those with bond strength values near the group mean were analyzed under SEM (Mira 3 XMU; Tescan, Brno, Czech Republic) at ×200 and ×500 magnifications to document morphological fracture characteristics.

Statistical analysis

The statistical evaluation was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The tooth was defined as the experimental unit. For each tooth, the µTBS values obtained from individual sticks were averaged to generate a single tooth-level mean value before statistical analysis. Descriptive parameters (mean ± standard deviation) were calculated for each data set. Data distribution normality and variance homogeneity were verified through the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. To examine the effects and interactions of the independent factors on bond strength, a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc comparisons was carried out. Differences in adhesive layer thickness among the experimental groups were determined using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD adjustment. The significance level for all analyses was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Microtensile bond strength analysis

No premature failures were detected during sample preparation or testing. The mean µTBS values and corresponding standard deviations for each group are displayed in Table 2. Statistical analysis with two-way ANOVA revealed adhesive type significantly affected µTBS values (F = 42,004, p<0.001).

The aging process performed by soaking adhesive agents in water increased the μTBS of Clearfil SE Bond while decreasing the μTBS of G2 Bond and G-Premio Bond. However, these effects were not significant among the adhesive agent groups (p>0.05).

The highest μTBS value before aging was observed in the Clearfil SE Bond group. This value was significant with G-Premio Bond (p<0.05) but not significant with G2 Bond Universal (p>0.05). The lowest μTBS value was observed in the G-Premio Bond group (p<0.05).

The highest μTBS value among the post-aging groups was observed in the Clearfil SE Bond group. This value was significant with G-Premio Bond (p<0.05) but not significant with G2 Bond Universal (p>0.05). The lowest μTBS value was observed in the G-Premio Bond group (p<0.05).

Adhesive layer thickness analysis

The mean values and standard deviations of adhesive layer thickness are presented in Table 3.

According to the one-way ANOVA, adhesive type had a statistically significant influence on the layer thickness (p<0.05). Thickness increased progressively from G-Premio Bond to Clearfil SE Bond, with G2-Bond Universal exhibiting the greatest mean thickness. The differences among the adhesives were statistically significant (p<0.05).

Fracture type analysis

The types of fractures observed in the tested groups are summarized in Figure 3. Example SEM images illustrating the different fracture modes are shown in Figure 4. Adhesive failure was identified as the predominant fracture type across all groups. Cohesive failures were noted exclusively within the composite resin, with no cohesive fractures observed in the dentin. In the G-Premio Bond group, all failures were categorized as adhesive. Mixed fracture types appeared in the 6-month Clearfil SE Bond group and both the immediate and 6-month G2-Bond Universal groups.

Discussion

According to the results of this investigation, the first null hypothesis, which proposed that there is no difference between the bond strength of different adhesive agents before and after aging, was partially rejected. Although the aging procedure did not cause a statistically significant reduction in overall bond strength, distinct variations were observed among the individual adhesive systems. The second null hypothesis, which stated that there is no difference between the adhesive layer thicknesses of different adhesive agents, was completely rejected, as the tested materials exhibited statistically significant differences in adhesive layer thickness.

From the outcomes of the present work, after six months of storage in water, μTBS values for Clearfil SE Bond and G2-Bond Universal showed a slight upward trend, whereas G-Premio Bond exhibited a minor decline; these alterations were not statistically significant. This pattern could be associated with the inherent chemical composition of the adhesives and differences in their adhesive layer thickness. G-Premio Bond, as a one-step universal adhesive, contains both hydrophilic and hydrophobic monomers within a single formulation, along with solvents, which may compromise its phase stability and bonding durability over time (25). In contrast, two-step adhesives such as Clearfil SE Bond and G2-Bond require separate application of a primer containing acidic monomers and solvents, followed by a hydrophobic bonding resin (25, 27, 28). This layered configuration provides a physical shield against hydrolytic degradation, enhances monomer penetration into dentin, and facilitates the development of a thicker, more uniform adhesive layer (15, 24). Such characteristics can help maintain or even improve bonding performance over time. Conversely, in single-bottle systems like G-Premio Bond, the thinner adhesive layer and highly hydrophilic nature may lead to greater water uptake and faster breakdown of the hybrid layer during long-term moisture exposure (29, 30).

The relatively stable µTBS values of G2-Bond Universal after 6 months of water storage may be attributed to its HEMA-free chemical composition and two-bottle application approach. Unlike HEMA-containing adhesives, which are prone to increased water sorption and hydrolytic degradation over time, the absence of HEMA in G2-Bond limits water uptake within the adhesive layer. Following polymerization, the separate hydrophobic bonding resin forms a more water-resistant adhesive interface, which may reduce permeability and protect the hybrid layer from hydrolytic breakdown during long-term water exposure. This hydrophobic character of the bonding layer has been reported to enhance the durability of resin–dentin bonds by acting as a barrier against water diffusion and enzymatic degradation, thereby contributing to improved bond stability over time (24, 31).

The functional monomer 10-MDP enhances adhesive performance by chemically bonding to calcium in hydroxyapatite, forming a stable calcium-phosphate salt that contributes to long-term adhesion. Additionally, its amphiphilic molecular structure offers resistance to hydrolytic degradation at the adhesive interface (1, 32, 33). In this study, the lack of a statistically significant effect of aging on bond strength across all adhesive groups may be attributed to the presence of 10-MDP in their formulations, which likely offered some protection against hydrolytic degradation. Nevertheless, a clear hierarchy in bond strength was observed, independent of aging: Clearfil SE Bond > G2-Bond > G-Premio Bond. The significantly lower μTBS values of G-Premio Bond compared to the other two adhesives may be explained by differences in their chemical compositions and bonding strategies. In particular, the reduced MDP concentration and the simplified one-step application protocol in G-Premio Bond may have negatively affected its bonding efficiency and interfacial durability.

G-Premio Bond, which has a relatively low pH, demonstrates limited etching ability when used in self-etch mode, potentially resulting in insufficient micromechanical retention to dentin (34). Moreover, its low MDP concentration, coupled with the coexistence of solvent and monomer in a single phase, may reduce the efficiency and stability of chemical bonding (35, 36). Its highly hydrophilic nature and thin film thickness might also decrease structural strength and overall adhesion performance (22, 37). Additionally, the use of acetone as a solvent may play a role; the rapid evaporation of acetone could leave residual moisture on the dentin surface, leading to incomplete polymerization and compromised interface integrity (38, 39). On the other hand, Clearfil SE Bond and G2-Bond both two-bottle self-etch adhesives that facilitate more effective demineralization and monomer diffusion due to separate primer and bonding steps (40). The formation of a thicker, elastic adhesive layer provides better stress distribution after polymerization, enhancing the overall bonding quality. The superior bond strength of Clearfil SE Bond compared with the other adhesives may also be related to its photoinitiator system. While most adhesives, including G2-Bond, use camphorquinone (CQ) as the primary photoinitiator (41). Clearfil SE Bond incorporates both CQ and a proprietary "Integrated Photoinitiator," which reportedly improves monomer conversion, enhances the mechanical properties of the adhesive, and contributes to higher bond strength (42).

Previous research supports the findings of this study (21, 28). Daneshpooy et al. (43) compared the dentin bonding capabilities of G2-Bond and G-Premio Bond, finding that G2-Bond had significantly superior bond strength. Similarly, studies from 2021 and 2023 evaluated G2-Bond's bonding performance against Clearfil SE Bond (24, 44). Although Clearfil SE Bond exhibited slightly higher bond strength, the difference between the two systems was not statistically significant, and G2-Bond demonstrated comparable performance.

The uniformity and thickness of the adhesive layer are widely acknowledged as critical factors influencing the durability of adhesion (5, 6, 25). Nevertheless, no consensus exists regarding the ideal film thickness for adhesive systems, as multiple parameters including resin viscosity, solvent type, application pressure, air-drying duration, and operator variability affect this characteristic (45). In the present study, significant differences were detected among the tested adhesives. G2-Bond Universal showed the greatest adhesive layer thickness (14.39 ± 9.76 µm), followed by Clearfil SE Bond (8.02 ± 5.31 µm) and G-Premio Bond (4.5 ± 1.64 µm). The increased viscosity of G2-Bond, which is likely related to its HEMA-free formulation and higher content of hydrophobic UDMA monomer, may have enhanced its wettability and produced a thicker and more stable adhesive interface (46). Moreover, the use of a separate primer in two-step adhesive systems facilitates deeper monomer penetration into dentin, improving interaction with the substrate and ultimately increasing the adhesive layer thickness (2, 47). In contrast, the thinner adhesive layer observed in G-Premio Bond can be attributed to its higher solvent content and the strong air-drying process recommended by the manufacturer, which promotes solvent evaporation but reduces film build-up (30). Overall, these findings correspond with earlier observations indicating that one-step universal adhesives generally form thinner adhesive layers compared with two-step systems (25, 48, 49).

It should be noted that adhesive layer thickness is not expected to show a direct linear correlation with bond strength values. Rather contributes indirectly by modulating stress distribution, permeability, and interfacial durability, rather than acting as a direct predictor of bond strength (14, 17, 45). Therefore, in the present study, adhesive layer thickness was evaluated as a supportive structural parameter rather than as a direct predictor of bond strength.

In fracture mode analysis, adhesive failure was predominant across all groups. This finding may be associated with the characteristics of the µTBS testing method, which distributes stress uniformly across the bonding interface. The exclusive presence of adhesive fractures in the G-Premio Bond group could be attributed to its thin adhesive film and limited micromechanical retention (30). In contrast, mixed fractures observed in Clearfil SE Bond and G2-Bond groups indicate stronger and more cohesive bonding interfaces.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted under in vitro conditions, which cannot fully replicate the complexities of the intraoral environment, such as occlusal stresses and blood-saliva contamination. Therefore, clinical studies are necessary to validate these findings and better understand the actual performance of the tested materials.

Conclusion

Based on the outcomes of this in vitro investigation, Clearfil SE Bond achieved the highest μTBS values in both the immediate testing and after six months of water storage. The G-Premio Bond group showed the lowest μTBS values at both time intervals, with all failures identified as adhesive in nature. G2-Bond displayed bond performance similar to that of Clearfil SE Bond and recorded the greatest adhesive layer thickness. Across all groups, cohesive fractures were observed only within the composite resin, indicating the absence of cohesive failures within the dentin substrate.

G2-Bond may offer a buffering effect on the dentin surface due to its dual-bottle structure and thicker adhesive layer, which can be particularly advantageous in stress-bearing restorative applications. While G2-Bond appears promising for improving dentin adhesion, further long-term clinical studies are needed to confirm its durability and effectiveness in various restorative scenarios.
