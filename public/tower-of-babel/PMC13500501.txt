Mathematical analysis of photoreceptor changes in conditions of separation from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium
Authors: Erika T Camacho, Miriam A Goldman, Lucia Gonzalez-Buendia, Daniel E Maidana, Demetrios G Vavvas, Stephen Wirkus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotoreceptors (PR) are responsible for absorbing and converting light into electrical signals necessary to create vision. To accomplish that, they are in an intimate relationship and attached to the underlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Separation of the PR from the underlying RPE is seen in many retina disorders and leads to PR cell death and subsequent vision loss. Separation of PR from the RPE (detachment) interferes with the normal phagocytosis and recycling of PR outer segments by the RPE, and disrupts nutrient and metabolite delivery, including glucose. All these factors contribute to cell dysfunction and eventual cell death. In this work, we sought to understand the importance of different factors in PR cell loss after detachment using mathematical modeling. We used known information of photoreceptor interactions in the healthy retina from the literature, and datasets for rod and cone degeneration after detachment. We also included three additional published datasets of PR cell death kinetics. A mathematical sensitivity analysis examined the impact of the parameters on the system over a detachment of 150 days and found that the parameters that significantly impact the rod and the cone population at the stages where “early intervention” happens (3–7 days after detachment) are not always the same ones that significantly impact the populations at 150 days. Additionally, some of the parameters negatively affected one population while having the opposite effect on the other. An increase in nutrient availability and efficiency of rod energy uptake were the only parameters that did not have a negative effect on either population. Similar results were obtained for reattachment. The interplay between these variables indicates that effective photoreceptor neuroprotection in retinal detachment may require multiple strategies. The prediction of these impactful parameters over time can be further assessed in experimental models and may provide guidance on the most effective ways to improve PR survival after their separation from RPE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53417-3.Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

The retina is the part of the eye responsible for phototransduction. It utilizes millions of photopigment molecules to absorb photons of different wavelengths and convert them into electrical signals that are processed and sent to the brain to form what we call vision. There are two distinct types of photoreceptors involved in phototransduction: rod photoreceptors, responsible for night vision, and cone photoreceptors, responsible for color and daylight vision. Rods and cones are in direct apposition to each other, and both lie in a close relationship with the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), which regulates nutrient flow as well as recycling of PR essential products via phagocytosis (see Fig. 1). Photoreceptors are supplied with oxygen from the choriocapillaris layer underneath the RPE. Rods are more numerous in mammals and make up about 97% of the total photoreceptors in the mouse eye, with cones making up about 3%1,2. The cell bodies of both are located in the outer nuclear layer (ONL)3.

Rods and cones are close in proximity and uptake nutrients, such as oxygen, and metabolites from the same resource pool. In a healthy eye, the rods and cones are able to share these resources and thrive in the same nuclear layer. However, it is poorly understood how resources are utilized in different states of retinal degeneration, and more importantly, how this differential utilization can further affect the survival of the remaining photoreceptors or neighboring cells. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge that excessive delivery can also be harmful. For example, excess oxygen availability can contribute to harmful free radical formation4,5.

Over the course of a day, normal vision processes cause rods and cones to undergo great changes. Each day, new disks are formed, and previously formed disks are shed towards the RPE, and ultimately phagocytosed by the latter. Although phagocytosis follows a circadian rhythm, the overall rate of formation and shedding is kept approximately equal over the course of a day6. Disruptions in shedding and renewal of the photoreceptors are important since they can affect the photoreceptors’ morphology and functionality and, in turn, sight3.

The RPE is a monolayer of pigmented cells that form the outer barrier between the blood and the retina. The RPE transports ions, water, and metabolic products from the sub-retinal space (a potential space) back into the blood. It absorbs nutrients, including glucose, retinol, fatty acids, and other essentials from the blood, and delivers them to the photoreceptors. In our model this is referred to as the trophic pool7. The rods, cones, and RPE have been modeled as a functional unit because of the dependence of the photoreceptors on the RPE8–11,.

Retinal detachment is the separation of the photoreceptors from the RPE. This occurs in many retinal diseases, such as rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, exudative age-related macular degeneration (AMD), central serous chorioretinopathy, diabetic retinopathy, and retinopathy of prematurity, among others. If the PR stay detached for prolonged time, significant cell death will ensue at least partly due to the dysregulation of the PR-RPE dynamics: nutrient delivery, trophic factor composition, and visual cycle homeostasis. Without the support and sufficient metabolic supply for survival, the photoreceptors undergo cell death through mainly caspase regulated apoptosis, and RIPK regulated necrosis. The longer time it takes to reattach the retina the more photoreceptor cell death occurs, with associated vision loss, thought large variability exists12–16. A recent manuscript proposed a math model to study rod dynamics during retinal detachment and our work is complementary to this as we consider cones as well17.

In this manuscript, we present our experimental and mathematical work on retinal detachment and also reattachment in the “Results” section. We consider two different approaches to study retinal detachment: (i) an equilibrium approach where we assume the system is at equilibrium and then determine the parameters to match the detachment data; and (ii) a time series approach where we assume that some parameters have changed as a result of the detachment and use inverse problem methodology to determine these new parameters. Both of these approaches are based on the mathematical model discussed in the “Materials and methods” section. In addition, the same mathematical model can be applied to reattachment of the retina. Thus, we also present (iii) a time series approach for reattachment of the retina, where we compare the resulting reattachment parameters with the corresponding situation of detachment. We present a sensitivity analysis for both time series approaches (detachment and reattachment).

This work aims to understand the importance of different factors and key mechanisms contributing to photoreceptor degeneration, survival, and function after their separation from the RPE through mathematical modeling, utilizing three previously published and one new dataset. Our modeling approach offers a valuable lens to interpret the intricate and interdependent metabolic, structural, and temporal changes that occur after retinal detachment; factors that are difficult, if not impossible, to isolate through experimental methods alone. Our mathematical model focuses on processes needed for the maintenance of photoreceptors. While many cells and processes are connected to their health, including Müller cells and ganglion cells, we focus on the renewal and shedding processes of the photoreceptors, nutrients, and trophic factors that are facilitated by RPE, as described above. Recent work has suggested that the rods, cones, and RPE work as a functional unit18. The results and their implications, as well as the assumptions and simplifications in our model, are important for future research and are further explored in the “Discussion” section.

In Eqs. (1–3), R, C, and T represent the number of rods, number of cones, and the trophic pool, respectively, where the trophic pool encompasses all nutrients, including glucose, oxygen, and metabolites, delivered to the photoreceptors by the RPE and choriocapillaris. Equation (1) describes rod dynamics: rods continuously renew their outer segments (OS), consuming energy in the process (captured by an), and periodically shed the tips of their OS to the RPE, which also requires energy (captured by µn). Equation (2) governs the trophic pool: in the absence of photoreceptors, nutrient inflow from the RPE would reach a maximum level of Γ/κ, where Γ describes nutrient inflow and κ represents the biological constraints that limit how much the RPE can deliver. When rods and cones are present, they draw down this pool at rates β and γ respectively. Equation (3) describes cone dynamics: cones take up glucose at a baseline rate p, which represents the intrinsic glucose demand of cones for OS renewal when no rod influence is present. This baseline uptake is augmented by RdCVF, a protein secreted by rods that stimulates cone glucose uptake by increasing the abundance of the glucose transporter GLUT1; the Holling type III term captures this accelerating, saturable effect, with Vmax representing the maximum achievable rate of GLUT1 formation, km the RdCVF concentration at which half that maximum is reached, and dn the amount of free RdCVF available. Finally, µc represents cone energy consumption associated with OS shedding. Full parameter definitions and values are provided in the “Materials and methods” section.

Discussion

Our mathematical model of photoreceptor interactions allows us to gain quantitative insight into key mechanisms that are at play in photoreceptor degeneration in the case of retinal detachment and reattachment. We used existing data to calibrate the model so that it can extend from qualitative predictions to quantitative ones using inverse problem methodology9,23. These datasets allowed us to examine the key parameters that separately affect rod OS and cone OS degeneration in the case of detachment and their growth after reattachment.

For retinal detachment, our model requires knowledge of the rods and cones separately. While we were able to provide estimates of nutrient decrease based on the three datasets from the literature, the results of our mathematical model required lumping the populations of rods and cones together. To test the importance of separately counting rod and cone populations, we conducted an experiment utilizing the same detachment procedure as the previous three datasets14; see also21. Our results suggest that rods and cones do not share identical death kinetics and thus should be considered separately in any experiments. In particular, our results show that cone death progresses significantly more than rod death and suggests that the cones are not able to adapt to the detachment in the same way that the rods adapt.

The overall nutrient carrying capacity, Dt = Γ/κ,that includes metabolites, oxygen, and glucose, is a key quantity whose change we considered over the course of the observed photoreceptor degeneration period following a retinal detachment. In all four curves of the death kinetics for combined photoreceptor death, see Fig. 2, we observed a decrease in Dt with a maximum change occurring when the photoreceptor death was at its peak. The percentage decrease in Dt ranged from 15.9% to 46.5%. With our estimated parameters, we conducted a sensitivity and uncertainty analysis for rods and cones separately at various time points during detachment, corresponding to early, mid-range, and late-stage interventions. These results showed that the rate of nutrient inflow into the system, Γ, and the limiting effect for the nutrient carrying capacity κ, are significant with the former positively correlated and the latter negatively correlated. The effect of these parameters is seen in the degeneration of both rods and cones. In addition, for cones the parameters µc and p (seen in µc /p: the ratio of cone energy consumption to natural uptake of glucose) are important with consider µc being positively correlated and p being negatively correlated. For rods specifically, the parameters µn and an (seen in Dn = µn /an: the ratio of energy consumption to uptake in the rods), are important with µn being positively correlated and an being negatively correlated. While Γ/κ represents the overall levels of metabolites, oxygen, and glucose going to the system, the cone-specific ratio µc /p and the rod-specific ratio µn /an can be thought of as representing the efficiency to utilize this influx. This suggests that besides ensuring that sufficient amounts of nutrients are available, that therapies can also target the efficiency by which these are utilized. An examination of the equilibrium of our mathematical model confirms results that are recurrent here: affecting nutrient carrying capacity and glucose levels has the greatest effect on the rods and cones during their decay.

In terms of the ratios we can consider µc /p (ratio of cone energy consumption to natural uptake of glucose), Dn = µn /an (the ratio of energy consumption to uptake in the rods), and Dt = Γ /κ (the nutrient saturation level in the RPE), at least one parameter of each of those quantities is seen to affect either the rods or cones or both at every time point. As both rods and cones utilize the same nutrient source and are part of an interrelated system, creating targeted strategies to help one type of photoreceptor may well negatively affect the other type. Increasing Γ and an (energy uptake in rods) are the only parameters that never have a negative effect on rods or cones and thus one could increase the nutrient levels and additionally target the energy uptake in rods without adversely affecting either photoreceptor population. Taken together, our results suggest the importance of experimental work that accounts for rods and cones separately, and for potential targeted therapies that may differ at key points in time.

We considered two modeling approaches: equilibrium and time-series. The fit to data was best for the equilibrium approach. We note that the time-series approach assumes that the initial detachment alters parameter values of the model and these remain unchanged thereafter. As the retina adapts over the course of the detachment, these parameters may in fact change. In particular, there was an initial decrease of the time-series data that was only matched through a significant decrease in Γ; however, leaving Γ at such a low level quickly brought the photoreceptor numbers to near zero and caused a poor fit for the data values at 10 and 14 days. Even with this observation, without specific knowledge of how these may change, we only considered them as an initial permanent change.

For reattachment of the retina, we then examined cone and rod growth and looked at various time points, which could correspond to check-ups after reattachment. For cone OS growth, the results predicted that increases in the parameters p and Γ, corresponding to the natural glucose intake of cones and the influx of trophic factors, respectively, result in the biggest increase in the length of the cone OS. The results also predicted that increases in the parameters κ and µc, corresponding with the limiting effects of available trophic factors and cone energy consumption and metabolism associated with shedding, respectively, result in the biggest decrease in the length of the cone OS. These results apply for a regrowth model, which can be particularly useful in the case of reattachment and suggests that targeting nutrient supplies including availability of metabolites and oxygen, together with glucose availability will have the greatest impact on the growth of the OS. While numerous experimental studies have also reached similar conclusions, our model provides researchers with a method to quantify the level of these contributions. Additionally, our results suggest that focusing some efforts on the energy and metabolism associated with cone OS shedding may be another way to best ensure OS growth after reattachment. We note that our model was that of an eye with rods and cones present in near normal numbers and thus small changes in RdCVF do not have a significant effect. This can be seen in Eq. (3) since the term dn quantifies the RdCVF and, for near normal or comparable levels of R and C, small changes in dn do not have a significant effect.

Of the 16 combinations of significance of parameters presented in Table 1, only one parameter that showed up did so less than four times (γ, corresponding to removal of nutrients, trophic factors, and glucose from the trophic pool). In other words, these results predict a consistent set of parameters that researchers can focus on for detachment and reattachment of rods and cones.

The results of this mathematical modeling study highlight that enhancing nutrient availability (Γ) and improving energy uptake efficiency in photoreceptors—specifically rod energy uptake (an) and cone glucose uptake (p)—are critical therapeutic targets for managing retinal detachment. Practically, this suggests that adjunctive therapies boosting nutrient delivery, such as increasing nutrients through elevated inflow (Γ), and optimizing photoreceptor metabolism, such as inducing metabolic dormancy during detachment to conserve energy and enhancing reparative metabolism after attachment, could significantly improve outcomes alongside surgical re-attachment. Immediate next steps involve developing drugs or supplements targeting these factors. Before re-attachment surgery, minimizing cell death could target glucose transporters and metabolic enzymes to enhance rod energy uptake (an) and cone glucose uptake (p), countering nutrient deprivation. Our sensitivity analysis identifies the nutrient carrying capacity (Γ/κ) and glucose uptake parameters (p, an) as significant determinants of photoreceptor survival. While the physical separation from the RPE almost certainly increases diffusion distance and thus reduces nutrient flux, our model captures this primarily through Γ. The significance of κ, p, and an suggests that even given a fixed reduction in nutrient supply, the efficiency of uptake and transport, which involves glucose transporter activity, may represent an additional modifiable factor. We acknowledge, however, that our model does not explicitly separate diffusion distance from transporter-mediated uptake, and experimental work directly measuring transporter expression and activity in detached retinas would be needed to validate this interpretation. After re-attachment, supporting recovery involves increasing nutrient inflow (Γ) and cone glucose uptake (p) by targeting retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) function and systemic nutrient delivery pathways, such as those regulating blood flow or metabolite transport, to promote photoreceptor regrowth and survival.

This study employs a mathematical model that, while providing valuable insight into the nutrient- and metabolism-driven mechanisms of photoreceptor loss and recovery, is inherently limited by several factors. First, the model omits key biological contributors such as Müller glia, inflammatory responses, and spatial heterogeneity, focusing solely on the dynamics of photoreceptors and nutrient pools. As such, the quantitative predictions represent upper-bound effects under a nutrient-limited scenario and may not be fully generalizable to clinical or experimental settings where additional cell types and inflammatory processes are influential. Second, parameter estimation draws from heterogeneous sources—combining data from different species and experimental designs—which introduces significant uncertainty. We have addressed this by using broad parameter ranges in sensitivity analyses; nonetheless, results should be interpreted qualitatively rather than as precise quantitative forecasts. Third, measurement artifacts (e.g., retinal stretching post-detachment) and the lack of Day 0 normalization in some datasets may result in overestimation of early cell loss, potentially biasing parameter inference. Additionally, the model extrapolates outcomes far beyond far beyond the most dense available experimental time window (up to 150 days), and predictions in these later phases should be viewed as speculative. Lastly, the model operates at the population level, without accounting for individual cell fate variability or local microenvironmental differences. These limitations collectively underscore the need for caution in translating these findings to clinical interventions and highlight directions for future work, including the integration of additional biological factors, spatial modeling, and more refined parameter calibration using consistent, species-specific data.

Mathematical models are always meant to be a starting point and numerous aspects of our model could be extended to explicitly include various factors, additional mechanisms of interaction, and even additional variables such as bipolar cells or Müller cells. A strategy that incorporates analytical and computational models together with experimental results that inform each other provide the best hope of understanding photoreceptor degeneration.

Materials and methods

Retinal detachment data

We induced retinal detachment in 8-week old C57BL/6J mice by subretinal injection of sodium hyaluronate at different time points14. We selected 3 consecutive areas (white rectangles) of the detached (D) and attached retina (A), beginning from the pars plana (pp); see Fig. 9. The first region, closest to the pars plana, was considered peripheral retina, and the two consecutive regions were considered central retina, according to the organizational characteristics in photoreceptor population previously described24. Recent results suggest that the local cell death differences may occur in the detached murine retina21. For this reason, we include in our analysis the apex region of retinal detachment, where photoreceptor cell death occurs more markedly.

A limitation of our approach in this paper could be the stretching of the retina after detachment. Comparing mean number of ONL cells between attached and detached retina can make us overestimate the death of PR due to the stretching of the detached part of the retina, and this can also affect the dramatic loss found at day 1 after detachment. This limitation was overcome in the 2021 paper by analyzing day 0 (immediately after detachment) but this current study didn’t include day 025. Additional data from14 that described TUNEL positive cells was also used. Equation (11) describes the change in the total number of rods. The parameter an in this equation is the energy uptake and metabolism of the rods associated their outer segment (OS) renewal. The parameter µn gives the rate of energy consumption and metabolism associate with OS shedding of the rods. Equation (12) is the equation for the trophic pool, represented by RPE cell function and transport of nutrients from the choriocapillaris through the RPE, T. In this equation β and γ represent the uptake of nutrients from RPE by rods and cones, respectively. With this modeling approach representing in-flow and out-flow for a given variable, these same parameters also incorporate the role of phagocytosis of shed photoreceptor outer segments. In the absence of rods and cones, nutrient flow reaches a carrying capacity, Γ/κ. Thus, we can view Γ as the natural nutrient flow facilitated by the RPE and κ as the term that limits this inflow.

Equation (13) describes the change in the total number of cones. The cone cells intake glucose at a natural rate, denoted by p; this rate of glucose intake is increased due to the effects of rod-derived factor RdCVF as described by29,30. To model this allosteric process, a Holling type III equation gives us an estimate for the glucose intake in the cones that is dependent on RdCVF. Since RdCVF production is directly proportional to how many rods are present in the eye, there is a functional relationship with glucose dependent on RdCVF because there is some point where saturation occurs in high density and at low volumes of glucose there is an accelerating use by the cones29,30. In Eq. (13), Vmax is the rate of formation of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) and dnR represents the total free unbound RdCVF content. Finally, the parameter km is the dissociation constant so that the RdCVF concentration is at a point that permits ½ Vmax or one half of the maximum rate of formation of GLUT1, while µc is the energy consumption and associated metabolism of the cone shedding8.

The qualitative long-term behavior of Eqs. (11–13) were studied in8 with a stability analysis via steady state solutions and bifurcations by examining mathematical relationships between the various parameters without considering any specific parameter values. However, to use these equations to model the effects of retinal detachment on the parameters in the overall death kinetics of the photoreceptors, we need to have an accurate set of parameters. We can use inverse problem methodology to obtain estimates on parameters not available in the literature31. We note that the model focuses on rods, cones, and the trophic pool but excludes other retinal cells (e.g., Müller glia, immune cells) that may influence photoreceptor survival. Müller glia, for instance, provide metabolic support and mediate inflammation, which are significant in detachment-induced degeneration. The model emphasizes nutrient dynamics but we omit other detachment-induced processes, such as inflammation and oxidative stress. Additionally, the model assumes a homogeneous distribution, such as is present in the murine mouse model, but which is different than a human where the distribution is not homogeneous. Extending the model in any of these directions would be a significant contribution to the field but is beyond the scope of this current work. Incorporation of these and other factors that significantly contribute to photoreceptor loss and should be integrated in future work for biological fidelity.

Equilibrium approach for detachment

The mathematical model, Eqs. (11–13) describe the photoreceptor interactions in a non-diseased retina. In the case of retinal detachment, the same model can be used as it is the parameters of the model that have undergone a change. The dataset (C57BL/6J) from our experiment separately counts the rods and cones alive after detachment as previously described and can thus be directly used by our model Eqs. (11–13). However, we also examine three previous datasets on retinal detachment even though these combine the results as total photoreceptors and mark cells as TUNEL positive14. TUNEL staining identifies cells actively undergoing apoptosis at a given moment, not the cumulative number of dead cells. To convert this death-rate signal into an estimate of surviving cells, we integrate the TUNEL-positive cell counts over time and subtract from the initial population, as given in Eq. (14). This approach assumes that the TUNEL signal captures the instantaneous death rate, which when integrated yields total cell loss. Sensitivity analysis and uncertainty analysis

The sensitivity analysis and uncertainty analysis were performed on the model to determine how sensitive the dynamics of the cones and rods are to changes in the parameters. For the method of uncertainty and sensitivity analysis we used a Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC), respectively, from22. We also used a uniform distribution for each parameter that is sampled, then ranked and put back into the model. Then the parameters to which the model output (i.e., cone OS, rod OS, and trophic factor) are most sensitive to were identified. These findings help us understand what can be changed to influence the model output the most. The steps of sensitivity analysis, as developed in22, are as follows:

input the initial guesses of the parameters into the model to achieve an output in terms of the dependent variables in this case rods, trophic factor, and cones;

pick approximate sampling distribution for all parameters within the model. In this case a uniform distribution was used, but a normal or other distribution can also be used;

pick approximate sampling distribution for all parameters within the model. In this case a uniform distribution was used, but a normal or other distribution can also be used;

perform a Monte Carlo simulation to generate the LHS, which allows for an unbiased estimate of the mean of the model output. For this to happen the random parameter distributions are divided into N equal probability intervals. The samples are randomly chosen with at least one value chosen for each interval without replacement. This means that the full range for each parameter is sampled. N is also equal to the amount sampled and should always be at least one more than the number of parameters. This method ensures that the sampling of all parameters are independent of one another. Interpretation of variables: detachment vs. reattachment

In retinal detachment or other conditions in which photoreceptors degenerate, such as retinitis pigmentosa (RP), the experimental data usually estimates dying or surviving cells, e.g., TUNEL assay, ONL count, or ONL thickness. In the death of a photoreceptor be it via apoptosis or necrosis it is not usually the case that the OS shortens to zero length and then the photoreceptor dies (although some shortening may occur); rather, a photoreceptor dies through one of those processes even though the OS is still present at some length (likely shorter than “normal” but also not likely of zero length). Thus, for cell death, the experimental data suggests interpreting the mathematical variables as the number of rods and cones remaining, understanding that they may not be full height. Experimental results also confirm the correlation between ONL thickness and cone cell density32. Thus, a decrease in the model variables R, C corresponds to a decrease in the overall number of rods and cones, respectively, and to a lesser extent, shorter OS. When this model is applied to detachment data a normalized output of 0.5, for example, is interpreted as half of the photoreceptor have died.

For reattachment, we re-define our variables R and C to be proportions of the rod OS and cone OS at full length, where the continuous renewal and periodic shedding of the OS allows for a continuous increase and decrease in the variables. The differential equation model is an “averaged model” that gives overall numbers but does not focus on individual photoreceptors. Thus, the mathematics presents an average over the variables scaled to overall number of OS or normalized number for easier comparison. When this model is applied to reattachment data a normalized output of 0.5, for example, is interpreted as all the OS are at half their full height. These interpretations allow for the death or regrowth of an entire photoreceptor to be represented by the same model.

In retinal detachment and degenerative diseases such as RP, the goal of the mathematical models can be to determine what processes can be altered to stop degeneration. As before, the data suggests that if R, C degenerate more slowly or remain constant, this will allow sight to persist for a longer time. The exact numbers of OS present vs. their exact height is ambiguous in the answer but the conclusion of “sight persists” holds regardless of interpretation. Thus, when we discuss “regrowth”, we are referring to OS height, knowing that the overall number of OS is not changing but their heights are, and assuming that OS regrowth is approximately the same for each OS. When we use the term “degeneration”, we consider cells and OS to be approximately equal and do not consider shortening of the OS to significantly affect this number.

Parameter estimation for reattachment

In order to calibrate our model given in Eqs. (11–13), time series data measuring the growth of OS for multiple time points is needed. In addition, our model requires separation of photoreceptors into rods and cones and not just an examination of, for example, total number of PR cell bodies in the ONL over time that is not able to demonstrate OS length of the photoreceptors. Given that our model was developed for adult organisms, we did not take into account developmental factors and thus excluded early post-natal datasets. Moreover, we chose data from reattachment experiments to estimate some of the parameters in our model, understanding that OS regrowth after reattachment is also not ideal33. As time series data is required and no mouse datasets with the above characteristics exist in the literature, we needed to use data from a different animal model (primate). However, because our model is scalable, any dataset of OS lengths is immediately scaled, so that an OS at full length has a value of 1. This re-scaling helps mitigate differences that may arise by using a non-mouse dataset. The times series data we use is from Guérin and gives reattachment data for the mean of 12 rhesus monkeys23. In this work, the retina was first surgically detached so that most of the rod and cone OS had been eliminated but not the inner segments.
