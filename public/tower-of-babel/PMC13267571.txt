Semaglutide targets muscle mitochondria to regulate glutamine metabolism and treat osteoarthritis
Authors: Yuchen Tian, Zhikai Zheng, Yiyang Ma, Fang Ye, Yidan Pang, Zhicheng Zhang, Zhiwei Li, Changqing Zhang, Junjie Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs a metabolic regulatory drug for lowering body weight, semaglutide has shown additional therapeutic effects in osteoarthritis (OA), but the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. Here, we employed cross-tissue single-cell RNA sequencing analysis and found that semaglutide improved mitochondrial metabolic disorders in muscle tissue under high-fat diet and OA conditions in mice. The results of multi-omics analysis indicated that semaglutide targeted muscle mitochondria to regulate glutamine metabolism during OA. Intramuscular injection of mitochondria from C2C12 cells, pre-stimulated by semaglutide, could alleviate pain symptoms and cartilage damage of OA, which was achieved by inhibiting muscle glutaminase activity and upregulating circulating glutamine concentration. In vitro experiments confirmed the alleviating effect of glutamine produced by C2C12 cells stimulated by semaglutide on chondrocyte inflammation. These findings reveal the mitochondrial regulatory mechanism in the muscle-cartilage axis and provide a perspective for the application of semaglutide in OA treatment.Subject areas: therapeutics, musculoskeletal medicine, human metabolism

Introduction

Metabolic disorders such as obesity have become one of the most severe global health challenges.1 With the continuous spread of the global obesity epidemic, the comorbidity of metabolic diseases such as obese sarcopenia and inflammatory diseases such as osteoarthritis (OA) has become increasingly prominent and a major public health challenge.2,3 Increasing evidences are demonstrating the relevance of metabolic disorders and OA,4,5 but the mechanism is not fully understood and there is still no effective treatment available.6 Recently, in addition to regulating metabolism, semaglutide as one of the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs),7 has shown therapeutic effects on metabolic disorders of sarcopenia,8 and has been validated to significantly improve the disease progression of OA in clinical trails.9,10 However, it is unclear whether the metabolism regulation effects of semaglutide contribute to the progression of OA.

Mitochondria, as the core metabolic organelles and main sites for aerobic respiration and energy production in cells, are important targets for semaglutide,11,12,13 but the regulatory mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Mitochondria participate in systemic metabolic regulation through various pathways such as dynamic network reconstruction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, and metabolite induction,14,15 especially in skeletal muscle, the key metabolic tissue.16 Research has shown that metabolic disorders and increased oxidative stress caused by mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to sarcopenia. Compared to healthy muscles, atrophied muscles produce more ROS and reduce oxygen consumption and ATP synthesis.17 Besides, skeletal muscle mitochondria not only regulate local energy metabolism, but also regulate system homeostasis by activating multiple signaling pathways.18,19 This cross-organizational regulation characteristic makes muscle mitochondria an important target for the treatment of systemic diseases.

Here, we found that semaglutide targeted muscle mitochondria to regulate glutamine metabolism, increasing glutamine concentration in muscle and blood circulation by inhibiting glutaminase (GL) activity, thereby alleviating the progression of OA. This cross-organ regulatory strategy breaks through the limitations of traditional local treatment and provides a paradigm for OA treatment, shifting from symptom control to systemic regulation, especially suitable for patients with OA with metabolic disorders.

Results

Semaglutide alleviated high-fat diet (HFD) and OA and induced changes at the single-cell levels in multiple organs

In order to investigate the interference of semaglutide on metabolic disorders and OA, we constructed HFD-induced obesity mouse models, surgically induced OA mouse models, and mouse models with both diseases coexisting, and used chow diet (CD) mice as controls. Subsequently, we treated four types of mice with semaglutide for a period of 4 weeks (Figure 1A). The weight change results of mice showed that 4-week treatment with semaglutide had no effect on the weight of CD mice, but significantly reduced the weight gain of HFD mice, reflecting the regulatory effect of semaglutide on systemic metabolic disorders (Figures 1B and 1C). Then, we conducted gait analysis experiments on all groups of mice. The results showed that the surgery induced gait pattern damage related to OA in both CD and HFD mice, with HFD OA mice exhibiting more severe symptoms, including increased swing/stance ratio and decreased average pressure of the affected limb, indicating that metabolic disorders further exacerbated the joint pain phenotype of OA mice. Semaglutide treatment significantly alleviated gait pattern impairment in CD OA mice and HFD OA mice. It is worth noting that HFD mice without surgery also showed a certain degree of gait damage, which was alleviated by semaglutide, manifested as a decrease in swing/stance ratio, suggesting that metabolic disorders may be a trigger for OA (Figure 1D). Histological analysis showed that HFD OA mice had more severe cartilage damage compared to the CD group, and the treatment with semaglutide restored the cartilage integrity and maintenance of aggrecans in both CD and HFD OA mice (Figure 1E). These results indicated that semaglutide not only treated OA by improving metabolic disorders under HFD conditions, but also had the potential to directly alleviate the progression of OA.

In order to decipher semaglutide-induced cross-tissue perturbation in HFD and OA mice and identify the main target organs of these regulations, we performed the single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) for immune cells in bone marrow and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) for skeletal muscle, brain, and liver, which are all important organs that have regulatory effects on the whole body. We generated 243,968 high-quality single-cell transcriptomes with 1,589 median genes detected after quality control (Figures 1F and S1A), which constituted a cross-tissue landscape of HFD and OA process and semaglutide treatment. We performed data integration after removing the experimental batch effect, and 57 major cell types across tissues (Figures S1B and S1D) and 71 tissue-specific cell types (Figure 1G) were identified, including Cd34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and variable B cell lineages in bone marrow, Pdgfra+ oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and comprehensive neurons in brain, Pax7+ muscle stem cells (MuSCs) in muscle, hepatoblasts and lymphocytes in liver and blood, respectively. Ptprc+ immune cells enriched in bone marrow and blood scRNA-seq were also found in snRNA-seq samples of liver, indicating a relatively abundant immune-related blood supply microenvironment within the liver. Additionally, neutrophils were predominantly enriched in bone marrow, not PBMCs, reflecting the expected cellular composition and supporting the high quality of the samples for further reliable analysis.

Single-cell transcriptome analysis showed that semaglutide induced cross-tissue metabolic reprogramming

To further discern HFD and OA-dependent cell type-specific effects with or without semaglutide treatment, we initially characterized differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in these two models. Certain studies have highlighted the cellular perturbation of HFD-induced obesity in tissues such as adipose-muscle tissues,20 islets,21 and kidney.22 HFD primarily triggers systemic inflammation, hematopoietic lineage shift,23 and inflammatory response in peripheral organs.24,25,26 In contrast, OA is characterized by inflammatory responses and accelerated aging degeneration of the entire musculoskeletal system.27 At the cellular level, a reciprocal induction relationship exists between HFD and OA.28 Therefore, identifying transcriptional perturbation targets of semaglutide under HFD, OA, and their coexisting conditions through DEGs analysis may provide insights into the underlying regulatory pathways. We first introduced Augur29 to estimate the cell state perturbation across tissues based on the overall transcriptome changes. We observed myocytes and fast-twitch myofibres (type IIx) in muscle were significantly responsive to semaglutide treatment (Figure 2A). Generally, DEGs under HFD and OA conditions after semaglutide treatment were further analyzed in bone marrow, brain and muscle, and the muscle harbored the highest number of DEGs (Figure 2B), with differentiated myocytes and myofibers showing most common and specific DEGs (SFig.1c). Comprehensive assessment revealed that although certain up-regulated genes were shared between muscle and brain, muscle exhibited a more pronounced transcriptional perturbation (SFig.2a), demonstrating that skeletal muscle was significantly more responsive to semaglutide treatment under both disease conditions compared to the immune (bone marrow) and central nervous (brain) systems. The number of common DEGs among the HFD, OA, and combined condition groups was relatively small (Figure S2B). It is worth noting that, in contrast to the DEGs shared between the two conditions with semaglutide treatment across more than two tissues (Figures 2C and S2C), the DEGs under OA conditions with semaglutide treatment demonstrated more up-regulated genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitochondrial gene translation functions, including mt-Nd1, mt-Atp6, and Lars2 (Figures 2D, S2D, and S2E), indicating that mitochondrial function may be a target of semaglutide for treating OA.

To verify the response of muscles to semaglutide, we conducted muscle function and histological analysis on all groups of mice. The ratio of grip strength to body weight showed a significant decrease in grip strength in HFD and HFD OA mice, but not in OA mice, indicating that HFD led to decreased muscle function, which was restored by semaglutide treatment (Figure 2E). Next, we collected and weighed the tibialis anterior muscle, gastrocnemius muscle, and quadriceps muscle of mice. The ratio of muscle weight to body weight showed that semaglutide effectively reversed the imbalance of muscle proportion in HFD mice, manifested as the correction of the inability of muscle growth rate to match body weight (Figure 2F). In addition, HE staining of muscle showed a decrease in cross-sectional area of muscle fiber in HFD OA mice compared to HFD mice, which recovered after treatment with semaglutide (Figure 2G), indicating an interaction between muscle function and OA. These results indicated that semaglutide could improve metabolic disorders and primarily targeted the recovery of sarcopenia and muscle dysfunction.

Given that HFD is a key inducer of OA and the more pronounced transcriptional disturbance of semaglutide in muscles, we focused subsequent analysis on the transcriptional perturbations of muscles from semaglutide treatment under HFD conditions (Figure 3A). We identified 249 specifically upregulated genes and 1,111 specifically downregulated genes (Figure 3B), with substantial transcriptional changes in myocytes and various muscle fiber types (Figure 3C). GO enrichment analysis showed that, in muscle tissue, top upregulated genes by semaglutide were associated with promoting muscle differentiation and muscle tissue development, and reducing muscle hypertrophy, suggesting the effect on muscle repair (Figure 3D). In addition, regulated GSVA pathways of KEGG entries showed that semaglutide treatment upregulated the glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism pathway under HFD conditions, which enhances protection against muscle wasting by activating anabolic pathways and inhibiting proteolytic gene expression30 (Figure 3E), different from the effect of semaglutide under OA conditions (Figure 3F). Besides, the analysis revealed that semaglutide treatment inhibited the cGAS-STING pathway in muscle tissues under HFD conditions (Figure S2F), suggesting that semaglutide alleviated sarcopenia by regulating metabolic-inflammatory interactions. Functional enrichment analysis of up- and down-regulated genes across all treatment groups revealed that semaglutide treatment effectively normalized energy metabolism in muscles under HFD conditions, which was most evident in the corrective downregulation of pathways related to various damage-stress responses and inflammation (Figure S3). These results indicated that semaglutide primarily caused disturbances in muscle metabolism to alleviate disease progression.

Machine learning identified transcriptome features in HFD and OA

We further employed the machine learning approach XGBoost31 to classify transcriptomic features under different experimental perturbations. By partitioning and training datasets from three tissues, bone marrow, brain, and muscle, we achieved robust receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and high true positive rates (TPRs), enabling the identification of characteristic genes within each experimental group (Figure S5). Cross-referencing these features with DEGs revealed notable features in tissues. In bone marrow, we identified Dusp1 as a negative feedback regulator of the MAPK signaling pathway known to inhibit OA progression (Figures S6 and S7). In the brain, we identified the nuclear transport receptor Xpo1, alongside Nr3c1, a well-established high-confidence target in human OA.32 Under OA conditions in muscle, Gsn is highlighted with cellular morphology and migration. Under HFD conditions, we also identified Lars2, which is involved in mitochondrial metabolism. Ces1d is associated with fatty acid metabolism. Notably, it regulates macrophage lipid homeostasis, and its catalytic role in lipid metabolism is critical for obesity. The Ces1d knockout models exhibited increased adipose tissue fibrosis and a metabolic inflammation phenotype.33 By integrating differential expression analysis with feature selection, we could uncover novel representative genes associated with OA and HFD.

Semaglutide targeted mitochondria to regulate the metabolism of muscle

In our cross-tissue differential perturbation analysis, the pronounced response in muscle led to the amelioration of inflammatory and degenerative changes induced by both HFD and OA. Therefore, we proceeded to dissect the cell-type-specific responses of muscle metabolism to semaglutide treatment across both disease models to elucidate the potential underlying mechanisms. We first characterized the sample proportion in muscle cell subtypes, and it was found that semaglutide treatment not only produced cell-type-specific muscle development-related disturbances under different disease conditions, but also regulated a wide range of metabolic processes involving glucose, carbohydrates, pyruvate, nucleic acid, and ATP in various muscle cell types (Figure S4).

Mitochondria are the main site for metabolic reactions within cells.34 The functional analysis of DEGs indicated an upregulation of mitochondrial gene and protein expression, coupled with improved energy metabolism, indicating that semaglutide may regulate muscle metabolism by targeting mitochondria. Thus, we further focused on the mitochondrial-related pathways in muscle cells, and found that mitochondrial gene expression was enhanced under OA and HFD conditions after semaglutide treatment (Figure 4A). The differentiation and regulation of MuSCs, also known as satellite cells, play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of both obesity and OA.35,36 Furthermore, semaglutide has been shown to attenuate skeletal muscle atrophy under obese conditions.8,37 Therefore, to investigate the potential mitochondrial-related mechanisms underlying these processes, we constructed a developmental trajectory of MuSCs, myocytes, and mature myofibers using Monocle338 (Figure 4B). Analysis of cell density along the pseudotime trajectory revealed a decrease in the proportion of MuSCs under disease conditions, which was restored following semaglutide treatment (Figure 4C). Enrichment scoring for mitochondria-related pathways along this trajectory showed that mitochondrial ATP transport and OXPHOS were predominantly enriched in the early stages of differentiation (Figure 4D). This suggested that semaglutide’s regulation of mitochondrial energy metabolism may mainly regulate muscle in the early stages of differentiation, and directly affect the differentiation and function of myogenic lineages.

We proceeded to identify potential effector gene targets rescued by semaglutide within muscle cell types along the pseudotime trajectory, and semaglutide treatment exhibited differential effects under HFD and OA conditions. Under HFD conditions, semaglutide restored the expression of various genes that promote energy metabolism, including Pfkfb3 and Smox in MuSCs, Odc1 and Slc38a4 in myocytes, and genes related to mitochondria in both fast- and slow-twitch myofibers. The restoration of these genes during the repair of muscle lineage differentiation appears to drive energy metabolism pathways that influence MuSC proliferation39 and protect the metabolic homeostasis of MuSCs by downregulating oxidative stress.40 In contrast, genes reversed under OA condition included Myl in MuSCs, Slc25a30 and Pdk4 in myocytes, and Acss1, Pde5a and Ucp3 in myofibers, which are associated with mitochondrial damage, increased oxidative stress, sarcopenia and inflammation41,42,43,44 (Figure 5A). We further integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data from various muscle conditions to construct gene-metabolite interaction networks. Under OA conditions treated with semaglutide, the network analysis revealed enrichment in glutamine metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways that are reported with significant changes in patients with OA45,46 (Figure 5B and Table S1). Conversely, the network under HFD conditions treated with semaglutide showed enrichment in the mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids, as well as purine metabolism and the metabolism of arginine, proline, glutamate, aspartate, and asparagine (Figures 5C and 5D; Table S2). This indicated that the pathways through which semaglutide regulated muscle metabolism under HFD and OA conditions were not consistent. Semaglutide does not rely solely on reducing body weight to alter joint damage in the treatment of OA.

In order to clarify the regulation of semaglutide on muscles under metabolic disorders caused by HFD, we further verified through qPCR that Fkbp5, Ankrd2, and Pde10a were significantly elevated in the muscle tissue under HFD conditions (Figure 6A). Research has shown that the elevation of Ankrd2 may be associated with an increase in oxidative stress levels after muscle injury, suggesting dysfunction of muscle mitochondria in obese mice,47 while muscle Fkbp5 expression has been shown to be associated with insulin resistance.48 Inhibition of Pde10a has been shown to enhance motor function recovery and reduce the expression of sarcopenia related genes.49 In addition, the significantly upregulated Slc8a3 in HFD mice was inhibited by semaglutide, which is a member of the Na+/Ca2+ exchange family involved in muscle excitation-contraction coupling. Its high expression may suggest self-rescue in the impaired state of muscle cells,50 suggesting that semaglutide alleviates muscle damage. These results further suggest that semaglutide may regulate metabolic disorders by targeting mitochondrial function in muscle. To verify the targeted mitochondrial function regulation of muscle homeostasis by semaglutide, we chose C2C12 cells (myogenic cell line) to represent the early stage of muscle differentiation and constructed a PA-induced high-fat model for it. We firstly validated the effect of PA concentration on the viability of C2C12 cells, and ultimately determined 100 μM as the subsequent experimental concentration (Figure 6B). Then, we applied either semaglutide treatment or mitochondrial transplantation therapy to C2C12 cells, respectively. The CCK-8 experimental results suggested that both semaglutide and mitochondria therapies could enhance the viability of high-fat C2C12 cells (Figure 6C). Oil red O staining showed that both therapies reduced lipid droplets in C2C12 cells, indicating enhanced lipid metabolism (Figure 6D). We further validated the OXPHOS, muscle differentiation, and fatty acid oxidation-related genes regulated by semaglutide. The results showed that PA induction significantly downregulated the OXPHOS-related genes of C2C12 cells (mt-Atp6 and mt-Co1), and this inhibition could be reversed by semaglutide treatment and mitochondrial transplantation. In addition, the fatty acid oxidation-related genes (Cpt1b and Hadha) that were downregulated under PA induction were also reversed by semaglutide (Figure 6E). These results confirmed that semaglutide regulated fatty acid oxidation by upregulating mitochondrial function in muscle cells with metabolic disorders, thereby restoring muscle function.

Semaglutide targeted muscle mitochondria to regulate glutamine metabolism for treating OA

The results of scRNA-seq and metabolome demonstrated that, under OA conditions, semaglutide could significantly regulate muscle metabolism by enhancing mitochondrial function through different metabolic pathways compared to HFD conditions, indicating muscle mitochondria might mediate the regulation effect of semaglutide on OA, which is a potential pathway for its cross-tissue treatment of OA. In addition, the analysis of pseudotime trajectory showed that the effect of semaglutide on mitochondrial-related functions was mainly enriched in the early stages of muscle differentiation. Based on this, we speculated that, in addition to improving systemic metabolic status and reducing body weight to alleviate OA symptoms, targeting the mitochondria of muscle cells in the early stages of differentiation to regulate muscle metabolism is another important pathway of semaglutide for cross-tissue treatment of OA. To verify the cross-tissue therapeutic effect of muscle mitochondrial regulation on OA, we transplanted mitochondria derived from C2C12 cells, pre-treated with (S-mito) or without (C-mito) semaglutide in vitro for 24 h, into the affected limb muscles of CD OA mice and conducted behavioral tests after four weeks of treatment. The Von Fray test for evaluating the mechanical allodynia induced by inflammatory microenvironment showed that the pain of mice receiving mitochondrial transplantation pre-treated with semaglutide was significantly reduced, compared to untreated mice (Sham) (Figure 7A). Results of gait analysis for evaluating asymmetric weight-bearing showed that the average stance area, maximum stance area, average pressure, and maximum pressure of the S-mito group and C-mito group were all significantly increased. Compared with the C-mito group, the S-mito group showed a greater increase in swing speed and a more significant decrease in swing/stance ratio (Figure 7B). The results of the open field test evaluating exercise activities showed that the S-mito group had restored their exercise ability, with the ratio of active time, exercise time in high speed and normal speed increased (Figures 7C and 7D). Subsequently, we collected samples from the mouse muscle and articular cartilage and performed histological staining. The SO staining results of articular cartilage showed that the surgery caused significant cartilage damage, manifested as an uneven cartilage surface and defects. After mitochondrial transplantation treatment, the smoothness of the cartilage surface was restored, and the effect of mitochondria pre-treated with semaglutide was more significant (Figure 7E). Results of HE staining and quantitative analysis of muscle showed that OA caused a certain degree of sarcopenia, and mitochondrial transplantation pre-treated with semaglutide could alleviate this pathological damage (Figure 7F).

Functional and histological analyses showed that direct regulation of muscle mitochondrial function by mitochondrial transplantation could effectively improve OA symptoms, and the treatment with semaglutide enhanced this effect. In order to further clarify the specific regulatory mechanism of semaglutide on the mitochondria of muscle cells for the treatment of OA, we extracted mitochondria for proteomic analysis. GSEA enrichment analysis showed that OXPHOS and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I pathways were upregulated in mitochondria stimulated by semaglutide, compared with normal mitochondria (Figures 7H–7J). Further, we noticed that the alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolic pathways were significantly altered by semaglutide, which is consistent with results of scRNA-seq and metabolomic of muscle (Figure 7K). Proteins involved in the alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolic pathway were extensively downregulated, particularly GL (Figure 7L), which is localized in mitochondria and responsible for breaking down glutamine to produce glutamate and NH3,51 inhibiting the inflammatory response of chondrocytes in OA.52 Immunofluorescence staining of muscle showed increase of Gls expression in muscle in the OA group, which was inhibited in the S-mito group (Figure 7M). Further validation of qPCR on muscle revealed that transcriptions of mitochondrial-related genes were downregulated and Gls gene transcription was upregulated in the OA group, which in the S-mito group were significantly reversed (Figure 7N). In addition, previous muscle metabolomics results showed a significant increase in glutamine levels in muscle tissue after treatment with semaglutide (Figure 7O), consistent with the decrease in Gls transcription level of muscle. These results indicated that semaglutide enhanced the regulatory ability of transplanted mitochondria on glutamine metabolism of muscle, targeting the inhibition of Gls, leading to cross-tissue treatment of OA.

However, as a peripheral metabolic organ, muscle cannot directly interact with distal articular cartilage, and the specific pathway of its metabolic remodeling to remotely regulate articular cartilage inflammation still needs to be elucidated. Existing research has shown that muscles are the core metabolic secretory tissues of the body, and their metabolites can be released into the blood to participate in systemic circulation. Blood circulating metabolites are key mediators of muscle-mediated regulation of distal tissues.53 Based on this, we first detected the peripheral circulating glutamine levels in mice, and the results showed that the circulating glutamine concentration in the S-mito group was significantly higher than that in the Sham group (Figure 7P). This result suggested that transplanted mitochondria pre-stimulated with semaglutide inhibited muscle Gls activity, reduced degradation of glutamine in muscles, enhanced muscle glutamine reserves, and promoted the release of accumulated glutamine into the blood, forming a circulating metabolic signal that could mediate distal regulation. To further confirm the muscle-cartilage cross-tissue regulatory effect mediated by circulating glutamine, we treated C2C12 cells with semaglutide and the GL-specific inhibitor telaglenastat. The results showed that both treatment methods significantly promoted the release of glutamine from C2C12 cells and increased the glutamine concentration in the cell culture medium (Figure 7Q). Subsequently, we collected conditioned medium from each group of C2C12 cells and intervened in IL-1β-induced inflammatory primary chondrocytes. The qPCR detection results showed that the transcription levels of inflammation-related genes Il1b, Il6, Mmp13, and Mmp3 in chondrocytes were downregulated, indicating that the high glutamine microenvironment derived from C2C12 cells could effectively alleviate chondrocyte inflammation. At the same time, we found that the conditioned medium derived from C2C12 cells treated with a combination of semaglutide and telaglenastat did not further enhance the anti-inflammatory effect, confirming that semaglutide mainly exerted its regulatory effect by targeting Gls (Figure 7R). To clarify the direct regulatory effect of glutamine on cartilage inflammation, we performed exogenous glutamine intervention on IL-1β-induced inflammatory primary chondrocytes, and the results confirmed that glutamine could significantly inhibit the inflammatory response of chondrocytes (Figure 7S).

In summary, we clarified the regulatory mechanism of semaglutide. By targeting muscle mitochondrial glutamine metabolism, inhibiting Gls-mediated glutamine degradation, and increasing the circulating glutamine level in the body, semaglutide could remotely inhibit joint cartilage inflammation and alleviate OA damage.

Discussion

In recent years, epidemiological studies have emphasized the link between metabolic diseases and OA.54 Especially, metabolic disorders leading to sarcopenia are associated with an increased risk of OA.55 Therefore, the interaction between immune and metabolic processes has received increasing attention. This study systematically mapped the scRNA-seq atlas of semaglutide treatment across tissues by constructing mouse models of HFD-induced obesity and surgically induced OA. For model construction, we successfully recapitulated core features of obesity and OA while demonstrating therapeutic effects of semaglutide on HFD- and OA-related phenotypes. Most muscle cell types exhibited greater sensitivity to semaglutide under HFD conditions. Semaglutide significantly remodeled muscle metabolism by downregulating fatty acid metabolism pathways and upregulating muscle differentiation-related genes, thereby improving HFD-induced functional changes. It also specifically suppressed HFD-triggered mitochondrial oxidative stress and tissue inflammation, manifested by marked downregulation of the cGAS-STING inflammatory pathway. Under OA conditions, semaglutide mainly regulates muscle metabolism and bone marrow immune cells. It is worth noting that under OA conditions, it significantly regulates muscle glutamine metabolism, indicating its potential to normalize muscle energy metabolism and improve metabolic dysfunction. These cross-tissue and cross-disease analyses corroborated previous findings on the role of GLP-1RA in energy metabolism and immune modulation,56,57,58 while revealing the therapeutic action of semaglutide via the metabolic and inflammatory crosstalk. Given the mutual induction between HFD and OA, these results may identify novel molecular targets for metabolic diseases-OA comorbidity treatment.

The results of scRNA-seq suggested that semaglutide targeted mitochondria to exert different effects on the metabolism of muscle under different conditions. Under HFD conditions, semaglutide enhanced mitochondrial metabolism of muscle cells, likely promoting energy utilization and fatty acid degradation. Under OA conditions, semaglutide regulates mitochondrial homeostasis to inhibit inflammatory activation and energy consumption in the cellular microenvironment,59 which is accompanied by increased oxidative stress, potentially exacerbating inflammation and atrophy in muscle tissue. This condition-dependent metabolic modulation by semaglutide highlights the complexity of its therapeutic action and underscores the importance of considering the specific pathological environment when evaluating its effects on muscle physiology.

More and more clinical evidence suggests a close interaction between muscle and OA. Meta-analysis found that low knee extensor and flexor muscle strength increased the risk of knee OA.60 On the other hand, patients with knee OA have higher fat infiltration and lower density in their lower limb muscles.61 However, there are currently few researches exploring the regulatory mechanisms between muscles and OA. Our study fills the mechanistic gap in this area, revealing the cross-tissue regulatory mechanism of muscle mitochondrial glutamine metabolism on cartilage inflammation, and proposing that semaglutide achieves therapeutic effects on OA by targeting this mechanism, further providing a mechanistic explanation for the clinical evidence of semaglutide in the treatment of OA.

Research has found that age-related mitochondrial dysfunction, accumulation of related metabolites, and oxidative stress may be the main causes of chondrocyte damage and joint cartilage destruction.62 At present, various therapeutic methods for alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and clearing ROS have made progress.63,64 Due to the important role of dysfunctional mitochondria in the development of degenerative joint diseases, researchers are focusing on a method that can directly improve damaged mitochondria - mitochondrial transplantation. Mitochondrial transplantation has the potential to increase the number of mitochondria and improve their functional performance.65 However, there is currently no clinical research indicating that exogenous mitochondrial therapy can improve symptoms in patients with OA. We conducted a four-week mitochondrial transplantation treatment on OA mice based on the results that semaglutide can alleviate symptoms in OA mice, as well as the molecular mechanism analysis of semaglutide targeting muscle mitochondria to regulate tissue metabolism. The results showed that mitochondrial transplantation not only improved the activity and pain expression of mice, but also improved cartilage damage. Compared with drugs, mitochondrial transplantation provides low immunogenicity and multi-target regulation, making it a breakthrough strategy for metabolic degenerative diseases and potentially avoiding drug side effects. It is expected to become a reliable alternative intervention for drugs. However, challenges remain in delivery efficiency and long-term safety. Future personalized and combination therapies may advance mitochondrial transplantation as a transformative alternative.

In summary, our analysis of the multi-tissue mechanism of semaglutide under HFD and OA conditions not only reveals its targets for treating OA, but also provides a theoretical basis for its targeted mitochondrial regulation of metabolism across tissues. By delving deeper into the role of semaglutide in different pathological conditions and tissues, we can discover therapeutic mechanisms in greater depth. By combining customized interventions targeting its target, it is possible to maintain the positive therapeutic effect of semaglutide while reducing adverse side effects, thereby achieving safer and more effective treatment methods.

Limitations of the study

While this study elucidates mechanisms of semaglutide in obesity and OA, several limitations exist. Mouse models may not fully replicate human disease progression, limiting clinical translatability of certain phenotypes, and the specific target on mitochondria of semaglutide still needs to be explored (e.g., conditional knockout or pharmacological interruption of related genes). Besides, the design lacks dose-response and long-term safety assessments. Future directions should prioritize: (1) targeted delivery systems (e.g., muscle-specific nanocarriers) for fiber-type-precise therapy; (2) multicenter clinical studies stratifying patients with obesity-OA by metabolic signatures and drug responses to develop personalized regimens; and (3) combination therapies with mitochondrial antioxidants or myogenic factors to overcome single-target limitations. These extensions will bridge mechanism discovery to clinical translation, offering new paradigms for the precision treatment of metabolic musculoskeletal comorbidities.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal grouping

Mice were divided into 8 groups, with control conditions including CD, without surgery, saline injection (CSV), and semaglutide injection (CSS); HFD, without surgery, saline injection (HSV), and semaglutide injection (HSS); CD, surgical induction of OA, saline injection (CAV), and semaglutide injection (CAS); and HFD, surgical induction of OA, saline injection (HAV), and semaglutide injection (HAS).

Mouse model

For HFD-induced obesity mouse model, four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice weighing 18–22 g were selected and acclimatized for 7 days before being randomly assigned by body weight into two groups: a sham group (fed CD containing 10% kcal from fat) and an HFD group (fed HFD containing 60% kcal from fat, primarily composed of lard and soybean oil). Both groups had ad libitum access to food and water and were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 22 ± 2°C with 50–60% humidity and a 12-h light-dark cycle. The modeling period lasted 35 weeks, during which body weight was measured and food intake was recorded weekly at fixed time points. Upon completion of the modeling period, successful model establishment was confirmed when the average body weight of the HFD group increased by ≥ 20% compared to the CD group with statistical significance (p < 0.05), and the HFD group showed a statistically significant decrease in average body weight-normalized grip strength compared to the control group (p < 0.05).

For surgery-induced OA mouse model for scRNA-seq analysis, CD and HFD mice aged 39 weeks (fed with 35 weeks of CD or HFD) were acclimatized for 7 days before being randomly assigned into a sham surgery group (Sham group) and an OA group. Following a 12-h preoperative fasting period, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation, and anterior cruciate ligament transection was performed on the right knee joint in the OA group, while the sham group underwent only surgical incision without anterior cruciate ligament transection. Four weeks post-surgery, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and tissue specimens were collected for analysis. For the surgical induced OA mouse model used for mitochondrial transplantation therapy, 8-week-old CD mice were selected, and all other procedures were consistent.

The proposal for the study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jinjiang Hospital, China (permit number: 20260001).

Only male mice were used in this study to avoid the metabolic and inflammatory fluctuations caused by the estrous cycle in female mice. However, the absence of female animals is a major limitation. Sex-dependent differences in drug sensitivity, metabolism, and OA progression were not evaluated. Further research is required to clarify their independent and interactive associations with relevant indicators.

Semaglutide treatment

Mice in HFD, OA, HFD combined with OA, and sham group were randomly divided into a semaglutide treatment group and a control group. Mice in the semaglutide treatment group received subcutaneous injections of semaglutide solution (concentration 10 nM, Sigma-Aldrich) on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, with the injection dose calculated at 1 μL per gram of body weight. Control group mice received equal volumes of subcutaneous saline injections during the same periods. Treatment continued for 3 weeks with a total of 9 injections. The injection site was selected at the subcutaneous area of the neck and back region, with each injection site disinfected using 75% alcohol prior to injection. Injections were administered using a 1 mL syringe equipped with a 27G needle at a slow and uniform rate. During the treatment period, the general condition of mice was observed daily, and body weight was measured and recorded weekly, with injection doses adjusted according to body weight changes. All injection procedures were performed during the same time window (9:00-10:00 AM) to minimize the influence of circadian rhythms on drug efficacy. Following completion of treatment, mice were observed for an additional week to evaluate the sustained effects of the drug.

Cell culture

C2C12 cells were purchased from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured in a growth medium composed of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) in humidified 5% CO2 at 37°C. Cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin EDTA and passaged every 2–3 days at a ratio of 1:3. All cell lines were mycoplasma-negative and were tested for authentication.

Articular chondrocytes were isolated from cartilaginous portion of femoral heads, femoral condyles and tibial plateau of male mice. Cartilage tissue was finely minced and digested with 0.1% collagenase type II in DMEM at 37°C for 60 min followed by 0.05% collagenase type II at 37°C overnight. Single cell suspension was passed through 70 mm cell strainer, centrifuged at 400 g for 4 min and resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS. Isolated cells were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2. Chondrocytes cultured for no more than passage 1 were used in further experiments.

Method details

Behavior tests

Mouse grip strength was assessed using a grip strength meter to evaluate forelimb muscle strength. Prior to testing, mice were placed in a quiet environment for 30 min of acclimatization. During testing, the experimenter grasped the mouse’s tail with one hand, allowing the mouse’s forepaws to grip the metal grid of the force gauge, then slowly and steadily pulled the mouse horizontally backward until the mouse released its forepaws. The maximum pulling force at that moment was recorded (unit: gram-force, g). Each mouse underwent 5 consecutive tests with 2-min intervals between measurements, and the maximum value from the 5 tests was taken as the grip strength value for that mouse. All tests were conducted by the same experimenter during the same time period (9:00-11:00 AM) to minimize the influence of circadian rhythms and inter-operator variability on the results.

Gait parameters of freely moving mice were measured using a CatWalk gait analysis system based on computer video. Each mouse underwent three experiments. In short, a mouse was placed on an elevated glass platform in a dark room and allowed to move freely. The beam of light below the platform illuminated the surface, forming an image of each footprint, which was recorded by the camera. Visugate software (Shanghai Xinruan Technology) calculates gait parameters for statistical analysis.

For open field experiments, mice were placed separately in a square transparent room (40 × 40 cm) and allowed to explore freely. Under normal lighting conditions, the movement and trajectory were recorded and analyzed through a computer system.

Histological staining and immunofluorescence

Following experimental completion, mice were euthanized and bilateral gastrocnemius muscles were rapidly dissected, rinsed with saline, and stripped of fascia. Muscle tissues were cut into approximately 5 mm × 5 mm × 3 mm blocks and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde fixative for 24–48 h. Mouse knee joints were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h and decalcified in 10% EDTA for 1 week. After fixation, tissues underwent sequential dehydration through graded ethanol solutions (75%, 85%, 95%, 100% ethanol, 2 h each), followed by xylene clearing twice (1 h each), and three rounds of paraffin infiltration (1 h each) before embedding into paraffin blocks. Using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2016), serial cross-sections of 5 μm thickness were cut and mounted on glass slides, then baked at 60°C for 2 h. For Safranin-O or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the tissue was dehydrated and embedded in paraffin, with a slice thickness of 5 μm. Under light microscopy, muscle fiber morphology, cross-sectional area, and arrangement were observed. For each muscle sample, five random fields of view were selected at 400× magnification for image acquisition and analysis.

For immunofluorescence, tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 4 h, embedded in OCT compound, cut into 10 μm slices, and mounted onto adhesive slides. Sections were washed in PBS, permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min at room temperature, and incubated with 3% BSA-PBS for 30 min at room temperature to block nonspecific antibody binding. The sections were incubated with Rabbit anti-Gls antibody (1:500) for night, and then incubated with donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (1:1000) and Alexa Fluor 647 phalloidin (1:250) for 1 h. Finally, the sections were incubated with DAPI for 15 min, and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade medium. Digital images were acquired using Olympus SpinSR.

Real-time quantitative PCR detection of muscle tissue and cells

Gastrocnemius muscle tissues (approximately 50–100 mg) stored in liquid nitrogen and cells were used for total RNA extraction using the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit PLUS. Tissues were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle, followed by RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.2 considered acceptable. Appropriate amounts of total RNA were reverse-transcribed to synthesize cDNA using 4×EZscript Reverse Transcription Mix II following the manufacturer’s recommended conditions. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using 2×Color SYBR green qPCR Master Mix on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System. The 10 μL reaction system consisted of: 5 μL of 2×Color SYBR green qPCR Master Mix, appropriate amounts of forward and reverse primers, 0.2 μL of cDNA template, and ddH2O to a final volume of 10 μL. PCR reaction conditions were set according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and melting curve analysis was performed after amplification to verify product specificity. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2ˆ(-ΔΔCt) method. Each sample was run in triplicate, and experiments were repeated three times.

Mitochondria isolation and transplantation

To isolate enough mitochondria, the C2C12 cells were cultured in a 15 cm dish. Collect cells at 90% fusion. Mitochondria were isolated using a mammalian cell mitochondrial isolation kit. In short, cell pellets containing 2 × 107 cells were suspended in 800 μL lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 5 min, vortexed at maximum speed per minute, and then centrifuged at 700 g at 4°C for 10 min. The supernatant was centrifuged at 12000 g for 15 min at 4°C. Then the mitochondrial pellets were rinsed and centrifuged at 12000 g for 5 min at 4°C. For in vitro transplantation, when cells reached 80% confluence, mitochondria were added in a 5:1 ratio and co-cultured for at least 24 h. For in vivo transplantation, each affected limb of the OA mouse after surgery received mitochondrial intramuscular injection once a week for four weeks, with 50 μL, 25 μL, and 50 μL mitochondrial suspension (0.1 μg/μL) injected into the quadriceps femoris, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius muscles, respectively.

Cell modeling and treatment

For high-fat induction of C2C12 cells, PA was used for at least 24 h. For semaglutide or telaglenastat therapy of C2C12 cells, the drug was applied to cells at a concentration of 1 μM and incubated for at least 24 h. For the inflammation model of chondrocytes, IL-1β was applied to cells at a concentration of 10 ng/mL and incubated for at least 24 h. Glutamine was added to chondrocytes at a concentration of 1 mM for 24 h.

Cell viability assay

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the cell viability of C2C12 cells was evaluated using Cell Counting Kit-8. In short, cells were seeded in a 96 well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well. After incubation for 24 h under standard conditions, different treatments were applied for at least 24 h according to experimental requirements. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. The plate was further incubated at 37°C in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each sample was run in triplicate, and experiments were repeated three times.

Oil red O staining

Cultured cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were rinsed with distilled water and incubated with 60% isopropanol for 20 s to enhance lipid droplet permeability. Oil Red O working solution (0.5% w/v in 60% isopropanol, filtered through a 0.22-μm syringe filter) was applied to cover the cells for 15 min at room temperature. Excess stain was removed by washing with 60% isopropanol (5 s) followed by three PBS washes. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min if required. For each stained sample, three random fields of view were imaged using an inverted bright-field microscope at 200–400× magnification. The area of lipid droplets is statistically analyzed using ImageJ. Experiments were repeated three times.

Single-cell RNA-seq

Samples for tissues were collected following eight experimental groups in HFD, OA and control conditions. Liver and blood samples were collected only from the CD OA mice with or without treatment. Bone marrow, brain and muscle samples were obtained from all eight experimental groups. The scRNA-seq libraries was generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Library Kit v3 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-cell suspensions from each group were loaded onto the Chromium single-cell controller to generate cell-bead droplets in the emulsion. Barcoded cDNA was amplified and fragmented for library construction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrations of the final libraries were determined by Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the fragment lengths were determined by Agilent 2100. The libraries were then pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Processing of single-cell RNA-seq data

Raw data (FASTQ files) of each sample was processed by Cell Ranger (Pipeline Version 3.1.0) with the default parameters. The function “cellranger count” was used for alignment, filtering, cell barcode counting, and UMI (unique molecular identifier) counting. Barcoded reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the mouse mm10 reference genome with using Cell Ranger with default parameters. The Seurat package (version 4) was used for subsequent analysis of processed data. Cells with >500 UMIs (>300 genes) and mitochondrial gene ratio <15% were kept for clustering and downstream analysis. The data integration across different samples were processed using Harmony66 with standard processes to remove the noise from different samples. The “FindClusters” function with resolution = 0.5. The “RunUMAP” function with the parameter dims = 1:30. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function with min.pct = 0.25 and logfc.threshold = 0.25. Cell types were identified using canonical markers of mouse tissues based on published literature.

Differentially expressed gene analysis and function enrichment

For experimental stimulus condition (HFD, OA, HFD&OA), we performed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis on tissue-level and cell types-level using the FindMarkers function in Seurat with logfc.threshold = 0.25 and min.pct = 0.25. For the gene function enrichment analysis, we used the clusterProfiler R package with pvalueCutoff = 0.05 and qvalueCutoff = 0.2.67 Gene ontology network was constructed using Metascape.68 All the DEGs were used to perform gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using GSVA R packages. The wilcoxon test was adopted and p value <0.05 was defined as statistically significant. The ssGSEA method in GSVA were used for GSEA enrichment analysis of specific cell types.

Cell state perturbations analysis

We applied Augur29 to analyze the experimental perturbation of major cell types across tissues (https://github.com/neurorestore/Augur). Experimental stimulus of OA and HFD were evaluated based on differentially expressed genes.

Gene set score analysis in muscle

Mitochondrial-related pathway gene sets scores for each input cell were calculated by the Seurat function “AddModuleScore”. Changes in the scores between merged 2-month and 8-month samples were analyzed using the ggpubr package via the Wilcox test (https://github.com/kassambara/ggpubr).

Trajectory analysis of muscle

Monocle338 were used to construct the pseudotime-trajectories for muscle cell types wit default workflow. The muscle stem cells were set as the root points in “order_cells” function (trajectory_graph_segment_size = 0.5). Pathway gene sets score was constructed using “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat.

Machine learning model for protein feature selection

We constructed a machine learning classification model with the XGBoost algorithm based on the single-cell RNA-seq data. The discovery cohort divided into training set and testing set (4:1). Different groups and all the DEPs were collected as input for the model construction. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis achieved an area under the curve (AUC) values around 0.92–0.99 for the internal testing set.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Exact value of n can be found in the figures and legends. N indicates the number of biologically independent samples, or mice per group. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size, and sample sizes were determined based on previous studies with similar experiments. Microscopy images shown are representative of at least 3 biologically independent samples. Quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (10.4.0) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical normality was made using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance for normally distributed data was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test for comparisons of two groups, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed with post hoc Šídák test for multiple comparisons. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed data processing, sample size and statistical methods for each result were shown in the corresponding figure legends.
