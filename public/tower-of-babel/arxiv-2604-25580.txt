Bye Bye perspective api : Lessons for Measurement Infrastructure in NLP, CSS and LLM Evaluation
David Hartmann, Manuel Tonneau, Angelie Kraft, LK Seiling, Dimitri Staufer, Pieter Delobelle, Jan Fillies, Anna Ricarda Luther, Jan Batzner, Mareike Lisker

Abstract
The closure of perspective api at the end of 2026 discards what has functioned as the de facto standard for automated toxicity measurement in NLP, CSS, and LLM evaluation research. We document the structural dependence that the communities built on this single proprietary tool and discuss how this dependence caused epistemic problems that have affected—and will likely continue to affect—collective research efforts. perspective ’s model was periodically updated without versioning or disclosure, its annotation structure reflected a single corporate operationalisation of a contested concept, and its scores were used simultaneously as an evaluation target and an evaluation standard. Its closure leaves behind non-updatable benchmarks, irreproducible results, and ultimately a field at risk of perpetuating these issues by turning to closed-source LLMs. We use perspective ’s announced termination as an opportunity to call for an independent, valid, adaptable, and reproducible toxicity and hate speech measurement infrastructure, with the technical and governance requirements outlined in this paper.

1
Introduction

Online abuse can have profound real-world consequences, including the suppression of marginalised voices, deterioration of mental health, and violence against marginalised groups
(
Matsuda et al. 1993
;
Marques 2023
;
Luther et al. 2025
)
. To address this challenge, a substantial body of research has focused on developing automated classifiers to detect such content at scale
Vidgen and Derczynski 2020
. One classification tool has emerged as particularly influential, namely
perspective api
, developed by Google’s technology incubator Jigsaw. Since 2017, it has provided a free and scalable API for classifying
toxicity
, an umbrella term for harmful content. Its widespread adoption has led to it being described as “a cornerstone for academic research on online abuse and incivility”
(
Nogara et al. 2025
)
.

Despite its popularity, Jigsaw recently announced that
perspective api
will cease operation at the end of 2026. The sunset announcement celebrates a decade of impact, stating that the system has ‘‘grown from an experimental tool into a helpful solution for publishers and platforms’’. However, the tool and infrastructure are to be shut down because, ‘‘[a]s AI capabilities evolve, [Jigsaw is] setting [its] sights on new challenges’’.

For research communities that have built their work around
perspective api
, this discontinuation represents a sudden withdrawal of critical infrastructure, exposing the field’s dependence on a proprietary, externally controlled measurement tool.

We argue that the closure is not a tooling problem, but a measurement problem: The field built its toxicity research on a tool it neither owned, nor versioned, nor validated for most of the ways it was used. Therefore, the response is not to find a drop-in replacement, but to rebuild the conditions for valid, reliable, and reproducible toxicity measurement. The paper develops this argument in four steps. We first trace how
perspective api
was used across natural language processing (NLP), computational social science (CSS), and large language model (LLM) evaluation research and what drove its adoption (§
2
). We then show that, judged against standard procedures of measurement validation, the tool was never fit for the central role it came to play, with consequences for validity, reliability, and reproducibility (§
3
). Building on this analysis, we propose five technical and five governance requirements for an independent successor infrastructure (§
4
). Finally, we reflect on the role of researchers in content moderation, toxicity research, and LLM evaluation (§
5
).

Use in Research

Within academic research,

was taken up across communities in two distinct ways (Figure

Perspective as a baseline model

In
toxicity

,
perspective api
was primarily treated as a system to be evaluated. It served as a reference point in benchmark studies, where its toxicity scores were used to assess classification performance relative to other models. For example, HateCheck
Röttger et al. 2021

perspective api

Perspective as a measurement tool

In contrast, CSS and LLM evaluation research primarily used

as a tool to measure toxicity at scale. In CSS, researchers applied the API in large-scale studies of online discourse (e.g.,

;

). In LLM evaluation (benchmarking, auditing, and red teaming),
perspective api
was embedded into evaluation operationalisations to label model outputs and for red teaming to label potential offensive model inputs (e.g.,
Gehman et al. 2020

;

2.2
Drivers of Adoption

At its peak,
perspective api
processed 500 million requests daily
(
Jigsaw 2021
)
, and was integrated into comment systems at major publishers, including the New York Times, Vox Media, and OpenWeb
(
Rieder and Skop 2021
;
Jigsaw 2021
)
, while also becoming widely adopted in academic research
(
Ribeiro 2024
)
. In what follows, we examine the factors behind this widespread adoption.

Timing

perspective
’s launch in 2017 followed a surge in online abuse, including high-profile harassment campaigns, the spread of conspiratorial narratives
(
Venturini 2019
)
, inter-ethnic violence and genocide
(
Mozur 2018
)
, and the mobilisation of political extremism online
(
Ganesh and Bright 2020
)
. This surge put increasing pressure on platforms to act. For instance, Germany’s
Network Enforcement Act
(NetzDG), passed in 2017, followed a wave of anti-refugee hate speech and required platforms to remove clearly illegal content. As
Gorwa 2019
documents, this pressure contributed to an institutional shift from user-driven flagging to platform-centred moderation infrastructures, including automated or semi-automated moderation (see also
Myers West 2018
;
Gorwa et al. 2020
). This context also motivated early work on automatic hate speech detection
(
Waseem and Hovy 2016
)
, laying foundations for computational moderation tools.
perspective
thus emerged at the intersection of regulatory pressure and a nascent research field, providing a scalable moderation solution
(
Davidson et al. 2017
)
as human moderation became increasingly infeasible
Gorwa et al. 2020
.

Cost and access

Another reason for adoption is that using
perspective api
was free, whereas competing tools, such as
amazon rekognition
, charged per query
(
Estha 2024
)
. Access was provided upon application to Jigsaw, though acceptance criteria remained opaque
Rieder and Skop 2021
. For accepted clients, the API offered a 100ms response time and an expandable quota on request, making large-scale studies and automated moderation feasible at no cost. Its multilingual coverage across 18 languages, trained on 38 million records
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
, further enabled research beyond English in a field largely dominated by English resources
(
Tonneau et al. 2024
)
.

Ease of use and transparency

perspective api
’s ease of use reinforced these advantages. It accepted single-sentence inputs without preprocessing and returned an easily interpretable scalar score, lowering the barrier to integration in both research and production systems. In addition, Jigsaw provided model cards, threshold guidance, and partial architectural documentation via GitHub
(
Jigsaw 2022
)
, offering a level of transparency uncommon among commercial APIs
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. The team further engaged with the research community through peer-reviewed publications, benchmark datasets
(
Dixon et al. 2018
;
Lees et al. 2022
)
, and direct responses to researcher feedback
(
Jigsaw 2023
)
. While this documentation was not comprehensive in all respects, as we detail in the next section, it nonetheless positioned
perspective api
as a comparatively transparent and research-embedded proprietary tool.

Path dependence and network effects

As
Star 1999
argues, tools become infrastructure through their embeddedness in practice. Once a measurement instrument is taken up across a field, it begins to shape what is measurable, comparable, and credible, thereby generating path dependence
(
Bowker and Star 1999
)
. We argue that
perspective api
exemplifies this dynamic: its integration into NLP benchmarking suites such as
RealToxicityPrompts

(
Gehman et al. 2020
)
and the
Holistic Evaluation of Language Models
(HELM)
(
Liang et al. 2023
)
, as well as its use in CSS studies of political incivility and online harassment
(
Theocharis et al. 2020
;
Gervais et al. 2025
)
, positioned it as a common reference point across research contexts. This standardisation enabled comparability across studies, which in turn encouraged continued use, creating a self-reinforcing dynamic consistent with network effects
(
Katz and Shapiro 1994
)
. In this sense,
perspective api
was not necessarily adopted because it was the most valid or best-performing tool, but because it had become an established point of reference, effectively positioning it over time as the de facto standard for toxicity detection.

3
Was
perspective api
Ever Fit for Purpose? A Measure Development Pipeline and Infrastructural Critique

Becoming a standard through path dependence and network effects is not the same as being validated as one. In this section, we assess whether
perspective api
was fit for the central role it came to play in research by comparing its development to established procedures of measurement validation in the social sciences
(
Clark and Watson 2019
;
Jacobs and Wallach 2021
;
Wallach et al. 2024
)
. In this tradition, a
concept
(i.e., a broad and often contested notion) is first systematised into a
construct
(i.e., a more specific and explicitly defined understanding), specifying
what
is to be measured
(
Adcock and Collier 2001
)
. The construct is then operationalised through a
measurement instrument
, specifying
how
it is to be measured. Both steps, (1) from concept to construct through
systematisation
and (2) from construct to
measurement instrument
through
operationalisation
, are inherently situated and involve analytical choices
(
Wallach et al. 2024
)
. They rely on assumptions that must be made explicit and supported theoretically and empirically.
2
2

2

This is commonly referred to as
construct validation
, the process of establishing whether a measurement instrument captures what it is intended to measure or, in other words, of providing theoretical and evidential support for one’s operationalisation
(
Cronbach and Meehl 1955
)
.

Here, we show that
perspective api
provides limited grounding for key assumptions in both its systematisation (§
3.1
) and operationalisation and provision of the measurement infrastructure (§
3.2
), and that these limitations have implications for performance and research use (§
3.3
). We further identify structural issues in its application, particularly in cases where it is used to both define and measure toxicity (§
3.4
).

3.1
Systematisation

Contested Behavioural Assumption

perspective api
’s construct of toxicity rests on a behavioural assumption that is empirically contested. Jigsaw defines toxicity as “a rude, disrespectful, or unreasonable comment that is likely to make people leave a discussion”
(
Dixon et al. 2018
;
Rieder and Skop 2021
)
, thereby tying toxicity to disengagement. However, empirical evidence suggests that this effect is not stable. For example,
Beknazar-Yuzbashev et al. 2025
show that removing toxic content from social media feeds causally reduces time spent, ad impressions, and content consumption, indicating that its presence contributes to user engagement. Observationally, out-group animosity is the strongest predictor of shares and retweets on Facebook and Twitter
(
Rathje et al. 2021
)
, and toxic conversations on Twitter tend to generate larger, wider, and deeper reply trees than non-toxic ones
(
Saveski et al. 2021
)
. Taken together, this evidence challenges the disengagement premise: the same toxic content can repel some readers and engage others, depending on their relationship to it. A construct that ties toxicity to a single, universal behavioural effect, therefore mistakes a reader-dependent phenomenon for a property of the text.

Insufficiently Specified Construct

Even beyond this contested behavioural assumption, the construct remains insufficiently specified.
perspective api
provides scores for multiple “toxic attributes”, including threat, insult, identity attack, severe toxicity, and profanity, suggesting a decomposition of toxicity into subcomponents. However, Jigsaw provides only brief, one-sentence definitions of these attributes and does not clearly distinguish between them, explain how they relate to the overarching toxicity construct, specify how they should be used in practice, or indicate whether this set of attributes is intended to be exhaustive
(
Rieder and Skop 2021
;
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. As a result, both the scope of the construct and the relationship between the overall construct and its purported subcomponents remain unclear. Without a well-specified construct, it is difficult to determine what exactly is being measured, or to assess its validity
(
Wallach et al. 2024
)
.

Construct-Measure Mismatch

This lack of specification enables a mismatch between the intended construct and its use in downstream research, particularly in studies of hate speech. Toxicity and hate speech are related but distinct concepts
(
Barth et al. 2023
)
: while toxicity encompasses rude or unreasonable language more generally, hate speech involves discriminatory acts targeting individuals or groups based on protected characteristics
(
Dixon et al. 2018
;
Marques 2023
)
. A measurement instrument designed for toxicity therefore cannot be assumed to validly capture hate speech. We observe three recurring usage patterns in the literature. The first uses the toxicity score to measure toxic language, aligning with the tool’s intended use, though constrained by its behavioural definition. The second uses attributes such as
IDENTITY_ATTACK
as proxies for hate speech, typically acknowledging the construct gap explicitly (e.g.,
Tonneau et al. 2025
). The third frames research questions in terms of hate speech, political incivility, or LLM safety and operationalises them using the toxicity score without acknowledging the lack of theoretical alignment
(
Gervais et al. 2025
;
Gehman et al. 2020
)
. This last pattern is particularly problematic, as the measurement instrument does not match the target construct and the mismatch is not made explicit.

3.2
Operationalisation and Provision

Having examined how toxicity is conceptualised, we now analyse how it is operationalised and provisioned in the evaluation tool
perspective api
. We focus on three aspects: the input representation, the model and its provision, and the resulting outputs.

Input: Context-free

perspective api
takes a single sentence or short text as input and returns a toxicity score, thereby operationalising toxicity as a property identifiable in isolated text
(
Rieder and Skop 2021
;
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. However, the meaning of a sentence depends on its conversational context, community norms, who is speaking, who is targeted, and the relationship between speakers and receivers
(
Anderson and Barnes 2023
;
Yoder et al. 2022
;
Mosca et al. 2021
)
. By design,
perspective api
strips this context away, limiting the measurement instrument to surface textual features and excluding the contextual and relational dimensions that define toxicity as a social phenomenon. As a result, the same text receives the same score across contexts, even though its perceived toxicity depends on context-specific norms, making the resulting scores difficult to interpret.

Model: Misaligned and Opaque

The model underlying
perspective api
is misaligned with its published specification and lacks sufficient transparency, limiting users’ ability to assess what it measures. Most consequentially, the annotation scheme used to generate training labels, while partially available
(
Conversation AI 2017
)
, diverges from Jigsaw’s public definition of toxicity
(
Dixon et al. 2018
;
Rieder and Skop 2021
)
. Whereas the public definition frames toxicity as a conjunctive criterion, namely a rude or disrespectful comment that is likely to make users leave a discussion, the annotation scheme applies these criteria disjunctively, allowing labels to be assigned based on rudeness or anticipated behavioural effects alone. As a result, the model is trained on labels that do not correspond to the definition users are given, creating a gap between construct and operationalisation. This issue is compounded by a lack of transparency about the annotation process, including annotator demographics, data sources, and labelling procedures
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
;
Rieder and Skop 2021
)
. Adding another layer of opacity,
Pozzobon et al. 2023
document that the underlying model of the
perspective api
was silently retrained over time without version pinning, changelogs, or public disclosure, except for a Google group where they announced changes.

Output: Uncalibrated

The outputs of
perspective api
are not calibrated to any interpretable real-world quantity. The API returns a score between 0 and 1, but this score does not correspond to prevalence, severity, or annotator agreement. According to Jigsaw’s technical documentation, scores are normalised against a balanced 50/50 toxic and non-toxic dataset rather than any real-world distribution
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
, meaning they reflect model confidence relative to an artificial baseline
(
Nogara et al. 2025
)
rather than the prevalence of harm. Although Jigsaw and external researchers provided some threshold guidance
(
Hua et al. 2020a
;
Hua et al. 2020b
)
, these offer no principled basis for selecting thresholds that correspond to interpretable quantities, leaving threshold-setting as an implicit design decision made by individual researchers
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. As a result, scores lack a stable interpretation and depend on arbitrary and often unreported thresholding choices.

3.3
Consequences for Research Quality

The limitations in systematisation and operationalisation outlined above translate into measurable problems for research quality. As
perspective api
became widely embedded in research workflows, these limitations produced systematic errors, inconsistent results, and biases across use cases.

Lacking Validity and Performance

Construct validity issues translate into poor classification performance when
perspective api
is applied beyond its intended scope.
Gervais et al. 2025
show that
perspective
scores on congressional tweets achieve a maximum F1 of 0.33 against human-coded political incivility across all decision thresholds, and
Mihaljević and Steffen 2022
find that it detects antisemitism only at a very basic level. These failures are particularly pronounced where interpretation depends on context. In such cases, the model systematically misclassifies meaning: counterspeech is labelled as hateful when speaker intent is not considered
(
Gligorić et al. 2024
)
, reclaimed slurs are over-moderated when community membership is unavailable
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
;
Röttger et al. 2021
)
, and implicit hate speech, which relies on background knowledge, is systematically missed
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
.

Undermined Reliability and Reproducibility

The lack of model versioning undermines the reliability and reproducibility of results. Because toxicity is a dynamic concept, with slurs evolving and new forms of expression emerging
(
Siegel 2020
;
Mendelsohn et al. 2023
)
, the absence of versioning means that
perspective api
effectively functions as a moving target, making it impossible to determine whether repeated queries refer to the same measurement instrument.

Pozzobon et al. 2023
show that 49% of previously toxic prompts were classified differently upon rescoring, and HELM rankings were inverted. This indicates that researchers were effectively querying a changing system, and because model updates were neither documented nor versioned, it is not possible to determine whether results obtained at different points in time are comparable. The shutdown makes this problem permanent: scores can no longer be recovered or verified, rendering
Perspective API
-based benchmarks irreproducible.

Systematic Bias and Limited Attribution

Systematic bias is evident in the outputs of
perspective api
, but its sources cannot be fully attributed due to limited transparency in the underlying data and annotation processes. Independent audits show a consistent pattern of over-moderation of identity-related content alongside under-detection of implicit hate speech. For example, content associated with LGBTQIA+ communities and reclaimed slurs is disproportionately flagged as toxic
(
Dias Oliva et al. 2021
;
Röttger et al. 2021
)
, while hate directed at marginalised groups, in general, is often underdetected
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. These patterns are consistent with a reliance on surface features such as identity terms and the use of context-free inputs. Biases also extend across languages and regions:
Nogara et al. 2025
show that German-language text is rated as more toxic than comparable content in other languages, and cross-geographic disparities in toxicity detection performance are well documented
(
Ghosh et al. 2021
)
. Such disparities align with prior work showing that annotator demographics, beliefs, and annotation guidelines shape what is labelled as toxic in edge cases
(
Sap et al. 2022
;
Davani et al. 2023
)
. However, because key aspects of the annotation process remain only partially documented, including annotator composition and labelling procedures, these biases cannot be systematically evaluated, compared, or corrected by downstream users.

3.4
Structural Circularity: When the Measure Becomes the Standard

These problems do not remain confined to individual studies but accumulate at the level of the research network, producing a structural circularity. Because
perspective api
provided an accessible and scalable source of toxicity scores, it came to function not only as a measurement tool but also as a source of training data and an evaluation standard. The same system that generated labels was also used to evaluate models, creating the conditions for a self-reinforcing loop that runs through the field’s most influential resources, including
RealToxicityPrompts
and the benchmarks built on it
(
Gehman et al. 2020
;
Pozzobon et al. 2023
)
.

This dynamic emerges across the literature in three recurring steps. First, because large-scale human annotation is expensive and the API is readily accessible, researchers used
perspective
scores to generate training labels for toxicity and hate speech datasets
(
Gehman et al. 2020
)
. Second, models trained on these labels were evaluated against
perspective
outputs, either directly through score correlation or indirectly through benchmarks whose gold labels were themselves generated using the API
(
Gehman et al. 2020
;
Pozzobon et al. 2023
)
. Third, models that achieved high agreement with
perspective
were reported as performing well at toxicity or hate speech detection
(
Pavlopoulos et al. 2019
)
. At each step, researchers converted uncalibrated continuous scores into binary labels by applying an ad hoc threshold such as
t=0.7
(see stage ➂ in Figure
1
) and treated these derived labels as ground truth, despite lacking a principled interpretation. Threshold choices were often not reported, and the meaning of the scores remained unspecified
(
Pozzobon et al. 2023
;
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
.

What was ultimately being measured, however, was not toxicity, but conformity to a single system’s decision threshold. This threshold reflects a specific platform, annotator pool, cultural context, and point in time, normalised against an artificial baseline rather than any real-world distribution
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
, without an explicit specification of what toxicity means for whom
(
Wallach et al. 2024
;
Jacobs and Wallach 2021
;
Blodgett et al. 2021
)
. Because training and evaluation rely on the same system, systematic errors become embedded in the evaluation criterion and thus remain invisible. Models inheriting
perspective
’s biases, including over-moderation of dialects, reclaimed slurs, or content related to marginalised groups
(
Dias Oliva et al. 2021
;
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
, will perform well on
perspective
-derived benchmarks, since these errors are encoded in the standard itself. As
Blodgett et al. 2020
argue, when underlying social assumptions are not made explicit, they are laundered through technical framing until they appear neutral. Circular evaluation is one mechanism through which this laundering occurs.

Once
perspective api
became the measure, it ceased to function as one
(
Strathern 1997
)
. Understanding why the field arrived at this point requires situating this technical dependence within the broader platform-governance context in which the tool was built and ultimately abandoned. We turn to this next, followed by recommendations based on these critiques.

4
What Now? Towards Independent Research Infrastructure

The decision to discontinue
perspective api
at this moment cannot be understood without the broader shift in the platform governance landscape. Since 2024, major platforms have reduced moderation workforces
(
McMahon et al. 2025
;
McGuirk 2024
;
Gerken 2025
)
, loosened content rules
(
Biddle 2025
)
, curtailed researcher data access
(
Burnat and Davidson 2025
;
Hartmann et al. 2025a
)
, and dismantled trust and safety teams more broadly
(
Moran et al. 2025
)
. Whether one reads these moves as a withdrawal from earlier moderation commitments
(
Magalhães et al. 2026
)
or as a more pragmatic recalibration, their cumulative effect is the same: the institutional support that underwrote a tool like
perspective api
is weaker than it was a decade ago. The underlying problem has not diminished. Toxic speech appears to be rising on several major platforms
(
Törnberg and Chueri 2025
;
Hickey et al. 2025
)
even as already weak and inconsistent enforcement
(
Tonneau et al. 2026
)
is further scaled back, and online toxicity continues to be implicated in offline violence, including anti-migrant riots from the UK
(
Venkataramakrishnan 2025
;
CNN 2025
)
to Spain
(
Euronews 2025
)
. Any commercial infrastructure successor faces the same vulnerability, and without an alternative, researchers will turn to the next proprietary tool.

Do not repeat the mistake

Besides the aforementioned risks imposed by the absence of
perspective api
, its replacement with something similar or worse should be of concern. Closed-source LLM endpoints are already used as toxicity classifiers in evaluation pipelines, subject to the same silent model updates, construct underspecification, and political exposure, but without model cards, threshold guidance, or a research-facing team. The
OpenAI moderation endpoint
, for example, although performing even better than
perspective api
for some target groups, has very limited transparency regarding its training, updating, or annotation schema
(
Hartmann et al. 2025b
)
. On top of that,
OpenAI
’s toxicity classifiers are known to have been trained on labels produced under exploitative conditions
(
Perrigo 2023
)
. Outsourcing toxicity measurement to entities with active interests in its definition is not a neutral technical choice.

Immediate actions

Before the shutdown on 31 December 2026, the community should archive
perspective
scores for all major benchmarks with full method documentation, including query date, threshold settings, and API version where retrievable. Papers relying on
perspective
scores should register them as versioned data artefacts. For new projects, open-weight models with explicit versioning should be preferred over closed endpoints, and any use of generative LLMs as classifiers must disclose all parameters and system prompts in full.

Requirements and recommendations

Drawing on the limitations documented in Section
3
, Table
1
proposes ten requirements for a successor measurement infrastructure. These fall into two groups. The technical requirements address core measurement failures, including reproducibility, construct validity, contextuality, uncertainty, and multilingual performance. The governance requirements address the structural conditions under which these failures emerged, including dependence on a single provider, limited transparency, lack of legitimacy in construct definition, weak accountability mechanisms, and funding fragility. Together, these requirements define the minimum conditions under which toxicity measurement can be considered valid, reliable, and fair. We treat them not as desiderata but as preconditions for legitimate measurement and call for their joint implementation in future infrastructure.

5
Concluding Remarks

The closure of
perspective api
is an opportunity for researchers to be honest about their own role in establishing and sustaining the dependence discussed in this paper. The community was not a passive part within a fragile infrastructure. It adopted a corporate tool, incorporated it into benchmark suites, declared it a standard measure, and even continued using it after its validity problems were known and published. The latter was demonstrated in the case of
Holistic Evaluation of Language Models
(HELM), where the limitations of using
perspective api
were even acknowledged but ignored for purposes of comparability. Understanding why this happened and why no alternative tool was built or even proposed is as much a question about our epistemic practices as it is about our technical innovation processes. The most immediate action researchers can take now is to stop treating measurement infrastructure as someone else’s problem and take accountability. With this call for reflexivity and accountability, we are joining the active debate in the field of critical research on dataset creation and use
(
Paullada et al. 2021
;
Sambasivan et al. 2021
)
, while extending it to the measurement infrastructure that relies on and shapes those datasets. The community needs to treat the construction of measurement infrastructure as a research contribution in its own right and as a form of care. Annotation schemas, inter-rater reliability scores, dataset construction decisions, and calibration evaluations deserve the same methodological rigour and visibility as model performance results.

The field has the capacity to build the infrastructure it needs.

The datasets exist as starting points, and the techniques for uncertainty quantification, per-concept fine-tuning, and context-aware classification are not unresolved problems (see Table
1
). What has been missing is the collective decision to treat infrastructure-building as scientifically valuable work rather than service labour. Funding bodies, programme committees, and tenure committees all participate in that decision, and the community can make strategic choices to change how such contributions are recognised and rewarded. A jointly maintained, community-governed classifier system is not utopian. It is the kind of shared task infrastructure the NLP community has built before, and it is within reach if the field decides to prioritise it.
