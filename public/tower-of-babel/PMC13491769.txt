Gaze perception fuels social anxiety in everyday life: a comparison of two ecological momentary assessment models
Authors: Mao Nanamori, Nozomi Tomita, Ayaka Yanagida, Hiroaki Kumano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-focused attention is a well-established factor in social anxiety disorder. Our previous study found that perceiving oneself as being watched or evaluated in social contexts exacerbates this tendency, but it remains unclear whether the perception of gaze or evaluation serves as the initial trigger for these cognitive processes. This study used ecological momentary assessment to investigate the temporal sequence of these perceptions and their impact on self-focused attention and social anxiety symptoms.MethodsThirty-eight participants (17 male, 21 female) completed self-reported web assessments three times daily for 10 days. Measures included perceived evaluation, gaze perception, self-focused attention, and anxiety experienced in social situations. A total of 569 data points were collected and analyzed with multilevel structural equation modeling to compare two competing theoretical models.ResultsOne model posited that perceived evaluation preceded gaze perception, and the other that gaze perception preceded perceived evaluation. The latter received stronger empirical support, indicating that gaze perception elicits the perceived evaluation, which subsequently increases self-focused attention and social anxiety symptoms. However, model fit differences were small, leaving the alternative model where perceived evaluation precedes gaze perception as a plausible explanation.ConclusionsGaze perception plays a pivotal role in triggering social anxiety symptoms by fostering the perceived evaluation and amplifying self-focused attention. Interventions targeting gaze perception and sensitivity to evaluation may help reduce social anxiety symptoms.Keywords: Self-focused attention, Social anxiety disorder, Ecological momentary assessment, Cognitive process, Gaze perception, Fear of evaluation

Background

Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is a psychiatric condition that is characterized by marked fear or anxiety regarding social situations [1]. SAD is a severe anxiety disorder and is associated with a marked reduction in quality of life [2]. The lifetime prevalence of SAD is estimated to be 10.7% [3], and it manifests a continuum of psychological characteristics, including cognitive and behavioral features, that differ between patients with SAD and individuals who have high levels of social anxiety but no SAD diagnosis [4]. Many people who have high levels of social anxiety have functional disabilities in everyday life. Self-focused attention, which relates to one’s attention to inner cues in social situations, emerges as a central factor in sustaining social anxiety in cognitive-behavioral models of SAD [5]. Self-focused attention features two attention states: the observer perspective and self-focus on body sensation. Self-focused attention from the observer perspective is a state of attention to oneself through the eyes of others [5] and can lead to increasing anxiety in social situations [6]. Self-focus on body sensation is also an important component of self-focused attention. The more bodily sensations highly socially anxious individuals notice, the more they overestimated how anxious they look and the observability of their negative behaviors [7]. Experimental manipulation of information about an increase in pulse rate leads to self-focused attention on body sensation and greater anxiety [8]. Thus, self-focused attention from the observer perspective and self-focus on body sensation are important maintenance factors in SAD that increase social anxiety.

In the cognitive model of Clark and Wells [5], when socially anxious individuals enter social situations, anxiety-related assumptions become activated, leading to heightened self-focus from the observer perspective and the generation of negative self-images, which subsequently result in anxiety symptoms and avoidance behaviors. The model of Heimberg et al. [9] highlights that individuals with social anxiety strongly fear evaluation by others and become hypervigilant toward anticipated negative outcomes and focus on their mental representations of how they believe they appear to others, along with cognitive, behavioral, and emotional cues linked to their anxiety. Socially anxious individuals tend to focus on the discrepancy between their mental representation of self from the audience’s perspective—amplified by self-focus—and the unattainable standards they perceive the audience to hold due to heightened concerns about others’ evaluations, and these cognitive appraisals and negative anticipations serve to reinforce their anxiety [9]. However, it remains uncertain whether the cognitive processes described in these models, including self-focused attention, manifest as predicted in real-life situations among socially anxious individuals. Specifically, the cognitive processes wherein internal activating events triggered by social stimuli lead to heightened self-focused attention and subsequently increased social anxiety have not been fully elucidated.

Our previous study [10] adopted ecological momentary assessment (EMA), which uses repeated collection of real-time data on the behavior and experience of subjects in their natural environments, to capture the variability of self-focused attention in an individual in social situations and identify the factors that elicit self-focused attention. One-time questionnaires may be suitable measures to explain differences in psychological traits between people; however, they are prone to memory bias and cannot describe moment-to-moment changes in behavior and emotions within individuals. EMA can capture intra-individual fluctuations in terms of daily behavior, cognition, and emotion as self-report data with a minimal recall bias [11]. Our previous study revealed that perceiving a gaze in social scenarios is a common trigger for self-focused attention, both for the observer perspective and for body sensation, and perceived evaluation by others was also linked to increased self-focused attention from the observer perspective [10].

However, sequential relations among multiple perceptions that trigger self-focused attention were beyond the scope of our previous study [10]. In real social situations, rather than a single stimulus, multiple stimuli coexist. A more comprehensive understanding of the cognitive processes that induce self-focused attention in daily life can be attained by concurrently examining being evaluated, as emphasized in conventional cognitive behavioral models, alongside gaze perception, which was closely associated with self-focused attention in our previous study [10]. It is crucial to determine whether it is the perception of gaze or of being evaluated that initially triggers heightened self-focused attention in social contexts. In considering the sequence of these perceptions, the fear of negative evaluation (FNE) should not be regarded as an adjunct symptom of social anxiety but as a potential predisposing factor. This view is supported by studies of twins that demonstrate its heritability [12]. Our previous study [10] found that controlling for fear of being evaluated completely eliminated the effect of perceived evaluation on self-focused attention, whereas controlling for fear of gaze did not eliminate the effect of gaze perception. This suggests that perceived evaluation is more strongly influenced by fear-related components and may therefore be conceptually closer to predisposing factors such as FNE. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that perceived evaluation precedes gaze perception in the sequence that leads to self-focused attention. To test this, we compared two hypothetical models: Model (a), in which perceived evaluation precedes gaze perception, and Model (b), in which gaze perception precedes perceived evaluation (Fig. 1). We assume that these sequences are primarily relevant within individuals, particularly as they experience events sequentially. However, variations among individuals, especially those with high levels of social anxiety, are likely to influence these sequences. Therefore, our analysis focuses mainly on intra-individual dynamics, while also considering inter-individual differences to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena. This dual-level approach allows us to assess how individual differences may impact the hypothesized sequences.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from a Japanese university in a rural region through a campus-wide portal advertisement. Participants were screened using the Japanese version of the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale [13], with a boundary score of 30 indicating a high tendency toward social anxiety. All participants were Japanese. During the prescreening process, participants were asked to self-report the following: (a) absence of any current illness or injury, (b) absence of current use of medication, (c) absence of extreme sleep deprivation or fatigue, (d) no current or past history of psychotherapy, and (e) no experience in the past month of being involved in, or having a close acquaintance involved in, a major accident that is sometimes recalled and found distressing. These questions were intended to exclude the possibility that participants were affected by psychiatric disorders, psychosomatic illnesses, or related medical treatment, thereby ensuring that participation would not pose undue risk. Individuals who met any exclusion criteria were not eligible to participate. This prescreening procedure and its exclusion criteria were reviewed and approved by the university ethics committee prior to the start of the study.

Data were collected from two groups of participants. The first group consisted of 22 participants (4 male, 18 female) who were recruited between September and November 2022 and had also participated in our previous study [10]. The second group included 17 new participants (13 male, 4 female) recruited between October and November 2024. Although our previous study [10] examined which stimuli in social situations initiate self-focused attention, it did not address sequential relations among multiple perceptions, which is the focus of the present investigation. To explore this new research question, we combined data from two groups: one previously recruited in our previous study [10], and another newly recruited for the current study. While we were mindful of the partial overlap between datasets and the associated risk of inflated statistical significance, the present hypotheses were independently developed based on theoretical considerations and prior literature rather than being derived from the previous study’s findings. Therefore, we considered it appropriate to include the overlapping data in our analyses. In our previous study, the sample showed a gender imbalance, with relatively few male participants. To address this issue, we specifically encouraged male participation during the additional recruitment phase.

Procedure

The participants were provided with an explanation of the study and gave their written informed consent. Prior to starting the EMA, they were presented with the questionnaires and instructed about how to answer the questions through an online meeting. For 10 consecutive days, participants were instructed to regularly complete a questionnaire on their smartphones through a URL sent via email. During the initial briefing, participants were informed that they could pause or discontinue completing the questionnaire at any time if they experienced stress or discomfort. However, no participants opted to do so during the EMA survey. Throughout the EMA period, reminder emails were sent three times daily: 12:00, 17:00, and 22:00. Reminders were sent at fixed, predetermined times, without any randomization to ensure consistency across participants. We adopted a five-hour recall window to reduce participant burden because some daily social situations (e.g., classes, part-time employment) last several hours, whereas others (e.g., team meetings) occur infrequently within a day. In this context, event-based protocols requiring immediate post-event responses may leave participants uncertain about which situations qualify and may result in extended nonresponse intervals, both of which can undermine compliance. Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires within one hour of receiving each reminder email. If the respondents failed to respond within the hour, they were requested to ignore the email. The participants were informed that online social situations, including video conferences, qualify as social situations. They were also told that the social situation with their family did not qualify as a social situation. After completing the survey, each participant received a book voucher worth 1,000 JPY as compensation.

Measure of social anxiety (Trait Measure)

The Japanese version of the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS-J)

The LSAS-J [13] was used to screen for social anxiety. It has demonstrated strong internal consistency (α = 0.95) and high test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.92). Additionally, it exhibits a correlation of 0.63 with the Japanese version of the Social Avoidance and Distress scale, which is used for assessing social anxiety, indicating convergent validity. The LSAS-J assesses fear and avoidance for 24 typical social performance and interaction situations. Participants rate a range of social situations on a scale of 0 (no fear) to 3 (severe fear) based on their level of fear. They also indicate their level of avoidance by choosing from four options: 0 (never), 1 (avoid 33% or less), 2 (avoid 50%), and 3 (avoid 67% to 100%). The total scores range from 0 to 144.

EMA measures (state measure)

Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires in the order of social situations with details about the social situations, perception of stimuli, fear of stimuli, self-focused attention from an observer perspective, self-focus on body sensation, and social anxiety.

Social situation

After instructing participants to “reflect on events within the past five hours and select the most recent one from the following social situations you have experienced,” we administered the social situation questions to determine whether they had encountered any of the nine specific social scenarios. The nine situations include “Participating in small groups or meetings,” “Eating or drinking in public places,” “Having a one-on-one conversation,” “Talking on the phone with someone,” “Acting in front of an audience,” “Giving a report to a group,” “Telephoning in public,” “Working while being observed,” and “Being in a crowded place.” If the participants experienced more than one of the nine social situations within the 5 h before responding, they were asked to select the most applicable one. After the respondents selected a social situation, they were instructed to “answer the following questions about the social situation you selected in the previous section.” They were then also asked who they were with during the situation. Those who had not experienced any social situation were asked to select the option “I did not experience any social situations.”

Perception of being evaluated and gaze in a social situation

After instructing participants to “reflect on the social situation you have experienced and answer if each of the following items applied to the situation,” we administered the perception-of-stimuli questions to assess whether they had perceived any of the nine specific stimuli. Responses were recorded on a two-point rating scale (0 = no, 1 = yes). For the purpose of the present study, we analyzed only two variables from these items: perceived evaluation and perceived gaze. We also administered fear-of-stimuli items, but these were not analyzed in the present study.

Self-focused attention from the observer perspective

After instructing participants to “reflect on the social situations you have experienced and answer to what extent each of the following items fit your behavior,” we administered the self-focused attention questions to assess the extent of self-focused attention from the observer perspective. The items were adapted from the Mental Perspective Scale for Social Anxiety Disorder [14], whose validity and reliability have been demonstrated. The scale consists of field perspective, observer perspective, and detached mindfulness perspective. This study used only the observer perspective. The following four questions were included in the EMA questionnaire: “I was worried about myself through other people’s eyes,” “I was concerned about how I appeared to others,” “I imagined how I looked like to others,” and “Although I did not want to worry about how I appeared to others, I couldn’t stop worrying about it.” Each item was evaluated on a 6-point Likert scale, spanning from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

Self-focus on body sensation

After instructing participants to “reflect on the social situations you have experienced and answer to what extent each of the following items fit your behavior,” we administered the self-focused attention questions to assess the extent of self-focus to body-sensation. The items were adapted from the Focused Attention Scale [15], and its validity and reliability have been demonstrated. This scale comprises two components: self-focused attention, which evaluates how much individuals notice their own bodily sensations, and other-focused attention, which evaluates their attention to others’ behavior. We used self-focused attention to assess self-focus on body sensation in EMA. The following four questions were included in the EMA questionnaire: “I was paying attention to my complexion,” “I was paying attention to my physical reactions (e.g., heart rate),” “I was paying attention to my body temperature,” and “I was paying attention to my sweat.” Each item was scored on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

Social anxiety

After instructing participants to “answer to what extent each of the following items fit you immediately following the social situation,” we administered the social anxiety question to assess the degree of social anxiety immediately after the participant experienced a social situation. Participants rated their anxiety immediately after experiencing a particular social situation on a 6-point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 6 = strongly agree).

Statistical analysis

For self-focused attention from the observer perspective and self-focus on body sensation, total scores for each of the four items that measured self-focused attention were used in the analysis. The data had a hierarchical structure: repeated assessments (within-individual level) were nested within individuals (between-individual level) and contained a combination of variance of the within-individual level and the between-individual level. We aimed to clarify the cognitive processes exhibited by individuals with social anxiety in daily social situations. This necessitated an evaluation of whether it was critical to distinguish between effects that occur across individuals from those that occur within individuals in our analysis. To accomplish this, we computed the overall averages (grand means) for the perceptions of evaluation and gaze, self-focused attention from the observer perspective, self-focus on body sensation, and social anxiety. We calculated intraclass correlations (ICCs) to assess the proportion of total variance attributable to between-individual differences. Larger ICC values indicate that a greater share of the variance is at the between-individual level, which suggests a stronger hierarchical data structure and supports the use of multilevel analysis. Next, we examined the correlations among the variables at the within-individual and between-individual levels using multilevel modeling.

To investigate the cognitive processes, we conducted multilevel structural equation modeling to analyze the structures at the within-individual and between-individual levels. We developed and compared two models (Fig. 1). Model (a) posits that perceived evaluation influences gaze perception, which increases self-focused attention and, in turn, social anxiety symptoms. Model (b) reverses the order of the first two constructs. We hypothesize that, in individuals with high social anxiety, evaluation sensitivity associated with FNE temporally precedes gaze perception in daily life; accordingly, Model (a) can be expected to show superior fit. We specified a multilevel structural equation model with a latent self-focused attention factor, indicated by self-focused attention from the observer perspective and self-focus on body sensation, and a latent symptom factor, indicated by social anxiety. These models were estimated using the maximum likelihood method. To assess the fit of the structural models, we employed the comparative fit index (CFI) and the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA). Following Bentler [16], CFI values above 0.90 were considered acceptable. For RMSEA, values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicated a good fit, while values in the range of 0.08 to 0.10 were regarded as indicative of a mediocre fit [17]. In addition, we incorporated the standardized root mean residual (SRMR) as a fit index, following Fukubayashi and Fuji [18], who applied multilevel structural equation modeling to data obtained through repeated measurements. The cut-off value for SRMR was set at 0.08, as recommended by Hu and Bentler [19]. SRMR was assessed separately for the within-level and between-level models to ensure a rigorous evaluation of model fit, consistent with recommendations in the multilevel modeling literature. Furthermore, we utilized the AIC [20] and the BIC [21] to compare the relative fit of the two models. Both AIC and BIC serve as model selection criteria, with lower values indicating a better fit. For the purpose of model comparison in this study, we selected the model with the lower AIC and BIC values. These analyses were performed using HAD (solver on ver. 18.003) [22].

Results

Number of EMA responses and final samples

Recruitment was conducted at the same university, had identical inclusion and exclusion criteria, and all study procedures were kept entirely consistent with those used in the earlier phase. As a result, we successfully recruited 17 additional participants (13 male, 4 female), none of whom were excluded at the point of entry, regardless of gender. One participant whose compliance rate (40%) was identified as an extreme outlier through boxplot analysis—falling below the 25th percentile minus three times the interquartile range—was excluded from the analysis. The final dataset comprised 38 participants (17 male, 21 female), with a mean age of 20.79 years (SD = 1.78) and a mean LSAS-J score of 64.74 (SD = 22.41, range = 30–128). A total of 1,140 surveys were distributed and 149 were unanswered, leaving the data from 991 responses available for inclusion. The average compliance rate was 86.9% (SD = 12, range = 57–100). When responses that reported no social situations (395, 39.9%), interactions with family members (11), or events occurring more than five hours before the response (16), 569 valid responses were available for inclusion in the final dataset.

Table 1 shows the number of reports per participant for each social situation (mean, SD, range). Among the nine social situations, the most frequently reported was being in a crowded place (162), followed by having a one-on-one conversation (138) and participating in small groups or meetings (117). The least frequent was giving a report to a group (5). Analysis of the average percentage of social situations reported by each participant found that being in crowded environments was the most prevalent (27.2%), followed by engaging in one-on-one conversations (23.4%) and participating in small group discussions or meetings (22.1%). Giving a report to a group was the least frequently encountered social situation (1.2%). The frequencies of the participants’ responses and the averages of the reported percentages were nearly congruent, suggesting uniformity in the experiences of the nine social situations among most participants.

Discussion

This study conducted an EMA to investigate the sequence of cognitive processes involved in social anxiety in everyday life. We compared two models of the relation, one in which perceived evaluation influences gaze perception and the other in the reverse direction. The results indicated that models where gaze perception is the starting point rather than perceived evaluation showed slightly better fit indices, including CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR at both within-individual and between-individual levels. Additionally, the AIC and BIC values were lower for the model where gaze perception is the initial element. Therefore, contrary to the hypothetical model in which perceived evaluation precedes other processes, the model with gaze perception as the starting point demonstrated a better fit. This study supports the model in which gaze perception affects perceived evaluation, thereby increasing self-focused attention and leading to symptoms of social anxiety. Furthermore, our findings indicate that perceived evaluation following gaze perception directly impacts symptoms of social anxiety, even without the involvement of self-focused attention. This suggests that gaze perception serves as an initial trigger that amplifies social anxiety symptoms through multiple pathways. Additionally, the results also indicate a strong relation between self-focused attention and social anxiety symptoms at the between-individual level. Specifically, individuals who generally exhibit high levels of self-focused attention are likely to experience severe symptoms of SAD. However, both gaze perception and perceived evaluation exhibited extremely low ICCs, indicating substantial within-individual variability. This suggests that the relation of these perceptions to self-focused attention and social anxiety symptoms is not easily detectable at the between-individual level. The EMA approach, by capturing moment-to-moment fluctuations within individuals, revealed that these perceptions dynamically vary and are closely linked to fluctuations in both self-focused attention and social anxiety symptoms, underscoring the importance of assessing within-individual fluctuations.

Contrary to our initial assumption, this study discovered that gaze perception acts as the first element of cognitive processes in individuals with high social anxiety, rather than perceived evaluation, even though the difference in fit indices was marginal. Social anxiety is positively related to the feeling of being looked at by others, such that the more socially anxious an individual is, the more they feel being looked at, especially when confronted with negative and neutral facial expressions [23]. The perceived range of gaze direction may exceed the actual amount of eye contact. Previous research has shown that greater perceived extent of gaze is positively associated with higher levels of social anxiety levels [24]. Individuals who have high levels of social anxiety may perceive excessive gaze from others even when actual eye contact is absent. Thus, due to hypersensitivity to others’ gazes, which is a characteristic of individuals with high social anxiety, gaze perception may precede the feeling of being evaluated by others in social situations. Chen and Drummond [25] further found that individuals with high FNE experienced heightened anxiety and increased awareness of bodily symptoms when they listened to a recording of themselves singing after maintaining eye contact for three minutes in a comparison with when no eye contact was made. Their findings suggest that prior eye contact increases the fear of evaluation and induces self-focused attention to body sensation during a listening task, which aligns with our findings that gaze perception plays a critical role in triggering cognitive processes related to social anxiety. Given that perceived evaluation likely involves an element of fear [10], it is reasonable to assume that this perception requires additional cognitive and emotional processing to integrate the external cue with an internal affective response. Therefore, it is theoretically plausible that evaluation-related cognitive processes occur after perceiving an external stimulus such as a gaze. Taken together, these results suggest that hypersensitivity to gaze may contribute to a cognitive sequence where gaze perception amplifies fear of evaluation and self-focused attention, ultimately leading to increased social anxiety symptoms. This supports the notion that gaze perception serves as an early cognitive cue in the processing of social threats and is a potential target for therapeutic intervention.

For the purpose of this study, the model in which gaze perception precedes perceived evaluation appears to be the more appropriate choice. However, based on fit indices, the alternative model, in which perceived evaluation precedes gaze perception, remains sufficiently valid as a sequential process and, therefore, cannot be rejected. Although the former model demonstrated a slightly better fit, the difference was not substantial enough to decisively favor one model over the other. Initially, we hypothesized that sensitivity to evaluation, driven by FNE, would precede the perception of others’ gazes. This assumption was rooted in the understanding that FNE is often viewed as a risk or vulnerability factor that is predictive of the severity of SAD from childhood, typically regarded as a stable trait with minimal exploration of its fluctuating nature. Wong and Rapee [26] suggest that FNE may signify a predisposing factor for SAD in teenagers. Fredrick and Luebbe [27] found that FNE predicted a heightened severity of social anxiety symptoms six months later. Furthermore, high parental levels of FNE can extend to apprehensions regarding negative evaluations of their own offspring by others, thereby establishing intergenerational pathways of social anxiety that impact children’s social anxiety levels as early as 7.5 years of age [28]. Therefore, the notion that fear of evaluation could precede gaze perception remains plausible; indeed, our data did not conclusively refute the evaluation-first sequence, which had an acceptable fit. In practice, the small difference between models suggests that in real life, perceptions of gaze and evaluation may occur in either order or reinforce each other, rather than one exclusively driving the other in all cases. Our results overall favored the gaze-first pathway, but the evaluation-first path cannot be discounted.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consisted solely of Japanese university students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, cultures, and social contexts. Given the role of cultural factors in shaping social anxiety [29], future studies should include more diverse populations. Second, individual differences such as FNE were not fully explored; cross-level analyses could clarify how such traits influence cognitive processing patterns. Third, although the EMA method was designed to capture experiences in real time, our procedure asked participants to recall the most recent social situation within the past five hours, which may have introduced recall bias. An event-based EMA design, though more demanding, could minimize this issue. Because all variables were measured concurrently, causal directions between cognitive processes could not be determined; longitudinal or time-lagged designs are needed. Fourth, the present study partially reused data from our previous work [10], which may have inflated statistical significance and introduced potential confounding influences, such as gender, timing of data collection, and their interaction. Although our analyses emphasized model fit comparisons rather than solely statistical significance, future research should aim to recruit participants in a single wave with balanced gender representation. Lastly, the small sample size limited analyses by type of social situation and individual level; larger samples are needed for such analyses.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that highly socially anxious individuals exhibit a cognitive process in which gaze perception increases perceived evaluation and of self-focused attention, ultimately leading to social anxiety symptoms. Although the model where perceived evaluation precedes gaze perception less well than the reverse model, its fit indices were still acceptable and thus it could not be rejected. A key finding was that the pathways leading to symptoms differed between the two models. When gaze perception preceded perceived evaluation, perceived evaluation directly influenced symptoms, whereas when perceived evaluation preceded gaze perception, there was no direct path from gaze perception to symptoms. This suggests that an increase in perceived evaluation derived from gaze perception can amplify social anxiety symptoms independently of self-focused attention. These findings highlight that the subsequent cognitive processes depend on which perception is initially activated. By employing EMA to capture the daily experiences of individuals with significant social anxiety, this study offered insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying social anxiety.
