Reversible control of CAR T cells through PROTAC compound targeting bromodomain mutant
Authors: Yeongrin Kim, Da Yeon Lee, Ji U. Choi, Moonjung Jeun, Heung Kyoung Lee, Sang Un Choi, Chi Hoon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) is an innovative strategy for selectively degrading target proteins. In this study, we demonstrate that a PROTAC compound, AGB1, specifically degrades a bromodomain L387V mutant (BD2m)-tagged chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-BD2m). Unlike ARV771, which degrades wild-type bromodomains, AGB1 does not impair normal T cell function, as it spares endogenous bromodomain-containing proteins such as BRD4. Notably, AGB1 degrades CAR-BD2m with approximately 10-fold higher efficiency than ARV771 targets the wild-type BD2-tagged CAR (CAR-BD2w). In vitro, AGB1 reversibly modulates the activity of CAR-BD2m T cells through targeted CAR degradation. In preclinical models, CAR-BD2m T cells exhibit potent antitumor activity comparable to that of conventional CAR T cells, and AGB1 effectively regulates the activity of CAR-BD2m T cells. Together, these results suggest that the BD2m system can serve as an efficient tagging platform for targeted CAR protein degradation, thereby enabling successful and reversible control of CAR T cell activity in patients.

Introduction

T cells engineered with CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) have demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy against blood cancer.1,2 However, these living drugs can cause significant adverse effects, including cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, and on-target, off-tumor effects, which pose substantial risks to patients.3,4 To address these challenges, many clinical trials have incorporated safety systems to allow the controlled removal of CAR T cells when necessary.5 One such system currently under clinical investigation is the inducible caspase-9 (iCasp9) system, which triggers apoptosis of CAR T cells engineered to express FKBP-fused caspase-9 upon administration of AP1903.6,7,8,9 In addition to iCasp9, several alternative off-switch strategies have been developed to modulate CAR T cell activity using small molecules or biologics. These include chemically disruptable heterodimer-based systems,10 destabilizing domain-based CARs,11 and antibody-mediated depletion approaches,12 each of which provides distinct mechanisms for controlling CAR T cell function but may involve specific design features or require continuous drug administration.

Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have emerged over the past decade as a promising therapeutic strategy, enabling the targeting of pathogenic proteins that are difficult to modulate with traditional small-molecule inhibitors. PROTACs exploit endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligases to selectively degrade proteins of interest via the ubiquitin-proteasome system in a catalytic and reversible manner. To enhance the efficacy of these compounds, recent studies have developed next-generation PROTACs, including small-molecule prodrugs, biomacromolecule-PROTAC conjugates, and nano-PROTACs, which improve in vivo activity, increase target specificity, and reduce off-target effects.13 Building on these features, we applied PROTAC technology to modulate the activity of CAR T cells.

Previously, we developed a safety system to modulate CAR expression using the bromodomain PROTAC ARV771 in the KHYG-1 natural killer (NK) cell line.14 While ARV771 successfully regulated the expression levels of bromodomain 2 wild-type (BD2w)-tagged CAR (CAR-BD2w), it also induced cytotoxicity in normal cells, including CAR T cells, due to the degradation of endogenous bromodomain-containing proteins. To address this issue, we employed a system utilizing bromodomain L387V mutant (BD2m)-tagged CAR (CAR-BD2m) and the AGB1 PROTAC. AGB1 selectively degrades BD2m15 without targeting endogenous bromodomain-containing proteins, thereby preventing cytotoxicity in normal cells.

In this study, we aimed to demonstrate that AGB1 could reversibly regulate CAR-BD2m expression and effectively control the activity of CAR-BD2m T cells. Furthermore, we validated whether CAR-BD2m T cells exhibit comparable anticancer activity to conventional CD19-targeted CAR T cells in an NSG mouse xenograft tumor model of B cell malignancy.

Results

BD2 mutant domain is efficient for inducing CAR degradation through the PROTAC compound

Previously, we evaluated the ability of ARV771 to degrade CAR-BD2w proteins.14 However, ARV771 also degraded endogenous bromodomain-containing proteins, leading to severe cytotoxicity. To enable efficient CAR degradation without inducing cytotoxicity, we screened various combinations of degron-tagged CAR and PROTAC systems. We tested combinations of CSNK1A1-tagged CAR with lenalidomide,16 GSPT1-tagged CAR with CC885,17 RIPK2-tagged CAR with RIPK degrader 2,18 and BD2m-tagged CAR with AGB1 (Figure S1A).15 Among these, lenalidomide and RIPK degrader 2 failed to degrade degron-tagged CARs. Although CC885 efficiently degraded GSPT1-tagged CAR, it exhibited high cytotoxicity in both Jurkat cells (IC50: 1.446 nM, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.9757–2.142) and primary T cells (IC50: 0.5568 nM, 95% CI, 0.3357–0.9234), as shown in Figures S1B and S1C. We selected AGB1 for further experiments because it efficiently degraded CAR-BD2m without severe cytotoxicity (Figure 1C). AGB1 contains a ligand specific to the BD2 mutant L387V (BD2m) (Figure 1A).15 As shown in Figure 1B, we assessed the IC50 values of ARV771 and AGB1 for BD2w and BD2m using an alphascreen assay. Alphascreen assay is a bead-based proximity assay used to measure the binding interaction between recombinant proteins, in this case allowing the determination of IC50 values for purified bromodomains. ARV771 bound to both BD2w and BD2m with similar affinities (IC50(BD2w): 0.6151 μM, 95% CI, 0.3245–1.166; IC50(BD2m): 0.1329 μM, 95% CI, 0.1410–0.4912). In contrast, AGB1 displayed approximately 25-fold higher selectivity for BD2m over BD2w (IC50(BD2w): 3.423 μM, 95% CI, 1.678–6.986; IC50(BD2m): 0.1386 μM, 95% CI, 0.07735–0.2485). To evaluate the cytotoxicity of PROTAC, we conducted a luminescence-based cell viability assay in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and primary T cells derived from the same donor, cultured with each compound for 72 h. AGB1 selectively degrades CAR-BD2m without degradation of endogenous BRD4

We constructed the degradation assay with the following CAR variants with or without the BD2 domain: FMC63-CD28-CD3ζ (CAR), FMC63-CD28-BD2-CD3ζ (CAR-BD2w), and FMC63-CD28-BD2(L387V)-CD3ζ (CAR-BD2m) (Figure 2A; Table S1). To investigate CAR proteolysis by PROTAC, Jurkat cells expressing CAR, CAR-BD2w, or CAR-BD2m were used. ARV771 degraded both CAR-BD2w and CAR-BD2m as well as endogenous BRD4 (Figure 2B). Notably, AGB1 induced the degradation of CAR-BD2m at a concentration as low as 1 nM, whereas ARV771 began degrading CAR-BD2w at 10 nM. This indicated that AGB1 exhibits approximately 10-fold higher potency in CAR-BD2m degradation compared to ARV771’s activity against CAR-BDw. To investigate the mechanism of CAR-BD2m degradation by AGB1, CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells were pre-incubated with 10 or 50 nM of the proteasome inhibitor epoxomicin for 1 h.19 Following this, AGB1 was added, and the cells were cultured overnight. Proteolysis of CAR-BD2m was effectively blocked by 50 nM epoxomicin, demonstrating that AGB1-mediated degradation of CAR-BD2m occurs through the proteasomal pathway. In the absence of epoxomicin, CAR-BD2m was degraded, with an IC50 of 0.002640 μM (Figure S2A). Furthermore, to assess whether AGB1 degrades other BD2m-tagged CAR proteins, we inserted the BD2m domain into two additional CAR constructs, anti-human-ganglioside-GD2 CAR and anti-human-CD79B CAR. Both BD2m-tagged anti-human-ganglioside-GD2 CAR (IC50: 0.0442 μM) and BD2m-tagged anti-human-CD79B CAR (IC50: 0.001891 μM) proteins were degraded in 293T cells (Figure S2B). To further examine the kinetics and reversibility of CAR-BD2m degradation by AGB1, we treated CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells with 100 nM AGB1 and monitored CAR protein levels over time. CAR-BD2m protein degradation was observed as early as 20 min after AGB1 treatment, reaching a substantial reduction relative to the DMSO control levels by 40 min (Figure S3A). Its expression began to recover within 2 h after compound removal and returned to nearly the level of the DMSO control by 4 h (Figure S3B). AGB1 regulates the activity of Jurkat and KHYG-1 cells engineered to express CAR-BD2m

To investigate whether AGB1 regulates the activity of CAR-BD2m, we measured IL-2 secreted by CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells with or without AGB1 treatment. CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells co-cultured with CD19-positive Nalm6 cells secreted significantly higher level of IL-2 compared to cells cultured without Nalm6 (Figure 2C). However, AGB1 treatment substantially reduced IL-2 secretion in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 0.1713 μM (95% CI, 0.0449–0.6533), indicating that AGB1 degrades CAR-BD2m proteins in CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells, thereby proportionally suppressing IL-2 secretion. Notably, at an AGB1 concentration of 10 μM, IL-2 secretion was restored due to the “hook effect.”20 These results demonstrate that the activity of CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells can be effectively regulated by AGB1. To further confirm that AGB1 regulates CAR-BD2m activity, we employed the KHYG-1 NK cell line, which exhibits cytolytic activity against target cells. KHYG-1, CAR KHYG-1, and CAR-BD2m KHYG-1 cell lines were generated (Figure 2D). CAR KHYG-1 or CAR-BD2m KHYG-1 cells were co-cultured with luciferase-expressing Nalm6 cells (Nalm6-Luc) in the presence of AGB1 for 4 h at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 10:1. Effector CAR KHYG-1 cells were pretreated with DMSO or AGB1 overnight before being co-cultured with target cells. In the presence of DMSO, CAR-BD2m KHYG-1 cells exhibited lytic activity against target cells comparable to that of CAR KHYG-1 cells. However, in the presence of AGB1, the lytic activity of the CAR-BD2m KHYG-1 cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 0.03715 μM (95% CI, 0.02147–0.06427), while the activity of unmodified KHYG-1 remained unaffected (Figure 2E). These findings demonstrate that AGB1 selectively regulates the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m KHYG-1 cells.

AGB1 regulates the cytolytic activity of CAR-BD2m T cells

Using Jurkat and KHYG-1 cell lines, we confirmed the degradation of CAR-BD2m. Next, we generated CAR-BD2m T cells from PBMCs obtained from healthy donors (Figure 3A). AGB1 induced dose-dependent degradation of the CAR-BD2m protein, whereas it did not degrade the CAR protein (Figure 3B). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that cell surface expression of CAR-BD2m was reduced following AGB1 treatment, while the surface expression of CAR remained unchanged (Figure 3C). To evaluate whether AGB1 regulates the activity of CAR-BD2m T cells, we prepared non-transduced T cells (NT), GFP-expressing T cells (GFP T), and CAR-BD2m T cells. Effector T cells were pre-treated overnight with either DMSO or AGB1. Subsequently, NT, GFP T, and CAR-BD2m T cells (3 × 104 cells) were co-cultured with Nalm6-Luc target cells (1 × 104 cells) for 4 or 24 h. CAR-BD2m T cells effectively lysed target cells in the presence of DMSO. As anticipated, AGB1 efficiently suppressed the cytolytic activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3D). These results demonstrate that AGB1 can effectively control the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells. To further investigate the kinetics of AGB1-induced CAR-BD2m degradation and its impact on CAR-BD2m T cell activity, we treated CAR-BD2m T cells with 1 μM AGB1 and monitored CAR protein levels over time along with their cytolytic activity. CAR-BD2m protein degradation was observed as early as 0.5 h after AGB1 treatment (Figure S4A), and the cytolytic activity of CAR-BD2m T cells was also significantly reduced at the same time point (Figure S4B). To determine whether the localization of the BD2m domain within the CAR construct affects AGB1-mediated degradation and functional regulation of CAR T cell activity, we designed an additional CAR construct, FMC63-CD28-CD3ζ-BD2(L387V) (CAR-BD2m-C) (Figure S5A). AGB1 reversibly regulates the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells

We conducted an experiment to see the restoration of anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells following the removal of AGB1. During transduction of the CAR or CAR-BD2m gene into the T cells using viral particles, 100 nM AGB1 was added to the culture medium. AGB1 was subsequently removed 3 days prior to sampling for western blot and flow cytometry (Figure 4A). AGB1 selectively degraded CAR-BD2m, and re-expression of the CAR-BD2m protein was observed after the removal of AGB1, which was confirmed by western blot and flow cytometry (Figures 4B and 4C). Next, we investigated whether the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells could be restored following AGB1 removal. As expected, CAR T cells demonstrated consistent lytic activity against Nalm6-Luc cells, regardless of the presence of AGB1 (Figure 4D). In contrast, the lytic activity of CAR-BD2m T cells was restored upon AGB1 removal (Figure 4E). AGB1 regulates the antitumor activity of CAR-BD2m T cells

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of CAR-BD2m T cells, NSG mice engrafted with CD19-positive Nalm6-Luc cells by tail vein injection were administered with NT, CAR T, or CAR-BD2m T cells intravenously (Figure 5A). All mice in the control (PBS) and NT groups died within day 20; in contrast, all mice treated with CAR T or CAR-BD2m T cells survived beyond 45 days (Figure 5B). To further assess the efficacy of CAR-BD2m T cells in a subcutaneous xenograft model, NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with Nalm6-Luc cells. Subsequently, NT, CAR T, or CAR-BD2m T cells were administered intravenously (Figure 5C). Both CAR T and CAR-BD2m T cells significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to NT (Figure 5D) and prolonged overall survival (Figure 5E). These results indicate that the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells was comparable to that of CAR T cells in vivo. To evaluate how effectively AGB1 regulates protein expression in vivo, we generated T cells expressing a luciferase reporter tagged with the BD2m domain (Luc-BD2m). Prior to in vivo experiments, we first performed in vitro validation using Luc-BD2m T cells. Treatment with 1 μM AGB1 led to a significant reduction in luminescence signal as early as 0.5 h after treatment (Figure S6A), indicating rapid degradation of the Luc-BD2m reporter. Consistent with the in vitro findings, in vivo administration of AGB1 to mice infused with Luc-BD2m T cells resulted in a clear decrease in luminescence signal as early as 2 h after treatment compared to mice that did not receive AGB1. Notably, over time, the luminescence signal in AGB1-treated mice gradually recovered (Figure S6B), indicating that BD2m-tagged protein expression can be regulated in a rapid and reversible manner in vivo. We next investigated whether AGB1 could regulate the antitumor activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in a subcutaneous xenograft model. The CAR-BD2m T group was divided into two groups. One group received only CAR-BD2m T cells, and the other group received both CAR-BD2m T cells and AGB1. NSG mice were subcutaneously engrafted with 2 × 106 Nalm6-Luc cells. 1 × 107 NT or CAR-BD2m T cells were injected intravenously. AGB1 (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 5 days per week for 3 weeks (Figure 5F). Figure 5G shows that tumor suppression by CAR-BD2m T cells was attenuated by AGB1 administration. To further evaluate whether our model could be applied to other types of cancer cells, we performed the same experiment using luciferase-expressing Ramos (Ramos-Luc) cells instead of Nalm6-Luc cells. NSG mice were subcutaneously engrafted with 2 × 106 Ramos-Luc cells, followed by intravenous injection of 1 × 107 NT or CAR-BD2m T cells (Figure S7A). AGB1 was administered with the same dosing regimen as described earlier. Consistent with the previous results, AGB1 treatment significantly attenuated the antitumor efficacy of CAR-BD2m T cells against Ramos tumors (Figure S7B). These results suggest that AGB1 regulates the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in a subcutaneous xenograft model. Discussion

CD19-targeted CAR T cell therapy has shown remarkable efficacy in relapsed or refractory B cell malignancies,21 leading to FDA approval for B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and diffuse large B cell lymphoma.4 However, its clinical application is limited by severe side effects, particularly on-target, off-tumor toxicity, cytokine release syndrome, and neurotoxicity, which result from hyperactivation of CAR T cells.21,22,23

Strategies to mitigate these adverse effects, such as controlling CAR T cell activation using drugs or small molecules, are actively being explored.24 Among these, a chemically disruptable heterodimer-based CAR system enables reversible control of CAR signaling via small-molecule-induced disruption of engineered protein interactions. This system employs specifically designed protein interfaces and matching ligands, which represent particular design features and may require additional design considerations.10 The iCasp9 system, a representative safety mechanism currently employed in clinical trials, allows for the induced apoptosis of CAR T cells via the administration of a chemical dimerization inducer such as AP1903, which triggers the activation of FKBP-fused caspase-9 in engineered CAR T cells.25 However, this approach can result in substantial depletion of CAR T cells, potentially requiring re-administration to maintain therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, safety systems using destabilizing domain-tagged CARs have been developed. In these systems, the continuous proteasomal degradation of destabilizing domain-tagged CARs expressed on engineered T cells is halted upon binding of a stabilizing drug to the destabilizing domain.11 This method requires ongoing administration of the stabilizing drug to maintain CAR T cell function. Antibodies can also be used as tools to control the activity of CAR T cells. For example, CAR T cells expressing truncated version of EGFR are depleted by cetuximab, thereby eliminating the side effects caused by CAR T cells. However, this method also has the disadvantage of depleting CAR T cells in the body.12

Compared to these approaches, our degron-based PROTAC system provides a reversible and tunable means of regulating CAR expression through targeted protein degradation, without requiring permanent genetic ablation or continuous drug-dependent stabilization. In addition, because our compound targets a protein that is not endogenously expressed in cells, it minimizes potential off-target effects. PROTAC is a bifunctional small molecule composed of a target protein-binding moiety, an E3 ubiquitin ligase recruitment moiety, and a linker.26 When a PROTAC brings the target protein into proximity with an E3 ligase, the E3 ligase ubiquitinates the target protein, leading to its degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome system.27 PROTAC is a rapid and reversible chemical knockdown tool that provides a complementary approach to other gene-editing technologies without the risk of spontaneous mutations or genetic compensation.28 When administered to patients experiencing side effects, PROTACs block further CAR T cell activity by inducing CAR degradation. Upon discontinuation of PROTAC treatment, CAR re-expression occurs in the remaining CAR T cells, offering a reversible mechanism to mitigate side effects without the need for continuous drug administration.

Recent studies have used PROTACs with genetic tags, such as eDHFR, to reversibly control CAR T cell activity in a dose-dependent manner, highlighting that PROTAC-mediated CAR regulation is an actively evolving area of research.29 Notably, our previous study demonstrated a PROTAC-based strategy to modulate CAR activity using a human-derived bromodomain degron targeted by the bromodomain-binding PROTAC, ARV771, in the KHYG-1 NK cell line.14 However, ARV771 was deemed unsuitable for clinical trials due to its high cytotoxicity against normal cells. As a result, we designed a safer regulatory system utilizing AGB1, a PROTAC that targets the BD2m. Because AGB1 selectively targets BD2m without affecting the endogenous bromodomain-containing proteins, such as BRD4, it has demonstrated significantly reduced cytotoxicity in normal cells.15 To further optimize specificity and safety, we evaluated multiple combinations of degron-tagged CAR and PROTAC systems (Figure S1A). Most combinations were either insufficient in degrading CAR expression or associated with increased cytotoxicity, particularly the CC885-based system, which showed potent toxicity in both Jurkat (IC50: 1.446 nM, 95% CI, 0.9757–2.142) and primary T cells (IC50: 0.5568 nM, 95% CI, 0.3357–0.9234) (Figures S1B and S1C), whereas the CAR-BD2m with AGB1 system demonstrated efficient degradation with minimal toxicity, supporting its selection for further study.

In this study, we compared the cytotoxicity of ARV771 and AGB1 and evaluated their effectiveness in degrading both CAR-BD2w and CAR-BD2m. AGB1 exhibited considerably lower cytotoxicity than ARV771 in PBMCs and T cells. Importantly, AGB1 was 10-fold more effective in degrading CAR-BD2m proteins compared to ARV771’s degradation of CAR-BD2w proteins. Furthermore, AGB1 exhibited comparable potency across biochemical (alphascreen) and cellular assays, including CAR-BD2m Jurkat and KHYG-1 cells, indicating that its activity is preserved in cellular contexts. AGB1 also reduced the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, CAR-BD2m T cells regained their cytotoxicity against target cells upon the removal of AGB1, highlighting the reversible nature of this regulatory system. Encouraged by these promising in vitro findings, we proceeded to evaluate the anticancer activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in vivo. Initially, we anticipated that the antitumor efficacy of CAR-BD2m T cells might be compromised due to potential interference from the degron domain in CAR function. However, in vivo data demonstrated that the antitumor activity of CAR-BD2m T cells was comparable to that of the conventional CAR T cells. Additionally, we confirmed that AGB1 effectively regulated the antitumor activity of CAR-BD2m T cells in a subcutaneous xenograft model. Although no apparent toxicity of AGB1 was observed under the specific dose conditions employed for effective CAR-BD2m T cell control in our in vitro studies with human cells or in our in vivo mouse studies, the compound has not yet been validated for clinical safety, and therefore its direct use in clinical trials remains limited. Our findings position the CAR-BD2m with AGB1 system as a complementary approach to existing off-switch strategies, offering a balance between controllability, reversibility, and minimized off-target effects.

In conclusion, CAR-BD2m T cells exhibited robust anticancer activity against CD19-positive cells, and AGB1 successfully regulated the activity of CAR-BD2m T cells both in vitro and in vivo. Together, these findings suggest that the BD2m degron serves as an efficient tagging system for targeted CAR protein degradation, enabling precise and reversible control of CAR T cell function.

Materials and methods

Alphascreen assay

Bromodomains were prepared as described previously.30 Amplified luminescent proximity homogeneous assay (Alpha) screening was performed on a 384-well assay plate (white polystyrene, Corning, #3572). The assay buffer was composed of 0.05% CHAPS (Sigma-Aldrich, #C9426)-added buffer, 50 mM HEPES (Biosesang, #HR2003-050-70), 100 mM NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich, #S9888), and 0.1% BSA (GenDEPOT, #A0100-010; pH 7.4). Compounds were diluted in a 384-well V-shaped plate (Greiner Bio-One, #781280), and 2.5 μL of the diluted solution was added to the assay plates. Next, 5 μL of the 10 μM peptide solution [SGRGK(Ac)GGK(Ac)GLGK(Ac) GGAK(Ac)RHRK-Biotin] (synthesized by Peptron) was added to the plates. Subsequently, 5 μL of a 1 mM solution containing glutathione S-transferase (GST) and His-tagged bromodomains were combined with 2.5 μL of 12.5 μg/mL Streptavidin-coated donor beads (PerkinElmer, #6760002) and 2.5 μL of 12.5 μg/mL anti-GST alpha-screened acceptor beads (PerkinElmer, #AL109C). The mixture was added to the assay plates under low light conditions and incubated at 25°C for 60 min using a Thermomixer C (Eppendorf). Luminescence signals were detected using an EnSpire alpha reader (PerkinElmer).

Luminescent cell viability assay

PBMCs were isolated from whole blood obtained from the Korean Red Cross Blood Services. The human whole blood was collected from healthy donors who had provided written informed consent in accordance with protocols approved by the Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy institutional review board (approval no. P01-201607-31-003). All experimental procedures involving PBMCs were approved by the institutional review board, and all methods using blood samples were performed in compliance with institutional biosafety guidelines. The CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay was used to determine the number of viable cells by quantifying ATP presence, which indicates metabolically active cells. A total of 1 × 105 PBMCs and T cells, which were isolated from PBMCs, were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with 0.00064–2 μM PROTAC for 72 h. Following incubation, an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo solution (Promega, #G7572) as added to each well. The plates were shaken for 10 min at 25°C, and the luminescence signals were measured using an EnSpire alpha reader.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity assay was assessed using the Bright-Glo luciferase assay system (Promega, #E2650). The Nalm6-Luc cell line was generated by infecting CD19-positive Nalm6 cells with luciferase-expressing lentivirus, followed by selection with 1 μg/mL puromycin. Effector CAR KHYG-1 or CAR T cells were co-cultured with Nalm6-Luc cells at the indicated E:T ratios for 4 or 24 h. Following co-incubation, an equal volume of Bright-Glo solution was added to the cell mixture in 96-well plates. The plates were shaken for 5 min at 25°C, and the mixture was transferred to white flat-bottomed 96-well plates (Costar, #3917). Luminescence signals were detected using an EnSpire alpha reader.

Construction of CAR variants

CAR constructs were generated to evaluate the degradation of BD2-tagged CAR proteins. The following CAR variants were generated with or without the BD2 domain: FMC63-CD28-CD3ζ, FMC63-CD28-BD2w-CD3ζ, and FMC63-CD28-BD2m(L387V)-CD3ζ. In the BD2-containing constructs, the BD2 domain (wild-type or L387V mutant) was inserted between the CD28 costimulatory domain and the CD3ζ signaling domain. All constructs were cloned into expression vectors using standard molecular cloning methods.

IL-2 measurement

Jurkat cells were transduced with CAR-BD2m lentivirus and selected with 4 μg/mL puromycin. CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells and control Jurkat cells were incubated with 0.001–10 μM of AGB1 for 24 h. Following this, 9 × 104 Jurkat cells and CAR-BD2m Jurkat cells were co-cultured with 3 × 104 Nalm6-Luc cells at an E:T ratio of 3:1 for 24 h. Cell supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, and the secreted IL-2 concentration was determined using an ELISA kit (BioLegend, #431804).

Western blot analysis

Cells were centrifuged at 400 rpm for 3 min, and the cell pellets were collected. The cell culture medium was removed and replaced with 1× sample buffer (10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 50 mM Tris [pH 6.8], 3% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.02% bromophenol blue). The cell lysates were boiled at 100°C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis on a 4%–15% precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, #456-1086) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad, #1620177). Membranes were blocked with 1× TBST buffer containing 2% Tween 20 (400 mM Tris, 3 M sodium chloride, 2% Tween 20) and 5% skim milk (LPS solution, #SKI500) for 1.5 h, followed by three washes with 1× TBST buffer. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. CD3ζ (BD Biosciences, #51-6527GR) and BRD4 (Cell Signaling Technology, #13440) antibodies were used to detect CAR and BRD4 proteins, respectively. After primary antibody incubation, membranes were stained with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein detection was performed using ECL reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #32209). Blots were visualized and analyzed using a Sensi-Q2000 ChemiDoc (LugenSci, #LGW-2000) and Sensi-Q2000 software.

Cell culture

Nalm6 and KHYG-1 cells were purchased from DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ), while Jurkat and 293T cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). 293T cells were maintained in DMEM (Hyclone, #SH30243.01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #16000-044). Nalm6 and Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Hyclone, #SH30027.01) containing 10% FBS. KHYG-1 and T cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 200 IU/mL recombinant human IL-2 (R&D Systems, #202-IL).

Lentivirus production and CAR Jurkat and CAR KHYG-1 cell manufacturing

Packaging, envelopes, and transfer vectors were prepared for lentivirus production. The packaging vector psPAX2 (Addgene, #12260), the envelope vector pMD2.G (Addgene, #12259), and the pLVX vector carrying the CAR construct as the transfer vector were used. The transfer vector was co-transfected into 293T cells along with the packaging and envelope vectors. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM. The culture media were harvested at 48 and 72 h and filtrated using a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane filter (Millex-HP, #SLHP033RB). Jurkat or KHYG-1 cells were infected with CAR lentivirus via spinoculation (1,000 × g, 90 min), and CAR Jurkat and CAR KHYG-1 cells were selected using puromycin.

Retrovirus production

Packaging, envelopes, and transfer vectors were prepared for retrovirus production. The transfer vector (MSCV retroviral vector) was co-transfected into 293T cells along with the packaging vector (pEQ-PAM3) and the envelope vector (pRDF), which were provided from Dr. Dario Campana (National University of Singapore). After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM. The culture media were harvested at 48 and 72 h and then filtrated using a 0.45 μm PES membrane filter.

Establishment of CAR T cells

Whole blood was diluted with an equal volume of PBS solution containing 2% FBS. Fifteen milliliters of lymphoprep solution (STEMCELL, #07851) was added to a 50 mL conical tube, and 30 mL of diluted whole blood was carefully layered on top. The tubes were centrifuged at 800 × g for 20 min without breaking, and the yellow middle mononuclear cell layer was carefully transferred to a new conical tube. The cells were washed twice by centrifugation at 300 × g for 8 min with PBS solution supplemented 2% FBS. The purified PBMCs were counted and resuspended in a freezing medium containing 90% FBS and 10% DMSO. The isolated PBMCs were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen tanks (1–3 × 107 cells per vial). For further experimentation, the cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, CD3/CD28 dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11132D), and 200 IU/mL human IL-2 to activate T cells. After 1 week of culture, the activated T cells were infected with the CAR retrovirus using spinoculation (1,500–1,800 × g, for 90 min) in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene. After centrifugation, the viral supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in fresh completed RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 200 IU/mL human IL-2.

Flow cytometry

NT or CAR T cells (1 × 106 cells) were resuspended in 100 μL of cold staining buffer (PBS containing 0.2% BSA and 0.08% sodium azide) and stained with 5 μL of Fc-tagged human CD19 protein (ACROBiosystems, # CD9-H5251) for 20 min on ice. After washing with cold staining buffer, the cells were incubated with 5 μL of PE-conjugated anti-human IgG Fc antibody (BioLegend, #410707) and 5 μL of PerCP-conjugated anti-human CD3 antibody (BioLegend, #300427) in 100 μL of cell suspension for 20 min on ice. The cells were then washed with cold staining buffer and fixed for 10 min at 4°C using 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Biosesang, #PC2031-050-00). After a final wash, the cells were resuspended in 5 mL of polystyrene tubes with cell-strainer caps (Falcon, #352235). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar).

Mouse xenograft model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology. Female NSG (NOD.Cg-PrkdcˆscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. For in vivo functional studies, mice were injected via tail vein with 1 × 106 Nalm6-Luc cells. One day later, 7.5 × 106 NT, CAR, or CAR-BD2m T cells were administered intravenously. In a subcutaneous xenograft model, NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 106 Nalm6-Luc cells, followed by intravenous administration of 1 × 107 NT and CAR T cells 1 day later. For AGB1 studies, NSG mice were subcutaneously engrafted with 2 × 106 Nalm6-Luc cells, followed by intravenous administration of 1 × 107 NT or CAR-BD2m T cells 1 day later. AGB1 (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 5 days per week for 3 weeks. The same experimental procedure was performed using Ramos-Luc cells. All experimental procedures were conducted in a blinded manner.

Luminescence assay for Luc-BD2m T cells

Luminescence was measured using the Bright-Glo luciferase assay system. Luc-BD2m T cells were generated by transducing T cells with a BD2m-tagged luciferase construct. The cells were treated with 1 μM AGB1 for the indicated time periods. Following incubation, an equal volume of Bright-Glo reagent was added to the cells in 96-well plates. The plates were gently shaken for 5 min at 25°C, and the lysates were transferred to white flat-bottomed 96-well plates. Luminescence signals were measured using an EnSpire alpha plate reader.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

NSG mice were intravenously injected with Luc-BD2m T cells. For in vivo imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg) (PerkinElmer, #122799). Luminescence signals were acquired using an in vivo imaging system (CELLGENTEK) at the indicated time points.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was assessed using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test (mean ± SEM). Survival analysis was performed using the log rank Mantel-Cox test. Tumor growth profiles were compared using two-way ANOVA (mean ± SEM). For dose-response analysis, IC50 values were determined by fitting the data to a three-parameter nonlinear regression model [log(inhibitor) vs. response]. To account for high maximal biological responses, the top plateau was constrained to the mean value of the untreated control. IC50 results are presented with 95% CIs. A p value <0.05 was considered significant (ns, non-significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). Absence of a p value in the figure indicates that the comparison did not reach statistical significance. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 6 (GraphPad Software).
