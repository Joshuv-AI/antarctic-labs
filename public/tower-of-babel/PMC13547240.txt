Towards trustworthy brain stroke diagnosis using a lightweight explainable deep learning framework for CT imaging
Authors: Md. Romzan Alom, Muhammad Aminur Rahaman, Md. Parvez Hossain, Chayan Mondal, Md. Nazmus Shakib, Md. Ahsan Habib, Md. Kamrul Hasan, A. B. M. Shawkat Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain stroke occurs due to blockage or rupture in the cerebral blood supply and represents a critical medical emergency requiring rapid and accurate diagnosis. However, manual interpretation of CT scans is time-consuming and may delay clinical decision-making. To address this challenge, this study proposes the Deep Neural Brain Stroke Detection (DNBSD) system, a lightweight deep learning–based framework for automated stroke detection from CT images. The proposed model employs a task-specific convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture consisting of Conv2D, MaxPooling, Batch Normalization, Flatten and Dense layers, containing only 1.67 million trainable parameters and a computational complexity of 0.2973 GFLOPs, making it suitable for resource-constrained clinical environments. Additionally, preprocessing techniques including image resizing and normalization were applied to optimize performance. The model is trained and evaluated on two publicly available datasets: Brain Stroke CT Image Dataset (BSCI) and Brain Stroke Prediction CT Scan Image Dataset (BSPCSI), each divided into training, validation, and testing subsets. Experimental results demonstrate that the DNBSD system achieves high performance, with an accuracy of 99.20% and an AUC of 99.96% on the BSCI dataset, and an accuracy of 99.31% with an AUC of 99.97% on the BSPCSI dataset, while showing improved performance compared with several baseline approaches and state-of-the-art deep learning models. To enhance interpretability and support clinical decision-making, explainable artificial intelligence techniques, including LIME and Grad-CAM, are integrated to highlight critical regions influencing predictions. Additionally, a web-based diagnostic tool is developed to enable real-time stroke prediction. The findings suggest that the proposed approach can serve as an effective and interpretable tool for automated stroke detection, with potential to enhance clinical diagnostic workflows.

Introduction

Stroke is a medical emergency that happens when blood flow to the brain is interrupted, resulting in possible brain damage and loss of function1,2. Brain stroke (BS) is a major global health concern, as evidenced by frightening statistics3,4. Every year, almost 15 million people worldwide suffer from strokes5. In 2021, stroke impacted a large number of people globally, with 93.8 million individuals living with its effects and 11.9 million new cases diagnosed that year6. A 2022 survey found an occurrence rate of 11.39 per 1,000 people, highlighting the importance of effective detection and intervention techniques7. Traditionally, the diagnosis of brain strokes has relied on manual evaluation of CT images by medical professionals8. This process, while essential, is inherently time-consuming and labor-intensive, often resulting in delays that can jeopardize patient outcomes9. As the need for rapid and accurate diagnosis becomes increasingly critical, researchers have turned to machine learning (ML) techniques to automate brain stroke detection, aiming to streamline the diagnostic process10,11.

However, traditional machine learning techniques have potential drawbacks like reliance on manual feature extraction and their inability to generalize across different datasets12,13. As a result, deep learning (DL) has become a more effective approach, using neural networks to find complex patterns in data14,15. Even though deep learning (DL) has had many successes there are some inherent limitations including a reliance on pretrained models, inconsistent performance in different situations16,17, and the inherent ’black box’ nature of the algorithms18, which makes it hard to understand how they make decisions. This lack of transparency significantly limits trust and understanding in clinical settings19,20. While explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods, such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)21 and Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GradCAM)22, offer potential solutions to enhance model transparency, integrating these techniques into existing models remains a complex challenge23,24. However, existing stroke detection models often rely on computationally intensive architectures and lack interpretable decision mechanisms, limiting their practical deployment in real-time clinical environments25. To overcome these challenges, we propose the Deep Neural Brain Stroke Detection (DNBSD) system that combines an efficient lightweight deep learning framework with XAI techniques to enhance accuracy and interpretability in the detection of brain stroke. We developed a task-specific convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture to accurately classify stroke and normal brain tissue cases using two datasets named the Brain Stroke CT Image (BSCI)26 dataset and the Brain Stroke Prediction CT Scan Image (BSPCSI)27 dataset, which provide computed tomography (CT) scan images. In addition, we designed and implemented a web-based diagnostic tool that integrates the proposed DNBSD system, enabling real-time CT scan analysis and intuitive visualization of prediction outcomes along with confidence scores, thereby facilitating practical deployment and usability in clinical environments. Through this innovative approach, the system aims to enhance diagnostic efficiency and provide valuable insights into the model’s decision-making process. Literature review

The detection of brain strokes has been extensively studied by many researchers using a variety of techniques, with a special emphasis on deep learning and machine learning algorithms28. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and other deep learning techniques, such as MobileNet29, VGG1630, ResNet-5031, EfficientNetB032, and InceptionV333 have demonstrated high accuracy in detecting brain strokes. Furthermore, conventional machine learning algorithms, such as Random Forest34, have proven to be successful in identifying strokes.

Deep learning approaches on the BSCI dataset

In this work, Bajaj et al.35 developed an ensemble CNN approach to improve stroke diagnosis supported by brain CT images. On 2,501 images of the Kaggle Brain Stroke CT images dataset, it attained 92.43% accuracy with AUC of 0.92. Although it is superior to OzNet and LR, it might not generalize well on small and diverse datasets. A hybrid machine learning framework was proposed by UmaMaheswaran et al.36 to improve non-contrast CT images for early acute stroke detection. Their XGBoost model obtained 97% accuracy and 0.015 false positive rate using a dataset of 2,501 brain CT scans. Potential overfitting and time-consuming parameter tuning are drawbacks despite the impressive results.

Attention-based deep learning models have also been explored to improve classification performance. Tahyudin et al.37 proposed a CBAM-ResNet18 model that integrates a Convolutional Block Attention Module to emphasize salient features in CT images. Using cross-validation, the model achieved 95% accuracy and an AUC of 0.99, outperforming the standard ResNet18 architecture. Nevertheless, the study highlighted the need for validation on more diverse clinical datasets. Similarly, Zhang et al.38 combined ResNet with CBAM to classify CT images, achieving an accuracy of 89.8% and an AUC of 0.957. Although the model outperformed traditional radiomic methods, the study was limited by its retrospective design, lack of comparison with radiologist diagnoses, and dependence on large, accurately labeled datasets.

Several studies have evaluated standalone CNN architectures on the BSCI dataset. Abdi et al.39 developed a CNN-based stroke detection model that achieved a validation accuracy of 97.2% using 2,501 CT images. However, when tested on an external dataset, the accuracy dropped to 89.73%, highlighting generalization issues. Irfan et al.40 assessed multiple CNN models and reported that MobileNetV2 achieved the best performance with an accuracy of 95.80%. Despite strong results, reliance on limited training data may reduce effectiveness in broader clinical settings. Diker et al.41 compared several pretrained CNNs, including GoogleNet, AlexNet, and VGG variants, and found that VGG-19 achieved the highest accuracy of 97.06%. However, the relatively small dataset size remains a limiting factor.

Beyond deep learning, traditional feature-based methods have also been investigated. Gudadhe et al.42 proposed a texture-based classification approach using Local Binary Pattern (LBP), Local Ternary Pattern (LTP), and Weber Local Descriptors (WLPs) combined with a Random Forest classifier. Their best-performing configuration achieved an accuracy of 86.55%. While effective, the reliance on handcrafted features may limit adaptability to complex and heterogeneous clinical data. Tehseen et al.43 proposed a federated learning-based stroke prediction framework that integrates Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, and KNN classifiers across decentralized data sources. Using 2,501 CT images and selected clinical features, their weighted voting approach achieved 97% accuracy. However, the framework is primarily designed for tabular and feature-based data, limiting its applicability to image-only diagnostic systems.

Deep learning approaches on the BSPCSI dataset

Several studies have utilized the Brain Stroke Prediction CT Scan Images (BSPCSI) dataset to further evaluate stroke detection methods. Ferdous et al.44 proposed an ensemble-based deep learning model named ENSNET, which combines InceptionV3, MobileNetV2, and Xception using transfer learning. Evaluated on a Kaggle dataset containing 2,515 CT images, ENSNET achieved an accuracy of 98.86%, outperforming individual models. Despite its high accuracy, the model requires substantial computational resources and lacks validation using real clinical data.

Thabit et al.45 introduced a hybrid stroke prediction approach that combines CT images with tabular medical records using SVM, DNN, and CNN (VGG-16). Their DNN model achieved 96.49% accuracy using 2,515 CT images and 5,110 medical records. However, dataset-specific tuning raises concerns about generalizability. EfficientNet-based models have also shown strong performance. Patel et al.46 achieved 97% accuracy using EfficientNetB0, while Prasher et al.47 reported 98.72% accuracy on the same dataset. Although effective, both studies acknowledged limitations related to dataset size and lack of multi-modal imaging integration. Bharti et al.48 further improved results by using cropped CT images to reduce noise, achieving 98.7% accuracy with EfficientNetB0, though reliance on a relatively small dataset may limit clinical generalization.

Alternative architectures have also been explored on the BSPCSI dataset. Tripura et al.49 proposed a custom BrainNet model that achieved 91.91% accuracy, while Tursynova et al.50 developed a CNN-based multi-class classification system capable of distinguishing normal, ischemic, and hemorrhagic strokes. Their model achieved 81% accuracy and was deployed through an interactive web application. However, limited dataset size and moderate recall indicate the need for further improvement.

Recent advances in transformer and large-model-based medical image analysis

Recently, transformer-based architectures and large-scale pre-trained models have gained attention in medical image analysis due to their ability to capture global contextual information. Transfer learning with vision transformers has been increasingly applied to brain-related disease diagnosis, improving performance on limited datasets51,52. Variants such as the Swin Transformer further enhance hierarchical feature learning by combining local and global representations53. Additionally, recent approaches integrate visual and textual information through report-guided alignment, reflecting a growing trend toward large language model (LLM)-inspired multimodal frameworks in medical imaging54. Moreover, large foundation models like the Segment Anything Model (SAM) have been adopted to improve feature extraction and region localization in brain imaging tasks51. However, these methods often require high computational resources, which may limit their applicability in real-time clinical environments.

Dataset

In this research, two public datasets were used. These datasets include the Brain Stroke CT Image dataset26 and the Brain Stroke Prediction CT Scan Image dataset27. Both datasets have been widely used by researchers to advance brain stroke detection and analysis. They are specifically designed for medical imaging research, particularly for the identification of brain stroke from CT scans. The BSCI dataset26 consists of 2,501 CT scan images in.jpg format, including 1,551 normal cases and 950 stroke cases. Similarly, the BSPCSI dataset27 has 2,515 CT scan images in.jpg format, of which 1,551 are normal and 950 are stroke images. The images in both datasets are classified as either normal or stroke. It is important to note that both datasets do not provide subject-level (patient-level) identifiers, and each image is treated as an independent sample. Preprocessing techniques

In this study, the system used the BSCI26 and BSPCSI27 datasets, which presented several challenges such as varying image sizes, differences in pixel quality, and limited diversity in imaging conditions. These issues, highlighted in Fig.2, could potentially affect the performance and reliability of the model. To address these challenges, the system applied a series of preprocessing steps to prepare the data for effective training and evaluation. The first step involved resizing all input images to a fixed dimension of 256 × 256 pixels with three color channels (RGB). This resizing is crucial to ensure that every image meets the input requirements of the DNBSD system, which helps maintain consistency across the dataset and improves computational efficiency. After resizing, the system normalized the pixel values by scaling them from their original range of [0, 255] down to [0, 1]. Normalization helps stabilize the training process by preventing excessively large gradient updates and enables the model to learn more effectively. Lastly, to ensure robust model evaluation and prevent overfitting, the datasets were carefully split into three subsets: training, testing, and validation. Each subset maintained the balance between normal and stroke cases to provide an unbiased assessment of the model’s performance. Together, these preprocessing steps ensure that the input data is consistent, balanced, and well-prepared for training the DNBSD system, ultimately contributing to more reliable and accurate brain stroke detection. Proposed model creation and training

The goal of creating the suggested model was to create a deep neural network that could accurately identify brain strokes. The data were carefully separated into training and validation sets to ensure reliable training and objective assessment. The system was able to learn significant patterns while being continuously evaluated on unseen data due to this fundamental step. Early on, the study investigated a number of traditional architectures that have proven effective in classification and medical imaging tasks. Among these were MobileNet29, known for its lightweight design, VGG1630, with its deep yet simple structure, ResNet-5031, offering residual connections to combat vanishing gradients, EfficientNetB032, praised for its balance of performance and efficiency, and InceptionV333, known for its inception modules. In addition, the Random Forest algorithm34 was also considered for its effectiveness in traditional machine learning scenarios. As the design of the custom model took shape, the focus shifted to building it layer by layer. Fundamental CNN components such as Conv2D for feature extraction, MaxPooling2D for downsampling, and BatchNormalization for training stability were integrated. To translate the learned features into meaningful predictions, fully connected (Dense) layers were added. Activation functions like ReLU brought non-linearity and efficiency to the learning process, while Sigmoid played a crucial role in the final classification layer.

Model evaluation and selection for detecting brain stroke

After the completion of training, the performance of each model is further checked on another distinct test data set, which is neither used for the development nor for the training of the models. This distinct data set is required for carrying out an unbiassed estimation on how well each model is capable of identifying strokes occurring in the human brain for distinct data samples. For carrying out this distinct estimation, several different criteria are used, which are accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, specificity, as well as the Area Under the Curve (AUC). All these parameters combined offer a deep insight into the predictive ability, consistency, and robustness of the model. Based on the overall assessment of the parameters, the best-performing model showing overall reliable results will be selected. The selected model will further assist in the tasks of brain stroke detection. The model will provide reliable results for clinical purposes.

Post-classification output explanation

To improve the interpretability of the proposed model, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques are used to visualize and interpret the decision-making process of the model. Specifically, LIME and Grad-CAM are utilized to highlight the most influential regions in CT images, providing insights into how the model identifies stroke-related patterns and supporting a better understanding of its predictions.

Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME)

The DNBSD system also uses Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) methodology for increasing the interpretability of its predictions on CT images by pointing out which parts of the images have a significant impact on the decision-making process55. LIME methods use a strategy that involves generating perturbed copies of the input image and studying the resulting modifications of predictions made by the complex model. A simple and understandable model is used for training based on these predictions56. With this approach, the relative importance of image regions is measured, and a visual mask can be produced to emphasize important regions to the clinician. The visual mask is layered on top of the original CT scan image, and the clinicians are able to understand clearly from this image why a particular outcome was arrived at. The system achieves this by clearly presenting the reasoning behind its decisions visually, hence enhances confidence in its results22.

Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)

To gain insight into which spatial regions most influence the DNBSD system’s classification decisions, the system employs Grad-CAM22 that generates a heatmap highlighting the regions within an image that most significantly impact the model’s output57. The process begins by preprocessing the input image and passing it through the model to obtain class probabilities. Gradients are then computed with respect to the feature maps of the last convolutional layer to quantify the contribution of each spatial location. These contributions are combined to generate a heatmap, which is normalized, resized, and overlaid on the original image, with the most influential point highlighted. This provides a clear, class-specific visual explanation, allowing users to understand which areas of the image were most critical for the model’s decision11.

Additionally, the performance analysis of the proposed DNBSD system revealed impressive results, demonstrating high accuracy and consistently outperforming baseline architectures in classifying brain strokes from CT scan images. A key feature is its integration of LIME and Grad-Cam visualizations, which provide transparency by highlighting critical regions in the images that influence predictions. LIME works by perturbing the input image and observing how these changes affect the model’s predictions. It generates a local approximation of the model’s decision boundary by training a simple interpretable model on the perturbed data. Again, Grad-CAM works by using the gradients of a target concept flowing into the final convolutional layer of a CNN to produce a localization map highlighting important regions in the image.It determines which regions of the image most strongly influence the model’s prediction by computing gradient-based importance scores over the feature maps. This enables the identification of the specific features, or in this context, image regions, that contribute most to the model’s classification decision. This interpretability fosters trust among healthcare professionals. Conclusion

In this study, we proposed the DNBSD system, an explainable AI framework for classifying brain strokes from CT scan images. The system demonstrates strong performance across two benchmark datasets, effectively distinguishing between stroke and normal cases. A web-based diagnostic tool was also developed to enable real-time prediction and visualization, enhancing practical usability. A key strength of the proposed approach is its lightweight architecture, which achieves high accuracy with low computational complexity, making it suitable for resource-constrained environments. The integration of LIME and Grad-CAM further improves transparency by highlighting regions that influence model decisions, supporting interpretability. A qualitative evaluation with a clinical radiologist shows general consistency between model predictions and expert assessments, although discrepancies occur in challenging cases, particularly for subtle or early-stage stroke patterns. This highlights both the potential and limitations of automated approaches in clinical settings. Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. Although DNBSD is more efficient than most deep learning models, it is still computationally heavier than traditional methods such as Random Forest. The datasets lack subject-level identifiers, preventing strict patient-wise data separation and potentially introducing bias. Additionally, the study relies solely on CT images without incorporating multimodal data or clinical history. The absence of pixel-level annotations also limits precise localization of stroke regions and reduces interpretability at a finer level. Future work will focus on using clinically annotated, multi-center datasets with patient-level information, incorporating multimodal data such as MRI and clinical records, and integrating segmentation-based methods to improve localization and explanation quality. These improvements are expected to enhance the robustness, clinical relevance, and real-world applicability of the proposed system.
