Effects of PeptiSleep™ on sleep modification and biometrics in adults with sleep impairment: A randomised, placebo-controlled pilot study
Authors: Niamh Máire Mohan, Brian Keogh, Alessandro Adelfio, Ricardo Maldonado, Amanda Rao, Marco Altini, Nora Khaldi, Andrew Franklyn-Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep is vital for longevity and daily function but is under threat due to modern culture. While pharmaceuticals induce and prolong sleep, dependency and side effects can be significant. Artificial intelligence and peptide science have accelerated the accurate identification of natural, bioavailable compounds in a way previously limited to pharmaceutical discovery. This randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel pilot study investigated the effects of a rice protein hydrolysate, PeptiSleep™, on sleep quality in 26 adults (18-65 years old) with sleep impairment over 56 days. This small, single-blinded study was designed as exploratory; findings should be interpreted accordingly, with no adjustments made for multiple comparisons on secondary endpoints. PeptiSleep™ group scores were higher than the placebo in all sub-categories of the Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire, with nominally significant changes in Getting To Sleep (p = 0.050) and Quality Of Sleep (p = 0.036) domains. The Profile of Mood States indicated a nominally significant difference (p = 0.050) in total mood disturbance alongside non-significant differences in sleep diaries and questionnaires related to stress, anxiety, sleepiness, and insomnia. Exploratory analysis of objective biometrics, tracked passively using Oura wearable technology suggested potential differences in heart rate variability, sleep architecture, and latency between groups. PeptiSleep™ is the first in silico predicted ingredient to be assessed for sleep quality in a placebo-controlled trial.Keywords: Sleep, Sleep quality, Peptides, LSEQ, Heart rate variability, Wearable monitoring

Introduction

Sleep is a naturally recurring rhythmical reversible behavioural state, characterised by relative immobility, perceptual disengagement, and subdued consciousness [1]. The control of this state and the modulation of sleep is known as the circadian rhythm, following a 24 h cycle maintained without external time cues [2]. The controlled release of cortisol occurs via the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis peaking in the morning, near habitual waketime in humans, and declining across the day, showing low levels in the early evening and increasing again during the night [3]. Rising melatonin levels suppress cortisol and induce the sleep phase of the circadian rhythm.

Assessing both the duration and quality of sleep has traditionally been assessed using polysomnography (PSG) in sleep clinics [4]. However, outside these conditions, wearable sensors have used a combination of heart rate variability (HRV), accelerometery and temperature sensors to estimate sleep stages non-invasively and comfortably in free-living conditions. Assessment using commercially available devices such as the Apple Watch, Garmin watches or the Oura ring, have shown 61% reliability with PSG in the assessment of multistate sleep categorisation and represent an accurate objective measures in a real-world environment [5]. Subjective measures such as sleep diaries, and patient reported outcome measures such as Pittsburgh Sleep Quality index (PSQI), and Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire (LSEQ) are also commonly used in research.

Sleep impairment is a common physiological and metabolic stressor. It is highly prevalent, affecting 1 in 3 people globally, many of whom struggle to initiate, maintain and get sufficient restorative sleep to feel refreshed [[6], [7], [8]]. Societal and behavioural factors such as work patterns, stressful lifestyles, increased screen time and technology use are increasingly recognised as key disruptors of healthy sleep [9]. Regular sleep deprivation can have critical impacts for health, with less than 7 h per night shown to increase the risk for coronary heart disease and diabetes [10] and self-reported sleep of 4 h per night associated with a cognitive decline equivalent to 8 years of aging across a battery of cognitive measures [11]. Overall, persistent sleep deficiency is associated with a 29% greater mortality risk, as shown in a recent community cohort study with over 46,000 people [12].

Those who experience sleep impairment often rely on over-the-counter products, hypnotics or anti-histamines many of which are ineffective, lead to undesirable side effects or dependency [13]. Synthetically produced versions of the hormone melatonin is one of the most commonly used sleep aids, however many users experience next day hangover feeling, grogginess and tolerance [14]. Moreover, recent studies contest its frequent use in older adults [15], children and teens, as the long term consequences remain unknown [16].

Interest in alternative sleep supplements and nutraceutical products is growing rapidly [17], however the lack of data from high quality placebo-controlled randomised trials represents a major obstacle to the discovery of clinically substantiated interventions for sleep support. Moreover, the discovery of novel non-pharmaceutical ingredients is costly, and often high risk which is why the majority of sleep nutraceuticals are newly branded combinations of historical ingredients such as valerian, magnesium and chamomile [18]. The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) in peptide science has accelerated the identification of bioactive, bioavailable compounds from nature with targeted mechanisms which may benefit sleep.

PeptiSleep™ is a hydrolysed ingredient from Oryza sativa containing an AI-discovered network of bioavailable peptides, isolated during enzymatic hydrolysis of a brown rice protein isolate. In an unpublished study currently under review, the PeptiSleep™ peptide matrix was shown to modulate orexin-induced cortisol release from human adrenal cortico-carcinoma H295R cells by 23% in vitro [19]. The proposed mechanism outlined below is based on this in-vitro data and whether this mechanism translates in-vivo remains to be established as evening cortisol was not measured in the present study. The authors reported that PeptiSleep™ was safe and well tolerated in a clinical study including 60 participants as a super-set and doses up to 1000 mg/day from which the present study was a sub-set [19].

Here, we aimed to assess if PeptiSleep™ could improve sleep quality and reduce stress, via objective and subjective measures in a randomised placebo-controlled trial in individuals with mild to moderate sleep impairment over 56 days. This was a preliminary, exploratory investigation. The sub-study primary outcome, sleep quality, was measured every 2 weeks with the Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire. Secondary outcome measures included questionnaires on mood, anxiety, insomnia and daytime sleepiness, daily diary, cortisol, serotonin, and various daily sleep biometrics via the Oura ring (Ōura Health, Oulu, Finland).

Proposed Mechanism of Action (from unpublished in-vitro data)

The orexin neuropeptides serve as master modulators of the HPA axis, stabilising the sleep-wake cycle through G-protein coupled receptors OXR1 and OXR2 [20]. Orexin and cortisol are linked through a bidirectional relationship where orexin directly stimulates cortisol production, and cortisol, in turn, influences orexin expression. In a study on orexin knockout in mice, a phenotype resembling narcolepsy developed, highlighting a crucial role in wakefulness [21,22]. Given its a critical role in the regulation of the circadian rhythm, supporting morning alertness and influencing the timing of sleep-wake transitions, the modulation of peripheral cortisol via orexin antagonism represents an interesting proposed mechanism beyond the brain active [18], or undefined mechanisms [[23], [24], [25]] commonly reported for sleep supplements. We hypothesise that peripheral orexin receptor antagonism in the adrenal cortex may inhibit local stress induced orexin-driven cortisol synthesis and modulation of glucocorticoid output, independent of central HPA axis suppression. However, this has not yet been demonstrated mechanistically in the present study due to sampling constraints and therefore further validation studies are warranted.

Methods

Subjects

The present study is a cohort of a larger unpublished study investigating the safety and tolerability of a dose range of PeptiSleep™ by McLoughlan et al. (2025) where the safety results, participant demographics and CONSORT flow diagram for the full study population are described [19]. In this cohort, investigating the efficacy of 250 mg PeptiSleep™ on sleep quality, 30 men and women (18-65 years) who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria (Table 1A) were recruited and randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the active or placebo groups, 15 per arm. After adjudicating for compliance with the study procedures and supplementation regime, a total of 26 participants (13 per arm) were investigated as the Per Protocol (PP) population (Fig. 1). Of the 30 participants enrolled (Intention To Treat - ITT population), 2 withdrew prior to outcome assessment, 1 was lost to follow-up, and 1 was non-compliant with study procedures. As 3 of the 4 excluded participants did not contribute any outcome data, a formal ITT analysis or last-observation-carried-forward sensitivity analysis was not considered meaningful, as there were no observations to carry forward. The remaining exclusion, one non-compliant participant, did contribute data; however, inclusion of this participant did not materially alter the direction or magnitude of findings, limiting the risk of attrition bias. The analysis therefore focused on the PP population, which reflects participants who completed the full intervention as intended. Study Design

This randomised, single-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study investigated the effects of PeptiSleep™ on sleep quality when supplemented before bed for 8-weeks in adults with mild to moderate sleep impairment. The single centre trial was conducted by RDC Clinical in Australia. After informed consent was obtained, participants completed a screening Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) questionnaire. Those scoring <14 were assigned to the PeptiSleep™ or placebo group using block randomisation and subsequently enrolled by the RDC clinical team. Participants completed baseline mood, sleep and stress questionnaires and began a 14-day run in period with the Oura device (Ōura Health, Oulu, Finland). During the day 0 clinic visit, participants completed baseline ISI questionnaires and a Reaction Time Test (RTT). Participants were also familiarised with cortisol swabbing technique, and a baseline blood sample was collected. Subsequently, on days 14, 28, 42 and 56, the LSEQ, Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Profile of Mood States (POMS), and Beck Anxiety Index (BAI) questionnaires were completed, and salivary swabs were collected in the home. Sleep diary data was logged during 3 days per week before a 2-week interval e.g. days 12, 13 and 14. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) was completed on days 28 and 56 only. At day 56, a final blood draw was collected and the ISI questionnaire and RTT were completed in clinic. The trial was first registered on the 02/02/2024 and has the registration number NCT06267586. This trial was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical approval was granted by the Bellberry Human Research Ethics Committee, 123 Glen Osmond Road Eastwood South Australia - 20,232-11-1367. The trial was conducted, and data were collected by RDC Clinical.

Supplementation Regimen

PeptiSleep™ is a brown rice (Oryza sativa) protein hydrolysate, containing a patented network of peptides. Participants supplemented with capsules of either 250 mg PeptiSleep™ or silicified microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) as a placebo 60 min before going to bed for 8-weeks. Both capsules were indistinguishable from each other. As this study was a sub-cohort of a larger multi-dose safety study, a single member of clinical team responsible for dispensing the intervention was unblinded as an operational requirement of the broader study design. This individual had no contact with participants and played no role in outcome assessment or data analysis. Accordingly, the risk of performance or assessment bias in subjective outcomes from this design feature is considered low but nevertheless acknowledged as a limitation. Participants remained fully blinded to their assigned intervention throughout, as did all personnel responsible for data analysis. During the 14-day run-in period, no supplements were consumed, however biometric baseline data was collected.

Outcomes

Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire (LSEQ)

Participants completed the LSEQ at baseline, at 2-week intervals over the 8-week study as a measure of self-reported sleep quality and the effects of impaired sleep on daily performance and mood. The questionnaire is comprised of 10 questions measured on a 100 mm visual analogue scale where higher scores are indicative of an improvement in sleep quality. Scoring categories report on the 4 key aspects of the sleep experience, from Getting To Sleep (GTS), Quality Of Sleep (QOS), Awakening Following Sleep (AFS) and Behaviour Following Wakening (BFW) [26].

Insomnia Severity Screening Index (ISI)

Participants completed the ISI at pre-screening, baseline, and day 56. The ISI is a self-reported questionnaire consisting of 7 items to assess the severity of both night-time and daytime components of insomnia [27]. A 5-point Likert scale was used to rate each item where 0 = no problem and 4 = very severe problem, yielding a total score from 0 to 28. Higher total scores indicate greater insomnia severity with scoring sub-categories divided as absence of insomnia (0–7); sub-threshold insomnia (8–14); moderate insomnia symptoms (15–21); and severe insomnia symptoms (22–28) [27]. The target population in the study included those with mild to moderate sleep impairment. As such, participants scoring >14 on the ISI were excluded from the study.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

Participants completed the PSS during screening, and then again at 2-week intervals over the course of the study. The PSS is a self-reported questionnaire [28] consisting of 10 items to assess the degree of perceived stress. Answers were rated on a 5-point Likert scale where 0 = no stress and 4 = high stress, yielding a total score from 0 to 40. Higher total scores indicated higher perceived stress with scores ranging from 0 to 13 as low stress, 14–26 as moderate stress and 27–40 as high stress conditions.

Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)

Participants completed the ESS during screening, day 28 and day 56. The ESS is a self-reported questionnaire [29] consisting of 8 items to assess the likelihood of dozing off or falling asleep while engaged in 8 different activities. Answers were rated on a 4-point scale. Higher scores indicated greater sleepiness where a score of 0–6 indicates normal sleepiness, 7-8 indicated general sleepiness, and 9–24 indicated excessive daytime sleepiness.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

Participants completed the BAI during screening, and then again at 2-week intervals as a measure of anxiety over the course of the study. The BAI is a self-reported questionnaire [30] consisting of 21 items to assess anxiety level. Answers were rated on a 4-point scale where 0 = Not at all and 3 = Severely, it bothered me a lot. Higher total scores indicated greater anxiety where 0-21 = low anxiety, 22-35 = moderate anxiety and 36 and above = potentially concerning levels of anxiety.

Profile of Mood States (POMS)

Participants completed POMS during screening, and then again at 2-week intervals over the course of the study. The POMS self-reported questionnaire consists of 40 items to assess 7 mood scales [31]. It was used to assess Total Mood Disturbance (TMD) in the study. A composite score was calculated by adding tension, depression, anger, fatigue, and confusion scores and then subtracting the vigour plus esteem score. Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘Not at all’ to ‘Extremely’. Lower TMD scores indicate an improved mood.

Reaction Time Test (RTT)

Participants performed a RTT at baseline and day 56. The RTT is a self-assessed electronic test to measure time to detect, recognise, and create a suitable response to a visual stimulus. An average of 5 attempts was calculated per participant.

Biometric and Heart Rate Variability Data via Oura Wearable

Biometric data related to sleep quality and quantity was obtained using the Gen 3 Heritage Oura wearable ring (Ōura Health, Oulu, Finland). Participants were asked to wear the device for the duration of the study as well as during a 14-day run-in period prior to supplementation to collect baseline sleep data pre-intervention. In addition, the run-in period determined inclusion or exclusion in the study as only those with an average sleep score of 89 or less were included in the study.

Using proprietary algorithms, combined with motion, temperature, heart rate (HR - BPM), and HRV (RMSSD-ms), the following biometrics were investigated in the study; sleep onset latency (min); total time in light, deep and REM sleep (min); time spent awake (min); sleep duration (min); sleep efficiency score (%); bedtime (min), wake time (min) and blood oxygen saturation (%). Of note, proprietary composite summary metrics on readiness score (%) and sleep score (%) were also assessed, although these have not yet been independently clinically validated. The device wear time was considered in study compliance whereby significant periods extending more than 24 consecutive hours of non-wear time, resulted in exclusion from the PP population. The device has high validity in the assessment of sleep outcomes in healthy adults [[32], [33], [34]].

Data was analysed and is represented as 6 timepoints as averaged data for the following time periods denoted as TP0 to TP5 and represent the following days during the study TP0 = Day −6 to 0 pre-intervention (baseline), TP1 = Day 8-14, TP2 = Day 22 -28, TP3 = Day 36-42, TP4 = Day 43-49 and TP5 = Day 50-56 in minutes (min). Importantly, the participants were blinded with no access to their Oura data portal on any device over the course of the trial to avoid potential bias.

Sleep Diaries

Sleep diaries were completed by participants in the 3 days prior to the 2-week intervals, i.e. Days 12, 13, 14 – 26, 27, 28 etc. Questions covered the following: sleep latency (min), time awake during the night (min), length of the wakening (min), sleep duration (min), sleep quality (0-5, where higher are better), how refreshed (0-5) and nap count (number of people who experienced at least 1 nap in the previous 3 days).

Salivary Cortisol Test

Cortisol was measured from saliva collected using buccal Salivette swabs (Sarstedt ltd, Mawson Lakes, South Australia) during the baseline clinic visit to familiarise participants with the swabbing procedure. At the same time in the morning before breakfast in their homes, participants collected salivary swabs during 2-week intervals over the course of the study. Swabs were removed from the tube, placed in the mouth for 2 min without chewing or sucking. Once saturated, the swab was frozen immediately.

Blood Draws

Blood was collected in vacutainers. Serum tubes were allowed to clot for 30 min before centrifugation (15 min at 1200 g at 4°C). Once spun, aliquots were transferred to cryovials and stored at −80°C.

Statistical Analysis

This exploratory pilot study was not powered to confirm efficacy. Population size was chosen based on similar studies investigating sleep aids in a similar population [13,[35], [36], [37]]. Primary data analysis was conducted blinded. Descriptive statistics are presented for biometric data obtained during the study to track sleep patterns, data summarising sleep markers (e.g. serotonin, cortisol), and self-assessed questionnaire data. The data was summarised for each group, and for differences between groups, using means, standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the mean. An alpha of 0.05 was used throughout the study. Hodges-Lehmann 95% CI were calculated for non-parametric tests. Cohen's d was calculated as a standardised mean difference using pooled standard deviations for all outcomes, regardless of the distributional test used, and is presented as a supplementary measure of effect magnitude; small (d < 0.2), medium (d > 0.21, d < 0.5), or large (d > 0.51) [38]. Oura ring measures were reported as computed averages during defined timepoints (TP0-5). A baseline difference approach was used, where baseline values were subtracted from values at specific time points. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. The differences obtained at each time point were compared using t-tests. All tests were two-tailed. In the case of questionnaire data, where categorical data is assumed to be continuous, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum) were used where appropriate. Data are represented graphically using either line graph plots or iceberg plots highlighting the SEM to compare group means, or boxplots highlighting the median, IQR, maximum and minimum values. All analyses were conducted using Stata (version 18). In this exploratory study, no correction for multiple-testing was applied to avoid missing potential signals, however this increases the risk of false-positive findings and should be considered in the context of the results. The results presented below refer to the PP data set.

Results

Safety and Tolerability

The safety and tolerability of PeptiSleep™ was comprehensively assessed and reported in the super-set population (n = 60) [19]. Briefly, vital signs, liver function, and metabolic and renal markers were assessed at baseline and day 57, with no clinically significant changes observed within or between groups. A statistically significant between-group difference in gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) at day 57 was noted; however, values remained within the normal range throughout and were deemed clinically irrelevant by the trial physician. A total of 22 subjects (36.7%) reported at least one adverse event, with no significant difference in incidence rate between groups. All adverse events were classified as non-serious.

Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire

Sleep quality was measured using LSEQ at 2-week intervals over the study. As shown in Fig. 2 and Table 2, the PeptiSleep™ group scored higher in all 4 domains of the LSEQ questionnaire with nominally significant differences and medium effect sizes compared to placebo in the GTS (p = 0.050; 95% CI: 0.10, 4.70, Cohen's d: 0.54) and QOS (p = 0.036; 95% CI: 0.10, 4.10 Cohen's d: 0.66) sections which correspond to sleep initiation and maintenance, respectively. While not statistically significant, exploratory trends and the largest score change from baseline was observed in the BFW sub-category which includes alertness, coordination and balance following wakening, with the PeptiSleep™ group showing an increase of 6 points by end of study. With a maximum of 30 points in this domain, the PeptiSleep™ group demonstrated a 20% difference versus 12.9% with the placebo, however this should be interpreted with caution given the lack of statistical significance and the exploratory nature of this analysis. In the GTS, QOS and AFS domains, between group differences of 7.1%, 11.7% and 7.3% were observed, respectively. Cumulatively, the total LSEQ score did not reach significance, but was increased by 14.0 points in the PeptiSleep™ supplemented group versus 7.3 points in the placebo group by day 56 (difference of 6.7%; 95% CI: −2.43, 16.63, Cohen's d: 0.60, p = 0.265).

BAI, PSS, ISI, ESS Questionnaires and Sleep Diaries

Additional questionnaires were used as self-reported outcomes to measure secondary endpoints including anxiety, stress, sleepiness and insomnia, see Table 3. At baseline, both groups scored in the “higher normal daytime sleepiness” range, 6-10. The ESS scores for both PeptiSleep™ and placebo groups exhibited reducing trends by day 28 and again at day 56 towards the “lower normal” range (<5), suggesting a directional improvement in daytime sleepiness across both groups. The ISI was used as a screening tool in the study to include participants with sleep impairment and exclude those with chronic insomnia i.e. scores >14. The placebo group showed a reduction of 3.6 points, remaining in the sub-threshold insomnia range, whilst the PeptiSleep™ group showed a slightly larger reduction of 4.5 points, transitioning into the “no insomnia” category. Although non-significant, this directional shift may be of exploratory clinical interest. Similarly, in the PSS questionnaire, the PeptiSleep™ group showed a directional trend from “moderate stress” to the “low stress” range over 56 days, which was not observed in the placebo. The BAI scores decreased in both groups over the study duration with a reduction of 0.52 and 1.62 points for the PeptiSleep™ and placebo groups, respectively. These reductions were modest and both groups remained within the “minimal anxiety” score range of 0-7.

Cortisol and Serotonin Measurement

Cortisol was measured using salivary swabs at the same time each morning on day 0, 14, 28, 42 and 56. From baseline to end of study, cortisol levels did not fluctuate significantly, with marginal decreases of 2.19 nmol/L in the placebo group and increases of 3.25 nmol/L in the PeptiSleep group (p = 0.174; 95% CI: −1.30, 0.17, Cohen's d: −0.57) (Fig. 4A). Over the supplementation period, mean levels were reported as 13.95 nmol/L and 14.98 nmol/L, in the placebo and PeptiSleepTM groups respectively. As the levels are within the normal range of 3.5 – 27 nmol/l, this suggests that the circadian rhythm of morning cortisol release was not disrupted by PeptiSleep™ and that the HPA axis is responding appropriately [39]. Of note, nocturnal sampling was not performed due to practical and logistical constraints inherent to free-living study design with sampling required up to midnight, risking disruption of sleep onset without a controlled sleep lab setting. The proposed orexin-mediated mechanistic pathway therefore remains unconfirmed in-vivo. Similarly, shown in Fig. 4B, serum serotonin levels did not differ between groups (p = 0.351; 95% CI: −0.44, 1.19, Cohen's d: 0.38).

Discussion

This exploratory, randomised placebo-controlled trial provides preliminary evidence that supplementation with PeptiSleep™ was associated with directional changes in subjective and objective sleep outcomes in adults with mild to moderate sleep impairment. Combining subjective questionnaires and wearable biometrics, this trial provides hypothesis-generating data that PeptiSleep™ may improve sleep health. However replication in larger, powered trials are warranted to confirm these observations.

The study population chosen reflects a real-world sample of adults with mild to moderate sleep impairment which supports ecological validity but limits the generalisability of findings to more severe clinical populations. This study has other important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The single-blind design, in which one unblinded team member was responsible for dispensing, represents a potential source of bias, though this individual had no contact with participants and no role in outcome assessment. The analysis was performed on the Per Protocol population (n = 26); formal Intention To Treat analysis was not performed as the 3 out of the 4 excluded participants contributed no outcome data. While formal multiple-comparison adjustments were considered, these were not applied due to the exploratory nature of the study and limited sample size, which increases the risk of Type I errors. Morning cortisol was within the normal range, suggesting no suppression of waking cortisol, however nocturnal sampling was not performed meaning the proposed orexin-mediated mechanistic pathway remains unconfirmed in-vivo. Evening cortisol measurement presented a practical challenge in free-living studies with sampling required up to midnight. Without indwelling catheterisation in a controlled sleep lab, sampling at this time would risk disrupting sleep onset. Nocturnal cortisol sampling therefore represents an important methodological consideration for future confirmatory trials. Finally, the Oura sleep score and readiness score are proprietary composite metrics not validated against standard clinical instruments and should be treated as exploratory indicators only.

The LSEQ is a validated psychometric tool to evaluate subjective sleep perceptions and intervention induced changes [40]. The PeptiSleep™ group experienced improvements in all 4 domains of the questionnaire, capturing shifts across the sleep-wake cycle, with nominally significant differences compared to placebo in GTS (p = 0.050) and QOS (p = 0.036). Exploratory wearable data showed directionally consistent signals in related metrics. The PeptiSleep™ group experienced improvements, reducing sleep initiation time across TP2, 3, and 4 by 5.2 min (p = 0.023), 1.51 min (p = 0.507) and 3.22 min (p = 0.155) compared to placebo which was recorded as 3.4 min, 2.8 min and 6.09 min from the sleep diaries. Reducing the within group change by up to 2.8 min, those supplementing with PeptiSleep™ trend towards an optimal sleep onset latency time of 11.7 min in healthy adults [41].

Non-significant differences were observed in the post-sleep domains of the LSEQ related to sleep inertia and next day functioning, with a 20.0%, 13.7% and 14.0% increase recorded in BFW, AFS and total LSEQ which were 7%, 7.3% and 6.7% greater than the placebo group. Fig. 2 illustrates a commonly observed placebo effect in sleep studies, where improvements in sleep hygiene behaviours may contribute to changes in both arms [35,42]. The observed effect sizes are broadly comparable to those reported for some other nutraceutical sleep ingredients including LZComplex3 [37,43], Magtein™ [32], Cyclamax [44] and valerian-hops [45]; however, direct comparisons are not appropriate given differences in study design, populations, and outcome measures. The PeptiSleep™ group increased 6-points from baseline in the BFW sub-category which assesses alertness and coordination following wakening. With grogginess and next day sleepiness reported as common side effects among melatonin users [46], the effects of PeptiSleep™ on next-day functioning compared to melatonin warrants direct investigation.

A nominally significant difference was observed in TMD (−7.3 vs + 1.03, p = 0.029) in the PeptiSleep™ and placebo groups respectively, which is consistent with evidence linking sleep quality and mood outcomes [47]. Non-statistically significant differences were also observed daytime sleepiness, insomnia severity measures and stress, reducing levels from moderate to mild.

HRV reflects autonomic nervous system modulation through beat-to-beat interval variability, and has been validated against gold-standard ECG for nightly average HRV and sleep staging [48,49]. High HRV is associated with greater parasympathetic activity, cardiovascular homeostasis and emotional regulation whilst lower HRV is indicative of increased sympathetic activity and periods of wakefulness during sleep [50,51]. An inverse correlation between HRV and HR was observed, whereby the PeptiSleep™ group showed a nominal increase in HRV of up to 4.72 ms and a decrease HR by 2.8 BPM compared with the placebo group in whom HRV decreased by 7.39 ms (p = 0.028) and HR increased by 2.0 BPM (p = 0.035). This pattern was most apparent between days 22–42 and may have been influenced by increased non-wear time in the latter stages of the study. Of note, the use of an emerging wearable device introduces inherent validation-related limitation. Stable high-quality R-R interval data is required for accurate sleep tracking via wearables as artefacts in R-R interval data can propagate through downstream sleep metrics. Paird with the increased non-wear time at the final timepoint, these may have influenced trends.

In the present study, a 4.72 ms increase, or 12 ms difference from placebo after 4-weeks in the PeptiSleep™ group, who were blinded to the Oura app interface was observed. The directional changes in HRV are broadly consistent with prior research linking improved sleep quality with increased parasympathetic activity with interventions such as botanical juice [52] and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [53], though cross-study comparisons are limited by differences in methodology and populations.

SpO2 levels in the PeptiSleep™ group were greater than baseline over the course of the study, peaking at TP3, with increases of 0.3% versus a mean reduction of 0.26% in the placebo group. While levels for all remained above the normal overnight mean oxygen saturation of 96% [54], the exploratory findings were directionally consistent with the HRV data.

Sleep architecture balance directly influences HRV and overall sleep quality. Earlier validation work on the Gen-2 Oura ring identified notable discrepancies in sleep stage classification for light (65%), deep (51%), and REM (61%) sleep compared to PSG [34], suggesting that distinguishing sleep stages with sleep trackers was a challenge in the past. The updated Gen-3 ring has shown meaningful improvement. In a study of 96 adults aged 20–70 comparing the Gen-3 ring against multi-night PSG reported no significant difference in time spent in light or deep sleep, with 88.9% accuracy in sleep stage classification, and noted that deep sleep estimation is most reliable when duration falls between 40 and 90 min [55], consistent with the group averages of 69.5–77.6 min observed here.

Both groups spent between 21 and 23% of sleep time in REM, within the healthy range [56], though the placebo group showed a decline from baseline while the PeptiSleep™ group increased, with maximal differences of 11.63 min. Deep sleep time increased from baseline in the PeptiSleep™ group at most timepoints, with large effect sizes, and nominally significant differences from placebo of 9.65 min (p = 0.045) and 12.7 min (p = 0.026) at TP2 and TP4. Light sleep remained relatively unchanged in the PeptiSleep™ group, whilst the placebo group exhibited a decline. This trend can be linked with other metrics including total sleep time, time in bed, bedtime, wake time, time awake, and sleep efficiency are presented in the supplementary material where trends were non-significant between groups. Directional improvements in sleep duration and efficiency were observed in the PeptiSleep™ group, which were broadly consistent with the subjective LSEQ findings. This correlated well with the sleep diaries where the PeptiSleep™ group reported more sleep from baseline to day 56 than the placebo with 38.94 min compared to 10.82 min, reduced nighttime awakenings from 3.07 to 1.7 vs. 2.42 to 1.88 times and greater increases in feeling refreshed by day 28 (10.6% vs. 4.4% for placebo).

Exploratory differences were observed in proprietary composite sleep (2.2% TP2, p = 0.030) and readiness scores (5.46% TP3, p = 0.027). Again, these composite proprietary metrics cannot be validated against standard clinical instruments and should be interpreted as indirect indicators reflecting aspects of restorative sleep, refreshed awakening, and daytime readiness rather than direct physiological measures. In a study investigating magnesium in sleep impaired adults, readiness via Oura wearable was assessed and improved, but by less than half that observed at TP2 in the current study [13]. Taken together, these preliminary findings support the continued investigation of PeptiSleep™ as a novel, natural, targeted approach to sleep support.

Conclusions

This exploratory pilot study provides preliminary evidence that PeptiSleep™, the first in-silico predicted plant peptide ingredient to be assessed in a placebo-controlled sleep trial, may support improvements in sleep onset, maintenance, and architecture in individuals with mild to moderate sleep impairment. Directional improvements in mood and stress were also observed, alongside exploratory signals across objective wearable biometrics. These findings are hypothesis-generating. Larger, adequately powered, fully blinded randomised controlled trials are warranted to confirm these initial observations.
