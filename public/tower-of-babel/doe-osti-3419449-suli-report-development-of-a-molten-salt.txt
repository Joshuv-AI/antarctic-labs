 ABSTRACT
 This project supports the development of real-time optical monitoring
capabilities for molten-salt reactor (MSR) environments by designing, testing,
and refining a molten-salt circulation loop suitable for combined laser-induced
breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) and ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption
measurements. Online spectroscopic monitoring is increasingly important for
nuclear safeguards, corrosion tracking, and material accountancy, yet MSR
process fluids present substantial challenges due to their chemical complexity
and hazards such as high temperatures and radiation. To address these needs, this
work focuses on Phase I, the development of a room-temperature aqueous
circulation loop that serves as a surrogate platform for evaluating flow behavior,
optical access, and component performance prior to high-temperature salt
operation. Initial testing identified several practical issues—including leaks,
obstructions, and two-phase flow through the absorption cell—that were
systematically resolved through hardware replacement, flow-path redesign, and
venturi pressure optimization. Relocating the flow cell upstream of the primary
venturi enabled periods of stable single-phase flow, demonstrating the feasibility
of integrating optical diagnostics into a circulation system. The results of Phase I
provide essential design insight for Phase II, which will incorporate
furnace-compatible materials and LiCl-KCl eutectic salt. Completion of the
molten-salt system will deliver a reusable testbed for evaluating multimodal
spectroscopic techniques, advancing non-destructive, real-time monitoring tools
for future MSR and nuclear-fuel-cycle applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Monitoring process fluids is relevant to molten salt reactor (MSR) applications as federal nuclear
safeguards regulations require close monitoring of critical materials in nuclear reactors. Furthermore, as
nuclear reprocessing becomes a stronger focus, so does nuclear material accountancy to couple federal
regulations and fuel reprocessing efficiency measurements. This task is further challenged in MSR
applications as the process fluid is hazardous due to its high temperature and strong radiation. The
dynamic composition of this radioactive molten salt mixture requires an additional quality which is real-
time monitoring to track the buildup of fission, corrosion, and reaction products. These monitoring targets
are of great importance as they indicate the level of risk associated with mechanical operation of the
reactor as well as environmental and safety hazards. There are two monitoring methods commonly used
(1) offline monitoring, which requires a sample to be removed from the system and analyzed externally,
and (2) online/inline monitoring, which allow the sample to be analyzed within the system without
external analysis. The difference between online and inline monitoring is that online requires the samples
to be sent through a bypass for analysis whereas inline monitoring allows for full integration into the
undisturbed path of the process fluid.

 Offline monitoring methods such as inductively-coupled plasma – mass spectrometry (ICP-MS),
while highly accurate and reliable, often long lead times as the sample is required to travel from the test
site to a separate lab to be analyzed. Therefore, this method does not allow for real-time monitoring of
special nuclear material in an MSR. In addition, sample removal in such a hazardous system is
undesirable for workers and environmental safety.

 Online and inline monitoring techniques are much more attractive given their capability of sending
real-time feedback as well as the added safety features by utilizing optical fiber cables to obtain relevant
information and bypass the need of removing a physical sample. Previous studies have explored
spectroscopy techniques in this application such as Raman, absorption, and laser-induced breakdown
spectroscopy (LIBS). Each spectroscopy technique has its own set of blind spots but can work together to
cover different areas of the desired analysis. For example, LIBS provides great insight into the elemental
information of a solution and can cover a broad range of the periodic table but misses important
information, like oxidation states, that would be captured with ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy.
On the other hand, Raman spectroscopy has great success identifying molecular compounds but struggles
with metallic species and oxidation states which can be analyzed with UV-Vis spectroscopy and LIBS.

 The need for complementary spectroscopic tools becomes even more apparent when considering the
chemical diversity present in MSR process streams. Recent studies have demonstrated that combining
multiple optical diagnostics provides a more complete and reliable picture of species relevant to
safeguards, corrosion monitoring, and fission-product transport. Hughey et al. (2020) established
quantitative infrared (IR) signatures for iodine monochloride (ICl), enabling IR-based detection of

 1

volatile halogen species that form in MSR off-gas environments. Felmy et al. (2021) expanded halogen
monitoring by demonstrating real-time Raman and fluorescence quantification of molecular iodine (I₂),
showing that chemometric analysis can extract concentration information even under dynamic flow
conditions. These halogen-focused studies highlight the importance of molecular-level detection in
systems where oxidation state and chemical speciation directly influence reactor safety and material
accountancy.

 In parallel, Andrews et al. (2022) demonstrated that laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS)
can quantify noble gases (Xe, Kr) and aerosolized fission-product metals (Cs, Rb) in flowing off-gas
streams. Their work shows that LIBS provides rapid elemental identification and concentration tracking
for species that cannot be easily monitored with vibrational spectroscopy. Together, these studies
illustrate how LIBS, UV-Vis absorption, Raman, fluorescence, and IR spectroscopy each fill distinct
analytical gaps—elemental detection, oxidation-state resolution, molecular identification, and
volatile-species monitoring.

 LeCroy et al. (2026) further demonstrated the value of multimodal spectroscopy by integrating
UV-Vis absorption and LIBS into a single online monitoring platform for lanthanide species in an
aqueous aerosol system.

 Following the successful real-time online monitoring of lanthanide species in the work conducted by
LeCroy et al., a high-temperature stainless steel system was constructed with the goal of running a
combined spectroscopy analysis to monitor lanthanide species dissolved in molten salt. This report
presents the development of a molten salt circulation loop given the initial stages of the stainless-steel
testbed. An analysis of the initial condition of the testbed is given followed by the implementation of the
required modifications and subsequent argon and aqueous testing.

 2. METHOD
 Figure 1 displays the general schematic and direction of flow for the circulation system coupled with
the LIBS/aerosol section. As the solution is delivered to the process fluid basin, Venturi 1 draws a
vacuum that promotes the fluid to travel through the flow cell, where UV-Vis spectroscopy can be
performed before the fluid is partially aerosolized by Venturi 1. This partially aerosolized flow is then
routed to the nebulizer tank where the nebulizer draws fluid from the base of the tank and mixes it with a
strong gust of argon through the nebulizer tip, causing the fluid to be fully aerosolized and pass through
the aerosol transfer tubing. The aerosols travel through the optical cell where LIBS can be performed
before being collected in the coalescing filter. For this project, only the circulation loop is considered,
meaning the optical cell and coalescing filter are not included in the testing and design modification.

 As observed in figures 1 and 2, the path of the fluid highlights the inconsistent placement of the
absorption flow cell and Venturi 1 between the initial system and the reference diagram. Since the venturi
vacuum outlet releases a mixture of argon gas and process fluid, the flow cell is met with two-phase flow
dominated by the argon gas. This leads to inaccurate readings with UV-Vis spectroscopy as the path
length is distorted and the additional material (argon gas) distracts from the process fluid information.
Therefore, the experimental scope of this study has the following defined steps:

 1. Initial testing with argon gas followed by argon gas and DI water combined.

 2. Manage leaks and obstructions observed.

 3

 3. Swap Venturi 1 and flow cell positions.

 4. Further testing with argon gas followed by argon gas and DI water combined.

 5. Perform required mechanical maintenance to obtain single-phase flow through flow cell and
 consistent circulation of process fluid.

 Step 1 was performed providing Venturi 1 and Venturi 2 (Norgren NJD-60M-S00M07) with
pressures of 70 psig and 25 psig, respectively, followed by the addition of 100 mL of DI water to the
process fluid basin before running the same pressure conditions. Flow through the flow cell was
qualitatively observed and the exit port of the aerosol transfer tube was also qualitatively monitored for
any aerosol production.

 Step 2 consisted of an evaluation of the system’s performance during initial testing and marking all
points of leakage and noting any obstruction in the tubing due to a buildup of debris. These leaks and
obstructions were then managed through the replacement of Swagelok fittings and tubing as well as
physical probing of the debris out of the existing tubing.

 Step 3 utilized polyethylene tubing to place the flow cell upstream of Venturi 1 to allow for ease of
rerouting and a clear view of the process fluid movement. For a similar reason, the connection from
Venturi 2 to the process fluid basin was replaced with transparent polyethylene tubing.

 Steps 4 and 5 were performed together in a cyclic manner as maintenance of the system was required
after an assessment of each test run. A qualitative evaluation of the flow through the flow cell and the
clear tubing informed the necessary design modifications and operating parameters (venturi pressures) to
achieve the desired single-phase flow through the flow cell.

 These experiments did not incorporate a nebulizer tip and instead connected polyethylene tubing to
the inlet of the nebulizer tank since this stage of the project focused solely on the circulation loop rather
than the coupling of the LIBS/aerosol system to the circulation loop testbed. This also allowed for more
process fluid to remain in the system and therefore less refilling of the process fluid basin.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
 Initial testing began with a deployment of argon gas through both venturis (70 psig through Venturi 1,
25 psig through Venturi 2) and the nebulizer tank (1 SCFM) to assess any significant leaks or
obstructions. Leaks were abundant throughout the system, particularly at the venturis and external flow
cell connections while obstructions were relatively minimal, with some chipped ceramic tubing stuck in
the outlet of the process fluid basin. Following the replacement and tightening of connections as well as
the addition of PTFE thread seal tape to the venturis, no leaks were observable with Ar testing. 100 mL of
DI water was then added to the process fluid basin, and the test was restarted with the same operating
parameters. As the smaller leaks were now more noticeable, similar measures were taken to fix the newly
found leaks.

 Following the leak prevention and obstruction clearance, Venturi 1 and the flow cell were swapped to
work towards a single-phase flow solution through the flow cell. As mentioned, polyethylene tubing was
used to reroute the flow path as observed in figure 3 and an initial argon gas test was performed followed
by the addition of 100 mL of DI water to process fluid basin once no leaks were observed.

 4

Figure 3. Circulation loop after swapping Venturi 1 and flow cell positions.

 The first issue observed in this test was the lack of fluid circulation through the testbed. When the
venturis were activated with the same operating parameters as prior, water would begin to travel from the
process fluid basin to the nebulizer but struggled to return to the basin. An isolated test of the venturis
showed that there was insufficient suction through the liquid inlet of the venturis to transport liquid back
up to the basin regardless of the argon flowrate supplied. To mitigate this, other venturis (Norgren NJS-
90M-S02M01 for Venturi 1 and Norgren NJD-60M-S02M01 for Venturi 2) were used as replacement and
promptly transported the fluid up to the basin at a more desirable rate visually.

 The next task was to find a balance between the suction of both the venturis to maintain a consistent
flowrate of the process fluid throughout the circulation loop. After varying the venturi pressures, it was
found that setting Venturi 1 to 40 psig and Venturi 2 to 50 psig led to the current most stable results.
While the circulation of the DI water was not kept constant, single-phase flow through the flow cell was
observed as consistent for about 100 seconds. Figure 4 displays the flow cell during this period of single-
phase flow. Since the cuvette has a square cross-section and the tubing has a circular and smaller cross-
section, there are gaps observed surrounding the flow. It may be beneficial to conform the tubing to the
cuvette directly before the flow cell inlet to achieve complete single-phase flow. Further testing on the
operating parameters must be done to determine a true balance between the two venturis for constant,
single-phase flow through the flow cell.

 4. CONCLUSION
 This project established the foundational steps required to develop a molten-salt-compatible
circulation loop for future online spectroscopic monitoring. Initial testing of the stainless-steel system
revealed several practical challenges, including leaks, tubing obstructions, and persistent two-phase flow
through the absorption cell caused by the original placement of Venturi 1. Systematic troubleshooting—
tightening fittings, replacing damaged tubing, applying PTFE thread seal tape, and clearing debris—
successfully eliminated leaks and restored basic circulation functionality. Relocating the flow cell
upstream of Venturi 1 further improved flow behavior by reducing gas intrusion into the optical path,
enabling periods of stable single-phase flow suitable for UV-Vis measurements. Subsequent testing
demonstrated that venturi performance was highly sensitive to pressure balance and hardware selection.
Replacing the original venturi with a more suitable model and tuning Venturi 1 and Venturi 2 to 40 psig
and 50 psig, respectively, produced the most reliable circulation observed to date. Although continuous
single-phase flow has not yet been achieved, the system now supports repeatable operation and provides a
clear pathway for refinement.

 The progress made in this first phase lays essential groundwork for transitioning to molten-salt
testing. The next stage will require integrating furnace-compatible components, validating material
compatibility with LiCl-KCl, and incorporating optical access points for combined LIBS and UV-Vis
diagnostics. Completing these steps will enable real-time monitoring of chemically complex molten-salt
environments, supporting future safeguards, corrosion studies, and nuclear-material accountancy
applications.
