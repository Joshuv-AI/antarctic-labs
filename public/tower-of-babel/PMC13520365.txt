Satellitome analysis of the black soldier fly Hermetia illucens reveals genome organization, interstocks dynamics, and insights into centromeric and telomeric repeat composition
Authors: Rhavenna Thais Alves-Gomes, Pablo Mora, José Manuel Rico-Porras, Teresa Palomeque, Pedro Lorite, Diogo C. Cabral-de-Mello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHermetia illucens (Diptera: Stratiomyidae), commonly known as the black soldier fly (BSF), is a species of growing industrial interest due to its efficiency in organic waste conversion and protein-rich biomass production. Despite the availability of extensive genomic resources, chromosomal-level information on repetitive DNA remains scarce. Here, we present the first comprehensive cytogenomic characterization of satellite DNAs (satDNAs) in H. illucens, integrating cytogenetic mapping with genome data to elucidate their organization and potential structural roles.ResultsThe karyotype (2n = 14, XY) was confirmed, showing concordance with the assembled pseudochromosomes and revealing heterochromatin polymorphisms between stocks from Brazil and Spain. We identified eleven satDNA families representing approximately 3–4% of the genome in both males and females, with monomer lengths ranging from 7 to 4,166 bp and A + T contents between 43 and 73%. Chromosomal mapping uncovered diverse distribution patterns, ranging from chromosome-specific to broadly dispersed repeats. HillSat03-160 localized to chromosome termini, suggesting a telomere-associated role, whereas HillSat04-7 was enriched in centromeric regions, likely contributing to centromere organization. Other satDNA families, such as HillSat01-162 and HillSat02-4166, showed broad autosomal distributions, whereas HillSat05-196 and HillSat06-109 exhibited stock-specific variation, indicating dynamic repeat evolution. Comparisons between in silico genome assemblies and FISH mapping revealed inconsistencies attributable to the collapse of repetitive regions during assembly.ConclusionsOverall, our results demonstrate the dynamic and functional significance of satDNAs in the H. illucens genome, highlighting their contribution to genome organization, telomere evolution, and chromosomal diversification, and providing a foundation for future pangenomic and evolutionary studies in this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02647-y.

Background

Hermetia illucens (Diptera: Stratiomyidae: Hermetiinae), commonly known as the black soldier fly (BSF), is a globally distributed species native to South America [1–4]. Its larvae are particularly notable for their efficiency in converting a wide range of organic wastes into protein- and lipid-rich biomass, reducing waste weight approximately 50% faster than conventional composting [5–8]. Compared with other edible insects, such as house crickets and mealworms, BSF larvae demonstrate superior bioconversion efficiency and rearing advantages [9, 10], making them a promising sustainable source for animal feed [11–13]. BSF farming is low-cost, resource-efficient, and well aligned with circular economy and zero-waste organic waste management strategies [9, 14–16]. Owing to this unique set of characteristics, H. illucens has become one of the most extensively studied insect species, with rapidly growing research exploring its structural and functional biology, industrial applications, food production, biofuels, and composting [17–25].

Eukaryotic genomes are predominantly composed of repetitive DNA, which includes tandemly repeated sequences, such as satellite DNAs (satDNAs), and dispersed sequences, such as transposable elements (TEs) [26]. Although satDNAs do not encode proteins, they are crucial for maintaining chromosomal integrity and show remarkable variation among species, thereby contributing to genomic diversity and evolution [27, 28]. They evolve in a concerted fashion, resulting in sequence homogenization and greater similarity among repeat units within species than between species [28, 29]. This process can drive genomic differentiation, promote lineage divergence, and even reduce hybridization success, highlighting the evolutionary significance of satDNAs [28, 30–34]. In terms of chromosomal location, satDNAs are classically found in pericentromeric and subtelomeric regions, although they can also occur at interstitial sites in both euchromatic and heterochromatic regions [28, 33, 35, 36]. Their strategic positioning underpins essential roles in chromosome architecture, proper segregation during cell division, and overall genome stability [28, 37–39].

Among Diptera, the most comprehensive analyses of satDNA content have been performed with the advent of genomic approaches, which enabled the characterization of satellitomes (the complete or nearly complete set of satDNA families) in several species, although the data are still biased toward representatives of the Drosophila genus. In this group, satDNAs are abundant components of constitutive heterochromatin, mainly located in pericentromeric and subtelomeric regions, but also found in euchromatic areas. Comparative genomic analyses have revealed wide variation in satDNA abundance, with closely related species sharing subsets of families consistent with the satDNA library hypothesis. Lineage-specific amplifications and sequence divergence contribute to genome size variation and chromosomal differentiation, underscoring the functional and evolutionary significance of satDNAs in Drosophila genome organization and reflecting the dynamic nature of Diptera genomes [40–44].

Despite significant progress in characterizing satellitomes in Drosophila, such information remains largely unexplored in other dipteran lineages, including the Stratiomyidae family, which includes the black soldier fly H. illucens. From a genetic perspective, extensive information is already available for this species, including studies on population structure, transcriptomics, and mitochondrial genome organization [4, 45–48], as well as chromosome-scale genome assemblies [49, 50]. Its karyotype was studied only through conventional staining, revealing 2n = 14, XY in males [51], with no information about chromosome banding patterns or detailed chromosomal structure. This species harbors one of the largest genomes among dipterans, with assembled genome size estimates ranging from ~ 888 Mb [50] to ~ 1.01 Gb [49]. Notably, repetitive DNA constitutes roughly two-thirds of the genome, averaging ~ 67.3% [46, 49, 50]. Despite this wealth of genomic data, a comprehensive characterization of repetitive elements, particularly satDNAs, remains largely absent, even though such analyses are fundamental for understanding genome organization.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive cytogenomic analysis of H. illucens satellitome by integrating cytogenetic techniques with bioinformatic approaches based on genomic data, aiming to advance the understanding of genome organization and evolution in this species. This combined strategy has proven particularly effective for elucidating the structure and dynamics of the repetitive DNA landscape in eukaryotes and for improving genome assemblies (e.g. [42, 52–58]). Our results revealed intraspecific variation in the distribution of heterochromatin and satDNAs and enabled the identification and detailed characterization of several satellite families, each displaying distinct chromosomal localization patterns. Some repeats were chromosome-specific, providing valuable markers for linking genome assemblies to physical chromosomes. Notably, the satellite family HillSat03-160 was detected in telomeric regions, while the HillSat04-7 was identified as a centromeric satDNA, further contributing to the refinement of the genome assembly. Altogether, our findings highlight the dynamic nature of repetitive DNA sequences in H. illucens, offering new insights into genome organization and the diversification of repetitive elements in Diptera, and providing a foundation for future pangenome analyses that integrate satDNA chromosomal mapping with genomic data.

Results

Karyotype of Hermetia illucens: general insights about repetitive DNA variability

To better characterize the H. illucens karyotype and advance understanding of its genome, we first performed a comprehensive analysis of general chromosomal structure using conventional staining with Giemsa or DAPI, C-banding, and rDNA mapping, techniques commonly employed in insect cytogenetics. This approach revealed some general chromosomal variability within the species. The H. illucens karyotype is 2n = 14, with XY sex chromosomes in males and XX in females. In both Brazilian and Spanish stocks, the karyotype comprises six pairs of autosomes. According to the arm ratio values, these include four metacentric pairs (Chr-1, Chr-2, Chr-3, and Chr-4), one acrocentric pair (Chr-5), and one submetacentric pair (Chr-6) (Table S1). Chr-5 exhibited polymorphism in the size of the short arm: the most common variant, designated Chr-5a, possesses an almost undetectable short arm, whereas the Chr-5b variant features a clearly visible short arm (Fig. 1). Both variants occurred in both stocks, with individuals identified as Chr-5a/Chr-5a (three from Brazil and four from Spain), Chr-5b/Chr-5b (three from Brazil and three from Spain), or Chr-5a/Chr-5b (four from Brazil and three from Spain). The sex chromosomes, the smallest in the complement, are acrocentric and similar in appearance under conventional staining, with the Chr-Y slightly smaller than the Chr-X (Fig. 1a,b). The C-banding technique revealed that heterochromatin was primarily located in the pericentromeric regions, forming large blocks mainly in Chr-1 to Chr-4, besides occurrence of interstitial blocks. Notably, these large heterochromatic blocks were composed of multiple smaller regions, as seen in less condensed mitotic chromosomes (Fig. 2a-c). The smallest chromosomes, Chr-5 and Chr-6, showed more variable heterochromatin distribution. Chr-6 exhibited several heterochromatic blocks along the entire long arm (Fig. 2b,c), and in less condensed chromosomes, a block was also observed on the short arm (Fig. 2a). In Chr-5, heterochromatin distribution was polymorphic, presenting two distinct patterns: (i) several blocks on the long arm for variant Chr-5a (Fig. 2d,f) or (ii) a strong heterochromatic band on the short arm with several faint bands on the long arm for variant Chr-5b (Fig. 2e,f). The Chr-5b variant corresponds to this chromosome presenting a more visible short arm in conventional Giemsa or DAPI staining. As expected, and consistent with conventional staining, we observed homozygous and heterozygous individuals for this polymorphism (Fig. 2d-f). Regarding the sex chromosomes, Chr-Y contained a heavily stained heterochromatic block restricted to the long arm, whereas Chr-X displayed heterochromatin along both arms, with weaker staining intensity compared to Chr-Y. The small Chr-Y is fully heterochromatic (Fig. 2c). The described heterochromatin patterns were consistent across both Brazilian and Spanish stocks. Differences between the sex chromosomes were confirmed by analyzing individuals with two identical sex chromosomes (females) versus heterogametic individuals (males). Heterochromatin distribution was also verified in male mitotic metaphases obtained from testes. Characterization of satellite DNAs improves the genome organization knowledge: repeat collapsing, interstock dynamics, and centromere and telomere organization

A total of 18 sequences were initially identified as satDNAs in the H. illucens genome by TAREAN using Illumina genomic short-reads from both male and female individuals. Manual curation revealed that some clusters corresponded to the same satDNA and were therefore merged. Additionally, two clusters identified by TAREAN were found to correspond to multigene families, including 5S rDNA and histone genes, due to their tandem organization, and were excluded from subsequent satDNA analyses. For the remaining sequences, no significant similarity was found with previously described repetitive elements in the consulted databases, including NCBI and Repbase. The head-to-tail arrangement of these satDNAs was verified using PacBio or Illumina reads, confirming tandem organization for all of them (Figure S1) except one cluster, which was excluded because tandem repeats were not observed. After this manual curation, subsequent analyses were performed on 11 satDNA families.

To investigate general evolutionary patterns of satDNAs in the H. illucens genome, we used K2P divergences and genomic abundances of each repeat as proxies, an approach commonly applied in satDNA analyses. This analysis provides insights into repeat amplification, differentiation, and homogenization within a genome. Additional characteristics reflecting genome dynamics, including A + T content and monomer size, were also assessed. The eleven satDNAs (File S1) varied in monomer lengths from 7 bp to 4,166 bp, and the A + T content ranged from 42.9% to 73.4%, with an average of 56.5%. They comprised approximately 3.62% of the male genome and 3.17% of the female genome based on the Illumina short-reads libraries. Only slight differences were observed between sexes for individual satDNAs, suggesting minor differential enrichment between sex chromosomes. The estimation of the satDNAs revealed much less abundance in the assembled genome, about 1.21%. The most abundant satDNA, HillSat01-162, accounted for 2.31% and 1.89% of the male and female genomes, respectively, representing more than half of the total satDNA fraction in the H. illucens genome. This same satDNA presented only about 0.13% of assembled chromosomes. In contrast, the least abundant satDNA, HillSat11-246, comprised only 0.0007% of both male and female genomes, representing about 0.0014% of assembled chromosomes. In general, the most abundant satDNAs in the genome (HillSat01-162 to HillSat07-1937) are underestimated relative to their abundance estimates based on Illumina reads. In contrast, for the less abundant satDNAs (HillSat08-329 to HillSat11-246), abundance estimates are higher in the assembled genome, which may be influenced by the underestimation of the most abundant families. The largest discrepancy between abundance estimates based on the assembled genome and raw reads was observed for HillSat06-109, which represents 0.095–0.079% in the read-based estimates, depending on sex, but only 0.0004% in the assembled genome. Different patterns of K2P divergence were observed, which were largely similar between sexes, ranging from 1.25% to 8.37% in the male genome and from 1.27% to 9.38% in the female genome (Table 1). Landscape analyses, which integrate K2P divergence with satDNA abundances, can reveal signatures of recent amplification and homogenization events, as well as older amplification waves. For both sexes, these analyses showed similar patterns, with the highest amplification peaks occurring at low K2P values, between 0–5% (Fig. 4; Figure S2). Notably, HillSat03-160 was localized to the terminal regions of all chromosomes, forming large blocks (Fig. 5a,c-f). In addition to the clear presence of this repeat at mitotic chromosome termini (Fig. 5a), analysis of polytene chromosomes revealed signals at the most distal chromosome band (Fig. 5e). Meiotic metaphases, with chromosomes aligned at the equatorial plate and centromeres oriented toward the poles, showed hybridization signals at opposite sites, confirming the terminal localization of HillSat03-160 on all chromosomes. Although this satDNA is present in telomeric regions, some variability was observed: in the Brazilian stock, HillSat03-160 was sometimes absent from Chr-6 (Fig. 5c), and signal size on Chr-5 varied between large and small in both Brazilian and Spanish stocks (Fig. 5d). CHRISMAPP analysis confirmed the terminal localization of HillSat03-160 and revealed additional small sites on some chromosomes not detected by FISH. However, the size of the arrays detected in the assembled chromosomes is very small compared with the signals obtained by FISH, suggesting that a large fraction of this satDNA may have been lost during the assembly process. For this satDNA multiple copies were detected on a single scaffold (CAJHNG010000006.1; Fig. 5g).

FISH analysis of the remaining four satDNAs revealed that their signals were predominantly located in heterochromatic regions, although in some cases signals were also detected in euchromatic regions, either localized to specific chromosomal sites or dispersed across chromosomes with varying intensities (Fig. 6). Variable patterns were observed between the Spanish and Brazilian stocks, in both chromosomal distribution and signal intensity. The most abundant satDNA, HillSat01-162, was broadly distributed across all chromosomes except Chr-Y, forming multiple strong hybridization signals in pericentromeric and interstitial heterochromatin. For Chr-5, the location and intensity of the signals resembled the heterochromatin distribution of the long arm, i.e. the Chr-5a has stronger and larger signals than the Chr-5b. This pattern was consistent between both stocks (Fig. 6a,b). Comparison of the FISH distribution of HillSat01-162 with CHRISMAPP mapping revealed largely discrepant pattern, as in the assembled chromosomes, this satDNA is detected as short arrays scattered along the chromosomal arms but not forming large blocks as revealed by FISH (Fig. 6c). The second most abundant satDNA, HillSat02-4166, produced weak scattered hybridization signals in both euchromatic and heterochromatic regions, being slightly accumulated in the pericentromeric areas. It was distributed across all autosomes but was less enriched on the sex chromosomes (Fig. 6d,e). CHRISMAPP analysis revealed very small scattered signals, consistent with the FISH mapping (Fig. 6f). In contrast, HillSat05-196 formed discrete blocks on multiple chromosomes, showing distinct patterns between the Spanish (Fig. 6g) and Brazilian (Fig. 6h) stocks for two of the large chromosomes. In the Spanish stock, all four large chromosomes exhibited signals, whereas in Brazil only three did. In both stocks, the conserved pattern included the Chr-1 with two signals on the short arm, one subterminal and one proximal and Chr-4 with two subterminal signals, one on each arm. Chr-5 and Chr-6 also showed identical patterns in both stocks, with one subterminal and one proximal signal on the long arm of Chr-6 and no signals on Chr-5. In addition, the X chromosome displayed a pericentromeric signal in both stocks, whereas the Y chromosome lacked signals. Differences were observed in the Chr-2 as in the Spanish stock, a single proximal signal was detected on the long arm, while in the Brazilian stock, an additional subterminal signal appeared on the short arm. Moreover, in the Spanish stock, the Chr-3 exhibited subterminal blocks on both arms, which were absent in the corresponding chromosome from the Brazilian stock. CHRISMAPP primarily detected subterminal signals, consistent with the FISH data for both stocks, although some proximal blocks present on some chromosomes are not recovered (Fig. 6i). Finally, HillSat06-109 was detected by FISH exclusively in the pericentromeric regions of Chr-5 and the sex chromosomes: on Chr-X (short arm) and Chr-Y (long arm) in the Spanish stock (Fig. 6j) and only on Chr-X in the Brazilian stock (Fig. 6k). In silico CHRISMAPP mapping detected this satDNA only on Chr-X; as observed with 18S rDNA, the pericentromeric block on Chr-5 identified by FISH was not represented in the assembled genome (Fig. 6l).

The other satDNAs identified in the H. illucens genome, due to their low abundance (representing less than 0.1% of the genome), were first mapped onto the assembled chromosomes and unplaced scaffolds using CHRISMAPP (Fig. 7). As expected, only a few signals were detected. HillSat07-1937 showed small mapping regions on Chr-2 and Chr-6, HillSat08-329 had a small mapping region on Chr-3; HillSat09-435 and HillSat10-1077 each mapped to small regions on Chr-2; and HillSat11-246 had three mapped regions on Chr-5. Since HillSat08-329 was located only on Chr-3, we performed FISH to link the assembled genome information with physical chromosomes, confirming the presence of this element at the cytological level, consistent with its size (Fig. 8a). Discussion

General chromosomal analysis of Hermetia illucens links genome assembly with karyotype structure and reveals heterochromatin polymorphisms

As an important representative of Diptera, H. illucens has received increasing genomic attention in recent years, including the production of chromosome-level genome assemblies [49, 50, 59]. However, to our knowledge, no detailed comprehensive karyotype study has been previously performed for such an important species. Our karyotype data agree with the number of pseudochromosomes assembled in females, corresponding to six pairs of autosomes plus the Chr-X. By analyzing male mitotic chromosomes, we further confirmed the presence of a Y chromosome, establishing the occurrence of an XY sex determination system, consistent with the most common pattern found in Diptera and in Stratiomyidae, as reported in 24 species [51, 60]. In line with earlier assembly data, pseudochromosome 7 was identified as one of the sex chromosomes (Chr-X) due to its reduced coverage in males compared to females [49] and corresponds to the smallest element of the female complement (~ 15 Mb), coincident with our cytogenetic data. The sizes of the remaining pseudochromosomes are also concordant with our karyotype data: four large chromosomes (~ 222–173 Mb in size; Chr-1 to Chr-4) and two medium-sized ones (~ 116 Mb, Chr-5 and ~ 103 Mb, Chr-6). Based on the presumed ancestral karyotype of Stratiomyidae (2n = 16), our results and genomic data indicate that at least one autosomal fusion occurred in H. illucens, giving rise to the karyotype 2n = 14, XY.

Beyond confirming chromosome number, we defined centromere positions and revealed the distribution of some satDNA enriched on heterochromatin, information that is difficult to obtain solely from genome assemblies with no further annotation. Centromere position was consistent across the two stocks analyzed, comprising four pairs of metacentric chromosomes, one submetacentric, and one acrocentric. This contrasts with earlier reports from Taipei (Taiwan) and Caracas (Venezuela), where only metacentric autosomes (Taipei) or five metacentrics plus one submetacentric (Caracas) were described [51]. Such differences suggest that, although the diploid number is stable across stocks, intrachromosomal rearrangements such as inversions may occur, causing changes in chromosomal morphology, as well as enlargement of chromosomal arms, as observed here for Chr-5. Inversion polymorphisms are common in insect species across spatial and temporal scales [61]. However, caution is warranted when interpreting the earlier reports from [51], as they differ substantially from our observations, likely due to (1) the use of meiotic cells, which hampers precise centromere identification, and/or (2) possible taxonomic misidentification of the species. We therefore propose that the chromosomal morphology described here, obtained from multiple individuals from two stocks, and based on mitotic metaphase analysis, more reliably represents the karyotype of H. illucens.

The analysis of repetitive DNA-rich regions through C-banding revealed extensive heterochromatin regions, forming multiple blocks and with differences between chromosomes from the same stock and between them. This highlights karyotype dynamism from repetitive DNA-rich regions, being evident mainly for Chr-5 in which heterochromatin distribution is variable. Additionally, the heterochromatin-rich Chr-Y is also polymorphic in size due to differences in heterochromatin blocks. The large pericentromeric heterochromatic blocks from H. illucens differ from patterns described in other dipterans, where C-banding generally reveals limited canonical centromeric heterochromatin [62–65]. The differences in Chr-Y size observed in Brazilian individuals echo well-documented processes for this chromosome degeneration in insects, where repeat turnover, deletions, and amplification cycles play a central role in its differentiation and degeneration, driving structural variability over short evolutionary timescales [66, 67].

The mapping of repetitive DNAs, including rDNA and satDNA sequences revealed further details about genome organization and genome dynamics in H. illucens, giving also more detailed information for genome assembly and annotation. Our FISH data showed consistent 18S rDNA signals on both sex chromosomes and Chr-5, yet the genome assembly only reflected the site on Chr-X. Such discrepancies illustrate how repetitive arrays can remain poorly resolved in genome projects, even those claiming chromosome-level assemblies. For other flies, in general, the location of rDNAs in non-drosophilid flies is poorly known [68]. As in H. illucens, occurrence of major rDNA sites associated to sex chromosomes have been reported in a handful of studies, as for example in Anastrepha [69, 70], Lucilia [64], Chrysomya [62], Bractocera oleae [71], and Ceratitis rosa [72]. In H. illucens occurrence of multiple clusters in both sex and autosomal chromosomes reinforces the plasticity and was also observed in Glossina pallidipes and G. palpalis [73]. In comparison to 18S rDNA, the 5S rDNA was better assembled, being coincident the location observed through cytological and in silico analyses.

Structural roles and evolutionary dynamics of satellite DNAs in the Hermetia illucens genome

The satellitome of H. illucens is composed of at least 11 satDNA families, together representing ~ 3–4% of the genome, with a broad spectrum of monomer lengths (7–4,166 bp) and base pair composition (43–73% A + T), demonstrating the heterogeneous nature of the satDNA landscape. The satDNAs represent only a low fraction of repetitive DNAs in the species that has repeat content corresponding to ~ 67% of its genome, with most repeats being TEs, more specifically the LINEs [59]. Abundance and divergence analyses indicate that most satDNA families are undergoing to a recent amplification and homogenization process, as shown by divergence peaks with values below 5%, consistent with concerted evolution patterns described for satDNAs [29, 74]. Only minor differences in genomic proportions were detected between males and females, suggesting limited enrichment of satDNA on the Chr-Y, in contrast to many species where sex chromosomes act as major repeat reservoirs [75–77]. Among non-drosophilid flies, although some satDNAs were identified [78], a complete set of satDNAs in one species was never performed. On the other hand, in Drosophila genus multiple species were studied, revealing high dynamism for satDNA fraction, with genome proportions ranging from 0.54% to as high as 38.8%. In some species positive correlation with the genome size was observed, as in species from montium group and Drosophila subgenus [42, 43], contrarily for Sophophora subgenus representatives the correlation is less evident [42]. Hermetia illucens has one of the largest genome sizes among dipterans [49, 50, 59] and considering the low abundance of satDNA in contrast to the high TE content, it is most parsimonious to attribute genome enlargement to TE activity.

The chromosomal mapping of satDNAs revealed both conserved and variable patterns between stocks, providing insights into their potential structural roles and in the differentiation for the genome. The mapping of HillSat03-160 at the chromosome termini across mitotic, meiotic, and polytene chromosomes suggests their involvement in telomere composition. Although its presence was generally conserved, stock-specific variation was observed, such as the absence of signals in Chr-6 of some Brazilian individuals and size variation of signals in Chr-5, suggestive of incomplete homogenization or instability for telomeric repeat composition, which may reflect ongoing turnover at chromosome ends. Telomeric long tandem repeats, like observed in H. illucens, have been identified in several dipterans, including lower dipterans such as mosquitoes and other flies as in Bactrocera dorsalis [79–82]. Among flies, in previous studies the long tandem repeats forming the telomere structure were observed in representatives from Tachinidae and Syrphidae with repeats ranging from 173 to 381 bp [81]. Since H. illucens belongs to the Stratiomyidae, an early-diverging clade within the Brachycera [83, 84], it is probable that this telomere organization represents an ancestral condition. Other mechanisms for telomere formation were also reported in Brachycera, as noticed in Drosophila species that is dependent on retroelements [81], evidencing the plasticity of telomere maintenance and organization within the group.

The HillSat04-7 was enriched in centromeric regions of all chromosomes, a typical feature of satellites involved in kinetochore function [39], suggesting a possible involvement in the centromeric organization of H. illucens. This was the only satDNA placed in the centromeric region, suggesting low complexity of satDNAs in H. illucens centromeres. In Drosophila species from ananassae subgroup, based on the analysis of assembled genomes, expanded satDNAs forming extensive and contiguous centromeric/pericentromeric arrays were documented [85]. Contrasting with this, D. melanogaster has the centromere formed by complex DNA sequences, involving retroelements flanked by satDNAs [86]. Complementary studies based on TE analysis and functional centromeres could provide more details about the centromere structure in H. illucens and confirm the role of HillSat04-7. In the Brazilian stock, the small sites of HillSat04-7 in terminal/subterminal positions reinforce the dynamic nature of satDNA and the differentiation between the studied stocks. We observed discrepancies between FISH and genome assembly for this satDNA, with large cytological blocks reduced to small and scattered signals in scaffolds, once again highlighting the problem of satDNA collapse in assemblies. Centromeric repeats, often among the most homogenized and abundant satellites in a genome [32, 87], are systematically challenging for genomic assembly [88–92], leading to incomplete reconstruction of chromosome structure. The enrichment of HillSat04-7 in scaffolds that are almost entirely composed of this repeat further demonstrates that while the assembly captures fragments of the satDNA, it fails to integrate them into complete chromosomal contexts.

Other analyzed satDNA families further illustrate the dual pattern of conservation and dynamism in the H. illucens satellitome. For example, HillSat01-162 and HillSat02-4166 were broadly distributed across the autosomes, forming blocks or being scattered along the chromosomal arms, respectively. The consistent distribution of these two repeats across both stocks suggests a conserved role in genome organization. In contrast, HillSat05-196 exhibited striking inter-stock polymorphisms, showing distinct chromosomal arrangements between Brazilian and Spanish stocks. HillSat06-109 further underscores this variability, as its variable presence on sex chromosomes between stocks, illustrates how satDNA sequences can shape sex chromosome differentiation in stock-specific ways. Such polymorphisms may reflect ongoing satDNA turnover through the differential accumulation or loss of repeats among stocks, resulting from processes such as unequal recombination, local amplification, or deletion events. These mechanisms are known to drive rapid changes in satDNA arrays and contribute to the structural variability of chromosomes [28]. As observed for the 18S rDNA, a discrepancy was observed between the genome assembly and our FISH data for HillSat06-109, which also occupies the short arm of Chr-5 but is absent from the assembled genome, further reinforcing the collapse of this genomic region during assembly.

Finally, other satDNAs with low abundance displayed a highly restricted chromosomal distribution, consistent with the expected pattern of minor repetitive elements. CHRISMAPP analyses revealed that most of these sequences were confined to small regions of a few chromosomes or unplaced scaffolds, suggesting limited local amplification. For instance, HillSat07-1937 was dispersed across Chr-2, Chr-6, and several unplaced scaffolds, indicating incomplete assembly of repetitive regions. In contrast, HillSat08-329 exhibited a unique location restricted to Chr-3, which was validated by FISH experiments that confirmed its presence at a single cytological locus. This correspondence between in silico mapping and chromosomal hybridization supports the reliability of the genome assembly for this region and reinforces the utility of integrating satellitome data with cytogenetic mapping.

Conclusions

By integrating cytogenetic and genomic approaches, our study demonstrates that reliance solely on genome assemblies is insufficient for accurate repeat assembly and annotation, particularly within heterochromatic regions. This limitation aligns with previous studies showing that genome assemblies often underrepresent repetitive DNA families, especially tandem repeats, due to the inherent difficulty of assembling highly repetitive sequences (e.g., [93]). Our analyses reveal that satDNAs, particularly those forming large chromosomal blocks, could be poorly assembled and annotated, limiting the resolution of chromosome-specific repeat landscapes. These findings highlight the value of satellitome-based cytogenomic analyses to refine genome assemblies and underscore the necessity of combining molecular and cytogenetic data to ensure assembly quality. Despite the general conservation of satDNA families, FISH mapping revealed stock-specific variation in their chromosomal distribution, reflecting turnover and dynamic evolution. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that satDNAs contribute to genomic plasticity in the H. illucens genome, offering new insights into genome architecture, telomere evolution, and the mechanisms underlying chromosomal differentiation. Such variability, while not altering the diploid number, may influence chromosomal pairing, meiotic stability, and stock divergence.

Methods

Biological material and chromosome obtaining

Biological material from two H. illucens stocks used for chromosomal analyses and was obtained from commercial suppliers: Paraqueda Náutica (Rio Claro, São Paulo, Brazil) and Artroposfera (https://artroposfera.es/, Toledo, Spain), originally collected in Rio Claro/São Paulo and Spain (exact locality unknown), respectively. Mitotic chromosomes were prepared from dissected brains of phase III larvae (5th–6th instar, 13 days old), following the larval stage classification described in [94, 95] and using the dissection protocol of [96]. Meiotic chromosomes were obtained from the testes of adult males. Dissected tissues were placed in insect physiological solution (128 mM NaCl, Na2HPO4 16.8 mM, KH2PO4 20 mM, pH 7.0) and transferred to 100 µL colchicine solution (0.02% in 0.9% NaCl). After 30 min, 200 µL of distilled water was added to induce a hypotonic shock; 10 min later, a pre-fixation step was performed by applying ~ 20 µL of modified Carnoy’s solution (3:1 absolute ethanol:glacial acetic acid). Fixation was then completed by transferring the material to 200 µL of the same solution. Polytene chromosomes were obtained from salivary glands of actively feeding phase II larvae, following [97].

To obtain chromosome spreads, brain, testes, and salivary gland tissues were transferred to a microtube containing 50 µL of 50% glacial acetic acid and macerated by pipetting. The suspension was then spread onto glass slides and placed on a hot plate at 42 °C. For conventional analysis, chromosome slides were stained with Giemsa 5% or DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Roche, Applied Science). For other techniques, slides were dehydrated through an ethanol series of increasing concentrations, 70% (1 min), 85% (30 s), and 100% (30 s), then air-dried and stored at –20 °C until use.

Karyotyping and heterochromatin identification through C-banding

To characterize the general karyotype structure of H. illucens, mitotic chromosomes from six male individuals (three from Spain and three from Brazil) were counted and measured, with five metaphases analyzed per individual. Chromosome morphology was established based on the arm ratio (r = long arm/short arm), according to the nomenclature proposed by [98]. Due to the detection of polymorphisms in chromosomes 5 and Y (see Results), an additional seven individuals were analyzed to estimate the frequency of these variants within each stock. The C-banding technique used to detect heterochromatin in mitotic neuroblast chromosomes followed the protocol described in [99], with minor modifications. Freshly prepared chromosome slides were first treated with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid for 15 min at room temperature, then incubated in 5% barium hydroxide for 1 min 30 s at 60 °C. Slides were rinsed sequentially with distilled water plus 1 mL absolute acetic acid, distilled water plus 0.5 mL absolute acetic acid, and finally with distilled water. After thorough rinsing, slides were incubated for 3 min in 2 × SSC (Saline Sodium Citrate) at 60 °C, washed again with distilled water, air-dried, and stained with either 5% Giemsa or DAPI.

Computational identification and analysis of satellite DNAs

The satDNAs in the H. illucens genome were identified using Illumina genomic short-reads from one male (SRA accession ERR4822642) and one female (SRA accession ERR4822643) individual samples maintained at the University of Cambridge, Department of Zoology, Cambridge, UK [49]. Putative satDNA sequences were retrieved using an integrated version of the Tandem Repeat Analyzer (TAREAN) tool, which enables automated identification of satellite repeats within the RepeatExplorer2 pipeline [54, 100], implemented on the Galaxy platform (https://repeatexplorer-elixir.cerit-sc.cz/galaxy/). Briefly, RepeatExplorer2 combines sequence reads from both samples into a single dataset, clustering sequences based on similarity to identify shared repeat families; clusters composed exclusively of reads from a single sample were considered sample-specific sequences. Analyses were performed using mostly default settings, with the following modifications: the number of processed reads was set to 12,000,000 (6,000,000 from each sex), and the minimum cluster size threshold was defined as 0.001% of the input reads. To improve detection of less abundant repeats, highly abundant repeats were automatically filtered out, partially removing their reads from the analysis. Additionally, the “long” queue option was used to provide greater computational resources compared to the default “basic” queue.

Putative satDNAs identified with RepeatExplorer2/TAREAN were then manually curated to verify sequence identity and genomic organization. Using available Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) reads (SRA accession ERX4692652), we assessed the tandem organization of each satDNA by performing BLAST searches using trimers of each sequence. The 15 PacBio reads with the lowest e-values and highest sequence similarity were selected for further analysis, which included annotation of monomer sequences using Geneious v11.1.5 [101]. For a satDNA with a 7-bp repeat (see Results), tandem organization was assessed directly in Illumina short-reads using the same approach. Annotation required at least 70% sequence similarity and coverage, and analyses were qualitative, recording the presence or absence of tandem arrayed monomers. Only sequences demonstrating tandem organization in the reads were considered satDNAs. Finally, to determine whether the identified satDNAs had been previously characterized, sequences were compared against repeats available in the Repbase/GIRI using CENSOR [102] and National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) databases, following parameters of similarity proposed by [36] i.e. minimum of 50% for monomer coverage and over 65% similarity.

We used RepeatMasker [103] to calculate the abundance and divergence of each satDNA by comparing Illumina short-reads from male and female genomes and the chromosome-scale female genome assembly (GCA_905115235.1; [49]). Satellite DNA sequence divergence was estimated using the Kimura two-parameter (K2P) distances [104]. To estimate genomic abundance, 1,000,000 randomly selected Illumina short-reads or the whole assembled chromosomes were aligned against a library of identified satDNA dimers. For library construction, satDNAs with repeat units shorter than 100 bp were concatenated to reach a minimum length of 200 bp, while repeat units longer than 100 bp were duplicated to generate dimers. The genomic fraction represented by each satDNA was calculated as the number of nucleotides mapped divided by the total number of nucleotides in the library. Satellite DNA sequences were named following [105], using the species abbreviation, “Sat,” and a number reflecting decreasing abundance based on the male genome (heteromorphic sex, XY) and repeat unit length (e.g., HillSat01-162).

Chromosomal mapping of repetitive DNAs

First, we determined the chromosomal locations of the satDNAs identified in this study, as well as the multigene families 18S and 5S ribosomal DNAs (rDNAs), by in silico mapping using the CHRomosome In Silico MAPPing (CHRISMAPP) pipeline [106] with the available chromosome-scale female genome assembly of H. illucens (GCA_905115235.1; [49]). The 18S and 5S rDNAs were selected as they serve as widely recognized chromosomal markers in insect cytogenetics, providing reliable landmarks for chromosome identification. Moreover, since these multigene families typically occur in tandem arrays and exhibit high evolutionary dynamism, they often vary in cluster number and position. Consequently, their mapping can facilitate the detection of genomic differences between H. illucens stocks. The assembly employed in this study comprises seven chromosomes and thirteen unplaced scaffolds. The GenBank accession numbers for the chromosomes are as follows: LR899009.1 (Chr-1), LR899010.1 (Chr-2), LR899011.1 (Chr-3), LR899012.1 (Chr-4), LR899013.1 (Chr-5), LR899014.1 (Chr-6), and LR899015.1 (Chr-7, corresponding to the X chromosome, named here as Chr-X). Each satDNA family was annotated in the genome using Geneious v11.1.5 with a minimum similarity and coverage threshold of 70%, and the resulting annotations were plotted with the CHRISMAPP v.2.1 R script (https://github.com/LoriteLab/CHRISMAPP, File S2).

To better understand the chromosomal organization of repetitive DNA in distinct stocks and to compare their positions in the genome assembly and physical chromosomes, we mapped selected satDNAs and multigene families in H. illucens stocks from Brazil and Spain. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from an adult male from Brazil stock using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI) following the manufacturer’s protocol to be used as template for Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) reactions. A set of primers from selected satDNA sequences were designed in Geneious v11.1.5 (https://www.geneious.com) based on consensus sequences of the selected satDNAs and 5S rDNA, and used to amplify these sequences from male gDNA by PCR (Table S2). Additionally, 18S rDNA was amplified from the noctuid moth Helicoverpa zea gDNA using non-species-specific primers described by [107]. PCR reactions were carried out in a final volume of 10 μL, containing 100 ng/μL gDNA, 2 mM of each primer, 1 × PCR buffer, 0.2 mM MgCl₂, 0.16 mM dNTPs, and 1 U Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA). Cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 80 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Amplicons generated by PCR were subsequently labeled either with digoxigenin-11-dUTP (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) or biotin-14-dATP (Invitrogen) by nick translation, while 18S and 5S rDNAs were labeled with digoxigenin-11-dUTP by PCR. These repetitive DNA probes were mapped to chromosomes using Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH) following the protocol of [108]. Digoxigenin-labeled probes were detected with anti-digoxigenin-Rhodamine (Roche), and biotin-labeled probes with Alexa Fluor-488 (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA). Slides were counterstained with DAPI and mounted in VECTASHIELD (Vector, Burlingame, CA, USA). Hybridized chromosomes were observed and imaged using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DP70 digital camera and appropriate filter sets (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany). Images were merged, pseudocolored, and adjusted for brightness and contrast using Adobe Photoshop® CS6. For each FISH experiment, at least five metaphases per individual were analyzed to determine the chromosomal positions of the probes, with three individuals examined per stock.
