A Mechanism Study of Delayed Loss Spikes in Batch-Normalized Linear Models
Peifeng Gao, Wenyi Fang, Yang Zheng, Difan Zou

Abstract
Delayed loss spikes have been reported in neural-network training, but existing theory mainly explains earlier non-monotone behavior caused by overly large fixed learning rates. We study one stylized hypothesis: normalization can postpone instability by gradually increasing the effective learning rate during otherwise stable descent. To test this hypothesis at theorem level, we analyze batch-normalized linear models. Our flagship result concerns whitened square-loss linear regression, where we derive explicit no-rising-edge and delayed-onset conditions, bound the waiting time to directional onset, and show that the rising edge self-stabilizes within finitely many iterations. Combined with a square-loss decomposition, this yields a concrete delayed-spike mechanism in the whitened regime. For logistic regression, under highly restrictive active-margin assumptions, we prove only a supporting finite-horizon directional precursor in a knife-edge regime, with an optional appendix-only loss lower bound under an extra non-degeneracy condition. The paper should therefore be read as a stylized mechanism study rather than a general explanation of neural-network loss spikes. Within that scope, the results isolate one concrete delayed-instability pathway induced by batch normalization.

Introduction

Delayed loss spikes have been reported in large-scale neural-network training; see, for example,
39
,
11
, and
24
. These events can require many additional iterations to recover and, in severe cases, are followed by collapse or restart from an earlier checkpoint. Recent mitigation-oriented work, including
20
and
33
, further underscores that spike suppression remains a practical concern in modern large-model pretraining. We use these papers only as operational motivation, not as direct theoretical predecessors and not as empirical validation of the mechanism analyzed below. The goal of this paper is narrower: to test, in a tractable normalized linear setting, whether batch normalization can already create one delayed-instability pathway.

One influential line of work is the Edge of Stability (EoS) phenomenon
(
12
)
, which studies optimization trajectories that remain stable even when the learning rate exceeds classical local-smoothness thresholds. In this regime, the loss can oscillate while still decreasing on longer timescales. Subsequent theory explains such behavior for carefully chosen scalar objectives
(
40
;
19
)
or under restrictive structural assumptions
(
2
;
22
)
. These results provide important insight into non-monotone training dynamics, but they do not explain the delayed spike pattern reported in large-scale practice, where instability often appears only after the loss has already become relatively small.

A more directly related line of work studies gradient descent with learning rates beyond inverse smoothness in simple linear or neural-network models
(
37
;
3
;
36
;
21
)
. These results show that stable, and sometimes accelerated, convergence can still occur. However, the instability predicted there typically appears near the beginning of training; see, in particular,
37
. By contrast, the empirical spikes that motivate this paper can emerge much later, after a long period of apparently stable descent. Existing theory in this direction also does not identify explicit trigger conditions for delayed spikes or characterize their subsequent shape.

Our starting point is the empirical observation that many reported loss spikes arise in models equipped with normalization layers, such as weight normalization
(
31
)
, batch normalization
(
17
)
, and layer normalization
(
6
)
. At the same time, prior theory shows that normalization can induce effective learning-rate auto-tuning or, more broadly, scale-adaptive dynamics; see
4
,
16
, and
25
. This suggests a plausible mechanism for delayed instability: normalization can stabilize early training while gradually amplifying the effective learning rate until directional instability is triggered.

To investigate this mechanism, we study the simplest setting in which it can be analyzed cleanly: linear models with batch normalization trained by full-batch gradient descent. The scope of our results is intentionally asymmetric. Our main theorem line treats whitened square-loss linear regression and gives a comparatively strong characterization of the delayed-instability mechanism, including explicit no-rising-edge and delayed-onset conditions, waiting-time control, and finite-time self-stabilization of the rising edge. Through the square-loss decomposition in Lemma
1
, this yields a concrete delayed-spike interpretation in the whitened regime. The logistic-regression analysis is narrower: under additional restrictive active-margin assumptions, it proves only a finite-horizon directional precursor, with an optional appendix-only non-degeneracy condition used solely for a loss lower bound. The paper should therefore be read as a mechanism study with one flagship theorem regime and one supporting extension. Relative to prior theory, the closest formal comparator for the flagship linear result is
4
, whose theorem is broader on generic batch-normalized convergence whereas ours is narrower but more explicit on delayed onset and self-stabilization. By contrast,
37
mainly provides a mechanism contrast because its instability is early and fixed-scale, while
9
provides a scope contrast because our logistic result does not strengthen the asymptotic max-margin guarantee.

The main contributions are as follows:

•

For whitened batch-normalized linear regression, we identify explicit sufficient conditions for no-rising-edge and delayed-onset regimes, bound the waiting time and duration of the
Rising Edge
, and show that the instability self-stabilizes because directional divergence drives the effective learning rate back down. Via Lemma
1
, this yields a concrete delayed-spike mechanism in the whitened square-loss setting.

•

We derive a scale-invariant directional convergence/divergence lemma for normalized parameterizations. This is the common technical bridge in both analyses, but the resulting theorem payloads remain asymmetric: a full delayed-onset/self-stabilization theorem line in the whitened linear regime and only a supporting finite-horizon precursor statement in logistic regression.

•

For batch-normalized logistic regression, we do not prove a full spike theorem. Instead, under highly restrictive active-margin assumptions, we characterize a supporting finite-horizon directional precursor: the directional error first contracts to a small threshold, and on a later positive-alignment branch there is an explicit exit threshold beyond which the directional rising edge cannot persist. This identifies how margin, conditioning, learning rate, and initialization interact in that narrow stylized regime.

Scope and limitations.

The strongest theorem in this paper is the whitened square-loss linear-regression result, which gives a full directional delayed-onset/self-stabilization picture in a deliberately stylized regime together with a square-loss spike interpretation. The logistic-regression analysis is narrower: the active-margin assumption enforces a knife-edge max-margin geometry, and Assumption
4
appears only in the appendix as an optional bridge from direction to a logistic-loss lower bound. The numerical section is included only as a qualitative mechanism illustration, not as comprehensive validation in modern nonlinear architectures. Section
3
introduces the notation, Sections
4
and
5
present the main theorem line and proof intuition, and the appendices contain the full technical details.

Problem Setup

Because we study both linear regression and logistic regression, we first introduce a unified notation.
Let
n

Dataset.

The training dataset is
S=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the feature vector and

We use the following matrix notation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n};\ \mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1};

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\text{diag}\left(\mathbf{y}\right);\ \mathbf{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{1}_{n},

where
\mathbf{1}_{n}
denotes the
n

We also use the inner product and norm induced by
\mathbf{\Sigma}
:
for any
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{b};\ \|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}=\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{a}.

Unless marked by the subscript
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, the directional quantities used for geometric intuition in the main text are Euclidean. We introduce
\mathbf{\Sigma}
-weighted variants only when the covariance geometry is essential to a bound.

Linear Model and Risk.

Following
9
, we consider the batch-normalized linear model

\displaystyle\text{logit}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)=\alpha\cdot\frac{\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{w}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}},\ \mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathbf{w}
is the linear parameter and
\alpha
is the batch-normalization scale.
We use matrix notation throughout the analysis. For the logits of all samples, we write

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\text{logit}\left(\mathbf{x}_{1};\mathbf{w},\alpha\right),\cdots,\text{logit}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n};\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)\end{array}\right]^{T}=\alpha\cdot\frac{\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

We consider losses
\ell\in\{\ell_{log},\ell_{squ}\}
, where

\ell_{log}\left(\cdot\right):=\log\left(1+\exp\left(-(\cdot)\right)\right)
.
Since the labels take values in
\{1,-1\}
, we define

\ell_{squ}\left(\cdot\right):={(1-(\cdot))^{2}}/{2}

so that the same notation covers both logistic regression and a square-loss linear surrogate. We write
\boldsymbol{\ell}\left(\cdot\right)
for the element-wise version of the loss. The empirical risk is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\ell}\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right),

Gradient Descent.

We study full-batch gradient descent with separate step sizes for
\mathbf{w}
and
\alpha
. The corresponding gradients are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)=\frac{\alpha}{n\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\alpha\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right);

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathbf{\alpha}}\left(\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\alpha\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\ell^{\prime}}
denotes the vector of element-wise derivatives of
\ell
.
We study the parameter sequence
(\mathbf{w}_{t},\alpha_{t})
generated by

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t};\ \ \alpha_{t+1}\leftarrow\alpha_{t}-\eta_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{t}}{\partial\alpha},

(1)

starting from
(\mathbf{w}_{0},\alpha_{0})
, where
\mathcal{R}_{t}:=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t},\alpha_{t}\right)
for brevity. Since
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}
always lies in
\text{span}\left(\mathbf{X}\right):=\text{span}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}
, only the component of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
inside
\text{span}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)
affects the dynamics. We therefore assume that the initialization lies in
\mathcal{X}:=\text{span}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)\backslash\{0\}

4
Main Results

We begin with the directional quantities that drive the paper’s mechanism. In the whitened linear-regression regime, sharp directional divergence yields explicit no-rising-edge and delayed-onset conditions together with finite-time self-stabilization; Lemma
1
then translates that event into the square-loss spike interpretation emphasized in the introduction. The logistic analysis below is included only to test whether a related directional precursor can still be proved beyond square loss under substantially stronger assumptions. Here, “direction” refers to the reference direction that gradient descent should ultimately approach in order to reduce the loss. We denote this direction by
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
: for linear regression it is the least-squares direction, while for logistic regression it is the max-margin SVM direction
(
9
;
37
)
. Roughly speaking, when
\mathbf{w}_{t}
remains aligned with
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
and the scale
\alpha_{t}
does not vary too abruptly, the loss continues to decrease. A rapid directional departure from
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
can instead trigger a sudden loss increase. To quantify this effect, we introduce
\rho_{t}
and
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{t}:=\rho\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}\right):=\frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}},\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|};\quad\rho_{t}^{\perp}:=\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}\right):=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\rho_{t}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\right\|.

The left panel of Figure
1
illustrates that
\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)
is the component of
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
along
\mathbf{w}
, whereas
\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)
is the orthogonal residual. By the Pythagorean theorem,

\left(\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right)^{2}+\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)^{2}=\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}
for every
\mathbf{w}
. In particular,
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
is the tangent of the angle between
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
, so the convergence of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
to
0
means that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
becomes increasingly aligned with the reference direction. We use these quantities first in linear regression and then, in a weaker form, in logistic regression. For ease of exposition, we introduce two system states according to the trend of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
, as illustrated on the right of Figure
1
:

•

Rising Edge
: the time span over which
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
increases;

•

Falling Edge
: the time span over which
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
decreases.

Figure 1:
Left
:
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
and
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
measure the directional deviation between
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{t}
.
Right
: an example trajectory of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
, where the
Rising Edge
and
Falling Edge
are defined by its monotonic trend.

Our goal is to characterize this directional onset/self-stabilization mechanism in detail, including its trigger condition and the durations of the rising and falling phases, and then interpret it in square loss through Lemma
1
.

4.1
Whitened Square-Loss Linear Regression

We begin with whitened square-loss linear regression because it is the paper’s strongest and most complete theorem regime. The reader can refer to Appendix
9
for full proofs. To simplify the analysis, we consider whitened data, so that the empirical covariance satisfies
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
. Relative to large-step convergence theory for normalized objectives and early-oscillation results without normalization, the present whitened setting is narrower but yields a more explicit delayed-instability package: no-rising-edge and delayed-onset conditions, a waiting-time bound, and finite-time self-stabilization of the rising edge. We start with a decomposition of the risk into directional components.

Lemma 1

(Decomposition of Mean Square Loss)
.

Let
\ell
be
\ell_{squ}
and let
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
be the least squares solution,
that is,
\hat{\mathbf{w}}:=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Suppose
\mathbf{\Sigma}=I
.
Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{w},\alpha}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=1-\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2};\quad\mathcal{R}_{t}=(\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t})^{2}+(\rho_{t}^{\perp})^{2}+1-\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2}.

This lemma reduces the square-loss question to the directional growth of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
, or equivalently of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
. It also shows that the square loss is already close to its optimum when both
|\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t}|
and
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
are small. We therefore analyze a near-alignment regime, encoded below by
\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
and
0<\alpha_{t_{0}}<\rho_{t_{0}}
, and ask when that regime can still transition into a delayed rising edge.

Theorem 2

(Delayed Onset of the Rising Edge)
.

Let
\ell=\ell_{squ}
be the square loss and
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Suppose
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
.
Consider the gradient descent (
1
)
for
t>t_{0}
with
\eta_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
, where
t_{0}
is such that

\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
and
0<\alpha_{t_{0}}<\rho_{t_{0}}
. The following results hold:

1.

Condition of No Rising Edge.

If
\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}<\frac{2}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}
, then no rising edge occurs for any
t\geq t_{0}
.

2.

Condition of Delayed Onset.

Let
k_{t_{0}}:=\alpha_{t_{0}}/\rho_{t_{0}}
and define

\displaystyle C_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle:=\min\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{t_{0}}\rho_{t_{0}}},\frac{3}{16\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}\frac{\eta_{\alpha}}{e^{2}\left(1-k_{t_{0}}\right)}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta T_{0}

\displaystyle:=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\eta_{\alpha}}\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{\alpha}\left(1-k_{t_{0}}\right)\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}{4\eta\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}\left(\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\right)^{2}}\right)+1\right\rfloor.

If
\eta
satisfies
\frac{8}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}<\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}\leq C_{t_{0}}
, then a rising edge starts within at most
\Delta T_{0}
iterations.
Formally speaking, there exists
t_{1}\in(t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta T_{0}]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t+1}}\leq\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}}\ \ \forall t\in[t_{0},t_{1})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{\rho_{t_{1}+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{1}+1}}\geq\frac{\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{1}}}.

Theorem
2
has three immediate implications:

•

A delayed rising edge occurs when the effective learning rate
\eta/\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}
exceeds a threshold; otherwise the directional ratio continues to decrease.

•

The waiting time scales as
-\log(\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}})
, so delayed onset is more pronounced when the iterate is already better aligned with
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
. In the whitened square-loss model, Lemma
1
shows that such near alignment is compatible with already-improved loss once
\alpha_{t_{0}}
tracks
\rho_{t_{0}}
, even though the theorem itself is stated in geometric variables.

•

The gap between the sufficient no-rising-edge threshold
2/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}
and the sufficient delayed-onset threshold
8/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}
is a proof-level gap rather than a separate claimed phase transition.

Given the delayed-onset condition in Theorem
2
, we next characterize the subsequent rising edge for iterations
t\geq t_{1}
.

Theorem 3

(Finite-Time Self-Stabilization of the Rising Edge)
.

Under the delayed-onset condition in Theorem
2
, starting from the onset time
t_{1}
, the Rising Edge lasts for at most

\displaystyle\Delta T_{1}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\frac{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{4}}{\alpha_{t_{1}}^{2}}\left(1/(\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp})^{2}-1/\rho_{t_{1}}^{2}\right)^{2}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\frac{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\rho_{t_{1}}^{2}}\left(\rho_{t_{1}}/\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}-\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\rceil

iterations and turn to the Falling Edge.
Specifically, there exists a
t_{2}\in(t_{1},t_{1}+\Delta T_{1}]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t+1}}\geq\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}}\ \ \forall t\in[t_{1},t_{2})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{\rho_{t_{2}+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{2}+1}}\leq\frac{\rho_{t_{2}}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{2}}},

Moreover, define a time
\phi\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
as the first iteration when
\alpha_{t}

catches up with
\rho_{t}
, i.e., the time such that

\alpha_{t}\leq\rho_{t}\ \forall t\in[t_{1},\phi]
and
\alpha_{t}\geq\rho_{t}\ \forall t\in(\phi,t_{2})
.
Then, it holds that:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[t_{1},\phi],\ \ (\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t})^{2}\leq 1-\frac{2\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}\alpha_{t_{1}}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}\sqrt{t-t_{1}};\quad\forall t\in(\phi,t_{2}],\ \ (\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t})^{2}\leq 1-\frac{2\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|}\sqrt{t-\phi}.

Theorem
3
shows that the rising phase terminates after finitely many iterations and then returns to a falling phase, so the growth of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
remains bounded. In particular, the mechanism self-stabilizes within at most
\Delta T_{1}
iterations and the peak value of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
never exceeds
1
. As discussed further in Section
5.2
, this happens because directional divergence activates a BN-induced negative-feedback adjustment that rapidly decreases the effective learning rate during the
Rising Edge
; see Figure
2
.

4.2
A Supporting Logistic-Regression Analysis

We now turn to a narrower logistic-regression analysis. This subsection records one stylized finite-horizon directional precursor under stronger assumptions. The reader can refer to Appendix
10
for the corresponding proofs. Following
9
, we consider the overparameterized setting, where the number of features exceeds the number of training examples.

Assumption 1

(Overparameterization)
.

Assume
n<d
and
\text{rank}\{\mathbf{X}\}=n
.

Assumption 2

(Logistic setup)
.

The dataset is linearly separable, so the hard-margin SVM problem in Definition
1
is feasible. In addition, the initialization satisfies
\mathbf{w}_{0}\in\text{span}(\mathbf{X})
.

Under Assumption
2
, each gradient update remains in
\text{span}(\mathbf{X})
, so
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\text{span}(\mathbf{X})
for all
t\geq 0
.
We now introduce the Support Vector Machine (SVM) solution, which serves as the
reference convergence direction of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
in the gradient descent dynamics.

Definition 1

(Support Vector Machine solution)
.

Let
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
be the SVM solution and define margin as
\gamma:=1/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}:=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2},\ \text{s.t.}\quad y_{i}\cdot\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{w}\rangle\geq 1,\ i\in[n].

Assumption 3

(Active-margin data)
.

The SVM solution in Definition
1
satisfies

y_{i}\cdot\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\rangle=1,\qquad\forall i\in[n].

This is a highly restrictive special-case assumption: every training sample lies exactly on the max-margin boundary, so all examples are support vectors. We use it only to obtain a clean finite-horizon directional argument in the logistic analysis.
Under Assumption
2
,
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\text{span}(\mathbf{X})
for all
t
, so we also use the extremal eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
restricted to

\mathcal{X}:=\text{span}(\mathbf{X})\backslash\{0\},

namely

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}}.

The next lemma upper bounds the risk in terms of the parameter direction and scale.

Lemma 4

(Upper Bound of Logistic Loss)
.

Let
\ell
be
\ell_{log}
and
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
be the SVM solution as presented in Definition
1
.
Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2

Consider the gradient descent (
1
),
for all
t\geq 0
, if
\rho_{t}>0
and
\alpha_{t}>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}\leq\ell\left(\alpha_{t}\right)+\alpha_{t}\left|\ell^{\prime}\left(\left[1-C_{0}\gamma\cdot\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right]\alpha_{t}\right)\right|\cdot C_{0}\gamma\cdot\rho_{t}^{\perp},

where

Lemma

A converse bridge is available in the appendix under an additional non-degeneracy condition, but the main theorem below remains purely directional. Proof Overview

We first explain, at a high level, how batch normalization reshapes the direction dynamics of gradient descent. We then specialize this mechanism to the whitened square-loss linear-regression analysis and the narrower logistic-regression analysis. The goal of this section is a roadmap rather than a derivation: it tells the reader which lemma drives which transition, while the appendix carries the constant tracking and proof details. Full proofs are deferred to Appendix

5.1
Directional Convergence Induced by BN

We begin at a level that is agnostic to the specific task and objective, and focus only on the direction dynamics induced by normalization.
The following lemma describes when a normalized model moves toward or away from a reference direction during gradient descent.

Lemma 6

(Directional Convergence and Divergence)
.

Suppose there exists a reference direction
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
.
Consider gradient descent (
1
) on an objective function

\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{w},\alpha\right)
, where
\mathbf{w}

is parameterized by normalization and
\alpha
represents the scaling factor of the normalization.
Assume further that the objective is scale-invariant in
\mathbf{w}
, namely

\mathcal{R}(k\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{w},\alpha),\qquad\forall k>0.

We have the following direction convergence condition:
if there exists a
t\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\rho_{t}>0\text{ and }\ \frac{\eta{\rho}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq-2\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}},\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rangle,

it holds that
\left({\rho}_{t+1}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\leq\left({\rho}_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}

and the following direction divergence condition:
if there exists a
t\geq 0

\begin{aligned} 0<\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}\leq 1,\ \ \alpha_{t}>0\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|\geq\frac{2{\rho}_{t}{\rho}_{t}^{\perp}}{{\rho}_{t}^{2}-\left({\rho}_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}}\end{aligned},

it holds that
\left({\rho}_{t+1}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\geq\left({\rho}_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}
.

Remark 1
.

Recall that smaller
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
means that the direction of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is closer to
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
. The lemma therefore gives generic convergence and divergence conditions for the direction dynamics of any scale-invariant normalized model. It is not restricted to batch normalization, but it does rely essentially on the scale-invariance assumption stated above. Once that assumption holds, the conclusion does not depend on the particular training objective or the specific choice of reference direction.

Lemma
6
is one of the main technical tools of the paper; its proof is given in Appendix
8
. In the next two subsections, we use it to explain how normalization-induced directional divergence can generate delayed instability after a sustained well-aligned phase.

For linear regression, the square-loss gradient admits an especially transparent reformulation in terms of

7
Conclusion

This paper isolates a concrete delayed-instability mechanism in batch-normalized linear models. In the whitened square-loss regime, batch normalization can induce a delayed rising edge through directional divergence, and that rising edge self-stabilizes rather than causing immediate runaway behavior. Via the square-loss decomposition, this yields the paper’s delayed-spike interpretation in the whitened regime. The logistic-regression analysis is weaker: under additional restrictive structural assumptions, it yields only a finite-horizon directional precursor together with an explicit exit threshold on a positive-alignment branch. Relative to prior work on batch-normalization auto-rate tuning, asymptotic implicit bias, and large-step logistic-regression oscillations, our contribution is therefore not broader generality but a sharper mechanism-level picture in a tractable normalized setting. The strongest theorem here relies essentially on the whitened linear regime, and it remains open which parts of that picture survive without whitening or in nonlinear architectures.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}\left(-\eta\left(\rho_{t}^{2}-\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|^{2}+2\rho_{t}^{\perp}\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\|\left(-\eta\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|\left(\rho_{t}^{2}-\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)+2\rho_{t}^{\perp}\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\right)

Now, let’s examine the signs within the parentheses.
Recall
0<\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}\leq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle-\eta\left(\rho_{t}^{2}-\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq 0

Hence the relevant case here is
0<\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}\leq 1
.
Since
1\geq\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}>0
,
it holds that
{\rho}_{t}^{2}-\left({\rho}_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}>0
,
and in particular
\rho_{t}>0
. Therefore, we can verify

\displaystyle\frac{\eta\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\|}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\geq\frac{2{\rho}_{t}{\rho}_{t}^{\perp}}{{\rho}_{t}^{2}-\left({\rho}_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}}

ensures that

\begin{aligned} -\eta\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|\left(\rho_{t}^{2}-\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)+2\rho_{t}^{\perp}\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\leq 0\end{aligned}.

This means
\rho_{t+1}^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2}\leq 0
.
∎

Detailed Proof for Linear Regression

Lemma
1
(Decomposition of Mean Square Loss)
.

Let
\ell=\ell_{squ}
,
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
.
Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t})^{2}+(\rho_{t}^{\perp})^{2}+1-\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2}

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=\frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}},\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}};\quad\rho_{t}^{\perp}=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\rho_{t}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left\|\alpha_{t}\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{2}+1-\frac{2\alpha_{t}}{n}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{1}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{2}+1-2\alpha_{t}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{u}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{2}+1-2\alpha_{t}\frac{\langle\mathbf{w}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{2}+1-2\alpha_{t}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t})^{2}+1-\rho_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t})^{2}+\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2}+1-\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{t}-\rho_{t})^{2}+\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}+1-\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}

Note that in
\mathbf{\Sigma}
-inner product space, Pythagorean theorem also holds,
which means
\rho_{t}^{2}+\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}=\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}
. It is easy to verify that a solution is reached when
\mathbf{w}
is colinear with
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
, which indicates that
\rho_{t}=\alpha_{t}=\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
and
\rho_{t}^{\perp}=0
. Therefore, we have
\inf_{\mathbf{w},\alpha}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=1-\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
7
(The Dynamics of BN Linear Regression)
.

Let
\ell=\ell_{squ}
,
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
.
Consider the gradient descent (
1
),
it holds that

\displaystyle(1).\frac{\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t+1}}=\frac{|\hat{\eta}_{t}-1|}{1+\hat{\eta}_{t}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}}\right)^{2}}\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}};\quad

\displaystyle(2).\alpha_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}-\alpha_{t}\right);\quad

\displaystyle(3).\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2},

where
\hat{\eta}_{t}
is effective learning rate, defined as
\hat{\eta}_{t}:={\eta\alpha_{t}\rho_{t}}/{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}
.

Proof.

(1).

We first reformulate the gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}
:

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{n\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\alpha\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{n\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{n}-\alpha\cdot\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}^{T}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}-\frac{\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}-\frac{\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}}\mathbf{w}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\mathbf{w}_{t}\right)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\|=\frac{|\alpha_{t}|}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\rho_{t}^{\perp};

\displaystyle-\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\mathbf{w}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}.

Next, We decompose
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
orthogonally into two components: one in the
\text{span}\left\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\}
and the other
in a direction orthogonal to it:

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|};\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}=\left(\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\rho_{t}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right)/\rho_{t}^{\perp}.

Recall the gradient descent update:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}

We have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{t+1},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t},\rho_{t}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\rho_{t}^{\perp}\mathbf{e}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|-\eta\rho_{t}^{\perp}\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|-\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{w}}-\rho_{t}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|-\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|+\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}

and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|^{2}-2\eta\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{t},\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2},

where we apply
\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{t},\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rangle=0
and

\left\langle\mathbf{e}_{1},\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rangle=0
.
Then, by definition of
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
and
\rho_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}-\rho_{t+1}^{2}}{\rho_{t+1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\|^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}-\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{t+1},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{t+1},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\rho_{t}^{2}+\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)-\left(\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|+\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|+\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(\frac{\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\rho_{t}^{2}+\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}-2\eta\alpha_{t}\rho_{t}\right)}{\left(\rho_{t}\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|+\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(\hat{\eta}_{t}-1\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\hat{\eta}_{t}\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}}

(2).

Then we consider the update of
\alpha_{t}
, which is much simpler:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)^{T}\left(\alpha_{t}\cdot\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\eta_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{\alpha}}=\alpha_{t}-\eta_{\alpha}\left(\alpha_{t}-\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\right)=\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}-\alpha_{t}\right)

∎

Theorem
2
(Delayed Onset of the Rising Edge)
.

Let
\ell=\ell_{squ}
,
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Suppose
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
.
Consider the gradient descent (
1
)
for
t>t_{0}
with
\eta_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
, where
t_{0}
is such that

\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
and
0<\alpha_{t_{0}}<\rho_{t_{0}}
. The following results hold:

1.

Condition of No Rising Edge.

If
\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}<\frac{2}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}
, there shall be no rising edge when
t\geq t_{0}
.

2.

Condition of Delayed Onset.

If
\eta
satisfies
\frac{8}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}<\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}\leq C
,
the dynamics shall stay in Falling Edge for at most
\Delta T_{0}
iterations,
and then enters Rising Edge state.
Formally speaking, there exists
t_{1}\in(t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta T_{0}]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t+1}}\leq\frac{\rho_{t}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t}}\ \ \forall t\in[t_{0},t_{1}),\ \ \frac{\rho_{t_{1}+1}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{1}+1}}\geq\frac{\rho_{t_{1}}^{\perp}}{\rho_{t_{1}}}

where

\displaystyle C=\min\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{t_{0}}\rho_{t_{0}}},\frac{3}{16\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}\frac{\eta_{\alpha}}{e^{2}\left(1-\alpha_{t_{0}}/\rho_{t_{0}}\right)}\right);

\displaystyle\Delta T_{0}\leq\left\lfloor\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{\alpha}\eta\left(1-k\right)\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}{4\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}\left(\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\right)^{2}}\right)/\eta_{\alpha}+1\right\rfloor.

Proof.

We first prove the no-rising-edge condition, then the delayed-onset condition.

Condition of No Rising Edge

We first prove
\alpha_{t}\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|\ \forall t\geq t_{0}
by induction.
When
t=t_{0}
, it holds that
\alpha_{t}\leq\rho_{t}\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
.
Then we assume
\alpha_{t}\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
for
t\geq t_{0}
and consider
\alpha_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}-\alpha_{t}\right)=\left(1-\eta_{\alpha}\right)\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\rho_{t}

Since
\eta_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
, we observe that
\alpha_{t+1}
is, in fact, the convex combination of

\rho_{t}
and
\alpha_{t}
, both of which are smaller than
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
.
Therefore,
\alpha_{t+1}\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|
.
By Lemma
7
, if

\hat{\eta}_{t}
is always smaller than
\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t})^{2}}
,
the rising edge can never start. We verify the value of
\hat{\eta}_{t}
.
For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}\rho_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\eta\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\eta\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}\leq 2\leq\frac{2}{1-\left(\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}\right)^{2}},

where the second inequality is by
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\geq\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|\ \forall t_{0}\leq t

and the third inequality is by the upper bound of
\eta
.

Condition of Delayed Onset.

By the upper bound of
\eta
, we can verify that following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\alpha_{t_{0}}\rho_{t_{0}}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{1-\left(\rho_{t_{0}}^{\perp}/\rho_{t_{0}}\right)^{2}},

which ensures that
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
is decreasing when
t=t_{0}
.

Without loss of generality, we treat the time interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta T_{0})
as
[0,\Delta T_{0})
by defining

t\leftarrow t-t_{0}
. This index shift does not affect the correctness of the proof.
By the upper bound of
\eta
in condition (1), we have
\hat{\eta}_{0}\leq\frac{2}{1-\left({\rho_{0}^{\perp}}/{\rho_{0}}\right)^{2}}
,
which guarantees that dynamics is still in
Falling Edge
state when
t=0
.
Next, we assume that
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
will be decreasing for all
t\geq 0
, then derive a contradiction to identify the delayed onset of the rising edge.
By this assumption, we have
\forall t\geq 0
,
\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}\leq\rho_{t}^{\perp}
and
\rho_{t+1}\geq\rho_{t}
.
By the update of
\alpha_{t}
and
\eta_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}-\alpha_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{t}+\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t}\right)

Take the negative of both sides and add
\rho_{0}
to obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t}-\eta_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\eta_{\alpha}\right)\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\eta_{\alpha})\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\eta_{\alpha}(t+1))\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)

Then we obtain the lower bound of
\alpha_{t}
:

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\ \ \alpha_{t}\geq\rho_{0}-e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)

Next, we calculate the upper bound of
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
:

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\ \ \|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\frac{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}\left(\rho_{\tau}^{\perp}\right)^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left(\rho_{\tau}^{\perp}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t

Now, we can lower bound the effective learning rate
\hat{\eta}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\ \ \hat{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}\rho_{t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}}\geq\frac{\eta\alpha_{t}\rho_{0}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t}\geq\frac{\eta\rho_{0}^{2}-\eta\rho_{0}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t}

By Lemma.
7
, if
\hat{\eta}_{t}
is larger than
\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t})^{2}}
,

\rho_{t+1}^{\perp}/\rho_{t+1}
would be larger than
\rho_{t}^{\perp}/\rho_{t}
, which means that the delayed rising edge has started.
We now investigate whether the lower bound of
\hat{\eta}_{t}
can exceed the threshold at some
t
.
Therefore, according to the Lemma.
7
, assuming
\rho_{t}^{\perp}
is always decreasing leads to a contradiction,
indicating the presence of a delayed onset event.
We investigate if there exists a
t>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\eta\rho_{0}^{2}-\eta\rho_{0}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t}\geq\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{t}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{t}^{2}}

(3)

Since
\rho_{0}^{\perp}\geq\rho_{t}^{\perp}
, we have

\displaystyle(\ref{eq:linear_spike_trigger_threshold})\ \boldsymbol{\Leftarrow}\ \frac{\eta\rho_{0}^{2}-\eta\rho_{0}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t}\geq\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}

(4)

Rearrange the inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\eta\rho_{0}^{2}-\frac{2\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}>\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}\right)^{2}t+\eta\rho_{0}\left(\rho_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}

Devide
\rho_{0}^{2}
on both side to obtain

\displaystyle\eta-\frac{2\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}}

\displaystyle>\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0}\right)^{2}t+\eta\left(1-\alpha_{0}/\rho_{0}\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0}\right)^{2}t+\eta\left(1-k\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}

where
k=\alpha_{0}/\rho_{0}\in(0,1)
.
We next study the left-hand side of (
5
):

\displaystyle\text{LHS of }(\ref{eq:aaa})

\displaystyle=\eta-\frac{2\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\eta-2\frac{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}\frac{1+(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0})^{2}}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\eta-\frac{4\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\eta

Then we look at the right hand side of the (
5
):

\displaystyle\text{RHS of }(\ref{eq:aaa})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0}\right)^{2}}{1-(\rho_{0}^{\perp})^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}}\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}t+\eta\left(1-k\right)e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{4\frac{\eta^{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{0}\|^{2}}\left(\rho_{0}^{\perp}/\rho_{0}\right)^{2}}_{B}t+\underbrace{\eta\left(1-k\right)}_{A}e^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}

We discuss the minimum of the function
f(t;a,b)=Ae^{-\eta_{\alpha}t}+Bt
.
Its minimum is achieved by
t_{\min}=\ln(A\eta_{\alpha}/B)/\eta_{\alpha}
.
As
t_{\min}
is unlikely to be an integer, we calculate
f(t)
at the integers closest to
t_{\min}
.
Since
f(t)
is monotonically decreasing in
t<t_{\min}
. We have

\displaystyle f(\lfloor t_{\min}+1\rfloor)\leq f(t_{\min}+1)

\displaystyle=f(t_{\min})+f(t_{\min}+1)-f(t_{\min})

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}-\cos^{2}\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|^{2}\gamma^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{\max}\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)\left(\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)+\cos\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\lambda_{\max}\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right),

where the second inequality is by the range of
\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)
. By our definition,

\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)\in[0,\pi]
.
Next, we apply
\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)=\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)/\hat{\mathbf{w}}

and

\cos\angle\left(\mathbf{w},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right)=\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)/\hat{\mathbf{w}}

to give

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}-\left(\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right)^{2}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{2}\lambda_{\max}\gamma\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\cdot\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)

Then we bound
\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right|
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}-\left(\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right)^{2}\right|}{\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}+\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}-\left(\gamma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\right)^{2}\right|/\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\lambda_{\max}\gamma\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\cdot\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)/\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

(2).

For the second result, by Cauchy inequality, for any
i\in[n]
, it holds that

\begin{aligned} y_{i}\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle=y_{i}{\tilde{\rho}}(\mathbf{w}){\tilde{\rho}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+y_{i}\left\langle\mathcal{P}^{\perp}(\mathbf{w}),\mathcal{P}^{\perp}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\rangle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\leq{\tilde{\rho}}(\mathbf{w})\gamma+\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\cdot{\tilde{\rho}}^{\perp}(\mathbf{w})\\
\geq{\tilde{\rho}}(\mathbf{w})\gamma-\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\cdot{\tilde{\rho}}^{\perp}(\mathbf{w})\\
\end{array}\right.\end{aligned},

we have

\displaystyle\left|y_{i}\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle-{\tilde{\rho}}(\mathbf{w})\gamma\right|\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\cdot{\tilde{\rho}}^{\perp}(\mathbf{w})\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}\cdot{\tilde{\rho}}^{\perp}(\mathbf{w})

Recall that

\tilde{\rho}^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\cdot\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|=\rho^{\perp}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\cdot\|\mathbf{w}\|
and

\tilde{\rho}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\cdot\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|=\rho\left(\mathbf{w}\right)\cdot\|\mathbf{w}\|
, we have the second result.
∎
