Non‐pharmacological interventions for anxiety and depression in Parkinson's disease
Authors: Tengfei Li, Jiyuan Shi, Qingyong Zheng, Jian-Guo Xu, Mingyue Zhang, Yalin Ren, BoWa Zhang, Zheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of non‐pharmacological interventions for reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression in individuals with Parkinson’s disease.

Background

Description of the condition

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide [1]. By 2050, an estimated 25.2 million people are expected to be living with PD globally, representing a 112% increase from 2021 [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), neurodegenerative diseases—including PD and Alzheimer’s disease—are projected to become the second leading cause of death worldwide by 2040, surpassing cancer‐related mortality [3].

The clinical hallmark of PD is a motor syndrome characterized by resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability [4]. PD is also associated with numerous non‐motor symptoms—such as neuropsychiatric disturbances, cognitive impairment, sleep disorders, and autonomic dysfunction—that severely impair patients’ overall functioning [5]. Anxiety and depression, which frequently co‐occur, affect approximately 40% to 50% of individuals with PD [6]. These conditions are associated with increased care dependency, impaired occupational and social functioning, accelerated physical and cognitive decline, elevated dementia risk, and higher mortality rates [7, 8].

Anxiety and depression in PD may result from PD‐related abnormalities in brain circuits that depend on dopamine, serotonin, and noradrenaline. These abnormalities particularly involve the limbic cortico‐striato‐thalamo‐cortical pathway and the 'fear' circuit, in which the amygdala plays a central role [9, 10].

Research indicates that people with late‐onset PD experience milder depressive symptoms compared to those with early‐onset PD [11]. Females with PD have a younger mean age at onset and more severe anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to males with PD [11]. Depressive and anxiety symptoms increase disproportionately with age in women compared with men [12]. Regarding intervention characteristics, duration and intensity may modulate effectiveness [13]. In addition, medication‐related factors may influence intervention outcomes [14].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

The clinical management of anxiety and depression in PD currently involves both pharmacological and non‐pharmacological approaches. However, pharmacological treatments are often poorly tolerated, may demonstrate limited efficacy, and can cause undesirable side effects [15]. In addition, their use is complicated by the high prevalence of polypharmacy among people with PD [16]. Non‐pharmacological interventions should therefore be considered as potential alternatives or adjuncts to existing pharmacological and neurosurgical therapies.

A wide range of non‐pharmacological interventions have been employed to alleviate anxiety and depression in individuals with PD. These interventions can be broadly categorized as follows: psychological therapies, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT); non‐invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS); exercise‐based interventions, including aerobic, resistance, and balance training; and complementary therapies, such as tai chi, qigong, yoga, meditation, acupuncture, auricular acupressure, massage, music therapy, bright light therapy (BLT), and dance therapy.

Psychological therapies are considered the gold standard for treating depressive and anxiety disorders. CBT is an umbrella term encompassing a range of therapeutic approaches based on cognitive and behavioral conditioning theories [17]. The use of CBT to treat depression and anxiety in individuals with PD has become increasingly widespread in clinical practice. A previous systematic review demonstrated that CBT significantly alleviates anxiety and depressive symptoms in individuals with PD compared with waiting‐list or treatment‐as‐usual control groups [18]. However, the neural mechanisms underlying CBT’s effects on anxiety and depression in PD remain largely unclear. It has been hypothesized that CBT exerts its therapeutic effects by strengthening brain circuits involved in cognitive control (frontoparietal network), emotion regulation, and response inhibition (cortico‐striato‐thalamo‐cortical circuits) [19].

NIBS techniques, such as rTMS and tDCS, have emerged as promising non‐pharmacological interventions for managing both motor and non‐motor symptoms in PD. Zheng and colleagues reported that NIBS was significantly more effective than sham stimulation or placebo in alleviating depressive symptoms in individuals with PD. Furthermore, rTMS combined with antidepressant therapy was found to be significantly more effective than antidepressant monotherapy in improving both depressive and anxiety symptoms in PD [20]. By targeting specific cortical regions that serve as ‘entry points’ to relevant neural circuits, rTMS and tDCS may normalize cortical excitability, modulate activity in interconnected brain regions, enhance brain network connectivity, and promote neuroplasticity [21, 22]. The therapeutic effects of NIBS on depression and anxiety are thought to result from the modulation of dysfunctional neural circuits involved in mood regulation, anxiety control, and cognitive processing [21, 22].

Exercise‐based interventions, alone or combined with other non‐pharmacological approaches, may effectively reduce depressive symptoms in individuals with PD. Feller and colleagues reported that exercise training produced significant improvements in depressive symptoms compared with usual care [23]. However, evidence supporting the effects of exercise on anxiety in PD is less robust. Ferreira and colleagues found that 24 weeks of resistance training significantly reduced anxiety levels and improved quality of life [24], whereas Wu and colleagues reported that low‐dose, home‐based exercise was not effective in reducing anxiety [25]. Exercise is also hypothesized to influence neurostructural and neurochemical processes in PD. Studies indicate that comorbid depression or anxiety in PD is associated with abnormalities in inflammatory cytokine expression, receptor availability, neurotrophin levels, brain volume and connectivity, and the availability of dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin [26]. It has been hypothesized that exercise may positively modulate these structural and neurochemical processes [26, 27].

Complementary therapies are non‐mainstream practices used alongside conventional medical treatments [28]. Survey‐based studies conducted before 2013 reported that 26% to 76% of individuals with PD used complementary therapies to manage various symptoms across different countries [29]. Li and colleagues found that acupuncture significantly improved depressive symptoms in PD [30]. In contrast, Yang and colleagues reported that tai chi did not significantly improve depressive symptoms in individuals with PD [31]. Kwok and colleagues assessed the effects of yoga on anxiety and depression using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and found that yoga significantly improved both anxiety and depressive symptoms in PD [32]. These improvements may be attributed to an enhanced internal locus of control, reflecting the extent to which patients believe they can influence their own health. Greater engagement in treatment often leads to increased optimism, reduced depressive symptoms, and improved quality of life [33].

BLT is an attractive treatment option as it has few contraindications and side effects. Sun and colleagues indicated that individuals with idiopathic PD who received BLT experienced significant improvements in depressive symptoms and sleep disturbance scores [34]. In PD, circadian rhythms can become disrupted due to multiple factors, which may contribute to both depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances [35]. BLT involves daily, rhythmic exposure to light‐emitting devices, which restores circadian rhythms by stimulating the suprachiasmatic nucleus through retinal melanopsin‐containing cells, thereby reducing depressive symptoms and improving sleep quality [17].

Why it is important to do this review

Most randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared non‐pharmacological interventions with placebo, waiting‐list controls, or usual care [24, 25, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. However, a limited number of RCTs have directly compared two different non‐pharmacological interventions [32, 44, 45, 46, 47]. Consequently, the relative effectiveness of different non‐pharmacological interventions remains unclear. Moreover, previous systematic reviews have assessed the effects of various non‐pharmacological interventions on anxiety and depression in individuals with PD; however, several of these systematic reviews included non‐RCTs or lacked quantitative analyses [48, 49, 50], thereby providing limited high‐quality evidence.

Wang and colleagues conducted a systematic review evaluating the effects of 35 non‐pharmacological interventions on depressive symptoms in individuals with PD [51]. However, the systematic review had several limitations. First, it did not simultaneously assess both anxiety and depression, which often co‐occur in PD. In fact, systematic reviews focusing specifically on anxiety remain scarce, leading to limited evidence regarding the effects of non‐pharmacological interventions on anxiety. Second, it did not include high‐quality RCTs published after April 2022, despite a substantial number of relevant studies being conducted in recent years. Third, from a methodological perspective, the systematic review did not apply the GRADE approach to assess the quality of evidence, limiting the credibility of its conclusions. Finally, the systematic review did not incorporate patient and public involvement, thus lacking consideration of the needs and values of the target population. It is therefore essential to conduct a systematic review and network meta‐analysis to comprehensively summarize the current evidence. This study aims to assess and compare the efficacy and safety of different non‐pharmacological interventions for anxiety and depression in individuals with PD, providing evidence‐based guidance for clinical practice.

Methods

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include parallel, cross‐over, and cluster RCTs that compare any non‐pharmacological intervention with placebo, no treatment, or another non‐pharmacological intervention. We will include cross‐over trials only if they provide an adequate washout period (≥ 2 weeks); otherwise, we will analyze only the data from the first treatment period. We will exclude RCTs that compare different methods of the same intervention.

Types of participants

We will include studies involving participants diagnosed with PD who have anxiety or depression, without restrictions on age, gender, race, nationality, education level, disease stage, or disease duration. The diagnosis of anxiety or depression will include both professional diagnoses made by qualified medical personnel using standardized diagnostic criteria—such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth or Fifth Edition (DSM‐IV/V), or the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD‐11)—and self‐reported cases from participants. We will include studies in which participants are assessed using validated anxiety or depression scales.

Types of interventions

We will include RCTs evaluating non‐pharmacological interventions for anxiety or depression, regardless of intervention frequency, duration, mode, period, format, or setting, compared with placebo, waiting‐list controls, usual care, or other non‐pharmacological interventions. We will consider the following non‐pharmacological interventions: psychological therapies, including CBT; non‐invasive brain stimulation (rTMS and tDCS); exercise therapy, including aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and balance training; and complementary therapies, such as tai chi, qigong, yoga, meditation, acupuncture, auricular pressure, massage, music therapy, BLT, and dance therapy. We have classified the interventions a priori and will adjust this classification if we detect any lack of transitivity.

Outcome measures

We will present results separately for short‐term (≤ 6 months) and long‐term (> 6 months) follow‐up, if data are available. When multiple assessments are reported within this interval, we will use the assessment closest to the end of the intervention.

Measures of treatment effect

Relative treatment effects

For continuous outcomes (e.g. anxiety, depression, and health‐related quality of life), we will calculate the mean difference (MD) for outcomes measured with the same instrument, or the standardized mean difference (SMD) for outcomes measured with different instruments, both with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For dichotomous outcomes (e.g. adverse events), we will calculate the risk ratio (RR) with 95% CIs. Where available, we will refer to published minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for each outcome to interpret clinical relevance. If no established MCID exists, we will use distribution‐based methods (e.g. 0.5*SD) or anchor‐based approaches to estimate meaningful changes.

Relative treatment ranking

We will assess the relative ranking of interventions using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values (P‐scores) [76]. The use of P‐scores will allow us, separately for each outcome of interest, to determine which treatment outperforms the largest proportion of its competitors [77].

Unit of analysis issues

We will use individual participants as the unit of analysis. If we include studies with designs other than parallel‐group randomized trials (e.g. cross‐over, cluster‐randomized, or multi‐arm trials), we will follow the recommendations in Chapter 23 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [75].

If this is not feasible, we will conduct each pair‐wise comparison separately, dividing intervention groups approximately evenly among comparisons. For dichotomous outcomes, we will divide both the number of events and the total participants; for continuous outcomes, we will divide only the total participants, leaving the means and SD unchanged.

Dealing with missing data

We will follow Cochrane recommendations for handling missing data, as described in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [78]. If individual participant data are missing or unclear, we will contact the authors of the included trials via email to request the missing information. If the authors do not respond, we will apply imputation techniques to replace missing data and treat them as observed.

We will calculate missing SD using other reported statistics from the trial (e.g. CIs, standard error (SE), t values, or P values), following the methods described in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [79]. We will perform sensitivity analyses during meta‐analysis to evaluate the potential impact of missing data.

Reporting bias assessment

If a meta‐analysis includes 10 or more studies, we will generate funnel plots and perform Egger’s linear regression tests to assess reporting biases, according to Section 12.3 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [80]. Where possible, we will compare eligible trials with their corresponding protocols to evaluate outcome reporting bias. For the network meta‐analysis, we will construct comparison‐adjusted funnel plots to examine the presence of small‐study effects across different treatment comparisons.

Synthesis methods

Methods for pair‐wise meta‐analysis

We will perform conventional pair‐wise meta‐analyses using RevMan software for each comparison and outcome that includes at least two studies, in accordance with Chapters 9 and 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [78, 81, 82]. We will calculate 95% CI for all effect measures and display the results graphically in forest plots. We will evaluate the feasibility of meta‐analysis based on the number of available studies and the degree of outcome heterogeneity. We will use an inverse‐variance random‐effects model for all meta‐analyses, as we anticipate clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. We will estimate between‐study variance (Tau²) using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. When at least three studies contribute to a pooled estimate and the heterogeneity estimate is greater than zero, we will calculate CIs using the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman (HKSJ) method. In pooled analyses of two studies, or when the heterogeneity estimate equals zero, we will apply the Wald‐type method. If data synthesis through meta‐analysis is not feasible, we will apply the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) approach, as outlined in Chapter 12 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [80].

Methods for network meta‐analysis

We will conduct network meta‐analyses separately for each intervention class. Within each class, we will compare the different interventions. We will conduct separate analyses for short‐term (≤ 6 months) and long‐term (> 6 months) follow‐up outcomes. We will conduct a frequentist network meta‐analysis for each outcome using a random‐effects model implemented through the network suite of commands in Stata 16 [83, 84, 85]. We will estimate the within‐network heterogeneity across comparisons using the REML method [86]. We will combine placebo, waiting‐list control, and usual care into a single comparator node for all analyses.

Assessment of the transitivity assumption: before conducting a network meta‐analysis, we will evaluate the transitivity assumption, which posits that effect modifiers are similarly distributed across treatment comparisons within the network [87]. To examine the plausibility of this assumption, we will visually inspect and compare the distributions of potential treatment effect modifiers, such as age, sex, and medication status, across all comparisons. Certainty of the evidence assessment

We will generate a summary of findings table following the guidelines outlined in Chapter 14 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [90]. Two review authors (TFL and JYS) will independently assess the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework, categorizing it as high, moderate, low, or very low. We will resolve any disagreements through discussion or in consultation with a third review author (ZL) if necessary.

We will assess the certainty of evidence based on the following domains: risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. For both direct and indirect comparisons, we will begin our assessment at high certainty. We will assess the certainty of indirect estimates by examining the dominant lowest‐order loop, which we define as the minimal‐order evidence circuit that contributes the largest share of information to the indirect comparison. We will identify this evidence circuit using the contribution matrix, which quantifies how much each direct comparison contributes to each indirect estimate. We will use this information to evaluate the reliability of the indirect evidence. We will assign the certainty rating for an indirect estimate based on the lowest certainty among the direct estimates contributing to that comparison. We will further consider downgrading the certainty of each indirect comparison due to intransitivity if we find that the interventions or populations differ between the direct comparisons informing the loop that contributes most to the indirect estimate. We will rate the certainty of network estimates according to the highest level of certainty among the direct and indirect estimates contributing to each network estimate. We will consider downgrading the certainty of a network estimate if we detect incoherence between indirect and direct estimates, or we observe imprecision [91, 92].

We will prepare separate summary of findings tables for the critical outcomes. We will present the network estimates derived from the network meta‐analysis for short‐term (≤ 6 months) and long‐term (> 6 months) follow‐up in each summary of findings table. We will also indicate the availability of direct evidence (e.g. number of direct comparison studies and participants) or whether the estimates were based on indirect evidence only.
