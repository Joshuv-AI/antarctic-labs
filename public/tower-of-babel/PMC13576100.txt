Targeting inflammation and coagulation: MMP-9 deficiency exacerbates sepsis induced acute lung injury through fibrinogen-driven inflammation
Authors: Jiabo Chen, Jinquan Zhang, Zhengzheng Yan, Chiying Zhu, Xiaona Chen, Ziqi Sun, Quan Li, Zhixia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, characterized by multiorgan failure in which the lung is the primary target. Although matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), a macrophage-derived protease, is known to degrade extracellular matrix proteins during inflammation, its specific role in sepsis-induced lung injury (SALI) remains to be elucidated. This study elucidates the protective function of MMP9 in SALI and validated its translational potential as an early diagnostic biomarker that integrates coagulation–inflammation crosstalk. First, we integrated human blood bulk RNA-seq data and an in-house LPS-induced murine ALI model to extract coagulation-related sepsis DEGs (Cos-Gs), constructed a PPI network, and applied two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), which identified MMP9 as a causal protection gene. Additionally, we performed molecular docking using the ZDOCK server and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations with Gromacs to explore the interaction between MMP9 and fibrinogen. Next, in vivo studies using CLP-operated MMP9−/− and WT mice demonstrated that MMP9 deficiency reduced survival, increased pulmonary fibrinogen accumulation, and elevated IL-6/TNF-α release. Furthermore, in macrophages, fibrinogen synergized with LPS to amplify cytokines via MAPK hyperactivation, a process that was suppressed by rmMMP9 through fibrinogen degradation and subsequent MAPK inhibition. Molecular docking results showed a ZDOCK score of 1845.832 for the interaction between MMP9 and the fibrinogen α-chain, and the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the MMP9–α-chain complex maintained stable RMSD (0.4 to 0.5 nm) in a low-energy state. Finally, The ROC/XGBoost-SHAP models confirmed that MMP9 (AUC = 0.711) was the dominant predictor of SALI, outperforming IL-6 and matching TNF-α/CRP with external nomogram validation. Collectively, our findings indicated that MMP9 protects against SALI by suppressing fibrin-driven MAPK hyperactivation and cytokine release through its fibrinolytic activity, thereby establishing MMMP9 as a mechanistically grounded biomarker for early risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03764-6.

Introduction

Sepsis, which is defined as a dysregulated host response to infection, often leads to multiorgan dysfunction and affects millions of people worldwide each year [1]. The lung is an organ particularly susceptible to sepsis, and more than half of septic patients develop acute lung injury (ALI) [2, 3]. Compounding pulmonary vulnerability in sepsis, vascular endothelial injury initiates sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC) is characterized by a systemic inflammatory response and dysregulated coagulation cascades [4]. Coagulation and inflammation exhibit bidirectional crosstalk, where the amplification of inflammatory responses further accelerates procoagulant activity [5]. Clinically, approximately 70% of patients progress from subclinical coagulopathy to overt disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). However, current diagnostic approaches predominantly focus on late-stage coagulation dysfunction, thereby overlooking the dynamic and evolving nature of SIC during sepsis progression [6]. Previous studies have indicated that inflammation and coagulation strongly contribute to the pathophysiology of the hyperinflammatory subphenotype of ALI [7, 8]. However, the interaction between coagulation and inflammation is not fully elucidated, necessitating further research into the mechanisms of sepsis-induced lung injury (SALI) to develop effective strategies. Early identification of coagulopathic risk factors, mechanistic studies of SALI and the development of targeted interventions are therefore paramount for reducing sepsis-related mortality.

Macrophages represent the predominant anti-infectious immune cells populations and play important roles in the pathogenesis of sepsis [9]. Upon foreign pathogens invasion, they not only secrete a range of inflammatory factors but also release other mediators, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which critically modulate the inflammatory response and influence its progression [10, 11]. MMPs belong to a family of zinc-dependent proteases primarily known for their ability to degrade the components of extracellular matrix (ECM). In previous work, we reported a significant upregulation of MMP9 in septic patients [12]. Beyond ECM remodeling, MMP9 activation has been implicated in fibrin-induced enhanced caveolae formation in the brain during blood–brain barrier disruption [13]. Furthermore, evidence from anti-glomerular basement membrane nephritis models indicates that MMP9 deficiency increases fibrin accumulation in the glomeruli and exacerbates nephritis [14], underscoring its complex role in inflammatory pathologies.

The biological functions of MMP9 are intricately associated with fibrin, a pivotal component at the interface of coagulation and inflammation. The initial pro-inflammatory response triggers coagulation cascades, which in turn amplify the inflammatory response by enhancing the activation of immune cells and the production of inflammatory cytokines [15]. Fibrinogen, a crucial component of the coagulation cascade and ECM, is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of inflammation in inflammatory-associated diseases [16–19]. For example, fibrinogen and fibrin stimulation increases the release of IL-6 and TNF-α in a dose-dependent manner in human peripheral blood monocytes [20]. Despite these insights, the specific role of MMP9 in modulating fibringen-driven inflammatory responses in macrophages has not yet been elucidated.

Current diagnosis of sepsis relies on canonical inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT; gene symbol CALCA), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [21]. CRP, an acute-phase reactant, is highly sensitive but non-specific due to elevation in sterile inflammatory states. PCT serves as a bacteria-specific indicator to guide antibiotic therapy, yet its predictive value for SALI is limited. Although circulating IL-6 and TNF-α levels correlated with sepsis severity, but they lack utility early recognition of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and are vulnerable to multiple confounders [22–24]. Consequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis is the benchmark for evaluating biomarkers performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.7 indicating satisfactory discriminatory. Recent clinical investigations have demonstrated that coagulation indices accurately reflect the severity of sepsis-induced coagulopathy and correlate with disease progression. Although the individual AUCs for CRP, PCT and IL-6 are promising, integrative algorithms combining these markers significantly improve the accuracy of sepsis stratification [22–24].

In this study, lung tissue sequencing identified an interaction between MMP9 and fibrinogen, with MMP9 expression localized predominantly to macrophages. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis indicated a protective role for MMP9 against ALI. Experimentally, MMP9 deficiency reduced survival in septic mice and aggravated SALI, along with increased pulmonary fibrinogen-α chain accumulation. Proteomic profiling of lung tissue proteomics revealed significant enriched in cytokine-related signaling pathways. In macrophages, fibrinogen was found to amplifiy LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine expression through MAPK pathway activation. Recombinant MMP9 (rmMMP9) suppressed this fibrinogen-mediated synergistic inflammation by inhibiting MAPK activation. Furthermore, MMP9 demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic accuracy for SALI. Collectively, these results reveal that MMP9 confers protection against SALI, likely by fibrinogen-driven macrophages hyperactivation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Methods

Data collection

Differential expression profiling was performed on (a) peripheral-blood transcriptomes of septic patients versus healthy controls (GEO accession GSE69063; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) and (b) our in-house LPS-intranasal ALI murine model versus naïve controls, applying stringent cutoff values of P < 0.05 and |log₂ fold-change| > 1.5 to derive sepsis-DEGs and LPS-DEGs(Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), respectively. For GSE69063, gene expression was profiled in peripheral blood samples from 57 septic patients and 11 healthy controls. Patients were recruited from three Australian emergency departments between March 2011 and June 2013. The microarray platform was GPL19983 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0 expression beadchip).

Next, two coagulation-centric gene sets—“HALLMARK_COAGULATION” and “HP_ABNORMALITY_OF_THE_COMMON_COAGULATION_PATHWAY”(Supplementary Tables 3 and 4)—were retrieved from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org), yielding a consolidated repertoire of 208 coagulation-related genes. Intersection of these genes with the aforementioned DEGs via Venn mapping revealed coagulation-related sepsis DEGs (Cos-Gs). Volcano and heatmap visualizations of Cos-Gs expression were subsequently generated within the murine LPS transcriptome, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) topology was elucidated through the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) to quantify inter gene connectivity at the proteomic level.

MR and survival analysis

The summary repositories of the genome-wide association study (GWAS) constitute an extensive information reservoir of genotype–phenotype relationships. Exploiting this resource, we investigated the influence of MMP9 on ARDS and formally quantified its causal relevance through a two-sample MR framework. Exposure instrumental variables (IVs) were derived from expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data released by the IEU Open GWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/, release 8.3.0), encompassing 19,942 transcripts profiled in 31,684 individuals [25]. SNPs were rigorously pruned by excluding proxies with P > 0.5, within 10,000 kb, linkage-disequilibrium (LD) r² > 0.001, or F-statistic < 10, yielding a stringent set of IVs for subsequent analyses [26]. Outcome summary statistics for ARDS were extracted from the FinnGen biobank (finngen_R10_J10_ARDS), comprising 357 incident ARDS cases and 406,536 population controls of European ancestry (https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r10/summary_stats/finngen_R10_J10_ARDS.gz). All datasets are publicly available and fully deidentified; therefore, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was needed.

For survival analysis, we utilized GEO dataset GSE54514, which contains whole blood transcriptome data from 127 ARDS patients (96 survivors and 31 non-survivors) and 36 healthy controls. In this dataset, whole blood samples were collected daily for up to 5 days from patients admitted to the intensive care unit with sepsis. The microarray platform was GPL6947 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 expression beadchip). RNA was assayed on Illumina HT-12 gene expression microarrays consisting of 48,804 probes. We specifically analyzed the relationship between MMP9 expression levels and patient survival outcomes.

Causal inference was implemented with the TwoSampleMR R package (v.0.5.7) [27]. Five complementary MR estimators—inverse-variance weighted (IVW), MR‒Egger, simple mode, weighted median and weighted model—were applied; IVW and weighted median served as the primary causal estimates. Heterogeneity was gauged with Cochran’s Q test (P < 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity). Directional pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR‒Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test (P < 0.05, suggesting pleiotropic bias) [26]. Robustness was further appraised via leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, sequentially omitting each IV to evaluate the stability of the causal estimate [28]. Finally, scatter plots, funnel plots and forest plots were generated to provide a visual synopsis of the MR findings.

KEGG pathway analysis of the proteomics data

Following cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) induced sepsis in both WT and MMP9-knockout (MMP9⁻/⁻) mice, lung tissue was harvested for high-resolution proteomic profiling. After LC-MS acquisition, protein identification, label-free quantification and functional annotation, the resulting proteome was BLAST-aligned against the online Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://geneontology.org/) to retrieve KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers, which were subsequently mapped onto canonical signaling pathways.

Nomogram construction and diagnostic prediction based on MMP9 and classic sepsis markers

To develop a predictive model for SALI progression severity, we utilized the GSE69063 dataset. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed by integrating the expression levels of key hub genes, enabling stratification of patients according to disease severity. Model calibration was assessed using 1000 bootstrap resampling cycles, with graphical concordance plots comparing predicted probabilities against observed outcomes. The discriminative power of the multigene model was evaluated via ROC analysis and compared to classifiers based on individual hub genes.

To facilitate clinical translation, two visualization tools were implemented: a static nomogram incorporating log-transformed gene expression values to estimate severity probability, and a combined probability projection diagram displaying individual risk trajectories with 95% confidence intervals via kernel density estimates and boxplots. Together, these approaches provide a clinically applicable framework for prognostic risk stratification in SALI.

Interpretable machine learning models

Capitalizing on the GSE69063 transcriptomic atlas, we executed high-order feature interrogation with the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm to evaluate the incremental prognostic value of MMP9 relative to that of canonical sepsis biomarkers. A stratified random-split protocol (70% training/30% independent testing) guaranteed proportional representation across phenotypic subcohorts. The hyperparameters were rigorously optimized: maximal tree depth = 3, learning rate η = 0.1, and boosting iterations = 10. Within a binary logistic loss framework, the prognostic salience of each variable was quantified by mean absolute SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, providing an additive decomposition of marginal contributions. Waterfall plots were generated to visualize the directional influence—protective or deleterious of individual biomarkers on the subject-specific survival probability. Complementary partial-dependence plots elucidated nonlinear covariation patterns between pivotal features and predicted survival risk. The mean |SHAP| metric served as the primary benchmark, delivering a transparent, quantitative index of relative feature importance. This integrative paradigm not only delineates the hierarchical relevance of candidate determinants but also furnishes a robust, interpretable architecture for survival prediction in patients with sepsis.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulation

The crystal structures of MMP9 and Fibrinogen were retrieved from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/). Prior to docking, water molecules were removed and hydrogen atoms were added using PyMOL. The preprocessed structures were then submitted to the ZDOCK server to obtain the optimal docking conformation along with its corresponding score. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using Gromacs (version 2024.3). The Amber99SB-ildn force field and the TIP3P water model were applied, with the system maintained at a constant temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar. Parameters and topology files were generated using Sobtop_1.0 and integrated into the system topology. An appropriate number of Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions were added to maintain system neutrality. Energy minimization was first conducted using the steepest descent algorithm. Subsequently, the system was equilibrated stepwise under NVT and NPT ensembles, each for 200 ps. A production MD simulation was then carried out for 100 ns. Trajectory analyses, including the calculation of root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), number of hydrogen bonds, and free energy landscape, were performed using built-in GROMACS tools. The binding free energy was evaluated using gmx_MMPBSA to comprehensively assess the stability and interaction characteristics of the complex.

Animal experiments

MMP-9−/− mice utilized in this investigation were acquired from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc. They were on a C57BL/6 genetic background, aged between 6 and 8 weeks, and weighing between 20 and 25 g. All operations received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Tenth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University, and the study was executed in complete compliance with the National Institutes of Health’s Guidance for the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals. All the mice had free access to food and water and were housed in a facility maintained at constant temperature and humidity with a 12-hour day/night cycle. CLP surgery is a classical approach for inducing sepsis in mice, and the modeling process used in this study was similar to that used in our previous study [29, 30]. The LPS-induced ALI model was established by intranasal administration of LPS under anesthesia. LPS was prepared at a concentration of 1.25 µg/µL in sterile PBS (total volume 40µL) and delivered in two consecutive doses of 20 µL each. Each dose was gently applied to the nasal to allow passive inhalation while preventing forced inhalation or coughing. For in vivo administration, rmMMP9 (Cat: 50560-MNAH, Sino Biological) was injected via the tail vein at a dosage of 25 µg/kg in a volume of 200 µL, administered 12 h prior to CLP. All mice were sacrificed 12 h after modeling, and subsequent experimental procedures involved collection of BALF and lung tissue samples for further analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

MH-S cells, which were procured from Procell, were cultivated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To investigate the effect of fibrinogen on LPS (0.1 µg/ml)-induced proinflammatory cytokine expression and release, we collected MH-S cells and supernatants at the indicated times. Recombinant mouse MMP9 (R&D, 909-MM) was activated according to a standard protocol. The appropriate ratios of p-amino phenylmercuric acetate (APMA) and MMP9 were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. To investigate the effect of MMP9, MH-S cells were treated with rmMMP9 (100 ng/ml) and fibrinogen (0.1 mg/ml) before being stimulated with LPS (0.1 µg/ml), and samples were collected for subsequent analyses.

Bronchoalveolar lavage

The trachea was incised along the trachea, and the surrounding connective tissue was separated immediately after the mice were sacrificed. The lungs were flushed in situ with 1 ml of PBS and repeatedly flushed three times. Approximately 0.7 ml of BALF was taken from each mouse. The total number of viable cells in the BALF was determined using a cell counter. The BALF was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for protein content assessment via the BCA method.

Lung wet/dry (W/D) weight ratio

The mice were sacrificed after blood collection, and their lungs were excised from the thoracic cavity. The lungs were subsequently weighed with an electronic balance, and the wet weight (W) of the lung tissue was recorded. The weighed lung tissue was incubated at 60 °C for 3 days and weighed again using an electronic balance to record the dry weight (D).

Pathology assessment

Lungs were excised and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, embedded in paraffin and cut into slices of appropriate thickness(3–5 μm). Lung sections were stained with hematoxylin‒eosin. The histological quantitative scoring criteria were described in a previous study [31].

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA extraction was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (#19211ES, Yeasen), and the RNA was reverse transcribed using Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (#11141ES, Yeasen). RT‒qPCR was conducted with Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (#11184ES, Yeasen) on a Light Cycler 480 real-time system (Roche). The expression of target genes was normalized to the expression of GAPDH. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used for quantitative analysis. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum and culture supernatants were collected and stored at -80 °C. The levels of IL-6 (#E-EL-M0044, Elabscience) and TNF-α (#E-EL-M3063) were measured with an ELISA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

The proteins in lung tissue and cultured cells were harvested with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. The protein concentration was measured with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit. Equivalent amounts of protein were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Macklin). The membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against anti-fibrinogen-α chain (#20645-1-AP; Proteintech), p38 (#8690; Cell Signaling), phospho-p38 (#4511; Cell Signaling), p44/42 (#4695; Cell Signaling), phospho-p44/42 (#4370; Cell Signaling), JNK (#9252; Cell Signaling), phospho-JNK (#4668; Cell Signaling) and β-actin (#4970; Cell Signaling) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were subsequently washed and incubated with a goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#7074, goat anti-rabbit IgG) at room temperature for 1 h and developed with enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (#1705060, Bio-Rad). Images were obtained with a G: BOXChemiXX9 imaging system (Syngene) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Lung tissue sections were obtained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were incubated with an anti-fibrinogen-α chain polyclonal antibody (#20645-1-AP; Proteintech) at 4 °C for 24 h and incubated with a secondary antibody at 37 °C for 60 min. Finally, the sections were rehydrated with graded ethanol, washed with xylene, and then visualized with a microscope.

Coomassie blue staining

Fibrinogen (0.1 mg/ml) was incubated with different concentrations of rmMMP9 for 12 h, electrophoresed on a 10% SDS‒PAGE gel, and then immersed in Coomassie blue staining solution (#P0003, Beyotime). Images were obtained with a G: BOXChemiXX9 imaging system (Syngene).

Statistical analysis

Comprehensive biostatistical analyses were conducted exclusively within the computational environments of R (v 4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism (v 9.5.0). Differential gene expression identification was executed through implementation of the DESeq2 package. The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 13.0 software with Student’s t test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). P values less than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. Statistical significance gradation was denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results

Identification of Cos-Gs in SALI and PPI network construction profiling

To delineate coagulation-related transcriptional perturbations during SALI, we applied stringent filters (P < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1.5) to integrated murine and human datasets, identifying 1,090 sepsis related DEGs and 1,798 LPS related DEGs. Overlap with a curated coagulation compendium (208 genes) revealed five Cos-Gs: Dpp4, Mmp-9, Prf1, Mmp8 and Serpinb2 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 5). Except Dpp4, all Cos-Gs were transcriptionally upregulated following LPS challenge (Fig. 1B and C). PPI network analysis revealed MMP-9 as the topologically central hub, with a degree of 8 (Fig. 1D). Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis of lung tissue from both CLP and intranasal LPS induced SALI models confirmed significant upregulation of MMP-9 expression (Fig. 1E and F).

MMP-9 deficiency increases the mortality of septic mice and exacerbates SALI

The role of MMP-9 in ALI remains incompletely understood. Using two-sample MR analysis, we identified an inverse causal relationship between genetically predicted MMP-9 expression and ARDS risk (Fig. 2A and B). Analysis of the GSE54514 survival cohort further revealed that sepsis patients with the lowest MMP-9 expression exhibited significantly higher mortality (Fig. 2C) suggesting a protective role of MMP-9. These findings were corroborated in an experimental CLP model: compared with wild-type (WT) CLP littermates, MMP-9−/−mice exhibited accelerated mortality (Fig. 2D). In addition, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from MMP-9−/−CLP mice showed elevated total cell counts and protein concentrations relative to WT CLP controls, indicating enhanced alveolocapillary leakage. The lung wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio, a marker of pulmonary edema was markedly increased following CLP and was further elevated in MMP-9 deficient mice (Fig. 2E). The histological injury scores also confirmed more severe parenchymal damage in MMP-9−/−mice (Fig. 2F). Remarkably, rmMMP9 pretreatment significantly attenuated the sepsis-induced upregulation of IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA in lung tissue, reduced BALF cell infiltration, and ameliorated histological lung injury compared to vehicle-treated WT CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 1). Together, these results demonstrate that loss of MMP-9 exacerbates SALI, shifting the phenotype from reversible to rapidly lethal.

MMP-9 deficiency promotes local and systemic inflammatory cytokine expression induced by sepsis

Sepsis-induced local and systemic inflammatory responses are known contributors to associated organ dysfunction [32]. To investigate the regulatory role of MMP-9 in proinflammatory cytokine signaling, we performed quantitative proteomic profiling of lung tissue obtained from MMP-9−/−and WT mice 12 h after CLP surgery, with a specific focus on the translatome. Differentially abundant proteins identified in this analysis were further subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment. In parallel, IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations were quantified in both lung homogenates and serum. MMP-9 deficient mice exhibited pronounced enrichment in several inflammatory pathways, including cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, IL-17 signaling and TNF-α pathways (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 6), suggesting that MMP9 deletion amplified the septic inflammatory cascade at the translational level. Following CLP, both WT and MMP-9−/− mice exhibited increased expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in lung tissue; however, these cytokine levels were significantly in MMP-9−/− mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 3B and C). Similarly, serum levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were more elevated in MMP-9−/−CLP mice than in WT CLP animals (Fig. 3D and E).

MMP-9 deficiency increases fibrinogen accumulation

A previous study indicated that fibrinogen and fibrin induce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines in innate immune cells [33]. Fibrinogen-α chain is one of three different polypeptide chains that constitute the complete fibrinogen molecule. To determine whether MMP9 deficiency affects fibrinogen accumulation, we examined the expression of the fibrinogen-α chain in liver and lung tissues from MMP-9−/− and WT mice after CLP. IHC analysis revealed extensive fibrinogen-α chain deposits throughout the lung parenchyma in MMP-9−/− CLP mice (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the protein level of fibrinogen-α chain in the liver and lungs indicated that fibrinogen levels were significantly higher in the liver and lung tissues of MMP-9−/− mice compared to WT CLP mice controls (Fig. 4B and C).

Fibrinogen amplifies LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine release and MAPK pathway activation in macrophages

While the role of fibrinogen in coagulation is well established, its influence on macrophage behavior remains less clear. To investigate the role of fibrinogen in inflammation, we used an in vitro model of LPS-stimulated macrophages. MH-S cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/ml) with or without fibrinogen (0.1 mg/ml) at the indicated times. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, stimulation with fibrinogen alone did not significantly alter the expression of IL-6 or TNF-α. However, when macrophages were co-stimulation with fibrinogen and LPS, the expression of both cytokines was significantly increased compared to LPS stimulation alone. ELISA further revealed that secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α in the LPS + Fib group increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 5C and D), whereas fibrinogen alone showed no effect on cytokine release. Given emerging evidence that MAPK signaling activation contributes to LPS-induced inflammatory response in macrophages [34], we also examined the phosphorylation levels of key MAPK components. Fibrinogen alone did not affect phosphorylation levels of p38, JNK, or ERK. As expected, LPS stimulation increased phosphorylation of all three kinases, and this effect was further increased in the LPS + Fib group (Fig. 5E and F). These results suggest that fibrinogen synergizes with LPS to amplify proinflammatory responses by enhancing MAPK signaling activation in macrophages.

MMP9 possesses fibrinolytic enzyme properties

Based on the observed accumulation of fibrinogen-α chain in the lungs of MMP9−/− CLP mice and the ability of fibrinogen to amplify LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine release in macrophages, we hypothesized that MMP9 exerts a protective effect against SALI by degrading fibrinogen. Previous study suggests that MMP9 possesses fibrinolytic activity [14]. To test whether MMP9 can directly degrade fibrinogen, we incubated fibrinogen (0.1 mg/ml) with increasing concentrations of rmMMP9 for 12 h and assessed fibrinogen structural changes using Thomas blue staining. As shown in Fig. 6A, significant degradation of the fibrinogen-α chain was observed at an rmMMP9 concentration of 100 ng/ml. We achieved a ZDOCK score of 1845.832 for the interaction between MMP9 and the fibrinogen α-chain via molecular docking. Structural study indicated that many residues of MMP9 establish hydrogen bonds with ARG-65, ASP-61, TYR-43, GLY-48, and LYS-44 of the α-chain, exhibiting bond lengths between 1.9 and 3.5 Å (Fig. 7A). Following 100 ns of molecular dynamics simulations, the MMP9–α-chain complex attained a low-energy stable state on the Free Energy Surface (FES), exhibiting a Rg of 2.44 to 2.49 nm and an RMSD of 0.4 to 0.5 nm (Fig. 7B). During the simulation, the RMSD values for all three systems consistently maintained below 0.7 nm. The RMSD of the MMP9–α-chain complex stabilized at around 0.5 nm after 20 ns, with a comparable trend noted for the MMP9 monomer, so affirming the binding mode’s great stability. Analysis of the Radius of Gyration (Rg) revealed a modest reduction in Rg for the complex, indicating enhanced structural compactness following α-chain binding (Fig. 7C). The gmx_MMPBSA calculations revealed that the binding free energy of the complex is ΔG_total = − 109.54 kcal mol⁻¹, signifying a thermodynamically favorable and very spontaneous binding mechanism. The gas-phase interactions generated ΔG_gas = − 611.26 kcal mol⁻¹, whereas the solvation effect supplied ΔG_solv = + 501.72 kcal mol⁻¹ (Fig. 7D, E; Supplementary Table 7). These results indicate that MMP9 acts as a fibrinolytic enzyme capable of specifically cleaving the α-chain of fibrinogen.

MMP9 inhibits the synergistic proinflammatory effect of fibrinogen in LPS-stimulated macrophages by suppressing MAPK activation

In vivo, MMP9 deficiency was found to exacerbate sepsis-induced proinflammatory cytokine release and promote fibrinogen-α chain accumulation. Correspondingly, in vitro experiments showed that fibrinogen synergistically enhances the proinflammatory effect response to LPS in macrophages, an effect that was inhibited by rmMMP9. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that MMP9 inhibits the synergistic proinflammatory effects of fibrinogen in LPS-stimulated macrophages. To test this, macrophages were pretreated with a combination of 100 ng/ml rmMMP9 and 0.1 mg/mL fibrinogen for 12 h prior to LPS stimulation. The mRNA levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were lower in the LPS + Fib+rmMMP9 group than in the LPS + Fib group (Fig. 6B and C). Similarly, ELISA revealed a marked reduction in the secretion of both cytokines in the cell supernatant (Fig. 6D and E). To determine whether rmMMP9 mediated inhibition involves the MAPK pathways, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of p38, JNK and ERK and found that they were significantly lower in the LPS + Fib+MMP9 group than in the LPS + Fib group (Fig. 6F).

Diagnostic efficacy of MMP9 and classic markers in interpretable models for sepsis

Based on the severity-stratified transcriptomic profiles of healthy controls and patients with SALI (GSE69063), we evaluated the diagnostic performance of the coagulopathy-related hub gene MMP9 against established sepsis markers—TNF-α, IL-6, CRP and CALCA (the gene encoding PCT). ROC analysis revealed that MMP9 achieved strong diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.711, exceeding the conventional threshold for clinical utility (AUC > 0.7) (Fig. 8A). TNF-α (AUC = 0.696), CRP (AUC = 0.660) and CALCA (AUC = 0.664) exhibited moderate performance (0.6 < AUC < 0.7), whereas IL-6 displayed limited discrimination power (AUC = 0.483). These data establish MMP9 as a diagnostically equivalent, and potentially superior biomarker to traditional indicators. A nomogram was developed to visualize the combined genetic risk of ARDS progression, indicating a cumulative gene score of 325 corresponded to an odds ratio of 6.55 × 10¹⁰ for ARDS development (Fig. 8B), highlighting the clinical utility of multigene integration in early risk stratification.

To predict SALI outcomes, we employed an XGBoost framework integrated with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for interpretability. SHAP values quantify the directional impact of each feature, wheres positive values increase sepsis probability and negative values indicate protection. The model captured interactions among key coagulation markers (MMP9), canonical diagnostic analytes and clinical endpoints. The global SHAP summary statistics revealed that MMP9 was the dominant prognostic feature (highest mean |SHAP|), followed by CALCA and IL-6 (Fig. 9A). Univariate SHAP analyses demonstrated that elevated expression of MMP9 (transcript level = 9.31) and CALCA (level = 3.4) markedly increased the predicted probability of disease, whereas IL-6, CRP and TNF-α contributed minimally (Fig. 9B). Discussion

MMP-9 is a pleiotropic protease positioned at the nexus of coagulation and innate immunity. Fibrinogen-activated monocytes upregulate and release enzymatically active MMP-9, which in turn amplifies monocyte-mediated procoagulant activity [35]. In our study, MMP-9 emerged as the topologically central hub within the coagulopathy signature of SALI (Fig. 1). Although MMP-9 dysregulation has been implicated in ALI of varying origins, which consistent with our previous report of its upregulation in both sepsis mice and patients [12]. However, its functional role remains controversial due to the heterogeneity of ALI pathogenesis. For instance, while anti-MMP-9 treatment ameliorates lung inflammation in LPS-induced ALI, MMP-9 knocking down with siRNA aggravated lung inflammation in SALI of mice [36].Similarly, Albaiceta et al. observed more severe ventilator-induced injury in MMP-9-deficient mice compared to controls [37].Our findings help resolve these discrepancies. Using Mendelian randomization, patient survival curves, and experimental CLP, we demonstrate that MMP-9 deficiency impairs host defense, increases mortality and amplifies pulmonary edema and inflammation (Figs. 2 and 3). Administration of rmMMP9 to WT mice prior to CLP significantly attenuated pulmonary inflammation and tissue injury (Supplementary Fig. 1), demonstrating that MMP9 is sufficient to confer protection. However, a definitive rescue experiment would require administration of rmMMP9 to MMP-9−/− mice to determine whether reintroduction of MMP-9 directly reverses the phenotype. Because rmMMP-9 was given only to WT mice in this study, the protective effect observed is limited to an additive or modulatory action and cannot formally establish causality through specific rescue of MMP9 deficiency. Nevertheless, together with the genetic and clinical evidence, these data support the conclusion that MMP9 exerts a net protective effect in SALI and that its induction during sepsis reflects a regulated, adaptive response rather than a maladaptive bystander effect.

To further explore the mechanistic basis of this protection, we investigated MMP9’s interaction with fibrinogen. As a proteolytic enzyme capable of degrading [38], MMP9 may influence fibrinogen homeostasis during sepsis. Previous studies indicate that MMP9 can bind to fibrin [39], though its functional consequences appear context-dependent. In glomerulonephritis, MMP9 degrades fibrinogen, and its deficiency leads to fibrin accumulation and aggravated kidney injury [14]. Conversely, in mild traumatic brain injury, the absence of MMP9 attenuated fibrinogen deposition and mitigated short-term memory loss [40]. In this study, MMP9−/− CLP exhibited more severe pulmonary fibrinogen α-chain deposition than WT controls (Fig. 4). In addition, we confirmed that rmMMP9 directly cleaves the α-chain of fibrinogen (Fig. 6A). Fibrinogen is a macromolecule composed of three pairs of non-identical polypeptide chains (α, β and γ). Although we were unable to demonstrate the effect of MMP9 on the fibrinogen β and γ chains, our results suggested that MMP9-mediated degradation of the fibrinogen α chain played a protective role in SALI.

Beyond the canonical role in fibrinolysis, emerging evidence suggests that fibrinolytic systems participate in immunomodulation during sepsis [33]. Fibrinogen and its regulatory proteases are increasingly recognized as critical modulators of acute inflammation and tissue injury [16–19]. As an acute-phase reactant, fibrinogen is upregulated in the liver during systemic inflammation [41]. Consistent with this, we observed elevated fibrinogen levels in both liver and lungs of WT CLP mice, which were further exacerbated in MMP9−/− CLP mice (Fig. 3). Fibrinogen also regulates leukocyte function, including transmigration and the production of cytokines [42],though its effects are highly microenvironment-dependent. However, the effects and mechanisms in macrophages have yet to be investigated. While fibrinogen stimulation alone did not significantly alter IL-6 or TNF-α expression or secretion in macrophages, it markedly enhanced the proinflammatory response when combined with LPS. These results indicate that fibrinogen acts synergistically with LPS to amplify macrophage mediated inflammation—a mechanism that may be suppressed when MMP9 is present to degrade accumulated fibrinogen.

We next examined the signaling pathway underlying this synergy. MAPK signaling pathway activation has been implicated in ALI across different etiologies, including sepsis [43], endotoxin [44] and I/R [45]. As a key regulator of acute inflammation in both in vivo and in vitro settings, MAPK activation has been linked to the synergistic upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines in macrophages exposed to LPS [43]. In this study, we revealed that fibrinogen-enhanced LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine secretion was accompanied with increased activation of the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the addition of rmMMP9 suppressed this fibrinogen-augmented MAPK activation in LPS-stimulated macrophages, leading to a concomitant reduction in proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6). These findings indicate MMP9 acts to limit the synergistic proinflammatory effects of fibrinogen in the context of LPS-stimulated macrophages.

Finally, to assess the translational relevance of MMP9, we evaluated its diagnostic and prognostic utility in SALI. Despite decades of research, the mortality attributable to sepsis remains high. Early recognition and accurate risk stratification are pivotal for timely intervention and improved outcome, highlighting the urgent need for eliable biomarkers and severity assessment tools [46]. Previous studies on MMP9 in sepsis have yielded conflicting results: one report indicated an AUC of 0.70 for predicting 28-day mortality, with circulating concentrations increasing across SIRS, sepsis and severe-sepsis stages but declining in septic shock [47]. Conversely, a multicenter study revealed only a modest, non-significant increase in systemic MMP9 among sepsis patients [48]. To clarify its role in SALI, we evaluated MMP9 against established biomarker (TNF-α, IL-6, CRP and CALCA) using both ROC and SHAP-interpretable machine-learning frameworks. MMP9 MMP9 achieved an AUC of 0.711, surpassing IL-6 (0.483) and performing comparably to TNF-α and CRP, supporting its utility in early SALI recognition. A composite nomogram incorporating MMP9 and other biomarkers yielded a cumulative genetic score of 325, corresponding to an odds ratio of 6.55 × 10¹⁰ for sepsis diagnosis. Although this extreme estimate may reflect limited sample size, it clearly establishes MMP9 as the dominant variable in the model. Under the XGBoost-SHAP paradigm, MMP9 emerged as the as the most influential predictor (highest mean |SHAP|), with a transcript abundance ≥ 9.31 substantially increasing SALI probability and exhibiting synergistic interactions with CALCA. In contrast, IL-6 and TNF-α contributed minimally to SHAP-based predictions, underscoring the limited diagnostic value of conventional inflammatory mediators in SALI. Collectively, these data validate MMP9 as an independent and central biomarker in both single- and multi-gene predictive models for SALI (Figs. 8 and 9).

We acknowledge that circulating protein biomarkers offer superior clinical accessibility compared to transcriptomic profiling. Our XGBoost-SHAP analysis identified MMP9 as the dominant predictive feature, providing direct rationale for developing MMP9 protein assays (ELISA/immunoturbidimetry) as clinically deployable alternatives. While RNA-based diagnostics currently face logistical constraints, point-of-care nucleic acid testing advances (e.g., CRISPR-Dx) are rapidly reducing turnaround times. We emphasize that our nomogram serves as a proof-of-concept framework for risk stratification, with MMP9 protein validation representing the immediate priority for clinical translation.

In summary, our study demonstrates that sepsis induces fibrinogen accumulation amplifies LPS-induced MAPK activation, and subsequent proinflammatory cytokine release, which is attenuated by MMP9. Moreover, MMP9 deficiency reduces the survival rate of septic mice and exacerbating both fibrinogen α-chain accumulation and inflammatory severity, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the MMP9–fibrinogen axis in sepsis management.
