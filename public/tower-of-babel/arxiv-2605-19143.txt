Weak cosmic censorship for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2 + 1 2+1 dimensions
Serban Cicortas

Abstract
We prove the weak cosmic censorship conjecture in 2 + 1 2+1 spacetime dimensions for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in the presence of a negative cosmological constant Λ < 0 \Lambda<0 . More precisely, we show that for any integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , the maximal development of generic C k C^{k} initial data does not contain naked singularities. An essential step of the proof is establishing the presence of a mass gap. In particular, this implies that all naked singularities have infinite blueshift, which represents the main instability mechanism.



1
Introduction

One of the main open problems in general relativity is the
weak cosmic censorship conjecture
, proposed by Penrose in
[
42
]
for general Einstein-matter systems. The conjecture states that, for generic initial data, so-called "naked singularities" do not form. That is to say, singularities are generically hidden inside black holes and invisible to far away observers who exist for all time (more formally, null infinity
\mathcal{I}
is future complete
[
28
,
15
]
). The conjecture is motivated by the fact that the future incompleteness of
\mathcal{I}
would threaten the predictability of general relativity as a physical theory.

While the weak cosmic censorship conjecture is completely open in its full generality, in the remarkable work
[
16
]
, Christodoulou proved a suitable formulation of the conjecture for the model problem of the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in
3+1
dimensions. The argument of
[
16
]
relies on a novel
blueshift
effect to show that, while explicit naked singularity spacetimes can be constructed
[
14
,
10
]
, all naked singularities are in fact
unstable
to black hole formation and the set of naked singularity spacetimes is indeed
non-generic
in a suitable sense. However, the instability mechanism in
[
16
]
required working in a topology defined by a low-regularity class of solutions. Thus, the question of weak cosmic censorship in a smoother topology remains open even in this setting. We discuss the work
[
16
]
in detail in
Section

1.4.1
.

In the present work, we initiate the mathematical study of gravitational collapse in
2+1
dimensions for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in the presence of a negative cosmological constant
\Lambda<0
, and we prove a version of the weak cosmic censorship conjecture for this system. We note that the natural problem in this setting is solving an initial-boundary value problem with reflective boundary conditions at null infinity
\mathcal{I}
(which is now in fact timelike). This model was first studied in the numerical relativity literature by Pretorius and Choptuik
[
43
]
, and we will discuss its motivation in detail in
Section

1.1
. We also discuss the existence of examples of naked singularities for this model in
Section

1.4.2
. To prove weak cosmic censorship, we use the blueshift effect as the main instability mechanism, similarly to
[
16
]
. A key new feature in our setting is the presence of a
mass gap
, which states that no singularities form in the domain of dependence of a disk bounded by a circle with Hawking mass strictly less than 1. (In particular, this allows us to confirm a conjecture of
[
7
]
). Moreover, the different scaling in the present model allows us to activate the blueshift instability in the class of
C^{k}
solutions, for any
k\geq 2
. Thus, unlike
[
16
]
, our formulation of weak cosmic censorship in the present setting does not rely on low-regularity perturbations and holds in a topology of arbitrary smoothness.

1.1
The Einstein-scalar field system in
2+1
dimensions with
\Lambda<0

We consider the Einstein-scalar field system of equations with a negative cosmological constant
\Lambda<0
:

\begin{cases}Ric_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}|\nabla\phi|^{2},\\
\square_{g}\phi=0.\end{cases}

(1.1)

In
2+1

[

perhaps the most elementary model for gravitational collapse in

dimensions which allows for the dynamical formation of singularities
[

The simplest solution of (

\big(\mathbb{R}^{3},g_{AdS},\phi_{AdS}\big):

g_{AdS}=-\big(1+|\Lambda|r^{2}\big)dt^{2}+\big(1+|\Lambda|r^{2}\big)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\sigma_{1}^{2},\ \phi_{AdS}=0,

where

is the standard metric on
S^{1}.
We note that AdS spacetime is circularly symmetric, does not contain any singularities, and is geodesically complete. Moreover, we can attach an asymptotic boundary at infinity, called
null infinity

\mathcal{I}
, which is timelike and complete.

In the present work, we initiate the rigorous mathematical study of the system (
1.1
). We consider the class of circularly symmetric solutions, which take the following form with respect to global double null coordinates
(u,v):

g(u,v)=-\Omega^{2}(u,v)dudv+r^{2}(u,v)d\sigma_{1}^{2},\ \phi=\phi(u,v).

(1.2)

The solutions of (
1.1
) studied in this paper will be the unique maximal developments of
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets, obtained by solving (
1.1

\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, we only consider solutions arising in
gravitational collapse
, i.e. solutions with a regular center
\Gamma=\{r=0\}.
In particular, AdS spacetime itself is contained in this class of solutions. We refer the reader to
Section

2
for a detailed introduction to the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in
2+1
dimensions.

1.3
Idea of the proof

We briefly outline the main ideas of the proof, then we flesh out our discussion with more details in Sections
1.3.1
-
1.3.5
. The overall strategy of the proof goes back to the work of Christodoulou
[
16
]
. In the present section we explain our argument, then we describe in detail in
Section

1.4.1
the similarities and differences to
[
16
]
. We refer the reader to
Section

2
for the precise notation and definitions used in this section.

The main step in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is establishing (
1.9
). To show that locally naked singularities are unstable to the formation of spacelike-only singularities, it suffices to prove that locally naked singularities can be perturbed to form trapped surfaces arbitrarily close to
b_{\Gamma}
.

The proof relies on two fundamental results about circularly symmetric solutions of (
1.1
). Firstly, in
Section

1.3.1
we state
Theorem

1.2
, which establishes the presence of a mass gap, showing that no singularities form in the domain of dependence of a disk bounded by a circle with Hawking mass strictly less than 1. We also state some notable consequences of
Theorem

1.2
. Secondly, in
Section

1.3.2
we state
Theorem

1.3
, which provides a quantitative criterion for the formation of trapped surfaces.

We explain in
Section

1.3.3
how
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
are used to prove a quantitative description of locally naked singularity spacetimes. In particular, we note that any first singularity at the center must have unbounded blueshift. In
Section

1.3.4
, we explain why locally naked singularities can be perturbed to form trapped surfaces arbitrarily close to
b_{\Gamma}
. The idea is to consider perturbations that leave the causal past of
b_{\Gamma}
unchanged and activate the blueshift instability. The blueshift effect will then amplify the initial data perturbation and allow us to apply
Theorem

1.3
to conclude the existence of trapped surfaces arbitrarily close to
b_{\Gamma}
. Finally, we explain in
Section

1.3.5
how to complete the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

1.3.1
The mass gap

The first essential ingredient of our proof is the presence of a mass gap for circularly symmetric solutions of (
1.1
). We define
4
4

4

Similarly to the BTZ family (
1.3
), we change the notation convention from the physics literature and translate the Hawking mass by 1.
the Hawking mass by:

m=-g(\nabla r,\nabla r)-\Lambda r^{2}+1.

(1.10)

We note that the Hawking mass is non-negative for solutions arising in gravitational collapse. Moreover, a solution is exactly AdS spacetime in the domain of dependence of a disk bounded by a circle if and only if the Hawking mass of the circle is zero.

For asymptotically AdS characteristic data
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathfrak{M},
we define the Bondi mass
M=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}m|_{C_{0}^{+}}(r).
We note that the reflective boundary conditions at
\mathcal{I}
imply that
m|_{\mathcal{I}}
is constant, so
M=m|_{\mathcal{I}}.

The numerical relativity work
[
7
]
conjectures that initial data must have Bondi mass at least 1 to form singularities in evolution, and provides some evidence in the case of very small Bondi mass. In
Section

3
, we prove the following local version of this conjecture in terms of the Hawking mass:

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
k\geq 2
, let
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
be the maximal globally hyperbolic development of
C^{k}
characteristic data on the outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+}=\{u=u_{0},\ v_{\Gamma}\leq v\leq\tilde{v}\}
with
r(u_{0},v_{\Gamma})=0,\ r(u_{0},\tilde{v})<\infty.
If
m(u_{0},v_{0})=1-\delta<1
for some
v_{\Gamma}<v_{0}<\tilde{v},
then
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
is regular in the domain of dependence of
C_{0}^{+}\cap\{v\leq v_{0}\}.

We briefly explain the idea of the proof of
Theorem

1.2
. We note that in
Section

3
it is convenient to work in Bondi coordinates
(u,r).
First, we show that the bound on
m
implies quantitative control of the geometry of the spacetime. We then prove a non-concentration estimate for the scalar field, which in particular implies that the scalar field cannot blow up at a faster than self-similar rate, so
r\partial_{r}\phi
is bounded. We further improve this estimate by a small power of
r,
showing in particular that
r^{1-\rho}\partial_{r}\phi
is bounded, where
\rho>0
is sufficiently small. The preliminary bounds for the geometry of the spacetime and the scalar field allow us to propagate from initial data boundedness for the local well-posedness norm, concluding that no singularities form in evolution.

The main use of
Theorem

1.2
for the purposes of this paper is the following description of first singularities at the center:

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
b_{\Gamma}=(u_{*},v_{*})
be a first singularity at the center
\Gamma.
Then
m(u,v_{*})\rightarrow 1^{+}
as
u\rightarrow u_{*}^{-}.

As a corollary of
Theorem

1.2
, we also obtain the mass gap result in terms of the Bondi mass, thus proving the conjecture of
[
7
]
:

Corollary 1.2
.

Solutions with Bondi mass less than 1 do not form singularities. More precisely, if
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathfrak{M}
has
M<1,
then
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathrm{int}\big(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{non}}\big).
In particular, small perturbations of AdS spacetime are non-collapsing.

This result marks a sharp contrast between AdS spacetime in
2+1
dimensions and AdS spacetime in
3+1
dimensions, since the latter is unstable to black hole formation
[
22
,
8
,
40
,
41
]
. In view of the numerical study of
[
7
]
, it is expected that AdS spacetime in
2+1
dimensions is dynamically unstable and perturbations of it exhibit weak turbulence. Finally, we refer the reader to
[
22

4+1

1.3.2
Formation of trapped surfaces criterion

The second essential ingredient in our argument is a quantitative criterion for trapped surface formation, inspired by the work of Christodoulou
[
12
]
. Intuitively, this result states that if the mass within an annular region bounded by two circles on the outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+}
is large compared to the size of the region, then a trapped surface will form in evolution. We prove the following result in
Section

4
:

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
k\geq 2
, let
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
be the maximal globally hyperbolic development of
C^{k}
characteristic data on the outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+}=\{u=u_{0},\ v_{\Gamma}\leq v\leq\tilde{v}\}
with
r(u_{0},v_{\Gamma})=0,\ r(u_{0},\tilde{v})<\infty.
Assume that there exists an ingoing cone
C_{1}=\{u_{0}\leq u<0,\ v=v_{1}\}
satisfying
r(u,v_{1})\rightarrow 0
as
u\rightarrow 0^{-}
and
m(u,v_{1})\geq 1
for all
u\in[u_{0},0)
. We consider any ingoing cone
C_{2}=\{u\geq u_{0},\ v=v_{2}\}
such that
v_{1}<v_{2}<\tilde{v},
and we denote:

\delta_{0}=\frac{r(u_{0},v_{2})}{r(u_{0},v_{1})}-1,\ \eta_{0}=\frac{m(u_{0},v_{2})-m(u_{0},v_{1})}{|\Lambda|r^{2}(u_{0},v_{2})}.

(1.11)

If
\delta_{0}>0
is small enough and
\eta_{0}>\delta_{0}
, then a marginally trapped surface
(u_{\mathcal{A}},v_{2})
forms on
C_{2}
with:

r(u_{\mathcal{A}},v_{2})\geq\frac{5\delta_{0}/4}{1+\delta_{0}}r(u_{0},v_{2}).

(1.12)

To prove
Theorem

1.3
, we adapt the proof of
[
12
]
to the
(2+1)
-dimensional setting. The main idea is to use the monotonicity properties of the system (
1.1
) in circular symmetry and derive a differential inequality for the quantity
\eta(u,v_{2})=|\Lambda|^{-1}r^{-2}(u,v_{2})\cdot\big(m(u,v_{2})-m(u,v_{1})\big).

Remark 1.2
.

In analogy with
[
12
]
, one might naively expect on dimensional grounds that
\eta_{0}
should be defined as
m(u_{0},v_{2})-m(u_{0},v_{1}).
However, since the cosmological constant
\Lambda
scales like inverse length squared, the quantity
|\Lambda|r^{2}
is dimensionless. Thus, the definition of
\eta_{0}
in (
1.11
) is dimensionally consistent.

1.3.4
The blueshift instability

In

1.4.1
Instability of naked singularities

In the remarkable work
[

, Christodoulou proved a version of the weak cosmic censorship conjecture for the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in

following the formulations in
[

Theorem 1.4

(
[
16
]
)
.

We parametrize initial data for the
(3+1)
-dimensional spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system of equations by a choice of
\vartheta=r\partial_{v}\phi
along an initial outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+},
normalized such that
\partial_{v}r=1/2.
We consider as the space of initial data the space
AC
of all absolutely continuous functions
\vartheta
of finite total variation on the half-line. We denote by
\mathcal{E}\subset AC
the subset consisting of initial data whose maximal development is a locally naked singularity spacetime. Then
\mathcal{E}
has positive codimension in

The present paper is inspired by the strategy of
[
16
]
, so we explain in more detail the common features and key differences of the two works. We begin with some comments regarding the differences in the statements of
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.4
. Firstly, the space of initial data in
Theorem

1.4
has a low-regularity topology, namely
AC
, which is necessary in order to activate the blueshift instability. On the other hand,
Theorem

1.1
holds in a topology of arbitrary smoothness, as the different scaling in our setting allows us to use the blueshift instability in the class of

solutions, for any

Secondly,

1.4
uses a stronger notion of genericity than

1.1
, showing that
\mathcal{E}
has positive codimension in

The argument outlined in

1.3.4

forces us to settle for the notion of genericity in
Theorem

1.1
. For similar reasons,
Theorem

1.1
only proves the non-genericity of naked singularities, rather than locally naked singularities.

We make some further comments regarding the proofs of
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.4
. Firstly, we follow the strategy of
[
16
]
and use the formation of trapped surfaces criterion in two different ways: we use
Theorem

1.3
directly in
Section

6

1.3
in

to get a priori control of the scalar field for any locally naked singularity spacetime. Secondly, both

1.1
and

1.4
rely on the fundamental fact that locally naked singularity spacetimes have unbounded blueshift. In our case, this follows as a consequence of the presence of a mass gap in

1.2

1.3.3
. (Unfortunately, there is no published account of this part of the proof in the case of

1.4

[

15
]

1.4

, see also
[

]
). Thirdly, we follow the idea of
[
16
]
and use the blueshift effect to prove the instability of locally naked singularities. We explain how the two approaches are somewhat different.

derives refined necessary conditions for a spacetime to be a locally naked singularity, in the form of compatibility conditions between the initial data on

and the solution on

We mention some further results regarding the instability of naked singularities for the Einstein-scalar field system in

1.4.2
Previous examples of naked singularities and upcoming work

Constructing examples of naked singularities plays a fundamental role in understanding their general properties and their instability. Moreover, the existence of such examples justifies the need to include genericity conditions in any formulation of the weak cosmic censorship conjecture. We outline some of the previous results on the construction of examples of naked singularities, and we refer the reader to
[
46
,
19
]
for further discussion. We also discuss the upcoming work
[
20
]
.

For the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in
3+1
dimensions,
[
14
]
constructed examples of naked singularities, which were also revisited by
[
47
]
. Notably, the examples of
[
14
]
arise from low regularity initial data (more precisely, in the notation of
Theorem

1.4
,
\vartheta
is only Hölder continuous). However, based on numerical relativity works on critical collapse
[
10
,
29
]
, it is conjectured that there exist naked singularities arising from smooth data on
\partial\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{black}}\cap\partial\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{non}},
which represents the moduli space threshold between black hole formation and non-collapse. Outside of spherical symmetry, the groundbreaking work
[
44
,
45
]
constructed examples of naked singularities for the Einstein vacuum equations in
3+1
dimensions, which also arise from low regularity initial data.

In the case of certain matter models, there are more elementary examples of naked singularities arising from smooth initial data. The first known examples of naked singularities arising in gravitational collapse are due to Christodoulou
[
11
]
for the spherically symmetric Einstein-dust system in
3+1
dimensions. Remarkably,
[
11
]
constructs examples of stable naked singularities, so weak cosmic censorship is false for this model. More recently, the remarkable work
[
30
]
constructed examples of naked singularities for the spherically symmetric Einstein–Euler system in
3+1
dimensions. Since these models are prone to singularity formation even in the absence of gravity, it is not clear however what is the physical significance of these results for the issue of weak cosmic censorship.

In
2+1
dimensions, to date, no rigorous examples of naked singularities for (
1.1
) have been constructed. Similarly to our discussion in the previous paragraph, the numerical relativity works on critical collapse
[
43
,
34
]
suggest that there exist such examples arising from smooth data on
\partial\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{black}}\cap\partial\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{non}}.
In the upcoming work
[
20
]
, we prove a local version of this result and we provide the first rigorous construction of locally naked singularities arising in (local) critical collapse. More precisely, on the finite outgoing cone
C_{0}^{+}=\{u=u_{0},\ 0\leq r<r_{0}\},
we construct a 2-parameter family of

characteristic data
\mathfrak{D}_{\lambda}^{q}
, for any integer

and
\lambda\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon),
which satisfies the following properties:

•

For
\lambda<0,
the development of
\mathfrak{D}_{\lambda}^{q}
does not form singularities.

•

For
\lambda=0,
the development of
\mathfrak{D}_{\lambda}^{q}
is a locally naked singularity spacetime and contains no trapped surfaces. Moreover, the case
(\lambda,q)=(0,4)
corresponds to the numerical examples of
[
43
,
34
]
.

•

For
\lambda>0,
the development of
\mathfrak{D}_{\lambda}^{q}
contains trapped surfaces.

In the above, it is then the
\lambda=0
solutions which represent examples of locally naked singularities arising in (local) critical collapse. An interesting quantitative aspect of the examples of
[

is that for
\lambda=0,
the blueshift defined in (

In the context of the moduli space

It would be an interesting problem to prove that the family

2.1
Equations in double null coordinates

In this section, we introduce global systems of double null coordinates for circularly symmetric spacetimes, which allow us to represent certain causal properties of spacetimes using Penrose diagrams. We also rewrite the Einstein-scalar field system of equations (
1.1
) in double null coordinates.

We first make precise the notion of circular symmetry:

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
is circularly symmetric if the group
U(1)
acts by isometry on
\mathcal{M}
and preserves the scalar field
\phi.
Moreover, we require that:

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{M}/U(1)

inherits the structure of a
1+1
dimensional Lorentzian manifold with boundary. Denoting by
\overline{g}
the metric induced on
\mathcal{Q},
we also require that the metric
g
on
\mathcal{M}
takes the form:

g=\overline{g}+r^{2}d\sigma_{1}^{2},

where
d\sigma_{1}^{2}
is the standard metric on
S^{1}.
Moreover,
r
and
\phi
are functions on
\mathcal{Q},
and
r
is nonnegative.

As a
1+1
dimensional Lorentzian manifold,
\mathcal{Q}
can be globally covered by double null coordinates
(u,v)
. In these coordinates, the induced metric
\overline{g}
takes the form:

\overline{g}=-\Omega^{2}dudv.

Thus, the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
can be covered globally by the double null coordinates
(u,v,\theta):

g=-\Omega^{2}dudv+r^{2}d\theta^{2}.

(2.1)

Since the metric on
\mathcal{Q}
is conformal to that of the
1+1
dimensional Minkowski space, we can represent

\mathcal{Q}
as a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
using Penrose diagrams. This approach offers a canonical way of attaching a boundary to the quotient
\mathcal{Q}
. We point out that we use the topology and causality of the ambient
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
.

Next, we define two components of the boundary of
\mathcal{Q}
: the
center

\Gamma
and
null infinity

\mathcal{I}.
We denote by
\Gamma
the portion of the boundary of
\mathcal{Q}
corresponding to the set of fixed points of the action of
U(1)
, and note that
r=0
on
\Gamma
. We also define the set
\mathcal{U}=\big\{u:\sup_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{Q}}r(u,v)=\infty\big\},
which is nonempty according to
Section

2.3
. We notice that for any
u\in\mathcal{U},
there exists a unique
v_{\mathcal{I}}(u)
such that
\big(u,v_{\mathcal{I}}(u)\big)\in\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\backslash\mathcal{Q}.

Definition 2.2
.

We define null infinity as the set:

\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\big(u,v_{\mathcal{I}}(u)\big).

According to
Section

2.3
, null infinity
\mathcal{I}
is a timelike curve. We also define the future limit point of
\mathcal{I}
to be
i^{+}=\big(\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}(u),\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}v_{\mathcal{I}}(u)\big).

1.1

\displaystyle 2r\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\phi

\displaystyle=-\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}\phi-\partial_{v}r\partial_{u}\phi,

(2.2)

\displaystyle 2\partial_{u}\partial_{v}r

\displaystyle=\Lambda\Omega^{2}r,

(2.3)

\displaystyle 2\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\log\Omega^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\partial_{u}\phi\partial_{v}\phi+\Lambda\Omega^{2},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\big(\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r\big)

\displaystyle=-\Omega^{-2}r(\partial_{u}\phi)^{2},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\big(\Omega^{-2}\partial_{v}r\big)

\displaystyle=-\Omega^{-2}r(\partial_{v}\phi)^{2}.

(2.6)

In double null coordinates, the Hawking mass defined in (
1.10

m(u,v)=4\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r-\Lambda r^{2}+1.

(2.7)

We impose the boundary condition at the center

\displaystyle\partial_{v}m

\displaystyle\partial_{u}m

\displaystyle=-4\Omega^{-2}\partial_{v}r\cdot r(\partial_{u}\phi)^{2}.

(2.9)

2.2
Asymptotically AdS characteristic data

It is essential to have a local well-posedness theory for circularly symmetric solutions of (
1.1
). In compact regions of spacetime, such results are standard in the literature, see for example
[
13
,
24
,
37
]
. The main difficulty lies in dealing with the boundary at infinity, as
\mathcal{I}
represents a timelike asymptotic boundary of spacetime. Our strategy is to adapt the
(3+1)
-dimensional theory of
[
31
]
to our
(2+1)
-dimensional setting. The main goal of this section is to define a suitable notion of asymptotically AdS characteristic data and the moduli space of initial data.

Since we want to prove a well-posedness theory which accounts for the boundary at infinity, the discussion only applies to the
J^{-}(\mathcal{I})
region of spacetime, i.e. the causal past of
\mathcal{I}.
In particular, in the case of black hole spacetimes, our discussion only applies to the exterior region as defined in
Section

2.4
. To emphasize the distinction from the Sections
2.1
and
2.4
, in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
we use the notation
(U,V)
for systems of double null coordinates that are adapted to the region
J^{-}(\mathcal{I})
.

The first goal is to define asymptotically AdS spacetimes. In particular, the definition must apply to the BTZ spacetimes (
1.3
). For the metric (
1.3
), we consider double null coordinates
(U,V)
which are Eddington-Finkelstein normalized as follows:

\frac{\partial_{V}r}{1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}}=\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{\partial_{U}r}{1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2},\ \text{and }U=V\text{ on }\mathcal{I}.

To achieve this, we define
U=t-r_{*},\ V=t+r_{*},
where
r_{*}
satisfies:

dr_{*}=\frac{dr}{1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}},\ \lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r_{*}(r)=0.

With respect to the Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates
(U,V),
the BTZ metric (
1.3
) becomes:

g_{M}=-\Big(1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}\Big)dUdV+r^{2}d\theta^{2}.

(2.13)

We remark that the leading behavior of the lapse at
\mathcal{I}
in (
2.13
) is dictated by the cosmological constant and is independent of the dimension. We adapt the definition of asymptotically AdS spacetimes in
[
31
, Section 2.4]
to our setting:

Definition 2.3
.

We say that a circularly symmetric spacetime
\big(\mathcal{M},g\big)
is asymptotically AdS if for every
p\in\mathcal{I}
there exists a coordinate system
(U,V)
and a neighborhood of
p
in
\mathcal{M}
:

\Big\{V\geq U_{0},\ U\in(V,U_{0}+\delta]\Big\},

such that
U=V
on
\mathcal{I},

\partial_{U}r<0,\ \partial_{V}r>0
and the metric satisfies the asymptotic behavior for some
M\geq 0
:

g=-\Big(1+O\big(r^{-3}\big)\Big)\Big(1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}\Big)dUdV+r^{2}d\theta^{2},

T(\Omega^{2})=O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ R_{*}(\Omega^{2})=O\big(r^{3}\big),\ T(r)=O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ R_{*}(r)=1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}+O\big(r^{-1}\big),

T(\partial_{U}r)=O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ R_{*}(\partial_{U}r)=O\big(r^{3}\big),\ T(\partial_{V}r)=O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ R_{*}(\partial_{V}r)=O\big(r^{3}\big),

where
T=\partial_{U}+\partial_{V},\ R_{*}=\partial_{V}-\partial_{U}.

Our next goal is to define characteristic initial data. We first define the notion of an asymptotically Eddington-Finkelstein coordinate, similarly to
[
31
, Proposition 3.1]
:

Definition 2.4
.

We consider the interval
[0,\infty)
parametrized by
r.
Let
V:[0,\infty)\rightarrow I=\big[V_{\Gamma},V_{\mathcal{I}}\big)
be a coordinate function such that
V(0)=V_{\Gamma},
and
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}V(r)=V_{\mathcal{I}}.
We say that the coordinate
V
on
I
is asymptotically Eddington-Finkelstein if there exists
M\geq 0
such that as
V\rightarrow V_{\mathcal{I}}
:

\partial_{V}r=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}\big)+o\big(r^{-1}\big),\ \partial_{V}\bigg(\frac{\partial_{V}r}{1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}}\bigg)=o(r^{-2}).

(2.14)

We introduce the weighted spaces used in the definition of initial data:

Definition 2.5
.

We define the weighted Sobolev norm
H^{n}\big([0,\infty)\big)
for any
n\geq 0:

\big\|f\big\|_{H^{n}([0,\infty))}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\big(1+r^{2}\big)^{n}\Big(\partial_{r}^{n}f\Big)^{2}+\ldots+\big(1+r^{2}\big)\Big(\partial_{r}f\Big)^{2}+f^{2}\bigg]rdr.

(2.15)

With respect to an asymptotically Eddington-Finkelstein coordinate
V
on
I=\big[V_{\Gamma},V_{\mathcal{I}}\big)
, we define:

\big\|f\big\|_{H^{n}(I)}^{2}=\int_{V_{\Gamma}}^{V_{\mathcal{I}}}\bigg[\big(1+r^{2}\big)^{n}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial_{V}}{\partial_{V}r}\bigg)^{n}f\bigg]^{2}+\ldots+\big(1+r^{2}\big)\bigg(\frac{\partial_{V}f}{\partial_{V}r}\bigg)^{2}+f^{2}\bigg]r(V)\partial_{V}rdV.

(2.16)

We define asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets, similarly to
[
31
, Definition 3.3]
:

Definition 2.6

(Asymptotically AdS characteristic data)
.

For any
k\geq 2,
we say that the tuple:

\mathfrak{D}=\big(r,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k+1}r,\Omega,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}\Omega,m,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}m,\phi,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}\phi,\mathcal{T}\phi,\ldots,\mathcal{T}^{k}\phi\big)

represents a
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data set if there exists
M\geq 0
and an asymptotically Eddington-Finkelstein coordinate
V
on
I=\big[V_{\Gamma},V_{\mathcal{I}}\big)
satisfying (
2.14
), such that the following hold:

1.

Regularity conditions:

\partial_{U}^{i}r\in C^{k+1-i}(I),i=\overline{0,k+1},\ \partial_{U}^{j}\Omega,\partial_{U}^{j}m\in C^{k-j}(I),j=\overline{0,k},

(2.17)

\phi\in H^{k+1}(I),\mathcal{T}\phi\in H^{k}(I),\ldots,\mathcal{T}^{k}\phi\in H^{1}(I),

(2.18)

\partial_{U}^{i}\phi\in C^{k-1-i}(I),i=\overline{0,k-1},\ \sqrt{r}\partial_{V}^{k-j}\partial_{U}^{j}\phi\in C^{0}(I),j=\overline{0,k}.

(2.19)

2.

Compatibility conditions: the elements of
\mathfrak{D}
satisfy the compatibility conditions obtained by differentiating equations (
2.2
)-(
2.9
) and taking the Kodama vector field
\mathcal{T}
to be given by:

\mathcal{T}=\Omega^{-2}\partial_{V}r\partial_{U}-\Omega^{-2}\partial_{U}r\partial_{V}.

(2.20)

3.

No trapped or anti-trapped surfaces:
\partial_{V}r>0,\ \partial_{U}r<0
on
I.

4.

Boundary conditions at
\Gamma:
for any
0\leq i\leq k+1,\ 0\leq j\leq k,
and
0\leq l\leq k-2,
we have that
\mathcal{T}^{i}r(V_{\Gamma})=0,\ \mathcal{T}^{j}m(V_{\Gamma})=0,

\mathcal{T}^{l}(\Omega^{-2}\partial_{V}r\partial_{U}\phi+\Omega^{-2}\partial_{U}r\partial_{V}\phi)(V_{\Gamma})=0.

5.

Boundary conditions at
\mathcal{I}:

\lim_{V\rightarrow V_{\mathcal{I}}}m(V)=M.

6.

Asymptotic behavior as
V\rightarrow V_{\mathcal{I}}
:

•

r
satisfies (
2.14
) and for
0\leq i\leq k+1:

\partial_{V}^{i}r=O\big(r^{i+1}\big).

(2.21)

•

\partial_{U}r
satisfies the asymptotics for
0\leq i\leq k+1:

\partial_{U}r=-\frac{1}{2}\big(1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}\big)+O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ \partial_{V}^{i}\partial_{U}r=O\big(r^{i+2}\big).

(2.22)

•

\Omega
satisfies the asymptotics for
0\leq i\leq k:

\Omega^{2}=1-M+|\Lambda|r^{2}+O\big(r^{-1}\big),\ \partial_{V}^{i}\Omega^{2}=O\big(r^{i+2}\big).

(2.23)

•

The scalar field satisfies the asymptotics for
0\leq i+j\leq k
:

\partial_{V}^{i}\partial_{U}^{j}\phi=O\big(r^{i+j-\frac{3}{2}}\big).

(2.24)

Remark 2.1
.

In
Definition

2.6
we adapted
[
31
, Definition 3.3]
from the
(3+1)
-dimensional case to the
(2+1)
-dimensional case, so we highlight some of the differences:

1.

The initial data in our case models the setting of gravitational collapse, so it extends from
\Gamma
to
\mathcal{I},
whereas the definition of
[
31
]
is localized to a neighborhood of
\mathcal{I}.
Thus, we also prescribe regularity conditions at
\Gamma.
Moreover, in view of the analysis of (
2.2
) near
\Gamma
in
(2+1)
-dimensions, for the scalar field it is convenient to assume Sobolev regularity at top order as in (
2.18
), which can be propagated by energy estimates, instead of only assuming the pointwise regularity conditions in (
2.19
).

2.

The initial data in
Definition

2.6
is prescribed on an outgoing null cone, whereas
[
31
]
prescribes initial data on an ingoing null cone. Therefore, unlike
[
31
]
, we cannot enforce the conditions in (
2.18
)-(
2.19
) by simply requiring suitable decay rates for
\phi,
so we need to impose quantitative conditions on
\mathcal{T}\phi,\ldots,\mathcal{T}^{k}\phi
. A similar approach is taken by
[
32
]
in the setting of spacelike initial data, see
[
32
, Remark 5.1]
.

3.

The notion of a characteristic data set
\mathfrak{D}
is over-determined, subject to the compatibility conditions and the boundary conditions at the center. Alternatively, one could attempt to prescribe only the function
\phi
as initial data (referred to as seed data in the literature, see for example
[
17
]
in the setting of the Einstein vacuum equations in
3+1
dimensions), similarly to
[
31
]
. However, since in our setting the initial data is prescribed on an outgoing null cone, (
2.18
) imposes nonlinear and non-local constraints on
\phi
, see already
Remark

A.3
, so the initial data conditions are stated more naturally for
\mathfrak{D}
.

4.

We make assumptions on the asymptotic rates up to order
k\geq 2
, whereas
[
31
]
only requires asymptotic rates up to order
2.

Remark 2.2
.

Equation (
2.18
) represents the main quantitative condition in the definition of asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets and motivates our definition of a distance function below. In particular, (
2.18
) implies (
2.19
) by Sobolev embedding. Moreover, (
2.18
) implies that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}m(r)
exists and that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{T}m(r)=0,
which are consistent with the reflective boundary conditions at infinity
m_{\mathcal{I}}=M.

The aim for the rest of the section is to define the moduli space of initial data. We define the following quantity measuring the size of asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets, which is independent of the choice of
(U,V)
coordinates:

Definition 2.7
.

We consider a
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data set
\mathfrak{D}
. We define:

\big\|\mathfrak{D}\big\|_{X([0,\infty))}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\phi\big\|_{H^{k+1-i}([0,\infty))}.

(2.25)

We note that the space of asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets is not a vector space, since the compatibility conditions in
Definition

2.6
are nonlinear, so
X([0,\infty))
is not a norm. In the asymptotically flat case, the solution to this issue is to consider the linear space of seed data consisting of
\phi
instead (see for example
[
16
]
). However, as explained in
Remark

A.3
, for asymptotically AdS characteristic data equation (
2.18
) imposes nonlinear constraints on
\phi
already. Nevertheless, we use
X([0,\infty))
to define a distance function as follows:

Definition 2.8
.

For any two
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime},
we define the distance function:

d_{X([0,\infty))}(\mathfrak{D},\mathfrak{D}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\phi-\big(\mathcal{T}^{i}\phi\big)^{\prime}\Big\|_{H^{k+1-i}([0,\infty))}.

(2.26)

We note that any two asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets satisfying
d_{X([0,\infty))}(\mathfrak{D},\mathfrak{D}^{\prime})=0
are related by a change of double null coordinates
U\rightarrow\tilde{U}(U),V\rightarrow\tilde{V}(V)
. If this holds, we denote
\mathfrak{D}\sim\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
, and we notice that
\sim
defines an equivalence relation on the space of
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data sets.

We can finally use the distance function (
2.26
) to define a topology on the set of initial data. More specifically, we define the moduli space of
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data as follows:

Definition 2.9
.

We define the moduli space of
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data as the metric space
\big(\mathfrak{M},d_{X([0,\infty))}\big),
where
\mathfrak{M}
is given by:

\mathfrak{M}=\big\{\mathfrak{D}:\ \mathfrak{D}\text{ is a }C^{k}\text{ asymptotically AdS characteristic data set}\big\}/_{\sim}.

2.3
Local well-posedness theory

We state the main local well-posedness result, which applies to asymptotically AdS characteristic data as in
Definition

2.6
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer.
For any
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data set
\mathfrak{D}=\big(r,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k+1}r,\Omega,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}\Omega,m,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}m,\phi,\ldots,\partial_{U}^{k}\phi,\mathcal{T}\phi,\ldots,\mathcal{T}^{k}\phi\big)
on the outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+}=\big\{V_{\mathcal{I}}\big\}\times\big[V_{\Gamma},V_{\mathcal{I}}\big)
, there exists
\delta>0
and a unique solution
\big(r,\Omega,\phi\big)
of the equations (
2.2
)-(
2.6
) with reflective boundary conditions at infinity
m_{\mathcal{I}}=M,
which is defined in the domain:

\mathcal{Q}=\Big\{(U,V):\ U\in\big[V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta\big],\ V\in\big[V_{\Gamma}(U),U\big)\Big\},

where we have that the center
\Gamma
and null infinity
\mathcal{I}
given by:

\Gamma=\Big\{\big(U,V_{\Gamma}(U)\big):\ U\in\big[V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta\big]\Big\},\ \mathcal{I}=\Big\{\big(U,U\big):\ U\in\big[V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta\big]\Big\}

represent timelike curves. Moreover, the corresponding circularly symmetric spacetime
\big(\mathcal{M},g\big)
is asymptotically AdS as in
Definition

2.3
, and for each
U\in\big[V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta\big]
it induces
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data on
\big\{U\big\}\times\big[V_{\Gamma}(U),U\big]
as in
Definition

2.6
.

The proof of
Proposition

2.1
uses standard methods and follows in detail the steps of
[
31
]
, so we do not repeat it here. We remark that one can also establish further standard properties, such as persistence of regularity and Cauchy stability. To illustrate how the methods of
[
31
]
are adapted to our setting, we prove a priori estimates for
C^{2}
solutions (in the sense of
Definition

2.6
) of the linear wave equation on asymptotically AdS spacetimes in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{I}
. It is clear that using the methods of
[
31
]
and a propagation of regularity argument, one can turn this proof into the local well-posedness result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\big(\mathcal{M},g\big)
be a circularly symmetric asymptotically AdS spacetime. We consider a coordinate system
(U,V)
and the domain:

\mathcal{D}=\Big\{(U,V):\ U\in\big[V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta\big],\ V\in\big[V_{0},U\big),\ \ V_{0}<V_{\mathcal{I}}\Big\},

such that the asymptotics in
Definition

2.3
hold in
\mathcal{D}
, and similar rates hold for higher order derivatives of
\Omega^{2}
and
r.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
solution of the wave equation with reflective boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}\square_{g}\psi=0,\\
\psi=0,\ \text{ on }\mathcal{I},\end{cases}

(2.27)

which satisfies the bound on
\mathcal{D}\cap\big\{U=V_{\mathcal{I}}\big\}
and
\mathcal{D}\cap\big\{V=V_{0}\big\}
:

\sum_{i=0}^{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\psi\big\|_{H^{3-i}\big(\{V_{\mathcal{I}}\}\times[V_{0},V_{\mathcal{I}})\big)}+\sum_{i=0}^{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\psi\big\|_{H^{3-i}\big([V_{\mathcal{I}},V_{\mathcal{I}}+\delta)\times\{V_{0}\}\big)}<C_{0}.

(2.28)

Then, we have that
\psi
satisfies the following estimates for any
\big(U,V\big)\in\mathcal{D}:

\sum_{i=0}^{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\psi\big\|_{H^{3-i}\big(\{U\}\times[V_{0},V]\big)}+\sum_{i=0}^{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{i}\psi\big\|_{H^{3-i}\big([\max(V,V_{\mathcal{I}}),U]\times\{V\}\big)}<C,

(2.29)

r^{3/2}|\psi|+r^{1/2}|\partial_{V}\psi|+r^{1/2}|\partial_{U}\psi|+r^{-1/2}|\partial_{V}^{2}\psi|+r^{-1/2}|\partial_{U}^{2}\psi|<C,

(2.30)

where the constant
C
depends on
C_{0}
and the background
g.

Proof.

We follow the same steps as
[
32
]
and
[
31
, Proposition 6.4]
. The starting point is the following approximate conservation law derived from (
2.2
):

\partial_{U}\Big(r(\partial_{V}\psi)^{2}\Big)+\partial_{V}\Big(r(\partial_{U}\psi)^{2}\Big)=-\partial_{U}\psi\partial_{V}\psi\cdot Tr,

where
T=\partial_{U}+\partial_{V}.
We integrate this equation in the domain
\mathcal{D}\cap\big\{U^{\prime}\leq U,\ V^{\prime}\leq V\big\}
using the reflective boundary conditions, which imply that the flux terms on
\mathcal{I}
vanish. The decay of
Tr
in
Definition

2.3
implies that the terms on the RHS are lower order, so we obtain the estimate:

\int_{V_{0}}^{V}r\big(\partial_{V}\psi\big)^{2}\big(U,V^{\prime}\big)dV^{\prime}+\int_{\max(V,V_{\mathcal{I}})}^{U}r\big(\partial_{U}\psi\big)^{2}\big(U^{\prime},V\big)dU^{\prime}<C.

(2.31)

Next, we commute (
2.2
) by
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{2}
. Repeating the same proof, we obtain that (
2.31
) also holds for
\mathcal{T}\psi
and
\mathcal{T}^{2}\psi.

The estimate (
2.31
) for
\psi,\mathcal{T}\psi,\mathcal{T}^{2}\psi,
and the reflective boundary conditions also imply the pointwise estimate
r|\psi|+r|\mathcal{T}\psi|+r|\mathcal{T}^{2}\psi|\leq C.
Furthermore, we have the following Hardy inequality for any function
f
such that the RHS of (
2.32
) is finite:

r^{2}f^{2}+\int_{r}^{\infty}\tilde{r}f^{2}d\tilde{r}\leq\lim_{\tilde{r}\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{r}^{2}f^{2}+\int_{r}^{\infty}\tilde{r}^{3}\big(\partial_{r}f\big)^{2}d\tilde{r}.

(2.32)

Using (
2.32
) for
\psi,\mathcal{T}\psi,
and
\mathcal{T}^{2}\psi
, we control the
H^{1}
norms in (
2.29
). Using (
2.2
) as well, the
H^{1}
bounds further imply that (
2.29
) holds. Moreover, we then get the pointwise estimates
|\partial_{U}\psi|+|\partial_{V}\psi|+|\partial_{U}\mathcal{T}\psi|+|\partial_{V}\mathcal{T}\psi|\leq C
and
|\partial_{U}^{2}\psi|+|\partial_{U}\partial_{V}\psi|+|\partial_{V}^{2}\psi|\leq Cr.

For the rest of the proof we show the improved pointwise estimate (
2.30
). We define the quantity:

\zeta=\sqrt{r}\frac{\partial_{U}\psi}{\partial_{U}r}.

We compute that:

\partial_{V}\zeta=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\partial_{V}\psi-2|\Lambda|r\cdot\frac{\partial_{V}r}{|\Lambda|r^{2}-m+1}\cdot\zeta=-\frac{T\psi}{2\sqrt{r}}+\bigg(\frac{\partial_{U}r}{2r^{2}}-\frac{2|\Lambda|\partial_{V}r}{|\Lambda|r^{2}-m+1}\bigg)r\zeta.

We define
A=r^{n}\zeta,
which satisfies the equation:

\partial_{V}A=|\Lambda|rA\bigg(\frac{n\partial_{V}r}{|\Lambda|r^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{U}r}{2|\Lambda|r^{2}}-\frac{2\partial_{V}r}{|\Lambda|r^{2}-m+1}\bigg)-r^{n}\frac{T\psi}{2\sqrt{r}}.

We take
n=2,
which results in having a bounded integrating factor. The remaining term on the RHS is lower order and can be controlled using the previous pointwise estimates. Therefore,
A
is bounded, so we obtain the improved estimate
r^{1/2}|\partial_{U}\psi|\leq C.
The pointwise bound for
\mathcal{T}\psi
also implies that
r^{1/2}|\partial_{V}\psi|\leq C
and
r^{3/2}|\psi|\leq C.
Using the wave equation (
2.2
), we also have that
r^{-1/2}|\partial_{U}\partial_{V}\psi|\leq C
. The bound
|\partial_{V}\mathcal{T}\psi|+|\partial_{U}\mathcal{T}\psi|\leq C
then implies
|\partial_{U}^{2}\psi|+|\partial_{V}^{2}\psi|\leq Cr^{1/2}.

∎

2.4
Global structure of spacetimes

In this section, we state some general properties of circularly symmetric solutions of (
1.1
), including: monotonicity properties, a description of first singularities, the existence of maximal globally hyperbolic developments, properties of black hole spacetimes, a description of the general structure of the boundary of spacetimes arising in gravitational collapse, and the decomposition of the moduli space of initial data. The results of this section are analogous to the
(3+1)
-dimensional setting, see for example
[
24
,
25
,
31
,
37
]
. Adapting the proofs to the
(2+1)
-dimensional setting is straightforward, so we simply record the results needed for the rest of the paper.

Monotonicity properties.

We first state some important monotonicity properties, which follow directly from (
2.3
), (
2.9
), (
2.8
), and the boundary conditions at
\Gamma:

Lemma 2.1
.

In
\mathcal{Q}
we have that:

\partial_{u}r<0,\ \partial_{u}\partial_{v}r\leq 0,\ m\geq 0,\ \partial_{v}m\geq 0.

(2.33)

Moreover,
\partial_{v}r
and
\partial_{u}m
have opposite signs. Additionally,
\partial_{u}\partial_{v}r<0
on
\mathcal{Q}\backslash\Gamma
. Finally, in the causal past of
\Gamma
we have
\partial_{v}r>0.

Depending on the behavior of
r,
we divide the spacetime
\mathcal{Q}
into the following regions:

Definition 2.10
.

We define the regular, trapped and marginally trapped regions of
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{Q}:\ \partial_{v}r(p)>0\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{Q}:\ \partial_{v}r(p)<0\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{Q}:\ \partial_{v}r(p)=0\Big\}.

We record some simple properties of the trapped region, which follow directly from (
2.6
),(
2.7
), and the monotonicity properties in (
2.33
):

Lemma 2.2
.

We have an equivalent characterization of the trapped region in terms of the Hawking mass:

p\in\mathcal{T}\Longleftrightarrow m(p)>1-\Lambda r^{2}(p).

(2.34)

Moreover, the trapped region is a future set: if
p\in\mathcal{T}
, then
J^{+}(p)\cap\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{T}.
Similarly, if
p\in\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{A}
, then
J^{+}(p)\cap\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{A}
and
J^{+}(p)\cap\mathcal{Q}\backslash\{p\}\subset\mathcal{T}.

First singularities.

We state the description of first singularities away from the center of
[
37
, Theorem 1.8]
(see also
[
31
, Proposition A.1]
), adapted to our setting:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
p\in\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\backslash\mathcal{Q}
be a first singularity away from the center, i.e.
p\notin\overline{\Gamma}\backslash\Gamma.
For any
q\in\big(I^{-}(p)\cap\mathcal{Q}\big)\backslash\{p\}
such that
\mathcal{D}=\big(J^{+}(q)\cap J^{-}(p)\big)\backslash\{p\}\subset\mathcal{Q}
we have that
\inf_{r\in\mathcal{D}}=0.

The proof of this result follows the same steps as
[
37
, Theorem 1.8]
, but is greatly simplified in our case due to the structure of equation (
2.3
). In particular,
Proposition

2.3
also implies that every first singularity away from the center must be preceded by trapped surfaces, which is analogous to the result of
[
24
]
. As a consequence, every first singularity in the regular region must occur at the center:

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
p\in\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\backslash\mathcal{Q}
be a first singularity such that
J^{-}(p)\backslash\{p\}\subset\mathcal{R}.
Then
p\in\overline{\Gamma}\backslash\Gamma.

Existence of maximal globally hyperbolic developments.

In view of the timelike boundary at infinity, we modify the usual definition of global hyperbolicity as follows, similarly to
[
31
, Definition 8.1]
:

Definition 2.11
.

An asymptotically AdS spacetime
\big(\mathcal{M},g\big)
is globally hyperbolic if there exists an outgoing null cone
C_{0}^{+}
such that
C_{0}^{+}\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset,\ \overline{C_{0}^{+}}\cap\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
and all past directed inextendible causal curves in
\mathcal{M}
either intersect
C_{0}^{+}
or have a unique limit endpoint on
\mathcal{I}
.

It is standard to use the local well-posedness result in
Proposition

2.1
to prove the existence of a unique maximal globally hyperbolic development of initial data. We refer the reader to
[
31
, Section 4]
and the references therein.

Proposition 2.4
.

For any
k\geq 2
and
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data set
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathfrak{M}
, there exists a maximal globally hyperbolic development
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
of the initial data, which is unique up to isometry.

Black hole spacetimes.

As a consequence of the monotonicity properties established so far, we have that
J^{-}(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{R}.
Thus, the black hole region
\mathcal{BH}=\mathcal{Q}\backslash J^{-}(\mathcal{I})
satisfies
\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{BH}.
In particular, if
\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset
then
\mathcal{BH}\neq\emptyset.
In the case when
\mathcal{BH}\neq\emptyset,
we define the event horizon
\mathcal{H}^{+}
as the future boundary of
J^{-}(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathcal{Q}
in
\mathcal{Q}.

We state the following result for black hole spacetimes, similarly to
[
25
]
. For the proof one adapts the arguments of
[
23
, Section 5]
and
[
31
, Section 8.2]
to the
(2+1)
-dimensional setting, once again using the good monotonicity properties in our system.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\big(\mathcal{M},g,\phi\big)
be the maximal globally hyperbolic development of a
C^{k}
asymptotically AdS characteristic data set with
k\geq 2
, and denote
M=m|_{\mathcal{I}}.
If
\mathcal{BH}\neq\emptyset,
then
\mathcal{H}^{+}
is future complete and satisfies the Penrose inequality
|\Lambda|r^{2}+1\leq M
. Moreover, in this case we also have that
\mathcal{I}
is future complete.

Global structure of spacetimes.

We conclude the section by explaining the general structure of the boundary of spacetimes, which allows us to state the decomposition of the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}
in (
1.6
). We first define the other possible components of the boundary, as in
[
15
,
37
]
.

Definition 2.12
.

We denote
b_{\Gamma}=\overline{\Gamma}\backslash\Gamma.
If
b_{\Gamma}\neq i^{+}
, we say that
b_{\Gamma}
is a first singularity at the center. In this case, we define the central component of the singular boundary:

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\Big\{(u_{b_{\Gamma}},v)\in\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\Big\}.

Finally, we define the singular component of the boundary:

\mathcal{B}=\Big\{(u,v)\in\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\backslash\mathcal{Q}:\ u_{i^{+}}<u<u_{b_{\Gamma}}\Big\}.

Using the description of first singularities in
Proposition

2.3
and the monotonicity properties (
2.33
), it is straightforward to show that
\mathcal{B}
is spacelike and
r=0
on
\mathcal{B}
. Similarly to the
(3+1)
-dimensional case (see
[
