Implant-supported oral rehabilitation after long-term stabilization of cherubism: a 23-year follow-up case report
Authors: Mergime P. Loxha, Vjosa H. Krasniqi, Kaltrina Kryeziu, Fatos Salihamixhiqi, David Stubljar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cherubism is a rare fibro-osseous disorder of childhood, characterized by bilateral jaw expansion, frequently resulting in dental abnormalities and functional impairment.Materials and methods:We report a 23-year follow-up of a female patient diagnosed with cherubism in childhood.Results:After long-term monitoring and confirmation of disease stabilization, implant-supported oral rehabilitation was performed using guided surgery without bone grafting. Favorable osseointegration and stable prosthetic outcomes were achieved, restoring oral function and facial esthetics.Conclusion:This case highlights the importance of long-term monitoring, careful timing of intervention after skeletal maturation, and individualized treatment planning. Implant-supported rehabilitation may represent a viable option for selected patients with stabilized cherubism.

Introduction

Cherubism is a rare, benign fibro-osseous disorder of childhood characterized by bilateral expansion of the jaws and the typical cherub-like facial appearance. The condition most commonly follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and is associated with pathogenic variants of the SH3BP2 gene in the majority of genetically characterized cases[1,2]. Histologically, the lesions consist of fibrous connective tissue containing multinucleated giant cells. Clinically, the disease usually manifests between 2 and 7 years of age, progresses during childhood, and frequently stabilizes or regresses after puberty. Management is generally individualized depending on disease severity and progression[3]. Several classification systems have been proposed to describe the extent and severity of cherubism. One commonly used grading system distinguishes three levels: Grade 1 involves bilateral enlargement limited to the mandibular rami; Grade 2 includes involvement of the rami, mandibular body, and maxillary tuberosities; and Grade 3 is characterized by extensive lesions affecting both jaws and resulting in marked facial changes[5]. Later, Raposo-Amaral and colleagues[6] expanded Motamedi’s system for cases showing orbital involvement. Although some authors rely on these grading scales to quantify disease extent, others avoid formal classification because the clinical presentation of cherubism varies widely among affected individuals[7]. From a clinical standpoint, the key aspects for evaluation remain the biological and behavioral characteristics of the lesions, specifically their growth dynamics, overall size, degree of cortical bone thinning or perforation, displacement of teeth, and the resulting functional impairments[7,8].

The influence of cherubism lesions on the formation and eruption of both primary and permanent teeth depends largely on the patient’s age at onset and the severity of the expansile bone changes. Primary dentition may exhibit altered alignment or spacing[9,10], whereas permanent teeth can demonstrate a range of abnormalities, including agenesis (most commonly affecting molars), underdeveloped or malformed molars, irregular tooth morphology, partial root resorption, and delayed or ectopic eruption[9,11]. In certain cases, tooth extraction becomes necessary, particularly when teeth appear “free-floating” within the cherubic lesions[5] or remain impacted in abnormal positions[11]. For patients with more severe involvement, dental prostheses may be required and periodically adjusted as growth continues or as intraoral swelling fluctuates, thereby improving chewing efficiency and enhancing self-esteem. Orthodontic management is typically reserved for the post-growth period and is initiated during the regression phase of cherubism to achieve optimal functional and esthetic outcomes[7].

Given the established success of dental implants in prosthetic rehabilitation after disease stabilization, we present a case of a female patient with cherubism who was followed over a 23-year period and underwent implant-supported rehabilitation.

Case report

This report represents a follow-up of our sporadic patient, who was not immediately detected and was diagnosed with cherubism at the age of 11[12]. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE checklist[13]. The patient initially presented at the age of 8 with cystic lesions in the anterior maxilla, which were initially diagnosed as central giant cell granuloma. Following multiple recurrences and progressive involvement of both jaws, the diagnosis of cherubism was established[12]. The patient was subsequently monitored with regular clinical examinations and radiographic follow-up, including panoramic radiography and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), at intervals of 6–12 months until the age of 16. During this period, no further disease progression was observed, and cyst formation ceased. However, significant mandibular tooth loss resulted in functional and esthetic impairment.

At the age of 30, the patient returned seeking prosthetic rehabilitation. She had not previously received any prosthetic treatment. CBCT imaging was performed and compared with earlier radiographs, confirming that the disease had remained stable without evidence of progression. Minor residual changes were observed in the maxilla, while the mandible showed no active pathological lesions. Laboratory tests, including alkaline phosphatase, calcium, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D levels, were within normal limits.

Given the ongoing debate regarding bone grafting in cherubism patients, a decision was made to place four dental implants in the mandible without prior grafting, using guided implant surgery. Digital treatment planning was performed using CBCT data and intraoral scans. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data were imported into implant planning software to allow prosthetically driven virtual implant positioning based on available bone volume, anatomical structures, and prosthetic requirements. A tooth-supported Computer-Aided Design and Computer-Aided Manufacturing surgical guide was fabricated to ensure accurate implant placement.

Implant surgery was performed under local anesthesia (articaine with epinephrine 1:100 000). A minimally invasive flapless approach was selected due to sufficient keratinized tissue and adequate bone width, as confirmed during digital planning. The surgical guide was positioned intraorally and stabilized, and sequential guided drilling was performed according to the manufacturer’s drilling protocol under copious sterile saline irrigation to prevent thermal bone injury. Four bone-level implants were placed in sites 35 (11.5 × 4.0 mm), 32 (10 × 4.0 mm), 42 (10 × 4.0 mm), and 45 (11.5 × 4.0 mm) (Fig. 1). Primary stability was achieved in all implant sites, with insertion torque values exceeding 35 Ncm. No intraoperative complications were observed. Multi-unit abutments were placed during the same surgical procedure to support a screw-retained provisional prosthesis. Owing to the high primary stability, immediate loading was performed within 48 hours. The provisional prosthesis was designed with reduced occlusal loading to minimize functional stress during osseointegration.

Postoperative care included antibiotics (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for 5–7 days), analgesics as needed, and chlorhexidine mouth rinses for 10–14 days. The patient was advised to follow a soft diet and maintain adequate oral hygiene. Healing was uneventful, with no signs of infection, pain, or implant mobility. Four months after surgery, CBCT evaluation confirmed stable peri-implant bone levels and successful osseointegration. A definitive screw-retained hybrid prosthesis was subsequently fabricated and delivered (Fig. 2). Discussion

Cherubism is a rare fibro-osseous condition that primarily affects the jaws during childhood and often stabilizes after puberty. Although spontaneous regression is common, residual skeletal deformities and dental abnormalities may persist into adulthood and require rehabilitative treatment[7,14].

In the present case, diagnosis was initially difficult due to the early presentation mimicking central giant cell granuloma. Only after progression and comprehensive radiographic evaluation was cherubism confirmed. Interestingly, genetic testing did not reveal a mutation in the SH3BP2 gene, which is typically involved in hereditary cases on chromosome 4p16.3[12,15–17]. The absence of a detectable SH3BP2 mutation supports the classification of this case as sporadic, consistent with literature indicating that approximately 20% of cases lack identifiable hereditary patterns[18].

Based on the classification proposed by Motamedi[5] and expanded by Raposo-Amaral[6], the clinical presentation corresponded to Grade 3 cherubism, with bilateral involvement of both jaws and facial asymmetry. As commonly reported, the disease stabilized after adolescence and remained inactive in adulthood[17].

One of the major clinical challenges in cherubism is the disruption of normal dental development. Delayed eruption, missing teeth, and abnormal tooth positioning frequently occur and may result in functional and esthetic problems[7,14,17]. In the present case, the patient had lost most of her mandibular dentition by early adulthood, highlighting the need for prosthetic rehabilitation and multidisciplinary management[5,7,12,19].

Implant placement in cherubism patients remains controversial because of potential alterations in bone remodeling and healing. However, several reports suggest that implants may be successfully placed in selected patients after disease stabilization[5,7]. In the present case, a conservative approach was adopted, avoiding bone grafting and utilizing guided implant surgery. This strategy resulted in favorable osseointegration and stable prosthetic rehabilitation during the short-term follow-up period. Similar outcomes have been reported by Dewan et al[20], who described successful implant-supported rehabilitation in an adult cherubism patient using guided planning and early loading protocols. Their findings emphasize the importance of careful case selection and precise surgical planning. In agreement with these observations, implant therapy in our patient was delayed until long-term radiographic and clinical stability had been confirmed.

This case provides long-term insight into the natural history of cherubism over a 23-year observation period and demonstrates the feasibility of implant-supported rehabilitation after disease stabilization. Unlike reports focusing on surgical intervention during the active phase of the disease, our approach emphasized long-term monitoring and delayed rehabilitation.

Importantly, the present findings suggest that bone quality in stabilized cherubism may be sufficient to support implant osseointegration when treatment is carefully planned. Therefore, implant therapy should not be considered absolutely contraindicated but may represent a viable option in selected cases.

Limitations

This report describes a single case with limited post-prosthetic follow-up. Longer observation periods are required to confirm long-term implant survival in patients with cherubism. Additionally, the absence of an identified genetic mutation limits genotype–phenotype correlation. Further multicenter studies are needed to establish evidence-based guidelines for implant therapy in this rare condition.

Conclusion

Long-term monitoring is essential in patients with cherubism, as disease stabilization after skeletal maturity may allow definitive oral rehabilitation. Implant-supported restoration can be considered in carefully selected patients once clinical and radiographic quiescence is confirmed. Careful treatment planning, appropriate timing of intervention, and individualized risk assessment are critical for achieving predictable functional and esthetic outcomes. This report supports a cautious but proactive approach to implant therapy in stabilized cherubism.
