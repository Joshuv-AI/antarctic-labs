Omakase : proactive assistance with actionable suggestions for evolving scientific research projects
Pao Siangliulue, Jonathan Bragg, Doug Downey, Joseph Chee Chang, Daniel S. Weld

Abstract
. As AI agents become increasingly capable of complex knowledge tasks, the lack of context limits their capability to proactively reason about a user’s latent needs throughout a long evolving project. In scientific research, many researchers still manually query a deep research system and compress their rich project contexts into short, targeted queries. Further, a deep research system produces exhaustive reports, making it difficult to identify concrete actions. To explore the opportunities of research assistants that are proactive throughout a research project, we conducted several studies ( N = 42 N=42 ) with a technology probe and an iterative prototype. The latest iteration of our system, Omakase , is a research assistant that monitors a user’s project documents to infer timely queries to a deep research system. Omakase then distills long reports into suggestions contextualized to their evolving projects. Our evaluations showed that participants found the generated queries to be useful and timely, and rated Omakase ’s suggestions as significantly more actionable than the original reports.

Introduction

AI agents can now complete complex tasks across domains, ranging from coding
(
8
;
4
;
40
)
to design
(

)
to scientific discovery
(
34
;
14

20
)
. These advanced agents have shown high-level autonomy in completing complex tasks by breaking them down into smaller steps, and synthesizing all the intermediate results into a coherent output.
However, as their capabilities rapidly grow, user interactions with them remain largely the same traditional request-response paradigm. Most agent systems only
react
to explicit instructions from users before taking action, either in single-turn queries or multi-turn conversations
(
4
;
2
;
40
)
.
The vision of proactive agents that can take initiative to assist users is still not fully realized
(
39
;
17
)
; most prior work on proactive agents primarily investigated proactive interactions within short conversation sessions instead of long-horizon interactions
(
43
)
. For example, asking clarification questions
(
41
)
, getting the user’s attention for confirming high-stake steps or fixing agent-failures
(
38
;
13
)
, or chiming in a multi-user conversation
(
56
;
33
;
55
)
.

In this paper, we explore an alternative interaction paradigm of supporting proactivity over long-horizon interactions with AI agents. More specifically, we study this in the context of designing a research support agent that observes a researcher’s evolving project document, and
proactively
provides literature-grounded suggestions that are
contextualized
to the rich project context. Literature-based research support is well-suited for this exploration due to the increased user interests in leveraging LLMs as research tools
(
30
;
36
)
and the rich prior work on building literature-based research support capabilities
(
28
;
22
;
5
)
.

The two most common user interaction paradigms for literature-based research support are 1) Querying a deep research system that leverages LLM’s reasoning powers to synthesize many retrieved documents; and 2) Recommender systems that proactively send paper recommendations to their users based on a list of seed papers.

Deep research systems (e.g.,
(
49
)
) have the potential of leveraging LLMs to understand the user’s rich ongoing context. However, the dominant request-response paradigm can be prohibitively laborious for users in two ways.
First, it can be effortful to
compress
the rich context of a complex project down to detailed instructions. Recent findings indicate that, while users increasingly add more “Complex Contextual Narratives” to their queries to a literature review AI agent
(
15
)
, providing context to an AI agent could be more effortful
(
43
)
.
Second, the lengthy and comprehensive output of LLMs can be overwhelming for users, requiring substantial cognitive effort to turn them into concrete
actionable
suggestions—that is, suggestions that propose a specific course of action such as citing a paper for a claim, adopting a baseline, or revising a hypothesis
(
21
;
10
;
23
;
52
)
.

In contrast, paper recommender systems proactively send users recommendations of papers similar to a user’s curated seed papers.
While this proactive approach alleviates the burden of constant query formulation, paper recommender systems also have limitations. First,
the static lists of seed papers can quickly go stale as the project’s direction and user interests shift
(
57
)
. Prior work has showed that users rarely update their preferences on recommendation systems, leading to stagnated feeds with similar and repetitive recommendations, causing user frustration and abandonment
(
57
)
. Even when a recommended paper is highly useful for a project,
how
it is useful may be buried in a monotonous list of recommendations and the full text of paper, requiring high effort for researchers to realize their usefulness
(
22
)
. For example, a paper might inform a researcher on how to design their user study, but this can easily be overlooked when receiving a paper recommendation email that typically lists only the titles and abstracts of the recommended papers.

These evidences point to a future where agents observe and leverage a user’s actively changing rich context to generate suggestions that are highly relevant and actionable to its users
(
39
;
17
)
.

In this work we explore
Omakase
, a system that observes a research project document, tracks the state of a project, formulates interesting and open questions, performs background queries, and expresses the results specifically in the context of the project. Instead of having users provide context at the moment of agent invocation, our approach leverages existing project documents.
Omakase
regularly checks for changes in the user’s project documents, issues timely literature questions based on the project’s state to a literature deep research system, and derives actionable suggestions from deep research reports. This approach resolves the problem of stale context often found in recommendation systems, requiring almost no extra effort from the user.

Concretely, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

Through an iterative design process spanning 15 months, we built three versions of a proactive research assistant, assessed each one with users (Table
1
), revised the design, and iterated. These experiments yielded the following insights:

•

Cost of context engineering: Researchers disliked having to invest time and effort crafting the context of their project as background for an agent. Instead, they
wanted the proactive assistant to monitor their existing artifacts and infer this context (Section
3.3.3
).

•

Cost of contextualization: Instead of a long deep-research style report, users wanted specific, actionable suggestions on how to improve their projects (Section
3.3.2
).

•

The final iteration of our system,
Omakase
, proactively generated agent tasks that participants found timely to their project context (Section
5.4.2
), and included tasks they themselves might not have thought of without the system (Section
5.4.4
).

•

These tasks led to suggestions that the participants rated as significantly more
actionable
,
relevant
, and
timely
when users spends similar amount of effort in the baseline condition of reading top sections from a deep research system’s output (Section
5.4.1
).

2.
Related work

Our work draws on two bodies of prior research: (1) proactive AI agents and the types of context they leverage, and (2) literature-based research support tools. We review each below and highlight where existing systems fall short of providing proactive, project-aware assistance.

2.1.
Context for proactive AI Agents

Research on conversational AI agents has investigated how proactive agents might interact with users. A body of work has focused on identifying the right moment for an agent to chime in during conversation
(
32
;
31
;
41
)
. For example, Liu et al. introduce inner thoughts, a mechanism that helps an AI agent determine when to chime in
(
32
)
. Other works explore how conversational agents might ask clarifying questions from users to resolve ambiguity and improve performance
(
29
;
18
;
12
;
53
;
59
;
26
)
. The context of these agents and models is mostly conversation history for synchronous interventions.

Other proactive agents embedded in domain-specific tools such as coding
(
45
;
37
;
9
)
, searching information
(
16
)
and writing
(
5
;
35
;
25
)
also have limited contexts in terms of scope and time. Some focus more on the appropriate time to offer users assistance without unnecessary interruptions
(
45
;
35
)
. Systems like Morae determine the decision points and proactively provide relevant task context for users to make informed decisions
(
42
)
. However, these systems focus mostly on synchronous interactions where the users engage actively with the system. The context they used was task-specific and mostly lasted for a short session.
Omakase
’s context, in contrast, is a long-horizon document for asynchronous proactive interactions.

On the other extreme, there are systems that consume a comprehensive long-horizon multi-modal context
(
47
;
48
;
58
)
. GUMBO observes a series of unstructured screenshots of a user over time to model users’ profiles and provide proactive personalized support
(
47
)
. Shaikh et al. also introduce LongNAP, a model that predicts user next actions from user multi-modal interaction history
(
48
)
. While showing potential in its flexibility, these approaches depend on interaction data which might not give a complete picture of a research project with multiple collaborators contributing from various sources. Further, there is a privacy concern when granting an agent access to all of a user’s activities.
Omakase
leverages existing research artifacts that are more reliable than user interaction history. No prior work has investigated a system that observes a user’s documents for a particular project throughout its lifetime.

2.2.
Literature-based Research Support Tools

There are multiple RAG-based LLM tools designed to support various research tasks from ideation
(
44
;
46
)
, literature understanding and synthesis
(
49
;
13
)
to paper critique
(
1
)
.

While useful for their tasks, the scope of context employed by these tools is quite limited. Recent work on paper recommendations has explored how to contextualize recommended papers to a user’s context. PaperWeaver uses an LLM pipeline to generate explanations of how a recommended paper is related to a user’s paper collection
(
28
)
. Kang et al. surface relevance between a recommended paper and a user’s publication and interaction history using a knowledge graph
(
22
)
. The paper collection still needs to be curated manually and the generated explanations are topical instead of actionable suggestions related to a project. Some other tools do not require manual paper curation but have limited context. PaperPing chimes in with related papers during a Slack conversation; however, like other conversational AI agents, its context is limited to prior discussions
(
55
)
. MyScholarQA focuses more on profiling the user based on their publication history
(
6
)
, which could be out of sync with their current research projects according to our formative study (Section
3
). Unlike these systems that focus on a specific research activity, our work considers the context of the whole research project where literature needs shift over time
(
51
;
19
)
.

3.
Formative Study with a Technology Probe

We built an initial technology probe that periodically generated paper recommendations using an LLM based on user’s
research interests document
. The recommendations were delivered via email to 28 participants (4 female and 24 male; NLP, ML, HCI, and CV researchers) over an eight-week period. Using this technology probe, we explored the following questions:

•

Q1: What types of literature-based information do researchers want from a proactive system?

•

Q2: Would researchers maintain a free-form document as the primary input to such a system?

•

Q3: How well could an LLM-based pipeline generate useful recommendations from this richer context?

3.1.
Technology Probe: Living Research Interests Document

The first iteration of our system is a technology probe consisted of three parts: (1) a shared Google Doc as a
research interests document
that can be freely edited by the participants; (2) a
paper recommendations pipeline
that periodically read the research interests document, retrieved papers from Semantic Scholar API
(
24
;
50
)
and an open-sourced deep research system
(
49
)
, and generated brief explanations of their relevance to the user interests; and (3)
recommendation emails
delivered twice a week containing the paper recommendations, from which users could add the paper back to their research interests document, asked an AI assistant about them, or gave feedback through the emails. Through the deployment study, we iterated the system based on user’s feedback.

3.1.1.
Research interests document

This served as a lightweight shared memory between the user and the system: users maintained the document as personal notes about their current interests, while the system used it as the source of context for generating recommendations. Users could describe each topic however they wanted (e.g., sentences, bullet lists, links to another documents). The only constraint imposed was that they kept different topics as their own separate sections and added a few relevant papers to each topic. To address the cold-start problem, we seeded each document with the user’s five most recent publications under a default “My papers” topic.

3.1.2.
Paper recommendations pipeline

Based on the research interest document, the system generate paper recommendations through an LLM-powered pipeline with the following steps:

(1)

Topic and paper extraction
The pipeline fetched the latest content of the user’s document and used an LLM to extract the following for each topic: title, description, and associated papers.
1
1

1

resolved to Semantic Scholar paper IDs
We also allowed participants to include links to other Google Docs, and follow those links to extract additional topics from them. This allowed us to tap into existing documents for their real research projects.

(2)

Paper retrieval.
The system retrieved paper recommendation using two methods. First, it used the seed papers to find their recent citations and similar papers using the Semantic Scholar APIs
(
24
)
. Second, based on the topic descriptions and notes from the user, the system generated natural language questions to query an open-sourced deep research system and extracted papers from its inline references
(
49
)
. Finally, we pooled papers from both methods and ranked them using a recent paper embedding technique
(
50
)
, keeping recommend ones that were most similar to the seed papers.

(3)

Explanation generation.
For each selected paper, an LLM generated a brief (2–3 sentence) explanation of why the paper was relevant to the user’s topic. The prompts took the recommended paper’s title and abstract and description of the topic as input (Appendix
B.6
). If it cited any of the seed papers, the citing sentence were also included (Appendix
B.4
). If the paper was from a deep research output, the question was provided as context (Appendix
B.5
).

3.1.3.
Recommendation emails

The system delivered recommendation emails (Figure
2
) twice a week, depending on the availability of highly relevant papers from recent publications.
Each email covered up to two topics, each containing up to two recommended papers. For each recommended paper, the email displayed:

•

The
paper title
linked to the corresponding paper details page on Semantic Scholar.

•

A
Context label
that includes the topic and how the recommended paper was found based on the above pipeline:
Cites [seed paper title]
) or
Related to [deep research question]
.

•

A 2–3 sentence
LLM-generated explanation
of why the paper is relevant to their topic as described in the document.

Users could add a paper back to their research interests document by clicking on "Add to my document", allowing it to serve as a seed paper for future recommendations. They could send paper as a context to start a conversation with an AI reading assistant by clicking “Ask X about this paper” button.

3.2.
Procedure

Participants were informed that they would be receiving research update emails based on their research documents and they could opt out of the study if they wished. We observed their interactions with the research interests document and the emails from February 3rd, 2025 to March 30th, 2025. During the 8-week observation period, the system sent 383 total emails to 28 participants (DP1–DP28).

Over the observation period, we tracked each participant’s interaction with the emails and their research interests document. Specifically, we tracked the following events:

•

Open paper
: how many times a participant clicked a paper link in the email.

•

Add paper to doc
: how many times a participant added a paper from the email to their research interests document.

•

Ask agent
: how many times a participant used a paper to start a conversation with the research chat agent.

•

# clicks
: the total number of clicks across all email actions.

•

# doc edits
: the number of edits a participant made to their research interests document.

3.3.
Findings

Overall, the level of engagement was comparable to prior paper recommendation systems in the literature. Each participant received 9–17 emails, and the median click-through rate (CTR) was 9.0%. For context, the CTR from a prior work on paper recommendation systems was 4.5–5.8%
(
22
)
.
Twelve of 28 participants did not trigger any tracked event, though interviews revealed that most had still opened and read the emails. Half of the participants made edits to their interest documents during the 8-week deployment period. With sufficient engagement, we focused our analysis on qualitative insights from the interviews
(
7
)
, with a goal of guiding the design of the next iteration of our system. We reached out to all 28 participants and 13 responded in a timely manner to be interviewed.

3.3.1.
Researchers found the recommendation emails helpful (Q3)

Eight (of 13) participants indicated that the suggestions were helpful. DP2, DP6, DP17 particularly liked the explanation of paper relevance as they told them what to pay attention to. DP15 spot a few papers that were useful as a citation in their related work.
Some participants with no logged click from the email said that they did read the emails (DP7, DP21). DP7 noted that the recommendations were more “directly relevant” than another paper recommendation service they were using. Deep research system was able to find more relevant papers compared to citation- and embedding-based approaches
(
28
)
. DP21 also said that they found the recommendations from leveraging the deep research system “starting to be genuinely useful.” These results shows high opportunities in leveraging existing deep research system for finding highly relevant paper recommendations, combining richer context in an interest document to generate better contextualized explanations.

3.3.2.
Participants wanted actionable insights for their active projects over general interests (Q1)

We collected feedback from six participants with low engagement. Participants mentioned that they had read the emails and found the recommendations to be generally interesting, but did not take any actions because they were also not “super relevant” to the problem they are solving with their active projects (DP23, DP26).
Contrastly, participants who engaged deeply with the technology probe pointed to how their recommendations were tied to their ongoing projects. Several participants described two tiers of relevance: papers that are “super relevant” to a current project—which they would add back to their document—and papers of general interest that they might skim or ignore (DP1, DP26). Some participants (10 out of these 14 that edited their documents) organized their research interests document using active projects on top of topical areas (DP1). DP1 specifically wanted a “scoop alert” for new papers that could affect their current project.

Further, beyond discovering relevant papers, participants wanted the system to deliver information they could act upon. The explanation from the probe did not tell them what to do with the information and could have missed important details that they would be interested in at the time. DP7 wanted something “specific and actionable” that could enable them to “close the decision loop”. For example when they would like to see baseline candidates during their experimental design phase. DP23’s comment reflected similar sentiment:
“[I want the system to tell] me some information on how to proceed on the project that I am working on.”

These results suggest participants desire literature-based support to provide
actionable insights
over information that are of high-level interests.

3.3.3.
Maintaining a dedicated research interests document is burdensome (Q2)

We used Google Doc as the primary means for users to communicate their interests to the system.
Participants were split: half of the 28 participants edited their research interests document during the 8-week deployment. Some participants found having an ongoing research interests document helpful for “organizing ongoing thoughts and threads” (DP2) or maintaining related work (DP20). Others, like DP23 stated that they were “too busy and unorganized to put things from [their] head to a maintained document.” DP17 noted that “it’s a hassle to go back and update the doc.”

Leveraging recent publications to improve cold-start was also insufficient. Multiple participants removed them because it was an “eclectic collection of things” (DP17) that no longer reflected their shifting interests (DP3).
These observations suggest that while a dedicated document
can
be valuable, expecting researchers to maintain one specifically for the sake of getting recommendations is unrealistic.

One opportunity emerged from the interviews: the system could draw context from documents researchers already maintain, such as project briefs, weekly meeting slides, or shared notes (DP3, DP7). DP7 suggested that for meeting logs the system should “just look at the recent one,” highlighting that the system should handle document staleness gracefully rather than depending on the user’s manual curation.

3.4.
Design goals

Based on insights from the formative study, we formulate the design goals for our proactive literature support system:

•

DG1
: Help users discover literature relevant to their active research projects instead of general interests.

•

DG2
: Suggest timely and actionable suggestions instead of individual paper recommendations.

•

DG3
: Leverage existing project documents so that users do not need to spend additional effort curating their interests.

4.
Omakase

Guided by our design goals, we developed
Omakase
, a proactive agent that monitors researchers’ project documents and delivers actionable, literature-based suggestions contextualized to the project. Instead of asking users to constantly curate up-to-date context,
Omakase
uses existing research documents that researchers already maintain —such as meeting notes or paper drafts (DG3). It then infers the project’s specific information needs to surface relevant suggestions (DG1) that are timely and actionable (DG2).

In the next sections, we will first describe the user experience of using
Omakase
in an example user scenario. Then we will describe two iterations and evaluation of the system.

4.1.
Example User Scenario

4.1.1.
Project creation and document upload

An HCI researcher starts a new research project on “multi-agent ideation” with a few research collaborators in her organization. She creates a Google Doc to keep track of the ideas related to this project. The document now contains a description of the project, research questions and a few reference papers. She has already done some literature review in the space but still wants to keep an eye open for relevant work specifically for this project. She adds her Google Doc to a new project on
Omakase
, and grants it read permission.

4.1.2.
Initial project details

Half an hour later, the researcher sees an email in her inbox with a link to
Omakase
’s dashboard where she can see new suggestions for her project (Fig.
1
A). Since this is a new project added to
Omakase
, she wants to make sure that
Omakase
correctly inferred information about the project. She opens the
Project details
page (Fig.
6
).

The system correctly indicates that the project is in an “Ideation and early experimental design” stage.

Omakase
further explains that it compared recent revisions of the document and found that the project had gone through a few iterations on its framing and pointed out that “Evaluation criteria” was described in the document but no results have been added yet.

Even though this inference is correct, she instead wants
Omakase
to only focus on supporting ideation so she can explore more broadly. She manually updates the project state to just “Ideation”, to steer
Omakase
’s future suggestions.

She also verifies that the system has identified all 17 papers mentioned in her document (Fig.
6
).
Omakase
generates short descriptions of how each paper relates to the project. She proceeds to remove two papers that belong to a previous iteration of the project and are no longer relevant.

4.1.3.
Suggestion-centric View

After inspecting
Omakase
’s inferred knowledge about the project, the researcher navigates back to the dashboard. She spots one
suggestion card
(Fig.
1

The researcher saves two papers from the suggestions to the project’s papers list.

4.1.4.
Document-centric View

Alternatively, she can also open the

and see the suggestions in-situ next to highlighted, relevant parts of her document (Fig.
1

4.1.5.
Adjusting and subscribing to questions

Omakase
generates questions to query a deep research system, and contextualizes its output as suggestions.
Omakase
exposes this step for transparency and steerability. In the
question page
, she can examine the list of questions as well as add her own question (Fig.
3
).
She can also see the full report of each question before they are contextualized into briefer suggestions in the document-centric and suggestion-centric views.

Further, she saw a question that directly addressed the project’s core research question: “How do existing interactive multi-agent systems allow users to steer, coordinate, and control agent interactions?” Knowing that she would want to see if there are new answers to this question, she clicked on the “Track” button, telling
Omakase
to keep track of whether there was any new relevant literature by periodically re-querying the deep research system.

4.1.6.
Ongoing suggestions as the project evolves

Two months pass. The researcher adjusts
Omakase
’s update frequency for this project from bi-weekly to weekly to accommodate its weekly meeting schedule (Fig.
6
). As the project moves to the experimental design stage,
Omakase
’s suggestions shift to focus more on information that could help with the experimental design. The researcher now sees a suggestion to look into metrics to measure the generated ideas from her system. From time to time, she also sees updates to tracked literature review questions when the answer to a question changes.

4.2.
Generation Pipeline Iteration 1

Once a project document was added to
Omakase
, it took the following steps to generate actionable suggestions based on the project context:

Step 1: Document Preprocessing

Omakase
retrieved a document (supported document types are Google Doc, Google Sheet, and Google Slides) from the link provided by the user. It parsed the document into markdown format and extracted a list of potential papers mentioned in the document (Appendix
D.2
). It then retrieved the potential papers metadata (full title) and filtered only those with corresponding papers.
Omakase
inserted the papers’ metadata (Semantic Scholar corpus id and title) to the parsed document.

Step 2: Infer project state and generate questions

From the document with papers’ metadata,
Omakase
inferred the project state with reasoning. It also extracts relevant questions that could provide information useful for the researcher in the current stage of the project. The prompt provides descriptions of questions a user might be interested in at different stages. It also specifies the model to rank the questions based on relevance and timeliness (Appendix
D.3
).

Step 3: Issue questions to a deep research system

Omakase
took top questions from the previous step and issued each question to a deep research system
(
49
)
. Once the answers were all retrieved the system forwarded the answers to the next step in the pipeline.

Step 4: Generate contextualized suggestions

Lastly, the suggestions were extracted from deep research responses and within the same prompt ranked by expected usefulness, importance, and relevance to the project phase (Appendix
D.4
). Each suggestion consisted of a text description, a list of mentioned papers, and relevant information from the paper.

4.3.
Generation Pipeline Iteration 1 Evaluation

To evaluate our initial pipeline, we conducted a study where researchers evaluated the pipeline outputs of their actual project documents. We also gathered researchers’ opinions on the cases where project-targeted actionable suggestions were helpful.

4.3.1.
Participants

We asked 10 research scientists (PP1–PP10) associated with our institution to participate in our study. The participants have different levels of research experience: 2 Ph.D. students, 2 post-doctoral researchers, 1 university professor, and 5 industrial researchers. Their research areas are HCI, NLP, and ML. Each participant provided documents from their ongoing research projects as input to evaluate the pipeline. Seven of the participants also participated in the recommendation emails study.

4.3.2.
Procedure

We collected 12 research documents from 11 projects. Seven documents were planning and presentation documents that got updated regularly (e.g., for weekly meetings). Two were paper drafts in LaTeX. Three were project proposals or sketches. For each document, we generated the outputs with our pipeline. Each output contained the inferred project state, rationale for the project state inference, and a list of 3-11 questions with 3-8 suggestions per questions. We saved the output of the pipeline in a separate document.

Participants were provided with output document(s) associated with their project(s). If a participant belonged to multiple research projects on our list, they could give feedback on as many documents as they liked. We asked participants to spend 15-20 minutes reading through the suggestions and
provided feedback on the pipeline output and their preferences on receiving updates from the system (see Appendix
F
for the full questions list).

4.3.3.
Findings

The inferred project state is generally correct

In general, our pipeline inferred the correct state from regularly updated project documents (e.g., meeting notes or weekly presentations) or project proposal in the beginning state.
Six researchers indicated that the inferred project state were correct while four others stated the project state were "close to correct" (PP1) or "Not perfect, but pretty good" (PP4).
Two participants (PP8, PP10) in particular added paper drafts instead of project documents to
Omakase
also noted that the inferred project states to be generally correct.

Project document-grounded questions and suggestions offers useful information beyond paper recommendations

In general, participants found useful information in generated suggestions. For example, PP1 found some "really good ideas or suggestions and worth taking a look at". Some also found the suggestions surfaces things they "might not have thought about" even after completing their own literature review (PP2). PP2 and PP8 commented that these questions and suggestions could surface blindspots in the project. In general, participants seeing suggestions based on "[their] meeting notes and researchers notes… could be useful" (PP10).

Some participants noted that the suggestions are more specific and better contextualized than paper recommendations feed, helping them to "know what to pay attention to" (PP6). PP9 commented that the suggestions are not redundant with the existing recommendations. While PP5 "like the document/project-specific nature of this [approach]".

Researchers want control over the generation and delivery of suggestions

Participants wanted to steer the output by getting more control of the generation process or giving feedback to the system. For example, PP7 wanted to be able to select the questions before issuing them to a deep research system. Participants in general wanted to be able to add their own questions (PP5, PP7, PP8). PP1 wanted to give feedback to the system on the quality of the suggestions. PP3 said that they wanted to edit inferred information and even provided more information to steer the output to fit their needs as their "project doc was not the most comprehensive… it’s kind of impossible for the system to know what [they were] working on" (PP3). Unlike real collaborators, the system could not have had background knowledge from past in-person discussion.

Five participants commented that the amount of information presented was too much to view at once. None of them finished reading all suggestions in allotted time. For example, PP7 only got through 2 questions out of 7. However, some had higher information overload threshold than others. For example, PP1 said they would like the information "to be delivered in a way that [wasn’t] overwhelming". On the other hand, PP8 said they were fine with the current amount of suggestions as long as they were diverse enough.

Participants also expressed that they wanted control over the timing of suggestion delivery. While some participants would like to receive the suggestions weekly (PP7) or bi-weekly (PP2, PP4), some preferred suggestions being delivered on demand (PP3, PP8, PP9). Some were also interested in the system delivering suggestions as the user makes edits or "when something changes in the environment that affects [their] project" (e.g., new relevant papers got published) (PP8).

The preferred timing varied — and even contradicted. Some wanted suggestions early during brainstorming (PP3), while others explicitly did not want them during ideation when they were already compiling information (PP10). PP10 stated they could "get excited about" receiving suggestions during paper write-up instead. Similarly, some wanted more suggestions at later stages (PP9), while others valued them most during active thinking or paper writing (PP7). This suggests that delivery timing cannot be one-size-fits-all and likely needs to adapt to both project phase and individual preference.

Other improvement opportunities

Beyond already-mentioned issues, we identified the following opportunities to improve the pipeline and eventual system design:

•

Better output generation.
Some (PP3, PP6, PP7) noted that the questions and suggestions, while generally helpful, sometimes had convoluted phrasing that were hard to parse ("word salad", PP6). PP5 commented that some generated questions should be broken down into sub-questions. This is a common issue with LLM text generation addressable with tighter prompting and post-processing.

•

Awareness of the time of generation.
Some participants wanted to put higher weight on suggestions related to recent actions. They flagged that the output suggestions showed that
Omakase
did not know that some tasks had already been completed or discarded as directions changed. PP3 noted that the pipeline should be able to infer that their formative had already concluded after 2 months "unless I’m that slow" (PP3). PP7 also noted that the output over-indexed on an abandoned early direction.

•

Suggestions needed to be properly contextualized.
PP3 and PP5 wished the suggestions were more contextualized. Particularly, PP3 wanted the system to explain "where the suggestions were derived". While some other wanted the explanation next to the relevant sections to its corresponding original project documents (e.g., as a comment).

•

Make sure suggestions are non-redundant.
Some suggestions can be redundant with what they already know (PP2), redundant with other suggestions (PP8) or what they are already planning to do (PP3, PP7). While some take this as a signal of knowing the pipeline could be useful, it could be annoying. Further improvement could focus on ensuring all suggestions do not repeat what was already planned in the documents, selecting a set of diverse suggestions and giving users a lightweight way to tell the system what they already know or are planning to do to avoid redundancy.

In summary, the evaluation showed that participants were interested in receiving project-document-based suggestions, considered them useful, specific and actionable. The pipeline could infer project state and information need (questions) from active documents that reflected the current state of the project. Participants stated that they want more control over the generation and delivery of suggestions. We took their feedback to improve the pipeline and design of
Omakase
.

4.4.
Generation Pipeline Iteration 2

Based on the findings above, we made the following improvements to both the generation pipeline and the interface of the final system.

4.4.1.
Pipeline improvements

Based on the findings from the pipeline evaluation (Section
4.3
), we made several improvements to the suggestion generation pipeline addressing the four key areas participants identified: time awareness, output quality, information overload, and redundancy. The final pipeline of
Omakase
uses Opus 4.6 for all LLM requests in the pipeline (Appendix
C
).

Time-aware generation

PP3 and PP7 noted that the initial pipeline sometimes generated suggestions that overindexed past information. We traced part of this to a document parser bug that dropped date information, causing the model to treat all content as current. After fixing the parser, we also instruct the model to discard open loops that have had no follow-up activity for several weeks (Appendix
C.4
).

Improved output clarity

Participants reported that some generated questions and suggestions had convoluted phrasing (PP3, PP6, PP7) and that compound questions should be broken into sub-questions (PP5). We updated the underlying model from O3 to Opus 4.6, which produced clearer and more concise text. We also refined the question generation prompt to avoid complicated questions (Appendix
C.4
).

Ranking and selection to manage information overload

None of the participants in the pipeline evaluation finished reading all generated suggestions within the allotted time, and five explicitly commented that the volume was overwhelming. To address this, we added two explicit ranking stages to the pipeline. First, we added a question selection step that selects a diverse subset of the top questions (Appendix
C.5
). Second, we added a suggestion selection step that selects the top suggestions based on relevance, actionability, and diversity (Appendix
C.7
).

Non-redundancy

Participants noted that some suggestions repeated what they already knew or planned to do (PP2, PP3, PP7), or overlapped with other suggestions (PP8). The suggestion ranking prompt (Appendix
C.7
) now explicitly instructs the model to penalize suggestions that are redundant with the document’s existing plans or with other selected suggestions. Additionally, the system tracks which suggestions a user has previously seen and excludes them from future batches.

4.4.2.
Document viewer

To better contextualized the suggestions (PP3, PP5),
Omakase
identifies which part of the document is most relevant to a suggestion (Appendix
C.8
). The user can view document section relevant to a suggestion by clicking on the suggestion in the document view (Fig.
1
B) to see relevant highlighted text.

4.4.3.
Updates mechanism and control

To send participants proactive updates, a background process runs at a configurable interval. For each project, it compares the linked document’s last-modified timestamp and the current inferred project state against those recorded at the last pipeline run. If the document has changed or the project state has shifted, the system triggers an
update workflow
that infers the project state and generates new questions to issue them together with tracked questions to a deep research system. The suggestion generation for new questions went as usual. For tracked questions,
Omakase
compares the new answer with the old answer and only extracts suggestions if there are substantial differences (Appendix
C.9
).

Omakase
gives user control over the timing of update delivery. User can specify how frequent they want
Omakase
to check for update (from daily to never) (Fig.
6
). They can also manually request new suggestions.

5.
Omakase
’s evaluation

We conducted a controlled evaluation of
Omakase
’s Generation Pipeline Iteration 2, focusing on the
quality
of the resulting suggestions, the
timeliness
of the generated questions and the overall user experience using
Omakase
’s interface. We asked the following:

•

RQ1
: Can
Omakase
understand the document to generate timely questions that reflect the current state of the project?

•

RQ2
: How relevant, timely, and actionable do researchers find
Omakase
’s suggestions compared to directly reading the answers from a deep research system?

•

RQ3
: Does
Omakase
help participants discover unexpected information needs that they had not considered?

•

RQ4
: How effortful is it to triage the suggestions?

For RQ1, we used
Omakase
to generate two sets of questions: The first set was based on the up-to-date version of participants’ project documents, and the second set was based on an earlier version of the same documents. We then asked participants to compare the two sets of question while being blind to which condition each came from to see if the first set is more timely. For RQ2, we compared
Omakase
’s suggestions with output of the same deep research report that was used to generate the suggestions, similar to common literature review practices. For RQ3-4, we report qualitative data from post-survey and interview.

5.1.
Participants

We recruited 11 researchers (SP1–SP11) via social media and academic mailing lists. The participants consisted of 8 Ph.D. students, 1 pre-doctoral researcher, 1 post-doctoral researcher, and 1 research scientist. Two participants had 6-10 years of research experience while the rest has 2-5 years of research experience. None of the participants had participated in the formative study or prior pipeline evaluation.

5.2.
Study protocol

The study consisted of a pre-study preparation step, followed by an asynchronous evaluation of the system’s output and an interview.

5.2.1.
Pre-study: document submissions

Each participant submitted two copies of their documents from an active research project–a current version and a past snapshot from the document’s history.
For each version, we generated questions that result in 12 deep search reports and 12
Omakase
suggestions.

5.2.2.
Output evaluation

The output evaluation form had three tasks.

Task 1: Evaluate questions (current vs past)

Participants received the shuffled list of 23–30 questions, blinded to conditions. For each question, they specified how much they agreed with the statement
“This question is relevant to my project and is a priority to address at its current stage.”
on a 7-point Likert scale (1=“strongly disagree”, 7=“strongly agree”). If a question is not relevant to project at any point in time, they flagged that question to catch the case where the system just generated random questions.

Task 2: Evaluate responses (
Omakase
’s suggestions vs deep research answer)

Participants evaluated 12 sets of responses to questions generated from their current document. Because answers from a deep research system can be much longer than a suggestion, we asked participants to select two topics (sections) from each answer that they thought would be most useful. Each set of responses therefore consisted of
Omakase
’s suggestions and two selected sections from a deep research answer for the same question. We use these two sections to construct two comparison baselines:
Equal effort
, where we compare against the section the participant expected to be most useful, and
Double deep research effort
, where we compare against whichever was rated higher on a given dimension (see below), requiring participants to have read both. Participants evaluated each response in randomized order, blind to condition, rating their agreement on a 7-point Likert scale (1=“strongly disagree”, 7=“strongly agree”) on three dimensions—
relevance
,
actionability
, and
timeliness
:

•

Relevance:
This response is relevant to my project.

•

Actionability:
This response suggests concrete actions I can take (e.g., cite for a claim, adopt a baseline, update a hypothesis).

•

Timeliness:
This response is appropriate for my current stage of work.

Task 3: Survey response

Participants shared their opinions about the questions and the responses in relation to their project. Specifically, we asked if the output helped them think about new ideas or questions (RQ3) and how easy it was to triage the responses (RQ4). See the full list of the survey questions at Appendix
G
.

5.2.3.
Interview

We conducted a semi-structured interview with participants. We asked follow-up clarification questions from their evaluation responses. Participants were also presented with
Omakase
for their current document. We observed how they interacted with the system and asked for their impressions of the system.

5.3.
Analysis

To test if
Omakase
generated questions that reflect the priority of the current stage (RQ1), we compared participant’s ratings on the relevance and timeliness for questions from the current document and the past document. We aggregated the responses to one score per participant per condition (the median of their ratings) and applied a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

To compare responses from
Omakase
and a deep research baseline (RQ2), we modeled participants’ response ratings using linear mixed-effect models (LMMs).

5.4.3.
Omakase
’s suggestions are easy to triage (RQ4)

Nine out of ten participants mentioned it was easy to tell whether the suggestions was worth reading in details. SP9 commented that
Omakase
’s suggestions were "short and to the point," making it easy to read in details. In contrast, deep research responses were not as easy to triage. SP7 commented that "A lot more skimming was needed for [deep research responses],… the likelihood of being worth reading is lower (probably 20%)."
However, two participants (SP6 and SP8) did not find it troublesome to skim through the deep research responses.

5.4.4.
Omakase
’s questions and suggestions surface new ideas and questions (RQ3)

Participants reported new ideas and questions inspired by
Omakase
’s questions and suggestions. SP4, SP5, SP7, and SP10 were made aware of communities and venues that could be closely relevant. Some participants found that the suggestions reminded them to consider experimental design choices that they had not thought of (SP1, SP3, SP10). Some participants also viewed
Omakase
as feedback giving system (SP1, SP7, SP10). SP1 stated that
Omakase
’s questions probed for "potential issues" with their project that they hadn’t thought of, preventing them from "overclaiming things". SP7 gave an example when
Omakase
misunderstood “multi-session”—a key concept in the project—for a “multi-turn”. That made them realized "how people could misunderstood [their] work". In a way,
Omakase
can be a mirror that reflects how the work is perceived. SP5 mentioned that having a system "push back on something if there’s literature refuting what [they] said" could be helpful.

Some participants also observed that
Omakase
’s questions were different from the queries they normally issue to their literature tools. SP1 said that the questions were more specific than their “underspecified queries”. SP5 found the details in
Omakase
’s questions “insightful and interesting that [they] wouldn’t have framed by [themselves].”

5.4.5.
Omakase
’s suggestions and deep research answers preference varied

Four of ten participants expressed clear preference for
Omakase
’s suggestions over deep research responses. SP1, SP10 felt that the suggestions were more actionable, similar to how an advisor might provide detailed feedback on their work. While SP2 and SP4 liked how the suggestions referred information from their own research materials. Comparing to a descriptive research answers that were "difficult to map back to what [they] were looking for", the suggestions could "lower the cognitive load of mapping the answers back to the project’s context" (SP4).

On the other hand, one participants strongly preferred deep research answers to suggestions. SP6 liked that the deep research answers has more citations details, especially when they were looking for technical depth.

In many cases, participants found that they would prefer one over another for different scenarios. SP5 found that the deep research answers were more helpful when they wanted an overview of a topic. Similarly, SP9 stated that deep research answers was helpful during the initial phases of the project when they are more open to read a lot, while suggestions are appropriate for experimental design and writing phase.

Omakase
provides both types of responses to the users. Providing targeted actionable suggestions for those who prefer them while also offering an option to explore more details from the full deep research answers.

5.4.6.
Rooms for improvement

Despite overall positive reception, participants identified cases where
Omakase
’s suggestions missed the mark. A recurring theme was that the system sometimes made premature or overconfident inferences about the project’s direction. SP7 noted that the system “sometimes made a ‘leap’ too early and then the suggestions sort of fall flat,” adding that the deep research answers were “more careful and sort of said there’s no quite anything comparable, which was more accurate.” SP5 described a case where
Omakase
overindexed on a topic—notebooks and literate programming—that they had briefly explored but abandoned, treating it as though it were still an active thread of thought.

Relatedly, SP2 was surprised by how often suggestions referenced ideas from the earlier version of their document that no longer appeared in the current draft. They recounted reading suggestions that initially seemed irrelevant, only to realize they traced back to early-stage project plans. SP2 suggested that explicitly noting what is no longer being pursued in the current document could help prevent such misalignment.

6.
Discussion and Future Work

6.1.
Granting AI agents access to project document

There are risks in granting an AI agent access to an ongoing research documents and artifacts
(
54
)
. When we were recruiting participants for the system evaluation, some interested researchers mentioned that their organization prohibited them from sharing their research documents externally. DP16 from the formative study said they did not want to grant an AI agent access to their working research document, citing privacy as the main reason. Indeed, privacy is one barrier to a proactive context-aware agent like Apple’s Knowledge Navigator
(
39
)
.

One common feature suggestion for
Omakase
is to add the capability for an agent to edit or comment on the document directly. However, users might have different preferences in giving comment or write access to their project document to an AI agent and the preferences also depend on the usage context. For example, a user can feel comfortable having an agent editing their document to add a paper to a literature review list but not editing their paper draft. More investigation is needed to understand how to manage AI agents’ access to different artifacts in various use cases.

6.2.
Multiple sources of project context

In this paper, we only explored project context from a single document as an initial exploration step. In practice, a research project can have many artifacts (e.g., project briefs, weekly presentations, paper drafts, experimental results, code, etc.). Multiple sources of project context can give an agent a more complete picture but increase complexity. Future work can explore how AI agents reason about what the user needs from multiple, potentially conflicting, sources.

6.3.
Over-reliance on AI

Omakase
’s suggestions are designed to be easy to triage. Meanwhile, there is concern that using LLM tools could lead to overreliance on AI
(
54
)
. Prior work has shown that AI models are often perceived as unbiased entities
(
11
)
. The risk is that researchers could accept suggestions from the system without verifying them. One way to mitigate this is by providing evidence links to AI-generated text, making it easier to verify
(
27
)
.

7.
Limitations

While
Omakase
was designed for long-horizon interactions over the course of a project’s lifetime, our evaluation of the system did not cover this aspect. Our user study’s attempt to address this issue with two different versions of the document had limited implications. A longer deployment study could provide a more complete picture about the effectiveness of the system.

8.
Conclusion

We presented
Omakase
, a proactive research assistant that monitors a researcher’s evolving project documents and generates literature-grounded, actionable suggestions without requiring explicit queries or manual context curation. Our evaluations showed that
Omakase
generated suggestions that were significantly more relevant, actionable, and timely than reading equivalent sections of a literature deep research output. Participants also reported that the system surfaced new ideas, relevant communities, and potential weaknesses they had not previously considered, while remaining easy to triage. These findings demonstrate the viability of leveraging existing project artifacts as living context for proactive AI assistance.

Appendix E
Output from existing conversational AI assistant with RAG (January 2026)

We investigated existing agentic systems to see how well they could extract project-specific suggestions from a project document. We created two versions of a project document from a finished project historical versions. One from a few weeks after the project started and the other from a few weeks before the paper was submitted. We created a prompt for each version and sent the prompt to ChatGPT
5
5

5

https://chatgpt.com/
and Gemini
6
6

6

https://gemini.google.com/app
in January 2026. These systems were chosen as strong RAG-enabled conversational AI assistants. See the prompts used below.

We found that the suggestions responses were quite generic (see example outputs below). One suggestions from ChatGPT state that the researchers should conduct a pilot experiment for an HCI research project at the experimental design phase. While the paper surfaced works well for the initial of the project, these systems failed to surface more targeted specific papers that were typically helpful at later stages of the project. Further, there was one instance where the Gemini’s responses contained hallucinated a paper.

Appendix F
Questions for pipeline evaluation

•

Is the inferred project state correct?

•

What do you like/dislike about the current output format (answers to questions and suggestions)?

•

Where would you like the information like this to be delivered (e.g., on the doc, email, slac, etc)?

•

How does this type of information compare to other literature updates you receive (e.g., S2 feed)? Is it redundant?

•

What kind of feedback would you give to Omakase to improve its output?

•

Do you want to receive an update like this for this document in the future? (If so, how regularly do you want them?)

Beyond these questions, participants were encouraged to provide free-form comments. They could also comment directly on the output document.

Appendix G
Output evaluation survey questions

(1)

Which three responses were the most useful for you? For each, specify the question number and whether it was a suggestion or an answer by topic, and explain why.

(2)

Have any questions, suggestions and answers by topic help you think about new ideas or questions that you haven’t thought of before? If so, please explain.

(3)

How did the suggestions and answers by topic differ in usefulness for your project? In what ways, if any, were they similar?

(4)

How easy was it to tell whether a suggestion was worth reading in detail?

(5)

How easy was it to tell whether an answer by topic was worth reading in detail?

(6)

Were any suggestions interesting but not directly related to your project’s current stage? If so, which ones and why were they interesting?

(7)

Were any suggestions very similar to what you already planned to do? If so, which ones? Could someone else reading your document have come to the conclusion that you already planned to do these things?

(8)

Did you notice any suggestions that overlapped or repeated each other? If so, which ones?

(9)

Did any suggestions make incorrect assumptions about your project? If so, which ones? Was the correct information available in your document, or what additional context would have been needed?
