 ABSTRACT

This report evaluates methods for predicting creep-fatigue life under high-temperature cyclic loading
conditions for SS316H, Alloy 617, and Alloy 709. The methods considered include the current ASME
time-fraction methodology as well as several alternative approaches, including stress-modified ductility
exhaustion (SMDE), dissipated work (DW), and an integrated elastic-perfectly plastic (EPP) analysis
combined with a simplified model testing (SMT), referred to here as the EPP+SMT method. The
evaluation models were calibrated using available standard uniaxial tensile, creep, fatigue, and creep-
fatigue test data, and their predictive capability was assessed against independent validation data from
internally pressurized stepped-cylinder failure tests, referred to as pressurized SMT (p-SMT).

The results demonstrate that no single approach provides perfect agreement with all experimental data;
however, the SMDE, DW, and EPP+SMT methods consistently yield more accurate predictions than the
current ASME time-fraction approach. Among the methods evaluated, one of the EPP+SMT based
approach offers several practical advantages while achieving accuracy comparable to the other work-
based methods and providing notably better predictions for the p-SMT validation data than the SMDE
method. The results for 316H and Alloy 617 further suggest that a separate creep-fatigue interaction
diagram may not be necessary when applying the same EPP+SMT approach. However, the Alloy 709
results indicate that additional investigation is needed to determine whether creep-fatigue interaction
effects should be explicitly incorporated for this material.

Overall, the study provides a technical basis for using the EPP+SMT method with a work-based ductility
approach to extrapolate the life of cyclic high-temperature tests. The results also support the use of a
power-law upper-shelf model for developing the final design curves used with the EPP+SMT
methodology.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Efforts within the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code
(BPVC), Section III, Division 5, have been directed toward the development of elevated-temperature design
rules intended to provide conservative structural criteria and reduce the risk of creep-fatigue failure in high-
temperature reactor components. Continued progress in creep-fatigue evaluation methods for structural
materials is essential to enhancing the economic competitiveness of high-temperature reactors. At present,
the creep-fatigue damage assessment procedure in Subsection HB, Subpart B of BPVC Section III, Division
5 is based on the bilinear creep-fatigue damage interaction diagram, commonly referred to as the damage
diagram, or 𝐷-diagram approach. Although widely used, this method is analytically complex and often
results in overly conservative designs. This conservatism stems both from the way the 𝐷-diagram was
established using standard creep-fatigue data and from the conservative assumptions embedded in the
associated design evaluation procedures.

Recently, two alternative methods for creep-fatigue evaluation have been under development and are
being actively considered by the ASME Code Committee for potential adoption. One is based on the
ductility exhaustion approach (Petkov, Messner, & Spindler, 2026), and the other is based on an
integrated elastic-perfectly plastic (EPP) analysis combined with a simplified model testing (SMT), or
EPP+SMT methodology (Wang, 2024) (Wang, Hou, & Sham, 2022). This report evaluates the accuracy
of the underlying assumptions associated with these emerging methods.

 2. BACKGROUND

Every approach to high temperature cyclic design, including the EPP+SMT method, faces the same
fundamental challenge: we do not have test data covering actual component transient cycles. Creep-
fatigue test data has larger strain ranges and shorter hold times compared to actual reactor component
transients. This is by necessity – many commercial high temperature reactors are being designed with 60-
year service lives, meaning a fully representative test would not fail for at least 60 years under the actual
operating conditions. To produce failure data in realistic lengths of time, researchers increase the strain
range and decrease the hold time compared to actual reactor transients. This yields a practical test with
the failure time on the order of weeks, but means we need to extrapolate this test data to cover actual
reactor conditions.

Oftentimes, creep-fatigue design methods will combine both cyclic and creep test data. Creep testing has
the same fundamental issue as cyclic testing – researchers cannot run experiments with failure times
comparable to reactor component design lives. Instead, creep tests are run at higher stresses or hotter
temperatures and correlated to actual component conditions through time-temperature parameters or other
similar models. However, the data for creep is extrapolated less compared to cyclic tests and for some
materials we have creep failure data at times in excess of 100,000 hours.

All approaches to cyclic design must then extrapolate, often by combining the accelerated cyclic tests
with the longer-term creep data. Extrapolating requires a model for how samples and operating
components accumulate damage and fail. This chapter reviews the details of several common creep-
fatigue damage models below, but for the EPP+SMT approach we have selected a model based on a
critical amount of dissipated work ( (Wang, Hou, & Sham, 2022) Mathematically, this model assumes the
material will fail once it dissipates a critical amount of inelastic work:

 • The accuracy of the work-based method in predicting creep, fatigue, and creep-fatigue failure

This chapter decouples the extrapolation approach, based on dissipated work, from the design method.
The EPP+SMT design method requires a means to estimate the mechanical response of the component
(i.e. an elastic, perfectly-plastic analysis) and makes simplifications reducing the designer’s task to
calculating a strain range for each location in the component, rather than the dissipation. These
approximations have again been examined in past work (Barua, Messner, Wang, Sham, & Jetter, 2021),
leaving the task of validating the extrapolation approach.

An additional goal of this work is to develop a model calibration process that can be applied to historical,
tabulated test data. More recent test campaigns, including the work discussed below on Alloy 617 and
Alloy 709, retained complete records of the mechanical tests, including full creep curves and the
stress/strain/time hysteresis from cyclic tests. This data is unavailable for historical test campaigns,
including the key data underlying the qualification of most of the material currently being used in high
temperature reactor construction. An ideal calibration process for any method would therefore not require
the detailed test data but be feasible based on tabulated test data of the type available from historical
material qualification campaigns. The approach developed here meets this requirement.

A version of this study, not including the EPP+SMT method in the comparison and not including the
Alloy 709 data, was previously published as part of the proceedings of the 2026 ASME Pressure Vessels
and Piping Conference (Petkov, Messner, & Spindler, 2026). This report expands that comparison to
consider the EPP+SMT method and to validate the EPP+SMT approach’s assumptions that no creep-
fatigue interaction diagram is required.

 3. CREEP-FATIGUE EVALUATION APPROACHES

This chapter aims to compare four model families for extrapolating creep and creep-fatigue data to predict
high temperature material failure under cyclic conditions. The comparison includes two variants of two
approaches, giving six model formulations in total. Our goal is to make as fair a comparison as possible.
To this end we use the same sets of material data, fit the models and evaluate their accuracy in the same
way, and ensure that the models have nearly the same number of tunable parameters when fitting the
models against the test data.

3.2.4 EPP+SMT: dissipated work over the entire cycle

Our model for extrapolating failure data for the EPP+SMT method is the same as the dissipated work
model (again considering both options for the critical work to failure). However, the EPP+SMT method
does not require separate creep and fatigue damage calculations. This means the integral carries over the
entire experiment for both creep and creep-fatigue tests. This means no creep-fatigue interaction diagram
is required. The model calibration approach adopted for this study will verify this hypothesis.

fatigue interaction diagram, combining the fatigue and creep damage fraction values. This interaction

because, as shown in Figure 2, it captures the full range of possible behaviors:

 • 𝑟 → 0: “strict” interaction – any amount of fatigue data means the material can accumulate no

3.4 Material data for creep-fatigue damage model calibration and validation

To calibrate and evaluate the models we collated test data for three materials: Alloy 617 (a nickel-based
superalloy), 316H (high carbon stainless steel), and Alloy 709 (an advanced stainless steel). The dataset
for each material includes:

 1) Tensile tests
 2) Creep tests
 3) Strain-controlled pure fatigue tests

 7

 4) Creep-fatigue tests
 5) p-SMT tests of the type described (Wang, Jetter, & Sham, 2016).

The first four types of tests are standard, uniaxial experiments using smooth-bar samples. The p-SMT
tests are unique. They combine internal pressure, axial strain-controlled displacement (with holds), and a
stress concentration in a single test/sample. The combination of internal pressure and axial displacement
means these samples fail under a multiaxial state of stress. These specimens come the closest to
representative operating conditions for reactor components and so are an almost-unique opportunity to
validate the models.

Our general approach then is to calibrate the models against the tensile, creep, fatigue, and creep-fatigue
data and then validate against the p-SMT tests. While the standard, uniaxial data covers a wide range of
temperatures, the p-SMT tests are concentrated at a single temperature for each material. Therefore, we
run the calibration/validation process only for the temperature with the bulk of the p-SMT data,
specifically:

        •   316H: 725 °C
        •   Alloy 617: 950 °C
        •   Alloy 709: 816 °C

For Alloy 617 and Alloy 709 we have complete records for all the test data, including the full
stress/strain/time hysteresis from the cyclic tests. For 316H we do not have this information, but rather
only tabulated test data. As noted above, this will be the situation for the other Section III, Division 5
Class A materials so any model must be able to accommodate historical test databases of this type.

To fit the models against the test data we then need a way to go from the tabulated data to the full
stress/strain/time data required to calculate damage with the different models.

We took two related approaches. For creep tests we use an analytic function to reconstruct a “reasonable”
creep curve from tabulated data. This function defines the creep rate as

3.5 Optimization approach

Each model contains either four or five parameters that must be calibrated against experimental test data.
The number of parameters depends on the number of coefficients used in the associated ductility or creep-
rupture correlation:

 • TF: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 3 parameters from the polynomial
 correlation for creep-rupture life, giving 4 parameters in total.
 • SMDE: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 3 parameters from the ductility
 model, giving 4 parameters in total.
 • DW1: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 3 parameters from the dissipated work
 correlation equation (11) giving 4 parameters in total.
 • DW2: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 5 parameters from the dissipated work
 correlation equation (12), giving 5 parameters in total.
 • EPP+SMT1: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 3 parameters from the
 dissipated work correlation equation (11), giving 4 parameters in total.
 • EPP+SMT2: 1 parameter, r, from the interaction equation (14) and 4 parameters from the
 dissipated work correlation (12), giving 5 parameters in total.

In this list DW1 and EPP+SMT1 mean we use 𝑊#,6 and DW2 and EPP+SMT2 mean we use 𝑊#,8 . We
pre-calibrate the fatigue correlation (all models), the tensile ductility (SMDE), and the Huddleston
coefficient (TF) and do not optimize these against the test data. We calculate the optimal interaction
equation even for models where a linear interaction is typically used (SMDE) or we expect not to show
significant interaction (EPP+SMT). The idea here is to validate these assumptions for the type of
interaction against the test data.

We specifically tune the parameters using the Adam method (Kingma & Ba, 2014) for gradient descent
optimization. We tune the learning rate and the number of iterations to produce a reliable descent to a
minimum in the loss ( Figure 5).

The loss function is the sum, over all tests, of the squared difference between the target creep damage at
failure and the creep damage predicted by the model. We can calculate the actual or target creep damage
at failure by:

 1) Calculating the fatigue damage for the test, 𝐷& . This damage is zero for a creep test by definition
 and can be calculated for creep-fatigue and p-SMT tests with the material’s fatigue diagram
 (independently calibrated against the fatigue test and then not varied during the optimization
 process) and the strain range.
 2) Use the interaction diagram to calculate the target fatigue damage with the expression 𝐷#J =

 3) Calculate the actual creep damage for the test, using one of the damage models described above.
 4) Sum the squared error over each test: ∑(𝐷#J − 𝐷# )8 .

We calibrate the models simultaneously against the creep and creep-fatigue data and retain the multiaxial
p-SMT tests for independent validation of the performance of each model.

For the p-SMT tests the stress, strain, and dissipated work vary both as a function of time but also with
location in the sample. To predict damage, we follow the procedure outlined in 1-3 at each location in the
sample and then choose the worst point with the highest damage to indicate the damage for the entire
sample.

4.3 Damage results

Figure 7 illustrates the accuracy of the calibrated models for all three materials and all six models. This is
a one-to-one plot comparing the target damage to the predicted damage for the trained model for all the
creep-fatigue and creep tests. A perfect model would have a straight line with a one-to-one slope: the
calibrated model would exactly match the target damage from the experiment. Recall the target damage
for a creep experiment is always 1, so these tests would fall on a single point at the upper right of the line.

There are a few trends evident from this comparison, both in terms of the model and material:

 • All models except time fraction have some difficulty in accurately capturing the creep test data.
 There are a few outliers for the SMDE and work-based models where the model greatly
 underpredicts the amount of damage accumulated in a few tests.
 • On the other hand, the time fraction model is highly inaccurate for the creep-fatigue data for all
 three materials.
 • The remaining models at least distribute the scatter in the creep-fatigue predictions both above
 and below the one-to-one line, implying there is at least no skew in their predictions for failure
 under cyclic conditions.
 • The EPP+SMT1 model in particular displays the expected independence of the creep and fatigue

4.4 Creep-fatigue results

Figure 8 and Figure 9 provide more specific comparisons of the model performance for the creep-fatigue
data.

Figure 8 uses the model predictions for damage to translate to the predicted cyclic life of each test. The
figure then shows a one-to-one plot between the actual and predicted sample lives. The diagrams include
the one-to-one line and 2x and 1/2x lines (dashed), as visual guides to how many samples the models can
place within at least two times the experimental life.

The methods all have comparable accuracy except for the time fraction approach. The TF method
substantially underpredicts the life of the Alloy 617 and Alloy 709 samples and skews the predicted
versus actual life distribution for the 316H data.

The remainder of the methods successfully place nearly all tests within a factor of two of the actual
experimental lives (except for a single outlier test for Alloy 709). There is no evident skew in the life
distributions, apart from perhaps the 316H data using the EPP+SMT1 approach.

While there is substantial variability for the three different materials, all of the SMDE, DW, and
EPP+SMT methods perform very comparably, down to the actual location of each test on the one-to-one
diagram.

Figure 9 then plots the calibrated creep-fatigue interaction diagram (red line) versus the tests (blue
points). The diagram also contains a reference interaction line – a bilinear interaction with a knee at
(0.3,0.3) or (0.1,0.1) for time fraction and a straight-line interaction for the remaining models.

These results mostly confirm our expectations for the type of interaction required for each model:

 1) Time fraction: aggressive interaction, corresponding to the ASME bilinear type diagrams.
 2) SMDE and DW: less interaction, approaching a linear interaction diagram. Some materials,
 particularly the Alloy 709 data, do benefit from nonlinear interaction but the need is
 substantially less compared to the TF approach.
 3) EPP+SMT1: no interaction required for 316H and Alloy 617, again confirming the advantage
 of the EPP+SMT method.

However, one unexpected behavior is the nonlinear interaction needed for the EPP+SMT2 approach and
for both EPP+SMT models for Alloy 709. This suggests we retain the original power law ductility
correlation for extrapolating the test data to generate EPP+SMT design curves. The cause of the stronger
interaction in Alloy 709, which can be observed for all the models, is under investigation.

While the difference in the EPP+SMT1 and EPP+SMT2 interaction diagram is somewhat surprising,
these results as well as past experiments strongly suggests the degree of interaction is more a function of
the model than the material. For example, for the same material the SMDE approach will use a different
interaction than the TF approach (specifically, linear interaction for SMDE and a strong nonlinear
interaction for the TF model). Small changes in the calculation of creep damage may therefore have a
significant impact on the required degree of interaction. This suggests that the calibrated interaction
diagrams are not an indication of any inherent material property but rather a product of the way we
analyze the creep-fatigue results.

 6. SUMMARY

This study comprehensively evaluated six methods for extrapolating creep, fatigue, and creep-fatigue
failure data to predict material failure under high-temperature cyclic loading conditions. Although no
method is perfect, the SMDE, DW, and EPP+SMT approaches outperformed the current ASME time-
fraction methodology in predicting creep-fatigue life and reduced its conservatism.

The EPP+SMT method, particularly using the first ductility function (i.e. EPP+SMT1) does have a few
practical advantages over the other methods we considered:

 1) It is as accurate as the other work-based approaches and notably outperforms the SMDE method
 for the p-SMT validation data.
 2) These results support the conclusion that no creep-fatigue interaction diagram is required when
 applying the EPP+SMT approach with the first ductility function (EPP+SMT1), at least for 316H
 and Alloy 617. Further work is needed to understand the Alloy 709 data, which suggests some
 need for interaction even for the unified EPP+SMT approach.

Overall, this study confirms the advantages of the EPP+SMT method and provides a detailed technical
basis for extrapolating the life of cyclic tests using a work-based ductility approach. Specifically, this
study can serve as the technical justification for using the power-law-upper-shelf model for determining
the final design curves used with the EPP+SMT method.
