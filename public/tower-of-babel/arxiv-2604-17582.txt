This paper develops an active sensing framework for designing the transmit and receive beamformers of a multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radar system. In the proposed technique, the beamformers are adaptively designed in each sensing stage based on the measurements made in the previous sensing stages. The beamformers are determined by minimizing the Bayesian Cramér-Rao bound (BCRB) for the estimation of the unknown sensing parameters at each stage via Lagrangian dual optimization. To address the exploration-exploitation tradeoff that is inherent to such an adaptive design, this paper proposes two variants of the BCRB optimization problem: an exploration-centric variant, that ensures that multiple orthogonal beamforming directions are probed in each sensing stage, and an exploitation-centric variant, that does not restrict the number of optimal beamformers. Each variant of the optimization problem is solved via an alternating optimization algorithm that alternates between solving for the transmit beamformers and solving for the receive beamformers. The algorithm is shown to converge to a stationary point provided that each optimization problem is solved to global optimality. Moreover, this paper studies each of the two BCRB optimization sub-problems in the Lagrangian dual domain and shows that despite the non-convexity, global optimality is guaranteed provided that certain sufficient conditions hold. The conditions pertain to the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of a specific direction matrix that can be analytically written in terms of the optimal dual variables. These conditions further imply the tightness of the semidefinite relaxation of the optimization problems. The analysis of the dual problems also provides insights on the number of transmit beamformers that are sufficient to achieve the optimal BCRB. Simulation results demonstrate the benefits of the proposed BCRB-based design compared to state-of-the-art adaptive beamforming strategies.



I
Introduction

Multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radar systems probe sensing targets by simultaneously sending multiple orthogonal waveforms, whose reflections are processed through multiple receive antennas. The primary advantage of MIMO radars is their ability to provide higher spatial resolution, better interference suppression and enhanced parameter estimation compared to conventional phased-array radars
[
27
]
. Harnessing these benefits necessitates the use of highly-directional signaling (i.e., beamforming) in order to effectively steer the energy in the direction of the targets while nulling the interference from other sources. This need is further compelled by practical hardware constraints that often limit the number of radio frequency (RF) chains at the transmit and receive antenna arrays of the radars, and hence the number of beamformers that can be used.

This paper considers an active sensing process in which a MIMO radar system aims to sense multiple targets by sequentially acquiring measurements across multiple sensing stages. In each sensing stage, the radar system can adaptively design the transmit and receive beamformers based on the available historical measurements obtained from previous sensing stages. After a given number of sensing stages, the radar system uses all the acquired measurements to compute an estimate of the desired parameters. This work focuses on the task of adaptively designing the beamformers in order to estimate the angles and the reflection coefficients corresponding to the sensing targets, which are referred to hereafter as
sensing parameters
.

In each sensing stage, the beamformers are designed by optimizing the Bayesian Cramér-Rao bound (BCRB), which is a lower bound on the mean-squared error (MSE) of any unbiased estimator of the sensing parameters
[
42
]
. This bound has been extensively used as a performance metric for sensing applications (e.g.,
[
4
,
28
,

,

,
2
,
43
]
). The main advantage of adopting a Bayesian approach is that non-Bayesian bounds, in general, may depend on the set of unknown parameters that are to be estimated, in which case the optimal beamformers would also depend on those parameters. Furthermore, in many applications such as target tracking (e.g.,
[
23
]
), some prior statistical information on the sensing parameters may be available, which makes the use of Bayesian bounds natural for such applications.

This work provides new insights into the BCRB optimization problem in the active sensing setting. In particular, this paper shows that the performance of an active sensing strategy via BCRB optimization is governed by an
exploration-exploitation tradeoff
. This tradeoff is a central theme in sequential decision making problems
[
41
]
. In the context of MIMO radar sensing, the exploration-exploitation tradeoff is manifested by the radar’s need to balance between
exploring
the environment in order to acquire new information about the targets and between
exploiting
the current knowledge in order to narrow down the search. This paper proposes to approach this tradeoff through a two-stage process: in the first stage, an exploration-centric variant of the BCRB optimization problem is considered, in which the transmit beamformers are constrained to be orthogonal, and in the second stage, an exploitation-centric variant of the BCRB optimization is considered, in which no such restriction is made. The exploration-centric variant ensures that multiple orthogonal beamforming directions are probed, whereas the exploitation-centric variant typically steers beamforming toward a single direction. The results of this paper demonstrate that using an initial exploration phase improves the sensing performance in the active sensing setting, particularly in the low signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) regime.

Furthermore, this paper makes some observations about the single-stage BCRB optimization problem (i.e., the passive sensing setting). In general, when the number of beamformers to be optimized is strictly less than the number of antennas, the minimization of the BCRB metric is a non-convex optimization problem, which makes it challenging to solve in practical systems. By analyzing the optimization problem in the Lagrangian dual domain, this paper derives conditions under which the BCRB optimization can be solved to global optimality, despite the non-convex constraints. More specifically, this paper shows that the BCRB optimization problem reduces to that of maximizing the beamforming gain with respect to a specific direction matrix that can be derived analytically in terms of the optimal dual variables. When this direction matrix satisfies certain conditions pertaining to the multiplicity of its eigenvalues, a globally optimal solution of the BCRB optimization problem can be found. The derived conditions provide a
certificate of optimality
for the proposed beamformer design under the BCRB metric. The analysis of the dual problem also gives insights on the number of transmit beamformers needed to optimize the BCRB metric.

I-B

Main Contributions

This paper develops an active sensing framework for designing the transmit and receive beamformers of a MIMO radar system via BCRB optimization. The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

To design the beamformers adaptively, this paper optimizes the BCRB metric in each sensing stage. Specifically, the posterior distribution of the sensing parameters is tracked and updated across the sensing stages, and used as the prior distribution for the design of the beamformers in the subsequent sensing stages. To address the exploration-exploitation tradeoff that governs the search over the sensing parameter space, this paper proposes a two-stage process in which two variants of the BCRB optimization problem are solved: an exploration-centric variant that constrains the transmit beamformers to be orthogonal, and an exploitation-centric variant that does not restrict the number of optimal transmit beamformers. The simulation results reported in this paper demonstrate that the use of an initial exploration phase significantly improves the sensing performance in the low-to-moderate SNR regime.

2.

For each of the two variants, the transmit and receive beamformers are optimized jointly via an alternating optimization algorithm. That is, for a fixed number of iterations, the algorithm alternates between solving for the transmit beamformers and solving for the receive beamformers. The proposed algorithm is guaranteed to converge to a stationary point as the number of iterations increases, provided that each sub-problem is solved to global optimality.

3.

Each optimization sub-problem (over the transmit/receive beamformers) is analyzed in the Lagrangian dual domain and is shown to simplify to that of maximizing the beamforming gain with respect to a direction matrix that can be analytically written in terms of the optimal dual variables. Despite the non-convexity of each optimization problem, this paper derives sufficient conditions that guarantee the global optimality of the proposed solution. The sufficient conditions pertain to the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of the direction matrix. Furthermore, it is shown that these conditions imply that the semidefinite relaxation of each optimization problem is tight. This analysis gives an alternative approach to find the globally optimal solution of the BCRB optimization problem via Lagrangian duality (provided that the sufficient conditions hold).

4.

In case the sufficient conditions do not hold, the analysis of the Lagrangian dual problem allows to identify the eigenspaces (corresponding to the direction matrix) which necessarily contain the globally optimal set of beamformers. Hence, the analysis allows to reduce the search space of the optimal beamformers. In the case of optimizing over the transmit beamformers, this analysis further implies that the minimum number of transmit beamformers that can achieve the optimal BCRB metric is at most equal to the multiplicity of the largest eigenvalue of the direction matrix. Collectively, these contributions provide a fairly comprehensive understanding of the optimal solutions of the BCRB optimization problem in the context of MIMO radar sensing.

II-B

Active Sensing

This paper considers the active sensing setup of cognitive radars

17
]

Problem Formulation

Based on the above performance metric, the active sensing problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\underset{\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{T},f}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3L}q_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\theta_{i}-{\hat{\theta}}_{i}\|^{2}\right]

(10a)

subject to

\displaystyle({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})=g_{k}({\bf Y}_{1:k-1},{\bf V}_{1:k-1},{\bf W}_{1:k-1})\,\,\forall k,

(10b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}=f({\bf Y}_{1:T},{\bf V}_{1:T},{\bf W}_{1:T}).

(10c)

Problem (
10

\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{T}
and the angle estimator function
f

[
1
,
7
]
, in which an adaptive bisection search of the targets over the angular space is done. The performance of such schemes is governed by the quality of the hierarchical codebook. Instead, it has been shown that the performance can be improved by a codebook-free design, in which problem (
10
) is solved in a data-driven fashion
[
38
,
39
]
. However, the learning-based solutions require training a new model for each specific set of channel parameters (e.g., signal-to-noise ratio, number of sensing stages, desired angle resolution, etc.).

This paper designs a codebook-free beamforming strategy that does not require re-training new models for varying channel conditions. In particular, we consider a Bayesian formulation in which a prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is assumed at the beginning, and then the prior is updated in each subsequent sensing stage and set equal to the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given the previous measurements. Indeed, the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a sufficient statistic for the estimation problem. In this Bayesian framework, we adopt sensing strategies
\{g_{k}^{\mathrm{BCRB}}\}_{k=1}^{T}
that minimize the Bayesian Cramér–Rao bound (BCRB) as a function of the beamforming matrices at each sensing stage, then finally a minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) estimator

\mathrm{WMSE}_{k}\,\geq\,\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})\right),

(12)

where
{\bf Q}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}q_{1},\ldots,q_{3L}\end{bmatrix}\right)
is the weighting matrix.

This paper proposes to minimize the lower bound given in (
12
) in each sensing stage. We consider two variants of this minimization. In the first variant, we design the beamforming matrices in the
k
-th stage by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathsf{(P_{1})}:\quad\,\,\underset{{\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})\right)

(13a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf V}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}})\leq P,

(13b)

where the only constraint is that the transmit beamforming matrix satisfies the power constraint
5
5

5

We remark that the problem formulation considered in this paper assumes constant power for each sensing stage. Alternatively, one can consider a setting with a total power constraint across all sensing stages, in which the power allocation among the sensing stages is to be optimized. Such a setting is left for future work and consideration.
. We remark that this problem formulation implicitly assumes that the sensing parameters are identifiable in each sensing stage, in which case there exists a set of transmit and receive beamformers
{\bf V}_{k}
and
{\bf W}_{k}
such that the BFIM
{\bf J}_{k}({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})
is invertible.

As we shall see later, the adaptive sensing strategy that optimizes (
13
) in each sensing stage has superior performance in the high SNR regime specifically, whereas its performance degrades at low and moderate SNRs. In particular, while the solution
{\bf V}_{k}^{*}
of the optimization problem (
13
) is typically rank-deficient
[
2
]
, we observe through simulations that utilizing a full-rank transmit beamforming matrix in an active setting improves the sensing performance in the low to moderate SNR regime
6
6

6

We note that another reason for the degradation of the performance in the low to moderate SNR regime is the fact that the BCRB lower bound tends to be a looser bound on the MSE in that regime.
. The use of a full-rank transmit beamforming matrix allows for further exploration in the spatial domain, which is particularly useful in the low SNR regime, where the measurements are dominated by additive noise. For this reason, we consider a second variant of the BCRB optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathsf{(P_{2})}:\quad\,\,\underset{{\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})\right)

(14a)

subject to

\displaystyle{\bf V}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf V}_{k}=\frac{P}{M_{T}}{\bf I}_{M_{T}},

(14b)

where the constraint (
14b
), in addition to satisfying the power constraint, also ensures that the transmit beamformers are mutually orthogonal. Hence, in this variant, the BCRB lower bound is minimized while ensuring that multiple orthogonal directions are explored. Following this exposition, we refer to
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
as the
exploitation-centric
variant of the BCRB optimization problem and to
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
as the
exploration-centric
variant of the BCRB optimization problem.

Ultimately, the proposed sensing strategy combines both variants: in the first
T_{\mathrm{explore}}
sensing stages (
0\leq T_{\mathrm{explore}}\leq T
), the beamforming matrices are designed to optimize
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
, thus allowing for some
exploration
in the spatial domain, and in the remaining
T-T_{\mathrm{explore}}
sensing stages, the beamformers are designed to optimize
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
, thus allowing to
exploit
the accumulated knowledge about the target locations. Indeed, the formulation of the active sensing problem touches upon the well-known exploration-exploitation tradeoff, which is a fundamental dilemma in sequential decision making
[
41
]
. In this context, this tradeoff is reflected by the radar’s need to balance between exploring different spatial directions in the search space and between exploiting the accumulated knowledge to locate the targets. Note that if
T_{\mathrm{explore}}=0
, then the sensing strategy optimizes
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
in all sensing stages, and if
T_{\mathrm{explore}}=T
, the sensing strategy optimizes
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
in all the sensing stages. Moreover, we note that, when the radar uses a single transmit beamformer (i.e.,
M_{T}=1
), the two optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
and
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
are equivalent.

For both variants, the beamforming matrices
{\bf V}_{k}
and
{\bf W}_{k}
are adapted based on the previous measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}
. After the receiver makes a new measurement
{\bf Y}_{k}
in the
k
-th sensing stage, the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given
{\bf Y}_{1:k}
is computed and used as the prior distribution for the design of
\mathbf{V}_{k+1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{k+1}
in the next sensing stage. After
T
sensing stages, an estimate
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}
of the sensing parameters is computed via the MMSE estimator, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}=\E\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\bf Y}_{1:T}\right].

(15)

In addition to the BCRB, we remark that there exist other bounds on the mean-squared error that have been considered in the literature, e.g., the Ziv-Zakai bound
[
51
]
, the Weiss-Weinstein bound
[
44
]
, and the Reuven-Messer bound
[
36
]
, which may be tighter bounds compared to the BCRB. Even within the Cramér-Rao bound family, the Miller-Chang bound
[
34
]
is reported to be tighter in certain scenarios. However, these bounds take complicated forms and are significantly more difficult to optimize. In this paper, we restrict our attention to the BCRB given in (
12
).

In the following two sections, we consider the exploitation-centric and the exploration-centric variants of the BCRB optimization problem separately. We reformulate the two problems in the covariance domain and approach each one using an alternating optimization algorithm based on Lagrangian duality. We begin the exposition with the exploitation-centric variant in the next section.

III
Exploitation-Centric BCRB Optimization

In this section, we focus on the exploitation-centric variant
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
of the BCRB optimization problem, which is given in (
13
). This variant of the problem optimizes the BCRB, where the only constraint on the transmit beamformers is that they satisfy the power constraint. This version of the problem is the one typically considered in the literature (e.g.,
[
4
,
28
,
22
,
30
,
50
,
46
,
2
,
3
,
47
,
43
]
). This setting is exploitative because it does not restrict the number of transmit beamforming directions that are probed (e.g., the optimizer of
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
may consist of a single transmit beamformer).

In the following, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the optimal solution of the optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
. First, we show that the problem can be equivalently written in the covariance domain as a rank-constrained semidefinite program (SDP). Given this reformulation, the problem is approached via an alternating optimization algorithm which alternates between solving for the transmit beamformers and solving for the receive beamformers, where each sub-problem is also a rank-constrained SDP. We show that this algorithm converges to a stationary point, provided that each sub-problem is solved to global optimality. By analyzing each sub-problem in the Lagrangian dual domain, we provide sufficient conditions under which global optimality is guaranteed. Under these conditions, it is shown that the semidefinite relaxation (SDR) of each sub-problem is tight.

Derivation of BFIM and Problem Reformulation

The BCRB has been used in prior work as a metric for designing the transmit beamformers assuming a fully-digital receiver architecture (e.g.,

[

(19)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}

\displaystyle={\bf V}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}.

(20)

Note that
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}

We now make some observations about the optimization problem (
13
). First, the BFIM depends on
({\bf V}_{k},{\bf W}_{k})
only through
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
and
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
. In fact, when expressed as
{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
, we observe that the BFIM is a biaffine function of its arguments. In particular, when
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
is held constant, the function
\tilde{{\bf J}}_{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}}^{(1)}({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})\triangleq{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
is an affine function of
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
, and when
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
is held constant, the function
\tilde{{\bf J}}_{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}}^{(2)}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\triangleq{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
is an affine function of
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
. This allows to rewrite the optimization problem (
13
) in terms of
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
and
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
as

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})\right)

(23a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq P,

(23b)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(23c)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(23d)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(23e)

where the constraints (
23b
) and (
23c
) are both necessary and sufficient to construct a matrix
{\bf V}_{k}
satisfying (
13b
), and the constraints (
23d
) and (
23e
) are both sufficient and necessary to construct a matrix
{\bf W}_{k}
such that
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}={\bf W}_{k}({\bf W}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf W}_{k})^{-1}{\bf W}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
.

Second, the minimization of the objective function in (
23
) has a convenient reformulation using the following Schur complement relation

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad d_{\ell}\geq q_{\ell}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}{\bf e}_{\ell}\geq 0,

(24)

where
{\bf e}_{\ell}
denotes the
\ell
-th column of the
3L\times 3L
identity matrix. In particular, using the relation (
24
), we can write
\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}\right)
as the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}\right)=\underset{d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(25a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad\forall\ell.

(25b)

Hence, the optimization problem (
23
) can be rewritten as the following rank-constrained SDP:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}},\\
d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(26a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad\forall\ell,

(26b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq P,

(26c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(26d)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(26e)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\succeq 0.

(26f)

This reformulation has been extensively used in MIMO radar sensing (e.g.,
[
28
,
22
]
), and is crucial for obtaining an efficient numerical algorithm for solving (
13
).

It is worthwhile to point out some key distinctions between (
26
) and other BCRB-based optimization problems considered in the literature. For example, in the sensing-only literature (e.g.,
[
28
,
22
]
), the goal is typically to optimize the transmitted waveform, and hence, it is common to assume a fully-digital receiver (i.e.,
M_{R}=N_{R}
), so that the BCRB optimization is only over the transmit beamformers (i.e., only over
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
). Moreover, the works in
[
28
,
22
]
implicitly assume that the number of beamformers to be optimized is equal to the number of antennas (i.e.,
M_{T}=N_{T}
), in which case the BCRB optimization does not have a rank constraint on
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
, thus making the optimization problem over
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
convex.

Non-convex versions of the BCRB optimization problem appear in the ISAC literature (e.g.,
[
30
,
50
,
47
,
3
,
31
]
). However, the non-convexity in these formulations is only due to the rank-1 constraints that are imposed on the auto-correlation matrices corresponding to the communication beamformers (i.e., a full set of
M_{T}=N_{T}
sensing beamformers is used in these papers). Hence, when specialized to the sensing-only model (i.e., with no communication users), the optimization problems in these works become convex.

An ISAC system that uses a limited number of sensing beamformers has been considered in
[
2
]
. Specifically, the goal of
[
2
]
is to derive upper bounds on the minimum number of sensing beamformers that are needed to optimize the BCRB metric. Since this number is to be minimized, the BCRB optimization problem in this case imposes a rank constraint on
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
, even when the problem is specialized to the sensing-only model. In
[
2
]
, the semidefinite relaxation method is adopted, in which the optimization problem is first solved without the rank constraint on
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
, and then a rank reduction method
[
21
,
35
]
is used to find a lower-rank solution with the same objective value. We note that the optimization considered in
[
2
]
is only over the transmit beamformers.

In the following, we focus on the non-convex optimization problem (
26
), in which both the transmit and receive beamformers are optimized. We approach this problem via an alternating optimization algorithm that alternates between solving for
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
and solving for
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
, where each optimization is studied in the Lagrangian dual domain, and sufficient conditions for global optimality are derived.

III-B

Alternating BCRB Optimization

Motivated by the biaffine structure of
{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
, this paper proposes to optimize the transmit and receive matrices
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
and
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
in (
26
) via an alternating optimization algorithm. That is, the algorithm iterates between solving for
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
for a fixed
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
, and solving for
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
for a fixed
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
. Specifically, for a fixed
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
, the optimization problem over
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(27a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\,\,\forall\ell,

(27b)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(27c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(27d)

and for a fixed
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
, the optimization problem over
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(28a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\,\,\forall\ell,

(28b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq P,

(28c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0.

(28d)

We propose to solve (
26
) using the following iterative algorithm:

1.

Initialize a random
{\bf V}_{k}^{(0)}
such that
\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf V}_{k}^{(0)}({\bf V}_{k}^{(0)})^{\mathsf{H}}\right)\leq P
.

2.

For iteration index
i=1,2,\ldots,I_{\mathrm{max}}
, do:

i)

Find the solution
{\bf W}_{k}^{(i)}
of (
27
) when
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}={\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}^{(i-1)}}
.

ii)

Find the solution
{\bf V}_{k}^{(i)}
of (
28
) when
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}={\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}^{(i)}}
.

This algorithm has the property that the BCRB metric does not increase across iterations. In particular, if we denote

\beta_{i}\triangleq\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf Q}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}\big({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}^{(i)}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}^{(i)}}\big)\right)

(29)

as the value of the objective function at the end of the
i
-th iteration, then the sequence
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{max}}}
is guaranteed to be non-negative and non-increasing. This implies that the alternating optimization algorithm converges to a limit point. Since the two constraint sets over
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
and
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}
are disjoint, the limit point is a stationary point of the optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
, provided that each sub-problem is solved to global optimality
[
15
]
.

The optimization problems (
27
) and (
28
) are non-convex due to the orthogonal projection constraint (
27c
) and the rank constraints (
27d
) and (
28d
). In the following, we show that despite the non-convex constraints, the optimization problems (
27
) and (
28
) can be solved to global optimality, provided that certain sufficient conditions hold. To derive these conditions, we approach the two optimization problems in the Lagrangian dual domain.

III-C

Receive Beamforming Via Lagrangian Duality

In this part, we focus on the optimization problem (
27
). We derive its dual problem, and show that the semidefinite relaxation of (
27
) is tight, under a specific multiplicity condition. In particular, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

The dual problem of (
27
) can be expressed as

\underset{{\bf\Lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{3L\times 3L}}{\mathrm{maximize}}\quad 2\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Lambda}{\bf Q}^{1/2})-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{({\mathsf{P}})}{\bf\Lambda}\right)-2\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right),

(30)

where
\mu_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
-th largest eigenvalue, and

{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\triangleq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{T}}\E\left[\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right],

(31)

in which
\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{{\bf H}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf v}_{k,m}&\cdots&\dot{{\bf H}}_{3L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf v}_{k,m}\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bf v}_{k,m}
is the
m
-th beamformer of
{\bf V}_{k}
. The dual problem (
30
) is unconstrained and convex. Let
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
denote the solution of (
30
). If

\mu_{M_{R}}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}\right)>\mu_{M_{R}+1}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}\right),

(32)

then

{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}={\bf W}_{k}^{*}({\bf W}_{k}^{*})^{\mathsf{H}},

(33)

is the globally optimal solution of (
27
), where

{\bf W}_{k}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf w}_{k,1}^{*}&\cdots&{\bf w}_{k,M_{R}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}

(34)

and
{\bf w}_{k,1}^{*},\ldots,{\bf w}_{k,M_{R}}^{*}
are the eigenvectors of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
(with unit norm) corresponding to the
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues.

Theorem
1
gives the representation of the optimization problem (
27
) in the Lagrangian dual domain. It shows that the dual problem is
unconstrained
and
convex
. Moreover, a globally optimal solution of the primal problem (
27
) can be derived from the optimal solution of the dual problem, provided that the condition (
32
) holds. This condition pertains to the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
: if there is a strictly positive gap between the
M_{R}
-th and
(M_{R}+1)
-th eigenvalues (counting multiplicities), then global optimality is guaranteed. Hence, the condition (
32
) serves as a certificate of optimality of the solution given in (
33
). In other words, once a solution
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
of the dual problem (
30
) is obtained, one can check the global optimality of the solution given in (
33
) by examining the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
. The proof of Theorem
1
is provided in Appendix
B
.

Remark 1
.

The proof of Theorem
1
implies that the optimal receive beamformers necessarily belong to the union of the eigenspaces corresponding to the
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
. If condition (
32
) holds, this union of eigenspaces is completely characterized by the optimal solution (
33

-th beamformer. While the optimal choice is guaranteed to belong to the eigenspace corresponding to

Corollary 2
.

If condition (
32

27

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(35a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\

(35b)

\displaystyle{\bf 0}\preceq{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\preceq{\bf I}_{N_{R}},

(35c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},

(35d)

is tight.

Proof.

Following similar steps as in the proof of Theorem
1
, it can be shown that the dual of the semidefinite relaxation (
35

) is equivalent to (

), Slater’s condition is satisfied for the constraint (

∎

Corollary
2
shows the tightness of the semidefinite relaxation (

) and (

Together, Theorem
1
and Corollary

suggest the following approach to solving the optimization problem (
27

35

\tilde{{\bf R}}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}

\tilde{{\bf R}}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
is not guaranteed to have the desired low rank. To get the desired low-rank solution, the proof of Theorem

shows that the optimal solution
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
of the dual problem can be recovered from

as

{\bf\Lambda}^{*}={\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},\tilde{{\bf R}}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}){\bf Q}^{1/2}.

(36)

Given

Remark 2
.

Alternatively, one can directly solve the dual problem (
30
) to get its optimal solution
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
. One way of solving the dual problem is by formulating it as an SDP. However, it can be shown that the SDP formulation of (
30
) imposes a semidefinite constraint on a matrix whose dimension is quadratic in the number of receive antennas, which renders its complexity too high for practical implementation, especially when
N_{R}
is large. Another approach is to solve (
30
) using a gradient-descent-based algorithm, which is guaranteed to converge to a globally optimal solution since the dual problem (
30
) is unconstrained and convex. However, it is not straightforward to choose an appropriate step size that guarantees a reasonable convergence speed. For these reasons, in this paper, we adopt the semidefinite relaxation approach which first solves (
35

{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
using (
36
), as described in the previous paragraph.

III-D

Transmit Beamforming Via Lagrangian Duality

In this part, we consider the optimization problem (
28
) for the design of the transmit beamformers. As in the previous section, we show that the dual problem of (
28
) can be expressed as an unconstrained convex problem, and we derive a sufficient condition under which the semidefinite relaxation of (
28
) is tight. The following theorem holds for the optimal design of the transmit beamformers.

Theorem 3
.

The dual problem of (
28

\underset{{\bf\Lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{3L\times 3L}}{\mathrm{maximize}}\quad 2\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Lambda}{\bf Q}^{1/2})-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{({\mathsf{P}})}{\bf\Lambda}\right)-2P\mu_{1}\left(\tilde{{\bf P}}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right),

(37)

where

denotes the largest eigenvalue, and

{\bf W}_{k}

37

(39)

then

{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}^{*}={\bf V}_{k}^{*}({\bf V}_{k}^{*})^{\mathsf{H}},

(40)

is the globally optimal solution of (

{\bf V}_{k}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{P}{M_{T}}}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf v}_{k,1}^{*}&\cdots&{\bf v}_{k,1}^{*}\end{bmatrix}

(41)

and

is the eigenvector of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}(\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
3
follows similar steps as that of Theorem
1
. A proof sketch is given in Appendix
C
.
∎

Notice the difference between the two dual problems (
30
) and (

) depends on the
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues of a specific direction matrix, (
37

32

orthogonal beamformers, whereas (

{\bf W}_{k}
in the channel model (
6
) affects the noise covariance matrix, which ultimately results in imposing the orthogonal projection constraint (
27c
).

Remark 3
.

The proof of Theorem
3
implies that the optimal transmit beamformers necessarily belong to the eigenspace corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}(\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
. If the multiplicity of the largest eigenvalue is one (i.e., condition (
39
) holds), the eigenspace is completely characterized by the unique largest eigenvector (i.e., the optimal solution (
41
)). If the multiplicity of the largest eigenvalue is larger than one (i.e., condition (
39
) does not hold), there is some ambiguity about the optimal choice of the beamformers within the eigenspace, in which case global optimality cannot be guaranteed.

Remark 4
.

The fact that the optimal transmit beamformers belong to the eigenspace corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}(\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}}
implies that the minimum number of transmit beamformers that is sufficient to optimize the BCRB metric is at most equal to the multiplicity of the largest eigenvalue. While the exact dependence of the minimum number of beamformers on the system parameters (e.g., the prior distribution, the number of sensing targets, the number of transmit/receive antennas) is complicated
[
2
]
, the identification of the optimal eigenspace allows to reduce the search space.

Corollary 4
.

If condition (
39
) holds, the semidefinite relaxation of the optimization problem (
28
), given by

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(42a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\,\,\forall\ell,

(42b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq P,

(42c)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(42d)

is tight.

Proof.

The semidefinite relaxation (
42
) can be obtained by dropping the rank constraint from the optimization problem (
28
). When condition (
39
) holds, Theorem
3
asserts that the optimization problem (
28

42

∎

Remark 5
.

Note that the dual of the semidefinite relaxation (
42
) with respect to the constraint (
42b
) is equivalent to the dual problem (
37
).

Theorem
3
and Corollary
4
imply that the BCRB optimization of the transmit beamformers can be conducted by solving the semidefinite relaxation (
42
). In particular, denoting the solution of (
42

\tilde{{\bf R}}_{{\bf V}_{k}}^{*}
, the optimal solution
\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}
of the dual problem can be computed as

\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}={\bf J}_{k}^{-1}(\tilde{{\bf R}}_{{\bf V}_{k}}^{*},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}){\bf Q}^{1/2},

(43)

which can be used to find a low-rank solution according to (

is one (i.e., condition (
39

Remark 6
.

Similar to the observation made in Remark
2
, one can alternatively find the optimal dual variable
\tilde{{\bf\Lambda}}^{*}
by directly solving the dual problem (
37
) (e.g., using its SDP formulation or using gradient descent). However, this approach tends to have a higher implementation complexity.

IV
Exploration-Centric BCRB Optimization

This section considers the exploration-centric variant
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
of the BCRB optimization problem, which is given in (
14
). This variant optimizes the BCRB metric, while restricting the transmit beamformers to be mutually orthogonal. Hence, the optimal solution is guaranteed to explore multiple orthogonal beamforming directions, which makes it suitable for an initial exploration phase. To solve the optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
, we proceed in a similar way as our approach to
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
in Section
III
. In particular, the problem is reformulated in the covariance domain and then approached via alternating optimization, while taking into account the orthogonal constraint on the transmit beamformers. Each sub-problem is then analyzed in the Lagrangian dual domain to derive sufficient conditions for global optimality.

The technical steps that are involved are relatively similar to those followed in Section
III
. Thus, we focus here on the key difference with respect to the optimization
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
, which is the orthogonal constraint (
14b
) on the transmit beamformers. More specifically, when the problem is expressed in terms of the correlation matrix
{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}={\bf V}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
, the orthogonal constraint can be equivalently written through two constraints: 1)
\frac{M_{T}}{P}{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}
is an orthogonal projection matrix, and 2) that
\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})=M_{T}
. These two constraints are both necessary and sufficient to construct a matrix
{\bf V}_{k}
satisfying the orthogonal constraint (
14b
). Hence, following similar steps that lead to (
26
) in Section
III
, the optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
can be expressed in the covariance domain as

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}},\\
d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(44a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad\forall\ell,

(44b)

\displaystyle\frac{M_{T}}{P}{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(44c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})=M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(44d)

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(44e)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\succeq 0.

(44f)

Notice that the only difference with (
26

), whereas in (

is constrained.

The optimization problem (

{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}

{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}

{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}d_{\ell}

(45a)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\,\,\forall\ell,

(45b)

\displaystyle\frac{M_{T}}{P}{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(45c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})=M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0.

can be approached by iterating between solving (
27

1

If condition (

V
Proposed Active Sensing Strategy

In this section, we describe how the two variants of the BCRB optimization problem can be combined in designing an active sensing strategy for MIMO radar systems. To utilize either of these variants, it is crucial to compute the posterior distribution of the sensing parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given the observed measurements. Hence, we first start by showing how this posterior distribution can be efficiently tracked across the sensing stages, before we proceed to describe the proposed active sensing algorithm.

V-A

Tracking of the Posterior Distribution

Solving the BCRB optimization problems (
27
) and (
28
) requires the computation of the BFIM
{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
given in equations (
III-A
)–(
21
). The expectation in these equations is taken over the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given the previous measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}
. To approximate this posterior distribution, one can consider a discretized grid of size
K
for each of the sensing parameters of
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathchar 572\relax\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\},\mathchar 573\relax\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3L}
and track the evaluations of the posterior distribution of each sensing parameter over each grid point. However, this approach requires
K^{3L}
posterior updates in each sensing stage, which is infeasible in practice, even when sensing a single target (i.e.,
L=1
). Instead, this paper makes the observation that the conditional distribution of the fading coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
given the AoA’s
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and the previous measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}
is complex Gaussian, for which only the mean and the covariance matrix need to be tracked. This follows from the linearity of the measurement model (
6
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Hence, a discretized grid is needed only for the posterior distribution of the AoA’s
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, thus requiring
K^{L}
posterior updates, which can be done efficiently for up to
L=2
sensing targets.

The mean and the covariance matrix of the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
given
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and the previous measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k}
can be tracked using Kalman filtering
[
13
,
38
]
. In particular, let
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}
denote the corresponding mean and covariance matrix respectively. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}{\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\big)^{-1}\left(\mathrm{vec}({\bf Y}_{k})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}{\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\big)^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}{\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\big)^{\mathsf{H}},

(51)

where we have used

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}

\displaystyle={\bf C}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},k}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}{\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}{\bf C}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},k}^{\mathsf{H}},

(52)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}

\displaystyle={\bf C}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}{\bf C}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},k}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bf W}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf W}_{k},

and

{\bf C}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},k}=\left({\bf V}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes{\bf W}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\right)\left(\overline{{\bf A}}_{\rm T}(\boldsymbol{\phi})*{\bf A}_{\rm R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right),

(53)

in which
{\bf A}_{\rm S}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}_{\rm S}(\phi_{1})&\cdots&{\bf a}_{\rm S}(\phi_{L})\end{bmatrix}
for
{\rm S}\in\{{\rm R},{\rm T}\}
,
\otimes
and
*
denote the Kronecker product and column-wise Khatri–Rao product of two matrices respectively, and
\overline{{\bf A}}_{\rm T}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
denotes the element-wise conjugate of
{\bf A}_{\rm T}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
. Since the fading coefficients are initially assumed to have a zero-mean complex Gaussian distribution with unit variance, the tracking of the mean and the covariance matrix is initialized with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}={\bf 0}_{L\times 1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}={\bf I}_{L}
for each
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

It remains to track the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
given the previous measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}
. This can be done by a standard application of Bayes’ rule. In particular, if
\pi_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\phi})}
denotes the posterior probability density function of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
given the first
k
measurements
{\bf Y}_{1:k}
, then
\pi_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\phi})}
can be computed recursively as follows:

\pi_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{\phi})}=\frac{\pi_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\phi})}f({\bf Y}_{k}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi},{\bf Y}_{1:k-1})}{\int_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}\pi_{k-1}^{(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime})}f({\bf Y}_{k}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},{\bf Y}_{1:k-1})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}},

(54)

where the denominator in (
54
) is a normalization term that does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. Note that
f({\bf Y}_{k}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi},{\bf Y}_{1:k-1})
is
\mathcal{CN}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1}^{({\bf y}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\boldsymbol{\phi})}
are as computed in (

VI
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed active sensing strategy as compared to several existing beamforming strategies in the literature. We consider the following baseline schemes:

– Random orthogonal beamforming:
In this baseline, the transmit and receive beamforming matrices for all
T
sensing stages are generated uniformly at random over the set of matrices with orthogonal columns, with the transmit beamformers being scaled to satisfy the power constraint. As in the proposed sensing strategy, the posterior distribution of the sensing parameters is tracked across the sensing stages, and their final estimates are computed via the MMSE estimator (
15
).

– Steering beamformers at the MMSE estimate of sensing parameters:
In this baseline scheme, the transmit and receive beamformers are steered based on the MMSE estimates of the sensing parameters. In particular, if
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{k}=\E\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right]
is the MMSE estimate of the sensing parameters in the
k
-th sensing stage, the scheme computes an estimate of the target response matrix
{\bf H}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{k})
, sets the receive beamforming matrix
{\bf W}_{k}
to its left singular vectors corresponding to the largest
M_{R}
singular values, and sets the transmit beamforming matrix
{\bf V}_{k}
to the right singular vector corresponding to the largest singular value, scaled appropriately to satisfy the power constraint. Note that this choice of the beamforming matrices maximizes the beamforming gain if the target response matrix were to be exactly equal to
{\bf H}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{k})
.

– Deep learning solutions:
Several deep-learning-based solutions have been proposed in the literature in order to adaptively design the beamforming matrices for radar sensing. In particular, the approach taken in
[
39
]
employs a long short-term memory (LSTM) to model the temporal correlation between the received symbols. At each sensing stage, a feedforward neural network is used to map the LSTM hidden state to the set of beamformers that are used in the next sensing stage. The model is trained
end-to-end
for a predetermined number of sensing stages. We note that, while the LSTM model in
[
39
]
was used to design the receive beamformers only (i.e., a single-antenna transmitter was assumed), a similar approach can be followed to design both the transmit and receive beamformers.

In the following, we conduct several experiments to compare these baseline schemes with the proposed active sensing strategy. For all the experiments, the AoAs are assumed to be uniformly distributed over
[\phi_{\mathrm{min}},\phi_{\mathrm{max}}]=[-\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{3}]
, and a grid set of size
K=1024
is used for the computation of the posterior distribution. The goal in these experiments is to estimate the AoAs only (not the fading coefficients), and hence, the weighting matrix is set to
{\bf Q}=\frac{1}{L}\mathrm{diag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 1}_{1\times L},{\bf 0}_{1\times 2L}\end{bmatrix}\right)
. In this case, the performance of the beamforming strategies is measured by the

\mathrm{WMSE}\triangleq\frac{1}{L}\E\left[\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}}\|^{2}\right],

(55)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{L})
are the AoAs.

VI-B

Single Target – Optimization of Transmit Beamformers

In a second experiment, the beamforming strategies are compared for a MIMO radar that is equipped with
N_{T}=8
transmit antennas and a single receive antenna. The radar aims to sense a single target in
T=8
sensing stages, and uses
M_{T}=4
transmit beamformers in each sensing stage. In this case, since
M_{T}>1
, the optimization problems
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
and
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
are different, and the choice of the parameter
T_{\mathrm{explore}}
determines the number of sensing stages in which orthogonal directions are
explored
through transmit beamforming. We note that since the optimization is only over the transmit beamformers, a single iteration of optimization is used (i.e.,
I_{\mathrm{max}}=1
).

We compare three instances of the proposed sensing strategy: 1)
T_{\mathrm{explore}}=0
, in which case the exploitation-centric optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
is considered in all sensing stages, 2)
T_{\mathrm{explore}}=4
, in which case the exploration-centric optimization
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
is optimized in the first 4 sensing stages and the exploitation-centric
\mathsf{(P_{1})}
is optimized in the remaining 4 sensing stages, and 3)
T_{\mathrm{explore}}=8
, in which case
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
is optimized in all sensing stages. Fig.
3
shows the performance of the different beamforming strategies as a function of the received SNR.

Several observations can be made. First, the optimal exploration-exploitation tradeoff in the proposed
BCRB optimization based active sensing strategy depends on the SNR. In the low-SNR regime, an exploration-only strategy shows the best sensing performance.
In the moderate SNR regime, exploration-followed-by-exploitation performs the best.
In the high-SNR regime, an exploitation-only strategy has the superior performance, which is closely approached by an exploration-followed-by-exploitation strategy.
Thus, exploring multiple orthogonal directions through solving the exploration-centric optimization problem
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
, particularly in the earlier sensing stages, can improve the sensing performance in the low and moderate SNR regimes.

Second, designing multiple orthogonal directions by solving

The LSTM model outperforms BCRB optimization because the BCRB tends to be a looser bound on the WMSE at low SNRs, thus an optimal BCRB metric does not necessarily translate to optimal WMSE performance.
Furthermore, in contrast to BCRB optimization, the LSTM model is trained end-to-end, and hence it can learn to explore spatial directions in earlier sensing stages, even with a single beamformer. In other words, using additional beamformers (with less power allocated to each beamformer) does not necessarily help the exploration in an end-to-end trained LSTM model. On the other hand, the analytic approach of optimizing the BCRB is naturally greedy and exploitative (i.e., each sensing stage focuses on optimizing a lower bound of the immediate WMSE), and hence exploration needs to be imposed by using multiple beamformers in the optimization formulation
\mathsf{(P_{2})}
.

These observations highlight the gain from considering an exploration phase in multi-stage active sensing, particularly in the low and moderate SNR regimes, and the different ways that such an exploration can be conducted within the active sensing strategy.

VII
Conclusion

This paper presents an active sensing framework for designing the transmit and receive beamformers of a MIMO radar system using BCRB optimization. It is shown that the sensing performance of a BCRB-based beamforming strategy is governed by an exploration-exploitation tradeoff, in which the radar has to balance between the use of many transmit beamformers in order to explore the sensing environment and the use of a few transmit beamformers in order to exploit the accumulated knowledge about the targets. This work also provides new insights about BCRB optimization. In particular, it is shown that, despite its non-convexity, the BCRB optimization problem can be solved to global optimality provided that certain sufficient conditions (pertaining to the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of a specific direction matrix) hold. These conditions are derived in terms of the optimal solution of the dual problem, which can be efficiently computed. These theoretical insights provide a comprehensive understanding of BCRB-based MIMO radar sensing, particularly in the active setting.

3
]
is to drop the non-convex constraints in the BCRB optimization problem and derive the Lagrangian dual of the resulting relaxed problem. This approach works since the relaxation is tight when a full set of
N_{T}
sensing beamformers is used (which is the running assumption in
[
3
]
). However, that is not necessarily the case when a limited number of
M_{T}
sensing beamformers (
M_{T}<N_{T}
) is used. Therefore, here we derive the Lagrangian dual problem without dropping the non-convex constraints, and the analysis of this problem allows to derive sufficient conditions under which global optimality is guaranteed (i.e., condition (
32
)).

Towards this end, consider the primal problem (
27
). We derive the Lagrangian dual with respect to the constraint (
27b
). Let
{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{3L}
denote the dual variables defined as

{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}&-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\\
-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&\nu_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}.

(65)

Then, the dual problem can be written as

\underset{{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{\ell}\succeq 0\,\forall\ell}{\text{max}}\,\,\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\in\mathcal{R},\\
d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}\end{subarray}}{\min}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}\Big(d_{\ell}(1-\nu_{\ell})+2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}{\bf J}_{k})\Big),

(66)

where
\mathcal{R}
denotes the non-convex constraints (
27c
) and (
27d
), and
{\bf J}_{k}\triangleq{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})
is used for notational convenience. Optimizing over
d_{1},\ldots,d_{3L}
, we conclude that
\nu_{\ell}^{*}=1
for all
\ell
. Then, the dual problem becomes

\underset{{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\forall\ell}{\text{max}}\quad\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\in\mathcal{R}}{\text{min}}\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}\Big(2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}{\bf J}_{k})\Big),

(67)

where the condition
{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}
follows from the Schur complement of the constraint
{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{\ell}\succeq 0
. Recall that

{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})={\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})+{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})},

(68)

hence, solving the inner minimization in (
67
) is equivalent to solving the following problem

\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\in\mathcal{R}}{\text{max}}\qquad\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf A}{\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})\right),

(69)

where
{\bf A}\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq 0
. Note that, for any
{\bf A}\succeq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf A}{\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3L}[{\bf A}]_{i,j}\left[{\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=2\mathchar 572\relax\hskip-1.19995pt\left\{\hskip-1.19995pt\mathrm{tr}\hskip-1.19995pt\left(\hskip-1.19995pt{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\hskip-1.19995pt\sum_{i,j=1}^{3L}[{\bf A}]_{i,j}\E\left[\dot{{\bf H}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\dot{{\bf H}}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right]\hskip-1.19995pt\right)\hskip-1.19995pt\right\}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}{\bf P}_{\bf A}\right),

(70)

where

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{{\bf A}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{i,j=1}^{3L}[{\bf A}]_{i,j}\E\left[\dot{{\bf H}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\dot{{\bf H}}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right]

(71)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{T}}\E\left[\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf A}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right],

(72)

in which
\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{{\bf H}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf v}_{k,m}&\cdots&\dot{{\bf H}}_{3L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf v}_{k,m}\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bf v}_{k,m}
is the
m
-th beamformer of
{\bf V}_{k}
. Since
{\bf A}\succeq 0
, we have
{\bf P}_{{\bf A}}\succeq 0
. Thus, the inner minimization in (
67
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}}{\text{maximize}}\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}{\bf P}_{\bf A}\right)

(73a)

subject to

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\text{ is an orthogonal projection matrix},

(73b)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}})=M_{R},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}\succeq 0.

(73c)

The key observation is that (
73
) has the following analytic solution based on the eigenvalue decomposition of
{\bf P}_{\bf A}
:

\tilde{{\bf R}}_{\tilde{{\bf W}}_{k}}=\tilde{{\bf W}}_{k}\tilde{{\bf W}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}},

(74)

where
\tilde{{\bf W}}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{{\bf w}}_{1}&\ldots&\tilde{{\bf w}}_{M_{R}}\end{bmatrix}
,
and
\tilde{{\bf w}}_{j}
is the eigenvector of
{\bf P}_{{\bf A}}
corresponding to the
j
-th largest eigenvalue. The solution (
74
) follows from the Ky Fan variational characterization for the sum of the
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix
[
10
]
. In particular, this characterization states that, for any Hermitian matrix
{\bf C}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times N_{R}}
, the sum of its largest
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues can be expressed as

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}({\bf C})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times M_{R}}:\\
{\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf U}={\bf I}\end{subarray}}{\max}\,\,\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf C}{\bf U}\right),

(75)

where the maximizer
{\bf U}^{*}
consists of the eigenvectors corresponding to the
M_{R}
largest eigenvalues of
{\bf C}
. Note that if we denote
{\bf R}_{{\bf U}}={\bf UU}^{\mathsf{H}}
, the objective function in (
75
) can be written as
\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf U}}{\bf C})
, and the constraint set in (
75
) can be equivalently expressed as the conditions that: 1)
{\bf R}_{{\bf U}}
is an orthogonal projection matrix, and 2)
\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf U}})=M_{R}
. Hence, this characterization gives the solution of the optimization problem (
73
).

The dual problem (
67
) can then be written as

\underset{{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\forall\ell}{\text{maximize}}\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}\Big(2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})})\Big)-2\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf A}}\right),

(76)

where
{\bf A}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq 0
, and
\mu_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
-th largest eigenvalue of a matrix.

Observe that, for any
{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}
, we have that
\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})})\geq\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})})
(since
{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})}
is a positive semidefinite matrix). Also, we have
{\bf A}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}\succeq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\triangleq{\bf B}
, and hence,

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf A}}\right)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}({\bf P}_{{\bf B}}).

(77)

It follows that the optimal solution of (
76
) should satisfy
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}
, so that
{\bf\Gamma}_{\ell}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}
for each
\ell
. Therefore, by denoting
{\bf\Lambda}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3L}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{3L\times 3L}
, we can write
{\bf B}={\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}
, and the optimization problem (
76
) becomes

\underset{{\bf\Lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{3L\times 3L}}{\text{maximize}}\quad 2\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Lambda}{\bf Q}^{1/2})-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})}{\bf\Lambda}\right)-2\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right).

(78)

Clearly, the optimization problem (
78
) is unconstrained, and since it is equivalent to the Lagrangian dual of the primal problem (
27
), the problem is also convex. To verify the convexity, we can check that the objective function in (
78
) is concave. To see this, notice that the function

f_{1}({\bf\Lambda})\triangleq\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})}{\bf\Lambda}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}

(79)

is a convex function (since
{\bf J}_{k-1}^{(\mathsf{P})}
is a positive semidefinite matrix). Also, consider the function

\displaystyle f_{2}({\bf\Lambda})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times M_{R}}:\\
{\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf U}={\bf I}\end{subarray}}{\max}\,\,\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\bf U}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times M_{R}}:\\
{\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf U}={\bf I}\end{subarray}}{\max}\,\,\sum_{m=1}^{M_{T}}\E\left[\left\|{\bf U}^{\mathsf{H}}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf\Lambda}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right]

(80)

where we used in
(a)
the Ky Fan variational characterization of the sum of the largest
M_{R}
eigenvalues
[
10
]
, and we used in
(b)
the definition of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}
. Since the function inside the expectation in (
B
) is the composition of a convex function and a linear function, and since the pointwise maximum of a family of convex functions is convex, it follows that the function
f_{2}
is convex. This confirms that the objective function in (
78
) is concave, and thus, that the optimization problem is convex.

Now, we proceed to show that
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
given in (
33
) is the optimal primal solution, provided that condition (
32
) holds. Let
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
denote the optimal solution of the dual problem (
30
). Hence, we need to show that
({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
is an optimal primal-dual pair of the optimization problem (
27
). To show this, it is sufficient to show that

1)

{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
is feasible for the primal problem,

2)

{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
is feasible for the dual problem,

3)

{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
is the minimizer of the Lagrangian function at
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
,

4)

({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
satisfy the complementary slackness condition.

The key here is that these conditions are sufficient for optimality even when the optimization problem (
27
) is non-convex
[
6
, Proposition 5.3.2]
.

In the following, we verify that these conditions are satisfied for the pair
({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
. First, condition (1) holds since
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
, as constructed in (
33
), is an orthogonal projection matrix of rank
M_{R}
, and hence, is feasible for the primal problem (
27
). Condition (2) clearly holds since
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
is the solution of the dual problem. Condition (3) holds by following a similar derivation as the one leading to (
74
) while replacing
{\bf A}
with
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}({\bf\Lambda}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}
. It remains to show the complementary slackness condition (4). Recall that at the optimal
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
, the dual variables in (
65
) can be written as

{\bf\tilde{\Lambda}}_{\ell}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}&-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}\\
-(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\forall\ell,

(81)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}
is the
\ell
-th column of
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
. Moreover, consider the slack variables

d_{\ell}^{*}=q_{\ell}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}){\bf e}_{\ell},

(82)

which satisfy the constraint (
27b
) at
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
. Hence, we need to show the complementary slackness condition:

\mathrm{tr}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*})&\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}\\
\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}&d_{\ell}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}&-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}\\
-(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}&1\end{bmatrix}\right)=0,\,\,\forall\,\ell,

(83)

which can be rewritten as the following condition:

(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}-2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}+d_{\ell}^{*}=0,\quad\forall\,\ell.

(84)

This condition holds if we can show that

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}=\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf J}_{k}^{-1}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}){\bf e}_{\ell},\quad\forall\,\ell.

(85)

In the following, we show that, when condition (
32
) holds, the equality (
85
) follows from the first-order optimality of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}
.

Towards this end, let

f({\bf\Lambda})=2\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Lambda}{\bf Q}^{1/2})-\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k-1}^{({\mathsf{P}})}{\bf\Lambda}\right)-2\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right)

(86)

denote the objective function in (
30
). Since
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
is the maximizer of
f
, and since
f
is a concave function, it follows that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}}f({\bf\Lambda})\Big|_{{\bf\Lambda}={\bf\Lambda}^{*}}=0,\qquad\forall\,\ell=1,\ldots,3L.

(87)

To compute the partial derivative of
f({\bf\Lambda})
with respect to
\Lambda_{j\ell}
, let us first focus on the last term in
f
, denoted by

\tilde{f}({\bf\Lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{R}}\mu_{i}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right).

(88)

We have

\frac{\partial\tilde{f}({\bf\Lambda})}{\partial\Lambda_{j\ell}}=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{f}({\bf\Lambda})}{\partial{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}\frac{\partial{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}}{\partial\Lambda_{j\ell}}\right),

(89)

which follows from the chain rule for matrix-valued functions. The key observation is that the function
\tilde{f}
is differentiable if and only if there is strict gap between the
M_{R}
-th and
(M_{R}+1)
-th largest eigenvalues, i.e., if and only if
\mu_{M_{R}}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right)>\mu_{M_{R}+1}\left({\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right)
. In this case, the gradient is smooth and unique, with

\frac{\partial\tilde{f}({\bf\Lambda})}{\partial{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}}=\tilde{{\bf U}}\tilde{{\bf U}}^{\mathsf{H}},

(90)

where
\tilde{{\bf U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{{\bf u}}_{1}&\cdots&\tilde{{\bf u}}_{M_{R}}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\tilde{{\bf u}}_{i}
is the eigenvector of
{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}
corresponding to the
i
-th largest eigenvalue. Hence, at the optimal
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
, provided that condition (
32
) holds, this gradient is equal to
{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}
given in (
33
). Moreover, using standard vector calculus, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf P}_{{\bf\Lambda\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}}{\partial\Lambda_{j\ell}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{T}}\E\big[

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}{\bf e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle+\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf e}_{j}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\big].

(91)

This implies that, at the optimal
{\bf\Lambda}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{f}({\bf\Lambda})}{\partial\Lambda_{j\ell}}\Bigg|_{{\bf\Lambda}={\bf\Lambda}^{*}}\hskip-2.5pt

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{T}}2\mathchar 572\relax\left\{{\bf e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\E\left[\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*}\dot{{\bf G}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right]\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}\right\}

\displaystyle={\bf e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*},

(92)

where the last equality follows from (
59
).
It follows that, for each
\ell=1,\ldots,3L
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}}f({\bf\Lambda})\Big|_{{\bf\Lambda}={\bf\Lambda}^{*}}\hskip-2.5pt

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}-2{\bf J}_{k-1}^{({\mathsf{P}})}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}-2{\bf J}_{k}^{(\mathsf{D})}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{q_{\ell}}{\bf e}_{\ell}-2{\bf J}_{k}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}},{\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}^{*}.

(93)

Using the first-order optimality condition (
87
), the equality (
85
) follows, which is sufficient to show that the pair
({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
satisfies the complementary slackness condition. This shows that the pair
({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
satisfies all conditions (1)–(4). Hence, strong duality holds for the optimization problem (
27
) provided that condition (
32
) holds, with
({\bf R}_{{\bf W}_{k}}^{*},{\bf\Lambda}^{*})
being an optimal primal-dual pair.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
3

The proof of Theorem
3
follows the same steps as that of Theorem
1
, with one key distinction. While the optimization problem (
27
) imposes the orthogonal projection constraint (
27c
), the optimization (
28
) imposes a trace constraint. Following the same steps that lead to (
69
) in Appendix
B
, it can be shown that the minimization of the Lagrangian function for the optimization problem (
28
) is equivalent to solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}}{\text{maximize}}\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\bf A}\right)

(94a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq P,

(94b)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits({\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}})\leq M_{T},\,\,{\bf R}_{{\bf V}_{k}}\succeq 0,

(94c)

where

\tilde{{\bf P}}_{{\bf A}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{R}}\E\left[\dot{\tilde{{\bf G}}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}){\bf A}\dot{\tilde{{\bf G}}}_{k,m}^{\mathsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\big|\,{\bf Y}_{1:k-1}\right],

(95)

and
\dot{\tilde{{\bf G}}}_{k,m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is as given in Theorem
3
. The solution to the minimization of the Lagrangian function is thus a rank-1 matrix given by the largest eigenvector of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\bf A}
, i.e.,

\tilde{{\bf R}}_{\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}}=P\tilde{{\bf v}}_{1}\tilde{{\bf v}}_{1}^{\mathsf{H}},

(96)

where
\tilde{{\bf v}}_{1}
is the eigenvector (with unit norm) corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{\bf A}
. The remaining steps to characterize the dual problem follow similarly as in Appendix
B
, while replacing the sum of eigenvalues in (
76
) by the single largest eigenvalue of
\tilde{{\bf P}}_{{\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}}
, multiplied by
P
. To prove that condition (
39
) is sufficient for finding a globally optimal solution, a similar procedure as in Appendix
B
is also followed. The key is to show the differentiability of the largest eigenvalue of the direction matrix, which holds if and only if its multiplicity is one (i.e., condition (
39
) holds).
