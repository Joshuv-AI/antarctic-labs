DyWeight: Dynamic Gradient Weighting for Few-Step Diffusion Sampling
Tong Zhao 1,2,∗ , Mingkun Lei 2,∗ , Liangyu Yuan 3,∗ , Yanming Yang 2 , Chenxi Song 2 , Yang Wang 2 , Beier Zhu 4 , Chi Zhang 2,† 1 Zhejiang University, 2 AGI Lab, Westlake University, 3 TongJi University, 4 University of Science and Technology of China https://github.com/Westlake-AGI-Lab/DyWeight

Abstract
Diffusion Models (DMs) have achieved state-of-the-art generative performance across multiple modalities, yet their sampling process remains prohibitively slow due to the need for hundreds of function evaluations. Recent progress in multi-step ODE solvers has greatly improved efficiency by reusing historical gradients, but existing methods rely on handcrafted coefficients that fail to adapt to the non-stationary dynamics of diffusion sampling. To address this limitation, we propose Dy namic Gradient Weight ing ( DyWeight ), a lightweight, learning-based multi-step solver that introduces a streamlined implicit coupling paradigm. By relaxing classical numerical constraints, DyWeight learns unconstrained time-varying parameters that adaptively aggregate historical gradients while intrinsically scaling the effective step size. This implicit time calibration accurately aligns the solver’s numerical trajectory with the model’s internal denoising dynamics under large integration steps, avoiding complex decoupled parameterizations and optimizations. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10, FFHQ, AFHQv2, ImageNet64, LSUN-Bedroom, Stable Diffusion and FLUX.1-dev demonstrate that DyWeight achieves superior visual fidelity and stability with significantly fewer function evaluations, establishing a new state-of-the-art among efficient diffusion solvers. Code is available at https://github.com/Westlake-AGI-Lab/DyWeight .

3.1
Preliminary

Diffusion model.
Diffusion Models (DMs)
45
;
12
;
47
progressively map a data distribution
\mathbf{x}_{0}\!\sim\!{p}_{\text{data}}
to a simple prior, typically Gaussian noise
\mathbf{x}_{T}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
, via a forward noising process. At inference, the model reverses this process, transforming noise back into data through a learned denoising function
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
, which can be directly modeled as an ordinary differential equation (ODE)
48
;
19
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=f(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)+\frac{g^{2}(t)}{2\sigma_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),

(1)

where
f(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is the drift coefficient,
g(t)
is the the diffusion coefficient,
\sigma_{t}
represents the noise level at time
t
. In this paper, we follow the parameterization of EDM
15
, which sets
f(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=0
,
g(t)=\sqrt{2t}
, and
\sigma_{t}=t
. This parameterization significantly simplifies equation
1
to:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)}{t}.

(2)

Here,
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is the noise prediction model
12
;
46
. The second equality defines the data prediction model
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=\mathbf{x}_{t}-t\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
, which is trained to directly estimate the clean data
\mathbf{x}_{0}

15
;
30
;
61
. Therefore, the generation process is equivalent to solving the ODE in equation
2
from an initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{T}
. We can express the exact solution for a single step:

\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n-1}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

Since the integral of the neural network
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
over the unknown path
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is intractable, numerical methods are required to approximate this integral. A baseline approach, used by DDIM
46
, applies the first-order Euler method:

\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}+\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},t_{n})\cdot(t_{n-1}-t_{n}).

(4)

The core challenge of diffusion sampling thus becomes how to accurately and efficiently approximate the intractable integral in equation
3
. This necessitates the use of high-order numerical solvers
28
;
15
beyond the simple first-order method.

Multi-step diffusion solvers.
To improve upon the first-order approximation and reduce truncation error, a variety of higher-order solvers have been developed. Among them, implicit single-step methods (
e.g
.
, Heun’s method
15
, DPM-Solver
31

\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
at future timesteps, typically doubling the number of function evaluations (NFE) per step, and often degrade when the total number of steps is very small.

Consequently, explicit multi-step solvers
28
;
60
;
30
;
61
are often preferred. These methods leverage historical information from previous steps to achieve a high-order approximation without additional NFE. A general form for a
k
-order explicit multi-step solver can be written as:

\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}+(t_{n-1}-t_{n})\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\cdot\underbrace{\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n+i}},t_{n+i})}_{\text{gradient term}}.

(5)

Different solvers utilize different strategies to derive these weights
w_{i}
. DPM-Solvers
31
;
30
;
62
leverage the semi-linear property of the underlying ODE and apply a Taylor expansion to incorporate high-order information. Meanwhile, iPNDM
28
;
60
approximates the integrand
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
by constructing an extrapolating polynomial
\mathbf{P}_{k}(t)
that passes through the
k
most recent gradients. When the time steps are uniform,
e.g
.
,
t_{n-1}-t_{n}=t_{n-2}-t_{n-1}=h
, the integral of the Lagrange polynomial simplifies to the classic
k
-th order Adams-Bashforth (AB) method
6
;
9
;
1

h\cdot\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(3\bm{\epsilon}_{n}-\bm{\epsilon}_{n+1}\right)/2,\!\!&\text{if }k=2,\\[4.0pt]
&\left(23\bm{\epsilon}_{n}-16\bm{\epsilon}_{n+1}+5\bm{\epsilon}_{n+2}\right)/12,\!\!&\text{if }k=3,\\[4.0pt]
&\left(55\bm{\epsilon}_{n}-59\bm{\epsilon}_{n+1}+37\bm{\epsilon}_{n+2}-9\bm{\epsilon}_{n+3}\right)/24,\!\!&\text{if }k=4.\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

While the classic AB methods in
Eq.

6
are derived for uniform steps

3.2
Numerical study of solver coefficients

The core hypothesis of our work is that the time-invariant, handcrafted coefficients of classical high-order ODE solvers are suboptimal for accelerating diffusion sampling, particularly under two challenges:
(\mathbf{1})
the extreme complexity of the denoising network’s learned function and
(\mathbf{2})
the aggressive reduction in solver steps required for fast sampling. To investigate this hypothesis, we first step away from the “black-box” diffusion model to build a “white-box” numerical experiment where all variables are known and controllable.

Setup.
To isolate the impact of solver coefficients under controlled yet realistic dynamics, we construct a synthetic ODE system
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})
on
t\in[0,1]

D=50

\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)

K

are sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,4\mathbf{I})

\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)
and its derivative
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\text{gt}}(t)

\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\text{gt}}(t)+\mathbf{A}\big[\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)\big],

(7)

where

Formulation.
We begin by re-examining the general k-order explicit multi-step solver in equation
5
. The update rule can be equivalently expressed as:

\!\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}\!\!=\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}\!+\!\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{n,i}h_{n}}_{\text{step scaling}}\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\!\frac{w_{n,i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{n,i}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n+i}},t_{n+i})}_{\text{gradients weighting}}.

(8)

Here,
h_{n}\!=\!t_{n-1}\!-t_{n}
denotes the time step size. This decomposition highlights two distinct mechanisms that emerge when the learnable weights

are unconstrained (
i.e

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{n,i}=1

\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}w_{n,i}h_{n}
, functions as an implicit
step scaling
, which modulates the overall magnitude of the update. Classical solvers (
e.g
.
, Adams-Bashforth
6
) inherently constrain
\sum w_{n,i}\!=\!1
. We relax this constraint, enabling the solver to learn both the aggregation strategy and the update magnitude simultaneously.

Learning unconstrained weights

\tilde{h}_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{n,i}h_{n}

We introduce
DyWeight
, a learning-based multi-step solver designed for efficient few-step diffusion sampling. Conventional solvers rely on static, handcrafted coefficients that are suboptimal for the highly non-stationary diffusion dynamics. To overcome this,
DyWeight
employs unconstrained, time-varying parameters to implicitly couple gradient aggregation with dynamic step-size modulation. By relaxing the constraint that weights must sum to one, the solver adaptively re-weights historical gradients while simultaneously performing time calibration (shifting and scaling) to maintain temporal alignment with the model’s internal dynamics. Trained via a lightweight teacher-student distillation framework, our method efficiently captures the evolving denoising behavior without the optimization bottlenecks of decoupled schedules, and without modifying the underlying diffusion model. Extensive experiments across diverse datasets demonstrate that
DyWeight
achieves superior visual fidelity with minimal NFEs, consistently outperforming prior methods and establishing a new state-of-the-art in the low-NFE regime.

Appendix A
Derivation of general multi-step solvers

In this section, we explicitly derive the coefficients for the generalized Adams-Bashforth solvers adapted for non-uniform time steps. We show that the classical coefficients
9
;
1
are special cases of our general formulation.

A.1
Problem formulation

The diffusion sampling update at step
n
requires solving the integral:

\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n-1}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t.

(A.1)

Let
h_{n}=t_{n-1}-t_{n}
be the current step size. We utilize historical gradients at time steps
\{t_{n},t_{n+1},\dots,t_{n+K-1}\}
. We define the time interval between the current time
t_{n}
and historical time
t_{n+i}
as
\tau_{i}=t_{n}-t_{n+i}
(where
\tau_{0}=0
).
To simplify integration, we apply a change of variables
u=t-t_{n}
, mapping the integration interval
[t_{n},t_{n-1}]
to
[0,h_{n}]
.

A.2
Second-order general solver (
K=2
)

We approximate
\epsilon_{\theta}
using a linear Lagrange polynomial constructed from points
\{t_{n},t_{n+1}\}
. The basis polynomials are:

L_{0}(u)=\frac{u+\tau_{1}}{\tau_{1}},\quad L_{1}(u)=\frac{u}{-\tau_{1}}.

(A.2)

Integrating these over
[0,h_{n}]
yields the weights:

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{0}^{h_{n}}\frac{u+\tau_{1}}{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}u=1+\frac{h_{n}}{2\tau_{1}},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{0}^{h_{n}}\frac{-u}{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}u=-\frac{h_{n}}{2\tau_{1}}.

(A.4)

The update rule is:

x_{t_{n-1}}=x_{t_{n}}+h_{n}\left(\beta_{0}^{(2)}\epsilon_{n}+\beta_{1}^{(2)}\epsilon_{n+1}\right).

(A.5)

After substitution, it matches the formulation of DPM-Solver++(2M)
30
.

Verification.
For uniform steps,
h_{n}=\tau_{1}=h
.

•

\beta_{0}^{(2)}=1+1/2=3/2
,

•

\beta_{1}^{(2)}=-1/2
.

These match the standard Adams-Bashforth (AB-2) coefficients.

A.3
Third-order general solver (
K=3
)

We use three points
\{t_{n},t_{n+1},t_{n+2}\}
with intervals
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
. The quadratic Lagrange basis for the current step
\epsilon_{n}
is:

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle L_{0}(u)=\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{(u+\tau_1)(u+\tau_2)}{\tau_1 \tau_2}=\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{u^2 + (\tau_1+\tau_2)u + \tau_1\tau_2}{\tau_1\tau_2}.$}}

(A.6)

Integrating
\int_{0}^{h_{n}}L_{0}(u)\mathrm{d}u
yields
\beta_{0}^{(3)}
:

\beta_{0}^{(3)}=1+\frac{h_{n}}{2}\frac{\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}}{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}+\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{3\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}.

(A.7)

For historical steps
i\in\{1,2\}
, the basis polynomials are:

L_{i}(u)=\frac{u(u+\tau_{3-i})}{\tau_{i}(\tau_{i}-\tau_{3-i})}.

(A.8)

Integration yields:

\beta_{i}^{(3)}=\frac{1}{\tau_{i}(\tau_{i}-\tau_{3-i})}\left(\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{3}+\frac{h_{n}\tau_{3-i}}{2}\right).

(A.9)

Verification.
For uniform steps (
\tau_{1}=h,\tau_{2}=2h
):

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=1\!+\!\frac{h(3h)}{2(2h^{2})}\!+\!\frac{h^{2}}{3(2h^{2})}=1\!+\!\frac{3}{4}\!+\!\frac{1}{6}=\frac{23}{12},

(A.10)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}/3+h(2h)/2}{h(-h)}=\frac{4h^{2}/3}{-h^{2}}=-\frac{16}{12},

\displaystyle\beta_{2}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}/3+h(h)/2}{2h(h)}=\frac{5h^{2}/6}{2h^{2}}=\frac{5}{12}.

These exactly recover the AB-3 coefficients.

A.4
Fourth-order general solver (
K=4
)

For the fourth-order solver, we integrate the cubic Lagrange polynomial constructed from
\{t_{n},t_{n+1},t_{n+2},t_{n+3}\}
. The general update rule is:

x_{t_{n-1}}=x_{t_{n}}+h_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{3}\beta_{i}^{(4)}\epsilon_{n+i}.

(A.11)

The explicit coefficients derived from integration are coefficient for current step (
\epsilon_{n}
):

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{(4)}=1+\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h_n}{2}\!\sum_{j=1}^{3}\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{\tau_j}+\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h_n^2}{3}\!\!\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq 3}\!\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{\tau_j \tau_k}+\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h_n^3}{4 \prod_{j=1}^3 \tau_j},$}}

(A.12)

and coefficients for historical steps (
\epsilon_{n+i},i\in\{1,2,3\}
):

\beta_{i}^{(4)}=\frac{\frac{h_{n}^{3}}{4}+\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{3}S_{1}^{(i)}+\frac{h_{n}}{2}S_{2}^{(i)}}{\prod_{j\neq i}(\tau_{j}-\tau_{i})}.

(A.13)

Where
S_{1}^{(i)}=\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{j}
and
S_{2}^{(i)}=\prod_{j\neq i,~j\neq 0}\tau_{j}
represent the sum and product of the other time intervals, respectively.

Verification.
We verify
\beta_{0}^{(4)}
under uniform steps where
\tau_{1}=h,\tau_{2}=2h,\tau_{3}=3h
:

\displaystyle\text{Sum terms:}\quad\sum\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{1}{h}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\right)=\frac{11}{6h},

\displaystyle\text{Pair terms:}\quad\sum\frac{1}{\tau\tau}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{h^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{Prod term:}\quad\prod\tau=6h^{3}.

Substituting these back:

\beta_{0}^{(4)}=1+\frac{h}{2}\left(\frac{11}{6h}\right)+\frac{h^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}\right)+\frac{h^{3}}{4(6h^{3})}=\frac{55}{24}.

(A.14)

Similarly, calculating the historical weights yields
\beta_{1}^{(4)}=-\frac{59}{24}
,
\beta_{2}^{(4)}=\frac{37}{24}
, and
\beta_{3}^{(4)}=-\frac{9}{24}
. This confirms that our derivation generalizes the standard AB-4 method.

Summary.
The derivation above explicitly demonstrates that the coefficients of classical solvers (
e.g
.
, iPNDM
28
;
60
) are predicated on the strict assumption of linear, uniform time schedules (where
\tau_{j}=j\cdot h
). However, practical diffusion sampling often utilizes non-linear schedules
15
;
47
to maximize image quality, rendering these fixed coefficients mathematically inaccurate. Furthermore, even under the ideal condition of uniform steps, our numerical analysis in
Section

3.2
reveals that low-order polynomial approximations are suboptimal when the denoising function exhibits high complexity, especially in few-step settings. As the function complexity increases and the number of steps decreases, the truncation error of these static solvers escalates significantly. This theoretical misalignment underscores the necessity of our proposed
DyWeight
: by optimizing the coefficients via learning, the solver can adaptively compensate for both the non-uniformity of few-step schedules and the complex, non-stationary dynamics of the diffusion process, yielding a more accurate sampling trajectory.

Appendix C
Impact of supervision objectives

We rely on
endpoint supervision
(supervising only the final generated sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}
) to train
DyWeight
. However, recent learning-based solvers such as AMED
63
, DLMS
25
, EPD
64
and DSS
52
use
path supervision
, forcing the student to match the teacher’s trajectory at every intermediate step. In this section, we verify the superiority of our choice.

Path supervision setup.
To implement path supervision on
DyWeight
, we require the high-fidelity teacher to provide ground-truth guidance for every intermediate step of the student. Since the student takes large strides (few steps), we execute the teacher solver for
M
smaller sub-steps between each student interval
[t_{n},t_{n-1}]
to generate a precise target.
Formally, let the student’s trajectory be
\{\mathbf{x}_{t_{N}},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}\}
. For a student step from
t_{n}
to
t_{n-1}
, the teacher generates a corresponding high-precision target
\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}^{\text{tea}}
. The path supervision loss is defined as the cumulative error across all steps:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{path}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\text{dist}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}^{\text{stu}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}^{\text{tea}}).

(A.15)

Here, the metric
\text{dist}(\cdot)
varies by step: for all intermediate steps (
n>1
), it computes the
L_{2}
loss to enforce trajectory alignment; for the final step (
n=1
), it employs the perceptual distance (
e.g
.
, Inception feature distance
49
) consistent with our main objective in
Eq.

10
.
In our experiments, we set the interval sub-steps
M=5
. Since the trajectory is generated sequentially, parameters at each step influence all subsequent states and are thus updated by back-propagating gradients accumulated from all following loss terms.

Results and analysis.
As shown in
Table

A.2
,
endpoint supervision
consistently outperforms
path supervision
by a significant margin. On CIFAR-10
21
at 3 NFEs,
endpoint supervision
achieves an FID of 8.16, whereas
path supervision
degrades to 11.74. A similar trend is observed on ImageNet-64
39
. We argue that the inferiority of path supervision in the few-step regime stems from
over-constraint
and
limited flexibility
.

•

Trajectory flexibility vs. imitation:
Path supervision
forces the student to strictly “imitate” the teacher’s high-precision trajectory. However, a few-step student (
e.g
.
, 3 steps) fundamentally lacks the capacity to physically follow the exact path of a high-step teacher, as the optimal trajectory for a large stride is often different from the accumulation of many small strides.

•

Goal-oriented optimization:
Endpoint supervision
relaxes these intermediate constraints. It treats the solver as a function approximator that only needs to map the initial noise
\mathbf{x}_{T}
to the correct data
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. This grants the solver the freedom to explore “shortcuts” or alternative trajectories that may deviate from the teacher in the middle but land more accurately on the data manifold at the end.

This experiment not only justifies our design choice but also highlights a key distinction between
DyWeight
and concurrent works like DLMS
25
and DSS
52
: for extreme few-step sampling,
where
you end up matters more than
how
you get there.

Appendix D
Experimental settings and implementation details

D.1
Controlled numerical study configuration

To rigorously validate our hypothesis that handcrafted coefficients are suboptimal for high-complexity dynamics under few-step settings (
Section

3.2
), we designed a “white-box” numerical testbed. This section details the construction of the synthetic problem, the solver parameterization, and the optimization procedure.

Problem construction.
We construct a high-dimensional Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) system
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})
defined on the time interval
t\in[0,1]
with state dimension
D=50
. The ground-truth trajectory
\textbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)
is defined as a polynomial of degree
K
:

\textbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)=\mathbf{C}\cdot[1,t,t^{2},\dots,t^{K}]^{\top},

(A.16)

where the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times(K+1)}
is sampled from a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,4\mathbf{I})
. To mimic the complex feature interactions in the diffusion U-Net
37
;
38
and DiT
23
;
33
, we introduce a stiff coupling term in the drift function:

\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\text{gt}}(t)+\mathbf{A}\big[\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)\big].

(A.17)

Here,
\mathbf{A}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is a random coupling matrix initialized with entries sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,0.1/\sqrt{D})
and shifted by
-0.5\mathbf{I}
to ensure system stability. This formulation ensures that
\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(t)
is the exact solution while introducing cross-dimensional dependencies that challenge the numerical solver.

Solver implementation.
We evaluate Adams-Bashforth (AB)
9
;
1
;
6
solvers of orders
O\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.

•

Standard solver
: Uses the classical, time-invariant coefficients derived from Lagrange polynomial integration on uniform grids
9
;
1
.

•

Optimized solver
: We parameterize the solver with time-varying learnable coefficients. For a total of
S
steps, the solver learns a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{(S-O+1)\times O}
. Unlike the standard solver which applies the same coefficients at every step, the optimized solver utilizes a specific set of weights for each integration step
n
, allowing it to find a more optimal path.

•

Initialization and start-up
: To ensure a fair comparison and isolate the solver’s integration error from start-up inaccuracies, both standard and optimized solvers are initialized with the exact ground-truth values for the first
O
history steps. Furthermore, the learnable weights of the optimized solver are initialized with the standard AB coefficients to provide a valid numerical baseline.

Optimization and evaluation.
The optimized solvers are trained to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted terminal state
\mathbf{y}_{\text{pred}}(1)
and the ground truth
\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(1)
(
endpoint supervision
). We use the Adam optimizer
18
with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
for 2,000 iterations.
For the comprehensive evaluation shown in
Fig.

A.2
, we traverse a dense grid of configurations: polynomial orders
K\in\{5,10,\dots,40\}
and step counts
S\in\{6,8,\dots,20\}
. For each configuration
(K,S)
, we perform 50 independent runs with randomly sampled problems and report the mean Relative
L_{2}
Error, defined as
||\mathbf{y}_{\text{pred}}(1)-\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(1)||_{2}/||\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(1)||_{2}
. As illustrated in
Fig.

A.2
, the optimized solvers consistently achieve lower relative errors compared to their standard counterparts across all tested configurations.

Appendix F
Limitation and future work

Like other advanced learning-based solvers, DyWeight is fundamentally bounded by the local linearization assumption of ODE trajectories, making extreme one- or two-step generation (
e.g
.
, 1-2 NFEs) highly challenging without introducing perceptual artifacts. Furthermore, our current parameterization is coupled with predefined, static sampling budgets. Future work will explore integrating DyWeight with score-distillation paradigms to breach the 2-NFE barrier, alongside developing an NFE-agnostic learning mechanism to seamlessly generalize the learned weights across arbitrary, dynamic integration steps.
