L 2 L^{2} -cutoff for the averaging process on random regular graphs
Pietro Caputo, Matteo Quattropani, and Federico Sau

Abstract
. We study the mixing time of the averaging process on a large random d d -regular graph, d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , and prove an L 2 L^{2} -cutoff with an explicit cutoff time. Somewhat surprisingly, we uncover a phase transition at the finite, fixed degree d = 10 d=10 : for small degrees, i.e., d ≤ 10 d\leq 10 , the averaging process mixes as fast as the corresponding random walk on the same graph, whereas for d > 10 d>10 its L 2 L^{2} -mixing is governed by a different, slower mechanism. Our proof relies on a detailed asymptotic analysis of an auxiliary biased birth-and-death chain with a slow bond. We also briefly discuss an analogous phase transition for the L 1 L^{1} -mixing.

1.
Introduction, model, and main result

The quantitative study of convergence to equilibrium for stochastic processes is a classical theme in probability theory.
A central object in this direction is the mixing profile of a Markov chain and, in particular, the possible
presence of the
cutoff phenomenon
: an abrupt transition from non-equilibrium to equilibrium over a time window
negligible compared to the mixing time.
Since its identification in the early 1980s through the pioneering work of Aldous and Diaconis
[
AD86
]
, explaining the occurrence of cutoff has become a
major problem in the modern theory of mixing times.
Despite a rapidly growing collection of examples and techniques, a general mechanism explaining when cutoff should
(or should not) occur is still missing; see, e.g.,
[
LP17
,
Sal24
,
Sal25b
]
for more background.

Among the first and most influential examples exhibiting cutoff are
random walks on sparse random graphs
. Here, the interplay
between geometric expansion and randomness gives rise to sharp mixing transitions (see, e.g.,
[
LS10
,
LP16
,
BHS17
,
BLPS18
,
BCS18
,
BCS19
]
and references
therein).
These models provide a natural testing ground for cutoff questions, as they often allow one to relate mixing to
spectral information, local tree-like structure, and concentration phenomena.

Comparatively, mixing-time results for continuous-state Markov processes are more sparse, and sharp cutoff
statements are known only for selected model classes; see, e.g.,

[
Smi13
,
Smi14
,
BJ20
,
CLL20
,
CLL22
,
Sal25a
,
CQS25
]
.
An example in this class is the
averaging process
, which is particularly simple to describe, yet it exhibits a rather interesting behavior. Since the seminal work of
[
AL12
]
, the literature on the averaging process has grown rapidly; see, e.g.,
[
CDSZ22
,
QS23
,
CQS23
,
MSW24
,
Sau24
,
Sau25
,
Eid24
,
GV25
,
EPP25
,
CFHS25
,
CQS24
,
CQS25
]
.

Let us now describe the process.
Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, fix an initial nonnegative mass
configuration
\eta=(\eta(x))_{x\in V}
with
\sum_{x\in V}\eta(x)=1
, and equip each edge of the graph with an independent rate-
1
Poisson clock.
Whenever the clock on an edge
xy\in E
rings at time
t>0
, the masses at
x
and
y
are replaced by their average: if
\eta_{s}
denotes the mass configuration at time
s\geq 0
, and
\eta_{s}(z)\in[0,1]
its mass at
z\in V
,

(1.1)

\eta_{t}(x)=\eta_{t}(y)=\frac{\eta_{t^{-}}(x)+\eta_{t^{-}}(y)}{2}\ ,\qquad\eta_{t}(z)=\eta_{t^{-}}(z)\ \text{for all}\ z\neq x,y\ .

If
G
is connected, then, for every initial configuration, the process converges, as
t\to\infty
, to the flat one

\pi\equiv(1/n,\dots,1/n)
.
It is worth emphasizing, however, the notion of “distance to equilibrium” under which this convergence is measured.
Since the steady state is a Dirac delta at
\pi
and
\eta_{t}
is, generally, genuinely random at all times
t>0
, convergence in total variation is too strong.
For this reason, the literature has focused instead on Wasserstein-type distances, and, in particular, on

(1.2)

\mathbf{E}_{\eta}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{\,p}\right],\qquad p\geq 1\ ,\ t\geq 0\ .

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the usual
L^{p}(\pi)
-norms of functions on
V
, while
\mathbf{P}_{\eta}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\eta}
denote, respectively, the law and expectation associated to
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
when
\eta_{0}=\eta
. It turns out that the quantities in (
1.2
) are non-increasing in
t
and are maximized by initial conditions
that concentrate all the mass at a single vertex. For further details and properties, we refer the interested reader to
[
CQS23
, Section 2]
.

When analyzing the averaging process’ mixing, it is natural to compare
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with the “lazy” random walk
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the same graph
G
(hereafter, also referred to as
{\rm RW}(G)
), that is, the process on
V
which jumps from
x
to
y
at rate
1/2
, provided that
xy\in E
. Indeed, letting
(\pi^{\eta}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the transition probabilities of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
started with distribution
\eta
, one has (see, e.g.,
[
QS23
]
)

(1.3)

\pi^{\eta}_{t}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{\eta}[\eta_{t}(x)]\ ,\qquad x\in V\,,\ t\geq 0\ .

As a consequence of this identity and Jensen inequality, one obtains the following general comparison between the mixing behavior of the averaging process and the random walk on the same graph:

(1.4)

\mathbf{E}_{\eta}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]\geq\left\|\frac{\pi_{t}^{\eta}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{\,p}\,,\qquad p\geq 1\ ,\ t\geq 0\ .

This inequality may be interpreted as saying that, for a fixed initial condition and on the same graph,
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
approaches equilibrium
no faster than
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the same graph.

The aim of this work is to investigate the mixing behavior of the averaging process on a (large) random
d
-regular graph
(with fixed degree
d\geq 3
) in the
L^{2}
-metric, when starting with all the mass in one vertex. Our main result highlights two main facts.
First, for every
d\geq 3
, with high probability over the choice of the graph and of the starting vertex, the
averaging process on random regular graphs exhibits an
L^{2}
-cutoff
.
Second, and maybe more unexpectedly, we identify a
finite
degree threshold in the comparison with the random walk: in the large-graph limit and for
p=2
, the inequality in
(
1.4
) is, asymptotically, in fact an equality if
d\leq 10
, while it becomes strict as soon as

d>10
.
In particular, beyond degree
d=10
the averaging process exhibits an intrinsic slowdown relative to the random walk.

1.1.
Main result

For all integers
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 3
, let
\mathcal{G}(n,d)

be the set of all labeled simple undirected graphs with vertex set
V
,
|V|=n
,
and all degrees equal to
d
. Let
G
be a random simple
d
-regular graph of
size
n
, i.e., an element of
\mathcal{G}(n,d)
sampled uniformly at random.
Equivalently,
G
can be generated via the configuration model conditioned on
the event that the resulting multigraph is simple. This graph
G=(V,E)

constitutes the underlying random environment, with
\mathds{P}
and

\mathds{E}
denoting the corresponding law and expectation.

For a given (quenched) realization of
G
, we let the averaging process run on it, and set
\eta_{0}=\delta_{1}
, that is, all the mass is initially concentrated at a generic vertex, say, with label
1
.
This is our main result, whose proof is postponed to Section
2
below.

Theorem 1.1

(
L^{2}
-cutoff)
.

Let, for every integer
d\geq 3
,

(1.5)

\gamma=\gamma_{d}:=\begin{cases}\varrho&\text{if}\ d\leq 10\\
\sigma&\text{if}\ d\geq 10\ ,\end{cases}

where

(1.6)

\varrho=\varrho_{d}:=\frac{2\sqrt{d-1}}{d}\ ,\qquad\sigma=\sigma_{d}:=\frac{\left(3\sqrt{d-1}-\sqrt{d-9}\right)\sqrt{d-1}}{4d}\ .

Further, define (note that
\gamma\in(0,1)
, for all
d\geq 3
; see (
1.10
) below)

(1.7)

T(a):=\frac{1}{d\left(1-\gamma\right)}\log n+a\log\log n\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Then,
for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to-\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}\bigg(\mathbf{E}_{\delta_{1}}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]>\varepsilon\bigg)

\displaystyle=1\ ,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to+\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}\bigg(\mathbf{E}_{\delta_{1}}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]<\varepsilon\bigg)

\displaystyle=1\ .

In words, this theorem establishes that the averaging process on large random
d
-regular graphs exhibits, with high probability (w.h.p.) with respect to the law of the underlying random graph and from a typical starting vertex, an
L^{2}
-cutoff at time
\frac{1}{d\left(1-\gamma\right)}\log n
, with a cutoff window of size at most
\log\log n
.
We collect below some further insights around our main result, and some related open questions.
Throughout, all asymptotic statements are taken in the limit
n\to\infty
(unless stated otherwise). We use standard asymptotic notation: for positive functions
f
and
g
, we write
f=O(g)
(resp.
o(g)
) if
\limsup(f/g)<\infty
(resp.
\lim(f/g)=0
). We write
f\asymp g
if
f=O(g)
and
g=O(f)
, whereas
f\sim g
if
\lim(f/g)=1
.

Further remarks and open problems

1.2.1.
Comparison with the random walk

Figure 1.1.
Left
: Plot of the function
d\mapsto\gamma_{d}
, for
d\geq 3
.
Right
: Plots of the functions
d\mapsto\varrho_{d}
(blue) and
d\mapsto\sigma_{d}
(orange), for
d\geq 10
.

The function
\gamma=\gamma_{d}
given in (
1.6
) satisfies the following three simple properties (see also Figure
1.1
):

(1.10)

\gamma\ \text{is strictly decreasing in}\ d\geq 3\ ,\qquad\gamma=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\ \text{for}\ d=3\ ,\qquad\gamma\xrightarrow{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\ .

Moreover, as shown in Lemma
3.2
below (see also Figure
1.1
),

(1.11)

\sigma>\varrho\ ,\qquad d>10\ .

This is the quantitative mark that the averaging process
L^{2}
-mixing time is strictly larger than its
{\rm RW}
counterpart when
d>10
; for
d\leq 10
, since
\gamma=\varrho
, the
L^{2}
-mixing of the two processes coincide, at first order. Indeed, by adapting
[
LP16
, Proposition 6]
to our continuous-time (lazy) setup, their result reads as follows:
for every
\varepsilon>0
and w.h.p.,

(1.12)

t_{\rm mix,2}^{\textsc{rw}}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\inf\bigg\{t>0:\bigg\|\frac{\pi_{t}^{\delta_{1}}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}<\varepsilon\bigg\}\sim\frac{1}{d(1-\varrho)}\log n\ ,

while, by Theorem
1.1
, one has for the averaging process

(1.13)

t_{\rm mix,2}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\inf\bigg\{t>0:\mathbf{E}_{\delta_{1}}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]<\varepsilon\bigg\}\sim\frac{1}{d(1-\gamma)}\log n\ ,

and the two right-hand sides above coincide only for
d\leq 10
.

See Remark
2.3
below for a further motivation lying behind this transition.

1.2.2.
Large-degree regime

It is well known that, when the degree

d
of a random

d
-regular graph grows sufficiently quickly as

n\to\infty
, many of its properties align with those of a mean-field model.
Mixing, both in
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
, of the averaging process on the complete graph with
n
vertices has been studied in
[
CDSZ22
]
, and cutoff has been proved for both metrics.
The
L^{2}
-cutoff time is given (in continuous-time) by
\frac{2}{n}\log n
, with a cutoff window of size
\frac{1}{n}
. By formally taking
d\sim n
in Theorem
1.1
, since
\gamma\to\frac{1}{2}
as
d\to\infty
(cf. (
1.10
)), one recovers the leading-order term of the mixing time on the complete graph.

Actually, after an inspection of the proof of Theorem
1.1
, one may note that our strategy for the lower bound—ultimately based on (
2.2
), (
2.9
), (
2.6
) below—is robust enough to rigorously justify a joint limit
d,n\to\infty
in (
1.8
), and predicts an
L^{2}
-mixing time asymptotically equal to
\frac{2}{d}\log n
. Our arguments for the upper bound (in particular, (
2.31
)) are too weak, though. Nevertheless, one may use a more direct and elementary approach via Aldous-Lanoue’s inequality for the averaging process
[
AL12
, Proposition 2]
, which holds on any graph
G
: for all mass configurations
\eta
,

(1.14)

\mathbf{E}_{\eta}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq e^{-{\rm gap}\,t}\left\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where
{\rm gap}={\rm gap}(G)\geq 0
denotes the spectral gap of
{\rm RW}(G)
.
By exploiting that, for random
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices with
d,n\to\infty
, one has, w.h.p.,
{\rm gap}\sim\frac{d}{2}
(see, e.g.,
[
BHKY20
,
He24
]
), one recovers from (
1.14
)
\frac{2}{d}\log n
as a matching upper bound.

For further details on this regime and the size of the cutoff window, we refer to Remark
3.3
.

1.2.3.
L^{p}
-cutoff and phase transitions

We focus on

L^{2}
-mixing; extending the analysis to

L^{p}
(especially

p=1
) is a natural direction. While we do not pursue a detailed analysis here, relatively soft arguments (see, e.g.,
[
CQS23
, Section 2]
for a survey) already allow us to derive some
L^{p}
-bounds,
p\in[1,2]
, and suggest an analogous phase transition for
p=1
, as we now briefly explain.

Thanks to the monotonicity in
p\geq 1
of the metrics, that is,

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\eta}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}_{\eta}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{q}^{q}\right]\ ,\qquad 1\leq p\leq q\ ,\ t\geq 0\ ,

our Theorem
1.1
already provides
\frac{1}{d(1-\gamma)}\log n+O(\log\log n)
as an upper bound for the
L^{p}
-mixing time, for all
p\leq 2
.

For the lower bound, we can proceed in two ways. One route compares the averaging process
to the random walk via (
1.4
), together with
[
LS10
]
and

[
LP16
, Proposition 6]
. Another route uses the
relative-entropy lower bound of
[
MSW24
]
, see also
[
CQS23
, Proposition 2.9]
.
Heuristically, these two
bounds capture different mechanisms required for the averaging process to be well mixed:
the comparison argument reflects the need for the mass to
explore
, in average, a substantial
portion of the graph, whereas the entropy argument reflects the need for sufficient

fragmentation
. In either case, we obtain lower bounds of the form
c\log n
, for some
c>0
depending on
d\geq 3
and possibly on the accuracy parameter
\varepsilon>0
in the mixing time
t_{{\rm mix},p}(\varepsilon)
. This yields a pre-cutoff (see, e.g.,
[
LP17
, Eq. (18.5)]
) for all
p\in[1,2)
.

Further, if specialized to the
L^{1}
-setting, these two lower bounds read, respectively, as

(1.16)

\displaystyle t_{{\rm mix},1}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{d-2}\frac{1}{\log(d-1)}\log n-O(\sqrt{\log n})\ ,

(1.17)

\displaystyle t_{{\rm mix},1}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{d\log 2}\log n-O(\sqrt{\log n})\ ,

w.h.p. and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough.
A simple manipulation shows that, if
d>7
, the second lower bound obtained via
[
MSW24
, Theorem 1]
improves upon the first one, which we know to coincide with the
L^{1}
-cutoff time of the random walk on random
d
-regular graphs,
d\geq 3
. In other words, a finite-degree phase transition for the averaging process’
L^{1}
-mixing may occur at some
3\leq d\leq 7
. Establishing the occurrence of
L^{1}
-cutoff and, possibly, its precise location for the averaging process on this and other random graph models remains an interesting open problem.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove our main result, and start by introducing some auxiliary processes which will play a central role in our analysis.

2.1.
Coupled random walks and the slow-bond birth-and-death chain

As already exploited in
[
AL12
]
, there is a two-walk version of (
1.3
): for all graphs
G=(V,E)
and initial configurations
\eta
,

(2.1)

\mathbf{E}_{\eta}[\eta_{t}(x)\eta_{t}(y)]=\mathbf{P}_{\eta\otimes\eta}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}(X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y)\ ,\qquad x,y\in V\ ,\ t\geq 0\ .

Here,
\mathbf{P}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}}
stands for the law of two
coupled random walks
on
V\times V
, and the subscript indicates the initial distribution of the two walks; when
\eta=\delta_{x}
,
x\in V
, we simply write
\mathbf{P}_{x,x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}
.
Their dynamics may be briefly described as follows (for more details, see, e.g.,
[
AL12
, Section 2.4]
or
[
CQS23
, Section 2.2]
): if the rate-1 Poisson clock attached to
xy\in E
rings, each walker currently at either
x
or
y
independently crosses the edge with probability
1/2
. In particular, both
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
marginally evolve as a
{\rm RW}(G)
, and their motion is independent as long as they sit at graph-distance larger than one.

By spelling out
\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\|_{2}^{2}=n\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{t}(x)^{2}-1
and taking expectation, one readily gets

(2.2)

\mathbf{E}_{x}\!\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=n\,\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}_{x,x}(X_{t}=Y_{t})-1\ ,\qquad x\in V\ ,\ t\geq 0\

The general idea behind the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to effectively compare the (random) probability on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) with

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})}_{o,o}(X_{t}=Y_{t})\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where
\mathds{T}_{d}
denotes the
d
-regular infinite tree,
o
is an arbitrary vertex (which we will refer to as the
root
), and
{\rm CRW}(\mathds{T}_{d})
stands for a system of coupled random walks evolving on
\mathds{T}_{d}
. Analogously,
\mathbf{P}_{o,o}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})}
denotes the law of
{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})
when both walks start from
o
.

The advantage of working with (
2.3
) is that it may be recast into a transition probability of a continuous-time birth-and-death (BD) chain, whose transition probabilities can be asymptotically estimated.

Definition 2.1

(Slow-bond BD chain)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
.
Let
R_{t}
denote the transition kernel of the continuous-time biased random walk on
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
with transition rates
p^{R}(i)
(resp.
q^{R}(i)
) of jumping from
i
to
i+1
(resp.
i-1
) given by

(2.4)

p^{R}(i):=\begin{cases}d/2&\text{if}\ i=0\\
d-1&\text{if}\ i\geq 1\end{cases}\ ,\qquad q^{R}(i):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ i=0\\
1/2&\text{if}\ i=1\\
1&\text{if}\ i\geq 2\ .\end{cases}

We refer to
R_{t}
as the (continuous-time)
slow-bond BD chain
.

From the definition of
{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})
and
R_{t}
, it is immediate to verify that, for all
t\geq 0
,

(2.5)

\mathbf{P}^{{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})}_{o,o}(X_{t}=Y_{t})=R_{t}(0,0)\ .

This identity will be the first key step for deriving the following sharp asymptotics for times

t=T(a)
of the form (
1.7
). Its proof takes the whole Section
3
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
d\geq 3
, we have

(2.6)

\lim_{a\to-\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbf{P}^{{\rm CRW}(\mathds{T}_{d})}_{o,o}(X_{T(a)}=Y_{T(a)})=\infty\ ,

(2.7)

\lim_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbf{P}^{{\rm CRW}(\mathds{T}_{d})}_{o,o}(X_{T(a)}=Y_{T(a)})=0\ .

Granted the validity of this result and the above relations, in the next two sections, we turn to the proof of the two claims in Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 2.3
.

A departure from the
{\rm RW}
behavior for the
L^{2}
-mixing time has to be expected for sufficiently large (but finite)
d\geq 3
, as the following crude argument shows.

By (
2.2
), the
L^{2}
-mixing is dictated by transition probabilities that two coupled random walks starting from the same site sit together at time
t
. By estimating this probability from below with the probability that the two walks stay together up to time
t>0
, we get

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{{\rm CRW}(G)}_{x,x}(X_{t}=Y_{t})\geq\mathbf{P}^{{\rm CRW}(G)}_{x,x}(X_{s}=Y_{s}\,,\,\text{for all}\ s\leq t)=e^{-\frac{d}{2}t}\ ,

yielding

t_{{\rm mix},2}(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{2}{d}\log n+\frac{a}{d}
, for all
\varepsilon>0
and for some
a=a_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
. This lower bound is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{d(1-\varrho)}\log n
as soon as
d\geq 15
. Nevertheless, on the one hand, this argument does not yet capture the critical value
d=10
and, on the other, it does not show that, for
d\geq 3
small enough, the inequality in (
1.4
) is (asymptotically) an identity.

2.2.
Lower bound

In view of (
1.4
) with
p=2
, which holds

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

(2.9)

\inf_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}_{x,x}(X_{t}=Y_{t})\geq\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})}_{o,o}(X_{t}=Y_{t})\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where we recall that
\mathds{T}_{d}
denotes the

-regular infinite tree and

is an arbitrary vertex.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(lower bound).

The claim in (
1.8
) readily follows from (
2.2
), Proposition
2.4
, and (
2.6
). ∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 3
, and let
G=(V,E)
be a (non-random)
d
-regular graph, with a given
x\in V
fixed all throughout. Let us consider the cover tree of
G
with root
o\in\mathbb{T}_{d}
labeled as
x\in V
. This prescribes a unique covering map (i.e., a graph homomorphism)
\varphi:\mathbb{T}_{d}\to G
with
\varphi(0)=x
.

We define a Markov process
(Z_{t},W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(shortened as
{\rm CRW}_{*}(\mathbb{T}_{d})
) on
\mathbb{T}_{d}\times\mathbb{T}_{d}

driven by the rate-
1
clocks on edges
xy\in E
as follows.
Whenever the clock of
xy

Lemma 2.6

Let
\bar{G}

d
-regular configuration model on
|V|=n

denoting law and expectation, and let

\mathds{E}\big[\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(G)}_{1,1}(\mathcal{W}_{t}^{c})\big]=\bar{\mathds{E}}\big[\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(\bar{G})}_{1,1}(\mathcal{W}_{t}^{c})\mid\mathscr{S}\big]\leq{\bar{\mathds{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm ACRW}_{1,1}(\mathcal{W}^{c}_{t})}\,/\,{\bar{\mathds{P}}(\mathscr{S})}\ ,

where, on the right-hand side, the numerator is an

x,y\in V
, the law
\bar{\mathds{P}}_{x,y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm ACRW}

(1)

Start with
|V|=n

d\geq 3

X_{0}=x
and
Y_{0}=y
.

(2)

s\geq 0
in which any of the two particles sits on a vertex that has not yet matched all of its stubs, sequentially match its unmatched ones with others, uniformly at random. Let
\bar{G}_{s}
denote the resulting graph, whose vertices are those that have at least one explored edge.

(3)

Let the two particles evolve as a
{\rm CRW}
-process on the explored graph, let

\bar{\mathds{P}}_{1,1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm ACRW}(\mathcal{W}^{c}_{t})\leq\frac{dA^{2}}{dn-2dA}+\mathds{P}({\rm Poi}(2dt)>A)\ ,

\bar{\mathds{P}}({\rm Poi}(2dt)>A)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(A-2dt)^{2}}{2A}\right)\ ,

when

. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

By the
first principle of coefficient asymptotics
(see, e.g.,
[
FS09
, Theorem IV.7]
), Lemma
3.2
is already enough to obtain the exponential decay rates prescribed in Proposition
3.1
(cf. (
3.17
)–(
3.18
)). In order to determine the coefficients
\beta
in (
3.1
), we use the standard machinery of singularity analysis; see, e.g.,
[
FS09
, Chapter VI]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

In view of the previous lemma, we get the desired claim (i.e., (
3.34
), (
3.37
) and (
3.39
) below) by proving that the coefficients
\beta=\beta_{d}
in (
3.1
) satisfy

(3.27)

\beta=\begin{cases}-\frac{3}{2}&\text{if}\ d\leq 9\\
-\frac{1}{2}&\text{if}\ d=10\\
0&\text{if}\ d\geq 11\ .\end{cases}

We split the proof into three cases:
d\leq 9
,
d=10
, and
d\geq 11
.

Case
d\leq 9
.
Lemma
3.2
ensures that
r=\varrho^{-1}
. Moreover,
\varrho^{-1}
and
\varrho
are two singularities and, by (
3.22
),
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are not singularities for all
d\in\{3,\ldots,7\}\cup\{9\}
. We argue that
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are not singularities also when
d=8
. Indeed, in this case,

(3.28)

\displaystyle z_{1}=\frac{3}{2}-\mathbf{i}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{7}}\ ,\qquad z_{2}=\frac{3}{2}+\mathbf{i}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{7}}\ ,

and, by expanding the numerator in (
3.15
) around
z=z_{j}
,
j=1,2
, the zeroth-order term equals

(3.29)

4-3\left(2p+1\right)z_{j}+4pz_{j}^{2}+z_{j}\sqrt{1-4pqz_{j}^{2}}=0\ ,\qquad j=1,2\ .

In conclusion, for
d\leq 9
,
\pm\varrho^{-1}
are the only two singularities at distance
r
from the origin.

Let us now expand the function
g(z)
around both singularities:

(3.30)

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=a_{+}-f_{+}\sqrt{1-\varrho z}+O\left(1-\varrho z\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\text{as}\ z\to\varrho^{-1}\ ,

(3.31)

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=a_{-}-f_{-}\sqrt{1+\varrho z}+O\left(1+\varrho z\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\text{as}\ z\to-\varrho^{-1}\ ,

for some constants
a_{+},a_{-}\in\mathbb{R}
and values

(3.32)

\displaystyle f_{+}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{\varrho^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}p\left(1-\varrho^{-1}\right)\left(z_{1}-\varrho^{-1}\right)\left(z_{2}-\varrho^{-1}\right)}>0\ ,

(3.33)

\displaystyle f_{-}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{-\varrho^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}p\left(1+\varrho^{-1}\right)\left(z_{1}+\varrho^{-1}\right)\left(z_{2}+\varrho^{-1}\right)}>0\ .

Since
f_{+}>f_{-}>0
, by
[
FS09
, Theorem VI.5]
, we conclude that

(3.34)

c_{k}\sim\frac{f_{+}+(-1)^{k}f_{-}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,k^{-3/2}\varrho^{k}\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ k\to\infty\ .

Case
d=10
.
In this case
r=z_{2}=\varrho^{-1}=\frac{5}{3}
. There are two singularities at distance
r=\frac{5}{3}
from the origin, namely
\pm\frac{5}{3}
. In particular,

(3.35)

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=-9+\frac{25}{6}{\sqrt{\frac{6}{5}}}\left(\frac{5}{3}-z\right)^{-1/2}+O\left(\frac{5}{3}-z\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\text{as}\ z\to\frac{5}{3}\ ,

(3.36)

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{12}-\frac{5\sqrt{2}}{144}\sqrt{1+\frac{3}{5}z}+O\left(\frac{5}{3}+z\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\text{as}\ z\to-\frac{5}{3}\ .

Hence, by
[
FS09
, Theorem VI.5]
, we conclude that the singularity at
-5/3
gives a lower order contribution to the the coefficients of
g
, thus,

(3.37)

c_{k}\sim 5\,\sqrt{\frac{5}{6\pi}}\,k^{-1/2}\varrho^{k}\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ k\to\infty\ .

Case
d\geq 11
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we have
r=z_{2}
. Moreover,
z_{2}<\varrho^{-1}
, and
z=z_{2}
is the only singularity at distance
r
from the origin. By expanding
g
around
z=z_{2}
, one finds explicit constants
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{d}\in(0.7,2)
satisfying

(3.38)

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{\mathfrak{h}}{z_{2}-z}+O\left(1\right)\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ z\to z_{2}\ .

By
[
FS09
, Theorem VI.4]
, the coefficients in (
3.14
) satisfy

(3.39)

\displaystyle c_{k}\sim\mathfrak{h}z_{2}^{-k}\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ k\to\infty\ .

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.
From discrete- to continuous-time: proof of Proposition
2.2

Recall that
R_{t}
denotes the transition kernel of the continuous-time biased random walk on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with a bias to the right given by
p=\frac{d-1}{d}
and with
01
as a slow bond, cf. (
2.4
).
Hence,

(3.40)

\displaystyle R_{t}(0,0)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-dt}\,\frac{(dt)^{k}}{k!}\,c_{k}\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are the discrete-time transition probabilities as introduced at the beginning of Section
3
. Because of this identity, Proposition
3.1
, and (
2.5
), we now have all the ingredients to prove Proposition
2.2
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

By Proposition
3.1
, which ensures that
c_{k}\asymp k^{\beta}\gamma^{k}
,
(
3.40
) further reads as

(3.41)

\displaystyle R_{t}(0,0)

\displaystyle\asymp e^{-dt}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-dt}\,\frac{(d\gamma t)^{k}}{k!}\,k^{\beta}=e^{-dt}+e^{-d\left(1-\gamma\right)t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-d\gamma t}\,\frac{(d\gamma t)^{k}}{k!}\,k^{\beta}\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ .

Since
\beta
-moments,
\beta\leq 0
, of a (positive) Poisson random variable with diverging rate
\lambda\to\infty
are asymptotically equal to
C\lambda^{\beta}
, for some
C=C_{\beta}>0
(see, e.g.,
[
JZ04
]
), we get

(3.42)

R_{t}(0,0)\asymp e^{-d(1-\gamma)t}\,t^{\beta}\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ t\to\infty\ .

After recalling from (
2.5
) that
R_{t}(0,0)=\mathbf{P}_{o,o}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}(\mathbb{T}_{d})}(X_{t}=Y_{t})
, both claims in (
2.6
)–(
2.7
) follow at once by inserting
t=T(a)
as given in (
1.7
) into (
3.42
).
∎

We conclude with two remarks on the large-degree regime.

Remark 3.3

(Sharpening of the cutoff window)
.

In the supercritical regime (
d>10
), we expect changes not only in the cutoff location but also in the cutoff window: since
\beta=0
, the window should shrink from order
t_{\rm rel}\log\log n
to order
t_{\rm rel}
. A rigorous proof of this claim would need an improved version of the upper bound in (
2.31
).

Remark 3.4

(Regime
d\to\infty
)
.

Since the constant
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{d}
appearing in (
3.39
) remains bounded away from zero and infinity, the asymptotic equivalence
c_{k}\asymp\sigma^{k}
(and, thus,
R_{t}(0,0)\asymp e^{-d(1-\sigma)t}
in (
3.42
)) holds uniformly over all
d\geq 11
.
