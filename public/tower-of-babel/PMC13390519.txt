Transposable element regulation in mammalian germ cells and other contexts: multilayered repression, fertility, and regulated activation
Authors: Peilin Li, Soichiro Yamanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransposable elements (TEs), particularly retrotransposons that dominate mammalian genomes, are pervasive components of mammalian genomes whose activation is constrained by multilayered repression systems. In germ cells, this repression architecture is particularly elaborate, integrating chromatin-based silencing, DNA methylation, and small RNA-guided pathways to safeguard genome integrity during epigenetic reprogramming. These mechanisms are coordinated yet mechanistically specialized, targeting distinct phases of the transposon life cycle and different TE families across developmental stages. Yet the distinctive chromatin landscape of germ cells also creates windows of developmental permissiveness during which TE transcription can occur. Beyond the germline, TE expression can emerge in defined stages of early embryogenesis, extraembryonic development, neural differentiation, and aging, with consequences ranging from chromatin remodeling and regulatory co-option to inflammatory signaling and genome instability. Together, these observations raise a central question: how do different mammalian lineages balance epigenetic plasticity with genome defense? Here, we synthesize current understanding of the molecular logic of TE repression, emphasizing the germline, and integrate evidence across development and aging. We highlight shared principles—such as epigenetic permissiveness and RNA-guided targeting—while underscoring a key difference in regulatory outcome: somatic contexts may tolerate, co-opt, or pathologically amplify TE activity, whereas the germline converts transient activation into heritable, sequence-specific silencing.

Introduction: TEs as pervasive and dynamic genomic components

TEs are mobile genetic sequences that can change their position within a genome. First discovered by Barbara McClintock through her pioneering genetic studies in maize [1], TEs are now recognized as ubiquitous components of eukaryotic genomes. Genome sequencing has revealed that TEs and their remnants account for a substantial fraction of genomic DNA across species, comprising nearly half of the mammalian genome and reaching more than 80–90% in some plant lineages [2–4]. These observations establish TEs not as rare anomalies, but as dominant contributors to genome composition and size variation.

A defining feature of TEs is their ability to mobilize, or “transpose,” from one genomic locus to another. This property distinguishes them from other repetitive sequences and underlies both their evolutionary success and their capacity to reshape host genomes. Based on the molecular nature of their transposition intermediate, TEs are broadly divided into two major classes [5,6]. Class I elements, or retrotransposons, mobilize through an RNA intermediate that is reverse-transcribed into DNA prior to integration, whereas Class II elements, or DNA transposons, transpose directly as DNA molecules.

Retrotransposons constitute the predominant class of TEs in mammalian genomes. They are further subdivided into long terminal repeat (LTR) and non-LTR retrotransposons. LTR retrotransposons, including endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), share structural and mechanistic similarities with infectious retroviruses, encoding gag and pol genes and mobilizing through virus-like particles before genomic integration [2,7]. Non-LTR retrotransposons include long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), which encode the enzymatic machinery required for their own mobilization, and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), which are non-autonomous and rely on LINE-encoded proteins for retrotransposition [8,9]. In contrast, DNA transposons typically mobilize via a “cut-and-paste” mechanism mediated by a transposase enzyme that recognizes terminal inverted repeats flanking the element and catalyzes its excision and reintegration [3,10]. Although retrotransposons account for the majority of transposable elements in mammalian genomes and constitute the primary focus of many mechanistic studies, we use the broader term “TEs” throughout this Review for consistency, unless a specific transposon class or family is explicitly indicated in the original studies discussed.

Despite these mechanistic distinctions, a unifying feature across TE classes is their capacity for genomic mobilization and long-term persistence. For this reason, TEs are often described as “selfish” or “parasitic” genetic elements [11–13], because their inherent capacity to compromise genome integrity and cellular homeostasis. In mammalian genomes, uncontrolled TE activation can induce insertional mutagenesis, chromosomal rearrangements, replication stress, and DNA damage, thereby threatening the stability of both somatic and germline genomes [14,15]. In particular, autonomous retrotransposons such as LINE-1 retain the ability to mobilize within the genome, and their retrotransposition can disrupt coding sequences or regulatory regions, alter chromatin organization, and generate structural genomic variation associated with human disease [15,16].

The threat posed by TE activation is particularly pronounced in germ cells, because TE-induced mutations or genome instability can be transmitted to the next generation. At the same time, germ cell development is accompanied by extensive epigenetic reprogramming, which can transiently weaken TE repression and create windows of vulnerability [17–19]. Thus, tight control of TE activity is especially important in this context. For example, aberrant LINE-1 activation has been associated with female germ cell loss [20,21], whereas defective repression of LINE-1 and LTR retrotransposons is linked to impaired spermatogenesis and male infertility in several mouse mutant models [22–26]. To counter TE activation in germ cells and somatic tissues, particularly that of evolutionarily young, intact, and potentially retrotransposons, host genomes have evolved multiple defense systems, including the PIWI-piRNA pathway, KRAB zinc-finger proteins, the HUSH complex, and others, to repress TEs at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. Because TEs continue to diversify, amplify, and evade host restriction, these defense systems also evolve in turn, creating an ongoing evolutionary “arms race” between TEs and their hosts [27].

Nevertheless, from a broader evolutionary perspective, TE activity is not solely deleterious. Over long evolutionary timescales, TE-induced mutations can provide raw material for genome innovation. The cumulative effects of TE mobilization have profoundly shaped genome structure, contributing to genome expansion, sequence diversification, and large-scale structural variation. Comparative genomic analyses reveal striking differences in TE abundance among taxa, reflecting variation in historical TE activity, rates of element decay, and population genetic forces such as effective population size and the efficiency of purifying selection [4,28].

Importantly, the vast majority of TE-derived sequences in extant genomes are no longer capable of active transposition. Progressive accumulation of mutations, truncations, and recombination events renders most TE copies immobile, leaving behind molecular fossils that retain recognizable sequence features but lack mobility [2]. Nevertheless, these remnants often retain regulatory potential and can be co-opted for host gene regulation by providing promoters, enhancers, transcription factor binding sites, splice sites, and polyadenylation signals. As originally anticipated in the “gene battery” model proposed by Britten and Davidson (1969), dispersed repetitive sequences can serve as substrates for the coordinated evolution of gene regulatory networks [29].

Consistent with this idea, genome-wide studies have demonstrated that TE-derived sequences contribute extensively to cis-regulatory landscapes. A substantial fraction of experimentally validated human promoters and enhancers contain TE-derived sequences [13,30], and lineage-specific TE families have dispersed regulatory modules throughout mammalian genomes in a punctuated, family-specific manner [31,32]. This dispersal provides an efficient mechanism for the rapid emergence and rewiring of gene regulatory networks, enabling coordinated changes in gene expression without requiring gradual accumulation of point mutations.

Beyond regulatory DNA, TEs have also contributed to the origin of novel genes and protein functions. Several host genes essential for immunity, development, and reproduction trace their origins to ancient transposases or retroviral proteins that have been molecularly domesticated and repurposed for host functions [33]. A classic example is the RAG1/RAG2 recombinase, which is essential for V(D)J recombination in adaptive immunity and is thought to have evolved from Transib-like DNA transposons [34,35].

A further layer of TE function involves retrotransposon-derived RNAs and ongoing retrotransposon expression. In somatic contexts, such RNAs, including LINE-1 RNA, have been implicated in chromatin regulation, innate immune signaling, and tissue remodeling [36]. These examples suggest that the biological impact of TEs extends beyond their genomic remnants, encompassing active molecular functions that can be deleterious or beneficial depending on cellular context.

Taken together, TEs are no longer viewed simply as inert repetitive DNA or genomic parasites. Rather, they are dynamic genomic entities whose capacity for mobility and amplification has left a lasting imprint on genome structure, gene regulation, and evolutionary innovation. In the following sections, we first review the general principles of TE repression. We then focus on their regulation in germ cells, where their activity poses a particular threat by generating heritable variation across generations, and highlight unresolved questions linking TE silencing to fertility. We then discuss the roles of TEs beyond the germline, highlighting the influence of LINE-1 activation in somatic cells and recent insights into how TE-derived sequences have been incorporated into host regulatory networks across several contexts.

Molecular mechanisms of TE repression: general principles

An important conceptual point is that TE repression mechanisms are layered but coordinated. Although different pathways tend to counteract particular phases of the TE life cycle or specific molecular vulnerabilities, they often interact with one another to reinforce TE repression in a context-dependent manner, thereby forming a multilayered defense architecture. Chromatin-based mechanisms such as HUSH and KRAB-ZFP-KAP1 primarily limit transcriptional output by establishing repressive chromatin environments [37,38]. In parallel, small RNA-mediated pathways operate at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels through sequence-guided recognition [39] (Figure 1). At a further downstream layer, APOBEC3 proteins act after transcription by directly compromising the integrity of retrotransposon replication intermediates [40]. Therefore, understanding transposon repression therefore requires not only identifying the pathways involved but also defining when, where, and how each mechanism is deployed within a multilayered defense architecture. HUSH complex: sequence-agnostic chromatin repression

The human silencing hub (HUSH) complex functions as a chromatin-based repressor that recognizes and silences newly integrated or intronless DNA in a largely sequence-agnostic manner (Figure 1). Initially identified as a factor required for stable silencing of integrated lentiviral reporters [41], HUSH was subsequently shown to repress endogenous LINE-1 retrotransposons [42,43], the only autonomous retrotransposons active in the human genome.

HUSH is composed of TASOR (FAM208A), MPP8, and periphilin [43]. Mechanistically, periphilin binds nascent transcripts produced from long, intronless loci, recruiting the complex co-transcriptionally [44]. TASOR acts as a central scaffold for HUSH assembly, and the complex engages the H3K9 methyltransferase SETDB1 to deposit repressive H3K9me3 marks, while MPP8 contributes to HUSH function and may help stabilize its association with chromatin [45,46]. Downstream chromatin compaction is reinforced by MORC2, an ATPase that promotes heterochromatin consolidation [47].

By targeting structural features of transcripts rather than specific DNA sequences, HUSH provides a rapid, innate defense against retroelements, integrated viruses, and other intronless foreign DNA. This sequence-agnostic property distinguishes HUSH from adaptive, sequence-specific silencing systems and positions it as a broadly acting layer of genome immunity [43].

A notable limitation of HUSH-mediated repression is its reliance on transcriptional engagement with intronless loci. Because HUSH is recruited co-transcriptionally, it does not preemptively silence quiescent elements but instead responds to transcriptional activity after it has already been initiated [37]. This feature distinguishes HUSH from sequence-specific DNA-binding repressors and suggests that it functions primarily as a containment mechanism rather than as a developmental “gatekeeper” that prevents activation altogether.

In the context of germ cells, where epigenetic reprogramming can transiently license widespread transcription, HUSH-mediated repression may be particularly relevant as an auxiliary layer that buffers transcriptional noise rather than as a primary determinant of long-term silencing.

APOBEC3 family: mutagenic restriction of retroelements

The APOBEC (apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide-like) family of cytidine deaminases constitutes another arm of intrinsic genome defense. In primates, expansion of the APOBEC3 (A3) subfamily has produced multiple paralogs that potently restrict retroviruses and endogenous retroelements [40,48,49].

APOBEC3 proteins primarily act by deaminating cytosines in single-stranded DNA intermediates generated during reverse transcription, introducing G-to-A hypermutations that disrupt retrotransposon cDNA sequence [50–52]. In addition, several APOBEC3 proteins inhibit retrotransposition through deaminase-independent mechanisms, such as blocking reverse transcription or interfering with ribonucleoprotein assembly [53,54] (Figure 1).

Unlike chromatin-based repression pathways, APOBEC3-mediated restriction does not establish heritable silencing marks but instead acts by directly compromising the genetic integrity of retrotransposon replication intermediates. This strategy is effective at limiting the propagation of invading elements; however, it can involve cytidine deamination that introduces permanent sequence alterations. Such mutagenic activity creates an inherent trade-off between defense and genome integrity. While this cost may be tolerable or even advantageous in somatic tissues, in the germline—where mutations are heritable—APOBEC3 expression and activity appear to be tightly regulated and comparatively limited.

Consistent with this view, genetic evidence suggests that APOBEC3 proteins do not play a dominant role in mammalian germline transposon control [55]. Instead, germ cells preferentially rely on epigenetic and RNA-based mechanisms that achieve repression without introducing DNA damage. This division of labor highlights how the same defense pathways can be differentially deployed depending on cellular context and evolutionary constraint.

KRAB zinc-finger proteins: sequence-specific transcriptional silencing

KRAB zinc-finger proteins (KRAB-ZFPs) represent the largest family of transcription factors in vertebrates and function as a sequence-specific defense system against TEs. Individual KRAB-ZFPs recognize specific TE-derived DNA motifs via C2H2 zinc-finger arrays and recruit the co-repressor KAP1 (TRIM28) through their KRAB domain [56] (Figure 1).

KAP1 serves as a scaffold for heterochromatin-forming factors, including SETDB1, HP1 proteins, and components of the NuRD complex, leading to deposition of H3K9me3 and stable transcriptional repression [57–59]. Genome-wide analyses reveal that most human KRAB-ZFPs preferentially bind retrotransposon sequences, often with specificity for particular families, reflecting an evolutionary arms race between host repressors and invading elements [38,60,61].

Despite their central role in transcriptional repression, KRAB-ZFP-mediated mechanisms are inherently limited by their dependence on DNA sequence recognition. Newly invading transposons can evade repression until a cognate KRAB-ZFP evolves, creating a temporal gap between invasion and effective silencing. Moreover, KRAB-ZFP repertoires differ substantially across species [60], reflecting lineage-specific transposon landscapes.

Integrative epigenomic and transcriptional analyses in human and mouse germ cells support a model in which KRAB-ZFPs participate in the developmental regulation of retroelements. In human primordial germ cells (hPGCs), SVA_A copies that retain higher DNA methylation during genome-wide demethylation are enriched for ZNF257 and ZNF28 binding peaks and motifs, suggesting that sequence-specific recognition by KRAB-ZFPs may contribute to epigenetic stability during germline reprogramming [62]. In parallel, studies of mouse spermatogenesis have shown that a subset of KZFPs is highly expressed in mitotically dividing spermatogonia and downregulated upon meiotic entry, coinciding with activation of ERV-derived enhancers and stage-specific transcriptional programs [63]. Together, these findings indicate that KZFP binding specificity and developmental expression dynamics are closely associated with retroelement regulation in the germline.

Complementing these observations, cluster-level deletion of KRAB-ZFP loci in pluripotent cells results in derepression of specific transposon families and loss of repressive chromatin marks [64]. In addition, studies in human pluripotent cells have identified individual KRAB-ZFP-transposon pairs, including ZNF93 acting on older L1PA lineages and ZNF91 on SVA elements [65], providing further support for a causal role of KZFPs in transposon control outside the germline. Nevertheless, direct evidence that disruption of a single, defined KRAB-ZFP in mammalian germ cells is sufficient to induce robust transposon activation remains limited. This gap likely reflects intrinsic biological and technical challenges of the system, including functional redundancy among KZFP paralogs, their organization into large genomic clusters that complicate precise targeting, lineage-specific co-expansion of KZFP-ERV pairs, and technical constraints associated with manipulating stage-specific germ cells. Future combinatorial and stage-resolved genetic approaches will be required to delineate the precise causal relationships between individual KZFPs and their transposon targets in the germline.

Small RNA-mediated suppression of transposon expression

Small RNA pathways constitute a conserved and multilayered defense system that suppresses TE expression through sequence-guided recognition and silencing. Among these, RNA interference (RNAi) was originally defined as double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)-triggered, Dicer-dependent mRNA degradation [66]. In this pathway, long dsRNA precursors are processed by the RNase III enzyme Dicer into ~21–22 nucleotide small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which are subsequently loaded onto Argonaute proteins to form RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISCs) [67,68]. The guide strand directs Argonaute to complementary RNA targets, leading to endonucleolytic cleavage and transcript degradation. In Drosophila, RNAi operates as a genetically specialized pathway dedicated to antiviral and transposon defense, utilizing distinct Dicer and Argonaute paralogs [69,70]. Endogenous siRNAs (endo-siRNAs) derived from repetitive elements contribute to TE repression primarily through post-transcriptional silencing. In mammals, endogenous RNAi activity has been documented in defined cellular contexts, particularly in mouse oocytes, including the processing of transposon-derived dsRNAs generated from sense-antisense transcription or inverted repeats [71,72]. Beyond cytoplasmic RNA cleavage, RNAi-related mechanisms in several organisms are coupled to chromatin-based repression of repetitive DNA, linking small RNA production to heterochromatin formation [73–75].

In parallel, a mechanistically distinct small RNA pathway employs PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), a class of 23–32 nucleotide small RNAs enriched in animal gonads and bound by PIWI proteins [76–79]. Unlike siRNAs, piRNAs arise from long single-stranded precursors and are generated through a Dicer-independent mechanism [76,79]. A major source of piRNAs is genomic loci termed piRNA clusters, which are enriched in fragmented transposon sequences and serve as repositories of transposon-derived information [79,80]. Thus, these clusters provide a heritable record of TE exposure and reflect the ongoing evolutionary arms race between TEs and host defense systems. This primary piRNA production can be further reinforced by a secondary amplification loop, the “ping-pong” cycle, characterized by reciprocal cleavage events that generate piRNAs with a signature 10-nucleotide overlap [79,81]. Functionally, PIWI-piRNA complexes recognize targets via sequence complementarity and mediate silencing either post-transcriptionally or transcriptionally through recruitment of chromatin-modifying activities [82,83].

Together, RNAi and PIWI-piRNA pathways illustrate complementary strategies of small RNA-guided TE control, linking cytoplasmic RNA degradation with chromatin-based repression. In the following section, we will examine how these pathways are deployed in mammalian germ cells, where they intersect with epigenetic reprogramming and developmental transitions to achieve stage-specific and robust suppression of TEs.

TE repression in mammalian germ cells

Given the multilayered TE defense system described above, the mammalian germ cells deploy a specialized strategy of transposon control that predominantly relies on small RNA-guided pathways, including piRNAs and endo-siRNAs, together with other chromatin regulators that reinforce epigenetic silencing. This specialization is closely associated with the extensive epigenetic reprogramming that occurs during germ cell development, including genome-wide DNA demethylation and large-scale chromatin remodeling (Figure 2). This epigenetic reprogramming, though essential for resetting developmental potential, transiently erases epigenetic barriers that normally constrain TE expression, creating windows of increased vulnerability to transposon activation [84]. Accordingly, TE defense in mammalian germ cells is characterized by the coordinated suppression of TE transcription and the reestablishment of long-term silencing marks, including H3K9me3 and DNA methylation, at TE loci. In parallel, additional defense layers—such as the HUSH complex, APOBEC3 family members, and KRAB zinc-finger proteins—although less extensively characterized in the germline context, may contribute to buffering TE transcription and maintaining baseline repression. In the following sections, we focus on TE defense strategies in male and female mammalian germ cells across distinct developmental windows and species, reviewing recent advances and highlighting outstanding questions in the field. Male germline

Fetal prospermatogonia: installation of long-term repression

In male fetal germ cells (prospermatogonia), global DNA demethylation creates a permissive environment for TE transcription, necessitating rapid and coordinated engagement of alternative repression mechanisms. In the cytoplasm, the PIWI-piRNA pathway provides an immediate post-transcriptional layer of defense: the PIWI protein MILI (PIWIL2) recognizes TE-derived RNAs through complementary piRNAs and cleaves them through its slicer activity, thereby limiting TE transcript accumulation [25]. This cleavage also fuels the ping-pong amplification cycle, reinforcing the production of TE-directed piRNAs, a fraction of which is loaded onto MIWI2 (PIWIL4) and carried into the nucleus to guide subsequent targeting of active TE loci [25,85,86] (Figure 3). In the nucleus, DNA methylation-dependent transcriptional silencing at TE loci is installed through two waves of de novo methylation. The first wave occurs largely independently of piRNAs and methylates most retrotransposon copies, whereas a second, more selective wave depends on the PIWI-piRNA pathway and targets active TE loci that evade initial methylation [87,88]. Consistent with this model, evidence from Pld6/Mitopld mutants, which exhibit defects in piRNA biogenesis, indicates that piRNA-dependent de novo DNA methylation is restricted to a subset of retrotransposon promoters, occurring most prominently at young LINE-1/L1Md loci such as L1Md_A and L1Md_Gf, as well as at selected LTR families such as MMERGLN [88,89]. In addition, higher-order chromatin reorganization has been associated with this second wave of de novo DNA methylation at TE loci, as broad TE-rich domains transiently open before their subsequent re-compaction and methylation in fetal gonocytes [19]. Together, these findings indicate that long-term TE silencing is installed through locus- and pathway-specific mechanisms, with piRNA-dependent playing a central role at a restricted subset of transcriptionally active escapee loci.

At these piRNA-dependent active loci, nuclear MIWI2 provides sequence-specific targeting through loaded piRNAs, whereas stable repression requires coupling to a specialized set of nuclear execution factors that recruit chromatin remodeling and DNA methylation machinery. Among these, SPOCD1 and TEX15 function downstream of piRNA recognition and MIWI2 nuclear localization, bridging sequence-guided TE targeting to the enzymatic installation of de novo DNA methylation [90,91]. These factors recruit core de novo methyltransferases, centered on DNMT3A together with its cofactor DNMT3L, and in rodents additionally the specialized methyltransferase DNMT3C [22,92,93], which shows a strong preference for young retrotransposon promoters. Genetic disruption of SPOCD1, TEX15, or DNMT3C results in selective failure to methylate young LINE-1 and ERVK families, leading to retrotransposon derepression, meiotic defects, and male infertility, despite largely intact piRNA biogenesis—highlighting a clear separation between TE recognition and execution [90,91,93].

Recent work has also provided insight into the interplay between chromatin state and piRNA-guided de novo DNA methylation. Subsets of young LINE-1 copies transiently adopt combinatorial chromatin signatures—co-occupancy of active (H3K4me3) and repressive (H3K9me3) marks. This specific bivalent signature is directly recognized by the chromatin reader SPIN1, which directly interacts with SPOCD1 and licenses these elements for subsequent piRNA-guided DNA methylation, providing the chromatin-based step of a two-factor authentication mechanism [94].

Following the transient opening of chromatin and increased transcriptional permissiveness at retrotransposon loci [19], reestablishment of heterochromatin becomes critical alongside the second wave of de novo DNA methylation. In this process, the DNA-binding protein MORC1 plays a central role in chromatin re-compaction and H3K9me3 reinforcement at young retrotransposon subfamilies [95,96]. Notably, defects in MORC1 lead to reduced H3K9me3 and DNA methylation at young TE loci, and MORC1-regulated targets substantially overlap with MIWI2-directed elements, supporting a model in which piRNA-guided locus selection is coupled to a heterochromatin-reinforcement step that stabilizes repression beyond the de novo DNA methylation phase [96]. Recent works has suggested that piRNA-dependent H3K9me3 maintenance beyond the fetal stage is also locus-specific, with prominent effects at young LINE-1 promoters rather than at LTR retrotransposons [89]. Together, these observations suggest that, at least for subsets of young LINE-1 elements, piRNA-guided targeting, de novo DNA methylation, and chromatin reorganization are closely linked during the establishment of long-term repression in male fetal germ cells.

An important implication of this fetal program is that its failure often manifests phenotypically at later stages of spermatogenesis rather than during fetal development itself. Although fetal gonocytes can tolerate partial transposon derepression through compensatory post-transcriptional mechanisms, defects in the installation of stable epigenetic repression become unmasked upon meiotic entry [88,97], particularly during early meiotic prophase I, when programmed DNA double-strand break formation, homolog pairing, and synapsis place stringent demands on genome integrity. Thus, fetal piRNA-directed repression can be viewed as a developmental investment: its primary function is not to ensure immediate cellular survival but to establish an epigenetic state that supports future meiotic progression and long-term male fertility.

Meiotic stages: post-transcriptional containment

Once germ cells enter meiosis, the logic of TE control shifts in emphasis. Despite the prior installation of DNA methylation and heterochromatin-based repression during fetal stages, TE transcription is not completely repressed in meiotic germ cells. In particular, LINE-1 (L1) transcripts can still be detected [24,98], implying that transcriptional repression alone is insufficient to fully restrain LINE-1 during meiosis. In this context, pachytene spermatocytes produce abundant piRNAs and express PIWI proteins that act primarily in the cytoplasm to recognize and restrain TE-derived RNAs [26,99] (Figure 3).

Although the bulk pachytene piRNA population is compositionally distinct from fetal and pre-pachytene piRNAs and is not dominated by young retrotransposon-derived sequences [100], genetic studies indicate that this post-transcriptional arm remains important in several contexts even when DNA methylation appears largely intact [99]. In particular, disruption of pachytene piRNA function can result in pronounced LINE-1 RNA and protein accumulation, often accompanied by elevated markers of DNA damage response and defects in meiotic progression [24,26,99,101]. These observations indicate that LINE-1 elements remain a significant threat during meiosis and require continuous RNA-level containment.

In contrast, ERVK-class retrotransposons, including intracisternal A-particles (IAPs), a rodent-specific ERV-like retrotransposon family [102], often exhibit a different regulatory profile during meiosis. In many genetic contexts, ERVK/IAP elements show less dramatic RNA-level derepression in pachytene spermatocytes compared with LINE-1 [103], consistent with the idea that these elements are more strongly governed by transcriptional heterochromatin-based mechanisms. Indeed, ERVK/IAPs are typically enriched for repressive chromatin marks such as H3K9me3 and appear to depend more heavily on stable chromatin repression established earlier in development, rather than on pachytene piRNA-mediated RNA cleavage [99,104]. As a result, ERVK/IAP activation in meiotic germ cells is often more variable and context dependent, becoming prominent primarily when upstream chromatin-based repression pathways are compromised [99,104].

This differential behavior underscores an important principle: distinct TE families rely on different layers of the silencing machinery at different developmental stages. Whereas evolutionarily young LINE-1 elements pose a persistent challenge during meiosis and are particularly sensitive to failure of post-transcriptional containment, many ERVs—including ERVK/IAP families—are more effectively restrained by transcriptional repression and heterochromatin maintenance. Consequently, meiotic-stage defects in RNA-level control tend to preferentially release LINE-1 activity, while defects in chromatin-based repression can unmask ERVK/IAP expression even when pachytene piRNA pathways remain functional [99,103–105].

Taken together, fetal and meiotic TE silencing represent two mechanistically distinct but complementary phases of germline defense. In fetal prospermatogonia, piRNA-guided recognition is closely linked to the establishment of de novo DNA methylation and stable transcriptional repression, a feature that is especially evident for young LINE-1 and ERVK/IAP elements [87], while also contributing to post-transcriptional repression of TE transcripts [88]. In meiotic germ cells, by contrast, TE control relies more heavily on a pachytene piRNA-dependent post-transcriptional program that restrains LINE-1 expression and helps preserve genome integrity during meiosis.

Female germline

Mouse oocytes: dominance of RNAi

Compared with the male germline—where fetal prospermatogonia install long-term transcriptional repression through piRNA-guided de novo DNA methylation and meiotic cells rely on pachytene piRNAs for RNA-level containment—TE control in the mouse female germline follows a distinct mechanistic logic. Two features are particularly important. First, genome-wide de novo DNA methylation in females is established primarily during postnatal oocyte growth rather than fetal development, creating a prolonged interval in which oocytes must maintain genome integrity during meiotic arrest before durable methylation-based repression is in place [106,107]. Second, mouse oocytes rely heavily on endogenous RNA interference (RNAi), whereas the canonical PIWI-piRNA pathway that dominates male germline defense is markedly attenuated especially in mouse [72] (Figure 4). Mechanistically, oocyte RNAi is driven by abundant endogenous siRNAs loaded into AGO2, which repress targets predominantly through slicing. Genetic dissection demonstrates that this pathway represents a specialized and essential layer rather than a generic small-RNA requirement: disruption of DICER or AGO2 catalytic activity in oocytes causes severe meiotic defects and infertility, whereas selective impairment of miRNA biogenesis results in much milder phenotypes [108,109]. Endogenous siRNAs in oocytes derive from both host transcripts and repetitive sequences, including retrotransposons, supporting a direct role for RNAi in limiting TE RNA accumulation during oocyte growth [72,110]. At the same time, because RNAi also targets numerous cellular mRNAs essential for meiosis, phenotypes in RNAi-deficient oocytes likely reflect combined effects of TE derepression and broader transcriptome dysregulation.

A distinctive refinement in murid oocytes is the expression of a truncated DICER isoform (DicerO) driven by a retrotransposon-derived promoter [111]. DicerO exhibits enhanced dsRNA-processing activity and underlies the unusually robust RNAi capacity of mouse oocytes, illustrating how TE-derived sequences can be co-opted to reinforce host defense. In parallel with RNA-level containment, transcriptional repression is progressively reestablished during oocyte growth through DNMT3A/3L-dependent de novo DNA methylation and chromatin maturation, providing a delayed but durable epigenetic lock that complements RNAi-based TE control.

Beyond mouse: PIWI-piRNA in female germ cells

A central caveat in synthesizing female germline TE defense is that the mouse is not a universal reference for mammalian oocytes. In many non-rodent mammals, oocyte small-RNA landscapes are piRNA-rich, reflecting the presence of PIWIL3—an ovary- and oocyte-enriched PIWI protein absent from mouse and rat [112–117] (Figure 4). This fundamental difference limits the extent to which key aspects of oocyte piRNA biology can be inferred from standard mouse models.

Biochemical studies in non-rodent species indicate an active oocyte piRNA pathway, with PIWIL3 associating with conserved piRNA factors such as TDRKH and PNLDC1 [113,115,117]. Importantly, functional genetics in golden hamster has begun to provide causal evidence that is difficult to obtain in larger mammals, revealing non-redundant roles for PIWI-family proteins in female fertility and early development, accompanied by TE-silencing phenotypes [116–120]. These findings suggest that, in species lacking dominant endogenous RNAi, PIWI-piRNA pathways can constitute a major axis of oocyte TE control.

At the same time, chromatin-based repression layers are clearly relevant beyond mouse [121]. Oocyte growth involves extensive chromatin remodeling and staged acquisition of DNA methylation, yet the molecular links between small-RNA targeting and stable chromatin outcomes in female germ cells remain far less defined than in the male fetal gonocyte paradigm. As a result, cross-species female TE defense is less mechanistically codified and must be interpreted through comparative approaches rather than extrapolated from mouse alone.

Linking TE control to fertility

Gap between TE control and male fertility

In mammalian spermatogenesis, multiple epigenetic mechanisms collaborate to restrict TE activity, including piRNA-directed DNA methylation, H3K9me3-mediated heterochromatin formation, and post-transcriptional degradation. Although disruptions in each of these regulatory layers are known to cause male infertility, why they lead to spermatogenic arrest at characteristic but different stages remains poorly understood. For example, loss of MOV10L1, an RNA helicase essential for piRNA biogenesis, in the fetal germline causes early meiotic arrest [122,123], whereas its conditional inactivation in spermatocytes depletes pachytene piRNAs and results in post-meiotic arrest at the round spermatid stage [124]. Loss of either MILI (PIWIL2) or MIWI2 (PIWIL4), both required for piRNA-directed de novo DNA methylation at TE loci in fetal male germ cells, results in meiotic prophase I arrest later during spermatogenesis [23,125]. In contrast, loss of MIWI (PIWIL1), which functions in post-transcriptional regulation during and after meiosis, results in arrest at the round spermatid stage [126]. These stage-specific phenotypes suggest that different TE control mechanisms may be differentially required at successive points in spermatogenesis. However, whether this specificity reflects the timing of each mechanism’s engagement, the particular TE families they target, or the distinct downstream consequences of TE dysregulation at each stage remains unclear.

A central unresolved question concerns the nature of LINE-1-mediated toxicity in the germline. LINE-1 upregulation has been frequently observed across various mutant models with spermatogenic phenotypes [24–26,80,99,101]. In such contexts, it remains unclear whether the pathogenic potential of LINE-1 stems primarily from its high transcriptional activity or its capacity for autonomous retrotransposition, or both. Canonically, LINE-1 derepression is associated with DNA damage, asynapsis, and activation of meiotic quality-control/checkpoint pathways [24,127–129]. However, it has been suggested in other contexts that LINE-1 can also exert genotoxic or cytotoxic effects through pathways that do not involve DNA double-strand breaks [130–132]. For instance, the accumulation of LINE-1 reverse transcription intermediates generated downstream of LINE-1 activation can trigger innate immune responses that may be detrimental to female germ cell survival [20,21].

Beyond defining the nature of LINE-1 toxicity, it is also crucial to determine how the level of LINE-1 activity shapes germ cell fate, specifically whether elevated LINE-1 expression imposes a negative selective pressure during germ cell development, as suggested by studies in the female germline [20,21]. This raises a broader question that extends to other TE families: whether LINE, ERV, and SINE elements differ in their genotoxic potential, and whether their pathogenic effects are primarily driven by insertional mutagenesis or by transcript-dependent mechanisms. This question is underscored by a notable example of non-genotoxic TE effects: in Miwi2- and Dnmt3l-deficient spermatogonia, IAP derepression rewires the transcriptome by driving expression of neighboring genes without detectable DNA damage [133]. Related TE-driven perturbation of neighboring gene expression is also evident in mutant meiotic spermatocytes, where derepressed LINE-1, MMERVK, and IAP copies can activate nearby transcripts [88].

Together, these questions converge on a broader issue: how the impact of TE dysregulation varies across the time course of spermatogenesis. However, it is equally important to recognize that TEs are not merely pathogenic agents. Sakashita et al. [134] revealed that endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), particularly the ERVK family, have been co-opted to function as species-specific transcriptional enhancers that activate germline genes upon entry into meiosis, shaping the unique gene expression programs of mammalian spermatogenesis. Although TE co-option has been extensively documented in other contexts such as early embryogenesis and placenta, reported cases in the mammalian germline remain rare. This raises an intriguing possibility: some TE activation events currently interpreted as “dysregulation” may in fact represent undiscovered cases of developmental co-option. Distinguishing between pathogenic TE expression and context-dependent beneficial functions remains a fundamental challenge for the field.

Gap between TE control and female fertility

In mammalian oogenesis, TE control relies on distinct small-RNA systems across species: mouse oocytes depend prominently on endo-siRNA/RNAi, whereas piRNA pathways are functional and important in species such as the golden hamster, and likely in other mammals. Despite these differences, disruptions of TE control are associated with female infertility, yet several fundamental gaps remain.

First, establishing causal links between TE dysregulation and oocyte failure has proven challenging. In mice, the most severe phenotypes arise from disruption of RNAi machinery (Dicer or AGO2 catalytic activity) [108,110]. However, these perturbations simultaneously affect TE repression and essential maternal mRNA programs, complicating causal interpretation. As a result, infertility phenotypes likely reflect mixed mechanisms [71,72,110], and whether TE derepression is a proximate driver of meiotic failure or a secondary indicator of broader regulatory collapse remains unresolved. Unlike in males, where stage-specific arrests following distinct genetic lesions have clarified TE-dependent vulnerabilities, comparable dissection in females awaits more targeted strategies that modulate TE activity without globally compromising oocyte RNAi.

Second, the molecular framework linking small RNA pathways to stable TE silencing remains poorly defined in females. In the male fetal germline, a relatively coherent framework can be discerned in which piRNA-guided recognition is coupled to the establishment of durable epigenetic silencing, particularly at specific TE families. In females—particularly in mice, where PIWI-piRNA pathways are attenuated—the rules linking small-RNA targeting to durable chromatin states and TE-family-specific repression are not well understood. Whether RNAi in mice, or piRNA pathways in other mammals, establish long-lasting silencing in oocytes, and how such silencing is maintained across the prolonged dictyate arrest, remains unclear.

Third, the relationship between TE activity and the ovarian reserve remains suggestive but incomplete. In mice, several studies link fetal oocyte attrition and oocyte aging to increased LINE-1 expression, genomic stress, and heterochromatin erosion [20,21,135–137]. These findings raise the possibility that TE control influences both initial oocyte selection and long-term oocyte competence. However, the mechanistic basis underlying this relationship remains to be elucidated, and the extent to which these findings extend beyond mice—particularly to species with functional piRNA pathways in females—has yet to be determined.

Together, these gaps highlight that our understanding of TE control in female germ cells lags behind that in males. Addressing whether TE dysregulation actively drives oocyte loss, how silencing is established and maintained, and whether cumulative TE activity shapes the ovarian reserve over time will require new experimental strategies that disentangle TE-specific effects from broader oocyte physiology.

TE activation across development, physiology, and aging: parallels and contrasts with germ cell regulation

TEs are subject to robust epigenetic repression in most mammalian somatic tissues to preserve genomic integrity. However, accumulating evidence across multiple biological contexts demonstrates that TE silencing is neither uniform nor static. Instead, defined developmental stages and physiological states—including early embryogenesis, placental development, neural differentiation and function, and ageing—are characterized by transient or context-dependent TE activation. When these contexts are considered together, common regulatory principles emerge, including epigenetic permissiveness, functional co-option of TE-derived sequences, and immune surveillance of TE-derived nucleic acids. Comparison with germ cell TE regulation further reveals both conserved logic and lineage-specific adaptations.

The influence of LINE-1 activation in somatic cells

LINE-1 is the only autonomously active retrotransposon family in mammals, comprising ~17% of the human genome. Although most copies are inactive, a subset remains competent for retrotransposition and is tightly repressed in somatic cells. At the same time, LINE-1 activity has been co-opted into regulatory and physiological processes, reflecting its dual role as both a genomic threat and a functional element.

In somatic cells, LINE-1 elements are primarily silenced by DNA methylation and repressive chromatin. Disruption of this repression, such as DNMT1 depletion in human neural progenitor cells, induces hypomethylation and selective activation of young LINE-1 elements, which acquire active chromatin features and can function as alternative promoters for nearby genes [138]. These findings indicate that LINE-1 activation is regulated by epigenetic context rather than occurring randomly. Beyond transcription, LINE-1-derived RNAs exert regulatory functions. In the developing mouse cortex, LINE-1 RNAs act as long noncoding RNAs that modulate chromatin through PRC2 and regulate neuronal differentiation [139]. Consistently, genome-wide analyses in human brain reveal abundant LINE-1-derived chimeric and noncoding transcripts that are developmentally regulated and contribute to transcriptome diversification [140]. LINE-1 activity also generates endogenous nucleic acids that engage innate immune pathways. Epigenetic derepression induces dsRNA or RNA-DNA hybrids that activate sensors such as MDA5 and trigger interferon responses [141]. In some contexts, this signaling is functionally integrated into physiology. For example, during bone regeneration, LINE-1 RNA activates a PKR-dependent stress pathway that modulates translation and promotes osteogenic differentiation [142], indicating that LINE-1 transcripts can act as regulatory effectors independently of retrotransposition. LINE-1 activation can also contribute to somatic defense. In blind mole rats, reduced DNMT1 activity leads to LINE-1 derepression, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, and interferon-mediated cell death, functioning as a tumor-suppressive mechanism [143]. In contrast, persistent LINE-1 activation in aging or disease contexts promotes chronic innate immune activation and tissue dysfunction. Finally, LINE-1 activity modulates cellular physiology across the lifespan. In human T cells, LINE-1 expression regulates protein synthesis via mTORC1-dependent pathways, with low expression promoting activation in neonatal cells and increased expression contributing to reduced translational capacity and immune senescence in aged cells [144].

Collectively, these studies indicate that LINE-1 activity in somatic cells operates across multiple layers, including transcriptional regulation, RNA-mediated chromatin control, and innate immune signaling. Rather than functioning solely as genomic parasites, LINE-1 elements are integrated into cellular regulatory networks that influence development, tissue repair, immune responses, and aging.

Transposon activation in embryogenesis

Preimplantation embryogenesis represents one of the most striking physiological windows of TE activation. Accumulating evidence has linked TE activation to programmed preimplantation development, with mechanistic evidence being most clearly established in the mouse and increasingly direct support now emerging from human embryos and embryo-model systems. In the mouse, this relationship has been investigated most extensively in the context of ZGA, which peaks at the 2-cell stage. During this period, global DNA methylation is low, and chromatin architecture undergoes extensive remodeling from a maternal to an embryonic configuration, establishing an epigenetically permissive state for TE transcription [145,146]. Consistent with this permissive state, LINE-1 transcripts accumulate from the zygote and peak at the 2-cell stage [147]. Functional perturbation experiments demonstrated that premature LINE-1 silencing reduces global chromatin accessibility, whereas prolonged activation interferes with developmental progression, indicating that LINE-1 RNA itself contributes to chromatin organization rather than merely reflecting epigenetic derepression. Mechanistically, LINE-1 RNA interacts with Nucleolin and KAP1/TRIM28 to repress Dux, a key regulator of the 2-cell/ZGA transcriptional program, thereby facilitating exit from the 2-cell state and coordinating progression beyond the totipotent-like transcriptional program [148]. In parallel, the ERVL family member MERVL is robustly activated downstream of the DUX-dependent 2-cell/ZGA transcriptional program and marks totipotent-like states [149,150]. Depletion of MERVL transcripts disrupts chromatin organization, lineage specification, and embryo viability, establishing that these transcripts are functionally integrated into the early developmental regulatory network [151,152].

In humans, TE activation has also been linked to preimplantation development. A notable parallel to the mouse system is DUX4-mediated TE activation, in which DUX4, a functional counterpart of mouse DUX, activates both endogenous genes and retroviral elements associated with the human 8-cell/EGA (embryonic genome activation)-like program, including HERVL and MLT2A1 [150,153–156]. Another classic example is HERVK, which is also activated at embryonic genome activation, with LTR-driven transcription and detectable retroviral proteins [157,158]. Recent advances in stem cell-based human embryo models, which recapitulate selected features of preimplantation and early post-implantation development in vitro, have provided new opportunities to examine developmental regulatory mechanisms in experimentally accessible systems [159,160]. These models are particularly useful for investigating human-specific or primate-specific regulatory features, including TE-derived sequences, whose functions are difficult to assess in conventional experimental settings. In this context, a recent study using human blastoids, HERVK-derived LTR5Hs elements were investigated in the context of early embryonic regulatory programs, showing that these elements contribute to the epiblast transcriptional program and that at least one human-specific LTR5Hs insertion influences blastoid-forming potential through cis-regulation of the nearby primate-specific ZNF729 gene [161]. Together, these findings illustrate how continued advances in stem cell-based embryo models may broaden experimental opportunities for studying TE-associated regulation and other molecular mechanisms shaping early human development.

Transposon activation in extraembryonic and placental development

The placenta represents an extraembryonic developmental context in which TE repression is comparatively relaxed but not uncontrolled. Relative to most somatic tissues, the placenta exhibits a globally hypomethylated epigenetic landscape [162,163], and LINE-1 methylation decreases during gestation in humans, correlating with increased RNA levels [164]. This hypomethylated state is thought not only to permit TE transcription but also to facilitate the evolutionary co-option of TE-derived sequences as functional regulatory elements and genes, thereby shaping trophoblast-specific transcriptional programs. Despite this permissive environment, genome-defense mechanisms such as Tex19.1 limit excessive retrotransposon activation in mouse placenta [162], indicating a controlled balance between transcriptional permissiveness and repression.

Recent studies have further shown that TE-derived sequences are incorporated into trophoblast regulatory programs in a lineage- and state-specific manner. In human trophoblast, ERV-derived elements can act as enhancers near trophoblast-preferential genes, including an LTR10A element within ENG that regulates soluble ENG secretion by syncytiotrophoblast [165]. ERV-derived enhancers such as MER50 elements also contribute to syncytiotrophoblast-associated gene expression and syncytium formation [166], while ERV-derived transcripts, including HERVH/LTR7C-derived lncRNAs such as UCA1, have been implicated in human trophoblast development [167].

TE-derived promoters and enhancers also contribute to placenta-specific endocrine and physiological regulatory circuits. For example, insertion of a HERV-derived promoter drives trophoblast-specific expression of PTN, a growth factor involved in angiogenesis and placental vascular remodeling [168]. Similarly, the hormone gene INSL4 is regulated by an ERV-derived LTR promoter [169]. TE-derived enhancers also control key endocrine pathways, including leptin expression via MER11-derived regulatory elements [170] and CRH expression via a primate-specific THE1B element that modulates gestational timing [171]. These observations highlight the role of TE sequences in establishing placenta-specific endocrine signaling networks.

In addition to regulatory elements, multiple studies have demonstrated that TE-derived protein-coding genes play essential roles in placental development. Retroviral envelope genes such as Syncytin-1 and Syncytin-2 mediate trophoblast cell fusion and are indispensable for syncytiotrophoblast formation [172,173]. Furthermore, domesticated retrotransposon-derived genes such as Peg10 and Peg11/Rtl1 are required for trophoblast differentiation and fetal capillary maintenance, respectively, with genetic disruption leading to embryonic or perinatal lethality [174,175]. These findings indicate that TE-derived coding sequences have been deeply integrated into core developmental processes of the placenta.

Beyond developmental and endocrine functions, TEs also contribute to maternal-fetal immune regulation. TE-derived regulatory elements have been implicated in the control of immune-modulatory genes such as HLA-G at the maternal-fetal interface [176], supporting the notion that TE co-option has facilitated the evolution of immune tolerance mechanisms during pregnancy. This is consistent with the broader view that TE-derived regulatory networks integrate trophoblast differentiation, invasion, vascular remodeling and immune signaling into coordinated placental programs. Together with retroviral envelope genes such as Syncytin [172,173], these findings illustrate how TE-derived DNA, RNA and protein-coding sequences have been integrated into placental lineage programs.

Collectively, these studies support a model in which TEs contribute to placental function through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including domestication of retroviral proteins, provision of alternative promoters, and expansion of enhancer networks. Importantly, these TE-derived components regulate fundamental processes such as syncytiotrophoblast formation, trophoblast invasion, angiogenesis, endocrine signaling and immune tolerance, indicating that TE activity has been evolutionarily repurposed as a central driver of placental biology rather than merely a tolerated byproduct of epigenetic relaxation. As stem cell-based embryo models increasingly recapitulate selected features of peri- and post-implantation embryogenesis, including interactions between embryonic and extraembryonic-like compartments [159,177], continued advances in these systems may further expand opportunities to investigate TE-associated regulation and other molecular mechanisms underlying early human development.

Transposon activation in nervous system

In the nervous system, TE regulation displays both developmental and activity-dependent dimensions. LINE-1 retrotransposition was first demonstrated in neuronal precursor cells, where engineered human LINE-1 elements mobilized during differentiation and generated somatic mosaicism [178,179]. Subsequent high-throughput sequencing analyses identified numerous candidate somatic LINE-1, Alu, and SVA insertions in adult human brain tissues [180], supporting the concept that neuronal genomes may acquire structural diversity during development. Although the frequency and functional consequences of these insertions remain debated, these studies established the principle that TE activity in the brain can extend beyond transcription to genomic integration.

A central emerging framework is that TE activity in neurons operates across multiple mechanistic layers, including somatic genomic diversification, transcriptional regulation, and epigenetic remodeling, rather than being restricted to insertional mutagenesis alone [181]. LINE-1 expression is also dynamically regulated by neuronal activity. Environmental stimulation increases hippocampal LINE-1 RNA and ORF2-associated insertions in mice [182], suggesting that retrotransposition-dependent genomic variation may contribute to memory-related plasticity.

Beyond insertional events, a substantial body of evidence indicates that TE-derived sequences function as cis-regulatory elements in neuronal genomes. TE-derived promoters, enhancers and transcription factor binding sites are widely distributed and contribute to neuron-specific transcriptional programs, including those involved in synaptic function and higher-order brain processes [183,184]. Genome-wide analyses further suggest that a significant fraction of accessible chromatin regions in neuronal tissues overlap with TE-derived sequences, supporting their role as integral components of gene regulatory architecture [184].

The regulation of TE activity is tightly coupled to neuronal epigenetic landscapes. DNA methylation and repressive histone marks such as H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 play central roles in TE silencing, while their dynamic modulation during neurogenesis can lead to transient TE derepression. This suggests that TE activation is not merely stochastic but is developmentally regulated and potentially contributes to lineage transitions and neuronal differentiation programs [181,185].

Conversely, in neurodegenerative disease contexts, elevated retrotransposon transcripts and retrotransposon-derived proteins activate innate immune pathways and contribute to microglial dysfunction and neuroinflammation [186,187]. Mechanistically, retrotransposon-derived nucleic acids can trigger innate immune signaling pathways, including interferon responses, while retrotransposon activation can also induce DNA damage and genome instability [132,188]. In addition, disease-associated factors such as TDP-43 have been shown to regulate retrotransposon expression, linking retrotransposon dysregulation to the molecular pathology of neurodegenerative diseases [189].

Thus, neural TE activation spans a continuum from potentially adaptive genomic diversification to maladaptive inflammatory signaling. In this framework, TEs are best understood as context-dependent components of neuronal gene regulatory networks, contributing both to the evolution of neural complexity and to the pathogenesis of neurological disease.

Transposon activation in aging tissues

In aging tissues, TE activation appears predominantly maladaptive. Age-associated loss of heterochromatin integrity and DNA methylation correlates with increased LINE-1 transcription [190,191]. This age-dependent derepression reflects a progressive erosion of multiple layers of TE silencing, including DNA methylation, H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin, KRAB-KAP1-mediated repression and nuclear lamina-associated constraints, highlighting TE activation as a consequence of global epigenomic instability rather than an isolated event.

In senescent cells, cytoplasmic accumulation of LINE-1-derived cDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway, inducing type I interferon responses and promoting the senescence-associated secretory phenotype [132]. Mechanistically, LINE-1 has emerged as a central driver of this process: loss of the chromatin regulator SIRT6 leads to LINE-1 derepression, accumulation of cytoplasmic cDNA, and activation of innate immune signaling, while pharmacological inhibition of reverse transcriptase suppresses interferon responses and ameliorates progeroid phenotypes [190,192]. Complementary studies further indicate that LINE-1 activation is a late event in cellular senescence, where increased LINE-1 transcription precedes cDNA accumulation and inflammatory signaling; inhibition of LINE-1 activity reduces senescence-associated inflammatory outputs, placing retrotransposon activation upstream of inflammation [132]. Beyond inflammatory signaling, recent work has revealed that LINE-1 RNA itself can directly contribute to chromatin destabilization. In progeroid contexts, LINE-1 transcripts interfere with heterochromatin maintenance, promoting further epigenetic erosion and establishing a feed-forward loop between TE derepression and chromatin instability [193].

In addition to LINE-1, endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) are also reactivated during aging and can contribute to tissue dysfunction. ERV-derived transcripts and particles have been shown to propagate senescence-associated phenotypes and activate innate immune pathways, suggesting that TE-driven aging phenotypes are not restricted to a single TE class but reflect a broader retrotransposon response [194]. Large-scale human transcriptomic analyses further associate retrotransposon expression with biological aging signatures [195]. Notably, TE expression correlates more strongly with biological age than chronological age, and different TE families exhibit distinct associations with cellular pathways, such as DNA repair or immune activation, indicating that TE activity reflects tissue state and functional aging rather than simply time-dependent accumulation [195].

These findings position TE derepression as a contributor to inflammaging and age-related pathology. Collectively, current evidence supports a model in which TE activation is both a consequence and an amplifier of aging: epigenetic erosion permits TE derepression, and TE-derived RNA, cDNA and proteins in turn promote genome instability, chromatin disruption and innate immune activation, thereby accelerating tissue decline.

Comparison between transposon activation in germ and somatic contexts

When compared with these somatic contexts, germ cells exhibit both shared and unique features of TE regulation. Similar to early embryos and placenta, germ cells undergo extensive epigenetic reprogramming, including global DNA demethylation, creating a window of potential TE permissiveness. In fetal male germ cells, transient TE transcription coincides with epigenomic remodeling. However, germ cells uniquely deploy small RNA-based genome-defense mechanisms, most prominently the piRNA pathway, and reestablish TE silencing through de novo DNA methylation and H3K9me3 deposition. Thus, whereas early embryogenesis and placental development integrate TE activity into regulatory innovation, and the brain and aging tissues experience TE activation with mixed or deleterious consequences, the germline imposes stringent sequence-specific repression following transient activation to safeguard heritable genome integrity.

Across development and aging, several common principles emerge. TE activation frequently coincides with epigenetic permissiveness; TE-derived transcripts can function independently of retrotransposition as regulators of chromatin or transcription; and innate immune pathways act as secondary checkpoints when TE-derived nucleic acids accumulate. However, the functional outcomes of TE activation differ profoundly depending on lineage context. Early embryos and placenta illustrate TE co-option and developmental integration, the nervous system reveals a balance between plasticity and pathology, aging tissues demonstrate the inflammatory consequences of derepression, and germ cells highlight the need for robust re-silencing to protect heritable genome integrity. Understanding how these contexts differentially balance permissiveness, co-option, and defense will be central to elucidating the overarching logic of TE regulation across the mammalian lifespan.

Future perspectives

Recent advances have reframed TEs from purely genomic threats to dynamic components of gene regulatory systems. In mammalian germ cells, multilayered repression mechanisms—including chromatin-based silencing, DNA methylation, and small RNA pathways—coordinate to manage TE activity during epigenetic reprogramming. A central challenge for the field will be to determine how distinct TE families are selectively controlled and how transient TE activation is integrated into developmental gene regulation.

Beyond genome defense, systematic characterization of TE transcription across development, aging, and disease may reveal molecular signatures that inform drug discovery and precision medicine. Emerging stem cell-based human embryo models may further expand the developmental contexts in which TE-associated regulation can be examined, particularly in early human embryonic and extraembryonic lineages. At the same time, the convergence of TE biology with genome engineering and epigenome editing raises the possibility that TE-derived regulatory modules could be harnessed to direct cell fate transitions. Such advances may ultimately reposition TEs as programmable elements of genome regulation and developmental engineering.
