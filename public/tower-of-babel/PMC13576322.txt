Impact of altered HDL2b/HDL3 ratio on the function of innate immune cells in sepsis
Authors: Shubi Zhao, Yichun Jiang, Puqiao Lian, Chaoguang Ji, Zewei Huang, Xueyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis is characterized by dysregulated immunologic reactions to infectious insults, representing a main cause of death for seriously ill patients. Studies have demonstrated correlations between rapidly declined high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels during initial sepsis phases with increased mortality and adverse clinical outcomes. Furthermore, HDL subcomponents, specifically HDL2b and HDL3, undergo significant alterations in composition in sepsis patients. This article was aimed at investigating the effects of alterations in the ratio of HDL2b to HDL3 (HDL2b/3) on the function of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and monocyte-macrophages (THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells).MethodsPlasma samples were collected from 10 sepsis patients and 10 healthy controls. High-density lipoprotein (HDL) subclasses (HDL2b and HDL3) were separated and quantified using fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) and ELISA. Human monocytic leukemia cells (THP-1) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were co-incubated with different reconstituted ratios of HDL2b/3 under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation to evaluate cell viability, inflammatory cytokine expression, angiogenesis, and cell migration.ResultsOur results showcase evidently reduced HDL2b/3 ratios for the sepsis group compared with their non-sepsis counterparts. Notably, HDL2b/3 from both groups attenuated the gene expression of lipopolysaccharide-stimulated inflammatory factors (interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α) within THP-1 cells, RAW264.7 cells, and HUVECs. Additionally, HDL2b/3 mitigated the detrimental effects of LPS on THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells.ConclusionThis research highlights that altered HDL subcomponent ratios can potentially modulate inflammation and cellular responses during sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03005-1.

Background

Being a primary reason responsible for deaths of individuals admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), sepsis refers to dysfunction of various organs as a consequence of dysregulated host immunologic reactions upon infections. Multi-organ function impairment caused by sepsis poses a huge social and economic burden [1–3]. Endothelial changes associated with sepsis affect bacterial spread and coordinate leukocyte recruitment and bacterial clearance [4]. The combined disruption of the endothelial and epithelial barriers is a key mechanism responsible for widespread and fatal organ dysfunction. In sepsis, injury to endothelial cells, which serve as the critical tissue-blood barrier and regulatory hub of microcirculation, constitutes the core mechanism of multiple organ failure [5]. Pathogens (e.g., bacterial lipopolysaccharide [LPS]) activate the NF-κB pathway in endothelial cells via TLR4, and inflammatory mediators released by monocytes-macrophages further amplify this activation effect [6]. This leads to degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx and disrupts adhesion proteins such as VE-cadherin, causing increased vascular permeability and tissue edema. Enhanced adhesion protein (i.e., VCAM-1 and ICAM-1) expression recruits inflammatory cells to exacerbate local damage, and exposure to procoagulant factor (tissue factor [TF]) coupled with an imbalance of the anticoagulant factor (thrombomodulin [TM]) induces microthrombi, obstructing microcirculation. Ultimately, the combination of insufficient organ perfusion, ischemia-hypoxia, and inflammatory injury drives the failure of various organs, e.g. the heart, kidneys and lungs [7].

Sepsis is considered a biphasic condition. During the initial period of high inflammation, which is often termed “cytokine storm,” the hyperactivation of natural immunity leads to excessive inflammatory mediator production, possibly causing tissue damage. After this peak period, the immunoreactions are attenuated, leading to natural immunity hypoactivation. Consequently, the depletion of systemic immunity occurs, and both lymphocytes and myeloid cells die, leading to immunocompromised patients, although both phases may occur simultaneously [8]. LPS comes from gram-negative bacteria outer cell walls, which are mainly composed of lipids and polysaccharides. LPS causes monocytes-macrophages to produce plentiful cytokines [9]. As reported previously, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) could bind with LPS to suppress inflammation, thereby playing a protective role in preventing excessive inflammatory responses during sepsis [10]. The binding between LPS and lipoproteins is highly specific; however, studies have shown that LPS preferentially binds to HDL over other lipoproteins. Lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) is an LPS-binding molecule within plasma (and is primarily associated with HDL). In the absence of lipoproteins, LPS will physically interact with CD14 located on immune cell plasma membrane, thereby activating these cells; however, when lipoproteins are present, the physical interaction between HDL and LPS is promoted by LBP, which inactivates LPS and facilitates its transport to the liver [11]. Nevertheless, the mechanism explaining how HDL interacts with LPS remains elusive. However, studies have demonstrated that Apo-AI knockout mice exhibit a reduced ability to neutralize LPS and are more susceptible to sepsis-induced death [12]. Phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP), an LBP family member, can bind to and neutralize LPS, thereby suppressing LPS-induced cellular inflammatory responses [13]. Proteomic studies have revealed that PLTP is more highly expressed in HDL3 [14].

HDL is the major protective lipoprotein particles in the serum, with protective effects such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and reversely transporting cholesterols [15]. Dramatic HDL (including its subfractions HDL2b and HDL3) function and composition alterations have been observed among sepsis patients, and these alterations may induce pathological processes, such as sepsis immune dysfunction and endothelial cell dysfunction, which ultimately affect the prognosis of the patients [16]. While regulating monocyte-macrophage immune responses, HDL also regulates the function of endothelial cells (ECs), which are the first cells in the circulatory system to be exposed to metabolism-related endogenous danger signals. In recent years, ECs have been regarded as conditioned innate immune cells [17]. Upon LPS stimulation, ECs release cytokines that exert biological effects [18, 19]. Endothelial cell dysfunction is one of the pathologic mechanisms leading to sepsis-associated damage of various organs, and according to recently published papers, there appears to be a clear correlation between HDL and endothelial cell dysfunction [20, 21]. Scientists found that after co-incubation with HDL, isolated macrophages enhanced bacterial phagocytosis and that mice with higher levels of recombinant HDL cleared bacterial infections more effectively [22].

The increasing rates of gram-negative bacterial resistance to a wide range of commonly used antimicrobials have raised concerns regarding the choice of antibiotic therapy [23, 24]. In clinical practice, antibiotic selection is mostly based on experience, which may increase the risk of death owing to inappropriate initial antibiotic therapy. Therefore, the identification of effective therapeutic agents remains challenging. HDL’s function in regulating immunoreactions in sepsis and, consequently, whether HDL can be utilized to treat the disease have attracted intensive research interest recently [25]. Previous studies have found that sepsis can lead to HDL dysfunction and its subfraction compositional variations [10]. HDL exerts a protective function during sepsis pathology, and its subfractions, such as HDL2b and HDL3, may contribute differently to the protective effects against sepsis. HDL achieves anti-inflammatory effects by binding with LPS, which plays a protective role in preventing excessive inflammatory responses during sepsis [19]. Decreased HDL abundance in plasma could stimulate inflammatory responses and activate suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1), which may be related to sepsis severity and poor prognosis. In contrast, it seems that sepsis prognosis and severity are more significantly affected by the abundance of HDL2b, rather than HDL3 [26]. HDL particles are highly heterogeneous and can be divided based on density into subclasses: HDL2 (lower-density, lipid-rich, including HDL2a and HDL2b) and HDL3 (higher-density, protein-rich, including HDL3a, 3b, and 3c). Structurally, HDL2b represents larger, more mature particles carrying abundant lipids, whereas HDL3 consists of smaller, denser particles with unique proteomic profiles, such as higher expression of phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP) [27].

The effects of HDL on EC function and innate immune cells such as monocytes-macrophages have been widely studied in cardiovascular diseases, but its effects on EC damage and monocyte-macrophage activation in sepsis are just beginning to be investigated [28–30]. Based on our previous study, which found that the ratio of HDL2b to HDL3 (HDL2b/3) was significantly decreased in sepsis patients, this study further explored how inflammatory mediator secretion from ECs and monocyte-macrophages is affected by HDL2b/3, as well as its protective effects, to lay the foundation for the subsequent development of the application of HDL2b/3 in sepsis treatment.

Materials and methods

Sepsis patient enrollment and sample collection

Inclusion criteria for sepsis patients: Aged 18–80 years; sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2 with doubtful symptoms indicating infections; meeting the diagnostic criteria within Sepsis 3.0; signed an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria for sepsis patients: sepsis patients who died within 24 h of enrollment; pregnant women; patients with organ transplantation; patients who recently received high-dose steroid therapy; cancer patients who recently received adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected patients; and those affected by hematologic cancers.

Inclusion criteria for healthy individuals: Aged 18–80 years; no history of sepsis or suspected infection symptoms; SOFA score < 2; normal clinical indicators for blood lipid and blood routine tests and kidney and liver functions; signed informed consent form; and ability to cooperate with blood sample collection. Exclusion criteria for healthy individuals: Meeting Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria, or suspected infection; history of organ transplantation, hematological malignancy, or HIV infection; recent receipt of high-dose steroid therapy, adjuvant radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; pregnancy; or severe underlying diseases affecting HDL metabolism or immune function.

Following inclusion of eligible individuals, blood samples were collected from them within 24 h since their admission to ICUs. Patient age, gender, basic medical history, disease diagnosis, SOFA score, APACHE II score, and other basic clinical data were recorded.

Sample collection

Four milliliters of blood was taken from ICU-admitted sepsis patients and healthy volunteers into EDTA tubes, from which plasma samples were instantly acquired through a 10-min 3000 rpm centrifugation and kept inside − 80 °C freezers.

Microfluidic chip

Plasma was collected from ICU sepsis (n = 40) and non-sepsis patients (n = 12) and sent to Guangdong Ardent BioMed Co., Ltd. for the detection of HDL. For analysis of plasma HDL3 and HDL2b components, we employed a microfluidic chip.

Cell culture

Human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1, Raw264.7 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were respectively acquired from ATCC, Wuhan Servicebio Company, and Shanghai Cellverse Company. HUVECs were maintained in endothelial cell-specific medium (ScienCell), whereas THP-1 and Raw264.7 were maintained respectively within RPMI 1640 (HyClone) and DMEM (Gibco) media supplemented with 1% streptomycin/penicillin and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). A humidified incubator filled with 5% CO2 was set to 37 °C for maintenance of all the three cell lines.

HDL fraction isolation and HDL2b/3 measurement

Blood from healthy volunteers (n = 10) and sepsis patient (n = 10) collected in EDTA-containing anticoagulant tubes was used for HDL extraction as follows: centrifuge at 4 °C, 3200 r/min for 15 min, extract plasma from 0.5 mL/EP tube of each plasma (with protease inhibitor added at 5 µL/tube), and freeze at -80 °C in a freezer until fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) separation [31].

HDL2b, HDL3, and HDL density measurement: The plasma was separated on a Superdex 200 increase 10/300 GL chromatography column connected in series with an AKTA advant automated protein purification system. After filtration through a filter, it was washed and separated with standard Tris buffer (0.3 mL/min flow rate) [30]. Fractions 10.9 to 12.9 were harvested and freezed inside − 80 °C freezers. To quantify individual subfractions, commercially available human ELISA kits (Tianjin Annuoruiang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) specific for HDL2b and HDL3 were utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absolute mass concentrations or particle densities of HDL2b (CHDL2b) and HDL3 (CHDL3) within the collected fractions were determined based on standard curves. The specific ratio was calculated as HDL2b/3 = CHDL2b/ CHDL3.

To account for inter-individual biological variations, we utilized distinct individual samples rather than pooled plasma for the cell incubation assays. For each designated HDL2b/3 ratio subgroup (healthy controls: C-0.4, C-0.5, C-0.6; sepsis patients: S-0.4, S-0.5, S-0.6), three individual samples were randomly selected from the respective cohorts. All subsequent in vitro cell treatments were performed as three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Effect of HDL on endothelial cell and monocyte function in sepsis and healthy volunteers

1 × 106 THP-1/RAW264.7/HUVECs were seeded in 60 mm dishes and co-cultured for 24 h with HDL2b/3 from the sepsis group or HDL2b/3 from the healthy group in the presence (8 µg/mL) or absence (0.8 µg/mL) of LPS, respectively. The mRNA abundances of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin (IL)-1β were detected through qRT-PCR (Bio-Rad, USA) with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene for relative quantification (Supplementary Methods and Table S1).

Effect of HDL2b/3 on THP-1 cell, RAW264.7 cell, and HUVEC viability

THP-1, Raw264.7 and HUVECs were respectively inoculated into 96-well plates at densities of 1 × 104, 6 × 103, and 1 × 104 cells per well. The cells were subjected to stimulation with various concentratons of LPS, with THP−1 cells being additionally treated with or without HDL2b/3. Twenty-four (for THP-1, Raw264.7 and HUVECs) or 48 h (for HUVECs) later, 100 µL of freshly prepared pre-warmed serum-free cell culture media containing 10% CCK-8 was supplemented. Following a 4-h, 50-min and 4-h incubation at 37 °C for THP-1, Raw264.7 and HUVECs, respectively, the 450-nm optical density (OD) values of each well were recorded.

Macrophage polarization assay

To investigate the M1/M2 phenotype of RAW264.7 cells, relevant cytokines were assayed. LPS was used to induce a basal inflammatory state and mimic an inflammatory infection. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into six-well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 1 day. Then RAW 264.7 cells were then co-incubated with various materials including HDL2b/3 (5 ug/mL) and LPS (0.8 ug/mL) for 1 day. Cellular RNA was collected and detected by qRT-PCR for IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS (Supplementary Methods and Table S1).

Effect of HDL2b/3 on HUVEC tube-forming assay

Matrigel™ was stood at 4°C for 12 h. Then, in a 96-well plate (ice box operation), added 50 µL of Matrigel™ per well and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h to form a gel. 6 × 104 HUVECs were inoculated per well, and then different HDL2b/3 ratios were added (control group [without any treatment], C-0.4, C-0.5, C-0.6, S-0.4, S-0.5, and S-0.6). And these plates were transferred to a 37 °C cell incubator and incubated for 12 h to form tubes. The formed tubes were imaged using an inverted optical microscope, and the images were quantified using ImageJ software.

In vitro cell migration

To investigate the effects of HDL2b/3 on the migration and repair capabilities of HUVECs, Inoculated into six-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ HUVECs per well. The experiment was initiated when cells formed a confluent monolayer. Using a pipette tip, scratches were made parallel or perpendicular to the horizontal lines on the back of the six-well plate, with reference to the plate lid or a ruler for alignment. After two washes with phosphate-buffered saline, HDL2b/3 from each group was added for co-incubation. At 0, 24, 48,and 72 h, the wells were observed under a microscope, images were captured, and the scratch gap area (denoted as A) was calculated. Statistical methods

Statistical analyses of all measurement data were carried out with Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance, with the results represented by mean ± standard deviation (p < 0.05).

Results

Separation of HDL by FPLC and calculation of HDL2b/3

Baseline observables and plasma samples were collected from 10 sepsis patients (sepsis group) and 10 healthy volunteers (healthy group). The data for the basic observation indicators are listed in Table 1. According to previous literature, plasma was separated by FPLC to obtain fractions 10.9–12.9, which were HDL particles (Fig. 1A-B) [31]. Based on their density, HDL particles can be divided into two major subclasses: HDL2 is a lower-density, lipid-rich HDL that includes HDL2a and HDL2b, and HDL3 is a higher-density, protein-rich HDL that includes HDL3a, HDL3c, and HDL3b [32, 33]. These heterogeneous particles exhibit various biological activities [34]. The purified HDL particle size range was 9.41 ± 0.81 nm as measured by dynamic light scattering (Fig. 1C-D).

Our clinical study found that the mean HDL2b to HDL3 density ratio (HDL2b/3) in the plasma of ICU sepsis patients was 0.664, which was significantly lower than that in the plasma of non-sepsis patients (1.381) (Fig. 1E). To further explore the functions of different HDL2b/3 ratios, we first calculated the density of HDL, HDL2b, and HDL3 in each purified sample by ELISA and then calculated the HDL2b/3 ratio. The results revealed that the HDL2b/3 ratios of the two groups of samples were distributed between 0.4 and 0.6 (Fig. 1F). Next, according to the level of HDL2b/3, we further grouped the extracted HDL (healthy group: C-0.4, C-0.5, and C-0.6, sepsis group: S-0.4, S-0.5, and S-0.6) and investigated the effects of different HDL2b/3 ratios on innate immune cells in the healthy and sepsis groups.

Effect of HDL2b/3 on monocyte-macrophage and endothelial cell viability

The CCK-8 assay revealed that LPS inhibited the proliferation of THP-1 cells in a dose-dependent manner after 1 day of co-culture (Fig. 2A). However, at very high concentrations of LPS, HUVECs were highly resistant to LPS for only a short period (24 and 48 h) (Fig. 2C, D). When RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS, a proliferation effect was observed (Fig. 2E, F). Therefore, we assessed only the effect of HDL2b/3 on THP-1 cell viability. Under the co-stimulation of higher concentration of LPS (80 µg/ml) and different HDL2b/3 groups (8 µg/mL), the THP-1 cells in each group had similar proliferative ability at 24 h, indicating that HDL2b/3 in the sepsis group still had the ability to maintain cell viability (Fig. 2B).

Anti-inflammatory effects of HDL2b/3 on THP-1 cells, RAW264.7 cells, and HUVECs

To evaluate the effects of various HDL2b/3 ratios, we detected the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in THP-1 cells by qRT-PCR with various ratios of HDL2b/3. The results revealed that HDL2b/3 downregulated TNF-α and IL-1β in both the healthy and sepsis groups compared with the LPS group. Importantly, there was no statistically significant difference between the anti-inflammatory efficacy of HDL2b/3 isolated from healthy controls versus that from sepsis patients (Fig. 3). All ratios of HDL2b/3 downregulated the mRNA expression of TNF-α by THP-1 cells, but there was no statistical significance between the different HDL2b/3 ratios (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, unlike sepsis groups, higher HDL2b/3 ratios in healthy groups were associated with a greater ability to downregulate IL-1β in THP-1 cells (Fig. 3B). The anti-inflammatory effect of HDL2b/3 on RAW264.7 cells was similar between the sepsis group and healthy controls, whereas the S-0.6 group exhibited the best inhibition of TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 3C-D). LPS stimulation of RAW264.7 cells significantly increased the expression of IL-6. After treatment with HDL2b/3 from the healthy group, the expression of IL-6 gradually decreased as the ratio of HDL2b/3 increased. HDL2b/3 from the sepsis group showed a similar trend but exhibited a higher expression of IL-6 (Fig. 3E), and the expression of iNOS exhibited a trend similar to that of IL-6 (Fig. 3F).

We also determined the expression levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in HUVECs by qRT-PCR under treatment with HDL2b/3 at different ratios. Data indicated that, in contrast to the LPS group, none of the HDL2b/3 subfractions from the sepsis group exhibited a suppressive effect on TNF-α and IL-1β expression in HUVECs (Fig. 4). However, in the healthy group, C-0.5 significantly downregulated TNF-α expression in LPS-stimulated HUVECs, while C-0.6 induced a significant reduction in IL-1β expression under the same conditions (Fig. 4). These results suggest that HDL2b/3 in the healthy group inhibited the mRNA expression of proinflammatory factors by HUVECs better than that in the sepsis group, but there was no evidence that this anti-inflammatory capacity was related to the value of HDL2b/3.

Effect of HDL2b/3 on the tube-forming effect of HUVECs

As shown in Fig. 5, LPS significantly inhibited HUVEC expression of platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Compared with that in the LPS group, the expression of PDGF and VEGF in HUVECs co-incubated with HDL2b/3 (8 µg/mL) was comparable to that in the control group. Surprisingly, PDGF and VEGF expression was significantly upregulated in the healthy group, a trend that correlated with the value of HDL2b/3.

Conclusion and discussion

Sepsis, with its high morbidity, mortality, and consumption of healthcare resources, has been regarded as a global health threat [35]. The epidemiology 2020 report showed that, in 2017, approximately tens of million cases of sepsis accounting for 20% of all global deaths occurred [36]. With the prevalence of multidrug-resistant “superbugs,” existing clinical treatments are in a difficult situation.

Sepsis is driven by immune dyshomeostasis, marked by concurrent hyper-inflammation and immunosuppression. For most infections, the host’s intrinsic immune system can clear pathogens by promoting an inflammatory response, including the cytokines and chemokines. However, in patients with sepsis, the host immune system becomes dysfunctional and fails to clear pathogens, causing profound activation of the intrinsic immune system, leading to excessive inflammatory responses and immunosuppression [37]. The structure and function of HDL are dramatically altered during inflammation and infection, disrupting normal cholesterol efflux capacity which is otherwise highly efficient in healthy controls. Notably, it is primarily the smaller, denser HDL subclasses (such as HDL3) that are responsible for acquiring free cholesterol from peripheral cells [38].

This study showed that HDL2b/3 was downregulated in the sepsis group. All HDL2b/3 reversed the proliferation inhibition of THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells induced by LPS. Among the inflammatory cytokines during sepsis, IL-1β secreted by immune cells after activation of inflammatory vesicles is crucial. Clinical researchers have found that patients who die from sepsis have higher levels of IL-1β than survivors, and more IL-1β are related to poor outcome in sepsis [39]. In vitro experiments showed that HDL2b/3 in both sepsis and healthy groups were able to downregulate LPS-induced IL-1β expression in innate immune cells. In addition, the greater the value of HDL2b/3 in healthy controls, the lower the transcription of inflammatory factor IL-1β mRNA. In inflammatory mechanisms, TNF-α produced by innate immune cells plays a significant role in infiltrating the site of infection and triggering pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion [40, 41]. Our data showed that HDL2b/3 from both the healthy and sepsis groups downregulated the transcription of TNF-α in THP-1 cells and RAW264.7 cells. In contrast, all HDL2b/3 from the healthy group downregulated the transcription of IL-6 and iNOS. Sepsis treatment generally requires the application of large amounts of antibiotics; however, the accompanying inflammatory response induces severe organ damage. Therefore, attention should be paid to the occurrence of a cytokine storm during the early stage of treatment of infectious diseases, which is important for the prevention of sepsis [42]. Although the effect of HDL on innate immune cells has been well described, the HDL subfractions has not been well studied [43–45]. The above data support that HDL2b/3 retains good anti-inflammatory activity in sepsis group but is not statistically different from healthy controls.

Endothelial cells play crucial roles during infection and sepsis [46]. Similar to monocyte-macrophages, endothelial cells act as sentinels when an invading pathogen or microbial infection is sensed [47]. Microvascular endothelial cells at the sites of inflammation are both active participants and regulators of the inflammatory process during the transition from acute to chronic inflammation and from innate to adaptive immunity [48, 49]. Endothelial cell homeostasis is closely linked to the development of sepsis and is therefore a major target for therapy [50]. Endothelial cells are both targets of inflammatory action and sources of pro-inflammatory factors that link local and systemic immune responses [50]. Endothelial cells and monocyte-macrophages lie at the intersection of the immune-inflammatory-coagulation network and serve as key nodes linking various pathological processes [51]. Studying these two systems allows for a comprehensive understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying sepsis, rather than merely focusing on a single pathway or molecule. Endothelial cell injury and monocyte-macrophage activation are early events in sepsis pathophysiology and play a decisive role in subsequent inflammatory amplification, microthrombus formation, and organ damage [52]. Early intervention in these two processes may block disease progression and prevent multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Compared to other pathological changes, endothelial dysfunction and monocyte-macrophage dysregulation may exhibit higher reversibility in the early stages of the disease, providing a valuable time window for clinical intervention [53, 54]. Biomarkers of endothelial injury and monocyte-macrophage activation are closely related to severity of illness and clinical outcomes in sepsis patients. In the present study, we showed that HDL2b/3 inhibited the mRNA expression of IL-1β and TNF-α by HUVECs in healthy individuals, (e.g., C-0.6), which was different from the sepsis group. HDL may attenuate inflammatory responses by influencing EC activation and maintaining the integrity of the endothelial barrier by activating eNOS [54]. Tube-forming experiments have shown that HDL2b/3 from the healthy group more significantly promotes angiogenesis and growth factor expression, such as enhanced expression of PDGF and VEGF [37]. In vitro cell migration assays also showed that HDL2b/3 from the healthy group more significantly promoted the migration of endothelial cells, which was conducive to endothelial repair. This suggests that more attention should be paid to the effects of HDL2b/3 alterations on sepsis-related vascular injury and repair. Restoration of endothelial function is of great importance in the prognosis of sepsis [38].

In summary, there is currently no gold standard for the measurement of HDL2b and HDL3, and individual differences affect the purification purity of HDL. HDL contains hundreds of bioactive substances, and it remains unclear whether one or more of these substances determine HDL2b/3. Additionally, the manner in which changes in HDL2b/3 affect inflammatory signaling pathways and the specific mechanisms by which it acts on endothelial cells are still not fully understood. In vitro experiments, we explored the effects of HDL2b/3 on individual immune cells and obtained relevant data. However, in vivo, extremely complex signaling interactions exist between immune cells and between immune cells and other cell types that cannot be replicated in in vitro experimental settings. Given that research on HDL2b/3 is still in its preliminary stages, we did not perform in vivo experiments in this study, which limited our understanding of HDL2b/3. Additionally, it should be noted that RAW264.7 is a murine macrophage cell line, which possesses inherent physiological differences from human-derived THP-1 cells. Although similar anti-inflammatory trends were observed in both cell lines in our study, cross-species discrepancies should be interpreted with caution [55]. Although this study generated a certain amount of clinical data and experimental findings, the impact of HDL2b/3 on the prognosis of sepsis patients remains unclear. The observed effects may not be generalizable to other populations or may require studies with larger sample sizes for validation. In the future, we will integrate multicenter data to amass more data and balance the age differences between groups through stratified sampling (e.g., age-stratified matching) or multiple regression adjustments to enhance the statistical power and reliability of the results. Furthermore, whether HDL2b/3 can serve as a diagnostic indicator of sepsis needs to be validated in more animal experiments and clinical trials, which will be our primary research objective moving forward.
