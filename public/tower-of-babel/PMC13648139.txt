Construction of a musculoskeletal job-exposure matrix in Korea
Authors: Myong-Hwan Kim, Eun-Soo Lee, Dongmug Kang, Ki-Hun Kim, Youngki Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a major occupational health issue in Korea; however, a standardized job-exposure matrix (JEM) has not been established. This study aimed to develop an MSD-specific JEM using data from the Korean Working Conditions Survey (KWCS) to evaluate the exposure time proportion by job category and body segment.MethodsData from the 2nd to 6th KWCS (2006–2020), covering 210,500 workers, were analyzed. The jobs of the subjects were reclassified based on the unit groups of the 7th Korean Standard Classification of Occupations. Exposure time proportion for key MSD risk factors—repetitive movements, awkward postures, heavy lifting, and prolonged standing—was categorized as high, moderate, or low based on self-reported responses. Validity was assessed through expert review and statistical analysis, including Cohen’s kappa coefficients and logistic regression.ResultsDuration-based exposure to musculoskeletal (MS) risk factors was estimated for each occupation. The JEM classified exposure time proportion for three body regions: upper extremity and neck, lower back, and lower extremity. Expert review showed high agreement for the upper extremity and neck and for the lower back (kappa > 0.8), whereas agreement was lower for the lower extremity. Logistic regression analysis indicated an exposure–response relationship between JEM-derived exposure time proportion and MS symptoms for the upper extremity and neck and the lower back. ConclusionsThis study developed Korea’s first MS-specific JEM, providing a standardized framework for occupational MSD risk assessment. The JEM demonstrated strong validity for most body regions and can be a valuable tool for research, workplace risk evaluation, and policy development. Further refinements incorporating detailed job classifications and ergonomic factors are recommended to enhance its applicability.

BACKGROUND

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) account for more than 60% of occupational diseases in Korea.1,2 Although the approval rate for the recognition of occupational diseases has recently been increasing in Korea, disputes about the work-relatedness of MSDs still occur. These disputes occur because MSDs are characteristically associated with a diverse set of factors, including individual, ergonomic, and psychosocial factors. Another reason underlying these disputes is the difficulty in making a quantitative assessment of exposure leading to the disorder because a wide range of exposures occur, depending on the type and nature of the occupation.

Job exposure matrices (JEMs) have played an important role in assessing exposure to risk factors.3 Therefore, JEMs have been constructed for various hazardous factors such as asbestos,4-7 noise,8,9 job stress,10,11 and solvents.12,13 These kinds of occupational risk factors do not universally exist in all industries, and the diseases caused by such hazardous factors in the working environment are limited. However, MSDs can occur in almost all types of industries and occupations, and there are various types of musculoskeletal (MS) conditions, making it difficult to construct an MSD-specific JEM. As a result, attempts to construct a JEM for MSDs were not made until 2010. One representative MSD-focused JEM is the Comparison Between a Self-Reported Job Exposure Matrix (CONSTANCES JEM), developed in France.14 This JEM consists of 407 job codes and 27 occupational and ergonomic factors. In Finland, a JEM for workers living in Helsinki has been proposed, and it comprises 40 job codes and the following three types of factors: physical effort, heavy lifting, and carrying.15 In addition, a JEM built in the Netherlands16 and a JEM using data from the Occupational Information Network (O*NET) in the United States17 have been presented, and there was also a prior study to construct a JEM limited to hip and knee arthritis.18 However, the existing JEMs available in these countries are limited by region or occupation, or specifically constructed for certain diseases in particular occupations, limiting their utility across all job types.

In Korea, it was possible to construct JEMs for occupational exposures to asbestos, benzene, and trichloroethylene because relevant data, such as measurement of exposure in the work environment and associated research, were available. On the other hand, in work-related MSDs, there was no data on the types of exposure in different types of industries and occupations. Since the initiation of the Korean Working Conditions Survey (KWCS) conducted by the Occupational Safety and Health Research Institute (OSHRI) every 3 years since 2006 to monitor changes in the working conditions of Koreans, it has become possible to identify risk factors for MSDs across industries. However, no attempt has yet been made to construct a JEM for MSDs for Korean workers. Moreover, although the assessments of occupational burden have been made for individual tasks or occupations, such assessments have not yet been made for all types of occupations. Therefore, this study describes the development of an MS JEM, constructed using KWCS data as a basic resource for assessing musculoskeletal burden, and we have already published a paper19 that used this JEM to analyze the effects of combined MS-related exposures.

METHODS

Data sources and study population

The KWCS has been carried out six times since 2006. This survey was developed based on the European Working Conditions Survey, and it is conducted triennially to examine the overall working environment, including the type of work, employment, occupation, industry, exposure to risk factors, and employment stability, among workers aged 15 years or older in Korea. In this study, data from the 2nd to 6th KWCSs were used. Data from the 1st KWCS, which used a different occupational classification system, were excluded. A total of 210,500 people were selected as subjects, excluding people whose occupations were difficult to classify. The subjects included 9,991 participants of the 2nd KWCS, 49,957 participants of the 3rd KWCS, 49,905 participants of the 4th KWCS, 50,110 participants of the 5th KWCS, and 50,537 participants of the 6th KWCS.

Job classification

In this study, the job classification was based on the Korean Standard Classification of Occupations (KSCO) developed by Statistics Korea. Initially, the KWCS data categorized occupations using either the unit group or the detailed occupational categories of the 6th KSCO. Subsequently, for this study, these classifications were updated to align with the unit group categories of the 7th KSCO, the updated version of which was published in 2017.

Ergonomic risk factors for the upper extremity/neck, lower back, and lower extremity

To assess the risk factors related to work-related MSDs, questionnaire items addressing local vibration as a physical risk factor and other ergonomic factors were used. To identify MS risk factors related to the upper extremity and neck, the questions included the following: (1) vibrations from hand tools or machines, (2) postures that cause fatigue or pain, and (3) repetitive hand or arm movements. The vibration item in KWCS reflects hand-arm vibration from tools/machines and was included primarily to capture upper-extremity exposure within the upper extremity and neck domain; it is not interpreted as evidence of a neck-specific causal relationship. To identify MS risk factors associated with the lower back, the questions included the following: (1) lifting, pushing, or carrying heavy objects, (2) posture that causes fatigue or pain, and (3) lifting or moving people. The MS risk factors of the lower extremity were examined using questions related to (1) lifting, pushing, or moving heavy objects and (2) prolonged standing posture. The exposure time proportion was measured on a 7-point scale. The duration of exposure to risk factors was classified as ‘high,’ ‘moderate,’ or ‘low’ exposure. When the exposure encompassed the entire working hours or most or 3/4th of the working hours, the responses were classified as ‘high exposure,’ and when the duration of exposure was 1/2 or 1/4th of the total working hours, the responses were classified as ‘moderate exposure.’ When the subjects responded, ‘rarely exposed/never exposed’, these cases were categorized as ‘low exposure.’

MS symptoms

MS symptoms were examined based on responses to questions about the presence or absence of upper extremity and neck pain, lower back pain, and lower extremity pain.

Exposure time proportion to MS risk factors by body segment and job categories

The exposure items from the questionnaire were assigned to each body region—specifically, three items for the upper extremity and neck, three items for the lower back, and two items for the lower extremity—based on the evidence from the Lower body JEM and National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (1997) study.18,20 These responses were aggregated to determine each participant’s exposure time proportion category for the corresponding body region. When one or more responses were classified as high exposure, the body-region exposure time proportion was classified as high. When two or more responses were classified as moderate exposure, the body-region exposure time proportion was classified as moderate. When all responses were low, or when only one response was moderate and the remaining responses were low, the body-region exposure time proportion was classified as low.

For each KSCO unit group, we generated a three-level occupation-level JEM (Supplementary Table 1). To construct this JEM, we calculated the proportions of workers classified as High, Moderate, and Low at the individual level for each body region (pHigh and pLow; reported in Supplementary Table 2). We applied a deterministic rule: an occupation was classified as High if pHigh ≥ Th, classified as Low if pLow ≥ Tl, and classified as Moderate otherwise. Because the KWCS exposure item sets differ by body region (in both number and construct), the occupation-level distributions of pHigh and pLow are inherently region-specific. Therefore, body-region–specific Th/Tl values (upper extremity/neck 60%/50%, lower back 40%/55%, lower extremity 40%/70%) were determined by research-team consensus after reviewing region-specific pHigh/pLow distributions and interpretability considerations to maintain a usable three-level classification within each body region. These thresholds were then fixed and applied deterministically to Supplementary Table 2 to generate Supplementary Table 1. We assessed robustness by varying Th and Tl by ±5 percentage points for each region; classifications were generally stable (minimum agreement: 0.81–0.87), with changes concentrated near the decision thresholds.

Validity of JEM

To assess the validity of the estimated exposure time proportion by job categories using the constructed JEM, two ergonomists were provided with the survey questions, job titles, and job descriptions as indicated by the KSCO and were asked to assess the exposure time proportion for each body segment. For the items where the evaluations of the two ergonomists were not in agreement, a consensus was derived through discussion. Then, the level of agreement between the assessment results of the two ergonomists and the estimates of exposure time proportion from the JEM was analyzed, and a kappa coefficient value of 0.8 or greater was considered to indicate a very high level of agreement.21 Additionally, a logistic regression analysis was performed to determine whether there was an exposure-response relationship between exposure time proportion by job category and MS symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and the chi-square test, t-test, logistic regression analysis, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient analysis were performed.

Ethics statement

This study was approved for an exemption from ethical review by the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital, given the use of anonymized KWCS data (IRB No. 55-2024-108; approved on August 22, 2024).

General characteristics of the subjects

The general characteristics of the subjects are shown in Table 1. The 40–49 and 50–59 age groups accounted for the largest proportions of the subjects. The mean age of the subjects was 48.4 years, and the mean ages of the males and females were 48.1 years and 48.8 years, respectively.

Distributions of exposure time proportion by question

The results of the exposure time proportion for each question in the questionnaire are shown in Table 2. The standing posture and repetitive hand or arm movements were associated with the highest proportions of high exposure, while hand tool or machine vibrations, and activities involving lifting, pushing, or moving heavy objects, were linked to lower proportions of high exposure.

Distributions of exposure time proportion by body segment

In terms of exposure time proportion by body region, the proportion of high exposure was greatest for the upper extremity and neck, followed by the lower extremity and the lower back (Table 3).

Validity of the JEM for MSDs

As described above, two ergonomics experts were asked to evaluate the exposure time proportion by occupation and body segment, and the exposure time proportion was determined by reaching a consensus through discussion. For items where there was disagreement between the two ergonomists, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to compare the results of the evaluation of the exposure time proportion by the two ergonomists with the exposure time proportion determined by the JEM, as shown in Table 4. In the case of the upper extremity and neck and the lower back, the evaluations of both experts showed kappa coefficient values of 0.8 or greater in terms of agreement with the JEM estimates of exposure time proportion. On the other hand, for the lower extremity, the kappa coefficient value was less than 0.8.

A logistic regression analysis adjusted for gender and age was conducted to examine the relationship between exposure time proportion and MS symptoms. As shown in Table 5, an exposure–response relationship was observed between exposure time proportion and MS symptoms in the upper extremity and neck, and the lower back. In contrast, for the lower extremity, the association was statistically significant only at the high exposure level.

Top 5 occupations with the highest burden ratio on each body region

Table 6 summarizes the KSCO unit groups with the highest proportions of individual-level high exposure (pHigh) by body region. High exposure for the upper extremity and neck was concentrated in craft/machine-operation/production jobs (e.g., textile and leather-related workers and welders). For the lower back, the highest pHigh values were observed in material-handling and construction-related elementary occupations; however, compared with the upper extremity and neck or lower extremity domains, the pHigh values for the lower back were generally lower in magnitude. For the lower extremity, high exposure clustered in construction trades and standing-intensive elementary jobs.

DISCUSSION

In Korea, the construction of a JEM for MSDs has been hampered by a lack of comprehensive data encompassing all the regions, types of industries, and occupations. Since 2004, investigations into MS risk factors have been conducted at all workplaces triennially in Korea. Although the detailed and accurate data from these investigations related to the burden of MS risk is available at each company, it is confidential and not in the public domain for use by researchers. Meanwhile, the data on the compensation for occupational injuries and diseases from the Korea Workers’ Compensation and Welfare Service (KCOMWEL) allows us to determine the presence or absence of the MS burden by job category for some occupations based on reports of on-site investigations and approval status. However, this data has limitations since it only contains data on some specific occupations. The KWCS, which has been implemented since 2006, is conducted by selecting a representative sample of the total population of workers. It provides data on various harmful factors and health status.22 Hence, it provides an opportunity to construct an MS JEM. Nevertheless, attempts to construct an MS JEM have not been made so far in Korea.

As is well known, multiple factors lead to the development of MSDs. Specifically, MS risk factors include not only occupational risk factors but also individual factors such as gender, age, and hobbies, thus making it difficult to exclusively associate MSDs with work. The reasons for the non-approval of the applications for recognition of MSDs as occupational diseases include the lack of a confirmed diagnosis of the disease for which the compensation claim was filed, a short period of employment, and a low level of occupational burden. Above all, differences in judgments regarding occupational burdens between occupational and environmental medicine (OEM) physicians can be problematic. In some cases, although the MS condition of the workers was judged to be strongly related to occupational exposure in special examinations performed by OEM physicians, compensation claims were rejected in the actual workers’ compensation adjudication on the grounds that the diseases had a low level of work-relatedness. There have also been cases with contrary outcomes. This phenomenon may be attributed to differences in the experience regarding occupational burdens between OEM physicians due to the differences in the types of main industries in each region. It is common to find cases where there are also differences in the judgments about occupational burdens. Since the MS JEM constructed in this study can provide data encompassing all types of industries and occupations, it is expected to reduce the differences among OEM physicians in determining the work-relatedness or occupational burden of MS conditions. Furthermore, in Korea, a fast-track system including ‘presumption principles’ has been implemented to resolve delays in handling workers’ compensation adjudication cases due to the on-site investigations of MSDs. Since the presumption principles have been basically established with respect to specific types of occupations, it is very important to determine the occupational burden for each occupation. To assess the burden of work caused by each type of occupation on the MS system, the construction of an MS JEM is considered to be of great significance.

The job classification used in this study was based on the unit groups of the 7th KSCO and some occupations such as nurses, designers, and welders were classified into separate job categories. However, some job categories corresponded to unit groups that included several occupations, such as construction-related technical workers, production-related elementary workers, and health and medical service-related workers. With respect to these categories, the job classification was not performed in a uniform, consistent manner but was based on mixed classification criteria in terms of the hierarchical levels of job classification. While the most ideal approach would be to classify jobs according to the detailed job categories of the KSCO, in the occupational classifications of the 2nd to 6th KWCS, the unit groups, and detailed job categories were mixed. Thus, in this study, they were reclassified into unit groups of jobs. However, the classification of occupations into unit group categories rather than detailed job categories is believed to have the advantage of reducing classification errors by investigators. To evaluate the validity of the estimated exposure time proportion for MS risk factors by job category and body region, this study asked two ergonomists to evaluate the exposure time proportion by job category and body region. For items for which the evaluation results of the two ergonomists were not in agreement, the exposure time proportion was determined through consensus. Subsequently, the evaluation results of the two ergonomists were compared with the exposure time proportion determined by the JEM. Thus, for the upper extremity and neck and the lower back, the kappa coefficient values were 0.8 or greater, indicating substantial agreement between the JEM estimates and expert evaluations, and an exposure–response relationship was also confirmed in the association between exposure time proportion and MS symptoms. In contrast, for the lower extremity, the consensus-based kappa coefficient value was below 0.8, and the exposure–response relationship was statistically significant only at the high exposure level. This difference may reflect the fact that the items used for the upper extremity and neck and the lower back more directly capture ergonomic loading related to MSDs (e.g., work posture and handling heavy objects), whereas the lower extremity domain relied on a more limited set of items. However, questions about the lower extremity were not directly related to ergonomic factors regarded as the actual MS burden, such as handling heavy objects and the duration of standing posture. The occupations with high exposure levels in Table 6 generally align with well-known job categories associated with regional physical strain. However, in the case of the lower extremity, occupations such as food preparation related elementary workers and textile production and processing machine operators primarily involve prolonged standing. While standing work can induce lower limb pain, it is not typically considered a primary cause of meniscal tears, a common occupational disease of the knee. Therefore, when using the JEM results of this study, it is possible to apply the results directly for the upper extremity and neck and the lower back; however, for the lower extremity, application is recommended only for the high exposure time proportion category.

The primary advantage of this study lies in its pioneering effort to construct a Korean MS JEM. In addition, the data from the KWCS is highly reliable because the survey is conducted by selecting a representative sample from the entire population of workers. Therefore, the MS JEM developed in this study has the distinct advantage of being based on a large-scale dataset of over 200,000 individuals and covering all occupational categories. Another advantage of the present study is that the MS JEM built in this study has high applicability since it was constructed to provide data for all the major body segments of the MS system, including the upper extremity and neck, lower back, and lower extremity. The JEM development process in this study is similar to that of European JEMs, involving large-scale surveys, standardized job codes, and expert assessments; however, the inter-expert agreement was found to be higher in our study, compared with previously published JEM studies.

One of the limitations of this study is that the questions on the physical and ergonomic factors used to examine the exposure time proportion were simple and did not include all the risk factors for MSDs. Specifically, the questions on the lower extremity were not directly related to the actual risk factors associated with MS symptoms or conditions. Another limitation is that while European JEMs include psychosocial factors as risk factors and are stratified by gender, this study did not incorporate these aspects. Moreover, the KWCS was conducted by survey interviewers using the one-on-one interview method. Thus, there is a possibility of misclassification of the occupation. In addition, data on ergonomic factors and MS symptoms may be inaccurate or have low reliability because they were based on the self-reports of survey participants. Individual factors, including height, weight, and recreational activities, could contribute to MS symptoms. However, such variables were excluded from this analysis due to their absence in the KWCS dataset.

Since it is considered more effective to construct a JEM by classifying jobs into detailed categories, it is necessary to reconstruct the JEM through the reclassification of occupations into detailed job categories in the future. Furthermore, while ergonomic factors are recognized as significant contributors to MS symptoms, the potential influence of social and psychological factors on MSDs should not be overlooked. Incorporating these dimensions into the construction of an MS JEM is expected to enhance its comprehensiveness and accuracy.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, an MS JEM was constructed in Korea for the first time. The body segments where MSDs commonly develop were divided into the following three regions: upper extremity and neck, lower back, and lower extremity. Exposure time proportion to risk factors was determined for each occupation, and high validity was demonstrated through expert reviews and an analysis of the association between the exposure time proportion and MS symptoms. The results for the upper extremity and neck and the lower back are expected to be useful in evaluating occupational MS risks. However, for the lower extremity, cautious interpretation is recommended, particularly for the high exposure category.
