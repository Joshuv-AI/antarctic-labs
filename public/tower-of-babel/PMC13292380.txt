Enhancement of content, purity and anti-myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury activity of persimmon leaf flavonoids: Ultrasonic-assisted extraction, purification and related mechanism
Authors: Liying Han, Qiang Zhang, Jinnan Huo, Chenxiao Guo, Shuai Wang, Yongrui Bao, Tianjiao Li, Yi Zheng, Yanqiu Li, Bing Wang, Xiansheng Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persimmon leaves (PL) are agricultural and forestry by-products rich in flavonoids with diverse pharmacological activities. To fully exploit their medicinal value, we established an optimized integrated process combining ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) and resin purification to efficiently prepare persimmon leaf flavonoids (PLF) with high content and purity. The chemical composition of PLF was comprehensively characterized by ultra performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS) and high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Furthermore, in vitro cell and in vivo rat models were employed to evaluate the activity and underlying mechanism of PLF against MIRI. Preliminary results showed that the content and purity of PLF prepared by the proposed process were significantly higher than those of crude PLF (C-PLF) obtained by conventional reflux extraction. The total flavonoid content (TFC) and mass fraction of total flavonoids in PL (TFPL) were 48.54 ± 0.86 mg/g and 87.78 ± 1.61%, respectively. In addition, in vitro pharmacological revealed that PLF exhibited significantly stronger anti-oxidative stress activity than C-PLF. Four monomeric compounds, namely hyperoside, isoquercitrin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside and astragalin, exerted synergistic effects. In vivo animal experiments demonstrated that PLF alleviated myocardial infarction and oxidative stress injury in MIRI rats, and exerted cardioprotective effects by regulating oxidation-related proteins. At present, there are few studies on high-quality PLF worldwide. This study not only verified the high efficiency of UAE in flavonoid preparation, but also proved that high-purity PLF possesses prominent advantages against MIRI. The results provide a theoretical basis for the comprehensive development and utilization of PL resources.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is a disorder characterized by insufficient myocardial blood and oxygen supply as well as impaired metabolite clearance, which eventually leads to myocardial damage [1]. From 1990 to 2023, IHD remained the leading cause of death worldwide [2]. In 2023, the number of deaths attributable to IHD exceeded 8.9 million, representing a substantial increase of 31.4% compared with the year 2000 [3]. Furthermore, myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a common complication during the clinical treatment of IHD and seriously threatens human health [4]. Reperfusion strategies including thrombolysis and percutaneous coronary intervention are vital approaches to rescue ischemic myocardium [5], [6]. However, a major clinical dilemma exists: restoration of blood flow in the ischemic region tends to exacerbate myocardial injury, which greatly compromises therapeutic efficacy and poses a severe challenge to global healthcare systems [7]. Currently, edaravone, atorvastatin, amlodipine and other agents are widely used clinically to treat MIRI [8], [9]. However, these drugs may cause adverse reactions such as rash, bronchospasm and hepatorenal damage, which can lead to irreversible harm to the human body [10], [11]. Accordingly, it is urgent to develop natural products with definite efficacy and high safety for the prevention and treatment of MIRI. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that flavonoids derived from natural plants can alleviate oxidative stress injury and inhibit cell apoptosis, thus exhibiting promising potential against MIRI [12], [13]. For instance, pueraria flavonoids, puerarin and daidzin can regulate oxidative stress indicators including malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), and thereby effectively relieve myocardial damage [14], [15].

Oxidative stress is a core factor driving the onset and progression of MIRI [16]. A large number of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals and hydrogen peroxide, are generated during myocardial reperfusion, disrupting the redox homeostasis and further exacerbating myocardial damage [17]. The metabolic balance of ROS is jointly regulated by oxidases and antioxidant enzyme systems [18]. Modern studies have demonstrated that even short-term intervention with lemon balm extract can markedly increase the activity of SOD and the content of glutathione (GSH) [19], thereby effectively antagonizing oxidative stress injury. Hence, avocado extract is a promising natural active substance for the prevention and treatment of MIRI. It is therefore of great significance to screen anti-MIRI candidate compounds based on antioxidant activity.

Persimmon leaves (PL), the dried foliage of Diospyros kaki Thunb. (Ebenaceae), are common by-products of persimmon cultivation, which are often discarded or incinerated, resulting in not only waste of plant resources but also environmental pollution [20]. Modern studies have revealed that PL are abundant in diverse bioactive components including flavonoids, organic acids and triterpenoids, and they exert multiple pharmacological activities such as cardiovascular protection, antioxidant action, immunoregulation, antibacterial and anti-tumor effects, and therefore possess high development value [21], [22], [23]. Ding et al.[24] found that flavonoids from PL could induce apoptosis of PC-3 cells by regulating oxidative stress. Jinpeng et al.[25] reported that flavonoid phospholipid complexes of PL could alleviate atherosclerosis via regulating cardiac endothelial function and antioxidant pathways. Ethanolic extracts of PL can activate platelets, inhibit blood coagulation, reduce cholesterol levels, and improve blood circulation and lipid metabolism in hyperlipidemic rats [26]. As the predominant bioactive constituents in PL [27], flavonoids generally yield products with low extraction rate and purity using current extraction technologies. In addition, their effects and underlying mechanisms against MIRI remain unclear, which requires further in-depth investigation.

Extraction and purification processes serve as a critical bridge for transforming natural plant materials into medicinal products [28]. Traditional techniques including solvent reflux, thermal decoction and maceration are widely applied for extracting PLF, which generally suffer from large solvent consumption, low extraction efficiency, poor product purity and long processing time [29]. In recent years, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has become a mainstream and advanced technique for the preparation of natural bioactive compounds due to its cavitation effect that accelerates the release of active ingredients, as well as its high efficiency and low energy consumption [30], [31]. Le et al.[32] optimized the UAE deep eutectic solvent extraction of triterpenoids from PL using response surface methodology, and further purification with AB-8 macroporous resin markedly enhanced the recovery rate and purity of the target products. Sun et al. [33] prepared PLF by ethanol-based UAE, and the resulting extracts showed excellent in vitro free radical scavenging ability and cellular antioxidant activity, which could effectively regulate oxidative stress-related indicators and relieve cellular oxidative damage. Accordingly, UAE is a reliable technique for efficient preparation of active substances and exploration of the medicinal value of PL.

Conventional extraction methods used in previous studies yield PLF with low purity and pose potential safety risks due to organic solvent residues. At present, there is no well-established integrated system combining UAE and resin purification. Therefore, it is urgent to develop optimized processes for the preparation of PLF with high purity and safety. In addition, although PLF has been proven to possess multiple pharmacological activities, its efficacy against MIRI remains unclarified. Accordingly, guided by antioxidant activity, this study optimized the parameters of UAE and purification for PLF. The chemical composition of PLF was comprehensively characterized by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and ultra performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS). Anoxic AC16 cell model was used to compare the biological activities between C-PLF and the PLF obtained in this work, and the synergistic effects of major active constituents against MIRI were also evaluated. Furthermore, rat MIRI models were adopted to explore the pharmacological effects and underlying mechanisms of PLF. This work provides a theoretical basis for the high-value utilization of PL by-products and the development of potential cardioprotective agents.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The PL were provided by Liaoning Dongxin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China, which met the quality standards of Guangxi Chinese Medicinal Materials after detection. Standard substance of rutin, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside, and astragalin were obtained from Sichuan Weikeqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China, with a purity of no less than 98%. Macroporous resins including HPD100, D101, and AB-8 were all purchased from Anhui Sanxing Resin Technology Co., Ltd, Anhui, China. Chemicals and solvents such as Al(NO3)3, NaNO2, NaOH, and anhydrous ethanol were all of analytical grade and purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Determination of flavonoid content

The flavonoid content was measured by NaNO2-Al(NO3)3-NaOH [34], [35]. The UV detection wavelength was set at 510 nm. The regression equation is Y = 1.2982X-0.0385, with R2 = 0.9994, where X represents the rutin concentration (mg/mL) and Y denotes the average absorbance of the corresponding solution. In the concentration range of 0.225 to 0.60 mg/mL, absorbance shows a positive correlation with rutin concentration. Based on the standard curve, the yield of the extract is calculated as the proportion of the dry weight of flavonoids extract in grams per gram of dried PL, expressed as total flavonoid content (TFC) (mg/g) [36].

Extraction of PLF

Extraction process

Add 1.0 g of PL powder to a flask, and perform extraction with the specified solvent, liquid-to-solid ratio, and extraction time (ultrasound:reflux = 1:1). Repeat the extraction for three cycles, filter, collect and combine the filtrates. Store the combined filtrate at 4 °C.

Extract single-factor design

A single-factor design was employed to investigate how ethanol concentration (0–90%), ultrasonic-assisted reflux extraction time (30–180 min), and liquid-to-solid ratio (8:1–18:1 mL/g) influence the TFC in PL. This allowed for a comprehensive understanding of how these variables affect the flavonoid extraction process from PL.

Extract orthogonal design

To further refine the extraction procedure for PLF, a single-factor experiment followed by an orthogonal experiment was conducted. An L9(34) orthogonal design (SPSS Statistics 26.0) was adopted to optimize three key variables: UAE time (factor A), liquid-to-solid ratio (factor B), and extraction cycles (factor C), as well as to investigate their effects on the TFC (mg/g). Conventional extraction of C-PLF

Add 1.0 g of PL powder into a flask, add a 12-fold volume of aqueous solution, heat under reflux for 120 min, repeat the extraction three times, filter and collect the filtrates after each extraction, combine the filtrates, and store the mixture at 4 °C.

Box Behnken design optimization of PLF purification process

Selection of macroporous adsorption resin

Accurately weigh a certain mass of resin of uniform volume and pack it into a resin column. Precisely load a defined volume (V1) of the PL extract (with a mass concentration of C1) onto the column. Subsequently, wash off the impurities with a fixed volume of distilled water, dilute the washing solution to a constant volume (V2) and determine its mass concentration (C2). Finally, the adsorbed components on the resin were eluted using a fixed volume of 60% ethanol solution. The eluate was diluted to volume V3 in a volumetric flask, and its flavonoid mass concentration (C3) was determined. Identification of flavonoid components

Testing conditions

An appropriate amount of freeze-dried powder of C-PLF and PLF were accurately weighed. Test solutions with a uniform crude drug concentration of 0.20 g/mL were prepared under the following conditions: 60% ethanol (as the extraction solvent), extraction temperature of 40 ℃, and UAE for 30 min (200 W, 40 kHz). Identification and determination of PLF using Agilent 6550 Q-TOF-MS and HPLC systems. The chromatographic column uses a Agilent Poroshell 120SB-C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm) with a detection wavelength of 280 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and methanol (B), and the gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2 min, 30%-35% B; 2–22 min, 35%-42% B; 22–25 min, 42%-46% B. Flow rate: 0.6 mL/min; injection volume: 10 µL; column temperature: 30 ℃. Flavonoids in PL were identified by comparing their UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS fragmentation patterns and retention times with those of reference substances.

Method validation

Method validation includes specificity, precision, stability, repeatability, and recovery rate.

Comparing the effects of C-PLF and PLF on cell viability in vitro

Cell culture and toxicity testing

Human AC16 cardiomyocytes were subcultured when cell confluence reached over 85%. Logarithmic-phase cells were then seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 24 h to allow attachment. AC16 cells were treated with C-PLF and PLF at concentrations ranging from 0 to 800 μg/mL for 24 h. Cell viability was subsequently determined using the CCK-8 assay.

Antioxidant stress resistance test

CoCl2 can increase intracellular ROS levels, disrupt intracellular redox balance, and simulate a cellular hypoxic environment [39], [40]. Cells were exposed to 400 µM CoCl2 in glucose–free, serum–free DMEM for 5 h to establish a hypoxic cellular model. After hypoxic induction, the cells were treated with either C-PLF or PLF, and the incubation was continued for a total of 24 h. MDA, SOD, CAT, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and ROS were measured to evaluate the antioxidant response.

The improvement effect of PLF on MIRI rats in vivo

MIRI modeling and drug administration

SPF (specific pathogen-free) grade male SD (sprague–dawley) rats, weighing 200 ± 10 g. The rats were randomly divided into six groups: control group (Control), model group (Model), diltiazem group (20 mg/kg), and PLF low-, medium-, and high-dose groups (1.35 g/kg, equivalent to the human clinical dose; 2.70 g/kg; 5.40 g/kg). Each group consisted of 12 rats. All rats were given intragastric administration at a volume of 10 mL/kg for 15 consecutive days. Referring to previous literature, a rat model of MIRI was established as follows: after anesthetizing the rats, the hearts were gently excised. The left anterior descending coronary artery was ligated 3 mm below its origin for 60 min to induce myocardial ischemia; subsequently, the thoracic retention suture was gently pulled to release the ligature, followed by 24 h of myocardial reperfusion [41].

Echocardiography detection

Capture dynamic 2D and m-ultrasound images by scanning rat hearts with small animal ultrasound images. Record and measure left ventricular internal diameter in systole (LVIDs) (mm), left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd) (mm), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (%), and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) (%).

Sample collection and measurement

After reperfusion, blood and organ tissues were collected from each group of rats, and the hearts were placed in −80 ℃ and 4% paraformaldehyde for western bolt and HE staining (hematoxylin labels nuclei blue-purple, eosin labels cytoplasm pink-red) experiments. The harvested rat blood was left undisturbed at 4 ℃ for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3500 r/min for 15 min (4 ℃). Plasma from each group was collected, aliquoted, and stored appropriately.

Heart DHE staining

Frozen heart tissues were sectioned, stained with DHE working solution at 37 °C for 60 min, then rinsed with PBS. Fluorescence intensity was observed and photographed, followed by quantitative analysis with ImageJ.

HE and TUNEL staining of heart tissues

Hearts fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were retrieved, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. Subsequently, the sections were stained with HE staining solution and TUNEL working solution, respectively. Photographs were taken under the microscope, and ImageJ was used for quantitative analysis.

Western blot detection

Extract cardiac protein samples using an ultra-low temperature grinder. Determine the protein concentration using a BioTeke protein assay kit, followed by protein separation via SDS-PAGE. Perform semi-quantitative analysis of each target protein using ImageJ software, and conduct statistical analysis with GraphPad Prism software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated by independent-sample t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Single-factor experimental analysis

The preparation procedure of PLF in this study is presented in Fig. 1A. Single-factor experiments were performed to investigate the effects of ethanol concentration, UAE time and liquid–solid ratio on TFC, as shown in Fig. 1B. As for ethanol concentration, the results demonstrated that 50% aqueous ethanol yielded the highest TFC, which maximized the extraction of moderately polar flavonoid glycosides and aglycones from PL. Solutions with low ethanol concentration have high polarity and inhibit the dissolution of flavonoids with moderate and weak polarity. Moderately increasing ethanol concentration can effectively promote the release of flavonoids. This result confirms that the polarity of 50% ethanol aqueous solution matches well with that of the major flavonoid components in PL. Combined analysis of TFC and extract yield showed that 50% aqueous ethanol achieved the best extraction efficiency for moderately polar flavonoid glycosides and aglycones in PL. However, the total yield of flavonoid solids obtained by water extraction was comparable to that of the 50% ethanol group. Water features stronger polarity, which can sufficiently extract abundant highly polar flavonoid glycosides from PL and compensate for its weak capacity to dissolve low-polarity flavonoid aglycones. In comparison with ethanol, water is low-cost, environmentally friendly and safe without the risk of organic solvent residues. It also eliminates the need for alcohol removal procedures, simplifying the overall process and making it more suitable for scale-up production and industrial application [43]. Considering extraction performance, product safety and process practicability, pure water was selected as the optimal extraction solvent in this study.

We further explored the influence of UAE time on TFC, and the results showed that TFC rose steadily as extraction time increased. The combined action of ultrasound and heating reflux can effectively disrupt plant cell walls and facilitate the release and dissolution of flavonoid components. Accordingly, the amount of dissolved flavonoids kept rising in the early extraction stage. When the extraction duration reached 120 min, most free flavonoids in the raw material were fully extracted, and the solid–liquid system gradually reached dissolution equilibrium. Further prolonging the extraction time only recovered a small amount of bound flavonoids and residual target components, leading to an insignificant improvement in extraction yield [44]. Taking extraction efficiency, time cost and energy consumption into consideration, 120 min was finally determined as the optimal extraction time.

The effect of liquid–solid ratio on TFC was investigated. The results showed that TFC presented an overall upward trend as the liquid–solid ratio increased, reaching the maximum at 18 mL/g. Analysis of variance indicated that no statistically significant difference was observed in TFC between the groups at 12 mL/g and 18 mL/g. Increasing solvent dosage enlarges the solid–liquid contact area and promotes the continuous dissolution of flavonoids, which accounts for the rise in TFC with the increase of liquid–solid ratio. When the liquid–solid ratio reached 12 mL/g, most flavonoids in the system had been extracted. Further increasing the solvent volume only led to a slight improvement in extraction efficiency, resulting in no significant difference between the two groups. To reduce solvent consumption, experimental costs and subsequent workload [45], 12 mL/g was selected as the optimal liquid–solid ratio in this study.

In addition, the TFC of C-PLF obtained via conventional reflux extraction was 30.27 ± 0.31 mg/g, which was significantly lower than the value achieved with our optimized process. The acoustic cavitation effect of ultrasound can thoroughly disrupt the cell walls of PL and accelerate the release of intracellular active components. Meanwhile, heating reflux enables continuous solvent circulation and maintains a stable solid–liquid concentration gradient to further promote the dissolution of active ingredients [46]. The physical synergism of the two techniques not only greatly reduces the extraction time required by conventional reflux extraction, but also remarkably improves the dissolution efficiency of flavonoids [47]. In conclusion, compared with the traditional single reflux method, the developed ultrasonic-assisted heating reflux coupling process features higher extraction efficiency, shorter operation time and better extraction performance. It effectively addresses the drawbacks of conventional extraction, including insufficient cell wall disruption and incomplete release of active components. The results further verify that this optimized process is superior and practical for extracting PLF.

Single-factor enrichment PLF analysis

The results of purifying PLF with macroporous resins are presented in Fig. 1C. The adsorption rate and desorption rate of PLF on NKA-9 resin were 91.72 ± 1.83% and 92.33 ± 1.49%, respectively, both higher than those of the other five resins. This is mainly because the skeleton structure of weakly polar NKA-9 macroporous resin matches well with the physicochemical properties of PLF, enabling effective adsorption via van der waals forces. Meanwhile, its pore structure facilitates the penetration of eluent, allowing the adsorbed flavonoids to be efficiently desorbed [48]. Given its excellent adsorption and desorption performance, NKA-9 resin was selected for the subsequent purification of PLF.

First, the effects of adsorption time were investigated. The TFPL increased gradually with the extension of adsorption time on NKA-9 macroporous resin and reached the maximum at 24 h. When the adsorption time was further prolonged to 48 h, the TFPL content remained stable, indicating that the system reached the dynamic equilibrium between adsorption and desorption. At the initial stage, the resin possessed abundant active sites, so flavonoid molecules could diffuse and be adsorbed rapidly, leading to a continuous rise in TFPL content. The adsorption sites were nearly saturated at 24 h, and the adsorption capacity no longer increased [49]. Since excessively long adsorption time would reduce the preparation efficiency, 24 h was determined as the optimal adsorption duration.

Secondly, the TFPL first increased and then decreased with the rise of ethanol volume fraction, peaking at 60%. Ethanol solution with a lower volume fraction has strong polarity and fails to elute PLF adsorbed on the resin, while excessively high ethanol concentration will elute impurities simultaneously and reduce the purity of PLF [50]. Therefore, 60% ethanol was selected as the eluent. Within the loading concentration range of 0.05–0.40 g/mL, the TFPL content increased continuously and reached the highest value at 0.40 g/mL. A further increase in loading concentration above 0.40 g/mL led to a gradual decline in TFPL content. Excessively high loading concentration may block the pore channels of the resin, preventing PLF molecules from fully binding to active sites and thus weakening the purification effect [51]. Accordingly, 0.40 g/mL was chosen as the optimal loading concentration.

In addition, the effect of the volume ratio of resin to sample solution on TFPL was evaluated. The TFPL remained at a high and stable level when the ratio ranged from 4.5:1 to 3:1. To maximize the adsorption capacity of the resin and avoid sample waste, the optimal volume ratio of resin to sample solution was set at 3:1. Elution volume also exerted a notable influence on TFPL. The highest TFPL content was obtained at an elution volume of 5 BV, while a further increase beyond 5 BV caused a gradual decrease in TFPL. Excessive eluent would elute large amounts of water-soluble impurities and dilute flavonoid components, thereby reducing the relative content of flavonoids in the purified product [52]. Consequently, 5 BV was selected as the optimal elution volume.

The response surface plots of the regression equation are shown in Fig. 1D. The curved surface for the interaction between A and B presented a large curvature with rapid color variation, indicating a significant interactive effect [53]. The interaction between A and C ranked second, while that between B and C exerted the weakest influence. Considering practical operation requirements, the parameters were adjusted to a loading concentration of 0.40 g/mL, ethanol volume fraction of 65.0%, and elution volume of 5 BV. After two rounds of purification under the revised conditions and three parallel tests, the final TFPL mass fraction reached 87.78 ± 1.61%.

Most previous studies extracted C-PLF using ethanol, and subsequent purification generally adopted organic solvents such as petroleum ether and ethyl acetate, with the final flavonoid purity mostly below 80% [24], [33]. In this study, an eco-friendly process combining water extraction with ultrasonic assistance was established, followed by purification using NKA-9 resin. This approach not only greatly improved the purity of PLF, but also completely avoided the application of toxic and harmful organic solvents. Featuring environmental friendliness, safety, simple operation and low cost, the proposed process shows obvious advantages over traditional methods. It also provides a feasible technical reference for large-scale preparation and industrial application of PLF.

The method established in this study for detecting four flavonoids in PLF exhibited a good linear relationship with the standard solution (R2 > 0.999). The precision, stability, and repeatability of each standard peak were all satisfactory (RSD < 2.0%), and the recovery rate ranged from 99.31% to 104.58%. PLF exhibits stronger antioxidant activity compared to C-PLF

The pathological mechanism of MIRI is mainly attributed to the burst of intracellular ROS and disrupted oxidative stress balance after blood flow restoration [16]. Oxidative stress regulates the activities of oxidases and antioxidant enzymes to balance the generation and elimination of intracellular ROS [18], thereby alleviating cellular oxidative damage. It plays a vital role in the occurrence and progression of MIRI. Therefore, evaluating the antioxidant capacity of extracts is an important approach to assess their pharmacological potential.

This study compared the antioxidant capacities of C-PLF and PLF. The results showed that both C-PLF and PLF exhibited the ability to reduce the levels of MDA, ROS, TUNEL-positive cells (P < 0.01), and apoptosis-related markers in oxidative stress-damaged cells, while increasing the contents of SOD, CAT, and T-AOC in the cells (P < 0.01). However, compared with C-PLF, PLF had significantly higher antioxidant capacity(Fig. 4A–E). Furthermore, this study demonstrated that PLF significantly enhanced the anti-oxidative stress activity. The flavonoid compounds in PLF exert synergistic effects

To further evaluate the anti-oxidative stress effects of the major flavonoid bioactive components in PLF, including hyperoside, isoquercitrin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside, and astragalin, gradient concentration experiments were performed. HPLC analysis revealed that the contents of these four compounds in PLF at an equivalent raw herb dosage of 800 µg/mL were 3.6, 6.26, 3.776, and 5.34 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 5A and B). Antioxidant stress capacity was evaluated by measuring MDA, the final product of oxidative stress. Compared with the CoCl2 group, all monomeric compounds showed a tendency toward reduced MDA levels at low doses, but without statistical significance (P > 0.05). Their MDA-lowering effect became increasingly pronounced as dosage rose. More importantly, low-dose combined intervention with the four monomers exerted a markedly better cellular anti-oxidative stress effect than administration of any single monomer at the equivalent dose. Previous studies have demonstrated that the four flavonoid monomers exert antioxidant effects via distinct molecular pathways. Hyperoside binds to NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) and cyclooxygenase (COX) proteins to inhibit ROS production and reverse myocardial injury [56], [57]. Isoquercitrin upregulates SOD and reduces MDA levels, thereby improving myocardial mitochondrial function [58], [59]. Kaempferol-3-O-galactoside the GSH/GSSG ratio and decreases the release of oxidative products [60], [61]. In zebrafish oxidative stress models, astragalin suppresses cluster of differentiation 38 (CD38) activity, upregulates deacetylases, and enhances antioxidant capacity [62]. Each monomer targets only a single molecular site and shows weak efficacy at low doses. In contrast, combined use of these components achieves synergistic regulation across multiple pathways. This explains the improved antioxidant and cardiomyocyte-protective activities of high-purity PLF, demonstrating that higher purity corresponds to stronger biological activity.

HE staining revealed obvious inflammatory infiltration and necrosis in the myocardial tissue of the model group, whereas such pathological changes were absent in the control group (Fig. 7A). Changes in oxidative stress proteins

We detected the expression changes of core proteins related to oxidative response in cardiac tissues of rats in each group. The results showed that the levels of tumor protein p53 (TP53), as well as antioxidant enzymes glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1) and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), were markedly decreased in MIRI rats (P < 0.01). In contrast, the expression of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) and the oxidase NOX2 was significantly upregulated (P < 0.01), indicating that MIRI disrupted oxidative homeostasis and induced myocardial injury. Compared with the model group, all PLF treatment groups presented notable reductions in EGFR, KRAS and NOX2 (P < 0.05), accompanied by increased levels of TP53, SOD2 and GPX1 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8A and B). Conclusions

This study optimized and established a preparation process combining UAE and resin purification, successfully obtaining high-purity PLF. Process verification demonstrated that the content and purity of PLF prepared by the developed method were markedly higher than those of C-PLF, showing prominent superiority in product quality. In vitro activity evaluation revealed that high-purity PLF exhibited significantly stronger anti-oxidative stress activity than C-PLF. The active monomers including hyperoside, isoquercitrin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside and astragalin exerted synergistic effects to enhance the biological activity. Further in vivo animal experiments confirmed that PLF could effectively reduce the myocardial infarct size and alleviate oxidative stress disorders in rats with MIRI. It exerted remarkable cardioprotective effects mainly by regulating the expression of oxidative stress-related proteins. Currently, research on high-purity PLF remains limited. This study not only verified the high efficiency of UAE for the preparation of flavonoids, but also clarified the potential of high-purity PLF against MIRI. In conclusion, this work systematically illustrated the preparation advantages and pharmacological mechanisms of high-purity PLF. It provides solid experimental evidence for the comprehensive utilization of PL resources and offers a novel strategy for the research and development of candidate drugs targeting MIRI.
