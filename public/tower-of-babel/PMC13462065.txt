First-trimester ovarian dysgerminoma in pregnancy: a case report highlighting the value of expert ultrasound
Authors: Natālija Vedmedovska, Zanda Ākule, Arta Spridzāne, Androniks Mitiļdžans, Alise Vinogradova, Vija Batalauska, Sergejs Isajevs, Francesca Moro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalignant germ cell tumors are rare ovarian malignancies, accounting for less than 5% of ovarian cancers. Dysgerminoma is the most common subtype but remains exceptionally rare during pregnancy, with an estimated incidence of 0.2–1 per 100,000 pregnancies, often complicating timely diagnosis and management.Case reportA 21-year-old primigravida at 12 weeks and 6 days of gestation was referred for expert ultrasound after detection of a pelvic mass. An ultrasound examination performed by an EFSUMB level III examiner demonstrated an 11.79 × 7.3 cm vascularized solid ovarian tumor (color score 3–4) with heterogeneous echogenicity, a lobulated internal architecture, and smooth outer contours, most suggestive of a primary ovarian malignancy, specifically a dysgerminoma. Serum lactate dehydrogenase was 847 U/L (reference range 135–214 U/L), while CA125 was 34.3 U/mL, remaining within the upper limit of normal (reference range 0.0–35.0 U/mL). Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a solid ovarian mass suspicious for dysgerminoma or a stromal tumor. At 15 + 6 weeks of gestation, laparoscopic left adnexectomy with lower transverse laparotomy for specimen extraction was performed. Histopathology confirmed stage IIB left ovarian dysgerminoma with lymphovascular and capsular invasion. Chemotherapy was deferred after multidisciplinary evaluation. The patient delivered spontaneously at 40 + 3 weeks, with no evidence of disease progression postpartum.DiscussionThe subjective assessment by expert (Level III) ultrasound examiners helps distinguish between benign and malignant ovarian tumors and refine the diagnosis to a specific histological subtype.

Introduction

With the widespread implementation of routine first-trimester screening, ultrasound increasingly detects incidentally discovered, asymptomatic adnexal masses during pregnancy. The vast majority of these lesions are benign, while fewer than 5% are malignant (1). Among ovarian malignancies, malignant germ cell tumors are rare overall, accounting for approximately 1–2% of ovarian cancers, but they occur relatively more frequently in pregnancy-associated ovarian cancer (2). Dysgerminoma represents the most common histological subtype within this group. Despite this, dysgerminoma arising during pregnancy remains exceptionally rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.2–1 per 100,000 pregnancies (3). Approximately 65% of dysgerminomas are stage I at diagnosis. 85-90% of stage I tumors are confined to one ovary (stage IA) while 10 – 15% are bilateral (stage IB) (4). This rarity may contribute to delayed diagnosis and pose challenges in clinical decision-making and treatment planning. In 2011, Guerriero and colleagues described a set of clinical and sonographic features that may facilitate earlier identification of dysgerminoma in pregnancy (5). Our case supports this approach: an expert ultrasound assessment pointed strongly toward dysgerminoma, while MRI provided a broader, less subtype-specific differential, underscoring the added value of advanced ultrasound interpretation in pregnancy.

Patient case

A 21-year-old primigravida in good general health, with no relevant medical history, non-smoker, and normal cervical cytology, was referred to Riga Maternity Hospital at 12+6 weeks’ gestation for first-trimester screening and further evaluation of a pelvic mass that had been noted at 10+4 weeks (9.5 × 5.4 cm).

First-trimester fetal assessment was reassuring: anatomy appeared normal, nuchal translucency and biochemistry were within the expected range, the nasal bone was present, and ductus venosus and tricuspid valve Doppler findings were normal, resulting in a low combined-screening risk for common aneuploidies. During the same visit, pelvic ultrasound provided a detailed characterization of the adnexal lesion. The mass was solid and markedly vascularized (color score 3–4), with heterogeneous echogenicity and a lobulated internal architecture; the outer contour was smooth but intermittently lobulated. It measured 11.79 × 7.3 × 12.3 cm (Figures 1A–C), with no acoustic shadowing and no ascites. Based on the overall clinical and sonographic appearance, dysgerminoma was considered the leading diagnosis (5). Biochemical testing reinforced a subtype-oriented suspicion of malignancy, most consistent with a germ cell tumor: LDH increased from 509 U/L (12+6) to 847 U/L (14+3) (reference 135–214 U/L), while CA-125 was 34.3 U/mL at 10+4 weeks (non-pregnant reference 0–35 U/mL). Pelvic MRI confirmed a solid ovarian mass measuring 12.4 x 6.4 cm, with the radiologic differential including dysgerminoma versus a stromal tumor (Figures 2A, B). As part of the preoperative workup, abdominal and retroperitoneal ultrasound demonstrated no abnormalities in the parenchymal organs. Surgical management was planned at 15/16 weeks of gestation.

On admission, laboratory testing showed mild anemia (hemoglobin 11.3 g/dL) and a mild elevation in liver enzymes, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 71.1 U/L (reference 8–65 U/L). At 15 + 6 weeks’ gestation, the patient underwent laparoscopic left adnexectomy with low transverse laparotomy for specimen extraction. After antiseptic skin preparation, a 3 cm incision was made above the umbilicus, and a 12 mm trocar was inserted using the Hasson technique. A 14–15 cm solid mass was observed behind the uterus, adherent to the mesorectum. The uterus was enlarged with a smooth surface, consistent with gestational age. No enlarged para-aortic or retroperitoneal lymph nodes were identified, and other abdominal and pelvic structures appeared normal. Intraoperative serous fluid cytology was negative for malignant cells. Surgery proceeded with mobilization of the sigmoid colon and opening of the retroperitoneal space beneath the round ligament. The mass was completely mobilized and removed via a lower transverse laparotomy to avoid specimen fragmentation. The left ovarian mass and left fallopian tube were excised with minimal blood loss (50 ml). Total surgery time was 145 minutes. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day two. Complete staging was not performed to minimize potential adverse effects on the ongoing pregnancy from prolonged surgery, anesthesia, or increased surgical volume. Gross pathology described a 14 × 9 × 5 cm ovary containing a solid, multiloculated, gray-yellow tumor (Figure 3D). Histopathology confirmed (Figures 3A–C) ovarian dysgerminoma with capsular penetration and lymphovascular invasion; immunohistochemistry showed SALL4 positivity and CD117 positivity (inhibin and calretinin negative). The fallopian tube showed no malignant involvement. Final pathologic staging was reported as pT1c Nx M0, L+V+, Pn−, R0. Following multidisciplinary onco-gynecology review, chemotherapy was not administered during the ongoing pregnancy.

The patient was counseled regarding induction of labor at term; however, she declined induction and preferred to await the spontaneous onset of labor. Consequently, expectant management was continued. The pregnancy progressed without complications, and the patient delivered spontaneously at 40 + 3 weeks of gestation, with normal Apgar scores at 5 and 10 minutes. At 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, as well as 2 years after delivery, there was no clinical or imaging evidence of disease recurrence.

Tumor markers remained within normal limits, and abdominal and pelvic MRI findings were unremarkable.

Discussion

A large, predominantly solid adnexal mass with a lobulated contour, heterogeneous internal echogenicity, and prominent vascularity on color or power Doppler imaging in women of reproductive age should prompt suspicion for ovarian dysgerminoma (5, 6).

In pregnancy, preoperative identification of dysgerminoma remains challenging. A study conducted at a referral oncological center described the ultrasound features of 22 malignancies detected during pregnancy; none were germ cell tumors, consisting instead of borderline tumors, primary invasive epithelial subtypes, or metastatic lesions (7). In a review of 34 reported cases of ovarian dysgerminoma diagnosed during pregnancy, Zhang et al. found that most tumors were discovered either incidentally or after the development of symptoms (8). In many reports, including more recent cases such as that of Sas et al., the ultrasound assessment typically established the presence of a suspicious adnexal mass but did not suggest dysgerminoma specifically (9). This pattern underscores both the rarity of these tumors and the limited specificity often attributed to imaging in everyday practice. The typical ultrasound characteristics of germ cell tumors have been described (5, 10–12), allowing an expert examiner to make a specific diagnosis. These tumors are usually unilateral and present as large multilocular-solid or solid masses, most often detected in young women. Among malignant germ cell tumors, dysgerminoma is characteristically described as a predominantly or purely solid mass, frequently with a lobulated appearance and prominent vascularity (13). The differential diagnosis of a solid ovarian mass during pregnancy includes other malignant and borderline ovarian tumors. Granulosa cell tumors may also appear as solid or predominantly solid vascular masses and may therefore mimic dysgerminoma on imaging. However, they are more often associated with sex-cord stromal differentiation, may show variable solid-cystic morphology, and are not typically linked to the same clinical and biochemical pattern observed in the present case (14). Immature teratomas should also be considered in young patients and during pregnancy, but they more commonly demonstrate a complex heterogeneous appearance with cystic components and echogenic areas related to sebaceous material, hair, calcifications or acoustic shadowing (11). In contrast, the lesion in the present case was a large unilateral solid mass with heterogeneous echogenicity, lobulated internal architecture, marked vascularity, no acoustic shadowing, no ascites, and increasing LDH levels, a combination that favored dysgerminoma over immature teratoma and supported this diagnosis over a stromal tumor, although definitive diagnosis remained histological.

Ovarian dysgerminoma can be associated with elevated serum levels of several biomarkers. Increased lactate dehydrogenase is the most frequently reported finding, while elevated human chorionic gonadotropin and placental-like alkaline phosphatase have also been described in some cases. In contrast, CA-125 is less consistently elevated and is often within the normal range in patients with dysgerminoma. In our case, CA-125 levels were not increased, further highlighting the limited diagnostic value of this marker in pregnancy. The interpretation of tumor markers during pregnancy requires caution, as physiological gestational changes may influence their serum concentrations. Therefore, these biomarkers should be considered supportive rather than diagnostic and interpreted together with clinical presentation and imaging findings (15).

In the present case, the tumor was detected early in the first trimester, when adnexal visualization is generally optimal and before uterine enlargement restricts pelvic assessment. Early recognition facilitated adherence to contemporary recommendations for adnexal masses in pregnancy, including prompt referral to a specialized center, multidisciplinary evaluation, and timely surgical management when malignancy is suspected (13).

In the present case, MRI provided useful complementary information by confirming a solid ovarian mass and supporting a differential diagnosis that included dysgerminoma and stromal tumor. However, its diagnostic contribution was limited by the fact that the examination was performed without intravenous contrast because of the ongoing pregnancy. The absence of contrast enhancement restricted assessment of enhancement patterns and limited further tissue characterization. Consequently, MRI could not establish a definitive preoperative histological diagnosis. This limitation underlines the importance of integrating MRI findings with expert ultrasound assessment, biochemical markers, and clinical context when characterizing adnexal masses during pregnancy.

Malignant ovarian germ cell tumors predominantly affect younger patients and are diagnosed at an early stage in most cases, with disease confined to one or both ovaries in approximately 60–75% of patients. Prognosis is favorable, with 5-year survival exceeding 85% following appropriate management. Nodal status is prognostically important; 5-year survival has been reported as 97.5% in patients with negative lymph nodes compared with 82.8% in those with nodal involvement (16).

For early-stage dysgerminoma, primary treatment is surgical, typically unilateral oophorectomy with limited staging. Given the young age of most patients, fertility preservation should be a central consideration whenever oncologically appropriate (4). Evidence regarding operative safety in pregnancy is largely derived from non-obstetric and non-oncologic surgical cohorts, with approximately 0.5–2% of pregnant patients undergoing non-obstetric surgery annually (5, 6). In a series of 12,000 cases, Cohen-Kerem et al. reported an increased risk of fetal loss primarily in the context of peritonitis, whereas other studies have not shown an increased rate of congenital malformations or fetal death after surgery under general anesthesia for various malignancies; most preterm births were iatrogenic. Overall, the principal pregnancy-related surgical risks include miscarriage, preterm delivery, and fetal distress (17). Because evidence is limited regarding the maximum safe duration of minimally invasive procedures in pregnancy, and because elevated intra-abdominal pressure may have adverse physiologic effects, operative time and insufflation pressure should be minimized whenever feasible.

Stage IIB dysgerminoma is generally managed with fertility-sparing surgery, when feasible, or complete surgical resection followed by platinum-based chemotherapy, most commonly the BEP regimen (bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin). Surgery alone is generally reserved for completely resected stage IA dysgerminoma, whereas adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended for stage II and higher disease to reduce the risk of recurrence (18, 19).

Chemotherapy should be avoided during the first trimester because organogenesis is associated with the highest risk of miscarriage and major congenital malformations. When clinically indicated, platinum-based chemotherapy may be administered during the second or third trimester, balancing maternal oncologic benefit against potential fetal risks. Although BEP remains the standard regimen outside pregnancy, some authors recommend omitting etoposide during pregnancy because of limited fetal safety data and concerns regarding fetal growth restriction, myelosuppression, and possible congenital anomalies following in utero exposure (20, 21).

Available evidence suggests that chemotherapy administered after 14 weeks’ gestation is not associated with a substantially increased risk of major congenital malformations. Nevertheless, treatment remains associated with an increased risk of preterm delivery, low birth weight, fetal growth restriction, transient neonatal myelosuppression, and neonatal intensive care admission, primarily related to prematurity. Cisplatin readily crosses the placenta, whereas bleomycin appears to have limited placental transfer. Long-term follow-up studies have generally demonstrated normal cognitive and neurological development among children exposed to chemotherapy during the second and third trimesters, although data remain limited, particularly for rare malignancies and chemotherapy regimens. International guidelines therefore recommend avoiding chemotherapy during the first trimester, administering it only when maternal benefit outweighs fetal risk, and scheduling the final cycle at least three weeks before delivery to minimize neonatal myelosuppression and infectious complications (20, 22).

In the present case, the patient strongly wished to continue the pregnancy, and her principal concern was fetal exposure to chemotherapy. Current recommendations are based largely on expert consensus, retrospective studies, and published case reports. A multidisciplinary approach involving gynecologic oncologists, medical oncologists, and the patient is therefore recommended to individualize treatment decisions (20, 23, 24). The main mother fear was the exposure of the fetus to chemotherapy drugs. Following comprehensive counselling regarding the potential maternal benefits and fetal risks of antenatal chemotherapy, together with review of the final pathological findings, management options were discussed within a multidisciplinary team. As we know most recommendations are based on individual case reports, small patient groups and expert opinion. Through a shared decision-making process, close surveillance was chosen instead of antenatal chemotherapy. Following delivery, given the prolonged interval since surgery and the continued absence of clinical, biochemical, and radiological evidence of disease recurrence, the patient remained in sustained remission, and deferred adjuvant chemotherapy was not administered.

Historically, staging for malignant ovarian germ cell tumors mirrored that of epithelial ovarian cancer. However, accumulating evidence suggests that comprehensive staging procedures—such as routine omentectomy and systematic lymph node dissection—may not be necessary in early-stage malignant ovarian germ cell tumors. The Multicenter Italian Trials in Ovarian Cancer trial 26 and a large database analysis 27 reported slightly higher recurrence rates after incomplete staging (omitting omentectomy and/or lymph node dissection) but no difference in overall survival compared with complete staging. Cross-sectional data similarly indicate a trend toward de-escalation of staging in young patients. In a large pediatric intergroup cohort, lymph node dissection was omitted in 97% of cases, contralateral ovarian biopsy in 59%, and omentectomy in 36%, while 6-year overall survival remained high (93–98%) (25). In the absence of clear evidence that comprehensive staging improves survival and given the maternal–fetal considerations associated with prolonged surgery, limiting the extent of staging during pregnancy was considered justified in this case.

Postoperatively, surveillance is recommended for stage I dysgerminoma, whereas adjuvant chemotherapy is advised for stage II–IV disease (16). Standard regimens include etoposide–platinum combinations (BEP or EP) (17). Nonetheless, experience with BEP during pregnancy is limited, and fetal malformations and other complications have been reported, supporting a cautious approach and reserving chemotherapy during pregnancy for situations in which maternal benefit is clear (4). Accordingly, after multidisciplinary discussion involving gynecologic oncologists and a medical oncologist—and incorporating the patient’s preferences and a detailed risk–benefit assessment—the management strategy was close oncologic surveillance and continuation of pregnancy monitoring according to the state-defined program.

Overall, this case highlights the potential value of expert ultrasound in recognizing characteristic sonographic phenotypes when interpreted together with biomarker results, MRI findings, and clinical context. However, as this is a single case report, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority or greater specificity of ultrasound over MRI in the preoperative characterization of dysgerminoma. Instead, this case represents an observation that requires confirmation in larger studies. It also reinforces the importance of structured adnexal and maternal pelvic assessment during first-trimester ultrasound examinations, as such findings may directly influence diagnostic pathways, counseling, and management.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that expert ultrasound assessment can facilitate preoperative suspicion of ovarian dysgerminoma during pregnancy and may contribute to histotype-specific characterization beyond that achieved by conventional imaging alone. Early recognition of characteristic sonographic features may support timely referral, multidisciplinary decision-making, and individualized management while preserving favorable maternal and fetal outcomes.
