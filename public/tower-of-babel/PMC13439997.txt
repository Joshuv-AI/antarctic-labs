Untangling standardization and administrative burden as microfoundations of agility in healthcare
Authors: Pierre-Luc Fournier, Mira Thoumy, Patrick Groulx, Desirée H. van Dun, Kevin J. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe capability of healthcare organizations to provide high-quality care is in part determined by their workers’ ability to adapt to a complex and uncertain environment. Since healthcare operations are often highly standardized and bureaucratized, adapting and responding to internal and external changes is no small feat. This study investigates these tensions as determinants of individual agility.MethodsUsing survey data collected from 2,173 Canadian nurses along with structural equation modelling, the study assesses a hypothetical model to understand how process standardization and administrative burden influence individual agility and, in turn, the quality of care provided to patients.ResultsThe results show that the quality of care provided by nurses to their patients is increased by their internal and external individual agility. These, in turn, are positively influenced by process standardization but inhibited by administrative burden.ConclusionThis study extends current literature on the microfoundations of agility in healthcare by providing theoretical and practical contributions on the impact of enabling and coercive bureaucracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-15179-6.

Background

Rising expenses, expanding demand, and a constantly changing regulatory environment are just a few of the healthcare sector’s challenges [1]. Consequently, healthcare organizations need to be flexible and nimble to deal with ever-increasing pressures to improve the quality of care, while also coping with a rapidly changing environment [2]. In recent years, the operations management literature has focused heavily on the dynamic capabilities (DC) essential for organizational effectiveness [3, 4]. DCs allow an organization to successfully integrate, build, and rearrange its internal and external resources to adapt to and thrive in a quickly changing business environment [5]. Strong DCs lead to firms exhibiting higher levels of innovation, competitive advantage, and financial performance (Teece et al., 1997). Information sharing, strategic flexibility, as well as a strong organizational culture can support the development and application of DCs [5]. These findings underline the importance of an organization’s capacity for adaptation in response to changing market circumstances [6]. Ultimately, organizations with DCs may recognize, capture, and shape opportunities while adjusting their capabilities and resources to successfully respond to outside disruptions and market dynamics [4, 7].

Among the different DCs identified in the literature, organizational agility has emerged as a higher-order DC that allows an organization to take advantage of changes in the market by adjusting internal operations and delivering up-to-date value propositions [8, 9]. This capability influences how an organization can swiftly and successfully seize opportunities [10], particularly in complex and unpredictable environments [4, 8], such as healthcare [11, 12]. According to the microfoundational view of DCs, organizational-level agility is largely predicated on individual-level agility [10, 13]. In healthcare organizations, individual agility takes place when clinicians and other employees are able to quickly respond to changing contingencies [14, 15]. To that end, investigations have identified a positive relationship between individual agility and the quality of care provided by clinicians [16–18].

In bureaucratic organizations such as hospitals, processes can have both enabling and constraining effects on individuals’ agility [19, 20]. On one hand, an enabling bureaucracy can enhance efficiency through standardization, thereby increasing employees’ ability to adapt to contingencies [20]. Standardization can serve as a performance-enhancing scaffold for agility by lowering process variation and clarifying responsibilities, rather than acting as mere rigidity [21]. On the other hand, a coercive bureaucracy, characterized by hierarchical decision-making and burdensome administrative requirements, can stifle innovation and adaptability to change [20, 22]. In healthcare specifically, coercive bureaucracies have been shown to undermine outcomes such as quality of care and employee well-being [23, 24].

The aim of this paper is to investigate the effects of process standardization and administrative burden on nurses’ individual agility as a micro-level DC in healthcare. Drawing on DC theory and bureaucracy theory, we developed and tested our hypothesized model using survey data from 2,173 Canadian nurses and structural equation modelling. This study extends the current literature on the microfoundations of agility in healthcare by providing both theoretical and practical contributions on the impact of enabling and coercive bureaucracy. More specifically, it offers insights into the effects of organizational routines and constraints on nurses’ ability to adapt to internal and external contingencies. The paper is structured as follows. First, we present our literature review and hypotheses. Then, we show our methodology, followed by the results and analysis. We present the theoretical and managerial implications of our research. Finally, we conclude with the limitations of our study and future research avenues.

Literature review and hypotheses

Agile organizations can effectively manage complexity during emergencies or unanticipated events, making agility a crucial capability and performance factor for organizations operating in volatile environments [25, 26]. An organization’s ability to foresee changes and their potential repercussions and react quickly and responsively is the foundation of agility [27]. More precisely, organizational agility is based on the ability to face external and internal changes [9]. Initially conceptualized at the organizational level, the microfoundational perspective argues that organizational DCs, such as agility, are based on micro-level DCs that manifest at the individual level [10, 13, 28]. In this paper, we define two distinct dimensions of individual agility, following an initial conceptualization of the internal and external drivers of organizational agility [29]. Internal individual agility is defined as the ability of healthcare workers to quickly adapt to internal changes such as changes to processes, organizational structure, and resource availability [29]. For nurses, examples of internal individual agility include things such as prioritizing workload during staff shortages and quickly delegating tasks or quickly adjusting care plans based on patient responses. External individual agility is defined as the ability of workers to adapt to external changes, such as shifts in customer demand. For nurses, external individual agility focuses on how they interact with changes stemming from outside of their immediate work setting, such as responding to new protocols and guidelines or handling crisis situations and infection outbreaks. In healthcare, studies have shown that workforce agility is positively linked to organizational agility [30–32]. In turn, organizational agility was found to have a positive impact on the quality of services in different industries [26, 29]. While research on the direct link between individual agility and quality of care remains scarce, a few studies have found that clinicians’ individual agility can positively impact the quality of care provided to patients [17, 18, 33]. For example, nurses’ agility is likely to enhance the quality of care because it enables them to reorganize resources, reprioritize tests, and coordinate with colleagues effectively, which should ensure timely interventions [18]. By anticipating and responding quickly to both internal disruptions (e.g., staff shortages, workflow changes) and external contingencies (e.g., fluctuating patient volumes, new clinical guidelines), nurses’ agility may support the continuity and responsiveness of care [14, 15]. This ability to adapt ensures that clinical care delivered to patients remains safe, effective, and patient-centred, thereby contributing directly to higher quality of care [18]. Thus, we first hypothesize that both internal and external individual agility will positively influence the quality of care provided to patients.

H1

Internal individual agility is positively linked with quality of care.

H2

External individual agility is positively linked with quality of care.

Standardization

DCs can allow an organization to detect and react efficiently to environmental changes, helping organizations develop flexibility by adapting their operations in response to shifting market conditions and consumer needs [34]. These capabilities are frequently manifested in routines, where synchronized and repeatable actions allow companies to adapt and succeed in changing environments [7]. Routines are a type of process standardization since they offer an organized way to carry out tasks [35]. Standardization refers to the implementation of a set of policies and processes that encourage uniform and effective operations inside an organization (Ferreira et al., 2018; Secchi et al., 2019). According to Adler’s theory of bureaucracy, these elements are reflective of an enabling bureaucracy, where processes help codify routines that stabilize and develop organizational capabilities [20]. Thus, it can be argued that process standardization, which entails the construction of routines, contributes to developing DCs such as agility [36]. Organizations lay the groundwork for effective and reliable operations by standardizing processes and routines. Clarity, reduced variability, and improved operational control are all benefits of standardization. Organizations can then spend resources wisely and react rapidly to environmental changes [35].

Additionally, process standardization empowers staff to carry out duties successfully and efficiently, giving them a distinct and standardized framework for activity, thus enabling the development of their skills and competencies [37]. Individuals become more aware of how their actions contribute to overarching organizational goals as they become more familiar with standardized processes. Their internal individual agility is increased due to their familiarity and competence, which improve their capacity for adaptation and response to new difficulties [33]. Organizations can also increase the effectiveness and efficiency of their relationships with external stakeholders by standardizing their processes [37]. For example, employees can more reliably satisfy consumer expectations, provide goods and services on schedule, and form solid alliances thanks to standardized processes [33]. The ability of the organization and its workers to navigate and adapt to external changes and demands is improved by these factors, which also help to establish trust and credibility with external stakeholders [37].

To respond to shifting patient needs and outside influences, healthcare organizations must balance the demands for structure, agility, and quality of care [38, 39]. This reality typically results in work processes being highly standardized [11]. Various authors viewed process standardization as a building block toward agility [22, 40], since studies have found that standardization enhances agility by reducing process variability and permitting faster implementation of changes [41]. It can also promote continuous improvement and innovation [42, 43], and improve communication and collaboration [44], enabling rapid adaptation to change. Thus, we hypothesize that high levels of standardization will lead to higher levels of external and internal individual agility:

H3

Process standardization is positively linked with internal (H3a) and external (H3b) individual agility.

Administrative burden

While administrative structures and requirements are regarded as crucial to ensuring that operations adhere to legal and regulatory requirements [27], they can also impede innovation and prevent organizations from building higher-order DCs when overly bureaucratic and hierarchical [35]. Since they are relatively invariant across units, administrative structures and rules are rarely adapted to daily activities, thus having a negative effect on adaptive behaviors [40, 45]. Focusing on compliance and adhering to rigid structures and administrative procedures can therefore limit employees’ development of innovative ideas and the investigation of new opportunities, limiting the organization’s flexibility and capacity to adjust to shifting market conditions. While structures can help decision-makers to enable changes more quickly, excessive administrative work can make it difficult for an organization to adapt rapidly to changing conditions [22]. For instance, in an organization theorized by Adler and Borys [20] as a coercive bureaucracy, decision-making frequently includes numerous authorization levels and complex approval processes [19]. In coercive bureaucracies, procedures are often designed to allow superiors to monitor compliance and performance, as opposed to supporting workers [46], which slows down the decision-making process and decreases the organization’s agility [47]. Furthermore, an excessive hierarchy might generate a top-down communication flow in which feedback and new ideas from lower-level employees may struggle to reach decision-makers on time, or in which lower-level staff members are less likely to provide feedback to higher-ups [48]. This lack of response to feedback might hinder an organization’s capacity to recognize and capitalize on emerging opportunities or manage possible dangers [47]. Moreover, administrative burden can impede agility by creating barriers to communication and collaboration among workers, which, in turn, can negatively affect performance outcomes [9, 27, 49]. Such is the case in healthcare, where administrative burden is seen as omnipresent and detrimental to patient care [42, 50]. In fine, procedures, hierarchy, and intensive administrative work can reduce an organization’s agility by impeding the flow of information, decision-making processes, and collaboration [51]. When administrative processes unnecessarily burden employees, it can divert their attention and resources from more value-added activities and impair their capacity to respond effectively to changing circumstances [52]. When “compliance” becomes a dominant orientation absent a supportive culture, employees’ latitude for problem-solving narrows and adaptive behavior declines [53]. At the same time, knowledge-sharing practices have been shown to buffer rigidity by accelerating sense-making and facilitating quicker, better-informed decisions, thereby counteracting the agility-reducing effects of red tape [54]. Thus, we hypothesize that administrative burden will negatively influence internal and external individual agility.

H4

Administrative burden is negatively linked with internal (H4a) and external (H4b) individual agility.

This study thus builds on previous theory with the aim of further contextualizing the emergence of individual agility in healthcare settings by examining the impact of process standardization and administrative burden, two phenomena that are especially prominent in healthcare organizations [11, 50, 55]. Our conceptual model explores the nature of the relationships between the constructs while putting forth the aforementioned hypotheses.

Methods

Our methodological design incorporated the recommendations of Edmondson and McManus [56] and Caniato, Doran, Sousa and Boer [57] regarding the coherence of the various aspects of a research project. We employed a deductive survey-based research approach as an appropriate method to assess hypotheses derived from a theoretical framework [56]. The following section presents our data collection procedure and the assessment of our measurement instrument.

Data collection

We collected data by administering an online survey using the Qualtrics platform. This survey was sent to 10,000 nurses working in the Canadian province of Quebec’s healthcare system through the province’s professional nursing association. Data collection was conducted in French and took place over a period of six weeks, with two reminders sent over two-week intervals. Potential respondents were identified based on the following criteria: each respondent had to be an actively practicing nurse in a governmentally regulated healthcare organization, as well as a legally registered nurse. The email was sent to respondents by the researchers and contained a summary of the research project as well as details regarding their participation. The anonymity of respondents was guaranteed, and a link to the online survey was provided at the end of the email. The participants’ informed consent was obtained upon their reading of an “information & consent” form at the start of the survey. This research also received approval from a university ethical committee, prior to data collection. Following data collection, the data were extracted from the Qualtrics platform in order to create an anonymous database to be used for further analysis. The survey is available as a supplementary file.

In total, 2,173 complete and usable responses were received, for a response rate of 21.73%. This data resulted in a representative sample [58] consisting of roughly 10% men and 90% women, the majority of whom were between 26 and 50 years old. Non-response bias was evaluated by comparing the first and last quartiles of respondents [59]. Analysis of variance techniques were used to compare the first 25% of respondents with the last 25% of respondents for all constructs included in the model. No significant differences between the two groups were observed following this analysis, thus indicating no serious concerns regarding non-response bias.

Measures

Tables 1 and 2 present the main variables operationalized for this study. Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics and correlations for all variables. All items were measured using seven-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). The process standardization scale used three items adapted from Secchi, Roth and Verma [60] that assessed nurses’ perception of how their clinical work was standardized through formal procedures and detailed instructions (α = 0.775). The items included the following example: “I have detailed instructions to manage the most unusual situations”. The administrative burden construct was assessed using a three-item scale from Van Loon [23] to assess how respondents felt that the organizational structure and its rules inhibited them in their daily work (α = 0.837). The items used for measuring this construct included the following: “Administrative procedures represent an important obstacle to my work”.

The internal and external individual agility constructs were also measured using 3 items, respectively, adapted from Lu and Ramamurthy [9]. Internal individual agility used three items from their original operational adjustment agility scale, which was rephrased to the individual level in order to assess respondents’ perceived ability to quickly and effectively respond to internal changes, such as changes to processes or a lack of resources (α = 0.767). An example of an item is: “Whenever there is a disruption in supply, I can quickly make the necessary adjustments to my work.” External individual agility employed a three-item scale based on Lu and Ramamurthy’s (2011) original market capitalizing agility scale, which we rephrased to measure respondents’ perceived ability to quickly and effectively respond to external changes such as a sudden increase in demand or changing patient needs (α = 0.856). The following is an example item used for this construct: “I quickly make and implement appropriate decisions in the face of patient changes.”.

We used a three-item scale adapted from Aiken, Clarke, Sloane and Consortium [61] to measure how respondents perceived the quality of care they provided to patients on their units (α = 0.813). Researchers have found that self-assessed quality of care is a reliable and valid measure since it is heavily related to clinical outcomes such as infections, medical errors, and mortality [62–64]. An example of an item for this construct is: “I provide my patients with a high level of nursing care”. To avoid possible bias, such as an underreporting of low-quality care, we encouraged respondents, once they had reached the quality of care measures, to provide their honest assessment and also re-emphasized the anonymity and confidentiality of their answers.

We also included control variables related to the individual and their work. In accordance with recommendations from the Organizational Behavior literature, we controlled for age and gender (0 = male, 1 = female) because of their possible effects on the variables included in our model [65]. Age was measured by categories of five years. We also implemented the dichotomous work setting (WS) variable to control whether participants worked inside a hospital (WS = 1) or in a non-hospital setting (WS = 0), such as medical clinics or nursing homes. This control variable is relevant due to previous research highlighting its possible effects on nurses’ behavioral responses related to individual agility, such as their ability to think outside the box and adapt rapidly to changing contingencies [18].

Measurement model

The two-stage approach of Graham [66] was used to assess the reliability of the latent constructs. The Tau-equivalent model was selected based on fit indices and chi-square. The Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability values were all above the generally accepted threshold of 0.70, ranging from 0.755 to 0.856 and 0.759 to 0.863, respectively.

The validity of the measurement model was tested by performing a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the covariance-based structural equation modelling (CB-SEM) feature in SmartPLS-4. Construct validity was assessed by implementing the Maximum Likelihood (ML) approach, which indicated a good fit with CFI = 0.962, NFI = 0.957, SRMR = 0.042, and RMSEA = 0.054. Convergent validity was tested in two ways. First, the standardized factor loadings were all evaluated and found to be significant (p < 0.001) while also being greater than the suggested value of 0.5, ranging from 0.632 to 0.942. These results provide support for the convergent validity of the constructs [67]. Second, the average variance extracted (AVE) values were analysed and found to account for more than 50% of the measured items’ variance, ranging from 0.522 to 0.672. These results provide further support for convergent validity [68]. Finally, the discriminant validity of the constructs was tested by contrasting the constructs’ square root of AVE values with the between-constructs correlations. Since all correlations between pairs of constructs were smaller than the square roots of AVE, discriminant validity is supported [69, 70]. Common method variance

Various approaches were used to assess common method bias [71]. First, procedural strategies were implemented to reduce the risks of common method variance (CMV). These procedural remedies included the following: providing respondents with a guarantee of anonymity, clear instructions regarding the completion of the survey, reverse-coded items to diminish the risk of stylistic responses. The controls for common method bias were also ameliorated by psychologically separating the predictor and criterion variables by creating separated sections in the Qualtrics platform. Second, statistical strategies were implemented to assess the presence of CMV. A CFA using the latent factor test indicated minimal CMV since none of the factor loadings lost significance and the model fit was not improved [71]. This was followed by the use of the marker variable technique based on the recommendations of Lindell and Whitney [72], by adding the “attitude toward the colour blue” marker variable to the model [73]. No loss of significance could be observed between the original and revised models. As a further control for CMV, we also inspected the correlations between the constructs of the measurement model, which did not reveal any exceptionally high correlations between the latent variables. Overall, our statistical controls suggest that CMV is not a threat to our study [71].

Analysis and results

Model estimation and analysis reproducibility

The structural model was assessed using the CB-SEM module in SmartPLS-4 [74], version 4.1.1.8. No missing data was found in the database. To test the hypotheses illustrated in Fig. 1, we employed the Maximum Likelihood estimation. As recommended [75] and to account for all possible paths from predictor to criterion variables, we initially included the direct paths from process standardization and administrative burden into quality of care. The results showed these paths to be non-significant with values higher than 0.05 (p = 0.279 and p = 0.146, respectively), and were thus removed from the model in order to assess the hypotheses. The resulting structural model used to test the hypotheses showed a good fit with RMSEA = 0.061, CFI = 0.951, NFI = 0.945, and SRMR = 0.069. The bootstrapping method at 5,000 samples was used to estimate the significance of the indirect effects. Direct effects

The results of the analysis provide support for the hypotheses presented in our conceptual model. Standardized path coefficients measure the magnitude and direction (positive or negative) of a hypothesized relationship between two variables. In social sciences, values situated around 0.10 are generally considered as small effects, whereas values approximating 0.30 and 0.50 are considered moderate and large, respectively [76]. Internal individual agility (β = 0.166, p < 0.001) was positively and significantly associated with quality of care in support of H1. H2 was also supported, as shown by the significant and positive association of external individual agility with quality of care (β = 0.178, p < 0.001). Process standardization loaded significantly on both individual agility constructs. More precisely, H3a was supported by process standardization having a positive and significant association with internal individual agility (β = 0.231, p < 0.001). The positive and significant relationship between process standardization and external individual agility (β = 0.180, p < 0.001) provided support for H3b. Finally, administrative burden was significantly associated with both internal individual agility (β = -0.245, p < 0.001 and external individual agility (β = -0.174, p < 0.001), in support of H4a and H4b, respectively. The analysis also revealed that age, gender, and work setting did not show any significant effects on variables included in the model. While the independent variables showed significant Pearson correlations with the quality of care variable, these relationships became non-significant once added to the structural model. This was also the case for the three control variables.

Indirect effects

The bootstrapping method with 5,000 samples was employed to perform a mediation analysis testing the indirect effects of the administrative burden and process standardization variables on the quality of care variable. The results indicate that process standardization has a positive and significant indirect effect (β = 0.070, p < 0.001) on quality of care through both individual agility variables, while administrative burden was shown to have a significant and negative indirect effect (β = -0.072, p < 0.001) on the quality of care through both mediators. All of the significant indirect effects fell within the 95% confidence interval.

Discussion

The results presented in the previous section provide support for our conceptual model. As research on the microfoundations of DCs continues to grow [3, 4, 18], our study enhances our understanding of nurses’ agility at the individual level and of the factors that influence it. Our structural model shows that process standardization was positively associated with nurses’ internal and external individual agility, whereas they are deleteriously associated with administrative burden. Our results also provide further evidence that the agility of clinical staff is consistent with providing high-quality care to patients. Ultimately, this research extends our understanding of agility in healthcare by juxtaposing DC theory and bureaucracy theory to untangle some of its antecedents.

The microfoundational perspective of DCs argues that micro-level factors play an important role in the development of organizational capabilities [10]. In other words, organizational-level DCs, such as organizational agility, are based on their corresponding individual-level DCs, such as individual agility [13, 18]. Consequently, these individual capabilities become critical for organizational performance, particularly in highly volatile and uncertain environments [25]. Our findings show that individual agility is consistent with quality of care, which aligns with the extent, albeit scarce, literature on agility in healthcare [16–18]. However, our model extends these findings by distinguishing two types of individual agility, internal and external. Internal individual agility relates to nurses’ ability to adapt to contingencies that stem from their intra-organizational environment, whereas external individual agility relates to nurses’ capacity to react to changes that are external to the organization but that can influence operations nonetheless. With similar effect sizes, both individual agility variables were found to be positively associated with the quality of care provided by nurses to their patients, meaning that nurses’ ability to quickly adapt to both internal and external changes is consistent with individual-level performance. This finding coincides with those of researchers who argue that employee agility is a key component of agile service provision [28, 77, 78].

To bolster our understanding of how micro-level DCs can develop, business scholars have called upon researchers to further investigate how organizational factors can contribute to their emergence [10, 13, 18, 79]. As an answer to this call, we took to the theoretical perspective of bureaucracy [19, 20] to investigate the effects of process standardization on individual agility, contrasting this effect with that of administrative burden. While some scholars argue that the homogeneity brought about by standardization might limit the ability of workers to react and adapt to changes [22, 80], we argued that standardization can actually enable the ability to respond quickly to operational changes. This was supported by our results, which showed that process standardization allowed nurses to adjust and adapt to changes within their internal and external environments. Such standardization can take the form of standard clinical protocols and detailed instructions used by nurses to break down the critical components of care to ensure quality of care and the safety of patients. These protocols create routines that allow nurses to quickly identify and implement the work that needs to be accomplished to provide care to their patients for various situations they might face in their daily work. In fine, our results show that the structure and relative rigidity of process standardization potentialize nurses’ internal and external agility, by reducing the time it takes to react to unforeseen contingencies (quickness) and by ensuring that the implemented solutions are effective (effectiveness). These two characteristics, quickness and effectiveness, are key components of agility [28, 77].

In contrast to process standardization, we argued that administrative burden represents a form of coercive bureaucracy [20] that inhibits internal and external agility. Indeed, we found that nurses who felt restricted in their work by administrative rules and structures proved to have more difficulty in adapting to changes in their environment. For example, nurses on a medical unit might be prevented from adapting their work schedules to account for unforeseen absences from other colleagues due to organizational rules and collective labour agreements not allowing such flexibility. Cumbersome administrative work and processes could also stifle efficient communication and collaboration among employees, reducing information flow and limiting their ability to adjust swiftly [60]. Our findings highlight how an organization’s rules, hierarchy, and structures can inhibit individual agility and lead to poorer performance outcomes. Though both of the dependent variables had stronger effects on nurses’ internal individual agility than they did on their external individual agility, their indirect effect sizes on the quality of care provided to patients are very similar, albeit opposite. These results point to the tensions that must be managed in order to achieve high performance in uncertain environments (Schad et al., 2016). In other words, our results show that process standardization is potently associated with agility and performance and may counteract the effects of excessively bureaucratic administrative rules and structures.

Through a combination of two theoretical frameworks, DC theory and bureaucracy theory, our research has unearthed interesting findings regarding the competing effects of process standardization and administrative burden. Notably, it highlights that process standardization can be a foundation for agility in healthcare. Our research also brings further support to arguments from other scholars that posit that excessive administrative work is actually what creates the inability to adapt to changing circumstances [22].

Practical implications

Our findings echo recommendations from practitioners on the importance of agility in healthcare [33, 81]. To this end, our results highlight the importance of standardized processes for nurses’ agility. Though it may appear counterintuitive to agile principles, this points to the need for healthcare operations to rely on standardized and arguably more rigid processes for nurses to be able to adapt to a highly dynamic environment. Since healthcare already operates in a highly standardized clinical environment, it may have a head start in developing agile capabilities. On the other hand, healthcare organizations are also notoriously siloed, which creates a more difficult setting for agility. Thus, healthcare organizations should strive to reduce the administrative burden often put on clinicians [50, 82]. From streamlining documentation by removing duplicate data entry across systems to improving electronic health record design through simpler interfaces and using voice-to-text dictation, healthcare organizations have various options at their disposal. Improving task delegation and the use of administrative support roles are also ways through which organizations can reduce administrative burden. While it is important to maintain some level of structure and control so as not to undermine accountability [83], limiting administrative burden is key to developing a work system that enables creativity, motivation, and employee engagement [20, 84]. Ultimately, healthcare organizations should put efforts forward to remove overly stringent administrative rules and structures that not only inhibit their ability to adapt to changes in their environment but also reduce nurses’ time available for care provision, while, at the same time, strengthening cross-functional standardized processes.

Finally, the findings do not only speak to healthcare organizations’ top managers but also to policymakers who are encouraged to put effort into reevaluating and reducing government regulations that can stifle agile care provision to patients. For example, developing national standards for clinical documentation and mandating interoperable digital systems can reduce administrative duplication while maintaining accountability through centralized, traceable records. Policy makers should also focus on providing sufficient funding for support staff so that clinicians can focus on patient care.

Strengths, limitations, and future research

The large and representative sample size reinforces the external validity of our model, especially considering the difficulty in collecting survey data from healthcare workers [85]. Our sample also did not suffer from low response rates, which are issues that can plague survey research [86].

Though the generalizability of our findings certainly benefits from our sample size, it is also possibly limited due to the single jurisdiction from which our respondents originate (Canada). Future research may want to consider the contextual differences found in other healthcare systems, such as distinctions between public and private systems. It may also want to focus on other measures of healthcare performance, such as accessibility and safety [87], and incorporate supplemental data from other healthcare professionals, such as physicians. Future research may also want to include organizational-level factors, such as digitalization and organizational structures [88], or team-level factors such as psychological safety and collaboration [89, 90], that can influence individual agility, while focusing on their interplay with individual-level factors.

Cross-sectional studies can also generate limitations linked to common method variance [71]. Our analysis and controls did not indicate this to be an issue. Finally, our study investigated process standardization and administrative burden as antecedents of individual agility in healthcare. However, agility also manifests at the team level [40]. Future research may want to consider the effects of these antecedents on team dynamics that also contribute to organizational agility.
