Forecasting monthly hepatitis B cases in China: a nationwide comparative study based on surveillance data
Authors: Shangwen Lu, Ziqiang Lin, Ching Yeung, Fengjuan Zou, Xuan Xie, Xiaofeng Liang, Yanhui Gao, Sui Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobust forecasting of hepatitis B trends is important for long-term surveillance and public health planning in China. However, evidence remains limited regarding how different forecasting frameworks perform when applied to a long-term national hepatitis B surveillance series under a unified out-of-sample evaluation design.MethodsMonthly reported hepatitis B cases in China from January 2004 to December 2024 were obtained from national public surveillance databases. Data from January 2004 to December 2023 were used as the training set, whereas data from January to December 2024 were reserved as an independent validation set. Ten forecasting models were evaluated, including Seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA), Bayesian structural time series (BSTS), four standalone machine-learning models (random forest, support vector machine, XGBoost, and LightGBM), and four residual-based SARIMA–machine-learning hybrid models. Model performance was assessed using mean absolute error (MAE), range-normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE). In addition, the average annual percentage change (AAPC) was estimated to summarize the long-term annual trend in reported case counts.The best-performing models were further used to forecast monthly hepatitis B case counts in 2025.ResultsA total of 252 monthly observations were included. The mean monthly number of reported hepatitis B cases was 91,901. The annual reported case counts did not show a statistically significant long-term trend (AAPC = − 0.561%, P = 0.12), whereas the monthly series exhibited a stable seasonal pattern, with peaks typically occurring in March and troughs in December followed by January. Among the candidate SARIMA structures, SARIMA(2, 1, 0)(1, 0, 3)12 was selected as the optimal model. In the overall model comparison, BSTS achieved the best validation performance, with the lowest MAE (10014.27), NRMSE (38.49%), MAPE (7.18%) and highest NSE(-0.92) among all ten models. Among the hybrid models, SARIMA-LightGBM performed best, with corresponding validation values of 10903.35, 40.14%, 8.07% and − 1.09, respectively. In 2025, BSTS achieved an MAE of 13,437.42, NRMSE of 9.53%, MAPE of 12.04%, and NSE of − 0.41, with 95% posterior predictive intervals used to quantify forecast uncertainty.ConclusionsForecasting performance differed across model classes in long-term national hepatitis B surveillance. BSTS showed the best overall predictive performance. These findings support empirical model selection and suggest that BSTS-based forecasting may serve as a supportive tool for early warning, resource allocation, and long-term hepatitis B surveillance planning when interpreted together with reporting-system and policy context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28190-7.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global public health challenge and is a leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Hepatitis Report 2024, viral hepatitis caused an estimated 1.3 million deaths worldwide in 2022, of which 1.1 million were attributable to hepatitis B and approximately 254 million people were living with chronic HBV infection [1]. Despite the substantial progress achieved through universal infant immunization and the prevention of mother-to-child transmission, China continues to bear one of the largest HBV burdens globally, with an estimated 75 million people living with chronic infection [2]. The WHO Global Health Sector Strategies for 2022–2030 set a target for eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030, defined as a 90% reduction in incidence and a 65% reduction in mortality relative to those in 2015 [3]. However, whether China can achieve these targets under the current prevention framework remains uncertain, given the persistent burden of chronic infection and the remaining gaps in diagnosis and treatment coverage [4]. In this context, robust forecasting of HBV trends is essential for assessing progress toward elimination, anticipating future disease burden, and informing the evidence-based allocation of public health resources [3].

Forecasting infectious diseases has traditionally relied on classical time series models that explicitly characterize temporal dependence, long-term trends, and seasonal patterns. Seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA) models are among the most widely used approaches in communicable disease surveillance because they offer a transparent framework for modeling linear stochastic structure and seasonal variation [5]. In HBV forecasting research, autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and SARIMA models have been applied to both national and local surveillance data in China and have generally shown acceptable performance for short-term prediction when temporal patterns are relatively stable [6, 7]. Similar time series approaches have also been applied to other notifiable infectious diseases, including tuberculosis and influenza [8, 9]. However, although these models are epidemiologically interpretable and straightforward to implement, they are primarily optimized for linear and seasonal dynamics and may perform worse when substantial nonlinear variation persists in the residual series [10].

More flexible forecasting frameworks have therefore been introduced to better address uncertainty and complex dynamic patterns in surveillance data. Bayesian structural time series (BSTS) models extend the state-space framework by estimating latent trends and seasonal components probabilistically, while naturally quantifying uncertainty and flexibly incorporating prior information [11, 12]. In HBV surveillance research, He et al. applied a BSTS model to monthly national surveillance data from mainland China to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 outbreak and project subsequent epidemiological trends, thereby demonstrating the feasibility of Bayesian state-space modeling in this setting [13]. However, that study was primarily designed for causal impact analysis in a pandemic context and was based on a single-model framework rather than a broad comparison of alternative forecasting strategies. In parallel, machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) have shown a strong capacity for learning complex nonlinear relationships from structured health data [14–17]. However, evidence for these approaches in HBV forecasting remains limited, and existing studies have largely focused on selected deep-learning architectures or specific prediction settings rather than on a systematic comparison across multiple model families [18]. Recent research on infectious disease forecasting further suggests that the relative performance of machine-learning and deep-learning models may vary substantially across surveillance settings, depending on the data structure, temporal dependence, and the evaluation framework used [19, 20].

Hybrid and ensemble strategies have emerged as a promising direction because they may integrate the complementary strengths of statistical time series models and machine learning approaches. In infectious disease forecasting, hybrid models that combine classical time series structure with machine learning-based residual correction have often outperformed single models, as reported for tuberculosis and influenza [8, 9]. Similar integrative strategies have also shown value in forecasting other environmentally sensitive infectious diseases [21]. These findings suggest that classical time series models, Bayesian state-space models, standalone machine learning algorithms, and serial hybrid models may capture different but complementary features of epidemic dynamics [12, 21]. Nevertheless, important gaps remain in HBV forecasting research, particularly for long-term surveillance in China. Existing studies have largely focused on a single model family, a relatively short observation window, a subnational dataset, or a specific analytic objective such as pandemic-related causal inference. As a result, there is still limited evidence on how classical seasonal models, Bayesian state-space models, standalone machine-learning algorithms, and hybrid frameworks compare when applied to a long-span national HBV surveillance series within a unified out-of-sample evaluation framework [7, 13]. Accordingly, a systematic comparison of these heterogeneous modeling strategies remains warranted.

Therefore, this study systematically compared ten forecasting models, including SARIMA, BSTS, four standalone machine-learning models, and four serial SARIMA-machine-learning hybrid models, using national monthly hepatitis B surveillance data from China for the period from 2004 to 2024. By evaluating out-of-sample predictive performance, we aimed to identify the framework best suited to jointly capture the seasonal linear structure and nonlinear fluctuations of HBV time series in a long-term national surveillance setting, thereby providing quantitative support for surveillance and public health planning in the context of HBV elimination [3, 4].

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

The monthly reported hepatitis B cases in China from January 2004 to December 2024 were obtained from official, publicly accessible national surveillance databases maintained by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention and the China Public Health Science Data Center [22, 23]. Because the objective was to forecast the monthly reported hepatitis B case burden for surveillance and public health planning, the models were developed using reported case counts rather than population-adjusted incidence rates, and no population offset term was included. Data from January 2004 to December 2023 were used as the training set, and data from January to December 2024 were reserved as an independent validation set for out-of-sample evaluation. This split was performed chronologically rather than randomly to preserve the temporal ordering of the surveillance series and to avoid information leakage. After model comparison, the best-performing model(s) were used to forecast monthly reported hepatitis B case counts from January to December 2025. Subsequently, reported monthly counts for 2025 became publicly available. These data were extracted from the monthly statistics of notifiable infectious diseases released on the official website of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, allowing for a descriptive comparison between the forecasts and the actual reported values to assess the models’ real-world generalizability beyond the formal validation period. Because this study used publicly available, de-identified aggregate surveillance data, ethical approval and informed consent were not needed.

SARIMA model

The SARIMA model was used to capture the linear dependence, temporal autocorrelation, and annual seasonality of the monthly hepatitis B case series. The general form of the model was specified as SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)12, where (p, d, q) denote the non-seasonal autoregressive, differencing, and moving-average orders, respectively, and (P, D, Q) denote the corresponding seasonal orders with a 12-month periodicity. Prior to model fitting, the original series was log-transformed when necessary to stabilize variance, and differencing was applied to remove nonstationarity [5].

Stationarity was evaluated using time series plots and the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test [24]. Candidate model orders were identified with reference to the Autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) and were further screened by information criterion-based model comparison [25]. Competing models were compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), corrected AIC (AICc), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Considering the relatively limited effective sample size and multiple seasonal parameters in the SARIMA framework, AICc was prioritized for optimal model selection to correct small-sample bias and mitigate overfitting risk [26]. The adequacy of the final model was evaluated using the Ljung–Box test for residual white noise [27].

BSTS model

The BSTS model was applied as a Bayesian state-space forecasting approach to decompose the reported series into latent components, including trend and seasonality, within a probabilistic framework. Compared with conventional autoregressive models, BSTS provides greater flexibility in representing structural evolution over time and yields full posterior predictive distributions, thereby enabling explicit uncertainty quantification [11, 12].

In this study, the BSTS model was specified to reflect the long-term temporal evolution and recurring annual pattern of monthly hepatitis B case counts [13]. Specifically, the state specification was configured with a local level trend component to capture stochastic baseline fluctuations over time and a seasonal component with a fixed period of 12 months to model annual cycles. Default prior settings implemented in the CausalImpact/bsts framework were used, without imposing informative external priors.

The model was fitted to the training series using Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations. After model fitting, convergence was assessed using the Geweke diagnostic, which did not indicate evidence of non-convergence (P > 0.05). Posterior forecasts and their 95% posterior predictive intervals were generated for out-of-sample prediction on the validation set [12].

Standalone machine learning models

Four standalone machine learning algorithms were implemented to model nonlinear patterns in the hepatitis B case series: RF, SVM, XGBoost, and LightGBM. RF is an ensemble learning algorithm that aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees built on bootstrap samples to improve robustness and reduce variance [14]. SVMs perform nonlinear regression by mapping the input data into a higher-dimensional feature space through kernel functions and estimating an optimal regression boundary [15]. XGBoost is a regularized gradient-boosting framework that fits additive decision trees through efficient second-order optimization [16]. LightGBM is a histogram-based gradient-boosting algorithm with high computational efficiency and strong predictive performance for structured data [17].

These models were trained on the processed monthly hepatitis B series to capture nonlinear variation that might not be fully represented by conventional statistical time series models [18, 20]. The hyperparameters were tuned within the training set, and the fitted models were subsequently used for out-of-sample prediction [19].

NRMSE was reported as a percentage, whereas MAE was reported in the original unit of the data, namely reported cases. Sensitivity analysis

To examine the robustness of the model ranking, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative 12-month validation periods from 2020 to 2023 under an expanding-window framework. For each validation year, all preceding monthly observations were used as the training set, and the subsequent 12 months were used as the hold-out validation set. Model performance was evaluated using the same metrics as in the main analysis, including MAE, NRMSE, MAPE, and NSE [29].

Statistical analysis and software implementation

To summarize the long-term annual trend, the average annual percentage change (AAPC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated on the basis of annually reported hepatitis B case counts. All the analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.3. Data organization and preprocessing were performed using tidyverse. Seasonal-trend decomposition and related time series processing were conducted using stats and relevant visualization packages [30]. The SARIMA model was developed using the forecast package, stationarity testing was performed using tseries, and residual diagnostics were conducted using functions in stats. The BSTS model was fitted using the bsts package. The machine learning models were implemented using randomForest, e1071, xgboost, and lightgbm. The figures were generated using ggplot2.

The standalone machine-learning models and the machine-learning components of the hybrid models incorporated sine and cosine transformations of calendar months to model annual cyclic seasonality, together with a relative year variable to account for long-term temporal trends [31]. To account for potential changes in hepatitis B reporting and case ascertainment, a binary reporting-policy indicator was additionally included in models that allowed external covariates, including BSTS, the standalone machine-learning models, and the machine-learning components of the hybrid models. The indicator was coded as 1 from January 2023 to December 2024, corresponding to the period affected by enhanced electronic case-reporting linkage and intensified case ascertainment, and 0 otherwise. SARIMA was specified as a univariate time-series model and therefore did not include this external indicator, whereas BSTS modeled temporal evolution and seasonality through its local-level trend and 12-month seasonal state components. Hyperparameter tuning for the machine learning models was conducted within the training set, and the final model settings are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Comparison of model performance and sensitivity analysis

The reported and predicted values of the ten models are shown in Fig. 2, and the performance metrics are summarized in Table 3. With respect to the training set, XGBoost yielded the lowest error values (MAE = 1621.83, NRMSE = 2.66%, MAPE = 1.78%, NSE = 0.97), followed by BSTS and several hybrid models. On the validation set, however, BSTS achieved the best overall performance, with the lowest MAE (10014.27), NRMSE (38.49%), MAPE (7.18%), and NSE (-0.92) among all models.

Forecast performance of the BSTS model in 2025

On the basis of the validation results, BSTS achieved the best overall predictive performance and was therefore used to generate forecasts for monthly hepatitis B case counts in China from January to December 2025.

As shown in Fig. 3, the BSTS forecast generally followed the overall monthly pattern of the reported data. From January to July, the BSTS model broadly captured the initial direction of change, but a visible tendency toward overestimation was already apparent. From August to October, the overall trends remained broadly consistent, although the predicted magnitudes differed from the reported values. From October to December, the reported series showed an upward pattern, whereas the BSTS model predicted a downward trend. Over the full 2025 forecast period, the BSTS model yielded an MAE of 13437.42, an NRMSE of 9.53%, and a MAPE of 12.04%. The NSE was − 0.41, reflecting the late-year divergence in which the model failed to capture the unexpected upward shift in reported case counts during the final quarter.

Discussion

Our study provides comparative evidence for model selection in long-term national hepatitis B surveillance by evaluating ten forecasting models across four methodological frameworks within a unified validation framework. Using a nationwide monthly hepatitis B case series spanning 2004 to 2024, we found that China continues to maintain a substantial reported hepatitis B burden, with persistently high monthly case counts and a stable seasonal pattern, and no statistically significant long-term change in annual reported case counts [32]. In terms of model comparison, predictive performance varied across frameworks, and the validation results demonstrated that in long-term national HBV surveillance, forecasting performance depends more on the correspondence between model structure and series characteristics than on model complexity alone [20]. Previous evidence has also suggested that, despite progress in HBV control, translating epidemiological gains into a rapid reduction in diagnosed and notified burden remains challenging, underscoring the need for continued surveillance optimization and policy-responsive intervention [33].

From an epidemiological perspective, the stable seasonal pattern, with peaks typically occurring in March and troughs in December followed by January, suggests that hepatitis B reporting is influenced by a recurrent healthcare utilization, health checkup activity, and reporting practices rather than random fluctuation alone [34, 35]. The irregular perturbations around 2020 and the subsequent phase-specific fluctuations should also be interpreted in the context of the COVID-19 period. A previous national BSTS analysis revealed that the COVID-19 outbreak was associated with a marked reduction in hepatitis B notifications in mainland China, and broader evidence has shown that healthcare utilization and notifiable disease reporting were disrupted during the pandemic [13, 36, 37]. These findings indicate that fluctuations in the reported hepatitis B series reflect not only the underlying HBV burden but also diagnostic activity, healthcare access, and notification processes.

Among the evaluated models, BSTS showed the best overall performance on the independent validation set, with the lowest MAE, NRMSE, MAPE and the highest NSE among all ten models. Although SARIMA and SARIMA-LightGBM performed less well than BSTS, both remained competitive in short-term prediction. The negative NSE values require careful interpretation because NSE is a relative skill score benchmarked against the mean of the observed validation series and is based on squared deviations. In the 12-month validation window, several months with relatively large deviations, particularly in the final quarter, could make the squared prediction errors exceed the variance around the local observed mean, resulting in negative NSE values. This finding indicates limited predictive skill relative to the observed-mean benchmark under a squared-error criterion, but it does not directly contradict the MAE, NRMSE, and MAPE results, which measure absolute or relative prediction errors. The seasonal naïve baseline also showed reduced validation performance, with NSE decreasing from 0.29 in the training set to − 3.15 in the validation set (Supplementary Table S3). In the validation set, BSTS outperformed the seasonal naïve baseline across all reported metrics, with lower MAE, NRMSE, MAPE and a higher NSE. Nevertheless, these results indicate that model performance should be interpreted using multiple complementary metrics, particularly when validation is based on a short out-of-sample window. These results indicate that the national hepatitis B series in China was more effectively captured by a framework able to represent stable seasonality, gradual background shifts, and occasional external perturbations within a probabilistic state-space structure [12]. Moreover, the relatively competitive performance of SARIMA suggests that a large proportion of the monthly series can still be explained by regular autocorrelation and annual seasonal dependence [5].

From a mechanistic perspective, the superior validation performance of BSTS suggests that this framework is well suited to long-span national surveillance data in which the underlying process may evolve over time. By decomposing the reported series into latent trend and seasonal components, BSTS can accommodate phase-specific structural fluctuations more flexibly than a single linear seasonal equation can [11]. In contrast, the standalone machine learning models did not outperform either BSTS or SARIMA on the validation set, suggesting limited generalizability when temporal dependence remains the dominant signal [19]. Although SARIMA-LightGBM performed better than the other hybrid models, its residual-based structure does not directly provide analytical 95% prediction intervals, because the variance structure from the SARIMA component cannot be directly propagated through the non-parametric LightGBM residual model. Therefore, we did not use the hybrid model for final forecasting recommendations. This finding indicates that hybridization should not be assumed to outperform a well-specified state-space model by default, particularly when uncertainty quantification is required for public health decision-making [12, 38].

The increase in 2025 forecast errors should be interpreted in the context of changes in hepatitis B reporting and case ascertainment. During 2023–2024, enhanced electronic case-reporting linkage and strengthened reporting supervision may have increased the capture of reported hepatitis B cases [39]. In particular, automatic extraction of infectious disease report cards from electronic medical records and health records may have improved reporting sensitivity, while also increasing the likelihood that asymptomatic HBV carriers or previously under-reported cases were included in the reported hepatitis B case series. At the same time, expanded screening, improved diagnostic awareness, and policy incentives related to standardized treatment and reimbursement may have further increased the number of newly reported chronic hepatitis B cases [40, 41].

These reporting- and ascertainment-related changes may have altered the observed surveillance series independently of the underlying transmission process. The subsequent deviation in 2025 may partly reflect correction or cleaning of misclassified or duplicate case reports after the intensified reporting linkage in 2023–2024. This provides a plausible explanation for the deterioration in 2025 forecast performance, particularly the model’s failure to capture the upward shift in the final quarter. Therefore, the 2025 forecast should be interpreted as a post hoc assessment of model generalizability under a changing surveillance system rather than as evidence that the model fully captured all operational changes in reporting [42]. Future operational forecasting should incorporate real-time surveillance metadata, reporting-system indicators, screening intensity, and policy changes when such information is available prospectively. Accordingly, the role of BSTS-based forecasting should be understood as a supportive early-warning and planning tool rather than a stand-alone decision-making instrument. Its practical use requires parallel interpretation with surveillance-system changes, reporting quality indicators, and policy context, particularly when abrupt shifts in case ascertainment occur.

These model comparisons have direct implications for surveillance practice and policy interpretation. For long-term national hepatitis B monitoring, the most useful forecasting framework is not necessarily the most flexible, but the one that best matches the temporal structure of the reported series and maintains stable out-of-sample performance. In this respect, our findings support BSTS as the preferred model for national surveillance forecasting, particularly because it combines favorable predictive accuracy with posterior uncertainty quantification. Although our forecasts were based on reported monthly case counts rather than direct measures of new infection or HBV-related mortality, the persistently high predicted case burden suggests that translating epidemiological progress into rapid reductions in diagnosed disease burden remains challenging under current conditions [43]. The forecasting results do not permit a direct conclusion as to whether China will fully achieve the WHO 2030 elimination targets, but they indicate that progress toward those targets is unlikely to be sustained without parallel improvements in testing, linkage to care, treatment coverage, and surveillance responsiveness [33]. In the Chinese context, the Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Viral Hepatitis (2025–2030) is highly relevant because its emphasis on expanded testing, standardized reporting, information management, and integrated service delivery may increase short-term case ascertainment while strengthening long-term control capacity [44]. The upward deviation of reported values from the BSTS forecast curve in late 2025 could be interpreted as potentially reflecting, at least in part, policy-driven enhancements in case detection or reporting capacity, rather than necessarily indicating an increase in underlying transmission alone. However, this explanation remains hypothetical and should be further evaluated as additional empirical evidence becomes available.

This study has several limitations. First, the models were developed from nationally aggregated monthly reported hepatitis B case counts rather than prospectively observed incident infections; thus, the reported series may reflect not only the underlying disease burden but also changes in healthcare seeking, diagnostic capacity, reporting completeness, and surveillance policy [22]. Structural shifts caused by external events or policy changes may weaken temporal stability and reduce forecast robustness [37]. Second, time series models primarily rely on historical autocorrelation and seasonal structure and therefore have a limited ability to capture abrupt exogenous shocks or fully explain fluctuations driven by vaccination, population mobility, screening intensity, healthcare access, and other contextual factors [45]. Because the analysis was based on aggregate public data, individual-level covariates and more detailed contextual variables could not be assessed. Third, the comparison involving standalone machine-learning models should be interpreted with caution. These models were configured using deterministic temporal features, including sine/cosine transformations of month, relative time indices, and a binary policy indicator but did not explicitly include autoregressive covariates such as lagged case counts or rolling averages. This feature-engineering choice may have reduced their ability to capture short-term serial dependence and may have placed them at a structural disadvantage relative to SARIMA and BSTS, which model temporal dependence more directly. Therefore, the weaker performance of standalone machine-learning models should not be interpreted as an inherent limitation of the algorithms themselves, but may partly reflect the parsimonious feature set used in the primary analysis. Fourth, the use of national aggregate data masks regional heterogeneity, which limits the applicability of the findings to subnational decision-making [45]. Finally, although multiple models were compared under a unified validation framework, model performance remained sensitive to training windows, feature construction, and hyperparameter settings; thus, the selected models should be interpreted as relatively optimal rather than universally optimal [19]. In the post hoc 2025 assessment, BSTS showed some reduction in predictive accuracy compared with the 2024 validation set, although the updated MAPE remained below 15%. The model still failed to fully capture the unexpected upward shift in reported cases during the final quarter, indicating that its practical use for early warning and resource planning requires further validation under changing reporting systems. Future studies should incorporate richer time series features, external covariates, rolling-origin validation, appropriate uncertainty-estimation frameworks for hybrid models, and region-specific modeling to improve interpretability, subnational applicability, and public health utility [21].

Conclusion

This nationwide long-series comparison shows that no single forecasting family should be assumed to dominate hepatitis B surveillance by default. BSTS provided the best overall predictive performance for the national monthly hepatitis B case series, supporting its relative suitability for long-term monthly hepatitis B surveillance in China. However, the post hoc 2025 assessment indicated that forecast performance may be affected by reporting-system changes, diagnostic policy shifts, screening intensity, and case-reporting correction. Therefore, BSTS-based forecasting should be interpreted as preliminary evidence and a supportive tool rather than a stand-alone basis for public health decision-making. The persistently high predicted case burden suggests that BSTS-based forecasting may help inform early warning, forward planning, timely resource allocation, surveillance and testing, linkage to care, and vaccination promotion when interpreted together with real-time reporting-system information and policy context. Further real-world validation is needed before routine operational application.
