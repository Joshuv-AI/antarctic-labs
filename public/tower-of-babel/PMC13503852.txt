A programmable living force sensor fabricated with surface engineered microbial network
Authors: Si Shi, Changhong Wang, Qiliner Feng, Hailin Li, Aike Shi, Jie Liu, Yongzheng Ma, Xiangjun Gong, Zifu Li, Baizhu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineered living materials have recently expanded into the field of bioelectronics when incorporating the rationally rewired microbes inside. Integrating living materials with force sensing provides an alternative route to overcome limitations of traditional sensors, such as fragility and stretching-sensitivity. Here, we present a programmable living force sensor fabricated by surface engineered microbial network with a bottom-up design for human-machine interfaces. Bacterial cells are genetically rewired to display surface-anchored affinity pairs that mediate the assembly of bacteria to macroscale. Functioning as the conductive and sensing layer, the microbial network is encapsulated in elastic tubes to fabricate the macroscopic force sensor. The resulting living sensor features a tunable measuring range, rapid self-recovery capacity within tens of milliseconds, and remarkably, excellent stretching-insensitivity with only ~30% increase in resistance under 800% of elongation. This bio-integrated approach enables microbes to prepare robust and reconfigurable force sensor suitable for tensile, dynamic and extreme mechanical conditions.

Introduction

The field of engineered living materials (ELMs) has attracted growing interest by integrating natural organisms with traditional materials1–3. Powered by the rationally designed gene circuits, natural cells in living materials can realize the sophisticated and tailored functions, such as proper responses to stimuli, efficient biosynthesis, bioremediation, and living therapeutics4–8. The engineered biofilm made of Escherichia Coli curli system is a representative type of living material for desired functions in a bottom-up strategy9–12. Alternatively, bacteria can be assembled as living materials directly by engineering the surface protein, which are self-recovering, scalable, and programmable13,14. Owing to the living properties, ELMs have extended to the electronic field by incorporating engineered biological functionalities of living cells, traditional materials, and electronic devices to acquire living electronics for the smart and reliable systems in diverse applications15.

Living electronics is an emerging field that incorporates traditional inorganic materials with living cells, microbial communities, or biomolecules as living functional components in electronic systems15–17. The biological components not only function as interfaces but also participate in signal transduction, information processing, energy conversion, and self-repair, thereby enabling the construction of living electronics13,18,19. Additionally, most of the living electronics are biodegradable, contributing to the preparation of green electronics20. By design on purpose, the incorporation of engineered living cells with electronic interfaces is capable to sense and response the surroundings with specific functions5,15. In contrast to conventional electronics that are typically rigid and brittle, living electronics can be elastic, which would bridge the gap between electronics and soft surfaces, and offer great potential for next-generation human–machine interfaces.

Human–machine interfaces exhibit the promise to capture and convey tactile information across spatial and temporal boundaries, which can be applied in biomedical applications, virtual reality, human-robot interaction, and skill training21,22. To this end, force sensors are always exploited to mimic the tactile sensing of human skin23. A desired human–machine interface should be wearable and capable to maintain stable and accurate signals under dynamic, repeated, or continuous surface conditions24,25. However, traditional piezoresistive force sensors often fail to simulate tactile perception accurately on dynamic surfaces such as moving joints, due to the extra resistance under tensile conditions. The tension-induced resistance changes are difficult to distinguish from those caused by force, resulting in inaccurate force responses26–29. Additionally, in applications such as tactile-based object recognition and electronic skin systems, force sensors should match different measurement demands ranging from slight touch to high burdens30,31. Besides, under drastic conditions such as rapid movements or repeated bending, traditional sensors tend to be damaged due to their rigidity and fragility32,33. Therefore, the force sensor that is self-recovering and insensitive to tensile strain while capable of capturing signals in a tunable measuring range is developed in this study to ensure the reliability, accuracy, and duration time.

Here, we present a programmable living force sensor fabricated by surface-engineered microbial network with the bottom-up design for human–machine interfaces (Fig. 1). Escherichia coli strain MG1655 cells are genetically rewired as monomers to individually display two different surface-anchored affinity pairs, the non-covalent interaction of C-tag (Ag) with anti-C-tag nanobodies (Nb), and the covalent interaction of SpyCatcher (SC) with SpyTag (ST). The assembly of bacterial cells (Ag cells with Nb cells, or SC cells with ST cells) leads to the living network in macroscale. The microbial network is further leveraged as the conductive and sensing layer and injected inside the integrally formed flexible tubes to fabricate the force sensor. The living sensor is responsive to force in a tunable measuring range, demonstrating self-recovery within 39 ms after rupture, and is stretching-insensitive with only a 30% increase in resistance at 800% of elongation. The living sensor is programmable across multiple scales, from the molecular level to macrostructure, to match the measurements in various conditions. This bio-integrated approach enables the use of microbes to prepare robust and reconfigurable force sensors for human–machine interfaces, which could be suitable for tensile, dynamic, and extreme mechanical conditions.Fig. 1Design of microbial network and preparation of the living force sensor.The use of two adhesion pairs, C-tag (Ag) and Anti-C-tag nanobody (Nb) for non-covalent interaction, SpyCatcher (SC) and SpyTag (ST) for covalent interaction. Results

The surface-engineered bacterial network was employed as the sensing layer of the living force sensor

In this study, two affinity pairs were employed to anchor at the outer membrane of bacterial cells using the surface display toolbox (Fig. 2a). Neae is a bacterial surface display system that is used to anchor the target protein at the outer membrane. The C-tag (antigen, noted as Ag) or anti-C-tag nanobodies (Nb) were displayed using the Neae system as described in a previous study14. The interaction of the antigen and nanobodies represented the non-covalent affinity among bacterial cells. We continued to employ the Neae system and displayed the SpyCatcher (SC) or SpyTag (ST) at the bacterial surface. Each kind of bacteria was induced for protein expression, and each outer membrane sample was prepared. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis confirmed the successful expression of Ag, Nb, SC, and ST proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1). The interaction of SC and ST drove the covalent affinity among bacterial cells. Each kind of engineered bacteria was used as monomers to assemble the living network. Mixing Ag cells with Nb cells, or SC cells with ST cells, resulted in the gradual aggregation (Fig. 2b). The aggregation of bacteria indicated the successful expression of the surface-anchored proteins (Ag, Nb, SC, or ST) that drove the assembly of bacterial cells effectively. As shown, ~70% of SC and ST cells assembled within 6 h after mixing, driven by the interaction of SC with ST on the bacterial surface, which was slower than that of Ag and Nb cells (3 h for ~70% of cells to form the bacterial assembly). After 12 h of incubation, ~90% of Ag with Nb cells and ~85% of SC with ST cells assembled to the living mixture. We then investigated dynamic shear rheology of the two kinds of living mixture and compared to the un-assembled non-induced cells (NI cells), including the storage modulus (G’), loss modulus (G”), viscosity (η), and the cross-linking density (Mc−1) (Fig. 2c–f). As shown, the affinity and assembly of the bacteria improved the storage modulus about two folds for Nb with Ag cells, and three folds for ST with SC cells compared with un-assembled NI cells (Fig. 2c, d). The viscosity of the two cell mixtures showed ~10 folds of increase compared with free bacterial cells (Fig. 2e). We also provided additional comparison among the non-mixed bacteria displaying one component of the adhesion pair (Ag, Nb, SC or ST) in Supplementary Fig. 3. As shown, without mixing the induced bacteria according to the adhesion pair, the non-assembled bacteria exhibited similar aggregation behavior and rheological properties. The mechanical properties were improved by the adhesion-mediated intercellular cross-linking rather than intrinsic differences among individual strains. Thus, the living sensor encapsulated with NI cells was used as a control in the following. According to the rheology assay, the cross-linking density for the SC with ST cells was higher than that of Ag with Nb cells (Fig. 2f). The assembly of the bacterial cells led to the hydrogel-like mixture, providing the potential for the fabrication and applications as a living sensor.

We then quantified the adhesive force (Fad) between the two adhesive bacterial cells (Ag with Nb cells, SC with ST cells) using the atomic force microscopy (AFM, Supplementary Fig. 4, Fig. 2g, h, n = 50). As shown, for each kind of surface-engineered bacteria, the average Fad was ~20 nN between two bacterial cells displaying the same motif (19.98 nN for Ag cells, 19.74 nN for Nb cells, 19.89 nN for SC cells, and 18.16 nN for ST cells, Supplementary Fig. 5). After assembly as design (Ag cells with Nb cells, SC cells with ST cells), the Fad was dramatically increased. The interaction between SC and ST cells was 92 nN, which was obviously higher than that of Ag and Nb cells at 73 nN. Referring to the rupture force, we then estimated the number of adhesion pairs between two bacterial cells. The rupture force was ~16 pN between a single complementary Ag and Nb34, and the number of interaction pairs between Ag and Nb cells was calculated as ~3300. As to an individual complementary SC and ST, the rupture force was 1 nN35, indicating that the adhesion number between SC-ST assembled living mixture was ~73. During the estimation, the intrinsic interaction between the bacteria themselves (~20 nN) was subtracted. The display of adhesion pairs drove the assembly of bacteria and strengthened the living networks. Results indicated that the mechanical strength of SC-ST bacterial assembly was higher than that of Ag-Nb as well as un-adhered NI cells.

To further investigate the assembly process of bacteria with two adhesion pairs, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was performed to quantify the binding affinity (Fig. 2i–k and Supplementary Fig. 6). As shown, the dissociation constant (Kd) of Ag-Nb bacterial assembly was 4.63 CFU L−1 during assembly, and 3.41 CFU L−1 for SC-ST assembly. Thus, the SC-ST bacterial assembly exhibited a stronger binding affinity. The higher Kd of Ag-Nb bacterial assembly conferred a more rapid response to external force compared to that of SC-ST, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of the living sensor. Alternatively, the stronger binding affinity of SC with ST cells conferred the potential advantages in terms of stability against external stress variations, such as stretching insensitivity. The assembly of bacterial cells obviously improved the conductivity owing to the extracellular adhesion13. After characterizing the physical and material properties, bacteria displaying Ag, Nb, SC, or ST on the surface were prepared and mixed accordingly. Bacterial assembly (Nb with Ag cells, SC with ST cells) was leveraged as the sensing layer and injected inside the integrally formed soft elastic tubes to prepare the living sensor. The soft tube was insulative and served as the mechanical support to encapsulate, shape, and fabricate the bacterial assembly.

The living sensor could be programmed to sense force in a tunable measuring range

Flexible force sensor plays a critical role in advanced applications such as robotic prosthetics, biomedical signal monitoring in smart healthcare, and human–machine interaction in virtual or augmented reality. The measuring range is one of the key parameters for their performance and applications of force sensors36. A desired force sensor should maintain its sensitivity and accuracy in a tunable measuring range from subtle tactile stimuli to high-intensity forces37. In our study, the engineered bacterial assembly was employed as the sensing layer, and the integrally formed elastic tube provides the mechanical support to shape and encapsulate the living cells. We firstly tested the tubes with different Young’s modulus (Supplementary Fig. 7), including silicone (1200 MPa), latex (1000 MPa), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC, 3000 MPa). As shown (Supplementary Fig. 7a), encapsulated into a silicone tube, the force sensor provided an applicable response. On the other hand, despite better stretchability, the latex tube is limited in withstanding high-force loads due to its softness. The PVC tube was too rigid to respond to force in time, with obvious change of resistance only occurring under high force (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Additionally, the thicker walls and larger inner diameters also delayed the response of normal force (Supplementary Fig. 7b and S7c). Thus, a silicone tube (with 1 mm of wall thickness and 1 mm of inner diameter) was used to fabricate the living force sensor.

To test the performance during the measurement of force, the bacterial assembly or NI cells were injected and encapsulated into the flexible silicone tubes (25 cm in length), which were then folded with intersecting points (Fig. 3a). The instruments for the experiments are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a–c. The fabricated living sensor was placed onto a customized high-precision 3D motion platform, which was designed to control the normal force through a panel and record the corresponding displacement as well as the ratio of relative resistance change (ΔR/R0) of the sensor. The finite element analysis (FEA) was first performed to mimic the shape change and the distribution of the surface stress on the intersecting points during the pressing process (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Movie 1). Accordingly, the stress was concentrated on the intersecting area. The force caused the deformation of the living sensing layer inside the tube and increased the resistance due to the reduced flow area for current, revealing the potential of the bacterial assembly to sense the force. We then performed the pressing experiments to confirm the force-sensing ability of the living sensor. When the displacement of the panel reached 1.4 mm, the ΔR/R0 of the living sensor began to change and demonstrated an obvious response to the external force, while the control group fabricated with NI cells inside kept stable. To further investigate the force-sensing ability of the living sensor, different numbers of prisms were equipped onto a plate to recur the intersecting points (Fig. 3c). The instruments were shown in Supplementary Fig. 8d–f. The force applied to the plate was indirectly measured at the flat anchored with the living sensor. The bacterial assembly (Ag with Nb cells) was exploited to fabricate the living force sensor (10 cm in length). The normal force was applied through the plate with prisms to the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor. Using one prism to press the living sensor, we tested the influence of the conical angle (30°, 60°, and 90°) on the force response (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 7d). During the pressing process, the displacement of the plate and force showed a linear correlation. The resistance change rate of the living sensor increased with the descent of the plate. The relationship could be obtained between the resistance change rate and force accordingly. As present, the prisms with a smaller angle (contacting area) concentrate the transmitted force to the living sensor, leading to the increasing sensitivity. We then used the prisms at 30° to further explore the performance of the living sensor.

Leveraging the concept of an interpenetrating network in the field of hydrogel, we investigated the force response under normal force by mixing Ag-Nb and SC-ST living network (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7e). The Ag-Nb cells assembled living sensor demonstrated a faster change of resistance during the pressing process than that of the sensor by SC-ST cells. Mixing different ratios (4:1, 1:1, 1:4) of the living network could modulate the force-sensing ability. We then calculated the sensitivity of the living sensors encapsulated with two kinds of bacterial assemblies as the sensing layer (Fig. 3f). The force sensitivity is revealed as how much the electrical signal of a sensor changed in response to the applied force, and is typically defined as the ratio of relative resistance change (ΔR/R0) to force variation. As shown, the force sensitivity of the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor could be divided into three distinct regions as 6.29–13.65, 13.65–21.26, and 21.26–27.63 N, and the sensitivity was calculated as 0.96, 0.36, and 0.14 N−1, respectively. On the other hand, the force sensitivity of the SC-ST assembled living sensor could be divided into two regions as 3.65–17.38 and 17.38–33.85 N, with the sensitivity calculated as 0.32 and 0.17 N−1, respectively. As a result, fabricated as the living force sensor, the Ag-Nb assembly exhibited superior sensitivity than that of the SC-ST assembly when the force ranged from 6.29 to 27.63 N, owing to the higher Kd of Ag-Nb. Thus, the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor was selected to test the force in the following due to its high-sensitivity to force.

We have found that the measuring range could be extended by pressing an increasing number of intersecting points (Fig. 3a). To acquire precise control during the pressing process, we used different numbers of prisms (1, 3, and 5) with the conical angle at 30°. As expected, the pressing process with five prisms realized the measurement of the normal force up to 80 N (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 7f). The minimum detectable force and the sensitivity across different force ranges were provided in Supplementary Table 1. As shown, even though the minimum detectable force increased with the number of loading points, the operational force range was correspondingly extended, and the sensitivity remained high within each working range.

In the experiments using prisms, the distance between two adjacent pressing points was fixed at 15 mm. We investigated the distance at which the two pressing points began to interact mechanically and altered the curvature of the tube. The distance between the two centers of the pressing points was measured. The results showed that the curvature started to change when the distance was shorter than 8 mm (Supplementary Figs. 9a and S9b). To further validate the influence of curvature to the sensor, we set the distance between two pressing points at 15 mm (discrete), 8 mm (critical), and 5 mm (dense) respectively, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 9c. Accordingly, when the distance was set at 15 mm or 8 mm, the response of the living sensor was almost the same, since the curvature of the tube was not influenced. When the distance between two pressing points decreased to 5 mm, the sensing performance deteriorated since the curvature of the tube changed. The deformation at two pressing points overlapped, which redistributed the stress. Thus, the distance between the two prisms of 15 mm could ensure the mechanical independence.

We then carried out the computational simulations to mimic the response of the normal force with more pressing points (10, 30, and 50, Fig. 3h). As shown, the assembled living force sensor could measure up to 2000 N of the normal force when the number of pressing points reached 50. To verify the simulation results experimentally, we evaluated the response (ΔR/R0) of the living sensors with 30 and 50 pressing points (Supplementary Fig. 10). One prism was set to press five points for the sensor with 30 pressing points. As to the sensor with 50 pressing points, one prism was set to press eight or nine points. The size of the sensor was about 6 cm × 6 cm × 1.6 cm. The distance between prisms was fixed at 1 cm. A participant with a body weight of 93 kg, who stepped on the living sensor to load the force. After stepping, the ΔR/R0 for the living sensor with 50 points was 434.87, which was higher than that of the sensor with 30 points (311.72). Results were consistent with the simulation results. Due to the limitations of the laboratory instruments, we did not perform the experiments directly with higher force loading. Consequently, increasing the number of pressing points could extend the measurement limit and realize the detection of force in a tunable measuring range. Thus, the living force sensor was programmable in microscale by adjusting the adhesion pairs in the bacterial network, and in macrostructure by modulating the number of pressing points or contacting area. The upper limit could be tailored on demand.

To evaluate the cycling stability of the living sensor, we performed 10 cycles of force loading and unloading at 10 N (Fig. 3i). The signal of the sensor could return to its original level consistently after each cycle. To ensure reproducibility, we repeated the experiments three times using independent tubes (Supplementary Fig. 11). As shown, each test demonstrated similar and stable results, indicating the repeatable performance of the living sensor.

We then investigated the environmental stability of the living sensor. Confined inside the tube, the bacterial network could be physically and electrically isolated from the external environment. Silicone tubing was selected due to its excellent insulation and environmental stability. Silicone elastomers typically exhibited a high-volume resistivity (1014–1016 Ω cm), effectively preventing electrical interference from the surroundings38–40. In addition, silicone has a very low water absorption rate, approximately ~1% after prolonged immersion41. The mechanical properties of silicone tubing remain largely unchanged under humid conditions. It also possessed a wide environmental tolerance, with a safe working pH range of ~4–10 and a temperature tolerance from −60 to 200 °C42–44. On the other hand, as the biological samples, the bacterial assembly inside the tube could be influenced by the temperature. Higher temperatures may cause the denaturation of proteins. Therefore, we investigated the influence of temperature on the sensing performance (Supplementary Fig. 12). As presented, the force response at 25 °C and 37 °C was nearly identical, while the sensing performance decreased at 4 °C, 42 °C, and 50 °C. The reduction was mainly attributed to changes in the biological conductive network rather than the tube. At 4 °C, the reduced cellular activity and membrane dynamics weaken intercellular electrical connectivity. At temperatures (42 °C, 50 °C) higher than the physical condition (37 °C), the partial denaturation of proteins would influence the continuity of the bacterial network, leading to decreased sensing.

We then explore the performance of the sensing layer over extended storage periods. Bacterial assemblies were prepared and stored at 4 °C. After different months, the living sensors were prepared using the stored bacterial assemblies. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, the response of force kept similar during the first 3 months. After 6 months, the living sensor could still function, but the maximum resistance change rate decreased by 7%, and further decline 10% after 9 months, suggesting partial deterioration of the biological conductive network during storage. Results indicated that the performance of the sensing layer can be preserved after prolonged storage properly at low temperature, with gradual deterioration in performance over time.

To explore the force-sensing mechanism of the living sensor, FEA was performed to simulate the change of adhesion pairs in the bacterial network in the microscale when responding to strain. The adhesion pairs on the bacterial surface between two cells were grouped and represented as three connecting entities in the model (Fig. 3j). Result showed that the trend of stress in the connecting entities (Fig. 3k) was consistent with those in the experiments (Fig. 3f) during the compression, suggesting the relationship between bacterial adhesion pairs and the response to force. In the FEA result, the trend of the stress could be divided in three stages. Initially, the stress changed slowly as the bacterial network was relatively intact (0–0.4 μm), revealing a stable response. Next, with the increase of stress (0.4–1 μm), the bacterial network failed to maintain due to the tight compression among bacteria, which underwent the continuous rupture of adhesion pairs and caused the progressive loss of the network. The critical stress for rupture depended on the strength of different adhesion pairs. During this stage, the resistance of the living sensor increased linearly as the force increased, which was the effective stage to sense force. In the third stage, with the further increase of the force, the adhesion pairs were almost consumed within the bacterial network. The insufficient interaction and continuous disruption of the bacterial network diminished force sensitivity, and the change of stress became flat, thus failing to respond to force. In brief, the adhesion pairs among the assembled bacterial network enabled the force response of the living sensor. Under pressing conditions, the continuous disruption of the adhesion pairs among the bacterial network in the living sensor led to the linear response in resistance to force.

The living sensor could be programmed to respond to force, enabling the signal transmission in various types, such as audio signals. We employed Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) technology to convert the force signal into an audio signal through an Arduino processor (Fig. 3l, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Movie 2). The single press could reach the upper limit at 5 kHz, while the double press could extend the upper limit to 10 kHz (Fig. 3m). By design, the force signal was mapped to the audio frequency in a tunable measuring range. The output audio signal demonstrated good stability under different forces (Supplementary Fig. 15a) and continuous usage (Supplementary Fig. 15b), confirming the potential applications of the living force sensor in acoustic communication and other fields besides force measurement.

However, traditional force sensors were limited by the degeneration and damage during the repeated usage, confining the applications in high reliability and continuous operations45. The frequent replacements of the worn-out sensors also restricted the stability and overall performance of the system. In our previous study, we confirmed the fast self-recovery ability of the living assembly by Ag-Nb cells13, providing the possibility of a living sensor for continuous usage. In this study, we further investigated and compared the self-recovery property of the living network made from the Ag-Nb cells as well as the SC-ST cells. To image the self-recovery process, the bacterial cells displaying GFP-Ag were mixed with those displaying mCherry-Nb and fabricated into the soft silicone tubes (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Movies 3–5). As shown, the fluorescent signal visualized and recorded the self-recovery process from the interrupted state to its recovery. We then measured the self-recovery time (Fig. 4c). The Ag-Nb cells assembled a living sensor recovered 99% of its original resistance within 39 ms after being fully interrupted. For the SC-ST assembled living sensor, the time was 90 ms for 90% of recovery of its original resistance, and 195 s for 99% of recovery (Fig. 4d). We then tested the recovery ability after incomplete interruption, which could also reveal the sensing process (Fig. 4e). The living sensor fabricated with Ag-Nb bacterial assembly demonstrated similar response time (110 ms) with that of SC-ST (130 ms) when loading force. On the other hand, after removal of the force, the recovery time was 30 ms for the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor, which was faster than that of SC-ST (80 ms). The faster response of the Ag-Nb bacterial assembly to force was attributed to their higher Kd. The faster recovery rate of the Ag-Nb bacterial assembly was contingent upon its faster aggregation. To further validate the self-recovery performance in repeated operations, we conducted the pressing test for 10 cycles by interrupting the assembled bacterial cells inside the tube for 5 s and then let the sensor recover (Fig. 4f). The Ag-Nb assembled living sensor could regenerate the original current quickly after each press. As to the SC-ST assembled living sensor, the recovered current demonstrated a gradual decrease, revealing the partial loss of conductivity during the rapid press. Moreover, the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor could maintain the consistent and stable force responses before or after the self-recovery process (Fig. 4g). We repeated the experiments for three times using independent tubes to confirm the reliability of the self-recovery process (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). As shown, the self-recovery behavior was repeatable with similar results. We then compared the living force sensor with a commercial flexible sensor under cracking conditions (Supplementary Fig. 18). The flexible sensor made from graphene was a commercial and common piezoresistive force sensor. As shown, due to the lack of self-recovery ability, the graphene sensor lost function after fracture.

To further understand the self-recovery process, FEA was then performed to simulate the behavior of adhesive bacteria at the microscale (Fig. 4h). Under force, the interruption of the bacterial network in the elastic tube led to the disconnection of the current. Upon the exertion of force, the interruption of the living network prevented the electrical connection among bacteria. After the removal of the external force, the elasticity of the tube extruded the separated bacteria back and allowed the re-adhesion among cells due to the affinity pairs, leading to the restoration of the electrical connectivity. The self-recovery ability of the living sensor could be attributed to dual factors. The external factor was the elasticity of the soft silicone tube that drove the separated bacteria back, whereas the internal factor was the re-formation of the adhesion pairs among the surface-engineered bacteria. Thus, the self-recovery rate of the living sensor was related to the aggregation rate of the bacterial cells. The higher Kd of Ag-Nb bacterial assembly enabled a more rapid response to external force compared to that of SC-ST.

The force sensing and fast self-recovery properties of the living sensor could be applied to the detection of large crash or punch that required the continuous measurement under high-intensity or drastic conditions, even with potential damage. To mimic the use in a punch, the living sensor encapsulated with Ag-Nb bacterial assembly was anchored on a sandbag to monitor punching behavior (Fig. 4i). To amplify the signal, a current boosting circuit was designed with two living sensors and two power sources connected in the forward direction of the circuit. We recorded the current change during punches and compared the current in the original and boosting circuits. As expected, the signal waveform generated by the boosting circuit was much stronger than that of the original circuit during punching (Fig. 4j). Thus, combining living sensors and the design of circuits could effectively enlarge the signals. Besides, even though the excessive impact caused interruptions during punching, the living force sensor was capable to recover the current output to the original state, also demonstrating the potential applications in wearable devices under high-force conditions.

The living force sensor was stretching-insensitive

The stretchable force sensor is an essential tool to monitor movements and external stimulations of human bodies46,47. However, under transverse tensile strain, the corresponding signals of the force sensors vary dramatically. The tension-induced extra resistance changes are difficult to distinguish from those caused by force, resulting in the unstable sensing performance48,49. In the following, we evaluated the performance of the living force sensor under the stretching condition. Latex tubes were highly stretchable and thereby employed to encapsulate the living network to prepare a living force sensor. To evaluate the sensing performance under stretching conditions, a shorter (8 cm in length) and thicker (2 mm for the inner diameter) tube was used to accommodate the large deformation within the displacement limits of the experimental instrument. The shear Fad of the living sensing layer was firstly quantified on the inner surface of the latex tube (Supplementary Fig. 19). The shear Fad was 3935 N m−² for Ag-Nb bacterial assembly, 7255 N m−² for SC-ST bacterial assembly, and 2340 N m−² for un-adhesive free bacterial cells. Thus, the bacterial assembly or NI cells demonstrated strong adhesion on the latex substrate, supporting their applicability under stretching conditions. Fabricated inside the latex tubes, the living sensor could be stretched up to eight folds of its original length (Fig. 5a). The FEA was carried out to mimic the stress distribution on the surface of the living sensor during the stretching process (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Movie 6). As shown, the corresponding stress was symmetrically distributed across the two ends of the latex tube. The excellent elasticity of the latex tube provided a stable stress distribution for the living sensor throughout the stretching process. The performance of the living force sensor was then experimentally evaluated under large deformation conditions by stretching, and the ratio of resistance change was recorded during the process. As shown in Fig. 5c, the living sensor encapsulated with SC-ST bacterial assembly showed seldom change after stretching to six folds of its original length. Upon stretching to eight folds, the resistance only increased by ~30% compared to the initial value. For Ag-Nb bacterial assembly in the living sensor, the resistance kept relatively stable up to 3 times of its original length. Nevertheless, further extension to eight folds caused the rise of resistance up to 500% of the initial value. As to the living sensor fabricated with un-adhered NI cells, the resistance changed up to 750% of the original value with the increase of length. Thus, the SC-ST assembled living sensor exhibited superior stretching-insensitive ability, which could transduce a stable signal under large deformation conditions. The living sensor was then performed 10 cycles of stretching tests (Fig. 5d). The signals of the SC-ST assembled living sensor kept stable during each stretching test. Encapsulated with Ag-Nb living assembly, the signals were relatively stable when stretched to three folds of its original length during the repeated tests. Nevertheless, further extension to eight folds of the original length, the resistance increased and fluctuated during the cyclic stretching. As to the sensor with un-adhered free bacterial cells, the resistance varied a lot during the cyclic tests. We also prepared the living sensor by mixing two kinds of living networks (Ag-Nb and SC-ST bacterial assembly) as the interpenetrating bacterial network at a ratio of 1:1. The resistance increased 200% of its initial value upon stretching to eight folds of the original length, indicating that the strain-sensitivity could be tailored and programmed on demand. Besides, the living sensor was investigated under different stretching frequencies (0.2, 0.5, and 1 Hz, Fig. 5e). The SC-ST assembled living sensor also presented the frequency stability under continuous usage. Consequently, the SC-ST assembled living sensor demonstrated well strain-insensitivity during cyclic stretching, offering a potential for stable transduction of signal under large deformation conditions. To explore the force sensing under the tensile condition, the living sensor encapsulated with Ag-Nb or SC-ST assembly was stretched to two, four, six, and eight folds of its original length, loaded onto the 3D motion platform and pressed by one prism (Supplementary Fig. 21). As shown, the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor maintained a consistent force sensitivity within the 0–20 N range at two folds of its original length. Further stretching to higher folds gradually decreased the force sensitivity. On the other hand, the SC-ST assembled living sensor exhibited higher stretching tolerance and maintained nearly unchanged force sensitivity within the 0–20 N range even at six folds of its original length. As a result, the living sensors could maintain a reliable measurement of normal force under large mechanical strain. The sensitivity gradually decreased at the extreme strain levels.

Theoretically, the continuous network enables the internal connections against rotation, bending, and twist rather than broken directly under tensile forces, which maintains the integrity and continuity of the network during stretching and ensures the strain insensitivity50. To investigate the mechanism of stretching-insensitivity for the living network, the FEA was employed to simulate the stretching process. Upon stretching, the stress gradually increased on the affinity pairs (Fig. 5f). At the initial of stretching, seldom stress was found on the affinity pairs, since the bacterial cells mainly underwent rotation, bending, or twisting. The stretching of the living sensor rearranged the bacterial cells from random orientations to a fixed direction along the stretching direction. During the re-arrangement process, the stress was at a low level on the affinity pairs, which preserved the integrity and continuity of the network away from direct rupture, and thus, in turn, kept the stable resistance of the living sensor (Fig. 5g). After further stretching, the stress was gradually increased on the affinity pairs. When the corresponding stress was over the critical stress of the affinity pairs (the Fad tested by AFM), the linkage would break down, leading to the disruption of the living network. The lower Kd and higher Fad of the SC-ST affinity pairs indicated that SC-ST living assembly possessed a stronger binding affinity with greater stretching-insensitivity to resist the external stress.

The stretching-insensitivity of the living sensor could be used under large deformations. We then applied the living sensor to measure the movements of the elbow (Fig. 5h) and wrist joints (Supplementary Fig. 22a), where significant strain occurred during joint flexion. The signal-to-noise ratio was always treated as the target of optimization due to the non-negligible background noise, which might come from the resistance change of the interconnects51. The resistance changed along with the joint movements for the Ag-Nb cells assembled living sensor, while revealing a stable signal for the SC-ST cells assembled living sensor. Mixing two assemblies as the interpenetrating network could modulate the signal intensity (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 22b). The change rate of resistance was close to 0 during the measurement for the SC-ST assembled living sensor at different bending degrees when detecting the bending of elbow (Fig. 5j). When placed on the wrist, we could still find a narrow range of resistance during the movement, which was close to 0 (Supplementary Fig. 22c). In brief, the SC-ST assembled living sensor showed the strain-insensitivity under tensile and large deformation conditions, providing an alternative approach for the development of wearable and flexible electronic devices with reduced background noise and enhanced signal-to-noise ratio.

Applications of the living force sensor in object recognition and electronic skin

Force sensors are always exploited to mimic the tactile sensing of human skin. Particularly, tactile perception plays a vital role in object identification in scenarios where visual recognition is limited or impaired. In this study, we employed the surface-engineered living network as the sensing layer to prepare the programmable living force sensor, which was force-responsive, self-recovering, and stretching-insensitive. In the following, we integrated the features of the living force sensor and applied it to the field of human–machine interactions, including object recognition and haptic sensing of electronic skins.

For object recognition, we demonstrated a smart glove equipped with the living force sensor for identifying and sorting fruits with different ripeness. The glove was equipped with five interconnected living force sensors extending from the knuckle to the fingertips, with each fingertip serving as one force perception channel (Fig. 6a), and was used to grasp bananas in different levels of ripeness (unripe, moderately ripe, soft). Each grasp was repeated for 10 times. As shown (Fig. 6b–d), the glove could effectively recognize bananas with different firmness. Equipped with an Ag-Nb assembled living sensor, the glove already generated signals even when the fingers were bent but not yet grasped the objects, leading to misjudgment from the machine. On the other hand, the SC-ST assembled living sensor was stretching-insensitive, which allowed the accurate signal recognition during grasping. Nevertheless, the perception signal was not as strong as that of the Ag-Nb. Mixing the two bacterial assemblies reduced the range of erroneous signals while increasing the intensity of the force perception signal. The distinction in the firmness of bananas after grasping was due to the sensing of force by living sensors on the smart glove. Unripe bananas were firmer and resisted deformation, concentrating force on a smaller contact area and thus generating a higher localized force on the sensor. In contrast, ripe bananas were softer and tended to deform over a larger area, thereby dispersing the applied force. Therefore, the glove detected lower force signals from softer fruits. The force variation enabled the glove to distinguish ripeness based on mechanical perception. The smart glove equipped with living sensors realized the identification of fruits at different stages of ripeness through the simulated tactile sensing, attributing to reduce mistakes caused by non-selective mechanical harvesting.

In another design, we installed the SC-ST assembled living sensor at the movement areas of finger joints of the glove to ensure signal accuracy, and Ag-Nb assembled living sensor at the fingertips to enhance the intensity of the force perception signal (Fig. 6e). As shown, due to the stretching-insensitivity of the SC-ST assembly, the smart glove could determine the ripeness of the banana without misled by joint bending. Meanwhile, the Ag-Nb assembly at the fingertips was more sensitive to force, ensuring sufficient signal strength for sorting. This case highlighted the feasibility and advantages of constructing smart wearable systems entirely from bacterial living materials. The glove utilizes two distinct bio-derived components: SC-ST assembly as the conductor and Ag-Nb assembly as the force-sensing layer, completely replacing traditional electronic conductors and sensors. The all-biological system fulfilled functional demands and overcame durability challenges commonly faced by conventional electronics in dynamic environments. Functionally, the smart glove could detect differences in fruit firmness through force variations, making it ideal for sorting and harvesting fruits with varying ripeness. By interpreting force responses of local positions, the system could accurately assess fruit maturity, enabling the non-destructive automated classification. We demonstrated a potential and promising role of living material-based smart devices in the applications of next-generation agriculture.

The design of human–machine interfaces is expected to integrate with electronic skin to endow haptic functions similar to those of humans, enabling to perceive and interact with surroundings keenly52. Stretchable force sensors have demonstrated broad prospects in soft robotics and electronic skin. However, when sensing external forces on the surface skin, the stretching deformation and damage risk of the devices dramatically interferes with the accuracy of the perceived information53. In the following, we applied the living sensor on the auxiliary electronic skin that required accurate responses to force under tensile conditions and resisted to potential damage. The living sensor was attached to the skin to identify different types of external forces, including finger touch, palm knock and fist strike (Fig. 6f). Due to the stretching-insensitivity, the SC-ST assembled living sensor obtained the same electrical signal in both bending and unbending states (Fig. 6g). The signal for the Ag-Nb assembled living sensor presented clear changes for each type of external force (Fig. 6h), while the interpenetrating bacterial network balanced the signal strength and authenticity (Fig. 6i). Additionally, the living sensor exhibited self-recovery capacity even after damaged by fist strikes. The integration of the two assemblies effectively tailored the force-sensing ability and signal authenticity. The electronic skin with tactile perception in our study could serve as part of a soft robot with stable signals during movements, highlighting the potential applications in the electronic skin system.

Discussion

ELMs have extended to the field of electronics by integrating the programmed cells with the materials as electronic devices. Powered by the rational design of gene circuits, cells in living materials can be rewired on purpose to acquire sophisticated systems. In this study, we exploit the surface-engineered bacterial network as the sensing layer to prepare the programmable living force sensor, and applied to human–machine interfaces for stable and reliable signal transductions. The living sensor is responsive to force in a tunable measuring range, self-recovering within 39 ms after rupture, and remarkably, stretching-insensitive with only a 30% increase in resistance at 800% of elongation.

The response of the living sensor to normal force is attributed to the protein-mediated conductive network formed by the surface-engineered bacteria. Through protein interaction, bacteria are linked to form a percolated network with intercellular junctions. The assembled sensing layer presents a multi-scale hierarchical structure. Escherichia coli possesses a complete cellular structure and is rod-shaped with a diameter of approximately 0.5 μm and a length of 1–3 μm54. An E. coli cell, together with its engineered surface proteins, is an integrated entity. Upon the application of an external normal force, the stress is transmitted across the network, which induces the obvious localized deformation and strain concentration at the interface of the adhered bacteria. The mechanical deformation drives the separation of bacteria from the adhered proteins displayed on the surface. During the deformation, the slight movement of bacteria leads to a larger relative displacement of protein adhesion pairs, disrupting the conductive connections gradually. This reduces the number of conductive pathways and increases the interfacial resistance, leading to an increase in the overall resistance. Within a moderate force range, this process occurs gradually, resulting in the observed linear response.

The living sensor is programmable. Structurally, the living force sensor can be designed and programmed at the molecular level, cellular level, macrolevel, and macrostructure. At the molecular level, two surface-anchored adhesion pairs (C-tag and anti-C-tag nanobody, SpyCatcher and SpyTag) are employed to drive the non-covalent or covalent interaction. Other protein linker pairs can also be applied to modulate the adhesion among the bacterial networks. At the cellular level, bacterial cells are genetically programmed to display and anchor each component of the adhesion pairs individually at the outer membrane using the surface display toolbox. To reach the macrolevel, the interaction between the adhesion pairs propels the surface-engineered bacterial cells to form living microbial networks. The performance of the sensing layer can be tailored by mixing different kinds of networks and adjusting the ratio. At the macrostructure, the sensing layer can be encapsulated into the soft tube with different mechanical properties. Additionally, the living sensor can be prepared with different lengths and fabricated into different structures. With the bottom-up design, the living force sensor can be programmed to match the measurements in various conditions.

The living force sensor is programmable functionally with a purpose-driven design in structure. The living sensor can be programmed to exhibit a tunable measuring range by changing the number of pressing points or tuning the ratio of different assemblies. Increasing the pressing points would redistribute the applied force across multiple localized regions, thereby reducing the effective load at each individual region and slowing the disruption rate of the network. The conductive pathway is then gradually deformed with the increase of force, thereby extending the measurable force range effectively. On the other hand, the integration of the two assemblies could tailor and balance the force sensing and tensile insensitivity. Compared with the two kinds of microbial networks in this study, the Ag-Nb bacterial assembly presented a higher Kd, while the adhesion force between SC and ST was stronger. The higher Kd of the adhesion pairs among the bacterial network conferred a more rapid response to external pressure and a faster reformation. The stronger interaction force among the living microbial network facilitated the stretching-insensitivity of the living sensor.

Besides, the living sensor is self-recovering. The adhesion pairs also empower the self-recovery ability of the living sensor in tens of milliseconds after rupture. The self-recovery arises from the ability of bacteria to re-establish intercellular connections mediated by surface adhesion pairs. After mechanical damage, the separated bacteria can reconnect through the interaction of the adhesin pairs for the reconstruction of the conductive network and the recovery of the electrical response. The higher Kd of the adhesion pairs among bacterial networks confers a more rapid response to external force and a faster reformation.

Furthermore, the stronger interaction force among the living microbial network facilitates the stretching-insensitivity of the living sensor. During stretching, the microbial network accommodates the deformation primarily through rotation, bending, and re-orientation of bacterial cells rather than direct rupture of intercellular connections. Stretching the living sensor re-arranges the bacterial cells from random orientations to a fixed direction along the stretching direction. The adhesion pairs preserve the integrity and continuity of the bacterial network against direct rupture, thereby maintaining the stable resistance of the living sensor. Taken together, these results establish a clear relationship of structure and property in the living sensor, where the interplay between the biologically mediated network and the engineered structural design governs the sensitivity, tunability, and mechanical robustness of the device.

To produce the living sensor on a large scale, the scalability of the living sensor is discussed from three aspects: the soft tube, the preparation of bacterial assembly, and the encapsulation of the living sensor. The soft tube used in this study is commercial, which has already been produced on a large scale. As to the engineered bacteria, they can be cultured on a large scale by fermenter. Since the bacterial network aggregates after assembly, they can be separated from the supernatant by filtration or centrifugation. Additionally, the encapsulation process relies on tube filling and end sealing, both of which are established and readily automated in industrial manufacturing, for example, through continuous dispensing and heat-sealing processes. These processes enable high-throughput and reproducible device production. After tubing the bacterial assembly, the prepared tube can be fabricated into devices in accordance with individual requirements. The component for pressing, such as the fixed prisms, can be designed and prepared on demand using 3D printing technology. The device does not require complex microfabrication or lithographic steps, further supporting its scalability and cost-effectiveness.

Nevertheless, despite the self-recovery capacity of the living sensing layer, the elastic tubes employed in this study are not self-recoverable, which may limit the performance of the living sensor if the soft tubes are damaged. The self-recovery elastic tubes can be used to prepare the living sensor in future work to realize better self-recovery capacity55,56. On the other hand, even though different protein linker pairs can be selected for the preparation of the bacterial network, it is not guaranteed that bacteria can display the surface-anchored proteins as desired. Screening the successful expression of the adhesion pairs in future work may also modulate the performance of the living sensor14,57.

To sum up, with the assistance of synthetic biology, protein engineering, and bioelectronics, bacterial cells can be engineered to prepare sensors for human–machine interfaces. We established a bio-integrated approach that enables microbes to prepare robust and reconfigurable force sensors for durable and reliable signals in continuous, dynamic, or even extreme mechanical conditions.

Methods

Ethics statement

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, Shenzhen, China (Approval No. ZB-KYIRB-AF/SC-08/02.0). The data were obtained with the written informed consent of the participants. Sex and gender were not considered as experimental variables because the study focused on demonstrating the functionalities of devices, which were not influenced by these factors.

Plasmids, strains, and growth media

The E. coli strain MG1655 was purchased from Tolo Biotech (Catalog No. 96147-01), transformed with the plasmid encoding the affinity part, and used for the fabrication of the living sensor. The plasmids (AmpR) were synthesized by the Genewiz Company (Soochow, China). Circuits rewiring cells to display C-tag (Ag) or Anti-C-tag nanobody (Nb) was published in the previous study14. Briefly, Ag or Nb was displayed onto the outer membrane using the Neae surface display toolbox. Neae is a bacterial surface display system from the intimin N-terminus of enterohemorrhagic E. coli., which includes a short N-terminal export tag for trafficking to the periplasm, a LysM domain for peptide glycan binding, and a β-barrel for transmembrane insertion.14 The Neae was also employed to anchor SC or ST onto the bacterial outer membrane in this study by replacing the sequence of the C-tag. For the fluorescent images, GFP or mCherry was fused to the N-terminus of Neae to acquire GFP-Neae-Ag or mCherry-Neae-Nb. The relative protein sequences could be found in the Supplementary Table 2. The protein expression was under the control of the tetracycline repressor (TetR) operator with pSC101 as the replication origin. The LB medium was prepared by mixing LB powder (Aladdin) with the deionized water at a concentration of 20 g L−1, autoclaved for 2 h, and cooled down to room temperature for further use. The medium was supplied with carbenicillin (70 μg mL−1) and anhydro-tetracycline (ATC, 100 ng mL−1) to induce protein expression when applicable.

Generation of the assembled bacteria

A single colony was picked, inoculated in LB medium supplied with carbenicillin, and cultured overnight (Nb cells, Ag cells, SC cells, and ST cells). As to the protein expression, each overnight culture was diluted to the fresh LB medium at a ratio of 1:100, supplemented with carbenicillin and induced by ATC. After culturing for 24 h, cells were mixed with an equal volume according to the adhesion pairs (Nb cells with Ag cells, and ST cells with SC cells). The mixture was settled down overnight to form the living network. For the interpenetrating assembly, Nb cells, Ag cells, SC cells, and ST cells were mixed at various ratios and settled down overnight. The bacterial assembly was collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min.

Characterization of the bacterial assembled living material

Bacterial affinity assay

Cells were cultured, and the protein expression of each kind of cell was induced as mentioned above. To describe the dynamic affinity process, each pair of cells was mixed with an equal volume and allowed to settle down at room temperature. 100 μL of the supernatant was collected at a series of time points for evaluating the optical density as the OD600 value. The assembled cells were calculated as (An−A0)/A0 × 100%, where An represented the OD600 value at a specific time, and A0 indicated the initial OD600 value at the start point.

Characterization of the adhesive force between the engineered bacterial cells

The atomic force microscope (AFM, Park System, XE-100) was used in the contact model to quantify the Fad between the surface-engineered bacterial cells (Ag cells with Nb cells, SC cells with ST cells). The Fad between two bacterial cells was detected according to the methods in previous studies58,59. The silicon dioxide (SiO2) particles (12.7 μm) were coated with polydopamine by adding to the solution of polydopamine (PDA, 4 mg/mL in 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.8) for 4 h at room temperature. The probe (NP-O10-A, Burker) was fixed with the PDA coated SiO2 particles and then dipped in the bacterial cells for further detection. The modified probe was turned down to measure the Fad with the bacteria on the substrate.

Force data obtained from AFM measurements were analyzed using the Gaussian fitting method to characterize the interaction between two individual bacterial cells. Fifty force-distance curves were collected for samples. Rheology measurement

The rheological properties of NI cells or bacterial assembly (Nb with Ag cells, ST with SC cells) were characterized using a strain-controlled rheometer (Anton Paar GmbH) equipped with an 8 mm diameter plate (200 µm the gap). The strain sweep tests were performed at a fixed frequency of 1 rad/s with a range from 0.1 to 10% strain amplitudes. The frequency sweep tests were conducted at a 1% strain amplitude with a range from 10 to 0.1 rad/s. The temperature was kept at 25 °C.

Calculation of the cross-linking density

The cross-linking density (Mc−1) of the living network was calculated according to the equation Mc−1 = E/ρRT, where E was the equilibrium storage modulus, ρ was the density of the material, R was the gas constant, and T was the absolute temperature in Kelvin. The equilibrium storage modulus (E) was obtained through frequency sweep measurements.

ITC assay

The binding affinities of Ag cells with Nb cells and SC cells with ST cells were measured using Nano ITC at 25 °C in PBS. Ag cells (4.8 × 106 CFU μL−1) were injected (2.5 μL per injection, for 14 times) into the stirred (300 rpm) Nb cells (300 μL, 4.8 × 105 CFU μL−1) at 300 s time intervals. SC cells were injected into ST cells under the same conditions. The Logistic model equation y = A2 + (A1−A2)/(1 + (x/x0)p) was employed to fit the curve, where y represented the change in heat and x denoted the count of ligand bacteria. A1 and A2 were the maximum and minimum values of the fitted curve, respectively. P was the exponential parameter that related to the shape and slope of the curve. X0 was the count of ligand bacteria at which the heat change reached 50% of the maximum response. The binding affinity was characterized by the equilibrium constant Ka, which was directly derived from x0. The dissociation constant Kd was given by Kd = Ka−1. The reverse titrations (Nb cells to Ag cells, ST cells to SC cells) were performed using the same method.

Fabrication of the living force sensor

Bacterial cells were cultured and induced for the expression of the affinity pairs as mentioned above. After mixing the corresponding bacterial cells (Ag cells with Nb cells, SC cells with ST cells) for assembly, the living material was harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 3 min. The bacterial assembly was washed twice with PBS to remove the LB culture medium, resuspended in PBS, and then transferred into a syringe without the plunger for further centrifugation (2800 × g, 15 min). The syringe tip was sealed with a Luer cap. The supernatant was discarded after centrifugation. The plunger was then reinserted into the syringe with bacterial assembly for injection into the tube. The living sensor was acquired by injecting the living network into the commercial soft tubes. Silicone tubes were used for the living force sensor with an inner diameter of 1 mm and an outer diameter of 3 mm. In Fig. 3a, b, the length of the tube was 25 cm, since the tube needed to be twined to acquire the intersecting points. In Fig. 3c–g, the length of the tube was 10 cm to match the experiment on the customized high-precision 3D motion platform. A latex tube was used for the preparation of a tensile living sensor with an inner diameter of 2 mm and an outer diameter of 4 mm. 8 cm of the latex tube was used for stretching up to eight folds of its original length in those measurements, which was necessary to accommodate the tensile within the displacement limits of the instrument. The conductive wires were inserted into both ends of the soft tubes and sealed with the hot melt glue to prevent leakage and ensure an electrical connection.

The measuring instruments

The HC 8E-S control system (SYNTEC Corporation) and a customized high-precision three-dimensional motion platform were utilized to regulate the force and shift. The force was measured using the SBT610RT force sensor (SIMBATOUCH Corporation) with a C2 accuracy class and a CYBSQ-2 single-channel analog transmitter. The tensile characteristics were assessed with an Instron 68SC-2 universal testing machine. The voltage, current, and resistance signals were captured using the NATIONAL INSTRUMENTS USB-6002 data acquisition system and the UNI-T UT39C+ digital multimeter.

Finite element analysis

FEA was conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 to investigate the surface stress of the living sensor under pression, interruption, and tensile conditions. The properties of each model were provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Surface stress analysis

For the surface stress analysis under pression, the bottom of the model was fixed with a constrained boundary, while the contact surface was free with the friction coefficient (setting as 0.3). The material was assumed to be linear elastic. A prescribed displacement was applied to the uppermost boundary of the model. For the surface stress analysis under tensile, the model was set as a rectangle with dimensions of 10 mm × 4 mm. Boundary loads were applied at 3000 N for both ends of the rectangle, while a fixed constraint was imposed at the center of the rectangle.

Pressing condition

Adhesive pairs between bacteria were categorized into three adhesion groups representing as three connected entities within the model. Under pression condition, bacterial cells were represented as the rod-shaped structures with 2.5 μm × 1 μm. A moving mesh was applied to the pressing plate, and deformation was specified along the z-axis. The deformation was controlled using an auxiliary sweep with an initial value of 0 nm, a step of 10 nm, and a final value of 1500 nm. The mesh was discretized using free tetrahedral elements.

Interruption condition

The simulation for the self-recovery process of the living network included the interruption and recovery stages. The adhesive stiffness in the bacterial model was determined by a contact force penalty factor controlled automatically. The cohesive force for debonding followed a displacement-based damage model. Displacements were prescribed along the x-axis at the boundaries of the bacterial model.

Tensile condition

The model was assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linear elastic under tensile conditions. Displacements along the x-axis were prescribed at the boundaries of the model using an auxiliary sweep, with an initial value of 0 nm, a step size of 10 nm, and a final value of 1000 nm. The mesh was discretized using free quadrilateral elements.

Assessment of the force-sensing ability in a tunable measuring range

Ag-Nb or SC-ST bacterial assembly was encapsulated inside the silicon tube (25 cm) to prepare the living force sensor. The prepared tube was twined into the desired geometry and fixed between two parallel rigid plates via mechanical limit, as shown (Fig. 3a). The intersecting points were then acquired. The prepared sensor was placed onto the customized high-precision 3D motion platform (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c), which ensured the stable positioning throughout the measurement. Controlled by the high-precision 3D motion platform, the pressure was applied uniformly onto a different number of the intersection points, and the displacement of the normal force ranged from 0 to 2.6 mm. A digital collector was used to monitor resistivity during the process.

After verifying the feasibility of the bacterial network to sense force, the force-sensing performance was then investigated in detail. The unfolded silicone tube (10 cm) encapsulated with a bacterial living network was placed onto the measuring instrument. The prepared tube was placed as a straight line on a rigid base at the customized high-precision 3D motion platform. Different numbers of prisms (1, 3, and 5) were designed as an integrated structure together with the cover panel. The cover panel can move under the control of the machine. The number and position of prisms were predetermined as shown (Supplementary Fig. 8d–f) and kept constant during each test, ensuring the reproducibility of contact geometry and stress distribution.

The normal force was monitored by a force sensor, while the displacement of the normal force was controlled by the high-precision 3D motion platform. The relative resistance change was recorded by a data collector for calculating the relationship of resistance with displacement, and resistance with the normal force under various conditions. The force was conducted by applying a single prism with various angles (30°, 60°, and 90°) to the unfolded living tube. In another strategy, the force was conducted by applying various number of conical structures (30°) to the unfolded tube. The force sensitivity (S) was calculated according to the equation S = ∆R/(R0·∆F), where R0 was the initial resistance, ΔR was the change in resistance, and ΔF was the change in force. We also performed the computational simulations to calculate the relationship between resistance and normal force with 10, 30, and 50 pressing points according to the independent point-by-point superposition.

Audio signal output

Arduino Uno was used to control the audio output signal. The change of resistance was modulated by Arduino Uno to the variations of audio and then output through a buzzer. The applied force and the detected audio were recorded. The high-precision 3D motion platform was used to control the living force sensor (10 cm) under different forces for 5 s. The changes in the audio were measured accordingly. The force to the sensor was cycled for 10 times with the audio recorded.

Evaluations of the self-recovery properties

The performance of the self-recovery ability in circuits

The living sensor encapsulated with Ag-Nb or SC-ST bacterial assembly inside the silicone tube (10 cm) was used to test the self-recovery ability. An ammeter was connected to the circuit in series with the living sensor, supplied with a power (10 V). The whole circuit was placed onto the high-precision 3D motion platform.

Fluorescent images

To image the self-recovery process, bacteria were genetically engineered to display GFP-Neae-Ag or mCherry-Neae-Nb. GFP or mCherry was fused to the N-terminus of Neae. The fluorescent microscopy (MF52, Mshot Corporation) was applied to image the living sensor (Excitation: 488 nm for GFP, 543 nm for mCherry).

Complete interruption of the living network

Using the high-precision 3D motion platform, the normal force was applied up to a displacement of 2.8 mm. The living network in the silicone tube (10 cm) exerted a continuously increased force, leading to the complete separation of the bacterial assembly and a resultant open circuit with zero current. Upon the release of the force, the engineered bacteria re-adhered via the affinity pairs. The living sensor (encapsulated with Ag-Nb or SC-ST bacterial assembly) recovered, and the current in the circuit regenerated gradually. During this process, the recovery time was recorded by a data collector. The living sensor made from SC-ST cells was further assessed over an extended period.

Incomplete separation of the living network

The displacement of 2.5 mm was kept, where the current was still present in the circuit to represent a state of incomplete separation. The response and recovery time were tested under the described conditions. The time was recorded for the stable resistance of the sensor after applying force. The force was then released, and the recovered time was measured. Upon releasing the force, the recovered resistance was also measured. The program was coded to cycle the normal force up to the displacement of 2.8 mm for 10 times using the high-precision 3D motion platform. The change in current was recorded during the process. In another strategy, the normal force was inflicted at the displacement of 2.8 mm using the high-precision 3D motion platform, which completely separated the living network inside the silicone tube (10 cm). The normal force was kept for 1 min and then removed. After the recovery of the living sensor, the normal force was addressed to the displacement of 2.5 mm for 10 cycles, and the resistance was recorded.

Application of the living sensor on the boxing sandbag

The living sensor was securely affixed to the sandbag and connected to a power source (10 V). A conical pressing plate was fixed above the silicone tube (10 cm) with an electrical circuit. The sandbag was stroke with punches using boxing gloves. The collision signals were recorded via original or boosting circuit conditions.

Evaluating the stretching properties of the living sensor

Ag-Nb or SC-ST bacterial assembly was encapsulated inside a latex tube (8 cm in length, 2 mm in the inner diameter) to prepare the living sensor. The NI cells without affinity pairs were also encapsulated inside the latex tube (8 cm) and used as a control. The living sensor was supplied with a power (10 V) and stretched to two, four, six, and eight times of its original length. The tensile characteristics were assessed with an Instron 68SC-2 universal testing machine. The changes in resistance were recorded. The living sensor was stretched to eight times of its original length by a tensile testing machine for 10 cycles. The changes of resistance were recorded during each stretching and contracting phase. Alternatively, the living sensor was stretched to six times of its original length at different stretching frequencies (0.2, 0.5, and 1 Hz) and the changes of resistances were recorded.

Transduction of signals by the living sensor during joint bending

The living sensor was encapsulated with Ag-Nb or SC-ST bacterial assembly in the latex tube (8 cm) and fixed to the elbow joints. The joints were controlled to bend to an angle of 150°. The bending motion was repeated for three times. The changes of resistance were recorded during the whole process. The stretching tests were performed with the living sensor fabricated with SC-ST bacterial assembly. The elbow was bent to 30°, 90°and 150°, respectively, with each angle being recorded for three times. The ratio of resistance to the initial resistance was calculated accordingly. The data were obtained from a healthy adult male volunteer with informed consent, and no compensation was provided.

Recognition objects during grasping

The living sensor was fabricated with Ag-Nb bacterial assembly, SC-ST bacterial assembly, or the mixture of the two assemblies (at a volume ratio of 1:1) in the latex tube (10 cm) and fixed on the black of the glove. The living sensors were tightly attached to the positions of five fingers and extended to the fingertips, with each part serving as an individual force perception channel. The glove was used to grasp bananas at three different ripeness stages (unripe, moderately ripe, and soft), during which the resistance signals from the five sensing channels connected in series were recorded. The total resistance change across the five sensors was measured during each grasping process. Alternatively, the living sensors contacted with the fingers (SC-ST bacterial assembly) or placed at the fingertips (Ag-Nb bacterial assembly) were connected via wires, and the changes in resistance were recorded during the grasping process. For each ripeness stage, the glove grasped the banana for 10 times. The data were obtained from a healthy adult male volunteer with informed consent, and no compensation was provided.

The application of the living sensor to haptic sensing

The living sensor was fabricated with Ag-Nb bacterial assembly, SC-ST bacterial assembly, or the mixture of the two assemblies (at a volume ratio of 1:1) in the latex tube (8 cm) and fixed to the wrist joint. The waveforms of each type were monitored. During the test, the living sensor was conducted in an unbent wrist position supplied with three sequential external forces in different types, including gentle touch with the fingertips, a knock with the palm, and a strike with the fist. The motions were repeated for three times and changes of resistance were recorded. The data were obtained from a healthy adult male volunteer with informed consent, and no compensation was provided.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using Origin 2018 and Excel 2019. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). The number of independent experiments (n) and the statistical tests used are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size, and the experiments were not randomized. No data were excluded from the analyses. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All experiments were independently repeated with similar results.
