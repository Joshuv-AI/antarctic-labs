Composite design and property assessment of high and medium entropy layers in TaCHfCZrCTiCVC/HfB₂ZrB₂TiB₂ system
Authors: Ekhlas Qays Al-shawway, Zohre Balak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research investigates the impact of carbonous and metallic additives on the microstructure of high entropy carbide (HEC) and medium entropy in-situ boride (ME-ISB) composite layers. The HEC layer, made from TaCHfCZrCTiCVC with Gr and Si additives, and the ME-ISB layer, composed of HfO2ZrO2TiO2 with nano carbon black (C.Bn) and B4C, were consolidated using spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 2000 °C under 40 MPa for 10 min. Bulk density was measured via the Archimedes method, and microstructural analysis was conducted using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The findings revealed that the HEC layer has a higher sinterability (99.7%) compared to the ME-ISB layer (78.4%). Hardness tests showed the HEC layer (≈ 15 GPa) is significantly harder than the ME-ISB layer (≈ 1.2 GPa), with average grain sizes of 430 ± 150 nm for HEC and 200 ± 100 nm for ME-ISB.Keywords: Two-layer composite, Carbide, Boride, Microstructure, Fracture toughness, High and medium entropy, SPSSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs) are a class of advanced structural materials known for their exceptional thermal, mechanical, and chemical stability at temperatures exceeding 2000 ℃, along with strong resistance to oxidation, corrosion, and thermal shock1–3. Due to these superior characteristics, UHTCs are widely used in aerospace, nuclear systems, and high‑temperature structural components1,4–6. Recently, the emergence of high‑entropy ceramics (HECs) has introduced new opportunities for enhancing the performance of UHTCs through multi‑element, near‑equimolar compositions that generate high configurationally entropy and lattice distortion7. Several studies4,8–10 have investigated synthesis routes for HECs—including mechanical alloying, chemical methods, and particularly SPS4,8,11,12 to achieve dense, homogeneous microstructures. HECs typically exhibit superior hardness, strength, and fracture toughness compared to conventional ceramics, attributed to entropy‑induced lattice distortion and hindered crack propagation4,10,13. Moreover, previous research indicates that carbon and metallic additives can significantly promote single‑phase formation and improve sinterability in HEC systems8,14,15.

J. Gild and co-workers7 synthesized (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂ and (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)C via RFSPS, demonstrating that 3 wt% carbon facilitates rapid single‑phase formation and yields > 99% relative density without extensive mechanical alloying8. In another study, three HEB compositions—(Hf₀.₂V₀.₂Ta₀.₂Ti₀.₂Nb₀.₂)B₂, (Zr₀.₂V₀.₂Ta₀.₂Ti₀.₂Nb₀.₂)B₂, and (Cr₀.₂V₀.₂Ta₀.₂Ti₀.₂Nb₀.₂)B₂—were synthesized via boro/carbothermal reduction, where the HEB‑Zr sample exhibited the highest hardness (24.2 ± 0.6 GPa) and fracture toughness (9.2 ± 0.3 MPa·m1/2)15. Additionally, Cf/HEC composites produced by RMI showed high bending strength (612.6 MPa) and low ablation rates, with XRD confirming single‑phase (TiZrHfNbTa)C formation16.

Studies on medium‑entropy carbides (MECs) such as (MoTaW)C and (NbTaW)C have shown that carbon‑induced lattice distortion enhances charge transfer and strengthens covalent bonding, resulting in improved mechanical properties compared to HEAs17. Hot‑pressed (TiZrHf)C exhibited high relative density (99.2%), fine grain size (1.9 μm), and strong flexural performance at both room and elevated temperatures18. Similarly, SPS‑sintered (TaTiZr)C showed flexural strength of 700 MPa, while higher temperatures led to partial phase degradation and increased fracture toughness19.

A recent advancement in this field is the development of dual‑phase high‑entropy UHTCs (DPHE‑UHTCs), combining HEC and HEB phases to achieve fine‑grained, chemically homogeneous, and mechanically robust structures suitable for extreme environments4. For example, (Ti‑Zr‑Nb‑Ta‑Hf)C/(Ti‑Zr‑Nb‑Ta‑Hf)B₂ composites produced via in‑situ boro/carbothermal reactions exhibit nano‑hardness values of 37–43 GPa and high phase stability4. An empirical rule with 93.33% accuracy has also been proposed for predicting single‑phase HEB formation, where < 3.66 indicates single‑phase stability14.

This work tackles the persistent lack of phase stability and oxidation resistance of conventional ultra‑high‑temperature ceramics (UHTCs) above 2000 °C in aerospace and hypersonic environments by introducing a dual‑layer, entropy‑engineered composite. The design couples a high‑entropy carbide layer (TaC–HfC–ZrC–TiC–VC), comprising five FCC‑compatible carbides that form a stable single‑phase solid solution with melting points exceeding 3500 °C, with a medium‑entropy boride layer (HfB₂–ZrB₂–TiB₂) of AlB₂‑type structure. Constituent selection was guided by thermodynamic stability and lattice compatibility, and the formation was mediated by C.Bn, B₄C, and selected metal additives. Entropy effects suppress grain growth and bolster phase and oxidation stability, yielding a multifunctional composite with enhanced thermal endurance, mechanical strength, and environmental resilience. These attributes position the material as a strong candidate for thermal protection systems, rocket nozzles, and other high‑temperature structural components in extreme service conditions.

This work further investigates the roles of C.Bn and B4C in promoting in‑situ medium‑entropy boride formation from ZrO₂–TiO₂–HfO₂ precursors, as well as the synergistic effects of graphite and Si on enhancing single‑phase HEC formation and sinterability in the carbide layer. SPS was selected due to its high efficiency in consolidating entropy‑engineered ceramics. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior study has reported HEC/ME‑ISB layered composites incorporating the combined effects of C.Bn, B4C, and metallic additives using SPS, highlighting the novelty of this research.

Table 3 shows that, despite the higher melting points of compounds in layer I compared to those in layer II, the relative density of the carbide layer is approximately 100%, significantly exceeding the 78.4% density of the oxide layer. This difference in density can be attributed to the simultaneous presence of metal and carbonaceous additives (Si and Gr) in the carbide layer, whereas the oxide layer contains only carbonaceous (C.Bn) and carbide (B4C) additives.

Layer I (five-component carbide) is created from the sintering of TaC, HfC, ZrC, TiC, and VC in the presence of silicon and graphite additives; While layer II is created from the in-situ sintering of HfB2, ZrB2, TiB2 from the oxide compounds (HfO2, TiO2, ZrO2) in the presence of C.Bn and B4C. In other words, in layer I, the sintering mechanism is liquid phase sintering plus the reactive agent, while in layer II, the sintering mechanism is only through the reactive agent.

In layer I, Si reacts with carbide compounds through Eq. (3) and creates a silicide phase (MSi2) which is feasible thermodynamically. The melting points of all silicide compounds TiSi2, ZrSi2, HfSi2 and VSi2 (respectively equal to 1470, 1620, 1680, and 1677 °C) except for TaSi2 (2200 °C) are lower than the sintering temperature (2000 °C). So, they present in the liquid phase in the microstructure and improve sinterability. In addition, Gr can facilitate removing oxide impurities through the reaction with oxide impurities on the surface of primary powder particles (Eqs. 4 and 5), thereby improving the sinterability.

To investigate the possibility of reactions (4) and (5) happening, thermodynamic calculations were conducted using HSC 5.1 software. For this purpose, the enthalpy and entropy values of the reactions as a function of temperature were obtained using thermodynamic HSC 5.1 software. In addition to the software calculations, the thermodynamic quantities were cross-checked using the data provided in the thermochemical handbook29. Then, based on the standard thermodynamic relation, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the ΔG vs. temperature plots were generated for each reaction and presented in Fig. 4.

According to what is explained about layer I, thermodynamic investigations show the possibility of Gr reaction with oxide impurities and their reduction to carbide compounds during reactions (4) and (5) at the temperature of 2000 °C (Fig. 3). Therefore, considering it, in this layer, besides the initial carbide grain, new TaC, HfC, ZrC, TiC, and VC grains as well as CO gases are formed which the CO gases can escape during the sintering process.

In layer II, the initial oxide powders can react with C.Bn and in-situ synthesis to the carbide phases through a carbothermal reaction (Equs. 4, 5 and 12) or react with C.Bn and B4C via boro/carbothermal reaction (Equ. 6) and create boride phases which all of them are feasible thermodynamically.

Importantly, transition metal diborides exhibit extremely high thermodynamic stability, with more negative formation energies compared to the corresponding oxides under reducing conditions. Therefore, once reduction is initiated, boride formation is strongly favored (Fig. 4).

In addition to the formed solid solution, carbon, and TiSi2 phases have also been detected in this layer. The carbon phase can be related to the primary Gr that did not react with the primary materials during reactions (3) to (4) or due to the release of carbon during reactions (2) and (7). It was reported that a TaC tends to decompose to Ta4C3 and C through reaction (7)35. As mentioned before, TiSi2 identified in the diffraction pattern is formed during reaction (2) and improves sinterability through the liquid phase sintering mechanism.

The XRD results of layer II show that no peaks associated with the initial powders of HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2 can be detected. It means the initial oxide powders are consumed through reactions (3) and (5) and synthesized into the carbide or boride phases. This issue is completely consistent with the thermodynamic prediction presented in Fig. 4. As seen in Fig. 4, the reduction of oxide phases to the carbides or borides is feasible thermodynamically through reactions (3) and (5), at a temperature of 2000 °C. However, no carbide phases are identified in the XRD pattern of layer II.

In addition, it can be seen that only the peaks of ZrB2 with some replacement are detected and no peaks related to the HfB2, and TiB2 can be identified in the diffraction pattern (Fig. 6); Hence, according to the XRD result (Fig. 6) and linear analysis (Fig. 5), it can be deduced, firstly, the initial oxide phases were synthesized to the boride phase via reaction (5) and secondly, Hf and Ti diffused and dissolved into ZrB2 crystal lattice and the formed a solid solution of (Zr1−2xHfxTix) B2 through reaction (11). In addition, regarding Fig. 4, the reduction of the oxide phases to the carbides is feasible thermodynamically through the reaction of (12). In other words, both boro/carbothermal and carbothermal reactions (4), (6) and (12) can occur in layer II thermodynamically but according to the XRD result (no carbide phases distinguished), only reaction (6) is happened. This issue can be associated with the lower starting temperature (1330 ◦C) and more negative ∆G (−153.4 kcal/mole) of reaction (6) rather than reaction (12) (TS=1560 ◦C, ∆G= −104.2 kcal/mole).

Although the formation of (TiHfZr)B2 (reaction 6) is thermodynamically more favorable than reaction 12, kinetic factors and local reaction conditions play a critical role. If B₄C is not uniformly distributed or C.Bn is locally in excess, oxide powders may preferentially react with carbon to form carbide phases. The reaction between oxides and C.Bn proceeds with faster kinetics due to its simpler mechanism and lower activation barrier compared to reaction 5 and may occur as an intermediate pathway. If the temperature or holding time is insufficient, oxides phases may not fully convert to boride phases and could remain as a residual phase in the final product. Considering the high temperature selected in this study (2000 °C), the sufficient reaction time, and the XRD results (Fig. 5), it appears that reaction (12) either did not occur or, if it did, its extent was so minimal (less than 2%) that it could not be detected by XRD analysis.

The evolution of CO gas during reductions 6 and 12 significantly influences reaction kinetics and microstructure. In the reaction 12, CO release occurs more uniformly due to the simpler reaction pathway, minimizing porosity and maintaining effective particle contact. In contrast, the more complex reaction 6 may lead to localized CO accumulation, increased porosity, and reduced interparticle connectivity, thereby slowing reaction progress.

Hardness

The hardness values of both layers are reported in Table 3. It can be seen that the carbide layer has a much higher hardness (≈ 15 GPa) than the oxide layer (≈ 1.2 GPa). FESEM images (Fig. 3) of both layers, relative density values (Table 3) plus the created indentation effect (Fig. 10) confirms this issue. The carbide layer has an almost complete relative density, while the oxide layer contains about 23% porosity, and since the porosity has no resistance against the applied force, it has caused a significant reduction in the hardness of the oxide layer. This is also clear in Fig. 11. By comparing the results of this study with reference36, it is observed that although the current work exhibits higher relative density and finer grain size than reference36, the resulting hardness is lower. This discrepancy can be attributed to the formation of a secondary titanium disilicide phase in the present study. In contrast, the formation of a single-phase solid solution without any secondary phases in reference36 likely contributed to its higher hardness compared to the current work. Another contributing factor to the reduced hardness observed in this study may be the higher applied load during Vickers hardness testing (20 kg) compared to reference36, where a lower load of 500 g was used; Because the hardness decreases with applied force.

In another study37 the hardness of 17.07 ± 0.54 GPa was reported for (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)C single phase synthesized in 1800 °C (15 min) + 2000 °C (0.5 min) which approximately is accordance with present research with hardness of 15 GPa in carbide layer. Seven equimolar, five-component, metal diborides were fabricated via high-energy ball milling and spark plasma sintering. The hardness of samples was obtained in the range of 17.5 ± 1.2 GPa to 23.7 ± 1.7 GPa which higher than this research. This difference is related to the very high amount of porosities (23%) in boride layer in this research rather than reference38( Also in reference39,40, samples (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Mo₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Mo₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, (Hf₀.₂Mo₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, (Mo₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Cr₀.₂Ti₀.₂)B₂, possess virtually one solid-solution boride phase of the hexagonal AlB2 structure while in this research beside the single HEB phase, some carbon was remained which can has negative effect of the hardness.

The measured hardness of approximately 1.2 GPa in the boride layer is notably low and can be attributed to several interconnected factors:

1) Complex Multiphase Composition: The boride layer is not a simple monolithic structure but a reactive composite containing various phases, including oxides (HfO₂, ZrO₂, TiO₂-derived), B₄C, C.Bn, and potential reaction products. These diverse components, especially the presence of oxides and carbon, significantly influence the material’s mechanical response compared to pure borides.

2) Residual Porosity: Despite high-pressure, high-temperature sintering, a porosity of about 23% was quantified. This residual porosity, likely due to short processing times and exothermic reactions, substantially softens the material. Using an empirical model (H = H0 exp(-bP))41, where H is measured hardness (1.2 GPa), P is porosity (0.23), and b is a sensitivity parameter (typically 4–7), the intrinsic hardness (H0) of a fully dense composite is estimated to be between 3.0 and 6.0 GPa. However, this estimated H0 is still considerably lower than the typical hardness of monolithic borides (18–30 GPa), indicating porosity is not the sole cause of softening.

3) Amorphous or Soft Phases: The high processing temperature (2000 °C) combined with the reactant mixture could lead to the formation of soft, amorphous intergranular phases (e.g., borosilicates). Such phases can drastically lower hardness by enabling grain boundary sliding. Additionally, the presence of C.Bn, which has an intrinsic low hardness of ~ 0.5–1 GPa, acts as a soft constituent, further reducing the composite’s overall measured hardness26.

4) Indentation Effects: The indentation process on a multi-layered composite can be complex. If the indentation depth was significant or approached interfaces, it might induce cracking or delamination, especially given the presence of weak interfaces or porosity. This could lead to an artificially reduced apparent hardness measurement.

Fracture toughness

To investigate the fracture toughness of both layers, the crack length measurement method was used. According to Fig. 12b, it is clear that there are no cracks in the oxide layer, which is due to the very low hardness of this layer and its high porosity amount. This issue is consistent with the results reported in a previous study42 about composites with low hardness and high porosity amount. Therefore, no value is reported for the fracture toughness of the oxide layer in this research. The value of fracture toughness in the carbide layer was 6.2 MPam1/2. To investigate the hardening mechanism in this layer, FESEM images of crack propagation paths are presented in Fig. 12. By examining the path of crack propagation in the carbide layer, it was found that the activation of toughening mechanisms such as crack deflection, bridging, and grain breakage (Trasgranular fracture) is the reason for improving the toughness of the layer compared to the oxide layer. All of these mechanisms are indicated in Figs. 12a and b.

High-entropy ceramics (HEC) with the composition (Hf₀.₂Zr₀.₂Ta₀.₂Nb₀.₂Ti₀.₂)C submicron grain sizes 0.578 ± 0.217 m was synthesized via SPS using a two-stage sintering approach. Structural analysis through X-ray and neutron diffraction confirmed the formation of a single-phase material with a rock salt crystal structure. The fracture toughness of 5.9 ± 0.7 MPa m1/2 was obtained which is slightly lower than present study (6.2 MPa m1/2). This issue can be attributed to the smaller grain size of carbide layer in the present study rather than reference submicron37. Also, the only toughening mechanism was reported in reference37 was crack deflection while here, more toughening mechanisms were activated (crack bridging and crack deflection) which can explain the higher fracture toughness obtained in this research rather than37.

Conclusion

1- Two-layer composite consisting HEC layer with the chemical composition of (TaHfZrTiV)C in the presence of Gr and Si additives and an ME-ISB with the chemical composition of (HfZrTi)B2 in the presence of C.Bn and B4C was successfully sintered by SPS at 2000 ◦C, holding time of 10 and under a pressure of 40 MPa.

2- Phase identification with XRD and elements distribution map using FESEM showed that a solid solution is formed in both layers.

3- The HEC layer with a relative density of 99.7% has better sinterability than the ME-ISB layer with a 78.4% relative density.

4- The HEC layer (≈ 15GPa) has a higher hardness than the ME-ISB layer (≈ 1.2 GPa).

5- By examining the path of crack propagation in the HEC layer, it was found that the activation of toughening mechanisms such as crack deflection, branching, and grain breakage is the reason for improving its toughness.
