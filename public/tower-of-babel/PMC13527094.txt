Comparative mechanical properties of polyester and polyethylene composites reinforced with sunflower seed hull fibers
Authors: Babak Mehdipour, Abbas Niknejad, Hadi Zare-Zardini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the development and characterization of novel composite materials using sunflower seed hull fibers as reinforcement in polyester and polyethylene matrices. The fibers were extracted via alkaline treatment (10% NaOH) and mechanical separation. Composites were fabricated with various fiber mass fractions (0–40% for polyester and 0–22% for polyethylene) using a compression molding technique. Mechanical properties were evaluated through tensile, flexural, impact, and compression tests according to ASTM standards. Results showed significant improvements in the mechanical properties of polyester-based composites. The polyester/sunflower seed fiber composite exhibited a 50% increase in tensile strength at 40% fiber mass fraction, while flexural strength improved by 63%, reaching 70.52 MPa. Impact strength was 3.3 times greater than that of neat polyester resin, and compressive strength reached 143.5 MPa. Polyethylene-based composites showed more modest improvements, with a 12% increase in tensile strength and a 35% improvement in flexural strength at 22% fiber content, although impact strength decreased with increasing fiber content. This study demonstrates the potential of sunflower seed hull fibers as an effective, sustainable reinforcement material for polymer composites, particularly in polyester-based systems. These novel composites could be viable alternatives for applications requiring enhanced mechanical properties while maintaining environmental sustainability.Keywords: Natural fiber composites, Sunflower seed hull fibers, Mechanical properties, Polyester composites, Polyethylene composites, Sustainable materialsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Polymer-based composite materials have gained increasing importance due to their favorable specific strength, low density, cost-effectiveness, recyclability, and reduced environmental footprint1. In these systems, mechanical performance is determined by the intrinsic characteristics of the polymer matrix, the reinforcing phase, and the quality of interfacial adhesion. Natural fibers have therefore emerged as promising substitutes for synthetic reinforcements, offering biodegradability, wide availability, and competitive stiffness-to-weight ratios1.

A broad spectrum of research has explored natural fibers and agro-based reinforcements as sustainable alternatives in polymer composites. Sunflower-derived residues have shown potential for biodegradable composite manufacturing2. Hybrid natural systems such as banana–sisal fibers have exhibited predictable mechanical behavior through mixture modelling3. Investigations of jute-reinforced polymers have demonstrated the strong influence of matrix type on tensile and flexural performance4. Additional studies involving cork–silicone structures5, sugarcane fibers6, and bamboo reinforcements7,8 have highlighted the importance of fiber morphology, processing, and interfacial compatibility in determining composite behavior. Research on coconut fibers, sunflower husk particulates, and other agricultural fillers has further demonstrated the feasibility of using plant residues in composite systems9–14.

In recent years, the scope of agro-based reinforcement studies has expanded considerably. Fibers extracted from bamboo, palm, and various lignocellulosic plants have been incorporated into epoxy, polyester, phenolic, polyurethane, and bio-resin matrices, revealing the significant effect of chemical treatment, fiber preparation, and matrix selection on mechanical responses15–23. Research on newly developed and emerging bio-based fibers24–29, hybrid natural–synthetic architectures31,32, and fibers functionalized with graphene oxide or metallic fillers32,33 has shown improvements in tensile strength, stiffness, energy absorption, and interfacial bonding. Further investigations have highlighted the critical influence of fiber–matrix ratio, fiber orientation, and surface morphology on composite performance34–37.

Despite extensive literature on natural fiber composites, a significant research gap remains in the effective utilization of sunflower seed hulls. Previous studies have primarily used these hulls in particulate (powder) form, mainly as a filler, often resulting in reduced mechanical integrity due to low aspect ratios and the formation of stress concentration points26,29. There is a notable lack of research on the systematic extraction of high-aspect-ratio fibers from these hulls. Additionally, comparative studies evaluating the performance of a single bio-derived reinforcement across fundamentally different polymer classes – specifically, thermosetting polyester versus thermoplastic polyethylene – are lacking. This research addresses these gaps by implementing a novel alkaline-mechanical extraction protocol to transform sunflower seed hulls from a low-value particulate filler into a structural fiber reinforcement. Furthermore, by conducting a systematic comparative analysis of these fibers within both thermoset and thermoplastic matrices, this study provides a detailed engineering perspective on how matrix chemistry, particularly the contrast between polar and non-polar systems, determines the efficiency of fiber-matrix interfacial adhesion and load-transfer mechanisms. This dual-matrix approach offers critical insights often overlooked in single-matrix studies, transforming a common agricultural by-product into a viable, high-performance engineering material.

Incorporating sunflower seed hull fibers significantly enhances the mechanical performance of polyester composites, resulting in notable improvements in tensile, flexural, and impact properties, as well as achieving a compressive strength of 143.5 MPa at the highest fiber content. Polyethylene composites show more moderate improvements, with increases of up to 12% in tensile strength and 35% in flexural strength, while impact resistance decreases as fiber content rises. These findings indicate that sunflower seed hull fibers have strong reinforcing potential, particularly in thermosetting matrices, and provide a sustainable, low-cost approach for developing eco-friendly composite materials.

Materials and methods

Materials

The natural fibers used to fabricate the composites in this study were obtained from the hulls of sunflower seeds. The seeds were sourced from sunflower fields in North Khuzestan, Iran, and the kernels were separated by machine for sale or oil extraction. To fabricate the composites, isophthalic polyester resin MUO-040 produced by Rositan and polyethylene HDPE 52518 produced by Jam Petrochemical Company were used. Methyl ethyl ketone peroxide (MEKP) with cobalt at 1% by weight and 0.2% by weight of resin was used as hardener and accelerator, respectively, during composite fabrication with polyester resin. Surface Alkali treatment

During this stage, the sunflower seed husks, previously separated from their kernels by the machine, were soaked in a relatively concentrated aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide (10%) for 48 h to remove lignin, wax, and oil from the outer surface of the fibers and to facilitate fiber separation. The choice of a 10% NaOH concentration for 48 h was determined through a systematic trial-and-error optimization process to balance effective fiber liberation with minimal structural degradation27. Initial trials using 5% NaOH for 24, 48, and 72 h, followed by mechanical separation, failed to completely dissolve lignin and pectin. Subsequent trials with 10% NaOH for 24 h were also insufficient. Complete fiber separation was only achieved at 10% concentration for 48 h combined with mechanical mixing. While prolonged alkaline exposure can induce some surface modification, this specific parameter set represents the minimum threshold required to successfully extract continuous fibers from the sunflower seed hulls without causing catastrophic structural degradation.

Fiber extraction

To prepare the fibers, the seed skin of the sunflower plant, previously separated from its kernel by the machine, was washed with distilled water after treatment. The washed skins were then mixed for 10 min using a mixer to separate the fibers. In the next step, final washing and separation of additional suspended particles were carried out to obtain the same final fibers. To remove moisture from the fibers, they were placed in an oven at 80 °C for 60 min to achieve dry and uniform fibers. The drying time and temperature were determined experimentally by testing different temperatures and durations. The extracted fibers exhibited a golden-yellow hue comparable to banana fibers and had an average cross-sectional area of 0.074 mm². Their lengths ranged from 7 to 17 mm, with a mean value of approximately 12 mm, indicating a relatively uniform morphology conducive to effective stress transfer in composite applications. The fiber density was measured as 1.60 g/cm³. To accurately determine the fiber cross-sectional area, an optical microscope equipped with a calibrated micrometer scale was used. Microscopic images of the fiber cross-sections were imported into SolidWorks CAD software, where the geometry was traced and measured. This process was repeated for five individual fibers, yielding an average cross-sectional area of 0.074 mm².

Composites fabrication

In this study, the specimens were fabricated using a custom-designed mound composed of two steel plates serving as the upper and lower shoes. The mandrel was mounted on the upper shoe, while the mound cavity was positioned in the lower shoe. Four M20 bolts were used to generate the required compressive force for consolidating the fibers and enabling the fabrication of composites with elevated fiber mass fractions. Two additional M16 bolts were used for thickness adjustment and to facilitate mound opening.

The upper mound plate was intentionally designed with substantial thickness, and high-strength bolts were selected to ensure adequate rigidity and eliminate deformation under high compressive loads. This robust structural design allowed effective compaction of the fiber–resin mixture and provided the capability to incorporate relatively high fiber loadings. In practice, the maximum achievable fiber mass fractions reached 0.40 for polyester and 0.22 for polyethylene; higher values could not be compacted sufficiently. The mound has overall dimensions of 35 × 35 cm and is capable of producing composite panels with diameters up to 200 mm and thicknesses up to 15 mm.

To ensure planar isotropy and uniform fiber dispersion, a manual random mat lay-up technique was used prior to compression molding, a method widely validated in the literature for natural fiber composites [3,4,6,7,9,13,16,22,24,27,28,36]. The fibers were carefully distributed by hand to avoid localized clustering. Visual and macroscopic inspections of the fiber preforms before resin infusion, during the wetting stage, and of the final cured composite panels confirmed a homogeneous, randomly oriented in-plane distribution (Fig. 1).

Polyester composite with sunflower seed fibers

To fabricate a planar isotropic polyester composite reinforced with sunflower seed fibers, a controlled compression molding process was used. The required mass of fibers was first placed in the mound, compressed, and formed into a panel. After applying a silicone release agent to the mound surface, the fiber panel was repositioned, and a mixture of polyester resin, cobalt accelerator, and acid hardener was poured over it. The mound was then closed, with its M16 bolts adjusted to set a consistent sample thickness of 3 mm, and tightened. After a 24-hour curing period at room temperature, the demolded sample was post-cured in an oven at 80 °C for 60 min according to the resin manufacturer’s instructions.

Composites with five different fiber mass fractions—0.09, 0.18, 0.26, 0.33, and 0.40—were successfully produced. Achieving the maximum fiber loading of 0.40 depended on a robust mold design and high compression capability, as higher fiber contents otherwise hindered proper compaction.

Porosity measurements within the composites revealed a significant trend: the void content decreased substantially with increasing fiber fraction. It was approximately 8.8% for the composite with 9% fiber by mass and dropped sharply to about 1.5% for the composite with 40% fiber. This significant reduction in porosity can be attributed to the considerably higher compression force required to fabricate composites with high fiber loading. The intense pressure applied during molding results in denser packing of the fibers and resin matrix, more effectively expelling air and minimizing interstitial voids. This produces a much more compact and nearly pore-free composite structure at the highest fiber content.

Polyethylene composite with sunflower seed fibers

To produce a composite with a specified weight ratio of polyethylene resin, the mass of sunflower seed fibers is first placed inside the mold, which is then closed and compressed. The mandrel is then removed, and the panel of compressed fibers is taken out. The required amount of polyethylene granules is poured into the mold. The temperature of the mold is controlled by an electric heater, causing the polyethylene granules to melt. At this stage, the sunflower seed fiber panel is placed on the melted granules. The mandrel is then inserted, and the mold screws are tightened. After 30 min, the mold is opened and the prepared sample is removed.

Planar isotropic composites were prepared using a polyethylene matrix to assess the reinforcing capacity of sunflower seed fibers. Composite samples with four different mass fractions of 0, 0.07, 0.15, and 0.22 were prepared from the sunflower seed fibers.

Higher fiber mass fractions could not be compacted adequately due to increased bulk and reduced resin mobility. Therefore, fiber contents above 0.22 were not feasible in the polyethylene system. The average porosity of the different polyethylene samples was calculated to be approximately 10.6%. Experimental methods for composite mechanical testing

The mechanical properties of the composites were evaluated using standard testing methods, as described in the following sections. The tests were designed to measure the tensile, flexural, impact, and compressive strength of the composite panels, which were fabricated by reinforcing a polymer matrix with sunflower seed husk fibers.

Composite tensile test

Each composite panel was cut into three dumbbell-shaped tensile test samples according to the ASTM D638 standard. The cutting process was performed using a Senfeng SF1318 laser cutting machine. Each dumbbell-shaped sample was then subjected to a standard tensile test. The tensile test was performed using a universal testing machine (UTM), model DMG 7166, which has a fixed jaw and a movable jaw. The test speed was 5 mm/min.

Composite flexural test

To measure the bending strength, three standard samples with dimensions of 127 × 12.7 × 3 mm were cut from each composite panel and subjected to a three-point bending test to assess the repeatability of the tests. The test was performed according to ASTM 790. The distance between the two supports was set at 48 mm. The test was conducted at a speed of 2 mm/min and ended when either the sample broke or the strain on the external surface reached 0.05. The test was performed using a Zwick/Roell Z050 machine.

Composite impact test

To measure the impact strength, three standard samples with dimensions of 62.5 × 12 × 3 mm were cut from each composite panel and subjected to an impact test to assess the repeatability of the tests. This test was performed according to the ASTM D256 standard.

Compression test

The compression tests of the composites were performed according to the ASTM D4762 standard. Specimens with dimensions of 10 × 10 × 3 mm were cut.

Statistical analysis

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental data, all mechanical tests (tensile, flexural, impact, and compression) were performed on at least three identical specimens for each fiber mass fraction. The results reported in the following sections represent the arithmetic mean of these measurements. Statistical variability is expressed as the standard deviation, which is represented as error bars in the corresponding figures. This approach was adopted to validate the consistency of the composite fabrication process and the accuracy of the mechanical characterizations.

Effect of sunflower seed husk fiber addition on tensile strength of polyester and polyethylene composites

The changes in tensile strength for the two types of composites are presented in Fig. 4 as a comparative chart. These results indicate that both the type of polymeric matrix and the fiber content play a decisive role in enhancing or reducing the tensile properties of the composites.

Variation of tensile strength for sunflower seed-hull fiber reinforced composites: (A) Polyester matrix at different fiber mass fractions, (B) Polyethylene matrix at different fiber mass fractions.

As polyester inherently exhibits brittle behavior, the addition of sunflower seed husk fibers leads to a more brittle mechanical response in this composite. Examination of the fracture surface shows that all fibers were completely severed at the fracture point, with no fiber pull-out or detachment from the matrix observed. This suggests that the interfacial bond between the fibers and matrix was relatively strong, and the applied load was effectively transferred to the fibers. Therefore, the failure mechanism in these composites is primarily due to fiber rupture and simultaneous matrix failure. In general, the failure of this group of samples is brittle and inter-fiber, highlighting the role of the fibers as a reinforcing agent for tensile strength. In the chart in Fig. 4, it is observed that with increasing fiber content up to 40 wt%, the tensile strength of the composite increases to 43.84 MPa. This upward trend is particularly noticeable after 18 wt%. Initially (up to 18 wt %), a slight decrease in strength is observed, likely due to poor fiber distribution. However, increasing the fiber content leads to a more uniform distribution and a significant increase in strength.

The strength increase at the highest fiber content compared to the fiber-free sample is about 50%, indicating the considerable reinforcing effect of these fibers in the polyester structure.

In polyethylene composites reinforced with sunflower seed husk fibers, the fracture surface shows that only a small amount of fibers were pulled out of the matrix. This suggests that, although the interfacial adhesion is not as strong as in polyester, it is sufficient for most fibers to rupture under load, with only a small amount of interfacial detachment occurring. This failure pattern is a combination of fiber rupture and limited fiber pull-out.

In the polyethylene section of Fig. 4, tensile strength gradually increases with higher fiber content. The initial tensile strength is 13.07 MPa, and at the highest fiber content (22%), it reaches 14.61 MPa. Although this increase is less than that observed for polyester, it still represents a noticeable mechanical improvement.

The difference in performance compared to polyester is due to the thermoplastic nature of polyethylene and its weaker adhesion to natural fibers, whereas polyester, a thermoset, has better permeability and a stronger ability to bond with plant fibers.

The fundamental reason for this performance disparity lies in the chemical and physical nature of the fiber–matrix interface. Polyester, a polar thermosetting resin with reactive functional groups (such as ester and hydroxyl), exhibits low pre-cure viscosity, enabling deep penetration into the cellular structure of the hydrophilic, cellulose-based sunflower fibers. This results in strong hydrogen bonding, excellent chemical adhesion, and efficient load transfer, so fibers rupture rather than pull out. In contrast, high-density polyethylene (HDPE) is a completely non-polar thermoplastic lacking reactive functional groups. It cannot form hydrogen or covalent bonds with the natural fibers and relies solely on weak Van der Waals forces. This chemical incompatibility leads to poor interfacial shear strength, causing premature interfacial debonding and fiber pull-out under mechanical loading, which severely limits the reinforcing efficiency compared to the polyester system.

A review of studies on natural fibers shows that the mechanical performance of these fibers as reinforcers depends on the fiber-matrix bond and fiber volume fraction. In some cases, improvements in properties have been reported, while in others, a decrease in strength has been observed.

In the study by Balaji et al.16, polyester composites reinforced with banana and snake grass fibers achieved a tensile strength of about 49 MPa at the highest fiber volume fraction (25%). In comparison, the present polyester composite, using sunflower seed husk fibers, showed a tensile strength of 43.84 MPa at the highest fiber mass fraction (40%). Although this value is slightly lower than that reported by Balaji et al., the 50% increase in strength compared to pure polyester indicates that sunflower fibers also have a high load transfer capability and fall within the effective performance range of natural fibers.

In the study by Reddy et al.1, polyester composites reinforced with the central stem fibers of the Palmyra tree were tested. The results showed that increasing the fiber volume fraction up to 40% resulted in a 46.54% increase in tensile strength compared to the pure resin. Although the percentage increase and absolute tensile values differ between this study and the present research, the mechanical strengthening patterns are quite comparable. In this study, adding sunflower seed husk fibers resulted in a 50% increase in the tensile strength of the polyester composite. The similarity in growth rate indicates that sunflower fibers have stress transfer capabilities comparable to Palmyra fibers and can provide the same level of reinforcement. In contrast, studies using sunflower seed husk powder reported a significant decrease in tensile strength. For example, Kouram14 showed that adding sunflower seed powder to acrylonitrile butadiene styrene composites, and Barchiewski et al.11 in epoxy composites, caused a significant reduction in tensile strength. This decrease is due to the low aspect ratio of the powder reinforcements and their predominant role as fillers. In the present study, however, the use of continuous fibers allowed effective stress transfer along the fiber length, resulting in a significant increase in tensile strength. This performance difference between powders and continuous fibers highlights the importance of selecting the appropriate form of reinforcement.

In summary, the comparison between the present research and the studies mentioned suggests that using continuous sunflower seed husk fibers in both polyester and polyethylene matrices not only addresses the weaknesses observed in powder-based systems but also provides competitive mechanical performance, even surpassing some other natural fibers such as bamboo, jute, banana, and Palmyra. Furthermore, the growth rate in strength compared to the pure resin indicates that these fibers, despite their low-cost agricultural origin and abundance, can produce composites with reliable tensile properties. The consistent performance across various fiber contents suggests that, with proper fiber extraction, preparation, and distribution processes, it is possible to produce a composite with significant mechanical efficiency and desirable performance stability from an inexpensive agricultural waste product.

Effect of sunflower seed husk fiber addition on flexural strength of polyester and polyethylene composites

The changes in the flexural strength of the two types of composites are presented in Fig. 5. Results from three-point bending tests indicate that the flexural strength of pure polyester is 43.2 MPa. With the gradual addition of fiber content, this value increases, showing approximately a 63% improvement over the pure state at the highest fiber content studied. This improvement is due to the formation of a more robust mechanical structure with good surface adhesion in the presence of fibers.

Variation of flexural strength for sunflower seed-hull fiber reinforced composites: (A) Polyester matrix at different fiber mass fractions, (B) Polyethylene matrix at different fiber mass fractions.

For polyethylene, the flexural strength increases from 19.77 MPa to 26.84 MPa in the range from 0% to 22% fiber content, reflecting an approximately 36% increase. Although this increase is lower than that of polyester, it still represents good performance for a thermoplastic matrix.

The poorer mechanical performance of polyethylene can be attributed to the polymer’s inherent interaction with natural fibers. In the absence of coupling agents, the bonding strength between the fibers and the polyethylene matrix is inherently weaker than in polyester.

Compared to previous research, Balaji et al.16 studied hybrid composites of polyester reinforced with banana and snake grass fibers under three-point bending tests. In their study, flexural strength increased from 35.5 MPa to 93 MPa as the fiber volume fraction increased from 5% to 25%. In the present study, the flexural strength of pure polyester was measured at 43.2 MPa, and with the addition of sunflower seed husk fibers up to the maximum studied fiber content, this value increased by approximately 63% to 70.52 MPa. Although the final flexural strength value in Balaji’s study is higher due to the use of two higher-strength fibers, the comparison demonstrates that sunflower fibers derived from an inexpensive agricultural waste product, namely sunflower seed husks, can exhibit competitive flexural performance in a polyester matrix, comparable to hybrid systems commonly used in industry.

Reddy et al.1 studied polyester composites containing Palmyra tree central stem fibers and reported consistent increases in flexural strength with increasing fiber volume fraction. Their findings showed a 64.62% increase in flexural strength at 40% fiber content. This trend is closely comparable to the results of the present research, where the flexural strength increased by approximately 63% with the addition of sunflower seed husk fibers in polyester. In contrast, studies on powdered sunflower seed husks have shown a reduction in flexural strength. For example, Kouram14 in acrylonitrile butadiene styrene composites and Barchiewski et al.11 in epoxy composites reported reduced flexural strength when sunflower husk powder was added. These findings highlight the significant role of continuous fibers in transferring energy and stress within the matrix, unlike powders, which tend to promote brittle failure. The use of continuous fibers in the present study led to increased flexural strength in both polyester (by 63%) and polyethylene (by 36%).

In conclusion, the findings of this research indicate that continuous sunflower seed husk fibers provide an effective solution for improving the flexural properties of both polyester and polyethylene composites, overcoming the limitations of powder-based systems, and offering competitive mechanical properties compared to other widely used natural fibers.

Effect of sunflower seed husk fiber addition on impact strength of polyester and polyethylene composites

Figure 6 clearly illustrates the contrasting behavior of the two composite systems under impact loading. For polyester composites, the impact strength increased significantly from an initial value of 30 J/m to 100 J/m at 40% fiber content. This more than threefold increase is attributed to the good structural compatibility between sunflower fibers and the polyester matrix. Sunflower fibers, with their fibrous structure, have a high capacity for absorbing fracture energy, resulting in a significant improvement in impact strength without any additives.

Variation of impact strength for sunflower seed-hull fiber reinforced composites: (A) Polyester matrix at different fiber mass fractions, (B) Polyethylene matrix at different fiber mass fractions.

Conversely, polyethylene exhibited different behavior. Although pure polyethylene had a relatively high initial impact strength (90 J/m), increasing the fiber content up to 22% caused a noticeable decrease in this strength (down to 36 J/m). This reduction is primarily due to the chemical incompatibility between polar natural fibers and non-polar polyethylene. In the absence of any compatibilizer, stress transfer is ineffective, and as a result, impact energy is concentrated within the matrix, leading to more brittle failure.

The impact test results from this study can be compared with previous research on natural fiber-reinforced composites. A comparative review of studies shows that the energy-absorbing deformation of natural fibers and their interaction with the polymer matrix play a crucial role in enhancing or reducing impact resistance.

In the study by Reddy et al.1, which examined the mechanical properties of polyester composites reinforced with Palmyra central stem fibers, the impact strength of pure polyester was about 10.5 kJ/m². When the fiber volume fraction increased to 40%, the impact strength rose to 20.99 kJ/m². This nearly twofold increase demonstrates the significant energy absorption capabilities of Palmyra fibers.

The present study, using sunflower seed husk fibers, shows a similar trend, with impact strength increasing from 30 J/m to approximately 100 J/m at 40% fiber content. Although the testing methods differ between the studies, the relative increase in fracture energy in both suggests that continuous natural fibers can absorb substantial impact energy through mechanisms such as fiber pull-out, internal deformation, and the formation of complex fracture paths.

In contrast, studies using sunflower husk powder report a decrease in impact strength with increasing filler content. For example, Kouram14in acrylonitrile butadiene styrene composites and Barchiewski et al.11 in epoxy composites reported a reduction in impact strength when sunflower seed powder was added. This difference in performance between powders and continuous fibers highlights the importance of the physical form of the reinforcement in creating effective energy-absorbing networks under impact loading.

In conclusion, the improvement in impact strength of polyester reinforced with sunflower seed husk fibers is comparable to that achieved with other natural fibers such as Palmyra. However, polyethylene, due to its inherent incompatibility with natural fibers, exhibited a decline in impact resistance, emphasizing the importance of selecting a compatible matrix and controlling interfacial factors to enhance impact performance.

Effect of sunflower seed husk fiber addition on compressive strength of polyester and polyethylene composites

Figures 7 present the comparative changes in compressive strength of polyester and polyethylene composites reinforced with sunflower seed husk fibers at various fiber content levels. The analysis of the data and comparison with previous studies are as follows:

Variation of compressive strength for sunflower seed-hull fiber reinforced composites: (A) Polyester matrix at different fiber mass fractions, (B) Polyethylene matrix at different fiber mass fractions.

The results of the compression test show that the compressive strength of the polyester composite gradually increased with the addition of sunflower seed husk fibers. The strength rose from 118 MPa in the pure sample to 143.5 MPa at 40% fiber content. This 21.6% increase, despite the absence of additives, indicates effective interaction and good adhesion between the polymeric phase and the natural fibers.

Polyethylene composites with sunflower seed husk fibers also performed well, with compressive strength increasing from an initial 35 MPa to 47.2 MPa at 22% fiber content. This 35% increase, despite weaker surface adhesion, is due to the inherent strength of the polyethylene matrix and the energy-absorbing properties of the fibers. It is important to note that in thermoplastic composites such as polyethylene, compressive behavior often remains superior to tensile and impact behavior because failure from cracks occurs more slowly.

Comparing the present results with previous studies, particularly the study by Balaji et al.16 ,provides further insight into the performance of sunflower seed husk fibers in a polyester matrix. In Balaji’s study, hybrid composites reinforced with banana and snake grass fibers showed a consistent increase in compressive strength with increasing fiber volume fraction, from 62.5 MPa at 5% fiber volume to 161 MPa at 25% fiber volume, reflecting a 157% increase. In the present study, the compressive strength of pure polyester increased by 21.6% with 40% sunflower seed husk fibers, indicating a similar, though less pronounced, trend in fiber reinforcement.

These differences in the magnitude of improvement can be attributed to the structural differences of the fibers. Banana and snake grass fibers have stronger reinforcing structures, which leads to a greater increase in compressive strength in Balaji’s research. Additionally, differences in fiber length and the actual volume fraction of fibers also play a significant role in stress-bearing capacity.

Despite these differences, comparison of both studies shows that natural fibers, even when extracted from inexpensive agricultural waste, can provide effective reinforcement in the compressive behavior of composites if they are continuous and properly bonded to the polymer matrix. The results from this study confirm that sunflower seed husk fibers significantly improve the compressive strength of the polyester composite, consistent with prior research and validating the use of natural fibers to enhance the mechanical properties of composites.

The disparity in mechanical performance between the two matrices can be fundamentally explained by the physics and chemistry of the fiber-matrix interface. Polyester, as a polar thermosetting resin with low molecular weight and viscosity before curing, exhibits superior wettability, allowing it to penetrate the cellular structure of the alkali-treated sunflower fibers and create robust mechanical interlocking and hydrogen bonding. In contrast, the polyethylene matrix is non-polar and hydrophobic, which limits its chemical compatibility with the hydrophilic, cellulose-based sunflower fibers to weak Van der Waals forces. This results in weaker interfacial adhesion, where load transfer efficiency is significantly lower than in the polyester system. Consequently, while the strong interface in the polyester system facilitates effective stress transfer and energy dissipation – enhancing both tensile strength and impact toughness – the weak interface in the polyethylene system acts as a zone of discontinuity. Under impact, this interface promotes premature debonding (delamination) rather than effective fiber-matrix interaction, causing the composite to fail at energy levels lower than those of the neat matrix. This fundamental difference underscores that, for thermoplastic matrices reinforced with polar natural fibers, improving interfacial adhesion through surface modification or compatibilizers is a necessary prerequisite for achieving structural toughness.

Based on the competitive mechanical performance demonstrated in this study, particularly the 50% increase in tensile strength and 63% improvement in flexural strength in polyester composites, sunflower seed hull fibers present a viable, low-cost alternative to conventional natural fibers. Given their mechanical properties and sustainable nature, these composites are highly suitable for non-load-bearing or semi-structural applications. Potential applications include building and construction materials (such as ceiling panels, flooring, and window frames) as well as automotive interior components (such as dashboards, roof liners, and decorative trim panels).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the viability of transforming low-value sunflower seed hulls into continuous structural fibers using a systematic alkaline-mechanical extraction protocol to reinforce both polar thermosetting and non-polar thermoplastic polymer matrices. The comprehensive comparative analysis showed that incorporating these continuous fibers into a polyester matrix produced substantial mechanical enhancements, driven by excellent interfacial adhesion and effective load transfer. This resulted in a 50% increase in tensile strength (43.84 MPa), a 63% improvement in flexural strength (70.52 MPa), a 21.6% rise in compressive strength (143.5 MPa), and a 3.3-fold increase in impact strength at a 40% fiber mass fraction. In contrast, the high-density polyethylene matrix exhibited more modest improvements, limited to a 22% maximum fiber loading, with increases of 12%, 36%, and 35% in tensile, flexural, and compressive strengths, respectively, alongside a significant reduction in impact resistance due to inherent chemical incompatibility and weaker van der Waals interfacial forces that promoted premature fiber pull-out. Unlike previous studies using sunflower husk powder, which resulted in degraded properties, the continuous fiber morphology developed here enabled effective stress distribution and overcame localized stress concentrations. From a practical perspective, these findings establish sunflower seed hull fibers as a highly competitive, low-cost, and sustainable alternative to conventional natural fibers for semi-structural industrial applications, particularly in automotive interior components and building materials when paired with thermosetting resins. Furthermore, this dual-matrix approach highlights that achieving structural toughness in thermoplastic systems requires interfacial modification strategies, such as the use of compatibilizers, thus providing a clear engineering roadmap for future research focused on surface treatments and advanced morphological characterization to fully unlock the potential of upcycled agricultural residues in eco-friendly composite manufacturing.
