Routine immunization uptake and timeliness in young children exposed to multidrug-resistant tuberculosis: A secondary analysis of the Tuberculosis Child Multidrug-Resistant Preventive Therapy multi-site randomised controlled trial
Authors: Faeezah Patel, Susan Purchase, Mrinmayee Dhar, Elizea Horne, Janet Grab, Hamisha Soma-Kasiram, Nadia Sabet, Neil Martinson, Suzanne Staples, Joanna Brigden, Thomas Wilkinson, Hendrik Simon Schaaf, James Seddon, Lee Fairlie, Anneke Hesseling, Trinh Duong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) reduces vaccine preventable disease-related childhood morbidity and mortality. Vaccine coverage and timeliness are crucial to maximise protection in children < 5 years old. This is especially important for children living in households affected by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and tuberculosis.MethodsTuberculosis Child Multidrug-Resistant Preventive Therapy (TB-CHAMP), a randomised controlled trial of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) prevention, enrolled participants at five sites across South Africa between 26 Sep 2017 and 21 Jan 2023. In this secondary exploratory analysis, we analysed the coverage and timeliness of EPI vaccinations due by 12 months of age and associations between delayed/non-vaccinations and pre-defined factors relating to child participant, adult MDR-TB index patient, and household. Early, on-time, and delayed vaccinations were defined as > 5 days before, within 28 days of, and > 28 days after the projected dates, respectively. Modified Poisson regression was used to assess factors associated with delayed/non-vaccination and vaccination completeness. The COVID-19 period was defined as the 6 months after the start of South African lockdown.ResultsOf 922 enrolled children, 814 children < 5 years with complete immunisation records were included. There was equal sex distribution, median age was 2.5 years (IQR 1.2–3.8), 35% were HIV-exposed, and 1% living with HIV. EPI coverage was over 80% across all vaccine types, excluding measles, with a trend towards an increase in delayed doses and non-vaccination as children aged. Two-dose measles vaccination coverage was 70.6% with 11.3% not having any measles vaccination. We found associations between delayed/non-vaccination with the primary caregiver being the MDR-TB index patient, lower household socioeconomic status and the site where children enrolled. We did not observe increased delays and non-vaccination during the COVID-19 period.ConclusionsEPI coverage in this vulnerable population was reasonable, except for measles, which was low. There was a trend towards increasing delays and non-vaccination with age. Targeting vaccination interventions at households affected by TB and poorer households emphasising caregiver education on routine vaccination especially as children age is important.Trial RegistrationSouth African National Clinical Trials Register (SANCTR) (RefDOH270117530) and UK’s Clinical Study Registry (Ref ISRCTN92634082, https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN92634082Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44263-026-00290-x.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO)’s recommended Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) schedule, introduced in 1974, has been the most cost-effective public health strategy, reducing childhood morbidity, disability and mortality from vaccine preventable diseases (VPD) worldwide [1–4]. Adequate vaccination coverage is a global goal and continues to be highlighted as a public health priority in the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030), which aligns with 14 of the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [2, 5].

South Africa adopted EPI in 1974, and as it evolved, expanded the schedule to cover additional infections [6]. The 2015 South African EPI (EPI(SA)) schedule, which replaced the 2009 schedule, included additional combination vaccines and adjustments to the age at which measles vaccine doses were given (Fig. 1) [7, 8]. All routine EPI(SA) vaccines should be recorded in the patient-held standardised Road-To-Health Book (RTHB), a medical record issued to all infants at birth to document children’s health in their first 5 years of life.

EPI(SA) coverage targets are ≥ 90% for children < 1 year of age (≥ 95% for measles and ≥ 80% for other vaccines) [6]. Vaccine coverage < 1 year of age, excluding Measles 2, increased from 81.9% in 2018/2019 to 83.5% in 2019/2020 [5, 9]. Measles vaccine coverage was lower, 71.7% and 68.8% for the first and second dose, respectively, between 2012 and 2017 [9]. Vaccine coverage < 1 year of age in the 2022/2023 reporting period, which included the SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) pandemic, dropped to 82.2% [5]. The pandemic affected access to routine health care, including immunisation services [5, 6, 10]. South Africa’s first SARS-CoV-2 case was reported on 05 March 2020 with subsequent rapid increases in patient numbers [11, 12]. The South African government proclaimed a National State of Disaster on 26 March 2020 with a 21-day national lock down [12, 13], with high level lock down measures remaining in place until 30 Apr 2020. Subsequently, adjustments were made to alert levels and safety measures until the official lifting of the National State of Disaster on 04 April 2022 [13, 14].

The WHO released routine immunisation services guidance at the height of the first COVID-19 wave [15] advising adaptations to routine immunisation services to prevent outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases in vulnerable populations, particularly in resource-constrained settings [15]. Additionally, the WHO recommended that countries should design robust catch-up vaccine strategies early and implement them once the pandemic was controlled [10]. Despite this, childhood vaccinations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) declined by over 10%. The largest declines were seen in the first three months of the pandemic [16, 17].

South Africa’s measles vaccination was a two-dose schedule (9 and 18 months) since 1995 [18]. In 2009, the WHO recommended that high-risk countries implement two-dose schedules initiated earlier, between 6 and 9 months, with the second dose administered 6–9 months later [19]. South Africa included this into the 2015 EPI(SA) schedule (Fig. 1) [8, 20]. Measles is highly contagious, potentially resulting in severe morbidity and mortality, particularly in under- or unvaccinated children aged < 5 years [21]. Measbio® (Biovac Institute (Pty) Ltd) was the measles vaccine procured and utilized by the Department of Health. Due to the lack of co-administration safety data, the recommendation was for Measbio® (Biovac Institute (Pty) Ltd) not to be given at the same time as any other vaccination [22, 23]. A measles outbreak in all provinces in South Africa, except for the Eastern Cape, occurred soon after the COVID-19 pandemic, directly demonstrating the impact of insufficient vaccine uptake and coverage [20, 21, 24]. Additionally, successful immunisation programmes rely on uninterrupted vaccine supply, and vaccine stock-outs have been challenging [25–28]. The COVID-19 pandemic compounded these challenges [29].

South Africa has a high tuberculosis (TB) burden [30]. Socioeconomic deprivation and high rates of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection (54%) contribute to this burden [30–33]. Socio-economic deprivation, including overcrowding, malnutrition, unemployment, poverty, and low levels of education are associated with both HIV infection and TB independently and are compounded by co-infection [31, 34]. In addition, socioeconomic deprivation, TB and HIV infection all independently impact uptake and accessibility of routine EPI vaccines in infants [35–38].

The Tuberculosis Child Multidrug-resistant Preventive Therapy (TB-CHAMP) study was conducted at a time when evidence to support treating individuals with multidrug-resistant (MDR)-TB infection to prevent progression to disease was lacking, despite MDR-TB being a global challenge to TB control [39]. The study provided evidence to support the safety and efficacy of levofloxacin as preventative therapy for children exposed to MDR-TB at a time when approximately 2 million children younger than 15 years of age where infected with multidrug-resistant (MDR)-TB, with MDR-TB developing in approximately 30 000 annually. The study’s primary outcomes results, published in 2024, showed that a six-monthly daily regimen of levofloxacin as preventative therapy for MDR-TB in children was safe and reduced the risk of TB in children by 56% compared to placebo [40]. This study, conducted before, during and post-COVID-19 pandemic (26 Sep 2017 to 21 Jan 2023), included children < 5 years old living in households affected by TB in diverse areas in South Africa. EPI(SA) vaccine uptake and timeliness is particularly important in this group of children who, due to immune immaturity, exposure risk and sociodemographic reasons, have a higher vulnerability to severe illness and death from VPD. The study collected routine immunisation data, providing an ideal opportunity to evaluate EPI(SA) vaccine uptake and timeliness in South African children exposed to MDR-TB before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design and participants

TB-CHAMP was registered with the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA) (Ref:20160128), the South African National Clinical Trials Register (SANCTR) (Ref: DOH-27-0117-5309, https://sanctr.samrc.ac.za/TrialDisplay.aspx?TrialID=4229), and approved by the Stellenbosch University Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC) (Ref: M16/02/009), the University of Witwatersrand HREC (Ref:160409) and United Kingdoms Clinical Study Registry (Ref: ISRCTN92634082, https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN92634082).

The study was a phase 3 multi-site, 1:1 cluster-randomised (by household), double-blind placebo-controlled MDR-TB prevention trial conducted at five South African sites (Desmond Tutu TB Centre (DTTC) in the Western Cape, Wits RHI Shandukani Research Centre in Gauteng, Perinatal Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) Research Unit (PHRU) in the North West Province, Tuberculosis & HIV Investigative Network (THINK) in KwaZulu-Natal, and Isango Lethemba TB Research Unit in the Eastern Cape) (Fig. 2) [39, 40]. There was no public or patient involvement in the design of this study.

The trial investigated the efficacy and safety of levofloxacin as preventative treatment in children who had been exposed to an adult individual with infectious MDR-TB within their household. Participants were recruited from urban, peri-urban and rural settings. The study initially enrolled children < 5 years old; a later amendment included children ≥ 5-<18 years of age. The trial enrolled children, regardless of Interferon-Gamma Release Assay (IGRA) or HIV status, residing in a household with an adult MDR-TB contact in the preceding 6 months. Prior to enrolment, all children were investigated for TB with the use of history taking, physical examination, plain chest film radiography and sputa collection if TB was suspected. Children with TB were not enrolled. Multiple children were enrolled per household and children were assigned at households level in a 1:1 allocation ratio to daily doses of levofloxacin or placebo. Each child received 24 weeks of the assigned regimen. The primary efficacy end point was incident TB by week 48 post randomisation. The primary safety end point was any adverse event of grade 3 or higher that was considered related to the trial regimen. Results showed that although preventative treatment with levofloxacin led to a lower incidence of TB in children exposed to MDR-TB compared to placebo the difference was not significant. Additionally, there were no safety concerns regarding 24 weeks of daily levofloxacin use [39, 40].

Here, we conducted an exploratory secondary analysis of the EPI(SA) coverage and vaccine timeliness in the TB-CHAMP cohort. There was no sample size calculation specific to this analysis. We a priori excluded children over the age of 5 years at enrolment (9% of the overall study population) as they would have received all immunizations according to the 2009 EPI(SA) and would not have had scheduled immunizations due during the study period; this group therefore represented a different cohort to children aged < 5 years. For the children < 5 years of age, depending on the age at enrolment, immunizations up to 18 months could all have been received prior to the study period or during the study period.

Data collection

Trial visits were at screening, enrolment, weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48 and 72. Data captured onto paper case report forms (CRF) were entered onto a centralised web-based database. Demographic and clinical data were collected at screening, including interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results, indicating Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection status. A detailed immunisation history from the RTHB was recorded onto an Immunisation CRF at all visits. Household (defined using a standard inclusive definition) characteristics, including household experiences, and socioeconomic status (SES) measures, were collected at screening through a household sociodemographic questionnaire (Supplementary Material 1) [41]. A per-household SES score was developed by applying the published principal component analysis coefficients of questions in the South African Demographic and Health Survey 2016 matched to applicable household characteristics of TB-CHAMP households enrolled in the study, including house type/materials, basic indicative assets, type of toilet facilities and water access. The SES score is represented by quintiles, where the first quintile represents the lowest SES score, and the fifth quintile represents the highest SES score [42].

Data analysis

Analysis included all children < 5 years at enrolment with a study Immunisation CRF completed. The projected date for each immunisation was calculated based on the date of birth of the child and the EPI(SA) schedule. Vaccinations were categorised as ‘On time’ if they were administered between 5 days before and 28 days after their projected date, ‘Early’ if administered more than 5 days before their projected date and ‘Delayed’ if administered more than 28 days after their projected date. For those vaccinations classified as ‘Delayed’, the median delays in number of weeks with interquartile range (IQR) were calculated. Doses classified as “Not received” were specifically recorded as not having been given on the RTHB and immunisation form. “Unknown data” and “Missing data” were records that did not have a definitive entry on the Immunisation CRF that indicated if the vaccine was given or not given.

The trial was initiated two years after the revised 2015 EPI(SA) schedule release when the measles vaccine timepoints changed. As the date of 2015 EPI(SA) implementation may have varied by facility, district, province, and age of the child at the time of the change, the reference point for expected dose age was inconsistent in this cohort. Therefore, a modified approach was undertaken in the analysis of the measles immunizations, where number of doses received and the duration between doses was analysed, rather than timeliness of the vaccine doses.

Bacillus Calmette Guérin (BCG), the third dose of Diphtheria, Tetanus, Pertussis, Inactivated Polio Vaccine and Haemophilus influenzae Type B (DTP/IPV/HiB 3) and measles coverage are most commonly assessed when reviewing vaccine programming [43]. These were therefore chosen a priori for analyses assessing factors associated with immunisation status. Here, immunisation status was defined as delayed/not received versus vaccination received on time/early or for measles vaccinations, as no dose received versus one dose or two doses.

The factors assessed were selected a priori based on sociological plausibility and included those relating to the child participant (sex, HIV exposure); the MDR-TB index patient (if they were a regular caregiver for the child); household factors (SES quintile); and study site. Modified Poisson regression was used to estimate risk ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals [44] accounting for household clustering. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for enrolment site and SES (chosen a priori), as well as any factors with corresponding p < 0.1 in univariable analyses. P-values were derived using Wald tests.

The COVID-19 pandemic “peak” period was defined as the first 6 months (26 Mar 2020 to 26 Sep 2020) after the initiation of the COVID-19 lockdown. As shown in a systematic review of childhood immunisation coverage in LMICs during the COVID pandemic, this was when the disruption to healthcare systems was expected to be at its worst and the period over which the country had a stepwise downgrade in alert levels and safety measures [17]. The potential impact of the COVID pandemic on vaccination rates was assessed. For each immunisation, the calendar period that the vaccination was due to be received was categorised as follows: 31/8/2012–25/03/2020 (pre-COVID), 26/03/2020–26/09/2020 (COVID) and 27/09/2020–31/01/2023 (post COVID). Within each period, the proportion of participants who received the vaccination late or not at all was estimated. The association between calendar period and immunisation status was assessed using modified Poisson regression models. Participants who were vaccinated before the start of the calendar period when their vaccination was due, were excluded.

Adjustment for multiple statistical testing was not deemed necessary given the exploratory nature of these secondary analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 or later (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

TB-CHAMP enrolled 922 participants from 497 households. For this analysis, we excluded 83 (9.0%) participants who were ≥ 5 years of age, and a further 25 (2.7%) participants < 5 years old who did not have an immunisation history recorded. Among children aged < 5 years, those who were excluded from analysis had slightly older median age than those included (3.4 years vs. 2.5 years), but otherwise the two groups were similar in terms of other baseline characteristics (Supplementary Material 2: Table S1). The 814 (88.3%) children, from 462 households, included in the analysis had an equal sex distribution, and a median age of 2.5 years (IQR 1.2–3.8) at baseline (Table 1). Of the 814 children, 286 (35%) were HIV-exposed but uninfected and 12 were living with HIV (1%). Median weight for height z-score was 0.3 (IQR − 0.5–1.0) and mid-upper arm circumference was 15.8 cm (IQR 14.7–17.0), indicating adequate growth and nutritional status in this cohort of children. Most were IGRA negative (n = 610, 75%) and had no previous TB diagnosis (99%).

DTTC in Cape Town accounted for more than half of the households enrolled (n = 254, 55%). Nearly half of the households’ SES fell within the 3rd quintile. The first (poorest) SES quintile comprised 6% of the households and none of the households fell into the 5th quintile. Based on parent/caregiver self-report, most households did not include anyone who had a university-level degree (n = 427, 92%).

Vaccine coverage (excluding measles) irrespective of timing was > 80%. However, timely coverage differed by vaccine type and timepoint (Table 2). Children received vaccines either early or on time at the birth, 6-week and 10-week timepoints in over 80% of cases but received less than 80% early or on time for all vaccines due after 10 weeks. Additionally, the proportion of early or on time vaccinations decreased incrementally from initial to final dose across each vaccine type (Fig. 3). Among children with delayed vaccinations, the median delay was 7–8 weeks for most vaccine types (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study, conducted between 2017 and 2023, found that, for all vaccines except measles, EPI(SA) vaccination coverage targets of over 80% were achieved in children under 5 years of age [6]. Our results in this vulnerable group of children were also comparable with the 2022 under-1-year immunisation coverage nationally (82.2%) and in Gauteng (83.2%), lower than that found in KwaZulu-Natal (92.2%), and higher than that recorded in the Western Cape (75.7%) [5]. Coverage was achieved regardless of HIV exposure and infection status. Measles vaccine 2-dose coverage was, however, suboptimal and significantly lower than the 95% coverage targets. Additionally, despite seemingly high vaccine coverage overall, the decrease in vaccine timeliness as the children aged was concerning.

Immunisation programmes traditionally use crude vaccination coverage to measure success, often using the proportion of children fully immunised with three doses of Diphtheria, Tetanus, Pertussis and two doses of measles vaccine as proxies [44]. Recently, however, there has been support for adding vaccine timeliness data alongside crude vaccine coverage to assess the success of immunisation programmes [45–47] We, therefore, opted to include both these measures for the same set of traditionally measured vaccines and, in addition, aligned our definition of delayed vaccinations (≥ 28 days after the recommended EPI age) with previous vaccine timeliness studies [46]. Around 6.0–8.5% of children received delayed vaccinations by 6-weeks, and this trend increased steadily as children aged, to 18.3% at 9 months. The median duration of delay was between 6.7 and 8.7 weeks, which is substantial and likely resulted in delays of subsequent vaccines. Delay in individual vaccinations increases the time at risk of acquiring the specific infection and negatively affects both herd immunity and under-five childhood mortality.

Recent scoping and systematic reviews of timeliness data in LMICs, where the burden of VPDs and under-5 mortality rates are highest, acknowledge that vaccine timeliness data is scarce but is becoming more available [45, 46]. African countries contribute to most of the LMIC vaccine timeliness data. Sub-Saharan African vaccine timeliness estimates are often derived from Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Clusters Surveys (MICS), which are administrative and can be inaccurate [42, 46, 48]. Good quality community-based vaccination coverage surveys are labour-intensive and costly [49]. Data from studies such as TB-CHAMP are crucial in augmenting programmatic and survey-based data and providing data on children living in households affected by TB and HIV.

BCG and OPV birth doses are given at a time when all children interact with a healthcare worker shortly after birth. Although these are the child’s entry vaccines into the immunisation programme, because they are given in a facility shortly after birth, it is more likely that coverage will be complete, compared to EPI vaccines which require specific EPI clinic visits [50]. Our study found that overall coverage for both BCG and OPV was below 95% with subsequent delayed vaccinations. The median delay in receiving these vaccines was likely due to catch-up doses given at the follow up EPI visits (6-weeks). Vaccine production and limited supplier options have compromised availability and procurement of BCG vaccines since 2013, despite subsequent improvements to the production and supply chain [49, 51–54]. The EPI National Coverage Survey Report 2020 further corroborated that stockouts were the biggest parent/caregiver-reported reason for missed BCG doses [27]. Two South African studies showed similar BCG uptake and delays, supporting findings from our birth BCG data [35, 48]. The timely coverage and the catch-up doses in our study and in the studies above are reassuring for the prevention of TB of all forms, especially in children < 5 years of age.

Non-vaccination, specifically for the two-dose measles vaccine, has been observed in other South African studies; similar rates were noted in our analysis [35, 48, 55]. We observed particularly poor measles vaccine coverage (70.6%), although most children who received measles vaccine had a 6-month interval between doses. The change in the measles schedule and the use of Measbio® in South Africa, a vaccine which cannot be co-administered with other vaccines, and potentially vaccine hesitancy may have contributed to these low rates [22, 23]. Recent measles outbreaks in South Africa emphasise the importance of two vaccine doses and > 95% vaccine coverage to improve herd immunity and prevent outbreaks. Based on these and our results, substantial work is still needed to be done to improve measles vaccination coverage [20, 21, 56].

In our analysis, site and/or SES was associated with both delayed and non-vaccination across all vaccines. In addition, delayed and non-vaccination appeared to be strongly associated with the primary caregiver being the MDR-TB index patient, the child living in a poorer household (lower SES), and the geographical area in which the child enrolled, as represented by site. Although most vaccine timeliness studies are from the pre-EPI(SA) 2015 period [28, 35, 55, 57], these studies suggest that health interventions should be directed towards reducing delays in EPI vaccinations, specifically in rural households, poor communities, and in children born to adolescent mothers, as delays in vaccinations are associated with low SES, lower levels of maternal education, and adolescent mothers [47, 58]. Data on the timeliness of vaccines in children exposed to HIV are limited, and there are no data on children living in TB-affected households, such as in our cohort, where all children were exposed to MDR-TB [37, 48, 55].

Similarly, studies have shown an association between EPI non-vaccination and lower SES, findings similar to ours [47, 51]. In addition, we also found differences between sites, each located in a different province with non-vaccination, which supports the existing evidence indicating that South Africa’s vaccine coverage varies by and within provinces [5, 27]. These findings likely indicate the socio-economic challenges faced by families affected by TB disease and underscores the need for enhanced support for these families or households, including community-oriented vaccination programmes, support grants and other forms of social support while they are most vulnerable [59, 60].

Given the timing of our trial, we reviewed data for BCG, DTP/IPV/HiB 3, Measles 1 and Measles 2 before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic period. We did not see increases of delay or non-vaccination in our cohort. This may have been related to the small number of EPI immunizations due during the time of the defined COVID-19 period, and the interactions with study teams who during that time may have proactively encouraged routine EPI at local routine healthcare clinics. Notably, none of the study teams supplied EPI vaccines outside of routine care.

Although this study was not designed to evaluate EPI coverage and timeliness of vaccines, it has several strengths. The large cohort of children < 5 years, enrolled at urban, peri-urban, and rural geographic locations, provides a diverse landscape in which to analyse standard of care EPI(SA) vaccine-specific coverage and timeliness. Considering that South Africa has 7.5 million adults (≥ 15 years) and 160 000 children (< 15 years) living with HIV, this cohort of HIV unexposed, HIV exposed but HIV-negative, and HIV-positive children, all from households affected by MDR-TB, provides valuable data on extremely vulnerable children [61]. Additionally, robust study documentation ensured increased accuracy and completeness of the data.

Limitations of our study include that the long-term study follow-up included visits during which study teams intervened with counselling for missed and delayed vaccinations. The COVID-19 pandemic introduced a potential bias to our results, as access to healthcare facilities during this period was erratic due to the shutdowns, particularly during the first 6 months. However, it was also a period when counselling and education regarding both EPI and COVID-19 vaccinations were emphasised at both the EPI healthcare facilities and at the study sites. The SES index method relied on specific indicators of socioeconomic status, including housing, utilities, and assets. It is possible that this method was insufficiently specific to capture the multidimensional nature of socioeconomic status; however, in the absence of more detailed household data, the index appeared to be a reasonable indication of relative SES. Due to study design, variables such as maternal age, maternal education level, the number of siblings in the household, or the distance of the home from the EPI provider, and any hospitalization were not measured. These could therefore not be assessed as factors contributing to delayed or missed doses. Finally, because of the exploratory nature of these analyses, formal adjustment for multiple testing was not undertaken, and the findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

Overall, in this vulnerable cohort of children, routine vaccination coverage under the EPI(SA) system was > 80% for most vaccines, except for measles. Measles vaccination was low for complete coverage in children < 5 years of age, and 11.3% of children receiving no measles vaccine dose at all. Despite good coverage, a delay in doses, which increases the risk of acquiring VPDs, was a notable trend, increasing with age. Socio-economic factors including a lower SES classification, province, and the primary caregiver being the MDR-TB index patient underscore the importance of social and financial support for households affected by MDR-TB. Continuous monitoring and interventions are needed to improve vaccination coverage and timeliness, especially as children age. The between-site differences, which may represent provincial and district variability in vaccine delivery processes, requires further interrogation.
