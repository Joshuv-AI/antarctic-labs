Concepts Worth Having: Refining VLM-Guided Concept Bottleneck Models with Minimal Annotations
Nicola Debole, Andrea Passerini, Stefano Teso, Andrea Pugnana, Emanuele Marconato

Abstract
Concept-bottleneck models (CBMs) are neural classifiers that compute predictions from high-level concepts extracted from the input. CBMs ensure stakeholders can understand the concepts – and the predictions they entail – by learning these from concept-level annotations, which are however seldom available. Recent CBM architectures work around this issue by obtaining annotations from Vision-Language Models (VLMs). While greatly broadening applicability, doing so can yield lower quality concepts and therefore less interpretable models. We strike for a middle ground by introducing Vision-plus-Human-guided CBM ( VH-CBM ), a hybrid approach that exploits both VLMs and a small amount of dense annotations. VH-CBM employs a Gaussian Process in the VLM’s embedding space, which captures useful global information about the target domain, to propagate the expert’s supervision to any target data point. Our empirical evaluation shows how VH-CBM predicts more accurate concepts than VLM-guided CBMs even when annotating as little as 1 % 1\% of the data, while sporting better concept calibration and supporting active learning.



1
Introduction

Concept-bottleneck models (CBMs)
[
26
]
are explainable-by-design neural classifiers that infer predictions from high-level concepts –
e.g.
, objects present in an input image – they extract from the input. CBMs achieve task performance comparable to black-box alternatives, all while enabling stakeholders to easily
inspect

the concepts responsible for any prediction.
To support these operations, CBMs encourage the concepts to be interpretable by learning them from
expert annotations
. Yet, these are often unavailable and expensive to obtain, hindering applicability.

State-of-the-art
vision-language-guided CBMs
(henceforth, VLM-CBMs) work around this limitation by replacing expert annotations with weak supervision obtained from large vision-language models (VLMs)
[
50
,
37
,
60
,
21
]
. While architectural details differ (cf.
Section

2
), these models enjoy broader applicability while sporting similar or better task performance than CBMs.
The downside is that VLM annotations are not entirely reliable
[
17
,
43
,
6
]
, meaning that VLM-CBMs’ concepts tend to be
less accurate
and therefore
less interpretable

[
7
]
. This is problematic, as it compromises the promise of interpretability that CBMs are meant to uphold.

: VLM-CBMs rely solely on concepts provided by VLMs – either at inference time (as above) or for distilling a concept extractor
g
, cf.
Section

2
– which can be inaccurate, compromising interpretability
[

]
.

exploits the VLM’s embedding space to propagate expert concept supervision to obtain concept statistics (i.e.,
{\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}}
and

from the GP) for any test point, improving concept accuracy with few annotations.

We tackle this overlooked
trade-off by introducing Vision-plus-Human-guided CBMs (
VH-CBM
s for short), a simple
hybrid
model that integrates widely available, but unreliable, VLMs with a modicum of trustworthy expert supervision.

VH-CBM
s build on the intuition that the VLM embedding space, despite yielding potentially poor
point-wise
annotations, encodes useful
global
information about the application domain.

VH-CBM
s realize this insight by introducing efficient, stochastic Gaussian Processes (GPs)
[
57
]
in embedding space: given expert concept annotations for selected examples, the GPs exploit its geometry to infer more accurate and calibrated concept activations for any incoming input.
These are fed to a transparent inference layer to obtain a prediction, cf.
Fig.

1
.

We experimentally evaluate
VH-CBM
s on four synthetic and real-world datasets.
Our results indicate that, compared to existing VLM-CBMs,
VH-CBM
s predict concepts more accurately from minimal amounts of expert annotations: even when annotating
a small portion of the dataset,
VH-CBM
s substantially outperform VLM-CBMs according to different concept-prediction quality metrics, while sporting better concept-level calibration.
Moreover, increasing the amount of supervision
monotonically improves concept accuracy, indicating how
VH-CBM
s can successfully make use of expert annotations when available, especially when allowed to actively query concept annotations.

Contributions
: Summarizing, we
(
i
) identify an interpretability-applicability trade-off affecting current CBM and VLM-CBM architectures;
(
ii
) introduce
VH-CBM
, a simple approach that addresses this trade-off by mediating between the VLMs and the CBMs with a suitably designed Gaussian Process; and
(
iii
) demonstrate the promise of
VH-CBM
in terms of concept accuracy and calibration on several data sets and two backbones w.r.t. regular and VLM-based CBMs.

2
Background

Notation
. We abbreviate
\{1,\ldots,n\}
to
[n]
.
We denote inputs as
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and labels as
y\in[\ell]
.
We consider
k
(binary or categorical) concepts, each with
v_{i}\geq 2
possible values, capturing task-relevant information present in the input,
e.g.
, objects and their properties. We encode them as concept activations in multi-hot format,
{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{v}
with
v:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}
.
We indicate with
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the input space; with
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{v}
,
v<d
, the space of concept activations; and with
\mathcal{Y}=[\ell]
the set of labels.

Concept-based Models
. CBMs sport a two stage architecture comprising (
i
) a
concept extractor

g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{C}
that maps the inputs (
e.g.
, images of fruits) to concept activations (
e.g.
, visual properties such as “
red
” and “
round
”), and (
ii
) a
task predictor

f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{Y}
– typically a linear classifier – that maps concepts to classes (
e.g.
, “
apple
” vs. “
pear
”).
Given a training set
\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{(i)},{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}
with
n
examples, CBMs can be trained (a)
independently
,
i.e.
,
g
and
f
are trained separately and later combined; (b)
sequentially
,
i.e.
,
g
is trained first, and its output is used to train
f
; or (c)
jointly
,
i.e.
,
g
and
f
are trained together by optimizing a joint objective such as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{CE}(f(g({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{(i)})),y^{(i)})+\frac{\lambda}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathsf{CE}(\hat{c}^{(i)}_{j},c^{(i)}_{j}),

(1)

where
\mathsf{CE}
is the (multi-class) cross-entropy loss and
\hat{c}^{(i)}_{j}
is the probability or logit returned by the concept extractor
g
for the
j
-th concept of
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{(i)}
.
Thanks to this two-stage architecture, CBMs can be
interpretable
, as one can assess which concepts affect the task prediction, and
intervenable
, as users can correct mispredicted concepts to improve the model’s predictions
[
47
,
39
]
.

Learning aligned concepts.
The interpretability of CBMs
hinges on whether the concepts they learn are aligned with human semantics
[
45
,
26
,
33
]
.
Unless this is the case, one can encounter or construct inputs
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
(
e.g.
, images of red apples) on which the CBM and stakeholders infer different concepts (
e.g.
, “orange” vs. “red”). Such semantic mismatches complicate interpretation: CBM explanations (
e.g.
, “this is an apple because it is red”) rely on the learned concepts, yet these may not possess the meaning that humans associate to them.
Ensuring that CBMs satisfy this basic property requires training them with an abundance of high-quality concept annotations
[
26
,
32
,
62
]
. This is essential, as CBMs trained
without
concept supervision can learn completely misaligned concepts
[
4
]
.
However, this restricts usage of CBMs to the few tasks for which high-quality concept supervision is available.

Vision-language-guided CBMs
. Recent CBM architectures work around this issue by leveraging a Vision-Language Model (VLM), such as
CLIP

[
40
]
or Grounding Dino
[
28
]
, to generate weak concept supervision in a zero-shot fashion.
At a high level, VLM-CBMs can be broken down into two steps.
First, if a
concept vocabulary
is not available, they generate one using a foundation model, such as Chat-GPT
[
1
]
. This amounts to querying
the latter for textual descriptions of
concepts that are both discriminative and human understandable,

e.g.
, “the object is orange” or “the object is round”. If a concept vocabulary is provided externally, this is used instead.
Next, VLM-CBMs obtain
weak supervision
for all concepts in the vocabulary. The specific setup depends on the architecture.
Some models do so by computing image-text similarity scores by comparing the embeddings of the input image and the textual descriptions of the concepts
[
37
,
60
]
; others query the VLM directly by asking questions like “is it true that the object is orange?”
[
50
]
; others still leverage a sparse autoencoder
[
42
]
.
The resulting concept activations are either employed as weak supervision to train a regular CBM, or extracted at inference time and fed to a linear inference layer directly, without the need for training a neural backbone
g

[
61
]
.
Our approach follows the latter strategy.

Interpretability of VLM-CBMs
. The main strength of VLM-CBMs is that they enjoy vastly wider applicability compared to regular CBMs, while offering comparable or better task performance.
However, this comes at a cost: the quality of the concepts they learn strongly depends on the VLM’s responses, which can be erratic. It is well known that VLMs are not entirely reliable, in that they suffer from hallucinations
[
17
]
and factual and logical inconsistencies
[
43
,
6
]
.
Such overreliance on VLM annotations implies that VLM-CBMs can learn misaligned concepts: as shown by
7
, VLM-CBMs’ concepts can be substantially worse than those acquired by regular CBMs according to several quality metrics, including concept accuracy, undermining their interpretability.

3
Hybrid Human-and-Vision Guided CBMs

In order to meet its promises, any CBM should satisfy at least the following desiderata:

D1

Task accuracy
: it should output high-quality task predictions.

D2

Concept accuracy
: it should learn accurate concepts, so as to support interpretation.

D3

Applicability
: it should rely on a minimal amount of expert annotations.

Importantly,
existing architectures fail at least one of our desiderata
. Regular CBMs are not widely applicable, failing
D3
, while VLM-CBMs suffer from impaired concept accuracy, thus failing
D2
.
This suggests current CBM architectures face a
trade-off between interpretability and applicability: in order to learn highly accurate concepts, it is paramount that CBMs have access to high-quality concept labels, and these are typically only obtainable from experts. Naturally, this requirement restricts applicability. At the same time, completely relying on weak labels produced by VLMs, while improving applicability, can drastically reduce concept accuracy
[
7
]
, and thus interpretability.

Beyond concept accuracy
. Desideratum
D2
focuses on concept accuracy, as this is a hard prerequisite for interpretability: unless a human and a CBM infer the same concepts for the same inputs, they might not agree on what those concepts mean.
However, concept quality encompasses other key factors.
An important metric is disentanglement
[
52
,
9
,
16
,
45
,
32
,
7
]
, which measures to what extent concepts encode unwanted information about each other. While higher accuracy can benefit disentanglement
[
32
]
, this is not always the case. At the same time, concepts should be calibrated, so that users can reliably infer whether the learned concepts and their semantics can be trusted
[
31
]
.
Hence, in our experiments we will evaluate accuracy, calibration, and disentanglement.

3.1
The
VH-CBM
Framework

We tackle the above trade-off by introducing
VH-CBM
, a simple but effective approach combining the strengths of regular and vision-guided CBMs.
VH-CBM
produces highly accurate task (
D1
) and concept (
D2
) predictions by integrating two sources of information: the VLM’s embedding space and a modicum of expert supervision. The latter is kept to a minimum so as to retain applicability (
D3
).

Overview
. A
VH-CBM
comprises three components, cf.
Fig.

1
:

i
) a VLM, mapping the inputs to a well-structured embedding space;

ii
) one
Gaussian Process
(GP) classifier
[
57
]
for each concept (
e.g.
, one for shape and one for color), living in the VLM’s embedding space. These are responsible for estimating the mean and variance of the concept activations; binary concepts (
e.g.
, wearing makeup) are predicted with binary GPs and categorical concepts (
e.g.
, color) with multi-class GPs; and

iii
) a linear inference layer that receives the per-concept statistics and infers a prediction.
Essentially, the GPs act as a middle man between the VLM and the inference layer, propagating the expert annotations by following the geometry of the embedding space, while providing all benefits of Bayesian models, such as improved calibration.

Training and inference
. Like other CBMs,
VH-CBM
assumes access to a concept vocabulary – defined either manually or through a foundation model – to determine the set of target concepts. It also requires two data sets: a larger data set
\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{(i)},y^{(i)}:i\in[n]\}
annotated with task labels only, used for training, and a smaller subset
\mathcal{D}_{s}\subset\mathcal{D}
of
n_{s}\ll n
examples accompanied by expert annotations
{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{(i)}
, used also at inference time.
VH-CBM
are trained as follows.
First, one obtains the VLM embeddings
{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all inputs
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}
, which are then standardized
and normalized.
Next, each GP classifier
is fit on the annotated data
\mathcal{D}_{s}
, as explained below.
Finally, the GPs’ predictions are employed to train a linear layer on the entire training set
\mathcal{D}
, thus implementing a form of
sequential
training (see
Section

2
).

For any new input
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
, the
VH-CBM
sequentially applies the VLM, GPs and inference layer
to obtain the concept activations and a prediction.
Crucially,
the GPs’ predictions exploit the expert’s supervision
\mathcal{D}_{s}
, propagating it according to the VLM’s embedding space
.
Next, we discuss the GP and inference layer in detail.

Inferring Concepts with Gaussian Processes
. GPs are a well-known class of Bayesian kernel-based models
[
57
,
36
]
.
VH-CBM
employs them for predicting concept activations from given densely annotated examples, working directly on the embeddings
{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}
.
Note that
VH-CBM
has to predict multiple concepts (
e.g.
, color and shape) for each example, and that each of them can be binary or categorical.

To this end, we adapt the multi-class architecture of
53
, stacking together one GP for each concept to predict all concepts jointly.
To illustrate how this works,
we focus on a single categorical concept
c_{i}
for some
i\in[k]
, say, color, with
v_{i}\geq 2
possible values.
We want to compute an activation for each such value while taking into account their mutual exclusivity: color can be either red or blue, but not both. Taking these dependencies into account helps performance – as confident positive predictions suppress all alternatives, most of which are incorrect – and reduces sample complexity.
We follow the same approach as
35
and perform regression on Dirichlet-transformed labels instead of solving a classification task. This Dirichlet formulation allows us to produce well-calibrated probability estimates, avoiding the intractable inference required by standard multi-class GP classifiers.
Moreover, similarly to
53
, we introduce
v_{i}

latent
GP regressors, indexed by
j\in[v_{i}]
. Each GP is parameterized by a mean function
m_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
over the embeddings, which acts as a prior on the value’s score, and a covariance function (or kernel)
K_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that defines a similarity between embeddings. Together, they determine a distribution over real-valued functions

s_{ij}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\sim\mathcal{GP}\big(m_{ij},K_{ij}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\,,

(2)

mapping from embeddings
{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}
to real-valued scores. Following
53
, we then linearly mix these values together as
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})
, where the vector
{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}
captures all
j
-th scores of the concept
c_{i}
, and
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{v_{i}\times v_{i}}
denotes a learnable matrix used to correlate the individual components.
The resulting
j
-th component
({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}))_{j}

acts as a logit
\hat{c}
for concept
i
attaining value
j
, cf.
Eq.

1
.
Finally, the predictive distribution over color values is computed as the softmax of the transformed scores, in expectation over the distribution induced by the
v_{i}
latent GPs, namely

p(c_{i}=j\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}}\left[\mathrm{softmax}\big({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\big)_{j}\right]\,.

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}=\prod_{j}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(m_{ij},K_{ij}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})
is the joint distribution over all concepts.
In practice, we model the mean function as a learnable constant and employ an RBF kernel,
i.e.
,

\textstyle K_{ij}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}^{(a)},{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}^{(b)})=\alpha_{ij}^{2}\exp\big(-\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}^{(a)}-{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}^{(b)}\lVert^{2}/2\rho_{ij}^{2}\big)\,.

(4)

The hyper-parameter
\alpha_{ij}
is the output scale and
\rho_{ij}
is the length scale which are fit on data.

The mixing matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}
is also learned during training, as done by
53
. To determine the prior on the concept score
m_{ij}
, selecting inducing points of the kernels
K_{ij}
and their hyper-parameters, we employ a stochastic variant of GPs
[
15
]
that maximises the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). The ELBO is computed between the latent GP posterior
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and the Dirichlet-transformed
[
35
]
concept annotations, which proved effective in predicting concept activations in representation space
[
56
]
.
The ELBO objective balances the complexity of the GP model (via the KL divergence from the prior) and the accuracy of the predicted class probabilities
p(c_{i}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})
relative to the annotated concept value for the embedding
{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}
.
Additional details can be found in
Appendix

D
.

Fitting the CBM
.
We use the
v
concept activations to fit a linear layer
f
mapping concepts to labels.
This takes as input the stacked concept scores
({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}))
sampled from the GP.
Let
{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}):=({\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{1}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})^{\top},\ldots,{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{k}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{v}
,
be the overall concept score for all
k
concepts sampled from the
joint GP distribution
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}}=\prod\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}=\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times v}
be the block-diagonal mixing matrix.
We train a linear classifier with weights

{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}=({\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times\ell}
and bias
{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
to maximize the likelihood of the true label for all pairs
({\bm{\mathrm{z}}},y)\in\mathcal{D}
, namely:

\log p(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})=\log\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}}\left[\mathrm{softmax}\big({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}+{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}\big)_{y}\right]\,.

(5)

As with other CBMs, the prediction can be explained in terms of activated concepts,
e.g.
, by presenting stakeholders their mean activations.

Uncertainty and active learning
. As we will see in
Section

4
, propagating expert supervision through a Bayesian model helps
VH-CBM
achieve substantially better concept calibration. This is useful for further reducing annotation cost through uncertainty-based active learning
[
46
]
. In practice, in our experiments we employ the GP’s predictive uncertainty to prioritize what concepts (but not what examples) to annotate. We provide further details in
Section

A.1
.

3.2
Benefits and Limitations

Annotation cost
.
VH-CBM
s aim at improving concept accuracy by integrating expert annotations into the VLM-CBM pipeline.
The main downside is the annotation cost. We argue this is limited and well justified. Our experiments in
Section

4
indicate that few concept annotations can substantially boost concept accuracy, which is essential, especially in high-stakes applications where interpretability is a hard requirement. Moreover, as we will show in
Section

4
, the GP uncertainty estimates improve the efficacy of active learning, further reducing annotation costs.
Thus,
VH-CBM
s sport better applicability (
D3
) than regular CBMs while performing as well as VLM-CBMs in terms of task accuracy (
D1
) and much better in terms of concept accuracy (
D2
).

Scalability
. A second concern is scalability. We remark that, in our case, GP inference scales gracefully with the number of concepts
k
(specifically,
linearly
) and with annotated set size. This is achieved by employing inducing points
[
49
]
. Moreover, the per-concept GPs are fit independently, making parallelization possible. We report further implementation details in
Section

D.1
.

In addition, despite having to invoke a VLM at inference time,
VH-CBM
is still efficient in practice: the embedding step relies on a frozen encoder, which requires no gradient computation and can be efficiently batched on modern hardware. This is in line with other VLM-CBMs
[
61
,
42
,
60
]
.
However, an alternative is to replace the VLM at inference time with a proper concept extractor distilled from the GP’s annotations
[
37
,
27
,
50
]
, potentially using inverse propensity scoring or distribution matching to avoid transferring information about concepts the GP is unsure about.

Access to embeddings
. Finally,
VH-CBM
s require access to the VLM’s embedding space. This is by design: the
global
geometry of the embedding space tends to be more reliable than its
local
weak annotations. Our results support this observation: in practice, the baselines using only
CLIP
’s annotations underperform compared to both regular CBMs and
VH-CBM
. This holds for two distinct VLM backbones:
CLIP

[
40
]
, the de-facto standard for contrastive image-textual embeddings; and
DINO
(v3)
[
48
]
, a state-of-the-art self-supervised vision backbone.

Related Work

Self-explainable Neural Networks.
Explainable-by-design neural networks have garnered considerable popularity
[
38
,
18
]
, owing to their promise of high task accuracy and interpretability. In all these approaches, the flow of information from input to prediction is mediated by high-level concepts learned from data.
Learning with only task labels supervision does not ensure the model’s concepts are aligned with human semantics
[
4
]
,
and known conditions to provably recover the concepts (
e.g.
,
1
-sparsity of the concepts
[
12
]
, access to multiple environments
[
63
]
) might not hold at scale.
CBMs
[
26
]
and their upgrades
[
62
,
44
,
13
,
32
,
22
,
54
]
supervise the concept bottleneck during training, cf.
Section

2
, and as such suffer from impaired applicability (
D3
).
Recent VLM-CBMs improve on this aspect by replacing expert annotations with a VLM’s
[
37
,
60
,
61
,
27
,
21
,
42
,
25
]
, but in doing so risk lowering concept accuracy (
D2
), as shown in
[
7
]
and in
Section

4
.

VH-CBM
s combine the best of both worlds: global domain-specific information from the VLMs embedding space and local information from expert annotations.
We exclude VLM-CBMs that focus primarily on concept discovery (
e.g.
,
[
25
,
42
]
), as they automatically identify the bottleneck concepts rather than relying on predefined ones, and therefore lack ground-truth concept annotations, making it difficult to measure concept accuracy and calibration.

Gaussian Processes in embedding space
.
The works most closely related to ours are two.
10
employ GPs to decide when to add new concepts in the vocabulary. This is orthogonal to our work and could be combined with it.

56
, like us, build on the GP architecture of
[
35
]
, to obtain confidence estimates for concept activations to be used for explaining the model. This technique is compatible with
VH-CBM
, although our method is mainly designed for propagating expert supervision instead and leverages expert annotations in an active manner.

Concept quality and interpretability
. While inaccurate concepts cannot match human semantics, achieving high concept accuracy might not guarantee full interpretability of the bottleneck
[
34
,
30
,
33
]
.
Recent works have shown that CBMs can maximize task performance by learning concepts that depend on spurious information, such as unrelated concepts – causing
entanglement

[
16
,
19
,
45
,
52
,
9
]
– and contextual or stylistic cues – causing
information leakage

[
34
,
30
,
32
,
13
]
and sensitivity to
non-local changes
of the concept bottleneck
[
41
,
50
]
.
While resorting to VLM-CBMs favors better applicability and promotes the access to rich embedding spaces, it hides interpretability issues leading to high leakage and low disentanglement in practice
[
50
,
7
]
.

6
Conclusion

We introduce
VH-CBM
, a simple yet effective concept-based approach that strikes a middle ground between interpretability and applicability.
VH-CBM
infers concept activations by (optionally, actively) collecting and propagating expert concept supervision according to the geometry of a VLM’s embedding space. Our results show that, with only a small amount of concept supervision,
VH-CBM
achieves substantial improvements in concept accuracy and calibration over standard VLM-CBMs. Well-calibrated concept predictions naturally enable active learning, allowing efficient selection of the most informative samples to annotate and further reducing the supervision burden. Finally, the improved concept accuracy yields more semantically aligned concepts and better downstream task performance.

While
VH-CBM
s are designed to rapidly propagate concept annotations, thus lessening the cost of traditional CBMs, pairing it with losses that promote alignment and discourage entanglement is an open future venue. We expect that
VH-CBM
s may also benefit by other post-hoc correction routines on latent embeddings that build on disentanglement analyses,
e.g.
,
[
20
]
, and structured latent spaces based on object-centric representations,
e.g.
,
[
51
]
.
In the same vein, another promising line of research involves exploiting the VLM’s embedding space, which spans outside the application domain, to construct out-of-distribution (OOD) acquisition strategies tailored for improving concept quality by removing entanglement and concept leakage
[
34
,
30
]
, as doing so requires OOD data in general
[
33
]
.

Appendix A
Pipeline

The choice of the number of annotated samples to start with, as well as the number of concepts to annotate at each step, is arbitrary. We choose 40 as the initial value to keep annotation costs low while providing enough samples for the GP to begin propagating information. We tested other values with consistent results. The same applies to the step size: the number of concepts annotated per iteration can be adjusted to reflect different user preferences. If minimal annotation effort is desired, smaller step sizes are preferable; if faster convergence is the priority, larger values can be used to reach good performance in fewer iterations.
At each iteration, the desired number of samples is collected and annotated. The selection of which concepts to annotate is based on an active acquisition function: we query the GP’s uncertainty estimates and prioritize the most uncertain concepts for annotation, leaving the remaining ones unannotated. Alternatively, concepts can be selected at random, which also yields competitive results. We compare the active and random acquisition functions in
Fig.

3
.

A.1
Uncertainty-based Acquisition Function

More specifically, our active acquisition function begins by randomly sampling a subset of the training data. This promotes exploration and avoids clustering annotations around uncertain samples that are nonetheless close to one another in the embedding space, which would be inefficient in terms of annotation cost. Within this subset, we measure the GP uncertainty and annotate only the concepts with high uncertainty, effectively discarding the least uncertain candidates.

For instance, consider
CelebA
, where each image has 39 concepts. In the random baseline, 60 images are drawn and all their concepts annotated, yielding 2340 concept annotations per iteration. In our active selection procedure, we instead draw a larger pool of 95 images (3705 concepts in total), measure the GP uncertainty for each concept, and select the 2340 most uncertain concept-image pairs. This way, both strategies share the same annotation budget, but active selection avoids redundant annotations on concepts the model is already confident about, allocating the budget where it is most informative.

The size of the random pool can be adjusted, governing the exploration-exploitation trade-off: a larger pool allows more selective concept annotation, but also increases the risk of drawing uninformative samples. This choice is ultimately data-dependent; in all our experiments we set it to 95 images, as in the
CelebA
example above, and we observed that small variations around this value do not significantly affect performance.

Appendix C
Calibration

To measure calibration, we evaluate both the baseline and competitor models using the ECCE metric
[
3
]
. Although the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is the standard measure of calibration, it is heavily dependent on the choice of binning; the ECCE metric is specifically designed to overcome this limitation.
In the results reported in
Table

2
we adopt the ECCE-R variant, which measures the full range of the cumulative deviations.
As in
[
3
]
, cumulative deviations
C_{k}
are defined as

C_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(R_{j}-S_{j})

(6)

where
S_{j}
are the predicted (sorted) scores and
R_{j}
the actual outcomes. ECCE-MAD is the maximum absolute value of the cumulative differences across all observations; ECE1 corresponds to the weighted sum of the absolute values of the differences between the average response and the average score in each bin, while ECE2 is the weighted sum of the squares of the differences between the average response and the average score in each bin.

For completeness, a table reporting all calibration metrics is also provided in
Table

4
.
It should be noted that calibration is measured with respect to the concept predictions — that is, the output of the backbone — within each CBM variant, rather than with respect to the final label predictions.
We observe that
VH-CBM
gives the best calibration results across datasets, except in
Shapes3d
where it ranks second-best and
LP-@
demonstrates particularly competitive.
LP-@
fares good also on
Derma
(it is second best on all columns), but sensibly downgrades w.r.t.
VH-CBM
on
CelebA
and
CUB
. Other supervised methods do not compete with
VH-CBM
, which shows low calibration scores and improved concept accuracy compared to VLM-CBMs. On calibration
LaBo
,
LF-CBM
, and
VLG-CBM
lag behind
VH-CBM
with seldom good results;
e.g.
,
LF-CBM
in
Derma
fares good, but lacks in all other datasets.

Appendix D
Implementation Details

We implement the GP using
GPytorch

[
11
]
.
In order to scale to larger annotated sets, we employ a sparse GP that leverages a smaller set of inducing points to approximate a full GP distribution over the data
[
49
]
, implemented through
gpytorch.models.ApproximateGP
.
As mentioned in
Section

3.1
, we use a constant mean function (
gpytorch.means.ConstantMean
) and an RBF kernel (
gpytorch.kernels.RBFKernel
).

D.1
Hyperparameters

In terms of the GP hyperparameters, for every latent GP we initialize
length scale

\rho_{ij}
to
1.41
, the
output scale

\alpha_{ij}
to
1.0
and the mean function to
0.0
. These are learned during training in order to find the optimal values.
The noise for the Dirichlet Likelihood is set as
0.01
and
the number of inducing points is set to that of annotated samples (
i.e.
, images that have expert annotations available).

D.2
Running times

The training time for reaching convergence in a single iteration step of the multi-label multi-class Gaussian process varies depending on the number of concept values (
v_{i}
).
On
Shapes3d
, it takes approximately 9 minutes using an A100 GPU.
For
CUB
and
CelebA
, the training time is longer (due to higher number of concepts) with an average total duration of 45 minutes. The fastest is
Derma
, with a run time of only 2 minutes on average per iteration step.
All reported times assume sequential training over concepts; since each concept GP is trained independently, parallelizing across available workers is possible and can reduce wall-clock time proportionally to the degree of parallelism. We leave this optimization to future work, as sequential training was sufficient in our experimental setting.

Appendix E
Metrics

We measure
F_{1}(C)
as the average of macro
F_{1}
-score for each concept activation
c_{i}
of the models, with
i\in[v]
.
For
VH-CBM
, measurements of
F_{1}(C)
are performed on the concept predictions through
\{p(c_{i}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\}
.

We evaluate
\mathsf{ROC_{auc}}(C)
for each component
c_{i}
, with
i\in[v]
.
For
VH-CBM
, measurements of
\mathsf{ROC_{auc}}(C)
are performed on the expected mean of the concept logits of
c_{i}
, namely
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}}({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}))
.

These metrics allows us to uniformly evaluate both competitors that consider categorical concepts, like
VH-CBM
, and others which model them as their one-hot binarized version, like
LaBo
,
LF-CBM
, and
VLG-CBM
.

We use the disentanglement metric from DCI
[
9
]
, which quantifies the degree to which each learned concept score encodes the variations of a single ground-truth concept, giving a lower score the more single concept scores encode of several ground-truth concepts.

For all VLM-CBM and
CBM
@,
disentanglement is directly measured from by learning the map from concept activations to ground-truth concept values, while for
VH-CBM
we use the expected mean of concept
c_{i}
from the GP,
i.e.
,
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}})\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}_{i}}({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{i}({\bm{\mathrm{z}}}))
.

DCI computes disentanglement as follows. For the general case of

k
ground-truth concepts
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
and
v
learned concepts
\hat{c}_{1},\ldots,\hat{c}_{v}
, the disentanglement score is defined as

D=\sum_{i=1}^{v}\rho_{i}D_{i},\quad\text{where}\quad\rho_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{ij}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{\ell j}}.

(7)

This essentially captures the weighted average of the per-learned concept disentanglement scores
D_{i}
.
Although
k
and
v
may differ in general, in our experiments we always have
v=k
(specifically,
v=39
for
CelebA
,
v=42
for
Shapes3d
,
v=7
for
Derma
, and
v=112
for
CUB
).
The relative importance matrix
R
is obtained from a regressor trained to predict each ground-truth concept
c_{j}
from the learned concepts
\hat{c}_{i}
.
Each entry
R_{ij}
quantifies the importance of
\hat{c}_{i}
in predicting
c_{j}
.

As in the original implementation
[
9
]
, we use a random forest regressor to obtain the coefficients
R_{ij}
, computed as the fraction of decision tree splits on
\hat{c}_{i}
when predicting
c_{j}

[
5
]
.

Appendix F
Architectural Details and Hyperparameters

Here, we report additional details on the different models we tested. For all experiments, we selected hyperparameters – including the initial learning rate, initialization values of the GP hyperparameters – to optimize for concept
F
-score on the validation set. The batch size was fixed to
512
for all models.

The weights for all pre-trained models were obtained from
PyTorch
and
imgclsmob
.
All methods were trained with sequential training, cf.
Section

2
(and
[
26
]
).

Concept-bottleneck Models.

CBM
is implemented using a ResNet18 backbone
[
14
]
followed by a two-stage prediction pipeline: concept prediction and class prediction.
All input images of size 224×224×3 are passed through a ResNet18
[
14
]
pretrained on ImageNet, which outputs a 1000-dimensional feature vector.
For
CUB
only, we use a ResNet18 finetuned on this dataset (as the original implementation of
[
26
]
) with output dimension is 200 (matching the number of classes). Also
LF-CBM
and
LaBo
adopt this backbone.

The 1000-dimensional (or 200 for
CUB
) feature vector is passed through a linear layer projecting to the concept bottleneck.
This layer returns concept activations, which is trained using standard cross-entropy loss as in
Eq.

1
.
When datasets are imbalanced, we use a reweighting term to mitigate class imbalances.
The sigmoid activation function is finally applied to the concept scores to model concept probabilities.
We use Adam optimizer
[
24
]
, with early stopping applied when validation loss does not decrease for
8
consecutive epochs.

The concepts scores are directly fed into the class linear layer mapping from concepts to task labels, giving the class logits.
We use the GLM-SAGA solver to train the linear layer, which optimizes for the elastic-net objective. This steers the training to producing a sparser linear classifier.

Label-free CBMs.

LF-CBM
adopt the same backbones of
CBM
.
We follow the same setup of the original paper
LF-CBM

[
60
]
.
Concept scores are learned by regressing on
CLIP
scores by maximizing the cosine similarity.
The linear layer of the classifier is also trained with the GLM-SAGA solver.

Language-in-a-Bottle.

LaBo
only features a linear layer to map
CLIP
scores to final task labels, which is trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss on ground-truth task labels (as in
[
60
]
) and constraints the columns of the weights to sum to one.
To prevent using concept filtering steps of the original implementation,
we set
clip cutoff=0.0
and
interpretability cutoff=0.0
). This forces
LaBo
to use all concept inside the ground-truth vocabulary.

Vision-Language-guided CBMs.
Following
[
50
]
, we implement
VLG-CBM
with the
CLIP
backbone. Concept annotation is obtained prompting Grounding Dino
[
28
]
(while setting
crop to concept
and
confidence threshold=0.15
) passing both input and label pairs.
The concept extractor is successively trained by fitting an MLP on
CLIP
(setting
cbl hidden layers=3
and
cbl lr=0.001
).
The final linear layer is also trained GLM-SAGA.

Vision-and-HUman-guided CBMs (
VH-CBM
s).
We first process the inputs (
i.e.
, images) using
CLIP
and gather the embeddings to feed into the GPs. Then, our method uses the trained GPs as a backbone for extracting concept activations.

All GPs are trained using a scheduler for the learning rate,
ReduceLROnPlateau
with patience
80
and an exponential decay of
0.8
. The learning rate starts at
0.01
and the training lasts for
8
k epochs or when the loss gets lower than
10^{-7}
.

The classifier consists of a linear layer trained with cross entropy loss on annotated task labels with a learning rate of
0.001
and stopping criterion based on whether the validation stalls for 3 epochs.
