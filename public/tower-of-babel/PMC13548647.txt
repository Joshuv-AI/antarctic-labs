Predicting mortality risk in hospitalized ACS patients with hypertensive comorbidity: an interpretable machine learning approach
Authors: Sazzli Kasim, Kiew Xue Ning, Sorayya Malek, Putri Nur Fatin Amir Rudin, Chien Wen Koh, Khairul Shafiq Ibrahim, Alan Yean Yip Fong, Wan Azman Wan Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension serves as a prevalent health issue, particularly in South Asia, where it is also a risk factor and comorbidity that affects the prognosis of Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) by increasing the likelihood of complications such as heart failure and arrhythmias. Conventional tools for predicting ACS mortality risk, such as the TIMI and GRACE scores, often fail to adequately represent the Asian population and do not specifically address the needs of ACS patients with comorbid hypertension. Our study focuses on developing a tailored and interpretable risk prediction model to assess mortality rate of Asian ACS-hypertensive patients.MethodsWe used in-hospital data from the NCVD Registry, which includes ACS patients with hypertension from 2006 to 2019 and selected key factors such as demographics, medications, and clinical details using Sequential Backward Elimination. We then used various ML techniques and combined them with ensemble meta-learners to predict mortality. We measured the model’s accuracy using the area under the curve (AUC) and fine-tuned the best model with Platt Scaling. Our model was compared with the TIMI score to calculate the net reclassification index (NRI). Finally, we applied LIME and SHAP to understand feature importance and interpret model performance.ResultsThe RF model with selected 28 features outperforms other prediction models with an AUC of 0.95. The calibrated RF model has raised Brier score (ACS: 0.046; STEMI: 0.051; NSTEMI: 0.041), and Chi-squared (ACS: 10.253; STEMI: 6.023; NSTEMI: 23.030). NRI verified our calibrated model’s better reclassification than the conventional TIMI risk score (improved accuracy by 15%-78%). The model’s robustness was proven by its greater AUC (0.841–0.898) than the TIMI risk score (0.641–0.83). SHAP and LIME analyses revealed that the Killip classification and peak CK levels significantly influenced mortality. Conversely, ACE medications were found to be more strongly associated with survival.ConclusionOur calibrated RF model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy in mortality risk for Asian hypertensive ACS patients, outperforming traditional TIMI scores. It efficiently identified key mortality predictors, which is further interpreted by SHAP and LIME, effectively underscore the significance of selected features for ACS mortality risk.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03626-7.Keywords: Acute Coronary Syndrome, Hypertension, Mortality Risk, Interpretable machine learning

Background

Hypertension, commonly referred to as high blood pressure, is a major risk factor in Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS). ACS remains a leading cause of mortality in Asia [1, 2]. It can be classified into ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), more than 43% of Southeast Asia’s population suffers from hypertension—significantly higher than the global average [3]. The incidence varies across Asian countries, ranging from 19.1% to 48%, demonstrating the regional impact of hypertension [4]. This relationship between hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality underscores its significant role in poor outcomes across the region [1].

Traditional ACS mortality risk prediction relies on scores such as the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) score and the Global Registry of Acute Cardiac Events (GRACE) score. However, these scores, developed using predominantly Western populations, are limited in their ability to capture the complex interactions seen in diverse populations with multiple comorbidities [5]. As a result, they often fail to provide accurate risk assessments for patients with conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease [6].

Machine learning (ML) models offer significant advantages over traditional risk scores like TIMI and GRACE, which are based on linear assumptions and were developed using Western populations. In contrast, ML models can handle non-linear interactions between patient characteristics, enabling more accurate predictions, particularly for populations with comorbidities [7, 8]. ML is also well-suited to high-dimensional datasets like the NCVD registry, allowing for precise, adaptable risk predictions across diverse populations [9, 10].

Machine learning algorithms used to predict ACS mortality risk have shown higher accuracy, as measured by the area under the curve (AUC), particularly in studies focusing on specific populations and risk factors, such as women, diabetic patients, post-PCI patients, and those with anaemia [11–16]. However, there is limited research on hypertensive patients, highlighting the need for tailored models for this subgroup.

Studies on Asian ACS cohorts have validated the superior performance of ML models, compared to the TIMI and GRACE score [8, 12, 14, 17, 18]. Malek et al. (2023a, 2023b) further emphasized the role of explainable ML methods in enhancing patient stratification and supporting individualized risk prediction [13, 19–22]. Although ML models demonstrate higher accuracy through metrics such as AUC and NRI, the inclusion of calibration techniques ensures the reliability of predictions in clinical settings [23]. Additionally, explainable ML tools like LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) offer transparency, enabling clinicians to understand the factors driving patient outcomes and building trust in AI-based models [24].

Previous studies utilizing the NCVD dataset have consistently demonstrated that ML models outperform conventional scores like TIMI and GRACE in predicting mortality among ACS patients. For instance, Kasim et al. (2022) and Aziz et al. (2021) showed superior discrimination, calibration, and reclassification performance of ML models [8, 12]. This advantage is particularly important for Asian populations with comorbidities, where the limitations of traditional scores become more evident.

The aim of this study is to develop a tailored and calibrated ML-based risk prediction model for mortality prediction among Asian hypertensive ACS patients, utilizing feature selection, calibration, and reclassification methods. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel study focusing on an Asian cohort with hypertension, an area that has not been extensively reported in previous ACS literature. By incorporating LIME and SHAP, we aim to enhance the transparency and interpretability of the model, ensuring it remains practical and clinically relevant for decision-making in real-world healthcare settings. As a proof-of-concept study, our findings provide initial evidence supporting the feasibility of explainable ML-based risk stratification in this population, while highlighting the need for future external validation and larger multicenter studies before broader clinical implementation.

Data source and study population

This study utilized a cohort of Asian patients hospitalized with both hypertension and ACS. Patient data were obtained from the Malaysian National Cardiovascular Database (NCVD-ACS) registry, which spans from 2006 to 2019. The data collection procedures of the NCVD registry were formally authorized by the Medical Review & Ethics Committee (MREC) of the Malaysian Ministry of Health in 2007, with the Approval Code: NMRR-07–20-250. Importantly, the MREC waived the requirement for explicit informed consent from patients included in the NCVD trial. The registry systematically gathered detailed clinical, demographic, procedural, and outcome information for every consecutive patient treated at affiliated healthcare facilities [25, 26].

This research project also received formal approval for data usage from the UITM Ethics Committee (Reference number: 600-TNCPI (5/1/6)) and the National Heart Association of Malaysia (NHAM).

The study included all ACS patients with hypertension as a comorbidity, without exclusion. This cohort comprised individuals receiving reperfusion therapies, such as primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), angiography demonstrating spontaneous reperfusion, fibrinolysis, or urgent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). NSTEMI was defined as Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI) indicated by positive cardiac markers without ST-segment elevation on electrocardiograms (ECG). STEMI was characterized by persistent ST-segment elevation of at least 1 mm in two contiguous leads or the presence of a new left bundle branch block with positive cardiac markers.

The primary outcome of interest was the survival status of patients, categorized as ‘Alive’ or ‘Death.’ The dataset included comprehensive details, such as patient demographics, clinical presentations, initial diagnostic tests, ECG findings, therapeutic interventions, and pharmacological treatments. To ensure data integrity, each patient was assigned a unique identification number to prevent duplication, and all data were anonymized to protect patient privacy.

The study analyzed 53 variables covering sociodemographic characteristics, CVD diagnoses and severity, risk factors, comorbidities, biomarkers, and medication use. Annual mortality data were validated through linkages with the Malaysian National Registration Department. Certain variables with extensive missing data were excluded from the analysis.

The primary goal of this study was to develop an ML-based algorithm to optimize policy objectives, with a specific focus on mortality prediction to enhance the study’s clinical relevance. This methodology aligns with established academic practices [27–29].

Data pre-processing

A total of 10,521 cases were used to create models specifically designed for predicting in-hospital outcomes. The study encompassed a wide range of 53 variables, consisting of 10 continuous variables and 43 categorical variables. The level of data incompleteness for each variable included in this analysis is specified in Table S1 in Additional file 1.

The dataset was randomly split into two main portions: 70% for model training and 30% for calibration and validation. Stratified sampling was applied to ensure that the proportion of outcomes (e.g., survival vs. mortality) remained consistent across subsets, minimizing the impact of class imbalance. The training set included feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, and predictive model development. The remaining 30% was divided into two equal parts: 15% for calibration using Platt scaling to fine-tune probability estimates and 15% for validation, kept independent to assess the final model’s performance. This approach ensured the validation results accurately reflected the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data, reducing the risk of data leakage and overfitting [30]. A random split was chosen over a temporal split to develop a generalized model suitable for diverse patient populations, rather than focusing on temporal trends.

To further address class imbalance and improve robustness, stratified 10-fold cross-validation was performed during model development. A stratified random split was selected over a temporal split to develop a generalized model for diverse patient populations. The performance metrics from each fold were averaged to provide reliable estimates of model performance and minimize the risk of overfitting.

To prevent bias due to multiple admissions, only one occurrence per patient was included, consolidating all admissions under unique patient IDs and ensuring no overlap between the training, calibration, and validation sets. Original patient identifiers were replaced with randomly generated IDs to ensure data confidentiality. We opted for a random split over a temporal split to develop a generalized model suitable for diverse patient populations, rather than focusing on trends over time.

To address class imbalance, we also adopted a combination of upsampling and downsampling methodologies, as outlined by Lunardon et al. [31]. This ensured balance between the minority class (death group) and the majority class (survival group). Additionally, data normalization was performed to minimize the influence of features with greater numerical scales, enhancing the discrimination between classes [31, 32]. We applied standardization (z-score normalization) to continuous variables such as age, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, triglyceride, and fasting blood glucose, ensuring their values followed a standard normal distribution.

Machine learning algorithms and model development

The selection of machine learning (ML) algorithms was based on previous studies focused on cardiovascular disease-related mortality, ensuring relevance to our objectives [10, 17, 33–36]. The algorithms employed in this study included Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), AdaBoost, Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Ensemble Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Ensemble Gradient Boosting Model (GBM), and Ensemble RF. The variety of algorithms allowed us to capture different predictive capacities and enhance the robustness of our models.

The prediction models were developed using the R programming language, with hyperparameters fine-tuned through a combination of random search and manual adjustments to optimize performance and generalization (Table 1). Each algorithm contributed unique strengths to the study’s goal of mortality prediction.

Support Vector Machine is a powerful ML algorithm that performs classification by identifying the hyperplane that optimally separates two classes [37]. For this study, both a linear kernel and a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel were used to account for both linear and non-linear patterns. RF leverages bagging with decision trees as base classifiers to generate multiple smaller decision trees, making predictions based on the majority vote of these trees [38]. XGBoost, a member of the gradient boosting family, is particularly effective due to its regularization techniques that reduce overfitting and improve generalization [39]. AdaBoost enhances weak learners through iterative training to create strong predictive models [40]. DT, a non-parametric supervised learning method, is widely used for both classification and regression tasks [41]. KNN is a straightforward but effective algorithm commonly used in healthcare settings for classification and regression problems [42].

In addition to individual algorithms, the study also utilized ensemble models, including Ensemble GLM, Ensemble GBM, and Ensemble RF, to further improve predictive performance. Ensemble stacking, a meta-learning technique, was applied by using the predictions of base learners as inputs for a meta-learner to generate the final prediction. This strategy has been shown to outperform individual classification or regression models [43–45].

The comprehensive selection of these algorithms, alongside careful hyperparameter tuning, ensured that the models were not only accurate but also generalized well across different patient subgroups. This approach provided a robust framework for developing reliable mortality prediction models tailored to the study population.

Features selection

The Sequential Backward Elimination (SBE) method was used to remove insignificant features in increasing order based on their model significance values. This process reduced the number of features from 53 to 28, balancing statistical significance with clinical relevance. During each iteration, ML models such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Logistic Regression (LR) were trained and evaluated using a 15% validation dataset, which remained independent from the model development phase.

To avoid overfitting, stratified 10-fold cross-validation was applied throughout model development on the training set. Each fold maintained a consistent distribution of outcome classes to ensure reliable performance estimates. The meta-learner evaluated the predictive performance of these models, selecting those with the best performance and fewest features for calibration. Stacked ensemble models were developed using the selected features to further improve predictive performance. Ensemble methods, such as RF and XGBoost, were employed to enhance model robustness and mitigate the risk of overfitting in high-dimensional datasets [38, 39].

We also incorporated SHAP and LIME to validate the interpretability and relevance of the selected features. SHAP provided a global perspective on feature importance, while LIME offered granular insights at the local level, ensuring the clinical relevance of individual predictions [21, 22].

For probability calibration, Platt scaling was employed. This method fine-tunes the predicted probabilities by fitting a logistic regression model to the classifier’s output, aligning predicted probabilities with observed outcomes. Platt scaling was selected for its simplicity and computational efficiency, ensuring reliable calibration across models [46].

Model calibration

Some machine learning algorithms, despite achieving high accuracy and AUC scores, may yield poorly calibrated class probabilities. Platt scaling is a widely recognized calibration technique that refines a model’s output into a well-calibrated probability distribution across classes. This approach involves fitting a logistic regression model to the classifier’s predicted scores, addressing sigmoidal distortions that can impair probability estimates. Prior studies have shown that Platt scaling effectively enhances the calibration of models in medical data settings [47, 48].

In our study, we used Platt scaling for probability calibration due to its simplicity and computational efficiency. Platt scaling fits a logistic regression to the classifier’s scores, optimizing two parameters, which is particularly effective for models like Support Vector Machines (SVMs) with sigmoidal outputs [49]. Although isotonic regression is a more flexible non-parametric alternative, it requires larger datasets and is prone to overfitting with smaller ones [50, 51]. Given these considerations, Platt scaling was selected to balance accuracy with computational efficiency.

SHAP and LIME

To enhance interpretability, we employed SHAP and LIME (to provide global and local feature importance, respectively. SHAP offers insights into how individual features contribute to the model’s output across the entire dataset, providing a comprehensive view of feature influence [52]. In the SHAP plot, the x-axis represents the impact of each feature on the model’s predictions, while the y-axis lists the features ranked by importance. Higher SHAP values indicate a greater influence on predictions. Red denotes higher feature values, and blue indicates lower values. Positive SHAP values suggest an increased likelihood of the positive class (e.g., risk of disease), while negative values indicate a reduction in that probability.

LIME complements this by evaluating feature importance at a local level, providing granular insights into individual predictions [53]. Each feature is represented by a horizontal bar, with the length and direction of the bar reflecting the feature’s strength and impact on predictions. Bars extending to the right indicate a higher likelihood of the outcome (e.g., death), while left-extending bars reduce the probability. LIME is model-agnostic, making it applicable to a wide range of machine learning models, regardless of complexity.

However, both SHAP and LIME have limitations that may impact their clinical deployment. SHAP’s global explanations can be complex, potentially making it difficult for clinicians to interpret results directly [54]. Additionally, LIME’s local explanations may vary across runs due to its approximation methods, leading to potential inconsistencies [53, 55]. Both methods are also computationally intensive, which could limit their practicality in time-sensitive clinical environments. Despite these challenges, SHAP and LIME offer valuable insights that bridge machine learning models with clinical decision-making when integrated effectively and accompanied by appropriate training [21].

By combining SHAP and LIME, our model provides both global feature importance and localized interpretability, enhancing transparency and supporting informed clinical decisions.

Performance evaluation

Discrimination

Standardized measures were applied to evaluate model performance [56]. The area under the AUC was the primary metric, valued for its independence from class distribution. Additional metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), offering a comprehensive assessment of model efficacy. A paired resampled t-test was used to compare the predictive accuracy of the ML and stacked ensemble models, particularly when models had similar AUC values [57].

Calibration

Calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with its chi-squared statistic and p-value determining the alignment between predicted and observed outcomes. A lower p-value indicated a greater disparity between the model’s predictions and actual results, suggesting the need for improved calibration. In addition, the Brier score and Log loss were computed to assess model calibration. The Brier score measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, while Log loss evaluates the divergence of predicted probabilities from actual labels, with lower scores indicating better calibration.

Comparison with TIMI

The performance of the best-calibrated machine learning model was compared to the conventional TIMI risk score, using AUC as the primary indicator of predictive accuracy. Evaluation was conducted on the 30% validation set. High-risk patients were defined as those with a TIMI score greater than 5 and a predicted mortality probability exceeding 50%. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) was also calculated to assess the enhanced classification of patients into correct risk categories by the ML model compared to the TIMI score [58]. NRI measures the effectiveness of reclassification for positive and negative events, providing a more nuanced understanding of the model’s clinical utility.

Patient characteristics

The baseline features of patients diagnosed with hypertensive ACS as reported in the NCVD over a hospitalization period is presented in Table S2 in Additional file 1 of our study. According to the data, the average age of these patients is 61 years, with a major proportion, almost 76%, being of the masculine gender. Notably, 6.0% of this cohort’s members have died. A thorough statistical analysis (p-value 0.001) revealed several important variables in the demographic, health, lifestyle, clinical, and treatment domains. Age, gender, smoking status, diabetes, premature cardiovascular disease in the family, and clinical signs such as heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, and renal illness are among the factors stated above. Peak creatine kinase (CK) levels, cholesterol profiles (total, HDL, LDL), triglycerides, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, Killip classification, and fasting blood glucose were also significant. The investigation’s ECG results revealed ST-segment elevation, ST-segment depression, bundle branch block, and anterior leads abnormalities. Cardiac catheterization, PCI, aspirin and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) administration, beta-blockers, angiotensin ace inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, statins, diuretics, calcium antagonists, oral hyperglycemic agents, insulin, and anti-arrhythmic agents were determined to be significant interventions.

Discrimination and calibration

In our study, we performed a comparison analysis to evaluate the discriminative performance of our ideal RF model, which employs 28 selected features, both before and after calibration. This comparison was conducted with an evaluation of the TIMI risk score model. We provide a comprehensive overview of the impact of calibration on our model and illustrate the extent of improvement achieved by our proposed method over the conventional risk score, specifically in the contexts of ACS, STEMI, and NSTEMI. Figures 3, 4, 5 explains the improvements in model performance after calibration, emphasizing the efficacy of our methodology in comparison to conventional risk assessment methods in cardiac care. For ACS patients, the calibrated Random Forest (RF) model maintains an AUC of 0.876 with an accuracy of 0.765. While the sensitivity remains low at 0.183, the model demonstrates excellent specificity at 0.988, indicating its strength in correctly identifying negative cases. The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) are 0.851 and 0.759, respectively, further showcasing its ability to predict outcomes accurately. In STEMI patients, our calibrated RF model achieves an AUC of 0.898 with an accuracy of 0.823. Sensitivity improves to 0.263, and the model maintains high specificity at 0.987. The PPV and NPV stand at 0.852 and 0.821, respectively. When compared to the TIMI score, which has an AUC of 0.830, sensitivity of 0.203, and specificity of 0.976, our model shows clear advantages in discrimination performance. For NSTEMI patients, the calibrated RF model attains an AUC of 0.841 with an accuracy of 0.803, sensitivity of 0.161, and high specificity at 0.985. The PPV and NPV are 0.758 and 0.805, respectively, demonstrating its robustness in classifying both positive and negative cases. Again, the TIMI score shows lower discrimination power, with an AUC of 0.616, sensitivity of 0.073, and specificity of 0.967.

Our calibration analysis of ACS, STEMI, and NSTEMI showed significant enhancements in the accuracy of our model. This was demonstrated by the chi-squared values and p-values obtained from the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was not applied to the TIMI risk score since its outputs are categorical risk scores rather than probabilities. This TIMI score class style is incompatible with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test requirements, which require probability inputs to create meaningful Chi-squared values and p-values. Previous study has noted that the TIMI model is already well-calibrated [59]. Furthermore, because TIMI’s outputs are not probabilistic, we did not apply them to Platt Scaling, which is normally reserved for correcting models that produce probability estimates.

These improvements provide evidence that the adjusted model exhibits a superior alignment with the data in comparison to its original unadjusted version. More precisely, the p-values for both the ACS and STEMI segments increased to a value greater than 0.05. This suggests that the differences between the observed and projected outcomes in these categories are not statistically significant. This indicates a higher level of precision in the alignment of the model with the observed data in these groups. However, although the NSTEMI section showed progress in calibration, which enhanced the performance of our original model and even surpassed the TIMI risk score in most of the metrics, its p-value did not reach the customary threshold of significance. However, this improvement still signifies a considerable progress in the accuracy of models for NSTEMI. In addition, there were significant improvements in both the Brier score and log loss measures across all categories, especially when compared to the TIMI scores in the STEMI and NSTEMI groups.

The enhancements in crucial statistical indicators highlight the overall efficiency of our calibration procedure, bolstering the dependability and accuracy of our model in the realm of cardiovascular risk evaluation.

Compare AUC of best calibrated model with TIMI risk score

We conducted a comparison between the AUC of our best model and the traditional TIMI risk score. The results indicated that our suggested approach is better than conventional TIMI score, exhibiting a higher AUC value of 0.898 in comparison to the TIMI score’s 0.83 in cases of STEMI. In addition, our technique achieved an AUC of 0.841, which was significantly higher than the AUC of 0.641 obtained by the TIMI risk score in NSTEMI cases.

Net reclassification index (NRI)

We conducted a NRI analysis to assess the improvement of our calibrated models for both STEMI and NSTEMI cases [see Table S3 in Additional file 1]. The analysis showed that, compared to the original TIMI risk score, the best-calibrated model demonstrated a 15.069% improvement in the reclassification of STEMI cases, and a 78.056% improvement in the reclassification of NSTEMI cases.

Discussion

In our study, we developed ML models to investigate key features and predict mortality risk among ACS patients with hypertension as a comorbidity. Our models achieved higher AUC values compared to conventional methods. A unique aspect of our research is the focus on patients with hypertension, coupled with the use of explainable machine learning techniques, an approach that is not extensively explored in existing ACS literature. Furthermore, we employed Platt scaling calibration to enhance the model’s accuracy in matching actual and predicted probabilities, addressing the issue of overfitting observed in non-calibrated machine learning models [49]. Our RF feature selection methodology successfully narrowed down the initial set of 53 features to 28 key factors. Among these, the most influential features identified through LIME or SHAP analyses include: patient age, family history of early CVD, Killip classification, peak CK levels, fasting blood glucose, use of ACE inhibitors, and oral hypoglycemic medications.

The RF algorithm has been used in several studies to assess mortality risk in ACS patients. In a study focusing on a Chinese population [60], researchers employed a hybrid strategy for feature selection in ACS risk prediction that combined gradient boosting trees and recursive feature deletion. This study identified five critical characteristics related to ACS and reported an RF model with 98.8% accuracy. Another study evaluated three ML algorithms, including RF, to identify high-risk ACS patients, finding that RF outperformed the GRACE risk score in 30-day mortality prediction after STEMI [8].

The importance of calibration methods is underscored by Boström’s 2008 work, particularly when using a smaller calibration dataset with RF models [61]. Although Platt scaling is often associated with SVMs [62], it can also enhance probability estimation for RF models, improving the calibration of probability estimates derived from decision trees. RF, known for generating probability estimates through bagging, can sometimes produce probabilities that are not fully calibrated.

While Platt scaling is a common calibration technique, it is not always ideal for ensemble models like Random Forest, which inherently estimate probabilities through bagging. Research shows that Platt scaling works best for addressing sigmoidal distortions in models such as SVMs, but it may introduce bias when applied to already well-calibrated models [49, 61]. In these cases, metrics such as AUC, NRI, and Brier score provide a more comprehensive assessment of model accuracy [47]. Our model demonstrated superior reclassification ability compared to the TIMI score, with a 15.07% improvement in STEMI cases and a 78.06% gain in NSTEMI cases, reinforcing its relevance for clinical decision-making.

Our calibrated model achieved an AUC of 0.876, significantly outperforming an in-hospital score system from another study [63], which had an AUC of 0.85. Additionally, the lower p-value in the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (0.13 vs. 0.175) suggests better alignment between projected and actual probabilities in our calibrated model. In another study [64], a non-calibrated RF model achieved an AUC of 0.918, but the absence of calibration analysis limits the ability to compare observed versus predicted probabilities. Our non-calibrated model, with an AUC of 0.925, suggests a slight improvement over the referenced study. Additionally, research integrating anemia as a comorbidity in mortality risk assessments for ACS patients [16] showed an AUC of 0.85, lower than our non-calibrated result. However, the lack of calibration analysis in that study restricts direct comparison.

Our study leverages the NCVD-ACS registry, which provides detailed data on over 10,000 patients from multiple healthcare centers. This comprehensive representation across different ACS presentations makes additional datasets unnecessary for achieving our objectives, ensuring the adequacy of the data used for model development and validation.

Although calibration remains important, we relied on multiple metrics such as AUC, NRI, and Brier score to comprehensively evaluate model performance. While calibrating NSTEMI cases presents inherent challenges, the model’s superior performance across other metrics justifies its clinical relevance and supports its application in practice.

Conventional models such as TIMI and GRACE rely on linear relationships that do not capture the complexity of patient interactions. This limitation is more pronounced in patients with multiple comorbidities, where ML models excel by capturing non-linear patterns and handling high-dimensional data [4, 65]. Additionally, ML models are adaptable, allowing continuous recalibration with new data to maintain accuracy in evolving clinical settings. These strengths contributed to the superior performance of our model, demonstrated by significant gains in AUC and NRI over TIMI/GRACE scores [12].

While SBE can introduce risks of overfitting or bias in high-dimensional datasets, we incorporated multiple strategies to mitigate these issues. Stratified 10-fold cross-validation ensured feature stability and generalization to unseen data [46]. RF and XGBoost enhanced model robustness, reducing the risk of overfitting [38, 39]. Furthermore, SHAP and LIME validated feature relevance, ensuring that the selected features were interpretable and clinically meaningful [21, 22]. Finally, Platt scaling improved calibration, as reflected by better Brier scores and more reliable probability estimates [46].

In explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), knowledge transfer between black-box models, such as deep neural networks, and white-box models, like decision trees, is essential. Black-box models provide high predictive power but lack transparency, while white-box models offer better interpretability. By transferring knowledge from black-box models to white-box models, it is possible to retain predictive accuracy while enhancing explainability, which is crucial in clinical settings where trust and transparency directly impact patient outcomes [66]. This approach ensures that AI-driven decisions align with clinical expectations, improving acceptance and utility in healthcare.

Our findings align with previous studies using the NCVD dataset, which demonstrated that ML models outperform traditional scores in both predictive accuracy and calibration. Kasim et al. (2022) and Aziz et al. (2021) reported better calibration and net reclassification index (NRI) with ML models compared to TIMI/GRACE scores, especially among patients with comorbid conditions [8, 12]. Our model’s NRI improvements—15.07% for STEMI and 78.06% for NSTEMI—demonstrate the value of ML in refining risk stratification and improving clinical practice [8, 12, 38, 67, 68].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this preliminary study demonstrates the feasibility and potential clinical utility of a calibrated Random Forest (RF) model for ACS risk assessment among hypertensive patients in Asian populations. Compared with the widely used TIMI risk score within the current study cohort, the proposed model showed improved predictive performance and enhanced patient risk stratification, further supported by Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) analysis. By reducing features from 53 to 28 through Sequential Backward Elimination (SBE) and incorporating SHAP and LIME analyses, the model improves interpretability while identifying clinically relevant predictors such as Killip classification and ACE inhibitors. These findings suggest that machine learning approaches may complement conventional risk stratification tools and support more personalized cardiovascular care. Nevertheless, external validation using independent multi-center datasets remains necessary before broader clinical generalisability can be established.
