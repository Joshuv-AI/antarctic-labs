Faster matrix product state preparation by exploiting symmetry-induced block-sparsity
Felix Rupprecht, Sabine Wölk

Abstract
Matrix product states (MPS) serve as a key tool for studying quantum systems from chemistry and condensed-matter physics, making their preparation on quantum computers an important task in interfacing classical and quantum simulation. Many systems of interest have U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) -symmetries induced by particle number and spin projection conservation, allowing to restrict the MPS tensors to be of block-sparse form, a property widely used in the implementation of classical algorithms such as the density matrix renormalization group. We reduce the cost of fault-tolerantly preparing block-sparse MPS within the standard ancilla-assisted linear-depth approach by implementing row and column permutations that transform the block-sparse matrices into block-diagonal form. These block-diagonal unitaries are then implemented via unitary synthesis, with the cost being determined by the size of the largest block. In this context, we modify the unitary synthesis approach of Berry et al. in order to reduce the Toffoli cost for real-valued unitaries by a factor of 2 \sqrt{2} . In numerical benchmarks, we achieve Toffoli cost improvement factors of 10 − 30 10-30 compared to the state-of-the-art for MPS of various molecular systems.

Introduction

The main difficulty in direct simulations of fermionic many-body systems from chemistry and condensed-matter physics is the exponential growth of the corresponding Hilbert spaces with increasing particle numbers.
In order to allow computational investigations of such systems, even in the presence of strong correlation, effective wave function representations that restrict to subspaces of the full Hilbert spaces have been developed, and sophisticated methods for calculations with such compressed states implemented
[
1

.
One of the most prominent compressed wavefunction formats are matrix product states (MPS), which follow an entanglement area law and efficiently parameterize the ground states of local one-dimensional gapped Hamiltonians
[
2

Closely related to the notion of MPS is the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) algorithm
[
3

, a widely used approach for finding approximate ground states of strongly correlated many-body systems
[

5
,
6

Due to their conceptual and practical relevance, MPS have taken a prominent role at the interface of classical and quantum computation, with the task of preparing MPS on quantum computers gaining more attention in recent years

While some of the papers consider the preparation task for particular subclasses of MPS

[

Motivated by the problem of preparing high-quality initial states

for ground-state energy estimation algorithms such as quantum phase estimation (QPE), the resource requirements for this approach in the framework of fault-tolerant quantum computing were analyzed and optimized in

Besides ground-state energy estimation, MPS have also been investigated in the context of quantum machine learning

In the pursuit of further bringing down the cost of end-to-end ground-state energy estimation and allowing the use of higher-quality initial states, it is therefore important to increase the efficiency of MPS state preparation, which we aim to do in this paper.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.
We answer the question from above in the affirmative by using the block-sparsity of

As is usual in fault-tolerant resource estimation, we take the number of Toffoli gates together with the number of required qubits as the figures of merit.

The resulting matrix is then implemented via unitary synthesis, with the Toffoli cost mostly determined by the size of the largest block.
Since for most systems of interest the MPS matrices are real-valued, in

[
8
]
based on Givens rotations to reduce the number of Toffoli gates required for real unitaries by a factor of

.
We benchmark the overall preparation approach on various molecular systems, such as active spaces of the P450 enzyme
[

, yielding Toffoli cost improvement factors of
10-30
.

The algorithms of the paper are implemented within the

The full code and other assets created for this paper are available on Zenodo

Unitary synthesis via Givens rotations

In this section, we develop a variation of the unitary synthesis approach of Berry et al.
[
8
]
that allows for a reduction in the number of Toffoli gates in the case of real unitaries by roughly a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

Given a unitary matrix
U
of dimension
l
, the task of (exact) unitary synthesis is to find a sequence of quantum gates
C_{1},\dotsc,C_{k}
such that
U=C_{k}\dotsm C_{1}
.
A standard building block for creating such sequences are Givens rotation matrices

G(\theta,\phi)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\phi}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{bmatrix}

with
\phi,\theta\in\mathbb{R}

The unitary Givens rotations have the property that for

\theta
and
\phi
such that
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}G(\phi,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{a}&0\end{bmatrix}

for some

If

and

This is a standard approach in numerical linear algebra for computing QR-decompositions
[

3
Preparation of block-sparse MPS

In this section, we show how the block-sparse structure of MPS of systems with global
U(1)
-symmetries can be exploited for more efficient preparation circuits.
We do this within the standard MPS preparation scheme explained in
Section

1
.

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the most common case of a fermionic system consisting of
n
sites with fixed particle number
N
and spin-projection number
S_{z}
, where the Hilbert spaces of the sites
i\in[n]
are spanned by a joint eigenbasis
(\ket{d_{i}^{j}})_{j\in[4]}\coloneq(\ket{0,0},\ket{1,+\frac{1}{2}},\ket{1,-\frac{1}{2}},\ket{2,0})
of the local particle number and spin projection operators
\hat{N}^{i}
and
\hat{S}_{z}^{i}
.
Prominent examples of such systems are fermionic lattice models, such as the Fermi– Hubbard model, or the electronic structure of molecular systems.
We note that the procedure works whenever we have a global abelian symmetry.

In the following, we briefly recall how the
U(1)
-symmetries induced by
\hat{N}
and
\hat{S}_{z}
lead to the block-sparsity of the MPS tensors
[
3
,
27
]
.
For this let
\hat{Q}\coloneq\sum_{i}\hat{Q}^{i}
be any of the operators
\hat{N}
and
\hat{S}_{z}
.
Given an eigenstate
\ket{x}
of
\hat{Q}_{i}
or more generally
\hat{Q}^{<i}\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\hat{Q}^{k}
we denote the corresponding quantum number by
q(x)
.
As the operators
\hat{N}
and
\hat{S}_{z}
commute and are therefore compatible we may apply the argument to both operators in parallel, i.e., when talking about an eigenvector of
\hat{Q}_{i}
one may think of it as a joint eigenvector of
\hat{N}^{i}
and
\hat{S_{z}}^{i}
.
Note that more generally, the operator
\hat{Q}=\sum_{i}\hat{Q}^{i}
may be any operator that gives rise to a global abelian symmetry.

We consider a site
i>1
and, for
u\in[\chi]
, look at the state

\ket{u}\coloneq\sum_{d_{1},\dotsc,d_{i-1}}(M_{1}^{d_{1}}M_{2}^{d_{2}}\dotsm M_{i-1}^{d_{i-1}})_{u}\ket{d_{1},\dotsc,d_{i-1}}\text{.}

Assuming that
(\ket{u})_{u\in[\chi]}
are eigenstates of
\hat{Q}^{<i}
, then the states
(\ket{v})_{v\in[\chi]}
given by

\ket{v}\coloneq\sum_{u,d_{i}}\ket{u}(M_{i}^{d_{i}})_{u,v}\ket{d_{i}}

are eigenstates of
\hat{Q}^{<i+1}
if and only if
(M_{i}^{d_{i}})_{u,v}
vanishes for

q(u)+q(d_{i})\neq q(v)\text{.}

(5)

Similarly, for
i=1
, the states
\ket{v}=\sum_{d_{1}}(M_{1}^{d_{1}})_{v}\ket{d_{1}}
are eigenstates of
\hat{Q}_{1}
iff
(M_{1}^{d_{1}})_{v}\neq 0
only for
d_{1},v
with
q(d_{1})=q(v)
.
By constraining the tensors accordingly, one can thus iteratively create MPS that are eigenstates of
\hat{Q}
.
Thinking about the indices of
M_{i}
as carrying a charge
q
, where
d_{i}
and
u
have incoming and
v
outgoing directions, the constraints can be interpreted as a charge conservation rule, with
q(u)
indicating how much charge the state
\ket{u}
has acquired in the first
(i-1)
sites.
At the end, we obtain an MPS
\ket{\Phi}
with quantum numbers
q(\Phi)=Q
that can be selected by discarding all sequences of quantum numbers that do not ultimately lead to
Q
.
The restriction (
5
), together with the constraint set by
Q
, strongly reduces the memory footprint of the MPS
\ket{\Phi}
.
These resource savings can be further increased by other global abelian symmetries, such as an abelian point group symmetry, as long as all symmetries are compatible.

In practical implementations, all non-zero elements
(M_{i}^{d_{i}})_{v,u}
whose indices carry the same charges
q(v)
,
q(d_{i})
,
q(u)
and fulfill
q(u)+q(d_{i})=q(v)
are combined in a block that carries the block index
(q(v),q(d_{i}),q(u))
, giving the tensor
M_{i}
a block-sparse structure.
Looking at the corresponding matrices
M_{i}^{d_{i}}
, the charge conservation implies that there is only a single non-zero block in each block row and block column.
We note that it was shown in
[
28
]
that, by exploiting the gauge degree of freedom, any MPS that is an eigenstate of an operator like
Q
may be written in terms of block-sparse tensors
M_{i}
as above.

We now want to prepare an MPS
\ket{\Phi}
whose tensors
M_{i}
are block-sparse.
As explained in
Section

1
, this task reduces to the implementation of the unitaries
U_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}U^{\prime}_{i}&*\end{bmatrix}
, where
U_{i}^{\prime}\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}M_{i}^{1}&\dotsm&M_{i}^{4}\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}
is a
(4\chi)\times\chi
matrix consisting of the stacked matrices
(M_{i}^{1})^{T},\dotsc,(M_{i}^{4})^{T}
, and
*
is an unspecified block matrix with shape
(4\chi)\times(3\chi)
that may be chosen freely as long as the overall matrix
U_{i}
is unitary.
The columns of
U_{i}^{\prime}
are orthonormal due to the right-canonical form of
\ket{\Phi}
and inherit the block-sparse structure of
M_{i}
, with each block row containing just a single non-zero block.
As shown in the first two boxes of
Figure

3
, we may therefore find a row permutation matrix
P_{r}
that merges the blocks of a block column, transforming the blue non-zero blocks of

into a chain of rectangles.
The rectangles are at least as high as they are wide and can be expanded into unitary blocks by complementary rectangles
R
.
In order to obtain a block-diagonal matrix consisting of these unitary blocks, we write the complementary rectangles
R
into the unspecified part
*

Figure

3

P_{c}
.
With a final permutation

V
and a decomposition

\begin{bmatrix}U^{\prime}&*\end{bmatrix}=WVQ^{\dagger}

for permutation matrices
W\coloneq P_{r}P_{o}
and

.

We implement the block-diagonal matrix

of dimension

by using the techniques from
Section

.
Denoting the unitary blocks of

The number of layers

We have

The goal is to combine all

In order to use the circuits from

4
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we have developed improved methods for preparing matrix product states with symmetry-induced block-sparse structure commonly encountered in quantum chemistry and condensed-matter systems.
In those systems,
U(1)
-symmetries from particle number and spin projection conservation result in the MPS tensors being block-sparse, which is widely used in classical algorithms such as DMRG.
We exploit the block-sparsity within the standard ancilla-assisted linear-depth preparation method of
[
10
]
by implementing row and column permutations that transform the block-sparse matrices into block-diagonal form.
These block-diagonal matrices can then be synthesized, with the cost being determined by the size of the largest block.
To this end, we also improve the Toffoli count of unitary synthesis for real unitaries within the approach of
[
8
]
by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

When transforming the block-sparse matrix into block-diagonal form, we need to insert non-trivial values in the unspecified part of the matrix in order to expand the rectangles into unitary blocks.
From a parameter counting perspective, these values are not needed and cause costs when loading the respective angles.
It is therefore an interesting question whether more efficient schemes for the blocking step, together with the subsequent unitary synthesis, are possible, e.g., via ideas from
[
9
]
.
Another direction to pursue is the adaptation of our scheme for MPS in spin-adapted
\mathit{SU}(2)
form
[
48
]
, where the computational basis states correspond to configuration state functions instead of Slater determinants.

Applying the new MPS preparation techniques to various systems from quantum chemistry, we see Toffoli cost improvement factors of around
10-30
compared to the state of the art
[
8
]
.
As due to recent algorithmic improvements for ground-state energy estimation
[
24
]
, the cost of preparing an initial state can be of similar magnitude as the phase estimation task itself, this is relevant for allowing potential QPE improvements to bring down the end-to-end cost of ground-state energy estimation further.
It moreover allows the preparation of higher-quality initial states.

In this context, it is an interesting question how the direct preparation of MPS compares to computing a sparse sum of Slater determinants (SOS) representation from the MPS and preparing this SOS state directly
[
7
,
46
]
.
Similarly, comparisons to techniques of adiabatic
[
15
,
49
]
or dissipative
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
nature would be desirable.
For the Fermi–Hubbard model, which is often seen as particularly suitable for quantum simulation due to efficient methods for time evolution
[
26
,
53
]
, the cost of preparing suitable initial states has not yet received much attention.
To this end, it would be interesting to see whether our approach allows for preparing MPS of sufficient quality
[
5
]
without changing the magnitudes of the resource requirements for the end-to-end algorithm.

The algorithms in this paper are implemented as
Qualtran
bloqs, with the more resource-intensive task of finding the Givens decompositions of the unitary matrices outsourced into Rust code.
The runtime of this task may be further improved by a GPU implementation.
Similarly, the implementation of the block-diagonalization procedure explained in
Section

3
, using general sparse arrays at the moment, may profit from directly working on the data structures from the DMRG-libraries such as
pyblock3
.
All code and other assets prepared for the paper are available on Zenodo
[
34
]
.

In conclusion, we expect our MPS preparation approach to be an integral part in connecting classical and quantum simulation of systems from chemistry and condensed-matter physics.

Appendix A
Measurement-based uncomputation

Throughout the paper, we often use measurement-based uncomputation for resetting ancilla qubits, allowing us to avoid physical uncomputation of those ancillas
[
8
,
46
,
55
,
56
]
.
In this section, we briefly recall this technique.

Given a state
\sum_{k}a_{k}\ket{k}\ket{f(k)}^{a}
, where
\ket{f(k)}^{a}
is a computational basis state in an ancilla register belonging to the basis state
\ket{k}
, the qubits of the ancilla register are measured in the
X
basis and discarded afterwards.
Recall that
\ket{0}=(\ket{+}+\ket{-})/\sqrt{2}
and
\ket{1}=(\ket{+}-\ket{-})/\sqrt{2}
.
Assuming for now that the ancilla register consists of a single qubit, the state after the measurement process is
\sum_{k}a_{k}\ket{k}
if
\ket{+}^{a}
was measured, and
\sum_{k}(-1)^{f(k)}a_{k}\ket{k}
if the measurement yields
\ket{-}^{a}
.
Generalizing this to multiple ancilla qubits results in the state
\sum_{k}(-1)^{F(i)}a_{k}\ket{k}
, where
F(i)\coloneq\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}(k)
sums over the values
f_{i}(k)
of
f(k)
at the bits
i\in I
for which the measurement result of the corresponding ancilla qubit was
\ket{-}
.

The sign flips inferred from the measurement results can then be taken into account in subsequent computations and, if required, be corrected using standard methods
[
9
,
39
]
.
When multiple registers need to be uncomputed at different times, it is sensible to try and commute the phase flips to a point in the circuit where the joint sign corrections can be applied at once, instead of fixing them directly after each measurement.
When doing so, the parts of the circuit that come after a measurement might need to be adapted in order to commute the sign flips through.
Since the measurement results are only available during runtime, one needs to be able to make the necessary adaptations on the fly, which means that the logic for finding those adaptations should not require large amounts of classical computation.

Appendix B
Details on the Berry et al. MPS preparation method

In this section, we give some details on our implementation of the MPS preparation scheme from the paper
[
8
]
.
In the main part, this is referred to as the dense preparation method.
We repeatedly make use of the following lemma.

Lemma
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times k}
be complex
m\times k
matrices such that the columns in the block matrix
\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}

are orthonormal, then there exist unitary matrices
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, a unitary
V\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
, and real diagonal matrices
D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&0\\
0&U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}D_{1}&-D_{2}\\
D_{2}&D_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V\\
0\end{bmatrix}

(6)

and the block matrix
D\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}D_{1}&-D_{2}\\
D_{2}&D_{1}\end{bmatrix}
in the middle has orthonormal columns.
If
A
and
B
are real, all matrices in the decomposition can be made real.

Proof.

From a singular value decomposition of
A
, we get unitary matrices
U_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}

to a unitary

with zeroes to a matrix

, we get

.
From the orthonormality of the columns of

B\end{bmatrix}
,
one has

, and it only remains to show that the middle matrix has orthonormal columns.

, making use of the fact that

and
D_{2}
are diagonal and
D_{2}^{\dagger}D_{2}=I-D_{1}^{\dagger}D_{1}
.
∎
