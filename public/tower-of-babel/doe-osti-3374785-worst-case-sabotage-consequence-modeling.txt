ABSTRACT
Under proposed rulemaking by the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission, advanced reactor
licensees may qualify for a reduction in onsite responders if the consequences from a sabotage attack
do not exceed 25 rem at the reactor site boundary 2 hours after a release occurs. This work presents
a preliminary assessment of the feasibility of meeting the 25 rem at the site boundary criteria. Using
advanced reactor radionuclide inventories radiological release estimates created by collaborators at
Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the MACCS consequence analysis code was used to investigate the
distance at the dose from hypothetical sabotage events reaches 25 rem for different advanced
reactor technologies. It was found that the distance to 25 rem depended heavily on sampled weather
conditions and sabotage release fractions, and further refinement of release fractions and MACCS
modeling parameters is recommended.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
In anticipation of near-term advanced reactor licensing, the United States Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) has proposed rulemaking in Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations Parts
53 (10 CFR 53) and 73 (limited-scope) (10 CFR 73) that would relax physical security requirements
for advanced reactor licensees based on dose at the site boundary following a sabotage event.
Ostensibly, the reduction in onsite responders will increase the cost competitiveness of ARs in the
domestic energy market. However, the utility of the proposed 10 CFR 53 and 73 rulemaking is
limited in that there is currently no NRC-approved guidance or methodology for demonstrating an
AR meets the proposed 25 rem at the site boundary criteria.
The objective of this work was to perform a preliminary consequence assessment of radiological
source terms resulting from hypothetical sabotage events for three advanced reactor technologies to
better understand if or how advanced reactor licensees could meet the proposed 10 CFR 53 or 10
CFR 73 site boundary dose criteria. The three reactors modeled were:
 • Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor (SFR) (Shah and Hartanto, Consequence analyses of sabotage-
 induced radiological releases in sodium-cooled fast microreactors 2025).
 • High Temperature helium-cooled Prismatic Microreactor (HTPM) (Shah and Hartanto,
 Consequence analyses of sabotage-induced radiological releases in high-temperature helium-
 cooled prismatic miroreactor 2025)
 • Heat Pipe-cooled Microreactor (HPMR) (Shah and Hartanto, What might radiological
 consequences from sabotage-induced releases look like in heat pipe-cooled microreactors?
 2025)
Using the MACCS consequence analysis code to simulate radionuclide environmental transport and
health consequences from post-sabotage radionuclide releases for the three advanced reactor designs
provided by collaborators at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the distance at which the peak dose
falls below 25 rem (D25rem) was calculated for each reactor. It was generally observed that D25rem was
between 0.5 to 1 km for lower power reactors (< 10 MWth) and up to 10 to 30 km for larger reactors
(>30 MWth). When compared against results from the RASCAL code for the same reactor releases,
it was found that MACCS predicted larger values for D25rem; it is expected that the differences in
results were driven by how MACCS and RASCAL perform nearfield modeling and different choices
in modeling parameters.
While the results of this study may provide preliminary insights into using the proposed 10 CFR 53
or 73 limited scope rulemaking for reduced physical security requirements, the technical rigor of this
work is well below what is expected will be required for advanced reactor licensees. Development of
technically defensible post-sabotage release estimates and modeling approaches is a priority for
future work in this area including:
 • Development of realistic, worst case release scenarios with resulting source terms for
 advanced reactor types based on design basis threat capabilities,
 • Investigating the effects of security delay barriers on radionuclide retention in containment
 or confinement buildings,
 • Exploring the impact of siting on consequence simulations by using weather data from
 various locations (including remote areas) for MACCS input,

 8

• Performing a parametric sensitivity study to identify which MACCS parameters have the
 greatest effect on D25rem.

1. INTRODUCTION
In anticipation of near-term advanced reactor licensing, the United States Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) has proposed rulemaking in Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations Parts
53 (10 CFR 53) and 73 (limited-scope) (10 CFR 73) that would relax physical security requirements
for advanced reactor licensees based on dose at the site boundary following a sabotage event.
Specifically, the proposed rulemaking reduces the number of onsite security responders required if
the consequences from a sabotage-induced radiological source term do not exceed 25 rem at the site
boundary 2 hours after the initial release either with (10 CFR 73) or without (10 CFR 53) mitigative
actions. Ostensibly, the reduction in onsite responders will increase the cost competitiveness of ARs
in the domestic energy market. However, the utility of the proposed 10 CFR 53 and 73 rulemaking is
limited in that there is currently no NRC-approved guidance or methodology for demonstrating an
AR meets the proposed 25 rem at the site boundary criteria.
To better understand if or how advanced reactor licensees could meet the proposed 10 CFR 53 or
10 CFR 73 site boundary dose criteria, this work presents a preliminary consequence assessment of
radiological source terms resulting from hypothetical sabotage events for three advanced reactor
technologies:
 • Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor (SFR) (Shah and Hartanto, Consequence analyses of sabotage-
 induced radiological releases in sodium-cooled fast microreactors 2025).
 • High Temperature helium-cooled Prismatic Microreactor (HTPM) (Shah and Hartanto,
 Consequence analyses of sabotage-induced radiological releases in high-temperature helium-
 cooled prismatic miroreactor 2025)
 • Heat Pipe-cooled Microreactor (HPMR) (Shah and Hartanto, What might radiological
 consequences from sabotage-induced releases look like in heat pipe-cooled microreactors?
 2025)
For each reactor type, multiple representative reactor models for each reactor type were created by
Shah and Hartanto for a range of power levels and fuel loadings. Using experimental fuel release
data, environment release estimates were provided by Shah and Hartanto for different sabotage
scenarios ranging from full core damage to smaller, fractional fuel damage events. Shah and
Hartanto go on to estimate does consequence for two sabotage-induced scenarios using RASCAL,
specifically focuses on the first two hours after the release. Their work is thoroughly summarized.
Additionally, the inventories and release fractions created by Shah and Hartanto were used as input
for the MACCS consequence analysis code. To identify worst case scenarios, MACCS simulations
were performed using random sampling of weather conditions at a notional reactor site. The key
figure of merit for this work is the distance from the release point where the dose from the different
scenarios fell below 25 rem. This work is intended as a preliminary assessment of consequences
from postulated sabotage release scenarios and should not be misinterpreted as a technical basis for
justifying a reduction in security requirements.

3. SABOTAGE CASES AND RELEASE PERCENTAGES
Sabotage scenarios and definitions varied by reactor type and are defined below:
For the SFR
 • Case A: Severe core damage with containment integrity compromised as a result of direct
 sabotage. Volatile and non-volatile fission products are released in significant amounts.
 • Case B: Indirect sabotage creates an accident with core degradation. Containment integrity is
 also compromised. However, limited mitigation measures are available. Volatile and non-
 volatile fission products are released.
 • Case C: Sabotage results in limited fuel damage. Containment integrity is partially degraded.
 Only volatile fission products are released.
For the HTPM
 • Case A: Reactor sabotage results in severe core damage with peak fuel temperatures
 exceeding 2,173 K. The high temperatures result in a significant number of failed TRISO
 particles and a large release of fission products. Containment integrity is severely
 compromised.
 • Case B: Adversaries create elevated fuel temperatures between 1,773 K and 2,173 K. A
 limited number of TRISO particles fail leading to small releases of fission products with high
 diffusion coefficients. Containment integrity is damaged.
For the HPMR
 • Case A: Reactor sabotage results in severe core damage with peak fuel temperatures
 exceeding 2,173 K. The high temperatures result in a significant number of failed TRISO
 particles and a large release of fission products. Containment integrity is severely
 compromised.
 • Case B: Indirect sabotage results in a severe depressurized loss of cooling scenario. Passive
 decay heat removal systems are unavailable. Fuel temperatures peak between 1,773 K and
 2,173 K. Containment integrity is damaged.

The release values for Case C for the SFR scenario are more equivalent to the release under Case B in
the HTPM and HPMR, and therefore, is used in place of Case B for demonstration of release
percentages. Dose consequences for both postulated sabotage-induced accidents were estimated for
each design using RASCAL tool (Ramsdell et al., 2013) and focused on the first 2 h after the simulated
release. The consequence characterizes the total effective dose equivalent (TEDE), which is the sum
of the effective dose equivalent–external exposure from plumeshine and (4-day early phase)
groundshine–and the committed effective dose equivalent (internal exposure). The radiological
releases were assumed to occur under standard meteorological conditions, characterized by
atmospheric stability class D (neutral conditions), wind speed of 6.44 kph, no precipitation, air
temperature of 21.1°C, and relative humidity of 50%. The releases were simulated to occur at 10 m
above ground level, and all doses were measured downwind along the plume centerline (0.15 to 1.2
km in 0.15 intervals) 1 m above ground level during the first 2 h following the onset. The assessment
assumed no protective actions (e.g., evacuation) and supported to evaluate physical protection
requirements against the criterion of the total dose not exceeding 25 rem over the first 2 h at the
exclusion zone boundary (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2017).

5. MACCS MODELING APPROACH
This section describes modeling inputs and assumptions used for MACCS simulations including
descriptions for each reactor and their respective release fractions.

5.1. MACCS Parameters and Assumptions
The MACCS consequence analysis code was used to simulate radionuclide environmental transport
and health consequences. Developed by Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) for the NRC, MACCS
simulates the consequences of a radiological release including dose to the public, land
contamination, and economic losses. Additionally, MACCS can automatically perform probabilistic
consequence assessments by sampling publicly available meteorological data for a user-specified
geographical location which allows for investigation of a broad range of scenarios including
combinations of weather that maximize dose to the public. While MACCS has historically been used
for severe accident consequence analysis, MACCS can model the release of any user-defined
radionuclide inventory such as the postulated source terms provided by ORNL for each reactor
type.
The following MACCS input parameters were used for all simulations:
 • Weather parameters (rain distances and intensities, wind speed and direction) were sampled
 from the site weather data file for the Surry Nuclear Power Station that is distributed with
 the MACCS code. Because this analysis is not site specific, the Surry weather data was used
 for convenience. Non-uniform random bin sampling was used based on the
 recommendations by Bixler et al. (Bixler, Compton, et al. 2022).
 • The radial nodalization uses 0.1 km increments between 0 km to 1 km, 1 km increments
 from 1 km to 10 km, and 10 km increments between 10 km to 100 km (i.e., the tick marks
 on a logarithmic plot scale). Additional grid points were added at 200 km, 500 km, and 1000
 km. The angular nodalization uses 16 evenly spaced subdivisions to align with data in the
 Surry weather file.
 • The reactor building was assumed to be 11 m tall with a 21 m by 21 m footprint. The reactor
 building dimensions were chosen based on an advanced reactor source term modeling
 demonstration by SNL (Wagner, et al. 2022). Figure 1 shows the orientation of the reactor
 building.
 • Releases were modeled as point sources using a Gaussian plume. The decay heat-driven
 buoyancy model was used with decay heat calculated using the inventories and release
 fractions given by ORNL. All releases were assumed to occur over a 1 h period. A constant
 0.15 µm mass median diameter was assumed.
 • The release height was assumed to be 1 m from the ground as it is expected that adversaries
 would breach the reactor building close to the ground (i.e., as opposed to the roof).
 • The Regulatory Guide 1.145 Full Model implemented in MACCS was used for nearfield
 modeling.
 • The recommended modeling parameters for aerosol transport and health effects from
 NUREG/CR-7270, Technical Bases for Consequence Analyses Using MACCS, were used (Bixler,
 Compton, et al. 2022). The recommended nuclides from NUREG/CR-7270 shown in Table
 7 were assumed to be applicable to the reactors modeled in this work.

conservatism.

5.2. Reactor Release Fractions

sustain criticality (i.e., keff < 1). For all reactors, release fractions for key nuclides were estimated for
hypothetical sabotage scenarios. The release fractions are intended as rough approximations; specific
adversary sabotage actions or post-sabotage accident sequences for each scenario were not provided.

6. CONSEQUENCE ANALYSIS
The key output metric for the consequence analysis was the distance at which the peak dose3 falls
below 25 rem (D25rem). However, because MACCS used randomly sampled weather data for each
run, each run produced a distribution of doses over the spatial grid i.e., there were not singular point
values for D25rem. As part of its output, MACCS provides statistical values for doses over the spatial
grid including the mean dose, the absolute peak dose, and various dose quantiles4. Doses
representing the 90th quantile5 were used to estimate D25rem. Only results for Case A are presented as
Case A is the bounding, worst case scenario for all reactor types.
The findings are intended as investigatory and should not be misinterpreted as a technical basis for
advanced reactor licensing.

6.2. MACCS and RASCAL Comparison
In their papers on SFM, HTPM, and HPMR releases, Shah and Hartanto estimated radiological
consequences for each reactor type using the Radiological Assessment System for Consequence
Analysis for radiological emergencies (RASCAL) health physics codes. Like MACCS, RASCAL is a
tool for estimating the consequences of a radiological release. Whereas MACCS is designed as a
comprehensive, systems-level consequence assessment tool, RASCAL is developed as a “a tool for
the rapid assessment of an incident or accident at an NRC-licensed facility” (Ramsdell Jr., et al.
2012). Because MACCS or RASCAL could be used for estimating D25rem, a brief comparison of
simulations for each reactor type was performed. The comparison was meant to be investigatory; a
detailed crosswalk of RASCAL and MACCS modeling capabilities was not performed.
Figure 5 compares SFM dose vs. distance curves produced using RASCAL and MACCS. The
HotSpot curves were plotted using data from Shah and Hartanto, and the MACCS curves are the
same as those shown in Figure 2 (Shah and Hartanto, Consequence analyses of sabotage-induced
radiological releases in sodium-cooled fast microreactors 2025). A significant divergence in results is
observed for all SFM power levels with RASCAL predicting higher doses than MACCS. This creates
a significant difference in D25rem for the 10 MWth core – RASCAL predicts D25rem between 0.9 to 1.2
km compared to 0.1 to 0.3 km for MACCS. The dose curves from Shah and Hartanto for the 30 and
50 MWth SFM cores do not decrease below 25 rem. Because the difference between RASCAL and

MACCS results is most prominent closest to the release point, this divergence was attributed to
MACCS having more mature near-field modeling capabilities than RASCAL.

6.3. Discussion
Because the SFM, HTPM, and HPMR all represent unique combinations of fuel loadings, thermal
power, and burnup, it is challenging to draw conclusions regarding D25rem by comparing reactor
types. For example, in addition to having different release fractions, the SFM cores have higher fuel
loadings than the HTPM and HPMR cores, but the HPMR and HTPM have higher burnups. Both
factors increase radionuclide inventories which in turn can create potentially larger source terms, but
the effect of both is difficult to assess without performing a parametric sensitivity study (which was
outside the scope of this work). However, high-level conclusion were drawn regarding identifying
D25rem:
 • The choice of quantile for assessing D25rem can have significant implications. As an example,
 Figure 8 shows dose vs. distance for all statistical values reported by MACCS for the 30
 MWth HPMR. If peak dose is used, D25rem is between 10 to 12 km compared to 3 to 5 km for
 the 90th quantile or 2 to 4 km for the 50th quantile. The authors are unaware of any regulatory
 guidance on what statistical value is an acceptable level of risk.
 • For this work, D25rem was presented as a broad range on a spatial grid as a precise estimate
 for was not necessary. However, minor changes to D25rem for real-world site design may have
 significant financial impacts (i.e., by increasing the site footprint), and additionally guidance

 33

 is needed regarding what spatial fidelity or error margin provides a technically defensible
 estimate for D25rem. Additional guidance on using techniques like interpolation between grid
 points for determining D25rem may also be helpful to prospective licensees.

7. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This work presented a preliminary scoping study into the feasibility of advanced reactor licensees
meeting criteria in the proposed 10 CFR 50 and 10 CFR 73 limited scope rulemaking for reduced
onsite responders. Using radionuclide inventories and release fractions for three different advanced
reactors, consequences for hypothetical sabotage scenarios were modeled using MACCS, and the
distance at which the doses from each scenario fell below 25 rem threshold were observed. It was
found that D25rem was highly dependent on both the sampled weather conditions in MACCS and the
postulated radionuclide release fraction from hypothetical sabotage events.
The analyses performed in this work were an initial scoping study into integrated security-safety
consequence modeling using coarse estimates for radiological source terms. While the results of this
study may provide preliminary insights into using the proposed 10 CFR 53 or 73 limited scope
rulemaking for reduced physical security requirements, the technical rigor of this work is well below
what is expected will be required for advanced reactor licensees. Therefore, development of
technically defensible post-sabotage release estimates and modeling approaches is a priority for
future work in this area. Future work planned for FY26 on this topic includes:
 • Development of realistic, worst case release scenarios with resulting source terms for
 advanced reactor types based on design basis threat capabilities,
 • Investigating the effects of security delay barriers on radionuclide retention in containment
 or confinement buildings,
 • Exploring the impact of siting on consequence simulations by using weather data from
 various locations (including remote areas) for MACCS input,
 • Performing a parametric sensitivity study to identify which MACCS parameters have the
 greatest effect on D25rem.
