Risk factors and prognosis of postoperative hepatic dysfunction after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hongtao Liu, Ronecca Arjune, Aini Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on postoperative hepatic dysfunction after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair remains limited. Existing studies have used heterogeneous definitions and have reported inconsistent perioperative factors and clinical outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction, summarize associated perioperative variables, and evaluate its association with short-term postoperative outcomes.MethodsPubMed, Embase (Ovid), Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched for clinical studies investigating postoperative hepatic dysfunction in patients undergoing surgical repair for Stanford type A aortic dissection. Pooled analyses were performed to estimate the incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction and its associations with perioperative variables and postoperative outcomes.ResultsA total of 960 records were identified, and nine single-center retrospective cohort studies involving 3,234 patients were included. The pooled incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction was 37%. Male sex was associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10–1.64, I2 = 0%). Compared with patients without postoperative hepatic dysfunction, those who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction had higher preoperative white blood cell counts (MD 0.94 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI 0.21–1.67, I2 = 67%), longer cardiopulmonary bypass time (MD 21.29 min, 95% CI 11.25–31.34, I2 = 85%), longer aortic cross-clamp time (MD 11.57 min, 95% CI 7.88–15.26, I2 = 52%), and greater perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume (MD 1.90 units, 95% CI 0.92–2.88, I2 = 90%). Postoperative hepatic dysfunction was also associated with higher odds of short-term mortality (OR 5.59, 95% CI 3.96–7.88, I2 = 0%) and acute kidney injury (OR 5.62, 95% CI 4.16–7.59, I2 = 30%), as well as longer intensive care unit stay (MD 60.18 h, 95% CI 54.83–65.53, I2 = 23%).ConclusionsPostoperative hepatic dysfunction was common after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair and was associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes. Several preoperative and intraoperative variables were associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction; however, the available evidence was limited by retrospective study designs, heterogeneous definitions, and residual confounding. Future multicenter studies using standardized definitions and systematically collected malperfusion-related, operative, and perfusion-related variables are needed to clarify its clinical significance and support risk-stratification research.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251234391.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04486-y.

Background

Stanford type A aortic dissection (TAAD) is one of the most life-threatening cardiovascular emergencies. Without treatment, mortality increases by approximately 1%–2% per hour after symptom onset [1], reaching approximately 30% within the first 24 h and 50% within 48 h [2]. Prompt surgical repair is therefore essential to reduce early mortality [3]. Although advances in surgical techniques, cerebral protection, and perioperative care have improved outcomes in recent years [4], postoperative complications remain common and continue to affect short-term prognosis after TAAD repair. Previous studies have provided substantial evidence regarding neurological complications and acute kidney injury after TAAD repair [5–9], whereas postoperative hepatic dysfunction has received less systematic attention despite its potential association with adverse postoperative outcomes.

Postoperative hepatic dysfunction after TAAD repair is clinically relevant but remains difficult to define and interpret. Existing studies have used heterogeneous diagnostic criteria, including score-based definitions such as the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease excluding international normalized ratio, as well as biomarker-based definitions based on postoperative transaminase or bilirubin elevations [10–18]. Differences in diagnostic criteria and assessment time windows may contribute to variation in reported incidence, associated perioperative factors, and clinical implications across studies. In this setting, postoperative hepatic dysfunction may reflect the combined effects of preoperative visceral or hepatic malperfusion, systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, operative complexity, intraoperative perfusion and ischemic stress, transfusion exposure, and postoperative critical illness.

Given these uncertainties, a systematic synthesis of the current evidence is warranted. Although several studies have examined postoperative hepatic dysfunction after TAAD repair, their findings remain difficult to compare because of differences in diagnostic definitions, assessment time windows, reported perioperative variables, and outcome measures. In particular, malperfusion status, operative complexity, and intraoperative perfusion management have not been consistently reported, limiting a clear understanding of the perioperative context in which postoperative hepatic dysfunction occurs. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction after TAAD repair, summarize perioperative variables associated with its occurrence, evaluate its association with clinically important short-term postoperative outcomes, and identify key evidence gaps for future research.

Methods

Study design and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated perioperative factors associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction and its association with short-term postoperative outcomes after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251234391). Because this study was based exclusively on previously published aggregate data and did not involve new participants, identifiable personal data, or clinical interventions, ethical approval was not required.

Literature search

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify eligible studies. Two investigators independently searched PubMed, Embase (Ovid), Web of Science Core Collection, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to November 30, 2025. The full search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1. After all records were imported into EndNote®, duplicate records were removed before title and abstract screening. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by full-text assessment according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Study selection was performed independently by two investigators, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third investigator with expertise in cardiovascular surgery and evidence-based medicine. For duplicate publications involving overlapping study populations, the report with the most complete and relevant data for the primary outcome was retained for quantitative synthesis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were predefined according to the PICOS framework. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years with Stanford type A aortic dissection who underwent surgical repair; (2) the original study clearly distinguished patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction from those without postoperative hepatic dysfunction, or provided sufficient data to enable between-group comparisons; (3) analyzable outcomes and original data were reported and could be extracted directly or converted for analysis; (4) the study design was a prospective or retrospective clinical cohort study or randomized controlled trial; and (5) an explicit diagnostic definition of postoperative hepatic dysfunction was provided.

Studies were not eligible if any of the following criteria were met: (1) the study population comprised patients with Stanford type B aortic dissection or other aortic diseases, and data for Stanford type A aortic dissection could not be extracted separately; (2) patients were managed medically or underwent endovascular intervention alone, without separately extractable data for surgical repair; (3) the study population comprised special groups, such as children, adolescents, or patients with hereditary aortic diseases; (4) postoperative hepatic dysfunction was not reported, or hepatic abnormalities were described without an explicit diagnostic definition; (5) no comparable control group was available, or key data required for extraction or conversion were unavailable; (6) the publication type was a review, meta-analysis, case report, expert consensus statement, guideline, conference abstract, animal experiment, or in vitro study; (7) key data were missing, statistical methods were clearly inappropriate, or obvious internal inconsistencies precluded quantitative synthesis; or (8) the publication was not written in English.

Definition of postoperative hepatic dysfunction

The diagnostic criteria for postoperative hepatic dysfunction varied across the included studies. Definitions were classified as score-based or biomarker-based according to the criteria used in the original studies. Score-based definitions were based on composite liver function scoring systems, including the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease excluding international normalized ratio. Biomarker-based definitions included postoperative transaminase elevations, such as aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase above a prespecified multiple of the upper limit of normal, and bilirubin-based definitions, such as total bilirubin > 3 mg/dL or > 51.3 µmol/L. The main characteristics of the included studies and the study-specific definitions, thresholds, and assessment time windows are summarized in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Outcome selection and data items

Outcomes and variables were selected according to clinical relevance, availability across the included studies, and their ability to characterize the perioperative course and short-term prognosis of postoperative hepatic dysfunction after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair. The primary outcome of interest was postoperative hepatic dysfunction. Perioperative variables included preoperative baseline characteristics and laboratory indicators, intraoperative variables, and perioperative treatment-related factors reported in the original studies. Postoperative outcomes included in-hospital or short-term mortality, acute kidney injury, intensive care unit length of stay, mechanical ventilation time, 24-hour postoperative drainage volume, and other clinically relevant outcomes when extractable data were available. Variables reported by at least two studies with compatible definitions were considered for quantitative synthesis. Variables reported by individual studies only, or variables with incompatible definitions or insufficient data for pooling, were summarized narratively when clinically relevant.

Data extraction and study quality assessment

Two investigators independently extracted data using a predefined Microsoft® Excel® spreadsheet, and all extracted entries were cross-checked. Extracted items included study characteristics, definitions of postoperative hepatic dysfunction, baseline characteristics, preoperative indicators, intraoperative variables, postoperative complications, and clinical outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved through re-examination of the original articles and discussion between the two investigators. If consensus could not be reached, adjudication was performed by the corresponding author (A.X.), who served as the third investigator and has expertise in cardiovascular surgery and evidence-based medicine.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Risk-of-bias judgments were performed across three NOS-derived domains: selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. For the selection domain, studies with 4, 3, and ≤ 2 stars were classified as having low, moderate, and high risk of bias, respectively. For comparability, studies with 2, 1, and 0 stars were classified as having low, moderate, and high risk, respectively. For outcome assessment, studies with 3, 2, and ≤ 1 stars were classified as having low, moderate, and high risk, respectively. The overall risk of bias for each study was defined as the highest risk level across the three domains. The results were summarized in an R-generated grayscale traffic-light plot using symbol coding (+, low risk; −, moderate risk; ×, high risk). The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach and summarized in a Summary of Findings table generated with GRADEpro GDT.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan® 5.4) and R software (version 4.5.2). For dichotomous outcomes, pooled effect estimates were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes, pooled effect estimates were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs, using the original measurement units of the corresponding variables. For continuous variables reported as medians with quartiles or ranges, values were converted to means and standard deviations according to the methods proposed by Wan et al. [19] and Luo et al. [20] before inclusion in the meta-analysis. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. I² values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Random-effects models were primarily used because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected across studies, including differences in definitions of postoperative hepatic dysfunction, assessment time windows, patient characteristics, and perioperative management. Fixed-effect models were applied when statistical heterogeneity was low and the contributing studies were considered sufficiently comparable. For outcomes with substantial heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results. When data permitted, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, particularly differences related to diagnostic criteria for postoperative hepatic dysfunction. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots only when at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome. For outcomes including fewer than 10 studies, publication bias was not formally evaluated because of limited statistical power. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study characteristics, definitions, and incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction

A total of 960 records were identified. After duplicate records were removed, 791 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 739 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The full texts of 52 articles were assessed, and 43 were excluded. Ultimately, nine studies were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

The main characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. All nine studies were single-center retrospective observational studies from China, with sample sizes ranging from 147 to 940 patients and a total of 3,234 patients. The pooled incidence of postoperative hepatic dysfunction after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair was 37% using a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions. Definitions of postoperative hepatic dysfunction varied across studies and included score-based definitions using MELD or MELD-XI thresholds and biomarker-based definitions based on postoperative transaminase or bilirubin elevations. Study-specific definitions, thresholds, and assessment time windows are summarized in Table 2.

Quality assessment showed that most studies had low-to-moderate risk of bias in the selection and outcome assessment domains, whereas ratings in the comparability domain varied across studies (Fig. 2). The certainty of evidence was rated as very low for all evaluated outcomes, mainly because of serious risk of bias and, for some outcomes, inconsistency (Supplementary Table S3).

Perioperative variables associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction

In pooled analyses, postoperative hepatic dysfunction was associated with several preoperative and intraoperative variables. Preoperative variables included male sex, higher white blood cell count, higher baseline liver function indicators, and lower platelet and fibrinogen levels. Intraoperative variables included longer cardiopulmonary bypass time, longer aortic cross-clamp time, longer operative time, and greater perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume.

Nine studies reported data on sex. Male sex was more frequent among patients who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction, with a pooled OR of 1.35 (95% CI, 1.10–1.64, P = 0.004, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3). Seven studies reported preoperative white blood cell counts. Patients who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction had higher preoperative white blood cell counts than those without postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 0.94 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI, 0.21–1.67, P = 0.01, I2 = 67%) (Fig. 4).

Additional preoperative laboratory variables with statistically significant between-group differences are shown in Supplementary Figures S1–S5. Compared with patients without postoperative hepatic dysfunction, those who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction had lower preoperative fibrinogen levels (MD -0.40 g/L, 95% CI, -0.56 to -0.23, I2 = 19%) and lower platelet counts (MD -18.16 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI, -29.93 to -6.39, I2 = 10%). Preoperative ALT (MD 7.93 U/L, 95% CI, 3.19–12.68, I2 = 89%), AST (MD 8.52 U/L, 95% CI, 2.53–14.52, I2 = 78%), and total bilirubin levels (MD 3.20 µmol/L, 95% CI, 2.37–4.03, I2 = 0%) were also higher in patients who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction.

Eight studies reported cardiopulmonary bypass time. Patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction had longer cardiopulmonary bypass time than those without postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 21.29 min, 95% CI, 11.25–31.34, P < 0.0001, I2 = 85%) (Fig. 5). Eight studies reported aortic cross-clamp time, which was also longer in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 11.57 min, 95% CI, 7.88–15.26, P < 0.00001, I2 = 52%) (Fig. 6). Six studies reported perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume. Patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction received greater red blood cell transfusion volume (MD 1.90 units, 95% CI, 0.92–2.88, P = 0.0001, I2 = 90%) (Fig. 7). In sensitivity analyses stratified by transfusion timing, both intraoperative and postoperative red blood cell transfusion remained significantly associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction. Total operative time was also longer in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 60.94 min, 95% CI, 15.15-106.72, I2 = 95%) (Supplementary Figure S6).

Postoperative outcomes associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction

Postoperative hepatic dysfunction was associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes. Seven studies reported in-hospital or short-term mortality. Compared with patients without postoperative hepatic dysfunction, those who developed postoperative hepatic dysfunction had higher odds of death. Because between-study heterogeneity was absent (I2 = 0%), a fixed-effect model was applied. The pooled OR was 5.59 (95% CI, 3.96–7.88, Z = 9.80, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 8).

Five studies reported intensive care unit length of stay. Fixed-effect pooling showed that patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction had a longer intensive care unit stay than those without postoperative hepatic dysfunction, with a pooled MD of 60.18 h (95% CI, 54.83–65.53, P < 0.00001, I2 = 23%) (Fig. 9).

Postoperative hepatic dysfunction was also associated with postoperative complications and resource-use outcomes. Six studies reported mechanical ventilation time, which was longer in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 33.87 h, 95% CI, 18.65–49.10, I2 = 95%) (Supplementary Figure S7). Three studies reported 24-hour postoperative pleural or mediastinal drainage volume, which was higher in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 128.92 mL, 95% CI, 72.57-185.27, I2 = 39%) (Supplementary Figure S8). Five studies reported postoperative acute kidney injury, and postoperative hepatic dysfunction was associated with higher odds of acute kidney injury (OR 5.62, 95% CI, 4.16–7.59, I2 = 30%) (Supplementary Figure S9).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

To explore whether diagnostic criteria influenced the pooled estimates, included studies were categorized into score-based and biomarker-based definition subgroups. For preoperative white blood cell count, the score-based subgroup showed significantly higher white blood cell counts in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction (MD 1.25 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI, 0.62–1.88, P = 0.0001, I2 = 46%), whereas the biomarker-based subgroup showed no significant difference (MD -0.14 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI, -2.46 to 2.19, P = 0.91, I2 = 82%) (Fig. 4).

For intraoperative process-related indicators, the score-based subgroup showed longer cardiopulmonary bypass time (MD 14.23 min, 95% CI, 7.78–20.69, I2 = 43%) and longer aortic cross-clamp time (MD 8.37 min, 95% CI, 5.49–11.24, I2 = 0%) in patients with postoperative hepatic dysfunction. The corresponding differences were larger in the biomarker-based subgroup for cardiopulmonary bypass time (MD 33.24 min, 95% CI, 14.99–51.49, I2 = 87%) and aortic cross-clamp time (MD 18.01 min, 95% CI, 13.21–22.82, I2 = 0%). Tests for subgroup differences suggested that diagnostic criteria may have influenced the strength of associations for cardiopulmonary bypass time (P for subgroup differences = 0.05) and aortic cross-clamp time (P for subgroup differences = 0.0007).

For perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume, the pooled effect remained positive in the score-based subgroup (MD 1.73 units, 95% CI, 0.52–2.94, I2 = 91%), whereas the effect was not statistically significant in the biomarker-based subgroup (MD 3.27 units, 95% CI, -2.11 to 8.64, I2 = 94%). The between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (P for subgroup differences = 0.58). Overall, stratification by diagnostic criteria did not fully explain between-study heterogeneity for several outcomes, suggesting that residual differences in patient characteristics, operative complexity, malperfusion status, and perioperative management may have contributed to the observed variability.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis showed that postoperative hepatic dysfunction occurred in approximately one-third of patients after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair and was associated with unfavorable perioperative characteristics and worse short-term postoperative outcomes. Several preoperative and intraoperative variables were associated with postoperative hepatic dysfunction, including male sex, higher preoperative white blood cell count and liver function indicators, lower platelet and fibrinogen levels, longer cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamp and operative times, and greater perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume [10–18].

These findings should be interpreted within the broader context of perioperative organ injury. In patients undergoing type A aortic dissection repair, the liver may be affected by preoperative malperfusion, systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, operative complexity, perfusion-related stress, transfusion exposure, and postoperative critical illness [21–23]. Therefore, postoperative hepatic dysfunction may be better understood as one component of a broader postoperative organ-dysfunction trajectory rather than as an isolated hepatic complication. Its clinical interpretation may be most informative when considered together with malperfusion-related, operative, and perfusion-related variables.

Preoperative inflammatory, hepatic, and coagulation-related profiles may reflect baseline physiological reserve and early systemic injury before surgical repair. Higher preoperative white blood cell count may indicate stress leukocytosis and innate immune activation in the early phase of acute type A aortic dissection, potentially related to neutrophil-predominant inflammation, tissue ischemia, hemodynamic instability, or unmeasured malperfusion [24–27]. Beyond white blood cell count alone, derived inflammatory indices, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, systemic inflammation response index, and monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, may provide a more integrated assessment of inflammatory burden and inflammation–lipid interaction, and could be explored as candidate variables in future risk-stratification models [28–31]. Elevated preoperative ALT, AST, and total bilirubin levels may indicate hepatic vulnerability or impaired hepatic perfusion, whereas lower platelet and fibrinogen levels may reflect coagulation activation, consumption, inflammation, or bleeding propensity. Collectively, these abnormalities may identify patients with greater inflammatory, malperfusion, and coagulation burden before surgery.

Intraoperative and treatment-related variables may reflect operative complexity, perfusion-related stress, and perioperative physiological burden. Longer cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, operative time, and greater perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume may indicate more complex aortic pathology, more extensive repair, longer ischemic exposure, bleeding severity, coagulopathy, and prolonged exposure to non-physiological circulation. Cardiopulmonary bypass may affect hepatic perfusion through altered flow patterns, perfusion pressure, hemodilution, inflammatory activation, and splanchnic microcirculatory disturbance [22, 23, 32]. Aortic cross-clamping and circulatory arrest may further contribute to ischemia-reperfusion injury, particularly in patients with pre-existing malperfusion or reduced hepatic reserve. Transfusion burden may also reflect a complicated perioperative course, although transfusion-related inflammation, hemolysis, oxidative stress, and microcirculatory disturbance may contribute to hepatic stress [33, 34]. These associations should therefore be interpreted primarily as markers of operative complexity and perfusion-related burden, and their independent modifiability requires further evaluation.

Malperfusion and perfusion-related variables may be central to understanding postoperative hepatic dysfunction in this population. Because the liver depends on both hepatic arterial and portal venous inflow, hepatic injury after type A aortic dissection repair may result from systemic hypoperfusion, visceral malperfusion, celiac or hepatic arterial involvement, branch-vessel obstruction, and non-physiological perfusion during cardiopulmonary bypass [22, 23]. Although some included studies reported celiac trunk malperfusion or celiac trunk involvement, these variables were not consistently available for quantitative synthesis [14, 17]. Related surgical studies suggest that operative extent may influence postoperative hepatic dysfunction or other organ-related outcomes, with less extensive arch repair or hybrid approaches potentially reducing the physiological burden of conventional total arch replacement combined with frozen elephant trunk implantation [35–37]. Similarly, perfusion-focused strategies, including aortic balloon occlusion, branch-organ perfusion, and adjunctive lower-body perfusion, may reduce distal or visceral ischemic exposure during complex arch repair [38–40]. However, heterogeneity in surgical indications, anatomical complexity, and institutional protocols limits direct comparison across studies. This evidence gap is clinically important because current pooled estimates quantify broad perioperative associations but do not define how anatomical malperfusion, operative extent, and intraoperative perfusion management contribute to postoperative hepatic dysfunction.

These findings support the clinical relevance of postoperative hepatic dysfunction as a marker of adverse postoperative trajectories after type A aortic dissection repair. Its associations with mortality, acute kidney injury, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and longer intensive care unit stay suggest that it may help identify patients with greater physiological vulnerability and a higher burden of postoperative organ support. From a research perspective, the perioperative variables identified in this meta-analysis may inform the selection of candidate predictors for future risk-stratification studies, particularly when routinely available clinical indicators are integrated with more granular malperfusion, perfusion, transfusion, and organ-support data. Future risk-stratification models would benefit from standardized definitions, systematic data collection, and external validation in multicenter cohorts [41].

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, all included studies were single-center retrospective cohorts, and most pooled estimates were based on unadjusted or incompletely adjusted data; therefore, residual confounding is likely. Second, definitions of postoperative hepatic dysfunction varied substantially across studies, including MELD- or MELD-XI-based definitions and biomarker-based definitions using transaminase or bilirubin thresholds. Differences in diagnostic criteria and assessment time windows may have contributed to variation in incidence estimates and pooled associations. Third, key malperfusion-related, operative, and perfusion-related variables were not consistently available, including detailed computed tomography angiography-defined visceral malperfusion patterns, celiac or hepatic arterial involvement, cannulation strategy, cardiopulmonary bypass flow, intraoperative blood pressure targets, circulatory arrest strategy, temperature management, and extent of aortic repair. Fourth, all included studies were conducted in China, which may reflect high surgical volume and research interest in this setting but limits external generalizability to other healthcare systems, surgical strategies, and perioperative management protocols. Finally, because the number of included studies was small, publication bias could not be reliably assessed, and long-term outcomes were insufficiently reported. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the pooled associations and their clinical implications.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis showed that postoperative hepatic dysfunction was common after Stanford type A aortic dissection repair and was associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes, including higher in-hospital mortality, acute kidney injury, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and longer intensive care unit stay. Several preoperative and intraoperative variables were associated with its occurrence, including male sex, higher preoperative white blood cell count, longer cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times, longer operative time, and greater perioperative red blood cell transfusion volume. However, the available evidence remains limited by retrospective study designs, heterogeneous definitions, and limited adjustment for confounding. Further multicenter studies using standardized definitions and systematically collected malperfusion-related, operative, and perfusion-related variables are needed to clarify the clinical significance of postoperative hepatic dysfunction and support future risk-stratification research.
