Phylogenetic analysis of enteroviruses from non-human primates suggests two new species within the genus Enterovirus and inter-species recombination
Authors: Corentin Aubé, Paulina Cruz de Casas, Matthieu Prot, Artem Baidaliuk, Marie-Claire Endegue Zanga, Etienne Simon-Lorière, Nolwenn Jouvenet, Serge A Sadeuh-Mba, Maël Bessaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo date, 15 species have been described within the genus Enterovirus. Previous studies suggested the existence of another species comprising strains isolated from stool specimens of non-human primates (NHPs) in Central Africa. Moreover, numerous full-length or partial genomic sequences of NHP enteroviruses (EVs) can be found in GenBank without being properly classified. To our knowledge, no comprehensive synthesis of NHP EV data exists, leaving genetic relationships between strains across independent studies unclear. To address these gaps, we sequenced the complete genome of four NHP EVs from our stool collection and conducted an extensive search of NHP EV sequences in GenBank to perform a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis. Our analyses highlight that a group of viruses closely related to simian members of the species Enterovirus betacoxsackie in the non-structural region of the genome was found to be highly divergent in the capsid. In our view, the level of divergence suggests the existence of a novel species, tentatively named Enterovirus mbel. Recombination events between members of these two distinct species would explain their close relationships downstream the capsid-encoding region, thus challenging the long-held view that recombination is confined within narrowly defined subsets of EVs belonging to the same species. We also identified new virus types within the species Enterovirus jesimi, some of which are relatively divergent from the others in the capsid and could belong to another species, tentatively named Enterovirus noa. We also performed the first comprehensive comparative analysis of full-length human and NHP EV genomes, focusing on guanine-cytosine (GC) content, dinucleotide frequencies, and codon-usage bias. GC content emerged as the most robust host-associated marker: all NHP-associated virus types within species Enterovirus alphacoxsackie and Enterovirus betacoxsackie displayed GC content below 47%, whereas human-derived virus types exhibited GC content above 47%. Dinucleotide frequency, effective number of codons, and relative synonymous codon usage highlighted distinct codon-bias clusters that mirror the phylogenetic relationships between EVs, but only partially correlate with their respective hosts. This work enhances our understanding of EVs circulating in NHPs and paves the way for future research aiming at understanding the mechanisms underlying host-adaptation among EVs.

1. Introduction

Enteroviruses (EVs) are small, non-enveloped viruses with an icosahedral capsid. Their genome is a single-stranded and positive-sense RNA molecule of ~7200–8070 nucleotides (nt) in length. It contains two untranslated regions (5′ UTR and 3′ UTR) flanking a large open reading frame (ORF) that encodes a polyprotein, which is subsequently cleaved into three precursors (P1, P2, and P3). P1 gives rise to the four capsid proteins (VP1 to VP4), while P2 and P3 are cleaved into different proteins involved in viral replication (Baggen et al. 2018, Tuthill et al. 2010). A short additional upstream ORF (uORF) encoding a single protein has been described in the genome of some EVs (Lulla et al. 2019).

The classification of EVs has undergone significant revisions since their discovery. Initially, EVs were classified into different groups based on their phenotypic properties (e.g. tropism and pathogenicity in mice) and physicochemical characteristics (density of the virus particles, sensibility to acid pHs) (Bessaud et al. 2018). Within each group, virus isolates were grouped into serotypes based on in vitro cross-neutralization assays. In the 2000s, genetic criteria replaced phenotypic and serological criteria as the basis for classification (Oberste et al. 1999a). Enteroviruses are now classified into species and virus types, depending on their phylogenetic relationships. According to the International Committee for Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) guidelines, EVs belong to the same species if they share at least 70% amino acid (aa) identity in the complete ORF and in the 2C + 3CD genes, and at least 60% aa identity in the capsid (P1 region) (‘Family: Picornaviridae | ICTV,’ n.d., Zell et al. 2017). The Enterovirus genus currently comprises 15 species: Enterovirus alphacoxsackie (EV-A), Enterovirus betacoxsackie (EV-B), Enterovirus coxsackiepol (EV-C), Enterovirus deconjuncti (EV-D), Enterovirus eibovi (EV-E), Enterovirus fitauri (EV-F), Enterovirus geswini (EV-G), Enterovirus hesimi (EV-H), Enterovirus idromi (EV-I), Enterovirus jesimi (EV-J), Enterovirus krodeni (EV-K), Enterovirus lesimi (EV-L), Enterovirus alpharhino (RV-A), Enterovirus betarhino (RV-B), and Enterovirus cerhino (RV-C), each of which comprise multiple virus types based on the VP1-encoding sequence (‘Current ICTV Taxonomy Release | ICTV,’ n.d.). Virus type within a species are in most cases effectively defined by >75% nt identity and >85%–88% aa identity in this region (Brown et al. 2009, Oberste et al. 1999b).

EVs infect a wide range of mammals, including humans, non-human primates (NHPs), small ruminants, pigs, cattle, camels, and rodents. While each EV appears adapted to a limited set of host species that constitutes its natural reservoirs, host barriers can be overcome, as demonstrated by numerous documented cross-species transmissions between humans and animals, bidirectionally, or among different animal species (Fieldhouse et al. 2018, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2023). Notably, EVs do not exhibit clear host-associated phylogenetic clustering, as some species include virus types circulating in different hosts. For instance, some EV-As, EV-Bs, and EV-Ds circulate in humans while others circulate in NHPs. Similarly, some EV-Gs circulate in pigs while others circulate in small ruminants.

The first identified NHP EVs were detected in simian cell cultures and laboratory animals in the mid-20th century (Oberste et al. 2002). Since then, new EV types have been discovered in captive, wild, healthy, and sick NHPs. Over the past 20 years, next-generation sequencing has increased the number of publicly available NHP EV sequences. However, data remain sparse and many sequences span diverse sub-genomic regions and remain to be properly classified. Moreover, no comprehensive synthesis of all available NHP EV molecular data exists, leaving the genetic relationships between NHP EVs across independent studies fragmented. Additionally, sequences of NHP EVs sampled in Central Africa have suggested the existence of at least one additional EV species (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014), but it has never been formally described. To address these gaps, we conducted an extensive search of NHP EV sequences in GenBank and performed a comprehensive phylogenic analysis. To make this analysis as complete as possible, we also sequenced the complete genome of four NHP EVs from our previous work (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014). Furthermore, we compared genetic characteristics, such as GC percentage (GC%), dinucleotide ratios, or codon usage (effective number of codons, ENC and relative synonymous codon usage, RSCU) between NHP and human EVs. Our analysis suggest the existence of one or two additional EV species and revealed that specific genomic signatures align with phylogeny and may be associated with host-adaptation.

2. Methods

2.1. Amplicon-based sequencing

EV-B114 strain Z057 was grown in RD cells and RNA was extracted from 250 μL of clarified cell culture supernatant using the High Pure Viral RNA kit (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Genome amplification was performed by RT-PCR with primers targeting previously described conserved regions of the EV genome (Joffret et al., 2018). Sequencing was performed by Illumina technology and de novo assembly conducted with CLC Genomics Workbench as previously described (Bessaud et al., 2016). As EV-J121 strain CHE20 could not be propagated in cell culture (Sadeuh-Mba et al., 2014), RNA was extracted directly from 140 μL of clarified 20% (Weight/Volume) stool suspension using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, France). Genome amplification was performed by RT-PCR with primers targeting the sub-genomic regions previously sequenced (Sadeuh-Mba et al., 2014), as well as a primer targeting the 3′ terminal poly A sequence. Sequencing was performed by the Sanger technique.

2.2. Metatranscriptomic next-generation sequencing

Two strains, RCHM3 and RCHM6, that could not be grown in cell culture (Hep-2c, LLC-MK2, Vero, and RD cells) (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014) were sequenced using a protocol previously described (Gámbaro et al. 2021). After extraction from 140 μl of faecal suspension using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) followed by Turbo DNAse treatment (Ambion), RNA was purified with Agencourt RNAClean XP beads. Host rRNA was depleted using custom probes and RNAse H treatment (Matranga et al. 2014). After purification using AMPure RNA clean beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics) and elution in 10 μl of RNase-free water, RNA was reverse transcribed using random primers and SuperScript IV (Invitrogen). Second-strand DNA was synthesized using Escherichia coli DNA ligase, RNAse H, and DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs) and purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Libraries were then prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), quality controlled using a Bioanalyzer high sensitivity DNA analysis kit (Agilent), and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq500 (2 × 75 cycles).

Adapters and low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic v0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014). Trimmed reads were assembled using megahit v1.2.9 (Li et al. 2016) with default parameters. The contigs were queried against the NCBI non-redundant protein database using DIAMOND v2.0.4 (Buchfink et al. 2015), leading to the identification of novel EVs sequences. The EVs genomes were then reconstructed by iterative mapping of the trimmed reads, using clc-assembly-cell v5.1.0. We used Samtools v1.10 (Li et al. 2009) to generate alignment statistics and ivar v1.0 (Grubaugh et al. 2019) to generate consensus sequences, using a minimum of 3× read depth coverage. The mapping data was visually checked to confirm the accuracy of the obtained genomes using Geneious Prime 2023 (www.geneious.com).

2.3. Phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignments were performed with MAFFT (v.7.526) with the options ‘--globalpair --maxiterate 1000’ (Katoh and Standley 2013) and maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were inferred with IQ-Tree (v.3.1.0) (Wong et al. 2026) with the option ‘-m TEST’ to choose the best-fit model and the branch support were computed with 1000 bootstraps. Tree representations were generated using FigTree (version 1.4.4) (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Links to Nextstrain interactive representations of trees from Figs. 2 and 6, Supplementary Fig. 1, and tanglegrams are available on GitHub: https://github.com/CorentinAube/NHP_EV_genomic_analysis.

2.4. Genomic comparison

Prototype genomes of human and NHP EVs were retrieved from the Picornaviridae database (http://www.picornaviridae.com), while more recent sequences were retrieved from the BV-BRC database (Olson et al. 2023). Other partial NHP EV sequences were retrieved from the scientific literature indexed in PubMed. Multiple sequence alignments were done with MAFFT, while protein sequences translation and nt and aa identity matrices were computed with CLC Main Workbench. The GC content, dinucleotide ratios, ENC, and RSCU values were calculated using custom Python (version 3.12.12) scripts available on GitHub with also the list of all accession numbers of sequences used (https://github.com/CorentinAube/NHP_EV_genomic_analysis). The formulas for dinucleotide ratio, ENC, and RSCU calculations were obtained from Zeng and colleagues (Zeng et al. 2022). The determination of GC3 bias was based on the method published by Butt and colleagues (Butt et al. 2016).

2.5. Data availability

Nucleotide sequences generated in this study were deposited on the GenBank database (accession numbers PX682287-PX682290). An isolate of the EV-B114 strain Z057 has been deposited in the EVAg repository (Romette et al. 2018).

3. Results

3.1. Whole-genome sequencing of four NHP EVs from our stool collection

Four EVs that had only been previously partially sequenced (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014) were subjected to whole-genome sequencing. Contigs covering nearly the entire viral genome were obtained for three viruses: Z057, CHE20, and RCHM3 (Fig. 1a). For RCMH6, ~150 nt could not successfully be sequenced at the 5′ extremity, probably because of low concentration and/or poor quality of virus RNA. All four viruses featured the canonical EV genomic organization, with a large ORF flanked by two UTRs. Comparing these sequences with annotated reference EV genomes, canonical polyprotein cleavage sites were identified, suggesting that the polyprotein processing in these viruses is similar to that of other EVs (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, the genomes of Z057, RCMH3, and RCMH6 contained a putative uORF similar to that already described in different EV species (Lulla et al. 2019). This putative uORF overlaps with the main ORF with a frameshift, and its putative start codon is located 133 or 136 nt upstream of the main ORF (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the EV-J121 CHE20 genome lacked a uORF, as previously reported for other members of the EV-J species (Lulla et al. 2019). All four genomes also contained a cis-replication element (CRE) within the 2C gene, matching the consensus sequence 5′-GXXXAAAXXXXXXA-3′ (Fig. 1c) (Cordey et al. 2008).

The data for EV-L are very sparce with only one known representative. Species EV-H includes only six sequences, four of which are epidemiologically related to each other. As previously described (Nguyen et al. 2014), theses sequences belong to a single virus type (EV-H1), sharing 84.09%–100% nt identity and 95.82%–100% aa identity (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Three EV species, EV-A, -B, and -D, include some EVs that originated from humans, while others were recovered from NHPs. Within the EV-B species, NHP EVs segregate from human-derived EVs and define a specific branch (Fig. 2). The unique exception to the host association of EV-B types is the case of GAB98 strain, isolated from wild chimpanzees, which is an EV-B107 known to circulate in humans in Asia (Maan et al. 2019, Yamashita et al. 2010). The isolation of strain GAB98 in a chimpanzee was explained as a sporadic human-to-NHP transmission, potentially facilitated by the adjacent or overlapping humans and chimpanzee habitats (Mombo et al. 2017). As previously reported, four distinct virus types exist in the NHP specific branch of the EV-B species. Strain Z057, of which a complete genome sequence was obtained in this work (Fig. 1), belongs to the EV-B114 virus type (Fig. 3a), that was first isolated in 1963 in NHP-derived cell cultures (Malherbe and Harwin 1963). Since its discovery, only two EV-B114 field strains have been reported: CPOV3344 (Nguyen et al. 2014) and Z057 (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014). They share 84.14%–84.52% nt and 92.86%–93.93% aa identities between each other in the VP1 sequence, thus confirming that they belong to the same virus type (Fig. 3b). In contrast to EV-B species, the phylogenetic boundaries between NHP and human EVs are less distinct in species EV-A and EV-D (Fig. 2). Members of the species EV-A fall into five different phylogroups (Fig. 2). Two groups (groups 1 and 2) are specific to humans, two are specific to NHPs (groups 4 and 5), while the last one (group 3) includes EVs detected both in human and NHPs (the details regarding virus types are featured in Supplementary Fig. 1). Similarly, within EV-D, some types were detected in humans only (EV-D70, EV-D68, and EV-D94), in NHPs only (EV-D120), while EV-D111 has been detected in both hosts (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2019). No unidentified or misidentified NHP derived EV-A, -B, or -D were found in public databases.

As mentioned above, RCMH3, RCMH6, CPML3961, CPML8109, GR2815, and NGR_NHP_1 VP1 sequences fall into a separate branch (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine whether these sequences belong to a single new species, we applied the ICTV’s species demarcation criteria, based on nt and aa sequence similarity: members of a species of the genus Enterovirus feature more than 70% aa similarity in the prolyprotein (whole ORF), more than 70% aa similarity in the sequence of the non-structural proteins 2C + 3CD, and more than 60% aa similarity in the capsid (‘Genus: Enterovirus | ICTV,’ n.d.). Our analysis showed that the corresponding viruses met the ICTV criteria for classification as the same species (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3): they share over 70% aa identity in the ORF (85.68%–95.02%) and in the 2C + 3CD sequences (97.84%–99.69%), as well as over 60% aa identity in the capsid sequences (69.11%–89.82%). Of note, as already reported (Mombo et al. 2023, 2021, 2017; Nguyen et al. 2014; Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014), these viruses were also relatively close to EV-B simian virus types in the non-structural part of the genome: >80% aa similarity in the 2C + 3CD region, far higher the ICTV’s species demarcation criterion in this region (Table 2). By contrast, they notably diverge from all known EV-Bs in the whole capsid-encoding region, with aa similarity below 51% (Table 2). Thus, these two ICTV’s species demarcation criteria gave conflicting results. To us, the high nt divergence between the new virus types and simian EV-Bs in the whole capsid-encoding region does not support the hypothesis of these viruses belonging to the species EV-B. Therefore, we propose the creation of a new species, tentatively named Enterovirus mbel (EV-M), after the name of Pterocarpus soyauxii, a tree native to the tropical forest in Central African (Albreht et al. 2025), known as mbel in various Bantu languages spoken in Central and Southern Cameroon, where the first representatives of this EV species were sampled (Galley 1964, Tsala 1956). Phylogenetic analyses of the VP1 gene revealed several clusters within this new EV-M species (Fig. 4). Based on the established thresholds for discriminating EV types (>75% nt identity and >85% aa identity in the VP1 gene) (Brown et al. 2009), we identified 4 distinct virus types (Supplementary Fig. 4): EV-M1 (prototype: NGR-NHP-1), EV-M2 (prototype: GR2815), EV-M3 (prototype: CPML8109), and EV-M4 (prototypes: CPML3961, RCMH3, and RCMH6). Within the EV-M4 type, three different groups seem to be distinguished and were tentatively named EV-M4a (prototype: CPML3961), EV-M4b (prototype: RCMH6), and EV-M4c (prototype: RCMH6) (Supplementary Fig. 4). These genomes fell slightly below the nt 75% threshold (<74.65%) and around the aa 85% threshold (between 84.72% and 87.50%) supporting the group distinction for these strains. Identity matrices showed that numerous VP1 sequences deposited in GenBank belong to these newly defined EV-M virus types (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5). EV-M2 and EV-M3 virus types gathered sequences that displayed >82.94% nt and >90.18% aa identities between each other. Within the EV-M4 type, EV-M4b and EV-M4c virus types gathered sequences that displayed >79.17% nt and >90.94% aa identities between each other. Within the EV-M4a type, all sequences except three (CPML8108, LM1962, and CPOV3334) displayed a nt and aa identity respectively >78.47% and >87.15% between each other. The nt and aa identities of CPML8108, LM1962, and CPOV3334 compared to another EV-M4a virus were mostly higher than the theoretical thresholds (nt: from 73.17% to 98.26%, aa: from 83.22% to 94.76%) supporting their classification as EV-M4a (Supplementary Fig. 5). The three groups share less than 74.65% and 87.80% in nt and aa similarity, supporting this group distinction. Besides the prototype NGR_NHP_1, no additional members of virus type EV-M1 were identified.

Partial VP1 or VP2 sequences, averaging 350 nt in length, have also been published and annotated as belonging to the species EV-J (Mombo et al. 2023, 2021, 2017). However, dendrograms based on these regions revealed that they actually cluster within the EV-M species (Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). The corresponding sequences are too short to allow accurate typing of the corresponding viruses.

Six EV-J virus types have been previously described. The capsid-based phylogram accurately delineated the EV-J103, EV-J108, and EV-J122 virus types and confirmed the EV-J121 type, of which CHE20 is the first known representative, that was previously characterized based on partial sequence (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014) (Fig. 5a). Identity matrices were calculated to assign several untyped EV-J sequences (Fig. 5a and b, Supplementary Fig. 7). NOLA1 and NOLA3 share over 75.41% and 85.61% at nt and aa level, respectively, with an EV-J103 strain and were thus assigned to this virus type. Similarly, the sequence comparison of the partial VP1 sequence of cg5275 with EV-J prototype strains supported its assignment to the EV-J103 type (>77.36% and >85.43 nt and aa identity, respectively, with other EV-J103 viruses, Supplementary Fig. 8). The Sev-nJ and CHN-BJ-LXL1 sequences shares 81.17% and 90.53% nt and aa identity with one another (Supplementary Fig. 7), which suggest that they belonged to the same virus type. The initial report of Sev-nJ strain suggested that it belonged to a novel virus type (Wang et al. 2016). Our analyses show that Sev-nJ and CHN-BJ-LXL1 feature nt similarity ranging from 70.99% through 74.36% and 81.40% through 85.31% similarity at nt and aa level, respectively, with other EV-Js (Supplementary Fig. 7). All pairwise values but one are below the threshold commonly used to discriminate virus types, which suggest a putative novel virus type, tentatively named EV-J123, following the current taxonomic nomenclature. Additionally, several partial VP1 and VP4/2 sequences remained to be assigned (Harvala et al. 2014). Based on partial VP1 sequences, the CPOV3336 strain is related to EV-J121 strain CHE20 (Supplementary Fig. 9), but their nt and aa identities (65.99% and 75.00%, Supplementary Fig. 8) indicate that CPOV3336 belongs to a putative distinct virus type (tentatively named EV-J124).

Some viruses previously identified as EV-Js (CHN-BJ-2J, -1J, -8J, -10J, and -9J) form a specific group (Fig. 5). As previously suggested (Li et al. 2020), they form two distinct virus types, as they share less than 74.45% nt and 81.18% aa identity (Supplementary Fig. 7) with one another. To determine whether these viruses belong to the EV-J species, we applied the ICTV’s species demarcation criteria described above. Compared to EV-Js, the CHN-BJ-2J, 1J, 8J, 10J, and 9J sequences share 68.90% to 72.61% identity in the ORF and 78.21% to 84.48% in the 2C + 3CD genes with other EV-J types (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 10A and B), and thus meet the two criteria of belonging to the same species. By contrast, as previously reported (Li et al. 2020), they share only between 55.69% and 60.05% aa identity in the capsid gene (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 10C), which is below of just above the threshold of the species demarcation criteria (60%). This is the same situation than the one described above between EV-Ms and simian EV-Bs: two groups of viruses that meet the species demarcation criterion in the 2C + 3CD region but not in the capsid (Table 2). Nonetheless, while EV-Ms and simian EV-Bs exhibit low similarity in the capsid region (<51%, Table 2), the similarity in this region between CHN-BJ-2J, 1J, 8J, 10J, and 9J in the one hand and EV-Js in the other is just below the species demarcation threshold of 60% (Table 2). This represents a dilemma that can be resolved in several ways: retaining the three existing criteria but considering that it is not essential for all three to be satisfied simultaneously, modifying the criterion pertaining to the capsid and accepting greater genetic diversity (up to 45%) within a species in this region, or maintaining the three existing criteria and considering them to be cumulative.

Under the two first scenarios, the strains CHN-BJ-2J, 1J, 8J, 10J, and CHN-BJ-2J would represent two new serotypes within species J, tentatively named EV-J125 and EV-J126. Under the latter scenario, these strains would be members of a separate novel species comprised of at least two virus types. Currently available data are insufficient to discriminate between these two scenarios. Should future data confirm the existence of this new species, we propose the name Enterovirus nao (EV-N), after the Chinese word náo (猱) meaning ‘monkey’ (Couvreur 1896, Loewe 1993), with virus types EV-N1 (for the CHN-BJ-1J, 8J, 9J, 10J strains) and EV-N2 (for the CHN-BJ-2J strain).

Collectively, these results reveal a complex landscape of NHP EV diversity, including one new species (EV-M), two putative new EV-J types (EV-J123 and EV-J124), and either a new species (tentatively named EV-N) or two additional EV-J types (tentatively names EV-J125 and EV-126). This refined classification will facilitate the proper assignment of NHP EV strains to their respective species and virus type.

3.3. Phylogenetic relationships in the non-structural genomic regions

Recombination events are frequent among co-circulating EVs, generating new viruses with chimeric genomes that may outcompete parental strains if they have a fitness advantage. Because recombination constantly reshuffles EV genomes, different genomic regions are not subjected to the same selective pressures and do not follow the same evolutionary trajectories. After clarifying the phylogenetic relationships among the NHP derived EVs in the capsid-encoding region, we investigated their evolutionary history by constructing phylogenetic trees considering 2A2B, 2C, 3A3B, 3C, and 3D regions, using all available full-length ORF sequences (Fig. 6). As previously reported (Mombo et al. 2017, Oberste et al. 2007, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2023), striking intra-species phylogenetic incongruences were observed between trees generated from structural (capsid) and non-structural (2A2B, 2C, 3A3B, and 3D) regions. Within the EV-B species, the NHP and human-derived EVs formed two distinct clades, consistent with the VP1 phylogeny (Fig. 6). Of note, all known EV-Ms clustered with the NHP EV-B clade across all non-structural regions. Consequently, EV-Ms and NHP EV-Bs are phylogenetically indistinguishable from each other across the non-structural part of the genome. Similarly, as previously reported (Oberste et al. 2007, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2023), EV-A125 strain BA13 did not cluster with other EV-As in the non-structural regions downstream the capsid. Our analyses further revealed that BA13 strain clustered with EV-J108 strains in the 2A2B, 2C, and 3A3B regions, with the EV-J103 strains in the 3C gene and with the members of the new species EV-N in the 3D gene (Fig. 6). Finally, the species EV-D, EV-H, and EV-L exhibited complex phylogenetic relationships. Indeed, they form a single group in the 2A2B region, while the other regions showed specific pairwise associations: EV-D grouped with EV-H and EV-L in the 2C/3A3B/3C regions and the 3D region, respectively.

3.4. Comparison of genomic signatures of human and NHP-derived EVs

To our knowledge, no comparative genomic analysis of human and NHP-derived EV species has been published so far. A previous study, limited to EV-A species, revealed that a subset of virus types associated with NHPs displayed distinct relative dinucleotide ratios and codon usage biases compared to EV-A virus types that circulate widely in humans (Zeng et al. 2022), suggesting that specific genomic signatures could be associated with the virus’ natural history and, particularly its host range. To determine whether NHP-derived EVs from different host species shared unique genomic traits, we expanded these analyses to all known NHP EVs. We compared GC content (GC%), dinucleotide ratio, and codon usage (ENC and RSCU analysis) between human and NHP-derived EVs. To minimize potential bias in these analyses, we included, where possible, at least the prototype strain and two additional genomes sampled from different countries for each virus type.

The GC% ranged from 41% to 49% across all species (Fig. 7a). Interestingly, the GC% strongly correlated with the host origin rather than EV species classification, with all NHP virus types featuring a GC% < 47% whichever their respective species. In particular, NHP-specific EV species (EV-J, EV-H, EV-L, EV-M, and EV-N) display low GC%, ranging from 43% to 46.4%, as did the few known EV-D virus types (41.0%–43.5%). Strikingly, virus types segregated with respect to their host of origin within the EV-A and EV-B species: all EV-A virus types of groups 1 and 2, originating from humans, displayed GC% > 47%, while NHP-associated groups 3 and 4 had GC% < 47% (Fig. 7b). A similar clustering was found within EV-B species, with all human-derived EV types featuring a GC% > 47%, while all NHP-derived EV types had a GC% < 45% (Fig. 7c). All known EV-C virus types circulate exclusively among humans but can be divided into three phylogenetic groups that match with their phenotype (Fig. 2): respiratory virus types (EV-C104, EV-C105, EV-C109, EV-C117, and EV-C118), cultivable enteric virus types (PV1, PV2, PV3, CVA11, CVA13, CVA17, CVA20, CVA21, CVA24, EV-C95, EV-C96, EV-C99, and EV-C102), and non-cultivable viruses (CVA1, CVA19, CVA22, EV-C113, EV-C116, and EV-C119). Notably, the GC% of respiratory virus types exhibited a high GC% (48%–48.5%), cultivable enteric virus types displayed an intermediate GC% (45%–46.5%), while non-cultivable viruses showed a low GC% (43%–44%) (Fig. 7d).

A previous study revealed that EV-As group 3 viruses display lower CpG and UpA dinucleotide frequencies compared to groups 1 and 2 (Zeng et al. 2022). To determine whether NHP-derived EV-As of group 4 share this trait, we analysed dinucleotide ratios of EV-A strains representative of the different groups (Supplementary Fig. 11). Our analysis revealed that EV-A viruses belonging to group 4 had the same low CpG ratio as group 3 EV-As (Supplementary Fig. 11, bottom panel). The same observation was made within EV-B species, with NHP-derived EV types displaying a CpG ratio substantially lower than that of human-derived EV types (NHP EVs < 0.42 versus human EVs > 0.56) (Supplementary Fig. 11, bottom panel). With the exception of EV-H, a low CpG ratio seems to be a common characteristic of NHP-derived EVs. Indeed, members of the EV-J, EV-L, EV-M, and EV-N species featured CpG ratios <0.5 close to those of NHP-derived EV-A and EV-B species (Supplementary Fig. 11). It is noteworthy that all EV-D virus types had CpG ratio <0.35, irrespective of their host of origin.

We also investigated codon usage bias in human and NHP-derived EVs. The ENCs, which ranges from 20 (strong bias, with one codon used for each amino acid) to 61 (no bias, even use of each codon) (Wright 1990), revealed distinct patterns. Human EV-As (groups 1 and 2), EV-Bs, respiratory EV-Cs, and NHP-derived EV-Hs had ENC greater than 54 (Supplementary Fig. 12A), while group 3, NHP-derived EV-As, NHP-derived EV-Bs, cultivable and non-cultivable EV-Cs, EV-Ds, EV-Js, and EV-Ms featured ENC values between 50 and 54. The sole known EV-L had an ENC value of 48. Since the GC% at the third codon position (GC3) could introduce a bias in the ENC analysis, we compared the ENC values for each EV species with the expected ENC based on the respective GC3 percentage (Butt et al. 2016) (Supplementary Fig. 13). All calculated ENC values fell below the expected curve, confirming that GC3 content did not account for the variations across virus group.

We also analysed the RSCU, which quantifies how frequently each synonymous codon is used in a gene or genome relative to the expected usage. The results, summarized in a cladogram, grouped EV types into three main clusters (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Group A contains only the EV-H1 type. Group B includes all other NHP-specific EV types (except EV-A123), whichever their species, all human cultivable EV-Cs, all EV-Ds, and EV-A groups 3 and 4. Group C contains all other human EV-Cs and EV-A123. These results suggest that codon-usage bias varies markedly among EV groups, yet it only partially mirrors the underlying phylogenetic relationships among them.

Collectively, these results reveal that dinucleotide ratios, ENC, and RSCU values vary among NHP EV species. Remarkably, GC% mirrors phylogenetic relationships between EVs and could serve as a predictive marker of human versus NHP origin of EVs.

4. Discussion

Initially, NHP-derived EVs were distinguished from each other by serological assays. They were not assigned to any specific species, and no standardized nomenclature was established. In the 1980s, it was proposed to designate these viruses under the common name “Simian enterovirus” followed by a number corresponding to a given serotype (Kalter 1982). In the 2000s and 2010s, the generalization of genetic sequencing brought about major changes in EV classification, as phylogenetic relationships replaced phenotypic criteria to delineate EV types and species. Based on their genetic relationships, NHP-derived EVs were classified into different species, some of which also included human-derived EVs. By considering all NHP EV genome sequences available in public databases and integrating our new full-genome sequences, our goal was to provide a clear overview of the relationships between viruses described in independent studies. The EV classification into species and virus types is made difficult by the frequent recombination events that constantly shuffle EV genomes. Consequently, EV genomes cannot be analysed as a whole since they are composed of genomic modules that have distinct evolutionary trajectories (Muslin et al. 2019). The quantitative demarcation criteria established by the ICTV’s Picornavirus Study Group are sometimes challenged by virus sequences that are very close to the thresholds, making difficult unambiguous assignations. Even more complicated is the situation where some criteria are met while others are not, as exemplified in this study by EV-Ms and simian EV-Bs on the one hand, and EV-Js and putative EV-Ns on the other hand. This situation creates a dilemma: either relax the thresholds, or acknowledge that viruses may be assigned to different species despite relative sequence similarity in some genomic coding regions. This dilemma can only be resolved by the ICTV’s Picornavirus Study Group on a case-by-case basis. Considering the capsid and the 2C + 3CD regions independently is logical, as the EV genome is organized into distinct modules that evolve relatively independently owing to recombination. Of these two regions, we regard the capsid as pre-eminent: it is the primary determinant of viral identity, interacting directly with cell receptors (thereby determining tropism) and carrying the major antigenic sites. Just as two EV strains that diverge in the capsid may be classified as distinct virus types regardless of their sequence identity in non-coding or non-structural regions, we would find it difficult to classify viruses showing relatively high amino acid divergence in the capsid within the same species. Turning to the third criterion (the whole ORF), we believe it is less informative. Although recombination is constrained by functional compatibilities that must be maintained across genomic regions, this criterion conflates segments with disparate evolutionary trajectories. Consequently, it cannot distinguish viruses with uniform divergence across the ORF from those that are distantly related in some regions and closely related in others, as for instance simian EV-Bs and EV-Ms. The relevance of this criterion is further challenged by EV-G strains whose ORF contains an in-frame coding exogenous insertion exceeding 650 nt (i.e. ~10% of the ORF) (Conceição-Neto et al. 2017, Knutson et al. 2017, Shang et al. 2017). For these reasons, we think that viruses whose capsids differ substantially should be classified as distinct species, even if they are closely related across the remainder of the ORF. It is worth noting that this rationale already applies to the 5′ UTR, which is not taken into account when assigning EVs to species, as EV-As and EV-Bs, for instance, are known to exchange 5′ UTR sequences (Santti et al. 1999). If inter-species exchanges upstream of the capsid are accepted, then so too should they be downstream. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the threshold to be applied to the capsid is difficult to establish. To us, the level of divergence observed in the capsid between EV-Bs and EV-Ms (>49% at aa level) supports the existence of this latter species. The locations from which representatives of this species were sampled [Gabon, Cameroon, and Nigeria (Mombo et al. 2023, 2021, 2017; Nguyen et al. 2014; Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014)] justify the proposed name, Enterovirus mbel, which is derived from an endemic tree of the region known in English as African padauk (Albreht et al. 2025). The existence of the EV-N species raised herein is subject to greater doubt in light of the capsid aa similarity between members of this putative species and EV-Js, which is just below the species demarcation criterion. Our analysis is further limited by the fact that all members of this putative species are epidemiologically linked, as they were collected during the same period within the same NHP group. Future identification of other EVs may allow us to determine whether some fill the apparent phylogenetic gaps between all these viruses, in which case they would form a single species, or whether EV-N truly exists. If confirmed, because this species was first documented in China, we suggesting naming it Enterovirus nao, after the Chinese word referring to a monkey in a poem written 2500–3000 years ago (Couvreur 1896, Loewe 1993).

The discovery of the uORF in the genome of some EVs a few years ago constituted a drastic change in our understanding of their biology. This uORF was experimentally shown to encode a non-essential protein that enhances virus replication in gut cells (Lulla et al. 2019). Interestingly, the putative uORFs identified in the genomes of EV-B114 Z057, EV-M1 RCMH6, and EV-M3 RCMH3 are respectively located 134 nt, 133 nt, and 136 nt upstream of the AUG start codon of the main ORF. These distances fall below the cutoff set by Lulla et al. for screening for uORF of EV genomic sequences (Lulla et al. 2019). Although functional validation of these candidate uORFs is still lacking, our findings suggest that the prevalence of uORFs across EVs may have been underestimated and warrants re-evaluation using a more permissive distance threshold.

The phylogenetic analyses performed on the non-structural part of the genome further confirmed the close genetic relationships between NHP EVs recovered from different species, as previouly documented (Fig. 6) (Mombo et al. 2017, Nguyen et al. 2014, Oberste et al. 2007, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014). This close genetic relatedness could be explained by convergent evolution, which may have driven EV genomes from different species to become phylogenetically more similar as a consequence of their replication within NHP hosts. However, EV-Bs and EV-Ms cluster together both in the 5′ UTR (Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014) and across the entire non-structural genomic region (Fig. 6). Therefore, convergent evolution would have uniformly affect all non-capsid regions, which seems unlikely. Furthermore, convergent evolution among NHP-derived EVs is not supported by the fact that most known NHP-derived EV-As do not cluster with other NHP-derived EVs, except for EV-A125 strain BA13, which is phylogenetically close to EV-Js downstream the capsid. If convergent evolution was shaping the evolution of EV-A125, it would be expected to similarly affect other NHP-derived EV-As, which is not the case. Therefore, these phylogenetic incongruences found in this study are probably due to recombination, a well-established driver of EV evolution (Muslin et al. 2019).

While interspecies recombination has been observed in the 5′ UTR (Boros et al. 2012, Muslin et al. 2015, Santti et al. 1999), it has never been documented downstream of the capsid-encoding region among human-derived EVs. Even intraspecies recombination is limited to specific subsets of EV types that can recombine with one another but not with members of other subsets. For instance, EV-As of group 3 do not exchange genetic sequences with EV-As of groups 1 and 2 (Oberste et al. 2005, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2014). Similarly, respiratory EV-Cs and non-cultivable EV-Cs do not recombine with each other or with cultivable EV-Cs (Brouwer et al. 2020). Several factors could explain the apparent inability of some EVs to generate recombinant viruses, such as functional incompatibilities between genomic regions from distant phylogenetic groups, lack of fitness advantages conferred to the recombinant viruses, or low frequency of co-infection due to different host and tissue tropisms. Our analysis provided substantial evidence to the existence of interspecies recombination between some NHP-derived EV species, particularly between EV-Ms and EV-Bs. These findings change our understanding of recombination between EVs, which appear to be capable of producing larger leaps than previously believed. This raises fundamental questions about the past and future evolutionary paths of these viruses. For instance, it is unknown whether NHP-derived EV-Bs acquired non-structural genes from EV-Ms, or vice versa, unless both inherited these genes from a yet-unidentified common source. The ability of EV-Ms to recombine with human-derived EV-Bs and the potential for such inter-species macro-evolutionary events to produce new human-adapted EV-Ms are also difficult to predict.

Previous studies have shown that genomes of group 3 EV-As, which were detected in both NHPs and humans, had a lower CpG frequency than human-specific EV-As and featured a specific codon usage bias that impairs their expression of these EV-A genomes in human cells (Zeng et al. 2022). These observations led us to hypothesize that some genetic characteristics shared by NHP-derived EVs may play a role in host adaptation. Nucleotide composition has been previously used to predict the host of novel picorna-like viruses (Kapoor et al. 2010). While our analysis of dinucleotide frequency and codon usage bias across the various EV species circulating in humans and in NHPs did not reveal a clear segregation between human and NHP-derived viruses (Supplementary Figs 11 and 12), our results identified a relatively low GC% as a signature shared by virus types circulating among NHPs, regardless of their virus species (Fig. 7). Unfortunately, the lack of data precluded robust statistical analysis, as some EV types are represented by hundreds of sequences while others, such as EV-L species, are represented by only one or two sequences. Based on genetic and epidemiological data, we and others have previously postulated that EV-Ds known in humans may have recently emerged from zoonotic sources, presumably NHPs (Kono et al. 1981, Miyamura et al. 1986, Sadeuh-Mba et al. 2019). The low GC% values displayed by all EV-D virus types, irrespective of their tissue tropism or host of origin, are consistent with that hypothesis. The relative proximity of EV-Ds and NHP-specific EV species in the non-structural part of the genome further supports the hypothesis of a zoonotic origin of EV-Ds. However, host adaptation is not the only driver of GC%, as illustrated by EV-C virus types, which display a broad range of GC% values despite all circulating in humans (Fig. 7). In addition, the GC% of human and NHP EV strains falls within the broad range observed in the human transcriptome (average 48%, varying from <40% to >60% among mRNAs) (Courel et al. 2019, Piovesan et al. 2019). Unfortunately, the GC% of transcriptomes of NHP infected with EVs remains uncharacterized to our knowledge. The GC% overlap between EVs and human transcriptome is insufficient to conclude host-species adaptation, and the lack of data prevents comparative analysis that could elucidate potential host-driven GC% adaptation in NHP EV strains. The genomic signature result from a dynamic equilibrium influenced by many factors, of which polymerase fidelity, RNA secondary structure requirements, host translational preferences, immune pressure, and genetic recombination. Furthermore, identifying genomic signatures that reliably distinguish NHP-derived EVs from human-derived EVs is currently limited by the paucity of fully sequenced strains, our limited knowledge of the natural history of these viruses, and particularly the uncertainty surrounding their host tropisms.
