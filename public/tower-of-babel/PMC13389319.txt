Intravascular Ultrasound in Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A Consensus Document from the UK
Authors: John Hung, Brian Cox, Michael Foley, Alan Bagnall, Sandeep Basavarajaiah, David Carrick, Anirban Choudhury, John Davies, Farzin Fath-Ordoubadi, Peter Haworth, Zaid Iskander, Thomas Keeble, Andrew Ladwiniec, Kristel Longman, Abdul Mozid, Sukhjinder Nijjer, Ricardo Petraco, Neil Ruparelia, Nicola Ryan, Henry Seligman, Andrew Sharp, Girish Viswanathan, Rupert Williams, Simon Wilson, Peter O’Kane, Rasha Al-Lamee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Following the recently upgraded recommendations for intravascular imaging use from the European Society of Cardiology and the American Heart Association, we present a UK consensus statement on the use of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in percutaneous coronary intervention. Developed by 26 senior interventional cardiologists across a range of UK centres, this statement reflects growing evidence and changing expectations for IVUS use. We discuss the indications for IVUS, the current patterns of use in the UK, anticipated future standards and the need for broader adoption. We propose a standardised workflow and recommend routine documentation of a core IVUS dataset to support quality and as a mechanism for improvement. Persistent barriers, such as perceived cost, training variability and procedural integration, are addressed with practical solutions, including the introduction of national training curricula incorporating intravascular imaging accreditation for operators and targeted education for allied health professionals. Our goal is to support clinicians, catheter lab teams and service leads in embedding IVUS more consistently into practice to optimise patient outcomes.

Intravascular Ultrasound Use in All Coronary Interventions in the UK

National Institute for Cardiovascular Outcomes Research data show a yearly increase in intravascular ultrasound use from 13% in 2021–2022 to 29% in 2023–2024, and it is projected to increase in the following years. If used as per guideline recommendation for all complex percutaneous coronary interventions, target use may exceed 80%.

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was first introduced in the late 1980s, but routine adoption has only occurred within the past decade due to improved engineering, robust randomised controlled trial data and rapidly growing clinical experience and expertise. In September 2024, the European Society of Cardiology recognised the clinical benefit of IVUS by upgrading the recommendation for the use of intravascular imaging (IVI) in complex lesions to Class IA.1 In February 2025, this was followed by updates from the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association, who also increased their level of recommendation from IIA to IA for the use of IVI for left main stem and complex lesions in patients with acute coronary syndromes.2 However, despite widespread acknowledgement of the impact of IVI in improving patient outcomes, global use remains variable, and significant barriers continue to affect widespread implementation within large public healthcare systems such as the National Health Service in the UK.

The aim of this consensus statement is to provide an update on current IVUS practice within the UK, suggest guidance on when and how IVUS should be used and recommend strategies to overcome existing barriers to adoption. The document was prepared by a consensus panel comprising 26 interventional cardiologists, representing a broad spectrum of experience and working practice across secondary and tertiary centres throughout the UK, following an in-person meeting on 13 December 2024. The primary focus of this document is on IVUS use. However, it is envisaged that many recommendations may reasonably be extrapolated to other modalities of IVI, such as optical coherence tomography, which have been well studied and validated.

Evidence for Use

There is substantial evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that IVI improves the safety and efficacy of PCI, resulting in more favourable clinical outcomes.3 IVUS is the most extensively studied and widely used modality in the UK and worldwide.4 Among key studies, the ULTIMATE trial provided high-quality data showing that IVUS guidance significantly reduced target vessel failure (6.6% versus 10.7%; p=0.01) at 3 years, primarily by lowering the rate of clinically driven target vessel revascularisation.5 The IVUS-XPL trial, which focused on long coronary lesions requiring extended stent lengths, demonstrated that IVUS guidance was associated with a large reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events at 5 years (5.6% versus 10.7%; HR 0.50; p=0.001), again driven by fewer repeat revascularisations.6 The RENOVATE-COMPLEX-PCI trial, which studied the use of IVI in a broad cohort of patients with complex coronary anatomy, found that the use of IVUS was associated with a reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (7.7% versus 12.3%; HR 0.64; p=0.008) and cardiac death (1.7% versus 3.8%; HR 0.47; p=0.03).7 Many large registry and other RCT studies have demonstrated similar findings, which are further reinforced by several meta-analyses.3,8–10

Barriers and Solutions to the Adoption of Intravascular Imaging

The perceived barriers to more widespread intravascular imaging adoption include perceptions that it is not needed and that it is associated with a financial burden. There is a need for more education and an agreed optimal workflow for use. The UK consensus panel’s suggested solutions are that cost should be viewed over the long term, greater dissemination of the robust evidence and recommendations is needed, a formal educational programme and curricula are required and users should refer to an ‘optimal workflow’. In pooled data from over 15,000 patients across 22 randomised trials, IVUS-guided PCI was associated with reductions in cardiac death (RR 0.55; p=0.0001), target vessel MI (RR 0.82; p=0.03) and target vessel revascularisation (RR 0.72; p=0.0002).3 This impressive risk reduction is likely to be driven largely by improved stent expansion with larger minimum stent areas, procedural outcomes that are consistently associated with improved clinical outcomes. International guidance mirrors these findings, namely that the benefit of IVI is most evident in anatomically complex lesions.

Current Practice

IVUS is the most widely used IVI modality in the UK, but adoption rates remain highly variable across centres and between operators.11 According to the latest data (April 2023–March 2024) from the National Institute for Cardiovascular Outcomes Research, IVI was used in 29% of all PCI procedures in England and Wales, and in just 32% of PCI procedures classified as complex.11 Although the target for target usage may be higher (Figure 1), adoption continues to rise rapidly, more than doubling over a 2-year period. Notably, within the UK consensus panel, the reported median use of intracoronary imaging to guide PCI was significantly higher, between 60% and 90%, suggesting a gap between guideline-aligned best practice and broader national implementation. Although some advocate universal use in all-comers, the panel recognised that this may not always be feasible or clinically appropriate.

When Should Intravascular Ultrasound Be Used?

The updated European Society of Cardiology guidelines for chronic coronary syndromes recommend using IVI when undertaking PCI of the left main stem, a true bifurcation or a long lesion (defined as ≥38 mm based on RENOVATE trial criteria).7 The UK consensus panel agree with these recommendations but suggest they are extended to include other common high-risk lesion subsets: first, to include patients with acute coronary syndromes, who are well represented in the data and constitute a significant proportion of patients, and, second, to expand the definition of ‘complex’ lesions to include chronic total occlusion, ostial lesions, in-stent restenosis, multivessel PCI and severe calcification (Figure 2). As per RCT data to date, and consistent with real-world experience, the potential impact of IVI is likely to be greatest in these additional scenarios.

Although the role for IVI in complex lesions now seems clear, its use in simple lesions remains more contentious. Although very high imaging users support universal use, the UK consensus panel recognised that the absolute risk reduction in this lower-risk cohort may be relatively small and thus difficult to justify within the constraints of a publicly funded healthcare system. However, it was emphasised that decisions should always be individualised.

One area that provoked a lot of debate was whether IVI should be used routinely in patients with ST-elevation MI. Although IVUS may offer the same or greater long-term benefit in these patients compared with other cohorts, the priority remains immediate patient safety, with rapid reperfusion achieved as simply and quickly as possible. Ongoing trials may provide further clarity in this subset.

Overcoming Barriers to Adoption

The UK consensus panel acknowledged multiple barriers that have prevented more widespread adoption of IVI and proposed practical and actionable solutions that are within reach and, in some cases, already exist (Figure 3).

The first and most frequently cited barrier to more widespread adoption of IVI is the persistent belief that IVI is not cost-effective. This is typically seen through the short-term lens of the upfront cost of the catheter itself. However, several longer-term analyses have demonstrated the converse; at a system-wide level, IVI reduces long-term costs by preventing expensive clinical events. In a cost-effectiveness analysis from RENOVATE, cumulative medical costs were significantly lower in the IVI arm (US$40,455 versus US$49,519), with consistently higher quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) observed (8.24 versus 7.89).12 Cost parity was reached at approximately 5 years. Similarly, a 2023 UK-based analysis demonstrated that, over a lifetime horizon for patients with acute coronary syndrome, IVI was associated with incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of £3,649 per QALY gained for ST-elevation MI and £5,706 for unstable angina and non-ST-elevation MI.13 Despite higher upfront costs, IVUS guidance led to substantial reductions in mortality, MI and repeat PCI, and had a 100% likelihood of being cost-effective at a £20,000/QALY threshold, the level at which the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence considers a therapy to be cost-effective.14

The second barrier relates to education and can be considered at multiple levels. For early career interventionists, training as specialist registrars and fellows can be highly variable. The existing UK training curriculum does not reflect the central role that IVI now plays in PCI, and exposure is highly dependent on local practice. The consensus panel proposed a formal update to the Joint Royal College of Physicians Training Board (JRCPTB) training portfolio, with requirements for minimum case volumes including IVUS and optical coherence tomography, and a recommendation for completion of accredited training, either through structured courses, online platforms or national training days.

Importantly, alongside clinicians, allied health professionals in the cardiac catheter lab play a critical role in the delivery of imaging-guided PCI. Targeted education for these team members is also essential to maintain procedural efficiency and ensure optimal workflow. As with operators and trainees, support from professional meetings and industry-sponsored education may be effective here.

Not all existing operators have been convinced by the data supporting the benefits of IVI-guided PCI, and this can also provide headwinds to local uptake. By definition, IVI-guided PCI may involve adapting a longstanding procedural technique, and adapting to this new workflow can sometimes prove challenging. Educational opportunities may include focused breakout sessions at national and international meetings with experienced operators in mind; online modules with accreditation may also provide educational exposure outside of busy catheter labs. Ultimately, given the Class IA recommendation, it is now incumbent on all operators to develop and maintain proficiency in this area.

To support clinicians and allied health professionals in the adoption and promulgation of best practice, the consensus panel believed that a standard optimal workflow for IVUS use would be beneficial. Simply advancing a catheter into the vessel does not translate into improved outcomes.5 Benefits are realised when imaging is integrated meaningfully: knowing when to use it, what to assess and how to define an acceptable result. Modelling the widely adopted algorithms used for optical coherence tomography (a ‘before, during and after’ stenting approach to guide optimal IVUS use) will provide a memorable and comprehensive framework that suits the majority of cases (Figure 4). After confirming an optimal result, we recommend a minimum dataset to record these objective measurements. These data should then be systematically returned not only as metrics of quality but also as a mechanism for quality improvement.

Conclusion

IVUS has a well-established role in improving outcomes in PCI, particularly for complex lesions. Strong evidence now supports its use, and recent guideline updates reflect this. However, national adoption in the UK remains inconsistent, and many patients who could benefit are not currently receiving image-guided PCI.

To change this, the practical barriers that remain must be addressed. Misconceptions around cost must be challenged with clear evidence. Training pathways for consultants, trainees and allied health professionals must be modernised. The workflow for IVUS use must be simplified and standardised to make the use of high-quality imaging feasible and routine across all catheter labs.

This consensus document provides a foundation for change, but implementation will depend on collective effort. The UK consensus panel calls on national training bodies, catheter lab leaders, commissioners and industry partners to work together to ensure IVI is not only available, but also used routinely and effectively to result in a meaningful improvement in patient care.
