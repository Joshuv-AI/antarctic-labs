Histology-derived prediction of a stromal remodeling program in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma with transcriptomic and spatial validation

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Histology predicts a stromal stiffness program in PDAC.•Foundation models capture ECM remodeling from routine H&E slides.•External validation confirms transcriptomic prediction in CPTAC PDAC.•Spatial transcriptomics links attention to COL12A1/LOX/LOXL2 signals.•The model identifies a histology-derived stromal biomarker candidate.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal solid malignancies, with only modest improvement in long-term survival over recent decades [1] For patients with advanced disease, FOLFIRINOX or modified FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel are widely used first-line cytotoxic regimens [2,3]. In clinical practice, treatment selection is largely influenced by performance status, toxicity profiles, comorbidities, and patient fitness, while population-level comparisons do not fully capture the biological heterogeneity of individual tumors [4,5]. Among tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental factors, the desmoplastic stroma of PDAC may contribute to therapeutic heterogeneity by shaping extracellular matrix organization, tissue stiffness, vascular compression, and drug delivery. However, stromal biology is not routinely quantified from diagnostic specimens, and histology-derived biomarkers that link routine tissue morphology to molecular features of the tumor microenvironment remain insufficiently developed [5,6].

The pro-fibrotic stroma of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a biologically reasonable but yet unutilized source of treatment heterogeneity. This dense stromal environment is characterized by increased interstitial pressure, compression of blood vessels, and impaired drug diffusion within the tumor, all of which are closely related to chemotherapy resistance(1, 6). The pancreatic stroma is not a passive scaffold but actively regulates tumor progression and drug delivery through collagen deposition, matrix cross-linking, and mechanical transduction [6]. Three genes occupy the core position in this stromal axis. Members of the LOX family catalyze collagen cross-linking, causing tissue hardening and hindering drug penetration [7]. Pancreatic stellate cells expressing LOXL2 promote the ordered extracellular matrix structure associated with aggressive diseases [8]. COL12A1 has become a cancer-associated fibroblast marker associated with poor interstitial remodeling in PDAC [9]. In summary, these genes define a coherent extracellular matrix sclerosis axis. Recent observations suggest that it may have therapeutic significance beyond prognosis. A 2025 imaging study showed that quantitatively assessed tumor fibrosis predicted response to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel [10], while prior work demonstrated that AG treatment itself can deplete cancer-associated fibroblasts and remodel the stroma [11]. The desmoplastic microenvironment may therefore provide a biologically motivated context for future studies of therapeutic relevance, rather than an established treatment-selection marker.

Advances in computational pathology make it timely to ask whether such a stromal program can be inferred from routine H&E slides. Pathology foundation models, pretrained on large histopathological image collections, have markedly improved the prediction of molecular and clinical phenotypes from tissue morphology [12,13]. Spatial transcriptomic platforms, in parallel, now permit independent verification of histology-derived predictions against measured gene expression at high spatial resolution [14,15]. Two questions, however, remain unresolved: whether a clinically meaningful stromal stiffness program in PDAC can be recovered from H&E morphology alone, and whether such a program exhibits spatial coherence when tested against an independent transcriptomic assay.

Here, we present StRoMA (Stiffness Regression from Morphological Attention), an encoder-decoder transformer framework designed to predict a composite Z-score, derived from COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2 expression, from H&E whole-slide images of the TCGA pancreatic cancer cohort. Three encoder families were compared: UNI, a large self-supervised pathology foundation model [12]; CONCH, a vision-language pathology foundation model [13]; and ResNet50, a generic ImageNet-pretrained convolutional baseline [16]. The best-performing models were subsequently validated on an independent CPTAC cohort. Spatial plausibility of the learned signal was tested against measured transcriptomic programs on a separate Visium HD 16 μm pancreatic cancer section. Of note, gene selection was not data-driven in isolation; it was grounded in the intersection of two studies linking stromal biology to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel responsiveness [10,17], followed by Cox-LASSO regularization and predefined median-split Kaplan-Meier survival screening in TCGA. Our central hypothesis was that this compact molecular axis could serve as an interpretable, histologically accessible surrogate of desmoplastic ECM remodeling suitable for cross-cohort and spatial validation.

Methods

Study design, cohorts and molecular target construction

A retrospective computational pathology design was employed with three validation tiers: internal cross-validation on TCGA, external validation on CPTAC, and spatial transcriptomic validation on an independent Visium HD section. The discovery cohort comprised 206 H&E-stained whole-slide images from 178 TCGA pancreatic cancer cases with matched transcriptomic data. The external validation cohort comprised 492 H&E slides from 153 CPTAC pancreatic cancer cases. For both cohorts, a continuous composite score was computed from COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2 by log-transforming expression values (log₂ (x + 1)), standardizing each gene across the cohort (Z-score normalization), and averaging the three gene-wise Z-scores. Binary high- and low-expression labels were computed for descriptive stratification and post hoc AUROC analyses but were not used during model training (Supplementary Table S7).

Derivation of the three gene stromal stiffness target

Target gene selection followed a structured, literature anchored workflow rather than arbitrary manual choice. We first compiled the overlap between genes identified in two independent PDAC studies linking stromal biology to gemcitabine plus nab paclitaxel responsiveness: a stromal response signature from patient derived xenograft models [17] and a fibrosis associated signature from quantitative CT imaging [10]. The intersection yielded 33 candidate genes. Using the TCGA pancreatic cancer cohort, we fit a penalized Cox proportional hazards model with LASSO regularization (glmnet, alpha = 1) [18]. Genes with nonzero coefficients at the cross validated optimal shrinkage parameter (lambda.min) were retained, yielding nine intermediate candidates (Supplementary Table S1). Each was then subjected to Kaplan Meier analysis using a predefined median split threshold. Final selection required simultaneous satisfaction of three criteria: literature overlap membership, nonzero Cox LASSO coefficient, and biologically coherent survival separation under the fixed median split. COL12A1, LOX and LOXL2 met all requirements (Supplementary Fig. S1). Biologically, this three-gene score was interpreted as an ECM-remodeling and stromal-stiffness-related molecular surrogate rather than a direct physical measurement of tissue stiffness. It was intended to capture a focused matrix remodeling axis involving fibroblast-associated collagen organization and collagen crosslinking, rather than global fibroblast abundance or a broad stromal phenotype.

Data preprocessing and cohort manifest generation

To establish the case-level training and external validation targets, we processed the RNA expression matrices from the TCGA and CPTAC datasets. Expression values of COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2 were log2(x + 1)-transformed and standardized within each cohort, and the arithmetic mean of the three gene-wise Z-scores was used as the composite stromal remodeling score. When multiple transcriptomic samples were available for the same patient, their composite scores were averaged to generate a single case-level molecular target. This case-level score and the corresponding median-split high/low label were then assigned to all matched WSIs from the same patient. During external validation, predictions were first generated for each slide by averaging predictions from the 10 fold-specific models, and patient-level predictions were obtained by averaging slide-level predictions across all WSIs belonging to the same case.

Whole slide image preprocessing and feature extraction

Tissue masks were generated from a low-resolution slide view, using OpenSlide level 2 when available or the highest available level otherwise. The RGB thumbnail was converted to grayscale, and candidate tissue regions were defined by intensity thresholding (15 < gray < 240), followed by 7 × 7 elliptical morphological closing. External contours with an area ≤ 2,000 pixels in mask space were discarded. Candidate tile coordinates were then projected back to the native level-0 coordinate system and retained when the corresponding mask region showed >50% tissue coverage (mean mask intensity >128). For each retained coordinate, a 512 × 512 RGB tile was read directly from OpenSlide level 0 with a nominal step size of 512 pixels. No microns-per-pixel resampling was applied before tile extraction; therefore, tile size was defined on the native level-0 pixel grid of each WSI. Tiles were Macenko-normalized [19] when tile-level intensity variation was sufficient. Encoder-specific transforms resized and center-cropped tiles to 224 × 224 pixels for UNI and ResNet50 or 448 × 448 pixels for CONCH, followed by ImageNet normalization.

Three pretrained feature extractors were evaluated to compare domain-specific versus general-purpose visual representations. ResNet50, a convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet-1K, served as the generic computer vision baseline; each tile was encoded into a 1,024-dimensional feature vector [16]. CONCH, a vision-language foundation model trained via contrastive learning on a large-scale pathology image-text corpus, produced 512-dimensional feature vectors per tile [13]. UNI, a self-supervised vision transformer (ViT-Large/16) pretrained on over 100,000 diagnostic histopathology slides across 20 tissue types, generated 1,536-dimensional feature vectors per tile [12]. Tile-level features and their spatial coordinates (x, y centroid positions) were stored in HDF5 format for downstream modeling.

StRoMA: encoder-decoder transformer architecture for continuous regression

Departing from conventional binary classification paradigms, StRoMA frames the prediction of target Z_score as a continuous regression task. Our prediction target is defined as a composite score derived from three specific genes. To compute this, cohort-wide transcripts per million (TPM) values for each gene were first log₂ (x + 1)-transformed and Z-score standardized. The arithmetic mean of these three gene-wise Z-scores subsequently served as the final composite target. By omitting discrete class labels during training, this design fully preserves the quantitative spectrum of the expression data, effectively mitigating the information loss inherent to arbitrary binarization thresholds.

At its core, StRoMA utilizes an encoder-decoder Transformer architecture designed to synthesize variable-length tile feature sequences into a slide-level scalar while actively exploiting inter-tile spatial context. Initially, raw embeddings extracted from the tiles (dimension 512 for CONCH, 1,024 for ResNet50, and 1,536 for UNI) undergo transformation via a linear layer equipped with ReLU activation and dropout. This maps them into a unified latent space (dmodel = 64). Beyond alleviating GPU memory constraints, this stringent dimensionality reduction functions as an information bottleneck, providing crucial regularization when training on a limited patient cohort. To retain spatial awareness in subsequent layers, two-dimensional coordinate data (x, y) are embedded using sinusoidal positional encodings and fused with the projected tile tokens. This sequence is then fed into a single-layer, 8-head Transformer encoder (feedforward dimension = 512, Pre-LayerNorm), enabling global self-attention across all sampled tiles of a given WSI. Subsequently, a learnable class token (CLS) queries these encoded representations through a single-layer, 8-head Transformer decoder. This cross-attention mechanism produces an attention-weighted, slide-level summary vector of dimension dmodel, which finally passes through a dropout layer and a terminal linear projection (dmodel → 1) to yield the continuous prediction of the standardized composite expression score.

Dynamic tile sampling strategy

Given the extensive tile sequences inherent to high-resolution WSIs, maintaining GPU memory tractability necessitated a capped budget of 1,024 tiles per slide, operationalized through two distinct sampling modes. During training with the UNI and CONCH encoders, a stochastic subsampling approach was adopted, wherein 1,024 tiles were uniformly drawn without replacement from each WSI per epoch, with their indices sorted to maintain spatial order. This strategy intrinsically acts as a spatial augmentation technique, dynamically exposing the network to varying tissue subregions across epochs. Conversely, during validation and testing—or throughout the entire training and inference lifecycle for the ResNet50 configuration—an equidistant sampling strategy was applied. Loss function and optimization strategy

Model training was driven by a composite loss formulation that integrates both pointwise and pairwise objectives: ℒ = ℒ_SmoothL1 + λ · ℒ_Ranking. The first term represents the Huber loss (δ = 1.0), which effectively penalizes absolute prediction errors while resisting the outsized influence of outliers. The second term is a pairwise margin ranking loss (margin m = 0.1), designed to strictly enforce correct ordinal relationships between any pair of samples whose ground-truth expression values differ by a margin greater than 0.05. The balancing coefficient λ was fixed at 0.5 across all experimental setups. For network optimization, parameters were updated using the AdamW optimizer paired with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule for a maximum of 200 epochs. To further combat overfitting, a comprehensive suite of regularization techniques was deployed, including feature dropout, Manifold Mixup utilizing Beta-distributed feature-level interpolation, and weight decay. Gradient accumulation was also employed to standardize the effective batch size to 8 across all configurations, despite differing physical batch limits. Base learning rates, regularization intensities, and early-stopping patience varied strictly by the chosen feature encoder to maximize their respective representations (e.g., a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻5 and dropout of 0.5 for UNI/CONCH, versus 2 × 10⁻⁴ and 0.25 for ResNet50). Exhaustive, encoder-specific hyperparameter configurations are detailed in Table 1.

Cross validation procedure. Cross validation was performed at the case level using StratifiedKFold on the median binarized composite score, ensuring that all slides from the same case remained in the same fold. Stratification was based on the median of the global composite expression score to ensure balanced target distributions across folds. Within each fold, the composite score was restandardized (Z_score) using the training partition mean and standard deviation only, ensuring that validation set distributional information did not inform model training. Internal performance was summarized as mean ± standard deviation across the 10 folds. External validation used the ensemble average of the 10 fold specific models on CPTAC.

Evaluation metrics

We conducted a comprehensive assessment of the model's performance using a set of complementary statistical indicators. As the primary evaluation criterion, we used Spearman rank correlation (ρ) to evaluate the monotonic consistency between the predicted values and the actual expression values. Since this indicator is based on rankings, it can clearly quantify the model's ability to correctly rank patients according to their relative expression levels, which is a key attribute for clinical risk stratification and is robust to non-linear transformations of the prediction scale. To complement this ranking indicator, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was calculated to capture the average absolute deviation along the standardized expression scale. Additionally, to evaluate the broader utility of the model in patient stratification, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for the expression threshold Z > 0. In this validation step, we binarized the true continuous Z scores with a zero mean to construct pseudo-labels for evaluation purposes, while directly using the original continuous model predictions as decision variables. The inclusion of this analysis highlights a key advantage of our design: the network specifically optimized for continuous regression goals can itself generate efficient binary discrimination capabilities, thereby successfully avoiding the information loss inherent in explicit classification. On the external cohort, Pearson correlation and AUROC [Label] were also reported. AUROC [Label] used the predefined case-level high/low labels that had been assigned before fold-specific restandardization of the continuous target.

Slide level attention heatmap generation

For qualitative interpretability, decoder cross attention weights were extracted for every tile from the trained models, averaged across the 10 folds, and projected back onto the corresponding H&E whole slide image using a continuous colormap overlay. This procedure was applied to generate slide level attention heatmaps as interpretability evidence.

Visium HD spatial validation

Spatial validation used a pancreatic cancer Visium HD 16 μm dataset. The H&E image corresponding to the Visium HD section was tiled on the same 512 × 512 grid used for model inference. Bin level transcriptomic program scores were computed directly from measured Visium HD expression, not from inferred cell type proportions. The core three gene score was defined as the mean Z scored expression of COL12A1, LOX and LOXL2 on each bin. To test whether broader stromal state programs performed better, we also computed myCAF and iCAF program scores directly from the same Visium HD bins, following established subtype definitions [20,21]. Visium HD bins were mapped to tiles using their full-resolution pixel coordinates; for each tile, all in-tissue 16 μm bins falling within the tile boundary [x, x + 512) and [y, y + 512) were aggregated by their mean program score. Tiles without valid in-tissue bins or finite program scores were excluded. For each encoder, attention weights were averaged across the 10 trained models and compared against tile level transcriptomic targets using Spearman correlation, IoU, Dice coefficient, Moran’s I, and permutation-based significance testing (499 random permutations with +1 correction). Spearman correlation was used as the primary concordance metric because spatial transcriptomic targets are zero inflated and can contain heavy tailed outliers [14,15].

Tangram based fibroblast sensitivity analysis

To evaluate whether the spatial signal could be explained by generic fibroblast enrichment, we transferred fibroblast labels from a pancreatic cancer single cell reference to Visium HD using Tangram [22]. The reference was assembled from two public PDAC scRNA seq GEO datasets, GSE205013 [23] and GSE154778 [24]. Raw 10x Genomics matrices were processed sample wise with quality control filtering for low complexity and high mitochondrial cells, doublet removal, integration in a shared latent space using Scanpy and Harmony [25,26], and manual annotation into broad pancreatic cancer cell classes. The final annotated reference contained 224,982 cells across 10 cell classes, including 17,468 fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Because Tangram outputs probabilistic cell identity maps rather than absolute abundance estimates [22,27], we evaluated both fibroblast probability and fibroblast fraction as auxiliary spatial targets in the comparison against model attention.

Statistical analysis

Internal and external prediction performance was evaluated using Spearman correlation, Pearson correlation, AUROC and MAE. Spatial concordance used Spearman ρ as the primary metric, supplemented by top percentile IoU, Dice overlap and Moran’s I for spatial autocorrelation. For spatial Spearman significance testing, empirical two-sided P values were obtained by randomly permuting the tile-level attention scores relative to the transcriptomic target scores while preserving the marginal distributions. Empirical P values were computed with a standard +1 permutation correction as Pemp = (b + 1) / (B + 1), where B = 499 denotes the number of random permutations and b denotes the number of permuted absolute Spearman correlations greater than or equal to the observed absolute correlation. This correction yields a minimum attainable empirical P value of 0.002. Moran's I significance was computed using the same +1 permutation correction with inverse-distance weights among the eight nearest neighboring tiles. All statistical tests were two sided unless otherwise stated.

Results

Clinically anchored derivation of the core three gene stromal stiffness target

Target gene derivation was anchored to two recent PDAC studies that directly linked stromal and fibrotic biology to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel responsiveness, instead of being chosen on purely computational grounds. The first was a 294-gene stromal response signature from patient-derived xenograft models [17]; the second, a tumor fibrosis-associated signature derived from quantitative CT imaging [10]. Intersection of these two candidate sets yielded 33 overlapping genes with converging evidence for stromal relevance in PDAC (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

A penalized Cox proportional-hazards model with LASSO regularization was then fitted in the TCGA pancreatic cancer cohort [18]. Nine genes retained nonzero coefficients at the optimal shrinkage parameter (lambda.min): CLIP3, COL12A1, ENAH, GPRC5C, INHBA, LOX, LOXL2, RRAD, and SCARF2 (Supplementary Table S1). To narrow this intermediate set toward a compact, mechanistically coherent target, each retained gene was subjected to Kaplan–Meier analysis using a predefined median split rather than adaptive cut-point optimization. Three genes (COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2) satisfied all prioritization criteria simultaneously: literature-overlap membership, a nonzero Cox–LASSO coefficient, and significant survival separation under the fixed median threshold (Supplementary Fig. S1B–F). These three genes also share direct mechanistic connections to extracellular matrix organization and collagen crosslinking, distinguishing them from the remaining six candidates. The final molecular target was therefore defined as the mean Z-score of COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2.

Cohort characteristics and transcriptomic target derivation

Bulk transcriptomic profiles were aggregated on the basis of mean z-scored expression of COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2 to establish transcript-derived supervision targets. Before imaging integration, 179 molecular profiles from 178 TCGA cases and 287 profiles from 168 CPTAC cases were available. Stratification at a case-level median threshold produced balanced cohorts: 89 high-expression and 89 low-expression cases in TCGA; 84 high-expression and 84 low-expression cases in CPTAC (Supplementary Table S2). Sample-level composite and individual gene-wise z-score distributions confirmed a robust, integrated contribution from all three genes to the final target signature (Supplementary Fig. S2). Upon mapping these case-level labels to the whole-slide image (WSI) repositories, the finalized TCGA dataset comprised 206 slides (178 cases; mean 1.16 slides per case), while the CPTAC dataset encompassed 492 slides (Supplementary Table S3). All transcript-profiled TCGA cases mapped to at least one WSI. By contrast, 15 CPTAC cases lacked corresponding imaging data and were excluded from downstream modeling.

External validation on the CPTAC cohort reveals stronger cross cohort transfer for CONCH

On the independent CPTAC cohort (492 slides, 153 cases), the ranking between UNI and CONCH reversed (Table 3; Fig. 3). CONCH emerged as the top-performing encoder for external generalization, achieving a Spearman ρ of 0.458 (P = 2.72 × 10⁻⁹), Pearson r of 0.589 (P = 1.15 × 10⁻¹⁵), AUROC [Z > 0] of 0.727, AUROC [Label] of 0.744, and MAE of 0.581. The monotonic consistency of UNI is slightly lower (ρ = 0.433), and the linear correlation is also significantly weaker (r = 0.453). However, its AUROC value based on labels is slightly superior (0.761 compared to 0.744). This kind of internal-to-external reversal is quite enlightening. There are differences between CPTAC and TCGA in sample processing, staining protocols, and scanner hardware; the quantification of performance decline between the two groups of people quantifies the impact of these domain transitions. That CONCH maintained the strongest monotonic and linear agreement under these conditions is consistent with the hypothesis that vision-language pretraining captures more generalizable histomorphological features [13]. For translational purposes, external performance should take precedence over internal rankings. By that criterion, CONCH represents the most defensible final model.

Spatial validation of CONCH and UNI StRoMA attention against the core three gene Visium HD program.

The strongest concordance was observed between CONCH attention and the core three-gene score (Spearman ρ = 0.211, empirical permutation P = 0.002, Dice = 0.240, Moran's I = 0.185; 1,795 valid tiles; Fig. 4A–H). A directionally consistent but weaker association was found for UNI (ρ = 0.110, Dice = 0.259, Moran's I = 0.340; Fig. 4I–P). ResNet50 showed no meaningful positive concordance (ρ = −0.022, P = 0.349). Thus, the relevant interpretation is not based solely on the absolute magnitude of ρ, but also on the fact that the compact ECM-remodeling axis showed stronger spatial concordance than broader CAF-state programs measured in the same Visium HD section.

A central question was whether broader fibroblast-state programs would outperform the compact target-defining axis. They did not. For CONCH, the myCAF program score reached only ρ = 0.078 and the iCAF score ρ = 0.151, both considerably weaker than the core three-gene result (ρ = 0.211). The same pattern held for UNI: core three-gene ρ = 0.110, myCAF ρ = 0.097, iCAF ρ = 0.102 (Fig. 4Q–R; Table 4). The myCAF program comprised COL12A1, LOX, LOXL2, POSTN, ACTA2, TAGLN, CTGF, THY1, COL1A1, and COL1A2; the iCAF program comprised CXCL12, IL6, LIF, CFD, DPT, C7, and CXCL14, following established cancer-associated fibroblast subtype definitions [20,21]. That the narrow three-gene axis outperformed these broader programs indicates the model was not simply aligned to a generic fibroblast-state signature but instead captured a more specific extracellular matrix-associated transcriptional axis.

Tangram fibroblast mapping does not reproduce the positive core program spatial signal

To test whether simple fibroblast enrichment could account for the positive Visium HD result, Tangram-based transfer of fibroblast annotations was performed using a combined PDAC single-cell reference. This reference comprised 224,982 annotated cells, including 17,468 fibroblasts, assembled from two public scRNA-seq datasets (GSE205013 and GSE154778; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5) [23,24].

The positive signal observed for the core three-gene targets was not reproduced (Supplementary Fig. S6; Supplementary Table S6). CONCH attention was negatively correlated with Tangram-derived fibroblast probability (ρ = −0.116) and more strongly so with fibroblast fraction (ρ = −0.338). UNI showed only marginal positive correlation with fibroblast probability (ρ = 0.050) and a slightly negative correlation with fibroblast fraction (ρ = −0.045). These patterns are contrary to what would be expected if the model simply identified fibroblast-rich tissue regions.

The most parsimonious interpretation is that the model recognizes a morphologically distinctive stromal state overlapping only partially with fibroblast density. This finding accords with the recognized heterogeneity among cancer-associated fibroblast populations in PDAC, where myCAF, iCAF, and other functional subtypes occupy overlapping but distinct spatial domains [20,21,28].

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the conventional H&E histological encoding of pancreatic cancer reflects the morphological correlation of the specific extracellular matrix remodeling program defined by COL12A1, LOX, and LOXL2. We employed a multi-instance learning (MIL) framework based on a lightweight Transformer and optimized it using a mixed regression-sorting loss to aggregate patch-level features from whole-slide images, thereby achieving robust predictions of the comprehensive scores of the above three genes. This conclusion is based on three complementary pieces of evidence: in both internal and external cohorts, the prediction results of the models trained on feature extractors specifically designed for pathology are stable and reliable at the patient level; the model's attention is consistent in space with the transcriptome scores of the three genes in independent Visium HD data; and the mapped fibroblast abundance maps fail to explain the same spatial patterns.

Compared with many organizations' predictive studies in transcriptomics, a notable feature of this work is that the molecular targets were clinically anchored before model training. The selection of genes was not based on their ease of prediction or their correlation with morphology in the initial screening. The starting point of the study was the overlapping part of two independent studies, both of which linked the efficacy of gemcitabine combined with albumin paclitaxel in PDAC stroma [10,17]; subsequently, the candidate gene set was narrowed down using a regularization method based on survival analysis from the TCGA database. This step ensured that the image-derived signals had a priori connection with the stromal remodeling question addressed in this study, without the need for post hoc rationalization. Therefore, the model was trained based on a compact matrix hardness axis that has established connections with treatment biology, rather than based on the general transcriptional average.

The reversal of internal and external rankings between UNI and CONCH is consistent with the broader experience in computational pathology. UNI is a self-supervised visual Transformer model trained on a large histopathology corpus [12], which performs best in intra-group fitting. While CONCH achieves visual-language alignment through image-text contrast pre-training [13], thereby obtaining superior external generalization ability. A reasonable explanation is that the text-based representation encodes more semantically rich tissue descriptors, which are less susceptible to specific visual interference by institutions, although this remains speculative. Similar patterns have also been reported in recent multimodal pathological modeling studies, and there is an increasing consensus that the pre-training objective and data composition jointly determine out-of-distribution robustness [13,29]. From the perspective of translational applications, the external performance series should have a higher weight than the internal ranking, so the model trained based on CONCH becomes the most reliable final model.

The best Visium HD result (ρ = 0.211 for CONCH versus the core three-gene score) is modest but biologically meaningful. Such a value is not unusually low for histology-to-spatial-transcriptomics validation. A recent benchmark in Nature Communications reported that even the best-performing method reached an average Pearson correlation of only 0.28, with overall correlation and structural similarity metrics remaining limited across all evaluated approaches [14]. SEQUOIA reported median spatial Pearson values of 0.255 for top-performing genes and 0.168 when averaged more broadly [15]. In a related attention-based validation design, Ng and colleagues observed spatial Spearman correlations of 0.31 and 0.24 for the best-correlated immune signature [30]. A reproducible correlation of approximately 0.21, obtained from an independently trained morphology model against a compact stromal program, falls squarely within the range considered publishable and biologically informative by the field [14,15,30].

The compact COL12A1/LOX/LOXL2 axis also outperformed both myCAF and iCAF expression programs in the spatial analysis, a finding with direct biological implications. This observation refutes the view that the model merely identifies tissue regions rich in fibroblasts without considering their functional status. The fibroblast clusters in PDAC exhibit heterogeneity, plasticity, and spatial stratification. Although the distinction between myCAF and iCAF is of reference value, it only captures a part of this complexity [20,21,28]. The focused matrix stiffness axis provides stronger spatial consistency than these broader annotations or mappings, indicating that the model detects a specific ECM-remodeling-associated stromal phenotype rather than reflecting overall fibroblast abundance. This comparison provides an important context for interpreting the modest spatial correlation. The value of ρ = 0.211 should not be viewed in isolation, because cross-modal comparisons between histologic attention and spatial transcriptomic expression are expected to be noisy. More importantly, the core COL12A1/LOX/LOXL2 program showed stronger concordance with model attention than broader myCAF or iCAF programs measured in the same Visium HD section. This supports the interpretation that StRoMA captures a focused ECM-remodeling-associated stromal state rather than simply quantifying generic CAF enrichment or bulk fibroblast density.

The biological characteristics of these three genes in historical research have strengthened this specificity. COL12A1 is associated with activated fibroblasts and poor matrix structure in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [9]. The LOX family enzymes catalyze collagen cross-linking, making the tissue harder and limiting the diffusion of drugs within the tumor, thereby promoting chemotherapy resistance [7]. LOXL2 further expands this axis: pancreatic stellate cells expressing LOXL2 have been proven to be able to drive the formation of ordered extracellular matrix tissue, and this tissue is associated with more aggressive disease behavior [8]. Current reviews on treatment resistance in PDAC unanimously state that extracellular matrix remodeling, tissue hardening, and mechanical transduction are the core mediators of treatment failure [6]. These consistent research results support the view that the predictive axis formed by these three genes is not a set of general matrix markers, but rather a mechanistic alternative indicator for promoting fibroproliferative matrix sclerosis. This kind of signal may leave features on conventional pathological sections that can be recognized by the basic pathological large model, and can support future studies evaluating the therapeutic relevance of stromal remodeling in PDAC.

Tangram is a mature framework used to convert single-cell annotations into spatial transcriptomic data [22,27]. However, its output is essentially a probability mapping score rather than a direct measurement of cell absolute abundance. In this study, the fibroblast atlas derived from Tangram failed to reproduce the positive spatial correlation observed in the core three-gene program. This result not only did not weaken the central conclusion but rather strengthened it: The model we constructed does not simply highlight the dense areas of fibroblasts, but identifies a more specific matrix state, which is represented by the expression of the derived COL12A1/LOX/LOXL2 axis as being more accurate than by the transformed fibroblast probabilities.

The practical significance of this research goes far beyond the current findings. Fibrosis-related biology is increasingly connected to differential treatment outcomes in PDAC. Yang and colleagues showed that CT-quantified tumor fibrosis predicted response to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel [10]; Miyashita and colleagues demonstrated that AG treatment can deplete cancer-associated fibroblasts and remodel the pancreatic stroma [11]. Biomarker-guided differentiation between FOLFIRINOX and AG has been identified as both clinically urgent and largely unresolved [5], while recent comparative-effectiveness studies continue to show that population-level regimen superiority does not address the subgroup question that ultimately matters for individual patients [4]. In this context, an H&E-based stromal stiffness assessment could offer real value. Such a tool would be scalable, inexpensive, and universally available, serving as a candidate biomarker for identifying patients whose tumors exhibit a severely desmoplastic state and who may therefore benefit from distinct therapeutic considerations or intensified stromal co-targeting strategies.

The translational implications of StRoMA should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than immediately practice-changing. Our findings indicate that routine H&E morphology contains information linked to an ECM-remodeling and stromal-stiffness-related molecular program, and that this signal can be externally validated and spatially corroborated. Because desmoplastic stroma has been implicated in drug delivery, tissue mechanics, and therapeutic resistance in PDAC[6,7], and because recent studies have linked stromal or fibrosis-related features to chemotherapy response in PDAC models or imaging cohorts[10,17], this image-derived phenotype may provide a scalable framework for future studies integrating computational pathology, stromal biology, and annotated treatment outcomes. However, the present analysis does not test differential benefit from FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, or any other chemotherapy regimen[[2], [3], [4], [5]], and should not be interpreted as evidence for chemotherapy selection.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cohorts used in this study were retrospective and were not designed to evaluate treatment-by-biomarker interactions. Therefore, StRoMA is not a proven predictive biomarker for chemotherapy response, because predictive biomarker claims require evidence that treatment effects differ according to biomarker status, ideally through treatment-by-biomarker interaction analyses or appropriately designed comparative treatment cohorts. We do not claim that high or low image-derived stromal scores identify patients who preferentially benefit from any specific regimen. Establishing such utility will require independent cohorts with annotated treatment regimens, response outcomes, survival data, and formal interaction testing. Second, Visium HD spatial validation was performed on a single tissue section because publicly available PDAC datasets with paired high-resolution H&E images, bulk transcriptomics, and spatial transcriptomics remain scarce. Although the spatial association was statistically significant by permutation testing, replication in additional spatial cohorts will be needed to determine generalizability. Third, Tangram-based fibroblast mapping was used as a sensitivity analysis, but it relies on probabilistic cell-type transfer from a reference atlas rather than direct measurement of absolute cell abundance. Finally, whole-slide attention heatmaps provide qualitative interpretability and should be complemented in future studies by pathologist annotations or regional-level histologic phenotyping.

In conclusion, StRoMA demonstrates that routine H&E whole-slide images can be used to infer a COL12A1/LOX/LOXL2-centered stromal remodeling program in PDAC. The model showed consistent patient-level prediction in internal and external cohorts, and its attention patterns were spatially concordant with the corresponding transcriptomic signal in Visium HD data. These findings support a histology-derived stromal remodeling phenotype as a candidate biomarker for future translational studies of PDAC biology and therapeutic relevance, rather than as a validated tool for chemotherapy selection.
