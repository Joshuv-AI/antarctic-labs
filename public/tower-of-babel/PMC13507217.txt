Analysis of the observed and projected changes in rainfall and temperature under climate change scenarios in Bale Mountains National Park, South Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Firdissa Sadeta Tiye, Diriba Korecha, Tariku Mekonnen Gutema, Dessalegn Obsi Gemeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined historical trends and variability, as well as future projections of rainfall and temperature under different emission scenarios in Bale Mountains National Park (BMNP), southeastern Ethiopia. For the historical analysis, thirty years (1994–2023) of daily rainfall and temperature data from eight meteorological stations within and surrounding the park were obtained from the National Meteorology Institute of Ethiopia. Future climate conditions were projected using an ensemble of eight Global Climate Models (ACCESS-CM2, CMCC-ESM2, CNRM-CM6-1, INM-CM4-8, MIROC-ES2L, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, NorESM2-MM, and MRI-ESM2-0) from CMIP6 under three emission scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5), covering the near future (2021–2040), mid-century (2041–2070), and end of the century (2071–2099). Statistical methods, including descriptive statistics, spatial interpolation, the Mann–Kendall trend test, and Sen’s slope estimator, were used to evaluate spatiotemporal patterns of historical climate variables. In addition, decadal deviations from long-term means were analyzed using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) technique in ArcGIS 10.8 to identify climate shifts. The Delta method was applied to downscale Global Climate Model outputs for improved regional relevance. Results revealed strong spatial variability in rainfall influenced by elevation and orographic effects, with higher precipitation observed in elevated areas. Despite noticeable year-to-year fluctuations, most stations showed declining rainfall trends, particularly after 2000, indicating a shift toward drier conditions. Over the last three decades (1994–2023), the annual mean rainfall decreased by about 100 mm, while the maximum and minimum temperatures increased by approximately 1.6 °C and 1.7 °C, respectively, between the first decade (1994–2003) and the last decade (2014–2023). Future projections indicate increases in both rainfall and temperature relative to the 1985–2014 baseline period. The most pronounced changes are expected under the high-emission scenario (SSP5-8.5) by 2071–2099, with rainfall increasing by about 265 mm and temperatures rising by up to 3.9 °C. These findings highlight the urgency of sustained climate monitoring and adaptive conservation strategies.Keywords: Bale mountains, Climate change, Rainfall variability, Scenarios, TrendsSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Climate change is one of the most critical environmental challenges of the twenty-first century, with significant impacts on ecosystems, hydrological systems, and human livelihoods worldwide. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), global mean surface temperature has already increased by about 1.1 °C above pre-industrial levels, with further warming projected under all emission scenarios1. This warming has intensified changes in precipitation patterns, increased the frequency of extreme events, and heightened climate-related hazards such as droughts, floods, and heatwaves2. These impacts threaten ecological stability, agricultural productivity, water resources, and biodiversity3.

Africa is among the most vulnerable regions to climate change due to its reliance on rain-fed agriculture, limited adaptive capacity, and strong climate–livelihood linkages. Recent studies show consistent warming across East and Southern Africa, alongside high rainfall variability, increasing unpredictability, and recurrent droughts3,4. Future projections indicate that Africa will continue to warm faster than the global average, with uncertain but potentially significant shifts in seasonal rainfall5.

In the Horn of Africa, including Ethiopia, climate variability is evident through shifts in rainfall patterns, changes in the timing of rainy seasons, and rising temperatures. Studies report warming rates of 0.2–0.4 °C per decade in Ethiopia’s highlands over the past 50 years6,7. Rainfall remains highly variable due to complex topography, with increasing drought frequency and erratic distribution8,9. These trends pose serious risks to hydrology, biodiversity, agriculture, and the national economy.

High mountain ecosystems in Ethiopia are particularly sensitive to climate change. These regions serve as biodiversity hotspots and vital water sources but are increasingly affected by warming temperatures, vegetation shifts, and habitat loss10,11. The Bale Mountains, located in southeastern Ethiopia, represent a key Afro-alpine ecosystem with exceptional biodiversity, including endemic species such as the Ethiopian wolf (Canis simensis) and giant mole-rat (Tachyoryctes macrocephalus). The Bale Mountains National Park (BMNP) also functions as a major water catchment for rivers such as the Web, Wabi-Shebelle, and Welmel.

Despite its importance, BMNP is increasingly threatened by climate change, including rising temperatures, altered precipitation regimes, and growing climate variability. However, localized, high-resolution assessments of climate trends within the park remain limited. The complex topography of the Bale massif complicates the application of regional climate patterns, and few studies integrate long-term observations with downscaled future projections under SSP scenarios, leaving significant knowledge gaps.

Understanding both historical climate variability and future projections is essential for effective conservation, land management, and climate-resilient development in BMNP. Downscaled CMIP6 projections provide valuable insights into future temperature and rainfall patterns under different emission scenarios12,13.When combined with observed data, these projections help identify vulnerabilities and inform adaptive strategies.

This study therefore analyzes observed and projected rainfall and temperature changes in BMNP under climate change scenarios by: (i) examining long-term meteorological data; (ii) identifying trends using statistical methods; and (iii) assessing future climate conditions under selected SSP scenarios. The findings support climate adaptation, biodiversity conservation, and sustainable resource management in this highly sensitive ecosystem.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located in the Bale Zone of the Oromia National Regional State, Ethiopia, approximately 400 km southeast of Addis Ababa, and extends between 6°30′ and 7°00′ N latitude and 39°30′–39°55′ E longitude (Fig. 1). It forms part of the Bale–Arsi massif, which constitutes the western section of the southeastern Ethiopian highlands. In terms of administrative boundaries, the park lies within five woredas of Bale Zone: Sinana-Dinsho to the north, Adaba to the west, Goba to the northeast, Mena-Angetu to the south, and Haro to the east. The climate of Bale Mountains National Park (BMNP) is largely influenced by pronounced altitudinal variation and moisture-laden easterly winds from the Indian Ocean, resulting in diverse environmental conditions ranging from relatively moderate climates in the northern areas to harsh, frost-prone conditions on the central plateau14–16.

Description of global circulation models (GCMs)

Global circulation models (GCMs) are numerical models which represent numerous developments in the land surface, oceans and atmosphere. They are the primary input sources for future climate change impact studies and climate patterns assessment. Over the past years, the number of GCMs available for possible future changes has significantly increased17. The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6), which was used for the IPCC AR6, was used for current study. This is because it is the most recent GCM, which has demonstrated qualitative and quantitative improvements over earlier generations of CMIP phases such as CMIP5 and CMIP3 both in the representation of physical processes and the simulated fields as well as higher spatial resolution18,19.

For this study ACCESS-CM2-01, CMCC-ESM2, CNRM-CM6-1, INM-CM4-8, MIROC-ES2L, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, NorESM2-MM and MRI-ESM2-0 GCMs were downscaled from the CMIP6 (Table 2). They are selected owing to their better performance in the Ethiopian environment20 and based on model availability for all variables (Precipitation, Tmax and Tmin) for the historical time period. Future climate scenario was generated for precipitation and minimum and maximum temperature for stations located in and around the study area.

Statistical downscaling method

Climate scenarios from a GCM are usually at a large scale and, hence they are not suitable for impact and adaptation studies that require detailed local data21.There for, downscaling is mandatory for these studies as they require detailed local data.

The Statistical Downscaling Model (SDSM) is a freely available tool that produces high resolution climate change scenarios result from GCMS for local scale climate change study17,22. It calculates the statistical relationships based on multiple linear regression techniques between large-scale (the predictors) and local climate variables (the predictand). It creates empirical relationships between historical large-scale atmospheric and local climate characteristics23. Once a relationship has been determined and validated, future large-scale atmospheric conditions projected by GCMs was used to predict future local climate characteristics. In situations where a low-cost, rapid assessment of highly localized climate change impacts is required, statistical downscaling represents more promising option24.

In this study, the Delta method of statistical downscaling analysis under the shared socio-economic pathways (SSPs) SSP 1–2.6, SSP 3–4.5 and SSP5-8.5 from ensemble of GCMs was used for building future climate scenario (precipitation and temperature) in Bale Mountains National park.

Thirty years (1993–2024) daily weather data collected from National meteorology was used as a baseline data for the statistical downscaling.

The Delta method is a statistical downscaling procedure that is based on the sum of interpolated anomalies to high-resolution monthly climate surfaces17. The method produces a smoothed (interpolated) surface of changes in climates (deltas or anomalies) and then applies this interpolated surface to the baseline climate, taking into account the possible bias due to the difference in baselines25.

The delta method is a simple, yet widely used method21 to create scenario time series from GCM output. The method uses the delta method of the SDSM for future projections and changes in extreme indices. The standard approach for the delta method is that the GCM-simulated difference for each calendar month (absolute difference for temperature and relative difference for precipitation) between a future period and the baseline period is determined and then this is superimposed on the historical daily temperature and precipitation series17.

Model calibration, validation and performance

Prior to future scenario construction, the results of the observed data of maximum temperature, minimum temperature and precipitation was correlated with the modeled data during the calibration and validation periods using the coefficients of determination (R2) and Standard Error (SE). The first 20 years of the station data (1994–2013) were used for model calibration, while data from 2014 to 2023 were used for model validation. The calibration algorithm reports the percentage of explained variance and the standard error for each regression model type (monthly, seasonal, or annual averages).

Different parameters were employed to evaluate the performance of climate models in simulating rainfall and temperature in the study area. These parameters are used to assess the ability of the climate models to reproduce the characteristics of rainfall and temperature events. They include model bias, the coefficient of determination, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). These metrics support bias correction. Bias correction is necessary because climate models often exhibit systematic errors (biases) resulting from limited spatial resolution, simplified physical and thermodynamic processes, numerical schemes, or incomplete understanding of climate system processes26. Therefore, bias correction is applied to avoid overestimation or underestimate the downscaled variables.

Results and discussion

Trends and spatiotemporal distribution of rainfall (1994–2023)

The spatiotemporal distribution of precipitation in Bale Mountains National Park (BMNP) exhibits marked variability, reflecting the park’s complex topography and diverse climatic conditions.

Rainfall statistics and trends (Table 3) indicate high spatial heterogeneity across the park. The annual average rainfall ranges from 801.6 mm at Hunte to 1293.1 mm at Dinsho, indicating a substantial spatial variation that is largely controlled by topography and elevation gradients.

Stations located at higher elevations generally exhibit higher mean values and greater variability, reflecting strong orographic effects and increased climate sensitivity. Tullu Dimtu and EWCP record relatively high mean annual rainfall values (1050 and 990.7 mm) respectively with moderate variability(coefficient of variation between 22 and 24%).In contrast, Dinsho shows the highest mean annual rainfall (1293.1) and the largest maximum value (2686.3 mm), accompanied by extremely high variability (CV = 36.8%).Such elevated variability at high and mid-high altitude stations has been widely reported in Ethiopian mountain regions, where complex topography enhances rainfall intensity, interannual fluctuations, and localized extremes due to orographic lifting and shifting atmospheric circulation patterns.

Low to mid-elevation stations, such as Hunte and Adaba, exhibit comparatively lower mean rainfall values and reduced variability. Hunte, in particular, records the lowest mean annual rainfall (801.6 mm) and one of the lowest coefficient of variation (19.1%).indicating relatively stable rainfall conditions over time. At Goba, rainfall variability is also high (CV = 36.4%, suggesting an irregular distribution of rainfall that may significantly influence local hydrological and ecological processes. Overall, this spatial variability underscores the combined influence of altitude and localized microclimatic conditions in shaping precipitation patterns within BMNP.

Moreover, the MK test and Sen’s slope values (Table 3) provide information on the direction and magnitude of rainfall trends over the 30-year period. Most stations indicate a negative trend, suggesting a decline in annual rainfall over time, though generally statistically weak except at Dinsho. Dinsho, with mk value of − 2.18 and a Sen’s slope of − 19.47 mm/year, reveals a significant decreasing trend, indicating substantial rainfall reduction. The significant decline in rainfall at Dinsho (− 19.47 mm/year) has major ecological implications for the sensitive highland ecosystem of the Bale Mountains. Moisture stress may lead to reduced vegetation cover, increased plant mortality, and shifts in species composition, favoring drought-tolerant species over moisture-dependent ones. This, in turn, can alter the structure and functioning of Afro-alpine and montane ecosystems, which are already highly specialized and climate-sensitive. Moreover, reduced rainfall can significantly impact hydrological systems that serve as critical habitats for many species and reduce the availability of drinking water for wildlife. Other stations, such as Goba, EWCP, and Angesso, also indicate negative Sen’s slopes, thus signaling slight but consistent declines. On the other hand, Hunte station shows a positive trend MK value of 1.27 and a Sen’s slope of + 4.44 mm/year, indicating a localized increase in rainfall, perhaps due to local topographical features and orographic influences, along with changes in atmospheric circulation patterns within the region. This conclusion is further confirmed by recent researches that suggest that rainfall trends in Ethiopia and the Horn of Africa have pronounced spatial variability, with both increases and decreases in rainfall trends occurring in close vicinity, albeit not statistically significant32,33. These results coincide well with the findings of recent studies conducted in Ethiopia and elsewhere. For example, a study in northwest Ethiopia found that the rainfall trends were predominantly statistically insignificant despite some slightly increasing tendencies, stressing the dominance of weak and heterogeneous patterns. Likewise, the study carried out in southern Ethiopia showed that the trends of annual and seasonal rainfall were mostly decreasing yet statistically insignificant at numerous stations. In contrast, the study from northeastern Ethiopia revealed the opposite patterns, since the annual rainfall trends were increasing, and the seasonal ones were heterogeneous.Besides, the recent findings from the Amhara region have proven that there were only a few statistically significant trends despite noticeable heterogeneity of trends at different stations.

Based on the 30-year interpolated rainfall data (1994–2023), the mean annual precipitation of the area is approximately 856.33 mm, with a minimum of 169.09 mm and a maximum of 1298.85 mm.

The rainfall distribution map (Fig. 2) clearly shows that the northern and northeastern parts of the area receive the highest amount of rainfall, while the southern and western lowland areas experience relatively lower precipitation. This pattern is primarily influenced by orographic effects, where the elevated terrain enhances moisture condensation, leading to higher rainfall in the highlands, whereas the leeward sides experience a rain-shadow effect resulting in drier conditions.

Spatial distribution of rain fall (1994–2023).

These findings align with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and other parts of East Africa. For instance8,34,35, reported declining rainfall trends across the Ethiopian highlands, attributed to regional climate variability and anthropogenic influences. Likewise,36 observed significant rainfall reductions in southeastern Ethiopia, particularly in areas with pronounced elevation gradients. Studies outside Ethiopia, such as Wesche,37 in Uganda’s Rwenzori Mountains and Ayugi et al.38 in Kenya’s Aberdare Range, have also documented similar topographically induced rainfall variability and downward trends linked to broader East African climate fluctuations. In summary, the analysis indicates that rainfall distribution in BMNP is spatially uneven and temporally unstable, with higher rainfall in high-altitude zones like Dinsho and Tulu Dimtu, and lower, more erratic rainfall in lowland areas such as Goba. The overall declining trend in rainfall across most stations raises concerns about potential impacts on the park’s hydrology, vegetation cover, and ecosystem services, particularly given the park’s critical role as a water catchment area for southeastern Ethiopia.

Temporally, the 30-year rainfall record indicates moderate interannual fluctuations, but the overall mean suggests that BMNP maintains a relatively humid environment in its upper zones, supporting its unique Afroalpine ecosystem. The rainfall regime follows a bimodal pattern typical of the Ethiopian highlands, characterized by a long rainy season (June–September) and a short rainy period (March–May). Such rainfall variability is critical for sustaining the park’s vegetation diversity, hydrological balance, and endemic wildlife.

These findings are consistent with earlier research in the region and other parts of Ethiopia. For instance, Shah et al.39 and Kebede and Bewket40 reported that high-altitude areas in southeastern Ethiopia, including the Bale Mountains, receive substantially higher precipitation due to topographic influences and proximity to moist air masses from the Indian Ocean. Similarly, Mulugeta41 observed comparable patterns in the Ethiopian highlands, where mean annual rainfall exceeds 1000 mm in elevated areas but drops below 400 mm in the adjacent lowlands. Outside Ethiopia, studies in other mountainous ecosystems, such as the Kenyan Aberdare Range42 and the Ruwenzori Mountains in Uganda37, also found that rainfall increases with altitude and that the highlands act as significant water towers sustaining downstream hydrological systems.

The relatively high mean rainfall in BMNP highlights its ecological importance as a vital water catchment for southeastern Ethiopia, feeding major rivers such as the Web and Shaya. However, spatial variations in rainfall also imply potential vulnerability to localized droughts and land degradation in the drier southern and western zones. These results underscore the need for sustainable watershed management and conservation measures to mitigate the effects of climate variability and maintain the park’s ecological balance.

The annual precipitation from 1994 to 2023 manifests significant interannual and spatial variation across the Bale Mountains region (Fig. 3) across the eight meteorological stations (Adaba, Angesso, Dinsho, EWCP, Goba, Homa, Hunte, and Tulu Dimtu). Generally, rainfall varied greatly during the study period, with no obvious long-term increasing or decreasing tendency, although a modest recovery was revealed after 2018. The most outstanding peak rainfall years occurred around 1996, 2000, 2008–2009, and 2020. During the extreme wet year of 2009, Dinsho station showed the highest precipitation, reaching almost 1900 mm. On the other hand, pronounced dry years were experienced between 2003 and 2005, 2011–2015, and 2017, where the total precipitation fell below 800 mm at several stations, especially at Goba and Homa. Precipitation on a spatial scale shows that high-altitude areas, such as Dinsho and Tulu Dimtu, receive relatively higher rainfall, probably because of orographic effects resulting from proximity to the Bale massif, while lower-lying stations like Adaba and Goba receive less precipitation.

These results are in line with findings from other studies conducted in and outside Ethiopia. For example, studie by33,43 indicated high interannual rainfall variability over the Ethiopian highlands, which is partly controlled by large-scale climate fluctuation mechanisms such as ENSO and IOD. Omondi et al.44 and Chignell et al.45 also found that the central and southeastern highlands in Ethiopia have rainfall variability characterized by successive wet and dry conditions linked with global and regional climate teleconnections. The relatively high amount of rainfall in the Bale highlands is in agreement with35observation where highland elevations receive high rainfall on account of orographic uplift. Similarly, outside of Ethiopia, in the East African highlands, research studies like Gebrechorkos et al.46 and Berihun et al.4 have observed similar rainfall fluctuations in time and space and drought episodes influenced by changes in monsoonal circulation and ocean–atmosphere interactions. In all, the precipitation pattern in the Bale Mountains area reflects the broader regional climate dynamics of the Horn of Africa. Wet and dry cycles in the area are influenced by both local topography and global climatic oscillations. The relatively wet conditions observed in recent years could therefore reflect partial recovery after earlier drought periods, as has indeed been noted in other studies of changing rainfall regimes across East Africa. This then underlines the need for continued hydroclimatic monitoring to support the management of water and ecosystem resilience in the Bale Mountains and similar high-altitude environments.

Trends and spatiotemporal distribution of temperature (1994–2023)

The long-term temperature records (1994–2023) analyzed for eight (8) different meteorological stations show a clear spatial and temporal variation in maximum and minimum temperatures(Tables 4 and 5). The summary statistics (Tables 4 and 5) indicated that the mean maximum temperatures were highly varied; while the highest mean value was recorded in Goba (22.6 °C), the minimum was registered in Tulu Dimtu (15.4 °C). Correspondingly, the extreme values are also reflective of this gradient: the highest maximum temperature has been registered at Hunte (25.1 °C) and the lowest at Adaba (19.1 °C). In addition, the measures of variability point out that there are marked station-specific differences with respect to the coefficients of variation; whereas the variability is relatively high in Tulu Dimtu (9.7%) and Angesso (8.8%-higher variation around their mean maximum temperatures), lower coefficients of variation were found in Adaba (2.8%) and Hunte (2%).

Trends and Statistical description of Minimum temperature (1994–2023).

The trend analyses of maximum and minimum temperatures from 1994 to 2023 (Tables 4 and 5) demonstrate that, most of the stations in the study area have a warming tendency. Regarding the maximum temperature, the overall trend is an increasing one, though the strength of the trend varies among stations, as does the level of statistical significance. Furthermore, a consistent pattern of more pronounced warming among Angesso, EWCP, Goba, Homa, and Tulu Dimtu is reflected by positive mk values and measureable Sen’s slope estimates. For example, noticeable increases in daily maximum temperatures are reflected in Goba at 0.13 °C, Homa at 0.14 °C, Angesso at 0.09 °C, and Tulu Dimtu at 0.10 °C. On the other hand, stations like Hunte and Adaba only depict moderate warming, with their small positive Sen’s slope indicating a relatively weak upward trend.

Minimum temperatures, in contrast, depict a more uniform and statistically significant warming trend across the study area. Most stations have consistently positive z-test values with positive Sen’s slopes that provide strong evidence of rising daily minimum temperatures. In many areas, the rise in minimum temperatures is higher than in maximum temperatures, indicating a faster rate of nighttime temperature increase compared with daytime. Such a pattern may suggest a possible decrease in Diurnal Temperature Range (DTR), a feature often associated with enhanced atmospheric moisture, greater cloud cover, or other altered conditions in the climate system. This asymmetric warming pattern is consistent with findings from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, which have reported stronger warming trends in minimum temperatures across different agro-climatic zones (e.g.,17,35,47). Similar trends have also been widely documented at regional and global scales, where enhanced nighttime warming has been linked to increased cloud cover, rising atmospheric moisture, and land-use changes1.

Overall, the Mann–Kendall and Sen’s slope analyses confirm that most of the stations are undergoing warming, although a few trends were not statistically significant at the 5% level. The most marked upward trends were Angesso (1.54), EWCP (1.29), Goba (1.26), Homa (1.29), and Tulu Dimtu (1.22), further supporting the presence of a coherent warming signal. Jointly, both suggest that maximum and minimum temperatures are rising, but the increase in minimum temperature is stronger and more pervasive, leading to a slow trend toward a reduction of the diurnal temperature range across the region.

Moreover, the long-term temperature trends (Figs. 4 and 5) with gradual increases of both annual maximum and minimum temperatures across several stations in the Bale Mountains from 1994 to 2023, punctuated by a pronounced peak around 2011–2012. The remarkable peaks observed across most stations in minimum temperatures in the years 2012 and 2022 (Fig. 5) was likely due to the influence of extreme climatic variability associated with the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). For instance, the years 2010–2012 were a strong La Niña transitioning toward neutral conditions, followed by weak El Niño tendencies, with the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) ranging approximately from − 1.5 °C in late 2010 to near + 0.5 °C by 201248. Similarly, the 2021–2022 period was dominated by a prolonged La Niña event, with ONI values reaching about − 1.0 °C to − 1.2 °C, which, despite being a cooling phase, can still induce regional climatic anomalies in East Africa, including elevated minimum temperatures due to changes in atmospheric circulation and cloud cover48,48).

These results are consistent with the previous findings in the Ethiopian highlands. For example,50,51 reported a statistically significant warming trend in the Bale and East Bale zone, with an estimated ~ 0.17–0.19 °C per decade increase in long-term mean temperatures. The local farmers from the Bale Highlands also reported increasing annual temperatures in recent decades, and regional studies on climate projections for the region showed continued warming under future emission scenarios51. In higher elevations, continuous ground-temperature monitoring in the Bale Mountains (3493–4377 m) further underlines the sensitivity of this tropical alpine environment to the climatic shift52. The Bale Mountain trend also belongs to a larger pattern seen worldwide in tropical montane regions. Different studies have shown that warming can cause altitudinal shifts in vegetation composition—for example, the increased dominance of lower-elevation, early-successional species in the African high-elevation forest53. Additionally, previous research within the Bale Mountains has also documented upward expansion of ericaceous vegetation in their elevational gradients, presumably reflecting a climate-induced range shift54.

The spatial distribution maps (Fig. 6a, b) indicate that the central and eastern parts of the study area generally experience higher temperatures (both maximum and minimum) compared to the western and northern regions.

Spatially, central and eastern parts generally have higher temperatures (maximum and minimum) compared to western and northern parts. These findings agree strongly with literature on climate change both globally and specifically in Ethiopia. This warming trend, along with a faster rise in minimum temperatures, agrees with worldwide warming and regional manifestations as reported by the5 and regional studies in Ethiopia35,55. A reduction in DTR, as indicated by data, is also a well-documented global phenomenon56 because of various atmospheric and anthropogenic factors47.

Similarly, spatial interpolation maps reinforce these tabular and graphical patterns, as maximum temperatures are higher in the central and eastern parts of the study region, especially around Goba, where the highest temperature values are concentrated. Areas with lower maximum temperatures are generally encountered in the western and northern parts of the entire area.

The spatial gradient is in good agreement with the statistical findings; likewise, Goba and its surroundings are a thermal hotspot in comparison with the rest of the region. Such observations are in good agreement with wider scientific evidence; for example, global climate assessment studies, especially the IPCC5, report a continued rise in surface temperatures, while regional studies over Ethiopia also report similar warming trends in maximum temperatures throughout highland and midland environments. Maximum temperature increases were also reported in studies by , highlighting both the long-term warming and spatial differences in warming intensity across Ethiopia.

Minimum temperature analysis further strengthens evidence of warming. Graphical visualization indicates persistent nighttime temperature increases in most stations. The mean minimum temperatures are substantially lower than the maximum values, with Goba recording the highest mean minimum temperature (7.4 °C) and Tulu Dimtu the lowest (3.0 °C). Extreme minimum values again reflect pronounced spatial variability, with Hunte registering the highest minimum (10.0 °C) and Adaba the lowest (0.3 °C). Coefficients of variation for minimum temperatures are generally higher than that for maximum temperatures, indicating greater relative fluctuations in nighttime temperatures. Tulu Dimtu (48.0%) and EWCP (41.2%) display particularly high variability, whereas Goba (2.1%) and Hunte (17.8%) show more stable conditions. Application of the MK test demonstrates strong and widespread warming in minimum temperatures, with most (except for Hunte) showing positive and relatively high z-test values, many exceeding or approaching thresholds for statistical significance. Corresponding Sen’s slope estimates are uniformly positive, indicating an increase in minimum temperatures across all stations. Goba shows the steepest increase at 0.13 mm/year, followed by Angesso, EWCP, Homa, and Tulu Dimtu, all around 0.10 mm/year. Stronger and more consistent warming in minimum temperatures compared to maximum temperatures indicates a decline in diurnal temperature range (DTR), as widely reported in global climate studies. A negative MK z-value combined with a small value of positive Sen’s slope for Hunte indicates little or no significant warming in minimum temperatures.

The pattern of the spatial distribution of minimum temperatures is similar to that of maximum temperatures, with higher values found in the centre and eastern part of the study area and lower values in the north and west. This is in agreement with the tabular data presented above and reflects wide regional climatic controls on temperature. Indeed, on a global scale, there is evidence of rapid rises in minimum temperatures, which have been attributed to changes in atmospheric moisture, increased cloud cover, and land-use changes like deforestation and urbanization. According to the work of57,58 DTR has decreased nearly everywhere due to faster rises in minimum temperatures. On the national scale, this observation is reflected in studies conducted within various Ethiopian climates. Indeed, studies in the Rift Valley and highland parts of Ethiopia consistently record warming night-time temperatures and reduced DTR, hence causing local climate systems and ecological processes to shift59,60.

Decadal changes of rainfall and temperature

Compared with long-term (1994–2023) mean, the decadal analysis of rainfall and temperature shows distinct and progressive shifts across the three decades (Table 6). The long-term mean annual rainfall for the 30-year period was 975.73 mm. Compared with the baseline, the first decade (1994–2003) was characterized by above-average rainfall, with a mean value of 1025.95 mm, which is approximately + 50 mm above the long-term mean. This indicates that the late 1990s and early 2000s were relatively wetter years. In contrast, rainfall during 2004–2013 shows a mean of 970.51 mm, which is slightly below (− 5 mm) the long-term mean, indicating the beginning of a drying pattern.

The decline becomes more pronounced in 2014–2023, where rainfall decreases to 923.35 mm, representing a substantial deficit of about − 52 mm relative to the long-term average. This decline in rainfall is mainly attributed to changes in large-scale climate systems and warming-induced atmospheric processes, a trend reportedly observed in most of the recent studies throughout Ethiopia and East Africa.

Ocean–atmosphere interaction variability, especially ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole, has weakened moisture transport and disrupted the time of the year and the duration of the rainy seasons. This continuous downward shift in rainfall aligns with documented long-term drying trends in many parts of Ethiopia, particularly in the central highlands and Rift Valley regions61,62. Similar declines in rainfall have also been observed across East Africa and the Horn of Africa, where warming and circulation changes have contributed to reduced precipitation and increased drought frequency63,64.

Temperature trends also show strong deviations from the 30-year mean (Table 6). Maximum temperature in the study area shows a progressive warming over recent decades. Using the 1994–2023 period mean maximum temperature of 18.73 °C as the baseline, the decade 1994–2003 registered a mean Tmax of 17.82 °C, about 0.91 °C below the long-term average. By 2004–2013, the decadal mean rose to 18.89 °C with a + 0.16 °C anomaly and in 2014–2023, it reached a mean of 19.45 °C, an increase of 0.72 °C above the long-term mean. In total, the difference between the first and most recent decade amounts to + 1.63 °C. This is particularly concerning and should be highlighted more prominently, as it indicates a substantial warming trend with potential implications for regional climate stability, ecosystems, and human livelihoods. The upward trend in maximum temperature is consistent with broader findings across Ethiopia and aligns with national and regional climate-trend studies. For instance, a comprehensive study of the station and agro-ecological zone data across Ethiopia reported that there was general warming up of maximum and minimum temperatures in most of the agro-ecological zones65,66. Another study also focused on the capital area around Addis Ababa and found a statistically increasing trend in maximum and minimum temperatures using Mann–Kendall and Sen’s-slope tests67,68. Recent watershed-level analyses, for instance, a paper on the Welmel Watershed-indicate significant rises in maximum daily temperatures and growing frequency of “warm extremes” (warmest day and warmest night69. At the same time, documented spatial variability exists: magnitude and pace of warming differ substantially among stations, agro-ecological zones, and watersheds4,65. In summary, the decadal analysis reflects the well-documented warming trend in Ethiopia but highlights the local-scale warming that may be more intense than the country-wide mean. The sharper increase in minimum temperatures compared to maximum temperatures is a well-documented feature of global climate change and has been observed in multiple Ethiopian studies, where nighttime warming has been found to exceed daytime warming due to increased atmospheric moisture and greenhouse gas concentration70,71. Similar patterns have also been reported across East Africa, the Sahel, and tropical regions of Asia, where minimum temperatures are rising more rapidly as part of a broader trend of reduced diurnal temperature ranges (IPCC 2021)72,73.

In general, the decadal departures from the long-term mean clearly indicate a climate trajectory characterized by declining rainfall and rising temperatures, with the most marked changes becoming evident after the early 2000s. These observed decadal departures also agree closely with the wider regional and global findings that indicate an intensification of warming and rainfall variability associated with climate change, with fundamental implications for agriculture, water resources, and ecological stability in Ethiopia and beyond Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change74.

Projections of rainfall and temperature distribution under the SSP2-6,SSP4-5,and SSP 8–5 scenarios provide insights in to how future precipitation and temperature patterns may respond to different levels of greenhouse gas emissions and socio-economic development pathways. These scenarios are widely used to assess potential changes in to rainfall and temperature amount, variability, and spatial distribution under future climate conditions.

Bias correction

Before projection, bias correction was performed to minimize systematic errors that may occur in simulating rainfall and temperature, which could affect the accuracy of future projections.

The evaluation of observed and projected rainfall and temperature for Bale Mountains National Park (Table 7) reveals generally strong model performance (Table). For precipitation, under the SSP2-2.6 scenario, the model produced a mean absolute error (MAE) of 18 mm/month, a root mean square error (RMSE) of 24 mm/month, a relative error (RE) of 0.9, a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.89, and a mean bias (MB) of 4 mm/month. These metrics indicate that the model closely simulates observed precipitation, which is within acceptable limits for regional climate modeling in complex terrain. The results also demonstrate that the model accurately represents mean precipitation in Bale Mountains National Park. This finding aligns with prior studies in the Ethiopian highlands, where CMIP6 ensemble models performed well in simulating precipitation under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios.

Rainfall projections of the area show high variability between the three emission scenarios and reflect a complex and uncertain character of hydro-climatic responses in Ethiopia and the wider East African region.

From this, it could be inferred that a wetter future may exist in pathways where mitigation by the world succeeds. This is supported by regional analyses that indicate lower radiative forcing leads to stabilized and enhanced monsoon activity. This consistent mixed change in rainfall in early decades is consistent with several climate modeling studies on Ethiopia. For example, in the ensemble projection on West Shewa, it was shown that future rainfall would be expected to increase in moderate to high emission scenarios, with strong fluctuations and local reductions, suggesting that future decades in some locations or periods might have lower rainfall despite future means indicating increased amounts (Lemma et al. 2025).

Under SSP4-5, rainfall initially declines by –153.61 mm in the 2030s but then increases modestly by the 2050s and substantially by the 2080s. The variability observed here reinforces the need for robust climate adaptation planning, particularly in sectors dependent on seasonal precipitation.

In conclusion, the results reveal that the future patterns of rainfall are highly scenario-dependent, but the overall trends show an increase in rainfall in the long run. In the case of lower emissions, rainfall is expected to rise more consistently, indicating stability in the future with sufficient global efforts towards mitigation. However, with high emissions, there are notable short-term variations, where there will be some decreases in rainfall before experiencing major increases by the end of the century.

Projection of maximum temperature

Projections of maximum temperatures (Fig. 8 and Table 8) indicate a consistent and marked warming trend in all scenarios. During the 2030s, the increase varies from 0.64 °C under SSP2-6 to 0.86 °C under SSP8-5. The 2050s show more pronounced warming of 1.07 °C under SSP2-6, 1.40 °C under SSP4-5, and 1.98 °C under SSP8-5. Maximum warming is projected during the 2080s, when temperatures are expected to rise by 1.19 °C, 2.01 °C, and 3.55 °C for the three emission pathways, respectively. These clearly show that the warming due to emissions becomes quite strong over time, especially in the high-emission scenario.

Projected maximum temperature distribution of BMNP under different emission scenarios.

This warming trend agrees closely with projections in the Ethiopian climate. According to Legese et al.76 and McSweeney78 in Ethiopia, the increase in maximum temperatures is projected to lie between 1 and 1.5 °C under moderate emissions by mid-century and between 2 and 4 °C under high emissions by the end of the century. Similar studies conducted over the highlands and Rift Valley regions in Ethiopia also project strong increases in maximum temperatures for RCP4.5 and RCP8.5. At the international level, the findings are supported by IPCC AR6 documentation, where increases in maximum temperature are seen across all SSPs consistently, but significantly stronger warming under high-emission trajectories such as SSP8-5. The projected warming trends in maximum temperature increase under higher emission scenarios—are also well supported by both regional studies and global assessments of future climate change. Downscaled climate projections across Ethiopia consistently show that maximum temperatures are expected to rise under a range of scenarios, with larger increases associated with higher greenhouse gas emissions. For example, multi model projections for urban centers in southwest Ethiopia indicate that maximum temperatures are projected to increase by about 1.1 °C by the 2050s and up to nearly 2.9 °C by the 2080s under a high emission RCP8.5 scenario, with consistent warming across all future periods relative to a historical baseline27,79. Likewise, evaluations of CMIP6 models for the Upper Blue Nile Basin indicate that mean annual maximum temperature is projected to rise by about 2.1 °C under SSP2 4.5 and about 2.8 °C under SSP5 8.5 from 2015 to 2100, demonstrating stronger warming under higher emission pathways61.

These patterns align with earlier regional projections often cited in the literature. Historical climate assessments indicate that Ethiopia’s mean annual temperature has already risen by about 1.3 °C between 1960 and 2006, with warming continuing into the future (Belay Simane1, et al. 2017). Future projections compiled by climate risk assessments suggest that temperatures across East Africa and specifically in Ethiopia are expected to increase by roughly 1.8 °C by the 2050s and by about 3.7 °C by the end of the century under a high emission scenario, reinforcing the pattern of stronger late century warming under higher emissions.

In conclusion, maximum temperature is projected to increase continuously across all scenarios irrespective of the pathway, with the amount of increase being highly influenced by the level of emissions. Although moderate increases are expected under low-emissions, substantially higher temperature is projected under high-emission scenarios, especially towards the end of the century.

Minimum temperature projections

Projection of minimum temperature (Fig. 9 and Tables 4, 5) show even stronger warming compared to maximum temperatures. Minimum temperatures are projected to increase from the baseline by 0.81°–1.03 °C in the 2030s, 1.21°–2.25 °C in the 2050s, and 1.20°–3.93 °C in the 2080s for the three scenarios. The highest increases across all time frames are seen under SSP8-5, showing that nighttime temperatures are more sensitive to increased greenhouse gas concentrations. The projected minimum temperature increases are well supported by both regional and global climate research. Multiple analysis report that minimum temperatures are rising more rapidly than maximum temperatures and that future warming magnitudes increase substantially under the high-emission scenarios of SSP5-8.5/SSP8.5, while projected minimum-temperature changes of the order of ~ 0.5–4.0 °C by mid- to late-century are plausible for many Ethiopian highland and urban locations. For example, downscaled CMIP projections for Ethiopian urban centers and highland sites show larger absolute increases in monthly minimum temperatures by the 2050s–2080s compared with maximum temperature, with ensemble mid-century increases and end-century increases reaching a few degrees under high-emission pathways,79 and multi-model ensembles. Observational trend studies for Addis Ababa and other stations likewise document a declining diurnal temperature range driven mainly by rising night-time minima, attributing the change to greenhouse-gas forcing, land-use/urbanization and cloud/soil-moisture effects—mechanisms that match the physical explanations. Site-specific projections in the Bale highlands and Sinana/Robe areas similarly report minimum-temperature increases in the near, 2030s, mid, 2050s, and late, 2080s, centuries under RCP/SSP scenarios,15, Bale-highlands studies, and recent CMIP6-based regional work also finds temperature rises in the same magnitude band and stronger responses under SSP8.5,.

The phenomenon-night-time warming exceeding daytime warming-is well-documented in climate science. Studies in Ethiopia by80–82 reported a similar pattern and attributed it to an increase in atmospheric moisture, stronger greenhouse feedbacks, and enhanced cloud cover during nighttime hours. At the global scale, accelerated night-time warming is confirmed by the IPCC AR6 as one of the key climate signals, with significant emphasis on impacts related to agriculture (reduced diurnal temperature range), ecosystems (altered behavior of species), and human health (increased heat stress).

The strong increases in minimum temperatures projected under SSP8-5 imply that late-century warming may cause significant changes to the ecology and socio-economic systems of the study area. The projected increase in the minimum temperature, especially at the high emissions scenario, is an indicator of a drastic change in the nocturnal thermal regime, with the temperature approaching or even exceeding ecological thresholds. The Afroalpine ecosystem will be highly impacted by any increase in minimum temperatures. For instance, even an increase of 2–4 °C will exceed the tolerance of cold adapted plants like Lobelia rhynchopetalum and Dendrosenecio, which require very cool temperatures to function physiologically. Any increase in the minimum temperature threshold will result in the upward movement of species, reduced habitats, and possibly extinction.

In conclusion, minimum temperature is expected to rise more rapidly compared to maximum temperature in all the scenarios considered, showing greater warming at night. The greatest temperature increase will be experienced during the high emission scenario towards the latter part of the century. This trend suggests that there will be lower daytime/nighttime temperature differentials, implying that nighttime warming has a very high sensitivity to greenhouse gases emissions.

Conclusions

This study examined observed and projected rainfall and temperature variability in Bale Mountains National Park (BMNP), Ethiopia, and assessed their potential implications for ecosystem functioning. The findings indicate that the area has experienced a decrease in rainfall and an increase in both maximum and minimum temperatures. Over the past three decades (1994–2023).

Rainfall exhibits strong spatial and interannual variability, largely influenced by elevation and orographic effects, with higher precipitation in high-altitude zones. Most meteorological stations indicate declining rainfall trends since 2000, suggesting an emerging drying tendency, whereas temperatures show a consistent warming trend, highlighting increasing hydro-climatic stress in the region.

Future climate projections indicate that both rainfall and temperatures are likely to increase compared to the baseline period (1985–2014), although the magnitude varies across emission scenarios. The largest rainfall increase is expected under high-emission pathways, while maximum and minimum temperatures are projected to rise by 3.6 °C and 3.9 °C, respectively, by the end of the century under SSP5-8.5. These changes suggest that BMNP will experience more variable and warmer conditions, with intensifying hydro-climatic stress over time.

The combination of rising temperatures and changing rainfall patterns has significant implications for BMNP’s Afro-alpine ecosystems, hydrological regulation, and ecosystem services. These findings underscore the importance of continuous climate monitoring, ecosystem-based adaptation strategies, and emission-sensitive conservation planning to safeguard the park’s biodiversity and water resources under a changing and more variable climate.
