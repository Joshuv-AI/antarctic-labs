Electrochemical direct air capture by local alkalinity generation at three-dimensional interfaces
Authors: Anmol Mathur, Zhengyuan Li, Aochunqiu Han, Krish N. Jayarapu, Megan Lim, Lavanya Gupta, Jeffrey Parkey, Yayuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical direct air capture (eDAC) is a promising strategy for carbon dioxide (CO2) removal, offering advantages in energy efficiency, modularity, and potential for distributed deployment. However, existing systems are limited by low capture rates, energy-intensive regeneration, and challenges in stability and scalability. Here, we present a robust eDAC platform that addresses these limitations through key architectural innovations. The system generates hydroxides locally at the air-electrode interface to improve absorption kinetics and alkalinity utilization compared to conventional bulk solvent approaches. CO2 is subsequently released via a proton-coupled electron transfer mediator, enabling low-energy regeneration and high-purity gas output. Importantly, a porous interposer extends the reactive interface three-dimensionally to enhance CO2 capture efficiency. With an optimized interposer design and a pulsed current protocol, the system achieves stable operation for over 50 hours at current densities an order of magnitude higher than those of previous eDAC demonstrations, while maintaining competitive energy consumption. Multiphysics modeling and techno-economic analysis further guide system optimization and outline a viable path toward cost-effective scalability.

Introduction

Direct air capture (DAC) of carbon dioxide (CO2) is a critical strategy for mitigating global greenhouse gas emissions and achieving net-zero climate targets1–3. Conventional thermochemical DAC methods, such as solvent absorption and solid adsorption, require substantial energy input for sorbent regeneration that is often sourced from fossil fuels4,5. In addition, their reliance on complex heat integration and large thermal infrastructures limits deployment to centralized facilities6–8. In contrast, electrochemical approaches that separate CO2 using low-carbon electricity have emerged as a promising alternative, offering operation under ambient conditions, improved energy efficiency, and compact, modular system designs well suited for distributed, multiscale applications9–12.

Electrochemically mediated carbon capture methods generally fall into two categories: redox-active carriers and pH swing systems. In the former, redox-active organic molecules, either dissolved in solution or immobilized on electrodes, reversibly bind and release CO2 through electrochemical reduction and oxidation cycles13–15. In the latter, solution pH is cyclically modulated between alkaline and acidic conditions to drive CO2 absorption and desorption via proton-involving redox reactions, such as water or salt electrolysis16–19, hydrogen looping20–22, or proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) mediators23–25. However, few electrochemically mediated carbon capture systems have been successfully adapted for DAC20,26–29. Redox-active carriers often suffer from oxygen (O2) sensitivity30–32, limiting their compatibility with ambient air. Meanwhile, pH swing processes typically decouple electrochemical alkalinity (hydroxide ions, OH−) generation from CO2 absorption20,21,28, imposing a major kinetic bottleneck as the reaction between trace atmospheric CO2 ( ~ 400 ppm) and bulk-phase OH− is inherently slow due to transport limitations33. For example, Carbon Engineering’s optimized air-contactor operates at only ~ 7% OH− utilization efficiency33–35. This necessitates the use of large liquid-air contactors and substantial solvent volumes, which increase system footprint and operational complexity. Importantly, for electrochemical systems, the presence of excessive unreacted OH− in solution substantially undermines the efficiency of subsequent regeneration34. Further, the generation of by-products such as hydrogen from water splitting or chlorine from salt electrolysis introduces additional challenges for system integration, especially for decentralized deployment36,37. Recent work has explored the direct production of OH− at the gas-liquid interface using solid-electrolyte reactors to facilitate CO2 absorption into carbonate ions (CO32−)26. However, performance degraded quickly when transitioning from point-source capture to DAC, due to the low absorption efficiency at the two-dimensional gas-liquid interface, leading to poor utilization of electrochemically generated alkalinity.

This work presents a new electrochemical direct air capture (eDAC) system that addresses key limitations of existing DAC approaches by coupling localized alkalinity generation with efficient CO2 absorption (Fig. 1a, b). Alkalinity is produced in situ at a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) via the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), where molecular O2 from ambient air is electrochemically reduced to OH− at the air-electrode interface. These OH− ions react with CO2 to form CO32−, which are transported across an anion exchange membrane (AEM) to the anode compartment. There, a PCET mediator is oxidized to release protons, acidifying the electrolyte to enable CO2 desorption. The mediator is then regenerated in a separate electrochemical cell to complete the cycle. To overcome the intrinsic kinetic limitations imposed by the low CO2 concentration in air, we introduce a key design innovation: a porous, ionically conductive, and gas-permeable interposer positioned between the GDE and the AEM (Fig. 1c). This non-catalytic spacer, which is an ion-exchange resin wafer with optimized composition and porosity, facilitates three-dimensional CO2 hydration and CO32− formation by extending the reactive interface. Specifically, OH− ions generated at the catalyst near the GDE are directed through the ion-conductive backbone of the interposer toward the membrane, while CO2 gas diffuses into its porous structure. This configuration maximizes CO32− flux across the AEM and minimizes unreacted OH− crossover, thereby enhancing the overall electron efficiency. In addition, we implement a pulsed current protocol to synchronize OH− generation with CO2 mass transport, extending the reaction time and further improving capture efficiency, particularly at high current densities.

By generating alkalinity locally at the air-electrode interface, our eDAC system accelerates CO2 capture kinetics and eliminates the need for external chemical inputs, air contactor, and bulk solvent handling, enabling a compact and efficient design. The use of highly reversible PCET mediators coupled with the oxygen redox pair ensures efficient pH modulation and favorable energy efficiency. We demonstrate stable, continuous eDAC operation for over 50 hours, producing a high-purity CO2 stream with water vapor as the only minor byproduct. We further employ multiphysics modeling to guide design optimization and conduct a techno-economic analysis (TEA) to assess scalability and identify future opportunities for cost and performance improvements.

Results

Design of the interposer

A central requirement for efficient eDAC operation is the effective conversion of electrochemically generated OH− into CO32−, as the direct transport of unreacted OH− across AEM will be detrimental to CO2 capture efficiency. The CO2 capture efficiency is defined here as the ratio of CO2 absorbed to OH− generated (equivalent to Faradic efficiency toward CO2 capture). An efficiency of 100% indicates complete conversion of OH− to CO32−, whereas 200% efficiency corresponds to full conversion of OH− to bicarbonates. Although bicarbonate formation can occur in aqueous carbon capture chemistry, it is not the primary focus of our eDAC system. We hypothesize that a rationally engineered porous interposer can create a hydroxide-rich, three-dimensional reaction zone where dilute CO2 from air can penetrate and react with OH−. In addition to facilitating gas-liquid contact, the interposer must meet several critical criteria. It must exhibit high ionic conductivity to enable efficient transport of OH− and CO32− ions from the catalyst layer to the membrane, while also retaining adequate hydration to sustain catalytic activity. Furthermore, the ion-conducting region of the interposer should be thin to maximize the reactive interface and minimize the inherently slow bulk-phase reactions between solubilized CO2 and OH−.

To evaluate the impact of the interposer on DAC performance, we systematically quantified CO2 capture efficiency and cell voltage at various current densities using different interposer designs and compared them against a baseline configuration consisting of only a GDE with an ORR catalyst. CO2 capture efficiency was determined by measuring the concentration difference between inlet and outlet streams using an infrared-based CO2 sensor. Standard commercial Pt/C was used as the ORR catalyst, and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) driven by an Ir/C catalyst was employed on the anode side for charge balancing. The experimental setup for interposer testing and operating condition optimization is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

We explored a range of interposer materials, including ionomer-loaded metallic foams, carbon papers, graphene oxide aerogels, and anion-exchange resin beads (Supplementary Figs. 2–6). For initial screening, a 2% CO2 and 10% O2 feed was used to amplify measurable performance differences during early optimization, as higher CO2 concentrations enhance capture efficiency and improve the detectability of system responses, before transitioning to DAC-relevant conditions (400 ppm CO2). Most materials yielded minimal improvements over the baseline (example data sets are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7). In contrast, the anion-exchange resin bead interposer showed promising enhancement in CO2 capture efficiency. For instance, at a current density of 10 mA cm–2, a packed bed of resin beads with a thickness of 800 µm yields a capture efficiency of 132% compared to 71% without an interposer (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, we observed a positive correlation between resin bead layer thickness and capture efficiency, supporting our hypothesis that extending OH− residence time within a porous, ion-conductive matrix enhances the likelihood of reaction with CO2 before reaching the membrane.

While resin beads have been previously used as solid electrolytes in electrochemical reactors20,26,38,39, their non-uniform packing poses challenges for reproducibility and scalability40. To address this limitation and build on the promising performance observed with packed resin beads, we developed a structured and scalable interposer in the form of a porous resin wafer (Fig. 2a). This architecture integrates anion-exchange resins for ionic conductivity with an inert polymeric binder that provides mechanical stability. A sacrificial porogen (sodium chloride, NaCl) is incorporated during fabrication and subsequently removed to generate a tunable pore network. The resin and porogen are first ground to average particle sizes of 21.75 µm and 16.50 µm, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). The three components are then mixed in specified ratios and packed into hot-press molds. After hot pressing, the porogen remains embedded within the wafer (Supplementary Fig. 10). The resin wafer design offers several advantages. It provides a uniform and mechanically stable structure (Supplementary Fig. 12), improves reproducibility across experiments, and simplifies integration into the eDAC cell. Importantly, it enables systematic tuning of critical parameters such as composition, porosity, resin chemistry, and interposer thickness. These features collectively determine the internal structure and physicochemical properties of the wafer to balance ionic conductivity, gas permeability, and water retention, all of which are essential to achieving optimal CO2 capture performance.

To systematically examine the impact of porogen content on CO2 capture performance, we quantified wafer porosity using X-ray computed tomography (µCT, Fig. 2c). The analysis confirmed that porosity increases with increasing porogen loading. When testing under simulated air (400 ppm CO2), capture efficiency improved from 24% to 36% and 44% at a current density of 2 mA cm–2 as porosity increased from 0.11 to 0.13 and 0.35 (Fig. 2b, with full-cell voltages provided in Supplementary Fig. 13a). However, further increasing porosity to 0.44 led to a decline in performance at the same current density (26%), likely due to insufficient OH− transport and reactive area. The results suggest a trade-off between maintaining a sufficient hydroxide-conductive region and preserving gas-phase permeability in the interposer. Notably, all wafers outperformed packed resin beads, at least at low current densities. At a higher current density (4.8 mA cm–2), the wafer with 0.35 porosity maintained superior performance, achieving a capture efficiency of 22% compared to 17% for the resin bead configuration.

Next, we investigated the effect of resin-to-binder ratio, aiming to maximize the proportion of ion-conductive domains within the interposer to facilitate mass transport and CO2 absorption. Nevertheless, the nonpolar binder plays a crucial role in maintaining wafer hydrophobicity, which helps prevent electrolyte accumulation at the cathode. Insufficient binder can lead to rapid GDE flooding, inhibiting gas transport and degrading eDAC performance. To isolate the effect of binder content, we held porosity constant at 0.35 and tested wafers with varying resin-to-binder volumetric ratios (Fig. 2e, with voltages provided in Supplementary Fig. 13b). At current densities below 2.8 mA cm–2, capture efficiency initially declined slightly as the ratio increased from 1.5 to 2, decreasing from 39% to 34% at 2 mA cm–2. However, performance improved as the ratio increased to 3, reaching a peak efficiency of 44% under the same conditions. Further increases in resin content to ratios of 5 and 6 resulted in reduced performance, with capture efficiencies dropping to 28% and 26%, respectively. Based on these results, we selected a porosity of 0.35 and a resin-to-binder ratio of 3.2 as the optimized wafer condition for subsequent studies. While pressure drop is an important consideration for large-scale DAC, in our system, it primarily arises from the electrolyzer flow field and GDE rather than the interposer (Supplementary Fig. 14). Both aspects remain active areas of research but are beyond the scope of this work.

Increasing the thickness of the resin wafer from 800 µm to 1600 µm yielded a modest improvement in capture efficiency (from 43% to 47% at 2 mA cm–2) but at the cost of higher full-cell voltages (from 1.65 V to 1.84 V at 2 mA cm–2, Supplementary Fig. 15). Conversely, a thinner wafer (400 µm) lowered both capture efficiencies (26% at 2 mA cm–2) and full-cell voltages (1.42 V at 2 mA cm–2) compared to the 800 µm wafer. Based on this trade-off, a thickness of 800 µm was selected as the optimal balance. The effect of interposer thickness on capture efficiency is further investigated through multiphysics modeling in a later section.

Across all interposer design optimizations, capture efficiencies converged at higher current densities, suggesting a fundamental limit imposed by the slow kinetics of CO2 hydration at the gas-liquid interface41,42. To address this, we developed a pulsed current protocol to further extend the reaction time between CO2 and OH−, thereby enhancing eDAC performance under mass transport-limited conditions (Fig. 2f). At current densities above 3.6 mA cm–2, all pulsed protocols outperformed the non-pulsed counterpart. When a pulsed current was applied with 20 seconds ON and 20 seconds OFF, capture efficiency at 4.8 mA cm–2 increased from 26% to 29%. Shortening the pulse period to 10 seconds (ON/OFF) further improved capture efficiency to 39%, while a 5-second pulse slightly reduced it to 35%, suggesting diminishing returns. Full-cell voltages remained largely unchanged across pulse frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 13c). At a gas flow rate of 100 sccm, 39% capture efficiency at 4.8 mA cm–2 corresponded to a CO2 outlet concentration of 78 ppm (80% CO2 removal). To sustain higher current densities without depleting headspace CO2, we increased the gas flow rate to 200 sccm (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 13d). Again, the 10-second pulse outperformed the 20-second pulse, with capture efficiencies maintained at ~ 40% up to 10 mA cm–2. Based on these findings, we adopted the 10 s pulsed current protocol for subsequent experiments. Representative CO2 sensor readings and full-cell voltages under this condition are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7c, d.

Enabling CO2 release with a PCET mediator

Having established the effectiveness of the interposer in enhancing CO2 capture, we next focused on identifying a suitable PCET mediator to facilitate the desorption of a concentrated CO2 product stream at the anode. We present a discussion on the possible PCET mediator candidates in Supplementary Note 1. We selected anthraquinone-2,7-disulfonic acid disodium salt (AQDS) as the mediator due to its favorable combination of high solubility, suitable pKa, and excellent electrochemical performance. First, AQDS is highly soluble in aqueous media ( ~ 1.3 M in deionized water)43. High solubility corresponds to an enhanced pH swing capacity, which translates into a smaller mediator electrolyte reservoir volume and reduced fluid pumping requirements. Second, mediators with high pKa values are desirable, as they can access a broader pH window for PCET-driven desorption. We experimentally determined the pKa values of AQDS by measuring redox potentials across varying pH and constructing a Pourbaix diagram (Supplementary Fig. 16). The redox potential shows a fitted slope close to the theoretical – 59 mV pH–1 up to a pH value of ~ 8.3 (pKa,1), indicating that AQDS can exchange one proton with the electrolyte media per electron transfer from pH 0 to 8.3. This pKa,1 is sufficiently higher than that of carbonic acid (pKa,1 = 6.0), which will enable favorable thermodynamics for CO2 outgassing during pH swing. Finally, AQDS exhibits high redox reversibility and kinetics, enabling low overpotentials and efficient energy utilization in the eDAC system.

In situ pH measurements in a cell with AQDS reduction at the cathode and OER at the anode confirmed successful protonation of AQDS during electroreduction, as evidenced by minimal changes in solution pH (Supplementary Fig. 17). For the eDAC system, we chose to operate the AQDS anolyte at pH 2.5–3.5, which is low enough to promote CO2 outgassing, yet above the threshold where AEM stability is compromised (pH < 2)44. To minimize mass transfer limitations at high current densities, a 0.2 M AQDS concentration was used (Supplementary Fig. 18).

We then evaluated the eDAC cell combining an air cathode with an optimized interposer for CO2 absorption and AQDS-mediated desorption (Fig. 3a). In this configuration, a tank containing 50% reduced 0.2 M AQDS (AQDSH2) at pH 2.65 was continuously purged with 20% CO2, which served as a carrier gas to enable accurate quantification of desorbed CO2. CO2 capture and release were monitored using infrared-based sensors positioned at both the cathode and anode outlets, while a gas mass flow meter (MFM) at the anode outlet provided independent validation of the CO2 release measurements. We evaluated key performance metrics of the eDAC system, including CO2 removal rate (defined as the percentage of CO2 removed from the inlet gas), capture efficiency, and full-cell voltage. Performance was benchmarked against two control configurations: (i) without the interposer and using OER at the anode (baseline), and (ii) with the interposer and using OER at the anode (Fig. 3g–i). The interposer markedly enhanced capture efficiency, yielding a 73% increase when paired with water oxidation and a 97% increase when paired with PCET oxidation at 10 mA cm–2 relative to the baseline. These gains directly translated to higher CO2 removal rates. For instance, the interposer increased CO2 removal by 73% over the baseline at 10 mA cm–2 when paired with water oxidation and by an additional 15% when paired with PCET oxidation. The superior capture performance with PCET is attributed to a steeper CO32− and OH− concentration gradients across the interposer, driven by enhanced CO2 outgassing at the anode due to the lower pH of the PCET anolyte compared to that of water oxidation (pH ~7). Furthermore, the use of a PCET mediator substantially reduced the full-cell voltage, owing to its lower thermodynamic oxidation potential than OER (Supplementary Fig. 16).

CO2 sensor data collected from the AQDS headspace confirmed effective desorption, with release-to-capture ratios of 91, 98, and 98% at current densities of 5, 8, and 10 mA cm–2, respectively (Fig. 3j). MFM data showed slightly lower CO2 release values for the same current densities (99, 82, and 88%, respectively), potentially due to viscosity-related measurement inaccuracies. Nonetheless, both sensors consistently confirmed that nearly all captured CO2 was successfully released, indicating the high reversibility of the eDAC process.

In a separate experiment, CO2 release without a carrier gas was quantified using the MFM at the AQDS tank headspace, yielding a release-to-capture ratio of 98% at 10 mA cm–2, which is consistent with values obtained when a carrier gas was used (Supplementary Fig. 19). Gas chromatography further verified that the AQDS headspace products were high-purity CO2, with the chromatogram shown in Supplementary Fig. 20 using 20% CO2 in argon as the carrier gas, and in Supplementary Fig. 21 without a carrier gas. These results highlight the synergistic role of the interposer design, pulsed operating protocol, and PCET-mediated desorption in achieving efficient, continuous electrochemical CO2 capture and release under realistic DAC conditions.

The system demonstrated stable operation over 54 hours at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 and an air flow rate of 200 sccm. After an initial stabilization period of approximately one hour, both cells maintained steady voltages, averaging 0.27 V for the eDAC cell and 1.79 V for the regeneration cell. The performance remained consistent throughout the experiment, with an average CO2 removal rate of 86.53% (from 400 ppm down to 54 ppm), a capture efficiency of 39.96%, and a release-to-capture ratio of 96.65% (Fig. 4b–h).

At 10 mA cm−2, CO2 depletion in the cathode headspace limited further increases in current density (Fig. 4d). Therefore, we increased the air flow rate to 500 sccm, achieving an 84% CO2 removal rate and 39% capture efficiency at 25 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4i, j). Increasing the flow rate to 1000 sccm further improved capture efficiency to 48%, although CO2 removal dropped to 35% at the same current density. At 50 mA cm−2, capture efficiency declined to 27% with a CO2 removal rate of 39%, likely limited by CO2 mass transfer across the gas-liquid interface.

To further boost capture efficiency and current density, we incorporated positively charged ionomers into the interposer. Using Sustainion, which possesses higher CO2 solubility due to its imidazolium functional groups, CO2 removal increased from 35% to 70% and capture efficiency rose from 48% to 97% at 25 mA cm−2 compared to the ionomer-free case (Fig. 4i, j). At 50 mA cm−2, CO2 removal improved from 39% to 82%, and capture efficiency increased from 27% to 57%. We also tested poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) (pDADMAC), which yielded capture efficiencies comparable to Sustainion but delivered a substantial reduction in cell voltage (1.73 V at 25 mA cm−2 and 1.88 V at 50 mA cm−2, Fig. 4k). This superior performance can be attributed to the high cation density of pDADMAC, which is three times greater than that of Sustainion45, thereby enhancing ion transport across the interposer (Supplementary Table S1). The ionomer-coated interposer layer remains stable for nearly 17 hours (Supplementary Fig. 23), maintaining a CO2 removal rate of 87% at an inlet flow rate of 500 sccm and a current density of 25 mA cm−2. At such a high air flow rate, adequate hydration at the GDE–interposer interface is critical to sustaining this performance, as maintaining hydration ensures efficient ion transport, suppresses voltage rise, and enables long-term stable operation at high current densities. Further enhancing interfacial CO2 delivery by an interposer with a thinner ion-conductive region and higher pore density may enable even higher throughput without sacrificing efficiency. This strategy is explored via multiphysics modeling in the next section.

The performance of this bench-scale prototype is benchmarked against the limited reported data for electrochemical carbon capture using a direct 400 ppm feed (Supplementary Fig. 24, detailed comparison to literature is provided in Supplementary Table S2). Previous studies often base energy estimates on idealized conditions, neglecting ohmic losses, full-cell operation, or non-electrochemical energy demands like gas-liquid contact45–47. Importantly, most prior systems have been evaluated using artificially concentrated CO32− or HCO3− solutions, rather than the dilute, OH−-rich effluents that would realistically emerge from an air contactor. Consequently, their reported efficiencies and energy requirements are likely underestimated by roughly an order of magnitude under true DAC operating conditions. In contrast, our measurements reflect actual operational conditions, including full-cell resistance and mass transfer limitations, yielding a more realistic assessment of practical performance.

Our system can operate at current densities that are at least one order of magnitude higher than existing electrochemical DAC demonstrations with competitive energetics (Supplementary Fig. 24). The energy consumption for current densities between 4 and 25 mA cm−2, when PCET mediator regeneration is paired with OER, ranges from 17.8 and 25.8 GJ tonCO2–1, due to the energy penalty associated with sluggish OER kinetics. Replacing OER with an alternative proton-generating oxidation process, such as glycerol oxidation reaction (GOR) or other biomass electro-oxidation reactions, offers a promising path to reduce energy demands while producing value-added co-products48–50. Replacing OER with GOR for the regeneration cell reduces the energy consumption to the range of 4 to 10.9 GJ tonCO2–1.

Engineering insights from multiphysics modeling

To guide system optimization and understand key design trade-offs, we developed a two-dimensional multiphysics model that captures the coupled transport and reaction processes within the interposer. The interposer was modeled as a porous structure with hydrated ionomer regions, represented by two aqueous layers flanking a gas channel, corresponding to the smallest repeating unit of the porous network (Fig. 5a). CO2 from the gas stream dissolves at the gas-liquid interface and diffuses both vertically into the depth of the aqueous layer and laterally towards the membrane. OH− ions are generated at a constant flux at the GDE-interposer interface (left boundaries of the aqueous layers) based on the applied current. Anionic species (OH−, CO32−, and HCO3–) then migrate through the aqueous layers toward the membrane (right boundaries of the aqueous layers). The simulations included three time-dependent study steps (SS, Fig. 5b). In SS1 (T0 to T1), CO2 enters the system and equilibrates with the aqueous layers according to Henry’s law. In SS2 (T1 to T2), a constant reductive current generates a uniform flux of OH− to initiate CO2 capture. SS3 (T2 to T4) simulates the continued reaction between residual OH− and CO2 after the current is turned off. Capture efficiency is calculated using the steady-state value at T2 as the ratio of the CO32− flux across the membrane to the electrogenerated OH− flux. Full model details and parameters are provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Tables S3, S4.

Figure 5c shows representative spatial distributions of dissolved CO2 in the aqueous layer at selected time points. At T0, the layer contains no CO2. By T1, equilibrium is established with the 400 ppm CO2 headspace, yielding an aqueous concentration of ~ 17 mmol m−3. Between T1 and T2, CO2 is rapidly consumed via reaction with OH−, with the steepest depletion near the GDE, as clearly visualized in the line profiles (Supplementary Fig. 25). CO2 concentration trends in the gas channel closely mirror those in the aqueous layer (Supplementary Figs. 26, 27), indicating that CO2 mass transfer across the gas-liquid interface limits the eDAC performance. Since OH− is a strong absorbent, gas phase resistance dominates. At low CO2 partial pressures, CO2 solubilization cannot keep up with OH− flux, restricting capture efficiency. From T2 to T3, CO2 capture continues until OH− ions are fully reacted and the aqueous layer is replenished with CO2.

To explore how interposer architecture impacts transport, we explored porosity (ε) as a tunable design parameter. Simulations were performed for four aqueous layer thicknesses corresponding to ε values of 0.5, 0.375, 0.25, and 0.125, while keeping pore density (i.e., total reactive interfacial area) constant (Fig. 5d). Higher ε consistently yields greater capture efficiency across all current densities. For example, at 3 mA cm−2, increasing ε from 0.125 to 0.25 improves capture efficiency by 123%; further increases to 0.375 and 0.5 result in additional gains of 52% and 34%, respectively. To better understand this effect, we visualized CO2 concentrations at time points T1 + 5 min, T1 + 10 min, and T2 at 1 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29). Low-porosity interposers show greater CO2 depletion in the aqueous layer, reducing absorption efficiency. For instance, at T1 + 10 min near the right boundary, CO2 concentration is 8.88 mmol m−3 for ε = 0.5 but drops to 3.50 mmol m−3 for ε = 0.125. At 5 mA cm−2, depletion at the right boundary is evident by T1 + 10 min even for ε = 0.5 (1.46 mmol m−3 versus 0.13 mmol m−3 for ε = 0.125), underscoring the cause of reduced efficiency at high current densities (Supplementary Figs. 30, 31). A summary across all current densities at T1 + 5 min (Supplementary Fig. 32) further illustrates that thinner aqueous layers enhance solubilized CO2 availability to promote absorption efficiency.

Experimental results corroborated the predictive accuracy of our model. Capture efficiencies obtained under identical operating conditions for two ε values closely matched simulation results and showed similar decay trends with increasing current density (Fig. 5d). For instance, increasing simulated ε from 0.125 to 0.375 closely matched an experimental increase from 0.13 to 0.35. This strong agreement confirms that the model reliably captures the dominant transport and reaction processes and can guide future design optimization of the interposer. Nevertheless, the decline in performance observed experimentally above a porosity threshold was not captured by our model, likely because the simplified geometry does not account for resin particle percolation and tortuosity effects that reduce effective ionic conductivity at high porosity.

Besides tuning the aqueous layer thickness, we varied the gas-liquid interfacial area by changing the pore density (Fig. 5e). Pore density was modulated by changing the height of the simulation cell. “1 ×” density represents the baseline (40 µm unit cell height), while “2 ×” indicates doubled pore density by halving the height to 20 µm. Increasing pore density from 0.5 × to 1 × while maintaining a constant ε increases the capture efficiency at 2 mA cm−2 by 85% and 84% for ε of 0.25 and 0.5, respectively. The same increase yields greater improvements (101% and 105%) for ε of 0.625 and 0.75, indicating that increasing pore density is even more effective at enhancing capture efficiency at higher ε. Further increasing the density to 2 × continues to enhance efficiency for lower ε values, but shows diminishing returns at higher ε, likely because the capture efficiency is already approaching its maximum under these conditions. These non-linear performance trends highlight the utility of modeling as a complement to experiments, offering quantitative insight into trade-offs that are not evident from intuition alone and guiding interposer optimization in ways that would otherwise require extensive experimental campaigns.

We then examined the effect of interposer length (Fig. 5f). At fixed ε, increasing the length improves capture efficiency by expanding the gas-liquid interfacial area and prolonging OH− residence time before reaching the AEM. For instance, at the lowest ε of 0.125, increasing the length from 200 µm to 800 µm reduces the outlet CO2 concentration from 320 ppm to 162 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 33), indicating improved removal rate at constant current density. However, at higher ε, the gains in capture efficiency taper off with further increases in interposer length. To investigate this further, Supplementary Fig. 34 compares the spatial distributions of OH− at lengths of 200–800 µm at T1 + 5 min for ε = 0.125 and 0.75. Midpoint concentrations plotted on a normalized axis (Supplementary Fig. 35) show steeper OH− gradients with increasing length, indicating progressive OH− depletion. For example, for the highest interposer thickness (800 µm) and ε (0.75), the OH− concentration is 1956 mmol m−3 at the right boundary, which is nearly three orders of magnitude lower than the initial value (~ 105 mmol m−3, pH 13). Thus, at high ε and sufficient interposer lengths, the capture efficiency approaches 100% as the residence time is sufficient to consume nearly all OH−.

Lastly, we simulated inlet CO2 concentrations ranging from 1% to 15%, representative of various point sources (Fig. 5g). As expected, higher concentrations enable operation at higher current densities using the same interposer design (400 µm length, ε = 0.5). For instance, at 15% CO2, an industrially relevant current density of 150 mA cm−2 results in a capture efficiency of 20%. At this concentration, the dissolved inorganic carbon equilibrium may shift toward bicarbonate, potentially raising the actual capture efficiency.

In summary, our simulations identify several key factors influencing CO2 capture performance, including aqueous layer height, pore density, interposer length, current density, and inlet CO2 concentration. These insights will be applied to future materials engineering efforts to further enhance eDAC performance.

Techno-economic assessment for scale-up

To assess the economic viability and scalability of our eDAC system, we performed TEA based on previously established models26,51, incorporating our experimental data. For the regeneration cell, polarization data for the anode reaction were taken from the literature, while data for the AQDS redox mediator were obtained from our own symmetric cell experiments (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Supplementary Table S5). The model estimates a base case cost of $753 tonCO2–1 for our best interposer structure (without ionomer coating) at an operating current of 25 mA cm–2 with a clear pathway for improvement (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 2). Baseline parameters are tabulated in Supplementary Table S6. Previous electrochemical DAC systems that capture CO2 at the air-electrode interface have generally operated at very low current densities, which reduces energy demand (Supplementary Fig. 24)26,52,53. However, the modest operating cost (OPEX) savings from lower energy consumption are outweighed by the substantial increase in capital cost (CAPEX) required to maintain the CO2 capture throughput (Supplementary Fig. 37). Figure 6a and Supplementary Fig. 38 present results from two types of single-variable sensitivity analyses. In Fig. 6a, each variable is tested across its experimentally determined optimistic and pessimistic bounds. In Supplementary Fig. 38, variables are perturbed by a fixed ± 20% to reveal their relative impact. Both analyses highlight current density, capture efficiency, and electricity cost as the top three priorities for cost reduction. In contrast, extending the lifetimes of the cell stack, membrane, or wafer offers comparatively minor economic gains, even under the most favorable conditions.

Since electricity is a major cost component (Supplementary Fig. 39), we further analyzed how capture cost varies non-linearly with current density at three different electricity prices (Supplementary Fig. 40). At current densities below 10 mA cm–2, costs can rise to ~ $2000 tonCO2–1 despite high capture efficiencies, due to low capture throughput and the resulting high capital cost. A cost minimum emerges between 20 and 30 mA cm–2. Beyond 30 mA cm–2, costs rise again due to reduced capture efficiencies and increased voltage requirement for PCET mediator regeneration.

Targeted improvements can yield substantial cost reductions (Fig. 6b). Based on our current best performance (57% capture efficiency at 50 mA cm–2), we consider a target of 70% efficiency at this current density feasible through further optimization of interposer chemistry, improved catalyst-interposer interface engineering, and enhanced water management (minimizing evaporation at high flow rates). Lowering the electricity price from $0.05 kWh–1 (baseline) to $0.01 kWh–1 (optimistic value) at a constant current density of 25 mA cm–2 achieves a cost reduction of $287 tonCO2–1. Enhancing the capture efficiency from a base of 50–70% reduces the cost by $151 tonCO2–1. Moreover, in the situation where critical factors such as CO2 mass transport and water management can be improved to support operation at 50 mA cm–2 with 70% capture efficiency, the overall cost drops by $170 tonCO2–1.

Another promising strategy involves replacing OER with alternative, lower-energy oxidation reactions. Inspired by recent advancements in CO2 electroreduction47, substituting OER with GOR or other biomass oxidation reactions could reduce energy demand and produce value-added chemicals like dihydroxyacetone and glyceraldehyde. These reactions can also utilize economical catalysts to reduce stack cost54–56. Based on polarization data from the literature (Supplementary Tables S7, S8), employing GOR could lower capture cost by $119 tonCO2–1, even without accounting for the value of the chemical byproducts. In addition, transitioning from traditional stainless steel or titanium flow channels to cost-effective carbon composite materials51,57could reduce stack cost to $100 kW–1, saving $131 tonCO2–1 while maintaining comparable lifetimes. Extending stack lifetime from 20 to 40 years offers a further reduction of $65 tonCO2–1.

It is important to note that our eDAC system eliminates the need for an external air contactor. In conventional DAC systems, the air contactor represents a dominant capital expense58, yet most TEAs of electrochemical carbon capture do not account for this cost, nor do they consider the poor OH− utilization efficiency of air contactors, which lowers the electron efficiency of subsequent regeneration steps. To illustrate this, we estimated the CAPEX contribution of an air contactor using Carbon Engineering’s optimized design, which alone adds ~ $44 tonCO2–1 to the capture cost (Supplementary Note 4). Finally, our cost analysis does not account for potential financial incentives such as subsidies, tax benefits, or carbon credits, which could further improve the economic feasibility of the process. As an initial assessment, this TEA highlights the strong potential of our system as a competitive, scalable, and sustainable approach to carbon removal. With the proposed improvements, the cost of eDAC could be reduced to ~ $152 tonCO2–1.

Similar analyses were performed for ionomer-coated wafers used as interposers, yielding base capture costs of $436 tonCO2–1 for Sustainion-coated wafer and $460 tonCO2–1 for pDADMAC-coated wafer (Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Fig. 41, and Supplementary Note 5; parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S9). With the proposed improvements, these costs could be lowered to $116 tonCO2–1 and $124 tonCO2–1, respectively.

Electrochemical carbon capture is an emerging technology area with limited systematic durability studies, and extended operation at higher current densities and under accelerated stress conditions will be essential to rigorously validate and refine lifetime assumptions. The efficiencies, energy consumption, and operating conditions reported in this work are measured at the single capture‑cell and single regeneration‑cell level and do not fully capture the additional complexities of distributed DAC deployment, including stack‑scale flow distribution, environmental variability, and long‑term degradation. As such, the present TEA is intended primarily as a scenario‑based comparative analysis, designed to align with methodologies and assumptions commonly employed in the literature to allow a fair relative evaluation of the proposed eDAC approach, rather than as a direct prediction of performance in fully integrated, field‑deployed systems. A comprehensive installed system cost assessment would require a more detailed model that explicitly accounts for energy supply and management, scale, and site‑specific factors, and future work will need to couple stack‑scale experiments with detailed process and system modeling to more rigorously assess how these laboratory‑scale efficiencies translate to industrial‑scale DAC implementations. In addition to this literature-aligned TEA based on projected costs, we also conduct a separate analysis in which base CO2 capture costs are recalculated using cost inputs from the 2025 European Commission report59; for which the assumed balance-of-plant and electrolyzer capital costs are substantially higher than in our original model. The detailed methodology and results of this latter analysis are provided in Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. 42.

Discussion

In conclusion, we developed a robust electrochemical system that efficiently captures and concentrates atmospheric CO2. By generating hydroxide ions directly at the air-electrode interface, the system bypasses the kinetic limitations of bulk-phase solvents to enable more complete utilization of alkalinity. The introduction of a porous, ion-conductive interposer further extends the reactive absorption interface three-dimensionally and increases the residence time of hydroxide ions for more efficient capture. CO2 release is driven by an organic PCET mediator, allowing high-purity CO2 recovery under continuous operation for over 50 h. Together, these design innovations allow operation at current densities an order of magnitude higher than prior electrochemical DAC demonstrations while maintaining highly competitive energy consumption. The structured interposer, fabricated as a porous resin wafer, is also straightforward to scale, combining commercially available anion-exchange resins for ionic conductivity with a polymeric binder for mechanical stability, and produced via simple press-based methods.

Complementing these technical achievements, our multiphysics modeling framework provides critical insights into the coupled transport and reaction processes within the interposer, guiding optimization of its structure and operating parameters. TEA further outlines a realistic and actionable pathway to scalability, identifying key cost levers (current density, electricity price, and capture efficiency) and projecting capture costs below $152tonCO2–1 with targeted improvements.

Altogether, this study establishes a versatile, scalable electrochemical platform toward practical carbon removal. Future efforts will focus on interfacial engineering of ionomer-coated wafers, structural optimization of the interposer, and further development of PCET chemistry to maximize capture efficiency, minimize energy penalties, and enable scale-up for distributed DAC deployment.

Methods

Electrode preparation

For the preparation of the cathode GDE, 20 mg Pt/C (Fuel Cell Store) and 10 µL Nafion polymer binder solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 20%) were suspended in 10 mL isopropyl alcohol (ACS reagent, Fisher) to prepare a catalyst ink. The ink was sonicated for an hour and then spray-coated onto a 5 cm2 Sigracet 39BB gas diffusion layer (GDL, Fuel Cell Store) to obtain a loading of 0.5–0.6 mg cm−2 catalyst. Ir/C (Fuel Cell Store) anode was prepared in the same way. IrO2 anode was prepared following the procedure described in the literature60. Ti-mesh (9 cm2, 10 mil, Fuel Cell Store) was etched for 60 min in boiling 0.5 M oxalic acid (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the mesh was dipped into a 10 mL isopropyl alcohol solution with 10 vol% concentrated HCl (Sigma Aldrich) containing 30 mg of dissolved IrCl3·xH2O (Sigma Aldrich). This was followed by drying at 100 °C for 10 min and calcination at 500 °C for 10 min in air. The procedure was repeated to achieve a catalyst loading of ~ 1 mg cm−2.

Assembly of the eDAC cell

Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a BioLogic SP-150 workstation. The active electrode area of the eDAC cell was 5.0 cm2 unless otherwise specified. The cathode consisted of a Pt/C GDE with or without an interposer. A 0.01-inch-thick polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) gasket with a 5.0 cm2 window was placed between the GDE and the interposer. The interposer was secured using a fluorosilicone gasket (McMaster), with thickness controlled by using gaskets of varying thicknesses. Various interposer materials were carefully positioned within the gasket window to avoid overfilling, which could cause membrane damage. An AEM (Sustainion X37-50 RT, Dioxide Materials) was placed on top of the interposer. Prior to use, the AEM was conditioned by soaking overnight in 1 M KOH and subsequently rinsed three times with deionized (DI) water, then immediately assembled into the cell61. The anode for AQDS oxidation was Sigracet 28 AA carbon paper (Fuel Cell Store), which was used without any treatment. All components of the electrochemical cells were mechanically compressed together using four pairs of nuts and bolts with a torque wrench (Vibrelli) set at 2.5 Nm.

Assembly of the PCET mediator regeneration cell

Ti mesh containing IrO2 catalyst as described above was used as the anode. Two layers of Sigracet 28 AA carbon paper (6.25 cm2) were used as the cathode. The electrodes were separated by a Nafion 117 (Ion Power) CEM. All components were secured using PTFE gaskets (0.01”, McMaster).

Electrochemical CO2 capture and release

All gas flow rates were precisely controlled using MFCs (Alicat Scientific). To quantify CO2 removal, the cathode outlet gas stream was passed through a desiccant-filled vial (Sigma-Aldrich) to remove moisture before being directed to a nondispersive infrared CO2 sensor (CO2meter.com). For experiments focused on optimizing the interposer design, OER at the anode was catalyzed by an Ir/C electrode, and a 0.1 M K2SO4 solution was circulated at a rate of 5 mL min−1 using a peristaltic pump (Ismatec Reglo). The cathode gas flow rate was maintained at 100 sccm.

To quantify CO2 release, as depicted in Fig. 3a, a 60 mL solution of 0.2 M AQDS was electrochemically reduced to 50% state of charge paired with OER (0.1 M K2SO4 on the anode side) under a constant current of 50 mA for approximately 3 hours. The partially reduced AQDS/AQDSH2 solution was then circulated through the CO2 capture cell at 5 mL min−1. The headspace of the PCET mediator tank was connected in series to a desiccant-filled vial, an MFM, and a CO2 sensor. The tank was purged with a gas mixture of 20% CO2 in argon at a flow rate of 5 mL min−1. The cathode gas flow rate was set at 200 sccm for all the experiments presented in Fig. 3.

For long-term continuous capture and release testing presented in Fig. 4, a configuration similar to that in Fig. 3 was employed with slight modifications. The cathode outlet of the PCET mediator regeneration cell was connected to the anode inlet of the CO2 capture cell, and the anode outlet of the CO2 capture cell was connected to the PCET mediator tank. After allowing time for the sensors and MFM to stabilize, the current of the CO2 capture cell was sequentially ramped: first 10 mA, then 25 mA (each for 10 min), before reaching the target 50 mA. The PCET mediator regeneration cell was activated one hour after starting the current in the CO2 capture cell.

CO2 sensor data were collected in real time at an interval of one second using a custom LabVIEW 2021 program. MFM data were collected using FlowVision software (Alicat) at an interval of one second. Integrations for calculating CO2 capture and release efficiency were performed using MATLAB.

Fabrication of the resin wafer

Resin wafers were fabricated using Amberlyst A26 anion exchange resin beads (OH− form, Sigma-Aldrich), which were first ground into a fine powder. NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich) was chosen as the porogen for its high solubility in water to facilitate easy removal after wafer fabrication. NaCl crystals were also ground to control pore size. Low-density polyethylene (Thermo Fisher) served as the binder. Unless otherwise specified, the ground resin, binder, and porogen were mixed in a 2:1:1 mass ratio and further ground together to ensure homogeneity. The resulting mixture was packed into a PTFE mold (typically 5 × 5 × 0.08 cm) and hot-pressed at 100–115 °C under a load of 2.5 metric tonnes for 1.5 h. After cooling for 2 h, a free-standing wafer was obtained.

The wafer was cut into a 5 cm2 square to fit the gasket window of the eDAC cell. To remove the prorogen and generate the porous structure, the wafer was rinsed three times in deionized water, with each rinse accompanied by 30 min of sonication. After porogen removal, the wafer was anion-exchanged from OH− to CO32− by soaking in 1 M K2CO3 using the same rinse and sonication protocol. Finally, the wafer was washed with deionized water to remove excess CO32− ions before use. For the ionomer-coated wafer (Fig. 4i–k), a 5 mg mL–1 solution of Sustainion XA-9 ionomer (Dioxide Materials) in ethanol was prepared and drop cast onto a dry wafer to achieve a loading of 0.5 mg cm–2. pDADMAC (20% solution, Ambeed) was dispersed in ethanol and drop cast onto a dry wafer to achieve an active content loading of 0.5 mg cm–2.

Characterizations

3D X-ray computed tomography (µCT) images were obtained with 3.41 micron/pixel resolution using an RX Solutions EasyTom 150/160 MicroCT. The beam energy for all measurements was 60 keV. The full reconstructed image for each resin wafer was first binarized to separate voids and polymer structure using the Trainable Weka Segmentation machine learning algorithm62. Each reconstruction was trained on at least 5 images. Multiple rounds of training were not required. 3D volumes were visualized by Volume Viewer of Fiji. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted on a Thermofisher Helios G4 DualBeam instrument. All samples were sputtered with 10 nm of Pt prior to SEM imaging.

Numerical methods

Multiphysics simulations were conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 (COMSOL Inc.). A detailed description of the equations and boundary conditions can be found in Supplementary Note 1. The equations were solved using the MUMPS general solver. The mesh was configured with a maximum element size of 1 μm to 3.5 μm, incorporating two-fold boundary refinements on the left and right boundaries of the gas channel and the two aqueous layers. The number of mesh elements varied from 8182 to 30,661, corresponding to 15,686 to 57,112 degrees of freedom, depending on the porosity and length of the interposer in the model. Simulations were run on an Intel Xeon W5 system with 12 CPU cores and 128 GB RAM. Under these conditions, typical computation times ranged from 3 to 4 h, with no optimizations applied.
