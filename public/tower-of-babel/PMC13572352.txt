PARK2 Inhibits the progression of colorectal cancer via ubiquitinating MARCKSL1 to inhibit the c-Myc pathway
Authors: Jian Lin, Xiaobin Cheng
Published: 2026

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with patient prognosis significantly worsening once the disease progresses to advanced stages or metastasis [1]. A major driver of CRC tumorigenesis is the c-Myc oncogene, a transcription factor that is aberrantly activated in up to 80% of sporadic CRC cases [2]. c-Myc plays a pivotal role in colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression by promoting cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming; indeed, the pathological consequences of tumor suppressor loss in the intestine largely depend on c-Myc upregulation and its downstream target gene expression [2]. Given its central role in CRC, the c-Myc pathway represents an attractive therapeutic target, but direct inhibition of c-Myc has proven challenging. Therefore, identifying regulatory mechanisms that restrain c-Myc activity in CRC is of high importance.

E3 ubiquitin ligases have emerged as critical modulators of oncogenic pathways by targeting specific substrates for proteasomal degradation. Parkin, encoded by PARK2, is an RBR family E3 ubiquitin ligase originally implicated in autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson’s disease [3]. Mounting evidence indicates that Parkin also functions as a tumor suppressor in various cancers, including renal cancer [4], cervical cancer [5] and lung cancer [6]. The human PARK2 gene resides on chromosome 6q25-27, a region frequently lost or downregulated in malignancies [3]. In CRC, PARK2 deletions and reduced expression have been reported, and PARK2 haploinsufficiency can cooperate with adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) mutations to accelerate intestinal tumorigenesis [3]. PARK2’s tumor-suppressive effects are thought to stem from its ability to ubiquitinate oncogenic proteins. For example, PARK2 has been shown to promote the degradation of cell cycle regulators such as Cyclin E [7]. In breast cancer and other contexts, PARK2 targets the hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α for ubiquitination, thereby limiting metastasis [3]. PARK2 loss, conversely, leads to accumulation of such substrates and can enhance tumor growth and invasive potential. However, the spectrum of PARK2’s substrates in cancer remains incompletely understood, and particularly in CRC, the key effectors through which Parkin exerts its tumor-suppressive role are still being elucidated.

Myristoylated alanine-rich C-kinase substrate like 1 (MARCKSL1, also known as MRP or MLP) is a notable candidate oncogenic protein that may be regulated by Parkin in CRC. MARCKSL1 is a membrane-associated protein involved in actin cytoskeletal regulation, cell motility, and signal transduction pathways downstream of protein kinase C (PKC) [8, 9]. High expression of MARCKSL1 has been observed in a broad range of cancer types, where it often correlates with enhanced cell migration and invasive behavior [10]. In CRC patients, recent clinical evidence indicates that elevated MARCKSL1 levels (for instance, in circulating extracellular vesicles) are associated with metastatic disease and poorer outcomes [1]. These findings suggest that MARCKSL1 may act as a pro-metastatic factor and a negative prognostic marker in CRC. Nevertheless, the mechanistic role of MARCKSL1 in CRC progression and its interplay with major oncogenic pathways such as c-Myc have not been fully characterized.

Here, we identify MARCKSL1 as a critical downstream target of PARK2 in CRC. Through an unbiased screening approach, MARCKSL1 emerged as a candidate gene whose expression is inversely associated with PARK2 activity. We show that PARK2 physically interacts with MARCKSL1 and promotes its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Functionally, MARCKSL1 drives CRC cell proliferation, migration, and in vitro tumorigenic properties, whereas PARK2-mediated loss of MARCKSL1 leads to suppression of these phenotypes. Importantly, we find that MARCKSL1 supports the c-Myc oncogenic pathway in CRC cells, and that Parkin’s tumor-suppressive effect is achieved by inhibiting this c-Myc pathway via MARCKSL1 degradation. Our study uncovers a novel PARK2/MARCKSL1/c-Myc signaling axis in CRC and provides a molecular mechanism by which PARK2 constrains CRC progression. These findings suggest that restoring PARK2 function or targeting the MARCKSL1/c-Myc axis could be promising strategies for CRC therapy.

Materials and methods

The antibody, experiment chemicals, shRNA, and primer sequence used in this study are listed on Table S1-S4.

Western blotting

After electrophoresis on 8% or 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), proteins were electro-transferred onto PVDF membranes (Roche, Shanghai, China). Membranes were then blocked for 20 min with a protein-free rapid blocking reagent (Epizyme, Shanghai, China), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h and visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence exposure to X-ray film. Details of all antibodies used are provided in Table S1, and uncropped blot images together with the SDS-PAGE gel are supplied in Supplementary Material 1.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

After removing the culture medium, cells were fixed using an immunostaining fixative (P0098, Beyotime), rinsed with immunostaining wash buffer (P0106, Beyotime), and then blocked with blocking solution (P0102, Beyotime). Following blocking, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibody. Subsequently, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied under light-protected conditions, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The primary and secondary antibodies for IF are provided in Table S1.

Cell lines and culture

Seven human colorectal-cancer lines—HCT116, SW480, DLD1, Caco-2, RKO, SW620 and HT29—and the normal colon epithelial line NCM460 were purchased from ATCC. Each line was propagated in the recommended base medium (DMEM or RPMI-1640) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. STR profiling confirmed cell identity, and routine screening verified absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paired CRC and adjacent mucosal specimens were obtained under approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval No. 2024 − 1081). After deparaffinization, rehydration and heat-induced antigen retrieval, 4 μm sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and signals were developed with DAB. Staining intensity was quantified by ImageJ coupled with the IHC Profiler plug-in (https://sourceforge.net/projects/ihcprofiler/). Raw data are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Molecular docking

Search for and obtain the corresponding protein structure (PARK2 and MARCKSL1) in the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb). The two target protein structures obtained were simultaneously input into the GRAMM system. The platform’s default protein-protein docking parameters were used to start the calculation and output the top ten docking results. Taking the binding energy as the core evaluation index (the general criterion is that the binding energy is less than − 4 kcal/mol, and the lower the value, the more stable the binding system), while binding the surface area of the protein interaction region, hydrogen bond interaction and key amino groups. The optimal docking conformation was analyzed in depth based on characteristics such as acid residues. After docking, the binding free energy was calculated using PDBePISA and visually analyzed using Pymol 3.1. The results of the connection process are detailed in Supplementary material 3.

Survival and prognostic analysis

In silico analysis of MARCKSL1 and PARK2 expression in larger CRC cohorts was conducted using the TCGA colorectal adenocarcinoma dataset. Correlations between MARCKSL1 expression and clinicopathological parameters (e.g. TNM stage) were examined by Pearson’s χ² test, and Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between PARK2 and MARCKSL1 transcript levels in tumors.

For post-progression survival analysis of PARK2 in CRC, the KMplot online platform (Kaplan-Meier Plotter, Colon module; https://kmplot.com/analysis/) was used. This database integrates gene expression profiling data and clinical outcome information from multiple publicly available GEO datasets. PARK2 expression was used as the variable of interest, and patients were automatically stratified into high and low expression groups using the best-performing threshold determined by the platform’s built-in auto-select cutoff algorithm. All available samples in the KMplot Colon dataset were included in the analysis without manual exclusion. Kaplan-Meier post-progression survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated and reported directly by the platform (HR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.52–0.95, log-rank P = 0.019).

Correlation analysis between PARK2/MARCKSL1 and the six-gene signature

To investigate the transcriptional relationship between PARK2 or MARCKSL1 expression and a six-gene c-Myc target signature (PLK1, MCM5, SRM, PUS1, SLC29A2, and SLC19A1), correlation analyses were performed in the TCGA-COAD/READ cohort and four independent GEO colorectal cancer datasets (GSE39582, GSE106582, GSE161158, and GSE156451). For each dataset, gene expression data were log₂-transformed, and the six-gene signature score was calculated as the mean log2(TPM) of the six genes. Pearson correlation coefficients (R) and corresponding P-values were computed between the signature score and the log2-transformed expression of PARK2 or MARCKSL1, respectively. Linear regression lines with 95% confidence intervals were added to all scatter plots for visualization. All analyses were performed in R using the ggplot2 and ggpubr packages. The complete R code and processed expression matrices used for these analyses are provided in Supplementary Material 4.

Search for the potential upstream E3 of MARCKSL1

The potential interacting proteins of MARCKSL1 were identified by the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/), and the potential E3 protein of MARCKSL1 was identified by the UbiBrowser database (http://ubibrowser.bio-it.cn/ubibrowser_v3/). Furthermore, the expression levels of the intersection genes in CRC were analyzed using the GEPIA database (https://gepia3.bioinfoliu.com/).

Plasmids, sh-RNA and transfection

The human PARK2 coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector with an N-terminal Myc epitope tag. Human MARCKSL1 cDNA was cloned into pcDNA3.1 with a FLAG tag. Short hairpin RNAs (sh-RNAs) targeting MARCKSL1 and PARK2 (sh-MARCKSL1 and sh-PARK2) and a non-targeting negative control sh-RNA (sh-NC) were obtained from Qingke Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). PARK2 and MARCKSL1 mutant plasmids were constructed using the KOD-Plus-Mutagenesis kit (TOYOBO) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All constructs were validated using DNA sequencing. Primers and sh-RNA sequence used in this study are provided in Table S3. For transient transfection, the cells at ~ 70% confluence were transfected with plasmids or sh-RNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Stable knockdown of MARCKSL1 and PARK2 were achieved by transducing cells with lentiviral shRNA constructs (pLKO.1-puro vector) containing sh-RNA sequences against MARCKSL1 and PARK2. Puromycin selection (2 µg/ml) for 7 days yielded polyclonal knockdown cell lines.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

To evaluate proliferation, CCK-8 reagent was added to 96-well plates seeded with 2 × 10³ transfected or knockdown CRC cells, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded every 24 h for 5 days. For long-term growth, 500 cells were plated in 6-well plates; after 10–14 days, colonies were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde), stained with crystal violet, and enumerated. Triplicate wells were analyzed for each condition. Relative colony numbers were calculated versus control, and statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test.

Cell Migration and invasion assays

Cell motility was assessed by wound-closure and Transwell assays. For wound-closure assays, confluent monolayers were pre-treated with mitomycin C (10 µg/mL) for 2 h to inhibit cell proliferation prior to scratch formation. A linear scratch was then created using a sterile pipette tip, plates were rinsed with PBS to remove detached cells, and maintained in serum-free medium. Gap width was recorded at 0 and 48 h, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated. For Transwell migration, 5 × 10⁴ serum-starved cells were seeded into bare 8 μm inserts (Corning), whereas invasion inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD). A 10% FBS gradient served as chemoattractant. Following 24 h incubation, non-invading cells were wiped away, membranes were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and five random fields per insert were counted. Each condition was run in triplicate.

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT kit (Vazyme, R433-01). qRT-PCR primer sequences are listed in Table S4, and the cycling protocol followed the method described in reference [11].

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed in IP buffer (Beyotime), cleared by centrifugation, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibody. The immune complexes were captured with protein A/G magnetic beads (pre-washed in IP buffer) for 2 h, washed twice with the same buffer and once with water, then eluted by boiling in 1×SDS loading buffer for 10 min prior to 8% or 10% SDS-PAGE gels. For Co-IP immunoblotting, the immunoprecipitating antibody was rabbit IgG, while the immunoblot primary antibody was mouse IgG and detection was performed using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L); therefore, rabbit IgG heavy/light chains from the IP antibody were not detected.

GST pull-down assays

GST-tagged bait was captured on Glutathione–Sepharose 4B beads (Amersham), pre-equilibrated in binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM MgCl₂, 1 µg ml⁻¹ leupeptin). After 2 h incubation with recombinant prey, the resin was washed five times with the same buffer, boiled in SDS sample buffer, and analysed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

Protein stability (cycloheximide chase) assays

To assess MARCKSL1 and c-Myc protein stability, cycloheximide (CHX, Sigma) chase experiments were performed. CRC cells transduced with vector control, sh-PARK2, or sh-MARCKSL1 were treated with CHX (100 µg/ml) to halt new protein synthesis. Cells were harvested at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h after CHX addition. MARCKSL1 and c-Myc levels at each time point were measured by Western blotting as above, and band intensities were normalized to GAPDH. The protein half-life of MARCKSL1 and c-Myc were estimated by plotting the remaining proteins (percentage of time 0) against time and fitting an exponential decay curve.

Ubiquitination assay

In vivo ubiquitination assay: To detect intracellular MARCKSL1 ubiquitination, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with Flag-MARCKSL1, HA-ubiquitin (HA-Ub), and the indicated Myc-PARK2 constructs or shRNA plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM) for 8 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation and allow accumulation of ubiquitinated species. Cells were then harvested under denaturing conditions by lysing in 1% SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA) and boiling for 10 min to disrupt non-covalent protein interactions. Lysates were diluted 10-fold with non-denaturing IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA) prior to immunoprecipitation with anti-Flag magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with IP buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer for 10 min. Ubiquitinated MARCKSL1 was detected by immunoblotting with anti-HA antibody.

In vitro ubiquitination assay: Reconstituted cell-free ubiquitination reactions were performed using the Ubiquitinylation Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. 20440ES10) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, purified recombinant His-MARCKSL1 (2 µg) was incubated with His-PARK2-WT or His-PARK2-ΔUBL (1 µg) as the E3 ligase, together with human recombinant E1 (UBE1, 100 nM), E2 (UBE2L3, 500 nM), ubiquitin (25 µM), and ATP (2 mM) in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM DTT) in a total volume of 50 µL. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h with gentle agitation. To enrich ubiquitinated MARCKSL1, the reaction mixture was diluted with 200 µL of IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA) and then incubated with anti-MARCKSL1 antibody pre-conjugated to protein A/G magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. Following five washes with IP buffer, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer for 5 min and resolved by SDS-PAGE. Ubiquitinated MARCKSL1 was detected by immunoblotting with anti-ubiquitin antibody. For input control, an aliquot (10 µL) of the reaction mixture was mixed with 1× SDS loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, and analyzed by Coomassie blue staining to confirm equivalent loading of all recombinant proteins.

Xenograft tumor model

Animal protocols were approved by the IACUC of Hangzhou Inch Biotech (INCH-IACUC-2024-12-123). Five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from the Nanjing Medical University Animal Center for the establishment of subcutaneous tumor xenografts. For the xenograft model, 1 × 106 transfected HCT116 cells suspended in 100 µL of PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right flanks of the mice (n = 5 per group). Tumor dimensions were measured using digital calipers every three days starting from the day when tumors became palpable. Tumor volume was calculated using the modified ellipsoid formula: V = (length × width2) / 2, where length represents the longest tumor diameter and width represents the shortest tumor diameter. Tumor volume growth curves were plotted over time for each group. At the experimental endpoint four weeks post-injection, mice were sacrificed, tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed using an analytical balance. Tumor tissues were subsequently subjected to IHC staining.

Human specimens

Thirty paired CRC and adjacent normal specimens were obtained from surgical patients at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University. Fresh samples were snap-frozen at − 80 °C within 30 min of resection. The study protocol (2024 − 1081) was approved by the local Human Ethics Committee, and written consent was obtained from each participant; individuals who had received pre-operative chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy were excluded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified, and a two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. For experiments involving two factors (e.g., tumor volume growth curves over time), two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Tumor volume data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and compared between groups using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. The optimal cutoff value for stratifying patients into high and low expression groups was determined using the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between continuous variables, including mRNA expression levels of genes across patient datasets and IHC scores in clinical specimens. For identification of differentially expressed genes in single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE146771 and GSE166555), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat R package. Associations between MARCKSL1 expression and clinicopathological parameters (e.g., TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, mucinous classification) were evaluated using Pearson’s χ² test. Statistical significance thresholds are indicated in each figure as follows: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

Results

MARCKSL1 was screened and identified as abnormally high expression in CRC and positively associated with poor prognosis

By screening for abnormally highly expressed genes in two CRC single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data and two RNA-seq data (Fig. 1.A-D), we identified 73 potential oncogenes that were highly expressed in CRC (Figure S1.A). Further, we conducted survival analysis on these genes. The data indicated that MARCKSL1 and SULT2B1 were closely related to poor prognosis in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) (Figure S1.B). Furthermore, protein-level differential analysis using the CPTAC dataset demonstrated that MARCKSL1 exhibited a greater magnitude of upregulation in primary CRC compared to adjacent normal tissue than SULT2B1, as reflected by both a larger expression fold change and a more significant P-value (Fig. 1.E). Consistently, immunohistochemical staining on a tissue microarray showed strong MARCKSL1 protein expression in the majority of colorectal carcinomas, whereas normal colonic epithelium exhibited only faint or no staining (Figure S2.A–B). We then evaluated whether MARCKSL1 overexpression correlates with clinical outcomes. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis in GEO database (GSE159216 and GSE72969) demonstrated that CRC patients with MARCKSL1-high expression had markedly shorter overall survival than those with MARCKSL1-low expression (Fig. 1.F). Moreover, scRNA-seq data show that MARCKSL1 is specifically highly expressed in stem cells of CRC tissues, suggesting its cancer-promoting effect (Fig. 1.G). Further IHC data, qRT-PCR and Western blotting experiments confirmed the high expression of MARCKSL1 in CRC in mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1.H–N). Moreover, high MARCKSL1 mRNA levels were positively associated with advanced tumor stage and lymph node metastasis at diagnosis (Figure S2.C–G). Consistent with this, at the protein level, MARCKSL1 were positively associated with advanced tumor stage (Figure S2.H). Interestingly, MARCKSL1 may also have a close relationship with the mucinous classification of CRC (Figure S2.I). These data indicate that MARCKSL1 upregulation is a common event in CRC and is linked to aggressive disease and poor prognosis, supporting the notion that MARCKSL1 may function as an oncogenic driver in colorectal tumor progression.

MARCKSL1 promotes the progression of CRC in vivo and in vitro

In this study, we selected SW620 and HCT116 cell lines for functional and mechanistic research due to their high MARCKSL1 expression levels, as shown in the Fig. 1.J, K. SW620 is derived from metastatic CRC [12], while HCT116 is from a primary CRC tumor [13], making them relevant models for studying MARCKSL1 in tumor progression and metastasis. Additionally, SW620 is known for its strong migratory and invasive properties, ideal for examining MARCKSL1’s role in cell migration, whereas HCT116 is frequently used in oncogene studies, helping us explore its interaction with other cancer-related genes.

To clarify the oncogenic phenotype of MARCKSL1, we performed MARCKSL1 knockdown on HCT116 and SW620 (Fig. 2.A, B). Subsequent CCK8 experiments and clone formation experiments demonstrated that MARCKSL1 could significantly promote the proliferation ability of CRC cells (Fig. 2.C–F). Meanwhile, the Transwell assay and scratch assay demonstrated that MARCKSL1 could enhance the migration and invasion abilities of CRC cells (Fig. 2.G–J). To exclude the possibility that the observed differences in migration and invasion were attributable to alterations in cell viability rather than genuine changes in cell motility, CCK-8 assays were performed under identical treatment conditions at the 24 h time point. No statistically significant differences in cell viability were detected between sh-NC, sh-MARCKSL1#1, and sh-MARCKSL1#2 groups in either HCT116 or SW620 cells (all comparisons P > 0.05, Figure S3.A, B), confirming that the pro-migratory and pro-invasive effects of MARCKSL1 are independent of cell proliferation or survival. Further xenograft tumor model in mice demonstrated that MARCKSL1 could promote the progression of CRC in vivo (Fig. 2.K–M). The IHC staining of the corresponding mouse CRC tissues indicated that the knockdown of MARCKL1 was accompanied by a weakened proliferation ability of CRC cells (Fig. 2.N–O).

Identify PARK2 as an interacting protein of MARCKSL1

To further clarify whether the tumor-promoting effect of MARCKSL1 in CRC is regulated by E3 ubiquitin ligases proteins, we used BioGRID and UbiBrowser databases for screening (Fig. 3.A). The data after intersection shows that BRCA1, CCAR1, PARK2, RNF183 and TRIM24 were identified as potential MARCKSL1 interacting proteins (Fig. 3.A). Further, based on the expression of these genes in CRC, we found that the expression of PARK2 in CRC was abnormally low (Fig. 3.B). Subsequently, our IHC experiments, survival curve analysis and expression difference analysis indicated that PARK2 protein was lowly expressed in CRC and was a tumor suppressor factor (Fig. 3.C–F). Coincidently, previous studies have shown that PARK2 acts as a tumor suppressor gene and is abnormally lowly expressed in CRC [14, 15]. To explore the potential mechanism underlying PARK2 downregulation in CRC, we analyzed the relationship between PARK2 promoter methylation and its mRNA expression using the cBioPortal database. A significant negative correlation was observed between PARK2 methylation levels and PARK2 mRNA expression in CRC (R = − 0.333, P < 0.0001, Figure S4.A), suggesting that promoter hypermethylation may represent a key epigenetic mechanism contributing to PARK2 transcriptional silencing in CRC.

To further investigate the physical interaction between PARK2 and MARCKSL1, Co-IP assays were conducted. Transfection of plasmids with distinct tags for PARK2 and MARCKSL1 validated the interaction between exogenous PARK2 and MARCKSL1 (Fig. 3.G). Additionally, compared to the IgG control group, the results showed a significant binding affinity between endogenous PARK2 and MARCKSL1 (Fig. 3.H). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed the co-localization of PARK2 and MARCKSL1 in the cytoplasm of CRC cells (Fig. 3.I).

To further delineate the specific domain of PARK2 responsible for MARCKSL1 binding, we generated three truncation mutants based on the PARK2 domain architecture and performed Co-IP assays. The results demonstrated that the region spanning amino acids 141–293 of PARK2 is required for its interaction with MARCKSL1, whereas the UBL domain is dispensable for this binding (Fig. 3.J, K). To dissect the individual contributions of the RING0 and RING1 domains within this region, we subsequently constructed three additional deletion mutants: ΔRING0, ΔRING1, and ΔRING0 + ΔRING1. Co-IP assays revealed that deletion of the RING1 domain, but not the RING0 domain, markedly abolished the interaction between PARK2 and MARCKSL1, identifying RING1 as the key binding determinant (Fig. 3.L). To validate this finding under cell-free conditions, GST pull-down assays were performed, which further confirmed that the RING1 domain of PARK2 is required for direct interaction with MARCKSL1 in vitro (Figure S5.A). Besides, we created three mutant plasmids based on the MARCKSL1 protein sequence, with the results showing that fragments 2 and 3 of MARCKSL1 bind to PARK2 (Fig. 3.M, N). Further, we adopted the GRAMM platform to conduct the rigid docking experiment of the PARK2 and MARCKSL1 proteins. Data show that glutamine (Q)-40, Q-71, histidine (H)-265, leucine (L)-287 and L-107 on PARK2 are closely connected with glutamic acid (E)-175, Q-172, proline (P)-171, E-166 and E-123 on MARCKSL1, respectively (Fig. 3.O). Further, we performed point mutations on these amino acids (Q-40, Q-71, H-265, L-287 and L-107) in PARK2 to convert them into Alanine (A), and it was named PARK2-MUT1. The subsequent GST pull-down assays confirmed that these five amino acids on PARK2 are key to interacting with MARCKSL1(Fig. 3.P).

PARK2 induces the protein proteasomal degradation and K48-linked ubiquitination of MARCKSL1

To explore whether PARK2 influences MARCKSL1 expression, we first examined the effect of PARK2 overexpression. Our results showed that increasing PARK2 expression in a gradient manner significantly reduced MARCKSL1 levels in both HCT116 and SW620 cells (Fig. 4.A). Conversely, silencing PARK2 using specific shRNAs led to a notable increase in MARCKSL1 protein levels in these cell lines (Fig. 4.B). However, changes in PARK2 expression did not affect MARCKSL1 mRNA levels, suggesting that PARK2 may regulate MARCKSL1 protein stability via post-transcriptional mechanisms (Figure S4.B, C). Next, we employed proteasome inhibitors (MG132 and Bortezomib) and a lysosomal inhibitor (Chloroquine) to investigate the degradation pathway through which PARK2 regulates MARCKSL1. The results indicated that PARK2-mediated MARCKSL1 degradation occurs through the proteasomal pathway (Fig. 4.C). Following the addition of CHX, a protein synthesis inhibitor, we found that PARK2 knockdown in CRC cells reduced the degradation rate of MARCKSL1, supporting the idea that PARK2 enhances MARCKSL1 degradation (Fig. 4.D–F).

Additionally, considering the E3 ligase activity of PARK2, we observed that reduced PARK2 levels led to decreased ubiquitination of MARCKSL1, whereas higher PARK2 levels increased MARCKSL1 ubiquitination (Fig. 4.G–H). To further delineate the structural requirements for PARK2-mediated MARCKSL1 ubiquitination, we performed domain-specific ubiquitination assays. In vivo ubiquitination assays demonstrated that Myc-PARK2-ΔUBL completely failed to promote MARCKSL1 ubiquitination in HEK293T cells, in contrast to Myc-PARK2-WT (Figure S5.B). This result was independently confirmed in a reconstituted cell-free system using purified recombinant proteins: His-PARK2-WT, but not His-PARK2-ΔUBL, efficiently catalyzed polyubiquitination of His-MARCKSL1 in the presence of E1, E2, ubiquitin (Ub), and ATP (Figure S5.C). Notably, although ΔUBL-PARK2 loses ubiquitination activity, it retains the ability to bind MARCKSL1 through the RING1 domain (Fig. 3.K–L), indicating that the UBL domain is specifically required for catalytic competence rather than substrate recognition.

We then generated mutants of ubiquitin, converting lysine (K) residues to arginine (R) (K6R, K11R, K27R, K29R, K33R, K48R, K63R) and to other residues (K6O, K11O, K27O, K29O, K33O, K48O, K63O). Our findings revealed that PARK2 predominantly mediates K48-linked ubiquitination of MARCKSL1 (Fig. 4.I–J). Furthermore, based on predictions from the PhosphoSitePlus and GPS-Uber databases, we identified potential ubiquitination sites on MARCKSL1 at K25 and K157 (Fig. 4.K). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that PARK2 facilitates the proteasomal degradation of MARCKSL1 in CRC cells through K48-linked ubiquitination.

PARK2-MARCKSL1 regulates the c-Myc pathway to affect CRC progression

To investigate the role of PARK2-mediated MARCKSL1 ubiquitination in CRC progression, we performed a bioinformatics analysis using the TCGA database. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed a significant association between the activation of the c-Myc pathway and reduced PARK2 expression, accompanied by increased MARCKSL1 levels. This finding was supported by two independent gene sets related to the c-Myc pathway (Fig. 5.A). Within these c-Myc-associated gene sets, we identified the top six gene (PLK1, MCM5, SRM, PUS1, SLC29A2, and SLC19A1) linked to downregulated PARK2 based on their rank metric scores (Fig. 5.B). Our GSEA analysis demonstrated a strong correlation between these genes and the upregulation of MARCKSL1 (Fig. 5.B). To validate these findings, we quantified the mRNA levels of these genes through qRT-PCR following PARK2 knockdown or MARCKSL1 overexpression (MARCKSL1-OE), with the results aligning closely with the GSEA data (Fig. 5.C). Further expression correlation analysis in TCGA database also confirmed this trend (Fig. 5.D–E). To further validate the robustness of these correlations across independent patient cohorts, we extended our analysis to four additional colorectal cancer datasets from the GEO database (GSE39582, GSE106582, GSE161158, and GSE156451). Correlation analyses were performed between the mRNA expression levels of PARK2 and MARCKSL1 and the six-gene signature in each dataset. The results consistently demonstrated significant negative correlations between PARK2 expression and the six-gene signature, and significant positive correlations between MARCKSL1 expression and the six-gene signature, across the majority of the validated cohorts (Figure S6.A–D). Notably, while the PARK2 correlation in GSE106582 did not reach statistical significance (R = − 0.21, P = 0.066), possibly attributable to the relatively small sample size and potential batch effects inherent to this dataset, the correlation direction remained consistent with all other cohorts. Additionally, data from the CPTAC database revealed changes in MARCKSL1 protein levels in CRC patients with altered c-Myc pathway activation (Fig. 5.F). We next assessed c-Myc protein levels by Western blotting, demonstrating that PARK2 negatively regulates c-Myc expression, while MARCKSL1 positively modulates it. Notably, the effect of PARK2 on c-Myc was primarily dependent on MARCKSL1, although a minor MARCKSL1-independent component may also contribute (Fig. 5.G, H). Co-IP assays confirmed an interaction between MARCKSL1 and c-Myc (Fig. 5.I). Following CHX treatment, we observed that MARCKSL1 reduced the degradation rate of c-Myc, thus enhancing its stability (Fig. 5.J, K).

To further investigate the functional significance of the PARK2/MARCKSL1/c-Myc axis in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using five experimental groups: sh-NC + EV, sh-PARK2 + EV, sh-PARK2 + MARCKSL1-OE, sh-PARK2 + MARCKSL1-OE + sh-c-Myc, and sh-c-Myc + EV (Fig. 5.L). Tumor growth curves and endpoint tumor weights demonstrated that sh-PARK2 significantly promoted tumor growth compared to sh-NC, while additional MARCKSL1 overexpression further accelerated tumor progression (Fig. 5.M, N). Notably, genetic silencing of c-Myc in the sh-PARK2 + MARCKSL1-OE background completely reversed this tumor-promoting effect, reducing tumor growth to levels comparable to the sh-c-Myc + EV group, with no statistically significant difference between these two groups (Fig. 5.L–N). These results provide definitive in vivo genetic evidence that the oncogenic function of MARCKSL1 is dependent on c-Myc.

To further validate the knockdown and rescue efficiency in the xenograft tumors, we performed qRT-PCR analysis using individual tumor samples from each group and western blotting analysis using tumor lysates from the indicated groups. These analyses confirmed efficient PARK2 knockdown, robust MARCKSL1 overexpression, and effective c-Myc knockdown in the corresponding xenograft tumors (Figure S7.A–D). Consistently, IHC staining of tumor tissues further confirmed the expected modulation of PARK2, MARCKSL1, and c-Myc protein expression across the five groups (Fig. 5.O). Furthermore, IHC analysis of CRC patient tissues revealed a significant negative correlation between PARK2 and MARCKSL1 protein expression, and a significant positive correlation between MARCKSL1 and c-Myc protein expression, confirming the clinical relevance of the PARK2/MARCKSL1/c-Myc signaling axis in human CRC (Fig. 5.P–Q).

Discussion

In this study, we define a previously unrecognized ubiquitin-dependent checkpoint that restrains CRC progression: the E3 ligase PARK2 directly targets the MARCKSL1 protein for K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby attenuating c-Myc pathway output. This model is supported by multi-layer evidence spanning patient datasets and specimens (high MARCKSL1 associates with poor survival and aggressive clinical features, while PARK2 is reduced in CRC), mechanistic biochemistry (PARK2-MARCKSL1 binding, K48-linked ubiquitination, proteasome dependence), and functional validation (MARCKSL1 promotes proliferation/migration/invasion and tumor growth; c-Myc inhibition counteracts phenotypes driven by PARK2 loss and/or MARCKSL1 gain). Furthermore, bioinformatics analysis from the cBioPortal database revealed a significant negative correlation between PARK2 promoter methylation levels and its mRNA expression in CRC, consistent with previous reports implicating epigenetic silencing as a driver of PARK2 loss in multiple cancer types. This finding suggests that epigenetic reactivation of PARK2 may represent a potential therapeutic strategy in CRC.

It is noteworthy that quantitative analysis of c-Myc protein levels revealed a minor but statistically significant residual elevation in the sh-PARK2/sh-MARCKSL1 double-knockdown condition compared to the sh-MARCKSL1 single-knockdown group, suggesting that a small MARCKSL1-independent component contributes to PARK2-mediated c-Myc regulation. This observation is biologically plausible given the multi-substrate nature of PARK2 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase. Previous studies have established that PARK2 promotes the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of HIF-1α [3] and Cyclin E [16]. HIF-1α has been shown to transcriptionally activate c-Myc expression under hypoxic conditions, while Cyclin E/CDK2-mediated phosphorylation of c-Myc at Ser-62 stabilizes the protein by protecting it from proteasomal degradation [17]. Loss of PARK2-mediated degradation of these substrates upon PARK2 silencing may therefore provide an additional, MARCKSL1-independent route through which PARK2 loss sustains c-Myc accumulation. Importantly, however, the magnitude of this residual effect is substantially smaller than the dominant MARCKSL1-dependent component, reinforcing the conclusion that MARCKSL1 is the primary effector through which PARK2 controls c-Myc output in CRC. These findings collectively highlight the complexity of PARK2 tumor suppressor function and suggest that its anti-oncogenic activity in CRC likely reflects the integrated degradation of multiple pro-tumorigenic substrates converging on the c-Myc pathway.

A key implication of our findings is that PARK2 likely serves as a broader “gatekeeper” of oncogenic protein homeostasis in intestinal tumorigenesis. PARK2 deletions and reduced expression are recurrent in sporadic CRC and can cooperate with APC mutations to accelerate adenoma formation, supporting a bona fide tumor suppressor role in the gut [14]. Consistent with this concept, PARK2 has been shown to control cell-cycle progression via ubiquitin-dependent regulation of G1/S cyclins (including Cyclin E), and cancer-associated disruption of PARK2 can lead to aberrant accumulation of such substrates [16, 18]. Our work expands the substrate spectrum of PARK2 in CRC by identifying MARCKSL1 as an actionable downstream effector: PARK2 physically associates with MARCKSL1 and promotes its degradation through the proteasome pathway, establishing a mechanistic basis by which PARK2 loss could simultaneously enhance cytoskeletal pro-migratory outputs and amplify oncogenic transcriptional programs.

A notable mechanistic feature uncovered in this study concerns the structural basis by which PARK2 selectively ubiquitinates MARCKSL1. Domain mapping experiments revealed that the RING1 domain of PARK2 is directly responsible for MARCKSL1 binding, whereas deletion of the UBL domain abolishes ubiquitination activity without disrupting substrate recognition. This apparent paradox — retained substrate binding yet complete loss of catalytic function — is explained by the distinct structural roles of these two domains. The UBL domain of PARK2 is known to maintain the protein in an autoinhibited conformation through intramolecular interactions with RING1, thereby regulating E2 enzyme recruitment and ubiquitin transfer competence [19, 20]. Deletion of UBL therefore disrupts the conformational dynamics required for productive catalysis, even though the RING1-mediated substrate-binding interface remains structurally intact. Interestingly, this substrate-specific recognition mechanism appears to differ from that reported for other Parkin substrates; for example, the mitochondrial GTPase Miro1 is recognized via a conserved hydrophobic motif in the flexible linker between the Ubl and RING0 domains rather than through RING1 [21]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PARK2 employs substrate-specific recognition modules while relying on the UBL-regulated catalytic machinery for ubiquitin transfer, providing a molecular explanation for the observed dissociation between substrate binding and ubiquitination activity in ΔUBL-PARK2.

On the substrate side, our bioinformatic analyses using the PhosphoSitePlus and GPS-Uber databases identified K25 and K157 as candidate ubiquitination sites on MARCKSL1. However, the present study did not experimentally validate whether these residues are the functional acceptor sites for PARK2-mediated K48-linked polyubiquitination. Future studies employing site-directed mutagenesis (e.g., K25R, K157R, and K25R/K157R double mutants) combined with in vivo and in vitro ubiquitination assays will be necessary to pinpoint the precise ubiquitination sites and to determine whether PARK2 modifies one or both residues. Such information would not only complete the molecular characterization of the PARK2-MARCKSL1 regulatory axis but may also facilitate the rational design of therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating MARCKSL1 protein turnover in CRC.

MARCKSL1 is well-positioned to act as such a convergence node. As a MARCKS-family protein, MARCKSL1 contributes to actin cytoskeleton remodeling and cell motility, and its phosphorylation-dependent regulation has been linked to actin stability and migration in both neuronal and cancer contexts. Clinically, elevated MARCKSL1 has been associated with aggressive CRC phenotypes; for example, circulating extracellular vesicle-derived MARCKSL1 has been proposed as a non-invasive biomarker in metastatic CRC [1]. These reports align with our functional data showing that MARCKSL1 promotes proliferation, migration/invasion, and in vivo tumor growth, placing MARCKSL1 as a biologically plausible driver rather than merely a correlative marker. The present study adds a new layer to this biology by positioning MARCKSL1 under direct ubiquitin control by PARK2, suggesting that MARCKSL1 upregulation in CRC may, at least in part, reflect impaired ubiquitin-mediated quality control rather than transcriptional activation alone. We acknowledge that the clinical sample size used for IHC correlation analysis in this study is relatively limited (n = 30). Although statistically significant correlations were observed between PARK2 and MARCKSL1 protein expression (R = − 0.3747, P = 0.0413) and between MARCKSL1 and c-Myc protein expression (R = 0.5032, P = 0.0046), the correlation coefficients are of weak-to-moderate magnitude, which may be attributable to the inherent heterogeneity of clinical specimens and the modest cohort size. Validation in larger, multi-center cohorts with detailed clinicopathological annotation will be essential to consolidate the prognostic and predictive value of the PARK2/MARCKSL1/c-Myc protein expression signature and to evaluate its potential utility for patient stratification in clinical practice.

In summary, our study supports a mechanistic framework in which PARK2 acts as a tumor suppressor in CRC by enforcing degradative ubiquitination of MARCKSL1, thereby limiting c-Myc protein stability and downstream oncogenic transcription. These insights nominate the PARK2-MARCKSL1-c-Myc pathway as a biologically coherent and therapeutically relevant circuit, and they motivate future efforts to (i) restore PARK2 function (including epigenetic reactivation), (ii) directly target MARCKSL1 abundance/function, and/or (iii) exploit emerging c-Myc-directed therapies in molecularly selected CRC subsets.
