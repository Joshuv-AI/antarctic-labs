A Spatial‐Temporal Epidemiology of Anxiety Incidence Rates in Iran (2010–2021): An Ecological Study
Authors: Fatemeh Majdolashrafi, Ali Delpisheh, Raha Davatgar, Goljamal Jorjani, Aram Halimi, Haniyeh Yeganeh, Mohammad Hossein Panahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAnxiety disorders are the most common mental health disorders worldwide that affect daily activities. These disorders are currently the second leading cause of mental health‐related disability globally. This study aimed to investigate the patterns and trends of anxiety disorders in Iranian provinces from 2010 to 2021.MethodsAn ecological study was conducted using data from the IHME site over 12 years, from 2010 to 2021. The annual age‐standardized incidence rates of anxiety were calculated as expressed per 100,000 individuals, both globally and specifically for Iran. A Spatial‐Temporal Analysis assessed these rates' geographical and temporal distribution.ResultsFrom 2010 to 2021, the ASR of anxiety in Iran was increased by 175.96 per 100,000 population (from 966.061 per 100,000 in 2010 to 1142.02 per 100,000 in 2021), which is higher than global trends. Iranian females experienced a higher incidence rate than males. Spatial analysis shows that southeastern provinces such as Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad and Sistan and Baluchestan have the highest incidence in both sexes. Based on the local Moran index, from 2010 to 2015, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad provinces were in the high‐low cluster, and Bushehr and Semnan were in the low‐high cluster. Between 2016 and 2021, North Khorasan province was identified as the anxiety hotspot. In addition, Kerman province was in the low‐high cluster for 12 years.ConclusionThe results of spatial distribution and temporal trends can help in the targeted allocation of resources for the management and promotion of mental health, especially in high‐risk provinces.

Introduction

Anxiety is a common mental disorder that can affect individual and social aspects [1]. Its prevalence is higher among females and in developed countries [2, 3]. In addition, reports indicate that a significant percentage of Iranians suffer from mental disorders [4].

According to the latest evidence, anxiety disorders are the second leading cause of mental health‐related disability in the world [5, 6]. The results of recent study have shown that between 1990 and 2019, the incidence of anxiety disorders increased by 47.19%, from 31.13 million in 1990 to 45.82 million in 2019. DALYs also increased by 53.70%, from 18.66 million in 1990 to 28.68 million in 2019 [6].

Several risk factors contribute to the development of anxiety disorders, including being female, having low educational attainment, and experiencing low socioeconomic status. Additional factors include a family history of anxiety disorders, an introverted personality in early childhood, behavioral inhibition, parental psychopathology, and conservative or authoritarian parenting styles. Adverse childhood experiences and traumatic life events also play significant roles. Anxiety disorders are associated with substantial personal, social, and economic burdens [7, 8, 9].

Studies show that there is a substantial relationship between the spatial distribution of anxiety and geographic variation. Therefore, further research in this field is warranted [10, 11]. This study aimed to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of anxiety disorder incidence across Iranian provinces from 2010 to 2021, with a focus on sex differences, temporal trends, and identification of spatial clusters and hotspots.

Methods

This ecological study was approved by the Safety Promotion and Injury Prevention Research Center of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, with the ethics code IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1402.603. The data was sourced from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) website, that provides timely, actionable, and scientifically valid evidence to improve health policy and practice worldwide, including Iran [12].

On the IHME website, we selected the option “Cause of death or injury” in the section “GBD Estimate” and the option “Anxiety disorders” in the section “Cause.” Then, an Excel file was extracted by sex and province. This enabled the ecological study to examine the age‐standardized incidence rates per 100,000 population from 2010 to 2021. For this study, GBD age‐standardized population was used and the authors did not use another source to calculate it.

Temporal Trend Analysis

A line graph of the trend in age‐standardized incidence rates (per 100,000 people) was created using Excel software. Using Stata software, a 95% confidence interval was calculated. Age‐standardized rates (ASRs) were used to account for differences in the age structure of populations across provinces and over time, allowing valid temporal comparisons. Evidence from Iranian studies suggests that the age pattern of anxiety is broadly consistent with international trends. For example, Alizadeh et al. (2020) reported that the risk of anxiety decreased by approximately 2% with each additional year of age, and Hajebi et al. (2018) found similar patterns of age‐specific prevalence in a national sample. Therefore, the use of ASRs is considered appropriate for describing temporal trend of anxiety in Iran.

Descriptive Map

First, we prepared a descriptive table based on each province's FID. Then, we entered the average age‐standardized incidence and mortality rates for the periods for over 10 years (2010–2019) in both sexes and separately. We drew descriptive maps using ArcMap 10.8 software.

Moran's Spatial Autocorrelation Analysis

Spatial autocorrelation analysis is one of the most widely used tools in spatial data analysis and a cornerstone of spatial modeling. It measures the degree of spatial dependence among observed phenomena [13].

Two Moran's indices can be used to show spatial correlation between data. The nature of a phenomenon in the entire region is described by the global Moran index (GMI), and the spatial distribution of hot and cold spots and their comparison with neighboring samples is shown by the local Moran index (LMI) [13, 14, 15].

Global Moran's Index

Global Moran's I was calculated to assess the overall spatial autocorrelation of anxiety incidence rates across provinces in Iran. This index quantifies whether provinces with similar incidence rates tend to cluster, disperse, or distribute randomly, with values ranging from −1 to +1. It is widely used in spatial epidemiology to detect global patterns that cannot be inferred from descriptive maps alone. To define the spatial relationships among provinces, a first‐order contiguity‐based spatial weights matrix was used, in which a weight of 1 was assigned to provinces that share a common border and 0 otherwise. This index is obtained from the following equation: (1) I=nS0×∑i=1n∑j=1nWij(Xi−X®)(Xj−X®)∑i=1n(Xi−X®)2

In this relation, Xi and Xj represent the values of the variables at locations j and i. Wij represents the spatial weight between i and j. S 0 and n represent the total sum of values and number of provinces, respectively. The results of this evaluation indicate the distribution of the expressed pattern [13, 16].

Local Moran's Index

Local Moran's I was applied to identify localized clusters or spatial outliers of anxiety incidence across provinces. This method enables the detection of small‐area patterns at the provincial level, which may differ from the national average, thereby guiding targeted public health interventions. The provinces were classified into five cluster types based on Local Moran's I: high‐high (HH), low‐low (LL), high‐low (HL), low‐high (LH), and no cluster (NS). Positive, significant values (HH and LL) indicate provinces with similar incidence rates clustered together, representing positive spatial autocorrelation, whereas negative, significant values (HL and LH) indicate provinces with dissimilar incidence rates adjacent to one another, representing negative spatial autocorrelation. These classifications highlight local spatial heterogeneity and help identify provinces that may require targeted public health interventions [17, 18]. Although the application of Local Moran's I allows for detection of local patterns, interpretation of these clusters should consider that the Global Moran's I for the overall spatial autocorrelation was not statistically significant. The local Moran statistic is obtained from the following equation [13]: (2) Ii=n(Xi−X®)∑j=1,≠inWij(Xj−X®)

In this equation, n is the sum of all values in terms of number and, represents the values of variables at locations j and i, and represents the spatial value of the weight j and i.

Hot Spot Analysis (Getis–Ord Gi)

Hot Spot Analysis (Getis–Ord Gi) was conducted to identify provinces with significantly high (hot spots) or low (cold spots) anxiety incidence rates, providing a complementary view to Local Moran's I and highlighting areas that may require targeted public health interventions. Its equation is: (3) Gi*=∑j=1nWijXj−X®∑j=1nWijn∑j=1nW2ij−∑j=1nWij2n−1

In this equation, is the feature value for j variations, is the weighted space value between variations, and j, i, and n represent the total number of variations.

The two southeastern provinces of Iran, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad and Sistan and Baluchistan, had the highest age‐standardized mean incidence rate in both sexes and males, respectively (Both sexes: 1000.48, 95% CI (803.16−1253.06), Male: 906.24, 95% CI (732.63‐1127.99)) (Table 2 and Figure 1, 1). The lowest incidence rates in males and females were reported in Khuzestan and Fars, located in southern Iran, respectively (Male: 900.76, 95% CI (725.14–1116.69), Female: 1006.85, 95% CI (795.19–1286.71)) (Table 2 and Figure 1, 1).

Global Moran's Index

Spatial autocorrelation analysis was performed to assess the average incidence of anxiety in both sexes over two 6‐year periods and over the entire 12‐year period. According to Figure 2, 2, the global Moran's I was not statistically significant in any of the time periods. In the period 2010–2015, the negative Moran's I (Z = −0.604, p = 0.545) only indicates a tendency for spatial dispersion, but since this pattern was not statistically confirmed, the presence of spatial dispersion cannot be inferred (Figure 2, 2). In the period 2016–2021, the positive but close to zero Moran's I (Z = 1.103, p = 0.270) did not provide evidence of spatial autocorrelation on a global scale, and the observed pattern was consistent with a random distribution (Figure 2, 2). Also, over the entire study period (2010–2021), the insignificant value of this statistic (Z = 0.800, p = 0.423) confirms the lack of meaningful spatial clustering at the global level (Figure 2, 2).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive spatial and temporal analysis of age‐standardized incidence rates of anxiety in Iran from 2010 to 2021 using robust spatial correlation methods. The findings improve understanding the impact of regional, cultural, and environmental factors on mental health and identify spatial distribution patterns of anxiety and their temporal variations. This information is crucial for designing targeted interventions and policies to address mental health inequalities at both national and provincial levels. The analysis shows that the incidence of anxiety in Iran has increased more rapidly than the global trend over the study period. While anxiety rates have been steadily rising worldwide, Iran exhibits a steeper upward trajectory, with both sexes affected, and females experiencing relatively higher increases. This pattern aligns with findings from the GBD 2019 study [19].

Spatial heterogeneity of anxiety can be interpreted by stress process theory (SPT). This theory states that exposure to stressors such as economic hardship, social stigma, and unequal access to health services interacts with protective resources such as social support or religiosity to influence mental health. Domestic and international evidence supports the validity of this theoretical framework. For example, Ghorbani et al. showed that perceived stress, through perceived control and anxiety, predicts depression in both Iran and the United States [20]. Also, Riahi et al. showed that religiosity acts as an important protective factor against mental disorders among Iranian students [21].

During the study period, the global anxiety rate increased by an average of 109.69 per 100,000 people; however, in Iran, this increase reached 175.96 per 100,000 people. Evidence suggests that the COVID‐19 pandemic has played a significant role in the rise in anxiety; international and national studies have shown that the prevalence of anxiety during the pandemic reached 30%–31% [22, 23, 24]. This trend suggests that major public health crises can exacerbate psychological stress and precipitate anxiety disorders, highlighting the importance of strengthening mental health systems.

The sex dimension of anxiety in Iran is also prominent. The increase in anxiety rates among Iranian females (from 1050.23 to 1253.61 per 100,000 population) was greater than that among males (from 883.99 to 1035.55 per 100,000 population). These differences are consistent with other studies that indicate the influence of biological, psychological, and social variables on females' vulnerability to anxiety [25]. Additionally, meta‐analyses have shown that self‐compassion and positive mood can reduce psychological distress caused by chronic stress [26].

This increasing trend highlights the potential limitations of mental health programs and resources in addressing anxiety disorders. Hormonal changes and cultural pressures may be contributing factors to the differences in anxiety rates between females and males. In addition, this pattern may reflect significant social, political, and economic changes that occurred during the study period, such as the COVID‐19 pandemic, expanding urbanization, or financial instability, all of which can exacerbate mental health issues among the general population. In this context, Yazdanpanah and colleagues applied cognitive stress theory to Iranian farmers during the COVID‐19 pandemic. They found that coping behaviors were strongly influenced by perceived control, socioeconomic vulnerability, and access to supportive resources. These findings are consistent with the regional variations identified in the present study [27].

Spatial analysis further revealed significant variation across provinces. Khuzestan and Fars recorded the lowest incidence rates for males and females, respectively, while Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad and Sistan and Baluchistan had the highest age‐standardized mean incidence rates. These disparities likely reflect differences in socioeconomic status, healthcare access, cultural attitudes toward mental health, and environmental stressors, emphasizing the importance of contextual factors in shaping anxiety patterns [28]. For instance, limited mental health resources in underserved or rural provinces, such as Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad, contribute to higher anxiety incidence [29, 30].

In the first 6 years (2010–2015), globally, Moran's Index showed a random or multipolar distribution (negative values); in the second half (2016–2021), the pattern became more clustered (positive values). According to the results of the local Moran, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad were in the HL (high‐low) cluster in the first period (2010–2015) surrounded by provinces with lower anxiety levels, indicating regional variation. On the other hand, North Khorasan became a high cluster in the second period (2016–2017), emphasizing its position as a center of anxiety. Therefore, appropriate regional mental health interventions are necessary [14].

In general, anxiety in Iran has been observed to be mostly sporadic and random over time, but there are distinct local clusters in some provinces. For example, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad provinces are in the HL cluster (high anxiety around low anxiety provinces) and North Khorasan in the HH cluster (high anxiety around high anxiety provinces). Additionally, provinces such as Bushehr and Semnan are part of the LH cluster, while Fars, Kohgiluyeh, and Boyer‐Ahmad provinces have been identified as cold spots. These local clusters indicate the concentration or dispersion of anxiety at the regional level and can be targeted by regional interventions. Designing effective regional interventions requires understanding the interactions between neighboring provinces, which may be reflected in the gradual change in correlation values from negative to positive values. These interactions can include demographic changes, health policies, or everyday social and economic challenges.

Over the 12‐year study period, several provinces were identified as hotspots, with South Khorasan having the highest risk. Yazd and Kerman provinces were similarly ranked next. In contrast, provinces such as Fars and Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad were repeatedly identified as cold zones, indicating a lower likelihood of anxiety in these areas. Potential protective features in these cold zones, such as stronger local social networks or effective mental health policies, are highlighted and can serve as models for designing interventions in high‐risk areas.

Studies in Iran, including the results of the extensive PERSIAN cohort study and a qualitative study, suggest that economic pressures, unemployment, social stigma associated with mental illness, and inequalities in access to mental health services explain a significant portion of regional differences in the prevalence of anxiety and other cognitive disorders; particularly in provinces with a more conservative cultural structure, fear of social judgment, and cultural shame delay treatment seeking and exacerbate anxiety disorders [30, 31]. In addition, recent evidence emphasizes that psychosocial factors, including income instability and social stigma, play an important role in exacerbating mental health problems in deprived areas [32]. In provinces identified as hotspots, such as South Khorasan and Yazd, there is an urgent need to investigate further the contributing factors, especially economic hardship and social stigma that may exacerbate anxiety levels. In contrast, provinces that are consistently classified as hot spots, such as Fars, may provide valuable insights into practical strategies for building resilience, including stronger mental health service systems and established coping mechanisms. These successful approaches could serve as valuable models for intervention planning in high‐risk areas. Overall, these findings support the interpretation of the results of the present spatial analysis, indicating that the identified hotspots are likely to be areas with higher socioeconomic vulnerability and weaker coping resources. In contrast, cold spots may benefit from stronger support networks and more resilient social structures.

This study emphasizes allocating resources, training professionals and the public, and providing interventions for females based on identifying high‐risk provinces such as South Khorasan and Yazd. Using spatial analysis results to inform mental health service delivery and resource allocation, especially in remote areas, is effective in decision‐making. Additionally, interprovincial collaboration and resource sharing are recommended to improve the prevalence and complications of anxiety disorders.

Limitations

There is a possibility of bias in the primary data on anxiety disorders and a lack of complete identification of the underlying causes and consequences at the county level, due to the spatial analysis being conducted at the provincial level. To better understand the determinants of anxiety, further research focusing on cultural, economic, and environmental factors is needed. Although the use of age‐standardized rates is appropriate for temporal and provincial comparisons, and evidence suggests that the age pattern of anxiety in Iranian adults is similar to international trends, substantial differences in age patterns could slightly affect the results. Additionally, some provinces showed wide confidence intervals, likely due to small case numbers, high variability between provinces, and the use of age‐standardized rates, which may reduce the precision of the estimates and affect the interpretation of spatial patterns. These limitations indicate that the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with more detailed and local‐level data could strengthen the findings.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the spatial‐temporal distribution of anxiety incidence rates over Iran between 2010 and 2021. Our results showed a general rising trend in anxiety disorders both domestically with clear sex discrepancies. Significant differences between provinces were revealed by spatial analysis; locations like Kohgiluyeh and Boyer‐Ahmad, North Khorasan, and Semnan emerged as high‐risk areas throughout different times. The existence of spatial autocorrelation emphasizes how geographical and maybe socioeconomic elements affect the distribution of anxiety. Finding hot and cold areas provides legislators with insightful information that will help them to prioritize resource allocation and apply focused mental health treatments. Provinces like South Khorasan, Fars, and Kerman require urgent intervention. Knowing these trends highlights the need of region‐specific policies to lower the burden of anxiety disorders in Iran, thereby stressing the need of spatial epidemiology in mental health planning and preventive campaigns.
