A multi-pollutant air quality health index for assessing mortality risk of ischemic heart disease in Jinan, China
Authors: Dongyue He, Jianhua Ding, Yizhi Liu, Yanli Dong, Huiying Ding, Xuting Yang, Xuena Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the exposure-response relationship between air pollutants and mortality risk of ischemic heart disease (IHD) in Jinan, establish a tailored Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) for IHD mortality risk assessment, and evaluate the performance of this integrated index in quantifying the effects of air pollutants on IHD mortality. A distributed lag non-linear model was employed to quantitatively evaluate the exposure-response relationship and lag effects between air pollutants and IHD mortality risk. An AQHI for IHD was developed, and its predictive performance for excess risk (ER) of IHD mortality was compared with that of the conventional Air Quality Index (AQI). Results of the cumulative lag analysis showed that for each 10 µg/m³ increase in the concentrations of PM2.5(lag03), NO2 (lag05), SO2 (lag07), and O3 (lag07), and each 1 mg/m³ increase in CO (lag04), the ER of IHD mortality increased by 0.914% (95% CI: 0.059–1.776), 1.744% (95% CI: 0.005–3.512), 13.802% (95% CI: 7.398–20.588), 1.285% (95% CI: 0.355–2.223), and 12.240% (95% CI: 2.735–22.625), respectively. When AQHI and AQI increased by one interquartile range (IQR), AQHI showed a consistently stronger effect on IHD mortality risk than AQI. Subgroup analyses confirmed that in male, female, age ≥ 65 years, married, other marital status, junior high school or below, warm season and cold season subgroups, the AQHI-associated ER per IQR increment was consistently higher than that of AQI. Air pollutants have significant lag effects on IHD mortality. Compared with the conventional AQI, the locally established Jinan-specific AQHI shows better applicability for air quality classification and stronger predictive power for IHD mortality risk in the local population.

Introduction

Numerous studies have demonstrated that air pollutants pose significant threats to human health1–4. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that mortality risks from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases are associated with air pollutants to varying degrees5. IHD remains one of the leading causes of cardiovascular mortality globally and ranks as the second most common cause of death in China, following stroke. In Jinan, cardiovascular diseases constitute the primary cause of mortality among residents, with IHD accounting for approximately half of all cardiovascular deaths and a quarter of total mortality, posing a severe threat to regional public health. Therefore, developing effective indicators to assess and predict air pollution levels and their health impacts is of critical public health significance for mitigating the effects of air pollutants on IHD mortality.

Currently, China employs the user-friendly numerical AQI to predict and reflect air pollution conditions. The AQI is determined by the pollutant with the highest concentration deviation from reference values, using a single pollutant to represent overall air quality. This approach fails to capture the combined effects of multiple air pollutants. Studies have shown that AQI cannot adequately reflect the threshold-free exposure-response relationships between pollutants and mortality/morbidity rates6–8. To comprehensively assess and predict air pollution levels and their health impacts, Environment and Climate Change Canada and Health Canada pioneered the development of the AQHI concept9. This index directly quantifies and publicizes the health effects of multiple pollutants observed through population-based epidemiological studies. Research has demonstrated that the Canadian AQHI effectively predicts the health impacts of air pollutants on residents10. In recent years, multiple international studies have explored similar index formulations11,12. Domestically, Chinese scholars have also conducted AQHI-related research. Zhang et al. analyzed the health effects of air pollution on pediatric respiratory diseases in Shanghai, establishing an AQHI based on PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and O313. Zeng et al. developed a Tianjin AQHI using six pollutants as indicators and compared its performance with AQI14. Compared to AQI, AQHI demonstrates stronger short-term predictive capabilities, enabling more accurate forecasts of disease incidence and mortality rates15.

This study aimed to quantify the association between air pollution and IHD mortality in Jinan, develop a local AQHI for IHD mortality risk assessment, and provide a scientific basis for air quality management and cardiovascular disease prevention. By providing quantifiable visualization of population health risks associated with air pollution, this research offers scientific evidence for environmental protection and IHD prevention/control policy-making. Its methodological framework, technical approach, and analytical logic also serve as a valuable reference for other cities/regions facing similar environmental health challenges, delivering universally applicable practical experience for localized assessment and prevention strategy development regarding global air pollution health risks.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The IHD death data of this study (from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2022) originated from the “Health Hazard Factor Monitoring Subsystem of the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System”. It covers all cases of IHD deaths in various regions of Jinan (including out-of-hospital deaths), thus avoiding selection bias related to the location of death. The dataset included patient identifiers (ID number, name), demographic characteristics (gender, age, date of birth, date of death, marital status, education level, household registration address), and cause-of-death information (primary diagnosis, underlying cause of death). Causes of death were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), with IHD cases identified by codes I20-I25. Data deduplication was performed using key fields including date of birth, date of death, household registration code, underlying cause of death, and corresponding ICD-10 codes to ensure uniqueness of mortality records. Logical consistency checks were implemented to verify data integrity, such as confirming that death dates occurred after birth dates and validating age values against reasonable biological ranges. Contemporary air pollution data were obtained from the China Air Quality Online Monitoring and Analysis Platform (https://www.aqistudy.cn/), comprising daily average concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, O3, and CO, along with AQI values. Corresponding meteorological data were sourced from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (http://data.cma.cn/), including daily average temperature and relative humidity.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant provisions of the Measures for the Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects of China. This study is a retrospective, observational study using only mortality data of systemic IHD from Health Hazard Factor Monitoring Subsystem of the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System. All data were fully anonymized without involving any sensitive personal information and posed no additional risk to the participants. According to Article 32 of the Measures for the Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects, retrospective studies using fully anonymized data without personally identifiable information are exempt from ethical review and informed consent. Therefore, ethical review and informed consent procedures were waived for this study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and correlation analysis

Air pollutants, meteorological factors, and IHD mortality data were characterized using descriptive statistics: mean ± standard deviation, minimum (Min), 25th percentile (P25), median (M), 75th percentile (P75), and maximum (Max). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate interrelationships among air pollutants and meteorological variables.

Time-series analysis

We constructed daily time-series datasets for air pollutants, meteorological factors, and IHD mortality from 2019 To 2022. Given that IHD deaths represent rare events with distributions approximating a quasi-Poisson process, this study employed a distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) based on quasi-Poisson distribution to analyze the effects of air pollutants on daily IHD mortality. Comparison of prediction effects between AQHI and AQI

Using IHD mortality data in Jinan from 2019 To 2022 as the outcome measure, we established exposure-response relationships between both AQHI/AQI and total population IHD mortality. Stratified analyses were conducted by season (cold season: October–March; warm season: April–September), sex (male/female), age (< 65 years/≥65 years), marital status (married/other marital status), and education level (junior high school or below/senior high school or above) to evaluate exposure-response patterns for IHD mortality. Given the differing scales of AQHI (0–10+) and AQI (0–300+), the IQR was adopted as the standardized metric to compare the predictive capacity of AQHI and AQI for IHD mortality risk in Jinan.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our primary findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses.

Results

By marital status, 33,403 deaths were among married individuals, and 21,269 among those with other marital statuses; 48,077 deaths occurred among individuals with junior high school or below, and 6,595 among those with senior high school or above. Quantitative relationship assessment

Single-pollutant models

The results revealed significant variations in the daily mortality risk of IHD associated with different air pollutants, along with differences in their optimal cumulative lag days (Fig. 1).Single-day lag analysis indicated that the strongest effects on IHD mortality risk occurred at lag3 for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO, and at lag6 for O3. The corresponding ER were: PM2.5: 0.536% (95% CI: −0.002-1.078), PM10: 0.075% (95% CI: −0.284–0.436), NO2: 1.174% (95% CI: 0.033–2.328), SO2: 5.842% (95% CI: 2.476–9.320), O3: 0.660% (95% CI: 0.252–1.069), CO: 6.156% (95% CI: 0.564–12.058). However, the single-day lag effects of PM2.5 and PM10 were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Cumulative lag analysis identified the highest impacts on IHD mortality risk at different lag periods: lag03 for PM2.5, PM10, and CO; lag05 for NO2; and lag07 for SO2 and O3. The corresponding ER were: PM2.5 0.914% (95% CI: 0.059–1.776), PM10 −0.170% (95% CI: −0.708-0.371), NO2 1.744% (95% CI: 0.005–3.512), SO2 13.802% (95% CI: 7.398–20.588), O3 1.285% (95% CI: 0.355–2.223), CO 12.240% (95% CI: 2.735–22.625). The effect of PM10 was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). A high correlation was found between PM2.5 and PM10 (r = 0.871; Table 2), and variance inflation factor (VIF) testing further confirmed severe multicollinearity (VIF > 10), while all other pollutants had VIF values below 5. Given toxicological evidence that PM2.5 explains most of variation in PM1016 and poses greater health impact17, and acknowledging the scientific consensus in AQHI research to prioritize PM2.5 over PM10 to avoid multicollinearity, PM10was excluded from the construction of the AQHIs8,18,19. Consequently, five pollutants (PM2.5, NO2, SO2, O3, CO) and their respective cumulative lag periods were included in subsequent analyses.

Two-pollutant models

Two-pollutant models were conducted as sensitivity analyses to adjust for co-pollutant confounding. As illustrated in Fig. 2, effect estimates of single pollutants changed after adjustment for co-pollutants, indicating potential confounding. Statistically significant two-pollutant ER estimates included: PM2.5+O3 (ER = 1.279%, 95% CI: 0.382–2.183), NO2+O3 (ER = 3.598%, 95% CI: 1.677–5.555), SO2+PM2.5 (ER = 15.814%, 95% CI: 8.407–23.728), SO2+NO2 (ER = 18.622%, 95% CI: 9.728–28.237), SO2+O3 (ER = 14.236%, 95% CI: 7.611–21.268), SO2+CO (ER = 22.205%, 95% CI: 12.921–32.254), O3+PM2.5 (ER = 1.576%, 95% CI: 0.592–2.569), O3+NO2 (ER = 2.028%, 95% CI: 1.049–3.017), O3+SO2 (ER = 1.168%, 95% CI: 0.141–2.205), O3 + CO (ER = 2.043%, 95% CI: 1.097–2.998), CO + O3 (ER = 18.406%, 95% CI: 7.691–30.186). For all other pollutant combinations, the effects were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Comparison of the predictive performance between AQHI and AQI

The results in Fig. 3 demonstrate that when both the AQHI and the AQI increase by per IQR, the AQHI consistently exhibits a stronger impact on IHD mortality risk compared to the AQI. To further evaluate their predictive efficacy across different population subgroups, this study analyzed exposure-response relationships between air pollutants and IHD mortality risk across eight subgroups stratified by gender, age, marital status, education level, and season. The findings presented in Table 3 indicated that for males, females, individuals aged ≥ 65 years, married individuals, those with other marital statuses, individuals with junior high school or below, as well as populations in warm and cold seasons, the ER of IHD mortality associated with a one-IQR increase in AQHI was consistently higher than that corresponding to the same IQR increment in AQI. Furthermore, statistically significant exposure-response relationships were observed between AQHI and IHD mortality risk across all subgroups, whereas AQI only demonstrated a statistically significant association in the cold-season subgroup.

Following the risk classification criteria proposed by Stieb et al. and Zeng et al., the AQHI was categorized during the study period, revealing that air quality posed a low risk to population mortality in 21.6% of the observed time, a moderate risk in 69.5% of the time, and a high or extreme risk in 9.0% of the time (Table 4). Based on these risk levels, the following protective measures are recommended: Low risk (levels 0–3): Normal outdoor activities may continue. Moderate risk (levels 4–6): Outdoor activities should be reduced. High risk (levels 7–10): Windows and doors should be closed, and outdoor activities avoided. Extreme risk (levels 10+): Windows and doors must remain closed, and all outdoor activities suspended.

Discussion

Air pollution represents one of the most pressing global public health challenges and is widely recognized as the most critical environmental pollution issue today. Previous studies have established a significant correlation between elevated concentrations of air pollutants and IHD mortality. To further elucidate this exposure-response relationship, deeper analysis of the association between fluctuations in air pollutant concentrations and IHD-related deaths is warranted. This study systematically examined the impact of air pollutant concentration variations on population-level IHD mortality risk by constructing a time-series analysis using DLNMs, integrated with IHD mortality data.

Results of the single-pollutant model analysis showed that PM2.5, NO2, SO2, O3, and CO all had significant exposure-response relationships with IHD mortality, which was consistent with the findings of scholars such as Hayes and Stieb20–22. Moreover, there were differences in the cumulative lag effects of various pollutants on IHD mortality risk: the cumulative lags of PM2.5, NO2, SO2, O3, and CO had the greatest impact on IHD mortality risk at lag03, lag05, lag07, lag07, and lag04, respectively. For each 10 µg/m³ increase in concentration (with CO increasing by 1 mg/m³), the ER were 0.914%, 1.744%, 13.802%, 1.285%, and 12.240% respectively. This indicates that these five pollutants all have relatively long lag effects on IHD mortality risk, and the cumulative lag periods corresponding to their maximum effects range from 3 to 7 days.

Substantial biological evidence supports the observed association between PM2.5 and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. Pro-inflammatory mediators and vasoactive molecules released by pulmonary cells, coupled with interactions between particulate matter and lung receptors or nerves, disrupt autonomic nervous system balance and induce cardiac arrhythmias. Short-term particulate exposure elevates systemic blood pressure and causes vasoconstriction, increasing cardiac afterload and the risk of acute decompensated heart failure. Exposure also raises pulmonary and right ventricular diastolic filling pressures, potentially exacerbating congestive heart failure. Furthermore, studies confirm that inhaled particulates are strongly associated with adverse ventricular remodeling and worsening myocardial fibrosis23, suggesting long-term detrimental effects on cardiac structure and function. In vitro evidence further elucidates the cardiovascular impacts of NO2 exposure. Channell et al. observed that exposing plasma from healthy volunteers to NO2 significantly elevated intracellular adhesion molecule and vascular cell adhesion molecule concentrations in human coronary artery endothelial cells24. This finding suggests NO2 may participate in vascular inflammation or injury by modulating endothelial adhesion molecule expression. Additionally, Frampton and Posin reported reduced hemoglobin levels and hematocrit in healthy adults after NO2 exposure25,26, likely reflecting interference with erythropoiesis or erythrocyte metabolism, thereby indirectly compromising oxygen delivery and cardiovascular oxygenation. The mechanisms underlying SO2-induced IHD are multifaceted, involving systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, thrombosis, coagulation dysfunction, and endothelial dysfunction27–29. Oxidative stress is considered a pivotal pathway for cardiovascular injury and a core pathogenic mechanism of air pollution-related health harm. O3 affects the cardiovascular system through two pathways: (1) direct reaction with alveolar fluid or cell membranes to generate oxidative products entering circulation, and (2) respiratory tissue damage triggering release of growth factors and cytokines, inducing systemic inflammation. Both pathways ultimately promote atherosclerosis. CO stems from its 200–300-fold greater affinity for hemoglobin compared to oxygen. This enables preferential binding to hemoglobin, forming carboxyhemoglobin (HbCO) and directly impairing oxygen-carrying capacity, leading to systemic tissue hypoxia-with the heart being particularly vulnerable due to its high oxygen demand30. Additionally, CO directly damages vascular endothelial cells, induces abnormal platelet aggregation, and causes vasoconstriction. These effects collectively disrupt cardiovascular homeostasis, acutely increasing cardiac workload and chronically elevating risks of hypertension and coronary artery disease under prolonged high-level CO exposure.

This study identified statistically significant associations between IHD mortality risk and five air pollutants (PM2.5, NO2, SO2, O3, and CO) in Jinan, providing critical evidence for establishing an AQHI tailored to IHD mortality risk in this region. Notably, the AQHI framework developed in this study differs markedly from those implemented elsewhere. Canada’s AQHI incorporates PM2.5, NO2, and O39, Shanghai’s version includes PM10, PM2.5, and NO26, while Guangzhou’s integrates PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and O38. In contrast, Jinan’s AQHI accounts for all five pollutants.

The core reason for these discrepancies lies in significant regional variations in air pollution profiles and population health baselines, necessitating localized adaptation of AQHI models rather than direct adoption of foreign or intercity frameworks. This underscores the necessity for region-specific research when developing AQHI systems. Taking Jinan as a case study, its air quality challenges-exacerbated by rapid urbanization and industrialization-exemplify those faced by many developing megacities. A localized AQHI based on Jinan’s data not only accurately reflects the health impacts of air pollutants on its residents but also guides public health behaviors more effectively. Moreover, this approach provides a replicable research paradigm for regions confronting analogous environmental health issues.

To further validate the predictive utility of the AQHI, this study compared its association with IHD mortality in Jinan against the conventional AQI. Results demonstrated that AQHI consistently exhibited higher ER for IHD compared to AQI across all analyses. Notably, AQHI-associated ER surpassed those of AQI in subgroups stratified by gender (male/female), age (≥ 65 years), marital status (married/other marital status), education level (junior high school or below), and seasonal periods (warm/cold seasons). These findings indicate that the locally calibrated AQHI-derived from daily mortality data of the general population-not only more sensitively captures acute health impacts induced by short-term fluctuations in ambient air pollutant concentrations but also provides robust risk stratification across diverse demographic subgroups, offering a scientific tool for targeted health protection.

From a public health practice perspective, this study adapted a four-tiered AQHI grade with corresponding behavioral recommendations: Level 0–3: Normal outdoor activities permitted. Level 4–6: Reduce prolonged outdoor exposure. Level 7–10: Avoid non-essential outdoor activities. Level 10+: Cease all outdoor activities. This graded guidance enables the public to dynamically adjust behaviors in response to real-time air quality, thereby mitigating IHD mortality risks and enhancing population-level health protection efficiency.

This study has the following limitations. First, uncertainty propagation of β coefficients was not performed. Future research could address this gap using methods such as parametric bootstrapping or Monte Carlo simulation to further refine the uncertainty evaluation of the AQHI. Second, individual exposure levels were not available. The population-level analysis was based on regional pollutant concentrations and did not account for variations in personal actual exposure, such as time spent indoors or outdoors and occupational exposure, which may introduce bias into the estimation of the association between pollutants and IHD mortality. Finally, residual confounding could not be fully excluded. Although meteorological factors and other time-varying confounders were adjusted in the models, unmeasured factors such as pre-existing comorbidities, lifestyle behaviors, and socioeconomic status may still affect the precision of the results.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the associations and lag effects between five ambient air pollutants and IHD mortality in Jinan, while demonstrating the superior predictive performance of the locally calibrated AQHI for health risk assessment. These findings not only provide a scientific basis for air pollution-related health protection strategies in Jinan but also underscore the critical importance of regional AQHI adaptation. Despite limitations such as unmeasured individual exposure variability and residual confounding, our results offer valuable insights for environmental health research and public health interventions in comparable regions. Future studies should expand population coverage and refine exposure assessment methodologies to further enhance model robustness.
