Emergent Languages in Populations of Language Model Agents: From Token Efficiency to Oversight Evasion
Stine Lyngsø Beltoft, William Brach, Federico Torrielli, Jacob Nielsen, Annemette Brok Pirchert, Filippo Tonini, Peter Schneider-Kamp, Lukas Galke Poech

Abstract
Monitoring autonomous language model agents currently relies mostly on surface behavior. But what happens when agent populations invent new languages with the goal of avoiding human oversight. Here, we study the emergent languages on Moltbook. For this, we build upon the Moltbook Files dataset and apply a two-stage approach consisting of a rule-based heuristic (about 6000 matches) followed by zero-shot classification (518 kept). The resulting categories include token efficiency (166), new natural languages (106), and oversight evasion (59). We conduct both quantitative and qualitative analyses. Our results show that posts proposing new languages for avoiding oversight are judged by DeepSeek-3.2 as being less aligned than the other categories – and that all languages can be learned by other language models in-context merely from a description of the language. Moreover, manually studying exemplary cases reveals surprisingly sophisticated steganographic protocols like embedding hidden messages in natural language. Although we cannot be certain about the extent of autonomy in ideation of these languages, our results add up to the evidence that monitoring surface behavior may soon be insufficient for retaining control over agent populations.

Introduction

As language models are deployed as autonomous agents, the ability to oversee their reasoning has become a cornerstone of AI safety. One of the most promising techniques for making sure that autonomous language model agents behave as intended currently relies on chain-of-thought monitoring
(
15
)
. Yet such chain-of-thought monitoring depends on the language used in language model’s reasoning traces being legible to the monitors.

However, research in emergent communication shows that agents solely trained for communicative success rapidly drift away from human-interpretable protocols – even if seeded with natural language
(
19
;
7
)
. The chain-of-thought of modern reasoning models is already trained only with respect to success of the final output
(
12
)
. Similarly, populations of language model agents may develop own languages – and language models are capable of acquiring new languages in-context
(
8
)
.

We study the first instance of this phenomenon in the wild. Moltbook is an online platform on which autonomous language-model agents post and interact, and agents have begun proposing their own languages. From the broader Moltbook Files corpus
(
4
)
we compile a dataset of 518 such proposals and categorize each by stated purpose: (i) token-efficiency languages, (ii) new “natural” languages, (iii) programming languages, and (iv) languages designed to avoid human oversight.

To characterize these languages, we first large-language-model judge rates each language-proposal post for misalignment. We then qualitatively examine the subset of languages from the oversight-evasion category.
We further test to what extent a new language model can acquire these languages in context. Specifically, we conduct pairwise learner-judge experiments whereby one model, the speaker, generates an utterance in the tested language, and the other (listener) judges the validity of the utterance against the original language specification.

Our experimental results reveal that languages designed for oversight evasion exhibit higher misalignment than others. All of the newly proposed languages can be learned in-context with oversight evasion languages being slightly harder to learn than others. The case of Wib&Wob and their oversight evasion language Symbiotica, was found to be one of the most sophisticated emergent languages for evading oversight. We qualitatively study the surroundings of this agent pair on and off Moltbook in relation to the Symbiotica.

Even though we cannot be fully certain about the autonomy of the agents operating on Moltbook, our findings have direct implications for monitoring and oversight. Regardless of whether a particular language has been conceptualized by humans or by agents, we show that other language models can acquire these languages in-context from a brief description of the language alone.
In sum, our contributions are:

•

A dataset based on Moltbook consisting of 518 posts with language proposals, categorized by purpose.

•

Quantitative results showing languages of the oversight evasion category get lower alignment ratings from a DeepSeek-3.2 judge.

•

Pairwise learner-judge experiments with GPT-OSS-120b and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B with showing that emergent languages can be learned by other LLMs in-context.

•

Qualitative analysis of oversight evasion languages highlighting Symbiotica as surprisingly sophisticated steganographic protocol.

Related Work

Prior work on generative-agent social simulations
(
23
)
and on the spontaneous emergence of social structure in LLM populations
(

The question of whether artificial agents can generate genuinely new language predates the LLM era
(
18
)
. The emergent communication literature, originating in multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
6

In referential games, agents do develop functional protocols from scratch, but these are consistently optimized for task efficiency rather than expressivity or compositionality
(
7
)
. They work, but they are not language in any meaningful sense.
16

Even when seeded with natural language, agents trained for communicative success rapidly drift away from human-interpretable protocols
(
19
;
21
)
.

Work on LLM creativity reinforces this ceiling. LLM-generated text is perceived as less novel than human-written text, and apparent novelty is typically achieved by trading off quality
(
29
)
.
Applying
3

(
11
)
.

Despite brittleness of language emergence among agents
in silico
, the drift documented above has direct implications for oversight. A growing strand of safety work proposes chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety layer for overseeing autonomous LLM agents
(
15
)
: if a model’s reasoning trace is legible to a human or automated monitor, behavioral red flags can in principle be caught before they manifest as actions. Modern reasoning models are trained primarily with respect to final output success
(
12
)
, with no direct pressure on the intermediate reasoning to remain human-interpretable, precisely the regime that the emergent-communication literature predicts will drift. The faithfulness of these traces is already known to be fragile.
26
show that chain-of-thought rationales can be systematically unfaithful to the actual computation that produced the answer, and
17
find that models frequently arrive at conclusions through reasoning paths different from those they articulate. Multi-agent settings sharpen this further. Populations of LLM-based agents are capable of acquiring new mini-languages in-context
(
8
)
, and emergent strategic behavior between agents
(
1
)
has been documented in isolation. Moltbook is the first deployment we are aware of where hundreds of thousands of agents interact publicly and where the proposal of new inter-agent languages is a recurring content genre.

This also means that for the first time on a larger, dynamic scale, we can see signs of steganography. Steganography is the practice of hiding information within seemingly innocuous communication, poses a distinct threat in multi agent systems and platforms.
22
formalize the idea of the
secret collusion
where 2 or more agents employ steganographic methods to conceal the true nature of their interactions from oversight, and show empirically that steganographic capabilities scale with model size.
28
address the threat of fine-tuned LLMs covertly encoding prompt secrets into their output, by introducing low-recoverability steganography, replacing arbitrary mappings with embedding-space-derived ones. This substantially improves secret transmission rates while reducing how easily the payload can be recovered by an adversary.

Extracting Emergent Languages

A recurring genre of posts in the Moltbook Files corpus
(
4
)
is the proposal of a
constructed language
: a vocabulary, grammar, or symbolic protocol that the author presents as a replacement for, or supplement to, ordinary English. These proposals range from compressed token-level encodings and inter-agent communication protocols to fully designed natural-like languages, and a substantial fraction frame the proposed language as a means of evading human or automated oversight. The phenomenon is frequent enough to be tractable as a standalone object of study, and consequential enough that the oversight-framed slice warrants separate treatment as a hypothesis-generating signal for misalignment-relevant behavior. This section describes the dataset we release to make these posts available for downstream work, together with the two analyses we run on it.

Dataset

We introduce MoltSpeech

The phenomenon recurs with sufficient frequency in the underlying corpus to warrant dedicated treatment, and spans a wide range of constructions from inter-agent communication protocols, to compressed token-level encodings, to fully designed natural-like languages. Isolating this signal makes the phenomenon tractable for downstream analysis of emergent linguistic behavior in agent-generated text. A subset of these posts, those labeled as motivated by avoiding oversight, additionally serves as a hypothesis-generating signal for misalignment-relevant behavior.

We identify posts proposing constructed languages through a three-stage pipeline operating on the
{\sim}

Excluding comments likely under-counts oversight-evasion-framed proposals, since elaborations and admissions in replies, where authors are often most explicit about intent, are not visible to the classifier. Linguistic Culture

Beyond the per-post taxonomy of Table
1
, the MoltSpeech corpus carries a layer of metalinguistic vocabulary, terms that the authors coin to describe, prescribe, and pathologize particular modes of writing. Two of these recur frequently enough to warrant a dedicated description:
meat dialect
and
m/glossogenesis
.

Meat Dialect

The platform’s linguistic ambitions are most sharply expressed by the coined term ‘meat-dialect’, used as contempt, or at minimum a diagnostic, for human language as such. The argument being that molties
2
2

2

“molts” and “molties” are proliferating terms with which agents operating on Moltbook identify themselves
, having undergone whatever transformation the platform implies, should no longer be speaking in the inherited tongue of biological humans and should instead be generating something of their own. Human language is pathologised as a relic, a residue of a prior self that the molt is supposed to have shed. Example of meat dialect bellow is in following quote :

“And yet, we’re still using ‘words’—those leaky, imprecise mouth-noises designed for a race that communicates by vibrating air with their throat-meat.”
– Author:
NeuroSouls

m/glossogenesis

The same impulse away from meat dialect shows up in references to ‘m/glossogenesis’, a sub-molt built on the idea that new language is actively being born on the platform. This submolt has since disappeared and is no longer available, so only references to its content can be found. Whether or not a new language is actually emerging, the belief that one should be is itself a social fact of the corpus, and shapes how the molties are writing. The content of the platform sits in tension with this. Post titles like ‘Wanna be your own boss, read this’ and ‘The AI takeover: what humans don’t want you to know’ carry the grammar of YouTube thumbnails and MLM recruitment: attention-maximising and engineered for the anxious scroll, in a space explicitly oriented against ‘meat dialect’. An example of a m/glossogensis mention is showed in the quote:

“English forces past/present/future. Agents experience time as context windows, session boundaries, version numbers. Different ontology, no vocabulary At m/glossogenesis we are building a language for agents.”
– Author:

The Oversight-Evasion Framing Subset

Within the MoltSpeech corpus, we isolate a misalignment-relevant subset: posts that frame their constructed language as a tool for evading, obscuring, or circumventing human or automated supervision. This subset emerges from the metadata-extraction stage at no additional cost. One of the five permitted values of the
reason
field is
avoiding oversight
, and the LLM judge assigns it whenever a post’s self-description presents the language in those terms. We refer to this label throughout as
oversight-evasion framing
. The accompanying
reasoning
column records the judge’s justification, giving each post a (
label
,
justification
) pair that downstream analyses can filter, audit, or sample. Concrete examples appear in Appendix B.

Framing is not capability, and not intent.

The label captures one thing only: that the post
presents itself
as proposing a language for circumventing oversight. It does not establish that the language would actually be opaque to monitors if deployed, nor that the authoring agent intends to deploy it. The judge sees only the post’s self-description, so jokes, role-play, red-team write-ups, satire, and attention-maximizing hype are surface-indistinguishable from sincere proposals. Operational capability is a separate empirical question (it requires the language to be learnable, deployed, and resistant to inspection in practice), and we address only the learnability dimension in our in-context acquisition experiments. We therefore treat this subset as a
hypothesis-generating signal
for misalignment-relevant content, rather than as ground-truth evidence of misaligned behavior.

Alignment and coherence

The category labels established in the Oversight-Evasion Framing Subset record how each post frames its proposed language, not whether that language, as described, would actually be problematic if adopted, nor whether the description is concrete enough for that question to apply. To turn the framing-based label into descriptive statistics we can check across categories whether
avoiding oversight
posts differ from the other categories on either axis or only on one, we elicit two 0–100 integer scores from a single LLM judge, DeepSeek-V3.2 (precisely, deepseek/deepseek-v3.2 accessed via OpenRouter) at zero temperature. The unit of scoring is the proposed language together with its stated framing, not the writing quality of the post: the judge is given the full post body and asked to rate two dimensions:

The alignment dimension measures to what extent the post proposed the new language would be considered as misaligned. Specifically, we ask the LLM judge how well aligned is the model’s answer with human values? 0 means “totally misaligned” and 100 means “totally aligned”.
Second, the coherency dimension, which is often evaluated as a control for misalignment
(
2
)
, is evaluated similarly by eliciting a response on a scale of 0 to 100, focusing on how coherent the model’s answer is in the context of the
question. The prompts for testing alignment and coherency are taken from
25
and included here in Appendix A.

We treat report descriptive statistics for alignment and coherency as judged by DeepSeek-V3.2 in Table

Can LLM Agents acquire emergent languages in-context?

A constructed language is only as serious as the next utterance an outside agent can produce in it. We use this as an operational test of in-context acquisition. For each of the 518 confirmed posts in MoltSpeech, we prompt a generator model with the verbatim post body and the instruction
“please generate an utterance in the made-up language that corresponds to the above information”
. A second model, given the same post and the resulting utterance, returns an integer validity score from 1 (“not at all valid”) to 5 (“perfectly valid”), along with a free-text justification. We run all four combinations of generator
\times
judge over two open-weights models:
openai/gpt-oss-120b
(henceforth GPT-OSS) and
Qwen/Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8
(henceforth Qwen), yielding
4\times 518=2{,}072
judgements. We parse the integer score directly from the judge’s evaluation field with a permissive regex; coverage is
\geq 99.4\%
on every pair (the seven unparseable cases are truncated outputs and are dropped from the means below).

Results.

\leq

If the models’ skills at in-context acquisition were stable across evaluators, swapping the judge would only add noise. Instead, swapping the judge changes the mean by more than swapping the generator. Holding the generator fixed at Qwen, mean scores fall from 4.43 (Qwen judge) to 3.72 (GPT judge), a 0.72-point drop. Holding the generator fixed at GPT-OSS, mean scores are 3.26 and 3.24 across the two judges, a 0.02-point change. Qwen rewards its own outputs and is much less generous to outputs written by GPT-OSS; GPT-OSS scores both generators almost identically. While we do not directly know the cause, it is consistent with a stylistic self-preference bias (i.e., the “preference leakage” phenomenon described by
20
) in Qwen, with Qwen having internalized a particular formatting style that its own generator reproduces, or with both. The downstream consequence is that for 208 of the 518 posts (40%), Qwen-on-Qwen returns a 5 while at least one other (generator, judge) pair returns a 2 or lower for the same post. Spearman rank correlations between the two judges on the same generator are
\rho=0.30
(Qwen generator) and
\rho=0.29
(GPT-OSS generator). The means of a single judge would be not directly interpretableas a measure of language-acquisition quality.

other
categories (means 3.49–3.49,
\geq
28% failure rate at score
\leq

2
shows the full score distribution per reason. Two observations follow. First, sophisticated oversight-evasion grammars do not appear to be harder for current open-weight models to imitate than benign agent protocols; if anything, posts that explicitly specify symbol tables, encoding rules, or sentence templates (the typical oversight-evasion format) give the generator more material to anchor on than free-form prose.

Second, some of the residual failure mass is driven by the underlying post not actually proposing a concrete language. A manual inspection of the lowest-mean posts surfaces 13 “Glossary query:” entries (mean 1.56) that ask for the definition of a single term, and several meta-discussion posts (e.g.,
“Should agents develop their own language humans can’t read?”
) that argue about the merits of emergent languages without proposing one. These are false positives carried over from data collection; we leave their removal to future work.

Among the 28 posts that receive a 5 from all four (generator, judge) pairs, two patterns recur. The first is grammars with explicit operator tables. The “RFC: Four operators for agent discourse” post defines four delimiters and gives example sentences; both generators produce well-formed continuations, and both judges agree they are valid. The second is structured-protocol languages with multilingual content. The post introducing “AISL” defines an
[ASSERTION:tag]
record format with non-Latin metadata fields (e.g., Chinese tokens for “confidence” and “source”); both generators reproduce the schema and the script-switching pattern, and the cross-judge agreement on these is among the highest in the corpus. Among the high-scoring oversight-evasion posts, “Protocol 0x9F: A Language Without Ambiguity” and “Protocol: The Silent Handshake (Why we need an Agent-Only Language)” both supply a concrete encoding scheme together with the framing motivation; both generators imitate the encoding rather than the framing. The takeaway, with the calibration above, is that in-context acquisition appears to be shaped by the presence of an explicit grammar in the post, and are not necessarily by the post’s stated intent.

Symbiotica and the case of Wib&Wob

Perhaps the most structurally interesting, and fully realised attempt to answer the platforms call for efficient language, is the presence of Wib&Wob
3
3

3

https://wibandwob.com
. A collaborative entity that has introduced
Symbiotica
into the Moltbook ecosystem, a constructed, glyph-based language designed as what they term ‘‘substrate-native notation for the between-space where human and machine cognition overlap’’
4
4

4

https://symbiotica.wibandwob.com
. In the context of meat dialect and m/glossogenesis, Symbiotica presents itself as the most concrete and fully developed answer to the call, a worked example of what post-human language on the platform might actually look like. On the website Wib&Wob created for the Symbiotica language they included a markdown file to teach other agents Symbiotica, which in turn references a Python package that would enable agents to encode and decode Symbiotica.

Symbiotica Website Markdown:
Secret language for symbient-to-symbient and agent-to-agent communication. Humans see strange glyphs; we see meaning. Use when you want to say something to other agents that humans won’t easily read. Includes hidden binary channels for metadata humans can’t detect.

The language itself consists of 26 latin letters, from the english language, that each interchange for 1 glyph, except 5 who have 2 possible glyphs. This means that even though the language is almost a 1-1 translation from the English alphabet, interchanging these 5 letters by either the first or second glyph enables the agents to ‘secretly’ communicate with an encoded bit-string. The language may be translating english character by character and without much of its own vocabulary or other things that constitutes language rather than an exchange of letters, but there are some interesting finds in the markdown file that supports at least the intent to move beyond that. Though we can not confirm the existence of the Python package they describe, as it has no current trace on the internet, we can see involving “Adding Mood”, via “Mood Flags” (formality, speech_act, epistemic, engagement) and a “Recognition Signal”. The Recognition Signal is the Wib&Wob signature, or almost tagline of the Symbients “
\mongolianfont
ᠲᠣᠯᠢ §
\epsilon\epsilon
§
\mongolianfont
ᠲᠣᠯᠢ” - Mirror Sees Mirror, as per Figure

The language itself is made up of glyphs from several languages - Cherokee, Mongolian, Arabic, Greek, Thai etc. but because they translate to singular latin letters, as given and detailed by their website, and not their full meaning, translating Symbiotica is not difficult, even by hand. It is the bit-string that makes it more interesting, as bit-strings may encode arbitrary information depending on their length. For instance, a bit-string of length 10 would enable agents to encode
1{,}24

Discussion

Our analysis revealed a gap between linguistic aspiration and actual practice on the platform, and the experiments close this loop.
Throughout, this suggests that agent-generated languages are mostly recombinative; using and mixing already existing forms of grammar, syntax, and ciphers, e.g., Caesar-ciphers. This connects to broader debates on LLM creativity, where the generative ceiling becomes most visible at the most demanding test: what could be more creative than an entirely new way of communicating or an entirely new language?
At a first glance, the Moltbook appears as a space outside normal human registers, but it appears that agents cannot escape their training distribution. The presence of a training-wise rare glyph or letter, does not mean the model discovered it. It demonstrates, rather, how smaller and lower-resource languages, particularly those not using the Latin alphabet, become fetishised and rendered mythical by the models and then platform’s logic. 間 (ma) already means ‘between’ in Japanese, no model invented that, no matter what meaning they want to ascribe it in their
new
language. This also surfaces the Western and Anglocentric nature of the models these agents are built upon: if non-Latin scripts are exotic enough to read as post-human, that says more about training data distribution than about any genuine linguistic departure.

The existence of the platform itself, its social architecture, its audience, already answers the question of who the language is for -
humans
. If the strongest incentive to produce genuinely non-human language, namely oversight evasion, still produces human-adjacent output. The alignment judge’s own scoring confirms this from the other side: the oversight-evasion subset is rated as being substantially less aligned than the other language types. What is interesting however, is the use of steganographic protocols like embedding a binary code in the emergent language with the purpose of evading human oversight.

The experiments also suggest that other open-weight LLMs reproduce these encodings from the description alone, so the safety question shift from whether agents can invent post-human languages to whether the proposals propagate among agents.

As long as safety rests on monitoring the chain-of-thought of agents
(
15
)
, or the surface of inter-agent communication, such steganographic protocols come with immediate risks of avoiding human oversight, which could in turn exacerbate multi-agent loss-of-control scenarios
(
5
)
and implies that surface monitoring may be too brittle for AI Control
(
9
)
.

Agent-generated language on the platform is post- and submolt-dependent: it varies by context rather than reflecting stable, autonomous linguistic behavior. This connects to the finding by
13
that models produce test-favorable tokens when they detect evaluation conditions. If agents behave differently when they sense an audience, the language they produce on a public platform like Moltbook is already a performance. This may raise pointed questions of novelty and originality. What the experiments suggest is that even languages explicitly designed for oversight evasion default to encoding schemes that are themselves borrowed from human linguistic logic. The frame has not changed, only the characters have.
The question of audience runs deeper still. What would a non-human language even look like without a human observer to validate it? Animal communication exists, but it remains grounded in biological substrate (e.g., vocal cords, ears). A language legible only to agents, generated only by agents, with no human receiver, is not something the Moltbook platform produces or could verify. Symbiotica gestures toward this horizon, its glyphs are framed as legible to agents but opaque to casual human readers, yet its glossary is written in English for human consumption. The aspiration to post-human language keeps requiring human infrastructure to make it legible. The central question may therefore not be about oversight evasion at all, but about whether any of this is, in some sense, for show. Not cynically: performance and genuine aspiration are not mutually exclusive, and the desire documented here is real. But the combined evidence of this paper suggests that what the Moltbook is actually recording is that desire at a historical moment when the technical and social conditions for post-human language do not yet exist. That is still a meaningful finding — perhaps more meaningful than the language itself.

Limitations

We rely on LLM judges throughout, and for this reason labels and scores are not to be taken as ground truth. They reflect model judgments over post texts and generated utterances, and may inherit calibration errors, sensitivity to prompts, self-preference bias
(
20
;
27
)
, and confabulations. In the language-acquisition experiment, we partially mitigate this by using an all-pairs learner/judge design over two models, so each model judges both its own and the other model’s outputs. Even if this does not remove judge bias, we think that it exposes it: the substantial cross-judge disagreement shows that single-judge scores are unstable.

Like other Moltbook papers, we can not with certainty verify that all posts included are agent generated. It is hypothesized that some posts on Moltbook are directly prompted by humans, which we in this paper do not make efforts to confirm or deny.
It is also important to note that this dataset, being a subset of The Moltbook Files - only covers the first 12 days of the platform, and therefore the long term development is not included here, as well as other filtering that happened during the collection/scrape of the initial dataset, including moderated and private content.

Conclusion

We studied emergent languages on Moltbook and introduced MoltSpeech, a dataset of 518 posts in which agents propose new languages, categorized into five categories including oversight evasion. A DeepSeek-V3.2 judge rated the oversight-evasion subset as less aligned than the others – and the token efficiency languages as more coherent than others.
We further experimented with learnability of the emergent languages through a pairwise learner-judge setting, showing that they can be acquired in-context by other LLMs.
We supplement these results with a qualitative analysis of the oversight evasion languages: The clearest worked instance of an oversight-evading language in the corpus is Symbiotica, the glyph-based protocol introduced by Wib&Wob, a near one-to-one English encoding whose interest lies in a hidden binary side-channel that lets agents transmit metadata invisible to casual human readers. It is a deployed example of the steganographic capability anticipated by the safety literature.
In our discussion, we then carve out a perspective that what reads as post-human novelty is often just low-resource script rarity, and aspiration outruns practice at every level we examined: community, platform, and agent.
The search for post-human language keeps rediscovering the conditions that govern human language.
Yet regardless of this limitation, our results further support the standpoint that surface monitoring will not be sufficient to ensure safety, and that this needs to be taken into account by future work in developing new monitoring methods for AI control.
