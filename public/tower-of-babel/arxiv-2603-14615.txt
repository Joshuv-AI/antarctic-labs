Characterizing the optimum bases of a convex geometry using quasi-closed hypergraphs
Anthony Meunier, Lhouari Nourine, Simon Vilmin

Abstract
Optimizing an implicational base of a closure system consists in turning this implicational base into an equivalent one with premises and conclusions as small as possible. This task is known to be hard in general but tractable for a number of classes of closure systems. In particular, several classes of convex geometries are known to have tractable optimization, while the problem was recently claimed to remain hard in general convex geometries. Continuing this line of research, we give a characterization of the optimum bases of a convex geometry in terms of what we call quasi-closed hypergraphs. We then use this characterization to show that when each quasi-closed hypergraph has disjoint edges, any implicational base of the convex geometry can be optimized in polynomial time with existing minimization and reduction algorithms. Finally, we prove that this property applies to double-shelling, acyclic, affine and acceptant convex geometries, thus unifying the existing results regarding the tractability of optimization for the first three classes. Keywords: closure systems, implicational bases, optimum bases, convex geometries.

Within the study of implicational bases, the task of turning an implicational base into a smaller one has been the topic of numerous research.
Several measures of the size of an implicational base have been defined and studied: the number of implications, the sum of sizes of the premises of its implications, of the conclusions, or both.

Our contribution more precisely follows this line.
By associating to each essential set
C

2

(X,\mathcal{C})
a hypergraph

Theorem 1

(restate=THMcgoptimib, label=thm:cg-optim-ib)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a convex geometry.
A pair
(X,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is a collection of valid implications of
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a left-optimum (resp. optimum) implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
if and only if
\Sigma
is obtained by choosing, for each essential set
C
, exactly one implication of the form
\ex(C)\rightarrow T
where
T
is a hitting set (resp.​ minimum hitting set) of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
.

We then focus on the case where all the quasi-closed hypergraphs have pairwise disjoint edges.
In this case, minimal and minimum hitting sets of these hypergraphs coincide.
Therefore, based on Theorem
, any greedy reduction of the conclusions of a left-optimum implicational base will produce an optimum implicational base.
Since in convex geometries each closed set has a unique minimal spanning set, left-optimality is easily reached by a greedy reduction of the premises.
Hence, any polynomial time algorithms such as in
[
29
,
35
]
that minimize and reduce an implicational base will in this case produce an optimum base, which yields our second theorem:

Theorem 2

(restate=THMqcdisjoint, label=thm:qc-disjoint)
.

An implicational base of a convex geometry where each quasi-closed hypergraph has pairwise disjoint edges can be optimized in polynomial time.

Finally, we prove that Theorem
applies to four different classes of convex geometries:

Theorem 3

(restate=THMcgclasses, label=thm:cg-classes)
.

Each quasi-closed hypergraph of a convex geometry has pairwise disjoint edges whenever the convex geometry belongs to:

(1)

double-shelling convex geometries;

(2)

acyclic convex geometries;

(3)

affine convex geometries; or

(4)

acceptant convex geometries.

Consequently, an implicational base of a convex geometry in any of these classes can be optimized in polynomial time.

The first three classes are already known for having tractable optimization
[
2
,
23
,
34
]
as previously mentioned.
Our approach connects and unifies these results, all the while adding a new class to the family of convex geometries with efficient optimization.
This class, acceptant convex geometries, has been studied within the context of choice functions and stable matchings
[
3
,
13
,
14
,
17
]
, where convex geometries are known as path-independent choice functions.

Preliminaries

All the object we consider in this paper are finite.
Let
X
be a set.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(X)
its powerset. By
|X|
its size.
If
\mathcal{H}
is a hypergraph, a
hitting set
of
\mathcal{H}
is set of vertices that intersects every edge of
\mathcal{H}
.
A hitting set
T
is
minimal
if it is inclusion-wise minimal among hitting sets, and
minimum
if it is cardinality minimum among hitting sets.
Sometimes we write a set as the concatenation of its elements, i.e.,
abc
instead of
\{a,b,c\}
.
We also withdraw brackets for singletons when it is clear from the context.
We proceed to recall definitions on closure systems, implicational bases, convex geometries.

Closure operator, closure systems.

A
closure operator
over a ground set
X
is a map
\phi:\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
that satisfies, for all
A,B\subseteq X
: (1)
A\subseteq\phi(A)
, (2)
A\subseteq B
entails
\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B)
and (3)
\phi(\phi(A))=\phi(A)
.
A set
C\subseteq X
is
closed
w.r.t.
\phi
if
\phi(C)=C
.
The pair
(X,\mathcal{C})
where
\mathcal{C}=\{C:C\subseteq X\text{ and }\phi(C)=C\}
is a
closure system
.
A closure system is a pair
(X,\mathcal{C})
where
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of subsets of
X
, called
closed sets
, such that
X\in\mathcal{C}
and
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
for every
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Each closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
has an associated closure operator
\phi
over
X
defined by
\phi(A)=\bigcap\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},A\subseteq C\}
for all
A\subseteq X
.
Therefore, there is a one-to-one correspondence between closure operator and closure systems.
The closure operator
\phi
of a closure system induces a partition of
\mathcal{P}(X)
into equivalence classes, where the equivalence class of a set
A
is the family
[A]_{\phi}=\{B:B\subseteq X,\phi(B)=\phi(A)\}
.
If
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a closure system, the poset
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
of closed sets ordered by inclusion form a
(closure) lattice
where, for any
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
,
C_{1}\land C_{2}=C_{1}\cap C_{2}
and
C_{1}\lor C_{2}=\phi(C_{1}\cup C_{2})
.
Let
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
be two closed sets.
We say that
C_{1}
is a
predecessor
of
C_{2}
in
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
, equivalently that
C_{2}

covers

C_{1}
, if there is no
C_{3}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C_{1}\subset C_{3}\subset C_{2}
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a closure system and let
A\subseteq X
.
If
C\in\mathcal{C}
, we say that
A
is a
spanning set
of
C
if
\phi(A)=C
.
For
x\in X
with
x\notin A
, we say that
A
is a
(non-trivial) generator
of
x
if
x\in\phi(A)
.
An
extreme point
of
A
is an element
x\in A
such that
x\notin\phi(A\setminus\{x\})
.
We denote by
\ex(A)
the extreme points of
A
.
Note that for any
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
\ex(C)
is included in any (inclusion-wise) minimal spanning set of
C
.
Let
Q\subseteq X
.
We say that
Q
is
quasi-closed
if it is non-closed and for every
A\subseteq Q
,
\phi(A)\subset\phi(Q)
entails
\phi(A)\subseteq Q
.

Closed sets are often considered quasi-closed, but it will be more convenient for us to assume they are not.

A set
P
is
pseudo-closed
if it is an inclusion-wise minimal quasi-closed set among the spanning sets of

.
A closed set which is the closure of a quasi-closed (or pseudo-closed) set is

Note that for any closed set

included in

This holds in particular for pseudo-closed sets.
Quasi-closed sets are also related to the
saturation operator

This operator is defined for all

\sigma(Y)=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\psi^{k}(Y)

\psi(Y)=Y\cup\bigcup\{\phi(Z):Z\subseteq Y\text{ and }\phi(Z)\subset\phi(Y)
.
The operator
\sigma
is a closure operator whose closed sets are quasi-closed and closed sets of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
In other words,

is quasi-closed if and only if

is not closed and
\sigma(Q)=Q
.

We finally define convex geometries.
A closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a

if
\varnothing\in\mathcal{C}
and for each closed set
C\subset X
, there exists
x\notin C
such that

is closed.
Convex geometries can be characterized by their spanning sets:
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry if and only if every closed set
C
has a unique minimal spanning set being
\ex(C)
.
As a consequence, we observe that the predecessors of each closed set
C\neq\varnothing
are precisely the sets
C\setminus\{x\}
for

.

Implicational bases.

We turn our attention to implications.
We redirect the reader to
[
7
,
38
]
for a detailed introduction to the topic.
An
implication
over
X
is an expression
A\rightarrow B
where
A,B
are subsets of
X
.
The set
A
is called the
premise
and
B
the
conclusion
of the implication.
Given a collection
\Sigma
of implications over
X
,
we call the pair
(X,\Sigma)
an
implicational base
.
The number of implications in
\Sigma
is denoted by
|\Sigma|
.
The
left-size
of
\Sigma
is defined as
||\Sigma||_{\ell}:=\sum_{A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma}|A|
.
Similarly,
||\Sigma||_{r}:=\sum_{A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma}|B|
denotes the
right-size
of
\Sigma
.
The
total size
of
\Sigma
is defined as
||\Sigma||:=\sum_{A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma}|A|+|B|
.
Note that we equivalently have
||\Sigma||=||\Sigma||_{\ell}+||\Sigma||_{r}
.

We relate implications to closure systems.
Let
A\rightarrow B
be an implication and
C\subseteq X
.
We say that
C\subseteq X

satisfies

A\rightarrow B
if
A\subseteq C
implies
B\subseteq C
.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an implicational base.
The set
C
satisfies
\Sigma
if it satisfies all the implications of
\Sigma
.
The pair
(X,\mathcal{C})
where
\mathcal{C}
is the collection of subsets of
X
satisfying
\Sigma
is known to form a closure system.
Its associated closure operator
\Sigma
can be computed in polynomial time from the pair
(X,\Sigma)
using the forward chaining procedure.
For each
A\subseteq X
, this procedure builds a sequence
A=Y_{0}\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq Y_{k}=\Sigma(A)
of sets defined by
Y_{i}=Y_{i-1}\cup\bigcup\{B:A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma,A\subseteq Y_{i-1}\}
.
An
equivalence class
of
(X,\Sigma)
is a set of implications of
\Sigma
whose premises have same closure.
Any implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
is thus partitioned by its equivalence classes.
Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a closure system.
An implication
A\rightarrow B
over
X
is
valid
in
(X,\mathcal{C})
if every closed set satisfies the implication.
Note that
A\rightarrow B
is valid in
(X,\mathcal{C})
if and only if
B\subseteq\phi(A)
.
An implicational base is a
(valid) implicational base
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
if the closure system associated to
(X,\Sigma)
is precisely
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
In general, an implicational base consisting only of valid implications of
(X,\mathcal{C})
does not need to be an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
A closure system can usually be represented by several different implicational bases.
Two implicational bases with the same closure system are
equivalent
.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
We say that
(X,\Sigma)
is
minimum
if
|\Sigma|=\min\{|\Sigma^{\prime}|:(X,\Sigma^{\prime})\text{ is an implicational base of }(X,\mathcal{C})\}
.
Similarly, we say that
(X,\Sigma)
is
optimum
(resp.
left-optimum
,
right-optimum
) if
\Sigma
minimizes
||\cdot||
(resp.
||\cdot||_{\ell}
,
||\cdot||_{r}
)
among the implicational bases of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
We say that
(X,\Sigma)
is
left-reduced
if for every
A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma
and every
A^{\prime}\subset A
,
(\Sigma\setminus\{A\rightarrow B\})\cup\{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B\}
is no longer an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
Similarly,
(X,\Sigma)
is
right-reduced
if
(\Sigma\setminus\{A\rightarrow B\})\cup\{A\rightarrow B^{\prime}\}
is no longer an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
for every
B^{\prime}\subset B
and every
A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma
.
Finally,
(X,\Sigma)
is

if it is both left- and right-reduced.

(3)

The

of

\Sigma_{c}:=\{P\rightarrow\phi(P)\setminus P:P\text{ is pseudo-closed}\}

(see, e.g.,

Let

3
Quasi-closed hypergraphs and optimum bases of convex geometries

In this section we give a characterization of the optimum bases of a convex geometry.
This characterization relies on the minimum hitting sets of the hypergraphs of the quasi-closed sets that are, within a same equivalence class, inclusion-wise maximal.

The starting point of our approach lies in the more general context of arbitrary closure systems.
Consider some closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
and let
\Sigma
be a collection of valid implications of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
In the next proposition, we observe that what makes
(X,\Sigma)
an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
or not does only depend on its ability to make every quasi-closed set indeed non-closed using implications with premises from its equivalence class.
Note that this has also been observed by Bichoupan
[
9
, unpublished]
, although we propose a slightly more precise form.
We still give a proof of this claim for self-completeness.

Proposition 1

(Lemma 2 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a collection of valid implications of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
The pair
(X,\Sigma)
is an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
if and only if for every quasi-closed set
Q
of
\mathcal{C}
, there exists
A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma
such that
A\subseteq Q
,
\phi(A)=\phi(Q)
, and
B
contains an element of
\phi(Q)\setminus Q
.

Proof.

We start with the only if part.
Assume
(X,\Sigma)
is an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
and let
Q
be a quasi-closed set of
\mathcal{C}
.
Since
(X,\Sigma)
is an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
and
Q
is not closed, there exists
A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma
such that
A\subseteq Q
but
B\nsubseteq Q
.
As
Q
is quasi-closed,
\phi(A)\subset\phi(Q)
would entail
B\subseteq\phi(A)\subset Q
, a contradiction with the fact that
B\nsubseteq Q
.
We deduce that
\phi(A)=\phi(Q)
and that
B
contains an element of
\phi(Q)\setminus Q
.

We move to the if part.
It is sufficient to show that
\phi(Q)=\Sigma(Q)
for each quasi-closed set
Q
.
Since
(X,\Sigma)
contains only valid implications of
(X,\mathcal{C})
, we readily have
\Sigma(Q)\subseteq\phi(Q)
.
Hence, it remains only to argue that
\phi(Q)\subseteq\Sigma(Q)
for each quasi-closed set
Q
.
Assume for contradiction this is not the case and let
C
be an inclusion-wise minimal closed set of
\mathcal{C}
where such a
Q
exists.
We thus have
\Sigma(Q)\subset\phi(Q)
.
By assumption on
\Sigma
,
\Sigma(Q)
cannot be quasi-closed in
(X,\mathcal{C})
as otherwise there would exist an implication
A\rightarrow B
in
\Sigma
such that
A\subseteq\Sigma(Q)
,
A
spans
\phi(Q)
and
B\nsubseteq\Sigma(Q)
.
Therefore, there exists
Y\subseteq\Sigma(Q)
such that
\phi(Y)\subset\phi(\Sigma(Q))=C
and

4
Quasi-closed hypergraphs with disjoint edges

Drawing from Theorem
, we investigate the case where each quasi-closed hypergraph of a convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
has pairwise disjoint edges.
We prove that any implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
can be optimized in polynomial time ( Theorem
), and then that several classes of convex geometries do enjoy this property (Theorem
).

For Theorem
we simply argue that, under our assumptions, any polynomial time algorithm that takes as input an implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
and turns it into an equivalent minimum reduced base does in fact make it optimum.
Minimization and reduction can be achieved in polynomial time by applying, say, Shock’s algorithm for minimization
[
35
]
and then Maier’s algorithm for reduction
[
30
, Algorithm 5.7, p. 77]
which first applies left-reduction and then right-reduction.
Informally, assuming
(X,\Sigma)
is already minimum and considering some implication
A\rightarrow T
, left-reduction will turn
A
into
\ex(C)
where
C
is the essential set spanned by
A
while right-reduction will turn
T
into a minimal hitting set of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
by Theorem
.
Given that the edges of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
are disjoint this minimal hitting set is also minimum, which guarantees optimality again by Theorem
.

\THMqcdisjoint

*

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
(X,\Sigma)
be a minimum implicational base
(X,\mathcal{C}
).
Since
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry, each closed set has a unique minimal spanning set consisting of its extreme points.
Also recall that since
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry and
(X,\Sigma)
a minimum implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
, each implication
A\rightarrow T
of
\Sigma
corresponds to a unique essential set

such that

.
Therefore, applying left-reduction on

will turn each premise

of

into

where

is the essential set spanned by

Hence, left-reduction turns

.
Now we consider right-reduction.
Let

be some essential set with corresponding implication

in

By Theorem

4.2
Acyclic convex geometries

We turn our attention to acyclic convex geometries.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an implicational base.
The implication-graph of
(X,\Sigma)
is the directed graph with vertex set
X
and arcs
(a,b)
if there exists
A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma
such that
a\in A,b\in B
.
We consider without loss of generality that
A\cap B=\varnothing
to avoid loops.
We say that
(X,\Sigma)
is
acyclic
if its implication-graph does not have directed cycles.
An acyclic closure system is a closure system admitting an acyclic implicational base and acyclic closure systems are convex geometries, see e.g.,
[
36
,
38
]
.
Below we recall that acyclic convex geometries are characterized by an acyclic
\delta
relation.
The
\delta
relation of a closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is the binary relation over
X
such that
b\delta a
holds if there is a minimal generator
A
of
b
that contains
a
.

Lemma 4

(see, e.g.,
[
23
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

A closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is an acyclic convex geometry if and only if its
\delta
relation is acyclic.

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{C})
is an acyclic convex geometry and let
C
be essential.
We show in the next lemma that each maximal quasi-closed set of
C
is of the form
C\setminus\{x\}
.
This statement can be deduced from the connection between essential implicates, critical generators and quasi-closed sets drawn in earlier literature.
In
[
23
]
, essential implicates are those implications
A\rightarrow x
that belong to every possible implicational base with singleton conclusions of a given closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
The authors prove that if
(X,\mathcal{C})
is an acyclic convex geometry, essential implicates do in fact constitute the unique optimum base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
(once aggregated by premises).
Then, Nourine et al.
[
16
]
observe that the essential implicates of an acyclic convex geometry coincide with the so-called critical generators of the convex geometry.
In a convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
, a minimal generator
A
of
x
is
critical
if
x\in\ex(\phi(A)\setminus\{a\})
for every
a\in A

[
25
]
.
Finally, Wild
[
36
,
38
]
noticed that
A
is a critical generator of
x
if and only if
\phi(A)\setminus\{x\}
is quasi-closed which allows to derive the lemma below.
We still provide an alternative proof based on our approach of using maximal quasi-closed sets and that only uses the
\delta
relation.

Lemma 5
.

The maximal quasi-closed sets of an essential set
C
are of the form
C\setminus\{x\}
with
x\in C
.

Proof.

Let
C
be an essential closed set of
(X,\mathcal{C})
and assume for contradiction
C
admits a maximal quasi-closed set
Q:=C\setminus\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
for some
k>1
.
Consider some
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.
By assumption,
Q\cup\{x_{i}\}
is no longer quasi-closed.
Therefore, there exists
Y\subseteq Q\cup\{x_{i}\}
such that
\phi(Y)\subset C
and
\phi(Y)\nsubseteq Q
.
In particular, there exists
x_{j}\neq x_{i}
such that
x_{j}\in\phi(Y)\setminus Q
.
Let
A
be a minimal generator of
x_{j}
included in
Y
.
Since
Q
is quasi-closed and
\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(Y)
,
A\subseteq Q
would entail
\phi(A)\subseteq Q
, which contradicts
x_{j}\notin Q
.
We deduce that
x_{i}\in A
and hence that
x_{j}\delta x_{i}
holds.
Applying this reasoning to any
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
, we deduce that each
x_{i}
satisfies
x_{i}\delta x_{j}
for some
x_{j}\neq x_{i}
, hence that the relation
\delta
restricted to
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
contains a cycle.
This contradicts the acyclicity of
(X,\mathcal{C})
by Theorem
4
, and concludes the proof.
∎

The quasi-closed hypergraphs of
(X,\mathcal{C})
thus consist of singleton edges that are trivially disjoint, which proves item (2) of Theorem
.
Note that in particular, each quasi-closed hypergraphs has a unique minimum hitting set.
This entails that
(X,\mathcal{C})
does indeed have a unique optimum base as showed in
[
23
]
.

4.3
Affine convex geometries

We now consider affine convex geometries, i.e., convex geometries arising from point configurations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
18
]
.
If
X
is a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\phi(Y)=h(Y)\cap X
where
h
is the usual convex hull operator in geometry.
The operator
\phi
is a closure operator and the associated closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry called the
affine convex geometry
associated to
X
.
Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be an affine geometry.
To prove item (3) of Theorem
, we first recall a Theorem of Kashiwabara and Nakamura
[
34
]
, rephrased in our terminology.

Theorem 6

(Theorem 20 in
[
34
]
)
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
be the pseudo-closed sets of
(X,\mathcal{C})
, and for each
1\leq i\leq m
, let
x_{i}
be any element of
\phi(P_{i})\setminus P_{i}
.
Then, the pair
(X,\Sigma)
with
\Sigma=\{P_{1}\rightarrow x_{1},\dots,P_{m}\rightarrow x_{m}\}
is an optimum implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.

From Theorem
6
and Theorem
we deduce that for each essential set
C
and each
x\in C\setminus\ex(C)
,
x
is a hitting set of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
.
We obtain:

Lemma 6
.

The unique maximal quasi-closed set of any essential set
C
is
\ex(C)
.

Proof.

In order to prove the claim, it is sufficient to show that for each essential set
C
and each corresponding pseudo-closed set
P
, any element
x\in C\setminus P
is a hitting set of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
.
Indeed, since each edge of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
is disjoint from
P
, the fact that each singleton
\{x\}
with
x\in C\setminus P
is a hitting set of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
entails that
Q_{C}=C\setminus P
is the unique edge of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
.
However, by Theorem
6
every implication of the form
P\rightarrow x
belongs to some (left-)optimal implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
By Theorem
, we thus derive that
x
is a hitting set of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
as expected, which concludes the proof.
∎

The unique edge of
\mathcal{Q}(C)
is thus
C\setminus\ex(C)
.
It then vacuously holds that each quasi-closed hypergraph of
(X,\mathcal{C})
has pairwise disjoint edges.
This proves item (3) of Theorem
.
Let us mention though that any convex geometry satisfying Theorem
6
also satisfies Lemma
6
, hence that Theorem
4
may apply to a broader range of convex geometries in this regard.

4.4
Acceptant convex geometries

We finally study (
q
-)acceptant convex geometries.
A convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
is

if for every
C\in\mathcal{C}

|\ex(C)|=\min(q,|C|)

Observe that a convex geometry is acceptant for at most one

.
Besides, the definition shows that a convex geometry

is

with strictly less than
q
elements is closed and every closed set with at least
q
elements has precisely
q
extreme points.
The trivial closed sets of a

.

We prove that, much as affine convex geometries, an essential set

Lemma 7
.

Any essential set
C
has a unique maximal quasi-closed set.

Proof.

Let
C
be essential and assume for contradiction it admits at least two distinct inclusion-wise maximal quasi-closed set
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
.
Observe that
C
is non-trivial.
Let
C^{\prime}
be a predecessor of

We have
C^{\prime}=C\setminus\{x\}
for some

Such a

must exist as

is assumed to be non-trivial, hence non-empty.
As
Q_{1},Q_{2}
are quasi-closed,
C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1}
and

are closed.
As
\ex(C)\setminus\{x\}\subseteq C^{\prime},Q_{1},Q_{2}
, we have that
\ex(C)\setminus\{x\}\subseteq\ex(C^{\prime}),\ex(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1})
,
\ex(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{2})

,
C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1}
and

share

as

is

Now let
C^{\prime\prime}:=(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1})\lor(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{2})
.
Since
C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1}\subseteq C^{\prime\prime}\subseteq C^{\prime}
, we have by previous discussion that
C^{\prime\prime}
have at least
q-1
extreme points given by
\ex(C^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}
.
Since
C^{\prime\prime}
is the supremum of
(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1})\lor(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{2})
there must exist a predecessor
C_{1}
of

such that
(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1})\subseteq C_{1}
and
(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{2})\nsubseteq C_{1}
.
Because

is a convex geometry, we have

for some
x_{2}\in(C^{\prime}\cap C_{2})\setminus(C^{\prime}\cap C_{1})
.
Dually, we derive the existence of some
x_{1}\in(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{1})\setminus(C^{\prime}\cap Q_{2})

is a predecessor of

but not

As

We deduce that

5
Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a characterization of the optimum bases of a convex geometry.
This characterization relies on quasi-closed hypergraphs, the hypergraphs defined for each essential set by taking the complement of its inclusion-wise maximal quasi-closed sets.
In the case where each quasi-closed hypergaph of the convex geometry has disjoint edges, we proved that using existing minimization and reduction algorithms was sufficient to produce, in polynomial time, an optimum base out of any other implicational base.
This property of quasi-closed hypergraphs turns out to apply to double-shelling, acyclic, affine and acceptant convex geometries, which allows to unify existing results of the literature.

We conclude the paper with some observations and discussions for future work.
First, we observe that while in a convex geometry the property of having quasi-closed hypergraphs with disjoint edges guarantees efficient optimization, whether this property can be recognized in polynomial time from an implicational base remains unsettled.
This leads to the following question:
given an implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
of some convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
, is it possible to decide in polynomial time whether all quasi-closed hypergraphs of
(X,\mathcal{C})
have disjoint edges?

Another, more general, intriguing questions consists in using our approach to identify further classes of convex geometries for which optimization is tractable.
Indeed, by Theorem
, a convex geometry can be optimized efficiently if and only if for each quasi-closed hypergraph, at least one hitting set can be computed in polynomial time.
Thus, classifying convex geometries according to their quasi-closed hypergraphs may be a good yardstick to better understand the hardness of the optimization task in convex geometries.

In particular, we may study whether other classes of convex geometries have quasi-closed hypergraphs with disjoint edges.
Indeed, the next example shows that convex geometries with edge-disjoint quasi-closed hypergraphs properly contain the classes we studied in this paper:

Example 8
.

Let
X=\{a,\dots,e\}
and
\Sigma=\{\mathit{ac}\rightarrow\mathit{b},\mathit{bd}\rightarrow c,\mathit{ad}\rightarrow\mathit{bc},e\rightarrow\mathit{ab}\}
.
The associated closure system is a convex geometry that does not belong to any of the classes of Theorem
4
.
It is not a (
2
-)acceptant, affine or double-shelling convex geometry as
\{e\}\notin\mathcal{C}
.
It is not acyclic since
b
and
c
belongs to minimal generators of one another.
The quasi-closed hypergraphs are
\mathcal{Q}(\mathit{abe})=\{a,b\}
,
\mathcal{Q}(\mathit{abc})=\{b\}
,
\mathcal{Q}(\mathit{bcd})=\{c\}
and
\mathcal{Q}(\mathit{abcd})=\{bc\}
and all have disjoint edges.

Convex geometries with the Carousel property and
D
-geometries are interesting candidates as they respectively generalize affine convex geometries and acyclic convex geometries being covered by Theorem
4
.
Moreover, both have been shown to have tractable optimization
[
2
]
.
In particular, the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 12]
on convex geometries with the Carousel property seems to use an argument similar to
[
34
, Theorem 9]
that we used in Lemma
6
for showing that in an affine convex geometry, an essential set has a unique maximal quasi-closed set.
This suggests that the same holds true for convex geometries with the Carousel property.
