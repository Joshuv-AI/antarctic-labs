Universal Planckian dissipation in the strange metal state of the cuprates
Authors: A. Shekhter, B. J. Ramshaw, M. K. Chan, N. Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A major puzzle in high-Tc superconductivity is the origin of the “Planckian” relaxation rate 1/τ underlying the linear-in-temperature resistivity in the strange-metal state, which persists up to very high temperatures. Implicit in theoretical discussions is the assumption that 1/τ must be universal. Experimentally, it is unclear, however, how such universality can be reconciled with the observed strong doping dependence of the resistivity over a wide doping range. We show, through an analysis of a large body of optical conductivity and electrical resistivity data, that a universal 1/τ requires only that the square optical plasma frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)$$\end{document}ωopt2(p) scales linearly with hole doping p across the entire doping range, as is observed experimentally. We further argue that this can be understood via a Gutzwiller factor in doped Mott insulators of the form proposed by Anderson.

Introduction

The discovery of high-temperature superconductivity in the cuprates1 was significant not only because of their unusually high superconducting transition temperatures, Tc, but also because their normal states represented the first clear example of a class of unconventional metals termed ‘strange metals’2, to distinguish them from conventional metals3. Of particular interest is the electrical resistivity of strange metals4–6, which unambiguously defies Fermi-liquid phenomenology7. Since the discovery of the cuprates, similar strange-metal behavior has been observed in a wide range of materials8–11, most of which also exhibit unconventional superconductivity. Today, the research of strange metals is closely tied to the search for unconventional superconductors.

The strange metal state in the cuprates2,4,10,12,13 is characterized by a linear-in-temperature (T-linear) resistivity6,14,15, expressed as ρ = A T, that persists up to temperatures of T ~ 1000 K—limited only by the chemical stability of these compounds. This form of resistivity is ‘scale-invariant’ because the only energy scale governing electrical transport is the externally imposed temperature, kBT, rather than an intrinsic energy scale16,17. This stands in stark contrast to conventional metals, whose transport is governed by the Fermi energy—an intrinsic property of the electronic system—together with temperature3,7.

While the microscopic mechanism of strange metal behavior remains heavily debated4,6,9,10,12,18–20, the T-linear resistivity implies the equivalence of two energy scales: the relaxation rate, ℏ/τ, and the thermal energy, kBT: 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hslash /\tau=\alpha \,{k}_{{\rm{B}}}T.$$\end{document}ℏ/τ=αkBT. This equivalency, where the factor α is of order unity, has been termed ‘Planckian’ due to a purported naturalness of the connection between these two energy scales within several theoretical frameworks4,5,9,10,12,19,21–24. Implicit in these theoretical discussions is a doping independence of α. In fact, values of α close to unity have been reported on the basis of low-temperature resistivity behavior at select dopings in several cuprate families8,25 (see “Methods” section), including p = 0.26 in La2−xSrxCuO4 (LSCO). Because the slope A(p) of the T-linear electrical resistivity in the strange metal state of the cuprates is strongly doping-dependent15,26–30, it is not at all clear how the purported universal value of α can be reconciled with the observed resistivity behavior throughout the phase diagram.

Existing approaches to analyzing the electrical resistivity in the context of Planckian dissipation use a Drude model8,25,31,32, in which the electrical conductivity is expressed in terms of the ratio of the carrier density n to an effective mass m*33. Such an approximation is warranted for isotropic systems, especially those with a parabolic band, for which Galilean invariance is either an intrinsic or emergent property (see “Methods” section). In these cases, both the electronic specific heat coefficient γ (and the associated effective mass) and the Hall coefficient RH are expected to be only weakly temperature dependent3,7.

In the cuprates, however, γ(p, T) 34,35 and RH(p, T) 36,37 exhibit strong, and in some cases non-monotonic, dependences on both temperature and doping. At the very least, these behaviors reflect a pronounced four-fold anisotropy in the electronic structure, arising in part from proximity to a van Hove singularity38 and from pseudogap effects39. Because of this anisotropy, the electrical transport in the cuprates must be understood in terms of Boltzmann transport40,41, rather than through the Drude approximation. Surprisingly, an approach based on Boltzmann transport has not yet been applied to the question of universal Planckian dissipation.

Boltzmann transport supersedes the Drude approximation because it correctly incorporates anisotropies and singularities in the electronic dispersion33. The experimentally relevant quantity for zero-field transport is the Fermi-surface velocity average entering the squared optical plasma frequency, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}$$\end{document}ωopt2 (see “Methods” section). This enables the universality of the Planckian relaxation rate across the cuprate phase diagram to be addressed in two important ways. First, because the conductivity is weighted toward regions of the Fermi surface with large Fermi velocity, the in-plane conductivity of the cuprates is weighted primarily toward the nodal states40,41, which remain comparatively robust across the phase diagram42. This removes the need, inherent in the Drude approximation, to consider n and m*, both of which have been argued to exhibit strong and non-monotonic dependences on doping and temperature15,34,43–50. Second, because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}$$\end{document}ωopt2 is determined independently using optical conductivity50–52, it enables a direct comparison between this quantity and the inverse slope of the T-linear resistivity.

Here, we show that a comparison between electrical transport data and the optically determined \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}$$\end{document}ωopt250–52 enables a quantitative analysis of the Planckian relaxation rate in the cuprates to be made across the phase diagram. A striking observation is that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)$$\end{document}ωopt2(p) varies linearly with doping (i.e., is p-linear) over the entire doping range, suggesting a true universality of Planckian dissipation. The observed p-linear doping dependence of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)$$\end{document}ωopt2(p) is such a conspicuously simple form that it begs its own explanation. We address this question in the second half of the paper by showing that the observed behavior arises naturally within a Mott picture53,54, in which the electronic dispersion is renormalized by a Gutzwiller factor q ≈ p55. This theoretical framework can be tested not only through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)$$\end{document}ωopt2(p), but also through a comparison with entropy measurements.

The electrical transport can be analyzed within the framework of the Einstein relation for conductivity, σ = e2κDe, which has been extensively applied to studies of two-dimensional low-carrier density electron liquids58. For parabolic bands typical for such low-carrier density systems, the conductivity σ = 1/ρ is expressed as the product of the electronic compressibility κ = ∂n/∂μ and the diffusion coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{{\rm{e}}}={v}_{{\rm{F}}}^{2}{\tau }_{{\rm{e}}}/2$$\end{document}De=vF2τe/2 at the Fermi surface, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{{\rm{F}}}={\hslash }^{-1}{(\partial {\epsilon }_{k}/\partial k)}_{k={k}_{{\rm{F}}}}$$\end{document}vF=ℏ−1(∂ϵk/∂k)k=kF is the Fermi velocity. When Fermi-liquid interaction effects are included, the electronic compressibility is renormalized by a dimensionless Landau interaction factor: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa={(1+{F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}})}^{-1}\nu $$\end{document}κ=(1+F0s)−1ν3, where ν = m*/πℏ2 is the electronic density of states (assuming a two-dimensional system). Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}}$$\end{document}F0s represents the zeroth harmonic Landau interaction parameter in the charge channel.

The effective mass near the Fermi surface is defined as m* = ℏ kF/vF, where ℏ kF is the Fermi momentum, and is directly determined by the electronic specific heat. Meanwhile, the relaxation time entering the diffusion coefficient is an effective quantity that incorporates Fermi-liquid interaction factors, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau }_{{\rm{e}}}=\tau (1+{F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}})$$\end{document}τe=τ(1+F0s)58,59, because it characterizes collective dynamics of particle-hole diffusion rather than the single-particle relaxation rate. Note here also that τe does not represent the characteristic time for quasiparticle decay60. It is well known that in the zero-frequency limit of the conductivity, the Fermi-liquid interaction factors cancel. Hence, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1+{F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}})$$\end{document}(1+F0s) renormalizes both the compressibility and the effective scattering rate.

The van Hove singularity (at p ≈ 0.20 in LSCO38) represents a particularly strong deviation from band parabolicity in the cuprates that cannot be neglected. Its effect is not confined to a single doping level, but extends across the entire doping range of interest. To take into consideration this effect, the Einstein relation must be recast in a form consistent with Boltzmann transport33: σ = e2〈κ(k)De(k)〉, where κ(k) and De(k) denote the partial compressibility and diffusion coefficient at each momentum k on the Fermi surface, respectively, and 〈⋯〉 indicates a weighted average over the Fermi surface. Note that because Planckian dissipation is a local phenomenon61, the resulting Planckian relaxation rate is expected to be the same everywhere on the Fermi surface, consistent with experimental observations40.

Mott physics as a possible mechanism for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}\propto p$$\end{document}ωopt2∝p

It is interesting to note that a similar Gutzwiller factor effect was previously identified in 3He68,70. While it was initially suggested that the observed pressure-dependence of the Landau parameters in 3He was due to proximity to a Stoner instability71, it was subsequently shown that such an instability could not simultaneously account for the observed magnitude of the compressibility and magnetic susceptibility53,54,72. Specifically, it was shown that Fermi liquid relations require the observed thermodynamic properties to be determined by an enhancement of the quasiparticle effective mass that occurs independently of Fermi liquid interactions.

The transformative idea that enabled a quantitative understanding of the pressure-dependent Fermi liquid parameters in 3He is that the enhancement of the quasiparticle effective mass is caused by the suppression of the mobility of 3He atoms by hard-core repulsive interactions. This is referred to as an ‘almost-localized Fermi liquid,’ in analogy with a Mott insulator doped away from half-filling by a small amount p53,54,72.

To account for the strong hard-core repulsion between atoms in 3He, Anderson and Vollhardt53,54,72 introduced the ‘lattice-gas model’ with an on-site repulsion energy parameter U. A key feature of this model is that the number of lattice sites differs from the number of atoms73. Consequently, the liquid is described by a new thermodynamic parameter p (denoted δ in refs. 53,54), akin to an order parameter without symmetry breaking74, which exists alongside pressure and temperature. This parameter defines a small difference between the volume per site of the lattice, vs, and the volume per atom, va, expressed as p = 1 − vs/va. Thus, it acts as an effective doping. Similar to the Landau description of phase transitions75, p itself is determined by a minimum in the free energy. While the Mott-insulating state occurs in this model at p = 0, it is preempted in 3He by solidification at p ≈ 0.05, corresponding to a pressure of 34.36 bar54,74.

In the limit of strong hard-core repulsion (U → ∞), the effective mass enhancement in 3He is inversely proportional to the doping: m/m* = q ≈ 1/2p53,54. Such a comprehensive understanding of 3He53,54,72 was predicated on the fact that the effective mass enhancement in 3He is driven by hard-core repulsion at short distances, which is the hallmark of Mott physics. In the cuprates, a discussion of the Mott-enhancement ratio q = m/m* requires consideration of the fact that m refers to a band mass76–78 that is itself doping-dependent (see “Methods” section). This is accounted for by a tight-binding approximation38 to the central Mott band. The empirically determined Eq. (6) might therefore be taken as evidence of underlying Mott physics in the cuprates. The approximately twofold larger enhancement factor in LSCO, as expressed by Eq. (6), was in fact anticipated by Anderson, who extended his analysis of 3He to the cuprates using a modified argument55,79.

In a 3He-inspired Mott scenario, the relation q ≈ p follows from strong on-site repulsion U, which suppresses double occupancy and produces a narrow Mott band separated from upper and lower Hubbard bands by a Mott gap53,54. Because this form of the dispersion featuring a Mott gap is not continuously connected to the broad non-interacting band, the self-energy in the effective description of the central Mott band in terms of the Gutzwiller factor q does not connect continuously to the non-interacting band when U is decreased.

Phenomenological relations such as τ* = τ(m/m*) 7,62,63,80, in fact, depend intrinsically on the analytic continuity of the interacting system to the non-interacting Fermi gas3,67,68,81. Such continuity is broken by the Mott physics discussed here, and therefore such relations do not apply to the discussion here. On the other hand, interaction factors that do not require adiabatic continuity to the non-interacting (U = 0) limit, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa={(1+{F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}})}^{-1}\nu $$\end{document}κ=(1+F0s)−1ν and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau }_{{\rm{e}}}=\tau (1+{F}_{0}^{{\rm{s}}})$$\end{document}τe=τ(1+F0s), remain valid53,54,58. Because the Mott state is separated from the non-interacting (U = 0) gas by a singularity, relations based on electron-phonon self-energy analogies80, which themselves rely on analytic continuity of the phonon self-energies to the non-interacting system, do not apply to the effective Mott band at low energies.

Discussion

We have shown that the universality of the Planckian relaxation rate follows directly from—and indeed requires—linearity of the square optical plasma frequency with doping: if the Planckian relaxation rate is universal, then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)$$\end{document}ωopt2(p) must scale linearly with p; conversely, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{{\rm{opt}}}^{2}(p)\propto p$$\end{document}ωopt2(p)∝p, then the Planckian relaxation rate is necessarily universal; i.e. it is independent of doping. This universality follows from a direct comparison of the slope of the T-linear resistivity with the optical plasma frequency, and does not require a specific microscopic model. Optical-conductivity measurements have long reported such a p-linear doping dependence50–52,64, although little attention has been drawn to this aspect of the data. Taken together with electrical resistivity measurements, these optical conductivity measurements establish the universality of Planckian dissipation in the cuprates on purely experimental grounds.

The universality of Planckian dissipation in the cuprates raises new questions. One concerns the physical mechanism responsible for the unusually simple p-linear doping dependence of the square optical plasma frequency. Insight into this question can be obtained by comparison of entropy measurements34,35, which at high dopings exhibit 1/p scaling. The observed doping dependence of the square optical plasma frequency and the specific heat is naturally explained with Mott physics—first applied in a similar form for liquid 3He53,54 and later applied to the cuprates by Anderson55—in which the electronic dispersion is renormalized by a Gutzwiller factor q ≈ p. This Mott physics is distinct from, yet coexists with, the quantum-critical fluctuations believed to control Planckian dissipation. The observed electrical resistivity therefore reflects the combined effects of these two phenomena.

In contrast to Planckian dissipation, the superconducting dome is strongly doping dependent6. It is natural to associate the doping dependence of Tc with the strong doping dependence of Tco(p) (see Fig. 1), rather than with the strength of quantum-critical fluctuations behind the universal relaxation rate. The doping dependence of Tco(p) singles out p* ≈ 0.18 as a special doping in the phase diagram15. On the underdoped side, the physics of Tco is associated with the pseudogap44,83. It is noteworthy, however, that Tco(p) collapses at p* ≈ 0.18, whereas there is evidence that the pseudogap continues to influence the thermodynamics at least up to p ≈ 0.2334,35. It also remains unclear what drives the strong doping dependence of Tco(p) on the overdoped side, where no pseudogap is present.

Factoring out the square plasma frequency in the conductivity allows precision measurements of the Planckian α. An important implication of this observed doping independence of the Planckian relaxation rate is that the scale-invariant transport of the strange metal in the cuprates is not governed by proximity to a quantum critical point at a specific doping. Rather, the dissipation mechanism appears to be intrinsic to the strange metal state itself, and need not arise from critical fluctuations associated with a particular zero-temperature ordering instability.

Prior reports of universality

Prior reports of universal Planckian dissipation in the cuprates pertain to the regime below Tc after suppressing the superconducting state by a strong magnetic field25,31,84. Because low-temperature resistivity is reported to exhibit the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho (T)=1/\left({({\rho }_{0}+{A}_{1}T+{A}_{2}{T}^{2})}^{-1}+{\rho }_{\max }^{-1}\right)$$\end{document}ρ(T)=1/(ρ0+A1T+A2T2)−1+ρmax−1, as suggested in ref. 31, it is not scale-invariant and therefore does not display the pure T-linear behavior of the strange metal state. This contributed to the low value of α = 0.9 at p = 0.26 reported by ref. 25.

We note that, more recently, it has been argued that the reported low-temperature T-linear resistivity in overdoped cuprates may be extrinsic, arising from disorder and from the influence of high magnetic fields on superconductivity85.

Effect of interlayer hopping on the Van Hove singularity

At temperatures below ~ 2 K, γ in Fig. 5d becomes sensitive to the interlayer hopping. Whereas ref. 38 finds tz/t = 0.07, ref. 89 finds tz/t = 0.03. The measurements of γ close to p = 0.23 were performed under strong magnetic fields46. It has been argued that orbital-averaging leads to a suppression of the interlayer hopping90, which could cause the effective ratio tz/t to drop to a significantly smaller value. For tz/t = 0, the logarithmic divergence in Fig. 5d continues down to T = 0.

Impurity scattering introduces a residual scattering rate at T = 0, which can also cause γ to saturate46. Angle-dependent magnetoresistance measurements have found the residual scattering rate to be momentum-dependent40. However, since the Fermi velocity vanishes along all momentum directions at the point of the Fermi surface where the Van Hove singularity occurs, angle-dependent magnetoresistance measurements are unable to constrain a value for this scattering rate at this point on the Fermi surface40. In order for the mean free path to remain non-vanishing at this point, τ−1 must vanish at this point.
