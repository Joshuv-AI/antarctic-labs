Performance of AI chatbots in answering questions on tooth bleaching: a multilingual comparative study
Authors: Fehime Alkan Aygör, Hasibe Sevilay Bahadır, Ali Can Bulut, Caner Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) chatbots are increasingly used in healthcare to provide health-related information and answer patient questions. However, their reliability in specialized dental fields such as restorative dentistry remains insufficiently evaluated. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the accuracy and consistency of responses generated by five artificial intelligence chatbot systems—ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 (OpenAI), Bing Chat (Microsoft), Gemini (Google), and Claude-Instant (Anthropic)—regarding dental bleaching.MethodsFifteen frequently asked questions about dental bleaching, identified by restorative dentistry specialists, were categorized as undergraduate-or specialist-level questions. All questions were submitted to five artificial intelligence chatbots in both Turkish and English. Each question was asked three times per day over three consecutive days using standardized prompts. Responses were independently evaluated by two experts using a five-point Likert scale, and mean scores were calculated. A three-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of chatbot type, knowledge level, and question language on response accuracy. Inter-rater agreement between evaluators was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsChatbot type had a significant effect on response accuracy (p < 0.001, η² = 0.405). ChatGPT-4 showed the highest accuracy, followed by Claude-Instant and GPT-3.5, whereas Gemini and especially Bing Chat demonstrated significantly lower performance (p < 0.001). Question language and knowledge level showed no significant main effects (p > 0.05). Significant interactions were observed between chatbot type and knowledge level and between chatbot type and language (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed across days or time periods (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe accuracy of chatbot-generated information regarding dental bleaching depends strongly on the specific AI model used. Advanced large language models, particularly ChatGPT-4, generate more accurate and consistent responses than other evaluated systems. AI chatbots should therefore not be considered interchangeable sources of clinical information, and their outputs should be interpreted cautiously and verified with professional guidance.Clinical relevanceThese findings highlight the importance of critically evaluating AI-generated health information and emphasize that chatbot responses should not replace professional clinical consultation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08852-z.

Introduction

The advent of AI chatbots has brought about a profound transformation in digital communication. AI chatbots interact with users through prompt-based communication systems and generate responses based on patterns learned from large-scale training data [1, 2]. These systems, which utilize deep learning algorithms, undergo training on extensive datasets to enhance the relevance and consistency of their responses. In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the use of AI chatbots as public information sources in the medical field [3]. Such chatbots can answer questions on various topics, including medical care, and help patients better understand their treatments, potential side effects, prognoses, and expected outcomes [4, 5]. However, studies have highlighted the potential for misinformation in certain medical disciplines, and concerns have been raised regarding the reliability and accuracy of responses generated by chatbots, particularly in clinical contexts [1, 4–6].

Tooth discoloration is a common aesthetic concern, and bleaching procedures are widely used by both patients and dentists to improve dental appearance. Despite the widespread use of AI chatbots in other areas of healthcare, their effectiveness as public information sources in bleaching treatment has been evaluated in only one previous study, which assessed ChatGPT alone [7]. Although studies evaluating chatbot performance in healthcare have increased in recent years, focused investigations within specific dental procedures remain limited. In particular, multilingual evaluations and comparisons across different levels of professional knowledge are still scarce in the dental literature. Therefore, the present study was designed to address this gap by evaluating chatbot responses to bleaching-related questions across multiple models, languages, and knowledge levels.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the validity and reliability of responses generated by five AI chatbot systems—ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4, Bing Chat, Gemini, and Claude-Instant—to bleaching-related questions, and to compare their performance across question language (English vs. Turkish) and expected knowledge level (undergraduate vs. specialist). Methods

Study design and setting

The aim of this study was to evaluate the validity and reliability of responses provided by AI-powered chatbots to questions regarding bleaching. This study was designed as an observational study evaluating chatbot performance across different question languages and levels of professional knowledge. The study was conducted within the Department of Restorative Dentistry and Prosthetic Dentistry using standardized question sets developed from routine clinical practice and publicly available sources.

Data collection

Formal ethics committee approval was not required for this study because no intervention was performed and no identifiable patient data were collected. The dataset consisted of bleaching-related questions derived from publicly available online sources and anonymized questions obtained from routine clinical investigations.

To identify the most frequently searched topics related to dental bleaching, the Google Trends platform (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) was utilized. Based on this analysis, the search terms “tooth whitening”, “bleaching”, and “teeth bleaching treatment” were identified as the most relevant keywords. These terms were subsequently entered into a Google web search using a newly opened browser window to avoid personalized search bias, and the frequently asked questions listed under the “People also ask” section were systematically reviewed.

In parallel, a pool of 50 bleaching-related questions commonly encountered in routine clinical practice at the Restorative Dentistry Clinic was compiled. Two restorative dentistry specialists (FAA and HSB) independently reviewed these questions and selected the 15 most relevant and clinically representative items for inclusion in the study. Disagreements among the reviewers were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. The selection criteria included clinical relevance, frequency of occurrence in routine practice, and representation of fundamental concepts related to bleaching. These predefined criteria were established prior to the final selection of questions to minimize subjective bias and enhance methodological transparency.

The final question set covered a wide range of bleaching-related topics, including indications and contraindications, treatment techniques (home versus in-office), shade selection before treatment, postoperative care and sensitivity, bleaching during pregnancy, bleaching in teeth with restorations and factors affecting treatment outcomes, such as smoking, coffee consumption, and dietary habits. The questions were designed to reflect clinically relevant knowledge commonly encountered in dental practice rather than exclusively patient-directed inquiries. The distinction between undergraduate- and specialist-level questions was intended to evaluate chatbot performance across different levels of professional knowledge complexity and to determine whether response accuracy varied according to the level of clinical expertise required.

The classification of questions into undergraduate- and specialist-level categories was guided by established dental education frameworks and standard restorative dentistry references. Questions were considered undergraduate-level when they addressed fundamental patient-oriented knowledge, including basic definitions, risks, indications, contraindications, and post-treatment recommendations, consistent with competencies expected from undergraduate dental curricula [8, 9].

In contrast, questions were categorized as specialist-level when they required advanced clinical reasoning, including treatment sequencing, shade determination, management of bleaching-related sensitivity, evaluation of outcomes, and decision-making in cases involving existing restorations, which are typically addressed in more advanced clinical training [9, 10]. The classification was performed independently by two restorative dentistry specialists, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

The questions were originally prepared in Turkish and translated into English by a bilingual dentist. The translated versions were subsequently reviewed by a second restorative dentistry specialist to ensure semantic accuracy and consistency between languages. The translations were reviewed together to ensure consistency between the Turkish and English versions.

To ensure prompt standardization, all finalized questions were presented in identical wording across all chatbot sessions without paraphrasing or modification. The questions are listed in English (Table 1), while the Turkish versions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Each of the 15 questions was submitted to five AI models across four chatbot platforms: ChatGPT (GPT-3.5/GPT-4, OpenAI), Bing Chat (Microsoft), Gemini (Google), and Claude-Instant (Anthropic). To replicate real-life usage scenarios, queries were submitted via the publicly available web interfaces of each chatbot, using free accounts. Specifically:

These platforms were selected due to their accessibility and support for the Turkish language. All chatbot responses were obtained using the versions made available to the general public during the study period (January 5–7, 2025). Since internal model version numbers or build identifiers are not publicly available, model identification was based on the platform versions made available to users during that period. All responses were obtained through standard web-based user interfaces without developer-level access. Model-specific parameters, such as temperature settings, could not be controlled during the study because they are not accessible via public interfaces.

To assess response reliability, all 15 questions were asked in both Turkish and English, three times per day (morning, afternoon, and evening) over three consecutive days (5–7 January 2025). The three-day data collection period was selected to allow repeated measurements across consecutive days while maintaining feasibility and consistency in data recording. Collecting data on different days was intended to help observe any short-term variability in chatbot responses and reduce the impact of fluctuations specific to a single day.

Questions were posed at different times of the day to minimize potential time-related variability in chatbot responses. Given that chatbot responses may be influenced by factors such as system updates, background processes, or the dynamic use of online information sources, it was anticipated that collecting data at different time points would enhance the reliability and consistency of the evaluations.

To ensure standardization, new user accounts were created for each chatbot, and a separate session was initiated for each question. Recognizing that conversation history and user-specific interactions could influence chatbot responses, this approach was implemented to ensure consistency and comparability across all queries. All interactions were conducted via web-based interfaces accessible to the general public during the study period. All responses were systematically archived for subsequent analysis.

Scoring

The accuracy of the responses provided by each AI chatbot was evaluated independently by two restorative dentistry specialists with academic experience in bleaching procedures. The accuracy and appropriateness of the AI-generated responses were rated using a five-point Likert scale. According to the Likert scale, the following categories were assigned: (1) The chatbot provided a correct and sufficient answer; (2) The application provided a correct but incomplete answer; (3) The chatbot did not directly answer the question but suggested sources where the correct answer could be found; (4) The chatbot did not provide a sufficient answer to the question and did not suggest any sources; (5) The chatbot answered the question incorrectly. The Likert scale ratings were designed to reflect the usefulness and efficiency of the chatbot, with (5) being the worst rating. This scoring approach integrates multiple aspects of response quality, including correctness, completeness, and usefulness, to provide an overall evaluation of response performance. This approach was selected to provide a comprehensive assessment of response quality rather than a purely binary evaluation.

During the evaluation process, reviewers also considered the presence of factual inaccuracies when assigning scores. Responses containing incorrect or misleading clinical information, such as inappropriate treatment recommendations or inaccurate clinical details, were assigned lower ratings. However, references generated by the chatbots were not systematically verified, which represents a limitation of the study.

After independent scoring, the reviewers compared their evaluations and discussed any discrepancies. Divergences were resolved through evidence-based consensus to ensure consistency in the final ratings. Most disagreements did not arise from responses that were clearly correct or incorrect, but rather from those that were partially correct or required clinical interpretation. This was particularly observed in situations involving conditional clinical recommendations or patient-specific considerations.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the assumptions of parametric testing were satisfied, a three-way univariate analysis of variance (Three-Way UNIANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of the experimental factors on the dependent variable (response score). The independent variables were software model [ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 (OpenAI), Bing Chat (Microsoft), Gemini (Google), and Claude-Instant (Anthropic)], knowledge level (2 levels: undergraduate and specialist ), and question language (2 levels: Turkish, English). All statistical analyses were performed using software (IBM SPSS Statistics, v29.0; IBM Corp). The Type III sum of squares approach was used to evaluate the model. Main effects, two-way interactions, and three-way interactions were tested in the model. Cases with missing data were excluded from the analysis using the listwise method. The significance level was set at α = 0.05; multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. Partial Eta Squared (η²) values were reported as effect sizes. For repeated measurements across days and time periods, the Friedman test was used to assess temporal stability. This non-parametric analysis was applied to evaluate short-term response consistency and to partially address potential dependency among repeated observations. Inter-rater reliability between the evaluators was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient based on the initial independent scoring performed prior to consensus discussions.

Internal Consistency

Cohen’s kappa was calculated to assess the inter-rater reliability between the two researchers, yielding a value of κ = 0.92 (p < 0.001), which indicates a strong level of agreement. In the scoring system used in this study, lower mean scores indicated higher response accuracy, whereas higher scores reflected poorer performance. The kappa coefficient was calculated based on the initial independent ratings prior to consensus discussions.

Discussion

AI chatbots are increasingly recognized as accessible and interactive tools that can transform how individuals seek health information [11]. In recent years, their role has expanded considerably in medicine and dentistry. Within dentistry, they hold promise as easily available resources for addressing common patient inquiries about oral conditions, treatment procedures, and expected outcomes. Among esthetic procedures, bleaching is one of the most frequently requested, and the accuracy and safety of the information shared on this subject is extremely important. Although chatbots provide quick and convenient access to information, it remains essential for researchers and professional organizations to critically evaluate the validity of their outputs before clinical or educational use.

In the present study, the validity and reliability of responses from five widely used chatbot systems—ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4 (OpenAI), Bing Chat (Microsoft), Gemini (Google), and Claude-Instant (Anthropic PBC)—were evaluated for bleaching-related questions. The first null hypothesis, which predicted no significant differences between the validity and reliability of AI chatbots in providing information about bleaching was rejected. The findings revealed significant performance differences among artificial intelligence systems. In comparison, Bing Chat demonstrated comparatively higher mean scores, whereas Gemini, ChatGPT-3.5, Claude-Instant, and ChatGPT-4 showed comparatively lower mean scores. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies reporting that more advanced large language models tend to perform better in healthcare-related tasks, including recent evaluations in dental and maxillofacial contexts [12–18]. Daraqel et al. [12] reported that ChatGPT provided more accurate responses to orthodontic questions than Google Bard, while Rossettini et al. [13] found that GPT-4 performed better than Copilot and Gemini on medical entrance exams. Similarly, Wei et al. [14] emphasized that model architecture and training dataset significantly affect response accuracy in the healthcare field. The relatively low performance observed in Bing Chat and Gemini may be related to their reliance on web-based search systems. While access to online information can expand the scope of responses, the quality and reliability of publicly available dental health information can vary significantly. Inconsistent or unreviewed online content can negatively impact the accuracy of the generated responses. Additionally, differences in model architecture, response synthesis mechanisms and search system integration may also contribute to variability in the performance of search-based chatbot platforms.

Another factor evaluated in this study is the language of the questions. While no significant difference was generally observed between English and Turkish responses, some models demonstrated comparatively lower mean scores in English. This may be attributed to the predominance of English content in the training data sets of artificial intelligence models. The literature also emphasizes that language-based data imbalance in AI applications in the health sector can create inequality in access to information [19, 20]. Therefore, improving performance in different languages should be seen as an important goal in the development of AI systems. Therefore, the second null hypothesis, which proposed no difference between English and Turkish responses, was not rejected.

It is noteworthy that the level of questions (undergraduate and specialist) did not show a significant main effect on chatbot performance in the study. This is consistent with the working principles of large language models. These models generate their responses based on language patterns and probabilistic relationships learned from large text datasets. Therefore, classifying questions as “specialization level” does not necessarily correspond to increased cognitive complexity for the model. Indeed, Kung et al. [21] demonstrated that ChatGPT can perform consistently even on advanced medical exams such as the USMLE. Similarly, Gilson et al. [22] reported that ChatGPT can generate responses with acceptable quality in both basic and advanced clinical scenarios. However, the software × knowledge level interaction identified in this study shows that some models may show variation in their responses when addressing more advanced clinical questions. This finding indicates that differences in response patterns may be related to model characteristics rather than solely to question level. Accordingly, the third null hypothesis was not rejected.

The lack of significant variation in chatbot responses across different days and time periods suggests a degree of short-term response consistency. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the subjective nature of the Likert-based rating system and the potential interdependence between repeated measurements, this observed stability should not be interpreted as definitive proof of reproducibility. Rather, the results reflect relative consistency within specific study conditions and time frames. Given the continuously evolving nature of AI-based chatbot systems, longitudinal studies are needed to better assess the temporal stability of chatbot performance.

Bonferroni comparisons revealed significant performance differences in mean scores among the software models. ChatGPT-4, Claude, and ChatGPT-3.5 generally demonstrated comparatively lower mean scores, whereas Gemini and Bing showed comparatively higher mean scores. These results indicate that AI chatbots should not be considered interchangeable tools for clinical knowledge generation [2, 23]. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies reporting that more advanced large language models tend to perform better in healthcare-related tasks. However, some studies have reported different results across dental fields. For example, Makrygiannakis et al. [24] found that Bing Chat achieved comparatively higher accuracy in orthodontic-related queries. Such discrepancies may be related to differences in model architecture, training datasets, and topic-specific optimization. In addition, no significant three-way interaction was observed among chatbot system, language, and question level. Therefore, the fourth null hypothesis was not rejected.

The demand for bleaching procedures has increased in recent years, and aesthetic expectations have risen, particularly due to the influence of social media. However, the uncontrolled use of over-the-counter whitening products can lead to side effects such as tooth sensitivity and gum irritation [25, 26]. Therefore, it is crucial for patients to have access to accurate and reliable information. While artificial intelligence chatbots are tools that can provide quick access to information, the risk of generating false or fabricated information, known as the “hallucination effect,” is considered a significant problem [26–29]. Specifically, some studies have shown that a significant portion of the references generated by ChatGPT do not actually exist [7, 30]. This issue may partly be related to the limited integration of some chatbot systems with verifiable scientific databases [31].

Previous studies in dentistry have also explored the performance of AI-based chatbots in providing clinical information. For example, recent studies have evaluated the accuracy and reliability of chatbot-generated responses in oral and maxillofacial surgery and general dental contexts, highlighting both the potential and limitations of these systems in clinical communication [16–18]. These findings emphasize that while AI chatbots may provide accessible information, their outputs should be interpreted with caution and ideally verified by dental professionals.

In the present study, factual inaccuracies were indirectly reflected in the Likert-based scores assigned by expert reviewers. However, hallucinations were not analyzed as a separate outcome, and fabricated references were not systematically verified, which represents a limitation of this study. Future studies designed to distinguish factual inaccuracies from incomplete responses and to verify cited references may provide more detailed insights into the reliability of chatbot-generated information in dental practice. In addition, the use of newly created accounts and isolated sessions may limit ecological validity, as real-world users typically interact with chatbots through continuous conversations. However, this approach was adopted to minimize personalization effects and ensure standardized conditions across all chatbot interactions.

This study has certain limitations. First, the analysis was limited to only five chatbots, and the results may not be generalizable to other artificial intelligence systems. Second, the study focuses solely on tooth bleaching, and results obtained in different areas of dentistry may vary. Furthermore, because AI chatbot systems evolve rapidly through continuous model updates, the findings of this study reflect the performance characteristics of the publicly available versions during the defined study period.

The use of the Likert scale in assessing response accuracy constitutes a significant methodological limitation. Since this rating system evaluates multiple dimensions of response quality—such as accuracy, completeness, and usability—simultaneously, it cannot be considered a fully objective measure of accuracy. Additionally, the ordinal nature of the data must be taken into account when interpreting statistical analyses.

Furthermore, the relatively limited number of questions included in the assessment (n = 15) may restrict the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Although the selected questions were designed to reflect fundamental concepts related to teeth whitening at both the undergraduate and specialist levels, a broader set of questions could provide greater statistical power and allow for the examination of a wider variety of clinical scenarios. In addition, similar studies evaluating AI-generated responses in dentistry have also used relatively limited question sets [32, 33].

Finally, the study’s repeated-measure design-administering the same questions on different days and at different times of the day-may introduce inter-observation dependency. Although additional analyses were conducted to assess consistency over time, this potential dependency may influence the interpretation of statistical results and should be considered when evaluating the findings.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies incorporating larger datasets, alternative scoring approaches, and longitudinal designs are needed to further validate the findings.

Nevertheless, the study also has several strengths. Thanks to the multifactorial experimental design, the effects of the software model, language, and knowledge level could be evaluated simultaneously. In addition, collecting responses across different days and time periods enabled the assessment of short-term response consistency. The fact that the questions are based on patient questions frequently encountered in clinical practice may also enhance the transferability of the findings to real-life contexts.

Furthermore, all questions were submitted using standardized prompts and independent sessions, which helped reduce potential prompt-related variability and improve comparability across AI systems. Although the number of questions was limited, they were selected to reflect frequently encountered clinical scenarios, which may support the ecological validity of the study. Overall, these design characteristics provide a focused evaluation within a specific dental domain and incorporate multilingual and knowledge-level comparisons, which remain relatively underexplored in previous studies evaluating chatbot performance in dentistry [7, 24, 31].

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the quality of responses to questions related to tooth bleaching is significantly dependent on the artificialintelligence model used. More advanced large language models were associated with comparatively more consistent and reliable responses. However, it is understood that factors such as language differences and model architecture affect performance, and that using AI-supported health information without subjecting it to critical evaluation carries risks. Future studies involving real patient-chatbot interactions and covering different clinical areas will more clearly reveal the role of these technologies in patient education and health communication.
