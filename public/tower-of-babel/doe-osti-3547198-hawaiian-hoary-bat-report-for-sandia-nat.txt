Abstract
The Hawaiian Hoary Bat Report for Sandia National Laboratories, Kauai Test Facility (SNL/KTF) provides
data and analysis of Hawaiian hoary bat occupancy of the SNL/KTF site. The monitoring period
was from 2023-2025 and used passive acoustic detection at three locations to determine whether the
Hawaiian hoary bat occurs at the site and if they roost and pup in the vegetation of the site. Bats
were detected at all three locations, however, given the vegetation type at SNL/KTF, it is very
unlikely the Hawaiian hoary bat is roosting or pupping onsite. This project should be revisited in the
future to ensure this is still the case and that no mitigation measures are needed.

1.0 Background
The Hawaiian hoary bat (Lasiurus cinereus semotus) is the only extant non-marine mammal native to
the Hawaiian Islands [1] and genetic studies have shown that this species is distinct from its North
American mainland counterparts [2]. In recognition of its vulnerability, the Hawaiian hoary bat was
designated as federally endangered in 1970 [3] and was officially declared the state mammal of
Hawaii in 2015.

The Hawaiian hoary bat faces several significant threats, including habitat loss [3], destruction of
roosting sites [6], exposure to pesticides [5], predation by introduced species [7], and collisions with
wind turbines [8].

This study aims to assess the presence of the Hawaiian hoary bat at the Kauai Test Facility (KTF). It
will investigate whether these bats are roosting and pupping in the area, as well as evaluate the
necessity for mitigation efforts to ensure compliance with relevant state and federal laws and
regulations. The monitoring results support the conclusion that Hawaiian hoary bat is unlikely to be
roosting or pupping within the KTF operational footprint based on acoustic activity patterns,
vegetation characteristics, and available survey data. By understanding the habitat use and
reproductive behaviors of the Hawaiian hoary bat, this research will contribute to informed
conservation strategies and regulatory compliance.

This report was prepared to satisfy Sandia/NNSA’s regulatory obligation under the Endangered
Species Act (ESA) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s concurrence letter to “perform
monitoring of Hawaiian hoary bat within KTF boundaries, using passive acoustic detectors and
other research methods and communicate any changes in Hawaiian hoary bat population status
within the KTF action area to the Service” [9]. It presents the results of a roughly three-year passive
acoustic survey on Sandia property to determine whether the ESA-listed Hawaiian hoary bat is
present during its critical pupping season and, consequently, whether mitigation measures are
required. The findings support Sandia/NNSA’s determination that KTF operations may affect but
are not likely to adversely affect this species by confirming its likely absence during pupping, while
also establishing the basis for any future mitigation should bats be detected roosting or pupping
onsite.

2.0 Materials and Methods

2.1. Study Area
Kauai Test Facility (KTF) is situated at approximately sea level, where the average temperature
remains relatively stable, ranging from 22.3°C in January to 26.6°C in August. Precipitation levels
vary throughout the year, with rainfall measuring 69.9 mm in November and dropping to 10.1 mm
in July [10]. The vegetation at KTF is predominantly non-native, primarily comprised of kiawe
(Prosopis pallida) and koa haole (Leucaena leucocephala), along with an understory of introduced forbs
and grasses [11].

2.2. Data Collection
Passive detection of the Hawaiian hoary bat commenced on July 24, 2023, originally utilizing Anabat
Swift bat detectors. Initially, one detector was deployed in the “Barnyard” and another in the
“Boneyard” (Figure 1), with both locations chosen for their vegetation and topography, which create
a natural funnel that is expected to concentrate bat activity.

From the outset of the study, both Anabat Swift units encountered functionality issues.
Consequently, the Anabat units were removed in April 2024 and replaced with Wildlife Acoustics
SM4Bat units in May 2024. However, the Boneyard unit continued to experience problems, and a
malfunction in the microphone was identified by the manufacturer. The microphone was sent for
repair in July 2024, replaced, and redeployed in August 2024. This unit ceased recording data in late
December 2024 and was confirmed to be non-functional in January and February of 2025. As a
result, it was removed from the field, and a new unit was deployed at the Launch Field location in
March 2025 (Figure 1).

2.3. Data Analysis
The data were processed using the bio-acoustic analysis software Kaleidoscope, which analyzes call
amplitude, shape, length, and frequency to suggest the most likely species. Given that the Hawaiian
hoary bat is the only bat species expected to inhabit the area, any call identified as a bat was
attributed to this species. Subsequently, bat calls and noise files were tallied for each site. Noise files
are any files that are not bat calls. Please note that “LASCIN” is the species code for Lasiurus cinereus
semotus.

3.0 Results
At the three sites, bats were recorded on a total of 568 nights (Table 1). Most bat calls were recorded
between August and October each year, which aligns with the post-lactation period (Figure 2) [12,
13]. This increase in calls is likely to be attributed to young bats becoming volant, thereby
augmenting the foraging population size [3, 14]. Additionally, a slight spike in bat calls was observed
in March, coinciding with the pre-pregnancy period [12, 13].

In contrast, the Boneyard unit consistently recorded data only in 2024 and primarily captured noise
files (Figure 6). The Launch Field unit was operational solely in 2025, where it recorded a
comparable number of noise and bat calls (Figure 7).

A mechanical or electrical source of noise was detected near the Boneyard detector, producing
sounds within the expected frequency range of the Hawaiian hoary bat, approximately 20 kHz.
While all units recorded an unusual amount of noise compared to recordings from SNL/New
Mexico, the Boneyard unit captured significantly more noise than bat calls.

Among the monitoring sites, the Barnyard unit recorded the highest number of bat calls and, despite
experiencing some functionality issues, proved to be the most reliable overall (Figure 3, Figure 4,
Figure 5).

4.0 Discussion
Hawaiian hoary bats are a tree-roosting species, primarily documented roosting in paperbark
(Melaleuca quinquenervia), lychee (Litchi chinensis), mango (Mangifera indica), and ʻōhiʻa (Metrosideros
polymorpha), along with various other native and non-native tree species [15]. No reports of them
roosting in either of the tree species present at KTF, kiawe (Prosopis pallida) and koa haole (Leucaena
leucocephala) were found. However, foraging behavior has been reported across multiple habitat types.
Jacobs identified bats foraging in four distinct habitat categories: native vegetation (44% of bats),
invasive vegetation (14% of bats), mixed native and invasive vegetation (9% of bats), and open
ocean (25% of bats) [3].

Interestingly, an acoustic survey conducted across the island of Kauai in 1987 and 1988 found no
evidence of bat activity at the site nearest to KTF. This led researchers to conclude that the activity
of Hawaiian hoary bats is likely confined to the northern forested regions of the island 6. Various
factors may explain the historical absence of detections in this area, from differences in composition
of habitat to the use of less sophisticated detection equipment.

U.S. Geological Survey conducted acoustic surveys at Pacific Missile Range Facility (PMRF) in
2010-2011 and determined presence and year-round use of the area by hoary bats [14]. They found
highest detections of bats in the fall and lowest detections in spring, both of which are consistent
with our results [14].

Determining definitively whether Hawaiian hoary bats are roosting and pupping at KTF would
necessitate the use of thermal cameras, which is beyond the scope of this study. However, based on
the types of vegetation present and the regularity of “feeding buzz” calls (calls that indicate a bat is
zeroing in on a prey item, Figure 8), it is most likely that the bats are foraging and navigating in the
area rather than roosting or pupping.

To ensure the ongoing conservation and management of the Hawaiian hoary bat, it is essential to
revisit acoustic detection studies approximately every three to five years. This regular reassessment
will help to monitor changes in bat presence and site use over time, providing valuable insights into
their habitat preferences and abundance. High-confidence, multi-season data enables negotiation for
fewer restrictions and potentially shorter monitoring windows, thereby reducing uncertainty and

contingency costs. In contrast, limited or single-season data may prompt regulators to impose
precautionary restrictions and allocate larger contingency budgets. Given the potential impacts of
environmental changes, such as climate fluctuations and habitat alterations, periodic evaluations will
enable Sandia to adapt our strategies and ensure compliance with regulatory requirements, ultimately
supporting the long-term survival of this species.

5.0 Management Implications
The results of this monitoring effort support the conclusion that the Hawaiian hoary bat is primarily
using KTF for transient foraging activity. As such, it is unlikely they are regularly roosting or
pupping within the KTF operational footprint. Based on these findings, no additional mitigation
measures are recommended at this time beyond continued environmental stewardship and periodic
monitoring. Monitoring should be revisited every three to five years, or sooner if substantial changes
occur to operational activities, vegetation composition, or observed bat activity patterns. Future
confirmation of roosting or pupping behavior within KTF bounds may require reevaluation of ESA
compliance determinations and consultation requirements.
