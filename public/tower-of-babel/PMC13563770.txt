Painful acute esotropia as a presenting sign of presumed post-infectious orbital myositis in a child: a case report
Authors: Fawzia Alhaimi, Rahaf Alluhaidan, Nawaf Alsanea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe a self-limited presentation of pediatric orbital myositis and discuss its implications for management.ObservationsA previously healthy 9-year-old boy presented with acute painful diplopia and esotropia following a recent febrile upper respiratory tract infection and presumed viral conjunctivitis. Introduction

Orbital myositis (OM) is a recognized subtype of idiopathic orbital inflammation (IOI), characterized by noninfectious inflammation of one or more extraocular muscles. It typically presents with acute onset of orbital pain, diplopia, and varying degrees of extraocular motility restriction, often accompanied by conjunctival injection and periorbital swelling [1–4]. Although OM most commonly affects young adult females, pediatric cases are well documented and may demonstrate distinct clinical features, including a higher likelihood of bilateral involvement [1, 5].

The etiology of OM remains incompletely understood. While most cases are idiopathic, proposed mechanisms include immune-mediated inflammation potentially triggered by infectious or other systemic processes, as well as associations with autoimmune and IgG4-related disease [2, 6, 7]. Radiologically, OM is characterized by enlargement and contrast enhancement of the affected extraocular muscle, often extending to involve the myotendinous junction—a feature that helps differentiate IOI from thyroid-associated orbitopathy, in which tendons are typically spared [2, 3].

Systemic corticosteroids are considered the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic IOI, with most patients demonstrating rapid clinical improvement following therapy [1, 8]. The diagnosis of OM is essentially one of exclusion, supported by clinical features, imaging findings, and, where feasible, treatment response.

Despite growing awareness of orbital myositis, its presentation as painful acute esotropia in a child—raising an important differential with acute acquired comitant esotropia (AACE), bilateral sixth nerve palsy, and accommodative esotropia—has been infrequently described. A knowledge gap exists regarding the full clinical spectrum of pediatric OM, including its self-limited forms and the distinguishing clinical and imaging features that allow it to be recognized early. We report a case of presumed post-infectious bilateral orbital myositis presenting as painful acute esotropia in a child, which demonstrated spontaneous resolution without corticosteroid therapy, and discuss the diagnostic challenges and management implications.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 9-year-old boy presented with acute diplopia and inward deviation of the eyes. One week prior to presentation, he developed bilateral eye redness and swelling and was evaluated in the emergency department, where he received a diagnosis of presumed viral conjunctivitis. During the same period, he experienced symptoms of an upper respiratory tract infection associated with fever.

According to his mother, inward deviation of the eyes had been noted for approximately one week prior to presentation, followed by onset of diplopia three days before the visit. The diplopia was associated with pain on ocular movements and was present at both distance and near.

Examination findings

On examination, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/40 in both eyes. Cycloplegic refraction revealed − 2.00/−4.25 × 167 in the right eye and − 2.00/−4.25 × 22 in the left eye, consistent with high bilateral astigmatism. The reduced BCVA may therefore be attributable to underlying refractive amblyopia and/or significant astigmatism. Intraocular pressure measured 20 mmHg in the right eye and 21 mmHg in the left eye. Color vision was normal in both eyes (14/14 Ishihara plates). There was no proptosis. Pupils were round, regular, and reactive to light bilaterally, with no relative afferent pupillary defect.

Slit-lamp examination revealed localized temporal conjunctival injection over the lateral rectus muscle insertions bilaterally, with associated superotemporal fullness and tenderness in the lacrimal gland regions, suggestive of dacryoadenitis (Fig. 1). The cornea was clear, the anterior chamber was deep and quiet, and the iris and lenses were unremarkable.

Clinical photographs demonstrating localized temporal conjunctival injection over the lateral rectus muscle insertions bilaterally (yellow arrows), with associated superotemporal fullness suggestive of lacrimal gland swelling (dacryoadenitis)

Ocular alignment was assessed using prism and alternate cover testing at distance and near fixation. Cycloplegic refraction and cover testing revealed a corrected distance esotropia of 30 prism diopters (PD) and a corrected near esotropia of 35 PD. Diplopia was predominantly present at distance fixation.

Contrast sensitivity and formal visual field testing were not performed because the clinical presentation and imaging findings were considered sufficient for diagnostic evaluation at presentation. A clinical photograph obtained at presentation demonstrated esotropia in primary gaze (Fig. 2).

Clinical photograph in primary gaze demonstrating esotropia, consistent with the patient’s presenting ocular misalignment. Cycloplegic refraction demonstrated corrected distance esotropia of 30 PD and corrected near esotropia of 35 PD

Anterior segment and fundus examination were within normal limits. Optic discs were healthy with clear margins and a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.3, maculae were normal with a good foveal reflex, and the retinae were flat bilaterally.

Laboratory evaluation

Laboratory evaluation included a complete blood count, which was within normal limits. Extended serological and inflammatory workup—including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, antinuclear antibody, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, angiotensin-converting enzyme, thyroid function tests, and IgG4 levels—was not performed during this acute admission. This represents a significant limitation of the diagnostic evaluation, as discussed below.

Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the orbits (Fig. 3) demonstrated bilateral enlargement and post-contrast enhancement of the lateral rectus muscles with increased FLAIR signal and mild tendon involvement, along with surrounding intra- and extraconal inflammatory changes. Imaging features were also consistent with bilateral dacryoadenitis. The inferior, medial, and superior rectus muscles appeared within normal limits. There was no evidence of subperiosteal abscess, intraorbital mass, or significant proptosis, and the optic nerves were normal in caliber and signal without abnormal enhancement. MRI of the brain was unremarkable.

Axial orbital MRI demonstrating bilateral enlargement of the lateral rectus muscles with increased signal intensity and post-contrast enhancement, with mild tendon involvement and surrounding inflammatory changes. Bilateral lacrimal gland changes consistent with dacryoadenitis are also noted. These findings are consistent with orbital myositis within the spectrum of idiopathic orbital inflammation

The MRI field strength and detailed acquisition parameters were not available for retrospective review from the archived imaging records and therefore could not be reported. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was based on characteristic radiologic findings documented in the official neuroradiology report and confirmed by review of the available imaging studies.

Clinical course and follow-up

A working diagnosis of presumed post-infectious orbital inflammatory disease within the spectrum of idiopathic orbital inflammation—involving the extraocular muscles (orbital myositis) and lacrimal glands (dacryoadenitis)—was established.

Examination suggested a reduction in the degree of esotropia compared with presentation, as noted clinically by both the treating team and the patient’s mother; however, the exact residual deviation was not formally measured at that visit. Diplopia had improved substantially and was limited to distance fixation.

Oral amoxicillin–clavulanate and systemic corticosteroids were subsequently prescribed. Corticosteroid therapy, however, was not initiated due to gastrointestinal intolerance (nausea and vomiting). Given the early and ongoing spontaneous improvement, the decision was made to continue clinical observation.

At follow-up visits at one and two weeks, there was marked and progressive resolution of conjunctival swelling, complete resolution of diplopia and esotropia, and full restoration of extraocular motility. At three-month follow-up, the patient was orthophoric with full extraocular motility and no recurrence of symptoms. Best-corrected visual acuity remained stable at 20/40 in both eyes.

Discussion

Acute painful esotropia in a child presents a broad and clinically important differential diagnosis. In the present case, the combination of pain on ocular movements, localized conjunctival injection over the lateral rectus insertions, lacrimal gland involvement, and characteristic MRI findings led to a working diagnosis of presumed post-infectious orbital myositis within the spectrum of idiopathic orbital inflammation (IOI). Pediatric orbital myositis has been previously reported in literature, including cases presenting with acute ocular motility disturbance and strabismus [9].

Differential diagnosis: key considerations

Bilateral sixth nerve palsy must be considered as an alternative diagnosis in any child presenting with bilateral abduction limitation and esotropia. Sixth nerve palsy may result from raised intracranial pressure, posterior fossa pathology, or post-infectious inflammation of the abducens nerves. In the present case, MRI of the brain was unremarkable, and there was no evidence of intracranial hypertension or posterior fossa lesion. However, it is acknowledged that MRI characterization of the abducens nerve itself may be technically limited, and isolated bilateral abducens involvement in the context of post-infectious orbital inflammation cannot be entirely excluded. The absence of typical features of sixth nerve palsy—such as isolated abduction deficit without orbital signs—and the presence of bilateral lateral rectus muscle enlargement with tendon involvement on MRI favor an orbital rather than a neurogenic etiology. Nevertheless, this remains an important diagnostic uncertainty and should be explicitly acknowledged.

Acute acquired comitant esotropia (AACE) is another important diagnostic consideration. AACE is characterized by sudden-onset diplopia and esotropia, typically comitant and without significant motility limitation or pain [10–12]. In the present case, however, several features argue against a primary diagnosis of AACE: the presence of pain on ocular movements, bilateral abduction limitation (− 1), localized conjunctival injection, lacrimal gland swelling, and MRI evidence of bilateral lateral rectus muscle enlargement with tendon involvement. Furthermore, cycloplegic refraction demonstrated a corrected near esotropia of 35 PD and a corrected distance esotropia of 30 PD, indicating a slight convergence excess pattern—a feature not typical of AACE. AACE cannot be entirely excluded as a contributing or overlapping phenomenon, but the orbital inflammatory findings make it an insufficient explanation for this presentation.

Accommodative esotropia is an important cause of acute esotropia in school-age children. In the present case, the presence of astigmatism and reduced BCVA raises the possibility of a refractive or accommodative component. However, accommodative esotropia is not typically associated with painful eye movements, bilateral extraocular muscle enlargement on MRI, conjunctival injection, or lacrimal gland swelling. The findings in this case are inconsistent with a purely accommodative mechanism, though a refractive contribution to the angle of deviation cannot be entirely discounted and should be reassessed as the patient matures.

Intracranial pathology must always be excluded in children presenting with acute esotropia [13]. Previous reports have documented AACE as a presenting manifestation of posterior fossa tumors and brainstem gliomas, even in the absence of overt neurological signs [14–16]. In the present case, MRI of the brain was unremarkable, providing reassurance against a structural intracranial etiology.

Thyroid-associated orbitopathy may also cause extraocular muscle enlargement; however, it is rare in young children, typically does not present acutely, and is generally associated with tendon sparing on imaging. The mild tendon involvement observed in this case supports IOI over a thyroid etiology [2].

IgG4-related orbital disease is an increasingly recognized cause of chronic or recurrent orbital inflammation that may affect extraocular muscles and lacrimal glands. In the absence of IgG4 serology and orbital biopsy, this diagnosis could not be formally excluded and represents a limitation of the present case.

Presumed post-infectious etiology

An immune-mediated, post-infectious mechanism has been proposed for a subset of OM cases. Orbital myositis has been reported following upper respiratory tract infections, streptococcal pharyngitis, and viral illnesses including Coxsackie virus infection, suggesting an inflammatory immune response rather than direct microbial invasion [17–20]. In the present patient, there was a close temporal relationship between a febrile upper respiratory tract infection, presumed viral conjunctivitis, and the subsequent onset of orbital symptoms. While this temporal association is consistent with a post-infectious mechanism, a causal relationship cannot be established in the absence of confirmed microbiological or serological evidence of a specific infectious trigger. A presumed post-infectious etiology is therefore proposed, while acknowledging that this remains unconfirmed.

Clinical course and role of treatment

A particularly notable aspect of this case is the early and spontaneous clinical improvement observed within 24 h of presentation, prior to initiation of any medical therapy. This sequence strongly suggests that clinical recovery was not attributable to treatment. The role of the subsequently prescribed amoxicillin–clavulanate is uncertain; the patient was reassessed only one week later, by which time significant resolution had already occurred. Idiopathic orbital inflammation is generally a noninfectious condition, and antibiotic therapy is not standard treatment [2]. While rare cases of antibiotic-responsive orbital myositis have been reported in the context of concurrent sinus disease, this likely represents a distinct clinical entity [21]. Whether the antibiotic contributed in any way to recovery in the present case cannot be determined.

The inability to initiate corticosteroid therapy—the standard treatment for symptomatic IOI—meant that a corticosteroid response could not be used to support the diagnosis or assess treatment effect, which is an acknowledged limitation.

Recurrence and long-term follow-up

Recurrence is a recognized feature of IOI, reported in approximately 30–50% of cases in some series [1, 8]. Although the patient was orthophoric at three-month follow-up, this interval is relatively brief, and longer-term surveillance is warranted to identify potential recurrence. Importantly, the possibility of IgG4-related disease—which may present with a chronic or recurrent course—cannot be excluded without more extensive evaluation.

Limitations

Several important limitations of this case must be acknowledged. First, extended inflammatory, serological, and infectious laboratory workup was not performed; this limits the ability to exclude secondary causes of orbital inflammation, including IgG4-related disease, systemic vasculitis, sarcoidosis, and specific infectious etiologies. Second, the post-infectious etiology remains presumed rather than confirmed. Third, the inability to initiate corticosteroid therapy precluded assessment of a diagnostic treatment response. Fourth, tissue biopsy was not performed, which would be required for definitive histopathological diagnosis. Fifth, the follow-up interval of three months, while encouraging, is insufficient to exclude recurrence, and longer-term monitoring is planned. Finally, the amblyopia management and formal orthoptic follow-up of the reduced BCVA were not yet completed at the time of this report.

Summary

This case illustrates a self-limited presentation of presumed post-infectious pediatric orbital myositis manifesting as painful acute esotropia with bilateral lateral rectus muscle and lacrimal gland involvement. The clinical and imaging features allowed meaningful differentiation from purely neurogenic, accommodative, and thyroid-related etiologies, while acknowledging residual diagnostic uncertainty—particularly regarding bilateral sixth nerve palsy as an alternative explanation for the abduction deficits. Awareness of this presentation may prompt timely orbital and neuroimaging, facilitate appropriate clinical workup, and facilitate individualized management and careful observation in selected patients who demonstrate early spontaneous clinical improvement.

Conclusion

We report a case of presumed post-infectious bilateral orbital myositis presenting as painful acute esotropia in a 9-year-old boy, with bilateral lateral rectus enlargement with tendon involvement and associated dacryoadenitis on MRI, and spontaneous resolution without corticosteroid therapy. The presence of pain on ocular movements, bilateral abduction limitation, localized conjunctival injection, and characteristic MRI findings were key features guiding the diagnosis. The differential diagnosis—including bilateral sixth nerve palsy, acute acquired comitant esotropia, and accommodative esotropia—was carefully considered but remained less consistent with the overall clinical and imaging picture. At three-month follow-up, the patient was orthophoric with full extraocular motility. This case expands the recognized spectrum of pediatric idiopathic orbital inflammation and emphasizes the importance of thorough clinical evaluation, appropriate orbital and neuroimaging, and extended follow-up in children presenting with painful acute esotropia. Future cases should include comprehensive inflammatory and serological workup, long-term follow-up for recurrence, and—where clinically appropriate—tissue biopsy to allow definitive diagnosis.
