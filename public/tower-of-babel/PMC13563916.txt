Overlapping bone transport as a bone-preserving docking strategy for a massive wedge-shaped infected tibial defect: a case report highlighting a selected reconstructive strategy
Authors: Koji Nozaka, Shuichi Chida, Naohisa Miyakoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge infected tibial defects remain amongst the most challenging problems in limb reconstruction, particularly when the defect is wedge-shaped rather than purely segmental. In such cases, conventional docking may require resection of irregular but viable cortex to create a flat surface, resulting in additional iatrogenic bone loss. We report a selected bone-preserving reconstruction in which the native geometry of a wedge-shaped defect was intentionally utilised during bone transport.Case presentationA 20-year-old man was referred after polytrauma and multiple failed surgeries. He presented with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection, a 90-mm wedge-shaped tibial defect, severe equinus deformity, and a draining soft-tissue defect. After irrigation and debridement, vancomycin-loaded cement beads were placed and intravenous daptomycin was administered. Bone transport was initiated after cultures became negative and inflammatory markers normalised. Pre-transport radiographs and computed tomography, including three-dimensional reconstructions, demonstrated a preserved posterior cortical remnant and a defect morphology compatible with overlap engagement. During debridement, the retained posterior cortex showed punctate cortical bleeding (paprika sign) and no gross sequestration. During the late transport phase, the distal posterior cortex overlapped with the proximal segment, creating a partial bone-in-bone engagement. The overlap length increased from approximately 8 mm before transport to 63 mm after docking, corresponding to 55 mm of additional cortical engagement; the estimated cortical overlap contact area was approximately 2,079 mm2. At 14-year follow-up, union was maintained without recurrence of infection. The patient had mild pain (VAS 10/100), independent ambulation without orthosis, excellent ASAMI bone results, and good functional results.ConclusionsThis case illustrates a bone-preserving docking strategy within an established bone transport framework. In selected wedge-shaped defects, viable residual cortex should not be routinely resected solely for geometric simplification if it can be safely incorporated into docking after infection control and careful assessment of biological viability.

Background

Large tibial bone defects associated with fracture-related infection remain a major challenge in reconstructive trauma surgery. Bone transport using circular external fixation is a well-established limb-salvage method because it can address infection, shortening, deformity, and osseous continuity within one reconstructive framework [1–5].

The induced membrane technique and vascularised bone grafting are also valuable reconstructive options [6–9]. However, Masquelet reconstruction generally requires reliable soft-tissue coverage and sufficient bone graft volume, whereas vascularised bone transfer requires microsurgical expertise and may be less predictable in heavily scarred or infected fields. By contrast, bone transport offers the additional biological advantage of distraction histogenesis, which can improve local vascularity and stimulate surrounding soft tissues during reconstruction [10, 11].

A persistent limitation of bone transport is docking-site management [12, 13]. In wedge-shaped defects, conventional reconstruction may involve resection of irregular but viable cortex to create a flat docking surface. Although this simplifies docking, it also enlarges the effective defect and sacrifices residual bone.

In the present case, we preserved a viable posterior cortical remnant and incorporated it into an overlapping docking configuration, followed by bone chipping [14, 15]. This report does not propose a new standard treatment concept. Rather, it illustrates a selected bone-preserving strategy within the established principles of Ilizarov bone transport.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old man was referred to our department from an affiliated hospital of another university for salvage of chronic infected nonunion of the femur and tibia after a severe traffic accident involving polytrauma. Both the femur and tibia had been affected by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

At presentation, the tibia showed chronic infected nonunion with a 90-mm wedge-shaped diaphyseal defect, severe equinus deformity, and marked soft-tissue compromise. A proximal lower-leg soft-tissue defect measuring approximately 5 × 10 cm with drainage was present (Fig. 1). Conventional reconstructive options were considered but were limited by the local biological and soft-tissue conditions. Vascularised fibular transfer combined with free flap reconstruction remained a theoretical option. However, infection had persisted for more than 1 year and the tissues surrounding the nonunion were extensively scarred after multiple previous operations, making microsurgical reconstruction technically demanding and potentially less predictable. Masquelet reconstruction was also considered, but reliable flap-based soft-tissue coverage would probably have been required before large-volume bone grafting. Given the chronic infection, compromised soft-tissue envelope, approximately 90-mm bone defect, multiple prior surgical failures, and limited donor-bone practicality, we selected a graft-sparing strategy based on bone transport.

We therefore planned staged limb salvage based on the five principles: infection control, preservation of viable residual cortex, bone transport for defect reconstruction, biological stimulation by bone chipping, and simultaneous correction of associated deformity.

Surgical strategy and technique

Frame conversion and infection control

The tibia was reconstructed using an Ilizarov circular external fixator. The goals were to maintain infection quiescence, restore tibial continuity, preserve viable residual cortex when appropriate, and allow subsequent correction of deformity.

MRSA was isolated from the draining wound. Irrigation and debridement were performed, vancomycin-loaded cement beads were implanted, and intravenous daptomycin at 6 mg/kg once daily was administered. As shown in Fig. 2a, debridement was followed by antibiotic bead placement. The posterior cortical remnant was retained only after meticulous debridement, because it demonstrated punctate cortical bleeding (paprika sign) and showed no gross sequestration or obvious non-viable bone. Following frame conversion and proximal tibial corticotomy, a latency period of 7 days was allowed before distraction was initiated. Bone transport was then performed according to standard Ilizarov principles at a rate of 1 mm/day in four increments of 0.25 mm.

Preoperative assessment and bone-preserving docking plan

Preoperative planning focussed on the morphology of the residual posterior cortex and its relationship to the opposing medullary canal. Serial anteroposterior and lateral radiographs and computed tomography, including axial images and three-dimensional reconstructions, were reviewed to assess the orientation, continuity, and structural suitability of the posterior cortical remnant. These images demonstrated a wedge-shaped defect in which the tapered posterior cortical remnant could potentially overlap with, and be accommodated by, the wider medullary region of the opposing fragment.

The residual posterior cortex was therefore regarded not as an obstacle but as a potential structural resource. The decision to preserve it was based on the combined findings of infection quiescence, negative cultures, normalised inflammatory markers, intraoperative punctate bleeding, absence of gross sequestration, and a favourable wedge-shaped geometry.

Overlapping bone transport and quantitative assessment of cortical engagement

Gradual bone transport was performed, with serial radiographs showing progressive reduction of the defect. During the late transport phase, the posterior cortical remnant of the distal fragment was guided to overlap with the proximal segment rather than simply abut a transverse docking surface. This relationship is shown in Fig. 2d–g.

The overlap length measured on lateral imaging increased from approximately 8 mm before transport to 63 mm immediately after completion of transport, corresponding to 55 mm of additional cortical engagement. Using the tibial transverse width at the docking level of approximately 33 mm, the estimated cortical overlap contact area was approximately 2,079 mm2. The exact intramedullary entry point could not be reproducibly defined on retrospective images; therefore, telescoping depth was not measured independently. Instead, overlap length and estimated cortical contact area were used as practical quantitative descriptors of the partial bone-in-bone engagement.

Bone chipping and alignment correction

Following overlap docking, sagittal plane translational deformity remained, with the distal fragment positioned anteriorly relative to the proximal segment on the lateral radiograph. Docking-site chipping was then performed as biological augmentation. Controlled local fragmentation and cortical refreshening were used to reactivate osteogenesis.

After chipping, alignment correction was performed by adjusting the distal ring construct and translating the distal segment posteriorly to restore sagittal alignment while maintaining the overlap engagement. Thus, biological stimulation by chipping and mechanical correction of alignment were performed as complementary steps within the same reconstructive stage (Fig. 2h and i).

This step was informed by previously reported chipping techniques [14, 15]. In the present case, chipping served as a graft-sparing biological adjunct after mechanical optimisation of the docking configuration.

Simultaneous correction of equinus deformity

Severe equinus deformity was corrected gradually in parallel with bone transport. At presentation, passive ankle dorsiflexion was − 40° (Fig. 3a). Soft-tissue drainage resolved during the course of transport. Vulpius Achilles tendon lengthening was then performed, followed by gradual correction using a Taylor Spatial Frame (Fig. 3b). Outcome and follow-up

Serial radiographs demonstrated progressive consolidation at the docking site after overlapping transport, bone chipping, and alignment correction. Bone gradually filled the docking region, and no recurrent signs of infection were observed. The progression from pre-transport assessment to overlap docking, chipping, and alignment correction is shown in Fig. 2.

The patient underwent full weight-bearing gait training during treatment. As part of the broader reconstructive course, distal femoral valgus deformity was also corrected using a hexapod strategy, and the patient returned to society approximately 3 years after injury, corresponding to about 1 year after transfer.

At final follow-up, 14 years after surgery, clinical and radiographic union had been maintained without recurrent infection. The tibia showed maintained union on anteroposterior and lateral radiographs (Fig. 4a and b), and the ankle showed no recurrence of equinus deformity (Fig. 4c and d). Mild residual pain was present, with a VAS score of 10/100. The patient did not require orthotic support and was able to ambulate independently. According to the ASAMI criteria, the bone result was excellent, with union, absence of infection, no tibial deformity, and no tibial limb length discrepancy. The functional result was good, with mild pain and a slight limp but no limitation in activities of daily living. Although tibial reconstruction achieved satisfactory alignment and no tibial shortening, residual femoral shortening likely contributed to the mild gait disturbance.

Discussion

This case should be interpreted within the broader framework of established Ilizarov reconstruction principles. Following infection control and debridement, the reconstruction was fundamentally based on acceptance of bone loss and restoration of length through proximal tibial distraction osteogenesis, or secondary callotasis. The distinctive feature of the present case was not bone transport itself, which is well established, but preservation and utilisation of the residual posterior cortex during docking.

In conventional bone transport, the goal is often simplified to closure of a gap between two bone ends. That approach is appropriate for true segmental defects. However, some post-traumatic defects are wedge-shaped or irregular, with residual oblique or pointed cortex that may remain biologically viable. In such situations, resection of irregular cortex may simplify docking, but it may also sacrifice useful bone.

The present case illustrates a selected bone-preserving modification within conventional bone transport. The native shape of the defect was not completely normalised; instead, the residual posterior cortical remnant was preserved and incorporated into an overlapping docking configuration. This may offer three practical advantages in selected patients: avoidance of unnecessary iatrogenic bone loss, increased cortical contact area, and improved intrinsic stability through partial bone-in-bone engagement.

Patient selection is critical. In our view, viable residual cortex should satisfy three conditions: infection has become clinically quiescent after debridement and antimicrobial treatment; the retained cortex demonstrates biological viability, including punctate cortical bleeding after debridement; and the residual cortex provides sufficient structural continuity to participate in docking. A suitable defect morphology is a wedge-shaped configuration in which the tapered cortical remnant of one fragment can overlap with, and be accommodated by, the medullary canal or cortical envelope of the opposing fragment.

Conversely, conventional segmental docking remains preferable when the residual cortex is extensively damaged, biologically non-viable, or geometrically unsuitable for overlap engagement. Examples include circumferential cortical destruction, gross sequestration, diffuse osteomyelitis involving the retained cortex, or a defect configuration in which the tapered fragment cannot be incorporated into the opposing medullary region. In these situations, additional resection to create a conventional docking surface may provide a more predictable reconstruction.

This case also requires careful interpretation in relation to current reconstructive options. Masquelet reconstruction remains highly valuable for many bone defects [6, 7], particularly because it allows staged reconstruction with favourable biological conditions for graft incorporation. However, successful application generally requires reliable soft-tissue coverage and sufficient bone graft volume. In this patient, reliable soft-tissue coverage would probably have required flap reconstruction before large-volume bone grafting could be undertaken.

Vascularised fibular grafting combined with free flap reconstruction can also be effective for combined bone and soft-tissue loss [8, 9]. In the present case, however, infection had persisted for more than 1 year and the tissues surrounding the nonunion were severely scarred after multiple previous operations. Under these conditions, microsurgical reconstruction was considered technically demanding and potentially less predictable.

Bone transport offered a different advantage profile. Through distraction histogenesis, it can improve local vascularity and stimulate surrounding soft tissues while reconstructing bone [10, 11]. This does not replace flap surgery in every case, but in selected complex cases with chronic infection, major scarring, limited donor bone stock, and difficult soft-tissue conditions, it may be more biologically and logistically suitable than graft-dependent reconstruction. In the present case, the draining soft-tissue defect healed during transport, as shown by comparison of Figs. 1 and 3. Thus, our choice of bone transport was not a rejection of alternative techniques, but a selection of a strategy better aligned with the compromised biological and soft-tissue environment.

Bone chipping served as the biological counterpart to this mechanical strategy. Previous reports have shown that chipping can be useful in difficult unions and reconstructive correction [14, 15]. In the present case, chipping was used after overlap docking to provide local biological stimulation and to facilitate final alignment correction.

The overlap configuration also differs from previously reported docking-site engagement techniques such as peg-in-hole docking [13]. Those methods improve congruity or stability, whereas the present strategy was primarily intended to avoid unnecessary bone sacrifice in a wedge-shaped defect while maintaining cortical engagement.

Another important feature was simultaneous correction of equinus deformity (Fig. 3). A united tibia alone would not have guaranteed useful function. The ability to combine infection control, bone transport, docking reconstruction, biological stimulation, and deformity correction within one staged strategy was central to final limb salvage.

At final follow-up, the tibial reconstruction achieved excellent ASAMI bone results and good ASAMI functional results. Although a slight limp remained, it was likely related mainly to residual femoral shortening rather than failure of tibial reconstruction. The durability of the tibial reconstruction is demonstrated by the 14-year follow-up findings in Fig. 4.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. It describes a single patient, so generalisability is limited. No biomechanical testing was performed, and the presumed advantages in contact area and stability are based on operative reasoning and clinical outcome rather than formal analysis. Although the overlap length and estimated cortical contact area were quantified retrospectively, the exact telescoping depth into the proximal medullary canal could not be measured independently because the medullary entry point was not reproducibly identifiable on the available images.

This was also a combined reconstructive strategy involving infection control, transport, overlap docking, chipping, alignment correction, equinus correction, and staged management of associated femoral pathology, so the independent contribution of each component cannot be isolated. In addition, although the retained posterior cortex showed punctate cortical bleeding and infection was clinically quiescent before transport, histological confirmation of every retained cortical segment was not available. Residual infected bone should not be preserved when viability or infection control is uncertain. More detailed patient-reported outcome measures and gait analyses were not included. Finally, although bone transport may improve local soft-tissue biology, it cannot replace flap reconstruction in every case.

Despite these limitations, this case illustrates a practical message: in selected wedge-shaped defects, residual viable cortex should not be sacrificed unnecessarily if it can be safely incorporated into docking after infection control and careful assessment of defect geometry.

Clinical relevance

This case suggests that selected wedge-shaped defects may benefit from a bone-preserving approach to docking. Surgeons should not routinely convert every irregular defect into a transverse segmental defect if viable residual cortex can be safely preserved and used to improve engagement. However, this strategy should be considered only in carefully selected patients with infection quiescence, viable residual cortex, and favourable wedge-shaped geometry.

Conclusions

Overlapping bone transport in this case functioned as a bone-preserving docking strategy within an established Ilizarov reconstruction framework. By preserving and utilising a viable posterior cortical remnant rather than resecting it solely to create a flat docking surface, the reconstruction avoided additional iatrogenic bone loss and may have increased docking contact and stability. This approach should not be regarded as a new standard treatment concept, but as a selected option for wedge-shaped defects when infection is controlled, residual cortex is viable, and the geometry permits cortical overlap engagement.
