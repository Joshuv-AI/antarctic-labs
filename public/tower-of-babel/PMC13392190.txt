Open Bite With Short Root Anomaly: A Case Report
Authors: Saleh Alwadei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe present case report aimed to illustrate the orthodontic therapy management in an 18‐year‐old Caucasian male with a rare open bite condition with a short root anomaly. An 18‐year‐old male presented with an anterior and lateral open bite, difficulty chewing, and poor oral hygiene. Radiographic examination revealed short roots on the maxillary anteriors and premolars, consistent with a short root anomaly. The patient was diagnosed with an anterior open bite associated with a short root anomaly and a history of scleroderma. A non‐surgical orthodontic treatment was performed using segmental mechanics and an extrusion archwire. After 22 months of treatment, the open bite was successfully closed, achieving Class I molar and canine relationships and improved facial esthetics without significant root resorption. The coexistence of an anterior open bite and short root anomaly complicates orthodontic management due to the risk of root resorption. The case demonstrates that an anterior open bite with short root anomaly can be effectively managed using a conservative orthodontic approach with careful force control and follow‐up, achieving stable and esthetic results.

Introduction

Anterior open bite (AOB) is a form of malocclusion when the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth do not overlap vertically. Therefore, a space exists when a patient bites in a centric occlusion [1]. AOB is usually characterized by divergent maxillary and mandibular occlusal planes, mesially angulated posterior teeth, extruded molars, and short posterior facial height. The skeletal anomalies, such as high mandibular plane angle or vertical maxillary excess, also complicate the condition and may lead to relapses following orthodontic treatment [2].

An open bite is linked to masticatory, articulation, and, in other instances, dysfunction of the temporomandibular joint. Its treatment is complicated and may involve orthodontic, orthopedic, and surgical procedures, especially when it is skeletal [3]. On the contrary, short‐root anomaly (SRA) is a rare condition characterized by short and blunted roots. It is commonly misdiagnosed with idiopathic root resorption [3]. Besides being a rare developmental disorder, SRA is linked with a predisposition to external apical root resorption during orthodontic therapy, and biomechanical planning should be done carefully. A retrospective CBCT study revealed that SRA patients may not experience noticeably higher resorption rates in general than in controls; however, individual differences should be taken into account and also require individual force control (17).

Moreover, open bite malocclusion in itself has been associated with an increased risk of root resorption, particularly in the anterior and premolar teeth. Recent evidence suggests that occlusal function and increased biomechanical demands in open bite cases may predispose these patients to greater resorptive changes during treatment (18).

Patients with systemic diseases, including scleroderma, can have periodontal ligament (PDL) and bone biology changes. Due to heightened collagen deposition and loss of vascularity, normal bone remodeling may not occur and orthodontic tooth movement may be slower, which increases the chances of root resorption. Thus, it is necessary to carefully regulate the force and monitor it during treatment (19).

The present case report aimed to highlight the orthodontic treatment of an AOB with SRA using a conservative treatment. The objective was to achieve ideal occlusion, functional stability, and optimal occlusal relationships, along with improved esthetics, while minimizing the risk of root damage.

Patient Information

An 18‐year‐old Caucasian male presented to the OPD with a chief complaint of unfavorable bite and difficulty chewing. His dentist referred the patient to the orthodontist, who recommended orthognathic surgery. The patient had no prior history of orthodontic treatment. The medical history revealed scleroderma and an allergy to the antibiotic Cefzil as a child. He had poor oral hygiene and no history, signs, or symptoms of temporomandibular joint dysfunction. The patient denied any history of thumb sucking, tongue thrusting, and other parafunctional habits. The SRA was diagnosed after extraoral and intraoral examination. SRA was suspected based on clinical findings and subsequently confirmed through panoramic and periapical radiographic analysis. This revealed reduced root‐to‐crown ratios and characteristic root morphology.

Clinical Findings

Upon examination, the tongue showed indentations on both sides and tongue‐tie due to the short lingual frenulum, also known as Ankyloglossia. Ankyloglossia was considered a possible contributing etiological factor in the AOB, particularly in relation to tongue posture and its potential influence on dentoalveolar stability. He had a symmetric mesoprosopic face with a convex soft tissue profile, mildly obtuse nasolabial angle, and retrusive upper and lower lips relative to the E‐line. Upon smiling, 80% of the maxillary central incisors were visible. The patient had an open bite from the maxillary first molar to the first molar, represented as an anterior open bite ranging from 3 to 5 mm and a lateral open bite of 2 mm on both sides. He had a Class I molar on the left side, a Class II end‐on on the right side, Class II canines on both sides, and a 4 mm overjet. Maxillary intermolar width was 2 mm less than mandibular intermolar width. There was a mild mandibular crowding and a 3 mm mandibular curve of spee. To minimize the risk of root resorption in teeth with SRA, a conservative biomechanical protocol was followed. This included the application of light, controlled forces using segmental mechanics, avoidance of heavy continuous forces, and gradual activation of extrusion cantilevers. Additionally, longer intervals between activations (5–6 weeks) were maintained to allow periodontal ligament recovery. Periodic periapical and anatomic radiographs were obtained to monitor root integrity throughout treatment.

The following three treatment options were discussed with the patient. The segmental upper arch mechanic uses an extrusion archwire to extrude the maxillary incisors. The second option followed consistent archwire mechanics in both arches, along with the posterior and anterior elastics. Both options may result in a worse open bite if the patient does not wear elastics to balance the drawbacks of the consistent archwire or the extrusion arch. The third alternative consisted of posterior intrusion based on segmental mechanics and mini‐implants secured by a rigid transpalatal arch, which may not have produced the best esthetic outcome. Intrusion of the posterior teeth may lead to open bite closure without extruding the maxillary incisors. A fourth option, consisting of combined orthodontic‐orthognathic surgical treatment, was also discussed, particularly to address vertical discrepancy more definitively. However, the patient declined surgical intervention due to personal preference. After a detailed explanation of the risks and advantages, the patient agreed to the first option, which involved using an extrusion archwire segmental mechanics.

A non‐surgical orthodontic treatment plan was suggested. The treatment started with teeth scaling and oral hygiene instructions. The pre‐adjusted fixed appliances (straight‐wire system) 0.022 × 0.028 MBT were bonded on the teeth. The upper arch teeth were straightened in section, whereas the lower arch teeth were adjusted using constant archwires. The open bite was closed using extrusion cantilevers of 0.019 × 0.025 TMA wires. The panoramic radiographs were performed periodically to follow the root position and morphology. The final step was coordinating the upper and lower archwires, 3–3 fixed retainers, and routine follow‐up of third molars. Pre‐adjusted fixed appliances (straight‐wire system) were used throughout treatment.

The upper first molars to the anterior segment were connected with an extrusion archwire composed of 0.017 × 0.025 TMA, which was anchored with seating elastics to help close the teeth. The lower anterior teeth were subjected to minor interproximal reduction. The assessment of root length revealed no significant variations in the root length in teeth with short root abnormality.

After oral hygiene was improved, bands were put on the first molars (6's) and other teeth, but not second molars (7's), which were bonded using 0.022 × 0.028 MBT fixed appliances (straight‐wire system). Moreover, no medications or painkillers were required throughout the course of treatment. The patient was instructed to avoid hard foods to prevent appliance breakage and maintain proper oral hygiene following the placement of fixed orthodontic appliances. Better control of the upper incisors (U2‐2) was achieved by bonding them a little gingivally. The leveling and alignment of the upper arch were done in stages with 0.016 NiTi, 0.016 × 0.022 NiTi, 0.018 × 0.025 NiTi, and 0.019 × 0.025 SS wires, and the lower arch was aligned with 0.016 NiTi, 0.016 × 0.022 NiTi, and 0.017 × 0.025 NiTi and SS, respectively.

A segmental 0.019 × 0.025 SS wire was applied with two extrusion cantilever 0.019 × 0.025 TMA wires protruding from the U6 bands to the U2‐2 segment to provide light extrusive forces to close the anterior open bite. The elastics were placed to close the lateral open bite. L3‐3 underwent minor interproximal reduction (IPR). Periapical (PA) radiographs were periodically performed to assess the short roots of U1, U2, U4, and U5, and a progress panoramic radiograph was performed to reposition brackets. These last treatment stages involved bonding the second molars (7's). The 0.016 × 0.022 TMA wires were used to finish and detail the upper and lower fixed 3–3 retainers, and positioner delivery was done. Dental health fluctuated throughout the treatment. The patient was highly compliant and attended regular follow‐up visits every 5–6 weeks, contributing to the overall success of the treatment. The overall treatment duration was 22 months. Retention

Maxillary and mandibular fixed 3–3 retainers were bonded. A tooth positioner was given to the patient to improve the buccal overlap of UL 6 and to provide a deeper overbite. The patient was instructed to wear the positioner 24 h a day, except while eating and brushing, for 1 week. During this week of full‐time wear, the patient was asked to clench the device, hold for 10–20 s, and then release. For the next 3 weeks, wear it actively for a minimum of 4 h during the day and passively while sleeping. Afterwards, a maxillary Hawley retainer was planned to be fabricated and given to the patient; however, he preferred to continue with the positioner. Lingual frenectomy and speech therapy were recommended to help correct tongue posture/function and to enhance long‐term post‐treatment stability. One‐year follow‐up showed a slight relapse and reduction in the overbite that was achieved during treatment, as shown in Figure 8.

Final Evaluation of Treatment

Patient's chief complaint was addressed, and treatment objectives were achieved in an efficient treatment time, with good esthetic and occlusal outcomes. Class I molar and canine relationship was achieved through a combination of uprighting. Maxillary posterior teeth and Left 6's and through autorotation of the mandible (due to removal of interferences). The overbite and overjet were achieved through upper anterior extrusion and retraction, as well as the mandibular autorotation forward and slightly up. Buccal overjet on Upper Left 6 could have finished in a more ideal relationship, but overall occlusion is excellent. In the final stages of treatment, the patient was burnt out and expressed that he wanted the braces removed as he was happy with the results, despite explaining to him that Upper Left 6 needs more correction. He was debonded based on his request and the fact that we were worried about root resorption on upper incisors. However, we monitored for root resorption periodically since the start of using extrusive force on incisors with PA films. After debond, a Positioner was given to the patient to improve buccal overlap of Upper Left 6 and to provide a deeper overbite, which he kept as a retainer. He was sent to his dentist for a 3rd molar evaluation.

Discussion

The risk of root resorption is high in SRA patients and makes orthodontic treatment in these patients a challenge. Amuk et al. highlighted that root resorption pathophysiology in SRA patients is primarily associated with their smaller root surface area, which rendered them susceptible to even minor orthodontic forces [4]. The treatment mechanisms and the force level are fundamental in maintaining root form during orthodontic surgery. Uzunçibuk et al. emphasized that intrusive forces, especially during open bite correction, are more dangerous to SRA patients since apical resorption may be enhanced by vertical root compression [5].

Radiographic monitoring is key to successfully managing the SRA patient undergoing orthodontic treatment. Periapical and cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) imaging can detect alterations in resorptive processes early, enabling clinicians to modify the force amount and mechanics before permanent damage is done [6]. Periodic monitoring every 3–6 months has been suggested in situations with severe vertical discrepancies, such as an open bite [7].

Earlier clinical reports have discussed the difficulty of managing SRA in orthodontics, such as the heightened risk of root resorption and the changed mechanics of treatment. Indicatively, case reports and reviews have highlighted that SRA can make orthodontic movement traditional and cannot be treated with standard approaches because it demands a different treatment plan to reduce negative effects [8, 9].

A close association has also been found between systemic diseases and root resorption during orthodontic treatment [10]. Systematic conditions, such as endocrine disorders, connective tissue diseases, and metabolic abnormalities, may significantly impact bone metabolism, root development, and periodontal health, increasing the risk of complications during orthodontic therapy [11]. Thorough evaluation of the underlying diseases is essential for safe and effective treatment planning and prevention of future complications. The case report also encourages involving a medical specialist to ensure proper care.

Very few reports have mentioned the co‐occurrence of SRA with AOB and the management guidelines. Interestingly, in a recent case report by Marechal et al., it was described how an orthodontic treatment coupled with orthognathic surgery was performed on an adolescent with generalized SRA and AOB, and the precautionary force control was necessary to prevent additional root injury [12].

The fibrosis and vascular alterations of the periodontal ligament (PDL), combined with the presence of scleroderma, complicate orthodontic treatment. Such changes can lead to decreased tissue responsiveness and a decline in bone remodeling with an impact on the speed of tooth movements and predisposition to root resorption. This risk is increased when it is coupled with Short Root Anomaly. Light and controlled forces are advisable in this situation to reduce the stress of the weakened roots. Segmental mechanics in the given case enabled an accurate application of force, whereas periodic radiographic surveillance guaranteed the safety and stability of treatment [10].

Although SRA or AOB have been reported individually, the simultaneous occurrence of AOB and SRA is not well reported, and there is no agreement concerning the conservative management guidelines. In the current case, the cephalometric ANB angle was reduced by 3° to 0° after treatment. This change can be described simply by the mandibular autorotation that is caused by the closure of the anterior open bite by extrusion of the maxillary incisors. Small‐scale dental reimbursements by uprighting the front teeth and retraction also enhanced this sagittal correction. Such skeletal and dental adaptations point to the uses of non‐surgical orthodontic mechanics in the functional improvement of sagittal relationships, even in complex cases that include SRA and anterior open bite. This case will add to the literature because it has shown that with the use of controlled light mechanics and periodic radiographic assessment, successful vertical correction could be made with minimal risk of additional root resorption.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The case report presents a unique clinical presentation of AOB and SRA in a patient with scleroderma. The case report offers essential information about the treatment planning and managing possible risks. The practical application of an extrusion arch shows the potential of the conservative orthodontic methods to produce bite closure with minimum root destruction. However, the results cannot be applied to all patients with such conditions. The lack of comparative treatment approaches, limited long‐term follow‐up, and absence of PA x‐rays are also limiting factors that prevent conducting general conclusions.

Patient Perspective

The patient expressed satisfaction with the treatment outcome and was pleased with the improvement in his bite and smile esthetics. He appreciated that the treatment was completed without surgery and reported that the orthodontic appliances were comfortable to wear after initial adjustment. The patient also stated that he would recommend similar conservative orthodontic approaches to others with comparable conditions.

Conclusion

The case report utilized an extrusion archwire to manage the AOB with SRA effectively. A traditional non‐surgical treatment plan achieved an ideal occlusion and enhanced smile esthetics. Cautious planning and routine follow‐ups are crucial in compromised orthodontic patients with SRA, particularly in the presence of medical conditions such as scleroderma.
