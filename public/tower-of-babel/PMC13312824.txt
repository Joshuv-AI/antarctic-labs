Carceral settings as institutional exposures in caries microbiome research
Authors: Ahmad Yusroni, Muhammad Khairul Anwar, Yuyun Eka ​Kartika Sari
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the article by Kijpornyongpan et al., ‘Microbial signatures of dental caries in the incarcerated elderly: a salivary microbiota study in a restricted environment’ [1]. By comparing caries-active and caries-free older prisoners, the authors offer a rare microbiological account of salivary dysbiosis in a population often missing from oral microbiome research. Their use of a prison setting, however, raises an interpretive issue that deserves closer attention.

The carceral setting should not be treated only as a source of methodological control. It may also act as an institutional exposure that shapes the ecological conditions in which caries-associated microbial signatures emerge. Regulated diet, limited access to dental care, hygiene resources, fluoride exposure, antibiotic use and extraction practices are not merely contextual details of prison health. They may be part of the microbial environment being measured.

This distinction matters for biomarker interpretation. A restricted environment may reduce some lifestyle variation, but microbial differences observed within it may still reflect institutionally patterned exposures. A global scoping review has shown that prison oral health services differ in terms of availability, accessibility and delivery models [2]. Similarly, evidence on prison-based oral health interventions indicates that oral health improvement in these settings depends on service design and institutional constraints, not only on individual risk profiles [3].

Future carceral microbiome studies could therefore pair microbial profiling with a minimum institutional dataset. Such a dataset might include dental attendance, access to oral hygiene supplies, dietary regulation, fluoride availability, antibiotic exposure and extraction history. These variables would not dilute the microbiological analysis. They would make microbial signatures more interpretable and more useful for prevention planning.

This institutional lens complements the authors' microbiological contribution. Treating restricted environments as exposures as well as controls may help translate salivary microbial biomarkers into preventive strategies that are biologically informed and feasible within carceral health systems.
