AR1-ZO: Topology-Aware Rank-1 Zeroth-Order Queries for High-Rank LoRA Fine-Tuning
Ziye Chen Hongbin Lin Chenyu Zhang Xiangda Yan Yongjie Yang Yao Shu

Abstract
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization enables large-language-model fine-tuning without storing backpropagation activations, while LoRA supplies compact trainable adapters. Combining them creates a rank paradox: increasing LoRA rank improves adapter capacity, but standard two-point ZO either perturbs a rank-dependent number of coordinates or, under atomwise updates, can make the finite-difference signal unobservable. This paper shows that the bottleneck is a measurement-topology problem rather than a need for an external subspace. LoRA already decomposes into matched rank- 1 1 atoms, each a complete factor-coordinate block of dimension d out + d in d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}} . Querying one atom per step keeps the stored adapter rank r r while removing r r from the single-query perturbation dimension. The naive atomwise query is still miscalibrated: if it inherits canonical LoRA scaling α / r \alpha/r , the active finite-difference signal shrinks as 1 / r 1/r and the active finite-difference signal-to-noise ratio (FD-SNR) as 1 / r 2 1/r^{2} , producing directional collapse under a fixed residual evaluation-noise floor. AR1-ZO pairs alternating rank- 1 1 atom queries with topology-aware scaling γ = α ​ r \gamma=\alpha r , restoring rank-invariant active signal without auxiliary bases, activation hooks, curvature estimates, or extra forward queries. Theory proves atom minimality, rank-independent active query dimension, directional collapse and restoration, and the remaining rank dependence as an amortized coverage cost. Experiments on OPT and Qwen3 models validate the signal mechanism and show that AR1-ZO makes high-rank LoRA effective among matched-budget ZO methods under the standard two-forward-pass query budget.

Introduction

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) under tight memory budgets requires controlling both activation storage and trainable degrees of freedom. Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization, exemplified by MeZO
mezo
, removes the activation-memory cost of backpropagation by estimating descent directions from forward-pass scalar differences. LoRA
hu2022lora
, together with related low-rank adaptation variants such as QLoRA
QLoRA
, DoRA
DoRA
, and AdaLoRA
adalora

Their combination is therefore natural, but it turns the LoRA rank into a double-edged quantity: the same rank that gives the adapter capacity can also determine what a two-point ZO oracle has to perturb and whether its scalar loss difference remains visible.

The usual explanation is the curse of dimensionality. Classical randomized gradient estimators incur variance that grows with the perturbation dimension

;
liu2018zeroth

, low-rank estimators

;
seung2025loren
;
sun2025tezo
, LoRA-manifold tangent updates
song2026rozo
, activation-guided perturbations
lin2026agzo
, and second-order reweighting
zhao2025secondorder
. Recent low-rank and geometry-aware methods such as TeZO and RoZO further show that temporal low-rank structure and LoRA-manifold geometry can improve ZO fine-tuning, but they still leave open the atom-level measurement unit and scale of a two-forward-pass LoRA-ZO oracle. In parallel, LoRA scaling and optimization studies such as rsLoRA
kala2023rslora
, RoRA
liu2025rora
, and LoRA+
hayou2024lora
refine rank normalization or factor-wise dynamics under first-order joint updates. These lines establish that both query geometry and scaling matter, but they do not settle the measurement unit of black-box LoRA-ZO:

1

Preliminaries: LoRA and Zeroth-Order Optimization

Applying ZO to LoRA is governed by three separable quantities: the trainable low-rank coordinates and output coefficient, the coordinates moved by one two-point query, and the evaluation noise that determines whether the scalar loss difference is observable. A black-box LoRA optimizer therefore faces two constraints: the queried coordinate set should not grow with rank, and the finite-difference numerator should not be scaled below the noise floor.

LoRA parameterization and rank scaling.
LoRA turns dense adaptation into a factored low-rank parameterization. For a frozen dense layer
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
and input activation
x
, LoRA injects a trainable rank-
r
update through two factors
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
and
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
:

h=\bm{W}_{0}x+\Delta\bm{W}x,\qquad\Delta\bm{W}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\bm{B}\bm{A}.

(1)

The canonical choice
\gamma=\alpha
gives coefficient
\alpha/r

hu2022lora
, coupling rank to both adapter capacity and the output change induced by factor movement.

Two-point ZO estimator and perturbation dimension.
ZO fine-tuning does not observe gradients of the LoRA factors directly; it observes how the loss changes under a random perturbation of the trainable coordinates. In the standard two-point Randomized Gradient Estimator (RGE)
nesterov2017random
, a perturbation
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{D})
and smoothing radius
\mu
produce

\hat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta+\mu\bm{z})-\mathcal{L}(\theta-\mu\bm{z})}{2\mu}\,\bm{z}.

(2)

Here
D
is the number of coordinates perturbed by one two-point query. Under standard
L
-smooth ZO analyses
nesterov2017random
;
liu2018zeroth
, the mean squared error scales as
\mathcal{O}(D\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}+L^{2}\mu^{2}D^{3})
. Perturbing all LoRA factors of one matrix sets
D=r(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
, so full-adapter ZO makes the rank chosen for capacity also control the estimator-variance dimension.

Finite-difference signal and evaluation noise.
The dimension term explains why large perturbation spaces are difficult, but it does not fully determine whether a ZO query is useful. The estimator is formed from the clean finite-difference numerator

\Delta_{\mu}(\theta;\bm{z})=\mathcal{L}(\theta+\mu\bm{z})-\mathcal{L}(\theta-\mu\bm{z}),

(3)

which carries the directional information in Eq. (
2
). In stochastic LLM fine-tuning, paired evaluations on the same mini-batch can reduce data-sampling variance, but residual oracle variation from numerical kernels, stochastic components, or controlled noise injection still enters the observed numerator. We write the observed numerator as
\Delta_{\mu}(\theta;\bm{z})+\xi_{+}-\xi_{-}
, where
\xi_{+}
and
\xi_{-}
are the residual errors of the positive and negative evaluations and
\sigma_{\xi}^{2}
denotes their variance scale after shared-batch control. A perturbation is informative only if its induced
\Delta_{\mu}
remains resolvable against this noise. Dimension reduction therefore solves only half of the ZO-LoRA problem: a small coordinate block can still be useless if the coefficient
\gamma/r
in Eq. (
1
) suppresses its output effect before the loss difference is measured.

3
AR1-ZO: Rank-1 Atom Queries with Topology-Aware Scaling

These constraints leave a narrow design target. Full-adapter ZO keeps the LoRA parameterization but perturbs all
r(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
factor coordinates, so the rank chosen for capacity immediately becomes a variance multiplier. Existing dimension-reduction methods remove this tax with auxiliary bases, activation hooks, curvature side information, or manifold-level geometry. AR1-ZO instead changes the query topology of the existing LoRA factors. Its first step is to expose one complete LoRA atom to the two-point oracle, making the per-query coordinate set independent of
r
while the stored adapter remains rank
r
. Its second step is to recalibrate the scale of that sequential measurement: once only one atom moves between the two endpoints, the joint LoRA coefficient
\alpha/r
no longer reflects the amount of signal that the queried block must carry.

3.1
Complete Rank-1 Atoms as Native ZO Query Blocks

Fix one adapted matrix and write
\Delta\bm{W}=(\gamma/r)\sum_{k=1}^{r}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
, where
\bm{b}_{k}
is the
k
th column of
\bm{B}
and
\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
is the
k
th row of
\bm{A}
. In first-order LoRA this decomposition is internal to joint-factor optimization, but in ZO the exposed coordinates define the search space. The matched atom
k
is the pair
(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})
: its first-order variations generate all local changes
\delta\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}+\bm{b}_{k}\delta\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
. It is also the finest complete LoRA-native block, since perturbing one side is incomplete and grouping
m>1
atoms raises the block dimension to
m(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
.

At iteration
t
, a schedule selects
k(t)\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, exposes atom
k(t)
to the oracle, and holds all other atoms fixed during the two endpoint evaluations. Let
q=d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}
be the number of stored coordinates in one atom, let
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the active query direction, and let
\bm{P}_{t}
inject
\bm{z}
into the selected factor slots while zeroing all other LoRA coordinates. We write
\hat{\nabla}_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}
for the resulting estimate of the gradient with respect to the active atom coordinates:

\hat{\nabla}_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta+\mu\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})-\mathcal{L}(\theta-\mu\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})}{2\mu}\bm{z},

(4)

using the same two loss evaluations as standard ZO. Equation (
4
) changes the dimension term without changing the information model: the oracle still observes two scalar losses, and frozen atoms are shared by both endpoints. The stored adapter remains rank
r
, but one matrix query perturbs
d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}
rather than
r(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
coordinates. Rank becomes a coverage index rather than an instantaneous variance multiplier. The active numerator, however, still inherits
\gamma/r
from Eq. (
1
).

3.2
Topology-Aware Scaling:
\gamma=\alpha r

Atomwise querying changes how the jointly normalized adapter is measured. The standard choice
\gamma=\alpha
assumes a joint update in which all
r
atoms contribute through the coefficient
\alpha/r
. Under Eq. (
4
), however, only one atom differs between the positive and negative endpoints, so that single atom must carry the observable finite-difference signal. To isolate this topology effect, fix the user-level active coefficient
\alpha
and set

\gamma=\alpha r,\qquad\frac{\gamma}{r}=\alpha.

(5)

With this calibration, the forward map seen by an active-atom query decomposes into an active term with rank-invariant coefficient and a frozen background shared by both endpoints:

\displaystyle h_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{0}x+\underbrace{\alpha\,\bm{b}_{k(t)}\bm{a}_{k(t)}^{\top}x\vphantom{\sum\nolimits_{j\neq k(t)}}}_{\text{active atom}}+\underbrace{\alpha\sum\nolimits_{j\neq k(t)}\bm{b}_{j}\bm{a}_{j}^{\top}x}_{\text{frozen background}}.

(6)

Equations (
5
)–(
6
) separate query dimension from measured signal scale at the model-output level. The active query dimension has already been reduced to
q
, and the frozen background can shape the current point, but it is identical in the two endpoint evaluations and therefore cannot rescue a suppressed active numerator. Under the naive fixed-scale choice
\gamma=\alpha
, the active coefficient would be
\alpha/r
, so the finite-difference signal amplitude is
\mathcal{O}(\alpha/r)
and the signal power is
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}/r^{2})
whenever the residual noise floor is rank-independent. Increasing rank can then increase representational capacity while making each atomwise measurement less reliable. A larger learning rate cannot repair this failure, because it multiplies the noisy estimate only after the finite-difference numerator has already lost directional information. Section
4.2
formalizes this collapse, and Section
4.3
proves restoration under topology-aware scaling.

Rank-invariant measurement therefore requires rank-invariant
\gamma/r
. AR1-ZO uses the rank-
1
measurement as the reference:
\alpha
is the output coefficient assigned to the queried atom, giving
\gamma=\alpha r
. This calibration is topology-specific rather than a revision of joint first-order LoRA scaling; sequential ZO asks one atom, not the joint adapter, to carry the observable directional signal in a two-point measurement.

AR1-ZO changes the role of LoRA rank in a two-point finite-difference

rather than
rq

Rank-

The matched atom is the smallest complete block in the LoRA factors.
Perturbing only

(Completeness and minimality of matched rank-
1

.

For nonzero factors, one-sided blocks are incomplete. The matched

(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})

mq

Minimality fixes the smallest complete LoRA-native coordinates

(Active-atom ZO dimension is independent of rank)

For

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{L}-\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}\right]\lesssim q\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}+L^{2}\mu^{2}q^{3}+\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}q}{\mu^{2}}.

4.2
Diagnosis: Directional Collapse under Naive Scaling

Sequential rank-
1
querying exposes the active gradient through the
coefficient
\gamma/r

and write
k=k(t)

\bm{g}_{t}=[\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k};\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}]

factor-coordinate gradient, where
\bm{G}=\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}
is the
dense matrix gradient; the gradient actually seen by the two-point

(\gamma/r)\bm{g}_{t}

scalar projection of this vector, the active FD-SNR, denoted

below, determines whether the direction remains

\frac{\Delta_{\mu}(\theta;\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})+\xi_{+}-\xi_{-}}{2\mu}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\langle\bm{g}_{t},\bm{z}\rangle+\frac{\xi_{+}-\xi_{-}}{2\mu}+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}),\quad\mathsf{SNR}_{t}\asymp\frac{(\gamma/r)^{2}\mu^{2}\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}.

(7)

This active FD-SNR belongs to the measured function difference, not the

\gamma=\alpha

Theorem 4.3

(Directional Collapse under Naive Scaling)
.

Naive

\Theta(r^{-1})
and

\frac{r}{r_{c}}\to\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cos\!\left(\hat{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{L},\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\right)\right]\to 0.

4.3
Repair: Signal Restoration and Coverage Cost

Topology-aware scaling applies the correction before endpoint losses are queried. Setting
\gamma=\alpha r

\gamma/r=\alpha

Theorem 4.4

(Signal Restoration and Active-Atom Stationarity)

With
\gamma=\alpha r

(i)

\mathsf{SNR}_{t}\asymp\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}/\sigma_{\xi}^{2}=\Theta(1)

(ii)
the projected RGE convergence bound holds,

T^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mu^{4}L^{2}q^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}q}{T^{1/2}\mu^{2}}\right).

Part (i) uses active-atom gradient regularity; part (ii) uses
forward-scale stability of the represented adapter.

The bound depends on

\sigma_{\xi}

Corollary 4.5

(Coverage cost for full-adapter stationarity)

For uniform atom sampling, or a cyclic schedule averaged over one

Full-adapter stationarity therefore costs atom coverage, not a larger

4.4
Structural Insight: Why Native Atoms Can Carry Dense Descent

A low-dimensional measurement is useful only when the measured atom carries dense descent signal. Let
\bm{G}=\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
, define
\beta=\cos^{2}(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{u}_{1})\cos^{2}(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{v}_{1})
as alignment with the top singular pair, and write
\bm{g}_{k}=[\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k};\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}]
for the unscaled active gradient. For
\Delta\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})
, the one-step bound first depends on active gradient energy and then relates that energy to spectral concentration and atom alignment.

Theorem 4.7

(Conditional Progress Advantage of Aligned Rank-
1
Atoms)
.

If
\sigma_{1}^{2}\geq\rho\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[-\Delta\mathcal{L}]

\displaystyle\gtrsim\eta\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}\right]-\mathcal{O}(\eta\mu^{4}L^{2}q^{2}),

\displaystyle\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\rho\beta\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}(\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}).

Alignment need not appear in every layer or at every training stage. When it is present, a single intrinsic rank-
1

\rho\beta
-scaled portion of dense descent signal while retaining active dimension

4.3
turns
(\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|^{2})
into a
\Theta(1)
multiplier; the signal advantage at active dimension

is then

when

, with the exact ratio against generic random-block estimators given in Appendix

, this separates the two ingredients behind faster practical convergence:

controls the estimator variance, and
\rho\beta
controls the useful signal. The experiments therefore measure spectral concentration and atom alignment, test the predicted active FD-SNR restoration under rank sweeps, and then evaluate whether restored high-rank capacity improves downstream performance under the same two-forward-pass budget.

Conclusion

We identified and resolved a topology-scaling mismatch in zeroth-order LoRA fine-tuning: inheriting
\alpha/r
under sequential atomwise queries drives the active signal below the finite-difference noise floor.
AR1-ZO
pairs alternating rank-
1
probes with
\gamma=\alpha r
, making high-rank LoRA usable under pure black-box constraints. Rank leaves the single-query dimension and active FD-SNR, remaining only as amortized atom coverage.

Limitations and Future Work.

Theorem
4.4
is active-atom stationarity, so full-adapter stationarity still pays the coverage factor in Corollary
4.5
. Experiments cover OPT (2.7B/13B) and Qwen3 (1.7B/32B); larger models, free-form generation, and tensorized adapters remain natural extensions.

Appendix A
Related Work

Optimizing LLMs under strict memory constraints has driven the convergence of LoRA-style adaptation and zeroth-order (ZO) optimization. The relevant literature is therefore not a single lineage but the intersection of three threads: LoRA’s rank-factor structure, dimensionality reduction in ZO, and LoRA scaling dynamics. AR1-ZO is defined by two specific claims rather than by the broad fact that LoRA is low-rank. First, it uses each matched column-row rank-
1
atom as the complete query block exposed to a two-point ZO oracle. Second, it shows that this sequential query topology changes the meaning of LoRA’s scale and requires the topology-aware correction
\gamma=\alpha r
to keep the finite-difference signal observable.

LoRA and Algebraic Decomposition.

LoRA freezes pre-trained weights and injects trainable low-rank modules. Its update
\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{B}\bm{A}
can be written as an additive ensemble of rank-
1
atoms, and structural refinements such as AdaLoRA
[
18
]
make related rank components explicit. Because these components are additively separable, they invite alternating and blockwise optimization: RoLoRA
[
1
]
alternates
\bm{A}
/
\bm{B}
to stabilize federated FO training, and MeZO-BCD
[
13
]
updates parameter subsets to reduce effective ZO dimensionality. These works establish that LoRA factors and parameter blocks can be exposed selectively, but they do not use the matched rank-
1
atom
(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})
as the minimal complete two-point ZO query block. The distinction matters because a one-sided factor update does not span all local variations of an atom, while larger blocks reintroduce rank-dependent query dimension.

Our positioning:
the atom contribution is not the identity
\bm{B}\bm{A}=\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
itself; it is the use of the complete rank-
1
atom as the LoRA-native measurement unit that reduces the per-query dimension without reducing stored adapter rank.

Subspace and Low-Rank Zeroth-Order Optimization.

Standard ZO methods such as MeZO
[
11
]
suffer variance proportional to the full parameter dimension, prompting several subspace remedies. SubZero
[
17
]
restricts perturbations to random low-dimensional subspaces, while LOZO
[
2
]
builds low-rank ZO estimators from sampled low-rank perturbations. TeZO
[
16
]
extends this low-rank viewpoint across training time by constructing perturbations through a temporal tensor decomposition, and LOREN
[
14
]
and HIZOO
[
19
]
improve search directions with curvature or Hessian-informed statistics. AGZO
[
7
]
constructs activation-guided perturbation subspaces from forward-pass information. RoZO
[
15
]
is the closest LoRA-aware geometry method: it treats LoRA adapters as a low-rank manifold and constrains ZO updates to tangent spaces with Riemannian tools.

Our positioning:
AR1-ZO is complementary to these subspace, low-rank, and geometry methods. It does not sample an auxiliary basis, use activation hooks, estimate curvature, decompose perturbations over time, or optimize on the full LoRA manifold tangent space. Instead, it queries the structural rank-
1
atoms already present in the parameterization. TeZO shows that temporal structure can reduce ZO perturbation overhead, and RoZO shows that LoRA geometry is useful for ZO; AR1-ZO studies the finer measurement topology obtained when one atom is queried at a time, and identifies the scaling pathology that appears only under this sequential atomwise oracle.

Optimization Topology and Scaling Dynamics.

LoRA scaling laws such as the canonical
\alpha/r

[
5
]
, rsLoRA’s
\alpha/\sqrt{r}

[
6
]
, and RoRA’s related rank-reliability correction
[
8
]
study how adapter magnitude should vary with rank during ordinary LoRA fine-tuning. LoRA+
[
4
]
addresses a different but related optimization imbalance by assigning different learning rates to
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
. These works are important because they show that LoRA scaling is not a harmless implementation detail. Their topology, however, is still joint first-order adaptation: all rank components participate in the update signal together.

Our positioning:
AR1-ZO’s scaling claim is not merely that a larger scalar can improve high-rank LoRA. The claim is that when a two-point ZO oracle perturbs only one rank-
1
atom, the inherited joint coefficient
\alpha/r
multiplies the clean finite-difference numerator while the residual evaluation noise is unchanged. This makes the active finite-difference signal-to-noise ratio (FD-SNR) decay as
1/r^{2}
. The correction
\gamma=\alpha r
is therefore a topology-aware measurement calibration: it assigns the active atom a rank-invariant observable signal while keeping the remaining atoms fixed across the paired evaluations.

Appendix C
Theoretical Proofs

C.1
Preliminaries and Assumptions

Before presenting the proofs, we formalize the necessary mathematical
assumptions and standard properties of the zeroth-order oracle.

Assumption C.1

(
L
-smoothness)
.

The objective function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is differentiable and its
gradient is
L
-Lipschitz. For any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
, there exists a
constant
L>0
such that:

\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|\leq L\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|.

(8)

This yields the standard quadratic upper bound:

\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta),\,\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}.

(9)

Assumption C.2

(Bounded Evaluation Noise)
.

In practice, we only have access to a stochastic realization of the
loss function. Paired evaluations may share the same mini-batch or
other common random numbers; after conditioning on this shared
information, we model the remaining oracle error as

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\xi
,
where
\xi
is an independent zero-mean residual satisfying:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi\right]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi^{2}\right]=\sigma_{\xi}^{2}.

(10)

The residual errors in two evaluations

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta+\mu\bm{z})
and

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta-\mu\bm{z})
are modeled as independent
realizations
\xi_{+}
and
\xi_{-}
.

Assumption C.3

(Active-atom gradient regularity)
.

Along the analyzed iterates, the unscaled active factor-coordinate
gradient

\bm{g}_{t}\;\triangleq\;\begin{bmatrix}\bm{G}\,\bm{a}_{k(t)}\\[2.0pt]
\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k(t)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\qquad q=d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}},

satisfies
\|\bm{g}_{t}\|=\Theta(1)
in the LoRA rank
r
; that is,

\|\bm{g}_{t}\|
admits upper and lower bounds that depend only on the
dense gradient
\bm{G}
and on the smoothness/Lipschitz constants of

\mathcal{L}
, not on
r
. We further assume the queried atom has
bounded factor norms,

\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|,\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|=\Theta(1)
, the standard local
regularity used to interpret the active alignment
\beta
in
Theorem
4.7
. Both conditions are

local
statements about the queried atom
k(t)
and do not
constrain the magnitudes of the remaining
r-1
stored atoms. The
upper-bound half
\|\bm{g}_{t}\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
alone suffices for the
convergence bound in Theorem
4.4
Part II; the
full
\Theta(1)
regularity is used by Part I to make

\mathsf{SNR}_{t}
rank-invariant in a precise rate sense.

Assumption C.4

(Stable Represented Adapter Scale)
.

The represented adapter remains in a bounded forward-scale regime
along the analyzed iterates:

\left\|\frac{\gamma}{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(1).

For AR1-ZO, where
\gamma/r=\alpha
, this is equivalent to

\bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{r}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}\bigr\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The
assumption rules out interpreting the scaling law as an uncontrolled
increase of the entire adapter output; the active FD-SNR algebra
itself only depends on the active coefficient
\gamma/r
.

Relation to Assumption
C.3
.

Assumption
C.4
concerns the represented adapter

\|\Delta\bm{W}\|_{F}
, a sum over all
r
stored atoms, while
Assumption
C.3
concerns the queried atom only (its
active gradient norm and its factor norms). The two statements
constrain different physical objects—one global summed, one local
active—and are used in disjoint segments of the proofs. In
particular, with per-atom factor norms
\Theta(1)
on the queried
atom, generic incoherent configurations of the remaining stored atoms
would give
\bigl\|\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}\bigr\|_{F}=\Theta(\sqrt{r})
, so
Assumption
C.4
is
not
an incoherence
condition: it is a spectrum-control condition on the trained adapter,
requiring
\bm{B}\bm{A}
to remain in a regime where a small number of
singular components dominate and the Frobenius norm of the sum stays

\mathcal{O}(1)
in
r
. This regime is observed empirically in
trained LoRA, where the effective rank of
\bm{B}\bm{A}
is well below the
nominal
r
; it is a property of the optimization trajectory rather
than a geometric prior on the atoms, and it imposes no constraint on
the active-atom regularity asserted by
Assumption
C.3
.

C.2
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma C.5

(Active-Atom Gradient under Factored Parameterization)
.

Consider the factored update

\Delta\bm{W}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
,
where
\bm{G}=\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}

is the dense matrix gradient. The active-atom gradient with
respect to atom
k
is, writing
\bm{b}
and
\bm{a}
in gradient
subscripts for the active factors
\bm{b}_{k}
and
\bm{a}_{k}
:

\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k},\qquad\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}.

(11)

Proof.

By the chain rule, since

\bm{W}=\bm{W}_{0}+\frac{\gamma}{r}\sum_{j}\bm{b}_{j}\bm{a}_{j}^{\top}
:

\frac{\partial\bm{W}}{\partial[\bm{b}]_{i}}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{e}_{i}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}.

Therefore:

[\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}]_{i}=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\bm{G}^{\top}\cdot\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{e}_{i}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,[\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}]_{i}.

Stacking over all
i
yields

\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}
.
The derivation for
\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}
is analogous.
∎

Lemma C.6

(Projected ZO-RGE moment bounds)
.

Let
d
denote the full LoRA coordinate dimension for the adapted
matrix, and let
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
be the
orthogonal basis spanning atom
k
’s active coordinates,
with
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{P}=\bm{I}_{q}
and

q=d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}
, and let

\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{q})
. The two-point
Randomized Gradient Estimator (RGE):

\hat{\bm{g}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta+\mu\bm{P}\bm{z})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta-\mu\bm{P}\bm{z})}{2\mu}\bm{z}

satisfies the following standard projected two-point moment bounds
under the smoothness conditions needed for the central finite-difference
remainder:

1.

Bias Bound:

\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},\xi}\!\left[\hat{\bm{g}}\right]-\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|=\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}L\,q)
.

2.

Second Moment Bound:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},\xi}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}\|^{2}\right]\leq(q+3)\,\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+C_{1}\mu^{2}L^{2}q^{3}+\frac{C_{2}\,q\,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}
, for universal constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.
The leading coefficient
(q+3)
is a clean upper
bound that absorbs signal–bias cross terms arising from
the
\bm{z}
-dependent Hessian remainder; the exact signal-only
second moment is
(q+2)\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
C.6
follows from standard
zeroth-order optimization literature

[
12
,
9
]
.

C.3
Proof of Lemma
4.1
: Complete Rank-
1
Atom Blocks

Proof.

For the atom map
\phi_{k}(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})=\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
, the
Fréchet derivative in direction
(\delta\bm{b},\delta\bm{a})
is

D\phi_{k}(\delta\bm{b},\delta\bm{a})=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}(\bm{b}_{k}+\epsilon\delta\bm{b})(\bm{a}_{k}+\epsilon\delta\bm{a})^{\top}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\delta\bm{b}\,\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}+\bm{b}_{k}\,\delta\bm{a}^{\top}.

If only
\bm{b}_{k}
is perturbed, the attainable tangent matrices have the
form
\delta\bm{b}\,\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}
and keep the right factor fixed at

\bm{a}_{k}
. If only
\bm{a}_{k}
is perturbed, they have the form

\bm{b}_{k}\,\delta\bm{a}^{\top}
and keep the left factor fixed at
\bm{b}_{k}
.
When both factors are nonzero, each one-sided family is a strict
subset of the full factor-coordinate tangent generated by

D\phi_{k}(\delta\bm{b},\delta\bm{a})
. Therefore a complete atom-local
LoRA-native factor-coordinate perturbation includes the matched pair

(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})
.

The stored coordinates of this matched pair have dimension

d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}
. The rank-
1
matrix manifold itself has one scaling
gauge redundancy, since

(\bm{b},\bm{a})
and
(c\bm{b},\bm{a}/c)
represent the same matrix for

c\neq 0
, so its intrinsic dimension is
d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}-1
. AR1-ZO,
however, queries the stored LoRA factors through a black-box oracle,
not a quotient manifold coordinate chart; the factor-coordinate query
therefore perturbs
d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}
variables. A complete block of
m

matched atoms is the direct product of
m
such blocks and has query
dimension
m(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
.
∎

C.4
Proof of Proposition
4.2
: Active-Atom ZO Dimension

Proof.

Fix iteration
t
and write
k=k(t)
. Let
\bm{P}_{t}
inject an active
perturbation
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
into the
factor coordinates of atom
k
, and define the proof-local
active-block Gaussian smoothing

\mathcal{L}_{\mu,k(t)}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta+\mu\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})\right]
.
The standard Gaussian smoothing identity for the two-point randomized
gradient estimator gives

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},\xi}\!\left[\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta+\mu\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta-\mu\bm{P}_{t}\bm{z})}{2\mu}\bm{z}\right]=\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,k(t)}(\theta),

because the residual oracle noise is zero mean after conditioning on
the paired query protocol.

Lemma
C.6
applies with active dimension

q=d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}
. Combining its second-moment and bias bounds gives
the usual mean-squared error form

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\nabla}_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}-\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}\right]\lesssim q\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}+L^{2}\mu^{2}q^{3}+\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}q}{\mu^{2}},

up to universal constants. The dimension appearing in every term is
the dimension of the sampled vector
\bm{z}
,
namely
q
. If instead
one perturbs all
r
LoRA atoms of the same matrix at once, the same
standard ZO bound is obtained with
q
replaced by
rq
.
∎

C.5
Proof of Theorem
4.3
: Directional Collapse under Naive Scaling

Theorem
4.3
(Restated).

Under Assumptions
C.1
,

C.2
,
C.3
and naive
\gamma=\alpha
,
the active signal is
\Theta(r^{-1})
and active FD-SNR

\mathsf{SNR}_{t}=\Theta(r^{-2})
. With

r_{c}=\Theta(\alpha\mu\|\bm{g}_{t}\|/\sigma_{\xi})=\Theta(\alpha\mu/\sigma_{\xi})
,

r\gg r_{c}
makes the expected cosine with the active gradient vanish.

Proof.

Let
\theta
denote the full parameter vector. At step
t
, we
freeze all parameters except atom
k
, the coordinate pair

(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})
with dimension
q=d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}
.

Step 1: Signal Magnitude Decay.

Under the naive scaling
\gamma=\alpha
,
Lemma
C.5
gives:

\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k},\qquad\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}.

(12)

Define the unscaled active factor-coordinate gradient:

\bm{g}_{k}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\\
\ \bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{q}.

(13)

Then at rank
r
:

\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\bm{g}_{k},\qquad\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\|\bm{g}_{k}\|.

(14)

By Assumption
C.3
,
\|\bm{g}_{k}\|=\Theta(1)
in
r
, so
the signal magnitude decays as
\Theta(1/r)
rather than merely as

\mathcal{O}(1/r)\cdot\|\bm{g}_{k}\|
with a possibly
r
-dependent
prefactor.
\square

Step 2: Finite-Difference Signal–Noise Decomposition.

The two-point FD observation along

\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{q})
is decomposed via
Taylor expansion:

\Delta_{\mu}\;=\;\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta+\mu\bm{P}\bm{z})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta-\mu\bm{P}\bm{z})}{2\mu}\;=\;\underbrace{\bm{z}^{\top}\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}}_{\text{signal}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2})}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{\frac{\xi_{+}-\xi_{-}}{2\mu}}_{\text{noise }\zeta}.

(15)

The full ZO estimator is then

\hat{\bm{g}}=\Delta_{\mu}\cdot\bm{z}=(\bm{z}^{\top}\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L})\,\bm{z}+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2})\,\bm{z}+\zeta\,\bm{z}
.

Signal power:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\bm{z}^{\top}\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L})^{2}\right]=\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{r^{2}}.

(16)

Residual noise power
(independent of
r
):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\zeta^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}.

(17)

\square

Step 3: Active FD-SNR and Critical Rank.

We define the active FD-SNR as the ratio of
signal power to residual noise power:

\mathsf{SNR}(r)=\frac{\alpha^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}/r^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}/(2\mu^{2})}=\frac{2\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{r^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}.

(18)

This decays as
\mathcal{O}(1/r^{2})
. Setting

\mathsf{SNR}=1
and solving for
r
:

r_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\alpha\mu\|\bm{g}_{k}\|}{\sigma_{\xi}}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{\alpha\mu\|\bm{g}_{k}\|}{\sigma_{\xi}}\right).

(19)

Under Assumption
C.3
,
\|\bm{g}_{k}\|=\Theta(1)
, so

r_{c}=\Theta(\alpha\mu/\sigma_{\xi})
depends only on the smoothing
radius, the active LoRA scale, and the residual evaluation noise.

\square

Step 4: Cosine Similarity Collapse.

From the decomposition in (
15
), the ZO estimator
is
\hat{\bm{g}}=(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{g})\,\bm{z}+\zeta\,\bm{z}

(dropping the
\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2})
bias under the standard
assumption that the smoothing parameter
\mu
is chosen
sufficiently small, e.g.,

\mu=\mathcal{O}(q^{-1/2})
, such that the
finite-difference bias is strictly dominated by the stochastic
noise
\zeta
),
where
\bm{g}=\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}
.

We analyze
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cos(\hat{\bm{g}},\,\bm{g})\right]
by
computing the numerator and denominator separately.

Numerator:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\bm{g}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{g})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\zeta\,(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{g})\right]=\|\bm{g}\|^{2}.

(20)

Here,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\zeta\,(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{g})\right]=0
since

\zeta
and
\bm{z}
are independent.

Denominator:

Since the bias has been dropped, the second moment of the
bias-free estimator
\hat{\bm{g}}=(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{g})\bm{z}+\zeta\bm{z}
uses the exact signal coefficient

(q+2)
, and the smoothness remainder

C_{1}\mu^{2}L^{2}q^{3}
from
Lemma
C.6
is likewise negligible under

\mu=\mathcal{O}(q^{-1/2})
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}\|^{2}\right]=(q+2)\|\bm{g}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}q}{2\mu^{2}}.

(21)

By the concentration of measure in high dimensions and standard
zeroth-order bounds (cf.
[
12
]
, Lemma 3),
the random variable
\|\hat{\bm{g}}\|^{2}
tightly concentrates around
its expectation. Thus, the expected cosine similarity can be
approximated as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cos(\hat{\bm{g}},\,\bm{g})\right]

\displaystyle\asymp\;\frac{\|\bm{g}\|^{2}}{\|\bm{g}\|\,\sqrt{(q+2)\,\|\bm{g}\|^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q/(2\mu^{2})}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\|\bm{g}\|}{\sqrt{(q+2)\,\|\bm{g}\|^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q/(2\mu^{2})}}.

Substituting
\|\bm{g}\|=\alpha\|\bm{g}_{k}\|/r
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cos(\hat{\bm{g}}_{\text{naive}},\,\bm{g})\right]\;\asymp\;\frac{\alpha\|\bm{g}_{k}\|/r}{\sqrt{(q+2)\,\alpha^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}/r^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q/(2\mu^{2})}}.

(23)

When
r\gg r_{c}
, the residual noise term dominates the denominator:

\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{2\mu^{2}}\;\gg\;\frac{(q+2)\,\alpha^{2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{r^{2}},

(24)

so the denominator

\approx\sqrt{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q/(2\mu^{2})}
,
and:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cos(\hat{\bm{g}}_{\text{naive}},\,\bm{g})\right]\;\approx\;\frac{\alpha\|\bm{g}_{k}\|/r}{\sigma_{\xi}\,\sqrt{q/(2\mu^{2})}}\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\alpha\mu\,\|\bm{g}_{k}\|}{r\,\sigma_{\xi}\,\sqrt{q}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{r_{c}}{r\,\sqrt{q}}\right)\;\to\;0.

(25)

This establishes
directional collapse
: the ZO estimate
loses all correlation with the true gradient direction. The
inverse dependence on both
r
and
\sqrt{q}

implies that in high-dimensional embedding spaces, even moderate
ranks can trigger collapse when
\sigma_{\xi}
is
non-vanishing.
∎

Remark C.7
.

This collapse is qualitatively different from slow convergence.
In first-order optimization, scaling the gradient by
1/r
merely
reduces step size while preserving direction. In ZO, once the
signal falls below the noise floor, the
direction itself

is destroyed. No learning rate adjustment can repair a
noise-dominated direction estimate.

\|\bm{g}_{t}\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
: the represented-adapter bound

\|(\gamma/r)\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(1)
controls a sum
over all atoms with gauge freedom
(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})\mapsto(c\bm{b}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}/c)
,
while
\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{G}\|_{2}^{2}(\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|^{2})

also depends on the dense gradient
\bm{G}
and on the active atom’s
individual factor norms. The two assumptions are therefore
complementary and both used in Part II.

Step 5: Absorbing the cross-term via
Young’s inequality.

The cross-term in (
34
) is handled by
Young’s inequality (
ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{4}+b^{2}
):

\eta_{t}\,C_{b}\,\mu^{2}L\,q\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|\leq\frac{\eta_{t}}{4}\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\eta_{t}\,C_{b}^{2}\,\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}.

(36)

Substituting into (
34
):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})\right]\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\frac{3\eta_{t}}{4}\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\eta_{t}\,C_{b}^{2}\,\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\|^{2}\right].

(37)

Step 6: Learning rate choice.

Choose a constant learning rate

\eta_{t}=\eta=\frac{c}{\sqrt{T}}

for a constant
c>0
. For sufficiently large
T

(specifically,
T\geq c^{2}\cdot 4L^{2}(q+3)^{2}
),
this satisfies:

\frac{\eta L}{2}(q+3)\leq\frac{1}{4},\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L(q+3)}.

(38)

This ensures that the

\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}(q+3)\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}

term from (
35
) can be absorbed
into the leading descent term.

Specifically, substituting (
35
)
into (
37
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\frac{3\eta}{4}\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}(q+3)\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\eta\,C_{b}^{2}\,\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\!\left(C_{1}\,\mu^{2}L^{2}\,q^{3}+\frac{C_{2}\,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{2\mu^{2}}\right).

(39)

Applying the learning rate constraint

\frac{\eta L}{2}(q+3)\leq\frac{1}{4}
:

-\frac{3\eta}{4}\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}=-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}.

(40)

This simplifies to:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})\right]\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\frac{\eta}{2}\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\eta\,\Phi,

(41)

where
\Phi
collects all terms independent of

\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}
:

\Phi\;=\;C_{b}^{2}\,\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}+\frac{\eta L}{2}\!\left(C_{1}\,\mu^{2}L^{2}\,q^{3}+\frac{C_{2}\,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{2\mu^{2}}\right).

(42)

Step 7: Telescoping sum.

Rearranging (
41
) and summing from

t=0
to
T-1
:

\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*}+T\eta\,\Phi.

(43)

Dividing both sides by
T\eta/2
:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2(\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*})}{T\eta}+2\Phi.

(44)

Step 8: Final bound.

Substituting
\eta=c/\sqrt{T}

into (
44
):

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\underbrace{\frac{2(\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*})}{c\sqrt{T}}}_{\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})}+2\Phi.

(45)

Expanding
\Phi
from (
42
) with

\eta=c/\sqrt{T}
:

\displaystyle 2\Phi

\displaystyle=2C_{b}^{2}\,\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}+\frac{cL}{\sqrt{T}}\!\left(C_{1}\,\mu^{2}L^{2}\,q^{3}+\frac{C_{2}\,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{2\mu^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2})}_{\text{smoothing bias (persistent)}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\mu^{2}L^{3}\,q^{3}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)}_{\text{smoothing--variance cross-term}\;\to\;0}+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{T^{1/2}\mu^{2}}\right)}_{\text{residual noise}\;\to\;0}.

(46)

For fixed
\mu,L,q,\sigma_{\xi}
,
the middle cross-term is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
and is
therefore absorbed into the leading

\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
optimization term.
The final convergence bound is:

\boxed{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q}{T^{1/2}\mu^{2}}\right).}

(47)

Consequences.

1.

The bound depends on

q=d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}

but
not on the total rank

r
, confirming
that topology-aware scaling removes rank-induced signal
degradation.

2.

The persistent bias

\mathcal{O}(\mu^{4}L^{2}q^{2})
is
controlled by the smoothing parameter
\mu
.
Choosing
\mu=\mathcal{O}(q^{-1/2})

makes this
\mathcal{O}(L^{2}/q^{0})=\mathcal{O}(L^{2})
.

3.

The residual noise term

\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,q/(T^{1/2}\mu^{2}))

vanishes as
T\to\infty
.

4.

As
T\to\infty
and
\mu\to 0
at an
appropriate rate, the bound reduces to the standard
non-convex ZO convergence rate

\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
.

∎

C.7
Proof of Corollary
4.5
: Coverage Cost for Full-Adapter Stationarity

Proof.

The atom factor-coordinate blocks are disjoint in the coordinates of

(\bm{B},\bm{A})
, so full-adapter stationarity is measured by the aggregate
quantity

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}
already used in
the statement.
If
k(t)
is sampled uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,r\}
, taking
expectation over the active atom gives

\mathbb{E}_{k(t)}\!\left[\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}.

For a deterministic cyclic schedule, the same identity holds after
averaging over one full cycle. Thus full-adapter stationarity
requires atom coverage, but this coverage factor is separate from the
single-query ZO estimator dimension.
∎

Remark C.8

(Rank and coverage)
.

Theorems
4.3
and
4.4

form a complete theoretical picture:

•

Theorem
4.3
shows that
under naive scaling, the ZO optimization quality

collapses
as rank increases.

•

Theorem
4.4
shows that
topology-aware scaling
\gamma=\alpha r

restores

the signal to a rank-invariant level, yielding a
finite-difference measurement whose active FD-SNR no longer decays with rank.

•

The convergence part of Theorem
4.4

places the restored signal inside a projected-gradient bound governed
by the active dimension, the smoothing parameter
\mu
, and the noise
level
\sigma_{\xi}
.

Consequently, increasing rank no longer directly degrades the
active finite-difference measurement once the atom is queried.
Full-adapter stationarity still depends on the atom schedule and
coverage over a cycle, which is why the main text states the gain as a
per-query and per-cycle estimator-budget improvement rather than a
worst-case global convergence dominance claim.

C.8
Proof of Corollary
4.6
: Rank-
m
Blocks and Other Factorizations

The corollary uses
\alpha
as the target coefficient for the active
rank-
m
block. Under this block-level convention, the active block
contains
m
atoms and the compensation rule becomes

\gamma=\alpha r/m
. The AR1-ZO specialization corresponds to

m=1
, which recovers
\gamma=\alpha r
.

Derivation.

Step 1: Block-
m
forward pass.

Consider the factored update partitioned into

\lceil r/m\rceil
blocks, each containing
m
rank-
1

atoms. The full update to the weight matrix is:

\Delta\bm{W}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}.

(48)

At step
t
, let
B_{t}
be the active block of
m
atoms.
Only the atoms in
B_{t}
are perturbed and updated. The
effective contribution of the active block to the
forward pass is:

\Delta\bm{W}_{\text{active}}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\sum_{k\in B_{t}}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{a}_{k}^{\top}.

(49)

Step 2: Explicit block gradient via Lemma 1.

By applying Lemma
C.5
to each atom

k\in B_{t}
, the active-block gradient for
the entire block is the concatenation:

\nabla_{B_{t}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bigoplus_{k\in B_{t}}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\\
\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

Its squared norm is:

\|\nabla_{B_{t}}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}=\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{\!2}\sum_{k\in B_{t}}\big(\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\big).

(51)

The summation term depends only on
\bm{G}
and the atoms
in
B_{t}
; all dependence on
\gamma
and
r

is isolated in the prefactor
(\gamma/r)^{2}
.

Step 3: Signal restoration condition.

The key observation is that each active atom contributes
to the forward pass with coefficient
\gamma/r
. Under
naive scaling (
\gamma=\alpha
), the per-atom
coefficient is
\alpha/r
, so the block of
m
atoms
has total effective coefficient
m\cdot\alpha/r
.
In a joint update of all
r
atoms simultaneously
(the full-rank reference), the total effective
coefficient is
r\cdot\alpha/r=\alpha
.

To match the full-rank reference signal, we require
the block’s total effective coefficient to equal

\alpha
:

\frac{\gamma}{r}\,m=\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\gamma=\frac{\alpha r}{m}.

(52)

Step 4: Verification.

Substituting
\gamma=\alpha r/m
into the
explicit formula from Step 2:

\|\nabla_{B_{t}}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}=\left(\frac{\alpha r/m}{r}\right)^{\!2}\sum_{k\in B_{t}}\big(\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\big)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{m^{2}}\sum_{k\in B_{t}}\big(\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\big),

(53)

which is independent of the ambient LoRA rank
r
for a fixed block
size
m
. Equivalently, the total active block coefficient is

(\gamma/r)m=\alpha
, so the block-level finite-difference signal does
not decay as the adapter rank grows. Setting
m=1
gives the
rank-
1
AR1-ZO rule
\gamma=\alpha r
.
∎

Remark C.9

(Scope of tensorized extensions)
.

The same compensation logic applies to CP, Tucker, or other
multilinear adapters once the joint-normalized parameterization and
the sequential active block are specified. Adapter-specific proofs
instantiate the corresponding complete block, tangent space,
normalization, and active scale, but the topology-scaling principle
is the same.

C.9
Proof of Theorem
4.7
:
Conditional Progress Advantage of Aligned Rank-
1

Atoms

We first state the additional assumptions required
beyond Assumptions
C.1
–
C.3
.

Assumption C.10

(Spectral Concentration)
.

The dense matrix gradient

\bm{G}=\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}

has SVD
\bm{G}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}

with spectral concentration parameter

\rho=\sigma_{1}^{2}/\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\in(0,1)
.

Assumption C.11

(Atom Alignment)
.

The active rank-
1
atom
(\bm{b},\bm{a})

has alignment

\beta=\cos^{2}(\bm{b},\bm{u}_{1})\,\cos^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{v}_{1})>0

with the top singular pair

(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{v}_{1})
of
\bm{G}
.

Theorem
4.7

(Restated).

Under
Assumptions
C.1
,
C.2
,

C.3
,
C.10
,
C.11
,
the one-step expected loss decrease admits the active-energy bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal{L}\right]\;\gtrsim\;\eta\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{\!2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}\right]-\eta\,\mathcal{O}(\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}),

(54)

and the spectral lower bound

\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}\;\geq\;\rho\beta\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\bigl(\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\bigr).

(55)

Assumption
C.3
guarantees

\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|^{2}=\Theta(1)
on the queried atom,
so the active query carries a
\rho\beta
-scaled fraction of the
dense gradient energy at active dimension
q
.

Proof.

Step 1: Active-atom gradient via SVD
decomposition.

By Lemma
C.5
, the active-atom
gradient for atom
k
is:

\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k},\qquad\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\gamma}{r}\,\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}.

(56)

Expanding
\bm{G}
via its SVD

\bm{G}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\,\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
:

\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{a}_{k})\bm{u}_{i},\qquad\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k})\bm{v}_{i}.

(57)

Step 2: Gradient energy decomposition.

The squared norms of the individual gradients are:

\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{a}_{k})^{2},\qquad\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k})^{2},

(58)

where we used the orthonormality of

\{\bm{u}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{v}_{i}\}
,
respectively.

The total active-atom gradient energy is:

\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}=\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{\!2}\big(\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\big).

(59)

Step 3: Lower bound via spectral
concentration and alignment.

We lower-bound each term using
Assumptions
C.10

and
C.11
.

For the first term, retaining only the
i=1

contribution:

\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{a}_{k})^{2}\geq\sigma_{1}^{2}(\bm{v}_{1}^{\top}\bm{a}_{k})^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{v}_{1})\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}.

(60)

(The last equality uses

(\bm{v}_{1}^{\top}\bm{a}_{k})^{2}=\cos^{2}(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{v}_{1})\,\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}

for general (non-unit)
\bm{a}_{k}
. The factor norms

\|\bm{a}_{k}\|,\|\bm{b}_{k}\|
are kept explicit; under
Assumption
C.3
they are
\Theta(1)
on the queried
atom, so the lower bound has the form
\Theta(1)\cdot\rho\beta\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}

in
r
.)

By Assumption
C.10
,

\sigma_{1}^{2}\geq\rho\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}
,
so:

\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}\geq\rho\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\cos^{2}(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{v}_{1})\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}.

(61)

Similarly:

\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\geq\rho\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\cos^{2}(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{u}_{1})\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}.

(62)

Define

c_{a}=\cos^{2}(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{v}_{1})
,

c_{b}=\cos^{2}(\bm{b}_{k},\bm{u}_{1})
,
and
\beta=c_{a}\cdot c_{b}

(Assumption
C.11
).
Combining:

\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\geq\rho\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}(c_{a}\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+c_{b}\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}).

(63)

Since
\beta=c_{a}c_{b}
and
c_{a},c_{b}\in[0,1]
, we have

\beta\leq c_{a}
and
\beta\leq c_{b}
. Hence

\|\bm{G}\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\geq\rho\beta\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\left(\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\right).

(64)

Substituting into (
59
):

\|\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}\gtrsim\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{\!2}\rho\beta\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\left(\|\bm{a}_{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k}\|^{2}\right).

(65)

Step 4: Per-step descent via the Descent
Lemma.

By the descent inequality established in the proof
of Theorem
4.4

(eq. (
32
)), the expected loss
decrease satisfies:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal{L}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})\right]\geq\eta\,\langle\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L},\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\right]\rangle-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\|^{2}\right].

(66)

From the bias bound in
Lemma
C.6
:

\langle\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L},\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\right]\rangle=\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\langle\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L},\,b_{t}\rangle\geq\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}-\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|\,\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}L\,q).

(67)

Therefore:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal{L}\right]\geq\eta\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}-\eta\,\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}L\,q)\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\|^{2}\right].

(68)

We handle the cross-term using Young’s inequality
(
ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{4\epsilon}+\epsilon b^{2}

with
\epsilon=1
):

\eta C_{b}\mu^{2}Lq\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|\leq\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}+\eta C_{b}^{2}\mu^{4}L^{2}q^{2}.

(69)

Absorbing the
\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla\|^{2}
term
into the leading descent:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal{L}\right]\geq\frac{3\eta}{4}\,\|\nabla_{k(t)}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}-\eta\,C_{b}^{2}\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\|^{2}\right].

(70)

Substituting the lower
bound (
65
) into the leading
term:

\boxed{\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal{L}\right]\gtrsim\eta\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}\right)^{\!2}\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}\|^{2}\right]-\eta\,\mathcal{O}(\mu^{4}L^{2}\,q^{2}).}

(71)

The lower bound on
\|\bm{g}_{k}\|^{2}
from
Step 3 gives the sufficient spectral-alignment version stated in
the main text.
∎

Remark C.12

(Comparison with generic random blocks)
.

For a

coordinate block
B
of the same
dimension

,
a standard random-block averaging calculation gives:

\mathbb{E}_{B}\!\left[\|\nabla_{B}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}\right]=\frac{q}{d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}}\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}.

(72)

The advantage ratio of the aligned rank-

\frac{\rho\beta\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}\,(\|\bm{a}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}\|^{2})}{\frac{q}{d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}}\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}}=\rho\beta\,\frac{d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}}}\,(\|\bm{a}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}\|^{2}).

(active factor norms

\frac{\rho\beta\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}(\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|^{2})}{(q/(d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}))\,\|\bm{G}\|_{F}^{2}}=\rho\beta\,\frac{d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}}{q}\,(\|\bm{a}_{k(t)}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}_{k(t)}\|^{2})=\Theta(\rho\beta\cdot d).

,

,

.
