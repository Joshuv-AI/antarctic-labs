Dynamic sparsity in tree-structured feed-forward layers at scale
Reza Sedghi, Robin Schiewer, Anand Subramoney, David Kappel

Abstract
At typical context lengths, the feed-forward MLP block accounts for a large share of a transformer’s compute budget, motivating sparse alternatives to dense MLP blocks. We study sparse, tree-structured feed-forward layers as drop-in replacements for MLP blocks in deep transformer architectures, enabling conditional computation via hard hierarchical routing without a separate router network. We demonstrate for the first time that this form of tree-structured conditional sparsity can be applied for autoregressive language modeling and downstream question answering, including zero- and few-shot settings, and its scalability beyond 1B parameters. Despite activating fewer than 5% of the feed-forward block’s units per token, our models match dense baselines under controlled training and fine-tuning protocols. We further analyze training dynamics and identify an emergent auto-pruning effect: the interaction of hard routing with asymmetric nonlinearities progressively deactivates unused paths, yielding partial conversion of dynamic routing into static structural sparsity. We show that simple architectural choices can modulate this behavior and recover balanced trees without auxiliary losses. Overall, our work demonstrates that tree-structured feed-forward layers provide a scalable and controllable mechanism for sparsifying large transformer models.

Feed-forward (FF) or MLP blocks account for a dominant fraction of parameters and computation in modern transformer architectures, particularly in large language models (LLMs). As models scale to billions of parameters, the cost of dense FF blocks has become a primary bottleneck, motivating extensive research into sparse alternatives. Among these, mixture-of-experts (MoE) models have emerged as a prominent approach to exploit dynamic sparsity by activating only a subset of parameters per token
(
23
)
(see Fig.
1
a,b). However, practical MoE systems typically rely on learned routing networks, auxiliary load-balancing losses, and specialized training and inference infrastructure
(
21
;
14
;
11
;
35
;
15
)
.

Tree-structured conditional computation offers a different sparsification mechanism, that organizes computation hierarchically, such that each input activates a single root-to-leaf path in a binary tree. This induces
predictable and structured sparsity
: routing decisions at each level deterministically select a subtree, allowing entire paths to be skipped without a separate router module or expert aggregation. This enables high levels of sparsity with minimal architectural overhead. Fast Feedforward (FFF) layers
(
3
;
2
)
instantiated this idea by replacing dense FF blocks by a sequence of hard binary routing decisions, activating only a small number of nodes while all other parameters remain inactive. As a result, FFF layers can achieve levels of dynamic sparsity exceeding
99\%
. However, apart from encoder-style BERT models for classification with ca. 300M parameters
(
2
)
, the capabilities and limitations of tree-structured conditional computation remains largely unexplored compared to widely used MoEs.

In this work, we address these open questions through a systematic empirical and analytical study of FFF layers in GPT-style transformers. We evaluate models trained from scratch and via fine-tuning across a wide range of scales, including models with over a billion parameters, and assess performance on language modeling as well as zero- and few-shot downstream benchmarks. Across these settings, we show that FFF-based models closely match dense feed-forward baselines while operating in highly sparse computational regimes.
Beyond scalability and performance, we discover a new
emergent
auto-pruning
effect, in which the interaction between hard routing decisions and asymmetric nonlinearities progressively deactivates entire tree paths during training. This process partially converts dynamic, input-dependent routing into stable structural sparsity, yielding additional computational savings without explicit pruning criteria or auxiliary regularization. We further show that this behavior is not incidental but can be modulated through simple architectural choices.

Overall, our results demonstrate that tree-structured feed-forward layers constitute a practical and scalable alternative to dense FF blocks and MoE architectures.

Contributions.

This work provides the first systematic investigation of hard-routed tree-structured feed-forward layers in large transformer models. Our contributions are:
(i) we demonstrate, for the first time, that FFF layers scale to billion-parameter autoregressive language models while maintaining performance comparable to dense feed-forward baselines;
(ii) we identify and analyze an emergent auto-pruning mechanism that converts dynamic tree routing into stable structural sparsity during training;
(iii) we show that routing imbalance and tree utilization can be controlled through simple architectural choices, without auxiliary losses or explicit expert-balancing mechanisms; and
(iv) we provide the first layer-level runtime benchmarks that quantify its efficiency trade-offs.

Related Work

Sparse and conditional computation has been widely studied as a means to scale neural networks efficiently. Early work on hierarchical mixtures of experts (HMEs) introduced tree-structured gating mechanisms to enable conditional execution in deep models
(
20
)
. More recent variants explore hierarchical or dynamic expert routing within transformer architectures
(
34
;
35
)
. Differentiable decision-tree layers have been proposed as neural network components that combine tree ensembles with end-to-end training
(
16
)
, but these architectures are typically evaluated on vision or tabular benchmarks rather than large-scale transformer language models. In parallel, large-scale Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have demonstrated substantial reductions in per-token computation by activating only a small subset of experts for each input
(
21
;
14
)
. Recent work such as Mixture of a Million Experts (PEER) further pushes this paradigm by retrieving from extremely large pools of fine-grained experts, improving compute–performance trade-offs at scale
(
17
)
. While effective, these approaches typically rely on separate routing networks, expert balancing mechanisms
(
21
;
14
;
35
)
, and specialized infrastructure
(
21
;
15
)
.

Another line of research focuses on contextual and activation-based sparsity. Methods such as Deja Vu predict at inference time which attention heads or MLP components are likely to be active/inactive for a given input, enabling conditional execution without retraining the model
(
22
)
. Related approaches, including ShadowLLM, explore predictor-based mechanisms to dynamically prune attention and feed-forward computation
(
1
)
. These techniques are effective for inference-time acceleration but generally operate on pretrained dense models and do not introduce sparsity during training or alter the feed-forward architecture itself.

Sparsity has also been explored through pruning and mask-based techniques. Structured and unstructured pruning methods aim to remove redundant parameters either during or after training, often inspired by the lottery ticket hypothesis and related frameworks
(
18
)
. In addition, gated activation functions such as GLU and its variants (e.g., SwiGLU) have been widely adopted in transformer feed-forward blocks to improve expressivity and optimization stability
(
26
)
. While these approaches improve performance, they do not significantly reduce computation, as all neurons remain active for every input.

Tree-based neural architectures and differentiable decision-tree layers have also been proposed as learnable components within neural networks
(
28
)
. Such models combine tree structures with gradient-based optimization and have shown promising results in tabular and vision domains. Separately, transformer variants incorporating tree or syntactic structure as an inductive bias have been studied in representation learning and attention mechanisms
(
29
)
. However, these approaches are typically not designed as drop-in replacements for dense feed-forward blocks in large-scale language models.

Compared to these prior approaches, tree-based feed-forward layers occupy a distinct design point. Rather than introducing separate expert routers, inference-time predictors, or post-hoc pruning, Fast Feedforward (FFF) layers integrate routing and computation within a single tree-structured feed-forward module. This enables extreme, input-dependent sparsity during both training and inference, with predictable and structured activation patterns. Prior work on FFF layers has been limited to relatively small models and restricted settings, leaving open questions regarding scalability, training stability, and performance in modern GPT-style large language models.

This work addresses these gaps by providing a systematic study of tree-based feed-forward layers as drop-in replacements for dense MLP blocks in large transformer architectures, evaluating their scalability, efficiency, and training dynamics under realistic large-scale training regimes.

Fast Feedforward Layer

Node parameterization.

An FFF layer
(
2
)
consists of
P
trainable, perfect binary trees of depth
D
, which are evaluated in parallel (see Fig.
1
c).
The computation proceeds sequentially through each tree root-to-leaf.
Each node is indexed by a triple
(p,\ell,s)
, where
p\in\{1,\dots,P\}
denotes the tree,

\ell\in\{0,\dots,D\}
the level, and
s\in\{0,\dots,2^{\ell}-1\}
the position within the level.
Each node
(p,\ell,s)
is associated with two sets of trainable parameters:

•

Routing (linear_in):

a weight vector
w^{\mathrm{in}}_{p,\ell,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and bias

b^{\mathrm{in}}_{p,\ell,s}\in\mathbb{R}
, producing a single scalar logit;

•

Output (linear_out):

a weight vector
w^{\mathrm{out}}_{p,\ell,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
.

Forward computation.

For each input sample
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and each tree

p\in\{1,\dots,P\}
, traversal starts at the root
s_{0}=0
.
For
\ell=0,\dots,D
, exactly one node is evaluated:

z_{p,\ell}(x)=\left\langle x,\;w^{\mathrm{in}}_{p,\ell,s_{\ell}}\right\rangle+b^{\mathrm{in}}_{p,\ell,s_{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}.

(1)

For
\ell=0,\dots,D-1
, the next node index is updated via a hard routing decision:

s_{\ell+1}=2s_{\ell}+\mathbf{1}\!\left[\mathrm{sg}\!\left(z_{p,\ell}(x)\right)\geq 0\right],

(2)

where
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function and
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
denotes the stop-gradient operator.
Consequently, each tree requires a fixed number of
D
routing steps and the layer has
P\times D
active nodes in total.
All active nodes contribute directly to the output, i.e. nodes on levels
0,\dots,D-1
have a double role of routing and output generation.

Sparse output computation.

The output
y=\mathrm{FFF}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
of the layer is obtained by summing the contributions of the visited nodes:

y=b^{\mathrm{out}}+\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{\ell=0}^{D}\mathrm{GELU}\!\left(z_{p,\ell}(x)\right)\;w^{\mathrm{out}}_{p,\ell,s_{\ell}}\;.

(3)

Tree Utilization and Routing Imbalance

Expert utilization and routing balance are long-standing challenges in conditional computation models, particularly in mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures.
Since the variant of FFF layers used here used no balancing mechanisms, we investigated whether similar utilization issues arise and how they manifest in practice.

Figure 3
:
Utilization in a d=5 tree.

Path utilization as the normalized fraction of samples visiting each path for FFF (left) and FFF
{}^{\text{post}}
(right). The x-axis denotes the leaf node index.

We measured tree utilization by recording how frequently each path (or subtree) is visited during training and inference. Across different model sizes, tree depths, datasets, and tasks, we observe a consistent pattern of severe imbalance in tree utilization (Fig.
3
). The distribution of branch visits is highly skewed: the leftmost path from the root is selected in more than
50\%
of cases, while the left child of the right subtree accounts for over
15\%
of visits. The remaining paths are activated only rarely (although we found only 1% ’dead’ leaf nodes, that were never visited, for
d=3
trees, 5% for
d=5
trees and 20% for
d=7
trees). This behavior mimics a heavy-tailed distribution over path utilization (see Appendix
I
for a discussion) and mirrors expert collapse observed in MoE models trained without balancing losses.

5.1
Architectural biases for balance / imbalance

In MoEs imbalance typically arises from learned routers, and can be accounted for by auxiliary balancing losses. However, since FFF has no dedictaed routing structure, the observed imbalance must stem from the interaction between the tree structure, the nonlinearity, and gradient-based training of the surrounding linear layers.
In Appendix
G
we show through a detailed empirical and analytical investigation that this is in fact the case: the combination of hard binary routing and the GELU nonlinearity introduces an asymmetric gradient flow, where updates associated with negative node logits are strongly suppressed, while positive logits dominate the embedding space. This induces a systematic drift in node logits that biases routing decisions, to systemically prefer the left child (the child with lower index) over the right child, in every node.

Interestingly, our analysis also reveals that a simple change in architecture can fix this imbalance. This alternative design applies the nonlinearity after the sparse output accumulation:

\mathrm{FFF}^{\mathrm{post}}(x)=\mathrm{GELU}\!\left(b^{\mathrm{out}}+\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{\ell=0}^{D}z_{p,\ell}(x)\;w^{\mathrm{out}}_{p,\ell,s_{\ell}}\right).

By adopting this post-GELU formulation, the gradient magnitude no longer directly depends on individual node logits.
As a result, the sign-dependent gradient suppression responsible for imbalanced routing is no longer present. As shown analytically in Appendix
G
and empirically in Figure
3
, this modification leads to substantially more balanced tree utilization across paths. Notably, this balancing mechanism does not rely on auxiliary losses, explicit load constraints, or router regularization, while maintaining all computational advantages of FFF.

5.2
Utilizing Imbalance: Auto-Pruning in Tree-Based Feed-Forward Layers

When we evaluated the balanced FFF version, we found to our surprise that task performance did not depend strongly on how balanced the trees were. In fact, our results presented in Appendix
A
show that the balanced FFF version even slightly underperformed the imbalanced version. This suggests the the sparse training used for FFF automatically leads to a competition for resources between tree paths and a uniform distribution of resources may not be the ideal strategy. We therefore wondered whether the observed imbalance could serve a computational advantage. As optimization progresses, certain paths of the tree become effectively and permanently inactive, resulting in a form of static structural sparsity on top of the input-dependent routing mechanism.
This phenomenon manifests as a progressive concentration of computation on a subset of tree paths, while other paths receive diminishing gradient updates and eventually cease to contribute to the model output. Importantly, this auto-pruning behavior emerges automatically during training and arises from the interaction between hard tree routing decisions and the asymmetric nonlinearity of GELU, a combination that is specific to the proposed layer design.

Figure
4
illustrates the effect of path pruning on validation performance. We observe that, as training proceeds, models with increasing levels of permanent path deactivation can closely match the performance of their unpruned counterparts. This indicates that auto-pruning preserves the most informative computation paths while removing redundant ones. Overall, auto-pruning provides an additional source of efficiency by converting dynamic routing decisions into stable, data-dependent structural sparsity during training. While not the primary focus of this work, this effect helps explain the robustness and scalability of tree-based feed-forward layers observed in our experiments.
In Appendix
G
we further evaluate the auto-pruning mechanism for the OPT 125M model, showing that pruning can be an effective mechanism to further sparsity deeper FFF layers.

6
Scaling Experiments

We have now developed the necessary tools to address the core goal of this work: to evaluate the scalability and practicality of the proposed Fast Feedforward (FFF) layer as an alternative to standard feed-forward blocks in large transformer models, that maintains high levels of sparsity during inference and training.

6.1
Language Modeling Setup

Model architecture.

We adopt a GPT-style transformer architecture for autoregressive language modeling
(
33
)
. In all experiments, the attention blocks remain structurally unchanged. We replace the conventional MLP layer following each attention block with the proposed FFF layer, using a hardware-compatible masked implementation (see Appendix
F
).

Training data and objective.

Models are trained using the standard next-token prediction objective on a large-scale language modeling corpus. Given a sequence of tokens
(x_{1},\dots,x_{T})
, training minimizes the cross-entropy loss

\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(x_{t}\mid x_{<t}).

(4)

All models were trained with a ratio of ca.
20:1
for the number of training tokens to parameters, following
(
19
)
.

We consider two training regimes. In the first, models were trained entirely from scratch, with all parameters initialized randomly. In the second regime, which we refer to as
fine-tuning
(FT), attention layers and other trainable components were initialized from a pretrained dense model, while the feed-forward blocks were replaced by the proposed sparse routing layers, with random initial parameters. Although replacing even a single layer requires retraining the full model, this setup reflects a practical scenario in which a pretrained model is adapted by swapping in a sparse feed-forward component as a drop-in replacement.

To contextualize our results, we report performance for several pretrained reference models trained on substantially larger corpora. In particular, we include a pretrained OPT model trained on approximately 300B tokens
(
33
)
, as well as other publicly available models such as Pythia
(
4
)
and TinyLLaMA
(
32
)
, which are trained on roughly 1T and 2T tokens, respectively. These models are provided for reference only and should not be considered direct baselines, as their training budgets far exceed those used in our experiments.

Our primary baselines are dense transformer models trained from scratch using the same architecture, optimization pipeline, and number of training tokens as our sparse models. This allows for a controlled comparison that isolates the effect of replacing the feed-forward layers. We report results for two main model sizes: a 125M-parameter model evaluated on language modeling and text generation tasks (see Section
4
and Appendix
A
), and a 1.3B-parameter model evaluated on both language modeling and downstream benchmarks. Additional results for larger models, including 2.7B and 6.7B parameters, are provided in the appendix. Technical details about implementation, optimization, and hyperparameters can be found in Appendix
H
.

6.2
Language Modeling Results

Training dynamics.

We evaluated FFF with depths 4 and 6, corresponding to approximately 83% and 94% sparsity, respectively, on a 1.3B-parameter OPT model. Depth 6 is selected based on the benchmark results in Section
7
as a favorable trade-off between sparsity and runtime efficiency, while depth 4 provides a more conservative sparsity setting. In addition to training from scratch, we also report fine-tuning results for both configurations, where attention layers are initialized from a pretrained dense model. Across all settings, the models with FFF layer maintain stable training dynamics and closely follow the dense feed-forward baseline (see Appendix
B
), indicating that highly sparse tree-based routing layers can be trained reliably and integrated into large language models under both training regimes.

Evaluation on held-out data.

For quantitative evaluation, we report perplexity on a standard held-out language modeling benchmark. Perplexity
(
13
)
is defined as

\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log p(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\right).

(5)

As shown in Table
1
, FFF models at 1.3B parameter scales achieve perplexities comparable to their dense baselines. Performance differences are typically within
10\%
, indicating that tree-based sparsification does not substantially degrade language modeling quality.

6.3
Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Evaluation

To assess generalization beyond the training distribution, we evaluate models on a diverse suite of downstream language understanding tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. We report both zero-shot and few-shot accuracy across multiple benchmarks, to reduce the impact of task-specific variance or chance.

Tables
2
and
3
summarizes zero-shot and few-shot performance. Across most tasks, FFF closely matches or exceeds the performance of dense feed-forward baselines when trained or fine-tuned on the same amount of data. In several cases, the tree-based variants outperform the dense model despite operating in significantly higher sparsity regimes.

We additionally include a pretrained OPT model trained on approximately 300B tokens as a reference point. Due to its substantially larger training budget compared to our models (trained on roughly 26B tokens), this model performs better on some tasks that benefit from broader topic coverage — such as Obqa. Nevertheless, on multiple benchmarks (see e.g. ARC-e) all FFF layer configurations, including highly sparse variants, outperform the pretrained OPT model. Similar trends are observed across other tasks, where fine-tuned FFF models consistently achieve equal or higher accuracy than their dense counterparts.

Notably, results on MMLU (Table
3
), reported as an average across all subtasks, show that sparser FFF models can achieve higher accuracy than dense feed-forward baselines. These findings indicate that tree-based sparse routing not only scales effectively to large language models, but can also preserve, and in some cases improve, generalization and in-context learning performance, even under extreme sparsity and reduced training data.

We also evaluated the proposed sparse routing layer in additional settings to further assess its applicability. In particular, we applied FFF to a transformer-based object detection model (DETR), showing that the sparse layers match the performance of their dense counterparts and exhibit stable training behavior. Efficiency of Tree-Based Feed-Forward Layers

Efficiency in terms of both runtime and computational cost is a central motivation for introducing tree-based feed-forward layers in large-scale models. While the Fast Feedforward (FFF) layer enables high structured sparsity, it also introduces a sequential, depth-wise execution pattern that differs fundamentally from the fully parallel execution of dense feed-forward blocks. This raises an important practical question: can such a highly sparse but hierarchical layer be computationally competitive, or even advantageous, at scale?

To address this question, we benchmark the execution of FFF under its intended sparse computation model and compare it against a dense feed-forward baseline. Rather than focusing on kernel-level optimizations on current accelerators, our goal is to evaluate whether the substantial reduction in computation enabled by tree-based sparsity can outweigh the overhead of sequential traversal.

7.1
Runtime Speedup

We evaluate the runtime behavior of tree-based feed-forward layers under their sequential sparse execution model, in which only the nodes along the active traversal path are evaluated and all inactive paths incur no computation. Runtime is measured at the layer level and compared against a dense feed-forward baseline under identical conditions.

As shown in Fig.
5
and summarized in Table
5
, increasing tree depth leads to substantial runtime improvements despite the introduction of depth-wise sequential traversal. For configurations relevant to large language models, a tree depth of 6 achieves the highest speedup, with an average improvement of approximately
8.7\times
over dense feed-forward layers. While deeper trees further reduce computation, diminishing returns and increased traversal overhead limit additional gains.

These results identify depth 6 as a practical trade-off between extreme sparsity and efficient execution. We therefore adopt this configuration in most of our large-scale experiments, alongside shallower settings that prioritize reduced routing depth.

7.2
Sparsity and FLOPs Analysis

We characterize sparsity and computational cost at both the layer and model levels.
Feed-forward layer sparsity
measures the fraction of inactive nodes within the tree-based feed-forward layers and isolates the sparsity induced by the proposed architecture.
Effective model sparsity
captures the resulting reduction in computation across the full transformer, accounting for the fact that attention layers remain dense.

As summarized in Table
4
, sparsity grows exponentially with tree depth. Even shallow configurations achieve high sparsity (e.g., approximately 83% for
d=4
), while deeper trees reach extreme regimes, exceeding 94% sparsity at depth 6 and more than 99% at depth 13. This behavior follows directly from the hierarchical structure of the trees and the activation of a single path per input.

To complement the runtime measurements, we also report analytical estimates of per-token floating-point operations (FLOPs), computed based on the number of activated nodes along each routing path. Table
4
reports FLOPs normalized to a dense feed-forward baseline and confirms an exponential reduction with increasing tree depth, consistent with the observed sparsity trends.

While total FLOPs decrease sharply with increasing sparsity, measured throughput in terms of FLOPs per second also decreases. This behavior is expected and desirable: as the amount of computation performed per token is reduced, the aggregate FLOPs executed per second naturally drop. In this regime, FLOPs/s reflects the reduced computational demand rather than a slowdown of the layer itself, as demonstrated by the measured speedups, where wall-clock runtime improves despite the lower FLOPs/s.

8
Discussion

In this work, we studied tree-based sparse feed-forward layers as structured alternatives to dense feed-forward (FF) layers, demonstrating that they perform on par with mixture-of-experts models. This indicates that tree-based routing can serve as a practical, scalable, and predictable sparsification mechanism. We demonstrated that replacing dense FF layers with tree-based sparse layers scales effectively to large language models and generalizes across tasks and domains while maintaining performance close to dense baselines.

Across multiple model sizes and evaluation settings, we showed that these layers can operate in high sparsity regimes—often exceeding 90%—while achieving comparable and, in several cases, superior performance relative to dense FF baselines trained under the same data and optimization budgets. Notably, we observed that sparse tree-based models can outperform dense counterparts despite activating only a small fraction of parameters, highlighting the effectiveness of structured, input-dependent computation.

Beyond empirical performance, we analyzed the routing dynamics induced by hierarchical binary trees and identified an emergent
auto-pruning
behavior. This mechanism converts dynamic, input-dependent routing into stable structural sparsity, enabling permanent deactivation of redundant computation paths without auxiliary losses or explicit pruning criteria. We further showed that routing imbalance—often viewed as a limitation in sparse architectures—is not necessarily harmful in tree-based routing and can be controlled through simple architectural choices when needed.

While our results indicate strong potential for both scalability and efficiency, we note that the practical runtime benefits of such layers are currently constrained by hardware and software support. Many highly optimized components in large language models rely on accelerator-specific kernels, whereas dynamic, tree-based sparse execution remains underexplored in current hardware stacks. As such, our efficiency results are intended to highlight theoretical and simulated potential rather than immediate deployment-ready speedups.

Overall, this work aims to encourage further exploration of tree-based sparse computation as a viable direction for efficient large-scale models. We believe that the combination of dynamic, predictable, and structured sparsity, along with emerging auto-pruning properties, makes tree-based feed-forward layers a promising foundation for future research in scalable and efficient neural architectures.

Impact Statement

This work aims to advance the field of machine learning by studying structured sparse feed-forward layers for modern transformer architectures. From an ethical perspective, the proposed tree-based design introduces a more structured and interpretable form of conditional computation, which may help improve understanding and transparency of model behavior compared to fully dense architectures.

From a societal standpoint, large transformer models are computationally and energy intensive. By enabling extreme and dynamic sparsity while preserving performance, this work contributes toward more computationally efficient model designs, which may help reduce energy consumption and environmental impact during training and deployment at scale. While the work does not directly address downstream applications or deployment risks, it encourages future research on efficient, scalable, and responsible large-scale machine learning systems.

Appendix G
Auto-Pruning Analysis

This appendix provides a detailed empirical and analytical investigation of the auto-pruning behavior observed in tree-based feed-forward layers. For simplicity, the analysis is presented using the masked formulation of the FFF layer, which is functionally equivalent to the sequential tree traversal described in the main text. We show how stable structural sparsity emerges from the interaction between hard tree routing decisions and the asymmetric nonlinearity of GELU.

G.1
Empirical Observations

To isolate the underlying dynamics of auto-pruning, we conduct controlled toy experiments using a large batch size of 10,000 to minimize stochastic effects during training.

The first observation concerns the distribution of activations at a randomly selected internal tree node. As shown in Fig.
9
(right), the node logit follows an approximately zero-mean normal distribution after training. This symmetry indicates that routing decisions are not biased toward either branch at initialization and that specialization arises through training dynamics.

The second observation analyzes the gradient signal received by the same node during backpropagation. Fig.
9
(left) shows the distribution of the upstream gradient after an initial transient phase. We observe that the gradient concentrates almost entirely in the negative regime. This persistent negative signal encourages the routing gate to suppress one branch consistently, driving the node toward permanent deactivation due to the combination of GELU as an asymmetric nonlinearity and the hard thresholding of the tree.

Together, these observations provide empirical evidence that motivates the analysis of the auto-pruning dynamics and mechanisms presented in the next section.

G.2
Analytical Explanation

Consider the routing logit for a single tree node,

z=w^{\top}h+b,

(35)

where
h
denotes the node input and
w,b
are the parameters of the routing projection. We define the mean logit as

\mu\triangleq\mathbb{E}[z].

(36)

Empirically, we observe that
z
remains approximately Gaussian across training,

z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}),

(37)

which motivates the following analysis.

The probability of selecting the positive branch is

p=\mathbb{P}(z>0)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}\right),

(38)

where
\Phi
denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Since
\Phi
is monotonic, any positive drift in
\mu
increases the likelihood of selecting the same branch.

During training with stochastic gradient descent, the expected change in the mean logit satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mu]=-\eta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z}(m^{\top}h+1)\right],\quad m\triangleq\mathbb{E}[h],

(39)

where
\eta
is the learning rate and
\mathcal{L}
the task loss.

For the standard FFF formulation,

Y=\mathrm{linear\_out}(M\odot\mathrm{GELU}(Z)),

(40)

the routing mask
M=\mathrm{BTS}(\mathrm{sg}(Z))
is treated as constant during backpropagation. Let

g\triangleq\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(M\,\mathrm{GELU}(z))}

(41)

denote the upstream gradient. The gradient with respect to
z
becomes

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z}=g\cdot M\cdot\mathrm{GELU}^{\prime}(z).

(42)

Since
\mathrm{GELU}^{\prime}(z)
is close to
1
for
z>0
and significantly smaller for
z<0
, gradient contributions from the negative region are suppressed. Empirically, we observe that the upstream gradient
g
has a negative mean, yielding

\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mu]>0,

(43)

which causes
\mu
to drift toward positive values. This reinforces the selection of the same branch and progressively suppresses alternatives.

G.3
From Dynamic Routing to Static Sparsity

As training progresses, nodes whose logits consistently drift negative are visited increasingly rarely. These nodes receive diminishing gradient updates and eventually become permanently inactive, leading to the deactivation of entire subtrees. This process converts dynamic, input-dependent routing into stable, data-dependent structural sparsity.

Figure
4
illustrates this effect by evaluating validation accuracy under fixed statistical pruning ratios. As training proceeds, models with increasing levels of permanent pruning closely match the performance of their unpruned counterparts, indicating that auto-pruning preserves the most informative computation paths.

G.4
Effect of Nonlinearity Placement

For the alternative
\mathrm{FFF}^{\mathrm{post}}
formulation,

Y=\mathrm{GELU}(\mathrm{linear\_out}(M\odot Z)),

(44)

the gradient with respect to
z
no longer depends directly on
\mathrm{GELU}^{\prime}(z)
, but on a mixed representation after aggregation. As a result, the sign-dependent suppression mechanism described above is absent, and we do not observe stable auto-pruning behavior empirically. This effect instead leads to a more balanced tree utilization, as discussed and tested in Section 3.

G.6
Summary

Together, these results show that auto-pruning arises from a specific interaction between hard binary routing and the asymmetric GELU nonlinearity in the pre-activation design. This mechanism naturally transforms dynamic routing into stable structural sparsity during training, without explicit pruning rules or auxiliary losses.

Appendix H
Implementation and Training Details

All experiments were conducted on a multi-node GPU cluster equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs.
For the OPT-125M experiments, we used multi compute nodes, each containing 4 GPUs.
Training was implemented using the Hugging Face Transformers framework with DeepSpeed integration and the Accelerate library for distributed execution and mixed-precision training.

We followed a Chinchilla-style training paradigm, where training proceeds for a single epoch while controlling the total number of tokens processed rather than training to convergence over multiple epochs.
The total number of tokens per optimization step was fixed to approximately
5\times 10^{5}

1\times 10^{6}

2\times 10^{6}

Per-device batch size and gradient accumulation steps were chosen accordingly to reach this target token budget across all GPUs.

Optimization was performed using AdamW with fused CUDA kernels, cosine learning rate scheduling with a linear warmup phase, and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0.
Training was conducted in FP16 precision.
No maximum number of steps was imposed, and training was terminated after completing one full pass over the dataset.

For simplicity, the analysis is presented using the masked formulation of the FFF layer, which is functionally equivalent to the sequential tree traversal version described in the main text.

The dataset was tokenized offline prior to training using a GPT-style tokenizer.

Appendix J
Visualization of Space Partitioning

To illustrate the structured behavior of tree-based routing, we analyze an FFF layer on a simple 2D toy task. The input is a point
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the target is a binary label defined by a checkered-field (checkerboard) pattern over the input space. This setting allows us to directly visualize how hierarchical routing partitions the input domain.

Figure
12
provides two complementary views of the learned partitioning. The left panel visualizes the decision boundaries induced by tree traversal, mapped back into the original input space. Each line corresponds to a routing split at some node, and together the boundaries show how the forest decomposes the 2D plane into a collection of routed regions. The right panel visualizes the resulting routed regions in representation space: we group inputs by their routing outcome (i.e., the set of active paths) and project the corresponding representations using PCA. Regions are color-coded, highlighting that different parts of the input space are consistently assigned to different routing patterns that contribute differently to the final output.
