Association between post-void residual urine volume trajectories and incident urinary tract infection in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Wen-Jing Yan, Yi-Na Wang, Ying Zheng, Jin-Feng Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) have a markedly increased incidence of urinary tract infection (UTI) because of impaired immune function, reduced urine output, and complications related to medical procedures. Post-void residual urine volume (PVR) is a key indicator of bladder emptying function. Elevated PVR is associated with urinary retention and bacterial colonization and is considered an indicator related to higher UTI risk. However, previous studies have mainly examined the cross-sectional association between a single PVR measurement and UTI risk. In patients receiving MHD, PVR often changes dynamically because of autonomic neuropathy, diabetic cystopathy, and fluctuations in volume status; therefore, a single assessment may not fully capture longitudinal changes. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) can identify distinct longitudinal patterns, but evidence regarding its use to characterize PVR trajectories in the MHD population and assess their association with UTI risk remains limited.MethodsClinical data were retrospectively collected from 302 patients who received regular MHD treatment at our hospital from January 2021 to December 2024. PVR was measured by ultrasonography every 3–6 months. GBTM was applied to repeated PVR measurements to identify trajectory classes. The optimal model was selected according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), average posterior probability (APP), odds of correct classification (OCC), and clinical interpretability. Baseline characteristics and UTI occurrence were compared among trajectory groups, and baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients were compared. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test were used to compare cumulative UTI incidence. Posterior-probability-weighted Cox proportional hazards regression and Fine-Gray competing-risk models with death as a competing event were used to assess the association between PVR trajectories and UTI risk. In an exploratory analysis, death and nonfatal study-exit events were also combined as competing events. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to explore the dose-response association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk. Robustness was evaluated using a clinically parsimonious Cox model, Firth penalized partial-likelihood Cox regression, and other sensitivity analyses.ResultsGBTM identified three PVR trajectories: low-level stable (138 patients, 45.7%), moderate-level increasing (103 patients, 34.1%), and persistently elevated (61 patients, 20.2%). During a median follow-up of 26.8 months, 60 patients (19.9%) developed a first UTI. The proportions of UTI in the low-level stable, moderate-level increasing, and persistently elevated groups were 10.1%, 22.3%, and 37.7%, respectively (χ²=20.78, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Cox model, the moderate-level increasing group showed a borderline association with higher first UTI risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.96, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–3.82, P = 0.047), but this association did not reach statistical significance in the Fine-Gray competing-risk model (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.94–3.64, P = 0.073). The persistently elevated group was independently associated with higher first UTI risk in both the Cox model (HR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.68–6.68, P = 0.001) and the Fine-Gray model (SHR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.53–6.36, P = 0.002). RCS exploratory analysis suggested a nonlinear association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk (overall association test P < 0.001; nonlinearity test P = 0.012); the slope of the HR curve tended to increase around approximately 80 mL, and this value was used to describe an exploratory curve feature in this study sample.ConclusionAmong MHD patients with measurable urine output who were able to complete serial PVR assessment, a persistently elevated PVR trajectory was independently associated with higher first UTI risk, and baseline PVR showed a nonlinear dose-response association with first UTI risk. Serial PVR assessment may provide supplementary risk-stratification information for MHD patients with measurable urine output.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05157-9.Keywords: Maintenance hemodialysis, Post-void residual urine volume, Group-based trajectory modeling, Urinary tract infection, Dose-response association

Introduction

Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) have a higher incidence of urinary tract infection (UTI) than the general population because of impaired immune function, reduced urine output, and repeated vascular access procedures; UTI has become an important complication affecting patient prognosis [1]. Post-void residual urine volume (PVR) is a key indicator of lower urinary tract emptying function. Elevated PVR is closely associated with urinary retention and bacterial colonization and is considered to be associated with higher UTI risk [2]. Previous studies have mainly focused on the cross-sectional association between a single PVR measurement and UTI. However, PVR levels in patients receiving MHD are not constant. PVR levels may be related to autonomic neuropathy, diabetic cystopathy, and long-term fluctuations in volume status, and they often change dynamically with substantial interindividual heterogeneity [3]. Therefore, PVR assessment at a single time point may fail to capture these longitudinal patterns and may miss higher-risk patients requiring further follow-up. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a finite-mixture modeling approach for longitudinal data that can identify latent subgroups with distinct change patterns based on repeated individual measurements, and it has been widely applied in chronic disease research [4]. However, data remain limited on the use of GBTM to identify longitudinal PVR trajectories in patients receiving MHD and to assess their relationship with UTI risk [5]. Therefore, this study retrospectively collected repeated PVR measurements from MHD patients, used GBTM to identify PVR trajectory subgroups, and analyzed the association between trajectory class and UTI risk, thereby providing evidence for subsequent risk-stratification research.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study retrospectively collected clinical data from patients receiving regular maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) at our hospital from January 2021 to December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital. All patient data were anonymized, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) dialysis vintage ≥ 3 months, with regular hemodialysis 2–3 times per week; (3) at least four ultrasound-based PVR measurements during the study period, with an interval between adjacent PVR measurements not exceeding 6 months; (4) complete clinical data, with key-variable missingness < 20%; and (5) 24-hour urine volume ≥ 100 mL, allowing PVR assessment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) known structural urinary tract abnormalities, such as neurogenic bladder, bladder tumor, or urethral stricture; (2) long-term indwelling urinary catheter or cystostomy; (3) active UTI within 3 months before study entry, with this extended time window intended to reduce bias in baseline measurements related to prior infection; (4) severe immunodeficiency, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or long-term immunosuppressive therapy after organ transplantation; (5) conversion to peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplantation, or transfer to another center before study entry, with unavailable baseline and follow-up data; (6) anuria, defined as 24-hour urine volume < 100 mL; and (7) substantial missingness of clinical data, with key-variable missingness ≥ 20%. Patients who underwent kidney transplantation, converted to peritoneal dialysis, or transferred to another center after enrollment were not excluded; they were censored at the date of the event in the primary analysis and treated as additional competing events in the exploratory analysis.

The starting point of follow-up was the date of the first ultrasound PVR assessment during the study period. The endpoint of follow-up was the earliest of the following events: (1) date of first UTI; (2) date of death; (3) date of kidney transplantation, conversion to peritoneal dialysis, transfer to another center, or another nonfatal study-exit event; or (4) the study cutoff date, December 31, 2024. In the Cox models, death and nonfatal study-exit events were treated as censoring events. In the Fine-Gray model, death was prespecified as a competing event. In the exploratory Fine-Gray model, death and nonfatal study-exit events were combined as competing events. The primary endpoint was first UTI.

Measurement of post-void residual urine volume

All patients underwent PVR measurement on a non-dialysis day, uniformly scheduled on the first non-dialysis day after dialysis, to standardize the influence of volume status on PVR assessment. Measurements were performed by trained sonographers using a portable bladder ultrasound scanner (BladderScan). The same equipment model was used throughout the study. No additional water intake was required before measurement. Without violating individualized fluid-restriction prescriptions, patients voided according to their usual drinking status or after experiencing a natural urge to urinate. Within 5 min after voiding, patients were placed in the supine position and underwent suprapubic bladder ultrasonography. Residual urine volume was calculated using the three-dimensional bladder formula: PVR (mL) = 0.523 × anteroposterior diameter (cm) × transverse diameter (cm) × craniocaudal diameter (cm). Each measurement was repeated twice, and the mean value was recorded. According to routine clinical practice, PVR was assessed every 3–6 months, and each patient had at least four PVR measurements during the study period.

The clinical categories of PVR were as follows: PVR < 50 mL was considered normal; 50–100 mL was considered mildly elevated; 100–200 mL was considered moderately elevated; and > 200 mL was considered severely elevated.

UTI diagnosis, urine specimen collection, and endpoint adjudication

UTI diagnosis was based on international guidelines, systematic reviews of UTI definitions in research, consensus on UTI classification, and diagnostic studies [6–10], integrating clinical symptoms or signs, evidence of urinary inflammation, and microbiological results. Typical UTI was required to meet the following criteria: (1) clinical manifestations such as urinary frequency, urgency, dysuria, suprapubic tenderness, flank pain, or fever (body temperature ≥ 38.0 °C); (2) positive leukocyte esterase on urinalysis and/or ≥ 5 leukocytes per high-power field on microscopic examination of urinary sediment; and (3) bacterial growth of ≥ 10^5 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL in a clean-catch midstream urine culture, or ≥ 10^3 CFU/mL in the presence of typical symptoms. For patients who had received antibiotics before testing at an outside hospital or outpatient clinic and had negative cultures or colony counts below the threshold, the event was adjudicated as clinical UTI only when typical symptoms, pyuria, a clear diagnosis by the attending physician, and symptom improvement after antimicrobial therapy were all present.

Urine specimens were collected before antibiotic use whenever possible. Before sampling, patients cleaned the vulva or urethral meatus, discarded the initial urine stream, and collected clean-catch midstream urine in a sterile container. Samples were sent to the laboratory within 2 h after collection; if timely delivery was not possible, they were stored at 4 °C and cultured within 24 h. Bedpan urine, urine-bag samples, and specimens collected in nonsterile containers were not used for culture-based adjudication. For oliguric patients with 24-hour urine volume of 100–400 mL who could not provide a specimen immediately, sampling was performed after spontaneous voiding when clinically necessary; the sampling frequency was not changed according to PVR trajectory group.

Asymptomatic bacteriuria was defined as a positive urine culture in the absence of urinary tract symptoms, fever, flank pain, suprapubic tenderness, or evidence of systemic inflammation, and it was not counted as a UTI endpoint. To reduce detection bias, UTI endpoints were independently adjudicated by two senior nephrologists who were unaware of patients’ PVR trajectory groups, baseline PVR values, and trajectory-model results, based on deidentified case summaries. Disagreements were first discussed; if consensus still could not be reached, a third chief physician made the final adjudication.

Identification of residual urine volume trajectories

GBTM was used to identify latent PVR trajectory classes over time in patients receiving MHD. GBTM is a semiparametric finite-mixture modeling method that assigns patients to subgroups with similar longitudinal patterns based on repeated individual measurements without requiring the number or shape of trajectories to be specified in advance.

During model fitting, because PVR values had a right-skewed distribution, natural logarithmic transformation was first applied (ln-PVR) to approximate a normal distribution. With ln-PVR as the dependent variable and follow-up time (months) as the independent variable, models with 2–5 trajectory classes were examined. Intercept-only, linear, quadratic, and cubic polynomial functions were fitted for each trajectory group. For data with irregular measurement intervals, GBTM allows individuals to be observed at different time points and uses the actual measurement time (months) as the independent variable; equally spaced measurements are not required. The optimal model was selected according to the following criteria: (1) optimal Bayesian information criterion (BIC); (2) average posterior probability (APP) ≥ 0.70 for each trajectory group; (3) odds of correct classification (OCC) ≥ 5.0 for each trajectory group; (4) observed proportion of each group ≥ 5% of the total population; and (5) clinical interpretability of the identified trajectory classes.

To further quantify uncertainty in individual trajectory assignment, the maximum posterior probability (MPP) was recorded for each patient and summarized into four categories: MPP ≥ 0.90, 0.80 to < 0.90, 0.70 to < 0.80, and < 0.70. The primary Cox models used posterior-probability weighting to reduce misclassification bias caused by hard classification [11].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, Stata 17.0, and R 4.3.3. The Stata “traj” plugin was used to fit 2–5 classes of trajectories for ln-PVR. The optimal model was selected according to BIC, APP ≥ 0.70, OCC ≥ 5.0, minimum group proportion ≥ 5%, and clinical interpretability. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Welch’s t-test. Skewed continuous variables were expressed as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) [M (P25, P75)], and between-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) [n (%)], and between-group comparisons were performed using the χ² test; Fisher’s exact test or Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was used when expected frequencies were small or low-frequency cells were present. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to estimate cumulative UTI incidence, and the log-rank test was used to compare differences among groups. With the low-level stable group as the reference, posterior-probability-weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to analyze the association between PVR trajectories and UTI risk, reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Fine-Gray competing-risk models were also used, with death prespecified as a competing event [12], and subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) and 95% CIs were reported. In addition to the prespecified Fine-Gray model with death as the competing event, an exploratory competing-risk analysis was performed because kidney transplantation, conversion to peritoneal dialysis, and transfer to another center may be related to overall health status, infection burden, and PVR trajectories. In this analysis, death and nonfatal study-exit events were combined as competing events, and SHRs and 95% CIs for first UTI under different PVR trajectories were re-estimated. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to explore the nonlinear dose-response association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk; this analysis was prespecified as exploratory and was used to describe the shape of the curve between continuous PVR and first UTI risk. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex, age, diabetes mellitus, dialysis vintage, and 24-hour urine volume, and P values for interaction were corrected using the Bonferroni method, with corrected P < 0.01 considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses included excluding UTI events occurring within the first 3 months of follow-up, using recurrent UTI as the endpoint, and replacing probability weighting with hard classification. Because only 60 primary endpoint events occurred, two additional robustness analyses were prespecified to reduce the risk of overfitting. First, a clinically parsimonious Cox model was constructed, including only clinically necessary covariates prespecified as being related to both PVR and UTI: age, sex, diabetes mellitus, ALB, and 24-hour urine volume. Second, under the same covariate structure as the fully adjusted model, Firth penalized partial-likelihood Cox regression was performed using the coxphf package in R [13] to reduce estimation bias related to limited sample size and number of events. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient screening flow and baseline comparison with excluded patients

From January 2021 to December 2024, 528 patients receiving regular MHD were initially screened at this center. To avoid repeated counting of the same patient for multiple reasons, exclusion reasons were classified as mutually exclusive according to the primary clinical reason. A total of 226 patients were excluded: age < 18 years (n = 4), dialysis vintage < 3 months (n = 22), fewer than four PVR measurements or an interval between adjacent measurements > 6 months (n = 70), 24-hour urine volume < 100 mL or anuria (n = 65), long-term indwelling urinary catheter or cystostomy (n = 27), known structural urinary tract abnormality (n = 11), active UTI within 3 months before study entry (n = 16), severe immunodeficiency or long-term immunosuppressive therapy (n = 5), and key-variable missingness ≥ 20% (n = 6). Finally, 302 patients were included in the primary analysis (Fig. 1).

PVR, post-void residual urine volume

Compared with excluded patients, included patients had a shorter dialysis vintage [36 (20, 60) months vs. 54 (29, 84) months, P < 0.001], higher 24-hour urine volume [500 (300, 760) mL vs. 80 (0, 210) mL, P < 0.001], more PVR measurements (5.0 ± 1.2 vs. 2.1 ± 1.1, P < 0.001), and a lower proportion of central venous catheter use (13.2% vs. 20.8%, P = 0.021). The two groups did not differ significantly in sex distribution, BMI, or diabetes prevalence. These results indicate that the included population mainly represents MHD patients with measurable urine output who were able to complete serial PVR monitoring; caution is needed when extrapolating the results to patients with anuria, long-term indwelling catheters, or incomplete PVR data (Supplementary Table 1).

Identification of PVR trajectories

GBTM models with 2–5 trajectory groups were fitted. Model fit indices showed that, compared with the 2-class model, the 3-class trajectory model substantially improved the BIC, and all trajectory groups met APP ≥ 0.70, OCC ≥ 5.0, and a minimum group proportion > 5%. Considering both statistical indices and clinical interpretability, the 3-class trajectory model was selected as the optimal model (Table 1).

The 3-class trajectory model identified the following PVR patterns: (1) low-level stable group (138 patients, 45.7%), with low baseline PVR that remained relatively stable over time; this trajectory included an intercept and linear term, the linear slope was close to 0 and not statistically significant (P = 0.317), APP = 0.92, and OCC = 8.2; (2) moderate-level increasing group (103 patients, 34.1%), with mildly to moderately elevated baseline PVR that slowly increased linearly over time; the linear term was statistically significant (P < 0.001), APP = 0.85, and OCC = 6.5; and (3) persistently elevated group (61 patients, 20.2%), with high baseline PVR and a persistently rising trajectory fitted by a quadratic polynomial function; both the linear term (P < 0.001) and quadratic term (P = 0.003) were statistically significant, APP = 0.88, and OCC = 7.8. The estimated geometric mean PVR values at the median follow-up time of approximately 26.8 months were 28 mL, 112 mL, and 252 mL, respectively.

In the low-level stable group, the ln-PVR intercept was 3.22 and the linear slope was 0.004 per month, suggesting that PVR changed very little over time. In the moderate-level increasing group, the intercept was 4.32 and the linear slope was 0.015 per month, indicating that PVR increased slowly on the logarithmic scale at approximately 1.5% per month. In the persistently elevated group, the intercept was 5.10, the linear term was 0.008 per month, and the quadratic term was 0.0003/month², suggesting that this group not only had a higher baseline PVR but also showed a gradually increasing rate of rise over time, consistent with a nonlinear accelerating pattern (Table 2) (Fig. 2).

PVR, post-void residual urine volume

Patient-level MPP analysis showed good overall classification certainty for the optimal 3-class model. Among the 302 patients, 188 (62.3%) had MPP ≥ 0.90, 69 (22.8%) had MPP from 0.80 to < 0.90, 27 (8.9%) had MPP from 0.70 to < 0.80, and 18 (6.0%) had MPP < 0.70. The numbers of patients with MPP < 0.70 in the low-level stable, moderate-level increasing, and persistently elevated groups were 5 (3.6%), 9 (8.7%), and 4 (6.6%), respectively. These results indicate that the proportion of patients with uncertain trajectory assignment was low, although classification uncertainty was relatively greater in the moderate-level increasing group, supporting the use of posterior-probability-weighted Cox models as the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 2).

Baseline characteristics stratified by PVR trajectory group

Baseline characteristics of the 302 patients were compared according to PVR trajectory group. Age, BMI, diabetes prevalence, the proportion of male benign prostatic hyperplasia, hs-CRP, FPG, baseline PVR, and HbA1c among patients with diabetes tended to be higher in the persistently elevated group, whereas ALB, Hb, and 24-hour urine volume tended to be lower in the persistently elevated group; between-group differences were statistically significant for all these variables (all P < 0.05). The distribution of primary kidney disease did not reach statistical significance by the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test (P = 0.102). There were no statistically significant differences among groups in sex distribution, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, dialysis vintage, dialysis frequency, duration of each dialysis session, vascular access type, ultrafiltration volume per dialysis session, interdialytic weight gain rate, Scr, BUN, Kt/V, iPTH, or number of PVR measurements (all P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Occurrence of urinary tract infection

The median follow-up among the 302 patients was 26.8 (16.5, 35.2) months. During follow-up, 60 patients (19.9%) developed a first UTI, including 42 lower urinary tract infections (70.0%) and 18 upper urinary tract infections (30.0%). During the study period, 25 patients (8.3%) died: 8 (5.8%) in the low-level stable group, 8 (7.8%) in the moderate-level increasing group, and 9 (14.8%) in the persistently elevated group. A total of 87 suspected UTI-related records were retrieved. After blinded endpoint adjudication, 60 patients met the definition of first UTI, 22 had asymptomatic bacteriuria and were not counted as endpoints, and 5 were judged to have infection of nonurinary origin or specimen contamination. The initial agreement between the two adjudicating physicians was 83/87 (95.4%), with a Cohen’s κ coefficient of 0.89; the remaining four cases were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third physician. All 60 patients with UTI underwent urine culture; 55 met the culture-positive criterion, and the remaining 5 had negative cultures or colony counts below the threshold after antibiotic exposure but met the clinical UTI adjudication criteria. Gram-negative bacteria predominated among pathogens (Table 4).

Association between PVR trajectories and UTI risk

Schoenfeld residual testing showed that all covariates satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (all P > 0.05). With the low-level stable group as the reference, posterior-probability-weighted Cox proportional hazards regression showed that, in the unadjusted model (Model 1), the moderate-level increasing and persistently elevated groups were both associated with higher first UTI risk. After stepwise adjustment for confounders, the persistently elevated group remained associated with higher first UTI risk in both the Cox model and the Fine-Gray model with death as a competing event (Table 6). In the fully adjusted Fine-Gray model (Model 3), the association between the moderate-level increasing group and first UTI risk did not reach statistical significance (SHR = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.94–3.64; P = 0.073) (Table 6).

Dose-response association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk

Baseline PVR was entered into the Cox regression model as a continuous variable using RCS with four knots. RCS exploratory analysis showed a nonlinear association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk (nonlinearity test P = 0.012). After adjustment for confounders, with PVR = 30 mL as the reference, the HR curve generally increased with increasing PVR, and the slope tended to increase around approximately 80 mL; the adjusted HR was 2.85 (95% CI: 1.52–5.35) at PVR = 150 mL, 3.82 (95% CI: 1.78–8.20) at PVR = 200 mL, and 5.12 (95% CI: 2.18–12.02) at PVR = 250 mL. The overall association test showed P < 0.001 (Fig. 4). Approximately 80 mL was used to describe the exploratory slope-change location in the RCS curve of this study sample.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses showed that, under different modeling strategies, the persistently elevated PVR trajectory was consistently associated with higher first UTI risk. In the clinically parsimonious Cox model, the HR for the persistently elevated group was 3.46 (95% CI: 1.78–6.72; P < 0.001), and the HR for the moderate-level increasing group was 2.04 (95% CI: 1.07–3.90; P = 0.031). In Firth penalized partial-likelihood Cox regression, the persistently elevated group remained statistically significant (HR = 3.21, 95% CI: 1.64–6.29; P = 0.001), whereas the moderate-level increasing group was borderline (HR = 1.89, 95% CI: 0.98–3.65; P = 0.057). Other sensitivity analyses, including excluding UTI events within the first 3 months of follow-up, using recurrent UTI as the endpoint, and hard-classification Cox regression, did not change the direction of the association or the main conclusion for the persistently elevated group (Table 8).

During follow-up, in addition to 25 deaths, 20 patients experienced nonfatal study-exit events, including 5 kidney transplantations, 6 conversions to peritoneal dialysis, and 9 transfers to other centers. Nonfatal study-exit events occurred in 7 patients (5.1%) in the low-level stable group, 7 (6.8%) in the moderate-level increasing group, and 6 (9.8%) in the persistently elevated group, with no statistically significant difference among groups (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, P = 0.428). When death and nonfatal study-exit events were combined as competing events, the Fine-Gray model showed that the persistently elevated group remained associated with higher first UTI risk (SHR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.45–6.12; P = 0.003), whereas the moderate-level increasing group did not reach statistical significance (SHR = 1.79, 95% CI: 0.91–3.52; P = 0.091) (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This study used GBTM to systematically identify longitudinal PVR trajectories in patients receiving MHD, found three clinically meaningful patterns, and suggested a relatively stable association between a persistently elevated PVR trajectory and higher first UTI risk.

Previous PVR-related studies have mainly focused on single PVR measurements or threshold-based judgments [14], overlooking temporal fluctuations in PVR among dialysis patients. In this study, the low-level stable group accounted for 45.7% of the study population, suggesting that nearly half of the patients maintained relatively preserved bladder emptying function, which is consistent with previous reports that a considerable proportion of dialysis patients still retain measurable residual urine output [15]. The persistently elevated group accounted for 20.2% of the study population and had a higher baseline PVR with an accelerating upward trend. This may reflect bladder emptying impairment related to a higher prevalence of diabetes, progressive autonomic neuropathy, and continuous deterioration of detrusor function [16]; these biological pathways require further clarification in mechanistic studies. The persistently elevated group also had higher HbA1c levels and lower 24-hour urine volume, consistent with these clinical features.

Cox regression showed that the persistently elevated group was associated with higher first UTI risk; this association remained stable in the model with death as a competing event, the exploratory model including other competing events, the clinically parsimonious Cox model, and the Firth penalized Cox model. In contrast, the moderate-level increasing group showed only borderline significance in the fully adjusted Cox model and did not reach statistical significance in the Fine-Gray competing-risk model or the Firth penalized model. Therefore, the moderate-level increasing trajectory should be interpreted as a potential association signal that requires evaluation in larger samples and prospective studies. Given that only 60 first UTI events occurred, the fully adjusted model may have a certain risk of overfitting. Additional clinically parsimonious Cox models and Firth penalized partial-likelihood Cox regression showed that the effect size for the persistently elevated group was close to that in the primary analysis and remained statistically significant in both models, supporting the robustness of this association.

RCS exploratory analysis showed a nonlinear association between baseline PVR and first UTI risk, and the slope of the HR curve tended to increase around approximately 80 mL. Previous studies have shown that elevated PVR is associated with urinary retention, bacterial colonization, and higher UTI risk; however, clinical assessment should integrate symptoms, bladder capacity, trends in residual urine volume, and patient population characteristics [17]. Therefore, the approximately 80 mL observed in this MHD population only characterizes the exploratory curve shape in this study sample.

Subgroup analysis suggested that the direction of association between PVR trajectories and UTI was more pronounced among patients with diabetes. Although the interaction did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction, diabetes-related nerve injury, long-term metabolic memory, tissue hypoxia, and reduced bladder sensation may be related to voiding dysfunction and susceptibility to infection [18, 19]. The pathogen distribution was dominated by Escherichia coli, consistent with previously reported microbiological characteristics of UTI [20].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design could not completely eliminate the influence of unmeasured confounders. Second, the study population was selective. Because GBTM requires repeated PVR measurements, this study required at least four PVR records, an interval between adjacent measurements not exceeding 6 months, and 24-hour urine volume ≥ 100 mL, and it excluded patients with anuria, long-term indwelling urinary catheter, or cystostomy. Therefore, these findings are mainly applicable to MHD patients with preserved voiding ability who can complete serial PVR assessment, and caution is needed when extrapolating them to patients with anuria, long-term indwelling catheters, short follow-up, or incomplete PVR data. In particular, UTI risk in patients with long-term indwelling catheters is more strongly related to catheter biofilm formation, catheter-care quality, antimicrobial exposure, and the hospital infection environment; these mechanisms are not completely the same as bladder emptying dysfunction reflected by PVR trajectories [21]. Third, the single-center sample limited external generalizability. Fourth, PVR was measured every 3–6 months, and the time resolution may have been insufficient to capture short-term fluctuations. Fifth, UTI symptoms in oliguric patients may be atypical, and missed diagnosis or classification bias may still exist. Sixth, in the primary analysis, nonfatal study-exit events such as kidney transplantation, conversion to peritoneal dialysis, and transfer to another center were treated as censoring events, but these events may not have occurred entirely at random. For example, kidney transplantation usually occurs in patients with better overall status, whereas conversion to peritoneal dialysis or transfer to another center may be related to infection burden, vascular access status, or healthcare accessibility; these factors may also be related to PVR trajectories and UTI risk. In the exploratory analysis, after death and nonfatal study-exit events were combined as competing events, the effect size and statistical significance of the persistently elevated PVR trajectory remained stable. However, because the number of nonfatal study-exit events was limited, this analysis still cannot completely rule out residual bias caused by informative censoring. Seventh, the slope change around approximately 80 mL in the RCS analysis was an exploratory within-sample curve feature and was influenced by sample size, PVR distribution, knot location, and model specification; its clinical applicability and generalizability need to be evaluated in independent prospective studies.

Future studies should use multicenter prospective designs, include patients with anuria, patients with long-term indwelling urinary catheters, and patients who experience dialysis modality transitions, and integrate standardized UTI endpoint adjudication and intervention outcomes to further evaluate the clinical effectiveness, feasibility, and resource-allocation value of PVR trajectory monitoring for risk stratification.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective study, three PVR trajectories were identified: low-level stable, moderate-level increasing, and persistently elevated. A persistently elevated PVR trajectory was independently associated with higher first UTI risk, and baseline PVR showed a nonlinear dose-response association with first UTI risk. RCS analysis suggested an exploratory slope-change feature around approximately 80 mL. These findings suggest that serial PVR assessment may provide risk-stratification information for MHD patients with measurable urine output. However, because the study population was selective and the study had a single-center retrospective design, the current findings are not sufficient to support routine PVR trajectory monitoring in all MHD patients.
