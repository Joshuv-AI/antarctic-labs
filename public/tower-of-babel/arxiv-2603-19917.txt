Abstract
. Party-Hecke algebras are introduced as a two-parameter deformation of party algebras, where one parameter deforms the party generators and the other deforms the elementary transpositions. We construct a basis for this algebra and show that it can be realized as a quotient of the algebra of braids and ties. Furthermore, we study the party monoid and its relationship with the tied symmetric monoid and their associated algebras.

1.
Introduction

Deformation algebras are central to the classification of algebraic structures, as well as to quantum physics and knot theory, among other fields. One class of deformations consists of those obtained by deforming the centralizer algebras of representations, such as the Hecke and BMW algebras, from which the HOMFLYPT and Kauffman polynomials are derived, respectively. These centralizers are cornerstones of the so-called Schur–Weyl duality, the framework from which the deformed algebra studied here also arises. This duality is a central topic in invariant theory and originates from the duality between the general linear group
G=\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
acting on the tensor space
V^{\otimes d}
, where
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In this setting, the former acts diagonally and the latter by permuting the tensor factors. These actions commute, and the Schur–Weyl duality theorem (see, for instance,
[
16
, Theorem 3.4]
) establishes deep connections between the irreducible representations of
G
and the representation theory of the symmetric group. In particular, for
n\geq d
, the centralizer
\operatorname{End}_{G}(V^{\otimes d})
is the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]
. Later, in
[
14
]
, Brauer studied the analogous situation where
G
is replaced by the orthogonal subgroup
O_{n}
. Specifically, when
n\geq d
, the centralizer
\operatorname{End}_{O_{n}}(V^{\otimes d})
, denoted by
\mathfrak{Br}_{n}(d)
and called the Brauer algebra, is completely described by transpositions and certain elements which, in diagrammatic language are known now as tangles. In
[
26
]
, Jones studied the centralizer
\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(V^{\otimes d})
, where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is regarded as a subgroup of
G
, and introduced the partition algebra. This algebra was also introduced independently by Martin in
[
35
]
. Regarding
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as the complex reflection group
G(1,1,n)
, it is natural to investigate the centralizers of the complex reflection group
G(d,r,n)
. Tanabe studied the centralizer and provided a presentation for it, in which the generators consist of the elementary transpositions and certain party elements (see
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]
). Subsequently, building on the aforementioned work by Tanabe, Kosuda introduced, for any
\delta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the party algebra
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\delta)
in
[
29
, Definition 1.1]
, defining it through a presentation by generators and relations. Moreover, he proved that
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\delta)
can be realized as the centralizer
\operatorname{End}_{G(d,1,n)}(V^{\otimes k})
for
n\geq k
and
d>k
, see
[
29
, Propositions 1.2 and 1.3]
; in fact,
\mathcal{P}_{k}{(1)}=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]
, where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
denotes the party monoid, also known as the monoid of uniform block transpositions. Recall that deformations of
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
and
\mathfrak{Br}_{n}(d)
yield the Hecke and BMW algebras, respectively. The key point of these deformations is that the elementary transpositions are deformed in both algebras. For instance, in the Hecke algebra setting, the relation
s^{2}=1
is
u
-deformed into
s^{2}=u+(u-1)s
, leading to non-trivial braid group representations used in the construction of knot invariants. This article introduces and studies a two-parameter deformation of the party algebra, which we call the
Party-Hecke algebra
. In short, the Party-Hecke algebra is a two-parameter deformation in which one parameter deforms the party generators while the other deforms the elementary permutation generators. In what follows, we outline the structure and main results of the article.

Throughout this paper,

\ldbrack a,b\rdbrack

a\leq x\leq b

2.1.
Set partitions

Recall that a
set partition
of a set
A
is a collection of disjoint nonempty subsets, called
blocks
, such that their union is
A
. The collection of set partitions of
A
is denoted by
P(A)
. For each nonempty
B\subset A
we write
f_{B}
to denote the set partition whose unique possibly nontrivial block is
B
.

Given
I,J\in P(A)
, we say that
J
is
coarser
than
I
, or that
I
is
finer
than
J
, denoted by
I\preceq J
, if each block of
J
is a union of blocks of
I
. This relation gives
P(A)
the structure of a lattice with
join operation

\vee
. Hence, the pair
(P(A),\vee)
defines an idempotent commutative monoid with identity
(\{a\}\mid a\in A)
, called the
monoid of set partitions
of
A
. For
I,J\in P(A)
, it is usual to write
IJ
instead of
I\vee J
.

For a subset
B\subset A
and a set partition
I\in P(A)
, we write
I|_{B}
to denote the set partition of
B
consisting of the nonempty intersections of the blocks of
I
with
B
. Conversely, if
I\in P(B)
, then it can be regarded as a set partition of
A
by completing
I
with the singleton blocks formed by the elements in
A\setminus B
. Accordingly, if
A,B
are any two sets, and
(I,J)\in P(A)\times P(B)
, we simply write
I\vee J
to denote the join of
I
with
J
regarded as set partitions of
A\cup B
.

As shown in
[
19
, Theorem 2]
, the monoid of set partitions
P_{n}:=P(\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack)
is presented by generators
f_{i,j}:=f_{\{i,j\}}
with
i<j
, subject to the relations:

(1)

\displaystyle f_{i,j}^{2}=f_{i,j},\qquad f_{i,j}f_{h,k}=f_{h,k}f_{i,j},\qquad f_{i,j}f_{i,k}=f_{i,j}f_{j,k}=f_{i,k}f_{j,k}.

It is known that
|P_{n}|
is the
n
th
Bell number

[
38
,
A000110
]
. Moreover, the elements of
P_{n}
admit the following normal form.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
11
, Section 3.1]
)
.

Let
I=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k})
be a set partition of
\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
. Then
I=f_{I_{1}}\cdots f_{I_{k}}
. More precisely, for
B=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\}\subseteq\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
with
k=|B|>1
, we have:

f_{B}=f_{i_{1},i_{2}}\cdots f_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}.

The submonoid
C_{n}
of
P_{n}
, generated by the elements
f_{i}:=f_{i,i+1}
for all
i\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
, is called the
monoid of compositions

[
9
, Subsection 2.1]
, and is isomorphic to the free idempotent commutative monoid of rank
n-1
, that is,
C_{n}
is presented by generators
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1}
, subject to the relations:

(2)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{2}=f_{i},\qquad f_{i}f_{j}=f_{j}f_{i}.

Remark 3.4

Observe that for any permutations

3.2.3.
Cellularity of the party algebra

The cellularity of the party algebra was originally established in
[
30
]
. However, we present here an alternative and streamlined proof by exploiting the structure of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\delta)
as a twisted monoid algebra described in Subsubsection
3.1.3
, alongside the characterization of the maximal subgroups of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

Recall that, for
g=fs
and
g^{\prime}=f^{\prime}s^{\prime}
,
\tau_{\beta}(g,g^{\prime})=\delta^{\beta(f,f^{\prime})}
, where
\beta(f,f^{\prime})
counts the number of common standard arcs. Our approach relies on the framework developed by Wilcox
[
47
, Theorem 5]
, who showed that a twisted monoid algebra with a twisting taking invertible values is cellular, provided it satisfies five conditions, referred to by him as Assumptions 1–5. However, according to
[
47
, Corollary 6]
, Assumption 4 can be omitted.

We now examine Assumptions 1 and 2, which require the monoid to be equipped with an anti-involution with which the twisting is compatible. To address this, recall from Subsubsection
3.2.1
that the monoid
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be equipped with an anti-involution
*:\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}
defined by
(fs)^{*}=s^{-1}f
for all
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
f\in P_{n}
. This compatibility is verified in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
g,h\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we have
\tau_{\beta}(g,h)=\tau_{\beta}(h^{*},g^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
g=se
and
h=ft
with
s,t\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
e,f\in P_{n}
. Then, the product
h^{*}g^{*}=t^{-1}fes^{-1}
satisfies
\tau_{\beta}(h^{*},g^{*})=\beta(f,e)
. Since
\beta(f,e)=\beta(e,f)
, we conclude
\tau_{\beta}(h^{*},g^{*})=\beta(e,f)=\tau_{\beta}(g,h)
.
∎

Before continuing, we need to recall some facts about Green’s relations. Two elements
g,h
of a monoid
M
are said to be
\mathcal{L}
-equivalent
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
-equivalent
, resp.
\mathcal{J}
-equivalent
), denoted by
g\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}h
(resp.
g\equiv_{\mathcal{R}}h
, resp.
g\equiv_{\mathcal{J}}h
), if
Mg=Mh
(resp.
gM=hM
, resp.
MgM=MhM
). The classes relative to these relations are called
\mathcal{L}
-classes
,
\mathcal{R}
-classes
, and
\mathcal{J}
-classes
; for
g\in M
, we denote the corresponding classes by
L_{g}
,
R_{g}
and
J_{g}
, respectively. The equivalence relation generated by the union
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}\cup\equiv_{\mathcal{R}}
is denoted by
\equiv_{\mathcal{D}}
, and its classes, called
\mathcal{D}
-classes
, are known to be unions of
\mathcal{L}
-classes and unions of
\mathcal{R}
-classes
[
32
, Section 2.1]
.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a finite inverse monoid, several structural simplifications arise. First, the relations
\equiv_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\equiv_{\mathcal{J}}
coincide

32
, Proposition 2.1.4]
. Furthermore, every
\mathcal{L}
-class and every
\mathcal{R}
-class in

-class contains at least one idempotent

For

and

(

Two elements

A set partition is said to be

Proposition 3.10

4.
Party-Hecke algebras

In this section, we introduce the Party-Hecke algebra (Definition
4.2
) and prove that it can be realized as a quotient of a twisted monoid algebra of the tied braid group. In Theorem
4.13
, we construct a tensorial representation of this algebra, which we then use to establish a linear basis. Finally, Theorem
4.15
shows that the Party-Hecke algebra is generically semisimple.

4.1.
The Party-Hecke algebra

In what follows, we set
p,q\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

4.1.1.

We begin our construction of the Party-Hecke algebra by introducing a new monoid, referred to as the
party braid monoid
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
party braid monoid

\mathcal{BP}_{n}
is the monoid presented by generators
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}
satisfying the braid relations in (
6
), and commuting generators
\bar{f}_{1},\ldots,\bar{f}_{n-1}
, together with the following mixed relations:

(51)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\bar{f}_{j}=\bar{f}_{j}\sigma_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|\neq 1,\qquad\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bar{f}_{i}=\bar{f}_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\quad\text{if }|i-j|=1.

Observe that
\mathcal{BP}_{n}
contains a copy of the braid monoid
B_{n}^{+}
. Also, note that the party monoid
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a quotient of the party braid monoid
\mathcal{BP}_{n}
.

4.1.2.

We introduce the Party-Hecke algebra as a quotient of the monoid algebra of the party braid monoid.

Definition 4.2
.

The
Party-Hecke algebra

\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is defined as the quotient of the monoid algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{BP}_{n}]
by the two-sided ideal generated by the following elements:

(52)

\sigma_{i}^{2}-pq^{2}-p(p-1)\bar{f}_{i},\qquad\sigma_{i}\bar{f}_{i}-pq\bar{f}_{i},\qquad\bar{f}_{i}^{2}-q^{2}\bar{f}_{i}

We denote by
G_{i}
the image of
\sigma_{i}
in the quotient and by
F_{i}
the image of
\bar{f}_{i}
. Hence,
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra presented by generators
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n-1}
and
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
, subject to the relations:

(53)

\displaystyle G_{i}^{2}=pq^{2}+p(p-1)F_{i},

\displaystyle\qquad G_{i}F_{i}=F_{i}G_{i}=pqF_{i},

(54)

\displaystyle G_{i}G_{j}=G_{j}G_{i}\qquad\text{if }|i-j|>1,

\displaystyle\qquad G_{i}G_{j}G_{i}=G_{j}G_{j}G_{i}\qquad\text{if }|i-j|=1,

(55)

\displaystyle F_{i}^{2}=q^{2}F_{i},

\displaystyle\qquad F_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}F_{i},

(56)

\displaystyle G_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}G_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|\neq 1,

\displaystyle\qquad G_{i}G_{j}F_{i}=F_{j}G_{i}G_{j},\quad\text{if }|i-j|=1.

Relation (
53
) implies that each
G_{i}
is invertible with inverse given by

(57)

\displaystyle G_{i}^{-1}=p^{-1}q^{-2}G_{i}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)F_{i}.

Observe that
\operatorname{P}_{n}(1,1)=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]
, the monoid algebra of the party monoid.

4.2.
The Party-Hecke algebra as a quotient of
TB_{n}(q^{2})

We now show that the algebra
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is a quotient of
TB_{n}(q^{2})
. To establish this, we first require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
i,j\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
with
|i-j|=1
, we have:

(1)

G_{i}F_{j}F_{i}=F_{j}G_{i}F_{j}=F_{i}F_{j}G_{i}
.

(2)

G_{i}G_{j}^{-1}F_{i}=F_{j}G_{i}G_{j}^{-1}
.

Proof.

First, we show (
1
) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\!F_{j}G_{i}F_{j}&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{P-H3}}}{{=}}&G_{i}G_{j}F_{i}G_{j}^{-1}F_{j}\\
&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^2}}}{{=}}&w^{-1}G_{i}G_{j}F_{i}F_{j}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^2}}}{{=}}\ G_{i}F_{j}F_{i}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^2}}}{{=}}\ wF_{i}F_{j}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^2}}}{{=}}\ F_{i}F_{j}G_{i}.\end{array}

Then, by applying (
1
), we get (
2
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G_{i}G_{j}^{-1}F_{i}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^-1}}}{{=}}&G_{i}(p^{-1}q^{-2}G_{j}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)F_{j})F_{i}\\[4.26773pt]
&=&p^{-1}q^{-2}G_{i}G_{j}F_{i}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)G_{i}F_{j}F_{i}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{P-H3}}}{{=}}&p^{-1}q^{-2}F_{j}G_{i}G_{j}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)F_{j}G_{i}F_{j}\\[4.26773pt]
&=&F_{j}G_{i}(p^{-1}q^{-2}G_{j}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)F_{j})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^-1}}}{{=}}&F_{j}G_{i}G_{j}^{-1}.\end{array}

∎

Proposition 4.4
.

The map sending
\sigma_{i}\mapsto G_{i}
and
\bar{e}_{i}\mapsto F_{i}
defines an algebra homomorphism
TB_{n}(q^{2})\to\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
. More precisely, the Party-Hecke algebra
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is the quotient of the twisted monoid algebra
TB_{n}(q^{2})
by the two-sided ideal generated by the elements
\sigma_{i}^{2}-pq^{2}-p(p-1)\bar{e}_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}\bar{e}_{i}-pq\bar{e}_{i}
for all
i\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Eq. (
57
), together with Lemma
4.3
and the presentation of
TB_{n}(q^{2})
given in Subsubsection
3.3.2
.
∎

4.3.
A linear basis

The goal of this subsection is to give a linear basis
\mathcal{G}_{n}
for
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
. To explain the elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we need the following notations.

Given
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we set
G_{s}:=G_{i_{1}}\cdots G_{i_{k}}\in\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
, where
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k}}
is a reduced expression of

. In particular,
G_{s_{j}}=G_{j}
. Now, relations in (
54
) and Matsumoto’s theorem
[
36
]
imply that

does not depend on the choice of the reduced expression.
Now, for each pair of indexes
i,j\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
with
i<j
, we set

\displaystyle F_{i,j}=F_{j,i}=G_{i}\cdots G_{j-2}F_{j-1}G_{j-2}^{-1}\cdots G_{i}^{-1}.

Corollary 4.5
.

The map sending
f_{i,j}\mapsto F_{i,j}
defines a monoid homomorphism from
P_{n}
to
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
. In consequence, each
F_{i,j}
is a commuting idempotent satisfying
G_{i}F_{j,k}=F_{s_{i}(j),s_{i}(k)}G_{i}
for all
i,j,k\in\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
with
j<k
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
4.4
, Equation (
17
), and the fact that
P_{n}
embeds into
TB_{n}
by means of the map
f_{i,j}\mapsto e_{i,j}
.
∎

As in
[
41
, Section 3]
, for each nonempty subset
B\subseteq\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
and for each set partition
I=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}
of
\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
, we set

F_{B}=\prod_{i,j\in B,\,i<j}F_{i,j}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{I}=F_{I_{1}}\cdots F_{I_{k}},

where
F_{B}=1
whenever
|B|=1
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The set of products
F_{I}G_{s}
, with
I\in P_{n}
and
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, spans
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of the quadratic relation in (
53
), Proposition
4.4
and the fact that
TB_{n}
is the semi direct product
P_{n}\rtimes B_{n}
.
∎

Define

\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\{F_{I}G_{s}\mid I\in P_{n}\text{ and }s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\text{ with }(I,s)\text{ coprime}\}.

Observe that, by virtue of Proposition
3.2
, the elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
are parametrized by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

To establish that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
spans
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
, we first need to introduce some additional notations and three technical lemmas.

For each
i,j\in\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
with
i<j
, we set

\displaystyle G_{i,j}=G_{j,i}=G_{i}\cdots G_{j-2}G_{j-1}G_{j-2}^{-1}\cdots G_{i}^{-1}.

Observe that, as generators
G_{i}
satisfy the braid relations, then, due to (
7
), the map
\sigma_{i,j}\mapsto G_{i,j}
defines a group homomorphism from
B_{n}
to
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For each
i,j,k,l
, we have:

(1)

G_{i,j}^{2}=pq^{2}+p(p-1)F_{i,j}
.

(2)

G_{i,j}F_{k,l}=F_{s_{i,j}(k),s_{i,j}(l)}G_{i,j}
.

(3)

G_{i,j}F_{i,j}=F_{i,j}G_{i,j}=pqF_{i,j}
.

(4)

G_{i,j}^{-1}=p^{-1}q^{-2}G_{i,j}+q^{-3}(p^{-1}-1)F_{i,j}
.

Proof.

We show (
1
) as follows

\begin{array}[]{rclll}G_{i,j}^{2}&\!\!\!=&G_{i}\cdots G_{j-2}G_{j-1}^{2}G_{j-2}^{-1}\cdots G_{i}^{-1}\\[4.26773pt]
&\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G^2}}}{{=}}&G_{i}\cdots G_{j-2}(pq^{2}+p(p-1)F_{j-1})G_{j-2}^{-1}\cdots G_{i}^{-1}&\!\!=&pq^{2}+p(p-1)F_{i,j}.\end{array}

We get (
2
) and (
3
) as direct consequences of (
53
) and Corollary
4.5
. Claim (
4
) is a direct consequence of claims (
1
) and (
3
).
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
i,j,k\in\ldbrack 1,n\rdbrack
and let
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
s_{i,j}=\tau s_{k}\tau^{-1}
. Then
F_{i,j}G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}=pqF_{i,j}
.

Proof.

Since
\tau^{-1}s_{i,j}\tau=s_{k}
, then, by Lemma
4.7
(
2
) and Lemma
4.7
(
3
), we get the result as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{i,j}G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}&=&G_{\tau}(G_{\tau}^{-1}F_{i,j}G_{\tau})G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}\\
&=&G_{\tau}F_{\tau^{-1}(i),\tau^{-1}(j)}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}\\
&=&G_{\tau}F_{k}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}\\
&=&pqG_{\tau}F_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}\,\,\,=\,\,\,pqF_{i,j}.\end{array}

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\nu\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and let
(i,j)
be an inversion of
\nu
. Then, there is
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
k\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
, such that
G_{\nu}=G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}G_{s_{i,j}\nu}
with
s_{i,j}=\tau s_{k}\tau^{-1}
.

Proof.

Let
r=\ell(\nu)
, that is,
\nu=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
for some reduced expression. Then, due to the strong exchange condition
[
33
, Subsection 5.8]
, there is an
m\in\ldbrack 1,r\rdbrack
such that
s_{i,j}\nu=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{m-1}}s_{i_{m+1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
. Let
\tau=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{m-1}}
,
k=i_{m}
, and
\epsilon=s_{i_{m+1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
. Thus, we get

\nu=\tau s_{k}\epsilon,\qquad s_{i,j}\nu=\tau\epsilon,\qquad s_{i,j}=(s_{i,j}\nu)\nu^{-1}=\tau\epsilon(\tau s_{k}\epsilon)^{-1}=\tau s_{k}\tau^{-1}.

Therefore
G_{\nu}=G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\epsilon}=G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}G_{\tau}G_{\epsilon}=G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}G_{s_{i,j}\nu}
.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

The set
\mathcal{G}_{n}
spans
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
.

Proof.

Due to Proposition
4.6
, it is enough to show that each product
F_{I}G_{\nu}
can be written as
\alpha F_{I}G_{\nu^{\prime}}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, where the pair
(I,\nu^{\prime})
is coprime.

Let
(i,j)
be an inversion of
\nu
satisfying
I=If_{i,j}
. Due to Corollary
4.5
and Lemma
4.9
, we have
F_{I}G_{\nu}=F_{I}F_{i,j}G_{\tau}G_{k}G_{\tau}^{-1}G_{s_{i,j}\nu}
for some
k\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
and
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
s_{i,j}=\tau s_{k}\tau^{-1}
. Then, by applying Lemma
4.8
, we get
F_{I}G_{\nu}=wF_{I}F_{i,j}G_{s_{i,j}\nu}=wF_{I}G_{s_{i,j}\nu}
. Since
\ell(s_{i,j}\nu)<\ell(\nu)
, we can repeat the process for all inversions of
\nu
occurring in

, until the length of the resulting permutation is minimal. This proves the proposition.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

By Proposition
3.2
, if
(I,v)
and
(J,w)
are two distinct coprime pairs, then the products
G_{v}F_{I}
and
G_{w}F_{J}
are necessarily distinct. In other words, the coprimality condition ensures that each element in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
corresponds to a unique coprime pair, and thus no two different pairs can yield the same product. This guarantees that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
consists of distinct elements.

Next, we introduce a deformation of Tanabe’s tensor representation for the party algebra. See
[
45
, Subsection 2.1]
and compare with
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
. This representation will be used to show that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is linearly independent.

Let
m
be a positive integer, and let
V
be the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space spanned by
B=\{v_{i}^{r}\mid i,r\in\ldbrack 1,m\rdbrack\}
. As usual, we denote by
B^{\otimes n}
the standard basis of
V^{\otimes n}
associated to
B
, consisting of the vectors:

v_{I}^{R}:=v_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{i_{n}}^{r_{n}},

where
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
R=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\in\ldbrack 1,m\rdbrack^{n}
. When there is no risk of confusion, we simply write
v_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}^{r_{1}\ldots r_{n}}
instead of
v_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})}
.

Define
\widetilde{F},\widetilde{G}\in\operatorname{End}(V^{\otimes 2})
by

\widetilde{F}(v_{i}^{r}\otimes v_{j}^{s})=\begin{cases}q^{2}v_{i}^{r}\otimes v_{j}^{s}&\text{if }r=s\\
0&\text{if }r\neq s,\end{cases}\qquad\widetilde{G}(v_{i}^{r}\otimes v_{j}^{s})=\begin{cases}pq\,v_{j}^{s}\otimes v_{i}^{r}&\text{if }r=s\\
pq\,v_{j}^{s}\otimes v_{i}^{r}&\text{if }r\neq s,\,i>j\\
q\sqrt{p}\,v_{j}^{s}\otimes v_{i}^{r}&\text{if }r\neq s,\,i=j\\
qv_{j}^{s}\otimes v_{i}^{r}&\text{if }r\neq s,\,i<j.\end{cases}

These operators satisfy the following Party-Hecke relations:

(58)

\widetilde{F}^{\,2}=q^{2}\widetilde{F},\qquad\widetilde{G}\widetilde{F}=\widetilde{F}\widetilde{G}=pq\widetilde{F},\qquad\widetilde{G}^{\,2}=pq^{2}+p(p-1)\widetilde{F}.

For
i\in\ldbrack 1,n-1\rdbrack
, define
\widetilde{F}_{i}
(resp.
\widetilde{G}_{i}
) as the endomorphism of
V^{\otimes n}
acting by
\widetilde{F}
(resp.
\widetilde{G}
) on the tensor factors in positions
(i,i+1)
, and as the identity otherwise. Evidently, we have:

(59)

\widetilde{F}_{k}\widetilde{F}_{l}=\widetilde{F}_{l}\widetilde{F}_{k},\quad\widetilde{F}_{i}\widetilde{G}_{j}=\widetilde{G}_{j}\widetilde{F}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{G}_{i}\widetilde{G}_{j}=\widetilde{G}_{j}\widetilde{G}_{i}\quad\text{for $|i-j|>1$.}

Remark 4.12
.

Since each
\widetilde{G}_{i}
acts, up to a scalar, as the elementary transposition
s_{i}
, it follows that there exist scalars
\lambda_{I,R}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(\widetilde{G}_{i_{1}}\cdots\widetilde{G}_{i_{j}})(v_{I}^{R})=\lambda_{I,R}v_{s(I)}^{s(R)},\quad\text{where}\quad s:=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{j}}.

Theorem 4.13
.

The mapping
G_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{G}_{i}
,
F_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{F}_{i}
defines a representation
\psi_{p,q}
of
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
in
V^{\otimes n}
. In particular,
\psi_{1,q}
is the Tanabe representation.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
n=3
. It suffices to show that the assignment respects the defining relations of
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
, namely (
53
)–(
56
).

From (
58
) we deduce that relations (
53
) and (
55
) hold for the operators
\widetilde{G}_{i}
’s and
\widetilde{F}_{i}
’s. Together with (
59
), this shows that the only relations left to be verified are the braid relation in (
54
) and the second relation in (
56
).

We begin with the second relation in (
56
). It is enough to prove that, for every basis element
v_{ijk}^{rst}
of
V^{\otimes 3}
, one has:

(60)

\big(\widetilde{F}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rst})=\big(\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\widetilde{F}_{2}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rst}).

If all upper indices of
v_{ijk}^{rst}
are equal, that is,
r=s=t
, then both
\widetilde{F}_{1}
and
\widetilde{F}_{2}
act as scalar multiplication by
q^{2}
, and so (
60
) is immediately satisfied. If instead all upper indices are distinct, both
\widetilde{F}_{1}
and
\widetilde{F}_{2}
act as zero, and again (
60
) holds. Thus, we only need to consider the three remaining cases:
r=s\neq t
,
r\neq s=t
and
r=t\neq s
.

In the first case we compute

\big(\widetilde{F}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rrt})=\big(\widetilde{F}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\big)(pqv_{jik}^{rrt})=pq\widetilde{F}_{1}\big(\widetilde{G}_{2}(v_{jik}^{rrt})\big)=0,

since
\widetilde{G}_{2}(v_{jik}^{rrt})
has upper indices of type
rtr
, on which
\tilde{F}_{1}
acts as zero. On the other hand,

\big(\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\widetilde{F}_{2}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rrt})=\big(\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\big)(0)=0.

Therefore (
60
) holds in this case. The other two cases are analogous. We conclude that the second relation in (
56
) is satisfied.

It remains to check the braid relation in (
54
), that is,

\big(\widetilde{G}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rst})=\big(\widetilde{G}_{2}\widetilde{G}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\big)(v_{ijk}^{rst}).

We proceed by distinguishing cases according to the upper indices
(r,s,t)
: (i)
r=s=t
, (ii)
r=s\neq t
, (iii)
r\neq s=t
, (iv)
r=t\neq s
, and (v)
r,s,t
all distinct.

Case (i)
. A direct calculation gives
(G_{1}G_{2}G_{1})(v_{ijk}^{rrr})=(G_{2}G_{1}G_{2})(v_{ijk}^{rrr})=p^{3}q^{3}\,v_{kji}^{rrr}
.

Case (ii)
. We subdivide into five types of lower indices
(i,j,k)
into the following five types: (a)
i=j=k
, (b)
i\neq j\neq k\neq i
, (c)
i=j\neq k
,
(d)

i\neq j=k
, and
(e)

i=k\neq j
.

For type (a), one obtains

\displaystyle(G_{1}G_{2}G_{1})(v_{iii}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=(G_{1}G_{2})(pq\,v_{iii}^{rrt})=pq^{2}\sqrt{p}\,G_{1}(v_{iii}^{rtr})=q^{3}p^{2}\,v_{iii}^{trr},

\displaystyle(G_{2}G_{1}G_{2})(v_{iii}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=(G_{2}G_{1})(q\sqrt{p}\,v_{iii}^{rtr})=q^{2}pG_{2}(v_{iii}^{trr})=q^{3}p^{2}v_{iii}^{trr}.

For type (b), we only analyze the most representative case, that is,
i<j,j>k
, and
i<k
:

\displaystyle(G_{1}G_{2}G_{1})(v_{ijk}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=(G_{1}G_{2})(pq\,v_{jik}^{rrt})=p\sqrt{p}q^{2}\,G_{1}(v_{jki}^{rtr})=p^{2}q^{3}\,v_{kji}^{trr},

\displaystyle(G_{2}G_{1}G_{2})(v_{ijk}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=(G_{2}G_{1})(q\sqrt{p}\,v_{ikj}^{rtr})=pq^{2}G_{2}(v_{kij}^{trr})=p^{2}q^{3}v_{kji}^{trr}.

For type (c), we will first analyze the possibility
i=j<k
:

\displaystyle(G_{1}G_{2}G_{1})(v_{iik}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=pq\,(G_{1}G_{2})(v_{iik}^{rrt})=pq^{2}\,G_{1}(v_{iki}^{rtr})=pq^{3}\,v_{kii}^{trr},

\displaystyle(G_{2}G_{1}G_{2})(v_{iik}^{rrt})

\displaystyle=q(G_{2}G_{1})(v_{iki}^{rtr})=q^{2}G_{2}(v_{kii}^{trr})=pq^{3}v_{kii}^{trr}.

The other possibility,
i=j>k
, is again a direct computation.

Cases (iii) (iv) are both analogous to case (ii).

Case (v)
. Here all five types (a) to (e) of lower indices occur. Most are straightforward, except for the subcase
i<j>k<i
in (b), which we compute in detail:

\displaystyle(G_{1}G_{2}G_{1})(v_{ijk}^{rst})=q(G_{1}G_{2})(v_{jik}^{srt})=pq^{2}\,G_{1}(v_{jki}^{str})=p^{2}q^{3}\,v_{kji}^{tsr},

\displaystyle(G_{2}G_{1}G_{2})(v_{ijk}^{rst})=pq\,(G_{2}G_{1})(v_{ikj}^{rts})=p^{2}q^{2}\,G_{2}(v_{kij}^{trs})=p^{2}q^{3}v_{kji}^{tsr}.

Thus, the relation holds in the most delicate situation, and therefore in all others as well.

Hence, the assignment
G_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{G}_{i}
,
F_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{F}_{i}
defines a representation of
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
.
∎

Theorem 4.14
.

For
n\leq m^{2}
, the representation
\psi_{p,q}
is faithful. Consequently,
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a linear basis for
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
, and so
\dim\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)=|\mathcal{P}_{n}|
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by showing that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a linear basis and that its image under
\psi_{p,q}
remains linearly independent. By Proposition
4.10
, to prove that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a basis, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is linearly independent. Set
V
to be the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space with basis
B=\{v_{i}^{r}\mid i,r\in\ldbrack 1,m\rdbrack\}
, where
n\leq m^{2}
. Then
V^{\otimes n}
has a basis
B^{\otimes n}
consisting of vectors of the form:

v_{I}^{R}:=v_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{i_{n}}^{r_{n}}.

Suppose we have the linear combination:

\sum_{(I,w)\atop\text{coprime}}\lambda_{(I,w)}G_{w}F_{I}=0.

Then, applying
\psi_{p,q}
, we obtain

(61)

\sum_{(I,w)\atop\text{coprime}}\lambda_{(I,w)}\widetilde{G}_{w}\widetilde{F}_{I}=0.

Put
v:=v^{\prime}\otimes v^{\prime\prime}
, where
v^{\prime}=v_{1}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n}^{1}
,
v^{\prime\prime}=v_{m}^{m}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}^{m}
with
m^{2}-n
factors. Evaluating the linear combination (
61
) at
v
, and observing that
\psi_{p,q}
acts trivially on
v^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain:

0=\sum_{(I,w)\atop\text{coprime}}\lambda_{(I,w)}\widetilde{G}_{w}\widetilde{F}_{I}(v)\otimes v^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{(I,w)\atop\text{coprime}}\lambda_{(I,w)}a_{I}\widetilde{G}_{w}(v)\otimes v^{\prime\prime},

where each
a_{I}
is certain positive integer. Notice that the vectors
\widetilde{G}_{w}(v)\otimes v^{\prime\prime}
are elements of the basis
B^{\otimes n}
. Therefore, using Remark
4.11
and Remark
4.12
, it follows that all coefficients
\lambda_{(I,w)}a_{I}
must be
0
. Since each
a_{I}
is positive, we conclude that
\lambda_{(I,w)}=0
for all
(I,w)
.
∎

4.4.
Generic representations

As noted in
[
40
, Subsection 3.5]
, by
[
44
, Corollary 9.4]
, the algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]=\operatorname{P}_{n}(1,1)
is semisimple. Moreover, since
\mathbb{C}
is an algebraically closed field, applying the Wedderburn–Artin theorem (cf.
[
18
, Chapter 5]
) yields that
\operatorname{P}_{n}(1,1)
is split semisimple.

Theorem 4.15
.

The Party-Hecke algebra
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is generically semisimple; that is, there exists a nonempty Zariski open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
for which
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is semisimple.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Lemma 1.6]
, the property of being split semisimple is generic; that is, it holds on a nonempty Zariski open subset of the parameter space. As explained in
[
15
, Section 1]
, since
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is a free module of finite rank over
\mathbb{C}[p,q]
(Theorem
4.14
), and since the specialization
\operatorname{P}_{n}(1,1)
is split semisimple, it follows that there exists a nonempty Zariski open subset
U
of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is split semisimple for all
(p,q)\in U
. In particular, since
\mathbb{C}
is algebraically closed, then
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
is generically semisimple.
∎

5.
The Party-Hecke algebra as a quotient of the bt-algebra and related quotients

In this section, we establish that the Party-Hecke algebra is a quotient of the bt-algebra (Subsection
5.1
). Furthermore, we determine two natural quotients of Temperley–Lieb type (Subsection
5.2
), and introduce a partition Temperley–Lieb-like quotient (Subsection
5.3
).

5.1.
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
as a quotient of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\sqrt{p})

The rescaling
H_{i}=(q\sqrt{p})^{\,-1}G_{i}
yields another presentation of the Party-Hecke algebra. Precisely, consider the generators
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
satisfying (
55
), together with generators
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n-1}
satisfying the same defining relations as in
\operatorname{P}_{n}(p,q)
, except that the relations in (
53
) are replaced, that is,

(62)

\displaystyle H_{i}^{2}=1+q^{-2}(p-1)F_{i},

\displaystyle\quad H_{i}F_{i}=F_{i}H_{i}=\sqrt{p}F_{i},

(63)

\displaystyle H_{i}H_{j}=H_{j}H_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|>1,

\displaystyle\quad H_{i}H_{j}H_{i}=H_{j}H_{j}H_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|=1,

(64)

\displaystyle H_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}H_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|\neq 1,

\displaystyle\quad H_{i}H_{j}F_{i}=F_{j}H_{i}H_{j},\quad\text{if }|i-j|=1.

We denote by
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
the Party-Hecke algebra with the above presentation. Note that:

(65)

H_{i}^{-1}=H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{i}.

By Proposition
3.1
, it is desirable to define a deformation of
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]
as a quotient of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(u,v)
. Owing to the definition of the congruence
R
, such a deformation can be obtained by deforming the defining generators of
R
. Thus, we consider the
x
-deformation given by
g_{i}e_{i}=xe_{i}
for all
i
. Multiplying (
35
) by
e_{i}
, we obtain
x^{2}e_{i}=e_{i}+(u-1)e_{i}+(v-1)xe_{i}
. Therefore, the parameter
x
must satisfy the quadratic equation
x^{2}-(v-1)x-u=0
. In particular, one solution is
x=u=v
, and so we make the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

Set
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{P}(\sqrt{p}):=\mathcal{E}_{n}(\sqrt{p})/I
, where
I
is the two-sided ideal generated by
g_{i}e_{i}-\sqrt{p}e_{i}
for all
i
. Whenever no confusion arises, we will not distinguish between
g_{i}
(resp.
e_{i}
) and their images in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{P}(\sqrt{p})
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that
e_{2}g_{2}=(g_{1}g_{2}e_{1}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1})g_{2}=g_{1}g_{2}e_{1}g_{1}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}=g_{1}g_{2}e_{1}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}=e_{2}
. Hence, an inductive argument shows that
I
is generated by
g_{1}e_{1}-\sqrt{p}e_{1}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The map
g_{i}\mapsto H_{i}
,
e_{i}\mapsto q^{-2}F_{i}
defines an isomorphism from
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{P}(\sqrt{p})
to
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
.

Proof.

To show that the map defines a homomorphism of algebras, it suffices to check that the
H_{i}
’s and
F_{i}
’s satisfy the defining relations of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(u)
together with
e_{i}g_{i}=ue_{i}
for all
i
. These verifications follow directly, except for relations in (
34
) and (
35
). Since
u=v=\sqrt{p}
, relation (
35
) becomes
g_{i}^{2}=1+(\sqrt{p}-1)e_{i}(1+g_{i})
. Under our map, the left-hand side goes to
H_{i}^{2}=1+q^{-2}(p-1)F_{i}
, while the right-hand side goes to

1+(\sqrt{p}-1)q^{-2}F_{i}(1+H_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^2}}}{{=}}1+q^{-2}(p-1)F_{i}.

So, the relation (
35
) is preserved.

For the first relation in (
34
) we need to prove
H_{i}H_{j}F_{i}=F_{j}H_{i}H_{j}
for
|i-j|>1
, or equivalently,
H_{j}F_{i}H_{j}^{-1}=H_{i}^{-1}F_{j}H_{i}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle H_{j}F_{i}H_{j}^{-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^-1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle H_{j}F_{i}(H_{j}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BP2}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle H_{i}F_{j}H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})H_{j}F_{j}F_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^2}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle H_{i}F_{j}H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})\sqrt{p}F_{j}F_{i}.

Since
\sqrt{p}F_{j}F_{i}=F_{j}\sqrt{p}F_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^2}}}{{=}}F_{j}F_{i}H_{i}=F_{i}F_{j}H_{i}
, then

\displaystyle H_{j}F_{i}H_{j}^{-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^-1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle H_{i}F_{j}H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{i}F_{j}H_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{i})F_{j}H_{i}\,\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^-1}}}{{=}}\,\,\,H_{i}^{-1}F_{j}H_{i}.

So, the first relation in (
34
) is preserved.

For the second relation in (
34
), we begin by pointing out that to prove this relation, it suffices to first show
H_{i}F_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}
when
|i-j|=1
, or equivalently,
F_{i}F_{j}=H_{i}^{-1}F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle H_{i}^{-1}F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^-1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle(H_{i}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{i})F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{i}F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{i}F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BP2},\eqref{Par1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle H_{j}F_{i}H_{j}F_{j}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})F_{j}F_{i}H_{i}F_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^2}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{p}H_{j}F_{i}F_{j}-q^{-2}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})\sqrt{p}F_{j}F_{i}F_{j}.

Since
\sqrt{p}H_{j}F_{i}F_{j}=\sqrt{p}H_{j}F_{j}F_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{H^2}}}{{=}}pF_{i}F_{j}
and
F_{j}F_{i}F_{j}=q^{2}F_{i}F_{j}
, we obtain

H_{i}^{-1}F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}=pF_{i}F_{j}-(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{\,-1})\sqrt{p}F_{i}F_{j}=F_{i}F_{j}.

Similarly, one proves
F_{i}F_{j}H_{i}=F_{j}H_{i}F_{j}
. So, the second relation in (
34
) is preserved.

In summary, the map is a homomorphism of algebras. Moreover, it is bijective, with inverse given by
H_{i}\mapsto g_{i}
and
F_{i}\mapsto q^{2}e_{i}
. Hence, the proof is concluded.
∎

5.2.
Two Temperley–Lieb-like quotients

In this subsection, we use the presentation
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
of the Party-Hecke algebra.
Define

(66)

T_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(H_{i}+q^{-2}(1-\sqrt{p})F_{i}+1\Big)\in\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q).

A straightforward computation shows that
T_{i}^{2}=T_{i}
. We now have the following result.

Proposition 5.4
.

The idempotents
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n-1}
, together with
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
define a presentation
\operatorname{P}(q)
of the Party-Hecke algebra, whose defining relations are:

(67)

\displaystyle T_{i}^{2}=T_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\quad T_{i}T_{j}=T_{j}T_{i}\quad\text{for }|i-j|>1,

(68)

\displaystyle 4T_{i}T_{j}T_{i}-T_{i}

\displaystyle=4T_{j}T_{i}T_{j}-T_{j}\quad\text{for }|i-j|=1,

(69)

\displaystyle F_{i}^{2}=q^{2}F_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\quad F_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}F_{i}\,,

(70)

\displaystyle T_{i}F_{i}=F_{i}T_{i}=F_{i},

\displaystyle\quad T_{i}F_{j}=F_{j}T_{i}\quad\text{for }|i-j|>1,

(71)

\displaystyle 4T_{i}T_{j}F_{i}-2T_{j}F_{i}-F_{i}

\displaystyle=4F_{j}T_{i}T_{j}-2F_{j}T_{i}-F_{j}\quad\text{for }|i-j|=1.

Proof.

Since each
T_{i}
is a linear combination of
1
,
H_{i}
and
F_{i}
, it follows that the Party-Hecke algebra is linearly generated by the
T_{i}
’s and
F_{i}
’s. Moreover, it is routine to check that the defining relations of
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
correspond, in terms of
T_{i}
and
F_{i}
, precisely to relations (
67
)–(
71
). This completes the proof.
∎

The presentation in Proposition
5.4
is analogous to one of the Iwahori–Hecke algebra that yields the Temperley–Lieb algebra as a quotient
[
46

]
. It is therefore natural to define a Temperley–Lieb quotient of the Party-Hecke algebra via the presentation of Proposition

\operatorname{P}_{n}(q)/I\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{P}_{n}(q)/J,

Proposition 5.5
.

The quotient
\operatorname{P}_{n}(q)/I
is the algebra
\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)
with
u=7\pm 4\sqrt{3}
.

Proof.

Multiplying the equation
4T_{i}T_{j}F_{i}=2T_{j}F_{i}+F_{i}
on the left by
T_{i}
, and using (
67
) and (

. Multiplying this equation on the left by
T_{j}
gives
T_{j}F_{i}=0
, and hence
F_{i}=0
. By (
30
) and (
31
), this shows that
\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)
is obtained with

∎

Proposition 5.6
.

The quotient
\operatorname{P}_{n}(q)/J

(resp.

On the other hand, multiplying again

4T_{j}T_{i}T_{j}=T_{j}

6.
Future work

The structural results established in this paper lay the groundwork for further research directions. In this section, we briefly outline two such directions: the representation theory of the related monoid

6.1.
Irreducible representations of
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}

Just as the maximal subgroups of
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are determined by the maximal subgroups of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, it is natural to expect that the irreducible representations of
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}
can be obtained by applying the methods in
[
40
, Section 3]
. As a first step, we note that, similarly to the party monoid
[
40
, Proposition 3.5]
, the
J

T\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are also indexed by the partitions of
n
. Indeed, for
e,e^{\prime}\in P_{n}
, one has
e^{\prime}=s(e)=ses^{-1}
for some
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
if and only if
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}e^{\prime}T\mathfrak{S}_{n}=T\mathfrak{S}_{n}ses^{-1}T\mathfrak{S}_{n}=T\mathfrak{S}_{n}eT\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, since
s
is invertible. Thus, by Remark
3.5
,

e\equiv_{J}e^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\|e\|=\|e^{\prime}\|.

On the other hand, if
g=es\in T\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
e\in P_{n}
and
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then
g\equiv_{J}e
since
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}gT\mathfrak{S}_{n}=T\mathfrak{S}_{n}esT\mathfrak{S}_{n}=T\mathfrak{S}_{n}eT\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Consequently, if
g=es
and
h=e^{\prime}s^{\prime}
with
\|e\|=\|e^{\prime}\|
, then
g\equiv_{J}h
; conversely, if
g\equiv_{J}h
, then
\|e\|=\|e^{\prime}\|
since
e\equiv_{J}g\equiv_{J}h\equiv_{J}e^{\prime}
. Hence,

g\equiv_{J}h\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\|e\|=\|e^{\prime}\|.

This establishes the claimed bijection.

Following this observation, for each partition
\lambda
of
n
, we write
J_{\lambda}
for the
J
-class of any
es\in T\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
\|e\|=\lambda
. Consequently,

T\mathfrak{S}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}}J_{\lambda},

where
\operatorname{Par}_{n}
denotes the set of partitions of
n
.

Furthermore, for
e\in P_{n}
with
\|e\|=\lambda
, one can easily check that
J_{\lambda}=\{ses^{\prime}\mid s,s^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\}
. To see this, note that for
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have
T\mathfrak{S}_{n}ses^{\prime}T\mathfrak{S}_{n}=T\mathfrak{S}_{n}eT\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, so
ses^{\prime}\equiv_{J}e
in
J_{\lambda}
. Conversely, if
e^{\prime}s^{\prime}\in J_{\lambda}
with
e^{\prime}\in P_{n}
and
s^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then there exists
s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
e^{\prime}=ses^{-1}
, whence
e^{\prime}s^{\prime}=se(s^{-1}s^{\prime})
. See Figure
14
for an illustration of this structure.

6.2.
Jones-type invariants of virtual knots

Because the Party-Hecke algebra is a quotient of the bt-algebra (Proposition
4.4
), which is indeed a knot algebra, one might wonder if the Party-Hecke algebra is also a knot algebra. In fact, we believe that the Party-Hecke algebra could be a knot algebra, as it could define a Jones-type invariant for virtual knots. This is based on the fact that the trace supported by the bt-algebra can be shown to pass to the Party-Hecke algebra (cf.
[
27
, Theorem 7.7]
,
[
22
, Section 5]
). This property, together with the representation of the virtual braid group explained below, may enable the construction of such an invariant.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, define
V_{i}=aH_{i}+bF_{i}
. A direct computation shows that the following relations hold in
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
.

(1)

V_{i}^{2}=q^{-2}(pa^{2}+2\sqrt{p}q^{2}ab+q^{4}b^{2}-a^{2})F_{i}+a^{2}
.

(2)

V_{i}V_{j}V_{i}=V_{j}V_{i}V_{j}
, for
|i-j|=1
.

(3)

V_{i}V_{j}H_{i}=H_{j}V_{i}V_{j}
, for
|i-j|=1
.

Recall that the virtual braid group
V\!B_{n}
is defined by the generators
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}
, which satisfy the braid relations (
6
), the generators
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}
, which satisfy the symmetric group relations (
3
), along with the following mixed relations:

(75)

\sigma_{i}s_{j}=s_{j}\sigma_{i}\quad\text{if }|i-j|>1,\quad\text{and}\quad s_{i}s_{j}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}s_{i}s_{j}\quad\text{if }|i-j|=1.

One proves the mapping
\sigma_{i}\mapsto H_{i}
,
s_{i}\mapsto V_{i}
defines a representation of
V\!B_{n}
in
\operatorname{P}_{n}^{\prime}(p,q)
, if

a=1\quad\text{and}\quad b=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{p}}{q^{2}},\quad\text{or}\quad a=-1\quad\text{and}\quad b=-\frac{1\pm\sqrt{p}}{q^{2}}.
