A Theory of Composition and Duality of Extremal Optimal Fixed-Point Algorithms
TaeHo Yoon, Benjamin Grimmer

Abstract
In this work, we reveal a rich combinatorial structure underlying exact minimax optimal algorithms for classical nonexpansive fixed-point problems. This viewpoint unifies all extremal optimal methods and provides a systematic and practical framework for designing new algorithms via diagrams. Specifically, we study fixed-step algorithms represented by a lower triangular matrix H H , and show that the set of optimal ( N − 1 ) (N-1) -step algorithms has exactly ( N − 1 ) ! (N-1)! vertices (extremal algorithms), each of which naturally corresponds to an arc diagram, a graph that encodes its convergence proof. Using these arc diagrams, we can compose, decompose, and analyze the properties of distinct optimal vertex algorithms. Furthermore, we determine when the H-dual operation, given by taking the anti-diagonal transpose of H H , preserves the optimality of a vertex algorithm, and in such cases we characterize the convergence proof of the dual algorithm. Based on this machinery, we develop new optimal algorithms with quasi-anytime guarantees; that is, they admit an increasing integer sequence { m k } k = 0 ∞ \{m_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty} such that each y m k y_{m_{k}} has the optimal residual guarantee, and are additionally robust to fixed-point operators that violate nonexpansiveness.

Introduction

Consider the class of nonexpansive operators
{\mathbb{T}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
on Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}
, and the associated fixed-point problems

\displaystyle\underset{y\in{\mathcal{X}}}{\text{find}}\quad y={\mathbb{T}}(y).

(1)

In first-order optimization, the problem of solving (
1
) efficiently is often formulated in the form

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathfrak{A}}{\textrm{minimize}}\,\,\,\underset{({\mathbb{T}},y_{0})}{\textrm{maximize}}\,\,\,{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathcal{A}},{\mathbb{T}},y_{0},N\right)

(2)

where the maximization is taken over all nonexpansive operators
{\mathbb{T}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
with at least one fixed point
y_{\star}
and over initial points
y_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
\mathfrak{A}
is the algorithm class of interest, and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a measure of error after
N
evaluations of the operator
{\mathbb{T}}
, where
{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot)=0
indicates convergence.

A natural, standard error metric for nonexpansive fixed-point problems is the squared norm of the fixed-point residual
\left\|y-{\mathbb{T}}(y)\right\|^{2}
.
For this setting, the optimal value of (
2
) is well-known;
[
23
]
shows that with
{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathcal{A}},{\mathbb{T}},y_{0},N\right)=\frac{\left\|y_{N-1}-{\mathbb{T}}(y_{N-1})\right\|^{2}}{\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}
, one has
\underset{({\mathbb{T}},y_{0})}{\sup}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathcal{A}},{\mathbb{T}},y_{0},N\right)\geq\frac{4}{N^{2}}
for any deterministic first-order algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, and
[
20
,
14
,
28
]
present algorithms exactly matching this lower bound (we use
y_{N-1}
because computing the fixed-point residual requires an additional evaluation of
{\mathbb{T}}
).
However, identifying the
solutions
to (
2
), i.e., understanding which
{\mathcal{A}}
achieve
minimax optimal
convergence is a deeper question.
This has only recently been answered by
[
29
]
for the class
\mathfrak{A}_{\textrm{H}}
of
H-matrix representable
algorithms, which use fixed step-size updates

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k}h_{k+1,j+1}\left(y_{j}-{\mathbb{T}}(y_{j})\right)

(3)

where
h_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}
are predetermined for
k=1,\dots,N-1
and
j=1,\dots,k
.
More precisely,
[
29
]
characterized the set
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
of all
(N-1)\times(N-1)
lower triangular matrices
H=[h_{k,j}]
of these step-sizes whose corresponding algorithms are minimax optimal, solving (
2
).

While the characterization provided in
[
29
]
is exhaustive, it has limited transparency.
Their description of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
is based on algebraic invariance and inequalities, rather than an explicit parametrization which can be used for constructing previously unknown elements of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
and analyzing them.
This motivates a framework that enables a more concrete treatment of the set
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, from which one can develop new algorithms and predict their properties.

Main contributions and organization.

We provide a tangible theory of optimal
vertex
algorithms, the extremal elements of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
with the simplest possible proof structure.
It translates vertex algorithms into elementary combinatorial objects and uses this viewpoint as a systematic language for understanding and discovering algorithms.
Specifically, our contributions are organized as follows.

•

Section
3
.
We develop the foundational theory of optimal vertex algorithms.
We establish a complete correspondence between vertices of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
and
arc diagrams
, and equip this representation with a gluing operation and decomposition, which explain how vertex algorithms are constructed and how their intermediate guarantees arise.

–

Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

We show that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
has precisely
(N-1)!
vertices, each uniquely determined by the set of inequalities used in its convergence proof (
Theorem

1
).
Each vertex algorithm is identified with an arc diagram on
N
nodes, a specific form of undirected tree graph encoding the sparsity pattern of the convergence proof (
Definition

1
).
Given an arc diagram, the associated optimal vertex algorithm’s H-matrix
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
can be computed via Meta Algorithms
1
and
3
.

–

Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

We introduce a gluing operation that composes arc diagrams or H-matrices to produce a larger vertex algorithm whose update rule and convergence proof inherit those of their components (Definitions
2
–
4
&
Theorem

2
).
Conversely, we define decomposable algorithms and diagrams, which arise from gluing smaller components (Definitions
5
–
6
).
A decomposable vertex algorithm enjoys optimal guarantees at intermediate iterates, whose indices can be read directly from its arc diagram (
Corollary

2
).

•

Section
4
.
We identify the condition for an optimal vertex algorithm to remain optimal under the so-called H-dual operation
[
15
,
28
]
, which is the anti-diagonal transpose of
H
.
While it is known that H-dual preserves a fixed-point algorithm’s performance in special cases, it has been only partially understood when that symmetry manifests.
We provide a clear and complete characterization of it for optimal vertex algorithms: H-dual optimality is equivalent to the arc diagram being non-crossing (
Theorem

4
), a purely combinatorial property.
Furthermore, we show that when this is the case, the H-dual is another vertex algorithm whose arc diagram can be computed via Meta Algorithm
2
.

•

Section
5
.
We introduce two classes of novel algorithms designed via arc diagrams:
Repeated Dual OHM (RDO)
and
Fractal Self-Dual Method (FSDM)
.
Both algorithms are quasi-anytime optimal, i.e., there exists an increasing integer sequence
\{m_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that each
y_{m_{k}}
is optimal.
We additionally show that having more intermediate-iterate guarantees comes at a cost of reducing an algorithm’s robustness, and OHM and Dual-OHM are at the opposite extremes of this trade-off spectrum (Propositions
7
–
8
), while the new algorithms, RDO and FSDM, lie in between.

We defer proofs involving substantial linear algebra or algebraic verification to the appendix.
While these are necessary for completeness, they are not the main conceptual contributions of the work.
This organization keeps the main text focused on the central ideas and preserves the flow of the exposition without interruptions from lengthy technical proofs.

2
Background and prior results on optimal algorithms

This section covers preliminary material that is needed for developing new results in subsequent sections.

2.1
Basic definitions and notations

For
y\in{\mathcal{X}}
and an operator
{\mathbb{T}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
, we denote
{\mathbb{T}}(y)={\mathbb{T}}y
for simplicity.
We say that an operator
{\mathbb{T}}
is nonexpansive if
\|{\mathbb{T}}x-{\mathbb{T}}y\|\leq\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
If
y_{\star}\in{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies
y_{\star}={\mathbb{T}}y_{\star}
, it is a fixed point of
{\mathbb{T}}
.
For
y\in{\mathcal{X}}
, its fixed-point residual is the vector
y-{\mathbb{T}}y
, which is
0\in{\mathcal{X}}
if and only if
y
is a fixed point of
{\mathbb{T}}
.
Hence, the norm of the fixed-point residual is a natural measure of convergence for the fixed-point problem (
1
).

Define
{\mathbb{A}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
as the set-valued operator (i.e.,
{\mathbb{A}}x:={\mathbb{A}}(x)\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
for
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
) uniquely characterized by
{\mathbb{T}}=2({\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}-{\mathbb{I}}
, where
{\mathbb{I}}
is the identity operator on
{\mathcal{X}}
.
Then,
{\mathbb{A}}
is maximally monotone:
\left\langle x-y,u-v\right\rangle\geq 0
for any
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
u\in{\mathbb{A}}x,v\in{\mathbb{A}}y
, and the graph
\mathrm{Gra}\,{\mathbb{A}}=\{(x,u)\,|\,x\in{\mathcal{X}},u\in{\mathbb{A}}x\}
of
{\mathbb{A}}
cannot be extended to a strictly larger graph of a monotone operator, which is a standard fact in convex analysis as shown in
[
3
, Proposition 23.8]
or
[
4
]
.
This establishes a one-to-one correspondence between maximally monotone operators and nonexpansive operators.
Furthermore, given
y\in{\mathcal{X}}
, if we write
x=\frac{y+{\mathbb{T}}y}{2}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{T}})(y)
, then

x=\frac{y+{\mathbb{T}}y}{2}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{T}})(y)\iff x\in({\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}(y)\iff y\in x+{\mathbb{A}}x\iff y-x\in{\mathbb{A}}x.

In particular, in the H-matrix representation (
3
) of an iterative algorithm, we let
x_{k+1}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{T}})(y_{k})
for
k=0,\dots,N-1
and
g_{k+1}=y_{k}-x_{k+1}\in{\mathbb{A}}x_{k+1}
.
Because
y_{j}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{j}=2g_{j+1}
, we can rewrite (
3
) as

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k}2h_{k+1,j+1}g_{j+1}.

(4)

Furthermore, the nonexpansivity inequality between the iterates
y_{k}
and
y_{j}
is a rescaled monotonicity inequality between
x_{k+1}
and
x_{j+1}
:

\displaystyle\left\|y_{k}-y_{j}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\mathbb{T}}y_{k}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{j}\right\|^{2}=4\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{j+1},g_{k+1}-g_{j+1}\right\rangle\geq 0

(5)

where
k,j=0,\dots,N-1
.

2.2
Complexity lower bound

The following result from
[
23
]
establishes a tight lower bound on the dimension-independent worst-case rate with respect to the squared fixed-point residual.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 4.6 of
[
23
]
)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given, and let
R>0
. Then, for any iterative algorithm making deterministic updates using
N-1
operator evaluations, there exists a nonexpansive operator
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 2N-2
that has a fixed point
y_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\|=R
, for which
\left\|y_{N-1}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{N-1}\right\|^{2}\geq\frac{4\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{N^{2}}
.

The rest of the paper will focus on algorithms that match this lower bound without any gap, i.e., exact minimax optimal algorithms for our nonexpansive fixed-point problem setting.

2.3
Optimal Halpern Method (OHM) and its H-dual

The Halpern iteration
[
12
]
is a classical algorithm that interpolates the operator evaluation
{\mathbb{T}}y_{k}
with the initial point
y_{0}
.
Two relatively recent works
[
14
,
20
]
independently showed that the following version of Halpern iteration:

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=\frac{1}{k+2}y_{0}+\frac{k+1}{k+2}{\mathbb{T}}y_{k}

(OHM)

which we refer to as
Optimal Halpern Method (OHM)
, converges at the rate

\displaystyle\left\|y_{N-1}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{N-1}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{N^{2}}

(opt-rate)

matching the lower bound of
Lemma

1

[
23
]
; hence it is minimax optimal.

The H-matrix for OHM can be obtained by rewriting its first
N-1
iterations in the form (
3
) (as done in
[
14
]
):

\displaystyle\left(H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1)\right)_{k,j}=\begin{cases}-\frac{j}{k(k+1)}&\text{if }j<k\\
\frac{k}{k+1}&\text{if }j=k\end{cases}=\begin{bmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\\
\nicefrac{{-1}}{{6}}&\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{N(N-1)}}&-\nicefrac{{2}}{{N(N-1)}}&\dots&\nicefrac{{(N-1)}}{{N}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}.

Given any lower triangular H-matrix
H=[h_{k,j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}
and the associated fixed-point algorithm defined via (
3
), we consider its
H-dual
, obtained by reflecting
H
along its anti-diagonal:

\displaystyle H^{\textsf{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{N-j,N-k}\end{bmatrix}_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\dots,N-1\\
j=1,\dots,k\end{subarray}}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{N-1,N-1}\\
h_{N-1,N-2}&h_{N-2,N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
h_{N-1,1}&h_{N-2,1}&\cdots&h_{1,1}\end{bmatrix}.

(H-dual)

This is also an
(N-1)\times(N-1)
lower triangular matrix, and therefore defines a fixed-step algorithm.
Furthermore, it is known in special cases that the H-dual operation preserves the worst-case performance of certain fixed-point algorithms
[
28
]
.
OHM is an example of such a correspondence—its H-dual, called
Dual-OHM
, has the H-matrix

\displaystyle\left(H_{\text{Dual-OHM}}(N-1)\right)_{k,j}=\begin{cases}-\frac{N-k}{(N-j)(N-j+1)}&\text{if }j<k\\
\frac{N-k}{N-k+1}&\text{if }j=k\end{cases}=\begin{bmatrix}\nicefrac{{(N-1)}}{{N}}\\
\nicefrac{{-(N-2)}}{{N(N-1)}}&\nicefrac{{(N-2)}}{{(N-1)}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{N(N-1)}}&-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(N-1)(N-2)}}&\dots&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\end{bmatrix}

and the update rule can be rewritten into a simple recurrence: for
k=0,\dots,N-2
,

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\frac{N-k-1}{N-k}\left({\mathbb{T}}y_{k}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{k-1}\right)

(Dual-OHM)

with the convention
{\mathbb{T}}y_{-1}=y_{0}
.
Dual-OHM also achieves (
opt-rate
) and thus is minimax optimal
[
28
]
.

2.4
Characterization of optimal H-matrices and Q-functions

Denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
the set of all lower triangular
(N-1)\times(N-1)
H-matrices whose associated algorithm defined by (
3
) satisfies (
opt-rate
) for any nonexpansive
{\mathbb{T}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
with a fixed point
y_{\star}
.
While the previous discussion shows

H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1),H_{\text{Dual-OHM}}(N-1)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1),

a more holistic characterization of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
was provided in
[
29
]
via equality and inequality constraints in
H
.
For
m,j=1,\dots,N-1
, the
Q-functions
of a lower triangular
H\in\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}
are defined as

\displaystyle Q(m,j;H)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=j(1)\leq i(1)<j(2)\leq i(2)<\cdots\leq i(m-1)<j(m)\leq i(m)\leq N-1\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=1}^{m}h_{i(r),j(r)},

following
[
29
]
.
This is the sum of all products of
m
entries of
H
, formed by starting at some
h_{i(1),j}
with
j\leq i(1)\leq N-1
and appending the product with another entry with column index
j(2)>i(1)
, and repeating a similar process until one gets
m
entries.
When
m+j>N
, the summation is vacuous (it is not possible to form such a product), so
Q(m,j;H)=0
.
When
m+j=N
, the only admissible choice of indices is when
i(r)=j(r)=j+r-1
, so
Q(m,j;H)=Q(N-j,j;H)=h_{j,j}h_{j+1,j+1}\cdots h_{N-1,N-1}
.
For a more concrete illustration, when
N=4
so
H
is
3\times 3
, we have

\displaystyle Q(1,1;H)=h_{1,1}+h_{2,1}+h_{3,1},\quad Q(1,2;H)=h_{2,2}+h_{3,2},\quad Q(1,3;H)=h_{3,3}

\displaystyle Q(2,1;H)=h_{1,1}h_{2,2}+h_{1,1}h_{3,2}+h_{1,1}h_{3,3}+h_{2,1}h_{3,3},\quad Q(2,2;H)=h_{2,2}h_{3,3},\quad Q(2,3;H)=0

\displaystyle Q(3,1;H)=h_{1,1}h_{2,2}h_{3,3},\quad Q(3,2;H)=0,\quad Q(3,3;H)=0.

Given these notions,
[
29
, Theorem 3]
proved that
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
if and only if it satisfies the equality constraints

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}Q(k,j;H)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-k}Q(k,j;H)=\frac{1}{N}\binom{N}{k+1}\qquad(k=1,\dots,N-1),

(6)

called the
H-invariance condition
, together with the inequality constraints

\displaystyle\lambda_{N,j}^{\star}(H)=N\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}(-1)^{m-1}Q(m,j;H)=N\sum_{m=1}^{N-j}(-1)^{m-1}Q(m,j;H)\geq 0\qquad

\displaystyle(j=1,\dots,N-1)

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)&=N\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}(-1)^{\ell+m-1}\binom{\ell+m}{m}Q(\ell,j;H)Q(m,k;H)\\
&=N\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-j}\sum_{m=1}^{N-k}(-1)^{\ell+m-1}\binom{\ell+m}{m}Q(\ell,j;H)Q(m,k;H)\geq 0\end{aligned}\quad

\displaystyle(1\leq j<k\leq N-1)

(8)

where
\lambda^{\star}(H)
are called
H-certificates
.

H-certificates are unique multipliers in convergence proof.

In
[
29
]
, it was also shown that for
H
satisfying (
6
),
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)
defined by (
7
) and (
8
) are unique values satisfying the following “universal proof template” for optimal algorithms:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&=N\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle g_{N},x_{N}-y_{0}\right\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)}{4}\left(\left\|y_{k-1}-y_{j-1}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\mathbb{T}}y_{k-1}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{j-1}\right\|^{2}\right)\\
&=N\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle g_{N},x_{N}-y_{0}\right\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)\left\langle x_{k}-x_{j},g_{k}-g_{j}\right\rangle\end{aligned}

(9)

where the second identity uses (
5
).
Therefore, if
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)\geq 0
for all
1\leq j<k\leq N
, the rightmost double summation is nonnegative, which implies

\displaystyle 0\geq N\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle g_{N},x_{N}-y_{0}\right\rangle\geq N\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle g_{N},y_{\star}-y_{0}\right\rangle\geq N\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}-\left(\frac{N}{2}\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2N}\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{N^{2}}\iff\left\|y_{N-1}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{N-1}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{N^{2}}

where we use
\left\langle g_{N},x_{N}-y_{\star}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left\|y_{N-1}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\mathbb{T}}y_{N-1}-{\mathbb{T}}y_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)\geq 0
for the second inequality.

3
Optimal vertex algorithms and diagram representations

The core objects of interest in this paper are the extremal points—i.e.,
vertices
—of the optimal H-matrix set
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
.
Each vertex is an intersection of a maximal number of zero sets of H-certificates
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)
, which are the boundary surfaces of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
.
In other words, vertices represent the algorithms with sparsest/simplest convergence proofs, where a minimal number of inequalities are active (positive) within the proof template (
9
) showing that
H
satisfies (
opt-rate
).

3.1
Characterization, existence and computation of vertex algorithms

We start by characterizing the minimal proofs associated with the vertex algorithms.
In the following, we drop the
H
-dependence if it is clear from the context.

Lemma 2
.

If
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, then for
j=1,\dots,N-1
, there exists at least one
k\in\{j+1,\dots,N\}
such that
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}>0
.

This result, whose proof is presented in Appendix
A
, shows that minimal proofs are the ones that have exactly one
k(j)\in\{j+1,\dots,N\}
satisfying
\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}>0
for each
j=1,\dots,N-1
.
Then a natural question is: do all
(N-1)!
possible combinations of
(k(1),\dots,k(N-1))
, each of which specifies a sparsity pattern of H-certificates, have a corresponding optimal vertex algorithm
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
?
We answer this question in the affirmative, as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k(1),k(2),\dots,k(N-1)
be given, where each
k(j)
is an integer satisfying
j<k(j)\leq N
.
For any such choice of
k(j)
’s, there uniquely exists an optimal vertex algorithm
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)>0\text{ and }\lambda_{\ell,j}^{\star}(H)=0\text{ for all }\ell\neq k(j),\,\,j<\ell\leq N

(10)

At a high level, we show that the selection of a sparsity pattern uniquely determines the values of

Lemma 3

There exists a one-to-one correspondence between lower triangular

3.2
Arc diagram representation of vertex algorithms

Given a vertex algorithm
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)

\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)>0

On the other hand, in the case of Dual-OHM, we have

Interestingly, however, stronger guarantees on intermediate iterates appear to come at the expense of the algorithm’s robustness, in the sense described in Section

4
Characterization of H-duality via arc diagrams

H-dual
[
13
,
15
,
16
]
is an operation that takes the anti-diagonal transpose of H-matrices, and it naturally preserves the H-invariance conditions (
6
)
[
28
]
.
On the other hand, there exist optimal H-matrices whose H-duals are not optimal, as H-certificates may become negative when
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
is dualized.
In particular, when
N=4
we have
(N-1)!=6
optimal vertex algorithms, and among them, there are two H-dual pairs, one is self-dual, and the remaining one becomes suboptimal under H-dual (i.e.,
H^{\textsf{A}}\notin{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
)
[
29
]
.

In this section, we thoroughly study the effect of H-dual on optimal vertex algorithms.
Based on the explicit characterization of the mapping between the H-certificates
\lambda^{\star}(H)
and
\lambda^{\star}(H^{\textsf{A}})
for
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, we prove that an optimal vertex algorithm stays optimal if and only if its arc diagram
{\mathcal{G}}
is basic, or equivalently non-crossing.
When this is the case,
H^{\textsf{A}}
is also a vertex of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, its arc diagram
{\mathcal{G}}\left(H^{\textsf{A}}\right)
can be directly computed from
{\mathcal{G}}
via a simple recursive procedure, and nonzero
\lambda^{\star}_{k,j}(H^{\textsf{A}})
are given by inverses of nonzero H-certificates
\lambda^{\star}(H)
of
H
under a combinatorial index correspondence.

4.1
Transformation of H-certificates under H-dual

Let
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
be an optimal vertex algorithm, and let
{\mathcal{G}}(H)=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},\lambda^{\star}(H))
be its arc diagram, weighted by
\lambda^{\star}(H)
.
Within this subsection, we denote
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)=\lambda_{k,j}
and
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}(H)
for simplicity.
Let
L({\mathcal{G}})
be the graph Laplacian of
{\mathcal{G}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle L({\mathcal{G}})=D({\mathcal{G}})-A({\mathcal{G}})

where
D({\mathcal{G}})
is the diagonal degree matrix

\displaystyle D({\mathcal{G}})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda_{2,1}+\lambda_{3,1}+\dots+\lambda_{N,1},\lambda_{2,1}+\lambda_{3,2}+\dots+\lambda_{N,2},\dots,\lambda_{N,1}+\lambda_{N,2}+\cdots+\lambda_{N,N-1}\right)

which is a diagonal matrix whose
(j,j)
-entry is
\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\lambda_{j,k}+\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\lambda_{k,j}
and
A({\mathcal{G}})
is the adjacency matrix

\displaystyle A({\mathcal{G}})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda_{2,1}&\lambda_{3,1}&\cdots&\lambda_{N,1}\\
\lambda_{2,1}&0&\lambda_{3,2}&\cdots&\lambda_{N,2}\\
\lambda_{3,1}&\lambda_{3,2}&0&\cdots&\lambda_{N,3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{N,1}&\lambda_{N,2}&\lambda_{N,3}&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}.

Then, let
\Lambda(H)=-E_{N,N}-L({\mathcal{G}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where
e_{N}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
E_{N,N}=e_{N}e_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
In other words,
\Lambda(H)
is the negative graph Laplacian with
1
additionally subtracted from the bottom-rightmost entry:

\displaystyle\Lambda(H)=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda_{2,1}-\lambda_{3,1}-\dots-\lambda_{N,1}&\lambda_{2,1}&\cdots&\lambda_{N,1}\\
\lambda_{2,1}&-\lambda_{2,1}-\lambda_{3,2}-\dots-\lambda_{N,2}&\cdots&\lambda_{N,2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{N,1}&\lambda_{N,2}&\cdots&-\lambda_{N,1}-\lambda_{N,2}-\cdots-\lambda_{N,N-1}-1\end{bmatrix}.

In fact,
\Lambda(H)
can be defined algebraically for any lower-triangular
H\in\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}
satisfying H-invariance (
6
), with possibly negative H-certificates as off-diagonal entries.
With that understanding, we state:

Theorem 3

(
[
24
]
)
.

For any
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
,
\Lambda(H)
is invertible and
\Lambda(H^{\textsf{A}})=\Delta\Lambda(H)^{-1}\Delta
where

\Delta=\begin{bmatrix}{}&{}&-1&1\\
&\iddots&1\\
-1&\iddots\\
1&\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

is the matrix of discrete derivatives with order reversing.

This is a particular case of
[
24
]
restricted to
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
.
While their result is for generic H-matrices, it is generally not guaranteed that
\Lambda(H)
is invertible, so they state it as an assumption.
In our case, invertibility of
\Lambda(H)
follows from the fact that
L({\mathcal{G}})
is the Laplacian of the weighted connected graph
{\mathcal{G}}
, so it is positive semidefinite and
\ker L({\mathcal{G}})=\mathrm{Span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
, which implies that

is positive definite.
For completeness, we present the self-contained proof of
Theorem

3
in Appendix
D
.

A relevant line of prior work is the so-called H-duality theorems
[
15
,
16
,
28
]
, which establish an explicit correspondence between convergence proofs for an algorithm and for its H-dual.
However, these results were limited to specific proof structures that use only a restricted subset of inequalities, and thus are not directly applicable to proving
Theorem

3
.
A more closely related work in spirit is
[
17
]
, which develops a general correspondence between proof certificates of H-dual algorithms, albeit in the setting of smooth convex minimization.
From this perspective,
Theorem

3
can be viewed as a completion of H-duality theory for exact optimal fixed-point algorithms, as it shows that
H
has an optimal H-dual if and only if
\Delta\Lambda(H)^{-1}\Delta
is nonnegative off its diagonal, and those values are the proof certificates for

.
On the other hand, the fully general H-duality, incorporating suboptimal algorithms such as Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration
[
18
,
21
]
, remains open.
Toward this end, the result of
[
24
]
characterizes a correspondence between proof certificates of generic
H
and
H^{\textsf{A}}
under a broad class of proof structures that often captures the tightest (best rate) proof associated with

.

For optimal vertex algorithms, the mapping from
\Lambda(H)
to
\Lambda(H^{\textsf{A}})
in

becomes more interpretable.

{\mathcal{U}}
and columns indexed by
{\mathcal{W}}
.
Then

\det L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{W}}}=(-1)^{\Sigma({\mathcal{U}})+\Sigma({\mathcal{W}})}\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{W}}}}\mathrm{sign}({\mathcal{F}})\,\lambda({\mathcal{F}}),

where
\mathrm{sign}({\mathcal{F}})=(-1)^{n(\pi_{\mathcal{F}})}
and
\lambda({\mathcal{F}})=\prod_{(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}}}\lambda_{i,j}
.

Applying
Lemma

5
to our context, we can characterize the determinant and inverse of
\Lambda(H)
.

Lemma 6
.

We have

\displaystyle\det\left(-\Lambda(H)\right)=\det\left(L({\mathcal{G}})+E_{N,N}\right)=\det L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\},{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{T}}:\text{spanning}\\
\text{tree of }{\mathcal{G}}\end{subarray}}\lambda({\mathcal{T}}):=\tau>0,

\nu_{N,i}=\nu_{i,N}=-1
for
i=1,\dots,N
, and

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\nu_{j,i}=-1-\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}}\lambda({\mathcal{F}})

for
i,j=1,\dots,N-1
.

Proof.

We have
L({\mathcal{G}})\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
, so the first
N-1
rows of
-\Lambda(H)=L({\mathcal{G}})+E_{N,N}
have row sum equal to
0
, while the last row sums up to
1
.
Hence, if we add the first
N-1
columns of
-\Lambda(H)
to its last column, it becomes

\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

This column operation does not change the determinant, and the expansion along the last column gives

\det(-\Lambda(H))=\det\widehat{L}

where
\widehat{L}=L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\},\,{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\}}
is the minor obtained by eliminating the last row and last column from
L({\mathcal{G}})
.
Then by the matrix-tree theorem,

\det\widehat{L}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{T}}:\text{spanning}\\
\text{tree of }{\mathcal{G}}\end{subarray}}\lambda({\mathcal{T}})=\tau

as desired.
Note that for
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, the support graph of
{\mathcal{G}}
is connected and all its positive-edge weights are nonnegative, so
\tau>0
, i.e.,
\widehat{L}
is invertible.

Next,
\Lambda(H)\mathbf{1}=-e_{N}\implies\Lambda(H)^{-1}e_{N}=-\mathbf{1}
and
\Lambda(H)^{-1}
is symmetric, so
\nu_{N,i}=\nu_{i,N}=-1
for
i=1,\dots,N
.
For the remaining
\nu_{i,j}
with
1\leq i,j\leq N-1
, we can write
-\Lambda(H)^{-1}
in the block structure

-\Lambda(H)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&1\end{bmatrix}

whose Schur complement for the upper left block is
R-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
, so we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}R&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(R-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}&*\\
*&*\end{bmatrix}=-\Lambda(H)=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{L}&*\\
*&*\end{bmatrix},

i.e.,
R=\widehat{L}^{-1}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
This implies
\nu_{i,j}=-1-(\widehat{L}^{-1})_{i,j}
for
1\leq i,j\leq N-1
.
Next, using the adjugate formula for matrix inverses, we have

(\widehat{L}^{-1})_{i,j}=\frac{(-1)^{i+j}\det\widehat{L}_{\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{j\},\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{i\}}}{\det\widehat{L}}=\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{\tau}\det\widehat{L}_{\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{j\},\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{i\}}.

Because
\widehat{L}=L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\},{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{N\}}
, we can rewrite the minor of
\widehat{L}
as

\widehat{L}_{\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{j\},\{1,\dots,N-1\}\setminus\{i\}}=L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{j,N\},{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{i,N\}}.

Hence, we can apply
Lemma

5
with
{\mathcal{U}}=\{j,N\}
and
{\mathcal{W}}=\{i,N\}
to obtain

\det L({\mathcal{G}})_{{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{j,N\},{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{i,N\}}=(-1)^{i+j}\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{j,N\},\{i,N\}}}\mathrm{sign}({\mathcal{F}})\,\lambda({\mathcal{F}}).

For any
{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{j,N\},\{i,N\}}
, the component containing
N
must pair
N
with itself, and the other component must pair
j
with
i
, so the induced bijection has no inversions, i.e.,
\mathrm{sign}({\mathcal{F}})=1
.
Also, because
\widehat{L}
is symmetric, we can rewrite the forest family as
\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}
. Together, this yields

(\widehat{L}^{-1})_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}}\lambda({\mathcal{F}})\implies\nu_{i,j}=-1-\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}}\lambda({\mathcal{F}})

as desired.
∎

Now, if we specialize to the case of vertex algorithms,
{\mathcal{G}}
itself is a tree (neglecting the edges of weight
0
) and thus,
Lemma

6
implies
\tau=\det\left(L({\mathcal{G}})+E_{N,N}\right)=\lambda({\mathcal{G}})=\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}
.
Additionally, for any
i,j<N
, a spanning forest
{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}
should have
N-2
edges and two connected components, so it will be obtained by eliminating exactly one edge from
{\mathcal{G}}
.
Recall that
{\mathcal{C}}(j)
denotes the unique increasing path to
N
, starting at
j
.
Then we have the following characterization.

Corollary 5
.

Let
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
be a vertex algorithm and consider its arc diagram
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}(H)
.
Then for
1\leq i,j\leq N
,

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=-1-\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}(i)\cap{\mathcal{C}}(j)\setminus\{N\}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k(v),v}}=-1-\sum_{v=1}^{N-1}\frac{1_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}(i)}1_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}(j)}}{\lambda_{k(v),v}}.

Proof.

If either
i=N
or
j=N
, then
{\mathcal{C}}(i)\cap{\mathcal{C}}(j)\setminus\{N\}=\emptyset
and the summation becomes vacuous. This aligns with the formula
\nu_{N,j}=\nu_{i,N}=-1
for
i,j=1,\dots,N
, proved in
Lemma

6
.

Note that because
H
is a vertex,
{\mathcal{G}}
is a tree, and thus it is the only spanning tree of itself.
That is,
\tau=\lambda({\mathcal{G}})=\prod_{u=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{k(u),u}
.
Now for
1\leq i,j\leq N-1
, we have
{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}
if and only if
{\mathcal{F}}
is obtained by removing an arc
(k(v),v)
from
{\mathcal{G}}
, and it has both
i
and
j
in its connected component not containing
N
.
When an arc
(k(v),v)
is removed, it separates a node

from
N
if and only if
v\in{\mathcal{C}}(u)\setminus\{N\}
, i.e., when
v
lies on the simple path
{\mathcal{C}}(u)
from
u
to
N
(note that
v
cannot be
N
because
N
is the terminal node).
Hence, removing
(k(v),v)
places both
i,j
in the component separated from
N
if and only if
v\in{\mathcal{C}}(i)\cap{\mathcal{C}}(j)\setminus\{N\}
.
For the resulting forest
{\mathcal{F}}
, we have
\lambda({\mathcal{F}})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq u\leq N-1\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}\lambda_{k(u),u}=\frac{\tau}{\lambda_{k(v),v}}
.
Therefore, by
Lemma

,

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=-1-\sum_{{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\{i,N\},\{j,N\}}}\frac{\lambda({\mathcal{F}})}{\tau}=-1-\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}(i)\cap{\mathcal{C}}(j)\setminus\{N\}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k(v),v}}.

∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a basic (i.e., non-crossing) arc diagram.
Suppose
{\mathcal{F}}
is a spanning forest of
{\mathcal{G}}
with two disjoint connected components:
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
containing
i,j
such that
j\leq i<N
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
containing
N
. Then we have
\{j,j+1,\dots,i\}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
v\notin{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
for some
j<v<i
. Then we must have
v\in{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
.
That is,
{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
is the connected component containing both
v
and
N
, so it must contain the unique simple path
{\mathcal{C}}(v)
joining them.
This implies,
{\mathcal{C}}(v)\cap{\mathcal{T}}_{1}=\emptyset
.
On the other hand,
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
contains the unique simple path between
i
and
j
, and thus contains the segment of
{\mathcal{C}}(j)
joining
j
to
m=\min\left({\mathcal{C}}(j)\cap{\mathcal{C}}(i)\right)
.
Note that we must have
i\leq m<N
; if
m=N
then
j\in{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
is joined to
N\in{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
within
{\mathcal{F}}
, a contradiction.

Let
v_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}(v)
be the node satisfying
v_{0}<m<k(v_{0})
. Such a vertex exists because the increasing path
v<k(v)<k(k(v))<\cdots
should terminate at
N
, and it does not contain
m
because
m\in{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
while
{\mathcal{C}}(v)\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
.
Similarly, as the increasing path
j<k(j)<k(k(j))<\cdots
should terminate at
m
without intersecting
{\mathcal{C}}(v)
, there exists
j_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}(j)
satisfying
j_{0}<v_{0}<k(j_{0})
.
This contradicts the assumption that
{\mathcal{G}}
is non-crossing, as
j_{0}<v_{0}<k(j_{0})\leq m<k(v_{0})
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a basic arc diagram.
For each
v=1,\dots,N-1
, the set of its descendants

{\mathcal{I}}(v):=\{\,j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}:v\in{\mathcal{C}}(j)\,\}

is an interval, i.e.,
{\mathcal{I}}(v)=\{\ell(v),\ell(v)+1,\dots,v\}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{G}}
is a tree, removing a single arc from
{\mathcal{G}}
yields a spanning forest with two connected components.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the forest obtained by removing the arc
(k(v),v)
, so that
v
lies in the component
{\mathcal{T}}
of
{\mathcal{F}}
not containing
N
.
Because
{\mathcal{F}}
contains all edges with starting node (smaller node) less than
v
, we see that any
j\in{\mathcal{I}}(v)
is joined to
v
by
{\mathcal{C}}(j)
within
{\mathcal{F}}
, so
{\mathcal{I}}(v)\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
.
Conversely, if
j\notin{\mathcal{I}}(v)
, then
v\notin{\mathcal{C}}(j)
, and
{\mathcal{F}}
contains all edges within the path
{\mathcal{C}}(j)
.
Therefore,
j
is joined to
N
in
{\mathcal{F}}
, so
j
belongs to a different connected component than
v
, i.e.,
j\notin{\mathcal{T}}
.
This shows that
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}(v)
, and eventually,
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{I}}(v)
.

Now, as defined in Section
4.1.1
,
\ell(v)
is the smallest descendant of
v
, so clearly
\ell(v)\in{\mathcal{I}}(v)={\mathcal{T}}
.
So
{\mathcal{T}}
is the connected component of
{\mathcal{F}}
containing
\ell(v)
and
v
but not
N
; hence, by
Lemma

7
, we have
\{\ell(v),\ell(v)+1,\dots,v\}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{I}}(v)
.
However, this inclusion is in fact an equality because
\ell(v)
is the smallest element of
{\mathcal{I}}(v)
, and by definition
{\mathcal{I}}(v)
cannot contain any node larger than
v
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Self-dual vertex algorithms

We say that an optimal vertex algorithm
H
is
self-dual
if
H^{\textsf{A}}=H
; equivalently, if its arc diagram satisfies
{\mathcal{G}}^{\textsf{A}}={\mathcal{G}}
.
The recursive dualization rule in
Corollary

4
suggests that a self-dual basic diagram must split into two blocks of equal size, with one block being the dual of the other.
This necessary condition is in fact also sufficient, and yields the exact count of self-dual vertex algorithms—this is again a Catalan number.

Theorem 5
.

Let
N\geq 2
, let
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
be an optimal vertex algorithm, and let
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}(H)
be its arc diagram.
Then
H
is self-dual if and only if
N
is even and there exists a basic arc diagram
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
on
\{1,\dots,N/2\}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}_{0}\mathbin{\bowtie}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\textsf{A}},

(16)

i.e.,
{\mathcal{G}}
is decomposable at
N/2
and removing the arc
(N,N/2)
separates it into subdiagrams that are dual to each other.
Consequently, there is no self-dual vertex algorithm when
N
is odd, while for even
N
the number of self-dual vertex algorithms is

C_{N/2-1}=\frac{1}{N/2}\binom{N-2}{N/2-1}.

Proof.

Assume first that
H^{\textsf{A}}=H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
. By
Theorem

4
, its arc diagram
{\mathcal{G}}
is basic, i.e., non-crossing.
By
Lemma

4
,
{\mathcal{G}}
is decomposable at

Conversely, suppose

Finally, the above characterization implies that no self-dual vertex algorithm exists when

5
Application to novel algorithm design and principled analyses

5.1
Two classes of new optimal algorithms

Here, we design two new fixed-point methods with natural arc diagram forms. By virtue of these forms, their analysis is immediate by our composing theory, ensuring they are minimax optimal without additional effort. Although both are optimal (as are OHM and Dual-OHM), our theory allows us to contrast these methods in terms of their level of guarantees at intermediate iterates and their robustness to violation of nonexpansiveness.

\displaystyle\left\|g_{N}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\left\|y_{0}-y_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{N^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2N}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)\right).

Through this analysis, we can interpret the sum of H-certificates as the algorithm’s sensitivity to the oracle’s violation of the nonexpansiveness assumption, so the smaller it is, the more robust the algorithm is to a perturbation of the problem class.
Define

\displaystyle\rho(H)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H).

(23)

Minimum of
\rho(H)
.

For any optimal
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{N,j}^{\star}(H)=N-1
by
Lemma

12
(b) (see also
[
29
, Theorem 5]
).
Therefore,
\rho(H)\geq N-1
and the equality is achieved if and only if
\lambda_{k,j}^{\star}(H)=0
for all
1\leq j<k\leq N-1
, i.e., iff
H=H_{\text{Dual-OHM}}(N-1)
.
On the other hand, for basic vertex algorithms, having a guarantee on an intermediate iterate
y_{N^{\prime}-1}
(i.e., being decomposable at
N^{\prime}
) incurs an increase in
\rho(H)
.
More precisely, we have:

Proposition 7
.

Let
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
be an optimal vertex algorithm and suppose that it is decomposable at
1\leq N^{\prime}\leq N-1
.
Then

\rho(H)\geq N-1+\frac{N^{\prime}(N^{\prime}-1)}{N}.

Proof.

Because
H
is decomposable, we can write
H=H_{1}\mathbin{\bowtie}H_{2}
, where
H_{1},H_{2}
are optimal vertex algorithms of sizes
N^{\prime}-1
and
N-N^{\prime}-1
, respectively (see the proof of
Corollary

2
).
Then, by
Theorem

2
, we have

\displaystyle\rho(H)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)+\lambda_{N,N^{\prime}}^{\star}(H)+\sum_{j=N^{\prime}+1}^{N-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H_{1})+\frac{N^{\prime}}{N-N^{\prime}}+\frac{N}{N-N^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-N^{\prime}-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N^{\prime}(N^{\prime}-1)}{N}+\frac{N^{\prime}}{N-N^{\prime}}+\frac{N(N-N^{\prime}-1)}{N-N^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{\prime}(N^{\prime}-1)}{N}+N-1

where the inequality uses
\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H_{1})=\rho(H_{1})\geq N^{\prime}-1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N-N^{\prime}-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H_{2})=\rho(H_{2})\geq N-N^{\prime}-1
.
∎

Maximum of
\rho(H)
.

The above result suggests that having more intermediate guarantees will increase
\rho(H)
, and OHM is at the extreme in this direction, as it is (the unique) anytime optimal algorithm
[
29
]
.
Naturally, we have

Proposition 8
.

For
N\geq 2
, OHM satisfies

\displaystyle\rho(H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1))=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{j+1,j}^{\star}(H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1))=\frac{N^{2}-1}{3},

and it is the unique maximum for
\rho(H)
over basic optimal vertex algorithms in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
.

Proof.

For
H=H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1)
, we have
k(j)=j+1
and
\lambda_{j+1,j}^{\star}(H)=\frac{j(j+1)}{N}
for
j=1,\dots,N-1
, so their sum is

\rho(H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1))=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{j+1,j}^{\star}(H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1))=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(j^{2}+j)\right)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{N(N-1)(2N-1)}{6}+\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\right)=\frac{N^{2}-1}{3}.

Next, we use induction on
N
to show that OHM is the unique maximum over basic optimal algorithms.
In the base case
N=2
,
H_{\text{OHM}}(1)=H_{\text{Dual-OHM}}(1)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}
is the only basic optimal algorithm and it has
\lambda_{2,1}^{\star}(H)=1
, and we have nothing to prove.

For
N\geq 3
, assume that
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H^{\prime})\leq\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{3}
for all basic optimal
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(\ell-1)
with equality holding if and only if
H^{\prime}=H_{\text{OHM}}(\ell-1)
for
\ell=2,\dots,N-1
.
Now let
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
be a basic optimal algorithm, so that it is decomposable at a unique
N^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
by
Lemma

4
and we can write
H=H_{1}\mathbin{\bowtie}H_{2}
.
Then, using the induction hypothesis and
Theorem

2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho(H)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{k(j),j}^{\star}(H)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\rho(H_{1})+\frac{N^{\prime}}{N-N^{\prime}}+\frac{N}{N-N^{\prime}}\rho(H_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\frac{(N^{\prime})^{2}-1}{3}+\frac{N^{\prime}}{N-N^{\prime}}+\frac{N}{N-N^{\prime}}\frac{(N-N^{\prime})^{2}-1}{3}:=\sigma(N^{\prime}).

Observe that we have
\sigma(N-1)=\frac{N^{2}-1}{3}
, and for
N^{\prime}<N-1
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(N-1)-\sigma(N^{\prime})=\frac{N^{\prime}(N+N^{\prime})((N-N^{\prime})^{2}-1)}{3N(N-N^{\prime})}>0

from direct simplification. Therefore, we have
\rho(H)\leq\frac{N^{2}-1}{3}
and the equality holds if and only if
N^{\prime}=N-1
and
\rho(H_{1})=\frac{(N-1)^{2}-1}{3}
, which is equivalent to

H_{1}=H_{\text{OHM}}(N-2)\iff H=H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1)

by induction hypothesis and the fact that
H_{\text{OHM}}(N-1)=H_{\text{OHM}}(N-2)\mathbin{\bowtie}[\,\,]
. This completes the induction, and hence the proof.
∎

The trade-off between anytime guarantees and robustness.

The previous discussion shows that OHM and Dual-OHM lie at opposite ends of the anytime–robustness spectrum.
Dual-OHM minimizes
\rho(H)
but provides no guarantees for intermediate iterates, whereas OHM guarantees every intermediate iterate at the cost of robustness, resulting in the largest
\rho(H)
among optimal vertex algorithms.
The algorithms from the previous section, RDO and FSDM, lie between these two extremes, each offering a different compromise between anytime guarantees and robustness.
Both algorithms are
quasi-anytime
, which we define as having a sequence of iterates
y_{m_{k}}
with convergence guarantees, where
\{m_{k}\}_{k=0,1,\dots}
is an increasing sequence of natural numbers.
On the other hand, their robustness can be analyzed as follows:

•

RDO (with period
p
) provides guarantees at linearly spaced iterates, and applying
Theorem

2
with
N=np+1
and
N^{\prime}=(n-1)p+1
it satisfies

\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n)}\right)=\sum_{k=(n-1)p+1}^{np}\lambda_{N,k}^{\star}\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n)}\right)+\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n-1)}\right)=np+\frac{(n-1)p+1}{np+1}\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n-1)}\right)

for
n=1,2,\dots
(with
\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(0)}\right)=0
).
Therefore, letting
a_{n}=(np+1)\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n)}\right)
, we obtain the recurrence
a_{n}=a_{n-1}+np(np+1)
and
a_{0}=0
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\rho\left(H_{\text{RDO}(p)}^{(n)}\right)=\frac{a_{n}}{np+1}=\frac{1}{np+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}kp(kp+1)=\frac{pn(n+1)(2np+p+3)}{6(np+1)}=\frac{n^{2}p}{3}+{\mathcal{O}}(n)\approx\frac{N^{2}}{3p}.

So the robustness is approximately improved by a factor of
p
over OHM.

•

For FSDM, applying
Theorem

2
with
N=2^{n}
and
N^{\prime}=2^{n-1}
, we observe that for
n=1,2,\dots

\displaystyle\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n-1}-1)\right)+\lambda_{N,N^{\prime}}^{\star}\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n}-1)\right)+\frac{N}{N-N^{\prime}}\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n-1}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{2}\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n-1}-1)\right)+1

where we use
\lambda_{N,N^{\prime}}^{\star}\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n}-1)\right)=\frac{N^{\prime}}{N-N^{\prime}}=1
.
Therefore,
b_{n}=\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n}-1)\right)+\frac{2}{3}
is a geometric sequence satisfying
b_{n}=\frac{5}{2}b_{n-1}
with
b_{0}=\frac{2}{3}
. This implies

\displaystyle\rho\left(H_{\text{FSDM}}(2^{n}-1)\right)=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{n}-\frac{2}{3}\approx\frac{2}{3}N^{\log_{2}\frac{5}{2}}<\frac{2}{3}N^{1.322}.

7
Conclusion

Together with
[
29
]
, this work marks another step toward a more comprehensive understanding of optimal acceleration for fixed-point algorithms and the closely related settings of monotone inclusions and minimax optimization
[
22
,
31
]
.
The story of acceleration for these problems began with Halpern iterations, or so-called anchor-type algorithms
[
12
,
20
,
14
,
8
,
30
,
19
,
26
]
.
These methods have been studied extensively and further developed through connections to ODE models and Nesterov acceleration
[
5
,
25
,
27
]
.
Although the algorithms proposed in the aforementioned works are formally distinct, they can still be broadly categorized as anchor-type variants, since they all focus on deriving anytime or asymptotic convergence guarantees.
H-duals of anchor-type algorithms (dual-anchor type)
[
28
]
were then proposed as a materially different acceleration mechanism that does not provide intermediate-iterate guarantees.
While this initially appeared to be a drawback, our results suggest that it is better understood as a benign trade-off for robustness.
Together with the new algorithms we propose, which attain distinct midpoints along this trade-off curve, this may enable a formal study of Pareto optimality and the extension of this perspective to other settings such as stochastic fixed-point and minimax problems
[
6
]
.
One could even develop additional tie-breaking criteria beyond intermediate-iterate guarantees or robustness to noise, such as average-case behavior.
Our composing theory may provide a tractable handle and insights for addressing these research directions in future works.

Lemma 10

(Chu–Vandermonde identity)
.

For any

∎

Lemma 11
.

Let

\sum_{m=t}^{r-1}(-1)^{m}\binom{r-t}{m-t}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-t-1}(-1)^{j+t}\binom{r-t}{j}=(-1)^{t}\left(-(-1)^{r-t}\binom{r-t}{r-t}\right)=(-1)^{r+1}.

Now, when

({\mathbf{R}}_{r}{\mathbf{d}}_{r})_{1}=\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m}\binom{r}{m}=(-1)^{r+1}
, which is the first entry of
(-1)^{r+1}({\mathbf{d}}_{r+1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{r+1})
.
For

, we use

to rewrite it as

\displaystyle({\mathbf{R}}_{r}{\mathbf{d}}_{r})_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m}\binom{r}{m}\left(\binom{m}{a-1}+\binom{m}{a-2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m}\binom{r}{m}\binom{m}{a-1}+\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m}\binom{r}{m}\binom{m}{a-2}.

(26)

If
a\leq r
, applying (
25
), the last expression yields

\displaystyle({\mathbf{R}}_{r}{\mathbf{d}}_{r})_{a}=(-1)^{r+1}\binom{r}{a-1}+(-1)^{r+1}\binom{r}{a-2}=(-1)^{r+1}\binom{r+1}{a-1}=(-1)^{r+1}({\mathbf{d}}_{r+1})_{a}.

When
a=r+1
, the first summation in (
26
) vanishes, and applying (
25
) to the second summation yields

\displaystyle({\mathbf{R}}_{r}{\mathbf{d}}_{r})_{a}=(-1)^{r+1}\binom{r}{r-1}=(-1)^{r+1}\left(({\mathbf{d}}_{r+1})_{r+1}-1\right)

which proves the desired identity.

∎

Proof of
Lemma

2
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
; write
r=N-j
and let

{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}Q(1,j)&Q(2,j)&\cdots&Q(r,j)\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

Assume to the contrary that
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
and
\lambda_{j+1,j}^{\star}=\lambda_{j+2,j}^{\star}=\cdots=\lambda_{N,j}^{\star}=0
.
We show that this forces
{\mathbf{S}}_{r}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=0
by induction.
The starting point is clear: we have

0=\frac{1}{N}\lambda_{N,j}^{\star}=\sum_{a=1}^{r}(-1)^{a-1}Q(a,j)=\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}\right)_{1,:}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right)_{1}.

Next, assuming that we have already established
\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right)_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell=1,\dots,s
, where
1\leq s<r
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{N-s,j}^{\star}

\displaystyle=N\sum_{a=1}^{s}\sum_{b=1}^{N-j}(-1)^{a+b-1}\binom{a+b}{a}Q(a,N-s)Q(b,j)

\displaystyle=N\sum_{a=1}^{s}Q(a,N-s)\sum_{b=1}^{r}(-1)^{a+b+1}\binom{a+b}{b}Q(b,j)

\displaystyle=N\sum_{a=1}^{s}Q(a,N-s)\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}\right)_{a+1,:}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}

\displaystyle=NQ(s,N-s)\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}\right)_{s+1,:}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}

where the last equality uses
\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}\right)_{\ell,:}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=0
for
\ell=2,\dots,s
by the induction hypothesis.
Because
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\star}(N-1)
, it satisfies the H-invariance conditions (
6
), and in particular
Q(N-1,1)=h_{1,1}h_{2,2}\cdots h_{N-1,N-1}=\frac{1}{N}\binom{N}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\neq 0
.
Therefore,
Q(s,N-s)=h_{N-s,N-s}h_{N-s+1,N-s+1}\dots h_{N-1,N-1}\neq 0
, and this implies
\left({\mathbf{S}}_{r}\right)_{s+1,:}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=0
, completing the induction.

Now we have established
{\mathbf{S}}_{r}{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=0
. However, by
Lemma

11
(i),
{\mathbf{S}}_{r}
is nonsingular, so this implies
{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=0
. In particular,

Q(r,j)=Q(N-j,j)=h_{j,j}h_{j+1,j+1}\cdots h_{N-1,N-1}=0,

which is a contradiction to
Q(N-1,1)=h_{1,1}h_{2,2}\cdots h_{N-1,N-1}\neq 0
.
∎

\displaystyle=y_{j-1}-2^{\nu_{2}(j)}g_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\left(y_{m(j)}-y_{j-1}\right)+\sum_{r=1}^{p-1}2^{\nu_{2}(j)-r}g_{t_{p-r+1}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=m(j)+1}^{j-1}b_{i-2^{n}}^{(n)}+b_{j-2^{n}}^{(n)}\right)g_{2^{n}}.

Therefore, the proof is complete once we show
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=m(j)+1}^{j-1}b_{i-2^{n}}^{(n)}+b_{j-2^{n}}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i^{\prime}=m(j^{\prime})+1}^{j^{\prime}-1}b_{i^{\prime}}^{(n)}+b_{j^{\prime}}^{(n)}=2^{\nu_{2}(j)-p}
.
Here, note that

distinct powers of 2, so we are done once we show the following lemma.

Lemma 16

For

m(u)=u-2^{\nu_{2}(u)}=2^{i_{1}}+\dots+2^{i_{q}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=m(u)+1}^{u-1}b_{i}^{(n)}+b_{u}^{(n)}=2^{\nu_{2}(u)-q-1}.

(45)

16

\displaystyle b^{(n+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(b^{(n)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\frac{2^{n}+1}{4}&\left(\frac{1}{2}b^{(n)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},
