INTRODUCTION

One of the most common chronic neurological conditions, epilepsy affects over 70 million individuals globally. 1 Recurrent seizures, which differ in intensity and frequency across individuals, are its defining feature. It can significantly lower someone's quality of life by interfering with their everyday routine and social relationships. 2 This brain disorder has several etiologies, such as infections, genetic disorders, traumatic brain injury, brain tumors, and metabolic abnormalities. 3 There is presently no cure for this condition; the anti‐epileptic drugs (AEDs) that are on the market are only meant to control the symptoms and reduce the frequency and intensity of seizures. 4 Lamotrigine and levetiracetam are two common AEDs that are taken regularly shown in Table 1 and have an effect on organs other than the brain. For instance, sexual dysfunction and reproductive problems are common in male epileptic patients, which may result in the systematic prescription of AEDs such as carbamazepine. Because of this, these disadvantages in epilepsy may be avoided with prudent drug administration and—above all—focused therapy. Regrettably, a number of biological factors have complicated the illness's therapy and reduced the efficacy of available therapies. 5 , 6 The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is one of the biggest barriers to the effectiveness of ASMs. 7 A robust physical barrier made up of pericytes, astrocytes, neurons, and endothelial cells lining brain capillaries keeps the majority of unwanted substances from the blood out of the brain. 8 There are several differences between the BBB in epilepsy and the normal BBB.

For medication to treat CNS disorders like epilepsy, it must cross the blood–brain barrier and get to brain tissue. However, it has been shown that only low molecular weight (<1000 Da) lipophilic compounds are able to traverse the BBB. BBB is estimated to block 100% of big molecules and around 98% of small molecules from getting through to the point where they may be therapeutically effective. This may reduce the effectiveness of drugs used to treat epilepsy. 9 , 10 Moreover, several efflux pumps, such as multidrug resistance protein (MRP1), breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), and multidrug resistance protein (MRP2) are present in the luminal membrane of endothelial cells. Drug resistance is encouraged by these pumps because they restrict the amount of medications that may enter the brain and prevent them from staying there. 4 Twenty to 25% of epileptic patients have drug resistance, which poses a serious challenge to treatment. Although this process has been connected to other mechanisms, the transporter idea has attracted a lot of attention. According to this notion, AEDs could serve as substrates for the transporters stated before. 11 Given the high frequency of epilepsy and the insufficient treatment of these patients, strategies to improve AED entrance and persistence in the brain must be taken into consideration to maximize their effectiveness. Creating innovative drug delivery methods that can evade or block these transporters is one way to combat medication resistance in epilepsy. Combination treatment, which combines many AEDs with distinct modes of action, may also be helpful in overcoming medication resistance. All things considered, a multimodal strategy that incorporates combination therapy and novel drug delivery methods may provide patients with medication‐resistant epilepsy with a more successful course of treatment. There is hope that by tackling the issues of medication resistance and transport, these patients' outcomes and quality of life may be enhanced.

Recent advancements in nanotechnology may open up new avenues for the treatment of epilepsy. Numerous synthetic nanocarriers, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and inorganic nanoparticles, have been used to carry ASMs (Figure 1). 12 In a similar vein, researchers have been interested in exosomes, which are naturally occurring nanocarriers, due to their potential for customized drug delivery. This narrative summary includes a number of synthetic nanocarrier types that have been studied for the transportation of ASMs. We also discuss the potential benefits of dispersing ASMs via exosomes. Furthermore, we highlight the challenges and limitations associated with employing these nanocarriers for the transportation of ASM and provide recommendations for further investigation and development in this area. 13 All things considered, learning more about the potential of both natural and manufactured nanocarriers may aid in the development of more effective drug delivery techniques for the treatment of various ailments.

TRANSPORT PATHWAYS ACROSS BBB

Transcellular transfer across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) is facilitated by three primary mechanisms: active efflux transport (AET), receptor‐mediated transport (RMT), and carrier‐mediated transport (CMT). RMT is a form of endocytosis that enables the passage of anticancer proteins and other macromolecules over the blood–brain barrier without causing any damage to individuals. The insulin‐like growth factor 1 receptor, transferrin receptor (TFR), and low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) are prominent examples of often utilized receptors. CMT is facilitated by several solute carrier (SLC) transporters that transfer various substances, including sugar, amino acids, organic cations or anions, and nutrients, into the brain. Important SLCs include nucleoside transporters, cationic and anionic organic ion transporters, monocarboxylate transporters, and glucose transporters (GLUTs). The main transcellular transport route is the ATP‐driven mechanism known as AET. It serves a cleansing function by keeping external chemicals—including potentially harmful ones and prescription medications—out of the brain and reintroducing substances that have passed the BBB into the system. The ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter superfamily is the main drug efflux transporter. By making full use of these three transport pathways, medicinal medicines can be transported by their associated transporters at a higher rate, boosting drug penetration of the blood–brain barrier and improving therapeutic results. 14

MECHANISM PATHWAY OF EPILEPSY

Gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) potentiation: the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, GABA is an ion channel consisting of 19 subunits and a pentameric chloride channel. Certain medications have the ability to augment the effects of GABA by potentiating GABA receptors. This can lead to an increase in neural inhibition and possible therapeutic advantages for disorders including anxiety and epilepsy. GABA In a receptor, 2‐α and 2‐β subunits are combined. 15 Chloride ions can enter these receptors thanks to GABA's binding, which causes the neuron to become hyperpolarized and less excitable. This process aids in controlling neuronal activity and preserving the proper ratio of brain stimulation to inhibition. Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, topiramate, and zonisamide are among the medications that slow down the channel's opening, increasing the amount of chloride ions that enter cells. This has an inhibitory impact and increases the hyperpolarization of the cells. 16 These drugs are referred to as GABA potentiators because they increase the sedative and calming effects of GABA in the brain. These medications work by raising GABA activity, which helps treat disorders including epilepsy and seizures, lessen anxiety, and encourage sleep. GABA potentiates are also used to treat the symptoms of alcohol withdrawal (Figures 2 and 3).

Sodium channel blockers are voltage‐gated channels comprised of several protein subunits that elevate intracellular sodium ion concentration, hence eliciting an excitatory response in cells. Anti‐convulsant medications, including phenytoin, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, oxycarbazepine, and topiramate, demonstrate a preferential affinity for sodium channel proteins in their inactivated state. Binding obstructs the conformational recycling process, leading to a shift of sodium channels into the inactivated state, from which recovery is extended. As a result, ASDs considerably extend the channel's ‘refractory’ period. Consequently, these drugs cause a specific use‐ and frequency‐dependent reduction in channel conductance, thereby limiting recurrent neuronal firing, while having negligible impact on the generation of single action potentials or low‐frequency (<1 Hz) firing. 17 , 18

Calcium channel blockers: During high depolarization, high‐voltage activated (HVA) channels are triggered, which controls the release of both R‐ and T‐type neurotransmitters. Low‐voltage activated channels, which slow down depolarization, govern T‐type channels. Calcium channels are blocked by anti‐convulsant drugs such as ethosuximide and zonisamide, which reduces the quantity of calcium ions that may enter cells. T‐type calcium channels are typically involved in the onset of absence seizures. 19 , 20

Glutamate inhibitors: are excitatory neurotransmitters based on glutamate that attach to glutamate receptors to produce excitatory action. Glutamate receptors may be categorized into two categories: ionotropic and metabotropic. 10 These ionotropic glutamate receptors can also be classified as (α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazole propionic acid (AMPA)), kainate, or N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptors. Cations can be conducted through these ionotropic receptors, which can have an excitatory or depolarizing impact. Sodium ion permeability mediates the action of fast excitatory neurotransmitters, such as AMPA and kainate receptors. The NMDA receptors, on the other hand, allow sodium and calcium ions through. It provokes an interesting reaction. Certain medications, such as perampanel and topiramate, block the function of AMPA receptors. Certain medications, such as valproate and felbamate, slow down the brain by blocking NMDA receptor activity. 21

Liposomes

The liposome, whose structure resembles that of a cell membrane, is one lipid bilayer structure that is useful for drug administration. Different chemicals may be transported into cells via liposomes, however, in clinical studies, the outcomes are often not what were anticipated. Liposomes are employed in clinical studies in spite of all of their drawbacks. 23 Including low stability, aggregation, fusion, or early release of encapsulated contents, particularly under unfavorable environmental circumstances such as temperature variations or pH alterations. Moreover, the shelf life of liposome‐based formulations may be limited due to lipid oxidation or hydrolysis, which can compromise both the liposomes and their contents. The synthesis of liposomes is intricate and expensive, presenting difficulties in attaining uniform size distribution and scalable production methods. 24 Moreover, liposomes may elicit immunological responses, resulting in their swift elimination from the bloodstream, thus reducing their therapeutic efficacy. Their capacity for drug loading is frequently constrained, and their efficacy in penetrating specific tissues, such as the blood–brain barrier, may be inadequate. The combination of these features plus the possible toxicity from stabilizers or surfactants renders liposomes less suitable for some uses, despite their potential in medication delivery and biotechnology. 25

Sphingosomes

According to Ravi et al. (2011), sphingosomes are concentric, bilayered vesicles with an aqueous volume completely encased by a membranous lipid bilayer primarily made of sphingolipid, either synthetic or natural. This was created to get around the instability that liposomes frequently exhibit. Utilizing lipids with ether or amide linkage rather than ester linkage—such as sphingolipid—or phospholipid derivatives with carbomoyloxy activity in place of the 2‐ester linkage might help prevent hydrolysis. Therefore, sphingosomes are stable liposomes that are made with sphingolipid to prevent liposome. 1

Niosomes

Niosomes are vesicles mostly made up of surfactants that create nonionic bilayers. They have structural similarities with liposome, however, the synthetic surfactants utilized provide advantages over phospholipids, including far lower costs and greater chemical stability compared with their naturally occurring counterparts. 26 Synthetic nonionic surfactants can be hydrated to produce niosomes, either with or without the addition of lipids such as cholesterol. Niosomes act similarly to liposome, increasing the drug's bioavailability and decreasing its elimination in the same way. Like liposome, niosomes may also be utilized to target medications to certain areas inside the body. Similar to liposome, the characteristics of niosomes are determined by the bilayers composition as well as the process used to produce them. Niosomes have also been used to transport tiny compounds and antigens. 27 Niosomes, on the other hand, are favored over liposomes because of their greater chemical stability and economy.

Transfersomes

The goal of developing transfersomes technology was to provide a means of delivering bioactive molecules across the skin barrier. 28 Transfersomes, which are made up of phospholipids and extra edge‐active amphiphiles such as bile salts that allow for excessive vesicle shape deformation, are basically ultra‐deformable liposomes. When the vesicle is distributed in a buffer, its diameter is around 100 nm. These pliable vesicles are believed to penetrate intact through the intact dermis in response to the hydrostatic gradient pressures in the skin. 29 These vesicles can be loaded with a drug or antigen in a fashion akin to liposomes. To capitalize on the benefits of phospholipids' vesicles as a transdermal medication carrier, transfersomes were created. With their ultra‐flexible membrane, these self‐optimized aggregates can transport the medicine with great efficiency and repeatability through or into the skin, depending on the application or administration method chosen. 30

Nanoparticles of polymers

Nanoparticles are composed of biocompatible materials such as chitosan or poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA), combined with Moringa oleifera extracts that contain various bioactive compounds, including flavonoids and phenolic acids. Key characteristics of these nanoparticles include a mean size ranging from 100 to 300 nm, which is advantageous for cellular uptake and penetration of biological barriers. 31 , 32 The zeta potential often falls between −20 mV and +30 mV, indicating good stability and the potential for effective interaction with cell membranes. Drug loading capacities can vary, but studies have reported loading efficiencies of 30%–70%, depending on the extraction method and formulation technique. Release profiles typically demonstrate a sustained release pattern, allowing for prolonged therapeutic effects. In terms of quantitative in vivo performance, have shown enhanced pharmacokinetics, with improved bioavailability and biodistribution to target tissues, particularly in models of inflammation and oxidative stress. These formulations have also demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy, reducing markers of inflammation and oxidative damage, thereby highlighting their potential as effective drug delivery systems in various therapeutic contexts. 33 Depending on their structure, PNPs may potentially be transportable and physiologically active. It is possible to create PNPs as nanospheres or nanocapsules, which would result in the drug enclosing or encasing the polymer matrix. 34 The ability of nonionic surfactants to decrease interactions with phagocytic systems by, for example, opsonizing them, which lowers phagocytosis, is a noteworthy advantage. 35 , 36 PNPs have enormous promise for delivering drugs to the central nervous system. PNPs' targeted distribution, which is made possible by surface modification, offers the benefit of allowing these CDD systems to be directed into the brain. 37 PNPs can prevent brain deterioration by delivering nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), proteins, and diagnostic chemicals to the brain in addition to small molecule therapies. 38

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs)

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanostructure lipid carriers (NLC), which are LNs of the second generation, are the two forms of lipid nanoparticles. Since their 1991 introduction, SLNs have supplanted conventional colloidal carriers, such as emulsions, liposome, and polymeric micro and nanoparticles. 12 SLNs are biocompatible lipid‐based nanocarrier systems mostly made up of submicron diameters of less than 1000 nm lipid or modified lipid nanostructures (triglycerides, fatty acids, or waxes). 39 The ability of SLNs to deliver hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs, as well as genes, oligonucleotides, peptides, and even smaller nanoparticles like superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, to a variety of diseased tissues should be noted as one of the main factors contributing to their widespread adoption. 40 SLNs transmit molecules from the reticuloendothelial system (RES) in addition to lowering toxicities and safeguarding therapeutic compounds. SLNs offer regulated and sustained release of encapsulated compounds because of their low water solubility; also, because of their higher long‐term stability, SLNs may be employed for extended periods of time. 41 These lipid carriers have several benefits, including the capacity to shield medications from harsh environmental conditions, easy synthesis scaling up by high‐pressure homogenization, biocompatibility, and ultimately, biodegradability. 42 A spectrum of lipids with comparable qualities, such as a low melting point and firmness at both room temperature and body temperature, are combined to create SLNs. Furthermore, SLNs have favorable effects on the end product's toxicity due to their biocompatibility, ease of sterilization, and lack of need for organic solvents during creation. Lipid nanocarriers have two further benefits: they are easily scaled up and suitable for industrial use. Targeting lipids can be added to SLNs to functionalize them and direct them toward certain organs. 43 , 44

Because they may pass through the blood–brain barrier, SLNs are among the least expensive and safest medication carriers available for treating neurological illnesses. Due to their efficacy and functionality of SLNs as drug carriers are contingent upon their constituents, size, structure, physico‐chemical properties, and synthetic procedures, it is necessary to elucidate the current fabrication technologies employed in their production. Lipid nanoparticles that have been recently synthesized have gradually enhanced the benefits and usefulness of SLNs as drug carriers. 45

Nanoemulsions (NEs)

Combining two immiscible liquids results in nanoemulsions, which are among the greatest medication delivery methods because of their enhanced solubility and kinetic stability? Nano contains droplet sizes ranging from 20 to 200 nm, as the name suggests. 46 Due to their capacity to solubilize non‐polar active compounds, NEs have been suggested as drug delivery methods in a number of pharmaceutical applications. But because of stability issues, nanoemulsions are only made immediately before use, and most recommended formulations are self‐emulsifying systems. 47 NEs are composed of minuscule emulsion droplets, often consisting of oil droplets suspended in water. From a thermodynamic point of view, NEs are in a non‐equilibrium condition, much like regular emulsions (with diameters >m). The three main parts of a stable nanoemulsion are the surfactant, the oil phase, and the aqueous phase. 15 , 48 NEs have some benefits in common with other lipid drug carriers, such as improved encapsulation efficiency, ease of scaling up production processes, protection of molecules from unfavorable environmental conditions, lack of toxicity because they are made of biocompatible, biodegradable, and approved pharmaceutical ingredients, and flexibility in using them in different administration routes. Nevertheless, a number of studies have discovered poor stability and post‐storage release of the encapsulated compounds. 49

NEs are used in a range of systemic modes of administration as medication delivery methods. Parenteral, sometimes known as injectable, nanoemulsion administration is utilized for a number of applications, including targeted medication delivery, vaccination delivery, gene carriers, and nutrition (such as lipids, carbs, and vitamins). 50

Inorganic nanoparticles

Preclinical and clinical trials are being conducted on inorganic nanoparticles, including gold, iron oxide, silica, and silver, to treat, diagnose, and detect a range of illnesses. Moreover, a large number of the inorganic materials that are utilized to create nanoparticles have been used in clinical settings for a variety of therapeutic applications for a long time. 51 , 52 Platinum (such as cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin, etc.), which is frequently used in cancer therapy, and silver ions, which are frequently employed as an antibacterial agent, are two well‐known examples of inorganic compounds having utility in therapies. 53 While polymeric and other conventionally utilized nanoparticles lack specific prospects for clinical diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, inorganic nanoparticles do. For instance, a clinical experiment utilizing AuNP and carbon nanotube functionalized biosensors is now being conducted to investigate ex vivo detection with inorganic nanoparticles to identify stomach lesions in patient breath. 54 , 55

The biomedical domain gains substantial advantages from the vast surface area, modifiable structures, varied surface chemistry, and distinct optical and physical characteristics of inorganic nanoparticles. Consequently, without producing any obvious acute toxicity, scientists from all over the world have exploited inorganic nanoparticles and their metal ions as therapeutic agents that are targeted to specific tissues or in the treatment of a variety of disorders. 56

The use of these nanoparticles to treat brain disorders is quite new. Because of their high surface‐to‐volume ratio and material‐specific intrinsic features, NPs offer alternative treatment options and facilitate the effective loading of medicinal chemicals. 57 , 58

Glioblastoma multiforme patients are treated with cisplatin‐tethered gold nanoparticles, which act as drug transporters and radiosensitizers during radiotherapy by releasing Auger electrons and ionizing photoelectron beams. 59

Equally, heat produced by iron oxide nanoparticles in an oscillating magnetic field can be utilized to: (i) alleviate hyperthermia in glioblastoma patients; (ii) temporarily open the blood–brain barrier to permit the delivery of nanoparticles to cerebral tissue; and (iii) boost NP uptake by opening up heat‐sensitive ion channels. 55 , 60 However, to effectively use NPs as therapeutic agents or drug delivery systems, a fuller understanding of the principles underlying their interaction and the functional repercussions in brain circuits is required. 61

Exosomes as naturally nanocarriers for epilepsy

Biological particles known as extracellular vesicles (EVs) are found in many bio‐fluids, such as blood, semen, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine. These particles are encased in a lipid bilayer membrane and are released by nearly all cells. 62 Three types of EVs are distinguished by their size and biogenesis mechanism: apoptotic bodies, micro‐vesicles, and exosomes. Although apoptotic bodies and micro‐vesicles are formed from apoptotic cells and plasma membranes, respectively, the biogenesis process of exosomes, which are the smallest EVs with a diameter of 30–100 nm, begins with endosomes. 63 Endosomes are first formed when the plasma membrane's clathrin‐coated regions proliferate inward. Following the inward budding of the endosome membrane, multivesicular bodies (MVBs) arise. MVBs have the ability to either release their intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), known as exosomes into the outside of the cell membrane, or they can adhere to the liposome and be broken down. 43 Exosomes were once thought to be cellular wastes, which is an interesting fact to know. However, additional research has shown that exosomes, based on their cellular origin, are packed with a variety of functional molecules like messenger RNA (mRNA), DNA, long non‐coding RNA (LncRNA), and micro RNAs (miRNAs), which can enter recipient cells and affects their behavior and features. 64 As a result, exosomes function as the body's own natural nanocarriers and may be used to transport a variety of drugs, including ASMs. There are several benefits of using exosomes for medicine delivery. 65 , 66 (i) Exosomes have a longer half‐life in the circulation than synthesized nanoparticles because of their endogenous origin, which also makes them less toxic and less likely to be eliminated by macrophages and reticuloendothelial cells. (ii) Exosomes may readily cross through the BBB and come into contact with brain tissue since it is permeable to them. As a result, they could provide good vehicles for the delivery of medications to treat epilepsy in the brain. (iii) Exosomes that are produced from various cellular sources have a range of membrane ligands that help them target particular tissues and efficiently absorb into cells. The lumen of exosomes contains a range of biological materials that may have therapeutic applications. 67 shown that giving exosomes generated by human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells intravenously maintained normal neurogenesis and reduced inflammation and neuron loss in mice suffering from status epileptics. These findings suggest that exosomes have the potential to be used as a treatment for neurological disorders. Further research is needed to explore the full therapeutic capabilities of exosomes in different disease models.

However, enhancing the techniques for isolating and purifying exosomes is a crucial matter that must be considered for their application in medicine. Furthermore, low loading efficiency is another issue that needs to be addressed despite the availability of several methods (such as electroporation, sonication, incubation, etc.) for drug loading into the exosomes. 68 , 69 The internal content of exosomes, which is inherited from their parent cells, should also be kept in mind as it can have both positive and negative effects. This emphasizes the significance of carefully choosing the exosome‐producing cell source for drug delivery.

CHALLENGES IN THE TREATMENT OF EPILEPSY

The absence of an accurate diagnosis is one of the main obstacles in the management of this illness. This can be recognized as a number of factors, involving a clinical expert's lack of knowledge and training, their incapacity to identify the disease's symptoms, or their misinterpretation of common symptoms from other conditions, such as migraine, movement disorders, psychosis, hysteria, brain injury, or epilepsy. 70 , 71 Additional factors contributing to the extended epileptic situations brought on by postponed treatment include inefficiency and a shortage of diagnostic tools. Without the right instruments, focal seizures are particularly challenging to diagnose and are sometimes mistaken for hysteria or psychosis. 72 The wide variety of epileptic syndromes makes it challenging to identify the precise etiology or molecular mechanisms underlying this illness. 73 The need for personalized medicine in the field of epilepsy is demonstrated by patients' resistance to seeking treatment from practitioners, their noncompliance, and their reaction to therapy. Customized therapy may be able to address drug development challenges such as tolerance, inefficacy, and delayed peak plasma concentration, which are thought to be present in the majority of the current AEDs. 74 Other problems with current medications include neurotoxicity and side effects from repeated usage (drowsiness, shaking, mental disorientation, etc.) of benzodiazepines and barbiturates. A substantial portion of the patient population (20%–30%) has resistant epilepsy, which makes it difficult to determine the best AED combinations for clinical studies. 75 , 76

CONCLUSION

Nanotechnology may be a more effective treatment for PWE than more traditional techniques like ASMs. Despite their many potentials, lipid‐based nano‐delivery systems have drawbacks, including instability and large‐scale manufacturing. To ensure their safety and efficacy, they must also cope with regulatory concerns and extensive clinical research. For lipid‐based nano‐delivery systems to completely reap the benefits of medication delivery, these issues must be resolved through continued research and development. To overcome these challenges and commercialize these cutting‐edge technologies, cooperation between researchers, industrial partners, and regulatory bodies will be essential. By enabling tailored therapy based on individual patient needs and responses, the use of nanotechnology in drug delivery presents opportunities for personalized medicine in the treatment of epilepsy. Additionally, lipid‐based nano‐delivery devices can be used to increase drug bioavailability and solubility, which will increase the overall efficacy of epilepsy therapies. The manner that drugs are supplied and delivered to people with epilepsy could be completely changed if this field continues to improve.
