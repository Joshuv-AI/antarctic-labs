Molecularly guided management of pediatric endobronchial inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor: a report of two cases on surgical and targeted intervention
Authors: Liqin Ke, Shuxian Li, Lei Wu, Guohong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMTs) of the airway are rare mesenchymal neoplasms of intermediate biological potential characterized by clonal proliferation and diverse clinical presentations. Owing to their nonspecific symptoms, such as persistent cough and stridor, these tumors are frequently misdiagnosed as more common pediatric conditions, including refractory asthma or foreign-body aspiration, often leading to delays in definitive treatment.Case DescriptionWe described 2 pediatric cases of endobronchial IMT managed according to anatomical location and molecular features. Case 1 was an 8-year-old boy with an anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-negative tumor located at the opening of the posterior segment of the right lower lobe bronchus, who underwent robot-assisted sleeve resection with preservation of functional lung parenchyma. Case 2 was a 6-year-old boy with an ALK-positive carinal tumor causing critical airway narrowing, who underwent primary endoscopic resection followed by adjuvant crizotinib therapy based on multidisciplinary assessment of molecular status and recurrence risk. No evidence of recurrence was observed at 6-month follow-up in Case 1 or at 3-month follow-up in Case 2.ConclusionsThese two cases suggest that integrating molecular diagnostics, particularly ALK status, with a stepwise multidisciplinary approach may help guide individualized management in pediatric airway IMT. However, the short follow-up duration of 6 months and 3 months limits conclusions regarding long-term recurrence and durability of treatment outcomes.Keywords: Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), crizotinib, robot-assisted surgery, case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• Two pediatric cases of airway inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) highlight that treatment must be tailored to tumor location and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) status: robotic sleeve resection for localized ALK-negative tumors and endoscopic resection plus crizotinib for ALK-positive carinal lesions. Both patients achieved complete recovery without recurrence.

What is known and what is new?

• Airway IMT is rare and often misdiagnosed as asthma or foreign-body aspiration, and ALK rearrangements occur in many cases.

• This report demonstrates a practical “stepwise” strategy integrating anatomical precision, minimally invasive surgery, and ALK-targeted therapy and offers a personalized management framework for complex pediatric airway IMT.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should consider IMT in children with unexplained airway obstruction and promptly obtain molecular profiling (especially ALK). Multidisciplinary teams must adopt individualized anatomy- and biomarker-guided pathways to avoid diagnostic delays and optimize lung preservation and outcomes.

Introduction

Background

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare and distinct clinical entity first characterized by Brunn in 1939 (1). It has since been formally categorized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a “mesenchymal neoplasm of intermediate biological potential”, reflecting its capacity for aggressive local growth and a low—but documented—risk of distant metastasis (2). For consistency, this WHO-based definition is used throughout the present manuscript when referring to the biological classification of IMT. Histologically, IMT is defined by a characteristic diagnostic triad consisting of proliferation of myofibroblastic spindle cells, a myxoid or collagenous stroma, and a prominent inflammatory infiltrate typically composed of plasma cells and lymphocytes (3). Although the exact etiology remains the subject of ongoing investigation, various potential triggers—such as prior infection, trauma, or autoimmune reactions—have been suggested in the literature (4). The biological behavior of IMT is highly diverse, ranging from benign, localized lesions to more persistent and locally invasive forms (5).

Based on analyses from the SEER database and German cancer registries, primary malignant tumors of the lung and airways are exceptionally rare in children, accounting for only 0.03% of all lung cancer cases with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.049 cases per 100,000 children (6,7). Among these, airway IMT is particularly infrequent, accounting for only 5–12% of all pediatric pulmonary IMT cases (8). Despite its low incidence, IMT is the most common primary endobronchial mesenchymal tumor in children (9), and epidemiological data indicate that the disease typically affects children aged 7–8 years and a slight male predominance has been observed (10). These tumors are frequently located within the trachea or major bronchi, and their presence can significantly compromise airway integrity and overall respiratory function (11).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Clinical management of pediatric airway IMT is complicated by its nonspecific clinical presentation (12). Affected children frequently exhibit persistent cough, localized wheezing, and dyspnea, symptoms that are common to many other childhood respiratory conditions (13). This diagnostic ambiguity contributes to a strikingly high misdiagnosis rate, reported to be between 40% and 70% across studies (14). Many affected children are initially treated for refractory asthma, recurrent pneumonia, or suspected foreign-body aspiration, which often leads to significant delays in establishing a definitive diagnosis (7). Such delays carry significant clinical consequences, as prolonged airway obstruction can lead to secondary pulmonary complications, including obstructive pneumonia, bronchiectasis, and irreversible lung atelectasis.

Objective

The recent integration of molecular diagnostics and advanced surgical techniques has reshaped the management of this mesenchymal neoplasm of intermediate biological potential toward more minimally invasive and individualized strategies (15). A critical development in this field has been the detection of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene rearrangements in approximately 50–60% of cases, which has enabled the use of targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as crizotinib, for ALK-positive tumors. In parallel, the advent of robot-assisted bronchoplastic and advanced interventional bronchoscopy has expanded the options for achieving R0 resection while maximizing the preservation of functional lung tissue. In this context, we pro-pose an optimized stepwise management algorithm based on two representative pediatric cases with distinct molecular profiles and anatomical locations, emphasizing the critical importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized therapeutic pathways. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0456/rc).

Case presentation

The first case was an 8-year-old male who presented with a 2-week history of persistent cough and a brief febrile episode with his temperature reaching 38.5 ℃. Physical examination and laboratory investigations, including those for inflammatory markers and respiratory pathogen panels, were largely unremarkable. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans revealed a focal soft-tissue protrusion within the medial wall of the right lower lobe posterior segment (S6) bronchus, accompanied by distal mucus impaction and obstructive atelectasis (Figure 1). Flexible bronchoscopy revealed a smooth, firm, and white endobronchial mass nearly obliterating the S6 orifice (Figure 2). Histopathological analysis of the biopsy specimen revealed spindle-cell proliferation, with an immunohistochemical profile of ALK (−), smooth muscle actin (SMA) (+), and desmin (+), and a high Ki-67 index of 60% (Figure 3). Although the Ki-67 index was unusually high for conventional IMT, the lesion did not show the predominant epithelioid morphology, marked nuclear atypia, or necrotic/destructive features expected for more aggressive differential diagnoses, and the overall histomorphology remained more consistent with spindle-cell IMT. Given the ALK-negative status and anatomical location of the tumor, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) recommended a lung-sparing approach. The elevated Ki-67 index was also considered during MDT discussion and supported the need for complete local excision with careful pathological assessment, rather than prolonged observational management. The patient underwent robot-assisted bronchoplastic sleeve resection of the S6 bronchus with hilar lymph node dissection to achieve negative margins and preserve functional lower lobes. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed complete resection with negative surgical margins (R0 resection). The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and follow-up imaging at 3 and 6 months confirmed the absence of recurrence.

The second case was a 6-year-old male admitted with an acute onset of cough 2 days prior, inspiratory wheezing, and hoarseness. Physical examination revealed prominent inspiratory stridor, although tumor markers and infection screenings were negative. CT imaging identified a round soft-tissue mass at the level of the tracheal carina (Figure 4). Endoscopic evaluation via direct laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy revealed a dark-red irregular mass situated just above the carina, which was significantly obstructing both the left and right main bronchi. Unlike the first case, this lesion was managed primarily through interventional bronchoscopy using a “three-step” technique involving an electrosurgical snare, argon plasma coagulation (APC) for hemostasis, and cryotherapy to treat the tumor base (Figure 5). Pathological examination con-firmed IMT with a myxoid background and spindle-cell infiltration. However, immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed ALK positivity (+) along with a low Ki-67 index of 5% (Figure 6). Although complete macroscopic resection was achieved endoscopically, the multidisciplinary team did not consider this alone sufficient to eliminate concern for local recurrence because the lesion arose at the tracheal carina, a critically important airway location in which even limited regrowth could rapidly produce clinically significant obstruction. In addition, the tumor was ALK positive, providing a plausible molecular target for postoperative suppression of residual microscopic disease. On this basis, adjuvant crizotinib was selected as an individualized MDT-guided strategy rather than as a routine standard of care after complete resection. The patient was prescribed crizotinib at a dosage of 100 mg twice daily for 3 months. Subsequent bronchoscopic and CT surveillance at the 3-month timepoint showed a stable surgical site with no evidence of tumor regrowth. However, this short follow-up interval was considered insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding the long-term preventive effect of adjuvant crizotinib on recurrence. Pathology and molecular profiling

The definitive diagnosis of IMT relies on a comprehensive histopathological evaluation characterized by a classic diagnostic triad, consisting of the proliferation of myofibroblastic spindle cells, a myxoid or collagenous stroma, and a prominent inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of lymphocytes and plasma cells. Microscopically, spindle cells typically exhibit a fascicular or storiform growth pattern with vesicular nuclei and inconspicuous nucleoli, although some degree of cellular atypia may be present. Based on the 2020 WHO classification, IMT can be further subdivided into three histological patterns: myxoid/vascular, hypercellular, and hypocellular fibrous. These patterns often overlap within a single lesion, reflecting the morphological plasticity of the myofibroblastic lineage. In the cases presented, Case 1 exhibited pronounced spindle-cell hyperplasia, whereas Case 2 demonstrated a predominantly myxoid stroma with a more scattered inflammatory component.

IHC plays a pivotal role in confirming myofibroblastic differentiation and excluding other spindle-cell neoplasms, as shown in Table 2. Myofibroblastic cells in IMT typically show strong and diffuse positivity for SMA and desmin, reflecting their myogenic origins. Other markers, such as calponin, are also frequently expressed in over 90% of cases. In our report, Case 1 showed strong positivity for SMA and desmin, consistent with myofibroblastic differentiation, along with CD68 positivity, which highlights the histiocytic component of the inflammatory background. Case 2 displayed desmin and STAT6 positivity with partial expression of SMA and CD34. Differentiation between these markers is essential to distinguish IMT from mimics, such as IgG4-related sclerosing disease, which is characterized by a high ratio of IgG4-positive plasma cells, and low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma, which typically exhibits higher nuclear pleomorphism and necrotic areas.

The molecular hallmark of IMT is the presence of gene rearrangements, most notably involving ALK, located on chromosome 2p23. Approximately 50–60% of pediatric IMT cases demonstrate ALK rearrangements, which lead to constitutive activation of the ALK signaling pathway and subsequent oncogenic transformation. On IHC, this molecular event manifests as diffuse cytoplasmic or granular staining, as seen in Case 2. In contrast, Case 1 was ALK negative, highlighting the molecular heterogeneity of the disease. Although ALK positivity is a strong diagnostic indicator, its absence does not preclude the diagnosis of IMT, because a subset of cases may harbor alternative fusions involving ROS1, NTRK3, or PDGFRB. The Ki-67 proliferation index serves as a useful adjunct marker when assessing biological aggressiveness, but it should not be interpreted in isolation. In the present series, Case 1 exhibited an unusually high Ki-67 index of 60%, which raised concern for a more proliferative lesion and warranted careful correlation with overall morphology and immunophenotype. Nevertheless, the tumor retained a spindle-cell architecture without a predominantly epithelioid growth pattern or overtly high-grade cytological features, supporting retention of the diagnosis of IMT rather than immediate reclassification as a more aggressive sarcomatous entity. By contrast, Case 2 showed a low Ki-67 index of 5%, consistent with a more indolent proliferative profile. Discussion

Clinical characteristics and diagnostic challenges

IMTs were first described by Brunn in 1939 and are currently recognized by the WHO as mesenchymal neoplasms of intermediate biological potential (16). Although IMT is recognized as the most common primary endobronchial mesenchymal tumor in children, involvement of the pediatric airway remains exceedingly rare, representing only 5–12% of all pediatric pulmonary IMT cases (17). Epidemiological data suggest a median patient age of 7–8 years, with a slight male predominance (ratio of approximately 1.2:1) (18).

The primary clinical challenge in the diagnosis of IMT lies in the absence of pathognomonic symptoms, as clinical presentations depend largely on the tumor’s anatomical location and degree of luminal obstruction (19). Patients with tracheal lesions typically present with inspiratory wheezing, hoarseness, and dyspnea and are frequently misdiagnosed as refractory asthma or foreign body aspiration (20), whereas bronchial lesions more often cause localized cough and obstructive pneumonia (21). These nonspecific presentations contribute to an extremely high preoperative misdiagnosis rate of 40–70%. Our study findings reinforce this complexity, as both patients initially presented with cough and wheezing, highlighting the importance of early endoscopic and radiological interventions when standard respiratory treatments fail (22).

Radiological and endoscopic diagnostic logic

The integration of multislice spiral CT scanning and interventional bronchoscopy is essential for accurate diagnosis and staging of IMT. On contrast-enhanced CT scans, pediatric airway IMTs typically appear as isolated intraluminal nodules with light-to-moderate enhancement (23). Associated radiological signs such as mucus impaction and distal lung atelectasis are common markers of significant obstruction. Furthermore, lymph node calcification is observed in 15–30% of cases, a rate notably lower than that observed in adult populations. Positron emission tomography-CT often demonstrates high metabolic activity, with maximum standardized uptake values ranging from 3 to 15, which can lead to diagnostic confusion with granulomatous dis-ease or low-grade sarcomas (24). In Case 1, CT imaging was instrumental in identifying focal soft-tissue protrusions and distal lung changes, guiding the planning of lung-sparing surgery. Bronchoscopy provides a vital direct view of the tumor morphology, typically revealing a firm, reddish mass with a smooth or irregular surface. Endoscopic assessment of the tumor base, whether pedunculated or broad-based, is a critical factor in deciding between interventional bronchoscopic resection and radical surgical excision (25).

Pathological evaluation and differential diagnosis

Definitive diagnosis of IMT relies on the identification of the characteristic “histological triad”: myofibroblastic spindle-cell proliferation, a myxoid or collagenous stroma, and a prominent inflammatory infiltrate composed of plasma cells and lymphocytes. IHC staining serves as a powerful tool to confirm myofibroblastic differentiation, with SMA-, desmin-, and calponin- positivity observed in more than 90% of cases (26). Differential diagnosis must exclude several mimics. Immunoglobulin (Ig) G4-related sclerosing disease is distinguished by a high density of IgG4-positive plasma cells and obliterative phlebitis. Low-grade myofibroblastic sarcomas typically exhibit greater cellular atypia, higher mitotic activity (Ki-67 index), and necrotic areas (27).

Another important consideration in the differential diagnosis of highly proliferative IMT-like lesions is epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma (EIMS), an aggressive variant characterized by a predominantly epithelioid morphology, round-to-plump tumor cells embedded in a myxoid stroma, marked cytological atypia, and a more destructive clinical course. EIMS is most often associated with ALK rearrangement and typically demonstrates ALK positivity on immunohistochemistry, often with distinctive staining patterns depending on the fusion partner. In our Case 1, despite the unusually elevated Ki-67 index, the lesion lacked a predominant epithelioid architecture, did not show the degree of cytological pleomorphism or necrotic change expected for EIMS, and was ALK negative on immunohistochemistry. Taken together, these features argued against EIMS and supported classification as a spindle-cell IMT with an unusually high proliferative index. Additionally, clinicians must rule out mucoepidermoid carcinomas and carcinoid tumors that possess distinct neuroendocrine or glandular architectures and specific molecular drivers (Table 3) (28). In lesions with an unusually high proliferative index, the differential diagnosis should also be broadened to include more aggressive spindle-cell or epithelioid neoplasms, and interpretation should rely on integrated assessment of morphology, immunophenotype, and clinical-anatomical context rather than on Ki-67 alone.

Molecular heterogeneity and the role of ALK

A pivotal advancement in understanding IMT has been the identification of ALK gene rearrangements in approximately 50–60% of cases (28). These rearrangements result in constitutive activation of the ALK signaling pathway, thereby driving oncogenic transformation. Interestingly, ALK positivity was more frequently associated with endobronchial locations, suggesting a correlation between the anatomical site and molecular events (29). In our series, Case 2 demonstrated ALK positivity, which provided a biologically relevant therapeutic target and contributed to the MDT decision to consider postoperative crizotinib in the context of a high-risk anatomical site. Importantly, this decision should be interpreted as individualized and case-specific rather than as evidence supporting routine adjuvant targeted therapy after complete resection. In ALK-negative cases, alternative fusions involving ROS1, NTRK3, or PDGFRB may be present, necessitating broader molecular profiling of high-risk patients (30).

Stepwise treatment strategies: endoscopy, surgery, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors

Current management of pediatric airway IMT can be conceptualized as a stepwise framework in which standard local treatment options, including interventional bronchoscopy and surgical resection, form the backbone of care, whereas targeted pharmacotherapy should be reserved for selected molecularly defined situations and interpreted within an individualized MDT context (Figure 7). Importantly, the molecularly guided branch should not be understood only as a postoperative adjunct. In carefully selected kinase-fusion-positive tumors, particularly when upfront resection would be highly morbid, disfiguring, or excessively lung-sacrificing, neoadjuvant targeted therapy may be considered to achieve tumor shrinkage and create a safer or more organ-preserving definitive procedure. Recent pediatric and translational reports have reinforced the importance of rapid molecular stratification and contemporary kinase-directed therapy in rare IMT presentations, supporting a more proactive role for molecular testing early in treatment planning. Accordingly, Figure 7 is intended to illustrate a practical management framework that includes both conventional local treatment and selected molecularly guided preoperative or adjunctive targeted-therapy considerations, rather than a prescriptive algorithm for all resected or ALK-positive cases. Interventional bronchoscopy is highly effective for pedunculated or small lesions with a base diameter of <1 cm (31). In Case 2, the use of electrosurgical snares and cryotherapy achieved R0 resection with no complications.

Nevertheless, the decision to add postoperative crizotinib in this patient requires careful qualification. The rationale was not that adjuvant ALK inhibition is an established standard after R0 resection, but rather that the tumor was located at the carina, where even a small-volume recurrence could have immediate and potentially life-threatening airway consequences. Furthermore, the ALK-positive status offered a targetable molecular abnormality, and the MDT judged that short-course postoperative inhibition could be considered as a risk-adapted adjunct in this exceptional anatomical context. Thus, the use of crizotinib in Case 2 should be interpreted as a highly individualized management decision based on anatomical criticality and molecular targetability, rather than as a broadly generalizable postoperative protocol.

At present, there is a lack of large-scale prospective evidence specifically supporting the adjuvant use of crizotinib after complete resection of pediatric airway IMT. Therefore, our experience should be understood as hypothesis-generating and clinically illustrative, not as proof of a standard postoperative targeted strategy. However, complete surgical excision remains the gold standard for broad-based tumors or those invading the airway walls. We recommend robot-assisted or thoracoscopic sleeve resection, as demonstrated in Case 1, to achieve definitive local control while maximizing lung sparing, a critical consideration for pediatric respiratory development (32).

The application of ALK inhibitors represents a major breakthrough in ALK-positive IMT treatment. The Children’s Oncology Group ADVL0912 study demonstrated an objective response rate of 86% (12/14 patients) and a complete response rate of 36% in pediatric patients with unresectable, recurrent, or refractory ALK-positive IMT, leading to FDA approval of crizotinib for this population in July 2022 (33,34). The EORTC 90101 CREATE study further confirmed durable responses, with 3-year follow-up data showing some patients maintaining remission beyond 60 months (16,35). The adjuvant use of crizotinib in our Case 2 illustrates the emerging role of precision medicine in managing high-risk pediatric IMT.

For ALK-negative IMTs, comprehensive molecular profiling is recommended, as approximately 50% harbor alternative kinase fusions (ROS1, NTRK3, PDGFRβ, RET), some of which may open a meaningful neoadjuvant or salvage therapeutic window (36,37). This point is especially relevant when primary surgery would be disfiguring or associated with major functional loss. Wachter et al. reported that rapid molecular characterization of pediatric ROS1 fusion-positive IMT was able to redirect management toward effective targeted inhibition with marked radiologic response, thereby underscoring the practical value of tumor shrinkage before definitive local control in selected cases (37). In fusion-negative cases, anti-inflammatory therapy (prednisone plus COX-2 inhibitor) may induce remission in unresectable tumors (38), while conventional chemotherapy (vinblastine plus methotrexate) maintains favorable response rates with comparable overall survival to ALK-positive patients (39).

Prognosis and long-term surveillance

The prognosis of pediatric airway IMT is generally favorable after complete resection, although the risk of local recurrence persists (Table 4). Ki-67 is a significant prognostic marker, with elevated expression associated with aggressive behavior, higher recurrence rates, and poorer survival (40,41). The literature suggests that while R0 resections have a recurrence rate of 2–8%, incomplete excisions (R1/R2) carry a risk as high as 25–60%. Because reported recurrence may occur beyond the early postoperative period, including a median interval of approximately 11–20 months in the literature, close surveillance is warranted, particularly in the first year after treatment (42). The follow-up periods for the two cases in this study were 3 and 6 months, respectively, which are insufficient to fully evaluate long-term outcomes. We recommend postoperative follow-up with bronchoscopy and low-dose CT scans at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, followed by annual surveillance for at least 5 years. For patients receiving targeted therapy, regular monitoring of adverse effects such as visual disturbances and hepatotoxicity is essential. Should recurrence occur, re-treatment with crizotinib has been shown to remain effective in most cases, and next-generation ALK inhibitors (e.g., lorlatinib, alectinib) offer alternative options for resistant cases. Ultimately, early recognition and an MDT approach are the cornerstones of successful management (43).

Limitations

Several limitations of this report should be acknowledged. First, this study is based on only 2 pediatric cases, and therefore the proposed stepwise management framework should not be interpreted as a validated treatment algorithm. Second, follow-up remained short, with 6 months in Case 1 and 3 months in Case 2, which limits our ability to assess long-term recurrence, sustained airway patency, and the durability of postoperative disease control. Third, the use of adjuvant crizotinib in the ALK-positive carinal lesion was a case-specific MDT decision made in the context of anatomical criticality and molecular targetability; it should not be generalized as routine therapy after complete resection. Fourth, there is currently no large-scale prospective pediatric evidence supporting the routine adjuvant use of crizotinib after R0 resection in airway IMT. Finally, because the available literature is composed largely of case reports and small retrospective studies, the level of evidence supporting many management decisions remains limited. Therefore, the present report should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and clinically illustrative rather than practice-defining.

Conclusions

Pediatric endobronchial IMTs are rare but treatable; however, their clinical management is frequently hampered by misdiagnosis. The key to improving early recognition and clinical outcomes lies in the routine application of chest CT scanning and bronchoscopy in children presenting with “non-refractory asthma” or “recurrent pneumonia.” These investigations are crucial for identifying intraluminal obstructions and facilitating timely interventions before irreversible pulmonary complications occur.

Furthermore, we advocate the establishment of an individualized MDT diagnostic and therapeutic model based on ALK expression status. A stepwise management framework, in which standard local treatment is prioritized and targeted therapy is considered only in selected molecularly defined scenarios, may help tailor care to tumor anatomy and biological features in pediatric airway IMT. Such a framework may help support margin-conscious, lung-preserving, and anatomically tailored decision-making in selected pediatric patients, but its broader applicability requires further validation in larger cohorts with longer follow-up. Future development of national registries and standardized molecular protocols will be essential to further refine the “endoscopic-minimally invasive-precision” ladder strategy for this unique pediatric malignancy.
