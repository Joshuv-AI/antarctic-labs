Supporting referral to pulmonary rehabilitation from primary care: A systematic evaluation of interventions using the ERIC taxonomy
Authors: Sarah Hug, Sanduni Madawala, Arwel W. Jones, Caitlin Dumergue, Anne E. Holland, Narelle S. Cox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is a key component in managing chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs). Primary care, often the first point of contact for people with CRD, is well-positioned to facilitate referral to PR, yet referral rates are low. This systematic review aimed to identify the key components of primary care interventions to support referral to PR and synthesise their effect on referral rate.MethodsFive electronic databases were searched to identify studies of any design that reported interventions implemented in primary care to support referral to PR for people with CRD. Interventions could target people with CRD and/or healthcare professionals. Screening, quality appraisal using the Downs and Black checklist, and data extraction were conducted independently by two reviewers. Interventions were mapped to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy—a framework of 73 strategies seeking to enhance the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of evidence-based interventions.ResultsThirteen studies were included, employing a range of quasi-experimental, observational, and randomised designs. Overall, the studies were of moderate quality (mean total score 14, range 10 to 25 out of 27). Interventions incorporated a mean of 12 ERIC strategies across five domains, most commonly education and training, interactive assistance, clinician support, and audit and feedback. Impact of interventions on referral rates was minimal (n = 3 randomised controlled trials, pooled mean difference 0%, 95% confidence interval −0.24 to 0.25).InterpretationMulti-component interventions, including numerous implementation strategies, have achieved minimal improvement in PR referral rates from primary care. Understanding how strategies are delivered and applied, rather than simply the number of strategies or the combination they are used in, may be important for operationalisation of referral focussed interventions. Addressing the behavioural processes underpinning referral decisions, together with the use of theory-informed, context-specific approaches may enhance effectiveness of referral focussed interventions.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRD) are highly prevalent non-communicable conditions affecting the airways and lung parenchyma1–3. The global burden of CRDs, particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), is substantial, with global experts underscoring its growing impact and calling for urgent research to address the escalating public health challenge4. While CRDs among adults are incurable, both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments can significantly improve quality of life, reduce symptoms, help with overall disease management and mitigate healthcare burden1. Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR), a structured and individualised program combining exercise and education5–7, is the primary non-pharmacological treatment associated with these benefits8–12. Despite being recommended as an important component in the management of CRDs5,13, PR is markedly underutilised14,15, with persistently low referral rates limiting patients’ ability to access and benefit from these programs.

The majority of people living with CRD are managed exclusively in primary care16. However rates of referral to PR from primary care are low, often less than 10%15,17,18. Given the evidence for its benefit, low rates of referral are problematic. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) working in primary care identify significant barriers to referral including lack of awareness of the program and referral pathways, low self-efficacy with making a referral and presumed low patient motivation19–23. Additionally, people with CRD report psychological barriers such as fear of breathlessness, uncertainty regarding what the program entails, shame and stigma with accessing healthcare, and transport difficulties hindering the ability to engage or attend19.

Previous efforts to increase referrals to PR from primary and tertiary care settings have centred on changing HCPs referral practices—through education, training, computer-generated prompts, and financial incentives—as well as supporting shared decision-making, for example by using tools that encourage discussion during routine reviews24,25, or showing a co-designed video to support patient understanding of the program26. However, effects were at best modest at improving referrals to PR (range 6% to 25%)24. Commonly the interventions under review were part of a larger, multi-component intervention to optimise CRD management, and the effects of individual components on implementation practices were not identified. Identifying which components are most effective for improving referral to PR is important because HCP referral practices and patient acceptance of referral are shaped by multiple interacting determinants—such as knowledge, skills, beliefs about consequences, environmental constraints, and social influences22. To support clearer specification and comparison of intervention components across studies, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy27 provides a comprehensive list of 73 intervention components, organised into nine domains28, and has demonstrated robust face validity for describing implementation strategies29. This taxonomy enables researchers to identify the active components within each intervention and provides a shared language for examining how implementation strategies are used to support changes in practice. Therefore, the research questions for this systematic review were:i.What are the key components of interventions delivered in primary care to support referral to PR for people with CRD?ii.What is the effect on referral rates of intervention(s) delivered in primary care to support referral to PR?

Methods

This systematic review was prospectively registered (PROSPERO CRD42024595844) and is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines30.

Identification and selection of studies

We searched Medline, Cinahl, PEDro, EMBASE (via Ovid) and PsycINFO from inception to 13 November 2025. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a librarian (see Supplement 1). We sought to find primary research articles of any trial design (excluding case studies), published as full-text articles in peer-reviewed journals that report on an intervention implemented in primary care to support referral to PR for adults with CRD. The interventions could target people with CRDs, HCPs who refer to PR, or both. Studies published in languages other than English were excluded. Studies of interventions that included the delivery of PR in a primary care setting, rather than referral to PR, were also excluded.

Study screening

Search results were uploaded to Covidence to support study screening. Duplicates were removed and two reviewers (SH, SM, NSC, AWJ or CD) independently screened title and abstracts for eligibility. Studies that could not be excluded based on reviewing title and abstracts proceeded to independent screening of the full text, by two reviewers, to determine final eligibility. Any conflicts in full text screening were managed by discussion and involvement of a third review author as required.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data pertaining to study characteristics, the intervention, mode of delivery, target group, and referral rates were extracted from each study. Using a data extraction template of the ERIC taxonomy24, two reviewers independently coded ERIC components applied in each intervention, which was checked and collated by a third reviewer. Methodological quality was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Downs and Black checklist25. The checklist allows for comparison of quality across different study designs, and assesses study quality, external validity, study bias, confounding and selection bias and power of the study. An overall quality score, and scores for each of the five sections was determined. Quality scoring discrepancies were resolved by involving a third reviewer.

Data management and analysis

The ERIC taxonomy was used to identify and map the implementation components applied in each intervention. To understand the implementation target of each intervention, ERIC domains were grouped according to macro, meso- or micro-levels. Macro-level grouping indicated domains that act at the health-system level through policy, funding or infrastructure; meso-level grouping indicated domains that support how services and professionals work together to deliver care; and micro-level grouping indicated domains that act at the individual HCP–patient interface to shape referral decisions. ERIC domain and component use were reported narratively. To examine how ERIC components were used together across studies, we calculated proportional co-occurrence between individual ERIC implementation components. For each component, we identified all studies in which it appeared and then calculated the proportion of those studies in which a second component also appeared. This normalisation accounts for large differences in how frequently individual components were used and provides a comparable measure of association strength (i.e., “given component X was used, how often was component Y also used?”). Co-occurrence analyses and visualisations were conducted in R (version 4.5.1)26.

Referral rates associated with the interventions were extracted as reported by study authors. Where possible, we planned to tabulate all referral data as total number of referrals (count data) or number/proportion of participants referred (dichotomous data). Where sufficient data were available from randomised controlled trials, results were meta-analysed in RevMan 527, and a pooled mean difference with 95% confidence intervals calculated.

Results

The flow of studies through this review is summarised in Fig. 1. Searches detected 1908 records. After removal of duplicates, 1426 title and abstracts were screened. 66 records progressed to full text review, of which 13 studies were included in this review28,29,31–41. Included studies were conducted in Australia, the United Kingdom, Denmark, and Sweden, comprising a mix of quasi-experimental pre–post designs, observational cohorts, mixed-methods approaches, and cluster randomised controlled trials. Of the included studies, 12 had interventions that were designed to change referral practices of primary care HCPs (e.g., general practitioners, practice nurses, pharmacists or physiotherapists). Only one study targeted the person with CRD with an intervention that encouraged shared-decision making with the general practitioner (GP)38. Intervention components according to ERIC taxonomy

Of the included studies, the average number of ERIC intervention components and domains used in the intervention of each study was 12 (range 4 to 25) and five (range 2 to 8), respectively (Fig. 2, Table S2). Figure 3 gives examples of interventions coded to ERIC domains within the macro-, meso- and micro groupings. The most frequently used intervention components came from the ‘train and educate stakeholders’ and ‘provide interactive assistance’ domains and included: conducting educational meetings (n = 11), ongoing training (n = 6), making training dynamic (n = 5), providing ongoing consultations (n = 5), providing local technical assistance (n = 6) and changing record systems (n = 6 [Table S2]). Patterns of how ERIC implementation components were combined within interventions were explored using proportional co-occurrence heatmaps (Figures S1 to S9). These visualisations show, for each ERIC domain (columns), the extent to which its individual components tended to be used alongside individual components from other domains (rows), independent of how frequently each component appeared overall. Stronger associations (darker shading) indicate components that were consistently implemented together when they were used. For example, components from the ‘train and educate stakeholders’ (Figure S1) and ‘provide interactive assistance’ (Figure S2) domains frequently co-occurred with ‘use evaluative and iterative’ components such as audit and feedback. Intervention effect on referral to PR

Data from three randomised controlled trials35,39,41 were pooled in a meta-analysis showing that, compared with the control group, the impact of interventions on referral rates was minimal (pooled mean difference 0%, 95% confidence interval −0.24% to 0.25%, Figure S10). Seven non-randomised studies provided pre- and post-intervention referral data29,31,33,34,37,38,40, showing an increase from an average referral rate of 15.3% pre-intervention (range: 1.4% to 45.6%, Table 3), to 21.3% post-intervention (range: 0.8% to 60.1%, Table 3). Three studies28,32,36 reported post-intervention data only, but did not provide the total number of participants eligible for referral. Among these studies, referral proportions ranged between 24% to 100% (Table 3). Of the studies that reported improvements in referral rate after the intervention was implemented (increase in referral rate ranging 4 to 25%), the greatest gains occurred in those that applied Discussion

While previous reviews15 have established that referrals to PR from primary care remain suboptimal and that existing interventions achieve minimal gains23, little attention has been given to examining how interventions are constructed and which implementation components may contribute to observed changes in referral behaviours. In addition to synthesising interventions to optimise referral to PR for people with CRD, this review is the first to apply the ERIC taxonomy to systematically document their implementation components. We identified that ERIC components were rarely used in isolation, and studies that demonstrated the greatest, albeit modest, improvements in referral rates29,33,34,38 combined a variety of ERIC components across domains including education and training for HCPs, interactive assistance, clinician support and data feedback/prompts. Interventions primarily focussed at the meso- and micro-levels, targeting referral practices of HCPs and, overall, may have resulted in small increases in PR referral rates. However, the certainty of effect size was low for RCTs, and risk of bias was high for non-RCT studies. Despite knowing what implementation strategies were applied, studies did not give information on how these interventions were delivered, leaving little understanding of their operationalisation and fidelity. There are also a number of ERIC components that were not applied, and their utility in supporting PR referral is unclear (Figures S1 to S9). As a result, it remains difficult to determine which implementation components were responsible for observed changes in referral practices, or why similar strategies produced variable effects across settings.

HCP training and education were the most frequently employed ERIC component across included studies (85%); however, their widespread use did not translate into large or consistent improvements in PR referral rates. This suggests that while HCP education may be a necessary component of interventions to support referral to PR, it is unlikely to be sufficient when used in isolation to change referral. How these educational strategies were operationalised was rarely reported in the included papers. In many studies, educational components focused on disseminating guideline-based knowledge; however, the mode of education delivery and content, as well as the time taken to deliver these strategies (e.g., dose) were not described. Recent qualitative work suggests that referral decisions are shaped by the quality and degree of engagement during a HCP–patient interaction, with successful referrals typically emerging from two-way conversations that provide nuanced information, identify patient goals, and address concerns to support shared decision-making19,42,43. Strategies in included studies did not address how HCPs communicate about PR or navigate the referral discussion with patients. While earlier work has highlighted the importance of HCP–patient communication in supporting referral to PR19,44, how to intentionally support these interactions within interventions to improve referral to PR requires exploration.

Other commonly used ERIC components in interventions were audit and feedback and facilitation of clinical data to providers; however, their application was typically limited to reporting referral rates back to providers at the conclusion of the study. While data feedback can raise awareness of referral practices and gaps, its focus on outcomes rather than the process underpinning referral practices may limit potential for change in referral behaviours. Data feedback could alternatively be operationalised by PR clinicians closing the loop to referrers by confirming whether referrals were received, actioned, and providing post-rehabilitation patient summaries. Doing so may increase the capacity of referrers to reinforce and sustain referral practices. Organisational and healthcare system level strategies such as referral prompts and record-system changes were also used. However, these strategies varied in how explicitly they supported referrals, with many focusing on reminding HCPs to refer rather than clarifying what information was required or embedding referral processes seamlessly into routine care. Collectively, these findings suggest that while ERIC components were commonly implemented, their operationalisation may not have consistently targeted the behavioural processes required to support sustained change in referral practices. Future work may benefit from explicitly linking implementation strategies (e.g., ERIC) with behaviour change frameworks45 to guide both strategy selection and their operationalisation within specific clinical contexts46.

Across included studies, interventions primarily focussed at the meso- and micro-levels, with a focus on modifying HCP referral practices through education and training, clinician support and clinical audits. While identifying macro-level levers were not the focus of this review, they, as well as patient-facing strategies, were rare. Only one study38 delivered an intervention that explicitly engaged both patients and HCPs through a patient-held, co-designed scorecard that encouraged shared decision-making during consultations. Compared with control practices, those that utilised the scorecard demonstrated an improvement in PR referrals (intervention, 1.7% to 7.8%; control, 2.0% to 4.6%), highlighting the value of directly involving patients in the referral process. Given that patients must actively engage with and commit to PR, interventions that overlook the patient perspective at the time of referral may miss an important opportunity to support PR referral and uptake. These findings support the need for future interventions to look holistically, and partner with both patients with CRD and HCPs, to co-design interventions that address their individual barriers to referral47,48. The importance of macro-level levers for improving referral rates to PR, as have been proposed for cardiac rehabilitation49, may also warrant exploration.

This review has several strengths. First, mapping interventions to the ERIC taxonomy enabled a standardised analysis of implementation strategies used to support referral to PR from primary care, providing clarity about what intervention components were delivered and facilitating comparison across studies. This structured approach highlights opportunities for more targeted, theory-informed implementation design. In addition, the inclusion of diverse study designs reflects real-world implementation contexts, enhancing the applicability and generalisability of findings across primary care settings. Some limitations should also be acknowledged. The methodological quality of included studies varied, and heterogeneity in study design and referral rate reporting limited the ability to directly compare effects across interventions. Furthermore, reporting of PR referral outcomes across included studies was inconsistent and interpretation was complicated by variability in how referral was defined, measured, and reported. Several studies26,29,34 relied on single post-intervention observational time points and did not report the number of patients eligible for referral, limiting the ability to quantify change over time and compare effects between settings. In a number of studies, referral to PR was embedded within broader packages of COPD care, making it difficult to attribute observed changes in referral rates specifically to referral-focused intervention components. While this reflects the complexity of real-world clinical practice—where PR referral is rarely delivered in isolation—it underscores the need for future interventions to more clearly specify referral as a behavioural target and to align implementation strategies and outcome measures accordingly.

Conclusion

Referral to PR remains suboptimal, and interventions to support referral from primary care have generally achieved minimal improvements. By applying the ERIC taxonomy to systematically map intervention components, this review indicates that the effectiveness of referral-focused interventions may depend not only on the selection of implementation strategies, but also the combinations they are used in. Irrespective of what combination of implementation components were used, details about how strategies were operationalised to shape referral decisions in primary care need to be better articulated. Future interventions to improve referral to PR should consider involvement of all stakeholders in the referral process. Moving beyond strategy selection alone and adopting theory-informed, context-specific approaches that deliberately link implementation strategies with locally relevant behavioural determinants may support meaningful and sustained improvements in PR referral practices from primary care.
