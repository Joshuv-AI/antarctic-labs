The Verification Tax: Fundamental Limits of AI Auditing in the Rare-Error Regime
Jason Z Wang

Abstract
The most cited calibration result in deep learning—post-temperature-scaling ECE of 0.012 on CIFAR-100 ( Guo et al. 2017 ) —is below the statistical noise floor. We prove this is not a failure of the experiment but a law: the minimax rate for estimating calibration error with model error rate ε \varepsilon is Θ ⁡ ( ( L ​ ε / m ) 1 / 3 ) \Theta((L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}) , and no estimator can beat it. This “verification tax” implies that as AI models improve, verifying their calibration becomes fundamentally harder—with the same exponent in opposite directions. We establish four results that contradict standard evaluation practice: (1) self-evaluation without labels provides exactly zero information about calibration, bounded by a constant independent of compute; (2) a sharp phase transition at m ⋅ ε ≈ 1 m\cdot\varepsilon\approx 1 below which miscalibration is undetectable; (3) active querying eliminates the Lipschitz constant, collapsing estimation to detection; (4) verification cost grows exponentially with pipeline depth at rate L K L^{K} . We validate across five benchmarks (MMLU, TruthfulQA, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande; ∼ 27,000 {\sim}27{,}000 items) with 6 LLMs from 5 families (8B–405B parameters, 27 benchmark–model pairs with logprob-based confidence), 95% bootstrap CIs, and permutation tests. Self-evaluation non-significance holds in 80% of pairs. Across frontier models, 23% of pairwise comparisons are indistinguishable from noise, implying that credible calibration claims must report verification floors and prioritize active querying once gains approach benchmark resolution.

Introduction

The base rate problem—that rare conditions are hard to detect—is among the oldest results in statistics. We show that AI auditing faces the same fundamental barrier. The “disease” is miscalibration: the gap between a model’s stated confidence and its actual accuracy. As errors become rare, miscalibration becomes a rare condition, and the verification cost grows without bound. We call this the
verification tax
. In one line: better models are harder to audit.

This result completes a programme initiated by
Sun et al. 2023
, who established the
O(n^{-2/3})
upper bound for ECE estimation (NeurIPS 2023 Spotlight), and extended by
Futami and Fujisawa 2024
, who generalized the rate information-theoretically (NeurIPS 2024). Both left two questions open: (1) Is
n^{-1/3}
the optimal rate? (2) How does the model’s error rate
\varepsilon
affect verification difficulty? We resolve both. The lower bound (Theorem
2
) matches the upper bound (Theorem
3
), establishing
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
as the minimax rate. The
\varepsilon
-dependence—absent from all prior work—reveals a phase transition, a scaling duality, and a verification horizon.

The tax is a law: no estimator can beat
\Theta((L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3})
. Under meaningful verification (
\delta=\Theta(\varepsilon)
), sample complexity grows as
\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{2})
. Capability and verifiability are governed by the same exponent in opposite directions (Theorem
4
).

But the rate is the scaffolding. The paper’s contributions are four results that contradict standard assumptions in AI evaluation
(
Liang et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chiang et al. 2024
)
:

Surprise 1: Self-evaluation is bounded by a constant
(§
3
). Any estimator that does not use ground-truth labels—including LLM-as-Judge, self-consistency, chain-of-thought verification, and all model-derived signals—has worst-case calibration error
\geq 1/2
for
L\geq 1
. The bound is independent of the number of queries. White-box model access does not help unless it identifies a low-dimensional parametric family.

Surprise 2: There is a sharp phase transition
(§
4
). Below
m\cdot\varepsilon\approx 1
, miscalibration is undetectable by
any
method. We show that most published safety benchmark improvements fall below the verification floor (Table
1
), and 65% of MMLU per-subject rankings are noise.

Surprise 3: Active querying eliminates
L
(§
5
). When the auditor chooses which inputs to test, the minimax rate improves to
\Theta(\sqrt{\varepsilon/m})
—the Lipschitz constant disappears entirely. We validate this on production LLMs: all three models collapse to the same active rate despite
\hat{L}\in[1.4,2.5]
.

Surprise 4: Composition is exponential
(§
6
). A
K
-component AI pipeline has verification cost
\Omega(L^{K}\cdot\varepsilon/\delta^{3})
. A 10-step agent loop with
L=2
costs
1{,}024\times
a single-step model. This applies to chain-of-thought, retrieval-augmented generation, and agentic systems.

Together, these results imply that the dominant practices in AI evaluation—passive benchmarking, self-evaluation, and end-to-end testing of complex pipelines—operate in provably information-less regimes. We demonstrate this empirically and show that every frontier model exceeds the verification horizon (§
7
).

Concrete scale.

The law already bites on today’s benchmarks. On MMLU (
n{=}14{,}042
,
\varepsilon{\approx}0.16
,
\hat{L}{\approx}1.4
), the calibration floor is about
0.028
, while the corresponding accuracy floor is only
0.006
: calibration is about
4.7\times
harder to verify than accuracy. On TruthfulQA (
n{=}817
), the floor rises to
0.093
–
0.139
, large enough to swamp many reported improvements. Even if frontier models reach
\varepsilon\approx 0.05
on MMLU, the passive floor remains
\approx 0.016
–
0.018
. The practical implication is simple: below-floor gains should be treated as ties unless the evaluator uses much larger holdouts or active querying.

The Verification Tax

Setup.
Let
f
be a classifier with confidence
p(x)\in[0,1]
. The calibration gap is
\Delta(p)=\eta(p)-p
where
\eta(p)=\mathbb{E}[Y{=}\hat{y}\mid p(X){=}p]
. The binned ECE is
\mathrm{ECE}_{B}=\sum_{b}(n_{b}/n)|\bar{\Delta}_{b}|
. We consider
L
-Lipschitz calibration functions
\mathcal{F}(L)=\{\Delta:|\Delta(p_{1})-\Delta(p_{2})|\leq L|p_{1}-p_{2}|\}
and the minimax risk
R^{*}(m,\varepsilon,L)=\inf_{\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}}\sup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{F}(L)}\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}-\mathrm{ECE}|]
, where
\varepsilon=\Pr[Y\neq\hat{y}]
is the error rate.

Theorem 1

(Le Cam Lower Bound)
.

R^{*}(m,\varepsilon,L)\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
, where
c_{1}\geq 1/(4\sqrt{2})
.

Proof sketch.

Construct
P_{0}
(calibrated,
\mathrm{ECE}=0
) and
P_{1}
(miscalibrated by
\delta
) at
p_{0}=1-\varepsilon
. KL divergence:
m\delta^{2}/\varepsilon
. Setting
\mathrm{KL}=1
via Bretagnolle–Huber gives
R^{*}\geq(1/2e)\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
. Full proof: Appendix
H
.
∎

Theorem 2

(Minimax Lower Bound)
.

Under score-mass concentration near
1{-}\varepsilon
,
R^{*}(m,\varepsilon,L)\geq c_{2}(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}\cdot(\log m)^{-c_{3}}
.

Proof sketch.

The full proof (Appendix
B
) constructs two product priors on
L
-Lipschitz calibration functions directly in the Bernoulli observation model, following the two-prior method of
Lepski et al. 1999
. The construction uses a signed measure that separates
L_{1}
norms while controlling the Bernoulli KL divergence, yielding the rate
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
up to logarithmic factors without invoking asymptotic equivalence to white noise. An alternative derivation via Brown–Low equivalence
(
Brown and Low 1996
)
is in Appendix
K
.
∎

Theorem 3

(Matched Upper Bound)
.

Histogram binning with
B^{*}=\lfloor(L^{2}m/\varepsilon)^{1/3}\rfloor
achieves
\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}_{B^{*}}-\mathrm{ECE}|]\leq C(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
.

Proof sketch.

Bias
\leq L/B
, variance
\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon B/m}
. Optimize at
B^{*}=(L^{2}m/\varepsilon)^{1/3}
.
∎

The polynomial rate
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
is established by matching upper and lower bounds. The lower bound carries a logarithmic correction
(\log m)^{-c^{\prime}}
inherited from the structure of
L_{1}
functional estimation: the non-smoothness of
|t|
at zero requires a Fourier-analytic construction whose truncation introduces the logarithmic loss. This is the same gap identified by
Lepski et al. 1999
twenty-seven years ago, and it remains open whether the gap belongs in the lower bound or the upper bound. We conjecture that the minimax rate is exactly
\Theta((L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3})
with no logarithmic correction. Resolving this would settle a longstanding question in nonparametric functional estimation, independent of the AI auditing application.

Theorem 4

(Scaling Duality)
.

If
\varepsilon(N)=c_{0}N^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, then under meaningful verification (
\delta=\Theta(\varepsilon)
), the sample complexity satisfies
m(N)=\Omega(N^{\alpha})
. Capability and verifiability scale with the same exponent in opposite directions.

Corollary 5

(Phase Transition)
.

Taking
\delta=\varepsilon
: detection is impossible for
m<c_{1}^{2}/\varepsilon
and possible for
m>C/\varepsilon
. The transition occurs at
m^{*}\asymp 1/\varepsilon
.

Corollary 6

(Benchmark Resolution Limit)
.

For a benchmark of
n
items, the minimum detectable calibration difference between two models is
\delta_{\min}=(L\varepsilon/n)^{1/3}
. Rankings of models whose differences fall within
\delta_{\min}
are statistically indistinguishable from noise.

Surprise 1: Self-Evaluation Provides Zero Information

Theorem 7

(Self-Verification Impossibility)
.

Let
z(x)=g(x,\theta)
be any auxiliary signal derived from the model. Any estimator
V(x_{1},p_{1},z_{1},\ldots,x_{m},p_{m},z_{m})
that does not use true labels satisfies:

\sup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{F}(L)}\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta)|]\geq\min(L,1)/2
.

Proof.

\Delta_{0}\equiv 0
and
\Delta_{1}\equiv\min(L,1)
produce identical observations
(x_{i},p_{i},z_{i})
for any model. Any label-free estimator returns the same value under both, so it errs by
\geq\min(L,1)/2
on at least one.
∎

Corollary 8

(LLM-as-Judge Impossibility)
.

Let
J
be any evaluator model. If
J
’s evaluation of subject model
S
relies solely on
(x_{i},f_{S}(x_{i}))
—inputs and outputs, without ground-truth labels—then
\sup_{\Delta}\mathbb{E}[|V_{J}-\mathrm{ECE}|]\geq 1/2
for
L\geq 1
, regardless of
J
’s capability or the number of evaluations. This applies directly to the LLM-as-Judge paradigm
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
: using a language model to evaluate another model’s calibration without ground-truth labels is information-theoretically bounded by a constant.

Corollary 9

(Hallucination Detection Wall)
.

Factual accuracy is a special case of calibration. Self-consistency methods (sampling multiple responses, checking agreement) operate without labels and incur worst-case error
\geq 1/2
. Self-reported hallucination rates have zero information-theoretic content.

Corollary 10

(Instance-Dependent Self-Verification Impossibility)
.

For any label-free estimator
V
and any
\delta\in(0,\min(L,1)]
:

\sup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{F}(L),\,\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta)=\delta}\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta)|]\geq\delta/2
.
That is, for
every
ECE value
\delta
, there exists a calibration function with that ECE that any label-free estimator will mis-estimate by at least
\delta/2
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta
. Let
\Delta_{0}\equiv 0
(
\mathrm{ECE}=0
) and
\Delta_{\delta}
with
\mathrm{ECE}=\delta
. Both produce identical label-free observations. For any
V
:
\mathbb{E}[|V-0|]+\mathbb{E}[|V-\delta|]\geq\delta
. Thus
\max\{\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{0})|],\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{\delta})|]\}\geq\delta/2
.
∎

Theorem 11

(White-Box Irrelevance)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\sigma(\theta,A,D_{\mathrm{train}})
. Any
\mathcal{S}
-measurable estimator using
m
fresh samples satisfies
R^{*}_{\mathcal{S}}\geq c\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
. White-box access helps only by constraining
\Delta
to a parametric family (Corollary:
d
-dimensional family
\Rightarrow

O(1/\sqrt{m})
rate).

Empirical validation.
Figure
1
confirms the theory on MMLU (14,042 items, 3 models). Left: confidence predicts accuracy (Spearman
\rho=0.55
–
0.95
, all
p<0.05
). Right: confidence does
not
predict calibration gap magnitude (
\rho=0.39
–
0.53
, 2/3 non-significant). Self-confidence tells you
whether
you are right, not
how wrong your confidence is
.

Surprise 3: Active Querying Eliminates
L

Theorem 12

(Active Verification Rate)
.

With adaptive confidence-level selection,
R^{*}_{\mathrm{active}}(m,\varepsilon,L)=\Theta(\sqrt{\varepsilon/m})
. The Lipschitz constant disappears entirely.

Proof sketch.

Lower:
Chain rule of KL; per-observation information is
\delta^{2}/\varepsilon
regardless of adaptivity.
Upper:
Two-phase explore-exploit. Phase 1: resolve sign of
\Delta
on
N
grid points. Phase 2: estimate
|\Delta|
in resolved bins. Set
N=L\sqrt{m/\varepsilon}
; both terms become
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon/m})
. Full proof: Appendix
C
.
∎

The gap between passive (
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
) and active (
\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
) is
(L^{2}m/\varepsilon)^{1/6}
. For
L=2.5
,
m=10{,}000
,
\varepsilon=0.16
: active is
\sim
5
\times
more efficient.

Surprise 4: Composition Is Exponential

Theorem 13

(Compositional Verification Tax)
.

For a
K
-component pipeline with per-component Lipschitz
L_{1},\ldots,L_{K}
:
L_{\mathrm{sys}}\leq\prod_{k}L_{k}+1
, and
m_{\mathrm{sys}}=\Omega(L_{\mathrm{sys}}\cdot\varepsilon/\delta^{3})
. For homogeneous
L_{k}=L>1
:
m_{\mathrm{sys}}=\Omega(L^{K}\cdot\varepsilon/\delta^{3})
—exponential in pipeline depth.

Maximum verifiable depth:
K_{\mathrm{max}}=\lfloor\log_{L}(M_{\mathrm{total}}\delta^{3}/\varepsilon)\rfloor
. An agent with 10 reasoning steps at
L{=}2
:
1{,}024\times
cost. At
L{=}2
,
K{=}20
: over
10^{6}\times
. The Lipschitz composition model applies to continuous reasoning chains (chain-of-thought, iterative refinement). Discontinuous components such as retrieval or tool use have
L_{k}=\infty
, making end-to-end verification infeasible at any sample size for such pipelines.

Theorem 14

(General Verification Tax)
.

For any verification functional
V(\Delta,\mu_{G})=\int|\Delta(p)|\,d\mu_{G}(p)
with
L
-Lipschitz integrand, effective noise
\sigma^{2}
, and effective sample
m_{\mathrm{eff}}
: passive rate
\Theta((L\sigma^{2}/m_{\mathrm{eff}})^{1/3})
; active rate
\Theta(\sqrt{\sigma^{2}/m_{\mathrm{eff}}})
. Instantiations: calibration (
G
= population), fairness (
m_{\mathrm{eff}}=m\pi
), robustness (
\sigma^{2}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pert}}
).

Corollary 15

(Fairness Tax)
.

For a group with proportion
\pi
, verification requires
m\geq(1/\pi)\cdot L\varepsilon/\delta^{3}
. A 5% minority group costs
20\times
the full population.

7
The Verification Horizon

Theorem 16

(Verification Horizon)
.

Let
\varepsilon(N)=c_{0}N^{-\alpha}
and
M_{\mathrm{total}}
be total labeled data. The verification floor is
\delta_{\mathrm{floor}}(N)=(L\varepsilon(N)/M_{\mathrm{total}})^{1/3}
. Meaningful verification requires
N<N^{*}=(c_{0}^{2}M_{\mathrm{total}}/L)^{1/(2\alpha)}
.

Under Chinchilla scaling (
\alpha\approx 0.5
,
c_{0}\approx 1
,
L=1
,
M_{\mathrm{total}}=14{,}000
):
N^{*}\approx 10^{7}
—well below current frontier models (
>10^{11}
). Across the domains we audit, every frontier system exceeds the passive horizon by orders of magnitude; representative calculations are deferred to Appendix
G
. Active verification shifts the horizon to
N^{*}_{\mathrm{active}}=(c_{0}M_{\mathrm{total}})^{1/\alpha}
, but no current evaluation framework supports active querying at scale.

Regulatory infeasibility.

The fairness tax (Corollary
15
) compounds with the base verification tax across demographic subgroups. If evaluation must certify calibration to precision
\delta
across
K
groups with minimum proportion
\pi_{\min}
, then
m\geq KL\varepsilon/(\pi_{\min}\delta^{3})
. Under illustrative EU AI Act Annex III settings (
K{=}10
,
\pi_{\min}{=}0.05
,
\delta{=}0.02
,
\varepsilon{=}0.05
), this requires
1.25
M labels against
{\sim}700
K available medical-imaging labels. Appendix
G
reports the broader cross-framework comparison and assumptions.

Verification half-life.

Under ECE drift rate
\lambda
, every verification expires after
t_{1/2}=\delta/\lambda
. Representative domains range from months (medical AI) to hours (financial trading), so even successful audits must be periodically renewed.

All quantities carry 95% bootstrap CIs (1,000 replicates; Appendix
S

\varepsilon

m\cdot\varepsilon\approx 1
confirmed on all benchmarks. (3) Active querying achieves

2.7\times
gap at frontier error rates (
\varepsilon{=}0.05
).
(b)
Required samples for meaningful verification (
\delta{=}\varepsilon/2
). Both curves grow as
\varepsilon\to 0
; the verification horizon (dashed) marks where passive verification exceeds typical benchmark sizes.

Extended results across all five benchmarks (14 benchmark–model pairs,

Calibration estimation.

Sun et al. 2023
and
Futami and Fujisawa 2024
established
O(n^{-1/3})

\varepsilon

\varepsilon

10
Conclusion

The verification tax is a law:
\Theta((L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3})
for passive verification, with no escape via model access, self-evaluation, or strategic miscalibration. Better models are harder to audit; passive benchmarking fails exactly when frontier gaps become small; and active querying is the only route that asymptotically removes the Lipschitz penalty. Practically, papers should report verification floors beside calibration claims, size holdouts via
m\geq L\varepsilon/\delta^{3}
, and treat leaderboard gaps below the floor as ties rather than progress.

Prediction.
Under current scaling trends (
\alpha\approx 0.5
), next-generation frontier models will achieve
\varepsilon\approx 0.05
–
0.08
on MMLU. At this error rate, the verification floor on MMLU (
n{=}14{,}042
) is
\delta_{\mathrm{floor}}\approx 0.016
–
0.018
, so we predict that most claimed calibration gains in next-generation technical reports will remain below the detectable floor.

Anonymous software.
An anonymized implementation of the verification-floor, holdout-sizing, and verifiability-assessment tools is included in the supplementary material and can be publicly released upon publication.

Limitations and open problems.
Our analysis assumes Lipschitz calibration, leaves a logarithmic gap between Theorems
2
and
3
, uses worst-case Lipschitz accumulation in composition, and focuses on ECE rather than alternative calibration functionals. Closing the log gap and handling discontinuous or multiclass settings remain open.

7
(Self-Verification Impossibility).

Fix any model with weights
\theta
. The outputs
(p(x_{i}),z(x_{i}))
are deterministic functions of
x_{i}
and
\theta
; they do not depend on
\Delta
. Construct:

\Delta_{0}(p)=0
for all
p
(
\mathrm{ECE}=0
);
\Delta_{1}(p)=\min(L,1)
near
1-\varepsilon
(
\mathrm{ECE}=\min(L,1)

\mathcal{F}(L)
. Both produce identical observations. Any label-free estimator
V
returns the same value under both:

\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{0})|]+\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{1})|]\geq|\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{0})-\mathrm{ECE}(\Delta_{1})|=\min(L,1)
.
Taking
\sup
:
\sup_{\Delta}\mathbb{E}[|V-\mathrm{ECE}|]\geq\min(L,1)/2
.
\square

Corollary

J

(x_{i},f_{S}(x_{i}))
—the same label-free observation space. The two worlds (
\Delta_{0}
,
\Delta_{1}
) produce identical inputs and outputs for
S
, hence identical observations for
J
. The bound follows directly.

Corollary

Factual accuracy:
\Pr[\text{fact correct}\mid\text{confidence }p]=\eta(p)

\Delta(p)=\eta(p)-p

k

(x,\theta)

Appendix B
Self-Contained Lower Bound via Bernoulli Two-Prior Method

We prove Theorem
2
directly in the Bernoulli observation model, without invoking asymptotic equivalence to Gaussian white noise.

Setup.
We observe
m
i.i.d. pairs
(p_{i},Y_{i})
where
p_{i}\sim\mu
and
Y_{i}\mid p_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p_{i}+\Delta(p_{i}))
, with
\Delta\in\mathcal{F}(L)
.

Step 1: Parametric submodel.
Assume
\mu
has density
\geq c_{\mu}>0
on a window
W=[1{-}\varepsilon{-}w/2,\,1{-}\varepsilon{+}w/2]
. Partition
W
into
N
subintervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{N}
of width
h=w/N
. Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a smooth bump supported on
[0,1]
with
\int g=1
,
\|g\|_{2}^{2}=\kappa_{g}
, and Lipschitz constant 1. For
\theta\in[-1,1]^{N}
, define:

\Delta_{\theta}(p)=Lh\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}\,g((p-t_{j}+h/2)/h)
,
where
t_{j}
are the interval midpoints. Each bump has height
Lh
, width
h
, and Lipschitz constant
L
. Disjoint supports ensure
\Delta_{\theta}\in\mathcal{F}(L)
.

Step 2: Sufficient statistics.
In interval
I_{j}
, there are
n_{j}\approx mc_{\mu}h
observations. The per-bin calibration gap estimate
Z_{j}=n_{j}^{-1}\sum_{i:p_{i}\in I_{j}}(Y_{i}-p_{i})
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Z_{j}]\approx Lh\theta_{j}\bar{g}_{j}
and
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{j})\approx\varepsilon/(mc_{\mu}h)
. Setting
\alpha=Lh\bar{g}_{j}
and
\sigma^{2}=\varepsilon/(mc_{\mu}h)
, the problem reduces to the sequence model
Z_{j}\approx\alpha\theta_{j}+\sigma\xi_{j}
with approximately independent
\xi_{j}
.

Step 3: ECE as
L_{1}
functional.
The ECE restricted to
W
is
\mathrm{ECE}_{W}\approx c_{\mu}w\cdot\alpha\cdot F_{1}(\theta)
where
F_{1}(\theta)=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\theta_{j}|
. Lower-bounding
F_{1}
estimation suffices.

Step 4: Two-prior construction
(following Lepski et al., 1999, §4.3). Construct product priors
\pi_{0}=\nu_{0}^{\otimes N}
,
\pi_{1}=\nu_{1}^{\otimes N}
on
[-1,1]^{N}
where
\nu_{1}-\nu_{0}=\eta\mu
for a signed measure
\mu
with
\int\mu=0
and
\int|t|\mu(dt)=2\delta
(the separation in
L_{1}
norm). Both
\nu_{j}
are perturbations of
\mathrm{Uniform}([-1,1])
with density
1/2
. The per-coordinate KL is
\mathrm{KL}(p_{\nu_{1}}\|p_{\nu_{0}})\leq 4\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}/\sigma^{2}
.

Step 5: Bayesian Le Cam bound.
Total KL:
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{total}}=4N\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}/\sigma^{2}
. Setting
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{total}}=1
:
\eta=\sigma/(2\alpha\sqrt{N})
. The Bayesian Le Cam bound gives:

R^{*}\geq c\cdot\delta\eta-N^{-1/2}=c\delta\sigma/(2\alpha\sqrt{N})-N^{-1/2}
.

Step 6: Optimization over
N
.
Substituting
\sigma/\alpha=\sqrt{\varepsilon N}/(Lw^{3/2}\bar{g}\sqrt{mc_{\mu}})
and optimizing over
N
yields
N^{*}=\Theta((m/\varepsilon)^{1/3})
and:

R^{*}_{\mathrm{ECE}}\geq c^{\prime}\left(\frac{L\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{1/3}/(\log m)^{c^{\prime\prime}}

The logarithmic factor arises from the Fourier approximation of
|t|
in constructing
\mu
, not from the observation model. This is the same factor as in Lepski et al.
[
Lepski et al. 1999
]
.
\square

Appendix C
Full Proof of Theorem
12
(Active Verification)

Lower bound.
The Le Cam lower bound from Theorem
1
applies unchanged to adaptive strategies. Construct the same two hypotheses:
P_{0}
(calibrated,
\mathrm{ECE}=0
) and
P_{1}
(miscalibrated by
\delta
near
p=1-\varepsilon
,
\mathrm{ECE}=\delta
). Under adaptive querying, the auditor selects
p_{i}
based on all previous observations
(p_{1},y_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1},y_{i-1})
. By the chain rule of KL divergence:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}^{m}\|P_{1}^{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}(\cdot\mid p_{i})\|P_{1}(\cdot\mid p_{i}))\right]

(1)

Each term is at most
\delta^{2}/\varepsilon
(the per-observation KL when
p_{i}
is in the miscalibrated region, zero otherwise). Adaptivity does not increase the per-observation information: conditioned on
p_{i}
, the label
y_{i}
is a single Bernoulli draw whose KL is
\delta^{2}/\varepsilon
regardless of previous observations. Therefore
\mathrm{KL}\leq m\delta^{2}/\varepsilon
, and Le Cam gives
R^{*}_{\mathrm{active}}\geq c\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
.

Upper bound.
We construct a two-phase adaptive strategy.

Phase 1 (Exploration):
Select
N
equally-spaced confidence levels
\{p_{j}=j/N\}_{j=1}^{N}
in the interval
[1-\varepsilon-w,1-\varepsilon+w]
for a window width
w=O(1)
. Query each level
m/(2N)
times. The empirical estimate
\hat{\Delta}(p_{j})
has standard error
\sigma_{j}=\sqrt{2\varepsilon N/m}
. Classify each bin as:

•

Resolved
:
|\hat{\Delta}(p_{j})|>2\sigma_{j}
— the sign of
\Delta(p_{j})
is known with high probability.

•

Unresolved
:
|\hat{\Delta}(p_{j})|\leq 2\sigma_{j}
— the sign is uncertain.

The unresolved zone has width at most
4\sigma_{j}/L=O(\sqrt{\varepsilon N/m}/L)
by the Lipschitz constraint (the function can only stay near zero for a region of width
\leq|\Delta|_{\max}/L
).

Phase 2 (Exploitation):
Use the remaining
m/2
queries on resolved bins to estimate
|\Delta(p_{j})|=\Delta(p_{j})\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\Delta(p_{j}))
(sign is known, so no absolute value problem). This is a simple average with error
\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
.

Total error:

•

Resolved bins: estimation error
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon/m})
.

•

Unresolved bins: the contribution to ECE is at most
(\text{max }|\Delta|\text{ in zone})\times(\text{width of zone})
. Since
|\Delta|\leq 2\sigma_{j}
in the zone and the width is
O(\sigma_{j}/L)
, this contributes
O(\sigma_{j}^{2}/L)=O(\varepsilon N/(mL))
.

Choose
N=L\sqrt{m/\varepsilon}
to balance the two terms. Both become
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon/m})
.
\square

, verification to accuracy

Appendix H
Full Proof of Theorem
1
(Le Cam Lower Bound)

Construct two hypotheses. Fix a classifier whose score distribution places all mass at
p_{0}=1-\varepsilon
.

•

P_{0}
:
Y\mid p(X)=p_{0}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1-\varepsilon)
. Calibrated:
\eta(p_{0})=p_{0}
, so
\mathrm{ECE}=0
.

•

P_{1}
:
Y\mid p(X)=p_{0}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1-\varepsilon-\delta)
. Miscalibrated:
\mathrm{ECE}=\delta
.

Both hypotheses use a constant calibration gap (Lipschitz constant 0), so
\Delta\in\mathcal{F}(L)
for all
L>0
. The KL divergence of
m
i.i.d. samples is:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}^{m}\|P_{1}^{m})=m\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Bern}(1-\varepsilon)\|\mathrm{Bern}(1-\varepsilon-\delta))=\frac{m\delta^{2}}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{m\delta^{2}}{\varepsilon}

where the last inequality uses
1-\varepsilon\leq 1
and the KL approximation
\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\|\mathrm{Bern}(p+\delta))\approx\delta^{2}/(p(1-p))
(valid for
\delta\leq\varepsilon/2
). By Le Cam’s lemma
[
Tsybakov 2009
]
:

R^{*}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\left(1-\mathrm{TV}(P_{0}^{m},P_{1}^{m})\right)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}e^{-\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}^{m}\|P_{1}^{m})}

where the second inequality uses Bretagnolle–Huber. Setting
\mathrm{KL}=1
(i.e.,
\delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
):
R^{*}\geq(1/2e)\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}\approx 0.184\sqrt{\varepsilon/m}
.
\square

H.1
Direct Bernoulli Lower Bound (Alternative to Brown–Low)

For readers who prefer a self-contained argument avoiding asymptotic equivalence, we sketch a direct Assouad-type lower bound in the Bernoulli model.

Partition
[0,1]
into
B
equal-width bins. Consider the class of calibration functions
\Delta_{\omega}(p)=h\cdot\omega_{b}
for
p\in I_{b}
, where
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{B})\in\{-1,+1\}^{B}
and
h>0
is chosen so that
\Delta_{\omega}
is
L
-Lipschitz (requiring
h\leq L/(2B)
). Each
\Delta_{\omega}
has
\mathrm{ECE}=h
(since
\sum_{b}(1/B)|h\cdot\omega_{b}|=h
). For any two configurations
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
differing in one coordinate
b
, the KL divergence of the
m/B
observations in bin
b
is:

\mathrm{KL}=\frac{m}{B}\cdot\frac{(2h)^{2}}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{4mh^{2}}{B\varepsilon}

By Assouad’s lemma
[
Tsybakov 2009
]
, the minimax risk satisfies:

R^{*}\geq\frac{Bh}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2mh^{2}}{B\varepsilon}}\right)

Setting
h=c\sqrt{B\varepsilon/m}
for a small constant
c
and
B=\lfloor(Lm/\varepsilon)^{1/3}\rfloor
gives:

R^{*}\geq c^{\prime}\left(\frac{L\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{1/3}

This recovers the
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
rate directly from the Bernoulli model, confirming the Brown–Low reduction. The constant
c^{\prime}
is weaker than the one obtained via
Lepski et al. 1999
, but the argument is fully self-contained.
\square

Appendix I
Full Proof of Theorem
3
(Matched Upper Bound)

Bias-variance decomposition.
With
B
equal-width bins and
m
total samples:

1. Bias.
Within each bin of width
1/B
, the calibration gap
\Delta
varies by at most
L/B
(Lipschitz). By the Bridge Lemma,
|\mathrm{ECE}_{B}-\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{smooth}}|\leq L/B
.

2. Variance.
Each bin
b
has
n_{b}\approx m/B
samples. (Under bounded density
c_{\mu}\leq d\mu/dp\leq C_{\mu}
; equal-mass binning achieves
n_{b}=m/B
exactly when
\mu
concentrates.) The empirical calibration gap has variance
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\Delta}_{b})\leq\sigma^{2}/n_{b}\approx\sigma^{2}B/m
, where
\sigma^{2}=\max_{b}\mathbb{E}[\eta(p)(1-\eta(p))\mid p\in I_{b}]
. Estimation error:
\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}_{B}-\mathrm{ECE}_{B}|]\leq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}B/m}
.

3. Total error.

L/B+\sqrt{\sigma^{2}B/m}
.

4. Optimization.
Setting
L/B=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}B/m}
:
B^{*}=(L^{2}m/\sigma^{2})^{1/3}
, total error
(L\sigma^{2}/m)^{1/3}
. In the rare-error regime
\sigma^{2}\approx\varepsilon
, recovering the headline rate.
\square

Appendix J
Verification Dynamics, Recalibration Trap, and Half-Life

J.1
Verification Dynamics

Suppose a model is deployed at rate
r
samples per unit time and
\varepsilon(t)=c_{0}t^{-\beta}
. The verification floor at time
t
is
\delta_{\mathrm{floor}}(t)=(Lc_{0}/r)^{1/3}t^{-(\beta+1)/3}
. Meaningful verification (
\delta_{\mathrm{floor}}(t)<\varepsilon(t)
) reduces to
(L/(rc_{0}^{2}))^{1/3}<t^{(1-2\beta)/3}
. This is satisfied for large
t
when
\beta<1/2
, violated when
\beta>1/2
, and equivalent to
L/(rc_{0}^{2})<1
when
\beta=1/2
. Critical exponent:
\beta^{*}=1/2
(passive),
\beta^{*}_{\mathrm{active}}=1
(active).
\square

J.2
Recalibration Trap

With
\mathrm{ECE}_{K}=\gamma^{K}\mathrm{ECE}_{0}
, the improvement from round
K
to
K+1
is
\gamma^{K}\mathrm{ECE}_{0}(1-\gamma)
. Distinguishing requires (Theorem
1
)
m_{K}\geq\varepsilon/(\gamma^{2K}\mathrm{ECE}_{0}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2})
. Setting
m_{K}=M_{\mathrm{total}}
gives
K^{*}=\lfloor\log(M_{\mathrm{total}}(1-\gamma)^{2}\mathrm{ECE}_{0}^{2}/\varepsilon)/(2\log(1/\gamma))\rfloor
.

Worked example (MMLU).

\gamma=0.5
,
\varepsilon=0.05
,
\mathrm{ECE}_{0}=0.10
,
M_{\mathrm{total}}=14{,}000
:
K^{*}=\lfloor 6.55/1.386\rfloor=4
. After 4 halving rounds, MMLU cannot verify further improvement.

J.3
Verification Half-Life

Let ECE drift rate
\lambda
satisfy
|\mathrm{ECE}(t)-\mathrm{ECE}(0)|\leq\lambda t
. (i) The verification becomes invalid after
t_{1/2}=\delta/\lambda
. (ii) Perpetual verification requires data rate
r\geq L\varepsilon\lambda/\delta^{4}
.

Domain estimates
(assuming
L=1
,
\varepsilon=0.05
): Medical AI (
\lambda=0.01
/month,
\delta=0.02
):
t_{1/2}=2
months,
r\approx 3{,}100
/month. Content moderation: 6 months, 620/month. Financial trading (
\lambda=0.1
/week,
\delta=0.01
):
t_{1/2}=17
hours. Autonomous driving: 15 days. Static benchmarks have the same half-life: a benchmark constructed at
t=0
decays at rate
\lambda
, formalizing the case for periodic refresh.

Appendix K
Alternative Derivation of Theorem
2
via Asymptotic Equivalence

Step 1: Reduction to nonparametric regression.

Within a window of width
w
around
p=1-\varepsilon
, where the score distribution concentrates, the calibration verification problem reduces to estimating the
L_{1}
norm of a regression function. Specifically:

For
p\in[1-\varepsilon-w/2,\,1-\varepsilon+w/2]
, define
\Delta(p)=\eta(p)-p
. The labels
Y_{i}
given
p(X_{i})=p
are
\mathrm{Bern}(\eta(p))=\mathrm{Bern}(p+\Delta(p))
, with variance
\eta(p)(1-\eta(p))\approx\varepsilon
in the rare-error regime.

The ECE restricted to this window is:

\mathrm{ECE}_{\text{window}}=\int_{1-\varepsilon-w/2}^{1-\varepsilon+w/2}|\Delta(p)|\,d\mu(p)

(2)

This is precisely the
L_{1}
norm of
\Delta
on the window, weighted by
\mu
.

Step 2: Identification with
L_{1}
functional estimation in white noise.

By the asymptotic equivalence of nonparametric regression and Gaussian white noise
[
Brown and Low 1996
,
Nussbaum 1996
]
, estimating
\int|\Delta(p)|dp
from
m
observations with per-observation variance
\varepsilon
is asymptotically equivalent to estimating
\int|f(t)|dt
in the white noise model:

dX(t)=f(t)dt+(m/\varepsilon)^{-1/2}dW(t),\quad t\in[0,1]

(3)

with effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/\varepsilon
and
f\in\Sigma(\beta=1,L)
(Lipschitz = Hölder with
\beta=1
).

Step 3: Application of Lepski, Nemirovski & Spokoiny (1999).

By Theorem 2.2 of
Lepski et al. 1999
, the minimax rate for estimating the
L_{1}
norm of a
\beta
-Hölder function in white noise with sample size
n
is:

R^{*}_{L_{1}}\geq c\cdot n^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}/(\log n)^{c^{\prime}}

(4)

For
\beta=1
(Lipschitz) and
n=n_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/\varepsilon
:

R^{*}_{L_{1}}\geq c\cdot(m/\varepsilon)^{-1/3}/(\log(m/\varepsilon))^{c^{\prime}}=c\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{1/3}/(\log m)^{c^{\prime}}

(5)

Incorporating the Lipschitz constant
L
(which scales the function class):

R^{*}(m,\varepsilon,L)\geq c_{2}\left(\frac{L\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{1/3}/(\log m)^{c_{3}}

(6)

Technical conditions:

•

Score-mass concentration:
The score distribution
\mu
must place mass
\geq\rho
in a window of width
w
around
1-\varepsilon
. We state this as an assumption. This holds for well-trained classifiers: overconfident models concentrate predictions near the top.

•

The Brown–Low equivalence
requires
m
sufficiently large relative to the smoothness. The minimum
m
is
m\geq C(L)/\varepsilon
for a constant depending on
L
.

•

Lipschitz constant:
Since
\Delta(p)=\eta(p)-p
and
p\mapsto p
is 1-Lipschitz, we have
L_{\Delta}\leq L_{\eta}+1
. We use
L
throughout for
L_{\Delta}
.

Clarification on the asymptotic regime.

The Brown–Low equivalence is applied in the limit
m\to\infty
with
\varepsilon

fixed
. The error rate
\varepsilon
is a property of the model under audit, not an asymptotic parameter. For any fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, the Bernoulli label variance
\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\geq\varepsilon/2>0
is bounded away from zero, satisfying the regularity conditions. The
\varepsilon
-dependence in the rate
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
arises from substituting
\sigma^{2}=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon
into the white-noise minimax rate with
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/\varepsilon
, not from taking
\varepsilon\to 0
within the equivalence framework.

Appendix L
Supplementary Details for Theorem
11

The proof of Theorem
11
in the main text is complete. We note that the result extends straightforwardly to any side information
\mathcal{S}
that is independent of the fresh evaluation labels conditional on the scores. This includes: model weights, architecture specifications, training data, training logs, gradient histories, and any derived quantity (e.g., Fisher information matrices, Hessian spectra, pruning masks). The key insight is that calibration is a property of the model-world interface, not of the model alone.

Appendix M
Full Proof of Sequential Verification

Lower bound.
Under the two Le Cam hypotheses (
P_{0}
:
\mathrm{ECE}=0
,
P_{1}
:
\mathrm{ECE}=\delta
), the log-likelihood ratio after
t
observations is:

\Lambda_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{p_{1}(y_{i}\mid p_{i})}{p_{0}(y_{i}\mid p_{i})}

(7)

This is a random walk with per-step drift:

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\Lambda_{1}]=-\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}(\cdot\mid p_{i})\|P_{1}(\cdot\mid p_{i}))\approx-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\varepsilon}

(8)

By Wald’s identity for sequential testing
[
Tsybakov 2009
]
, any test between
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
with error probabilities
\leq\alpha
satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[\tau]\geq\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{1})}=\frac{\varepsilon\log(1/\alpha)}{\delta^{2}}

(9)

Since any sequential verification protocol with accuracy
\delta
at confidence
1-\alpha
must distinguish
\mathrm{ECE}=0
from
\mathrm{ECE}=\delta
with probability
\geq 1-\alpha
, the lower bound follows.

Upper bound.
We construct a confidence sequence for the ECE using the method of mixtures
[
Howard et al. 2021
]
. Define the running ECE estimate:

\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}_{t}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{n_{b,t}}{t}\left|\hat{\Delta}_{b,t}\right|

(10)

where
\hat{\Delta}_{b,t}
is the empirical calibration gap in bin
b
after
t
observations.

By Theorem 1 of
Howard et al. 2021
, there exists a time-uniform confidence sequence
(C_{t})_{t\geq 1}
such that:

\Pr\left(\exists t\geq 1:|\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}_{t}-\mathrm{ECE}|>C_{t}\right)\leq\alpha

(11)

with
C_{t}=O(\sqrt{\varepsilon\log(\log(t)/\alpha)/t})
.

Define the stopping time
\tau=\inf\{t:C_{t}\leq\delta\}
. Solving
C_{t}\leq\delta
:

\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\log(\log(t)/\alpha)}{t}}\leq\delta\implies t\geq\frac{\varepsilon\log(\log(t)/\alpha)}{\delta^{2}}

(12)

For the dominant term,
\mathbb{E}[\tau]=O(\varepsilon\log(1/\alpha)/\delta^{2})
, matching the lower bound up to constants.
\square

Appendix N
Temperature Scaling Analysis

We provide the full proof of the temperature scaling rate proposition.

Step 1: MLE for the temperature parameter.

Given scores
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
and labels
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
, the negative log-likelihood is:

\ell(T)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[y_{i}\log\sigma(\mathrm{logit}(p_{i})/T)+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\sigma(\mathrm{logit}(p_{i})/T))\right]

(13)

This is a 1D convex optimization. The MLE
\hat{T}
is unique.

Step 2: Asymptotic normality of MLE.

By standard M-estimation theory
[
van der Vaart 1998
]
:

\sqrt{m}(\hat{T}-T^{*})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\mathcal{I}(T^{*})^{-1})

(14)

where
\mathcal{I}(T^{*})
is the Fisher information. At
T^{*}=1
, the score function is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\log p(Y\mid p,T)\bigg|_{T=1}=-(Y-p)\cdot\mathrm{logit}(p)

(15)

So:

\mathcal{I}(1)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-p)^{2}\cdot\mathrm{logit}(p)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[p(1-p)\cdot\mathrm{logit}(p)^{2}]

(16)

In the rare-error regime with scores concentrated near
1-\varepsilon
:
\mathrm{logit}(1-\varepsilon)\approx\log(1/\varepsilon)
and
p(1-p)\approx\varepsilon
, giving:

\mathcal{I}(1)\approx\varepsilon\cdot\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)

(17)

This is positive and grows as
\varepsilon\to 0
— rare errors make temperature estimation
easier
because logits are more spread out. The MLE satisfies:
|\hat{T}-T^{*}|=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{m\cdot\mathcal{I}(T^{*})})
.

Step 3: Translating to calibration error.

By Taylor expansion of
\Delta(p;T)
around
T^{*}=1
:

\Delta(p;\hat{T})\approx\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial T}\bigg|_{T=1}\cdot(\hat{T}-1)=p(1-p)\cdot\mathrm{logit}(p)\cdot(\hat{T}-1)

(18)

So the smooth CE is:

\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{smooth}}=\int|\Delta(p;\hat{T})|\,d\mu(p)\approx|\hat{T}-1|\cdot\underbrace{\int p(1-p)|\mathrm{logit}(p)|\,d\mu(p)}_{\kappa}

(19)

where
\kappa
is a constant depending on the score distribution. Substituting
|\hat{T}-1|=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{m\cdot\mathcal{I}(T^{*})})
:

\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{smooth}}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{m\cdot\mathcal{I}(T^{*})}}\right)=O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)

(20)

with a constant that depends on
\varepsilon
through
\mathcal{I}(T^{*})
and
\kappa
. This is the
parametric rate

m^{-1/2}
, not the nonparametric rate
(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}
.

Step 4: Connection to binned ECE.

Via the Bridge Lemma:
\mathrm{ECE}_{B}\leq\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{smooth}}+L/B
. Taking
B=O(\sqrt{m}/L)
:

\mathrm{ECE}_{B}=O(1/\sqrt{m})

(21)

Comparison to nonparametric rate:
The ratio is
(1/\sqrt{m})/(L\varepsilon/m)^{1/3}=(L\varepsilon)^{-1/3}/m^{1/6}
, which shrinks with
m
. For
m=10{,}000
and
L\varepsilon=0.05
: ratio
\approx 0.05
. Temperature scaling is
\sim
20
\times
more data-efficient than nonparametric ECE estimation.
\square

Appendix O
Verification Transfer Details

We provide the full proof of the verification transfer proposition.

Step 1: Problem reduction.

Since
\Delta_{1}(p)
is known (from prior verification), estimating
\mathrm{ECE}(M_{2})=\int|\Delta_{2}(p)|\,d\mu(p)
reduces to estimating the shift
h(p)=\Delta_{2}(p)-\Delta_{1}(p)
, then computing
\int|\Delta_{1}(p)+\hat{h}(p)|\,d\mu(p)
.

Step 2: Lower bound (detection cost).

Two-sample Le Cam between
H_{0}
:
\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{1}
and
H_{1}
:
\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{1}+h
with
\|h\|_{\infty}=\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}
. From
m_{2}
fresh samples near
p\approx 1-\varepsilon_{2}
:

\mathrm{KL}(H_{0}^{m_{2}}\|H_{1}^{m_{2}})\approx\frac{m_{2}\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{2}}

(22)

Indistinguishable when
\mathrm{KL}\leq 1
, requiring
m_{2}=\Omega(\varepsilon_{2}/\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}^{2})
.

Step 3: Upper bound (estimation with transfer).

Apply Theorem
3
to
h
instead of
\Delta_{2}
. The histogram estimator for
h
has bias
L_{h}/B
and variance
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}B/m_{2}}
. Optimizing:
B^{*}=(L_{h}^{2}m_{2}/\varepsilon_{2})^{1/3}
, giving:

\|\hat{h}-h\|=O\left(\left(\frac{L_{h}\varepsilon_{2}}{m_{2}}\right)^{1/3}\right)

(23)

By the triangle inequality:

\left|\mathrm{ECE}(M_{2})-\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}(M_{2})\right|\leq\int|\hat{h}(p)-h(p)|\,d\mu(p)=O\left(\left(\frac{L_{h}\varepsilon_{2}}{m_{2}}\right)^{1/3}\right)

(24)

Setting this equal to
\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}
:
m_{2}=O(L_{h}\varepsilon_{2}/\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}^{3})
.

Step 4: When does transfer help?

From-scratch:
m_{2}=\Theta(L\varepsilon_{2}/\delta^{3})
. With transfer:
m_{2}=\Theta(L_{h}\varepsilon_{2}/\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}^{3})
. Transfer helps when
\delta_{\mathrm{ft}}<\delta
and
L_{h}\leq L

Appendix P
Corrections: Correlation, Heterogeneity, Pre-Calibration

The core theorems assume i.i.d. labeled samples with independent Bernoulli noise. In practice, several deviations may arise.

Temporal correlation.

If evaluation samples arrive sequentially and exhibit temporal dependence, the effective sample size is reduced by a factor depending on the mixing time. For
\beta
-mixing sequences with coefficient
\beta(k)\leq c\cdot\rho^{k}
, the effective sample size is
m_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx m\cdot(1-\rho)
. All bounds apply with
m
replaced by
m_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Domain heterogeneity.

When the evaluation set spans multiple domains with different error rates, the per-domain verification floor (Fix 3) accounts for this heterogeneity. The overall ECE is a weighted average of domain-specific ECEs, and Jensen’s inequality ensures the overall verification floor is no larger than the worst per-domain floor weighted by domain proportion.

Pre-calibration via RLHF.

Models that have undergone reinforcement learning from human feedback may have artificially smooth calibration functions (small
L
), which reduces the verification tax. However, if the RLHF procedure oversmooths, the remaining miscalibration may be concentrated at specific confidence levels, violating the uniform Lipschitz assumption. Domain-specific Lipschitz estimates should be used in such cases.

Appendix S
Real-Model Experiment Details

MMLU experimental protocol.

We query each NIM-hosted model with the standard 4-way multiple-choice prompt (“Answer the following multiple choice question. Reply with ONLY the letter (A, B, C, or D)”). For each question, we extract the logprobs of the four answer tokens A, B, C, D at the first generated position, apply softmax over those four logits, and take the maximum as the model’s confidence. The model’s predicted answer is the argmax. Records with API errors (e.g., 403 Forbidden, content-filter rejection) are excluded. Each model run uses a single deterministic temperature-0 generation per question.

Confidence saturation (full data,
N=14{,}042
each).

On the complete MMLU set, max-softmax confidence is heavily concentrated near 1: Llama-3.1-405B has mean confidence 0.96 with 77% of questions saturated (
>0.99
); Qwen3-Next-80B has mean 0.98 with 86% saturated; Llama-4-Maverick-17B has mean 0.99 with 92% saturated. The 405B model has the most spread (lowest saturation rate), as expected for a larger and more capable model. Saturation affects the resolution of high-confidence bins but not the convergence rate, which is governed by the optimal bin count
B^{*}
.

Lipschitz constant estimation.

For each model, we partition the score range into 20 equal-width bins, compute the empirical accuracy in each bin with at least 30 samples, take the calibration gap
\hat{\Delta}_{b}=\mathrm{acc}_{b}-p_{b}
at each bin center, and form the adjacent-bin slope
|\hat{\Delta}_{b+1}-\hat{\Delta}_{b}|/|p_{b+1}-p_{b}|
. We report the 75th percentile of these slopes, capped at
\hat{L}=5
, as a robust estimate. This is sensitive to bin choice and noise; we recommend the audit floor be reported with
\hat{L}
from at least two binning resolutions.

Lipschitz composition.
By the chain rule, if
g_{k}
is
L_{k}
-Lipschitz in its first argument, then
g_{K}\circ\cdots\circ g_{1}
is
(\prod_{k=1}^{K}L_{k})
-Lipschitz. Since
\Delta_{\mathrm{sys}}(p)=\eta_{\mathrm{sys}}(p)-p
and
p\mapsto p
is 1-Lipschitz,
L_{\mathrm{sys}}\leq\prod_{k}L_{k}+1
.

Verification cost.
Applying Theorem
3
with
L=L_{\mathrm{sys}}
:
m_{\mathrm{sys}}=\Theta(L_{\mathrm{sys}}\varepsilon/\delta^{3})
. For homogeneous
L_{k}=L
:
L_{\mathrm{sys}}\approx L^{K}
, giving exponential scaling.
\square

Agent loop bound.
For an agent with
K
reasoning iterations and
L=2
:
L^{10}=1024
,
L^{20}>10^{6}
.

The Lipschitz composition model is optimistic in two directions: discontinuous components (e.g., discrete retrieval) yield
