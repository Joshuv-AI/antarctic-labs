 ABSTRACT
 This report details evaluation and demonstration on the potential of SPNDs
to measure the neutron energy spectrum in real time including methods to
reconstruct the shape of the spectrum based on the SPND measured responses.
 Based on previous material studies in FY24, a set of potential SPND emitter
materials—gadolinium, hafnium, and tantalum—were evaluated based on their
potential for neutron spectrum unfolding. A computational model using MCNP
was created to determine sensor response to neutrons based on their incident
energies. This is used to solve the inverse problem for spectrum unfolding. A
non-linear, least-squares method, called GRAVEL, was chosen due to its
common use in spectrum unfolding for flux foils, its simplicity in
implementation, its convergent behavior, and its restriction of only positive
solutions.
 Four types of SPND prototypes utilizing gadolinium, hafnium, tantalum, and
vanadium emitter wires were fabricated in-house. The experiment setup uses the
neutron qualification device, developed under the Advanced Sensors and
Instrument program. Reactor irradiations with and without cadmium filtering
were performed in the exposure rooms of the Armed Forces Radiobiology
Research Institute facility.
 The experiment demonstrated significant signal contribution from the sheath
of the SPND from both neutron and gamma interactions. This resulted in an
inversely proportional current output from the SPNDs in response to reactor
power. Therefore, a decomposition method was derived to provide outputs to the
GRAVEL unfolding method.
 Results from GRAVEL imply that the SPND response functions are useful
for unfolding the thermal region from the broad epithermal-fast region, however,
inadequate for separation between epithermal and fast region in the unfolding
algorithm.
 Future work to improve real-time unfolding would require includes assessing
the gamma-emitter sensitivity of SPNDs and alternative sensor technologies such
as ion chambers with (n, α) and (n, fission) reactions to yield additional spectral
information in the fast region.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Present in-core neutron detection technologies are comprised of ionization chambers, self-powered
detectors, and activation detectors [1]. However, despite the wide range of detectors spanned by these
technologies, the ability to measure the in-core neutron energy spectrum in real-time while exposed to the
harsh environment of a nuclear reactor is currently a blind spot in the nuclear industry. While this
limitation has been navigated around thus far, the ability to measure neutron energy spectrum in reactors
in real-time could have immense benefits to safety, experimental accuracy, and could prove pivotal in the
application of advanced reactors.
 When it comes to reactor safety, the ability to detect localized neutron energy spectrum shifts could
help detect phenomena that could lead to large reactivity or temperature changes such as the introduction
of reflective material near the detector, or the formation of localized phase changes of coolant [2], [3].
This safety implication could be even greater for advanced reactors, such as molten salt reactors or fast
spectrum reactors with mobilized fuels [4]. As for their use in irradiation experiments, real-time spectrum
measurements could provide improved estimates for sample power-coupling factors, burn-up, and
material displacements per atom which would increase the accuracy of their results. Other applications
include isotope production tracking at facilities that use reactors as their neutron source, as this could
provide a real-time approximation of the activation occurring within the target material. Finally, neutron
spectrum measurements could potentially even be used in the realm of nuclear safeguards, where changes
in fuel burnup can be detected based on the energy spectrum measured [5], [6].
 Activation detectors such as fission or dosimetry wires and foils are the industry standard in
measuring neutron spectrum in reactors, but they require their activities to be measured post-irradiation to
derive an integrated neutron fluence experienced, which cannot be accomplished in real-time [7], [8].
Ionization chambers, usually lined with boron or high-enriched fissile material, are widely used for
measuring real-time power outputs in reactors but presently do not have the ability to filter their outputs
by incident neutron energy. Though there are some active explorations to do so with fission chambers,
their lifespan may be limited by their high burn-up rates, and their relative size could limit their
applications. SPNDs stand out as a prime candidate for such measurements due to their relatively small
size, their ability to provide energy dependent responses in real-time at a low rate of burn-up, no
requirement for a high voltage source, and robustness in extreme environments such as those found in
reactors.
 The primary objective of the research detailed in this report is to demonstrate the potential of SPNDs
to measure the neutron energy spectrum in real time. The research in this report also demonstrates
potential selection criteria to be used in selecting SPND materials and potential methods to reconstruct the
shape of the spectrum based on the SPND measured responses. Specifically, this report details the
selection and irradiation of SPND materials in the Armed Forces Radiobiology Research Institute’s
(AFRRI) 1 MW Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics (TRIGA) reactor exposure room, their
responses to a change in thermal neutron spectrum, and a potential method of unfolding the spectrum in
real-time [9]. The result of this work informs future development of neutron spectroscopy methods suited
for harsh radiation and temperature environments.

 2. THEORY
 2.1. SPND Operational Theory
 Self-powered detectors are common in-core detectors used in nuclear industry given their small and
robust design (Figure 1). Taking the form of a mineral insulated cable, the detector is operable at
temperatures up to ~4-500°C and has temperature survivability exceeding 750°C with recoverable
operational capability upon cooldown [10]. While limited by its low-sensitivity with outputs in the order
of 1E-21 amps/neutrons/cm2/s, the detector passively generates an electrical current proportional to the
radiation field without the need of a high-voltage power supply with a detection range that is only limited
by the readout electronics.

 In the case of neutron interactions, the neutron capture results in the emission of a gamma particle and
the transmutation of the emitter material isotope. A prompt current can be generated through the low to
moderate energy gamma freeing electrons through the photoelectric and Compton scattering effect.
Additionally, depending on the half-life of the new transmuted material isotope, a delayed current could
be generated by the subsequent beta decay of the radioactive material. As such, SPNDs are further
divided into two categories, prompt SPNDs and delayed SPNDs, depending on the dominant steady-
current contributor.
 2.2. Selection of Candidate Emitter Materials
 Based on previous material studies in FY24 [11], a set of potential SPND emitter materials were
evaluated based on their potential for neutron spectrum unfolding. The initial phase of evaluation
consisted of compiling ENDF cross-section data from JANIS to compute energy-dependent reaction rates
for each of the elements of interest for different neutron energy spectrums [12]. The resulting reaction
rates were plotted as a heat map against each other in the following figures to qualitatively evaluate the
energy dependent contribution to the total neutron flux for each material and its changes from spectrum to
spectrum. Figure 3, shown below, shows the initial distribution of (n, γ) reaction rates for a bare TRIGA
spectrum. Figure 4 then demonstrates the shift in the (n, γ) reaction rates that occurs following a large
change in the spectrum, such as shifting from a bare TRIGA spectrum to a gadolinium filtered TRIGA
spectrum. The gadolinium filter effectively removes most thermal neutrons below approximately 1E-6
MeV from the environment due to its high thermal absorption below that energy, which gives a
comparison between a total response and a non-thermal response for each of these elements.

 A computational model using MCNP was created to model the gadolinium, hafnium, and tantalum
response to neutrons based on their incident energies [13]. The modeled SPND geometry, as shown in
Figure 3, is a simple geometry mimicking a typical SPND design: a central emitter with 0.018-inch outer
diameter (OD), 3.5-inch length, surrounded by a 0.012-inch thick magnesia (MgO) insulator, and a 0.010-
inch cm thick Inconel 600 sheath.

Figure 5: XY plane view of MCNP model used

 5

 For each computation, the emitter wire material was changed to be that of the natural composition of
the candidate material being tested. The neutron source was defined as a surface source along the outer
surface of the SPND sheath. The neutron source was evenly distributed in energy ranging from 0 MeV to
30 MeV and angularly focused on the centerline of the SPND emitter. Two surface tallies to calculate the
electron flux and approximate electric current were defined on the emitter-insulator and insulator-sheath
interfaces. Each tally was binned in both the initial energy of the sampled neutron and the angle of the
electron crossing the surface. Gadolinium, hafnium, and tantalum were each modeled using these
conditions. Rhodium was also modeled and compared to previous experiment results to generate an
approximate correlation between the electron flux generated and a real current observed during past
irradiation experiments. The results for gadolinium, hafnium, and tantalum were then compared to the
rhodium results to produce an energy dependent correlation between the incident neutron energy and the
resulting contribution to the generated current—the emitter material response function.
 This assumes that the current generated is dominated by the raw electron flux flowing across the
emitter surface and into the sheath. The resulting response functions are plotted against each other in
Figure 6, which showcases the energy dependency in each material response, analogous to differences in
cross-sections for flux foils which are used in traditional spectrum unfolding.

 4. EXPERIMENT DESIGN AND SETUP
 To test the capability of SPNDs to perform spectral measurements, Gd-, Hf-, and Ta-SPND
prototypes were fabricated in-house. An additional V-SPND was also prototyped to benchmark the
performance of in-house fabricated SPNDs to known performances of commercial SPNDs. The prototype
spectral SPNDs were tested using the neutron qualification device (NQD) placed into ER-1 and ER-2 of
the AFRRI facility.
 4.1. SPND Prototype
 Four types of SPND prototypes utilizing gadolinium, hafnium, tantalum, and vanadium emitter wires
were fabricated in-house. The design features Inconel-600 sheath, crushable MgO insulator, and a single
Inconel-600 lead wire. SPND assembly consists of welding the emitter wire to the lead wire, stringing
crushable single-hole MgO insulators over the wires, sliding the insulated assembly into the Inconel-600
sheath, and swaging the entire assembly which resembles a mineral insulated cable (MIC). Each of these
steps includes care in minimizing moisture in the MgO insulator by use of desiccators, tube heaters, and
heat guns. Final assembly includes sealing the MIC ends and adding soft-cabling and connectors.
The final calculated nominal geometries of the completed SPNDs are given in The calculated emitter OD
values are based on measured elongation values for each SPND prototype after swaging. A particularly
difficult portion of the fabrication is providing a robust transitional weld between the emitter wire and the
Inconel-600 lead wire. Welding tests were performed using laser welders and micro-TIG with micro-TIG
demonstrating a more consistent and robust weld. However, the overall compaction and resultant detector
diameter had to be limited to 0.070-inch during swaging process for Hf- and Gd-SPND due to their high
failure rates.

 4.2. The Neutron Qualification Device
 The experiment setup uses a low-activation furnace rig, called the NQD, developed under the
Advanced Sensors and Instrument (ASI) program shown in Figure 9 [15].

Figure 9. Design of the NQD.

 The purpose of the NQD is to enable the testing of neutron flux sensors at relevant temperatures of
reactor designs while minimizing flux perturbation between sensors. Design constraints of the NQD was
determined through MCNP evaluation of multiple potential sensor types—dosimetry wires, miniature ion
or fission chambers, and self-powered neutron detectors—and their local flux perturbation from albedo
and shadowing effects. The results of the evaluation provided a minimum design criteria—10-degree
sensor edge-to-edge offset and 0.83-inch sensor-to-sensor spacing to achieve less than 1% flux
perturbation between sensors [16]. Figure 10 demonstrates a test case using a 15-degree offset angle with
1-inch radius.

Figure 10. Demonstration placement of sensors and resultant flux perturbation to determin NQD design
constraints.

 11

 The final NQD design provided ten sensor positions split into three columns. Based on the conceptual
design requirements, the NQD has a with center-to-center offset of 17-degrees between each column of
detectors and minimum detector spacing of 1-inch. The front two columns (positions 1-7 shown in Figure
11) satisfy the minimum flux perturbation requirement and are designated for flux sensor testing while the
last column (positions 8-10) is used for temperature monitoring.

Figure 11. Sensor position designation for the NQD.

 4.3. Experiment Setup
 The design requirements of the NQD demand a larger space which limits the irradiation facility to
neutron sources, beamlines, or exposure rooms. The Armed Forces Radiobiology Research Institute
(AFRRI) was ultimately chosen for its exposure rooms located on either end of the reactor pool.
 The reactor at AFRRI is a 1.0-megawatt Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics (TRIGA)
reactor. A unique aspect of the AFRRI reactor is its ability to move the reactor core towards either
exposure rooms leaving only 1-inch water gap and a 3/8-inch-thick aluminum wall between the room and
the reactor core (Figure 13).

 6. DISCUSSION
 The general performance of the SPNDs indicates that the gamma-to-neutron ratios in ER-1 and ER-2
were significantly larger than expected in the typical in-core/near-core dry-tube experiments. The
improper account of this gamma contribution resulted in a large negative signal contribution such that the
SPND responses became inverted with respect to reactor power. Additionally, the overall delayed signals
of the SPNDs at reactor power hold points had opposite polarities between ER-1 and ER-2. This indicates
an additional unknown interaction in the SPND that has not been observed before.
 To separate the unwanted contributors discussed above from the emitter-only results, the
decomposition calculations detailed Appendix A-1 was derived. While this method provided a reasonably
physical output to be used in the GRAVEL unfolding method, it still resulted in a negative V-SPND
output in ER-1. This is attributed to the addition of a prompt gamma-emitter contribution that was
assumed to be zero in typical applications to enable the method. Future work will further assess the
gamma-emitter sensitivity of SPNDs as well as SPGD.
 Separately, both the unfolded synthetic results and the unfolded experiment results from GRAVEL
imply that the SPND response functions are useful for unfolding the thermal region from the broad
epithermal-fast region, however, inadequate for separation between epithermal and fast region in the
unfolding algorithm. This is due to the high thermal dependence of PN designs using (n, γ) reactions to
drive the detector response. Alternative reactions that have unique resonance regions or thresholds could
yield additional spectral information. This would include (n, α) and (n, fission) reactions. Future work
would explore potential material sets using these materials sensitive to these reactions in ionization
chambers, with the goal of eventual miniaturized designs compatible with collocated installation
alongside PN ’s.
