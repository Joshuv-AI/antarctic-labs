Optimising CMIP6 Multi-model ensembles using ETCCDI–TOPSIS–Monte carlo framework for reliable climate projections over Odisha, India
Authors: Nishikanta Kar, Sarat Chandra Sahu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable regional climate projections are essential for adaptation in climate-sensitive regions such as Odisha, India. This study develops an integrated framework combining Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI)-based extremes, the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), and Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis to evaluate and optimise 35 bias-corrected Global Climate Models (GCMs) from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6). The optimised Multi-Model Ensemble (MME) reproduces historical temperature and precipitation patterns (1985–2014) with modest bias and strong seasonal agreement, and is applied to projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 (2015–2100). Results indicate progressive warming: maximum and minimum temperatures increase by ~ 1.7 °C under SSP2-4.5 and ~ 3.3–3.4 °C under SSP5-8.5 by late century, with summer days (SU) rising by ~ 20–46 days and tropical nights (TR) by up to ~ 112 days. The extreme temperature range (ETR) widens significantly under SSP5-8.5. Precipitation increases are largest during winter and post-monsoon seasons, while monsoon-season changes remain small and slightly negative under SSP2-4.5. Very wet (R95pTOT) and extremely wet (R99pTOT) precipitation increase by ~ 3% and ~ 2% under SSP2-4.5, and by ~ 5% and ~ 4% under SSP5-8.5, indicating a growing contribution of extreme rainfall events to annual precipitation totals. Northern and coastal Odisha show higher precipitation increases, while inland regions experience stronger warming and enhanced heat stress. Introduction

Climate change is reshaping climate systems worldwide, with tropical regions projected to experience some of the strongest impacts. Global mean temperature has risen by more than 1 °C above pre-industrial levels1, and South Asia is among the most vulnerable regions2. The summer monsoon underpins food security, water resources, and disaster risk management for over a billion people3. Even modest changes in rainfall timing, intensity, or spatial distribution can trigger major socio-economic consequences, including reduced crop yields, increased flood damage, and heightened water stress4.

Odisha, located along India’s eastern coast with 480 km of Bay of Bengal shoreline and a population of approximately 46 million5, is particularly climate-sensitive. Its economy depends heavily on rain-fed agriculture, fisheries, and coastal ecosystems6. The state is highly exposed to tropical cyclones, including Phailin (2013), Fani (2019), and Yaas (2021) 50. Observational evidence indicates rising temperatures, intensified monsoon rainfall, drier winters, and stronger pre-monsoon heat since the 1980s7–12. These changes vary across Odisha’s four meteorological regions—Northern Interior (NI), Northern Coastal (NC), Southern Interior (SI), and Southern Coastal (SC)—reflecting diverse topography from the Eastern Ghats to cyclone-prone coastal plains (Kumar13–15). National-scale assessments often mask such regional contrasts, underscoring the need for high-resolution, region-specific projections16,17.

Despite advances in national-scale CMIP6 assessments, Odisha remains underrepresented and projections continue to exhibit substantial model uncertainty. CMIP6 GCMs show disagreement in regional rainfall responses, with projected spreads exceeding 2.3–2.6 mm day⁻1 18. Conventional ensemble approaches based on simple averaging treat all models equally, irrespective of skill 19. Even with bias-corrected and downscaled datasets such as NASA’s NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 (~ 25 km resolution), the large number of available models raises a critical question: which models are most reliable for Odisha? Basin-scale studies further demonstrate that model rankings can be sensitive to evaluation criteria and weighting schemes. For example, Keesara et al.20 highlighted subjectivity in CMIP6 model selection over the Munneru River Basin. These findings indicate the need for transparent and statistically grounded frameworks for regional model evaluation.

Most previous studies in Odisha and across India have evaluated models primarily using mean climatology or conventional statistical metrics such as bias and correlation11,21–23. While informative, such metrics are insufficient for adaptation planning because climate impacts are often driven by extremes rather than mean conditions. Heatwaves, tropical nights, intense rainfall events, and prolonged dry spells directly affect agriculture, water availability, and public health. The Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI) developed standardized indices to quantify such extremes24,25. Although these indices have been applied to assess historical and future changes in Odisha26, they are rarely used as criteria for model selection.

This study addresses that gap by employing ETCCDI indices as evaluation benchmarks for CMIP6 GCM performance over Odisha. Model skill in reproducing observed extremes is quantified and integrated using the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), a multi-criteria decision-making framework27–29. Unlike previous applications that relied solely on conventional statistical metrics, this framework integrates extreme-event diagnostics within a structured TOPSIS–Monte Carlo approach to enhance transparency and consistency in ensemble construction. The highest-ranked models form a Multi-Model Ensemble (MME) for regional projections.

To assess ranking stability, Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis is performed by generating multiple weight combinations and recalculating model rankings. Models consistently appearing among the top performers are identified as robust choices, reducing dependence on subjective weighting assumptions. To our knowledge, this represents the first Odisha-focused application of ETCCDI-based evaluation within a structured ensemble optimisation framework.

The selected MMEs for precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax), and minimum temperature (Tmin) are validated against Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) observations for the 1985–2014 baseline30,31 and applied to projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios across three future periods: near future (2026–2050), mid future (2051–2075), and far future (2076–2100). Mean changes, seasonal anomalies, and trends in ETCCDI indices are evaluated using the Modified Mann–Kendall test and Sen’s slope estimator.

By integrating ETCCDI-based extreme indices into ensemble evaluation, this framework provides a transparent and reproducible approach to regional climate model assessment and ensemble optimisation. Linking extreme-event diagnostics to model performance advances beyond earlier Odisha-focused studies that relied primarily on mean climatology. The resulting spatially explicit projections offer a scientific basis for assessing future changes in flood risk, heat stress, monsoon intensity, and seasonal hydroclimatic variability under different emission scenarios.

Data and methodology

Datasets: building the climate baseline and future

Daily climate data were obtained from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6) archive. This dataset provides statistically downscaled and bias-corrected outputs from CMIP6 Global Climate Models (GCMs) using the Bias-Correction Spatial Disaggregation (BCSD) method32,33. The ~ 25 km spatial resolution makes it suitable for sub-national analyses such as the Odisha region34,35. The full archive contains 35 GCMs that provide bias-corrected daily precipitation over the historical baseline (1985–2014); for daily Tmax and Tmin, only 32 of these 35 GCMs provide complete and consistent records over the same baseline at the time of data download. The three GCMs with incomplete or unavailable Tmax/Tmin daily fields (CESM2, CESM2-WACCM, and IITM-ESM) were excluded from the temperature analyses but retained for precipitation. Consequently, the multi-criteria evaluation reported in Figs. 1 and 2 uses n = 35 for precipitation and n = 32 for Tmax and Tmin (Supplementary Table S1 lists all models and their variable availability). Following WMO guidelines, we adopted a 30-year climatological baseline36. Although both IMD and NEX-GDDP extend back to 1950, we selected 1985–2014 as the baseline because IMD observations are more reliable after the mid-1980s. This provides both methodological robustness and a policy-relevant reference, while avoiding earlier uncertainties. Since NEX-GDDP is already bias-corrected and downscaled, no further adjustments were applied. Our emphasis was on evaluating model performance against IMD to identify the most reliable GCMs for MME construction.

Why NEX-GDDP instead of CORDEX?

We selected NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 for three reasons:It offers a larger and more diverse ensemble (35 CMIP6 GCMs) compared to CORDEX South Asia, which relies mainly on CMIP5 and includes fewer models.NEX-GDDP applies uniform bias-correction and statistical downscaling, ensuring consistency across models. In contrast, CORDEX outputs are dynamically downscaled but not bias-corrected, complicating inter-model comparisons.Its ~ 25 km resolution is better suited to Odisha’s state-level analysis than CORDEX’s ~ 50 km grid.

Choosing the right models: performance evaluation and ensemble selection

To identify the best-performing models, ETCCDI-based climate indices were evaluated for precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin during the historical baseline period (1985–2014). These indices capture both extremes and seasonality, providing a comprehensive assessment of climate representation. For precipitation, 11 indices were used (RX1day, RX5day, SDII, R10mm, R20mm, CDD, CWD, PRCPTOT, R95pTOT, R99pTOT, and seasonality). For Tmax, 7 indices were evaluated (TXx, TXn, SU > 37 °C, TX10p, TX90p, WSDI, and seasonality), and for Tmin, 7 indices were considered (TNx, TNn, TR > 20 °C, TN10p, TN90p, CSDI, and seasonality). All evaluations were conducted exclusively over the Odisha sub-region. For each GCM, daily outputs were spatially subset to the Odisha administrative boundary and regridded to match the 0.25° IMD gridded observational dataset. ETCCDI indices were calculated for both IMD observations and model simulations over this domain and baseline period, and performance metrics were derived consistently to ensure direct comparability between simulations and observations.

Multi-criteria ranking using TOPSIS

To integrate model skill across multiple climate indices and evaluation metrics, the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was applied37. Monte carlo sensitivity analysis of TOPSIS

To evaluate the robustness of the TOPSIS-based ranking to uncertainty in metric weighting, a Monte Carlo sensitivity experiment was conducted38. A total of 3,000 weight realizations were generated by sampling from a Dirichlet distribution39,40 ensuring that all weights were positive and summed to one while preserving equal expected importance across performance metrics. For each realization, the TOPSIS ranking procedure was recomputed, and the frequency with which each model appeared in the top five was recorded. Trend and significance analysis

Long-term trends in mean climate variables and ETCCDI indices were assessed using the Modified Mann–Kendall (MMK) test, which accounts for serial autocorrelation in climate time series. The MMK approach adjusts the variance of the Mann–Kendall statistic using the effective sample size, thereby reducing the risk of inflated significance due to persistence41,42. Trend magnitude was estimated using Sen’s slope estimator, a non-parametric method robust to outliers43–45. Sen’s slope provides the median rate of change per year (°C yr⁻1, mm yr⁻1, days yr⁻1, or % yr⁻1). For long-term trend assessment (2015–2100), cumulative projected change (Δ) was calculated as Sen’s slope multiplied by the number of yearly intervals within the projection period (n = 85). Trends were considered statistically significant at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). All analyses were performed using the pymannkendall Python package46.

To evaluate whether projected late-century conditions differ significantly from the historical baseline, annual means from the baseline period (1985–2014) were compared with those from the late-century period (2071–2100), each comprising 30 years. A two-sample Welch’s t-test was applied at each grid cell to account for potential unequal variances between periods47–49. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05, and significant grid cells are indicated by stippling in the corresponding spatial maps.

Results & discussion

Spatio-Temporal historical skill of the ensemble

Before projecting future climate, the performance of the TOPSIS-selected top-five models for precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin was evaluated over Odisha. Corresponding MMEs were constructed and compared against IMD observations for the historical period 1985–2014, with Monte Carlo simulations used to assess the robustness of model selection.

Observed precipitation exhibits clear spatial heterogeneity across Odisha’s four meteorological regions, with higher rainfall over the northern interior (NI) and northern coastal (NC) regions and comparatively lower precipitation over the southern interior (SI). The MME captures these regional contrasts realistically, although with a modest dry bias (− 0.36 mm day⁻1) relative to IMD. The spatial RMSE of 0.45 mm day⁻1 indicates limited grid-scale deviations, suggesting good agreement in the representation of the rainfall distribution. The domain-median precipitation is 3.67 mm day⁻1 compared to 4.01 mm day⁻1 in IMD. For temperature, the ensemble reproduces the broad spatial pattern of warmer inland conditions (NI and SI) and relatively cooler coastal regions (NC and SC). The ensemble medians for Tmax (31.65 °C) and Tmin (20.77 °C) are slightly lower than IMD values (32.08 °C and 21.13 °C, respectively), indicating systematic but moderate cold biases (− 0.82 °C for Tmax and − 0.21 °C for Tmin). The spatial RMSE values of 1.45 °C for Tmax and 1.30 °C for Tmin reflect moderate spatial discrepancies, primarily associated with differences in land–coastal thermal gradients and dataset resolution. Figure 3b illustrates the annual cycle of precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin over Odisha during 1985–2014, comparing IMD observations with the selected MME. Both the daily climatological cycle (DOY) and the monthly mean seasonal cycle are evaluated. For precipitation, the ensemble successfully captures the onset, peak (July–August), and withdrawal of the monsoon. Daily precipitation exhibits RMSE = 1.61 mm day⁻1, Bias = 0.37 mm day⁻1, and KGE = 0.87, improving at the monthly scale (RMSE = 0.61 mm day⁻1, KGE = 0.90). Tmax reproduces the pre-monsoon maximum during April–May and subsequent monsoon cooling with RMSE ≈ 0.98 °C (DOY) and 0.93 °C (monthly), while Tmin shows strong agreement throughout the seasonal cycle (RMSE ≤ 0.43 °C for DOY and 0.32 °C for monthly). Overall, the ensemble demonstrates high efficiency (KGE ranging from 0.87 to 0.90) and low bias across both precipitation and temperature cycles, indicating robust representation of seasonal variability.

Mean climate futures for Odisha

After confirming the reliability of the historical ensemble, we examined how future climate conditions are likely to unfold across Odisha. After validating the historical ensemble performance, we assessed projected late-century (2071–2100) changes relative to the 1985–2014 baseline. Figure 4 shows ensemble mean changes alongside interannual variability and statistical significance. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-sample Welch’s t-test comparing historical and future annual means at each grid cell (p < 0.05). Precipitation changes are modest under both scenarios (+ 0.09 mm day⁻1 under SSP2-4.5 and + 0.50 mm day⁻1 under SSP5-8.5). However, statistically significant increases are limited, with some signals over northern and coastal regions. The relatively large interannual variability reduces confidence in precipitation projections. In contrast, temperature projections show robust and spatially consistent warming across the entire state. Tmax increases by ~ 1.71 °C under SSP2-4.5 and ~ 3.36 °C under SSP5-8.5, while Tmin rises by ~ 1.60 °C and ~ 3.37 °C, respectively. Nearly all grid cells exhibit statistically significant warming, with comparatively low interannual variability (< 1 °C), indicating good model agreement and high confidence in projected temperature increases. These results demonstrate that while precipitation projections remain uncertain and spatially variable, warming trends are statistically robust and consistent as indicated by widespread grid-cell significance across Odisha, particularly under the high-emission scenario.

Extremes redefined: ETCCDI indices to quantify climate shocks

Beyond changes in averages, climate change is strongly expressed through extremes. To capture these signals, we applied the internationally recognized ETCCDI indices, derived from daily temperature and precipitation data. These indices quantify the most relevant aspects of climate shocks such as hot days, warm nights, heavy rainfall spells, or prolonged dry periods. The set includes temperature-based indices (absolute extremes: TXx, TNx, TXn, TNn, DTR, ETR; percentile-based: TN10p, TX10p, TN90p, TX90p; threshold-based: SU, TR; and duration indices: WSDI, CSDI) and precipitation-based indices (absolute extremes: Rx1day, Rx5day; threshold-based: R10mm, R20mm; intensity and duration: SDII, CDD, CWD; and totals: PRCPTOT, R95pTOT, R99pTOT) (see Supplementary Table S2 for detailed descriptions).

Temperature extremes

Temperature extremes over Odisha exhibit statistically significant warming across nearly all indices under SSP2-4.5 (Fig. 7a). The hottest days (TXx) increase at + 0.03 °C yr⁻1 (p = 0.02), while warmest nights (TNx) rise at + 0.02 °C yr⁻1 (p = 0.02), indicating intensifying heat extremes. Cold extremes weaken as minimum and cold-season temperatures increase, with coldest maxima (TXn) and coldest nights (TNn) warming at + 0.03 °C yr⁻1 (p = 0.01) and + 0.02 °C yr⁻1 (p = 0.03), respectively. The frequency of cold nights (TN10p) and cold days (TX10p) declines significantly (− 0.04 and − 0.05% yr⁻1; p = 0.02 for both). In contrast, warm extremes intensify, with warm nights (TN90p) and warm days (TX90p) increasing at + 0.13% yr⁻1 (p = 0.004) and + 0.06% yr⁻1 (p = 0.02), respectively. Threshold indices show longer hot periods, with summer days (SU) and tropical nights (TR) increasing by + 0.23 and + 0.50 days yr⁻1 (p = 0.02 and 0.01, respectively), while warm spell duration (WSDI) increases (+ 0.23 days yr⁻1; p = 0.02) and cold spell duration (CSDI) decreases (− 0.08 days yr⁻1; p = 0.03). Changes in diurnal temperature range (DTR) and extreme temperature range (ETR) remain weak and statistically insignificant (p = 0.07 and 0.22, respectively), indicating that under the moderate forcing pathway warming proceeds uniformly across the temperature distribution without significant widening of the temperature spread.

Precipitation extremes

Precipitation extremes over Odisha show mixed signals (Fig. 7b). Annual maximum 1-day (Rx1day) and 5-day (Rx5day) rainfall exhibit statistically significant increasing trends (+ 0.13 mm yr⁻1, p = 0.03 and + 0.24 mm yr⁻1, p = 0.03), indicating strengthening short-duration extreme rainfall. Rainfall intensity (SDII) shows no significant long-term change (~ 0.00 mm day⁻1 yr⁻1, p = 0.22). The frequency of moderate rainfall days (R10mm) decreases slightly (− 0.02 days yr⁻1, p = 0.38), while heavy rainfall days (R20mm) show no discernible trend (~ 0.00 days yr⁻1); the Mann–Kendall test could not return a valid p-value for this index due to insufficient variance in the annual series, and the result is therefore excluded from significance assessment. Consecutive dry days (CDD) exhibit a weak declining tendency (− 0.05 days yr⁻1, p = 0.25), whereas consecutive wet days (CWD) also decrease slightly (− 0.03 days yr⁻1, p = 0.21). Total annual precipitation (PRCPTOT) shows no significant trend (− 0.16 mm yr⁻1, p = 0.58). The contribution of very wet days (R95pTOT) and extremely wet days (R99pTOT) to annual precipitation increases significantly (+ 0.03% yr⁻1, p = 0.01 and + 0.02% yr⁻1, p = 0.02), indicating a growing share of extreme rainfall events in the annual precipitation budget.

Overall, under SSP2-4.5 Odisha shows a gradual but consistent warming trend, with noticeable increases in warm nights and longer periods of heat, while cold extremes become less frequent. Rainfall extremes remain variable, yet the increasing contribution of very wet and extremely wet days to total rainfall suggests a subtle but important shift toward higher rainfall-related risks under future warming.

Under SSP5-8.5, temperature extremes over Odisha intensify strongly and consistently across all indices (Fig. 8a). Overall, Sections "Trends in climate extremes under SSP2-4.5" and "Trends in climate extremes under SSP5-8.5" demonstrate that MME trends in ETCCDI indices reveal progressive intensification of warm extremes and emerging shifts in precipitation characteristics, particularly under SSP5-8.5. The regional expression of these changes is explored in Section "Regional climate amplification under SSP2-4.5".

Under SSP5-8.5, both warming magnitude and regional contrasts in rainfall intensify from NFC through FFC. Conclusion

This study applied an integrated ETCCDI–TOPSIS–Monte Carlo framework to evaluate 35 NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 models and construct an optimised MME for the Odisha sub-region. Model performance was assessed using RMSE, ABias, and KGE, followed by multi-criteria ranking and robustness testing through Dirichlet-based Monte Carlo simulations. The selected ensemble reproduces the historical climate (1985–2014) with modest bias, good agreement in the seasonal cycle, and reasonable spatial consistency, providing a reliable basis for future projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Projections are based on statistically downscaled NEX-GDDP data (~ 0.25° resolution), which may not fully represent fine-scale or topographic influences. Statistical significance was assessed independently at each grid cell without applying field significance correction, and therefore results should be interpreted with caution at local scales. Precipitation projections show higher interannual variability and lower statistical confidence compared to temperature. Uncertainty associated with emission scenarios and internal climate variability also remains in long-term projections.
