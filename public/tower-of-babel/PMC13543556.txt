Plastome evolution and phylogenomic relationships in Ajuga (Lamiaceae, Ajugoideae)
Authors: Shu-Han Li, Li-Qiong Jiang, Ferhat Celep, Alan J. Paton, Trevor C. Wilson, Orzimat T. Turginov, Tuncay Dirmenci, Chun-Lei Xiang, Fei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAjuga is currently known to include approximately 69 species, with a combined distribution extending throughout Eurasia, Africa, and Australia. Its popularity and significance are largely based on an extensive history of medicinal and horticultural use. It is divided into two sections based on morphological characters, and this sectional classification is also reflected in pronounced geographic patterns. Although previous studies have largely focused on Ajuga sect. Ajuga in East Asia, A. sect. Chamaepithys, which ranges from the Mediterranean to Central Asia, remains insufficiently sampled, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of infrageneric sectional relationships within the genus. Here, we generated complete plastid genomes for 12 species representing both sections of the genus and used these data to characterize plastome structure and infer evolutionary relationships.ResultsIn this study, 21 Ajuga plastomes were analyzed, including 12 newly sequenced plastomes and 9 previously published plastomes representing 19 species. Comparative analyses showed that all plastomes exhibited a highly conserved quadripartite structure, with genome sizes ranging from 149,963 to 150,740 bp and GC contents varying from 38.2% to 38.3%. Each plastome contained 133 genes, including 88 protein-coding genes, 37 transfer RNA genes, and 8 ribosomal RNA genes. The boundaries between the inverted repeat (IR) and single-copy (SC) regions were also highly conserved across species. In addition, 796 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), 874 long repeat sequences (LRSs), and 12 highly variable regions (ccsA-ndhD, ndhF-rpl32, petA-psbJ, rpl32-trnL-UAG, rps2-rpoC2, trnH-GUG-psbA, trnK-UUU-rps16, trnP-UGG-psaJ, trnT-UGU-trnL-UAA, ycf15-trnL-CAA, ndhF, and ycf1) were identified among the 21 plastomes. Phylogenetic analyses based on four datasets and conducted using Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian Inference recovered two major clades corresponding to the traditionally recognized sectional classification, with one distributed from the Mediterranean to Central Asia and the other in East Asia.ConclusionThis study represents the most comprehensive plastome-based sampling of Ajuga to date, including representative species from the Mediterranean, Central Asia, and East Asia. Our results have significantly enhanced our understanding of its infrageneric relationships. The plastome resources generated in this study provide a valuable foundation for future research on species delimitation, phylogeny, and the evolutionary history of Ajuga.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09307-x.

Background

Ajuga L. (Lamiaceae) is a well-known group of mostly perennial herbs due to their versatility as ornamental plants (e.g., A. reptans L.) [1], contribution as nectar sources within pollinator networks (e.g., A. reptans, A. pyramidalis L. and A. chamaepitys L.) [2], and therapeutic use in several traditional folk medicines. More specifically, some Asian species (e.g., A. bracteosa Wall. ex Benth) recognized by Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) are used for heat-clearing, detoxification, blood-cooling, and anti-inflammatory effects [3–6], and further west, such as in Türkiye, species like A. chamaepitys (L.) Schreb. are used for the treatment of hemorrhoids and skin diseases [7–10]. Nonetheless, despite its reputation, surprisingly little progress has been made to understand the systematics and taxonomy of Ajuga.

Ajuga is a rather unique member of the tribe Ajugoideae in that it is herbaceous or suffrutescent shrub, rather than a woody one, and it consists of approximately 69 species distributed across temperate regions of Eurasia, Africa, and Australia [11, 12]. Based on the number of flowers per verticillaster and the structure of the upper corolla lip, Bentham [13, 14] divided the genus into three sections: Ajuga sect. Bugula Benth. (= sect. Ajuga), A. sect. Chamaepithys Benth., and A. sect. Pseudanisomeles Benth., the latter comprising species endemic to Australia. Subsequently, Boissier [15] established sect. Phleboanthe Tausch. to accommodate A. laxmannii (L.) Benthm. Later, based on a comprehensive morphological study, Briquet [16] recognized two sections, sect. Chamaepithys Benth. and sect. Ajuga, further subdividing sect. Chamaepithys into two subsections (Ivae Briq. and Phleboanthe Briq.) and organizing sect. Ajuga into four informal groups. This infrageneric classification framework has been widely adopted and revised in several regional floras, including the Flora of the USSR [17] and the Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae [18]. As of yet, phylogenetic or phenetic analyses have not been applied to assess the relevance of these higher taxonomic classifications to an evolutionary history.

The contribution of work in the systematics of Ajuga has been focused at species level, largely depending on leaf anatomy and the morphology of pollen and nutlets [19–22]. Molecular phylogenetic investigations are scarce, constrained to the use of nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (nrITS) sequence to understand relationship between the Ajuga (11 spp.) of Türkiye [23], and an investigation of the hybridization origin of A. × mixta Makino in Japan [24]. A sparing amount of sequence has since been assembled for Ajuga despite the recent genomic revolution, consisting only of a mainly structural investigation of the A. forrestii Diels plastid genome [25], and a comparative plastome analysis and phylogeny for 11 Ajuga species restricted mostly to East Asia [26]. These studies consistently supported the monophyly of Ajuga and provided insights into relationships among major lineages within the genus. However, a more comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of Ajuga, particularly one based on broader sampling beyond East Asia, is still required to validate its sectional classification at the global scale.

With the rapid development and decreasing cost of next generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, complete plastid genome data have become increasingly available in recent years [27–29]. In land plants, plastomes are typically characterized by a relatively small size, conserved gene content and structure, and a moderate evolutionary rate, making them powerful tools for resolving phylogenetic and taxonomic relationships at various taxonomic levels [12, 30–35]. Within Lamiaceae, plastome-based studies have successfully clarified taxonomic relationships at multiple taxonomic categories, including the level of family [12], subfamily [36], tribe [37, 38], and genus [39–45].

In order to provide a valuable genomic resource for taxonomy, species identification, and population genetic studies, we sequenced and assembled the complete plastid genomes for 12 species of Ajuga representing the geographic range and infrageneric classification, although Australian and African species were not included in this study for certain reasons. These were combined with existing plastomes to conduct the most comprehensive phylogenomic study of Ajuga to date. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize plastome structural and gene content in Ajuga; (2) identify highly variable regions with potential utility as molecular markers; and (3) infer phylogenetic relationships within Ajuga using complete plastome sequence.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling

Among the 12 newly sequenced Ajuga species, fresh leaf material from eight species was collected in the field and then dried using silica-gel, whereas four others were sampled from herbarium specimens held at K or TASH (Table 1). Additional plastid genome sequences were downloaded for eight Ajuga species from GenBank, and another Ajuga plastome was reassembled from reads obtained from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA). In total, 21 accessions representing 19 Ajuga species were included as the ingroup. On the basis of previous phylogenetic studies of Ajugeae [12, 46, 47], seven additional species were selected as outgroups, representing Amethystea L. (A. caerulea L.), Caryopteris Bunge (C. forrestii Diels, C. incana (Thunb.) Miq., C. mongholica Bunge, C. tangutica Maxim., C. trichosphaera W.W.Sm.), and Pseudocaryopteris (Briq.) P. D. Cantino (P. foetida (D.Don) P.D. Cantino). Detailed information on all samples, including voucher specimens and Genbank accession numbers, is provided in the Table S1. Voucher specimens from newly sequenced specimens are lodged at the Herbarium of Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (KUN).

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from silica gel-dried leaf material using a modified CTAB method [48]. For herbarium specimens, DNA extraction was performed using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and UV spectrophotometry.

Sequencing libraries with an average insert size of approximately 300 bp were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra II™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA). Paired-end (PE) sequencing with a read length of 150 bp was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq-2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) at BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China), generating approximately 5 or 10 Gb of raw data per sample.

Plastome assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing reads were filtered using Fastp v.0.23.2 [49] with default parameters to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. High-quality clean reads were used for the de novo assembly of complete plastid genomes with GetOrganelle v1.7.7.1 [50], using the parameters: -R 15 -k 21,45,65,85,105 -F embplant_pt. Assembly graphs were visualized and manually inspected using Bandage v.0.9.0 [51].

Genome annotation was performed with PGA [52], using Ajuga bracteosa (OM630151) as the reference. Start and stop codons, as well as intron/exon boundaries, were manually verified and adjusted in Geneious v.11.0.4 [53]. Gene number, gene length, and GC content were calculated using CPStools v.2.0.1 [54]. Circular plastome maps were visualized using OGDRAW v1.3.1 [55] and subsequently edited manually.

Chloroplast genome comparison and sequence divergence analysis

In order to assess sequence variation among the 21 Ajuga plastomes, genome sequences were converted into mVISTA format using CPStools v.2.0.1 [54] and compared in mVISTA [56] in Shuffle-LAGAN mode, with Ajuga bracteosa (OM630151) as the reference. Expansion and contraction of the inverted repeat (IR) regions were examined using IRscope [57] by comparing gene positions at the LSC/IR and SSC/IR junctions. To identify hypervariable regions and estimate nucleotide diversity pi (π), 21 plastome sequences were aligned using MAFFT v.7.475 [58] with default settings. Nucleotide diversity was calculated separately for coding and non-coding regions using the CPStools pipeline [54].

Codon usage and simple sequence repeat analysis

Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values and amino acid frequencies of protein-coding regions were calculated using the RSCU module implemented in CPStools v.2.0.1 [54]. An interactive RSCU plot generated with the RSCU-Plot Shiny app (https://pcg-lab.shinyapps.io/RSCU-Plot/) was used to visualize the resulting RSCU patterns [59]. Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) were identified using MISA (MIcroSAtellite) [60], with minimum repeat thresholds set to 10 for mononucleotide repeats, 5 for dinucleotides, 4 for trinucleotides, and 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. Long repeat sequences, including forward (F), reverse (R), complement (C), and palindromic (P) repeats, were detected using REPuter [61], with a minimum repeat length of 30 bp, a hamming distance of 3, and a maximum of 300 repeats. The resulting data were further processed and visualized using the LSRs module in CPStools v2.0.1 [54].

Phylogenetic analyses

Four datasets were used for phylogenetic reconstruction: (1) complete plastome sequences (CP), (2) concatenated coding regions (CR), (3) non-coding regions (NCR), including intergenic spacers and introns, and (4) a combined dataset of 12 hypervariable regions (12 VAR) identified in this study. Sequences were initially aligned using MAFFT v7.471 [58] with default settings, and ambiguously aligned regions were trimmed with Trimal v1.5.0 [62] using the ‘-automated1’ option. In order to avoid redundancy, only one copy of the IR region was retained in the CP dataset.

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods on the Cyberinfrastructure for Phylogenetic Research (CIPRES) Science Gateway (http://www.phylo.org/) [63]. ML analyses were conducted using RAxML HPC2 v. 8.2.12 [64] under the GTR + Gamma model, with 1000 bootstrap replicates (–#|–N). Bayesian analyses were conducted in MrBayes v.3.2.7a [65], The best-fit nucleotide substitution model for each dataset was selected using jModelTest2 [66], based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). with four Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains (one cold and three heated) were run for 20 million generations, sampling every 1000 generations. Convergence and the effective sample size (ESS) were assessed using Tracer v1.7.0 [67]. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in, and the remaining trees were summarized to generate a 50% majority-rule consensus tree. All phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using Figtree v.1.4.4 [68].

Validation of hypervariable molecular markers

To evaluate the phylogenetic utility of the 12 candidate hypervariable regions in Ajuga, these loci were extracted from the complete plastomes and analyzed as separate datasets. Concatenated datasets comprising three, four, and five loci were subsequently constructed based on their Pi values. In addition, following previous studies [26], we generated a combined dataset consisting of the two intergenic regions with high Pi values (ndhF-rpl32 and trnH-GUG-psbA) together with the protein-coding gene ndhF. The Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses were performed using the same procedures described above.

Results

Chloroplast genome features

In this study, complete plastid genomes from 21 accessions representing 19 species of Ajuga were analyzed, including 12 newly sequenced plastomes and nine previously published sequences. All 12 newly sequenced plastomes exhibited the typical circular quadripartite structure, with genome sizes ranging from 149,963 bp in A. reptans L. to 150,749 bp in A. zakhoensis Rech.f. and the mean coverage of base from 507 (ⅹ) in A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus to 1,138 (ⅹ) in A. linearifolia (Table 1). Each genome consisted of a large single-copy (LSC) region (81,770–82,514 bp), a small single-copy (SSC) region (17,097–17,356 bp), and a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions (25,442–25,591 bp) (Table 1; Fig. 1, FigS1–2).

The overall GC content was highly conserved across species, ranging narrowly from 38.2% to 38.3%. As commonly observed in most angiosperm plastomes, GC content was higher in the IR regions (43.3%) than in the LSC (36.3%–36.4%) and SSC (32.0%–32.3%) regions (Table 1). All newly sequenced plastomes were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under accession numbers PX933193–PX933204.

Each plastome encoded a total of 133 genes, including 88 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 37 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and eight ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. After removing duplicate genes in the IR regions, 114 unique genes were identified, comprising 80 PCGs, 30 tRNAs, and four rRNAs (Table 2). A total of 18 genes were duplicated in the IR regions, including of seven PCGs, seven tRNAs, and four rRNAs (Table 2). Among the 80 PCGs, nine genes contained a single intron, while three genes (clpP, rps12, and ycf3) harbored two introns (Table 2). Detailed annotation information is provided in Table 2.

Relative synonymous codon usage analysis

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated based on all protein-coding sequences. The total number of codons ranged from 19,462 to 21,235 across the Ajuga plastomes (Table S2). A total of 64 codons were identified, including three stop codons (UAA, UAG, and UGA) and 61 sense codons encoding the 20 standard amino acids (Fig. 2, Table S2). Methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) were each encoded by a single codon, whereas the remaining amino acids were represented by two to six synonymous codons. Leucine (Leu) was the most abundant amino acid, with a total of 46,277 codons, whereas Cysteine (Cys) was the least abundant, represented by only 4701 codons (Table S2). Codon usage patterns were largely consistent across species, with only slight variation. Across all species, leucine (Leu) was the most frequently encoded amino acid, with its codon UUA exhibiting the highest RSCU values ranging from 1.90 to 1.93. In contrast, cysteine (Cys) was among the least frequently encoded amino acids, and UGU was its most preferred codon with RSCU values ranging from 1.53 to 1.56. The codons AUG (Met) and UGG (Trp) showed no usage bias (RSCU = 1.00) in all plastomes. After excluding stop codons, most preferred codons in Ajuga plastomes ended with A or U at the third codon position (RSCU > 1), indicating a strong bias toward A/U-ending codons in Ajuga plastomes. (Fig. 2, Table S2).

Analysis of Long Repeat Sequences (LRSs) and Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs)

Three types of long repeat sequences (LRSs): forward, reverse, and palindromic repeats were detected across the 21 plastomes of Ajuga, yielding a total of 874 repeats (Fig. 3A). The number of repeats per plastome ranged from 35 in A. reptans to 48 in A. campylantha Diels 1. Palindromic and forward repeats were detected in all taxa, with palindromic repeats being more abundant (19–26) than forward repeats (16–21). Reverse repeats were rare and were detected only in A. campylantha 1 and A. pantantha Hand.-Mazz., each of which contained two such repeats (Fig. 3A).

Most repeats ranged from 30 to 50 bp in length, whereas only six repeats of 100 bp were identified, all occurring in the two individuals of A. campylantha (Fig. 3B). Repeats longer than 30 bp were distributed throughout the plastome, with 52.86% located in coding regions (mainly psaB and ycf2), 34.10% in intergenic regions, and 13.04% in intronic regions (Table S3).

A total of 796 SSRs were identified across the 21 plastomes, with the number per species ranging from 34 in A. chamaepitys to 41 in A. bombycina Boiss., A. macrosperma and A. nubigena Diels (Fig. 4A, Table S4). Mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant (60.18%), followed by tetranucleotide repeats (30.15%). Pentonucleotide repeats were rare, with only two detected in total, one in A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus Ging. ex Benth. and one in A. turkestanica (Regel) Briq., together accounting for only 0.25% of all SSRs (Fig. 4A). SSRs were unevenly distributed within the chloroplast genome. Specifically, 692 SSRs were identified in intronic and intergenic regions, representing 86.93% of the total SSRs, whereas the remaining 104 SSRs were located in coding sequence (CDS) regions (Table S4).

After considering cyclic permutations and reverse complements as identical motifs, the SSRs were classified into 14 distinct repeat types (Fig. 4B). A/T mononucleotide repeats dominated (91.65%), whereas C/G motifs were relatively rare (8.35%). All dinucleotide SSRs belonged to the AT/AT type. Tetranucleotide repeats showed the greatest diversity, comprising seven types, of which AAAT/ATTT (32.50%) and AAAG/CTTT (24.58%) were the most common. Trinucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeats were infrequent (Fig. 4B, Table S4).

IR contraction and expansion

Comparative analysis of IR/SC boundaries revealed only minor variation among the 21 Ajuga plastomes, indicating a high degree of structural conservation (Fig. 5). At the LSC/IRb (JLB) boundary, the rps19 gene extended 60–61 bp into the IRb region. The IRb/SSC (JSB) boundary was located between a truncated copy of ycf1 and ndhF, with ndhF extending 22 bp into IRb in A. zakhoensis and up to 46 bp in A. pantantha and A. forrestii. The SSC/IRa (JSA) boundary consistently occurred within ycf1, and the length of the ycf1 fragment in IRa ranged from 1,199 bp in A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus, A. chamaepitys, A. turkestanica, and A. zakhoensis to 1,232 bp in A. forrestii (Fig. 5). The IRa/LSC (JLA) boundary was located between rpl2 and trnH in all plastomes, with the distance to trnH ranging from 3 to 11 bp.

Genomic divergence and nucleotide diversity

Comparative analyses using mVISTA revealed a high level of sequence conservation among the 21 Ajuga plastomes. However, a distinct divergent region between ycf3, trnS-GGA, rps4 and trnT-UGU was detected in A. campylantha 1 (Fig. S3). Overall, IR regions were more conserved than LSC and SSC regions, and non-coding regions exhibited higher variability than coding regions.

A total of 114 gene regions and 130 intergenic spacer (IGS) regions were extracted to estimate nucleotide diversity (Pi). For gene regions, Pi values ranged from 0 to 0.021, with a mean of 0.0026, indicating high sequence conservation. Thirty-five genes showed no variation (Pi = 0), whereas only three genes (rps16, ndhF, and ycf1) exhibited Pi values greater than 0.01 (Fig. 6A, Table S5). Among these, ndhF and ycf1 were relatively long (> 1000 bp), whereas rps16 was short (261 bp). Consequently, ndhF and ycf1 were selected as candidate coding-region markers for further consideration. IGS regions were more variable, with Pi values ranging from 0 to 0.05746 and a mean of 0.007. Sixteen IGS regions showed no variation (Pi = 0), while twenty regions had Pi values ≥ 0.015. When evaluating hypervariable regions as candidate molecular markers, both sequence variation and fragment length should be taken into account, as short regions may not contain sufficient informative sites. Based on the selection criteria of nucleotide diversity ≥ 0.015 and length > 250 bp, ten IGS regions were identified as candidate molecular markers: ccsA-ndhD, ndhF-rpl32, petA-psbJ,, rpl32-trnL-UAG, rps2-rpoC2, trnH-GUG-psbA, trnK-UUU_1-rps16_2, trnP-UGG-psaJ, trnT-UGU-trnL-UAA_1, and ycf15-trnL-CAA. (Fig. 6B. Tables S5).

Phylogenetic analysis based on Hypervariable regions

Most BI and ML trees inferred from individual regions recovered topologies consistent with those obtained from the concatenated 12 VAR and CP datasets, although support values for deep nodes varied among datasets. Overall, the major two-clade pattern was consistently recovered. Nevertheless, several discrepancies were observed. In the trees inferred from the three short regions (ccsA-ndhD, trnH-GUG-psbA, and rps2-rpoC2), all species formed a polytomy (Fig. S7 A, G, F). The ycf15-trnL-CAA and ndhF datasets recovered Ajuga turkestanica and A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus as the first clade, whereas the remaining taxa were unresolved (Fig. S7 L, B). In the rpl32-trnL-UAG tree, the first clade comprised A. bombycina, A. chamaepitys, and A. zakhoensis, whereas the second clade included A. turkestanica, A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus, and members of A. sect. Ajuga (Fig. S7 E). Among the 12 individual-region trees, the combined dataset provided support for most clades, and the ycf1 tree (Fig. S7 K) yielded the highest support values for most nodes. In addition, the concatenated datasets composed of loci selected based on Pi values (3-gene, 2 IGS+1CDS, 4-gene, and 5-gene datasets) all recovered the same topology as the 12 VAR dataset (Fig. S7 N-P).

Discussion

Plastome structure and characteristics

The plastomes of Ajuga are highly conserved in overall structure, gene content, gene order, and orientation, consistent with previous comparative studies in Lamiaceae [39, 43, 69, 70]. All sampled plastomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure of angiosperm plastomes (Fig. 1; Table 1), and no major structural rearrangements were detected, indicating substantial structural stability within the genus. The limited variation in plastome size is likely attributable mainly to insertions and deletions in non-coding regions, a common feature of land plant plastomes [29, 33], and remains within the reported range for Ajugoideae (approximately 145–157 kb; [46, 71]). Likewise, GC content was highly similar among species, whereas the relatively elevated GC content in the IR regions is most likely associated with the retention of GC-rich rRNA genes, as commonly observed in plastid genomes [39, 69, 71].

Codon usage patterns were generally conserved across the 21 Ajuga plastomes. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values showed similar distributions among species, and most preferred codons ended with A or U at the third codon position, reflecting the AT-rich nature of plastid genomes (Fig. 2, Table S3). Approximately 30 codons exhibited RSCU values > 1 in species of Ajuga sect. Chamaepithys, compared with 31 codons in sect. Ajuga. A notable difference in leucine (CTG) codon preference was detected between these two sections, suggesting subtle lineage-specific variation in codon usage bias.

Three types of LRSs (forward, reverse, and palindromic) were detected (Fig. 2A) in Ajuga, whereas complementary repeats were absent, consistent with observations in Schnabelia [71] and Scutellarioideae [36]. Although Large repeat sequences (LRSs) have been proposed to facilitate plastome rearrangements in land plants [72, 73], their functional significance in structurally conserved plastomes remains unclear [36]. In Ajuga, the slightly higher abundance of repeats in coding regions than in non-coding regions appears to be mainly driven by repeat accumulation in ycf2, a gene previously recognized as a hotspot for repeat enrichment in plastomes [74, 75].

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are widely recognized as useful plastid markers for species identification, phylogenetics, and population genetic studies because of their relative conservation and high polymorphism [76–79]. The predominance of A/T-rich SSRs in Ajuga is consistent with patterns reported in other Lamiaceae genera [36, 43], and with previous findings in Ajuga by Shang et al. [26]. Notably, some trinucleotide and pentanucleotide repeats were detected only in species from the Mediterranean and Central Asia, but not in East Asian species, suggesting possible geographic structure in SSR distribution, as also observed in Salvia [45]. In addition, the tetranucleotide repeat ACAT/ATGT were uniquely detected in A. reptans. Collectively, these newly identified SSRs provide useful resources for the developing plastid-based molecular markers in Ajuga.

The IR boundary regions (JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA) were also highly conserved across Ajuga plastomes, with only minor variation in adjacent gene positions and repeat lengths (Fig. 5),. Because expansion and contraction of IRs are important drivers of plastome size variation [80] the observed stability of these junctions suggests that IR dynamics have played only a limited role in plastome evolution in Ajuga. This pattern agrees with previous observations Ajuga [26] and other Lamiaceae lineages [69, 71, 81].

Phylogenetic relationships

In this study, we present the most comprehensively sampled plastid genome phylogeny of the genus Ajuga to date, with results consistently revealing a phylogeny with two clades matching the current sectional classification. Based on four datasets analyzed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI), all analyses strongly supported the monophyly of Ajuga and consistently recovered two major clades corresponding to the traditionally recognized sections, although internal relationships among some species varied among datasets (Fig. 7, Figs. S4–S6).

One of two major lineages of Ajuga comprised five species of Ajuga sect. Chamaepithys Benth. and was recovered with strong support (Fig. 7, Figs. S4–S6). Within this clade, A. chamaecistus subsp. chamaecistus and A. turkestanica formed a well-supported subclade (Fig. 7, Figs. S4–S6). These two taxa, distributed from Iran to Afghanistan, share several diagnostic morphological traits, including entire leaves, a closed corolla tube, a broadly and shallowly 2-lobed upper lip, and a corolla covered with jointed hairs. Based on these characters, Pis’yaukova [17] placed them in the subsection Pseudophleboanthe Pissjauk. The remaining three species of Ajuga sect. Chanaepithys correspond morphologically to subsection Ivae [17], characterized by lobate or 3-partite cauline leaves with narrow or linear lobes and a weakly developed upper corolla lip.

Previous studies based on nrITS sequences suggested that A. bombycina was nested within the A. chamaepitys complex, implying that it might represent a subspecies [23]. In contrast, our plastome-based phylogeny recovered A. bombycina as sister to A. chamaepitys. Importantly, this study represents the most comprehensive plastome-based sampling of A. sect. Chamaepithys to date and provides strong support for its monophyly and sister relationship to sect. Ajuga. Nevertheless, broader sampling remains necessary to further test this relationship.

The second of the two major Ajuga lineages comprised 14 species, predominantly distributed in East Asia, with the exception of the widespread Eurasian species A. reptans. This lineage was further divided into four well-supported subclades (Subclades A–D) (Fig. 7, Figs. S4–S6). In subclade A, the close relationship between A. macrosperma and A. bracteosa was confirmed, consistent with the findings of Shang et al. [26]. However, the morphologically distinct species A. lobata, which belongs to subsection Biflorae, was shown to be their closest sister group. This result further indicates that the subordinate classification system proposed by Wu and Chen [82] needs to be redefined. Subclade B consisted solely of A. reptans, a species notable for its ecological plasticity and widespread horticultural use. Because this species readily hybridizes with other sympatrically occurring Ajuga species, additional sampling and further studies are needed to clarify its phylogenetic position. Subclade C included two morphologically similar species A. decumbens and A. nipponensis. Our results support their recognition as distinct evolutionary lineages, and consisted with the earlier conclusions by Shang et al. [26]. Subclade D comprised seven species exhibiting substantial morphological and geographic diversity, particularly in southwestern China. For example, A. nubigena is morphologically distinctive in having verticillasters bearing only two flowers, whereas the remaining species typically possess six or more flowers per verticillaster. Geographically, A. linearifolia is distributed from central to northeastern China, while A. lupulina exhibits a disjunct distribution spanning northern to southwestern China. The remaining species are largely restricted to mountainous regions of southwestern China, particularly Yunnan and Sichuan [18, 83].

Previous estimates by Shang et al. [26] suggested that Ajuga originated around 28.92 Mya (stem age) and began diversifying around 7.78 Mya (crown age), possibly during the final stages of the rapid uplift of the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau [84]. Our phylogenetic analyses recovered two well-supported lineages with distinct geographic and morphological characteristics: one lineage distributed from the Mediterranean to arid Central Asia, corresponding to A. sect. Chamaepithys, and another lineage centered in East Asia, largely corresponding to A. sect. Ajuga. This pattern suggests that geographic isolation and ecological differentiation may have played important roles in the divergence of Ajuga. Nevertheless, the origin and divergence times of the genus require further evaluation.

Identification of molecular markers for Ajuga

DNA barcoding uses standardized DNA regions to enable the rapid and accurate species identification based on nucleotide variation, and has been widely applied in biodiversity assessment and species identification [85–89]. This is unsurprising since rbcL and matK have been found to provide insufficient power for phylogenetic inference in other groups [90], particularly in taxonomically complex groups [91–93]. In Ajuga, rbcL and matK exhibited low nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.005 and 0.006, respectively), indicating limited discriminatory power for the purposes of DNA barcoding (Fig. 6, Table S5). Hypervariable plastome regions have since been examined for their efficacy as DNA barcodes [36, 94, 95], hence, by jointly considering nucleotide variability and fragment length, we identified 12 highly variable plastome regions (ccsA-ndhD, ndhF, ndhF-rpl32, petA-psbJ,, rpl32-trnL-UAG, rps2-rpoC2, trnH-GUG-psbA, trnK-UUU_1-rps16_2, trnP-UGG-psaJ, trnT-UGU-trnL-UAA_1, ycf1, and ycf15-trnL-CAA) as candidates for Ajuga DNA barcodes (Fig. 6, Table S5). Some of these regions (i.e., ndhF-rpl32, rpl32-trnL-UAG, and ycf15-trnL-CAA) have been widely applied in other genera of the Lamiaceae [43, 69, 71]. Moreover, despite differences in sampling, our results are largely congruent with those of Shang et al. [26], supporting ndhF–rpl32, ndhF, trnH-GUG–psbA, and ycf1 as promising hypervariable loci, that may serve as candidate DNA barcodes for Ajuga in future studies.

The 12 highly variable plastid regions identified in this study showed overall good phylogenetic utility in Ajuga. Phylogenetic analyses based on most single-locus and concatenated datasets recovered largely congruent topologies, indicating that these regions provide useful phylogenetic signal for resolving relationships within the genus. Similar observations have been reported in other angiosperm groups, where plastid hotspot regions were shown to be informative for phylogenetic inference at low taxonomic levels [36, 59].A notable result is that the three of these (i.e.the short regions, ccsA-ndhD, trnH-GUG-psbA, and rps2-rpoC2) produced an unresolved tree, failing to resolve most relationships among Ajuga species (Fig. S7 A, F, G). This pattern suggests that high sequence variability alone does not necessarily guarantee strong phylogenetic performance. Although these regions were identified as highly variable, their short sequence lengths likely limited the total number of phylogenetically informative characters, thereby reducing their power to resolve interspecific relationships. Similar limitations of short plastid regions have been noted in previous studies, particularly in taxa with clades that are shown to have undergone recent radiations [44, 69].Among the single loci, ycf1 performed best, yielding the highest support for most nodes (Fig, S7 K). Thus, we suggest that ycf1 will be an excellent marker for phylogenetic inferences in future studies within Ajuga. By contrast, the Pi-based 3-gene, 4-gene, and 5-gene concatenated datasets greatly improved resolution and support, and all recovered the same topology asa the 12 VAR and CP datasets. This supports that combining multiple informative loci is an effective strategy for reconstructing plastid phylogeny in Ajuga and may provide a practical and more economical alternative to a complete plastome dataset when producing a full-species phylogeny in the future. Nevertheless, the instability of some deeper relationships suggests that plastid data alone may not fully capture the evolutionary history of the genus, and additional evidence from nuclear markers and broader taxon sampling will be required to further resolve relationships within Ajuga.

Conclusions

The 12 new plastomes generated in this study substantially enrich the genomic resources available for Ajuga and provide a solid foundation for future comparative and evolutionary studies. Comparative analyses show that the plastome structure in Ajuga is highly conserved, whereas the 12 hypervariable regions identified in this study exhibit markedly higher levels of sequence variation than most previously used universal plastid markers. These regions therefore represent promising candidates for future phylogenetic, phylogeographic, and species identification studies. Phylogenetic analyses based on plastome data support the monophyly of the traditionally recognized sections, while revealing clear incongruence with the previously proposed subsectional classification [16, 82]. This incongruence indicates that a revision of the subsectional system is warranted. However, because the sampling in this study only represents about 30% of species, a phylogenetic study incorporating a higher number of species, including representatives from Australia and Africa, is critical towards understanding systematics and biogeographic history of Ajuga. Moreover, including a wider taxon sampling using multiple genomic compartments (i.e., nuclear genes) will be essential to improve our perspective on the influence of hybridization or biparental inheritance on the infrageneric systematics and evolutionary history of Ajuga.
