A conductive antibacterial composite film containing chopped fibers and ciprofloxacin loaded PCL particles for wound dressing application
Authors: Naghmeh Nademi, Pouriya Toloee, Mojdeh Mohseni, Mostafa Dahmardehei, Azam Molafilabi, Najmeh Najmoddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesigning multifunctional wound dressings with admirable mechanical virtues, appropriate electrical conductivity, and good antibacterial performance is critical for preventing infection and promoting tissue repair. Herein, a conductive gellan–agar composite film reinforced with chopped PCL–gelatin electrospun fibers containing polyaniline–graphene (PAG) and loaded with ciprofloxacin (Cip)–encapsulated PCL particles, was developed. The highest electrical conductivity (5.6 × 10–5 S.cm–1) was donated to the PCL-gelatin fibers with 1.5 wt.% PAG. The conductive film revealed desirable mechanical characteristics including tensile strength (8.51 ± 0.06 MPa) and elastic modulus (5.92 ± 0.09 MPa) by addition of PCL-gelatin-1.5 wt.% PAG chopped fibers, indicating effective reinforcement of the hydrogel-based matrix. Moreover, the system enabled sustained Cip release, and the release kinetics was well described by a first-order model. The developed film demonstrated antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, showing inhibition zones of 9 ± 3 mm for Staphylococcus aureus and 16 ± 1 mm for Escherichia coli. In addition, the film displayed appropriate swelling and degradation behavior, supporting its suitability as a wound-contact dressing. The developed multifunctional film in this research would be a worthy asset to prevent infection and accelerate wound repair in future studies.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Skin as the body’s largest organ serves as a natural barrier against the external environment (e.g., pathogens) along with regulating body temperature [1]. Once the skin is injured, its healing process comprises lengthy and complex overlapping steps such as hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and wound remodeling [2]. The following criteria have been expected from a wound dressing for use in clinical settings: biocompatibility, having admirable gaseous exchange, keeping a moist environment, antibacterial activity, managing exudate, non-adherence to wound, suitable mechanical properties and promote the skin regeneration [3, 4]. However, traditional passive wound dressings such as bandage, gauze and cotton wool are difficult to meet the demand, hence designing multi-functional wound dressing with good healing properties seems to be vital in modern medical technology [3, 5].

Bacterial infection has a major role in the prohibition of the effective skin healing process and may cause the formation of chronic wounds [6, 7]. Beyond various antibacterial agents, ciprofloxacin (Cip), one of the fluoroquinolone groups of antibiotics, has shown remarkable antimicrobial efficacy in repairing complicated wound infections [8, 9]. Ju et al. fabricated alginate/PVA fibers loaded with Cip and reported that it was beneficial for suppressing bacterial infection such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) [10]. Darabi et al. developed starch-PVA fibrous mat containing Cip antibiotic drug loaded on graphene oxide‑silver nanowire for wound repair [11]. Nanoparticle-mediated encapsulation of Cip holds significant potential for enhancing localized delivery to the injury site, while ensuring a sustained and controlled therapeutic release profile [12, 13].

Besides the above-mentioned features, another vital characteristic of human skin is possessing conductive nature [3]. It has been stated that the electrical stimulation facilitates the wound repair via diverse pathways such as directing dermal angiogenesis, enhancing re-epithelialization, and modulating genes related to wound repair [3, 14, 15]. Moreover, electrical stimulation using conductive materials by recruiting neutrophils and macrophages would promote reducing the bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation [16]. Thus, developing a construct more closely matching the conductive nature of human skin has garnered much scientific interest [17]. Graphene with two dimensional sheet of sp2 possess notable properties including superior electrical and thermal conductivity together with large surface area [18, 19]. However, aggregation of graphene sheets causes reduction of capacitance and its surface area [20]. Addition of conductive polymer such as polyaniline (PANI) to the graphene with the aid of composite formation could improve electrochemical performance with lower possible aggregation of graphene [21, 22].

Biopolymer wound dressing based on hydrogels with peculiar properties including 3D network structure, good water and oxygen permeability, high-water content, ability to absorb exudates, providing moist milieu at the wound site and the capability of sustained release of different bioactive cues such as drug and biological factors has been a focal point of research in the wound dressing field [23, 24]. Gellan gum as a Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved biomaterial and agar are well-known polysaccharides with the advantages of biosafety, biodegradability, hydrophilicity, gel-forming ability, and supportive effects could be good candidates for wound dressing [25, 26]. Nonetheless, the poor mechanical features of hydrogel may jeopardize the structural integrity of the wound dressing. Therefore, it is rationale to strengthen the mechanical competency of hydrogel by choosing an appropriate reinforcement [27]. In this regard, short electrospun fibers could offer the advantages of structural biomimicking and high reinforcing efficacy [28]. Moreover, the homogenous dispersion of short fibers whining the hydrogel would be easier than continuous ones [29]. This homogeneous dispersion enhances load distribution, reduces local stress concentration, and contributes to more isotropic mechanical behavior. In addition, short fibers allow easier processability during solution casting and provide better control over reinforcement content and spatial distribution compared to continuous electrospun layers. Therefore, the incorporation of chopped electrospun fibers represents an effective strategy to strengthen mechanically weak hydrogel matrices while maintaining flexibility and film uniformity [30–32]. Wang et al. fabricated cotton nonwoven dressings functionalized with a gelatin hydrogel coating containing PHBV short nanofibers loaded with herbal compounds; the resulting composite showed improved water retention and mechanical performance, and significantly accelerated diabetic wound closure in vivo [33]. Kim et al. reported that embedding fragmented electrospun PCL fibers within a hyaluronic-acid hydrogel enhanced cell adhesion and mechanical integrity, supporting the concept that fragmented/short fibrous reinforcements can overcome the intrinsic weakness of hydrogel matrices used in soft-tissue and wound-related applications [34]. Jamali et al. developed chitosan hydrogel scaffolds reinforced with surface-modified chopped PCL microfibers, demonstrating improved structural performance of hydrogel/fiber composites, which is directly relevant to designing mechanically robust hydrogel-based wound dressings [35]. Among various synthetic polymers, poly ɛ-caprolactone (PCL) was chosen for electrospinning owing to its biocompatibility, sufficient mechanical strength and chemical stability [36]. To overcome PCL drawbacks such as low biodegradation rate, hydrophobicity, and lack of cell recognition site, gelatin was blended with PCL [9]. Gelatin is a hydrophilic natural polymer derived from partial hydrolysis of collagen, the principal extracellular matrix building protein [37].

In this study, a conductive wound dressing based on gellan-agar containing PCL-gelatin-PAG short fibers and Cip-loaded PCL particles has been developed. At first, PCL-gelatin fibers containing various amount of PAG were electrospun and cut to short fibers using mechanical homogenizer. Then, Cip-loaded PCL particles were synthesized using two-emulsion method and its morphology was assessed using SEM technique. Next, both conductive short fibers and drug loaded PCL particles were incorporated to the gellan-agar film. Although gellan-based materials are frequently used as bulk hydrogels, a film-based architecture was intentionally adopted in this work to overcome the inherent mechanical weakness of soft hydrogels. Film structures provide improved tensile strength, easier handling, better shape retention, and more controlled spatial distribution of reinforcing fibers and drug-loaded particles. These characteristics are particularly important for conductive composite systems, where structural uniformity and stable integration of functional fillers are required [15, 38, 39]. Conductivity, mechanical properties and antibacterial behavior of the fabricated film were assessed. Moreover, the kinetic release of Cip was evaluated by various mathematical models.

Materials and methods

Materials

Polycaprolactone (PCL, (C6H10O2)n, Mw = 80,000 g.mol–1, >99.5%), gellan gum (low acyl), agar (purity >99%,), ammonium persulfate (APS, (NH4)2S2O8), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, NaC12H25SO4) and graphene were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich/USA. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, hydrolyzation degree >98.0%, Mw = 72,000 g.mol–1), gelatin Type A (porcine skin), N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, C3H7NO), acetic acid (glacial, CH3COOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), chloroform (CHCl3), and dichloromethane (DCM, CH2Cl2) were obtained from Merck/Germany.

Synthesis of Cip-loaded PCL particles

To synthesize PCL particles, the double emulsion method was used. A concentration of 3.17% (w/v) PCL and 0.84% (w/v) PVA solutions were prepared in 15 mL DCM and 30 ml distilled water, respectively. The prepared PCL solution was poured to the 15 mL of PVA solution dropwise under magnetic stirring to form a single emulsion. The emulsion was added to the other 15 mL of PVA solution under magnetic stirrer to gain a double emulsion. The final emulsion was homogenized for 6 min at the speed of 22,000 rpm using mechanical homogenizer (T25 digital ULTRA-TURRAX-IKA/ 00372500) and consequently was ultrasonicated (UPS400s, Hielscher, Germany) (power 90, time 20 min). The mixture was centrifuged (SIGMA 3-30KS 07.549662) for 10 min at the speed of 15,000 rpm. Lastly, the obtained powder was washed three times, frozen at –80 °C and freeze-dried for 24 h.

The drug loading procedure was followed by similar steps to the synthesis of PCL particles. For this purpose, 0.0476 g of Cip was dissolved in 1 mL DCM and added to the double-emulsion. It was kept at a constant situation at room temperature for 1 h.

Synthesis of polyaniline graphene (PAG) powder

PAG nanoparticle was synthesized according to the protocol of our previous research [(1, 2)]. A homogenous dispersion of graphene powder was achieved by sonicating it in 100 mL of HCl (1 M) by an ultrasound device set to 80 W for duration of 15 min. Aniline monomer (0.745 g) was added to the obtained dispersion. Thereafter, 5.767 g of SDS was added to the mixture under a nitrogen atmosphere at ambient temperature. In the next step, APS (0.913 g) was added drop-by-drop to the suspension. The reaction was continued until the color changed from milky to bluish tint. Methanol was poured to the mixture, followed by centrifugation for 30 min at 10,000 rpm to stop the reaction. The sediment was rinsed several times with 50 mL of methanol, ethanol, and water, respectively (minimum 3 cycles for each washing step) and dried at 50 °C for 48 h.

Electrospinning and chopping of PCL-gelatin/PAG nanofibers

The electrospinning process of PCL-gelatin nanofibers was accomplished by the protocol of our previous research [40, 41]. PCL solution with a concentration of 10% (w/v) was prepared in chloroform/DMF (10:1) at room temperature by a magnetic stirrer. A pre-dispersed PAG suspension was obtained with various concentrations (0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5 wt.% with respect to the mass of polymers) in chloroform, followed by ultrasonication for 10 min (power: 80 W). Then, PAG suspension was added dropwise to the PCL solution under continuous stirring for 2 h to ensure uniform dispersion and minimize agglomeration. Gelatin solution was made with a concentration of 25% (w/v) in acetic acid of 40% (v/v), separately. Dual electrospinning process with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/h, voltage of 17 kV, tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm, a 21 guage needle and rotating drum speed of 250 rpm at 23 ± 2 °C and 40 ± 5% humidity was set to fabricate PCL-gelatin/ PAG fibrous mat.

The conductive chopped fiber of PCL-gelatin-PAG was prepared using a mechanical homogenizer. For this purpose, nanofibers mat (1 × 1 cm) was immersed in water/acetone solution (75:25) and chopped using a mechanical homogenizer with the speed of 2000 rpm for 10 min by the assistance of shear stress.

Fabrication of conductive film containing Cip-loaded PCL particles and chopped fibers

In total, 3% (w/v) of Gellan and agar were dissolved in distilled water at 90 °C under magnetic stirring for 1 h, separately, followed by mixing with a ratio of 55:45.

For donating conductive feature to the film, PCL-gelatin-PAG chopped fibers were dispersed in the prepared polymeric solutions using ultrasonication (power 90, 1 min). For incorporation of drug to the polymeric film, Cip-loaded PCL particles were dispersed in 0.5 mL of deionized water and then were poured in gellan-agar solution with 1, 2 and 3% (w/v) under gentle magnetic stirring. The mixture was homogenized using a magnetic stirrer for 10 min. Lastly, the conductive drug-loaded film was achieved by solvent-cast technique at room temperature. In this vein, the prepared solutions were cast in Petri dish with an inner diameter of 88 mm and a lid of 92.4 mm. During 48 h the process of drying was complete and final films of gellan and agar were obtained. The resulting films exhibited an average thickness of ~200 μm. Such thickness was selected to balance practical handling/mechanical integrity with mass transport requirements (e. g. drug diffusion and release) [42, 43].

Characterization

Morphology and size analysis

The morphology of PCL particles, Cip-loaded PCL particles, PAG powder, PCL-gelatin-PAG fibers, chopped fibers, and final conductive film were analyzed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) (SIGMA VP-ZEISS Germany) under gold coating before observation at a voltage of 25 kV. The average fiber diameters were estimated by Image-J software.

For Cip-loaded PCL particles size assessment and distribution, dynamic light scattering (DLS, NANO-flex 180°, Particle metrix, Germany) analysis was also performed. Samples were dispersed in phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) with a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, followed by sonication (10 min, power 90) to reduce transient agglomeration. Measurements were performed at 23 °C after an equilibration time of 90 s. Reported particle sizes correspond to the hydrodynamic diameter obtained from the instrument software, and the presented size distributions are number-based.

Chemical properties of PAG powder, PCL, gelatin, and PCL-gelatin- PAG electrospun nanofibers were identified using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectroscopy (Thermo Nicolet avatar, USA) in a range of 4000–500 cm–1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to investigate the crystalline structure of the prepared PAG. XRD patterns were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (PW1730, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) with Cu-kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) at 30 mA and 40 kV. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 0–70°.

Results

Cip-loaded PCL particles

The morphology of PCL and Cip-loaded PCL particles were visualized by SEM analysis (Fig. 1A, B). The SEM images represent the spherical shape of the particles with a smooth surface and minimal aggregation with and without drug. As can be seen in Fig. 1B, the semi-uniform, spherical features of PCL particles were preserved after incorporation of Cip drug.

FESEM images of (A) PCL and B Cip-loaded PCL particles; C DLS analysis of the size distribution of (C) PCL and D Cip-loaded PCL particles

The particle size of PCL and Cip-loaded PCL was found to be 156 ± 5 and 260 ± 7 nm, respectively with a PDI of 1.43 via DLS method (Fig. 1C, D). The higher particle size of Cip loaded PCL particles could be ascribed to the encapsulation of drug within the particles.

PAG characterization

PAG powder was synthesized via oxidative polymerization of aniline in the presence of graphene powder using APS as the oxidant. Briefly, graphene powder was first dispersed in an acidic aqueous medium (e. g. HCl) under sonication to obtain a stable suspension. Aniline monomer was then added and allowed to interact with graphene through π–π interactions, which facilitates nucleation of the polymer on the graphitic surface. Polymerization was initiated by the dropwise addition of APS under cooling conditions, generating aniline radical cations and promoting chain growth. Under acidic conditions, the resulting polyaniline forms predominantly in the doped emeraldine salt state, and the growing PANI chains deposit/coat the graphene sheets, leading to the formation of a conductive PAG nanocomposite. Finally, the product was thoroughly washed to remove residual oxidant, acid, and unreacted monomer, and then dried. The obtained PAG is expected to exhibit enhanced electrical conductivity due to the combination of the doped PANI conductive phase with graphene-enabled electron transport pathways.

The morphological features of PAG nanocomposite was visualized by SEM image (Fig. 2A). The sheet-like morphology of graphene was evident in images, specifically at higher magnification.

A FESEM image, B FT-IR spectrum and C XRD pattern of PAG nanocomposite

For chemical characterization, the FTIR spectrum of PAG nanocomposite was taken (Fig. 2B). N-H stretching was detected at about 3446 cm–1 and deprotonation was observed at 1230 cm–1. Also, the stretching of C-N (benzenics and quinonics rings) was determined at 1500–1600 cm–1. The peak located at 515 cm–1 was ascribed to the SO3- group from SDS. Presence of the mentioned peaks in the FTIR spectrum verifies the successful synthesis of PAG nanocomposites. Furthermore, promotion of quinoid against benzoid rings associated with electron delocalization was stated as a principal mechanism of improved electrical behavior in PAG nanocomposite [49].

The crystalline structure of PAG was assessed by XRD analysis (Fig. 2C). The sharp peak attributed to the graphene nano-sheet is observed at 2θ = 26.5°. The PANI also reveals one peak centered near 25.5° along with other two intensity peaks with their position near 20.4° and 14.96°, related to (200), (020), and (011) semi-crystalline planes of PANI in its emeraldine salt (ES) form, respectively [50].

Continuous and chopped PCL-gelatin-PAG nanofibers

SEM micrographs of PCL-gelatin fibrous mats containing 0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5% (wt.) PAG are shown in Fig. 3A–D. Based on the images, the majority of fibers exhibited smooth, continuous, semi-uniform structure without any beads. Although some micron-scale fiber bundles (≈1–2 µm) are visible in SEM images, these structures correspond to local fiber aggregation or stacking rather than single-fiber morphology. The presence of occasional fiber bundling and rupture can be attributed to local instabilities during electrospinning, such as fluctuations in jet stretching, partial solvent evaporation, and local changes in solution conductivity and viscosity.

FESEM images and fiber size distribution of PCL-gelatin fibers containing A 0, B 0.5, C 1 and D 1.5 wt.% PAG composite; E FTIR spectra of PCL, gelatin and PCL-gelatin/ 1.5 wt.% PAG, F and G FESEM images of chopped PCL-gelatin/1.5 wt.% PAG composite fibers at two different magnifications

With the alteration of PAG loading, the fiber diameter of the electrospun mats had been changed with the amount of 93.5 ± 25, 46.8 ± 28, 72.4 ± 26, and 121.9 ± 34 nm for 0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5% (wt.) PAG, respectively. The variations in fiber diameter can be attributed to the role of different solution parameters such as viscosity, conductivity, and etc. induced by PAG incorporation. The presence of PAG increases the viscosity of the spinning solution which can result in thicker jets and consequently larger fiber diameters [51]. On the other hand, the conductive nature of PAG may enhance charge density during electrospinning, which can increase jet stretching and locally reduce fiber diameter. Therefore, the final fiber size reflects a balance between these opposing factors.

The ATR-FTIR spectra of PCL, gelatin, and PCL-gelatin fibrous mat containing 1.5% (wt.) PAG are shown in Fig. 3E. The FTIR peaks of PCL reveal the C = O stretching at 1730 cm–1, C–O and C–C stretching at 1296 cm–1, asymmetric C–O–C stretching at 1240 cm–1and saturated C-H bond vibration at 2947 cm–1 [34]. The bands of gelatin found at 3400 cm–1, 1645 cm–1, 1540 cm–1, and 1240 cm–1 were associated with the N–H stretching, C = O vibrations, C–N stretching, and N–H, respectively. The appeared peaks in the range of 1500-1600 cm-1 indicate the clues of PAG incorporation in PCL-gelatin nanofibers.

The continuous electrospun conductive PCL-gelatin-PAG nanofibers were segmented under shear stress using a high-speed mechanical homogenizer. The SEM images (Fig. 3F, G) confirm the formation of chopped fibers at two different magnifications. However, some irregular agglomerated domains are still visible. These clusters are mainly attributed to homogenizer-induced fiber entanglement and re-aggregation, which can be further promoted during solvent evaporation/drying (capillary-driven compaction). Therefore, these agglomerates do not indicate a separate phase; rather, they are compacted bundles formed as a consequence of the mechanical chopping and subsequent drying steps.

Mechanical properties of conductive film

It necessitates that the wound dressing has adequate mechanical integrity to provide structural support for a required period of time. Therefore, the mechanical properties of conductive gellan-agar film comprising various amount of PAG including tensile strength (σ) and Young’s modulus (E) are evaluated and the data is reported in Fig. 4A–C.

Stress-strain curve, tensile strength and elastic modulus of gellan-agar film, film with Cip-loaded PCL and drug loaded film containing chopped fibers with 0.5, 1, and 1.5% (wt.) PAG

The gellan-agar film, film with Cip-loaded PCL and drug loaded film containing chopped fibers with 0.5, 1 and 1.5 (wt.%) PAG exhibited the σ value of 11.7 ± 0.1, 3.0 ± 0.3, 8.3 ± 0.1, 8.3 ± 0.1 and 8.5 ± 0.1 MPa, respectively (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the E value of gellan-agar film, film with Cip-loaded PCL and drug loaded film containing chopped fibers with 0.5, 1 and 1.5 (wt.%) PAG was 1.4 ± 0.1, 3.1 ± 0.1, 4.8 ± 0.1, 4.4 ± 0.1 and 5.9 ± 0.1 MPa, respectively (Fig. 4C). The incorporation of Cip-loaded PCL particles into the gellan–agar matrix caused a pronounced reduction in σ and E values compared to the neat film (p value < 0.05). This behavior could be ascribed to the disruption of the continuous polymer network by the dispersed particulate phase. The embedded particles act as structural discontinuities and stress concentration points, which weaken stress transfer across the matrix and facilitate crack initiation under tensile loading. In addition, the presence of drug-loaded particles may reduce intermolecular interactions within the hydrogel network and introduce local heterogeneities or microvoids during film formation, leading to a softer and mechanically less coherent structure.

Although incorporation of Cip-loaded PCL drastically decreased the mechanical virtues of gellan-agar film, addition of PCL-gelatin chopped fibers containing various amount of PAG, may remedy this problem. Such improvement on σ and E values could be ascribed to the high surface to volume ratio of chopped fibers which can improve the interfacial interactions between the reinforcing phase and polymeric film. Therefore, the load transfer would be assisted causing reducing stress concentration at the defect region. The enhancement of E and σ values was evident that the interface between the polymeric film and chopped fibers had been sufficiently strong and was not a defect site. Moreover, presence of PAG with intrinsic high E value in the chopped fibers acts as a reinforcement, thus improving the integrity of gellan-agar film. Enhancement of mechanical properties of matrix by incorporation of short fibers has been also reported by many scholars [30–32].

Drug loading, encapsulation efficiency and drug release

Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency was found to be 41.5 ± 2% and 90.6 ± 4%, respectively. Since the release of Cip from conductive film has a great inhibitory effect on bacterial survival and growth, the quantity of Cip from the PCL particles and conductive film were evaluated in PBS (pH = 7.2) using UV–Vis spectroscopy at different time points (Fig. 5). A burst release of Cip from PCL particles (10.3 ± 0.7%) was evident within 6 h of immersion and then the profile showed ascending trend up to 74.9 ± 2.8% after 552 h. The cumulative drug release from conductive film with 1, 2, and 3% (w/v) Cip-loaded PCL particles had an increment trend as a function of Cip-loaded PCL particles content. The amount of released Cip reached 79.2 ± 3.3%, 86.66 ± 0.5% and 91.65 ± 0.4% values for conductive film with 1, 2, and 3% (w/v) Cip-loaded PCL particles, respectively after 720 h.

Swelling capacity and degradation rate

The swelling ratio of conducive film with 3% (w/v) Cip-loaded PCL particle was assessed in deionized water and the data reported in Table 4. The swelling ratio of such sample increased with prolonged submersion, stabilizing at an equilibrium point after approximately 25 min. This behavior is likely attributed to the gradual diffusion of water between polymeric chains as time progresses. The swelling process eventually attains a balance point as a result of the counteracting elastic forces inherent within the three-dimensional framework [2]. Swelling capacity of conductive film reached the value of 177 ± 3% after 20 min. The high swelling ratio of conductive film could be due to the hydrophilic nature of the film with peculiar structure, enabling it to keep the moisture in the wound site. Moreover, this characteristic facilitates a more efficient release of bioactive agents from the 3D matrix of film.

Discussion

Despite extensive efforts devoted to healing various wound types and the development of diverse wound dressings, effective wound repair using different substrates continues to pose a significant challenge. Conductive structures constitute one class of wound dressings that have successfully demonstrated their efficacy in facilitating the wound healing process. In this study, a film structure containing electrically conductive chopped fibers with ciprofloxacin release capability was designed as a wound dressing.

In this study, ciprofloxacin (Cip) carriers were prepared using the emulsion method. The obtained results revealed that spherical carriers with an approximate size of 157 nm were achieved, and drug loading led to an increase in particle size.

The analyses of PAG particles revealed that the nanosheet morphology of graphene was preserved, and chemical characterization confirmed successful composite synthesis via in-situ polymerization. Azizollahi et al. corroborated the efficacy of this polymerization method for conductive nanocomposite synthesis [56].

To form a biomimetic cellular scaffold mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM), chopped conductive fibers were incorporated into the gellan-agar film [58]. Morphological analyses, size distribution, and chemical characterization confirmed the successful fabrication of chopped PCL-gelatin-PAG fibers. Converting electrospun membranes into short fibers is a well-recognized route to achieve a more homogeneous reinforcement distribution within cast/gelled matrices [30–32]. This consideration is particularly important for hydrogel films, where a pre-formed continuous mat may behave as a distinct “interleaf” and create a sharp interfacial plane that is susceptible to imperfect wetting/void formation and potential delamination under deformation or during swelling–drying cycles [32, 33]. In contrast, dispersing chopped fibers throughout the gellan–agar matrix minimizes a single weak plane and promotes more uniform stress transfer and crack-bridging/stress redistribution within the bulk film, while also allowing straightforward tuning of fiber loading to simultaneously engineer mechanical reinforcement and a more continuous conductive pathway from the PAG-containing fiber fragments [32].

To investigate the effect of PAG particles on fiber conductivity, the four-point probe measurement were conducted. These results demonstrated that conductivity increased with higher loading of PAG, attributed to enhanced electron delocalization at1.5% (wt.) PAG.

Since mechanical properties, particularly tensile strength, represent the most critical characteristic of wound dressings, this property was thoroughly evaluated. In summary, addition of chopped fibers comprising PAG has a positive effect on mechanical and electrical properties of the polymeric film.

Since PCL-gelatin fibers with 1.5 wt.% PAG showed highest electrical conductivity and film containing chopped fibers with 1.5 wt.% PAG revealed desirable mechanical features, the further research was conducted with this composition. No difference (p value > 0.05) was detected between the σ and E values of films containing various amount of PAG. The value for the tensile strength of native skin tissue was reported between 0.8 and 18 MPa in the literature [44]. The developed film in this research displayed strength lying in this interval.

The loading of Cip into the PCL particles and then incorporating them into the conductive film efficiently controlled the release behavior of the drug over a long period of time. Drug release was evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C as a standard and widely used medium for investigating release kinetics of wound dressing systems. This condition provides a stable and reproducible environment to compare the effect of structural parameters on drug release behavior. Controlling the drug release using such platform would provide long-term drug availability at the site of action, maintenance of drug concentrations within optimal levels, reduction in adverse side effects and improved patient compliance. The sustained release of Cip from the conductive film could be ascribed to the fact that Cip needs to be separated from PCL particles and subsequently diffused inside the film toward the surface and lastly from the surface to the surrounding environment. It is worth mentioning that swelling and degradation amount of the film could influence the release behavior of Cip. The release behavior of Cip from conductive film containing 3% (w/v) Cip-loaded PCL particles was analyzed by comparing the experimental release data to five different kinetic models.

Sustained antibiotic release is known to play an important role in preventing early-stage bacterial colonization and biofilm establishment. Thus, the antibacterial activity of the conductive film with varying concentrations of Cip- loaded PCL particles (1, 2, and 3%/w/v) was evaluated against E coli and S. aureus was examined using a disk diffusion assay. As described, by other scholars, graphene-based materials such as PAG could create a direct contact with bacterial cell wall and then inhibit their proliferation. Such effect could be ascribed to their various functional groups, abundance of free electrons, small dimensions, and sharp edges graphene sheets [10].

Incorporation of Cip drug to the conductive film has a synergistic effect on the antibacterial performance against both bacterial stains and it is more pronounce for gram negative E coli. It is evident that the diameter of the inhibition zone has an ascending trend by increasing the amount of Cip drug. Incorporation of Cip-loaded PCL particles into natural-based conductive film leads to providing a suitable platform for sustained drug release for the determined time point which is responsible for the antibacterial activity of the film.

The present study was designed to establish the structure-property-function relationships of the multifunctional gellan–agar platform including mechanics, conductivity, controlled Cip release, and antibacterial performance. The biocompatibility outlook is supported by substantial prior evidence showing that gellan gum hydrogel films developed for wound care can be cytocompatible and can promote cell viability/migration in relevant fibroblast models [58, 59]. However, future studies should focus on comprehensive biological tests to determine the dressing’s clinical utility. To conclude, such conductive film containing drug-loaded PCL particles with skin disinfection capability due to desirable bacterial inhibitory effect could be a promising candidate for wound healing application. Swelling and degradation studies demonstrated that the synthesized film exhibits stability and water adsorption capacity compatible with skin tissue and the wound healing process.

Conclusion

Fabrication of a novel conductive antibacterial gellan-agar film has been demonstrated. At first, ciprofloxacin loaded PCL particles were synthesized via two emulsion method and their spherical shape was confirmed by FESEM image. Next, ployaniline-graphene (PAG) composite was successfully synthesized using oxidative chemical polymerization. After electrospinning of PCL/gelation with various amounts of PAG, the obtained fibers were chopped by applying high shear stress using mechanical homogenizer. The electrical conductivity of PCL-gelatin fibers was enhanced from 3.1 × 10–5 S.cm–1 for 0.5 wt. % PAG to 3.1 × 10–5 S.cm–1 for 1.5 wt. % PAG. Presence of chopped conductive fibers within the film also improved the mechanical features of the construct. The release study exhibited initial burst of ciprofloxacin drug, followed by a sustained release over a prolonged period of time. The antibacterial test was run against gram-negative (E. coli) and gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria with formation of 16 ± 1 and 9 ± 3 mm inhibition zone against E. coli and S. aureus, confirming the pertinent antibacterial features of fabricated conductive film with 3% (w/v) Cip-loaded PCL particles. It is expected that such peculiar conductive film could be a good platform for wound healing application.
