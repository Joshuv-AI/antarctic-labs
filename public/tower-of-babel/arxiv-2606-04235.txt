A Certified Higher-Order Quantum Framework for CSA and Margin-Aware Collateral Optimization
Tao Jin, Stuart Florescu

Abstract
Collateral allocation for uncleared derivatives is a legally constrained, operationally discrete, and economically material optimization problem. Financial institutions must satisfy margin requirements while respecting Credit Support Annex (CSA) eligibility rules, valuation percentages, rounding, minimum transfer amounts, concentration limits, custody conditions, inventory availability, and variation-margin (VM), initial-margin (IM), or independent-amount (IA) side constraints. This extended manuscript develops CR-HO-QAOA , a certified higher-order quantum candidate-generation framework for margin- and CSA-aware collateral allocation. The framework is adapter-first. Official SIMM, proxy SIMM, legacy IA, VM-only, RQV, or hybrid margin sources are normalized upstream into a common MarginRequirement ; the optimizer does not calculate or replace official SIMM. Given the normalized requirement, CSA-derived legal terms, and collateral inventory, the optimization layer builds a bounded active neighborhood of pledge, recall, substitution, batch, and slack actions. These actions define a higher-order binary optimization model whose hyperedges capture interactions that are not naturally pairwise, including concentration pressure, custody batches, substitution tickets, chunky lots, liquidity effects, overshoot, and side-specific VM/IM/IA requirements. The quantum layer maps higher-order hyperedges into a Pauli- Z Z cost Hamiltonian and uses collateral-specific feasible-subspace mixers to preserve one-hot choices, movement budgets, side assignments, and substitution structure. The studied near-term regime is N ∈ { 8 , 12 , 16 } N\in\{8,12,16\} and K ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 } K\in\{2,3,4\} , where N N is the active local neighborhood size and K K is the maximum hyperedge order. Quantum-generated candidates are decoded, repaired when necessary, evaluated under an eight-term production objective, and certified by a deterministic CP-SAT master solver before any recommendation is reported. Synthetic benchmarks show that higher-order, constraint-preserving candidate generation can improve certified sample quality relative to QUBO-style and generic-mixer baselines, while CP-SAT remains the feasibility, objective-consistency, and governance arbiter. The results are synthetic workflow-validation evidence only; they do not establish hardware quantum advantage or production bank savings.

Introduction

Collateral optimization is a natural candidate problem for quantum optimization because it is discrete, constrained, multi-objective, and economically material. In uncleared derivatives, a financial institution must select collateral assets that satisfy a credit support annex (CSA), variation margin (VM), initial margin (IM), eligibility schedules, haircut rules, concentration limits, inventory limits, custody restrictions, and operational movement costs. In SIMM-aware IM workflows, a regulatory margin engine first converts CRIF risk sensitivities into an IM requirement; the collateral optimizer must then satisfy that requirement using eligible and often segregated collateral.

The production bank process is therefore layered. Trades and risk systems produce sensitivities, usually exchanged through the Common Risk Interchange Format (CRIF); a SIMM calculator converts those sensitivities into an IM requirement; CSA or IM CSA terms define the legal collateral rules; the collateral inventory defines what can be posted; and the optimization layer chooses a certified allocation. This separation is central to the framework. The inventory list is not an input to SIMM. The CSA does not itself compute SIMM. The optimizer consumes the resulting required margin amount and legal collateral rules, then solves the downstream collateral allocation problem.

A plain QUBO is not always the natural model for real collateral workflows. Production decisions contain higher-order interactions: three securities can jointly breach a currency cap; two recall/post actions can activate the same triparty instruction; a substitution can be cheap in funding terms but costly after IM segregation; and lot-size or CSA-rounding effects can make small surplus acceptable while shortfall remains prohibited. Reducing all such structure to pairwise penalties can increase auxiliary-variable count, distort penalty calibration, and reduce the probability of sampling useful feasible candidates.

We propose a quantum optimization framework that treats collateral allocation as a
higher-order local search problem
rather than as a monolithic global QUBO. The proposed method,
Constraint-Preserving Higher-Order QAOA
(CR-HO-QAOA), starts from a feasible allocation produced by a deterministic master solver, extracts a compact active neighborhood of collateral actions, constructs a higher-order binary optimization (HOBO/HUBO) Hamiltonian, and applies a constraint-preserving QAOA ansatz to sample local improvements. The sampled candidates are decoded, optionally repaired, and certified by the CP-SAT master problem before any production recommendation is made. To our knowledge, CR-HO-QAOA is among the first higher-order, constraint-preserving QAOA formulations for CSA- and margin-requirement-aware collateral allocation in which official or proxy SIMM/legacy margin outputs are normalized upstream and the quantum layer optimizes certified collateral actions under CSA-derived legal and operational constraints.

The novelty is therefore not that a quantum circuit replaces the margin engine or the deterministic optimizer. The novelty is a domain-specific quantum optimization layer with four design choices:

1.

Higher-order cost Hamiltonian.
Collateral actions are represented as a hypergraph whose edges encode funding, movement, coverage, concentration, segregation, and governance interactions of order up to four.

2.

Constraint-preserving mixers.
Instead of relying on a transverse-field mixer and large penalties, the ansatz uses XY-style and substitution-aware mixers that preserve feasible subspaces such as one-hot alternatives, fixed-cardinality movement budgets, VM/IM side assignment, and recall-post pair structure.

3.

Warm-started local quantum search.
The initial state is concentrated around a CP-SAT feasible allocation or current production allocation, reflecting the operational reality that collateral desks usually seek controlled reallocations, not unconstrained global reshuffling.

4.

Certified hybrid execution.
The quantum layer is used for candidate generation; CP-SAT remains the system of record for feasibility, objective validation, and audit certification.

1.1
Positioning and Innovation in the Collateral Optimization

The contribution of this work is not to replace existing bank risk engines, or collateral workflow platforms. In production, ISDA SIMM remains the appropriate upstream methodology for calculating regulatory initial margin, and banks may continue to rely on approved internal or vendor SIMM engines. The proposed framework instead begins after margin-source normalization: official SIMM outputs, CRIF-driven proxy margins, or legacy margin requirements are converted into a common
MarginRequirement
object. The collateral optimizer then consumes only the normalized required amount, together with CSA-derived legal terms and inventory data.

This distinction is important for bank deployment. A real collateral decision is not determined by the SIMM number alone. The SIMM or legacy margin requirement defines how much collateral must be posted, while the CSA defines what collateral is legally eligible, how it is valued, what haircuts apply, what rounding and minimum transfer rules govern delivery, and whether segregation or rehypothecation restrictions apply. The inventory system then defines what collateral is actually available. Pyligent therefore targets the decision layer that connects these inputs: legal agreement terms, margin requirements, and available collateral inventory.

The main innovation is a CSA- and margin-source-aware collateral optimization framework that treats real collateral allocation as a higher-order constrained decision problem. Unlike generic portfolio optimization, collateral management contains discontinuities and bundle effects that are not naturally pairwise. Examples include settlement-batch costs, concentration limits, current-allocation substitutions, chunky collateral lots, overshoot from rounding, minimum transfer amounts, and IM segregation or non-reuse costs. These interactions motivate a higher-order binary optimization formulation rather than a purely quadratic QUBO representation.

The proposed CR-HO-QAOA layer is designed as a structured candidate-generation mechanism for these higher-order collateral interactions. It is not treated as an autonomous production decision-maker. Quantum-generated candidates are decoded, repaired if necessary, evaluated under the production objective, and certified by a deterministic CP-SAT master solver before any recommendation is reported. This hybrid design reflects the requirements of regulated financial infrastructure: exploratory quantum or quantum-inspired search may propose candidate reallocations, but deterministic certification remains the final arbiter of feasibility, objective consistency, and governance.

This architecture provides three practical advantages. First, it is compatible with bank and vendor SIMM engines because the optimizer does not need to implement official SIMM internally. Second, it supports both modern SIMM/CRIF workflows and legacy pre-SIMM collateral processes through the same normalized margin interface. Third, it produces audit-ready outputs: margin-source provenance, CSA evidence, inventory hashes, objective breakdowns, higher-order Hamiltonian metadata, and CP-SAT certificates. As a result, the framework positions quantum optimization as part of a governed collateral decision pipeline rather than as a black-box replacement for existing bank systems.

In summary, the novelty of this work lies in combining adapter-first margin-source normalization, CSA-aware higher-order collateral modeling, constraint-preserving quantum candidate generation, and deterministic CP-SAT certification. This combination is intended to make quantum optimization relevant to a concrete financial-infrastructure problem: producing auditable, legally grounded, and inventory-aware collateral allocation recommendations under SIMM and legacy margin regimes.

Related Work

2.1
Collateral Optimization and QUBO Baselines

Collateral allocation is classically modeled using integer or mixed-integer programming. The most directly related quantum-finance work is Giron et al.
[

This paper builds on that direction but focuses on the gap between QUBO demonstration and production collateral structure. The proposed algorithm does not merely construct a QUBO; it builds a local higher-order Hamiltonian and uses constraint-preserving quantum dynamics to improve candidate feasibility and diversity.

2.2
QAOA, Alternating Operators, and Feasible Subspaces

QAOA was introduced as a variational algorithm for approximate combinatorial optimization
[
3
]
. The more general quantum alternating operator ansatz extends QAOA by allowing custom phase separators and mixers matched to the problem domain
[
4
]
. This generalization is essential for finance applications where hard constraints cannot be treated as merely soft preferences.

A standard transverse-field mixer,

H_{X}=\sum_{i}X_{i},

(1)

explores the entire Boolean hypercube and therefore frequently leaves the feasible region. In collateral optimization, infeasible states can violate eligibility, inventory, segregation, or cardinality requirements. Penalty terms can suppress such states, but large penalties distort the energy landscape and can make optimization unstable. Constraint-preserving mixers solve this problem by restricting evolution to a feasible subspace. Recent XY-mixer and constrained-portfolio studies motivate this design for financial selection problems with cardinality or budget constraints
[
7
,
8
]
.

2.3
Higher-Order Binary Optimization in Quantum Finance

Many quantum finance examples use QUBO because pairwise Ising Hamiltonians are simple to implement. However, realistic finance can naturally produce higher-order interactions. Uotila et al.
[
5
]
show that portfolio optimization with skewness and kurtosis leads to higher-order terms and a HUBO formulation. Collateral optimization exhibits an analogous phenomenon, not through higher statistical moments, but through legal and operational interactions among groups of collateral actions. A batch instruction, issuer cap, currency cap, or IM segregation threshold can depend on a combination of decisions rather than on any single security.

This paper adopts an adapter-first SIMM boundary. In production, a bank may provide CRIF plus an official SIMM result from an internal engine, a vendor engine, or another approved platform. The quantum-application gap addressed by this paper is therefore precise: prior collateral QUBO work shows feasibility of quantum-ready encoding; CR-HO-QAOA proposes a higher-order, constraint-preserving, warm-started quantum optimization layer suitable for CSA/SIMM and legacy margin workflows without claiming to replace the approved margin engine.

3.1
Bank Process and Adapter-First Margin Boundary

In a bank deployment, collateral optimization occurs after margin calculation. Trades are valued by front-office and risk systems; sensitivities are exported, commonly as CRIF; a SIMM engine or equivalent margin service calculates IM; CSA or IM CSA terms define the legal collateral rules; and the inventory system provides the available collateral pool. Pyligent’s optimization layer begins after the required amount is known. It may consume an official external SIMM result, a bank internal SIMM result, a vendor result, a clearly labeled demo proxy, or a legacy margin calculation. It then normalizes the result into a

Higher-Order Quantum Optimization Model

4.1
Local Collateral Action Variables

The quantum subproblem is constructed over local actions rather than over all inventory. Let

\mathcal{A}=\{1,\ldots,N\}

(11)

be a set of candidate actions extracted from the CP-SAT baseline. An action may post a block, recall a block, substitute one asset for another, activate a custody batch, or select a surplus slack denomination. The binary variable

x_{i}\in\{0,1\}

(12)

indicates whether action
i
is selected.

Near-term quantum execution imposes a bounded active set. In this paper,

N\triangleq|\mathcal{A}|,\qquad K\triangleq\max_{e\in\mathcal{E}}|e|,

(13)

where
N
is the number of active local collateral-action variables embedded in the quantum subproblem and
K
is the maximum hyperedge order. Importantly,
N
is not the total number of collateral assets in the inventory. It is the size of the selected active neighborhood around the current or CP-SAT feasible allocation.

The experimental operating regime is

N\in\{8,12,16\},\qquad K\in\{2,3,4\}.

(14)

These values should be read as a practical ablation grid, not as a universal optimality theorem. The construction can be recursively applied across multiple active neighborhoods.

Quadratizing every interaction into auxiliary variables can be useful for QUBO backends, but it may obscure the financial semantics and enlarge the binary space. CR-HO-QAOA keeps the hypergraph representation as the primary mathematical object.

4.4
Pauli-
Z
Hamiltonian Mapping

Each binary variable is mapped to a qubit by

x_{i}=\frac{1-Z_{i}}{2},

(22)

where
Z_{i}
is the Pauli-
Z
operator on qubit

. A hyperedge term becomes

c_{e}\prod_{i\in e}x_{i}=\frac{c_{e}}{2^{|e|}}\prod_{i\in e}(I-Z_{i})=\frac{c_{e}}{2^{|e|}}\sum_{S\subseteq e}(-1)^{|S|}\prod_{j\in S}Z_{j}.

(23)

The quantum cost Hamiltonian is therefore

\hat{H}_{C}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{c_{e}}{2^{|e|}}\sum_{S\subseteq e}(-1)^{|S|}\prod_{j\in S}Z_{j}.

(24)

For
K\leq 4
, each hyperedge expands into at most
16
Pauli-
Z
strings. This bound keeps the local phase separator executable on simulators and small hardware experiments.

4.5
One-Sided Margin Coverage Slack

A symmetric penalty
(U_{s}(x)-R_{s})^{2}
incorrectly treats collateral surplus and shortfall as equally undesirable. In collateral management, shortfall is unacceptable, while small surplus may be unavoidable because of lot size, CSA rounding, and eligible-inventory discreteness. We therefore encode coverage using nonnegative slack bits.

Let local side coverage be

U_{s}(x)=U_{s}(q^{0})+\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i,s}x_{i},

(25)

where
s\in\{VM,IM\}
. Introduce binary surplus slack variables
z_{s,d}
with denominations
\delta_{d}>0
:

S_{s}(z)=\sum_{d}\delta_{d}z_{s,d}.

(26)

The side-specific coverage penalty is

P_{cov,s}(x,z)=\lambda_{s}\left(R_{s}-U_{s}(x)+S_{s}(z)\right)^{2}.

(27)

When
U_{s}(x)>R_{s}
, the surplus ladder can absorb the excess. When
U_{s}(x)<R_{s}
, nonnegative surplus slack cannot eliminate the shortfall, so a penalty remains. This creates a compact quantum-compatible approximation to a one-sided coverage constraint.

The denominations can be binary powers, CSA rounding increments, or collateral lot increments:

\delta_{d}=2^{d}\delta_{0},\qquad d=0,\ldots,D_{s}.

(28)

5.1
Phase Separator

Given the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
24
), the CR-HO-QAOA phase separator is

U_{C}(\gamma)=\exp(-i\gamma\hat{H}_{C}).

(29)

Because all Pauli-
Z
strings commute, the phase separator can be decomposed into a product of string phases:

U_{C}(\gamma)=\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\prod_{S\subseteq e}\exp\left(-i\gamma\theta_{e,S}\prod_{j\in S}Z_{j}\right),

(30)

where

\theta_{e,S}=\frac{c_{e}}{2^{|e|}}(-1)^{|S|}.

(31)

For a Pauli string
Z_{j_{1}}\cdots Z_{j_{m}}
, the unitary

\exp(-i\alpha Z_{j_{1}}\cdots Z_{j_{m}})

(32)

can be implemented with a parity network: compute parity onto one qubit using CNOT gates, apply a single
R_{Z}(2\alpha)
rotation, and uncompute. For string length
m>1
, this requires
2(m-1)
CNOT gates and one
R_{Z}
rotation before hardware transpilation. For
K\leq 4
, the highest-order native string is at most four-local.

\exp(-i\alpha Z_{a}Z_{b}Z_{c}Z_{d})

However, quadratization is treated as a backend compilation step, not as the primary modeling layer. This preserves the higher-order collateral semantics during formulation and analysis.

5.2
Mixer Families

The mixer Hamiltonian is not a generic transverse-field mixer. It is a sum of collateral-specific feasible-subspace mixers:

\hat{H}_{M}=\hat{H}_{onehot}+\hat{H}_{card}+\hat{H}_{side}+\hat{H}_{sub}+\hat{H}_{batch}.

(33)

The corresponding mixer unitary is

U_{M}(\beta)=\exp(-i\beta\hat{H}_{M}).

(34)

One-hot eligibility mixer.

Suppose a group
G
contains mutually exclusive alternatives, such as block-size choices for one asset or replacement choices for one recalled position. Feasibility requires

\sum_{i\in G}x_{i}=1.

(35)

An XY mixer over the group preserves this Hamming weight:

\hat{H}_{XY}^{G}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{G}}\frac{1}{2}(X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}),

(36)

where
E_{G}
may be a ring, line, or clique topology. A clique gives richer mixing but higher depth; a ring is shallower and more hardware-friendly.

Cardinality movement-budget mixer.

Collateral desks often impose a maximum or target number of operational movements. For a movement group
B
, a fixed-cardinality mixer preserves

\sum_{i\in B}x_{i}=K_{B}.

(37)

The same XY construction in Eq. (
36
) can be used with a Dicke-state or hot-started initialization inside the fixed-Hamming-weight subspace.

Side-preserving VM/IM mixer.

VM and IM collateral usually have different legal and economic treatment. Let
\mathcal{V}_{VM}
and
\mathcal{V}_{IM}
be side-specific action sets. The side mixer is block diagonal:

\hat{H}_{side}=\hat{H}_{XY}^{\mathcal{V}_{VM}}\oplus\hat{H}_{XY}^{\mathcal{V}_{IM}},

(38)

so that VM actions do not mix into IM actions unless the legal state explicitly permits cross-side substitution.

Substitution-pair mixer.

A collateral substitution often has a paired structure: recall an expensive posted block and post a cheaper eligible replacement. For substitution pairs
(r,p)
, define local alternatives that preserve the net substitution logic. The mixer connects alternatives only when the resulting recall-post pair remains inventory- and side-compatible. This avoids sampling states that recall collateral without replacing it or post replacement collateral without releasing the corresponding expensive block.

Batch mixer.

Custody and triparty workflows create batch effects. A batch variable
b_{m}
is activated when one or more actions in batch
m
are selected. The batch mixer toggles feasible alternatives within a batch while respecting the logical activation relation. In near-term implementations, the batch logic may be enforced in the phase Hamiltonian while the mixer operates on the action variables.

5.3
Warm-Started Initial States

Let
x^{0}
be the binary encoding of the current or CP-SAT feasible allocation. For unconstrained variables, a biased product warm start is

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i}\left(\sqrt{1-p_{i}}|0\rangle+\sqrt{p_{i}}|1\rangle\right),

(39)

where
p_{i}
is derived from action desirability, reduced cost, or similarity to
x_{i}^{0}
. For one-hot groups, the initial state is concentrated on the incumbent alternative and its nearest economically plausible substitutions:

|\psi_{0}^{G}\rangle=\sum_{i\in G}\alpha_{i}|e_{i}\rangle,\qquad\sum_{i\in G}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}=1.

(40)

For fixed-cardinality movement budgets, a Dicke state or approximate Dicke state may be used. The practical goal is not to prepare a perfectly uniform feasible state, but to concentrate amplitude on business-relevant reallocations.

5.4
Variational State and Optimization Objective

At depth
p
, the CR-HO-QAOA state is

|\psi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}U_{M}(\beta_{\ell})U_{C}(\gamma_{\ell})|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(41)

The standard objective is energy minimization:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\langle\psi_{p}|\hat{H}_{C}|\psi_{p}\rangle.

(42)

For collateral, a CVaR-style objective over the best sampled tail can be more robust:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}\left(C(x)\right),

(43)

where
\alpha
is the retained quantile of low-cost samples. A multi-objective variant sweeps treasury weights

\omega=(w_{F},w_{M},w_{L},w_{O},w_{C},w_{S},w_{G})

(44)

across funding, movement, liquidity, overshoot, concentration, substitution, and governance costs. This produces a sampled Pareto frontier rather than a single allocation.

7
SIMM-Aware Quantum Objective Design

SIMM enters the quantum optimization problem through requirements, costs, eligibility, and neighborhood selection. It does not enter by allowing the optimizer to alter SIMM sensitivities or margin methodology. A CRIF row may be abstractly represented as

g_{j,r,\tau,b,q},

(48)

where
j
indexes the trade,
r
the SIMM risk class,
\tau
the risk type,
b
the bucket, and
q
the qualifier. The approved margin engine or external SIMM adapter maps the CRIF set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
to
R_{n}^{IM}
. The quantum optimizer then minimizes the cost of satisfying
R_{n}^{IM}
together with any VM or legacy IA requirement.

The design deliberately supports two deployment modes. In bank integration mode,
R_{n}^{IM}
is supplied by an official internal or vendor SIMM engine and is accompanied by metadata such as SIMM version, valuation date, CRIF hash, source system, and approval status. In research mode, a CRIF-driven demo proxy may supply a non-official IM proxy for validating the optimization pipeline. The latter must be labeled as a proxy result and is not used as evidence of official SIMM calculation. In both modes, the downstream quantum and CP-SAT layers consume only the normalized requirement and source metadata.

The IM funding component can be written as

C_{F}^{IM}(x)=\sum_{i}a_{i,IM}\Delta q_{i,IM}(x)\left(r_{i}+\ell_{i}+s_{xccy,i}+s_{seg,i}+s_{nonreuse,i}\right)

(49)

where
s_{seg,i}
captures segregation drag and
s_{nonreuse,i}
captures the opportunity cost of collateral that cannot be reused. This term is side-specific: the same collateral action can have different economics under VM and IM.

A SIMM-aware neighborhood score for action
i
may be defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}={}

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\mathrm{ReducedCost}_{i}+\alpha_{2}\mathrm{IMCost}_{i}+\alpha_{3}\mathrm{SegDrag}_{i}

(50)

\displaystyle+\alpha_{4}\mathrm{BucketRelevance}_{i}+\alpha_{5}\mathrm{CapPressure}_{i}

where
\mathrm{BucketRelevance}_{i}
uses SIMM attribution or desk-level IM decomposition only to prioritize local collateral actions. It does not modify the SIMM calculation itself.

This design gives the quantum layer a meaningful SIMM-specific role: it explores the discrete collateral reallocation space created by IM requirements, segregation economics, and side-specific eligibility. The regulatory margin model remains outside the variational loop.

8
Circuit Resource and Execution Considerations

The proposed quantum layer is intentionally local. For
N\leq 16
active actions and maximum hyperedge order
K\leq 4
, the cost Hamiltonian can be evaluated on statevector simulators, tensor-network simulators, quantum-inspired samplers, or small quantum processors. The relevant resource drivers are:

•

number of active qubits
N
plus slack and auxiliary qubits;

•

number of hyperedges
|\mathcal{E}|
;

•

maximum hyperedge order
K
;

•

Pauli-string count after expansion;

•

mixer topology depth;

•

QAOA depth
p
;

•

shot budget and optimizer iterations.

For a hyperedge of order
m
, Eq. (
23
) produces up to
2^{m}
Pauli strings, though constant terms and duplicate strings can be compressed. A Pauli string of length
m
can be implemented with
2(m-1)
CNOT gates and one
R_{Z}
rotation using a parity-network decomposition. Thus, high-order terms are manageable only when the active neighborhood and hyperedge count are bounded.

The initial experimental regime should use shallow depths,

p\in\{1,2,3\},

(51)

with parameter optimization by COBYLA, SPSA, grid transfer, or learned schedules from smaller instances. For hardware demonstrations, the paper should report transpiled two-qubit gate counts, circuit depth, shot budget, noise model, and backend topology. For quantum-inspired demonstrations, the paper should report sampler type, seed, candidate budget, and exact certification rate.

The intended claim is not near-term quantum advantage over CP-SAT. The intended claim is that a higher-order, constraint-preserving ansatz can produce better certified local candidate distributions than a generic QUBO or transverse-field QAOA under the same active-neighborhood budget.

10
Discussion

The proposed framework is a quantum-finance application for three reasons. First, the core optimization object is a cost Hamiltonian over collateral-action qubits, not a generic API call to a classical optimizer. Second, the higher-order collateral interactions are represented as Pauli-
Z
phase terms that can be implemented through quantum phase separators or compiled into quadratized backends. Third, the mixers are designed from feasible-subspace quantum dynamics rather than from classical penalty-only modeling.

The financial domain is not decorative. It determines the Hamiltonian terms, feasible subspaces, warm start, and certification constraints. The adapter-first margin route tells the algorithm which VM, IM, IA, or legacy RQV requirements must be covered, while preserving the boundary that SIMM calculation occurs upstream. Haircuts determine effective coverage coefficients. Segregation and non-reuse determine IM-side cost coefficients. Custody and ticketing rules generate higher-order movement terms. Concentration limits create hyperedges. This is precisely where a domain-specific quantum optimization contribution can be stronger than a generic QAOA demonstration.

At the same time, the design avoids overclaiming. CP-SAT remains the arbiter for final feasibility. SIMM remains an approved upstream margin engine or external result source rather than a variational subroutine. Quantum search is used where it is most defensible in the NISQ era: as a structured local candidate generator for higher-order, multi-objective, constrained discrete reallocations.

11
Limitations and Future Work

CR-HO-QAOA does not prove quantum advantage. The near-term active neighborhood is deliberately small, and a deterministic solver can still certify or solve many local instances. The relevant near-term question is whether the quantum ansatz improves certified sample quality, feasible-amplitude concentration, and candidate diversity relative to QUBO-QAOA or quantum-inspired baselines under fixed budget.

This work also does not claim to implement or replace an approved ISDA SIMM engine. In production, the preferred architecture is adapter-first: Pyligent consumes official SIMM outputs from a bank, vendor, or approved internal engine and normalizes them into a margin requirement. Demo CRIF-driven margin proxies are useful for software validation and benchmarking, but they must not be interpreted as official or regulatory SIMM results.

Future work has five directions. First, run full benchmark studies across VM-only, SIMM-only, external-official-SIMM, legacy-IA, RQV, Grid-only, and Hybrid instances. Second, compare direct higher-order phase separators against quadratized QUBO circuits under equal hardware depth. Third, test parameter-transfer schedules across collateral neighborhoods with similar legal and economic structure. Fourth, evaluate whether multi-objective QAOA can generate useful collateral Pareto frontiers for treasury desks, especially across funding cost, operational movement, and IM segregation cost. Fifth, integrate with bank or vendor SIMM systems through an auditable external adapter so that official margin requirements and proxy validation cases are clearly separated.

12
Conclusion

This manuscript presented an adapter-first, SIMM-aware collateral optimization framework in which the quantum component is a bounded local candidate generator and CP-SAT remains the deterministic certification layer. The main architectural takeaway is the separation of responsibilities: SIMM or legacy margin requirements are calculated or loaded upstream and normalized into a
MarginRequirement
; CSA terms define legal eligibility, haircuts, rounding, custody, and VM/IM side rules; inventory defines the available collateral universe; and CR-HO-QAOA searches a higher-order local action space whose candidates are decoded, repaired when needed, and certified by CP-SAT before any recommendation is reported.

The algorithmic takeaway is that production collateral allocation contains higher-order structure that is not naturally captured by plain QUBO models. Concentration limits, settlement batches, substitution bundles, chunky lots, overshoot, and segregation costs can couple three or more collateral actions. CR-HO-QAOA models these interactions as higher-order collateral hyperedges, compiles them into a Pauli-
Z
cost Hamiltonian, and uses collateral-specific feasible-subspace mixers to generate structured candidate reallocations.

The numerical results should be read as synthetic benchmark evidence, not as hardware quantum advantage or production bank savings. In the
N
–
K
ablation, increasing the active neighborhood size and interaction order improves certified objective gap across the reported synthetic fixtures, while also increasing circuit-resource requirements. In the method comparison, CR-HO-QAOA achieves the best non-CP-SAT certified objective quality among the sampled and heuristic baselines, while CP-SAT remains the normalized deterministic reference. The production KPI table further illustrates how the same certified workflow can report business-facing metrics such as objective reduction, overshoot, funding cost, certification status, and feasibility recovery in a shadow-mode bank replay.

The overall conclusion is that near-term quantum optimization is most credible in this setting as a governed local-improvement engine, not as an autonomous collateral decision-maker. The proposed architecture provides a practical path for evaluating higher-order quantum candidate generation inside regulated collateral workflows: margin-source provenance is explicit, legal constraints are preserved, and every reported allocation is certified by a deterministic master solver. Future work should replace the synthetic fixtures with historical margin-call replay data, integrate external official SIMM adapters, and test whether the same CR-HO-QAOA design improves certified candidate quality under realistic bank inventory, settlement, and custody constraints.
