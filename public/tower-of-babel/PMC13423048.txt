Scoping review protocol of interventions for widening access to healthcare services in people living with rare diseases in Africa
Authors: Mapaseka Seheri, Dini Mawela, Lerato Kgosana, Chantelle Baker, Wesley van Hougenhouck-Tulleken, Olanrewaju Oladimeji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPeople living with rare diseases in Africa face substantial barriers to accessing healthcare services, including delayed diagnoses, limited treatment options and fragmented care systems. The main objective of the scoping review is to explore and map existing interventions designed to improve access to healthcare services for individuals living with rare diseases across the African continent.MethodsDesign: The scoping review will be conducted using the Arksey and O’Malley framework, further refined by the enhancements proposed by Levac and colleagues. The process will follow six key stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting eligible studies, (4) charting the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results, and (6) stakeholder consultation. Sources: A comprehensive search strategy will be implemented across several electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. Additionally, grey literature sources such as conference abstracts and institutional reports will be included. Eligibility criteria: Studies will be eligible if they were conducted in Africa, published between 2004 and 2024 and focused on interventions aimed at improving access to healthcare for people living with rare diseases. Data extraction and synthesis: Two independent reviewers will screen all titles, abstracts and full texts, with disagreements resolved through discussion or a third reviewer. Data will be extracted using a standardised extraction form.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol has obtained waiver from the ethics committee of Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University (SMUREC/M/365/2025). The findings will provide valuable insights for physicians, laboratory scientists, researchers and policymakers, highlighting pragmatic and cost-effective strategies to enhance healthcare access for individuals with rare diseases in Africa. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, policy briefs, conference presentations and stakeholder engagement activities.Keywords: Rare Diseases, Health Equity, PUBLIC HEALTH, Africa South of the Sahara

Background

Rare diseases represent a vast and heterogeneous group of over 7000 medical conditions, each affecting a relatively small number of individuals.1 However, their collective burden is considerable, impacting an estimated 400 million people globally.2 Most rare diseases are caused by genetic disorders, tend to be chronic and often progress over time. Many begin in childhood and can lead to serious health complications, lifelong disability or early death.3

Due to their low prevalence, people living with rare diseases (PLWRDs) frequently experience a lack of public awareness, limited clinical expertise and insufficient research investment. These gaps contribute to major challenges in diagnosis and treatment.4 Consequently, PLWRDs are particularly vulnerable to systemic health inequities, including prolonged diagnostic journeys, restricted access to appropriate therapies, and fragmented or even absent care coordination.5

In Africa, these challenges are exacerbated by broader health system limitations. Many countries on the continent face severe shortages of trained healthcare professionals, under-resourced health facilities, weak health information systems, and limited access to diagnostic tools and specialised services.6 These systemic weaknesses significantly hinder the ability to detect, diagnose and manage rare diseases effectively. Furthermore, the absence of national rare disease policies, coupled with low awareness among healthcare providers, caregivers and the general public, further compounds the barriers faced by PLWRDs.

Africa’s under-representation in global genomic and clinical research reinforces a cycle of neglect. Most rare disease diagnostics and clinical guidelines are developed based on studies from high-income countries and may not be directly applicable to African populations due to differences in genetic diversity, environmental exposures and healthcare infrastructure.7 8 As a result, many rare diseases in African populations remain undiagnosed, misdiagnosed or poorly understood.

Sociocultural factors such as stigma, traditional health beliefs and limited health literacy also discourage affected individuals from seeking care or adhering to medical advice, thereby widening health disparities.9 Nonetheless, several innovative and context-specific initiatives have emerged to address these gaps. Promising approaches include community-based healthcare delivery models, task-shifting strategies, mobile health (mHealth) platforms, genetic counselling services and whole genome sequencing research.10 One notable example is the Human Heredity and Health in Africa (H3Africa) consortium, which aims to strengthen Africa’s capacity for genomic research by identifying region-specific genetic variants and building local research infrastructure.7 Additionally, some countries have begun to recognise rare diseases as a public health priority, though implementation of supportive policies remains inconsistent.

Despite encouraging progress, there is still limited understanding of the types, coverage and impact of interventions aimed at improving healthcare access for PLWRDs in Africa. Many of these efforts are fragmented, poorly documented or not evaluated for long-term sustainability.11 This lack of consolidated evidence hinders effective policymaking, funding allocation and the development of clinical pathways. As such, there is an urgent need for a systematic scoping review to identify and synthesise the available literature on interventions designed to improve healthcare access for individuals living with rare diseases in African settings.12

This article presents a scoping review protocol that seeks to identify pragmatic and cost-effective interventions aimed at improving access to healthcare services for PLWRDs in Africa. The findings from this review will offer valuable insights for healthcare practitioners, researchers, policymakers and other stakeholders, ultimately contributing to the broader goal of advancing health equity across the continent.

Methods and analysis

Protocol design

The methodology for this scoping review is based on the framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley13 and further refined by Levac et al.14 The review is planned to commence in March 2026 and is expected to be completed by December 2026 and will be conducted in six stages, as illustrated in figure 1. Our approach will also integrate methodological guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute.15 This protocol is not registered with PROSPERO, as the platform currently does not accept registrations for scoping reviews. In developing the protocol, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines16 (online supplemental file 1). The presentation of the results will be guided by the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)17 (see figure 2). Additionally, we reviewed and compared previously published scoping review protocols to ensure methodological consistency and incorporated further expectations outlined by Methods and Protocols for protocol submissions.18 19

Stage 1: identifying the research question

We developed the research question using the technique proposed by Arksey and O'Malley,13 and further refined by Levac et al.14 The main research question guiding this review is: “Have interventions improved access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases in Africa?”

Two sub-questions were formulated to support this objective:

What are the challenges associated with access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases?

What are the challenges associated with access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases?

What interventions have been implemented to improve access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases in Africa?

What interventions have been implemented to improve access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases in Africa?

To ensure alignment between the research question and study selection, this review will adopt the Population, Concept and Context (PCC) framework (table 1). In this framework, the population includes people living with rare diseases in Africa; the concept focuses on interventions aimed at improving healthcare access; and the context is limited to African settings. As this is a scoping review, no comparison group is included. The outcomes of interest are the challenges encountered and the strategies employed to improve healthcare access. The review will consider literature published between 2004 and 2024, selected to capture two decades of evidence during which rare diseases received increasing global policy attention, alongside advances in genomics initiatives relevant to African settings.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted using three major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. These databases have been selected due to their extensive coverage of biomedical, public health and global health research, ensuring a robust and diverse collection of relevant studies.18 19 In addition, we will search grey literature sources, including Google Scholar, OpenGrey and the WHO Global Health Library, to capture unpublished or non-indexed material that may provide valuable insights. To identify potentially relevant grey literature, we will also conduct targeted searches in databases for dissertations and theses (eg, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global) and conference proceedings (eg, EMBASE Conference Abstracts, Conference Proceedings Citation Index – Science and Social Science & Humanities).18 19

To minimise the risk of missing relevant studies, the search strategy will include umbrella terms, keywords and subject headings (eg, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms) related to interventions, improved healthcare access, rare diseases and Africa. Keywords such as scoping review, interventions, improved access, healthcare services, people living with rare diseases and Africa will be used in the titles, abstracts and indexed subject terms of retrieved articles.18 19 Additionally, we will hand-search the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles to identify any other eligible studies. Reputable organisational websites, such as that of the WHO, will also be consulted for additional grey literature and reports.18 19

Table 2 provides a detailed outline of the search strategy. The search terms will be finalised collaboratively by the lead researcher, members of the research team and an academic librarian. All retrieved records will be imported into EndNote reference management software for efficient tracking, de-duplication and organisation. The final reference list will be incorporated into the review report.18 19

It is anticipated that the selection of studies for the review will be completed within 8 weeks, and this process will adhere to the guidelines provided by the PRISMA-ScR checklist.17 Additionally, the selection process will be mapped out using the PRISMA-P chart.16 Due to resource constraints, this review will be limited to studies published in English. This may result in language bias, particularly given the multilingual research landscape in Africa.

Types of studies

We will consider experimental (randomised or non-randomised), observational studies (longitudinal, cross-sectional) and mixed-methods.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The data will be analysed using appropriate methods for each type of study included in the scoping review. Stakeholder consultation data will be analysed using thematic analysis. Findings from the consultations will be integrated with the scoping review results during the interpretation phase to contextualise evidence, validate emerging themes and identify practical implications and research gaps. Stakeholder data will not be treated as standalone empirical findings.18 19

For qualitative studies, we will employ thematic content analysis to identify themes related to how pragmatic and cost-effective interventions could improve access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases in Africa. These will be any notable quotes or narratives from participants that shed light on their experiences, perceptions or attitudes towards the interventions to improve access to care.18 19 This process will involve the following steps: Two trained members of our research team will independently code the data. To enhance the reliability and validity of our analysis, the data will be independently dual-coded. Any discrepancies in the coding process will be resolved through discussion and consensus, involving a third researcher if necessary. Themes will be developed using an inductive approach, where themes will emerge organically from the data. This will allow for a rich, grounded understanding of the research topic. While our approach is primarily inductive, the overarching PCC framework (table 1) will provide a broad conceptual guide for our theme development. We will extract information as stated in the data charting form. We will present our findings as narrative summaries alongside tables, graphs and diagrams to visually represent key results as shown in figure 2.

Stage 6: consultation

A diverse group of stakeholders including physicians, laboratory scientists, researchers, policymakers and representatives from affected population will be engaged to validate and interpret the findings of this scoping review.18 19 Particular attention will be given to frontline healthcare workers who provide care for people living with rare diseases, such as pharmacists, professional nurses, geneticists/genetic counsellors and individuals with lived experience of rare disease. Although stakeholders were not directly involved in the design of this review, we are actively developing mechanisms to incorporate their perspectives during the interpretation phase. For example, we plan to host stakeholder meetings or workshops to present our preliminary findings and facilitate open dialogue and feedback. Additionally, we aim to conduct individual consultations, either in-person or virtually, depending on stakeholder location and preference.

To reach these participants, we will leverage our existing networks and partnerships within the public health sector. We are committed to making the consultation process inclusive and respectful, recognising the unique expertise each stakeholder contributes. Their insights will be instrumental in identifying research gaps, contextualising findings and shaping practical and policy-relevant recommendations for future research.

There is no fixed sample size for stakeholder engagement. Participants will be purposively selected based on their relevance to the study objectives, and recruitment will continue until thematic saturation is reached. Saturation will be deemed reached when two consecutive interviews or stakeholder engagements within the same stakeholder category do not produce new codes or themes.

Stakeholder consultations will be conducted using a semi-structured interview format. A consultation guide will be used to ensure consistency across participants. Key topic areas will include: (1) perceived barriers to healthcare access for people living with rare diseases; (2) experiences with existing interventions or service delivery models; (3) feasibility and sustainability of identified interventions in African health system contexts; (4) equity, acceptability and implementation considerations; and (5) perceived research and policy gaps.

Consultation will be conducted after written informed consent, discussions will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. All transcripts will be anonymised by removing names, institutions and any potentially identifying information. Each participant will be assigned a unique study code, and data will be stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team.

Discussion

The findings of this scoping review will provide valuable insights into the breadth and nature of interventions aimed at improving access to healthcare services for people living with rare diseases (PLWRDs) in Africa. While each rare disease affects only a small number of individuals, their collective burden is immensely impacting millions across the continent. Many PLWRDs face prolonged diagnostic delays, limited availability of treatments and fragmented care pathways. These individual challenges are compounded by broader systemic issues in African healthcare systems, such as underdeveloped infrastructure, shortages of trained healthcare professionals, inadequate health information systems and a lack of coordinated policy responses.

By mapping and synthesising existing interventions, this review will document how different African countries are addressing the healthcare needs of this vulnerable population. It will examine the geographic distribution, thematic focus and practical implementation of various strategies, ranging from mHealth technologies and community-based care models to genomic initiatives like the H3Africa programme. In doing so, the review will not only surface innovative and potentially scalable models but also highlight enduring barriers such as limited funding, low public awareness and policy fragmentation. This synthesis will be critical for clarifying what is known, identifying gaps in the current evidence base and prioritising areas for future research and investment.

Moreover, the review emphasises the importance of centering African experiences and local realities in the global rare disease dialogue. Much of the existing literature on rare diseases is informed by research conducted in high-income countries, which may not be directly transferable to African settings due to differences in genetic diversity, health infrastructure and socio-economic conditions. By focusing on interventions within the African context, this review aims to elevate locally relevant evidence and contribute to the design of context-sensitive, equity-driven health systems that are responsive to the needs of PLWRDs.

Ultimately, this scoping review seeks to serve as a foundational resource for healthcare practitioners, researchers, policy actors and advocates working to improve rare disease care in Africa. The evidence generated will inform strategic decision-making and support the development of practical, cost-effective and culturally appropriate interventions. In doing so, the review contributes to advancing the health and well-being of PLWRDs and broader efforts aimed at strengthening health equity, universal health coverage and inclusive policy development across the continent.

Ethics and dissemination

The protocol has obtained waiver from the ethics committee of Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University (SMUREC/M/365/2025). Stakeholder participation will be voluntary and participants may withdraw at any time without consequences. No vulnerable populations will be directly recruited for consultation and no financial remuneration will be provided. Confidentiality and data protection will be maintained throughout the study.

We acknowledge that ensuring our research reaches those it affects most is a critical step in conducting ethically sound and impactful research. In addition to publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and presentations at academic conferences and webinars, we intend to take multiple approaches to ensure our research findings reach the target communities and stakeholders. Our dissemination strategies will be customised based on the needs and characteristics of the target communities. For instance, we plan to organise community meetings or workshops in collaboration with health planners at the local health departments and community-based organisations, where we can present our findings in an understandable and accessible manner.
