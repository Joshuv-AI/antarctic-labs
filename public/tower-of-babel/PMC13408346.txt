Integrating microbial cell factory with new-to-nature photobiocatalysis for de novo biosynthesis of d-homotryptophan
Authors: Zhiqi Guo, Gengrong Gao, Guangming Xiang, Xiaoxi Yuan, Jinhai Yu, Bin Chen, Xiaoqiang Huang, Hongzhong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial cell factory is a powerful biological tool for synthesizing value-added molecules due to its unmatched sustainability and selectivity. However, the inherent catalytic specificity of natural enzymes limits product diversity. Although photobiocatalysis has expanded enzyme catalytic capabilities, challenges such as laborious enzyme purification, incompatibility with in vivo conditions, and complex unnatural substrate synthesis hinder photobiomanufacturing efforts. Herein, we combine a microbial cell factory with a new-to-nature photobiocatalytic transformation via a modular design that combines aerobic fermentation (Module I) and anaerobic photocatalysis (Module II) to achieve de novo biosynthesis of d-homotryptophan. In Module I, we engineer Escherichia coli equipped with a biosynthetic gene cluster to produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) via modifying metabolic flux and multi-copy genetic amplification strategies. In Module II, we develop an efficient synergistic photoredox/enzymatic synthesis of d-homotryptophan from l-serine and IAA by a pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent tryptophan synthase beta-subunit (TrpB) variant. This work synergistically merges emerging photobiocatalytic reactivity with natural biosynthesis, demonstrating a platform with potential for future biomanufacturing of non-natural products.

Introduction

Microbial cell factories (MCFs) have gained significant attention owing to their capabilities in enhancing natural metabolite production or enabling the synthesis of value-added unnatural compounds in an economic, green, and sustainable fashion (Fig. 1a)1,2. For instance, production of non-canonical amino acids (ncAAs) via microbial cell factories represents an attractive route with great industrial potential3, as ncAAs are increasingly used in drug development, biomaterials synthesis, enzymatic active-site remodeling, and post-translational protein modification4–6. Previous research has successfully developed photosynthetic biohybrid systems (PBS) by integrating photocatalytic materials with biocatalysis. These systems leverage cellular metabolism to drive the selective, efficient, and sustainable conversion of solar energy into chemical products7–10. However, current MCFs approaches mainly rely on naturally occurring enzymes and existing biochemical pathways, limiting the structural diversity of the compounds produced compared to chemical catalysis. Unnatural photobiocatalysis has offered an innovative paradigm in expanding enzyme reactivity and C–C bond formation to mediate critical biochemical transformations and stereoselective functionalization by synergistically integrating the diverse reaction patterns of photocatalysis with the inherent advantages of enzymes (Fig. 1a)11–33.Fig. 1Integrating microbial cell factory with unnatural photobiocatalysis.a Combining MCFs and photobiocatalysis facilitates asymmetric synthesis for non-natural products. b Milestones in integrating MCFs with repurposed metalloenzymes35,36. c Modular design of light-driven MCF. l-Trp, l-tryptophan.

Recent progress that integrates repurposed metalloenzymes into microbial metabolic networks has opened an elegant avenue for diversifying the spectrum of biosynthetically accessible molecules34. In 2021, Keasling, Hartwig, and co-workers integrated the limonene metabolic pathway with artificial enzymes, demonstrating that engineered E. coli utilized extracellular ethyl diazoacetate to achieve intracellular Ir-CYP119-mediated limonene cyclopropanation at a titer of 0.25 mg/L35. More recently, they engineered heme-dependent carbene transferases in vivo. Through engineered genetic clusters in Streptomyces albus, the modified strain endogenously synthesized α-diazo azaserine as a carbene precursor, enabling cyclopropanation of intracellular styrene to yield non-natural cyclopropyl amino acids (0.22 mg/L of the cyclopropane product) (Fig. 1b)36. The present study establishes that integrating photobiocatalysis with MCFs can effectively broaden the scope of production.

Despite a few examples of integrating photoenzymatic decarboxylation based on natural FAP with MCFs37–40, the integration of unnatural photobio reactivity and MCFs has been little explored due to several persistent challenges. First, the heterologous-expressed repurposed photoenzymes and modulation of the endogenous metabolic network exacerbate the metabolic burden, thereby impairing cell viability and enzyme activity41. Secondly, photoredox/enzymatic systems typically require anaerobic conditions for non-natural reactions because O2 disrupts radical-mediated catalytic processes, presenting fundamental integration challenges with aerobic microbial cell factories. Notably, during the revision of this manuscript, an elegant study by the Zhao group demonstrated the integration of light-driven flavin-based unnatural biocatalysis into the cellular metabolism of E. coli42.

Herein, we develop an unnatural photobiocatalytic radical C–C formation reaction and demonstrate its integration with microbial systems, establishing a light-driven E. coli to achieve the de novo biosynthesis of ncAAs (Fig. 1c). As a proof of concept, this study reshapes the PLP-dependent tryptophan synthase beta-subunit (TrpB) for the photobiocatalytic synthesis of d-homotryptophan from l-serine and IAA. To mitigate the metabolic burden imposed by heterologous gene expression, a modular engineering strategy partitions biosynthetic pathways across distinct functional units: E. coli I harbours substrate indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) synthesis genes, producing reaction precursors via oxygen-dependent fermentation. E. coli II expresses TrpB, enabling synthesis of d-homotryptophan under anaerobic conditions (299.75 mg/L of d-homotryptophan). This workflow shows the feasibility of a light-driven microbial cell factory, which bypasses enzyme purification, extracellular inactivation, and complex substrate chemical synthesis.

Developing an unnatural photobiocatalysis to synthesize d-homotryptophan

To establish the photobiocatalytic reactivity, we targeted repurposing PLP-dependent TrpB. In nature, TrpB catalyzes the synthesis of tryptophan from indole and l-serine via a two-electron pathway44. More recently, elegant studies from Yang and Hyster, respectively, demonstrated that dual photoredox with PLP-dependent biocatalysis enables highly efficient stereoselective synthesis of ncAAs via single electron radical mechanism29–33. In our design (Fig. 2a), the key electrophilic aminoacrylate Int. B, which forms via the native sequential amino-exchange/dehydration of l-serine with PLP cofactor within the active site, is expected to trap the concurrently photogenerated nucleophilic radical Int. C. Following electron transfer/proton transfers of the resultant aza-allyl radical intermediate Int. D generates the external aldimine intermediate Int. E, which upon hydrolysis affords the target unnatural amino acid 3. The proposed mechanism is supported by complementary experimental evidence. Introduction of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMPO) as a radical scavenger fully suppressed product formation; concomitant detection of a TEMPO adduct by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) confirmed the generation of radical Int. C (Supplementary Fig. 1). UV–vis spectroscopy revealed that the PLP-dependent enzyme (λmax = 392 nm) was unaffected by indole-3-acetic acid but exhibited a distinct red-shift to 404 nm upon l-serine addition, consistent with substrate binding to the PLP cofactor (Supplementary Fig. 2). Together, these data align with prior reports and substantiate the mechanistic pathway depicted in Fig. 2a.

To validate the proposed photobiocatalysis, a library of PLP-dependent enzymes was screened at 1 mol% loading under 440–450 nm blue LED irradiation (Fig. 2b, entries 1–5). Under the optimal condition, the PfTrpB7E6 that was originally constructed by Arnold, Buller and coworkers afforded the unnatural amino acid d-homotryptophan (3) in 66% isolated yield with a 96:4 enantiomeric ratio (e.r., d/l). To gain structural insight into the reactivity differences among the screened TrpB enzymes from different species, we predicted their structures in complex with Int. B. Structural alignment revealed that the five enzymes share highly similar overall folds (Supplementary Fig. 3 and 4), with only three residues differing within a 6 Å radius around Int. B (Supplementary Table 3). Representative enzymes AfTrpB and PfTrpB7E6 were selected for further comparison. Molecular dynamics simulations combined with CAVER analysis showed that the narrowest region of the substrate-access tunnel in PfTrpB7E6 measures 4.2 Å, whereas that in AfTrpB is only 2.4 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5)45. The replacement of A161 in PfTrpB7E6 with L173 in AfTrpB significantly increased steric hindrance in the substrate-access pathway, thereby accounting for the superior reactivity of PfTrpB7E6 and the poor performance of AfTrpB. Screening of photocatalysts identified RhB (10 mol%) as optimal, while 4CZIPN (5 mol%), acridine (5 mol%) and Ir (2 mol%) photocatalysts had no promoting effect on the reaction. Optimization of reaction conditions revealed that substrate concentration, buffer pH, and light wavelength significantly influenced catalytic yield. Notably, pH exerted a significant effect on the enantioselectivity of 3a (Supplementary Tables 4–8). Control experiments established the essential roles of light irradiation, the PfTrpB7E6, and the photoredox catalyst in enabling the transformation. This reaction can be run on a 0.1-mmol scale and provided 3 in 66% without a decrease in stereoselectivity (Supplementary Table 9). To assess the practical catalyst economy, the reaction was evaluated at increased substrate loadings (1–5 equivalents) while maintaining a constant enzyme concentration (0.04 mM) (Supplementary Table 10). The total turnover number (TON) increased markedly from 66 to 107. Subsequent evaluation of the substrate scope showed IAA bearing 2-methyl-, 6-fluoro-, or 6-chloro-substituents were efficiently converted to the corresponding products (4–6), with e.r. of 98:2, 83:17, and 84:16, respectively. Meanwhile, substitution of l-serine with l-threonine enabled the reaction with both 2-(6-fluoro-1H-indol-3-yl)acetic acid and 6-chloroindole-3-acetic acid, providing the desired products in 21% and 29% yield, respectively. The enantiomeric ratios (e.r.) were 86:14 and 89:11, with both products exhibiting a diastereomeric ratio (d.r.) of 19:1 (Fig. 2c). The lower e.r. is largely attributable to the increased steric hindrance introduced by the halogen substituents on the radical precursor, which impedes optimal binding within the enzyme’s catalytic pocket. Therefore, targeted directed evolution of these specific substrates represents a promising strategy to enhance enantioselectivity.

Improvement of intermediate tryptophan synthesis through modifying metabolic flux

To enhance l-tryptophan (l-Trp) precursor supply for IAA biosynthesis, we engineered E. coli’s aromatic amino acid metabolism (Fig. 3a): (1) Deletion of pheA and tyrA redirected chorismate flux towards l-Trp46; (2) Eliminating allosteric control of l-Trp synthase boosted activity47; (3) Knocking out tnaA prevented l-Trp catabolism; and (4) Overexpressing trpEDCBA increased pathway flux46–48. Phenotypic analysis revealed that deletion of tyrA caused a severe growth defect, reducing the specific growth rate by 70.78% (Fig. 3b), revealing tyrosine auxotrophy. To address the critical bottleneck of TrpE feedback inhibition by the end product l-tryptophan, the TrpES40F mutation was introduced to abrogate allosteric regulation48. Constitutive co-expression of the trpCBA and trpED gene clusters under the strong promoter PJ23101 in engineered strain MGHT-9 elevated tryptophan production to 153.13 mg/L (Fig. 3c). Notably, the introduction of the TrpES40F mutant (strain MGHT-10) only marginally increased titer to 153.42 mg/L, suggesting that feedback inhibition relief contributes minimally to metabolic flux enhancement under low-biomass conditions. Given the biomass decline caused by the tyrA knockout, we implemented dynamic metabolic regulation: conditional complementation of tyrA during the stationary phase using the growth-responsive promoter Pflic49. This strategy restored biomass by 2–4-fold in engineered strains MGHT-7/8/9/10 (Fig. 3b), while simultaneously enabling tryptophan titers to exceed 500 mg/L (Fig. 3c). Among all optimized strains, MGHT-10-1 achieved a final tryptophan titer of 506.72 mg/L in batch fermentation, representing a 2.3-fold improvement over the non-complemented strain (MGHT-10). Although the tryptophan production of strain MGHT-10-1 did not reach the current reported levels50, our strain does not contain plasmids and is therefore more stable.

Enhancement of precursor indole-3-acetic acid through heterologous expression of the IAM pathway and the strategy of multiple-copy gene genetic amplification strategy

This study systematically reconstructed the IAM-dependent pathway for IAA production in the l-tryptophan-hyperproducing strain MGHT-10-1 through two enzymatic steps: (1) conversion of tryptophan to IAM via Pseudomonas savastanoi-derived tryptophan-2-monooxygenase (Iaam, encoded by iaaM)51, followed by (2) hydrolysis of IAM to IAA catalyzed by either Arabidopsis thaliana amidase 1 (Ami1, encoded by ami1) or by indoleacetamide hydrolases, such as IaaH (encoded by iaaH from Actinomadura graeca) or Tms2 (encoded by tms2 from Agrobacterium fabrum) (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b–d, Comparative analysis of five engineered strains (MGHT-11 to MGHT-15) revealed significant performance variations: strain MGHT-11, co-expressing iaaM and ami1 under the constitutive strong promoter PJ23101, accumulated 485.94 mg/L IAM but only 21.30 mg/L IAA with severe growth inhibition. Neither dual-promoter systems (MGHT-12) nor fusion expression strategies (MGHT-13) alleviated the IAA production bottleneck, while heterologous expression of iaaM and iaaH under PJ23101 yielded negligible IAA (2.86 mg/L). Notably, strain MGHT-15 expressing tms2 achieved a 6.5-fold improvement in IAA titer (158.99 mg/L) compared to MGHT-11, though residual IAM (372.15 mg/L) indicated persistent hydrolysis limitations. Multi-copy chromosomal integration, a pivotal metabolic engineering approach for amplifying biosynthetic pathways, has been widely employed in microbial natural product synthesis52. To overcome catalytic bottlenecks in IAM hydrolysis, we applied multi-copy chromosomal integration of A. thaliana amidase (ami1) or A. fabrum amidohydrolase (tms2) genes at four neutral loci (mbhA, ldhA, sdaB, and poxB) (Fig. 4e). Experimental data demonstrated that increasing ami1 copy number from one (MGHT-11) to four (MGHT-18) elevated IAA production from 21.30 mg/L to 79.02 mg/L, accompanied by a reduction in residual IAM (Fig. 4f, g). By contrast, the four-copy tms2 strain (MGHT-21) exhibited superior performance, achieving 471.62 mg/L IAA (~ 2-fold improvement over the single-copy strain) with residual IAM decreased to 141.20 mg/L (Fig. 4h, i). This demonstrates that enzyme-level limitations can be overcome through copy number-driven flux redistribution, thus improving the efficiency of IAA production.

Engineering a light-driven microbial cell factory for d-homotryptophan synthesis

In module I, IAA was produced at scale via bioreactor aerobic fermentation, followed by freeze-drying concentration and pH adjustment to 7.0 (“Methods”). To ensure the supply of substrates and assess the industrial potential of engineered strain MGHT-21 for IAA biosynthesis, we performed fed-batch fermentation in a 3-L bioreactor. As shown in Fig. 5b, strain MGHT-21 exhibited suboptimal growth, with a maximum biomass concentration of 14.1 (OD600). The intermediate IAM exhibited a biphasic accumulation pattern - rising to a peak value (225.69 mg/L) before 16 h, coinciding with biomass growth, followed by a decline to a critical value (116.84 mg/L) between 16–40 h, due to increased hydrolytic enzyme activity. After 40 h, IAM levels increased again to 273.17 mg/L, likely due to product inhibition. The target product, IAA, accumulated continuously in a linear fashion, reaching 906.13 mg/L at 78 h (yield 11.62 mg/L/h), with the high-rate synthesis phase between 30 and 36 h achieving a yield of 18.84 mg/L/h.

To couple aerobic fermentation with an anoxygenic photobiocatalytic system, Module II conditions were first optimized using commercial IAA in KPi buffer, adapting parameters of photoredox/enzymatic synthesis with PfTrpB7E6 (Fig. 5c). At OD600 = 20, proportional substrate scaling from standard conditions increased d-homotryptophan production correlatively with concentration. At 5× concentration, E. coli expressing PfTrpB7E6 under 440–450 nm blue LED irradiation produced 183.14 mg/L d-homotryptophan in 12 h. Cell density affects the production efficiency of the system. When OD600 increased to 40, the yield of production reduced to 55.33 mg/L (Supplementary Fig. 8). Simultaneously, wild-type E. coli BL21(DE3) cannot synthesize d-homotryptophan, which confirms that d-homotryptophan is produced by the light-driven microbial cell factory and not through E. coli metabolism (Fig. 5c). These results demonstrated the feasibility of the light-driven MCF.

Subsequently, we compared the d-homotryptophan production capability of Module II using either commercial IAA or fermented IAA as substrates (Fig. 5d). Under the light, Module II expressing PfTrpB7E6 enzyme supplemented with fermented IAA generated 186.73 mg/L d-homotryptophan after 12 h. The yield was comparable to the control employing commercial IAA, confirming successful coupling of metabolic networks and photobiocatalysis (Fig. 5e). However, further medium optimization is required to sustain Module II bioactivity due to the deficiency of essential nutrients for cell growth in KPi buffer. Additionally, components of common microbial fermentation media exhibit substantial variation, potentially introducing inhibitory compounds. Consequently, we systematically screened the media for Module II to assess reaction efficacy under standard fermentation conditions. As shown in Fig. 5d, Module II expressing PfTrpB7E6 enzyme synthesized d-homotryptophan in both TB and M9 media. After 12 h of illumination, the d-homotryptophan yield in M9 medium reached 191.38 mg/L with fermented IAA from Module I. However, catalytic capacity in TB medium was markedly inhibited, resulting in a 38.49% reduction relative to M9 medium. Notably, Module II exhibited no detectable d-homotryptophan production in LB medium. To identify the inhibitory component(s) in LB medium, we performed concentration gradient experiments with tryptone, yeast extract, and NaCl individually. Adjusting any single component alone failed to restore reaction activity, indicating that no individual component directly inhibits the reaction. Replacing NaCl in LB with a phosphate buffer pair (9.4 g/L K₂HPO₄ and 2.2 g/L KH₂PO₄) resulted in a reaction titer of 98.04 mg/L (Supplementary Fig. 9). This finding suggests that the buffering capacity of the medium is critical for the successful operation of this photobiocatalytic system. Owing to its weak buffering capacity, LB medium may induce pH fluctuations during the reaction, thereby inhibiting enzyme activity. To further evaluate the production capacity of the light-driven MCF, we monitored d-homotryptophan synthesis kinetics in 10 mL of M9 medium (Fig. 5f). The influence of light intensity on the catalytic efficiency of the light‑driven system was systematically evaluated. Reaction yields were comparable at 1 mL and 10 mL scales, indicating scale‑independence within this range. Yield increased with light intensity from 75 to 135 mW/cm², then plateaued between 135 and 180 mW/cm², reaching a maximum of 179.61 mg/L after 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 10). These data confirm that light intensity is a critical operational parameter. Using fermented IAA as the precursor, the concentration of d-homotryptophan increased steadily under illumination in 15 h, followed by a reduced production rate until peaking at 30 h (299.75 mg/L). Based on this conversion, the complete system achieves a productivity of 1.0 mg of d-homotryptophan per gram of glucose. In contrast, no target product accumulated in dark controls (Supplementary Fig. 11). To assess the scalability of the light‑driven microbial cell factory, the system was operated at a 100 mL. d‑homotryptophan titer increased steadily over time, achieving a final concentration of 177.65 mg/L after 60 h (Supplementary Fig. 12). These results demonstrate that the light-driven MCF holds industrial potential for producing value-added chemicals.

Discussion

Inspired by TrpB-based photocatalytic C–C formations, this study utilizes PfTrpB7E6 to catalyze the conversion of IAA and l-serine into the unnatural amino acid d-homotryptophan under photocatalyst RhB and blue light. The substrate scope extends to IAA derivatives bearing 2-methyl, 6-fluoro, or 6-chloro substituents. Furthermore, replacing l-serine with l-threonine enables the reaction to proceed with IAA derivatives bearing 6-fluoro or 6-chloro substituents. To integrate a microbial cell factory with unnatural photobiocatalysis, we developed a light-driven MCF for synthesizing d-homotryptophan. The light-driven MCF’s success hinges on two synergistic design principles. First, the biosynthesis of substrates (IAA) enables coupling of MCF with photobiocatalysis, alleviating dependence on exogenous chemicals. Second, directional electron and energy transfer from the photocatalyst generates reactive species that drive enzymatic C–C coupling, while PfTrpB7E6 engineered active pocket enforces stereoselectivity. This modular design decouples aerobic fermentation from anaerobic photobiocatalysis, establishing a prototype for light-driven MCFs.

To address the limited supply of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a critical bottleneck for scaling up production, we systematically restructured the metabolic network of E. coli in three phases. Initially, the tryptophan biosynthesis flux was systematically enhanced to boost precursor availability. Subsequently, a heterologous IAM hydrolysis pathway was introduced, coupled with multi-copy chromosomal integration of tms2 to alleviate IAM cytotoxicity and accelerate IAA production. Finally, fed-batch fermentation in a 3-L bioreactor enabled scale-up of IAA synthesis. Through this integrated approach, de novo synthesis of IAA reached 906.13 mg/L in the bioreactor, demonstrating the efficacy of metabolic rewiring for scalable auxin production. Although the maximum IAA yield did not reach the highest reported value53, the process offers notable technical advantages: (1) no requirement for exogenous tryptophan substrates, inducers, or antibiotics; and (2) excellent fermentation stability. Future work will focus on constructing an efflux system to address the issues of product inhibition-induced biomass decline and periodic accumulation of IAM, laying the groundwork for industrial-scale production.

The light-driven MCF represents a modular innovation in unnatural amino acid synthesis, enabling de novo production of d-homotryptophan through synergistic photobiocatalysis. In the whole-cell photobiocatalysis, a substrate concentration five times that of a pure enzyme is required to achieve a better catalytic effect (Fig. 5c). The increase in substrate requirement may arise from restricted transmembrane transport of small molecules54. Furthermore, culture medium composition significantly influences the light-driven MCF, revealing medium optimization as critical for enhancing catalytic efficiency (Fig. 5f). Collectively, these factors contribute to diminished photoredox/enzymatic efficiency in complex intracellular environments. When the reaction volume was increased from 10 mL to 100 mL, under constant light intensity and similar reactor geometry, the increased optical path length appeared to result in a notable reduction in the photon flux per unit volume of fermentation broth. This may have led to a decline in photocatalytic efficiency, contributing to the approximately 40% decrease in the final titer. This observation suggests a key engineering challenge in scaling up photobiocatalytic processes: light penetration and distribution within the reactor. It is acknowledged that the current cascade operates as a proof of concept, with product concentrations remaining at a milligram per litre level (299.75 mg/L of the d-homotryptophan). This output does not yet rival the efficiency of established industrial processes, such as the hydantoinase process and chemo-enzymatic deracemization. Consequently, future work will prioritize optimization through enzyme and host engineering, process intensification, and scale-up to evaluate its potential for practical application.

In summary, a light-driven MCF has been successfully developed, enabling the synthesis of the unnatural amino acid d-homotryptophan through a modular strategy that couples aerobic metabolic networks with anaerobic photobiocatalysis. The integration has demonstrated the efficacy of synergistic light-driven MCF, providing generalizable guidelines through the modular platform construction. The expanded substrate scope underscores the synthetic versatility of the system toward various non-canonical amino acids. This study expands the toolbox for engineering biosynthetic pathways toward value-added chemicals.

Methods

Strains and plasmids

All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. E. coli DH5α was employed for plasmid construction and transformation. The photoenzyme-encoding gene and its mutants were cloned into the pET-22b plasmid. The pUC19 plasmid served as the backbone for assembling homologous recombination donor fragments. The CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system consisted of two plasmids: pEcCas, encoding Cas9 under an L-arabinose-inducible promoter, and pEcgRNA, carrying the target-specific sgRNA expression cassette55. E. coli BL21(DE3) was used for protein expression, and wild-type E. coli MG1655 served as the starting strain for construction of the IAA-producing chassis. Chemically competent DH5α and BL21(DE3) cells were purchased from Sangon Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Wild-type E. coli MG1655, and the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing plasmids pEcCas and pEcgRNA, were kindly provided by Prof. Zaigao Tan (Shanghai Jiao Tong University). The expression vector pET-22b and the cloning vector pUC19 were obtained from Miaoling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China).

Plasmid and strain construction

Plasmid assembly for the pUC19 backbone and pEcgRNA-derived constructs was performed using Gibson Assembly technology. Linearized vector backbones, upstream homology arms (~500 bp), downstream homology arms (~500 bp), and target gene modules were amplified with KOD One High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (TOYOBO, Shanghai, China), followed by seamless multi-fragment assembly using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Electroporation was performed to co-transform the pEcCas plasmid, pEcgRNA plasmid, and donor fragment containing the target gene into E. coli competent cells. Transformants were selected via dual-antibiotic selection (spectinomycin at 50 μg/mL and kanamycin at 50 μg/mL). Subsequently, a two-step plasmid elimination strategy was employed: the pEcgRNA plasmid was eliminated by culturing in LB medium supplemented with 10 mM rhamnose to induce plasmid loss, while the pEcCas plasmid was counter-selected with LB medium containing 1% sucrose to complete the elimination cascade55. All primers are provided in Supplementary Table 2. All oligonucleotides and plasmids were verified by sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Shake-flask fermentation

Shake-flask fermentation of E. coli MG1655 and its derivatives was conducted following a two-phase cultivation protocol optimized for metabolic engineering48. Seed cultivation stage: A 15 mL seed medium (250 mL shake flasks) was incubated at 37 °C and 220 rpm for 12 h. The composition of the seed medium was as follows: 20.0 g/L glucose, 2 g/L yeast extract, 2 g/L citric acid, 4 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 2 g/L KH2PO4, 1 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 2.8 mg/L FeSO4·7H2O, 1.2 mg/L MnSO4·H2O, 0.5 mg/L VB1, 0.1 mg/L VH, and 1 mL of microelement mixture (10 g/L CaCl2·2H2O, 0.765 g/L CuSO4·5H2O, 4.9 g/L CoCl2·6H2O, and 9.316 g/L ZnSO4·7H2O), pH 7.0. Fermentation cultivation stage: A 2.5 mL logarithmic-phase seed culture was inoculated into 22.5 mL of fermentation medium (250 mL shake flasks) and subjected to 48 h of fed-batch fermentation under the same cultivation conditions. The composition of the fermentation medium included: 20 g/L glucose, 3 g/L yeast extract, 2 g/L citric acid, 4 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 2.5 g/L KH2PO4, 1 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 20 mg/L FeSO4·7H2O, 1.2 mg/L MnSO4·H2O, 0.5 mg/L VB1, 0.1 mg/L VH, 1 mL of microelement mixture, pH 7.0. To monitor pH fluctuations in real time, 2% (w/v) phenol red was added to the medium, and 28 v/v% NH4OH was used to adjust the pH, maintaining a neutral fermentation environment.

Fed-batch fermentation in a 3-L bioreactor

Fed-batch fermentation was conducted in a 3-L bioreactor (containing 1.8 L) using a basal medium identical to the shake-flask fermentation system, except for the omission of phenol red indicator to avoid analytical interference. Following 12 h of seed culture incubation at 37 °C and 220 rpm, 180 mL of log-phase cell suspension (10% v/v inoculum) was transferred into 1.62 L of fermentation medium. Process parameters were controlled as follows: initial agitation speed set to 200 rpm, aeration rate maintained at 0.5 vvm, pH stabilized at 7.0 via automated feeding of 28 v/v% NH4OH, the temperature was set at 37 °C, and dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration regulated at 30% saturation through a cascade control strategy47. The glucose was supplied when residual glucose dropped below 5 g/L, with an exponential feeding strategy applied to maintain residual glucose concentrations between 5 and 10 g/L, thereby preventing substrate inhibition while ensuring metabolic homeostasis. The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 8700 × g for 10 min to remove precipitates, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then concentrated by vacuum freeze-drying. The resulting concentrate was subsequently extracted with ethyl acetate to remove residual salt ions.

Whole-cell photocatalysis conditions

The whole-cell photocatalytic system was established as follows: 500 μL of the seed culture was transferred to 250 mL shaking flasks containing 30 mL TB medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and was cultured at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Cultures were then induced with 0.5 mM β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 25 °C for 20 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 5 min. All reactions were assembled in a Vigor glove box with an O2 concentration below 3 ppm. In the glove box, to a 10.0 mL vial containing a magnetic stir bar, solutions of E. coli overexpressing PfTrpB7E6 (OD600 = 200 in 100 mM KPi, 100 μL), indole-3-acetic acid 1 (20 μL of a 1000 mM DMSO stock, 0.02 mmol), l-serine 2 (24 μL of a 2500 mM stock in 100 mM KPi, 0.06 mmol), pyridoxal phosphate (40 μL of a 25 mM stock in 100 mM KPi, 5 mol%), Rhodamine B (30 μL of a 66.5 mM DMSO stock, 10 mol%) and glucose (100 μL of a 40 g/L stock in 100 mM KPi, 4 mg) were added to ~686 μL of KPi medium. The total volume of the reaction mixture was 1.0 mL, and the final concentration of DMSO was 5 v/v%. The vial was sealed with a screw cap, removed from the glove box, illuminated with 440–450 nm LEDs, and stirred for 12 h at 50 °C.

Light-driven microbial cell factory process

For the light-driven microbial cell factory process, the fermentation broth from the bioreactor was centrifuged (8700 × g, 10 min) to remove cells. Subsequently, the fermentation supernatant was lyophilized, and the concentrated liquid was pretreated with ethyl acetate. This was followed by concentration under reduced pressure at 40 °C. Finally, the residue was purified and dissolved in M9 medium to solubilize IAA, followed by 30 min nitrogen purging to establish strict anaerobiosis. The photocatalytic stage was consistent with the protocol for the whole-cell photocatalytic system.

Analytical methods

Analytical methods for quantifying glucose, tryptophan, indole-3-acetamide, and indole-3-acetic acid

Glucose concentrations were measured using a biosensor analyzer, with fermentation supernatants diluted to glucose levels below 1 g/L prior to analysis. l-tryptophan, IAM, and IAA were quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1220, USA). For tryptophan analysis, chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent Eclipse AAA column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm) at 39 °C with UV detection at 278 nm, using a mobile phase of 0.3 g/L KH2PO4 (phase A) and methanol (phase B) at a 9:1 ratio (A:B) and a flow rate of 1 mL/min46. For IAM and IAA quantification, the same column type was operated at 40 °C with UV detection at 270 nm51. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 v/v% formic acid (phase A) and methanol (phase B), with a gradient elution program: 0–15 min (20% to 60% B), 15–16 min (60% to 20% B), 16–18 min (20% B), at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min and an injection volume of 10 μL.

Quantitative analysis of d-homotryptophan via HPLC-MS/MS

d-homotryptophan quantification was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry system (HPLC-MS/MS, Agilent 1290, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 4 μm, Agilent) maintained at 25 °C, with UV detection at 210 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 v/v% formic acid (phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B). A gradient elution program was optimized as follows: 0–2 min at 95% A (flow rate 1 mL/min), 2–8 min linear gradient to 5% A, and 8–9 min hold at 5% A. Mass spectrometric parameters were set to electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive ion mode with a capillary voltage of 3.5 kV, nebulizer gas pressure of 45 psi, and dry gas temperature of 325 °C. A multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) channel was established for the tryptophan precursor ion m/z 219.11 [M + H]+.

Statistical analysis

All enzymatic reactions in Fig. 2 were performed in duplicate. Other biological experiments were carried out with three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA tests.
