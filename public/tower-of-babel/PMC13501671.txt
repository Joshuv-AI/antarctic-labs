Modeling striatal development and disease with human pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Sopak Supakul, Yuya Sasaki, Kosuke Karasawa, Yoshitaka Kase, Mitsuru Ishikawa, Hideyuki Okano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe striatum plays a central role in motor control, cognition, reward processing, and habit formation, and its dysfunction is implicated in a broad spectrum of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Although animal models have provided important insights into striatal development and disease mechanisms, species-specific differences in cellular composition, developmental timing, and circuit organization limit their translational relevance to the human brain. In this context, human pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), including embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells, have emerged as valuable platforms for modeling human striatal development and pathology in vitro. In this review, we summarize current approaches for generating striatal cell types from PSCs, with a particular focus on medium spiny neurons (MSNs), the principal projection neurons of the striatum. We discuss key developmental principles underlying dorsal and ventral striatal specification and highlight the protracted maturation of human MSNs, which may contribute to human-specific disease vulnerability. Advances in differentiation strategies, including small molecule-based patterning, transcription factor-driven induction, and three-dimensional organoid and assembloid systems, have progressively improved the efficiency, reproducibility, and cellular complexity of PSC-derived striatal models. We further review applications of PSC-derived striatal systems in disease modeling, noting that most studies to date have focused on Huntington’s disease, where these models have revealed early developmental, transcriptional, synaptic, and network-level abnormalities. More recent studies have begun to extend these approaches to other neurological conditions and to incorporate circuit-level analyses using cortico-striatal assembloids. In parallel, the growing availability of single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic datasets from the human striatum provides powerful reference frameworks for benchmarking the identity and maturation state of PSC-derived striatal cells. Finally, we discuss current challenges and limitations of PSC-based striatal models, including incomplete maturation, limited representation of non-neuronal cell types, and restricted applicability to psychiatric disorders. Introduction

The striatum is a crucial component of the brain that plays central roles in motor control, cognition, reward processing, and habit formation [1, 2]. Anatomically, it is located beneath the cerebral cortex, lateral to the thalamus, and superior to the brainstem, and it serves as a major input nucleus of the basal ganglia. Neurons in the striatum receive extensive afferent inputs from cortical, thalamic, and midbrain regions and comprise a diverse population of neuronal subtypes with distinct functional properties [3, 4]. Among these, medium spiny neurons (MSNs) represent the predominant neuronal population and are characterized by their dense dendritic spines. MSNs are GABAergic inhibitory neurons integral to dopaminergic signaling pathways and can be broadly classified based on their expression of dopamine receptors, including dopamine D1 receptor (D1R) and dopamine D2 receptor (D2R), which underlie the direct and indirect pathways of basal ganglia circuitry [5, 6]. Dysfunction of MSNs is a hallmark of several neurodegenerative disorders, most notably Huntington’s disease, multiple system atrophy, and progressive Parkinson’s disease [7, 8]. In addition, growing evidence implicates striatal neuronal dysfunction in a range of psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, addiction, and obsessive–compulsive disorder, where altered dopamine signaling and corticostriatal circuit dynamics are central features [9–11]. Moreover, since most psychiatric drugs target the D2R, which is expressed in the MSNs within the striatum, the functional abnormality of MSNs is thought to be associated with these psychiatric diseases.

Animal models, particularly rodents, have been extensively used to investigate striatal development, circuit function, and disease mechanisms [12–14]. These models have provided fundamental insights into basal ganglia organization, neurotransmitter signaling, and disease-associated cellular phenotypes, including changes in MSN excitability, synaptic plasticity, and corticostriatal connectivity [4, 5]. Genetically engineered mouse models have been especially informative for dissecting gene-function relationships and the temporal progression of striatal pathology, particularly in monogenic disorders such as Huntington’s disease [12–14]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that species-specific differences in striatal development, neuronal composition, and transcriptional programs substantially influence disease manifestation and therapeutic responses [15, 16]. Comparative transcriptomic and cell-type-resolved analyses have revealed marked divergence between mouse and human striatal neurons, including differences in gene regulatory networks, developmental timing, and neuromodulatory signaling pathways [16, 17]. Moreover, many neuropsychiatric disorders are driven by subtle, polygenic risk architectures, and higher-order cognitive dysfunctions that are difficult to fully recapitulate in rodent models [18, 19]. Together, these limitations underscore the need for human cell-based experimental systems that can more faithfully model striatal development and disease-relevant phenotypes.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), including embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which possess the capacity to differentiate into any cell type, have emerged as powerful platforms for modeling human brain development and disease in vitro. In particular, iPSCs can be generated directly from the somatic cells of healthy donors or patients, enabling the study of patient-specific genetic backgrounds [20]. Neural models derived from iPSCs have become essential tools for investigating human neurodevelopment, neuronal physiology, and disease-associated phenotypes that are otherwise inaccessible in living patients [21, 22]. In this review, we discuss how PSC-based approaches have been applied to model the striatum and its development, and we highlight their emerging applications in disease modeling and drug discovery.

Striatum development

During early brain development, the telencephalon is patterned into dorsal (pallial) and ventral (subpallial) domains through coordinated signaling interactions, including morphogen gradients such as FGFs, WNTs, and SHH, which establish the ventral telencephalic progenitor fields that give rise to striatal neurons [23]. The dorsal striatum, comprising the caudate nucleus and putamen, originates from progenitor domains within the lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE), a transient embryonic structure in the ventral telencephalon that produces the vast majority of medium spiny neurons (MSNs). As development proceeds, differentiating striatal neurons begin to receive afferent inputs from the cerebral cortex, thalamus, and midbrain dopaminergic neurons, integrating into nascent basal ganglia circuits [23, 24]. Compared with many other brain regions, the human dorsal striatum continues to maturate throughout childhood and adolescence, undergoing progressive synaptogenesis, dendritic arbor expansion, and refinement of frontostriatal connectivity, reflecting a prolonged developmental trajectory [25–27].

In parallel, the ventral striatum, including the nucleus accumbens (NAc), arises from adjacent ventral and caudal ganglionic eminence progenitors and integrates early inputs from limbic structures such as the amygdala and hippocampus, as well as from the prefrontal cortex and dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA). Functional neuroimaging and connectivity studies indicate that ventral striatal circuits are progressively incorporated into reward, motivational, and affective networks across adolescence, highlighting the dynamic maturation of limbic-striatal connections [28] (Fig. 1).

Within both dorsal and ventral striatal compartments, MSNs represent the principal projection neuron population and exhibit a particularly protracted maturation process in humans. Following neurogenesis in utero, MSNs continue to elaborate dendritic arbors, form dendritic spines, and refine synaptic connectivity well into adolescence. The development of the LGE, including the striatum, is orchestrated by complex networks of transcription factors, including GSX2, ASCL1, DLX1/2, and others, that regulate progenitor identity, neurogenic competence, and differentiation timing. Perturbations in these programs can result in striatal malformations [23, 24]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the development of MSN during striatal development are still underappreciated, stem cell-based modeling studies suggest that the extended maturation period is critical for the full establishment of MSN subtype identity, including the segregation of direct-pathway (D1R-expressing) and indirect-pathway (D2R-expressing) neurons, as well as for their proper circuit integration [29, 30]. This protracted developmental window may underlie a human-specific period of vulnerability during which disruptions to striatal maturation contribute to the emergence of neuropsychiatric disorders.

Human pluripotent stem cells (ESCs/iPSCs) derived neural cell models of striatum

Human PSCs, either ESCs or iPSCs, are stem cells that can proliferate indefinitely and retain pluripotency, meaning they can differentiate into any cell type in the body. In particular, iPSCs, which can be generated from donor somatic cells, serve as an alternative to PSCs in human research. Under well-controlled conditions, these stem cells can be differentiated into a 2D cell model, or a 3D tissue that recapitulates the striatal structure. Human ESCs must be obtained naturally from the embryos, while human iPSCs can be generated by introducing certain transcription factors (Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc) to the somatic cells [20]. iPSCs can be generated from most of the cell types in the body, for example, fibroblasts, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and the non-invasively obtainable urine-derived cells (UDCs) [31, 32]. Since iPSCs retain the donor’s genetic information, cells differentiated from iPSCs can reflect the donor’s disease pathology within specific tissues [33]. Therefore, cellular models differentiated from the iPSCs of patients with known genetic and clinical data are useful for the studies of disease analyses and drug discovery. There were several examples of research in other neurological disorders including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and Alzheimer’s disease [34, 35].

The modeling of human MSNs, the principal GABAergic projection neurons of the striatum, has advanced rapidly through multiple stem-cell and reprogramming approaches. Early strategies demonstrated that fibroblasts could be directly converted into striatal-like neurons by forced expression of miR-9/9* and miR-124, combined with lineage-defining transcription factors such as CTIP2, DLX1/2, and MYT1L, yielding neurons that exhibit DARPP-32 expression and electrophysiological features of MSNs without passing through a pluripotent state [36, 37]. This microRNA-dependent direct conversion approach provides a fast and relatively homogeneous route to generate patient-specific neurons, thereby preserving age-related epigenetic signatures that are often erased in iPSC reprogramming. However, although direct conversion captures mature neuronal phenotypes, the neurons were directly converted from somatic cells; thus, the cells were not differentiated through the developmental process. This character limits its use in studies of developmental regulation and early disease pathogenesis. Moreover, obtaining the differentiated cells with high purity is also challenging, often requiring the isolation of the target cells even after differentiation [38, 39].

In contrary, protocols combining dual SMAD inhibition, WNT inhibition, and SHH activation efficiently drive human PSCs toward LGE-like progenitors, which then mature into DARPP-32, GABAergic, and CTIP2-espressing MSNs that display characteristic D1R and D2R expression and synaptic activity [29, 30]. Recent optimizations achieved rapid and high-purity MSN differentiation, revealing disease-relevant phenotypes such as LMNB1 hypofunction and highlighting the need to distinguish D1/D2 subtypes in modeling Huntington’s disease [40]. More recently, 3D organoid and assembloid systems have extended this paradigm by allowing the self-organization of striatal structures and functional connectivity with cortical inputs. Human pluripotent stem cell-derived striatal organoids and cortico-striatal assembloids recapitulate key features of striatal architecture, including compartmentalization, dopaminergic innervation, and corticostriatal synaptic integration, thus providing a powerful platform to study development, circuit formation, and disease interactions in a spatially and physiologically relevant context [41–43]. Together, these complementary systems, including direct conversion, 2D iPSC-derived models, and 3D organoids, offer a continuum of approaches for dissecting human striatal development, circuit function, and neurodegenerative mechanisms.

Applications for disease analyses

Human PSC-based models of the striatum have been applied most extensively to Huntington’s disease (HD), establishing HD as a reference framework for dissecting striatal development and vulnerability in vitro. Early studies using human embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells demonstrated that mutant HTT perturbs striatal differentiation programs, revealing molecular and functional abnormalities in medium spiny neuron (MSN)-like cells even in the absence of overt neurodegeneration. Subsequent work employing refined patterning strategies and functional assays showed that HD iPSC-derived MSNs exhibit impaired maturation, altered synaptic signaling, abnormal calcium dynamics, and metabolic dysregulation (Table 1). More recent transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic analyses, including single-cell approaches, further demonstrated that disease-associated phenotypes emerge during striatal lineage specification, supporting the concept that developmental dysregulation precedes and shapes later degenerative vulnerability.

Summary of studies using PSC-derived striatal models for disease modeling

Beyond neurodegeneration, PSC-derived striatal models are increasingly positioned to inform psychiatric disease mechanisms, where striatal dysfunction is central but difficult to interrogate in humans. Three-dimensional striatal organoids and cortico-striatal assembloids enable the reconstruction of human corticostriatal circuitry and have already revealed network-level phenotypes in neurodevelopmental conditions such as 22q13.3 deletion syndrome [42]. In addition, the ability to generate NAc neurons from PSCs raises the possibility of reconstructing mesolimbic reward circuitry in vitro by integrating VTA dopaminergic neurons with NAc neurons. Such VTA–NAc circuit models could provide a powerful experimental platform for investigating dopamine-dependent reward processing and the cellular mechanisms underlying substance use disorders [52, 53]. These advances are particularly relevant to psychiatric disorders including schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, and mood disorders, in which altered dopamine signaling, imbalanced D1/D2 MSN populations, and disrupted corticostriatal communication are core pathophysiological features. Dopamine receptors expressed on MSNs engage distinct intracellular signaling cascades: D1R is Gs/olf-coupled G protein-coupled receptors that activate adenylyl cyclase and protein kinase A signaling, whereas D2R is Gi/o-coupled receptors that suppress cAMP production and downstream phosphorylation pathways [3, 6]. As a consequence, activation of D1R- and D2R-expressing MSNs leads to fundamentally different modes of neuronal excitability, synaptic integration, and circuit-level output. Perturbations in the balance or maturation of these signaling pathways are therefore expected to alter striatal network dynamics in a manner highly relevant to psychiatric disease mechanisms (Fig. 2).

Distinct intracellular signaling cascades engaged by D1R and D2R in striatal medium spiny neurons (MSNs). Left, activation of D1R, which is coupled to Gs/olf proteins, stimulates adenylyl cyclase activity, leading to increased intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels and activation of protein kinase A (PKA). Enhanced PKA signaling promotes phosphorylation of downstream protein substrates, resulting in increased neuronal excitability and facilitation of direct-pathway signaling. Right, activation of D2R, which are coupled to Gi/o proteins, suppresses adenylyl cyclase activity, reducing cAMP production and PKA activity. This leads to decreased phosphorylation of downstream targets and reduced neuronal excitability, characteristic of indirect-pathway signaling

Human PSC-derived striatal models that resolve D1 and D2 MSN identities and their downstream signaling properties thus provide a powerful framework for interrogating dopamine-dependent circuit dysfunction in neuropsychiatric disorders. As differentiation protocols continue to improve in subtype resolution and maturation state, PSC-based models are expected to evolve from capturing gross developmental abnormalities to resolving subtle, polygenic risk-associated phenotypes. Integration of patient stratification by genetic risk, coupled with circuit-level and neuromodulatory paradigms, will likely position human PSC-derived striatal systems as a powerful platform for mechanistic dissection and therapeutic discovery in psychiatric disorders.

Strategies for generation and evaluation of MSNs differentiated from PSCs

As summarized in Table 1, MSNs can be efficiently induced from PSCs using either small molecule-based patterning strategies or the overexpression of transcription factors that are highly enriched in specific MSN lineages. To rationally design differentiation vectors and to robustly evaluate PSC-derived MSNs, we propose leveraging previously reported single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (sc/snRNA-seq) reference datasets. These datasets, derived primarily from postmortem human brain tissue and animal models, provide high-resolution molecular benchmarks for striatal cell identity. Advances in sequencing technologies and reduced costs have greatly expanded the availability of such datasets, enabling their broad use as reference atlases for in vitro cell fate evaluation.

In this review, the publicly available human nucleus accumbens single-nucleus RNA sequencing dataset from Tran et al. (2021), comprising 19,892 nuclei from eight donors, was re-analyzed using Seurat in RStudio [54]. Raw count matrices and associated metadata were extracted from the SingleCellExperiment object and used to generate a Seurat object with minimum thresholds of 3 cells per gene and 200 detected genes per nucleus. Mitochondrial transcript percentages were calculated, and nuclei were filtered based on gene counts (nFeature_RNA > 200 and < 6000) and mitochondrial content (< 15%). Normalization and variance stabilization were performed using SCTransform, with mitochondrial percentage regressed out. Principal component analysis was conducted for dimensionality reduction, followed by graph-based clustering using FindNeighbors and FindClusters. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied based on the first 30 principal components for two-dimensional visualization. Cell-type annotations provided in the original metadata were incorporated for cluster identification, and marker gene expression was visualized using feature plots and dot plots.

The resulting UMAP identified major striatal cell populations, including MSNs, other inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, and astrocytes (Fig. 3A). Feature plots revealed the expression of MSN identity (PPP1R1B) and the ventral striatal developmental origin (MEIS2). Furthermore, PPP1R1B-expressing cells could be further subdivided based on DRD1 and DRD2 expression, clearly distinguishing D1-type and D2-type MSNs (Fig. 3B), which illustrates the known mutually exclusive characteristics of MSNs, in which D1-type and D2-type are normally distinguished. In addition, BCL11B (CTIP2) and FOXP1, transcription factors implicated in ventral striatal development, were robustly expressed across MSN clusters in the NAc dataset. These molecular signatures provide valuable guidance both for selecting transcription factors used in MSN induction protocols and for benchmarking the identity and maturation state of PSC-derived striatal neurons generated via either small-molecule patterning or transcription factor-based approaches.

In the Dot plot, MSNs exhibit robust and selective expression of striatal identity markers including PPP1R1B (DARPP-32), GPR88, and BCL11B, distinguishing them clearly from other neuronal populations. GABAergic interneurons are characterized by high expression of GAD1 and SLC6A1, whereas glutamatergic neurons preferentially express SATB2 and SLC17A7. Glial cell populations display distinct and non-overlapping transcriptional signatures, with astrocytes marked by ALDH1L1, SLC1A3, and AQP4; microglia by C1QA, TMEM119, and P2RY12; and oligodendrocyte lineage cells by MBP, PLP1, MOG, and OLIG2, with PDGFRA and VCAN identifying oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (Fig. 3C). This visualization highlights the specificity and relative enrichment of striatal MSN markers within the human ventral striatum and provides a practical benchmark for assessing cellular identity, purity, and off-target populations in PSC-derived striatal differentiation systems.

Importantly, evaluation of PSC-derived MSNs should not rely solely on marker gene expression but should also incorporate detailed functional characterization. Compared with other neuronal subtypes, MSNs display relatively hyperpolarized resting membrane potentials and prominent inward-rectifier potassium currents, electrophysiological features that are essential for shaping their low baseline excitability and characteristic firing properties. These currents are largely mediated by inward-rectifier potassium channels such as Kir2 family members (e.g., KCNJ2 and KCNJ4), which are highly enriched in mature striatal projection neurons and are considered key determinants of MSN identity [36, 55]. Such electrophysiological properties can be quantitatively assessed using patch-clamp recordings or microelectrode array (MEA) platforms in PSC-derived neuronal cultures.

In addition, pharmacological responsiveness assays provide a critical functional layer for evaluating MSN subtype specification and intracellular signaling competence. D1R is a Gs/olf-coupled G protein-coupled receptor that activates the adenylyl cyclase–cAMP–protein kinase A (PKA) pathway, whereas D2R is a Gi/o-coupled receptor that suppresses cAMP production and downstream phosphorylation signaling [3, 6]. Accordingly, treatment with D1R agonists induces increased phosphorylation of downstream targets such as GluR1 or ribosomal protein S6 in direct-pathway MSNs, while D2R agonists reduce phosphorylation of these targets in indirect-pathway MSNs [56]. D2R antagonists, which are widely used in the treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders, can likewise be applied to PSC-derived MSN models to interrogate disease-relevant signaling dynamics and drug responsiveness.

Current status, challenges, and limitations

Despite substantial progress in the generation of striatal lineages from PSCs, the application of these models to psychiatric disorders remains limited. To date, the majority of PSC-derived striatal studies have focused on Huntington’s disease, where monogenic etiology and robust MSN degeneration provide clear phenotypic readouts. In contrast, relatively few studies have leveraged PSC-derived striatal models to investigate psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, addiction, or obsessive–compulsive disorder, despite strong evidence implicating striatal dysfunction in these conditions. This disparity likely reflects the polygenic architecture and subtle, circuit-level phenotypes characteristic of psychiatric diseases, which pose greater challenges for in vitro modeling and phenotypic interpretation.

Another important limitation is the incomplete representation of non-neuronal cell types within current PSC-derived striatal models. While medium spiny neurons constitute the principal neuronal population of the striatum, glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglia) also play essential roles in synaptic maturation, metabolic support, myelination, and circuit refinement. Most existing 2D differentiation protocols primarily generate neuronal populations and lack mature, region-specific glial components. Although some 3D organoid and assembloid systems capture a broader range of cell types, the extent to which these glial populations accurately recapitulate striatal-specific identities and functions remains unclear and requires further systematic evaluation.

More broadly, while differentiation strategies have evolved from early small molecule-based patterning approaches to transcription factor-driven protocols and increasingly complex 3D models that partially mimic developmental processes, no current system fully recapitulates the cellular composition, spatial organization, and prolonged maturation of the human striatum. Continuous methodological improvements will be necessary to generate models that more accurately reflect human-specific developmental timing, MSN subtype specification, and disease-relevant circuit integration.

As summarized in Table 1, 2D monolayer cultures and 3D spheroids and organoid systems each have distinct advantages and limitations when it comes to modeling striatal development and disease. In general, 2D PSC-derived MSN cultures provide relatively homogeneous cellular populations, improved experimental reproducibility, and easier accessibility for electrophysiological recordings, live-cell imaging, transcriptomic analyses, and high-throughput pharmacological screening. These systems are therefore particularly well-suited to mechanistic studies focusing on cell-autonomous phenotypes, intracellular signaling pathways, and subtype-specific neuronal properties, including D1/D2 receptor-associated signaling responses. In contrast, 3D striatal spheroids and organoid-based systems more closely recapitulate aspects of tissue architecture, prolonged maturation, cellular heterogeneity, and spatial organization observed in the developing human striatum. Furthermore, assembloid systems integrating cortical, striatal, and dopaminergic regions enable the reconstruction of inter-regional neuronal connectivity and circuit-level activity that cannot be adequately modeled in conventional 2D cultures. These properties make 3D culture systems particularly valuable for investigating developmental interactions, synaptic network formation, corticostriatal and mesolimbic circuit dynamics, and complex disease phenotypes involving non-cell-autonomous mechanisms. Importantly, the choice of modeling strategy, whether 2D monoculture, co-culture systems, or 3D organoids, should be guided by the specific biological question being addressed. Simpler 2D models may be well suited to mechanistic dissection and high-throughput screening, whereas 3D and circuit-integrated systems may be required to interrogate complex developmental and psychiatric phenotypes. Recognizing these trade-offs will be critical for the appropriate interpretation and future advancement of PSC-derived striatal disease models.

Conclusion

Human PSC-based models have emerged as powerful platforms for studying striatal development and disease in a human-specific context. By integrating insights from developmental biology with advances in differentiation strategies, transcriptional programming, and multi-omics analyses, PSC-derived striatal systems have enabled increasingly precise modeling of MSN identity, maturation, and function. Although most progress to date has been achieved in the context of Huntington’s disease, the expanding availability of high-resolution human reference datasets, together with improved differentiation and functional evaluation methods, positions these models to move toward more complex and subtle disease paradigms, including psychiatric disorders. Importantly, the use of patient-derived PSCs also enables stratification of disease-relevant cellular phenotypes across genetically and clinically defined patient subgroups, providing a foundation for precision studies of striatal dysfunction. In parallel, emerging approaches that combine cell-type-specific regulatory elements with nucleic acid delivery technologies enable the development of MSN-targeted therapeutic strategies to correct disease-associated molecular abnormalities in a cell-selective manner. Continued refinement of PSC-based striatal models, including extended maturation, incorporation of glial components, and circuit-level integration, will be essential to more fully capture the complexity of the human striatum. Ultimately, the complementary application of 2D and 3D PSC-derived systems, guided by clearly defined experimental objectives, will be critical for advancing our understanding of striatal biology and translating in vitro discoveries into meaningful therapeutic development.
