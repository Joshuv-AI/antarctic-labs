A Case of Concomitant Inferior Epigastric Artery Injury and Superior Vesical Artery Pseudoaneurysm after Cesarean Section Treated with Transarterial and Transvaginal Embolization
Authors: Atsunobu Hino, Shohei Chatani, Motoki Tanaka, Yugo Imai, Akitoshi Inoue, Yuki Tomozawa, Yoko Murakami, Akinaga Sonoda, Daisuke Katsura, Yoshiyuki Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHemorrhagic complications following cesarean section, including rectus sheath hematoma and bladder flap hematoma, are uncommon but potentially life threatening. We describe a rare case of concurrent inferior epigastric artery injury and superior vesical artery pseudoaneurysm after cesarean delivery in a 27-year-old woman with a prior cesarean section. Despite laparotomy, hemostasis was not achieved, and she required massive transfusion. Transarterial embolization successfully controlled inferior epigastric artery bleeding, but tortuosity and collateral circulation limited embolization of the superior vesical artery pseudoaneurysm. Subsequent transvaginal ultrasound-guided direct puncture with injection of a mixture of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate and Lipiodol resulted in hemostasis. The patient recovered without rebleeding. This case underscores the importance of considering rare vascular complications after cesarean section and demonstrates transvaginal ultrasound-guided puncture as a valuable alternative when conventional methods fail.Keywords: postpartum hemorrhage, cesarean section, complication, transarterial embolization, transvaginal puncture

Introduction

Postpartum hemorrhage is an obstetric emergency characterized by bleeding following delivery and represents one of the leading causes of maternal mortality [1]. While uterine atony and retained products of conception are common etiologies, surgical interventions during cesarean section may also serve as a cause of bleeding. In particular, cesarean delivery can be complicated by rectus sheath hematoma or bladder flap hematoma associated with uterine or abdominal wall incisions, and arterial injury may occasionally result in life-threatening hemorrhage [2, 3]. Furthermore, intraoperative arterial injury may result in pseudoaneurysm formation, leading to delayed hemorrhage [2, 3].

Herein, we report a case of concomitant inferior epigastric artery (IEA) injury and superior vesical artery (SVA) pseudoaneurysm following cesarean section, in which surgical hemostasis was unsuccessful. Successful bleeding control was achieved by transarterial embolization (TAE) in combination with transvaginal ultrasound-guided (TVUS-guided) direct puncture and embolization using a mixture of NBCA (Histoacryl, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and Lipiodol (Guerbet, Villepinte, France).

Case Report

History

A 27-year-old woman with a medical history of prior cesarean section, adhesive small bowel obstruction, and ventricular septal defect underwent cesarean delivery at a local obstetric clinic at 39 weeks of gestation. During the procedure, dense adhesions were observed between the parietal peritoneum, uterus, and bladder, and a serosal injury to the bladder was suspected during adhesiolysis. Active bleeding from a vessel near the bladder serosa was identified and controlled with a Z-suture. In addition, bleeding from the right side of the uterine incision was noted and was controlled with an additional Z-suture. The estimated intraoperative blood loss, including amniotic fluid, was 1,650 mL. Due to persistent gross hematuria and decreased hemoglobin levels after childbirth, the patient was referred to our hospital for evaluation and treatment on postoperative day 1 after cesarean section.

At the time of initial presentation to our hospital, laboratory tests revealed anemia and mild thrombocytopenia, with the following values: red blood cell count 2.82 × 1012/L, hemoglobin 7.5 g/dL, hematocrit 23%, white blood cell count 10.2 × 109/L, platelet count 11.6 × 109/L, prothrombin time–international normalized ratio 1.17, activated partial thromboplastin time 39.1 s, and fibrinogen 443 mg/dL. Cystoscopy was performed first and showed no accumulation of hematoma in the bladder and no hematuria from either ureteral orifice. An elevated lesion was noted at the right side of the bladder dome. Contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) was subsequently performed, which revealed a 25 × 13 mm pseudoaneurysm at the corresponding location without active extravasation. Hemoperitoneum around the bladder and a hematoma in the lower abdominal wall were also observed (Figure 1).

(A-C) Contrast-enhanced CT.

White arrow indicates a pseudoaneurysm (25 × 13 mm) at the right side of the bladder dome (black arrowheads), and white arrowheads indicate a thick hematoma in the lower abdominal wall.

An emergent laparotomy was performed on postoperative day 1 after cesarean section. The laparotomy revealed hematoma beneath the lower abdominal fascia, in the peritoneal cavity, and surrounding the bladder; however, the pseudoaneurysm was not clearly identified. Considering that prominent vessels around the bladder may have contributed to bleeding, these vessels were ligated with suture. Additional sutures were placed at the uterine incision site. Following laparotomy, mild hematuria persisted, and the patient required total transfusion of 18 units of red blood cells and 14 units of fresh frozen plasma from admission through postoperative day 2 after cesarean section. Thereafter, as hematuria gradually improved without further decline in hemoglobin levels, she was managed conservatively with close observation. However, on postoperative day 5 after cesarean section, the patient developed left lower abdominal pain. CECT revealed extravasation of contrast media within the left rectus abdominis muscle, as well as a pseudoaneurysm measuring 16 × 8 mm at the right side of the bladder dome (Figure 2), which was smaller than the initially measured size (25 × 13 mm). These lesions were supplied by the left IEA and the right SVA, respectively. Given the previous failure to identify the bladder pseudoaneurysm during laparotomy, surgical management was considered difficult. Urgent TAE was performed targeting the left IEA and the right SVA to achieve hemostasis.

Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT after laparotomy.

White arrow indicates a residual pseudoaneurysm (16 × 8 mm), and a white arrowhead indicates extravasation of contrast media from the left inferior epigastric artery.

Embolization of the left IEA

TAE of the left IEA was performed first. A 5-F sheath was inserted via the right femoral artery. A 4-F Cobra-shaped catheter (Hanako Medical, Saitama, Japan) was advanced to the external iliac artery, and a triple coaxial system consisting of a 2.6/2.8-F high-flow microcatheter (Masters HF; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) and a 1.9-F nontapered microcatheter (Carry Leon NSX; UTM, Aichi, Japan) was carried to the left IEA using a 0.014-inch guidewire (BEGIN; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan). Selective angiography showed extravasation from peripheral branches (Figure 3A), and three microcoils (Tornado 2 mm/3 mm, 2 cm; Cook Medical, Tokyo, Japan) were deployed in the distal portion of the parent branch to modify blood flow. Subsequently, the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture at a 1:4 ratio was injected from the proximal IEA to embolize the target branch with a total volume of 0.8 mL (Figure 3B).

(B) The distal portion of the parent branch was coiled (black arrow) to modify blood flow, followed by embolization of the target branch (black arrowheads) using a mixture of NBCA and Lipiodol at a 1:4 ratio.

IEA: inferior epigastric artery

Embolization for pseudoaneurysm of the right SVA

The right internal iliac artery was selected using a 5-F modified Mohri-type (NOE-UT; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan), and a triple coaxial system consisting of a 2.6/2.8-F high-flow microcatheter and a 1.9-F nontapered microcatheter (Carnelian Marvel; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was advanced using a 0.014-inch guidewire (BEGIN; Asahi Intecc). However, the system failed to approach the proximal portion of the pseudoaneurysm of the right SVA due to steep angulation of the arterial origin and its tortuous course (Figure 4A). The warmed NBCA–Lipiodol mixture at a 1:8 ratio was injected, with a total volume of 1.2 mL; however, incomplete occlusion of the pseudoaneurysm was achieved (Figure 4B). Subsequently, angiography of the left internal iliac artery revealed multiple collateral arteries to the pseudoaneurysm from the distal portion of the left SVA (Figure 4C). Considering the risk of bladder necrosis from bilateral SVA embolization, embolization of the left SVA was not performed, and the patient was managed conservatively.

(A) Selective angiography of the right superior vesical artery demonstrates a tortuous artery (black arrowheads) and a distal pseudoaneurysm (black arrow). (B) A mixture of NBCA and Lipiodol at a 1:8 ratio was injected from the proximal superior vesical artery, but only partial filling of the pseudoaneurysm was achieved (black arrow). (C) Angiography of the left internal iliac artery demonstrates rich collateral vessels (black arrows) supplying the pseudoaneurysm (black arrowhead).

Although mild hematuria persisted without anemia progression, a residual pseudoaneurysm of the bladder was clearly visualized on TVUS posing potential risk of rebleeding. Direct puncture for hemostasis was considered; however, transabdominal ultrasonography failed to clearly delineate the pseudoaneurysm because it was located deep and was obscured by the overlying thick rectus sheath hematoma. In contrast, the lesion was clearly visualized on TVUS performed by the obstetrics team. Based on these findings and after discussion with the obstetricians, TVUS-guided direct puncture was selected to achieve hemostasis on postoperative day 8 after cesarean section.

Embolization for the right SVA pseudoaneurysm by TVUS-guided direct puncture

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lithotomy position in a hybrid operating room. The TVUS examination and needle puncture were performed by an obstetrician. The pseudoaneurysm was visualized on TVUS (Figure 5), and a 20-gauge needle with a length of 35 cm (Kitazato OPU Needle; Kitazato Corporation, Shizuoka, Japan) was transvaginally advanced into the pseudoaneurysm using a needle guide. Next, an extension tube with a 1.1-mm inner diameter, 100-cm length, and 1.0-mL volume (SAFEED Extension Tube; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was connected to the needle. Contrast medium was injected through the punctured needle via the extension tube by the interventional radiology team. Contrast medium within the pseudoaneurysm was rapidly washed out through abundant collateral pathways without contrast extravasation. Given the high-flow circulation, the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture was prepared at a relatively concentrated ratio of 1:2 to achieve rapid polymerization and to minimize unintended distal spread. Embolization was subsequently performed by the interventional radiology team under fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 6). During injection, careful fluoroscopic monitoring was performed to assess nontarget embolization, and injection was immediately terminated once the pseudoaneurysm was completely filled with contrast media under fluoroscopy. Following injection of 1.2 mL of the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture, the needle was flushed with 5% dextrose solution to prevent premature polymerization within the metallic needle and to allow for additional NBCA–Lipiodol mixture injection if necessary. After confirming complete obliteration of intralesional flow on TVUS, the needle was withdrawn.

The pseudoaneurysm was directly punctured with a 20-gauge needle under transvaginal ultrasonography guidance. The pseudoaneurysm and feeding arteries were retrogradely filled with a mixture of NBCA and Lipiodol at a 1:2 ratio (black arrow). Postoperatively, hematuria resolved and hemoglobin levels improved. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 after cesarean section. CT performed on postoperative day 38 after cesarean section showed no evidence of residual pseudoaneurysm and preserved bladder wall enhancement without evidence of ischemia or necrosis (Figure 7). No complications related to TVUS-guided embolization were observed.

Contrast-enhanced CT on postoperative day 38 after cesarean section shows the pseudoaneurysm completely filled with a mixture of NBCA and Lipiodol (white arrow). There were no findings suggestive of bladder ischemia.

Discussion

Rectus sheath hematoma and bladder flap hematoma are uncommon complications after cesarean section. However, clinically significant cases may be life threatening. The coexistence of both entities, particularly associated with SVA pseudoaneurysm, is exceedingly rare. In this case, hemostasis of the rectus sheath hematoma was successfully achieved by TAE; however, the bladder flap hematoma due to a pseudoaneurysm of the SVA could not be controlled by surgery and TAE. TVUS-guided direct puncture followed by embolization with the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture successfully treated the pseudoaneurysm.

Although small bladder flap hematomas may be detected on imaging, clinically significant cases requiring intervention are uncommon [2, 3]. They occur due to insufficient hemostasis and bleeding at the incision site between the lower uterine segment and the bladder [2, 3]. In this case, injury to the SVA associated with bladder injury during cesarean section resulted in a pseudoaneurysm accompanied by bladder flap hematoma. Bladder flap hematoma is typically associated with injury to the uterine vessels; however, injury to the vesical artery as an underlying cause is extremely rare [2, 3]. The reported incidence of bladder injury during cesarean delivery ranges from 0.08% to 0.94% [4], with previous cesarean section and adhesions recognized as significant risk factors [5]. In our case, dense adhesions around the uterus and bladder from previous cesarean deliveries may have contributed to injury to the SVA during intraoperative dissection.

In this case, surgical hemostasis failed to identify the SVA pseudoaneurysm, and although TAE was subsequently performed, adequate hemostasis could not be achieved. Several factors may account for inadequate hemostasis following TAE. First, difficulty in advancing the microcatheter to the distal site was a major limitation. In this case, the right SVA branched from the right internal iliac artery at a steep angle and exhibited marked tortuosity. Selective catheterization of the SVA at its origin was achieved using a triple coaxial system; however, distal advancement was not possible. Previous reports suggested that steerable high-flow microcatheters may improve selectivity and distal accessibility in vessels with sharp angulation and severe tortuosity [6], and such an approach might have been beneficial in our case. In addition, other technical elements, including microcatheter profile and flexibility, guidewire characteristics (e.g., tip stiffness and torque control), guiding catheter shape and stability, and coaxial support, may influence distal access [7, 8]. The use of advanced support techniques, such as ten-wire or flow-directed microcatheters, may improve distal navigability in anatomically challenging vessels [9]. Second, abundant collateral circulation within the bladder wall may have limited embolization effectiveness. Although the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture was injected proximally into the right SVA, blood flow into the pseudoaneurysm was maintained via collateral supply from the left SVA, resulting in incomplete embolization. Bilateral vesical artery embolization has been reported to be effective for intractable hematuria [10] but was not performed in this case because multiple small branches of the vesical artery were involved. When superselective embolization is technically challenging, bilateral vesical embolization should be avoided due to ischemic complications such as bladder necrosis [11].

In this case, abdominal ultrasonography was initially performed to evaluate the feasibility of percutaneous direct puncture; however, visualization of the pseudoaneurysm was limited due to a rectus sheath hematoma, and the lesion was located too deep from the body surface. Therefore, the percutaneous approach was considered technically difficult. In contrast, TVUS provided clear visualization of the pseudoaneurysm arising from the SVA, enabling direct puncture and embolization. Although several reports describe successful hemostasis using TVUS-guided direct puncture and thrombin injection for uterine artery pseudoaneurysms following cesarean section [12-14], to the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have described TVUS-guided direct puncture for an SVA pseudoaneurysm. The TVUS-guided approach offers advantages because the uterus and bladder are anatomically adjacent, allowing easier access, and visualization of the lesion is less affected by subcutaneous hematoma or bowel gas. Nevertheless, the puncture route is limited, and thus the indication for this technique should be carefully considered. In this case, TVUS-guided direct puncture embolization was successfully performed through close collaboration between interventional radiologists and obstetricians. Although this was an off-label use, an oocyte retrieval needle was employed, as the performing obstetrician was proficient in its use. While some previous reports have described the use of 22-gauge needles [12, 13], we selected a 20-gauge needle not only for its stiffness but also to enhance ultrasound visibility, facilitating the precise puncture to the target lesion by penetrating the bladder as well as adhesive tissues. Generally, the transvaginal approach requires a longer needle than the percutaneous approach due to the vaginal canal. While previous reports on the TVUS-guided direct puncture treatment of uterine artery pseudoaneurysms used 25- to 30-cm needles, we selected a 35-cm needle due to the deeper location of the target SVA compared to the uterine artery. Furthermore, because the lithotomy position and the constrained puncture route restricted the operator’s working space, an extension tube was used to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure and ensure stable embolization. Unlike previous reports using thrombin [12-14], the NBCA–Lipiodol mixture was selected because it is compatible with intravascular injection, and its distribution could be monitored under fluoroscopy, which was considered advantageous for safe treatment.

Rectus sheath hematoma is an uncommon complication associated with cesarean section, although its exact incidence remains unknown. The transverse incision crossing the rectus abdominis muscle may contribute to injury to the IEA and its branches [3, 15]. While rectus sheath hematoma is frequently self-limiting, it can result in severe complications, with an overall reported mortality rate of 4% [16]. Although alternative approaches, such as laparotomy, ultrasound-guided percutaneous thrombin injection for pseudoaneurysms, and ultrasound-guided compression, have been reported to be effective [15-17], TAE is commonly employed for hemostasis of rectus sheath hematoma with a high hemostasis rate of 90% [18]. In this case, successful hemostasis was achieved using a combination of microcoils and an NBCA–Lipiodol mixture.

In this case, NBCA–Lipiodol ratios were tailored to hemodynamic conditions and access routes. During TAE of the IEA, a moderately concentrated mixture was used to achieve effective cast formation in the nonselectable distal branch after flow redistribution with coils. Conversely, a diluted mixture was chosen for the SVA to facilitate distal penetration from a proximal site where superselective access was unfeasible. Furthermore, for TVUS-guided direct puncture, a concentrated mixture was selected to ensure rapid polymerization within the high-flow pseudoaneurysm, minimizing nontarget embolization risk. In embolization using an NBCA–Lipiodol mixture, the selection of the NBCA–Lipiodol ratio must be carefully adjusted based on flow dynamics and vessel characteristics to ensure both safety and efficacy [19].

This case suggests that TAE was effective for IEA injury in which the bleeding source could not be identified surgically. Furthermore, for the SVA pseudoaneurysm, for which both surgery and TAE failed to achieve hemostasis, a TVUS-guided approach, when anatomically feasible, may serve as a valuable therapeutic alternative.
