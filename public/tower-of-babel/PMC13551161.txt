When Care Becomes Digital: Shared Experiences in Psychiatric Nursing—A Phenomenological Study
Authors: Fatih Şahin, Özlem Ulaşoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis qualitative study explores how digitalisation primarily through electronic health records and digital documentation systems is experienced by psychiatric nurses and patients within a state hospital in eastern Turkey, with particular attention to its perceived influence on professional identity, the therapeutic relationship, and ethical responsibilities. References to artificial intelligence in this paper reflect participants' anticipatory perceptions rather than documented routine AI use in this setting.MethodsUsing a qualitative, phenomenologically informed approach, data were collected from 16 psychiatric nurses and 14 patients in a Turkish state hospital through semi‐structured interviews and analysed using Colaizzi's seven‐step descriptive phenomenological method.FindingsThe findings suggest separate themes for nurses and patients, followed by three integrated themes reflecting their shared experiences: ‘Identity Under the Pressure of Digitalisation: The Silent Rewriting of the Professional Role,’ ‘The Fragility of the Therapeutic Relationship in Screen‐Mediated Care,’ and ‘The Digital Trust Paradox: Between Privacy, Surveillance, and Responsibility.’ Participants described that digitalisation was associated with increased efficiency and professional visibility, while also being experienced as contributing to more structured and technically oriented care practices, perceived reductions in empathic engagement, and concerns related to trust and privacy.ConclusionOverall, the findings suggest that digitalisation in psychiatric nursing is experienced as extending beyond technical efficiency to include changes in how professional identity and therapeutic relationships are perceived. Participants described that digital practices may be experienced as introducing more technical and screen‐mediated elements into care, which may be perceived as limiting empathic engagement and aspects of the therapeutic relationship. In addition, participants reported concerns related to digital trust, including issues of privacy, surveillance, and responsibility. Specifically, participants described a perceived shift from relational to technical care, a ‘digital trust paradox’ in which surveillance and accountability coexist with distrust, and a concern that screen‐mediated work distances nurses from the humanistic core of their role. These findings highlight the importance of developing context‐sensitive and ethically informed approaches to the use of digital systems, rather than assuming widespread or routine use of artificial intelligence in clinical practice.Keywords: digitalisation, ethics, mental health nursing, professional identity, therapeutic relationship

Introduction

The widespread adoption of digital technologies in healthcare is reshaping the organization of care processes, the roles of health professionals, and patient experiences (Meskó et al. 2017). For the purposes of this paper, digitalisation refers broadly to the integration of electronic health records, digital communication platforms, and data‐driven clinical tools into everyday care settings not to artificial intelligence specifically, which remains limited in routine in‐patient psychiatric practice. This shift cannot be understood solely as a technical development, particularly in psychiatric nursing, where care is inherently relational. Digitalisation can be understood as a sociotechnical context that shapes how care is experienced within clinical interactions (Baxter and Sommerville 2011). It is worth noting that protocol‐driven and screen‐mediated work in in‐patient settings predates current AI applications; nurses have long been observed spending considerable time on documentation and administrative tasks rather than direct patient contact (Lakeman 2020). In Turkish state hospitals, psychiatric care is predominantly delivered within physician‐led, protocol‐driven structures. Nurses' roles are largely defined by institutional documentation requirements, medication management, and procedural tasks rather than autonomous therapeutic intervention. The recent integration of hospital information management systems (HIMS) has further formalised documentation obligations, making screen‐mediated work a dominant feature of daily nursing practice.

Psychiatric nursing is grounded not only in the delivery of care, but in its mutual experience and shared enactment. Accordingly, the integration of digital technologies into psychiatric care settings may influence nurses' professional practices and patients' experiences of care (Yıldız 2025). While Watson's Theory of Human Caring provides a useful conceptual lens for understanding relational dimensions of care (Watson 1985), it is acknowledged that its application to inpatient psychiatric nursing where nurses' roles are largely institutionally defined requires contextual qualification. Rather than asserting what psychiatric nursing ought to be, this study draws on Watson's framework as a sensitizing concept to foreground how participants themselves experience and value relational engagement.

Digitalisation may also influence the professional identities of psychiatric nurses. Professional identity constitutes a dynamic constellation of meanings that nurses attribute to their roles, values, and ethical responsibilities (Hinkley et al. 2023). Social Identity Theory proposes that professional identity is formed through group affiliation and intergroup differentiation (Tajfel and Turner 1986); individuals derive a sense of self partly from their membership of professional groups and from the meanings shared within those groups. In this context, digital tools may prompt nurses to reconsider aspects of their professional roles and interactions with patients (Johnson et al. 2012).

The integration of digital systems into clinical practice increasingly positions nurses as participants in algorithmic decision‐making processes (Knop et al. 2024). However, this shift does not imply a reduction of professional identity in psychiatric nursing to purely technological functions. Rather, nurses emerge as active agents who interpret digital outputs, negotiate their boundaries, and safeguard the continuity of human‐centred care (Knop et al. 2024; Nashwan et al. 2025). Role Theory proposes that social behaviour is shaped by the expectations and obligations associated with recognized social positions (Biddle 1986). Within this framework, digitalisation can be understood as altering the role expectations attached to psychiatric nursing: expanding certain functions (e.g., data management, digital communication) while potentially blurring others (e.g., direct relational care), thereby influencing nurses' perceptions of their own professional autonomy, responsibility, and ethical sensitivity. It should be noted that Biddle's (1986) foundational work predates the widespread adoption of the internet, let alone AI; its propositions are therefore applied here at a conceptual level rather than as direct empirical claims about digital technologies. This transformation is closely related not only to how nurses experience and negotiate their professional identities, but also to how patients perceive and interpret nursing care within digitally mediated clinical contexts.

The therapeutic relationship, as one of the core elements of psychiatric nursing, acquires particular significance within the context of digitalisation. Trust, empathy, and emotional support lie at the heart of psychiatric care and fundamentally shape the quality of the nurse–patient relationship (Moreno‐Poyato et al. 2021). Classical nursing theory including Peplau's model of interpersonal relations has long positioned the nurse–patient relationship as the site of therapeutic work. While nurses in contemporary inpatient settings may not formally apply such frameworks, these traditions inform the normative expectations against which digitally mediated changes in care are experienced and evaluated (Peplau 1997). Nevertheless, the theory's emphasis on reciprocal interaction and the relational dimensions of nursing provides a relevant conceptual anchor for examining how digitally mediated environments may alter the conditions under which therapeutic relationships are established and sustained. Although the notion of a digital therapeutic alliance has gained attention in this context, the extent to which artificial intelligence–supported systems can foster or undermine empathy and trust remains a subject of ongoing debate (Malouin‐Lachance et al. 2025). From a relational ethics perspective, digitally mediated care environments reshape the therapeutic relationship not only at the level of communication, but also in ethical terms (Bergum and Dossetor 2005). While some studies suggest that digital interventions may weaken empathic engagement (Berryhill et al. 2019), others indicate that such systems can enhance the care process by supporting nurses' clinical responses (Kearns et al. 2024). However, much of the existing literature provides limited qualitative insight into how digitalisation is actually experienced by both nurses and patients within everyday clinical practice (El‐Abidi et al. 2024).

Digitalisation also brings the ethical dimensions of psychiatric nursing into sharper relief. It is important to distinguish between routine digital practices and less common AI‐driven decision‐making systems in clinical settings. Core ethical principles such as privacy, autonomy, nonmaleficence, and justice require reconsideration as digital systems become more embedded in care processes particularly where those systems involve automated or data‐driven functions (American Nurses Association 2022; Topaz and Pruinelli 2017). In the field of mental health, the heightened vulnerability of patients further accentuates concerns related to digital trust, surveillance, and responsibility. In this context, the primary concern is not algorithmic decision‐making alone, but the broader ethical implications of digital record‐keeping, surveillance, and the reduced visibility of clinical reasoning in mental health settings. The literature identifies data bias, lack of transparency, and accountability challenges as significant ethical risks associated with digital health technologies (Beauchamp and Childress 2019; Dehbozorgi et al. 2025). Studies have indicated that the lack of user control in speech‐based AI systems may lead to ethical violations, and that large language models have the potential to generate stigmatizing content (Chen et al. 2025; Moore et al. 2025). In contrast, Pandey (2024) acknowledges the potential benefits of AI systems but emphasizes the need for clearly defined boundaries regarding privacy and accuracy (Pandey 2024). From an ethical governance perspective relevant to psychiatric nursing practice, the concept of algorithmic accountability has been proposed as a critical safeguard to ensure that clinical judgement, moral responsibility, and human oversight remain central in AI‐assisted care (Floridi et al. 2018). Accordingly, enhancing nurses' digital ethical literacy skills and maintaining human oversight are critically important for ensuring ethical safety.

The preceding discussion points to three interconnected gaps in the existing literature, which together provide the rationale for the present study. First, research on artificial intelligence and digital transformation has largely been situated within the broader framework of health informatics, while discipline‐specific analyses that centre on the unique context of mental health nursing, particularly its relational care orientation and the characteristics of the therapeutic process, remain limited (Milasan and Scott‐Purdy 2025). Mental health nursing is inherently a relational field: its core therapeutic work is enacted through direct interpersonal contact, and its distinctive professional identity is constituted through that relational practice (Knop et al. 2024; Milasan and Scott‐Purdy 2025). However, a substantial literature has documented the erosion of this professional identity among mental health nurses, attributed variously to deinstitutionalisation, workforce restructuring, and the diminished recognition of nursing's distinctive therapeutic contribution (Hurley 2009; Happell 2014) a concern that the present study extends to the domain of digitalisation. Accordingly, the experiences and meanings associated with digitalisation need to be examined through approaches that integrate both nurse and patient perspectives. Second, most existing studies focus on only one dimension of digitalisation—technical, operational, or ethical—highlighting a lack of integrated and multidimensional frameworks that address the interrelated dynamics of professional identity, the therapeutic relationship, and ethical principles within psychiatric nursing (ElArab et al. 2025). Third, the clinical impacts of artificial intelligence are frequently confined to protocol‐based or controlled experimental scenarios, with limited qualitative and contextual analyses that capture how digital care practices are experienced by nurses and individuals living with mental health conditions in real‐world clinical settings (Hassan and El‐Ashry 2024).

Against this background, the present study aims to explore how digitalisation—understood within this Turkish state hospital context as the routine use of electronic health records, HIMS‐based documentation, and screen‐mediated communication—is experienced by psychiatric nurses and individuals receiving psychiatric care, with particular attention to its perceived influence on professional identity, the therapeutic relationship, and ethical responsibilities. Specifically, the study examines perceived influences on professional identity, the therapeutic relationship, and ethical responsibilities, drawing on participants' own accounts as the primary analytical resource. The study draws on concepts from interpersonal nursing theory, caring science, and role theory as interpretive lenses, while remaining grounded in the participants' own accounts of their experiences.

Method

Study Design

This study was designed as qualitative research based on a phenomenological approach. A methodological note is warranted regarding the inclusion of two distinct participant groups: mental health nurses and patients within a single Colaizzi‐based phenomenological analysis. Colaizzi's method was originally formulated for investigating a single, homogeneous experiential group. However, a growing body of phenomenological nursing research has employed parallel dual‐group designs in which the same phenomenon is explored from two complementary subject positions: here, the provider and the recipient of care, with each group analysed independently before a synthesized structural description is derived (Caelli 2001; Connelly 2010). This approach was adopted because digitalisation in psychiatric nursing is inherently a relational phenomenon: its experiential meaning cannot be fully apprehended from either the nurse's or the patient's perspective alone. Analysing the two groups in parallel, while maintaining the integrity of each group's independent phenomenological stream prior to integration, preserves the methodological rigour of Colaizzi's approach while capturing the relational totality of the phenomenon under study. Where the two groups' experiential accounts converged, this convergence was treated as indicative of shared experiential structures; where they diverged, divergence was retained and reported as a constitutive feature of the phenomenon rather than a methodological inconsistency (Morrow et al. 2015).

Study Population and Sample

The study population consisted of psychiatric nurses working in a state hospital located in eastern Turkey and individuals receiving psychiatric treatment at the same institution. The sample was determined using criterion sampling, a type of purposive sampling method. Participant selection was based on individuals who met predetermined criteria aligned with the aim of the study. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached defined as the point at which new data no longer contributed meaningful variation and repeated codes began to emerge—with 16 participants in the nurse group and 14 participants in the patient group.

The institutional context of the study is relevant to interpreting the findings. Within the study setting, nurses' daily responsibilities primarily encompassed medication administration, digital documentation via the hospital information management system, patient observation, and coordination with physicians. Autonomous therapeutic or psychotherapeutic interventions were not a formally designated component of the nursing role in this institution.

In the Turkish healthcare system, psychiatric nursing is not regulated as a distinct, legally protected specialty with a separate licensure category. Nurses working in psychiatric settings are registered nurses who acquire relevant competencies through clinical experience and in‐service training. In this study, the term ‘mental health nurse’ refers to nurses employed in psychiatric units within the study institution.

Data Collection Tools

Descriptive Information Form

Developed by the researchers to collect demographic information such as participants' age, gender, educational background, duration of employment or treatment, and experience with digital health applications.

Semi‐Structured Interview Form

In the study, two separate semi structured interview forms were developed for psychiatric nurses and mental health service users. The nurse interview form includes open‐ended questions addressing the impact of digital technologies and artificial intelligence on professional identity, therapeutic relationships, clinical decision‐making, ethical responsibilities, and data privacy. The patient interview form explores experiences with digital health applications, nurse–patient communication, empathy, trust, and perceptions of privacy. The questions were developed based on a literature review and revised according to the feedback of three experts experienced in psychiatric nursing. Within the context of the study setting, ‘digital interaction’ refers to the use of hospital information management systems (e.g., electronic health records), digital documentation practices, and, where applicable, screen‐mediated communication processes that shape nurse patient interaction. These systems are routinely used in state hospitals in Turkey and constitute the primary digital interface through which care processes are organized and recorded.

Although some interview questions are formulated in a reflective manner (e.g., ‘what are your thoughts’), all interviews were conducted using phenomenological interviewing techniques that encouraged participants to move beyond abstract opinions and describe concrete lived experiences. Probing questions such as ‘Can you describe a specific situation?’, ‘How did you experience this in practice?’, and ‘What was that experience like for you?’ were used consistently to elicit detailed experiential accounts. Thus, the analysis was grounded in participants' lived experiences rather than general attitudes or evaluations.

Data Collection Process

The research was conducted between October and December 2025. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and the principles of confidentiality; both written and verbal consent were obtained. Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face in a quiet and private setting where participants felt comfortable. Prior to each interview, a 5–10 min introductory conversation was held with the participant, which was not included in the analysis. Semi‐structured interviews lasted between 15 and 45 min. The variation in duration reflected differences in individual participants' communicative style and the richness of their accounts rather than inconsistencies in the interview protocol; shorter interviews typically occurred with patients who were less forthcoming, and these contextual differences were documented in the researcher's field notes and taken into account during analysis. All interviews were audio‐recorded with participants' explicit consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim by the research team. Transcripts were checked against audio recordings for accuracy prior to analysis.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The study was conducted by a research team with expertise in psychiatric nursing and qualitative research methodology. Interviews were carried out by the second author; data analysis and coding were conducted collaboratively across the team. Researcher reflexivity was treated not as a supplementary quality measure but as an integral methodological commitment throughout the study (Finlay 2002). The researchers maintained ongoing awareness of how their professional backgrounds, prior clinical experience, and theoretical assumptions might shape both the conduct of interviews and the interpretation of data. A bracketing process was employed from the outset: each researcher documented their prior assumptions about digitalisation and nursing practice in a reflexive journal maintained throughout the study, and these were revisited at key analytical decision points to ensure that emerging interpretations remained grounded in participants' accounts rather than researchers' expectations (Tufford and Newman 2012). Regular team meetings served as a forum for discussing interpretive disagreements and reaching consensual analytical decisions.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using Colaizzi's (1978) seven‐step descriptive phenomenological method (Colaizzi 1978). Colaizzi's approach was selected because it provides a transparent, auditable analytical process that moves systematically from raw participant statements to the essential structure of the lived experience, making it both rigorous and well suited to the nursing research context (Shosha 2012). Nurse and patient data were analysed in parallel streams before being integrated into a unified structural description, allowing both convergences and divergences across the two groups to be identified (Morrow et al. 2015; Smith et al. 2021). Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al. 2007).

Researcher Reflexivity

Researcher reflexivity in phenomenological inquiry goes beyond the management of ‘bias’: it is a positive methodological resource through which the researcher's situatedness, their professional background, clinical assumptions, and theoretical commitments are made explicit and used to deepen interpretive awareness (Finlay and Gough 2003). In this study, reflexivity was operationalized at three levels. At the pre‐analytical level, each researcher documented their assumptions about the impact of digitalisation on nursing practice prior to entering the field, establishing a baseline against which evolving interpretations could be tested. At the analytical level, reflexive journaling recorded the reasoning behind key coding and clustering decisions, creating a transparent interpretive audit trail. At the post‐analytical level, team discussions reviewed whether the fundamental structure of the experience had been derived from participants' accounts or inadvertently shaped by researchers' prior frameworks. This multi‐level reflexive process is consistent with best practice in phenomenological nursing research (Dowling 2007; Koch 1995).

Theme 1—Identity Erosion and Redefinition

This theme reveals how nurses described the interplay between routine digital systems and, where referenced, AI‐related imaginaries on professional identity in mental health nursing both reinforcing and eroding it. While digital records and traceability enhance the perception of professionalism, screen‐centered modes of work were experienced as pushing the humanistic and contact‐based essence of nursing into the background. This theme indicates that while digitalization was described as streamlining certain processes (e.g., documentation and coordination) that shape the flow and timing of encounters, it simultaneously constrains its emotional depth. Nurses' narratives reveal that empathy, closeness, and emotional connection are strengthened through face‐to‐face interaction, whereas reliance on digital systems and what participants described as protocol‐, checklist‐, or system‐generated prompts (and, in some accounts, ‘algorithmic/AI‐like’ guidance) renders the relationship more superficial and mechanistic. Patient Themes

Theme 1—Empathy and the Digital Test of Nursing Identity

Participants define empathy as a foundational element of nursing identity, emphasizing that it gains meaning through face‐to‐face interaction, physical presence, and contextual sensitivity. Participants reported that screen‐mediated and record‐focused practices can limit nonverbal cues that convey empathic engagement, thereby pushing the humanistic dimension of nursing into the background and contributing to a perceived shift in nursing identity from a relational profession toward a more technical and distanced role. Some participants additionally framed AI as a hypothetical extension of current digitalisation and expressed anticipatory concerns about how care might feel if responses were to become more AI‐mediated. This shift leads patients to perceive nurses less as empathic caregivers and more as functional implementers; the weakening of the therapeutic bond established through empathy consequently redefines the meaning and visibility of professional identity (Table 4).

Discussion

The rise of digitalization in health care may be experienced as shaping nurses' professional identity in ways that are deeper and more complex than what is visible on the surface. In the present study setting, digitalisation primarily referred to screen‐mediated documentation and digital recordkeeping; participants' references to artificial intelligence are interpreted as anticipatory concerns rather than accounts of routine clinical AI use (Lakeman 2020; Poissant et al. 2005). As Lakeman (2008) observed, the institutionally mandated requirements to document, diagnose, and frame clinical encounters within prescribed systems are not merely administrative burdens; they fundamentally shape how nurses inhabit their professional world.

In the present study, nurse participants reported that innovations such as digital record systems make their professional practice appear more ‘professional’; however, these tools were described by some participants as being associated with a gradual re‐orientation of their professional identity. Similarly, the literature emphasizes that technological tools can create barriers in nurse–patient interactions, which have been associated with reduced empathy, and that this process may weaken professional autonomy and moral agency (Dumbre et al. 2025; Knop et al. 2024; Taan et al. 2025). This pattern aligns with earlier accounts of identity erosion in mental health nursing, where structural and organizational constraints have long been identified as factors that distance nurses from the relational work they consider central to their professional role (Hurley 2009). Nevertheless, participants described that this new identity perception strengthened by technology also triggers concerns about the loss of humanity and professional expertise; this dilemma points to a perceived shift in which nurses' professional roles are being silently rewritten (Knop et al. 2024).

In the present study, nurse participants reported that screen‐mediated care was experienced as creating a sense that the therapeutic relationship is weakened and that care becomes mechanized. A review of the literature indicates that in telecare and artificial intelligence–supported applications, nurses have reported findings pointing to a weakening of the therapeutic relationship (Abou Hashish 2025; Bodur et al. 2025; Cao et al. 2025; Yıldız 2025). Our findings suggest that participants perceived this interaction as potentially weakened in screen‐mediated care; where ‘artificial intelligence’ was mentioned, it typically reflected anticipatory concerns rather than documented routine AI‐mediated encounters in this inpatient setting (unspecified). In line with the literature, it is emphasized that digital empathy skills need to be developed in order to preserve the human dimension of digital care (Abou Hashish 2025; Yıldız 2025).

In the present study, nurse participants stated that concerns regarding data privacy, surveillance, and AI‐supported decision‐making and routine digital record‐keeping were perceived as contributing to an environment of ethical vulnerability. Although nurses believe that digital records increase accountability in care, they emphasized that anxiety about unauthorized sharing of patient information or cyber risks may undermine trust. A review of the literature indicates that, despite artificial intelligence and digital health systems enhancing transparency and traceability in clinical decision‐making and care processes, uncertainties regarding data security breaches, secondary data use, and the ethical responsibility of algorithmic decisions may create a fragile ground affecting nurses' professional autonomy, patient trust, and ethical sensitivity (Abuhammad 2025; Stokes 2025; Wallace 2018). In conclusion, our findings reveal that this environment of ‘ethical vulnerability,’ shaped by surveillance and data use, may carry the risk of weakening the bond of trust in patient–nurse relationships.

In this study, patient participants reported that they place physical presence and face‐to‐face interaction at the center of the therapeutic relationship in mental health care. The findings suggest that screen‐mediated encounters and/or the prospect of digitally mediated contact (unspecified whether routinely experienced in this ward) are perceived by patients as lacking sincerity, being distracting, and making it difficult to establish an emotional bond. A review of the literature indicates that the therapeutic alliance is a fundamental determinant of treatment adherence and clinical outcomes in mental health care; despite the potential of digital mental health services to ensure continuity, the absence of body language, eye contact, and physical presence may weaken the empathic bond and render the therapeutic relationship more fragile compared to face‐to‐face interaction (Berryhill et al. 2019; Bordin 1979; Horvath et al. 2011; Shore et al. 2018). In this context, the findings of the study reveal that although digital care is technically accessible, it is not perceived by patients as a ‘real’ and ‘serious’ therapeutic experience and limits the quality of the relationship.

In the study, it was determined that patient participants experienced intense concerns regarding privacy and data security in digital mental health services. Participants stated that uncertainty about who can access the personal and sensitive information they share limits their participation in the therapeutic process. A review of the literature indicates that confidentiality is one of the fundamental ethical components of the therapeutic trust relationship in mental health care; however, practices related to data storage, record‐keeping, and third‐party access on digital platforms strengthen patients' perceptions of being constantly monitored, reduce self‐disclosure, and weaken therapeutic openness (American Psychiatric Association 1996; Graham et al. 2019; Lupton 2014; Wies et al. 2021). The findings of this study are consistent with the literature in suggesting that, for some patients, rather than enhancing feelings of trust, digital records create a perception of threat for some patients and may weaken therapeutic openness.

In this study, some patient participants expressed distance and distrust toward artificial intelligence in a subjective and emotional field such as mental health. Artificial intelligence–supported systems are perceived by participants as ‘emotionless’ and as structures that ‘cannot fully understand the human being,’ and the guidance of clinical decisions by algorithms creates discomfort. A review of the literature indicates that while artificial intelligence provides technical contributions to diagnostic and monitoring processes in mental health, it remains limited in situations requiring empathy, ethical reasoning, and contextual understanding; therefore, patients tend to favour human experts over algorithms in high‐risk and emotionally charged clinical decisions (Longoni et al. 2019; Topol 2019; Yıldız 2025). These findings suggest that, if introduced, artificial intelligence may be best positioned as a supportive tool in mental health care; human‐based decision‐making and the therapeutic relationship should not be replaced.

In the present study, participants articulated perceived changes in the role of the nursing profession alongside digitalization as a striking shared theme by both nurse and patient participants. Nurses stated that conducting care processes in accordance with digital protocols limits professional autonomy and makes them feel like data managers, whereas patients reported that the figure of the nurse is increasingly transforming into a more technical, distant, and system‐oriented role. This situation is described in the literature as the ‘silent rewriting of professional identity.’ (Knop et al. 2024; Navarro Martínez and Leyva‐Moral 2024; Pepito and Locsin 2019). It has been reported that while digital systems increase professional visibility, they may cause human contact in nursing to recede into the background. This may be experienced as a nurse becoming more of a component of the system rather than a therapeutic caregiver (Pepito and Locsin 2019; Yıldız 2025). In this context, this point of rupture where nurse and patient experiences intersect suggests that participants experienced digitalisation as affecting not only technical workflows but also the perceived meaning of caring work and professional presence in inpatient settings.

In this study, both nurse and patient participants described digital environments as creating serious disruptions in the process of establishing an empathic bond. Nurses reported that empathy is weakened in screen‐mediated interviews due to the loss of nonverbal communication channels such as tactile contact, facial expressions, and eye contact. Similarly, patients stated that the sense of ‘genuine interest’ decreases in digital communication, triggering feelings of loneliness and not being understood. The literature has reached similar conclusions, showing that the limitation of face‐to‐face communication renders the humanistic dimension of nursing invisible and mechanizes the therapeutic relationship. In particular, emotional closeness, trust, and empathic listening are emphasized as being nurtured through face‐to‐face interaction (Abou Hashish 2025; Al‐Khafaji and Al‐Samarrai 2025; Knop et al. 2024). The literature suggests that the conveniences offered by digital technologies may carry the risk of overshadowing the emotional depth and humanistic quality of care.

One of the most prominent shared concerns among participants regarding the digital care environment is the vulnerability of data security and privacy. While nurses carry the ethical responsibility for information recorded within digital systems, patients demonstrate withdrawal and silence due to uncertainty about who may access these data. Research has likewise shown that compromised perceptions of privacy in digital health services negatively affect patient trust and openness (Alhammad et al. 2024; Knop et al. 2024). This dual surveillance of patients by nurses, and of nurses by the institution may contribute to the ethical vulnerability described by participants (Wallace 2018). Moreover, participants' accounts and the literature raise the possibility that the integration of artificial intelligence systems into decision‐making processes generates ethical ambiguity regarding the distribution of responsibility, thereby increasing nurses' sense of burden and fear of error (Stokes 2025). From a shared perspective, sustaining trust in digital care environments requires transparent data governance, education in ethical responsibility, and the design of systems that are structured around a human‐centered approach (Mondal and Mondal 2024).

Conclusion

This study suggests that digitalisation in mental health nursing practice may be associated with a multilayered transformation that affects not only operational processes but also professional identity, ethical responsibility, and the dynamics of the therapeutic relationship. The findings suggest that while digital systems have the potential to enhance professional visibility and organizational order, this process may also lead to a weakening of the humanistic and contact‐based dimensions of the nursing role.

While maintaining the therapeutic relationship in digital environments offers advantages in terms of accessibility and continuity, it may also constrain core elements such as empathy and emotional bonding. Both nurse and patient perspectives reveal shared concerns regarding privacy and ethical responsibility, indicating that trust in digital systems is closely related to system design and the extent to which a human‐centred approach is adopted.

In this context, approaching digitalisation not solely as a technological integration, but as a process that must actively preserve the relational, ethical, and humanistic core of nursing practice, remains essential. Ultimately, this study contributes to a longstanding argument within psychiatric nursing: that high‐touch, relationally grounded care cannot be fully substituted by high‐tech efficiency. As digital systems become more deeply embedded in clinical environments, preserving the conditions for genuine human encounter in which empathy, presence, and therapeutic relationship remain central may be one of the most pressing challenges facing mental health nursing practice.

Limitations of the Study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the research was conducted in a single psychiatric care setting, which may limit the transferability of the results to other clinical or cultural contexts. Second, although the sample size was appropriate for phenomenological inquiry and data saturation was achieved, the findings may not reflect the full diversity of experiences across different diagnoses or digital health technologies. Third, given that the majority of nurse participants were employed within a physician‐led institutional structure and had no professional experience predating electronic documentation systems, their accounts necessarily reflect a digitally embedded baseline rather than a comparative perspective on change over time. This limits the extent to which findings can speak to how digitalisation has altered nursing practice, as opposed to how it currently shapes it. Finally, the study relied on self‐reported interview data, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

This study suggests that digitalisation in mental health nursing is associated with changes in how care processes, professional roles, and therapeutic relationships are experienced by both nurses and patients. Participants' accounts indicate that digital practices may be perceived as limiting the human and relational aspects of care, while also introducing more structured and technical elements into nursing practice.

These findings highlight the importance of supporting mental health nurses in navigating digital systems in ways that preserve empathy, relational engagement, and patient trust. Rather than assuming widespread use of artificial intelligence in clinical practice, the results point to the need for guidance and education focused on the ethical and relational use of existing digital systems. In addition, incorporating feedback from both nurses and patients may contribute to more context‐sensitive and human‐centred implementation of digital technologies in mental health care.

Recommendations

Based on participants' reported experiences and perceptions, it may be beneficial to support the human‐centred use of digital systems in mental health nursing practice. In this context, professional development initiatives focusing on maintaining empathy, emotional connection, and therapeutic engagement within digitally mediated environments could be considered. Educational approaches that support nurses' relational roles, alongside their technical responsibilities, may also contribute to more balanced care practices.

Participants' concerns regarding privacy and data security suggest the importance of clear and transparent communication with patients about how digital information is recorded and used. In addition, rather than assuming widespread use of artificial intelligence, it may be useful to strengthen nurses' general clinical decision‐making and ethical awareness in relation to digital systems. Finally, institutional mechanisms that incorporate nurses' and patients' feedback may help to better understand how digitalisation is experienced in practice and how it can be aligned with the relational nature of mental health care.
