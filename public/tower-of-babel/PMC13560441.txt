Evaluation of metal ion retention in tooth structure following the removal of dental amalgam restorations: an ex vivo study
Authors: Musa Acartürk, Seda Gürsoy, Ibrahim Filazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the persistence of metal ions within tooth structure after the removal of dental amalgam restorations and to assess the relationship between metal accumulation and restoration size.MethodsSixty extracted molar teeth were divided into four groups: control (intact teeth), and teeth with one-, two-, and three-surface amalgam restorations. After storage in artificial saliva, the restorations were removed, and the samples were subjected to acid digestion. Metal ion concentrations (Hg, Ag, Cu, Zn, Sn, Cr, Ni, Co, and Pb) were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Differences between groups were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsAll analyzed metals were detected in tooth structure, including those in the control group. Hg, Cu, Zn, Sn, Cr, Ni, and Co levels were higher in restored teeth compared to controls, whereas Ag and Pb showed higher levels in the control group. A consistent increase with restoration size was observed only for Cr and Ni, while other elements did not show a clear surface-dependent pattern.ConclusionsMetal ions associated with dental amalgam were shown to persist within tooth structure even after restoration removal. However, accumulation patterns vary among elements and are not solely dependent on restoration size. The clinical significance of these findings remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Introduction

Amalgam fillings have been used for over a century due to their strength, longevity, and affordability, despite concerns regarding mercury and other metal components [1]. Amalgam consists of approximately 50% elemental mercury and 50% alloy composed primarily of silver, copper, and tin [2]. The use of dental amalgam has been controversial since 1845, with numerous studies linking it to toxicity due to mercury’s strong affinity for proteins and amino acids [3]. Elevated mercury levels have been detected in the blood, urine, and saliva of patients with amalgam restorations, raising concerns about its accumulation in organs such as the liver, kidneys, and central nervous system (CNS) [4–8].

Autopsy studies have reported higher mercury concentrations in the brains of individuals with dental amalgam restorations compared to those without, suggesting that amalgam may represent a significant source of mercury accumulation in human tissues [9]. Emerging epidemiological data suggest possible associations between amalgam fillings and neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s [10, 11]. Mercury exposure is recognized as a major environmental health concern and has been implicated in various systemic effects, particularly neurotoxicity and cardiovascular damage, through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and direct cellular injury [12].

Other metals present in amalgam, such as tin, silver, and nickel, also accumulate in tissues, though they are less studied [13]. Trace amounts of these metals can also be acquired from dietary and environmental sources, making it important to distinguish the contribution of dental materials [14]. In response to health concerns, international agreements such as the Minamata Convention and regulatory frameworks including Regulation (EU) 2017/852 have introduced restrictions on the use of dental amalgam, with several regions, particularly in Europe, moving toward stricter limitations [15–17].

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) is a widely used analytical technique for trace metal detection and has been extensively applied in the analysis of metal ions in biological samples due to its high sensitivity and accuracy [18, 19].

Despite extensive research on mercury release from dental amalgam and its systemic distribution, limited attention has been paid to the persistence of metal ions within dental tissues following the removal of amalgam restorations. Furthermore, the potential relationship between the surface area of amalgam restorations and the degree of metal accumulation within tooth structure remains insufficiently investigated. Understanding whether these metals remain embedded in dental tissues after restoration removal, and whether restoration size influences their accumulation, is essential for interpreting the long-term implications of amalgam use.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively evaluated both the persistence of multiple metal ions within tooth structure after amalgam removal and their relationship with restoration size using ICP-MS analysis.

Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate both the persistence of metal ions within tooth structure and their potential association with restoration size.

The null hypothesis of this study was that the removal of dental amalgam restorations does not affect the presence of metal ions in tooth structure, and that there is no association between restoration size and the level of metal accumulation.

Materials and methods

This experimental ex vivo study was conducted to evaluate the diffusion of metal ions from dental amalgam restorations into tooth structure.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on previous experimental studies reporting moderate to large differences in metal ion concentrations between teeth with amalgam fillings and teeth without fillings [20, 21]. Accordingly, a conservative approach was adopted in the study, accepting a moderate-to-high effect size (f = 0.40) according to Cohen’s classification [22]. Power analysis was performed using GPower* software (version 3.1.9.7) based on one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), assuming comparisons between four independent groups [23]. When the significance level was set at α = 0.05 and the statistical power at 1–β = 0.80, a minimum of 14 tooth samples per group was calculated. To compensate for possible sample losses and analytical variation, 15 teeth were included in each group, and the total sample size was set at 60.

Sample collection and experimental design

Following approval by the Çankırı Karatekin University Ethics Committee (December 15, 2022, meeting no: 4), sixty extracted first and second molars were collected. Teeth were obtained following routine dental extractions performed for clinical indications unrelated to this study. Teeth with visible cracks, caries, restorations other than amalgam, or structural defects were excluded. Only intact teeth suitable for standardized analysis were included. To minimize variability, all samples were visually inspected and selected to ensure similar structural integrity. Informed consent was waived because the study was conducted on extracted teeth and did not involve any identifiable human participants. Each group contained 15 teeth. After all teeth were cleaned of soft tissue, plaque, and tartar, they were stored in artificial saliva at 37 °C for 22 months to simulate oral conditions and to allow sufficient time for metal diffusion into the tooth structure. This duration was selected to allow sufficient time for cumulative ion diffusion and interaction between the restorative material and dental tissues under controlled conditions. Prolonged storage has been used in previous ex vivo studies to simulate long-term material–tissue interactions and to enhance the detection of ion penetration within dental structures. After incubation, amalgam restorations were removed using a high-speed handpiece under continuous water cooling, followed by manual instruments to ensure complete removal of residual material. The procedure was performed carefully to minimize heat generation and prevent additional structural alterations to the tooth. All removal procedures were carried out under standardized conditions by the same operator to ensure consistency. The teeth were then rinsed, dried, and weighed prior to digestion.

Each tooth was digested with 8 mL of 65% HNO₃ and 2 mL of 37% H₂O₂ in TFM vessels using microwave-assisted digestion. After digestion, samples were diluted to 20 mL with ASTM Type 1 ultrapure water and filtered.

Analytical procedures

Metal concentrations were analyzed using ICP-MS (Thermo Scientific iCap QC model). Each sample was measured in triplicate. External calibration curves were generated using a range of standard solutions. Final concentrations were adjusted based on dilution factors.

The ICP-MS analysis was conducted under standardized operating conditions, including an RF power of 1550 W, the use of Cu/Ni cones, a plasma gas (Ar) flow rate of 0.88 L/min, a nebulizer gas flow rate of 0.96 L/min, a nebulizer pressure of 3.01 bar, a spray chamber temperature of 3.7 °C, and a helium gas flow rate of 4.5 L/min.

Quality assurance

The quality assurance of the analytical results was ensured by using certified reference materials, calibration with standard solutions, and performing all measurements in triplicate. The ICP-MS instrument was routinely calibrated before each analytical batch, and the accuracy and precision of the data were verified by repeated analysis of quality control samples. Blank samples were analyzed to monitor potential contamination, and all data were corrected accordingly.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the data obtained in the study was conducted using the IBM-SPSS 25.0 software package (Statistics for Windows Version 25.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Initially, normality tests were performed for each group. Since there were more than two groups and the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to evaluate differences in mean element concentrations among the groups. When statistically significant differences were found, pairwise comparisons were carried out using the Dunn test. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Normality analysis revealed that the data were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Therefore, non-parametric tests were used. Differences in metal ion concentrations among the groups were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The groups were defined as control (C), single-surface (1 S), two-surface (2 S), and three-surface (3 S) restorations. The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated statistically significant differences among the groups for all analyzed elements (p < 0.05).

Median values of Hg, Cu, Zn, Sn, Cr, Ni, and Co were higher in the restored groups compared to the control group. In contrast, Ag and Pb showed higher median values in the control group. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that all analyzed metals were detectable in tooth structure. Several elements commonly associated with dental amalgam, including Hg, Cu, Zn, Sn, Cr, Ni, and Co, were present at significantly higher levels in restored teeth compared to the control group. Pairwise comparisons further indicated that these differences were predominantly observed between the control group and the restored groups. However, significant differences were also identified among certain restoration groups, supporting the persistence of metal retention in dental tissues even after amalgam removal. The relationship between restoration size and metal accumulation was not uniform across all elements. Among the analyzed metals, Cr and Ni showed an increasing trend with increasing restoration surface area, whereas the remaining elements did not exhibit a consistent surface-dependent pattern. Overall, these findings suggest that amalgam restorations may contribute to long-term metal retention within tooth structure, although the degree of accumulation appears to vary depending on the specific element. Based on these findings, the null hypothesis that the removal of dental amalgam restorations does not affect the presence of metal ions in tooth structure was rejected. However, the null hypothesis regarding the absence of an association between restoration size and metal accumulation was only partially rejected, as an increasing trend was observed for Cr and Ni, but not for the other elements.

An important finding of the present study was the detection of all analyzed metals in the control group. This observation may be explained by background environmental exposure rather than dental restorations alone. Individuals are continuously exposed to trace amounts of metal ions through various sources, including diet, air, and water, which may lead to their gradual accumulation in dental tissues over time [19]. In addition, differences in environmental conditions and individual lifestyle factors may contribute to variations in baseline metal levels [24]. Although the possibility of contamination during sample preparation cannot be completely excluded, strict quality control procedures, including the use of blanks and standardized analytical protocols, were implemented to minimize this risk. Therefore, the presence of metals in the control group is more likely to reflect baseline exposure rather than an experimental artifact. In addition, metal ions released from other existing amalgam restorations in the oral environment may also contribute to background levels through saliva-mediated diffusion. Previous studies have demonstrated the release of mercury and other metals from amalgam into saliva [6, 25].

Similarly, control teeth may have been exposed to different intraoral conditions, particularly in the presence of other amalgam restorations, which could contribute to variability in baseline metal levels. Previous studies have focused on the release of mercury and other elements from amalgam into saliva and systemic circulation [4–8]. However, limited attention has been given to their retention within dental tissues. The present findings are consistent with earlier reports indicating that metallic elements from restorative materials can penetrate and persist within dental hard tissues over time [13, 26]. The persistence of these elements can be attributed to their physicochemical interactions with the organic and inorganic components of dentin, which facilitate ion exchange and binding processes [27]. Therefore, even after the removal of amalgam restorations, previously incorporated metal ions may remain embedded within the tooth structure. This finding supports a cumulative exposure pattern rather than transient contamination. Previous studies have demonstrated that mercury release from amalgam restorations increases over time and is influenced by the extent of restored surfaces, supporting the concept of time-dependent accumulation [25]. In this context, the duration of exposure may be an important factor, as prolonged contact between restorative materials and dental tissues can facilitate gradual ion diffusion and retention.

In addition to the overall differences observed between groups, the relationship between restoration size and metal accumulation was also evaluated. A gradual increase in the concentrations of both Cr and Ni was observed with increasing restoration surface area, suggesting that restoration extent may influence the accumulation of certain metal ions within the tooth structure. The observed pattern may be attributed to increased contact area and prolonged interaction between the amalgam material and dental tissues. These factors may further facilitate the diffusion and retention of these elements [6, 25]. However, this surface-dependent pattern was not observed for all analyzed metals, indicating that metal-specific physicochemical properties, including variations in alloy composition and microstructural interactions, may also influence their behavior within dental tissues [28]. In addition, the number of restoration surfaces may not accurately reflect the actual volume or total contact area between the restorative material and the tooth structure, as cavity dimensions can vary considerably between teeth. For example, a restoration with a greater volume involving two surfaces may present a larger contact area than a smaller three-surface restoration. Furthermore, variations in the composition of dental amalgam materials, including differences in alloy formulation and metal proportions, may influence the release and subsequent diffusion of metal ions [29]. Finally, the absence of a consistent increase in metal concentrations across all groups may be related to variations in intraoral exposure duration prior to extraction, which may have affected the extent of contact with amalgam materials and the ions released from them, thereby contributing to variability in metal diffusion and accumulation.

In contrast to most analyzed elements, Pb and Ag concentrations were higher in the control group compared to the restored groups. This finding may be attributed to background environmental exposure, dietary intake, and baseline accumulation patterns independent of dental restorations. In particular, Pb is a well-known environmental contaminant, and its presence in dental tissues may reflect cumulative exposure over time rather than a direct association with restorative materials [30, 31]. Previous studies have shown that Pb accumulates in dental tissues in an age-dependent manner, with increasing concentrations over time [19, 32]. This accumulation may be explained by the high affinity of Pb for collagen fibers and its tendency to deposit within mineralized tissues such as dentin, facilitating long-term retention [33]. In addition, environmental pollution and lifestyle factors, such as smoking, may further contribute to elevated Pb levels in dental tissues [34, 35]. Similarly, the higher Ag levels observed in the control group may not be solely explained by restorative materials. Although silver is a major component of dental amalgam, several studies have demonstrated that metal ions, including Ag, can be released from amalgam restorations over time [25]. These ions may dissolve in the oral environment and diffuse into saliva and tooth structure, with their behavior influenced by their physicochemical properties [28]. Therefore, differences in the solubility, diffusion, and binding characteristics of Ag may affect its distribution within dental tissues. In addition, low-level environmental and dietary exposure may also contribute to baseline Ag levels [36]. These findings suggest that the higher Ag and Pb levels observed in the control group cannot be attributed solely to dental restorations and should be interpreted in the context of environmental and biological factors.

Although the present study primarily focused on the detection and distribution of metal ions within tooth structure, the retention of these elements may have biological implications. Metals such as Cu and Zn have been reported to exhibit cytotoxic effects, while Ni is known to induce allergic reactions even at relatively low concentrations [37, 38]. In addition, several elements identified in this study, including Hg, Cu, Zn, Ni, and Pb, have been associated with cytotoxic, allergenic, or systemic effects in the literature [39, 40]. However, it is important to emphasize that the present findings are based on an ex vivo model and do not provide direct evidence of systemic exposure or biological impact. Therefore, although the persistence of these metals within dental tissues may raise concerns, their clinical significance remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the ex vivo design does not fully replicate the dynamic conditions of the oral environment, including salivary flow, pH fluctuations, microbial activity, and mechanical forces, all of which may affect the release, diffusion, and redistribution of metal ions. Therefore, the direct translation of these findings to clinical conditions remains limited, as in vivo environments may result in different patterns of metal behavior and tissue interaction. In addition, although the teeth were stored under controlled conditions, environmental exposure prior to extraction could not be fully standardized, which may have contributed to the presence of trace metals in the control group. Furthermore, the study focused on metal accumulation within tooth structure and did not evaluate the potential systemic distribution or biological effects of these ions, limiting the interpretation of their clinical relevance. Finally, the actual contact surface area between amalgam restorations and tooth structure was not quantitatively measured, and the classification of restorations based solely on surface number may not accurately reflect the true extent of material–tissue interaction, which could have influenced the observed patterns of metal accumulation.

Recommendations for future research

Future studies should investigate the long-term behavior of metal ions within dental tissues under in vivo conditions to better reflect the dynamic oral environment. Longitudinal clinical studies are needed to evaluate the potential release of retained metal ions into the oral cavity and to clarify their possible systemic effects over time. In addition, further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying metal diffusion and retention in dental tissues, as well as the influence of restoration types, material composition, and clinical variables on metal accumulation patterns.

Conclusions

The present study showed that multiple metal ions associated with dental amalgam, including mercury, silver, copper, zinc, tin, chromium, nickel, cobalt, and lead, can be detected within tooth structure. These ions may persist even after the removal of amalgam restorations, indicating long-term retention in dental tissues. However, it is unclear whether these retained ions are released into the oral environment or contribute to systemic exposure. Among the analyzed elements, only chromium and nickel showed an increase with restoration size. No consistent relationship was observed for the other metals. This suggests that metal-specific properties and exposure conditions may affect their accumulation. Although the persistence of these metals may raise biological concerns, their clinical significance remains unclear. Further in vivo and longitudinal studies are needed to better understand their long-term behavior, potential release, and clinical effects.
