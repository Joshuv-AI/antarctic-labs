Blinatumomab bypasses CD28 blockade to sustain T-cell cytotoxicity and improve survival in a xenograft B-ALL model
Authors: Geovana S F Leite, John Sullivan, Dimitrios Filioglou, Matina Kounelis, Helena Batatinha, Richard J Simpson, Emmanuel Katsanis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRelapse and graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) remain primary causes of treatment failure in patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT). While abatacept (ABATA) effectively mitigates GvHD via CD28-costimulation blockade, there is significant concern that it concurrently diminishes graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) effects, potentially leading to higher relapse rates. We investigated whether blinatumomab (BLINA) retains antileukemic efficacy in the presence of ABATA using in vitro assays and humanized NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(IL15)1Sz/SzJ (NSG-IL15) and NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mouse models.MethodsIn vitro and ex vivo flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity, degranulation (CD107a), cytokine production (interferon (IFN)-γ), and activation-marker (CD25, CD69, CD137, OX40) assays were performed using healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (pretreated±ABATA) against leukemia or lymphoma cell lines (RS4;11, NALM-6, and JEKO-1) in the presence or not of BLINA. In vivo human PBMC-reconstituted NSG-IL-15 mice bearing RS4;11 B-ALL xenografts were treated with vehicle, monotherapy (BLINA or ABATA), or combination therapy. Leukemia burden and immune reconstitution were assessed through week 4; GvHD scores and overall survival were monitored longitudinally.ResultsABATA did not impair BLINA-mediated cytotoxicity across multiple effector-to-target ratios against any of the cell lines, nor did it reduce BLINA-induced degranulation, IFN-γ production, or activation marker expression. In vivo, BLINA monotherapy significantly reduced leukemia burden, but resulted in early mortality due to severe GvHD. Conversely, ABATA markedly reduced GvHD severity. The combination of BLINA and ABATA preserved potent antileukemic efficacy while significantly extending median survival by 35 days compared with tumor controls (p<0.0001). Although ABATA attenuated T-cell expansion and differentiation, BLINA-driven cytotoxic function was maintained, even after prolonged in vivo exposure to cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4-immunoglobulin (CTLA-4-Ig).ConclusionThese findings provide preclinical proof-of-concept that BLINA and ABATA can be combined to uncouple GvL from GvHD. This strategy preserves CD28-independent T-cell cytotoxicity while limiting allo-reactivity, providing a strong rationale for investigating this combination in the post-transplant setting.Keywords: Bispecific T cell engager - BiTE, Graft versus host disease - GVHD, Graft versus leukemia, Leukemia, Immunotherapy

Introduction

B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is the most common leukemia in children, and it remains a critical and often aggressive malignancy in the adult population.1 Despite therapeutic advances, relapse and refractory disease remain major causes of mortality.2 Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) can be curative for high-risk patients, but graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) is a frequent complication and a leading cause of post-transplant morbidity and mortality.3 4 Post-transplant immunosuppression limits GvHD but can blunt graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) and increase relapse risk.3 5

Abatacept (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4-immunoglobulin (CTLA-4-Ig) (ABATA) is a recently approved GvHD prophylaxis agent used with calcineurin inhibitors.6 By binding CD80/CD86 and blocking CD28-mediated co-stimulation, ABATA reduces acute GvHD risk in allo-HCT.7,9 Although it dampens T-cell activation, preclinical data suggest cytotoxic function can be preserved when T cells are activated through alternative pathways, and ABATA can mitigate inflammatory toxicities without negating antitumor activity in some models.10

Blinatumomab (BLINA), a CD19×CD3 bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE), is effective across adult and pediatric CD19+ B-ALL in frontline and relapsed settings.11,15 By linking T cells to CD19+ leukemic cells, it drives activation, proliferation, and perforin/granzyme-mediated cytotoxicity.16 17 Post-HCT or following donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI), BLINA is increasingly used to prevent or treat relapse in high-risk patients.18,24

Because BLINA depends on T-cell activation and ABATA inhibits CD28 co-stimulation, their combined use raises concern about antagonistic effects. However, whether CTLA-4-Ig impairs BiTE-driven cytotoxicity remains unclear. Here, using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) reconstituted NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(IL15)1Sz/SzJ (NSG-IL15) and NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice bearing B-ALL, we test whether ABATA can suppress xeno-GvHD while preserving BLINA’s antileukemic efficacy, providing a preclinical framework for safer, effective combination therapy across adult and pediatric B-ALL.

Methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human B-ALL cell lines RS4;11 (CRL-1873; harboring the t(4;11) KMT2A rearrangement) and NALM-6 (CRL-3273), as well as the mantle cell lymphoma line JEKO-1 (CRL-3006), were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. For in vivo experiments, luciferase-expressing RS4;11 cells (RS4;11-Luc) were used. Cells were expanded for at least three passages prior to experiments and routinely tested for mycoplasma. All cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. PBMCs were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 (Cytiva, Cat# SH30027.01) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; GeminiBio, Cat# 100-500), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). RS4;11-Luc and NALM-6 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-NN'-2-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (Gibco, Cat# 15630-080), sodium pyruvate (Cytiva, SH30239.01), and P/S (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122). JEKO-1 cells were cultured in the same media formulation but supplemented with 20% FBS (GeminiBio, Cat# 100-500), 1% HEPES (Gibco, Cat# 15630-080), sodium pyruvate (Cytiva, Cat# SH30239.01), and P/S (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122) at 37°C and 5% CO2. BLINA was purchased from IchorBio (Cat# ICH5025) and ABATA (InVivoMAb recombinant human CTLA-4-Ig) was purchased from Bio X Cell (Cat# BE0099).

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assay

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors via Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and counted using a hemocytometer (Hausser Scientific Cat# 3200). PBMCs were then pre-incubated with or without ABATA (10 mg/mL) for 48 hours. Following pre-incubation, cells were washed, counted, and stimulated with interleukin (IL)-15 (1000 U/mL; Miltenyi, Cat# 130-095-760) for 1 hour to generate effector cells. Target cells (RS4;11, NALM-6, and JEKO-1) were stained for 30 min with antigen-presenting cell (APC)-conjugated anti-CD-71 (BioLegend Cat# 334108) or anti-CD20 (BioLegend Cat# 302310). Stained targets were washed, resuspended, and plated in 96-well plates with or without BLINA (66 ng/mL). Effector cells were added to achieve effector-to-target (E:T) ratios ranging from 0.6:1 to 5:1. The co-cultures were incubated for 24 hours. Four experimental conditions were assessed: PBMC alone, PBMC+BLINA, PBMC+ABATA, and PBMC+BLINA+ABATA. Immediately prior to flow cytometric analysis (BD LSR Fortessa), propidium iodide (PI; Invitrogen Cat# 00-6990-50) was added to each sample. Live target cells were defined as CD71+/PI−, while dead target cells were defined as CD71+/PI+. Target cell cytolysis was calculated, as previously described,25 using the following formula: (%CD71+PI+ of treated sample)–(%CD71+PI+ of control sample), where the control represents target cells incubated without effectors. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo V.10 (Inivai Technologies, Mentone, Victoria, Australia).

Flow cytometry-based blocking assay

To assess whether the co-stimulatory domains CD137 and OX40 play a role in BLINA-mediated killing, PBMCs were pre-incubated for 48 hours with or without ABATA (10 ng/mL). Following pre-incubation, PBMCs were co-cultured with CD71 (BioLegend Cat# 334108) B-ALL target cells (RS4;11) at a 1:1 E:T ratio in the presence or absence of 16 μg/mL of unconjugated CD137 blocking antibody (BioLegend, clone W18063F, Cat# 944904) and/or 20 µg/mL of unconjugated OX40 blocking antibody (BioLegend, clone W22033K, Cat# 632654). After 40 min at room temperature and in the absence of light, BLINA (66 ng/mL) or PBS (Cytiva, Cat# SH30256.01) was added. The co-cultures were incubated for 24 hours at 37°C and 5% CO2, followed by a flow-based cytotoxicity assay as described above. The following experimental conditions were assessed: PBMC alone, PBMC+BLINA, PBMC+ABATA, and PBMC+BLINA+ABATA with CD137 only or OX40 only blocking or the combination CD137+OX40 blocking antibody.

Assessment of PBMC degranulation, interferon-γ production, and activation markers

To assess degranulation and cellular interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) levels, PBMCs were pre-incubated for 48 hours with or without ABATA (10 ng/mL). Following pre-incubation, PBMCs were co-cultured with CD19-labeled (BioLegend Cat# 302231) B-ALL target cells (RS4;11) at a 1:1 E:T ratio. Co-culture was performed at 37°C and 5% CO2 in the presence of antihuman CD107a antibody (Miltenyi Cat# 130-111-628), with or without BLINA (66 ng/mL). After overnight co-culture, protein transport was blocked by the addition of brefeldin A (GolgiPlug; BD Biosciences) and monensin (GolgiStop; BD Biosciences). Cells were incubated for an additional 2.5 hours, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS (Cytiva). Surface staining was performed for 30 min using the following antibodies: CD25 fluorescein isothiocyanate (BioLegend Cat# 356106), CD3 BV421 (BioLegend Cat# 317332), CD4 APC-Cy7 (BioLegend Cat# 317418), CD8 BV785 (BioLegend Cat# 301046), CD137 (Miltenyi Cat# 130-110-763), CD69 PE-Texas Red (BioLegend Cat# 310942), OX40 UV575 (Miltenyi Cat# 130-120-723), and CD56 PE-Cy5 (BioLegend Cat# 362516). Subsequently, cells were fixed and permeabilized (Invitrogen), followed by intracellular staining with antihuman IFN-γ Alexa Fluor 670 (BioLegend Cat# 502516) for 30 min on ice in the dark. Single-color controls and fluorescence minus one controls were used for compensation and gating thresholds, respectively. Samples were resuspended in 200 μL PBS and analyzed using a BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer.

In vivo xenograft model

In vivo experiments were performed using female immunodeficient NSG mice transgenic for human IL-15 (NSG-Tg(Hu-IL15)) aged 8–11 weeks. On day −2, mice received sublethal irradiation (150 cGy). On day −1, mice were inoculated intravenously (via the tail vein) with 5×105 RS4;11 cells. On day 0, mice received an intravenous injection of PBMC isolated from a healthy donor. Mice were randomized by weight into five groups: (1) tumor control (no treatment), (2) PBMC control (RS4;11+PBMC), (3) BLINA monotherapy (PBMC+BLINA), (4) ABATA monotherapy (PBMC+ABATA), and (5) combination therapy (PBMC+BLINA+ABATA). BLINA (or vehicle) was administered intraperitoneally 5 days per week for 2 weeks starting on day 0. ABATA was administered intraperitoneally every other day from day 0 to day 10. Mice were monitored daily for clinical signs and weighed twice weekly to calculate the percentage of body weight change. GvHD was assessed by scoring skin integrity, fur texture, posture, and activity, from which a cumulative GvHD score was derived. Humane end points for euthanasia included: moribund status, a cumulative GvHD score ≥8 after day +8, weight loss exceeding 30% of starting weight for two consecutive measurements, or hind-limb paralysis.

Bioluminescence imaging and survival analysis

Leukemia burden was monitored weekly using the Spectral Instruments Lago-X bioluminescence imaging (BLI) system (Tucson, Arizona, USA), and overall survival was tracked throughout the study. Briefly, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 microL D-luciferin (15 mg/mL; GoldBio, Cat# LUCK-1G). Following injection, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and imaged with a 5-minute exposure time. Bioluminescence was quantified using the Aura imaging software and expressed as photons/s within a region of interest encompassing the entire animal. BLI images were collected following a 5-minute exposure. Images were standardized to a radiance scale minimum of 1.7×104 and maximum of 5.5×107 and exported as JPEG files.

Ex vivo functional assays

To assess the functional status of engrafted T cells, ex vivo experiments were conducted using female NSG-Hu-IL15 mice. Mice were irradiated (150 cGy) on day −1 and reconstituted with fresh human PBMC on day 0. From day 0 to day 10, mice were treated with or without ABATA via intraperitoneal injection. The cohorts included a control group (PBMC only) and a treatment group (PBMC+ABATA). On day +11, mice were euthanized, and spleens were processed into single-cell suspensions. Human CD45+ cells were isolated using an autoMACS NEO Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). Isolation was performed via a two-step process: depletion of mouse cells using a Mouse CD45 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Cat# 130-052-301), followed by positive selection of the negative fraction using a Human CD45 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Cat# 130-045-801). The final purity of hCD45+ cells was >97%. Following isolation, cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 (supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S) and stimulated with IL-15 (1000 U/mL) for 1 hour at 37°C. Cells were then washed, centrifuged, and co-cultured with RS4;11-Luc target cells (labeled with anti-CD19 Ultra Violet 575) for 24 hours in 96-well plates, with or without BLINA. After co-incubation, degranulation, phenotypic marker expression, and cytotoxicity were assessed by flow cytometry as described above.

Results

Abatacept does not impair cytotoxicity induced by blinatumomab

To assess whether ABATA affects BLINA-mediated antileukemia activity, we performed flow-based cytotoxicity assays using healthy donor PBMC against three representative B-cell lines (RS4;11, NALM-6, and JEKO-1). BLINA alone significantly increased cytotoxicity across E:T ratios from 0.6:1 to 5:1 compared with untreated PBMC and ABATA alone (p=0.0011) against all three cell lines (figure 1A). ABATA alone showed no additional cytotoxic effect compared with PBMC only (p=0.98). The combination of BLINA+ABATA induced cytotoxicity comparable to BLINA alone and significantly greater than that of PBMC only or ABATA alone (p<0.0001). At peak lysis, BLINA+ABATA enhanced cytotoxicity relative to PBMC only by 29.68% against RS4;11, 38.15% against NALM-6, and 47.2% against JEKO-1 (figure 1A). Even at low E:T ratios, cytotoxicity remained elevated in BLINA-treated groups, regardless of the presence or absence of ABATA. Even at a ninefold higher ABATA concentration, BLINA-induced cytolysis was unaffected (online supplemental figure S1). Collectively, these data indicate that ABATA does not compromise the antileukemia activity of BLINA in vitro.

Abatacept does not compromise blinatumomab-induced degranulation or IFN-γ production

To determine whether ABATA interferes with T-cell effector functions, we evaluated degranulation and IFN-γ secretion in vitro using PBMC co-cultured with RS4;11 target cells. Degranulation was measured by CD107a expression, and IFN-γ production was quantified by flow cytometry after 24 hours of co-incubation with RS4;11 (figure 1B). BLINA markedly increased expression of CD107a and IFN-γ compared with untreated PBMC and those treated with ABATA only (figure 1C). ABATA alone did not affect either parameter. The combination ABATA+BLINA yielded CD107a and IFN-γ expression levels comparable to BLINA alone and significantly higher than untreated and those treated with ABATA only (figure 1C). These results indicate that ABATA does not attenuate BLINA-driven degranulation or IFN-γ release, suggesting that T-cell cytotoxic machinery remains intact in the presence of CTLA-4-Ig.

Blinatumomab preserves T-cell activation marker expression in the presence of abatacept

To examine whether ABATA modulates the upregulated T-cell activation markers such as CD25, CD69, OX40 and CD137 induced by BLINA, PBMCs were precultured for 48 hours with or without ABATA, followed by co-incubation±BLINA in the presence of RS4;11 target cells. Both BLINA and BLINA+ABATA significantly increased CD25+, CD69+, and CD137+ on CD3+ T cells compared with PBMC untreated or ABATA only (figure 1D). BLINA+ABATA enhanced CD25, CD69, and CD137 expression to a similar extent as BLINA alone. While OX40 expression trended higher in the BLINA-treated groups, this did not reach statistical significance (p=0.09). Collectively, these results indicate that ABATA does not alter T-cell surface activation marker expression in vitro (figure 1D).

Blinatumomab mediates tumor lysis independently of secondary co-stimulatory domains (CD28, CD137, and OX40)

To determine whether BLINA requires alternative co-stimulatory signaling when CD28 is blocked by ABATA, we performed in vitro cytotoxicity assays with blockade of CD137 and/or OX40 in the presence or absence of ABATA. BLINA-mediated killing was unchanged by CD137 and/or OX40 blockade, regardless of ABATA (online supplemental figure S2), indicating that BLINA mediates leukemia lysis independently of secondary co-stimulation.

Abatacept improves survival and mitigates GvHD without compromising blinatumomab’s antitumor activity in vivo

To assess the in vivo combination of ABATA and BLINA, we employed an RS4;11 B-ALL xenograft model in NSG-IL-15 mice. RS4;11 cells were injected intravenously on day −1 followed by fresh human PBMC on day 0 (figure 2A). Mice were randomized to PBMC only, ABATA, BLINA, or BLINA+ABATA. Tumor burden was monitored by BLI and overall survival was recorded. PBMC alone significantly reduced tumor burden versus control (p=0.003) but shortened survival due to GvHD (median 23 days vs 43 days; p<0.0001) (figure 2B,C). BLINA monotherapy similarly decreased tumor burden (p=0.003), without extending survival (median 20 days; p<0.0001) also attributable to GvHD. GvHD scores were significantly higher in PBMC only (4.69±1.18) and BLINA alone (4.60±1.17) compared with ABATA only (2.38±0.50) and BLINA+ABATA (2.44±0.51) (figure 2D), resulting in mortality by day 23 and no overall survival benefit over control (figure 2B). ABATA alone exerted an anti-GvHD effect, modestly extending survival to a median of 65 days versus control (median 43 days) (figure 2B) and significantly reducing tumor burden (p<0.0001) (figure 2C,E). Importantly, BLINA+ABATA further delayed tumor progression compared with ABATA alone (p=0.001), untreated control (p=0.0012), PBMC only (p<0.0001), and BLINA only (p<0.0001). This combination extended overall survival by 35 days versus control (p<0.0001) and 13 days versus ABATA alone (p=0.005) (figure 2B). Similar results were obtained in a cohort of standard NSG mice (RRID: BCBC_4142), indicating that IL-15 did not influence the observed effects (online supplemental figure S3). Because ABATA is currently co-administered with calcineurin inhibitors in the clinical setting,26 we next assessed in a separate in vivo experiment whether cyclosporine A (CSA) would alter the outcomes of the combined ABATA+BLINA treatment. Concurrent CSA+ABATA administration did not compromise the antileukemic efficacy of BLINA (online supplemental figure S4). Together, these data indicate that, consistent with the in vitro results, BLINA+ABATA does not compromise BLINA’s antitumor activity in the xenograft model. Notably, adding ABATA provides a survival benefit by mitigating GvHD while preserving BLINA’s antileukemic efficacy.

Blinatumomab expands T cells while abatacept partially modulates the blinatumomab-mediated responses

To assess PBMC persistence after transplantation, peripheral blood was collected on days 7, 14, and 21 post-PBMC injection. We quantified total human CD45+ (hCD45+) cells, total T cells (hCD3+), and T-cell subsets, including CD8+ and CD4+ populations and naïve, central memory (CM), and effector memory (EM) subsets. On day 7, the highest circulating levels of hCD45+, total T cells, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells were observed in mice receiving BLINA either alone or combined with ABATA. Specifically, the levels of human leukocytes (hCD45+) were significantly higher in the BLINA-treated groups (BLINA only and combination) compared with the PBMC and ABATA controls (figure 3). Regarding total T cells (hCD3+), the BLINA monotherapy group exhibited the highest expansion, significantly exceeding all other groups. The combination group (BLINA+ABATA) also demonstrated higher hCD3+ levels compared with the non-BLINA groups. CD4+ T cells: the BLINA monotherapy group exhibited the highest number of CD4+ T cells, significantly exceeding the PBMC-only, ABATA-only, and combination (BLINA+ABATA) groups. CD8+ T cells: similarly, CD8+ T-cell levels were highest in the BLINA monotherapy group compared with all other conditions. However, unlike the CD4+ subset, the combination group (BLINA+ABATA) also showed significantly higher CD8+ values compared with the PBMC-only and ABATA-only controls (figure 3). These data indicate that while BLINA drives robust T-cell expansion, the addition of ABATA partially modulates the magnitude of this response. As expected, given the inhibitory effects of ABATA on T-cell activation and proliferation, the ABATA-only group displayed the lowest circulating T-cell levels across all populations. Notably, despite the smaller graft size in this group, no complete graft failures were observed.

By day 14, circulating populations of hCD45+, total T cells, CD4+, and CD8+ cells had increased significantly in the PBMC-only and BLINA-only groups compared with day 7. At this time point, the PBMC-only and BLINA-only groups maintained the highest engraftment levels compared with the ABATA-treated cohorts. These data indicate that while ABATA blunts T-cell expansion, groups receiving BLINA exhibited transient T-cell proliferation during the first week, a trend that was also confirmed in the spleen and bone marrow (online supplemental figure S5). By day 21, the PBMC-only and BLINA-only groups retained significantly higher circulating hCD45+ and total T cells relative to ABATA-treated groups (figure 3), although there was no significant difference between the BLINA-only and PBMC-only groups.

BLINA promoted T-cell differentiation, particularly within CD8+ subsets, while ABATA attenuated this effect (figure 4). By day 7, and more prominently by day 14, BLINA alone increased EM CD8+ frequencies compared with ABATA and BLINA+ABATA. By day 21, CM CD8+ frequencies remained higher in BLINA and PBMC-only groups than in ABATA-treated groups. In CD4+ cells, BLINA increased EM (and to a lesser extent CM) by day 14, whereas ABATA consistently suppressed CD4+ differentiation, yielding predominantly naïve phenotypes. Overall, BLINA transiently expands and differentiates CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, whereas ABATA blunts these responses.

Impact of prior abatacept treatment on blinatumomab-mediated cytotoxicity

To evaluate whether the efficacy of subsequent BLINA therapy is compromised by prior ABATA exposure, we used an NSG-IL15 xenograft model. Mice were engrafted with fresh PBMC on day 0 and treated with ABATA or left untreated until day 10. Thereafter, spleens were harvested, and isolated hCD45+ cells were co-cultured with RS4;11 target cells in the presence or absence of BLINA. After 24 hours, cytotoxicity, T-cell function, and activation marker expression were assessed (figure 5A). Against RS4;11 targets, BLINA monotherapy significantly increased cytotoxicity across all E:T ratios compared with untreated PBMC (p<0.0001) and ABATA-only controls (p<0.0001). While the BLINA monotherapy group showed slightly higher cytotoxicity than the combination group (ABATA+BLINA) (p=0.04), the combination group still demonstrated significantly greater killing than PBMC only (p=0.01) or ABATA only (p=0.001). ABATA alone showed no additional cytotoxic effect compared with PBMC (p=0.18) (figure 5B). These data demonstrate that even after 10 days of systemic ABATA treatment, T cells retain the capacity to mount a significant cytotoxic response within 24 hours of BLINA exposure, significantly exceeding the baseline activity of untreated PBMC.

Supporting these observations, the phenotypic analysis revealed that both BLINA monotherapy and the combination (prior ABATA+BLINA) significantly enhanced the expression of activation markers on CD3+ T cells. Specifically, the proportions of CD25+, CD69+, and CD137+ cells were significantly increased in both treatment groups compared with PBMC alone or ABATA alone (figure 5C). Importantly, the combination group exhibited CD25, CD69, and CD137 expression levels comparable to BLINA monotherapy. This indicates that prior exposure to ABATA does not inhibit the BLINA-mediated upregulation of these key surface activation markers (figure 5C). The CD107 expression increased twofold (PBMC only vs BLINA) and 2.3-fold (ABATA vs BLINA+ABATA), and IFN-γ production increased 5.6-fold (PBMC only vs BLINA), although not reaching statistical significance (data not shown).

Discussion

Allo-HCT remains the only curative option for many patients with high-risk B-ALL. However, its success is constrained by the competing risks of post-transplant relapse and GvHD.27,30 Our study identifies a therapeutic strategy that balances these opposing forces: the combination of the CD3/CD19 bispecific engager BLINA with the CTLA-4-Ig fusion protein ABATA. In a xenograft B-ALL model, this regimen significantly delayed leukemia progression and extended overall survival compared with controls and monotherapy arms. Crucially, we demonstrate that ABATA mitigates lethal GvHD without abrogating the potent T-cell cytotoxicity driven by BLINA.

ABATA is Food and Drug Administration-approved for acute GvHD prophylaxis in combination with calcineurin inhibitors and methotrexate.6 Mechanistically, it prevents GvHD by binding CD80/CD86 on APCs, thereby blocking the CD28 co-stimulatory signal required for pathogenic T-cell expansion.6 7 Consistent with its clinical profile, we observed that ABATA alone, or in combination with BLINA, effectively prevented GvHD-related mortality and reduced total T-cell expansion in circulation, spleen, and bone marrow. Importantly, despite this reduction in T-cell numbers (hCD45+), ABATA did not impair engraftment. While BLINA monotherapy exerted strong antitumor effects, treated mice succumbed early to severe GvHD. By adding ABATA, we achieved a synergistic benefit: the immune-dampening effects of ABATA prevented lethal GvHD, while the preserved cytotoxic potential of BLINA controlled the leukemia. This combination produced the longest survival of any cohort, extending survival by 35 days over tumor controls and 13 days over ABATA monotherapy.

A central concern in combining these agents is the potential for antagonism. Since ABATA blocks CD28 co-stimulation, it could theoretically impair the T-cell functionality required for BLINA efficacy.10 21 Indeed, previous studies by Gao et al suggested that CTLA-4-Ig could reduce engraftment rates, effector CD8+ expansion, and cytokine production (IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor).31 In contrast, our in vitro data revealed that ABATA did not impair BLINA-induced cytotoxicity against multiple human B-cell lines (RS4;11, NALM-6, and JEKO-1), nor did it suppress the degranulation and IFN-γ secretion from T cells. Furthermore, our ex vivo analysis, using T cells retrieved from mice treated with ABATA for 10 days, confirmed that these cells retained the capacity to mount a significant cytotoxic response on BLINA stimulation. Although prior ABATA exposure resulted in a reduction in peak lysis compared with BLINA monotherapy (figure 5), the combination remained highly effective, enhancing killing over baseline. This indicates that while ABATA limits the expansion of T cells, it does not permanently render them anergic or incapable of killing when engaged by a bispecific antibody.

Degranulation and cytokine production are fundamental to T-cell cytotoxicity, with IFN-γ serving a critical role in sustaining CD8+ proliferation and activation.10 Our assays confirmed that neither short-term in vitro exposure (48 hours) nor extended in vivo treatment (10 days) with ABATA compromised these effector functions. This demonstrates that the functional activation driven by direct CD3/CD19 engagement is maintained even when the CD28 pathway is suppressed.32 Mechanistically, this may be explained by the fact that ABATA binds CD80/CD86 on APCs rather than directly blocking the CD28 receptor on T cells.33 Consequently, while natural co-stimulation is reduced, the T cell remains physically accessible and responsive to the potent alternative activation signal provided by BLINA.

The preservation of BLINA-mediated killing in the presence of ABATA suggests that BLINA acts independently of the CD28 co-stimulatory pathway. Effective T-cell activation typically requires T-cell receptor/major histocompatibility complex engagement (signal 1) plus CD28 co-stimulation (signal 2).34 35 While ABATA blocks the CD28-CD80/86 axis, BLINA acts as a potent signal 1 agonist (mimicking anti-CD3) and drives the upregulation of alternative co-stimulatory molecules. Our data show that BLINA induces robust expression of CD25, CD69, and CD137 (4-1BB) on T cells and that ABATA treatment does not inhibit upregulation of these markers. Moreover, in vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that BLINA-mediated killing proceeds independently of co-stimulatory signaling through CD28, CD137, or OX40 (online supplemental figure S2). These findings are consistent with prior reports that BLINA bypasses the requirement for co-stimulation to drive T-cell activation and cytolysis.36,38 Although BLINA-induced upregulation of co-stimulatory receptors may still contribute to amplifying and sustaining T-cell responses, for example, in effector memory formation, these secondary signals do not appear essential for BLINA-mediated cytotoxicity. BLINA therefore bypasses the blockade imposed by ABATA, preserving selective GvL activity even when the ‘classical’ GvHD-driving CD28 pathway is suppressed.34 39

The timing of administration in our model, with ABATA given during the early allo-response phase, likely played a critical role. By restraining early T-cell expansion, ABATA may blunt the ‘cytokine storm’ and inflammatory toxicity often associated with both GvHD and BiTE therapy, while leaving the T-cell repertoire intact for subsequent BLINA engagement.9 40 One limitation of our in vivo model is that cells were exposed to BLINA intermittently. In clinical practice, BLINA is administered via continuous infusion, which may drive even more profound activation.41,44 Consequently, the magnitude of cytotoxicity observed in our in vivo model may underestimate the potential efficacy in a clinical setting where T cells are continuously exposed to the drug.

Following HCT or DLI, BLINA is frequently employed to manage relapse in high-risk patients.18 19 21 23 24 This approach is clinically timely, as evidenced by multiple active trials investigating DLI plus BLINA for CD19+ malignancies (NCT03849651, NCT07254793, NCT07105579, NCT07052370). However, DLI monotherapy often shows limited efficacy in B-ALL and is complicated by GvHD. Our data suggest that adding ABATA to the BLINA/DLI regimen (BLINA+ABATA+DLI) shows promise in controlling this DLI-induced GvHD without compromising the antitumor activity of BLINA.45 46

In conclusion, successful GvHD prophylaxis requires agents that suppress pathological allo-responses without impairing the GvL effect.7 47 48 Our study provides preclinical proof-of-concept that ABATA and BLINA can be combined to achieve this balance. ABATA restricts GvHD-promoting T-cell expansion, while BLINA acts independently of co-stimulation to maintain potent antileukemic activity. These findings support the translation of this combination into clinical trials, offering a promising strategy to improve survival outcomes in high-risk B-ALL patients undergoing allo-HCT.
