On the Expressive Power of Contextual Relations in Transformers
Demián Fraiman

Abstract
Transformer architectures have achieved remarkable empirical success in modeling contextual relationships in natural language, yet a precise mathematical characterization of their expressive power remains incomplete. In this work, we introduce a measure-theoretic framework for contextual representations in which texts are modeled as probability measures over a semantic embedding space, and contextual relations between words, are represented as coupling measures between them. Within this setting, we introduce Sinkhorn Transformers , a transformer-like architecture. Our main result is a universal approximation theorem: any continuous coupling function between probability measures, that encodes the semantic relation coupling measure, can be uniformly approximated by a Sinkhorn Transformer with appropriate parameters.



1
Introduction

Transformer architectures have achieved remarkable empirical success in modeling contextual relationships in natural language and other structured data, and its precisely its biggest breakthrough with respect to older recurrent neural networks. Despite this success, a precise mathematical characterization of the class of contextual relations representable by attention mechanisms remains incomplete. Existing analyses typically describe attention as a heuristic weighting scheme over representations, leaving open the question of what functional objects attention layers are fundamentally capable of approximating.

In this work, we study whether Transformers can learn every possible system of semantic contextual relations. Where each contextual system is an assignment that maps text sequences to contextual semantic graphs, representing semantic relations between words. The case of a single text is similar to self-attention (note that the coefficient could be asymmetric), an example of a semantic graph would be Figure 1. whereas the case of two texts is linked with cross-attention mechanisms, such as those arising in translation, where the model outputs a word-to-word semantic similarity structure across texts as it can be seen in Figure 2. For clarity, coefficients with small magnitude are omitted.

For dealing with arbitrary length sequences, we propose a measure-theoretic framework in which contextual representations are modeled as probability measures over a semantic space, and contextual relations between inputs are represented as coupling measures between these distributions. Within this setting, attention mechanisms are naturally interpreted as operators that map pairs of input measures to joint distributions with prescribed marginals. This perspective shifts the focus from pointwise similarity scores to the approximation of structured probabilistic relations.

Our main result establishes a universal approximation theorem for contextual relations. Under mild regularity assumptions on the semantic space, we prove that a class of Transformer-style attention mechanisms is dense in the space of continuous coupling-valued maps between probability measures. In other words, attention-based architectures can approximate arbitrary continuous assignments of joint distributions with fixed marginals, providing a rigorous characterization of their expressive power at the level of semantic relations.

3
A Measure-Theoretic Framework for Semantic Relations

In this section we introduce the conceptual and mathematical framework underlying our
approach.
We explain why texts are naturally modeled as probability measures and how semantic
relations between texts can be formalized as coupling-valued mappings.

3.1
Texts as Probability Measures

Texts are inherently variable-length objects: different sentences or documents may contain
different numbers of tokens, words, or subword units.
Any mathematical framework aiming to model semantic relations between texts should therefore
be able to manage texts with variable and unbounded length.

To this end, we represent a text as a probability measure over a semantic embedding space.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact space representing token embeddings.
Given a text consisting of tokens
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
with embeddings
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X
, we associate the
probability measure

\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(X),

Where
\mathcal{P}(X)
is the space of probability measures over
X
. Note that the position of the tokens could be encoded as a positional embedding in the space of
X
.

This representation allows texts of arbitrary length to be embedded into a common compact space. Also

\mathcal{P}(X)
enables the use of tools from measure theory and optimal transport to study
relations between texts.

3.2
From Attention to Joint Distributions

The attention mechanism assigns weights to pairs of tokens, measuring how strongly one token
attends to another in a row normalized manner.
Let
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
be a sequence of tokens with embeddings
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X
. In a standard Transformer, attention coefficients are defined as

A(w_{i},w_{j})=\frac{\exp(W_{Q}x_{i}\cdot W_{K}x_{j})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}\exp(W_{Q}x_{i}\cdot W_{K}x_{j})}.

So
A(w_{i},\cdot)
could be interpreted as a conditional probability over tokens.

From a probabilistic perspective, conditional distributions naturally induce joint
distributions.
Given a source measure
\mu_{text}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and attention weights
A(x,y)
, one can define a joint measure
\pi
by

\pi(dx,dy)=A(x,y)\,\mu(dx).

The resulting measure
\pi
encodes joint relevance between elements of the two
representations.

This observation motivates modeling attention mechanisms directly as joint probability
measures, since joint measures are more tractable than conditional probabilities.

Since we are interested in the case of having also two texts, that could be input and output of transformer for instance, we would then have two texts
\mu_{input}
and
\nu_{output}
, Then, the contextual relation between both is a
probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
whose marginals are
\mu_{input}
and
\nu_{output}
. We will call the joint probability measures encoding contextual relations as coupling, and the set of all such couplings between two measures
\mu,\nu
is denoted by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

For now on, we will work only with the case of having two texts, since in the case we have two equal texts we recover the one text only case.

We interpret couplings as representations of contextual relations.
Intuitively, a coupling specifies how semantic meaning of one text words relates to the other text words.
This joint structure captures relational information that cannot be reduced to independent
or pointwise similarities. We could then define a whole system that computes for each pair of texts
\mu_{input},\nu_{output}
their contextual relations
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{input},\nu_{output})
.

We formalize this idea by introducing the notion of a coupling system.

Definition 3.1

(Coupling System)
.

A
coupling system
is a continuous mapping in the weak topology

F:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)

such that for all
(\mu,\nu)
, the measure
F(\mu,\nu)
belongs to
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

Transformers as maps of probabilities

We follow the construction of the transformers as in

)
. This formulation will serve as the building block for our proposed architecture in section 6 the
Sinkhorn Transformers
.

Definition 3.2

(Measure-valued multi-head attention)
.

Given a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and a point
x\in X
, we define the attention map

\Gamma_{\theta}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

by

\Gamma_{\theta}(\mu,x)=x+\sum_{h=1}^{H}W^{h}\int_{X}\operatorname{softmax}^{\theta^{h}}_{\mu}(x)(y)\,V^{h}y\,d\mu(y),

where
\theta=(\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{H})
,
\theta^{h}=(Q^{h},K^{h},V^{h},W^{h})
, and

\operatorname{softmax}^{\theta^{h}}_{\mu}(x)(y)=\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,K^{h}y\rangle\big)}{\int_{X}\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,K^{h}z\rangle\big)\,d\mu(z)}.

This definition extends classical multi-head attention, recovering the standard formulation when
\mu
is an empirical measure supported on finitely many points, since the integral with respect to a a discrete measure turns into a sum, we have

\displaystyle\int_{X}\operatorname{softmax}_{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}}^{\theta^{h}}(x)(y)\,V^{h}y\,d(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}})(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{k}}}\langle Q^{h}x,\,K^{h}x_{j}\rangle\big)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,\,K^{h}x_{i}\rangle\big)}\,V^{h}x_{j}.

Since the
\frac{1}{n}
factors cancels each other out, we recover the classical case.

Definition 3.3

(Context maps and context operators)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be locally compact metric spaces. A map

\Gamma:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Y

is called a
context map
if
\Gamma(\mu,\cdot)
is continuous on
X
for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
.

The associated
context operator

\mathcal{C}(\Gamma):\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(Y)
is defined by

\mathcal{C}(\Gamma)(\mu)=\Gamma(\mu,\cdot)_{\#}\mu.

Definition 3.4

(Composition of context maps)
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Y
and
\Gamma_{2}:\mathcal{P}(Y)\times Y\to Z
be context maps. Their composition

\Gamma_{2}\diamond\Gamma_{1}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Z

is defined by

(\Gamma_{2}\diamond\Gamma_{1})(\mu,x)=\Gamma_{2}(\mu_{1},\Gamma_{1}(\mu,x)),\qquad\mu_{1}=\mathcal{C}(\Gamma_{1})(\mu).

Definition 3.5

(Deep Transformer)
.

A Transformer with
L
layers is defined as the composition

F_{\xi_{L}}\diamond\Gamma_{\theta_{L}}\diamond\cdots\diamond F_{\xi_{1}}\diamond\Gamma_{\theta_{1}},

where each
\Gamma_{\theta_{\ell}}
is a measure-valued attention map of the form (
3.2
) and each
F_{\xi_{\ell}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}}
is a multilayer perceptron acting pointwise.

3.4
Problem Statement

The central question addressed in this work is the following:

Are the Transformer-architecture capable of learning any coupling system?

Our goal is to characterize the expressive capacity of such architectures. This perspective shifts the focus from general function approximation to relational structure, which is at the core of Transformer architectures.

4
The Space of Coupling systems

We now study the functional-analytic structure of semantic coupling functions.
Our goal is to characterize the space in which these objects live and to identify
natural regularity assumptions under which approximation results can be established.

We consider the space of continuous functions:

\mathcal{C}=C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y),\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)),

where
\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
denotes the space of signed Radon measures on
X\times Y
equipped with the weak* topology (i.e., the topology induced by the dual pairing with
C(X\times Y)
). We endow
\mathcal{C}
with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets, which in this case is equivalent to uniform convergence on the entire space since
\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)
is compact. It is a known fact from functional analysis that
\mathcal{C}
is a locally convex topological vector space.

Define the reference product map
F_{0}(\mu,\nu)=\mu\otimes\nu
and the linear subspace
\mathcal{F}
defined as:

\{G\in\mathcal{C}:\operatorname{proj}_{X}G(\mu,\nu)=0,\ \operatorname{proj}_{Y}G(\mu,\nu)=0\ \forall(\mu,\nu)\}.

We have the affine representation
\mathcal{A}=F_{0}+\mathcal{F}
.

Using the weak continuity of the projection operator we have that:

Proposition 4.1
.

The affine space
\mathcal{A}
is closed.

Now consider the subset of functions taking values in probability measures:

\mathcal{C}_{P}=\{F\in\mathcal{C}:F(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)\ \forall(\mu,\nu)\}.

we observe that
\mathcal{C}_{P}
is convex since
\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
it is.

Proposition 4.2
.

The convex set
\mathcal{C}_{P}
is closed.

Observe that the space of coupling systems
\mathcal{A_{P}}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}_{P}
is the intersection between those previous spaces. Thus,

Corollary 4.3
.

The space of coupling systems
\mathcal{A_{P}}
is convex and closed.

5
Sinkhorn Operator

We now show that entropy-regularized optimal transport provides a rich and stable class
of coupling systems. Since this problem is very closely related to the attention mechanism, we will use it as final layer in our proposed architecture.
It is worth highliting that in the discrete case this problem is efficiently resolved by the Sinkhorn algorithm
(
1
)
.

Let
X,Y
be two polish spaces, the entropic regularized optimal transport problem is:

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\,\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu),

where
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous cost function and
KL
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The unique minimizer
\pi^{\varepsilon}
is called the
Sinkhorn plan
and admits the factorization
(
7
)
:

\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}=u(x)K(x,y)v(y),\quad K(x,y)=e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon},

with continuous potentials
u,v>0
. The mapping
S_{c,\varepsilon}:(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\pi^{\varepsilon}
defines the
Sinkhorn operator
. In the following we will usually use
\varepsilon=1
, and we will note the correspondent Sinkhorn operator as
S_{c}
.

The Sinkhorn Operator checks the next stability properties:

Proposition 5.1
.

(

The Sinkhorn Operator is Lipschitz with respect to the cost function:

W_{1}\Big(S_{c}(\mu_{,}\nu),\,S_{c^{\prime}}(\mu,\nu)\Big)\;\leq\;L_{\varepsilon}\|c-c^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where
L_{\varepsilon}
is independent of
\mu,\nu
.

Proposition 5.2
.

(
10
)

Let
\mu_{n}\to\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu_{n}\to\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
then the density of the solution of the optimal transport problem converges uniformly
\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})}\rightrightarrows\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}
.

and we prove that also checks:

Fix
\mu,\nu
and let
c_{n}\xrightarrow{L_{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)}c\in C(X\times Y)

In order to prove the universality of a Transformer architecture with the Sinkhorn operator, we must ensure first that the Sinkhorn operator it is itself universal. As a first step we prove that we can approximate pointwise any transport plan by a entropic optimal transport solution, as we can see in the next lemma:

Lemma 5.4
.

Every transport plan
\pi
in
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
can be approximated by the solution of of an entropic optimal transport problem, i.e. a transport plan with continuous density with respect to the product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
.

Sketch of the proof.

The first step is to prove that we can approximate each transport plan
\pi
for a transport plan
\tilde{\pi}\ll\mu\otimes\nu

L_{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
. Finally to recover
\mu,\nu
marginals, we approximate it for the solution of the optimal transport problem with cost
-\log(f)
, being
f
the density. Using the stability of the minimizers with respect to the cost, we conclude the desired lemma.
∎

Using this lemma, we arrive the next important theorem.

Theorem 5.5
.

The family of Sinkhorn Operators with continuous costs
\{S_{c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y)}\}
is dense in the space of coupling systems.

Sketch of the proof.

Let
F
be the semantic coupling, using the Lemma 5.4 we can construct a multivalued function
\Gamma
that for each
\mu,\nu
we considered the set of densities of
\mu\otimes\nu
such that their correspondent measure lives in a small neighborhood of
F(\mu,\nu)
, then by the approximate selection theorem
(
2
)
, we can extract an approximate selection
s:P(X)\times P(Y)\to C(X\times Y)
of that densities. Finally, using
-\log(s)
as the cost, and the stability of the Sinkhorn Operator between the costs, we conclude the theorem.
∎

The Sinkhorn Transformer

We introduce the
Sinkhorn Transformer

\mathcal{T}:P(X)\times P(Y)\to P(X\times Y)
, which consists of three main components:

Figure 3
:
Sinkhorn Transformer.

Two Transformer-based encoders
Q
and
K
that process input measures into query and key embeddings:

\displaystyle Q(\mu,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle K(\nu,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

These encoders are implemented as deep Transformer like in Definition 3.5.

Then we define a cost function based on the dissimilarity between embeddings:

c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y)=-\langle Q(\mu,x),K(\nu,y)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

This generalizes the
QK^{\top}
affinity in standard attention.

Instead of softmax normalization (which produces conditional probabilities), we apply the Sinkhorn operator to obtain a joint measure:

\mathcal{T}(\mu,\nu)=S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu).

6.1
Practical Implications and Relation to Standard Transformers

In practical applications, the proposed architecture admits a direct implementation. In the discrete setting, texts correspond to uniform empirical measures (as discussed in Section 3.3), and Transformer encoders are implemented in the standard way. The Sinkhorn operator can be efficiently computed using the Sinkhorn algorithm, resulting in an architecture that closely matches a standard Transformer with a final Sinkhorn normalization applied to the score matrix
\langle Q_{i},K_{j}\rangle
.

Regarding the processing of inputs, we distinguish two cases. In the single-text setting (i.e., self-attention), both inputs coincide, and the architecture processes the same text through the encoders. This corresponds to a standard Transformer without cross connection between attention heads and MLP of each branch. As illustrated in Figure
4
, the architecture follows a classical part of the Transformer architecture, with the Sinkhorn operator applied as a final layer.

Figure 4
:
Sinkhorn Transformer architecture in the single-text setting.

In the two-text setting, the architecture follows the standard encoder-decoder paradigm: each text is first processed independently, and the interaction between them is introduced at the final stage through the Sinkhorn layer. This plays a role analogous to cross-attention. Figure
5
illustrates the corresponding structure and highlights the part of the standard Transformer architecture that is preserved.

Figure 5
:
Sinkhorn Transformer architecture in the two-text setting.

We now discuss the role of the Sinkhorn operator compared to the classical softmax attention. The softmax operation produces row-stochastic matrices, corresponding to conditional probability distributions. In contrast, the Sinkhorn operator produces approximately doubly stochastic matrices, enforcing both row and column normalization. This additional structure can be interpreted as promoting balanced interactions between tokens.

Importantly, empirical evidence (e.g.,
(
13
)
) suggests that attention matrices in trained models are often close to doubly stochastic. The Sinkhorn operator can be efficiently implemented as an iterative normalization procedure, typically converging in a small number of iterations, and is fully differentiable.

In this sense, our architecture remains very close to standard Transformers: it preserves classical attention mechanisms in all intermediate layers and modifies only the final normalization step. This is in contrast with approaches such as
(
13
)
, which replace all softmax layers with Sinkhorn normalization. This shows that the proposed framework does not depart from standard Transformer architectures, but rather provides a structured reinterpretation of their final interaction mechanism.

Main Theorem

Theorem 7.1

(Universal Approximation of Semantic Couplings)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces, for any F semantic coupling, and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a Sinkhorn Transformer

\mathcal{T}^{*}
such that

\sup_{(\mu,\nu)\in P(X)\times P(Y)}W_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu),F(\mu,\nu)\bigr)<\varepsilon.

sketch of the proof.

Let
F
be a coupling system, the first step is to approximate it using Theorem 5.5 with a Sinkhorn operator, then by Stone-Weirstrass theorem, we can approximate the cost
c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y)
by a dot-product between two functions
\langle Q(\mu,x),K(\nu,y)\rangle
. Finally using Transformers universality for in context functions
(
5
)
we can approximate each function by transformers.
∎

Our result shows that a single Sinkhorn Transformer can approximate arbitrary joint relations between texts, i.e., it can represent all contextual relations simultaneously as it is approximating the whole coupling system.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced a measure-theoretic framework for contextual representations in which attention mechanisms are modeled as operators that assign coupling measures between semantic distributions. Within this framework, we established a universal approximation result characterizing the expressive power of Transformer-style attention mechanisms at the level of contextual relations.

Beyond the formal result, our analysis provides a principled interpretation of semantic representation in attention-based models. Rather than treating attention weights as heuristic similarity scores, our framework views semantic understanding as the ability to represent structured probabilistic relations between meanings, formalized as coupling measures with prescribed marginals.

Our work focuses on representational expressivity and does not address questions of learning dynamics, statistical efficiency, or generalization. Understanding how such coupling-valued representations can be learned from finite data, and under what conditions they arise in practice, remains an open problem.

Several directions for future work naturally emerge from our results. First, it would be of interest to derive quantitative approximation rates and sample complexity bounds for coupling-valued attention mechanisms. Second, extending the present framework to sequential or dynamical settings could provide insight into the role of attention in autoregressive and generative models. Finally, exploring alternative regularization schemes beyond entropic optimal transport may lead to richer classes of contextual relations while preserving analytical tractability.

9
Impact Statement

This paper presents theoretical work whose primary goal is to advance the mathematical understanding of Transformer architectures and attention mechanisms. In particular, it develops a rigorous framework connecting contextual attention with optimal transport and establishes a universal approximation result for semantic coupling functions.
The results are foundational in nature and do not introduce new models, training procedures, datasets, or deployment strategies. As such, the work does not pose direct ethical risks nor immediate societal consequences beyond those already well established in the broader field of machine learning.
More broadly, improved theoretical understanding of widely used architectures may contribute in the long term to the development of more interpretable, reliable, and principled learning systems. Any potential societal impact of this work is therefore indirect and mediated by future applications and deployments, which fall outside the scope of the present paper.

\operatorname{proj}_{X}G_{n}(\mu,\nu)=0
, so:

\left|\int_{X}\psi\,d(\operatorname{proj}_{X}G(\mu,\nu))\right|<\varepsilon\quad\forall\psi\in C(X),\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

This implies
\|\operatorname{proj}_{X}G(\mu,\nu)\|_{TV}\leq\varepsilon
uniformly in
(\mu,\nu)
. Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,
\operatorname{proj}_{X}G(\mu,\nu)=0
uniformly. The same holds for the second marginal. Hence,
\mathcal{F}
is closed.
∎

Theorem A.2
.

The convex set
\mathcal{C}_{P}
is closed.

Proof.

Closedness:
Let
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{C}_{P}
converge uniformly to
F\in\mathcal{C}
in the weak* sense. We need to show that
F(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
for all
(\mu,\nu)
.

Fix
(\mu,\nu)
. For any non-negative
\varphi\in C(X\times Y)
, we have:

\int_{X\times Y}\varphi\,dF_{n}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0.

By weak* convergence,

\int_{X\times Y}\varphi\,dF(\mu,\nu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\varphi\,dF_{n}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0,

so
F(\mu,\nu)
is a non-negative measure.

Now take
\varphi\equiv 1
. Then:

F(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)=\int_{X\times Y}1\,dF(\mu,\nu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X\times Y}1\,dF_{n}(\mu,\nu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}1=1.

Thus,
F(\mu,\nu)
is a probability measure for each
(\mu,\nu)
, so
F\in\mathcal{C}_{P}
.

The uniform convergence ensures that these properties hold uniformly: for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N
such that for all
n\geq N
and all
(\mu,\nu)
,

\left|\int_{X\times Y}\varphi\,d(F_{n}(\mu,\nu)-F(\mu,\nu))\right|<\varepsilon\quad\forall\varphi\in C(X\times Y)\text{ with }\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

In particular, taking
\varphi\equiv 1
, we get
|F_{n}(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)-F(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)|<\varepsilon
, but
F_{n}(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)=1
, so
|1-F(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)|<\varepsilon
uniformly. Hence,
F(\mu,\nu)(X\times Y)=1
uniformly.

Similarly, for non-negativity: for any non-negative
\varphi
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have:

\int\varphi\,dF(\mu,\nu)\geq\int\varphi\,dF_{n}(\mu,\nu)-\varepsilon\geq-\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary and this holds for all such
\varphi
, we conclude
F(\mu,\nu)\geq 0
uniformly.
∎

Lemma A.3

(Approximation by Absolutely Continuous Plans)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces, and let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be a transport plan. Then there exists a sequence of measures
\pi_{k}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
k
:

1.

\pi_{k}\ll\mu\otimes\nu
(i.e., absolutely continuous with respect to the product measure),

2.

the density
\frac{d\pi_{k}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}
is bounded,

3.

\pi_{k}\rightharpoonup\pi
as
k\to\infty
(weak convergence).

Moreover, if
\pi
is a probability measure, then
\pi_{k}
are also probability measures.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in several constructive steps.

Step 1: Construction of partitions of
X\times Y
.

Since
X
and
Y
are compact,
X\times Y
is compact. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider a finite partition
P_{k}
of
X\times Y
into measurable rectangles of the form
A_{i}\times B_{j}
, where
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
is a measurable partition of
X
and
\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M_{k}}
is a measurable partition of
Y
, such that the diameter of each rectangle
A_{i}\times B_{j}
satisfies
\text{diam}(A_{i}\times B_{j})<\frac{1}{k}
.

This partition can be constructed as follows: consider on
X\times Y
the metric given by
d((x,y),(\bar{x},\bar{y}))=d_{X}(x,\bar{x})+d_{Y}(y,\bar{y})
. Then:

1.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider coverings of
X
and
Y
by closed balls of radius
\frac{1}{4k}
.

2.

By compactness, extract finite subcoverings:

X\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\overline{B}_{X}(x_{i},\tfrac{1}{4k}),\quad Y\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\overline{B}_{Y}(y_{j},\tfrac{1}{4k}).

3.

Disjointify the coverings by defining inductively:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{X}(x_{1},\tfrac{1}{4k}),

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{X}(x_{i},\tfrac{1}{4k})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}A_{j},\quad i=2,\ldots,N_{k},

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{Y}(y_{1},\tfrac{1}{4k}),

\displaystyle B_{j}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{Y}(y_{j},\tfrac{1}{4k})\setminus\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{j-1}B_{\ell},\quad j=2,\ldots,M_{k}.

4.

The rectangles
A_{i}\times B_{j}
form the partition
P_{k}
of
X\times Y
. Note that
\text{diam}_{X}(B(x,r))\leq 2r
and
\text{diam}(A\times B)\leq\text{diam}_{X}(A)+\text{diam}_{Y}(B)
, from which it follows that
\text{diam}(A_{i}\times B_{j})<\frac{1}{k}
.

Step 2: Definition of
\pi_{k}
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the measure
\pi_{k}
via its density with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. Specifically, for each rectangle
A_{i}\times B_{j}
in
P_{k}
, define:

f_{k}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\quad\text{for }(x,y)\in X\times Y,

where we adopt the convention that if
\mu(A_{i})=0
or
\nu(B_{j})=0
, then the corresponding term is zero (which is consistent since in that case
\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})=0
because
\pi
is a transport plan).

Define then:

d\pi_{k}(x,y)=f_{k}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y).

Clearly,
\pi_{k}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
.

Note that if
\pi
is a probability measure,
\pi_{k}
is also a probability measure:

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(X\times Y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}f_{k}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\int_{X\times Y}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y).

The integral
\int_{X\times Y}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)
equals
(\mu\otimes\nu)(A_{i}\times B_{j})=\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})
. Thus,

\pi_{k}(X\times Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\cdot\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j}).

Since
\{A_{i}\times B_{j}\}_{i,j}
is a partition of
X\times Y
, the sum above is
\pi(X\times Y)=1
. Therefore,
\pi_{k}(X\times Y)=1
, and
\pi_{k}
is a probability measure.

Step 3: Verification that
\pi_{k}
is a transport plan.

We must show that the marginals of
\pi_{k}
are exactly
\mu
and
\nu
. Compute the marginal on
X
(the other is completely analogous). For any measurable set
A\subset X
:

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(A\times Y)

\displaystyle=\int_{A\times Y}f_{k}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\int_{A\times Y}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y).

Note that:

\int_{A\times Y}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)=\mu(A\cap A_{i})\nu(B_{j}),

since
\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(x,y)=1
if and only if
x\in A_{i}
and
y\in B_{j}
. Therefore:

\pi_{k}(A\times Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\mu(A\cap A_{i})\nu(B_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})\frac{\mu(A\cap A_{i})}{\mu(A_{i})}.

For each fixed
i
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})=\pi(A_{i}\times Y)=\mu(A_{i})
, since the marginal of
\pi
on
X
is
\mu
. Hence:

\pi_{k}(A\times Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{\mu(A\cap A_{i})}{\mu(A_{i})}\mu(A_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\mu(A\cap A_{i})=\mu(A),

where the last equality follows because the
A_{i}
form a partition of
X
. Therefore, the first marginal of
\pi_{k}
is
\mu
.

Step 4: Weak convergence of
\pi_{k}
to
\pi
.

Let
f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Since
X\times Y
is compact,
f
is uniformly continuous. Therefore, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
and for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in the same rectangle
A_{i}\times B_{j}
of
P_{k}
, we have:

|f(x,y)-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|<\delta.

Now, for each rectangle
A_{i}\times B_{j}
, choose a point
(x_{i},y_{j})\in A_{i}\times B_{j}
and consider the integral:

\displaystyle\int f\,d\pi_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)f_{k}(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\frac{\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\int_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}f(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y).

(1)

For each term in the sum, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}f(x,y)\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)-f(x_{i},y_{j})\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}|f(x,y)-f(x_{i},y_{j})|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)\leq\delta\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j}).

Therefore:

\left|\frac{1}{\mu(A_{i})\nu(B_{j})}\int_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}(f(x,y)-f(x_{i},y_{j}))\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)(x,y)\right|\leq\delta.

Combining (3.2) and (3.3):

\left|\int f\,d\pi_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})f(x_{i},y_{j})\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})\delta=\pi(X\times Y)\delta.

On the other hand, consider the integral with respect to
\pi
and using (3.1):

\displaystyle\left|\int f\,d\pi-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}f(x_{i},y_{j})\pi(A_{i}\times B_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{k}}\int_{A_{i}\times B_{j}}|f(x,y)-f(x_{i},y_{j})|\,d\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\pi(X\times Y)\delta.

(3.5)

Combining inequalities (3.4) and (3.5) yields:

\left|\int f\,d\pi_{k}-\int f\,d\pi\right|\leq 2\delta\pi(X\times Y).

Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int f\,d\pi_{k}=\int f\,d\pi.

Therefore,
\pi_{k}\rightharpoonup\pi
weakly.
∎

Lemma A.4

(Entropic Representation)
.

Fix
(\mu,\nu)
probability measures. Let
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with density
\rho:=\frac{d\gamma}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}>0
on
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\times\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
. Define
c(x,y):=-\epsilon\log\rho(x,y)
. Then
\gamma
is the unique minimizer of the functional

J(\gamma^{\prime}):=\int_{X\times Y}c\,d\gamma^{\prime}+\epsilon\text{KL}(\gamma^{\prime}\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)

among
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

Proof.

It is convenient to write everything with respect to the reference measure
\mu\otimes\nu
: if
\gamma^{\prime}\ll\mu\otimes\nu
we write
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{d\gamma^{\prime}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}\geq 0
. (If
\gamma^{\prime}
had a singular part with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
, then
\text{KL}(\gamma^{\prime}\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)=+\infty
and it cannot be a minimizer; thus we only consider measures with densities
\rho^{\prime}
.)

Now compute
J(\gamma^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle J(\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int(-\epsilon\log\rho)\rho^{\prime}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)+\epsilon\int\rho^{\prime}\log\rho^{\prime}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int\rho^{\prime}\log\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\epsilon\int\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}\log\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}\,d\gamma

\displaystyle=\epsilon\text{KL}(\gamma^{\prime}\parallel\gamma)\geq 0.

We conclude that
J(\gamma^{\prime})=0
if and only if
\rho^{\prime}=\rho

\mu\otimes\nu
-almost everywhere. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.5

(Gamma Convergence of Entropic Optimal Transport)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
fixed probability measures, and
\varepsilon>0
a regularization parameter. Consider a sequence of cost functions
c_{n}:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
and a cost function
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

c_{n}
are continuous for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

2.

There exists
K>0
such that
|c_{n}(x,y)|\leq K
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x,y)\in X\times Y
(equiboundedness),

3.

c_{n}\to c
in
L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
.

For each
n
, define the functional
J_{n}:\Pi(\mu,\nu)\to\mathbb{R}
by:

J_{n}(\pi)=\int_{X\times Y}c_{n}\,d\pi+\varepsilon\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu),

and define
J:\Pi(\mu,\nu)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
by:

J(\pi)=\int_{X\times Y}c\,d\pi+\varepsilon\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu).

Then,
J_{n}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}J
in
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with the weak* topology.

Proof.

We prove each part of the Gamma convergence separately.

1. Liminf inequality.
Let
\pi_{n}\rightharpoonup\pi
in
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. We want to show that

J(\pi)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}(\pi_{n}).

Without loss of generality, assume
\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}(\pi_{n})<\infty
, otherwise the inequality is trivial. Then in particular:

\text{KL}(\pi_{n}\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

which implies

\pi_{n}\ll\mu\otimes\nu\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, since the KL divergence is lower semicontinuous:

\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\text{KL}(\pi_{n}\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty,

so
\pi
is also absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. Let
f_{n},f
be the densities of
\pi_{n},\pi
with respect to the product measure. Using Vallée-Poussin’s theorem for the function
x\log(x)
, we conclude that
\{f_{n}\}
are uniformly integrable.

Since the KL divergence is lower semicontinuous, to prove the liminf inequality it suffices to show convergence of the linear term, i.e.:

\int c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\to\int cf\,d(\mu\otimes\nu).

By Egorov’s theorem, for any
\delta>0
, there exists a set
E_{\delta}
such that:

c_{n}\to c\text{ in }E_{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu\otimes\nu(X\setminus E_{\delta})<\delta.

Then,

\int_{X}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\int_{E_{\delta}}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)+\int_{X\setminus E_{\delta}}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu).

The second term can be made arbitrarily small because:

\left|\int_{X\setminus E_{\delta}}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\right|\leq\int_{X\setminus E_{\delta}}|c_{n}||f_{n}|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu).

Using that the
c_{n}
are uniformly bounded and the
f_{n}
are uniformly integrable, taking an appropriate
\delta
yields:

\left|\int_{X\setminus E_{\delta}}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\right|\leq M\epsilon.

For the first term,

\int_{E_{\delta}}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\int_{E_{\delta}}(c_{n}-c)f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)+\int_{E_{\delta}}cf_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu).

Since
\pi_{n}\rightharpoonup\pi
,
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{1}
, so
\{f_{n}\}
are bounded in
L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
. Thus,

\int_{E_{\delta}}(c_{n}-c)f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\leq\|f_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)}\|c_{n}-c\|_{L^{\infty}(E_{\delta})}\to 0,

because
c_{n}
converges uniformly to
c
on
E_{\delta}
. Meanwhile, since
c
is bounded, by weak convergence of
f_{n}
:

\int_{E_{\delta}}cf_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\to\int_{E_{\delta}}cf\,d(\mu\otimes\nu).

Putting everything together, we obtain:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{X\times Y}c_{n}f_{n}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\geq\int_{E_{\delta}}cf\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)-M\varepsilon.

Taking
\delta,\varepsilon\to 0
, we conclude the desired inequality.

2. Limsup inequality.
Let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. If
J(\pi)=\infty
, the inequality is trivial. Suppose
J(\pi)<\infty
, which implies
\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty
and thus
\pi\ll\mu\otimes\nu
. Take the constant sequence
\pi_{n}=\pi
for all
n
, which clearly satisfies
\pi_{n}\rightharpoonup\pi
. It suffices to show that:

J_{n}(\pi)\to J(\pi).

Note that the KL divergence term is the same for all
n
. Therefore, we only need to analyze the linear term. Given a subsequence (still denoted by
(c_{n})
), since
c_{n}\to c
in
L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
, there exists a subsequence that converges
\mu\otimes\nu
-almost everywhere, and since
\pi\ll\mu\otimes\nu
, it also converges
\pi
-almost everywhere. Moreover, using that the costs are uniformly bounded
|c_{n}|\leq M
, we have
|c_{n}-c|\leq 2M
. Then by the dominated convergence theorem:

\int c_{n}\,d\pi\to\int c\,d\pi.

This completes the proof.
∎

We would like to regularize the measures to facilitate calculations. With this in mind, we prove a regularization lemma.

Lemma A.6

(Regularization Lemma)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
probability measures, and
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
a transport plan with density
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mu\otimes\nu)
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a transport plan
\pi_{\varepsilon}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with density
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in C(X\times Y)
continuous and strictly positive (
\rho_{\varepsilon}>\eta>0
) such that
W_{p}(\pi,\pi_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
. Where
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is independent of
\mu,\nu
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. The proof proceeds in three constructive stages.

Stage 1: Regularization for strict positivity.

Let
0<\delta<1
. Define the regularized measure:

\pi_{\delta}=(1-\delta)\pi+\delta(\mu\otimes\nu).

Since
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is convex, it follows that
\pi_{\delta}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Moreover, the density of
\pi_{\delta}
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
is given by
\rho_{\delta}=(1-\delta)\rho+\delta
, and since
\rho\geq 0
and
\delta>0
, we have
\rho_{\delta}\geq\delta>0
.

Clearly,
\pi_{\delta}\to\pi
as
\delta\to 0
in total variation norm. Therefore, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that:

W_{p}(\pi,\pi_{\delta_{0}})<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Define
\pi_{1}=\pi_{\delta_{0}}
and denote
\rho_{1}=\rho_{\delta_{0}}
its density.

Stage 2: Approximation by continuous density.

Consider the cost
c=-\log(\rho_{1})
. Since
\rho_{1}
is bounded and strictly positive,
c
is bounded.

By density of continuous functions in
L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
, given
\delta>0
, there exists a continuous function
c_{\delta}\in C(X\times Y)
such that:

\|c-c_{\delta}\|_{L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)}<\delta.

Also note that since
\rho_{1}
is bounded from above independently of
\mu,\nu
,
c
is bounded from below, so we can choose
c_{\delta}
to be bounded from above too.

Now consider the entropic optimal transport problem:

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int c_{\delta}\,d\pi+\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)\right\}.

By
(
7
)
, this problem has a unique solution
\pi_{\delta}
with density:

\rho_{\delta}=u_{\delta}e^{-c_{\delta}}v_{\delta},

where
u_{\delta}\in C(X)
and
v_{\delta}\in C(Y)
are continuous positive functions. Therefore,
\rho_{\delta}
is continuous.

When
\delta\to 0
,
c_{\delta}\to c
in
L^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
. By Lemma A.5 on stability of entropic solutions,
\pi_{\delta}
converges in
W_{p}
to the solution of the problem with cost
c
, which is precisely
\pi_{1}
.

Therefore, for
\delta
sufficiently small:

W_{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{\delta})<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Fix
\delta_{1}
with this property and call
\pi_{2}=\pi_{\delta_{1}}
.

Stage 3: Final regularization for uniform positivity.

Reasoning similarly to Stage 1, we can approximate
\pi_{2}
by another transport plan
\pi_{3}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that it is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
with strictly positive density, and from the construction it follows that the density of
\pi_{3}
is continuous since the density of
\pi_{2}
is continuous. Moreover, this plan
\pi_{3}
satisfies:

W_{p}(\pi_{2},\pi_{3})<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Note that since
X,Y
are bounded metric spaces we can choose
\pi_{3}
by controlling the total variation norm we have a lower bound independent of
\mu,\nu
.

Conclusion.

Combining the three estimates:

W_{p}(\pi,\pi_{3})\leq W_{p}(\pi,\pi_{1})+W_{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})+W_{p}(\pi_{2},\pi_{3})<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}=\varepsilon.

The plan
\pi_{3}
has density
\rho_{3}
continuous and strictly positive (
\rho_{3}\geq\eta>0
), which completes the proof.
∎

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
each transport plan
\pi\in P(\mu,\nu)
can be approximated by a solution of the entropic optimal transport problem
S_{c}(\mu,\nu)
.

Proof.

Combining Lemma A.3 with Lemma A.6, we obtain that for any transport plan, we can approximate it by one with
continuous density with respect to the product measure. Applying Lemma A.4 on regularization, we conclude the proof.

Theorem A.8

(Approximation Theorem for Coupling Functions by Sinkhorn Operators)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces, and let
F:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
be a continuous mapping such that
F(\mu,\nu)\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
for every pair
(\mu,\nu)
.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a continuous mapping

c:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to C(X\times Y)

such that, for each
(\mu,\nu)
, if
S_{c(\mu,\nu)}
denotes the entropically regularized transport plan associated with the cost
c(\mu,\nu)
, we have

\sup_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)}W_{p}\left(S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu),F(\mu,\nu)\right)<\varepsilon.

Proof.

The proof is organized in six main steps.

Step 1: Equivalence of distances and reduction to
W_{1}
.

Since
X
and
Y
are compact metric spaces, all Wasserstein distances
W_{p}
are equivalent. Therefore, without loss of generality, we work with the distance
W_{1}
throughout the proof.

Step 2: Definition of the multifunction
\Gamma
.

Let
\eta>0
be the constant from the previous lemma A.6,

For each pair
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)
, define the set:

\Gamma(\mu,\nu):=\left\{f\in C(X\times Y)\ \middle|\ \begin{aligned} &(1)\ f>\eta\text{ on }X\times Y,\\
&(2)\ f\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),\\
&(3)\ f\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)\in B_{W_{1}}\left(F(\mu,\nu),\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\end{aligned}\right\}.

Step 3: Properties of
\Gamma
.

We show that
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
satisfies the following properties:

•

Non-empty:
By the continuity and approximation lemmas (Lemmas A.3 and A.6), for each
(\mu,\nu)
there exists at least one continuous density
f\geq\eta
that approximates
F(\mu,\nu)
within
W_{1}
distance less than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Therefore,
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\neq\emptyset
for all
(\mu,\nu)
.

•

Convex:
Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
and
t\in[0,1]
. Define
f_{t}=tf_{1}+(1-t)f_{2}
. Verify each condition:

–

Positivity:

f_{t}=tf_{1}+(1-t)f_{2}>t\eta+(1-t)\eta=\eta
.

–

Normalization:

\int f_{t}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=t\int f_{1}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)+(1-t)\int f_{2}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=t+(1-t)=1
.

–

Marginals:
For any measurable
A\subset X
,

\int_{A\times Y}f_{t}\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=t\mu(A)+(1-t)\mu(A)=\mu(A),

and similarly for
\nu
. Hence,
f_{t}\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

•

Distance to
F(\mu,\nu)
:
Let
\pi_{1}=f_{1}\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)
,
\pi_{2}=f_{2}\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)
, and
\pi_{t}=f_{t}\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)=t\pi_{1}+(1-t)\pi_{2}
. Using the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual formulation it is easy to prove that the ball is convex, so we have

W_{1}(\pi_{t},F(\mu,\nu))\leq tW_{1}(\pi_{1},F(\mu,\nu))+(1-t)W_{1}(\pi_{2},F(\mu,\nu))<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Therefore,
f_{t}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
and
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is convex.

Moreover, as shown in Proposition A.9, the multifunction
\Gamma
is lower hemicontinuous.

Step 4: Approximate continuous selection.

Since
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is convex, non-empty, and
\Gamma
is lower hemicontinuous, we can apply the approximate selection theorem
(
2
)
. Then, there exists then an approximate continuous selection

s:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to C(X\times Y)

such that

\inf_{t\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\|s(\mu,\nu)-t\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2L_{\text{sink}}L_{\log}},

where:

•

L_{\log}
is the Lipschitz constant of the transformation
-\log:f\mapsto-\log f
for functions greater than some
f>\eta>0
:

|-\log s(x,y)+\log t(x,y)|\leq\frac{1}{\eta}|s(x,y)-t(x,y)|,

i.e.,
\|-\log(s)+\log(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\|s-t\|_{\infty}
.

•

L_{\text{sink}}
is the Lipschitz constant of the Sinkhorn operator.

Note that, since
s(\mu,\nu)
is uniformly close to strictly positive functions (greater than
\eta
), for fixed
(\mu,\nu)
,
s(\mu,\nu)
is also strictly positive.

Step 5: Definition of the cost.

Define the cost
c
as

c(\mu,\nu)(x,y):=-\log s(\mu,\nu)(x,y).

This function remains continuous because
s(\mu,\nu)
is continuous and we have shown that applying the logarithm to such functions preserves continuity.

Step 6: Final verification of the approximation.

For each
(\mu,\nu)
, let
t\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
such that
\|s(\mu,\nu)-t\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2L_{\text{sink}}L_{\log}}
. Then,

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(F(\mu,\nu),S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu)\right)

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}\left(F(\mu,\nu),S_{-\log t}(\mu,\nu)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+W_{1}\left(S_{-\log t}(\mu,\nu),S_{-\log s(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu)\right).

Analyze each term separately:

•

First term:
By construction of
\Gamma
, and since
S_{-\log t}(\mu,\nu)=t\cdot(\mu\otimes\nu)
(Lemma A.4), we have

W_{1}\left(F(\mu,\nu),S_{-\log t}(\mu,\nu)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

•

Second term:
By the Lipschitz continuity of the Sinkhorn operator
(
9
)
and the logarithmic transformation, we have

W_{1}\left(S_{-\log t}(\mu,\nu),S_{-\log s(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu)\right)\leq L_{\text{sink}}L_{\log}\|t-s(\mu,\nu)\|_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Combining both estimates, we obtain

W_{1}\left(F(\mu,\nu),S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Proposition A.9

(Lower Hemicontinuity of
\Gamma
)
.

The multifunction

\Gamma:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to C(X\times Y)

is lower hemicontinuous. That is, for every
(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
,
s_{0}\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
and every sequence
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\to(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
in the weak topology, there exists a sequence
s_{n}\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
such that
s_{n}\to s_{0}
uniformly.

Proof.

The proof is organized in four constructive steps.

Step 1: Construction of the sequence
s_{n}
.

Let
s_{0}\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
with
s_{0}>\eta>0
. Consider the cost function
c=-\log(s_{0})
. Since
s_{0}
is continuous and strictly positive,
c
is continuous and bounded.

By
(
7
)
on existence and uniqueness for the entropic problem, for each
n
there exist continuous functions
u_{n},v_{n}>0
such that:

s_{n}(x,y):=u_{n}(x)s_{0}(x,y)v_{n}(y)

satisfies
s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})\in\Pi(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
.

For
\mu_{0},\nu_{0}
, the solution of the entropic problem with cost
c
is unique and corresponds to
s_{0}(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0})
, with potentials
u_{0}=1
and
v_{0}=1
.

Step 2: Uniform convergence of
s_{n}
to
s_{0}
.

By
(
10
)
on uniform convergence of Schrödinger potentials, we have:

\|u_{n}v_{n}-1\|_{\infty}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Then, for the convergence of
s_{n}
:

\|s_{n}-s_{0}\|_{\infty}=\|s_{0}u_{n}v_{n}-s_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\|s_{0}\|_{\infty}\|u_{n}v_{n}-1\|_{\infty}\to 0.

Moreover, by compactness and continuity,
s_{0}
attains its minimum:

\min_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}s_{0}(x,y)>\eta>0.

For
n
sufficiently large:

s_{n}(x,y)=s_{0}(x,y)u_{n}(x)v_{n}(y)>\eta\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in X\times Y.

Step 3: Weak convergence of the measures.

Consider the convergence of the measures
s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})
to
s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0})
. For any test function
\varphi\in C(X\times Y)
, analyze:

\displaystyle I_{n}(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\int\varphi\,d(s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}))-\int\varphi\,d(s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}))

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(s_{n}-s_{0})\,d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})+\int\varphi s_{0}\,d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})-\int\varphi s_{0}\,d(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}).

•

For
A_{n}(\varphi)=\int\varphi(s_{n}-s_{0})\,d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})
:

|A_{n}(\varphi)|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|s_{n}-s_{0}\|_{\infty}\to 0.

•

For
B_{n}(\varphi)=\int\varphi s_{0}\,d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})-\int\varphi s_{0}\,d(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0})
: since
\varphi s_{0}
is continuous and
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\rightharpoonup(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
weakly, we have
B_{n}(\varphi)\to 0
.

Therefore,
s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})\rightharpoonup s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0})
.

Step 4: Control of the
W_{1}
distance.

Verify that for
n
sufficiently large,
s_{n}\in B_{W_{1}}\left(F(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}),\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)
. Let
D:=\text{diam}(X\times Y)<\infty
and define:

d_{0}:=W_{1}(s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}),F(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}))<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

where the strict inequality follows from
s_{0}\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
. Let
\delta_{0}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-d_{0}>0
.

By the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle W_{1}(s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}),F(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{W_{1}(s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}),s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}))}_{T_{1,n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{W_{1}(s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}),s_{0}\cdot(\mu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}))}_{T_{2,n}}

\displaystyle\quad+d_{0}+\underbrace{W_{1}(F(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),F(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}))}_{T_{3,n}}.

Analyze each term:

•

T_{1,n}\leq\frac{D}{2}\|s_{n}-s_{0}\|_{\infty}\to 0
by uniform convergence.

•

T_{2,n}\to 0
by weak convergence on compact space.

•

T_{3,n}\to 0
by continuity of
F
.

Consequently, there exists
N
such that for all
n\geq N
:
T_{1,n},T_{2,n},T_{3,n}\leq\delta_{0}/3
. Then:

W_{1}(s_{n}\cdot(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n}),F(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}))<\frac{\delta_{0}}{3}+\frac{\delta_{0}}{3}+d_{0}+\frac{\delta_{0}}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

By construction,
s_{n}
is continuous,
s_{n}>\eta
,
s_{n}(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})\in\Pi(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
, and
s_{n}\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
for
n
sufficiently large. This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem A.11

(Universal Approximation by Sinkhorn-Transformers)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
be continuous and such that
F(\mu,\nu)\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
for all
(\mu,\nu)
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a Sinkhorn-Transformer architecture
\mathcal{T}^{*}
such that

\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu),F(\mu,\nu))<\varepsilon.

Proof.

We separate the proof into five steps.

Step 1: Reduction to continuous costs via entropic optimal transport.

By the Entropic Approximation Theorem (Theorem A.8), for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a continuous function

c:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to C(X\times Y)

such that

\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu),F(\mu,\nu))<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

where
S_{c(\mu,\nu)}
is the Sinkhorn plan associated with the cost
c(\mu,\nu)
.

Step 2: Error control via Lipschitz continuity of Sinkhorn.

Let
L_{\text{Sink}}
be the Lipschitz constant of the operator
c\mapsto S_{c}
with respect to the supremum norm on bounded costs, as stated in
(
9
)
. Then

W_{1}(S_{c}(\mu,\nu),S_{c^{\prime}}(\mu,\nu))\leq L_{\text{Sink}}\|c-c^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where
L_{\text{Sink}}
is independent of
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)
.

Step 3: Approximation of the cost by inner products.

Note that the space of continuous functions
C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y),C(X\times Y))
is equivalent to
C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\times X\times Y)
.

Moreover, the space
\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\times X\times Y
is compact, because
\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\mathcal{P}(Y)
are compact (in the weak-* topology) and
X
and
Y
are compact. Consider the algebra of functions of the form:

f(\mu,\nu,x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}G_{k}(\mu,x)H_{k}(\nu,y),

where
G_{k}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to\mathbb{R}
and
H_{k}:\mathcal{P}(Y)\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous. This algebra:

•

Contains constants: if
d=1
and
G_{1}\equiv 1
,
H_{1}\equiv c
, then
f\equiv c
.

•

Separates points: for any pair of distinct points
(\mu,\nu,x,y)\neq(\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, there exist functions
G_{k}
and
H_{k}
that distinguish these points.

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, this algebra is dense in
C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\times X\times Y)
.

Since
c\in C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\times X\times Y)
, for any
\delta>0
there exist continuous functions
G_{k},H_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
such that:

\left|c(\mu,\nu)(x,y)-\sum_{k=1}^{d}G_{k}(\mu,x)H_{k}(\nu,y)\right|<\delta\quad\forall(\mu,\nu,x,y).

Define:

\displaystyle G(\mu,x)

\displaystyle=(G_{1}(\mu,x),\ldots,G_{d}(\mu,x))\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle H(\nu,y)

\displaystyle=(H_{1}(\nu,y),\ldots,H_{d}(\nu,y))\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the approximated cost:

c^{\prime}(\mu,\nu)(x,y)=\langle G(\mu,x),H(\nu,y)\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}G_{k}(\mu,x)H_{k}(\nu,y).

Then:

\|c-c^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\delta.

Step 4: Approximation of
G
and
H
by Transformers.

Now, by the Universal Approximation Theorem for in-context Transformers

(
5
)
, the continuous functions
G
and
H
, defined
on a compact domain, can be approximated uniformly by two Transformer encoders

Q^{*}
and
K^{*}
. Using linearity of the Euclidean inner product, we write

\displaystyle\langle Q^{*}(\mu,x),K^{*}(\nu,y)\rangle-\langle G(\mu,x),H(\nu,y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Q^{*}(\mu,x)-G(\mu,x),\,K^{*}(\nu,y)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle G(\mu,x),\,K^{*}(\nu,y)-H(\nu,y)\rangle.

Applying the triangle inequality and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big|\langle Q^{*}(\mu,x),K^{*}(\nu,y)\rangle-\langle G(\mu,x),H(\nu,y)\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|Q^{*}(\mu,x)-G(\mu,x)\|_{2}\,\|K^{*}(\nu,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|G(\mu,x)\|_{2}\,\|K^{*}(\nu,y)-H(\nu,y)\|_{2}.

Since

\|K^{*}(\nu,y)\|_{2}\leq\|H(\nu,y)\|_{2}+\|K^{*}(\nu,y)-H(\nu,y)\|_{2},

taking the supremum over the compact domain yields

\displaystyle\sup\big|\langle Q^{*},K^{*}\rangle-\langle G,H\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|Q^{*}-G\|_{\infty}\,\|H\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\|Q^{*}-G\|_{\infty}+\|G\|_{\infty}\big)\,\|K^{*}-H\|_{\infty}.

Since
G
and
H
are fixed and uniformly bounded, for any
\delta>0
the
right-hand side can be made smaller than
\delta
by choosing the
approximations sufficiently accurate.

Step 5: Putting everything together.

Define the Sinkhorn-Transformer
\mathcal{T}^{*}=S_{c^{*}}
with

c^{*}(\mu,\nu)(x,y)=\langle Q^{*}(\mu,x),K^{*}(\nu,y)\rangle.

Then
\mathcal{T}^{*}
has the Sinkhorn-Transformer architecture. Using the Lipschitz bound on the Sinkhorn operator and the triangle inequality, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(F(\mu,\nu),\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu))

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(F(\mu,\nu),S_{c^{*}(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu))

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(S_{c^{*}(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu),\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu))

By choosing
\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{2L_{\text{Sink}}}
, we finally get

\sup_{(\mu,\nu)}W_{1}(F(\mu,\nu),\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu))<\varepsilon,

which concludes the proof.

∎
