ABSTRACT
Geoengineering via stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) has potential to mitigate the negative
effects of rising global temperatures. SAI simulation campaigns have, so far, largely focused
on idealized scenarios which inject aerosols in the tropical and subtropical zones, at altitudes
unattainable by modern transport aircraft. To reflect the reality of existing aviation technology, a
scenario has been designed to model SAI injections at high latitudes and low altitudes (HiLLA),
titled G6-1.5K-HiLLA. This report details a contribution of Energy Exascale Earth System Model
version 3 (E3SMv3) simulations to a proposed model intercomparison project funded and organized
by Reflective, a nonprofit group studying the possible global impacts of SAI. Using annual feedback
control and seasonally-variable injection sites to achieve a desired global near-surface temperature
target, the simulated SAI campaign indicates robust maintenance of the 2020-2039 climatic state
over 60 years into the future. This result is encouraging, but may be model-dependent, and
necessitates further simulation campaigns modifying injection altitude. Comparisons between
other Earth system models will be conducted by the research community, in part supported by
Reflective grants, and published separately.

1. INTRODUCTION

Solar radiation management (SRM) has been posed as a potential solution to rising global tem-
peratures in the absence of drastic reductions in anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions [1]. In
brief, SRM classifies any method by which the amount of solar radiation absorbed by the Earth
is deliberately altered, modifying the Earth’s total energy budget in an attempt to adjust surface
or atmospheric temperatures. Proposed approaches include stratospheric aerosol injections (SAI),
marine cloud brightening [2], and surface albedo modification [3]. As such modifications of the
climate would have global effects, and thus present a challenging governance landscape [4], SRM
experiments have been extremely limited. Instead, scientists rely on earth system models (ESMs) to
simulate the effects of SRM methods and better understand their complex interactions with climate
dynamics.

SAI is arguably the most-studied SRM approach, entailing the dispersal of minuscule liquid droplets
(or gas precursors) at very high altitudes [5]. These aerosols reflect a portion of incoming solar
radiation back into space, reducing the total amount of energy input to the Earth system and theo-
retically reducing surface temperatures. Some confidence in this approach derives from historical
observations of explosive volcanic eruptions, which launch sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere and
result in some measure of surface cooling, thus serving as natural proxies for SAI [6]. The practical
implementation of a similar anthropogenic scenario is understandably complex, and has inspired
a wide array of modeling research over the past decade. This includes questions on the location
to inject the aerosols [7], the time periods over which to distribute them [8], how to actively adapt
injection strategies to changes in the climate [9], and the logistics of manufacturing the aerosols
and the aircraft required to disperse them [10].

The Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP) [11] provides a consistent protocol
for simulating SAI scenarios across ESMs and distributing simulation results. Any modeling
group can contribute to the experiment following the protocol. Experiment protocols include
specification of the simulation time span, background emissions, injection locations, aerosol mass,
and time periods, and climate targets for experiments with controlled SAI. This report details a
contribution of Energy Exascale Earth System Model version 3 (E3SMv3) simulations to a Testbed
GeoMIP scenario that was initially presented at the 2025 GeoMIP meeting [12] and funded by the
nonprofit organization Reflective [13]. The scenario entails SAI at high latitudes and low altitudes,
seasonal injection periods to maximize aerosol effectiveness, and feedback control of injection
rates to maintain a target global mean near-surface temperature. This report only details the
E3SMv3 contribution and methodology; two other ESM groups, UKESM and WACCM, are also
contributing simulations. The results from these three ESMs will be analyzed by Reflective funding
awardees. This analysis is being coordinated by Reflective to advance the scientific understanding
of the potential benefits and risks of SAI, to support informed future decision-making.

1.1. SAI scenario

The SAI scenario simulated in this work is a proposed experiment of high-latitude, low-altitude
(HiLLA) SAI, referred to as G6-1.5K-HiLLA. The experiment follows the background emissions
and target design of the G6-1.5K-SAI experiment [14], and was originally described and proposed
at the GeoMIP 2025 meeting [12]. Early results from idealized HiLLA injections without feedback
control were detailed by Duffey et al. [15]. The core elements of the G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario are
outlined in Table 1-1.

 Table 1-1. Simulation specifications for G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario

       Length                   50 years (2035-2085)
 SAI initiation 2035
 Injection altitude 15 km
 Injection latitudes 60◦ N and 60◦ S
 Injection longitude 180◦ E
 Injection season MAMJ at 60◦ N, SOND at 60◦ S

Prior GeoMIP scenarios have proposed injection latitudes significantly closer to the equator, where
SAI must be conducted at significantly higher altitudes (e.g., 30◦ N/S at 21 km altitude [14]).
However, current aviation technology is incapable of injecting the large masses of aerosols required
for a viable SAI campaign at these altitudes. Injection closer to the poles, at 60◦ N and 60◦ S, is
considered much more feasible with existing technology [16]. On the other hand, polar injections
are expected to be much less efficient (resulting in a lower cooling effect per unit mass aerosol) due
to significantly lower levels of incident solar radiation near the poles, relative to the equator [17]. To
partially accommodate for this, the G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario proposes targeted seasonal injections
of SO2 gas: injection at 60◦ N in the Northern Hemisphere spring (March through June, MAMJ),
and injection at 60◦ S in the Southern Hemisphere spring (September through December, SOND).
This reduces “wasted” stratospheric aerosol during winter months when the polar regions receive
negligible amounts of incident solar radiation.
The G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario specifies a “target” of maintaining the global average near-surface
temperature, computed as the time average over the period 2020-2039 under the SSP2-4.5 emissions
scenario. As will be discussed in the following section, reaching and maintaining this target, while
minimizing deviations of under- or overcooling the climate, is the task of a feedback controller.
Deriving from classic control system theory, an SAI feedback controller observes the climate’s
deviation from the desired target at a specified time interval (here, annually), and adjusts the mass
of injected aerosols to eliminate that deviation. This idea originates from a study of direct solar
irradiance modification with feedback control by Kravitz et al. [9]. While the design of the feedback
controller is not specified by the scenario, it does specify that an equal mass of SO2 be injected at
each site (in this case, each hemisphere) for a given year. That is, for a given year the controller
will request a total aerosol mass to be injected over the following year; this total is split evenly
between MAMJ at 60◦ N and SOND at 60◦ S. This contrasts some prior literature, which allows for

 18

independently-variable mass injections at different SAI sites [18, 7, 19]. Such tactics are useful for
targeting latitudinally-nonuniform targets, such as the equator-to-pole temperature differential, but
is less meaningful for a latitudinally-independent target such as global mean temperature.

2. METHODS

2.1. Model description and control simulations

All results presented in this report were simulated using the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy
Exascale Earth System Model version 3 (E3SMv3) [20]. This recent update to E3SM includes a
variety of atmospheric gas chemistry modeling enhancements, most notably the inclusion of an
additional stratospheric sulfate aerosol mode through the Modal Aerosol Module with Prognostic
Stratospheric Aerosol (originally published as “MAM5-PSA,” now generally referred to simply as
“MAM5”) [21]. Further, the atmosphere vertical discretization was increased from 72 levels in
E3SMv2 to 80 vertical levels in E3SMv3, with additional levels in the lower stratosphere and a
model top at ∼60 km. These improvements specifically target increased fidelity in representing
stratospheric chemistry and physics, towards accurate modeling of SAI dynamics and the effect
of SAI on the broader climate. More broadly, this update seeks to rectify prior model biases in
important quantities of interest such as precipitation, radiative fluxes, aerosol optical depth (AOD),
and historical temperature evolution over the 20th century.

All simulations are computed on the ne30pg2 mesh, with a horizontal resolution of ∼110 km
on the dynamics grid and ∼165 km on the physics grid. Initial conditions starting on 1 January
2015 are taken from a historical simulation ensemble computed over the period 1870-2014 [22].
Control simulations, i.e. those without any SAI forcing, are computed over the period 2015-2084
for a total of seventy simulated years per ensemble member. Five total ensemble members are
simulated under the control scenario (case IDs 0101, 0151, 0201, 0251, and 0301). Projected
future atmospheric forcing is derived from the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 2-4.5 (SSP245)
scenario, representing an “intermediate” increase in anthropogenic radiative forcing through much
of the 2000s, resulting in an estimated global average warming of approximately 2.7◦ C by 2100,
relative to the preindustrial period [23].

The climate system dynamics under this control scenario, as simulated with E3SMv3, are detailed
more carefully in Section 3. For now, we simply show the annual global average reference height
temperature (TREFHT, computed at two meters above surface level) over time in Figure 2-1. The
ensemble mean indicates a roughly linear increase in TREFHT from around 14.75◦ C in 2015 to
approximately 16.8◦ C in 2085. In applying SAI with feedback control, we will seek to limit this
warming to the average control TREFHT value over the period 2020-2039. This target is calculated
to be 𝑇0,ref = 15.3004◦ C, marked by the horizontal dashed red line, with the solid line indicating
the averaging period.

2.2. System identification simulation

To design a feedback controller which maintains the climate system at this global average TREFHT
target, we generally follow the system identification (system ID) procedure outlined by Tilmes et
al. [18]. This process characterizes the response of the climate system to SAI under the injection
scenario outlined in Table 1-1. Again, the G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario dictates equal injections
(annualized) at two separate sites, one at (60◦ N, 180◦ E) during Northern Hemisphere spring
(MAMJ), and the other at (60◦ S, 180◦ E) during Southern Hemisphere spring (SOND), both at 15
km altitude. We construct a system ID emissions scenario consisting of a 10 Tg SO2 gas injection
per site, per year, over the period 2015-2024. That is, the 60◦ N site injects 10 Tg each year during
MAMJ, and 60◦ S site injects 10 Tg each year during SOND, for a total of 20 Tg injected per
year. Injections occur in a 3◦ × 3◦ latitude by longitude grid centered on the injection site, and
within a 1 km-thick level of the atmosphere centered on the injection altitude. This simulation
approximates the steady-state behavior of the climate system under a fairly large SAI campaign,
and will roughly quantify the global AOD and TREFHT response per terragram SO2 injected.
Only one ensemble member is used for this system ID process, initialized from the the same
initial conditions as the first control simulation ensemble member of the G6-1.5K-SAI E3SMv3
simulation campaign [24]. In the following analysis, the effect of SAI will similarly be computed
relative to this G6-1.5K-SAI ensemble member. Note that this is a slight modeling discrepancy:
we compute the system ID response for a G6-1.5K-SAI control simulation ensemble member under
the G6-1.5K-HiLLA injection scenario, rather than using the initial conditions from an ensemble
member of the G6-1.5K-HiLLA. In our opinion, any error introduced by this is likely dwarfed by
internal and intra-ensemble variability, and the quality of SAI feedback controllers is generally
robust to such discrepancies.

2.3. Feedback controller design

To maintain the climate system at a fixed global mean TREFHT target, we design a feedback
control system which adjusts the amount of SAI injected per year in response to changes to the
actual global mean TREFHT. Following the work of Kravitz et al. [19], we seek a proportional-
integral (PI) controller which aims to modify the global average AODVIS ℓ0 according to the

target, how much the system might overshoot the target, and even whether the controller is stable
(not inducing wild swings in the global mean TREFHT).
Designing a feedback controller from scratch requires a very careful exploration of the ESM
dynamics, and tuning an analytical model of the climate (e.g., a semi-infinite diffusion model [25])
to roughly match these dynamics. Analysis of this analytical model (e.g., in frequency space
using the Laplace transform) can arrive at “optimal” values of the gains which produce a stable
controller that rapidly achieves the desired temperature target. The computational cost of this

3. RESULTS

This section describes preliminary results from the simulations described in Section 2. In the
following section, we provide analyses for the ensemble of three model simulations (case IDs 0101,
0151, and 0201). We begin by verifying the impact of and response to the sulfate aerosols through
the radiative forcing, temperature, precipitation, and AOD. First we look at the annual and global
mean impacts, then analyze the seasonal cycle.

3.1. Forcing

Figure 3-1 displays the total mass of SO2 injected annually across both injection sites, as dictated
by the feedback controller. Following a similar trend observed in the G6-1.5K-SAI E3SMv3
simulations presented by Walker et al. [24], the annual injection mass initially increases rather
quickly, here at a rate of approximately one additional teragram per year for the first 15 years of
the SAI campaign. This SAI acceleration slows to approximate a quarter teragram of additional
SO2 added to the total injection mass per year for the final 25 years of the simulated campaign.
However, the actual injection magnitude observed here differs significantly from that observed in
the G6-1.5K-SAI scenario: the former case injects almost 27 Tg per year by 2085, while the latter
case only injects only ∼17 Tg by 2085. This indicates a drastically reduced SAI efficiency resulting
from the high latitude, low altitude injections.

3.2. Temperature

The primary goal of SAI is to lower the surface temperatures to that of the reference period, which
is generally achieved in our simulations (Figure 3-6). In fact, these simulations indicate a slight
over-cooling in the last two decades.

Figure 3-7 (A) shows that the largest warming in the control simulations occurs in the Arctic
region and Northern Hemisphere high latitudes, which relates to melting sea ice and other positive
feedbacks causing Arctic polar amplification of warming. We discuss this further in Section 3.4,
showing the strong seasonal cycle of SAI and corresponding increase in sea ice coverage. Under
this control scenario, the global mean temperature increases by +1.4◦ C. Compared to the control
future, the SAI future prevents the Arctic amplification and maintains lower temperatures globally
by an average of −1.53◦ C (Figure 3-7 (B)). Broadly, the SAI scenario is able to avoid warming in
the global mean, though there are still regions that experience warmer temperatures than that of
the reference period, particularly in the tropical and subtropical regions. Unsurprisingly, the polar
regions are noticeably cooler in the SAI scenario than in the reference period (Figure 3-7 (C)).

4. DISCUSSION

Section 2 describes the E3SMv3 model, system ID simulations, and controller mechanism used to
produce the three ensemble members for the G6-1.5K-HiLLA SAI simulations and corresponding
control simulations following the SSP2-4.5 scenario. Section 3 presents the preliminary results
using the three ensemble members of the SAI simulations, comparing the mid-late 21st century
to the control case where SAI was not implemented. We presented the modeled impact of SAI
on temperature, precipitation, AOD, and sea ice through the negative aerosol radiative forcing
(reflecting sunlight back to space). The SAI simulations implemented seasonal injections in SOND
at 60◦ S and MAMJ at 60◦ N, showing a strong negative aerosol forcing in the respective summer
months of each hemisphere when solar insolation is greatest. While most of the sulfate aerosol
remains in the stratosphere, there is an increase in tropospheric SO4 as well. The SAI projections
showed decreased surface temperatures compared to the same time period (2065-2084) in the
control simulation. The mean temperature in the SAI projections is more similar to that of the
reference period, even slightly cooler on average (slight over-cooling due to SAI). The pattern of
temperature in the SAI simulations are similar to the reference period (2020-2039). Specifically, the
SAI shows strong cooling in the polar regions, especially the Northern Hemisphere where boreal
winter Arctic amplification causes over 6◦ C warming in the control future. The sea ice fraction
correspondingly increases in the SAI projections, confirming the positive ice-albedo feedback
relationship.
The precipitation impacts are not as clear. There seems to be a widening or weakening of the ITCZ
in the SAI simulation compared to the control simulation projections. In the control simulation,
an increase in mean precipitation seems to come from the increase in the convective to stratiform
precipitation ratio (i.e., a warmer and more moist atmosphere leads to stronger/more convective
precipitation) compared to the reference period. However, the SAI simulation shows no such
increase, remaining consistent with the ratio of convective to stratiform precipitation in the reference
period. The SAI case compared to the reference period ensemble mean appears to have a La
Niña-type precipitation pattern. This work was a preliminary assessment of the model response,
and further work should verify the statistical confidence of these precipitation patterns in the
simulations. Due to the seasonal injections, the hydrological cycle may also show a stronger impact
on the seasonal precipitation, which could be important for the global infrastructure, agriculture,
and human health.

5. CONCLUSION

This report provides a description of the model implementation for the G6-1.5K-HiLLA experiment
in E3SMv3, as well as preliminary results comparing the ensemble mean temperature, AOD,
radiative forcing, and precipitation for two alternate futures: control simulations following the
SSP2-4.5 background emissions scenario and the G6-1.5K-HiLLA scenario outlined in Table 1-1
and Section 1.1.
A preliminary analysis of the results show that in E3SMv3, the G6-1.5K-HiLLA SAI injection
scenario remarkably counteracts most of the temperature and precipitation pattern changes due to
warming. The mean temperature change is +1.42◦ C in the control future and −0.11◦ C in the SAI
future compared to the reference period. The global mean precipitation increases in the control
future, mostly as a result of a higher ratio of convective to stratiform precipitation whereas patterns
of precipitation in the SAI future share more similarities with the reference period. The controller
is designed only with the global mean value of temperature as its goal, so the improvement in the
pattern of temperature and precipitation was not by design, but shows the potential effectiveness of
the HiLLA SAI strategy in E3SMv3.
These results are robust across the three ensemble members, but may still be model dependent.
Additionally, these results may also change with injection location and altitude. A planned variant
on the experiment as part of the G6-1.5K-HiLLA protocols, with injections at 13 km altitude,
would better assess the robustness of these results. Outputs from these simulations are planned to
be released publicly by Reflective, and future work will include more detailed analyses of these
results in a multi model framework by Reflective awardees.
