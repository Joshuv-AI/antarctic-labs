Neutrophil Dynamics in Viral Infections: Drivers of Inflammation and Targets for Pro-Resolving Therapies
Authors: Filipe Resende, Felipe Emanuel Oliveira Rocha, Celso Martins Queiroz-Junior, Mauro Martins Teixeira, Vivian Vasconcelos Costa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeutrophils are essential components of host defense, traditionally recognized for their well-described antimicrobial functions in bacterial infections. Far from being merely short-lived and functionally limited, emerging evidence demonstrates that neutrophils are also key players in viral infections. Through mechanisms such as degranulation and formation of NETs, neutrophils play an important role in virus-induced inflammation and pathogenesis. However, their persistence and hyperactivated states also contribute to tissue damage and failed resolution of acute inflammation.SummaryHere, we summarize recent data regarding the role of neutrophils in respiratory and arboviral infections, from clinical evidence to mechanistic insights available from the last 10 years. Finally, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies that target neutrophil fate and function to promote the resolution of inflammation.Key MessagesOverall, although well characterized in some viral infections, for a few other viral diseases the role of neutrophils remains elusive. We highlight the importance of modulating neutrophil death in a timely and noninflammatory manner, as some viruses influence apoptosis for their own benefit, favoring viral replication. For those infections, and also due to non-resolving inflammation provoked by these viruses, pro-resolving mediators certainly display a beneficial effect. Rather than broadly suppressing antiviral immunity, pro-resolving approaches aimed at recalibrating neutrophil activation, lifespan, and clearance may represent a promising and safer avenue for the treatment of viral diseases characterized by excessive and non-resolving inflammation.

Introduction

Neutrophils in Acute Inflammation

Originating in the bone marrow and released as terminally differentiated cells, neutrophils represent the most abundant leukocyte population in human circulation (approximately 50–70% of all white blood cells) and act as central effector cells of innate immunity [1, 2]. Approximately 50% of these granulocytes circulate as a form of marginated pool, which is weakly attached to the endothelium of vasculature, characterized as CXCR4-low/SDF-1-low neutrophils, especially in the vascular bed of lungs, spleen, and liver [3–5]. These neutrophils are rapidly recruited by pro-inflammatory signals in order to employ their defense mechanisms within the tissue [6, 7].

Although the half-life of neutrophils is relatively short (6–12 h), molecules such as interferon (IFN)-γ, tumor necrosis factor, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) prolong neutrophil survival during their recruitment to the site of injuries, ensuring rapid mobilization and an effective response against pathogens [5, 8]. Their essential role in controlling bacterial infections is well established through mechanisms such as phagocytosis [9], production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10], degranulation [11], and, more recently described, the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [12]. During the last decade, their involvement in antiviral immunity has gained attention, especially NET induction by respiratory viruses [13–18] and by arboviruses [19–21].

Emerging evidence supports the notion that, to some extent, some of the mechanisms that are crucial for bacterial killing by neutrophils are also used against viral infections. Clear evidence of this is the NET production, which was demonstrated to exert direct virucidal activity, for instance, against SARS-CoV-2 [22]. Additionally, alpha defensins and serine proteases such as neutrophil elastases (NEs), proteinase-3, and cathepsin G have been demonstrated to exert direct virucidal activity in vitro, acting through mechanisms that include direct membrane disruption and proteolytic cleavage of viral proteins [23–25]. Some molecules that are produced not exclusively by neutrophils but also by these cells and were formally demonstrated to exert virucidal activity include the cathelicidin LL-37 and matrix metalloproteinase 9 [26–29]. However, direct evidence linking classical neutrophil degranulation per se to antiviral activity remains comparatively limited. While some of the aforementioned studies have demonstrated virucidal effects of purified neutrophil-derived granule components or NET-associated proteins, comparatively few investigations have established that neutrophils, through degranulation under defined experimental conditions, release factors that directly reduce viral infectivity. As such, the field still relies largely on reductionist approaches, and definitive evidence of degranulation-driven antiviral activity at the cellular level remains relatively scarce.

Once activated, neutrophils amplify inflammation by releasing these granules and secreting cytokines and chemokines [30, 31]. In doing so, these cells shape the immune landscape and orchestrate interactions with multiple leukocyte subsets [32, 33]. Moreover, molecules such as type I IFNs and IFN-stimulated genes fine-tune neutrophil activity, ensuring a balanced response to viral infections [34–36]. When appropriately regulated, these events contribute to the desired scenario in acute infection: pathogen elimination and restoration of tissue homeostasis.

However, the properties that make neutrophils efficient defenders also can cause tissue damage. Increasing evidence indicates that their contribution varies widely depending on the virus involved [37–39]. While indispensable for viral control and the initiation of effective immunity [40], excessive or sustained neutrophil activity can exacerbate tissue damage and might impair the transition to the resolution phase, ultimately contributing to failed resolution [31, 41–44]. Thus, understanding neutrophil biology in viral infections is incomplete without considering how their responses must be actively terminated and rewired to allow inflammation to resolve. In the next section, we will discuss how acute inflammation shapes neutrophil production and function, reprogramming its phenotype.

Ontogeny of Neutrophils during Acute Inflammation

Under homeostatic conditions, neutrophils are viewed as continuously patrolling the organism, contributing to the maintenance of tissue integrity. Even in the absence of overt infection and acute inflammation, neutrophils do not constitute a homogenous population, and diverse phenotypic subsets have been described under steady-state conditions [5, 45]. Upon inflammatory challenge, however, this equilibrium is profoundly altered. Acute inflammation not only promotes the accelerated release of neutrophils from the bone marrow, including immature forms, through a process known as emergency granulopoiesis [46, 47], but also reshapes neutrophil functional properties across the different compartments involved in the response.

Beyond the formation of NETs, neutrophil activity relies extensively on the regulated mobilization of granule contents. These granules are generated in a hierarchical manner during granulopoiesis, with distinct subsets of granules being formed at successive stages of neutrophil differentiation [48]. As a consequence, emergency granulopoiesis frequently leads to the release of immature neutrophils that may lack a full complement of granule subsets and, therefore, may not complete the spectrum of antimicrobial effector function characteristic of fully differentiated polymorphonuclear cells [47, 49]. In addition to these intrinsic functional limitations, exposure to pathogen-associated molecular patterns may further shape neutrophil responses, promoting a predominantly pro-inflammatory activation state [50]. Overall, neutrophil responses during acute inflammation are best understood as the result of a continuous ontogenic process, in which signals arising from the infection and tissue injury shape neutrophil development in the bone marrow and extend their influence across maturation states, ultimately determining the functional behavior of both immature and mature neutrophils within distinct tissue compartments and at sites of infection.

Neutrophils Reprogramming across Compartments: From Hematopoiesis to Trained Immunity in Acute Viral Infections

Emergency hematopoiesis is driven by inflammatory cytokines such as G-CSF, IL-6, and type I IFNs, promoting expansion of the myeloid lineage and accelerated neutrophil differentiation [51–53]. This process is not only quantitative but also qualitative, involving epigenetic reprogramming in hematopoietic progenitors [54]. Histone modifications, such as H3K4me3, increase chromatin accessibility at inflammatory gene loci, enabling more robust responses to subsequent stimuli [55]. In parallel, metabolic reprogramming occurs, with increased aerobic glycolysis and activation of regulatory pathways such as mTOR and HIF-1α, which are central features of trained immunity [56].

The concept of trained immunity has demonstrated that myeloid cells can undergo long-lasting functional reprogramming following inflammatory stimuli [57, 58]. Although initially described in monocytes and macrophages, recent evidence suggests that neutrophils also participate in this phenomenon, mainly through the reprogramming of hematopoietic progenitors [54, 59–61]. During infectious processes, hematopoiesis is profoundly remodeled, favoring the production of myeloid cells and altering their functional profile.

Trained immunity involves long-term epigenetic and metabolic changes that modulate subsequent immune responses [58]. Although mature neutrophils are short-lived, evidence indicates that reprogramming of hematopoietic progenitors can generate successive populations of neutrophils with altered functional characteristics [54]. Stimuli such as vaccination with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin demonstrate the capacity to modulate responses to viral infections, such as yellow fever, through trained immunity, reinforcing the relevance of this phenomenon in innate immunity [62].

Peripheral circulation during viral infections reveals significant phenotypic diversity among neutrophils. Studies show an increased presence of immature and low-density neutrophils with distinct functional profiles [45, 49]. These populations exhibit transcriptomic signatures associated with IFN responses and systemic inflammation, particularly in severe viral infections, as observed in patients with COVID-19 [63]. Functionally, these neutrophils display increased production of ROS, a higher propensity for NET formation, and, in some cases, immunosuppressive activity.

After migrating to inflamed tissues, neutrophils are exposed to microenvironments characterized by hypoxia, metabolites, and inflammatory cytokines. Activation of HIF-1α promotes metabolic adaptation and prolongs cell survival, while metabolites such as succinate amplify the inflammatory response [56]. These changes modulate key functions, including phagocytosis, degranulation, and the formation of NETs, which play important roles in both antiviral defense and inflammatory pathogenesis [64]. Interactions with other immune cells also contribute to the amplification or resolution of inflammation.

An emerging aspect of tissue reprogramming is the ability of neutrophils to undergo reverse migration, a process in which these cells return from inflamed tissue to the systemic circulation. The study by Colom and colleagues [65] demonstrates that the leukotriene B4 (LTB4)-NE axis acts as a central regulator of neutrophil reverse migration in vivo. LTB4, an eicosanoid derived from arachidonic acid and a potent chemotactic mediator, promotes neutrophil activation and recruitment via BLT1 receptors. LTB4 signaling induces the release of NE, which in turn cleaves components of endothelial junctions, facilitating retrograde migration across the endothelium.

Subsequently, expanding this concept at a functional and physiological level, Wang and colleagues [66] demonstrated through intravital microscopy that neutrophils in models of sterile liver injury not only actively participate in tissue repair but can also reenter the systemic circulation after fulfilling their functions. This study revealed that neutrophils contribute to vascular remodeling, clearance of cellular debris, and tissue regeneration before undergoing reverse migration. After completing their functions, these neutrophils do not undergo apoptosis at the inflammatory site nor are they predominantly phagocytosed by resident macrophages. Instead, a significant proportion follow a programmed intercompartmental trajectory, initially passing through the lungs, where increased expression of CXCR4 – a chemokine receptor critical for bone marrow homing – occurs. This upregulation of CXCR4 promotes neutrophil recirculation to the bone marrow, where they ultimately undergo apoptosis. This functional circuit establishes a continuous tissue-circulation-bone marrow axis, in which neutrophils integrate local and systemic inflammatory signals before being eliminated.

Neutrophil reprogramming plays a dual role in viral infections. On one hand, it contributes to early viral control and amplification of the innate immune response. On the other hand, it may result in immunopathology, including cytokine storm and tissue damage [67]. Excessive NET formation has been implicated in tissue injury and organ dysfunction, particularly in severe viral infections [68]. Thus, the balance between protective and pathological functions is critical for clinical outcomes.

The Resolution of Inflammation

The resolution of inflammation is understood as an active, highly coordinated phase that occurs by the end of the acute phase of inflammation. It is driven by a specialized group of molecules known as pro-resolving mediators, which are produced by our own cells, especially leukocytes. These include proteins, such as annexin A1 (AnxA1), peptides, like angiotensin (1–7), and lipids, like resolvin D1 and lipoxin A4 [69–72]. In contrast to classical anti-inflammatory mediators, which primarily act by suppressing inflammation intensity, pro-resolving mediators operate in a different manner. These molecules guide leukocyte behaviors within inflamed tissues, so that the response does not become chronic and the affected site can return to physiological homeostasis. Importantly, dampening the initial inflammatory response does not necessarily accelerate resolution, which underscores how distinct these mediators are from traditional anti-inflammatory molecules [73, 74]. In addition to curbing pro-inflammatory signals like cytokine production, the main features of inflammation resolution include neutrophil apoptosis, macrophage efferocytosis with their subsequent alternative activation, restoration of tissue function, and efficient removal of the original insult, such as viral or bacterial pathogens [43, 44].

When this coordinated program is disrupted, whether by persistent pro-inflammatory signals, failure to clear apoptotic neutrophils, or impaired production of pro-resolving mediators, the process enters a state of failed resolution. In this scenario, inflammation does not terminate properly, tissue repair becomes dysregulated, and homeostasis is not restored, setting the stage for chronic inflammatory pathology or increased inflammatory insults that ultimately lead to death. This scenario is described to occur in diverse viral infections, such as those provoked by H1N1 and SARS-CoV-2 [74]. Moreover, the proposed “post-resolution phase” raises the possibility that inadequate resolution may lead to sustained impairments in adaptive immunity following infection [70]. Therefore, resolution-based therapies are emerging as promising strategies for treating viral infections.

Here, we integrate recent evidence illustrating both the protective and the harmful roles of neutrophils during viral diseases. We focus particularly on evidence from the last 10 years. The broader functions of neutrophils during the acute inflammatory phase of viral infections have been extensively discussed in previous reviews [39, 75, 76]. Here, we sought to integrate and reexamined these aspects in light of the recent findings. Finally, we argue that neutrophils might be a suitable target for resolution-based therapies, as recent evidence demonstrates that pro-resolving mediators often stimulate neutrophil apoptosis, accelerating the resolution of inflammation.

Neutrophils and Respiratory Viruses

Neutrophils and Influenza A Virus

The contribution of neutrophils to influenza A virus (IAV)-induced disease has long been recognized, but some studies were pivotal in establishing the duality of their actions. Historically, Wang and colleagues [77] demonstrated that IAV directly stimulates both human and murine neutrophils, highlighting these cells as active sensors of viral infection rather than mere bystanders. Shortly after, it was shown that, in mice, the depletion of neutrophils through the use of specific anti-Ly6G antibodies led to increased viral loads, exacerbated pulmonary inflammation, and more severe edema [40]. Together, these seminal studies established neutrophils as essential components of antiviral defense during influenza infection. A decade later, Niu and colleagues [139] added mechanistic depth by demonstrating that the pathway associated with NLRP3-mediated IL-1β promotes neutrophil recruitment and improves host resistance to IAV infection, using mutant mice that superexpress NLRP3/IL-1-βKO, consequently enhancing neutrophil infiltration. Moreover, the pathophysiology of different subtypes of IAV might also cause slightly different disease profiles, at least as demonstrated in mouse models [140].

Given the diversity of studies and the evolving understanding of neutrophil biology, we focused our analysis on findings from the past 10 years. Indeed, several reviews regarding neutrophils and IAV infection or other respiratory viruses are available elsewhere [75, 76, 141, 142]. Collectively, these results from the last decade underscore a common theme: the importance of neutrophil activity during IAV infection depends on the fine balance between their number, activation state, and phenotype, particularly in the lung microenvironment.

On the protective side, several studies highlight how neutrophils enhance host defense, support viral control, and participate in immune regulation. It is known that a sort of endogenous molecules act by recruiting neutrophils into sites of inflammation during IAV infection, such as FGF2 and IL-1-β, contributing to a protective role of neutrophils [139, 143]. Indeed, probably as a counter-regulatory viral mechanism, it was shown that IAV reduces G-CSF levels, leading to diminished myeloperoxidase (MPO) content and impaired bacterial killing by neutrophils, thereby increasing susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections [144]. In the same line, it was demonstrated that IAV-induced STAT1 signaling suppresses neutrophil recruitment, thereby worsening susceptibility to Aspergillus secondary infection [145]. In human neutrophils in vitro, Malachowa and colleagues [84] showed that seasonal IAV primes neutrophils to more efficiently kill Staphylococcus aureus, suggesting beneficial training effects of viral exposure. Indeed, it was shown that neutrophil depletion after disease onset reduces IAV-specific CD8+ T-cell memory, linking early neutrophil responses to long-term immunity [80]. Collectively, these results demonstrate how a well-orchestrated neutrophil response is a sine qua non condition not only to effect early response but also to determine late outcomes of disease, after the resolution of the primary viral infection.

More recently, it was shown that genetically determined characteristics in neutrophil responsiveness of different strains of mice (A/J vs. C57BL/6 mice) exacerbate IAV-induced inflammation. A/J mice failed to control viral replication and display deficient release of NETs and generation of ROS compared to C57/BL6 lineage [81]. Overall, beyond the NETs and ROS generation, B6 and A/J neutrophils occupy different compartments within the lungs and migrate in different time points post-infection. While B6 neutrophils migrate toward the lungs more rapidly and are present in overall lung parenchyma, A/J neutrophils display a late migration pattern and occupy primarily the bronchoalveolar space [81]. This suggests that not only the rapid migration toward the lungs but also efficient microbicidal capacities are both fundamental to restrain IAV pathology.

Beyond their physiological importance, the efficacy of some therapeutic strategies may certainly depend on neutrophil adequate responses. For instance, a recent study demonstrated that the therapeutic efficacy of sirtuin-1 modulators correlates directly to neutrophil number and functional quality [82]. Sirtuin-1 is a pivotal regulator of gene expression in leukocytes, especially in neutrophils, by deacetylating histones in the nucleus of the cells [146]. Importantly, these results were observed in a mild model of IAV infection. Viral inocula used to infect mice certainly influence the outcome. In line with this, evidence points toward excessive neutrophil infiltration as one of the main mechanisms of exacerbated inflammation and disease [147].

These studies collectively illustrate that insufficient or functionally impaired neutrophil responses can compromise viral clearance, weaken adaptive immunity, and increase vulnerability to secondary infections. Nonetheless, it must be considered that the impact of infection on extrapulmonary sites might also affect neutrophils in circulation or in the bone marrow. The phenotype assumed by neutrophils in the alveolar space largely reflects infection-driven alterations in bone marrow development, emphasizing that neutrophil function is shaped early, before they reach infected tissues [82, 83].

Yet, the growing body of literature has also revealed circumstances in which neutrophils become harmful. Increasing evidence indicates that neutrophil death accompanied by NET release represents a critical checkpoint that promotes exacerbated inflammation. It is reasonable to assume that this shift toward alternative death pathways, rather than apoptosis, may further undermine the resolution process. For instance, it was demonstrated that high circulating MPO-DNA complexes (indicative of NET release) correlate strongly with worse disease in influenza patients [18]. In addition, in a prospective human study, Tang and colleagues [79] found that the top 30 differentially expressed genes in a blood microarray from severe influenza-infected patients were associated with NET formation, including elastase, cathepsin, and MPO, indicating that neutrophil activity could predict patient outcomes [79].

Nonetheless, it was demonstrated that both H5N1 and H1N1 directly stimulate NET formation and transmigration in human neutrophils in vitro, supplying mechanistic support for clinical observations [78]. In fact, H5N1 infection in cynomolgus macaques led to massive infiltration of neutrophils and NET release in both alveolar space and lung parenchyma [148]. In the same line, Yi and colleagues [149] observed that NETs exacerbate lung inflammation in IAV and methicillin-resistant S. aureus mouse model of coinfection, partially due to neutrophil death and release of inflammatory contents. More recently, therapeutics targeting neutrophil-driven pathology have emerged, reporting that suppressing NET formation can reduce IAV immunopathology [150].

Together, these findings converge on a central concept: neutrophils are indispensable for the defense during IAV infection, but their protective effect depends on their controlled recruitment, appropriate maturation, and balanced activation. When neutrophil responses are insufficient, whether due to impaired recruitment, reduced microbicidal capacity, or developmental biases, the host becomes more susceptible to viral persistence and secondary infections. Conversely, when neutrophils are over-recruited, hyperactivated, or prone to forming excessive NETs, they drive diffuse alveolar damage, amplify inflammatory cascades, and worsen outcomes.

Thus, the equilibrium between neutrophil quantity, activation profile, and functional state emerges as a decisive factor in determining whether neutrophils act as protectors or as contributors to immunopathology during IAV infection. Understanding how IAV shapes neutrophil biology – from bone marrow generation to lung tissue effector function – offers a promising path toward therapeutic strategies that modulate neutrophils not by suppressing them globally, but by restoring their balanced activity and contributing to limited lifespan, facilitating apoptosis.

Neutrophils and COVID-19

Initial studies during the COVID-19 pandemics identified neutrophil circulatory levels and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as important markers for worsened disease and COVID-19 severity [85–89]. The accumulation of neutrophils within the lung parenchyma was also observed in severe cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection and was related to severe inflammation, organ dysfunction, and death [151, 152].

In the context of COVID-19, several studies have shown that the antimicrobial machinery of neutrophils becomes markedly amplified. For instance, severe COVID-19 patients exhibit elevated levels of calprotectin (S100A8/A9) in peripheral blood, a neutrophil-derived alarmin associated with NET formation and excessive inflammation [90, 91]. S100A8/A9 is one of the most abundant proteins in the cytoplasm of neutrophils, and once released, it binds to TLR4 to exert pro-inflammatory signaling, possibly amplifying immune response and tissue damage during COVID-19 [153]. Additionally, MPO was also detected in high levels in nasopharyngeal samples [74, 75, 92, 93] and also in the blood of severe COVID-19 patients [94]. In general, gene expression patterns and different proteins associated with neutrophil degranulation were found in severe COVID-19 patients, like calprotectins and elastases [95–100]. Indeed, an activated neutrophil signature was demonstrated to predict mortality from COVID-19 [154]. Moreover, NE inhibitor sivelestat treatment shortened ICU stay and improved survival of critically ill COVID-19 patients in a large multicentric study [155]. In the same line, evidence suggests that MPO inhibition might decrease endothelial glycocalyx shedding, which could be beneficial for COVID-19 patients, once endothelial glycocalyx shedding may expose adhesion molecules and increase vascular permeability [156]. Therefore, the inhibition of neutrophil components during COVID-19 offers an interesting strategy to inhibit excessive inflammation and tissue damage that ultimately may lead to death.

Several recent studies demonstrate the presence of NET in the peripheral blood of severely ill COVID-19 patients, pointing toward a possible implication of NETs in COVID-19 pathology, even in asymptomatic individuals compared to healthy subjects [157–160]. Although a longitudinal study found no correlation between COVID-19 severity and NET formation [161], several recently published works suggest that NET formation might be directly related to COVID-19 severity. NET-associated components are abundant in the plasma, trachea, and lung tissue of critically ill individuals and have been implicated in immunothrombosis [103–105]. More recently, Vahabi and colleagues [109] demonstrated that IL-8 and MPO-DNA complexes were increased in severe COVID-19 patients. In a small cohort of deceased COVID-19 patients, MPO and citrullinated histone H3 were detected in 10 of 43 patients in lung biopsies, by immunohistochemistry [106]. Additionally, distinct neutrophil subsets – such as immature or low-density neutrophils – display spontaneous NET release and heightened inflammatory potential, correlating with coagulopathy and disease severity [107, 108]. Therefore, although the beneficial effects of NETs are a main virucidal activity against SARS-CoV-2 [22, 101], the excessive formation of NETs might be related to increased severity of COVID-19. Mechanistically, besides the established implication of NETs in the formation of immunothrombosis and vascular occlusion [101, 103–109, 162], their associate proteins (HMGB1, MPO, NE) are known to bind to different immune receptors that activate leukocytes, which probably contribute to hyperactivation of immune response. These include HMGB-1 binding to TLR4, extracellular histones binding to TLR2 and TLR4, and even phagocytosed NET-derived DNA by macrophages, which are recognized by cGAS cytosolic sensor, contributing to the production of type I IFN [163–165].

Interestingly, studies exploring mechanistic insights into neutrophil function during COVID-19 beyond NET formation are emerging in recent years. In a recent study, Formiga and colleagues [166] elegantly showed that proliferating cytosolic nuclear antigen, a protein typically located in the nucleus, actively binds to the calprotectins S100A8/S100A9 in the cytosol, forming intracellular hubs that enhance ROS production in neutrophils. In the same line, the inhibition of host-derived neuraminidase by oseltamivir decreased ROS production by human neutrophils in vitro and decreased neutrophil infiltration and lung inflammation in murine coronavirus infection [167].

Altogether, these findings highlight that in COVID-19, the same neutrophil-derived products that help restrain viral dissemination may also drive tissue damage when excessively activated. In addition, the recruitment of immature neutrophils with a pro-inflammatory phenotype, along with cell death skewed toward NET formation rather than apoptosis, appears to be a key determinant of COVID-19 pathology. In fact, despite SARS-CoV-2 infection not replicating in neutrophils, neutrophils incubated with SARS-CoV-2 exhibit decreased levels of caspase-3 expression and apoptosis in culture, and increased NET formation [102]. Nevertheless, compromised efferocytosis of neutrophil apoptotic bodies was also reported in aged mice infected with SARS-CoV-2, increasing disease severity [168]. As one of the cardinal signs of resolution, efferocytosis is the most desirable outcome after neutrophil death. Indeed, our group recently showed that endogenous AnxA1 is essential for limiting neutrophil migration during murine coronavirus infection, and that the AnxA1 mimetic peptide Ac2-26 holds therapeutic potential by inhibiting leukocyte migration and reducing SARS-CoV-2-induced lethality in mice [169]. These processes have therefore emerged as pivotal therapeutic targets in strategies focused on neutrophil regulation. In this context, several neutrophil-centered interventions can be envisioned for COVID-19, including modulation of neutrophil phenotype, inhibition of neutrophil effector components such as proteases, and stimulation of neutrophil apoptosis, an approach particularly attainable through pro-resolving mediators.

Neutrophils and Respiratory Syncytial Virus

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection is consistently associated with robust neutrophil responses across experimental models and human studies. In a human lung implant model in mice, RSV infection induced a pronounced neutrophil infiltration into the infected tissue [110], reinforcing the idea that neutrophils are rapidly mobilized to the airways during early infection. This neutrophil migration was elegantly demonstrated by Kirsebom and colleagues [115], as partially dependent on Myd88/TRIF signaling pathways. Complementing these observations, transcriptomic profiling of tracheobronchial aspirates from RSV-infected infants revealed highly pro-inflammatory neutrophil signatures, including enhanced NF-κB and IL-6-related pathways, when compared with paired blood neutrophils [170]. This suggests that tissue-recruited neutrophils acquire a more inflammatory phenotype upon entering RSV-infected mucosa. The possible contributions of neutrophils in RSV pathology were previously reviewed by others [171–174]. Here, we outline key points demonstrating that the contribution of neutrophils to RSV infection remains controversial, with unresolved questions especially about their antiviral capabilities.

Neutrophil activation appears to be substantially influenced by the microenvironment and host age. Ex vivo comparisons demonstrated that infant neutrophils exhibit elevated expression of activation markers, such as CD11b, CD64, MPO, and NE, at the baseline, after harvested, potentially making infants inherently more susceptible to RSV pathology [116]. In addition, other studies demonstrate that in vivo, infant neutrophils display delayed apoptosis [175–177]. Consistent with this, the same group showed that trans-epithelial migration is a critical step for full neutrophil activation during RSV exposure [178], highlighting that epithelial-neutrophil interactions shape neutrophil function.

Other mechanistic studies also show that neutrophils can influence viral clearance and pathology in complex and sometimes contradictory ways. In an airway epithelial cell-neutrophil interaction model, RSV was shown to delay neutrophil apoptosis and enhance MPO release, leading to increased inflammatory mediator production while still contributing to viral reduction [179]. However, whether neutrophils are required for viral clearance remains controversial: antibody-mediated neutrophil depletion did not increase viral loads in both RSV [180] and pneumonia mice virus, a suitable model that mimics severe disease aspects of RSV infection of humans, but in mice [111]. Neutrophil depletion in pneumonia mice virus-infected mice likewise resulted in no differences in viral titers or histopathological scores [111]. These findings contrast with clinical and experimental evidence of neutrophil involvement in disease severity, suggesting that neutrophils may contribute more to immunopathology than to direct antiviral defense in many contexts.

Interestingly, the cytokine environment also critically shapes neutrophil function. In human studies, neutrophilic infiltration in the infected lung was associated with reduced IL-17A levels in the mucosa, which preceded symptom onset and predicted clinical progression [112]. Experimentally, intranasal IL-17 administration reduced viral loads and improved survival in RSV-infected mice [113], further supporting a protective role for IL-17 signaling. Together, these studies suggest that insufficient IL-17-driven neutrophil responses may predispose to more severe RSV infection.

Neutrophils may also contribute to downstream complications of RSV. For instance, mice previously infected with RSV displayed impaired neutrophil-mediated control of S. aureus, attributed to RSV-induced neutrophil overactivation and dysfunction [114]. Similarly, exacerbation of allergic airway inflammation in house dust mite models occurred when mice were co-infected with RSV, an effect largely driven by enhanced neutrophil infiltration [117]. These findings demonstrate that RSV can reprogram neutrophil responses in ways that heighten susceptibility to secondary insults or comorbid inflammatory stimuli.

The formation of NETs is another key pathological feature. RSV directly induces NET formation by human neutrophils in vitro, and this has also been observed in tracheal samples from infants with severe lower respiratory tract infections [181]. Therapeutic modulation of NETs may therefore be beneficial: in RSV-infected calves, combined ibuprofen and FPI treatment reduced NET formation decreasing clinical disease [182], suggesting that NET-targeted interventions may mitigate tissue injury.

Across clinical studies, neutrophil-related biomarkers show age-dependent predictive value. In pediatric populations, the NLR has yielded conflicting results: while elevated NLR predicted severe outcomes in pediatric ICU patients [183], another study found that NLR did not predict disease severity in infants with acute bronchiolitis [184]. In contrast, among elderly patients, rising NLR levels in the first 48 h served as a strong prognostic indicator of adverse outcomes [185], underscoring how host age shapes the utility of neutrophil biomarkers.

Neutrophils and Arboviruses

Evidence supporting the participation of neutrophils in the pathogenesis of arboviral diseases remains limited, fragmented, and highly model-dependent. For instance, studies with flaviviruses such as Zika virus (ZIKV) reveal dual and sometimes contradictory roles of neutrophils, ranging from early enhancement of viral replication (often influenced by mosquito saliva components) to later protective functions during neuroinvasion. Similarly, in alphavirus infections, particularly chikungunya virus (CHIKV), neutrophils are prominent in inflamed tissues and can contribute to both antiviral activity and chronic immunopathology, yet conclusions remain constrained by the diversity of experimental systems and the indirect nature of many findings. In the next sections, we summarize evidence demonstrating that while neutrophils clearly modulate early inflammatory events during arboviral infection and are often detected at high levels in early time points from clinical samples, their precise pathogenic relevance is still uncertain and lacks robust, unified evidence across arbovirus families.

Neutrophils and Dengue Virus

Although some effort has been made in understanding the contribution of neutrophils and its effector mechanisms that may contribute to the pathogenesis of dengue, these cells are still understudied during such infection. Regarding systemic levels of neutrophils, neutropenia was reported in the early phase of infection [118, 119], commonly found in the defervescence phase, by day 3–5 post-admission. The decrease in circulating levels of these cells does not seem to be correlated to the incidence of secondary bacterial infections [119]. Interestingly, the study by Thein and colleagues [119] showed that severe dengue (SD) patients and dengue without alarm signs differed significantly in the late days of disease, when SD patients showed approximately double the absolute neutrophil counts when compared to dengue without alarm sign group. This may suggest that elevated cell counts during late phases of disease could be related to complicated cases which evolve to hemorrhagic shock. Indeed, some studies suggest that high levels of neutrophils during the acute phase could function as predictors of SD [124, 125].

Apart from the absolute count or the percentage of these cells, their phenotype and activation states certainly influence dengue outcome. Some studies demonstrate an increase in neutrophil markers in the blood of SD patients, suggesting that neutrophils might be hyperactivated. For instance, it has been shown that, during the early phase of the disease, SD patients can be distinguished from those with uncomplicated dengue by their whole-blood transcriptional signature, which reveals alterations in genes associated with neutrophil activation, such as elastase 2 and defensin 1-alpha. Importantly, this was not correlated to the absolute number of circulating neutrophils [120]. Moreover, systemic levels of MPO, a common marker of neutrophil activity, were found to be higher in dengue patients comparing to healthy volunteers, and also in SD patients comparing to uncomplicated dengue cases, probably contributing to heart damage and other complications in these patients [123]. In the same line, a study has shown that the inhibition of MPO in AG129 mice infected with dengue virus (DENV)-2 showed a decrease in NETosis and a decrease of vascular leakage in mice [126], highlighting the importance of neutrophil components in the pathogenesis of DENV infection.

Some other studies demonstrated altered levels of important neutrophil proteins, although without predictive value, such as increased levels of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin in uncomplicated dengue patients with high leukocyte counts [186]. Moreover, citrullinated histone H3 was found to increase in SD patients compared to dengue without alarm sign patients, which displayed uncomplicated disease [127]. Nevertheless, although in a small cohort, Kunder and colleagues [128] showed that NE was significantly increased in SD patients compared to DF patients. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the phenotype and hyperactivation of these cells, instead of merely the absolute number of circulating neutrophils, might be important contributors to DENV pathogenesis.

In recent years, evidence suggests that NET release also might be related to DENV pathogenesis. For instance, it was shown that neutrophils from acute phase DENV patients displayed phenotypic alterations, like spontaneous delobulation, suggesting NET formation. Additionally, increased MPO-DNA complex levels were found in the serum of these patients [19]. Following this line, Sung and colleagues [20] demonstrated that DENV-2 serotype stimulates NET release, via CLEC5A and TLR2, in mice. Later, it was demonstrated that DENV-2 stimulates NET formation in an NADPH oxidase (NOX)-independent manner in ex vivo human neutrophils [187]. Importantly, the authors evaluated NETs containing MPO-H4K8Ac complexes, arguing that these NETs are formed in an NOX-independent pathway. Although NET components were indeed detected in this study, there is no strong evidence that MPO-H4K8Ac forms exclusively through an NOX-independent pathway; in fact, it may occur under both NOX-dependent and NOX-independent conditions [188]. Finally, in contrast to the aforementioned findings, Moreno-Altamirano and colleagues [189] demonstrated that, at least in vitro, DENV-2 does not stimulate NET release in neutrophils obtained from peripheral blood of healthy subjects. Despite this controversy, neutrophil-specific proteins and NET components were detected in DENV patient samples in independent studies, which strongly suggest that neutrophils could be related to DENV exacerbated inflammation and worse outcomes.

Finally, some evidence suggests that DENV serotypes 2 and 3 might delay apoptosis in neutrophils. Rawat and colleagues [122] showed that DENV-2 delayed apoptosis of neutrophils [121], and Kamsom and colleagues [122] demonstrated that DENV-3 upregulated Bcl-XL gene and Mcl-1 gene, also delaying apoptosis. Rawat and colleagues [121] also showed that DENV-2 provoked lobulation of neutrophils and increased MPO in the nucleus. However, the authors incubated neutrophils not only with DENV-2, but also in combination with PMA, which is known to stimulate NET formation [190, 191]. Interestingly, the abovementioned study by Moreno-Altamirano et al. [189] evaluated the NET formation capacity of DENV-2 and DENV-2 + PMA incubation and found that only the later was able to induce NET formation in vitro, which suggests that NET formation in the study of Rawat et al. [121] might be caused by PMA activation. Nevertheless, the delayed apoptosis of neutrophils caused by DENV-2 and DENV-3 certainly impacts the resolution of inflammation, contributing to extended lifespan of neutrophils.

Neutrophils and ZIKV

Recent studies highlight that mosquito salivary proteins themselves, rather than viral components, can shape early neutrophil-mediated responses during flavivirus transmission, especially during ZIKV infection. A key example is the Aedes aegypti protein AaNRP, a salivary factor specifically expressed in female mosquito glands, which was shown to engage TLR1 and TLR4 on skin-resident macrophages, triggering MyD88-NF-κB signaling and the release of neutrophil-attracting chemokines. This cascade promotes a rapid influx of neutrophils followed by the recruitment of virus-permissive myeloid cells, ultimately facilitating the establishment of local ZIKV and DENV-2 infection, enhancing systemic dissemination [192]. Notably, this work also showed that resveratrol treatment blocked this macrophage-dependent pathway, reducing neutrophil influx and the ability of mosquitoes to potentiate flaviviral transmission [192]. However, caution is warranted when drawing conclusions, as resveratrol was administered as a pretreatment prior to ZIKV infection. Nevertheless, these findings underscore that mosquito-borne proteins can orchestrate early neutrophilic inflammation through multiple routes, revealing an additional layer of complexity in the immunobiology of arboviral infections.

In the same line, Hastings and collaborators [131] identified the mosquito salivary protein NeSt1 as an activator of neutrophils in vitro, where it induced IL-1β, CXCL2, and CCL2 expression and promoted the recruitment of monocytes/macrophages susceptible to infection. However, although these data indicate that neutrophils can respond to stimuli related to ZIKV infection, the conclusions regarding their contribution to in vivo pathogenesis remain preliminary. Importantly, the in vivo experiments relied on prior immunization of AG129 mice with NeSt1, and while immunization increased animal survival following exposure to ZIKV-infected mosquitoes, it did not reduce overall viral burden at the evaluated time points. Therefore, viral replication does not appear to be the determining factor explaining the improved survival. Moreover, the authors did not demonstrate reduced neutrophil activation or diminished neutrophil infiltration in any infected tissues of immunized versus nonimmunized animals. As a result, the proposed link between NeSt1-mediated neutrophil activation and disease outcome is inferred primarily from the in vitro findings rather than directly demonstrated in vivo. Therefore, while these data strongly suggest that neutrophils may participate in early ZIKV-driven inflammation, definitive conclusions about their pathogenic role require further investigation.

In fact, in adult AG129 mice directly infected with a contemporary ZIKV strain, neutrophils showed a protective effect. In this model, the virus infects astrocytes and neurons, inducing astrogliosis and infiltration of T cells and neutrophils; increased neutrophil recruitment correlated inversely with paresis, suggesting that these cells help control viral neuropathology [129]. Discrepancies between studies likely reflect methodological differences, especially the presence or absence of mosquito saliva, which alters the first immune events in ZIKV infection.

There is scarce literature examining the role of neutrophils in mechanistic terms during ZIKV infection, or the functional consequences of ZIKV infection on neutrophil antimicrobial capacities. For instance, literature on NETs during ZIKV infection itself remains scarce. Notably, a study by Ding and colleagues [132] provides one of the few insights into this process by examining how prenatal ZIKV exposure shapes NET-related responses in the offspring. Evidence indicates that maternal ZIKV infection can imprint long-lasting, sex-specific alterations on offspring immunity, with neutrophils emerging as central targets of this prenatal programming. Using a model of chronic placental inflammation induced by low-dose ZIKV, these authors show that male and female placentas differ markedly in their innate immune responses, with male placentas displaying stronger activation of pathways linked to IFNs, IL-1β, and neutrophil function. These early placental signals translate into lasting functional changes: male offspring exhibit heightened systemic inflammation and exaggerated neutrophil migration and activation following postnatal bacterial challenge. Transcriptomic and functional analyses reveal that in utero ZIKV exposure disrupts key neutrophil effector programs, including cytokine production and NET formation, in a sex-dimorphic manner, accompanied by altered expression of regulatory genes such as A20 [132]. Together, these findings highlight neutrophils as critical mediators of the immunological consequences of prenatal ZIKV exposure and underscore the potential for early-life viral inflammation to shape susceptibility to later infectious challenges.

While this study focuses on how ZIKV modulates neutrophil development and future NET competence at the systemic level, other recent evidence demonstrates that ZIKV can also shape neutrophil behavior in peripheral tissues in specific ways. It was shown that although ZIKV inoculation in the sciatic nerve recruits neutrophils and induces classical NET-associated cytokines, the virus itself suppresses NET release, except when mediated by the viral NS1 protein, which potently triggers NET formation and consequent neurotoxicity [130]. Taken together, it is reasonable to assume that ZIKV does not simply activate or inhibit neutrophils, but strategically reshapes NET biology across developmental stages and tissue contexts, ultimately influencing both systemic immunity and localized neuropathology.

Finally, neutrophils may even act as transversal players across flaviviral diseases, shaping outcomes during disease. Recent findings reveal that preexisting IgA antibodies induced by a prior ZIKV infection can drive neutrophil activation during subsequent DENV-2 infection, contributing to SD outcomes independently of viral load [193]. Specifically, IgA targeting the NS1 protein forms immune complexes that intensify neutrophil effector functions, preceding and correlating with vascular leakage. This identifies an alternative pathogenic pathway beyond classical IgG-mediated ADE. These results highlight neutrophils as key determinants of disease severity induced by IgA during flaviviral infection. Overall, the role of neutrophils during ZIKV infection, especially in mechanistic terms, requires further investigation.

Neutrophils and West Nile Virus

West Nile virus (WNV) is a neuroinvasive pathogen whose symptomatic infection occurs predominantly in the elderly and immunocompromised individuals, and may progress to meningitis or encephalitis [194]. Regarding WNV infection, there is a scarce number of studies evaluating neutrophil participation during WNV-induced disease. For instance, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies evaluating NET participation or neutrophil-derived classical components, such as MPO or NE in the context of WNV infection.

Clinically, Rawal and colleagues [133] showed that in human patients, elevated numbers of neutrophils were found in the cerebrospinal fluid, representing 50% of the total leukocyte fraction. Importantly, data refer to 3 days post-symptom onset, and beyond day 3 the mean neutrophil fraction decreased by 37%, which suggests that neutrophils may participate in the early inflammatory responses associated with disease onset [133]. In the same line, examining 250 CSF samples from WNV patients, Tyler and colleagues [134] demonstrated that both meningitis and encephalitis patients showed at least 50% of neutrophils in CSF samples. Taken together, these studies suggest an active participation of neutrophils in viral pathogenesis, although mechanistic explanations remain elusive.

In a murine model, it was shown that WNV infection induces rapid recruitment of neutrophils to the inoculation site, favoring viral replication, concomitant with an increase in CXCL1 and CXCL2 by infected macrophages [42]. In this study, cell depletion experiments demonstrate a biphasic role of neutrophils: when depleted before the infection, WNV-infected mice displayed decreased viremia and increased survival. Contrary, neutrophil depletion after WNV infection led to increased viral burden [42]. Although it is known that neutrophils can serve as reservoirs for many pathogens, such as IAV, S. aureus, and Leishmania sp. [195–198], the study by Bai and colleagues [42] does not provide mechanistic insight into how neutrophil involvement, in the context of the temporal dynamics of inflammation, modulates viral clearance and disease progression. Nevertheless, other studies corroborate the idea that early neutrophil infiltration indeed might be deleterious for disease outcome. Paul and colleagues [198] showed that WNV+ neutrophils are recruited to the brain via osteopontin production, contributing to worsened disease. In the same line, Wang and colleagues [199] showed that IL-22 genetic ablated mice displayed decreased neutrophil infiltration and viral loads in the brain. Taken together, the studies from Amber et al. [198] and Wang et al. [199] demonstrate that early neutrophil infiltration under normal conditions can occur through different recruitment mechanisms (osteopontin, IL-22) and might certainly contribute to increased inflammation and worsened outcomes.

Finally, it was found that neutrophils were identified as a major physiological source of B-cell-activating factor (BAFF), expressing high levels across blood and lymphoid tissues and modestly upregulating BAFF in response to viral RNA sensing. Unlike dendritic cell-derived BAFF, however, neutrophil-derived BAFF did not contribute to protective antiviral immunity, instead supporting T-independent type-2 antibody responses. Thus, data suggest that neutrophil BAFF might act as an immunomodulatory, but not as an antiviral factor [200]. Although the study does not show BAFF as a disease driver in neutrophils, its inducibility by inflammatory stimuli raises, but does not confirm, the possibility of broader roles in shaping inflammatory responses. Thus, despite the lack of more studies, preliminary evidence suggests that neutrophils can act both as reservoirs that facilitate the spread of WNV and also as fundamental elements of the antiviral response in subsequent phases of infection.

Neutrophils and Alphaviruses

Alphaviruses comprise several arboviruses with a global distribution and are traditionally classified as New World or Old World viruses based on their geographic origin. Although useful, this distinction remains relatively simplistic, as exemplified by Mayaro virus (MAYV), which circulates in the Americas (New World) but displays a predominantly arthritogenic clinical profile that closely resembles that of Old World alphaviruses, such as CHIKV [201, 202]. Studies addressing the role of neutrophils in alphavirus infections remain limited and are largely restricted to MAYV and CHIKV. Notably, these infections are predominantly characterized by a macrophage-rich inflammatory infiltrate, which may partly explain the relatively limited investigation of neutrophil functions. Below, we integrate the available findings to provide a cohesive overview of their potential roles.

Early in infection, alphaviruses such as CHIKV and MAYV trigger strong granulopoietic programs, with elevated systemic G-CSF levels observed in humans and mice. It was demonstrated that G-CSFR signaling maintains neutrophil-monocyte balance, whereas its absence skews hematopoiesis toward inflammatory monocytes and B cells and disrupts type I IFN-dependent disease control [203]. Given that G-CSF-stimulated neutrophils modulate monocyte activation, these findings suggest that neutrophils function not only as effector cells but also as regulators of early myeloid dynamics shaping the host response to arthritogenic alphaviruses.

At peripheral sites of replication, neutrophils accumulate and acquire more complex and sometimes deleterious functions. In CHIKV and Ross River virus infection, excessive neutrophil influx exacerbates tissue pathology by promoting edema and driving CCR2-dependent recruitment of additional myeloid cells. Consistently, de Castro-Jorge and colleagues [136] showed that NLRP3 activation enhances early neutrophil recruitment in MAYV infection, supporting inflammatory swelling while limiting NK cell infiltration and contributing to pain. Neutrophils recruited to sites of arboviral inoculation, such as mosquito bites, can also facilitate viral establishment in CHIKV infection, a pattern echoed across arboviruses, including WNV. Importantly, classical timing-dependent effects are evident: early neutrophil depletion worsens WNV disease, while delayed depletion becomes protective. Conversely, later during alphavirus infection or under conditions where monocyte influx is impaired, neutrophils can assume protective antiviral roles. In CCR2−/− mice infected with MAYV, our group has shown that a shift from macrophage-driven to neutrophil-dominated infiltrates reduced viral loads and bone loss, a phenotype supported by elevated CXCL1 levels [137].

Additional layers of neutrophil involvement arise from their metabolic and immunoregulatory functions. Stoermer and colleagues [135] demonstrated that neutrophils and macrophages in Ross River virus-infected tissues express arginase 1, suppressing T-cell responses and impairing late viral clearance – pointing to neutrophil-driven immunosuppression that may promote viral persistence and chronic arthropathy [135]. Accordingly, it was further reported abnormally activated splenic neutrophils during MAYV infection, marked by high CD62L expression, suggesting systemic dysregulation of neutrophil activation states that influence trafficking, priming, and effector responses [204]. Integrating these findings, neutrophils cannot be viewed as uniformly pathogenic or antiviral. Rather, their contributions reflect a continuum of inflammatory amplification, immunometabolic regulation, and direct antiviral activity. Understanding how cues such as G-CSF, CCR2/CCL2 signaling, NLRP3 activation, and arginase 1 expression shape neutrophil phenotypes will be essential for designing interventions capable of mitigating alphavirus-induced pathology while preserving critical antiviral functions.

Concluding Remarks: Targeting Neutrophils through Resolution Pharmacology

Several groups have investigated therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating neutrophil effector functions, with an emphasis on restricting their migration, degranulation, and NET formation. Among these approaches, the use of anti-GM-CSF monoclonal antibodies, specific MPO inhibitors, and IL-8-CXCR1/2 axis blockers stands out, as well as compounds capable of interfering with inflammatory mediators that regulate the expression and release of neutrophilic chemoattractants [205–208]. In addition, recent advances include strategies aimed at controlling the exacerbated formation of NETs, such as the prevention of the PAD-4 enzyme, the use of ROS scavengers, and the direct cleavage of NETs by DNases [209–213]. Although these approaches present relevant therapeutic potential, we propose that interventions based on promoting neutrophil apoptosis represent a particularly promising pathway in the context of viral infections, by favoring efficient efferocytosis and the subsequent resolution of acute inflammation.

An expanding body of work has established that the resolution of inflammation is an active and highly coordinated process, and that pro-resolving mediators hold promise against a myriad of bacterial and virus infections, as thoroughly reviewed by our group in Costa and collaborators [74]. Within this framework, neutrophils, traditionally viewed as short-lived cells, emerge as central determinants of whether inflammation resolves efficiently or progresses toward tissue injury. Viral infections such as DENV and SARS-CoV-2 are known to delay neutrophil apoptosis, thereby prolonging their presence and favoring activation states associated with excessive degranulation, ROS production, and NET formation [102, 121, 122]. This shift in neutrophil death pathways directly links severe viral infection to failed resolution, since apoptotic neutrophils are required to initiate efferocytosis and the subsequent pro-resolving reprogramming of macrophages [43]. In addition to neutrophil death types and its implications, recent evidence demonstrates the contribution of neutrophil phenotypes, during its lifespan within the tissue, in the resolution of inflammation. Tavares and collaborators [214] elegantly demonstrated a previously unidentified population of Siglec-F+ neutrophils that are crucial to the resolution of inflammation by producing pro-resolving mediators via ALOX15 (12/15-lipoxygenase). These changes stimulate the differentiation of monocytes into macrophages, contributing to the resolution of acute lung injury [214]. Further investigation will clarify whether these recently identified Siglec-F+ neutrophils are present in respiratory viral infections and also other viruses as well.

Among the pro-resolving mediators that counteract viral infections, our group has shown that these molecules hold great promise against viral infections, including the melanocortin-based agonist AP1189 in accelerating recovery from COVID-19 hospitalized patients, angiotensin (1–7) also against SARS-CoV-2 in mouse models, and, lately, lipoxin A4 to increase apoptosis in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of mice infected with IAV [215–217]. In the same line, we demonstrate that angiotensin (1–7) was also effective against H1N1, increasing neutrophil apoptosis and efferocytosis by alveolar macrophages in mice [218]. Additionally, other independent groups corroborate our results on pro-resolving mediators and viral infections: Walker and colleagues [219] demonstrated that protectins PCTR1 and PD1 decreased viral loads and attenuated inflammation in RSV-infected mice, suggesting that targeting excessive neutrophil activation, by promoting timely resolution, may represent a valuable approach for limiting RSV pathology.

Besides these molecules, AnxA1 has emerged as a critical regulator of neutrophil trafficking and lifespan. We have shown that plasma levels of AnxA1 are decreased in DENV-infected patients compared to healthy controls, and also in severe DENV-infected patients compared to non-severe DENV-infected patients, which suggests a fundamental role for endogenous AnxA1 in restraining disease severity [220]. Indeed, AnxA1-mimetic peptide Ac2-26 decreased disease parameters and accelerated resolution in a mouse model of DENV infection. We also achieved improved disease parameters during CHIKV infection, although in an anti-inflammatory manner, due to differences in treatment schedules and experimental approaches when compared to the DENV infection model [220, 221]. Similarly, during murine coronavirus infection, we recently demonstrated that AnxA1 genetic ablation results in heightened neutrophil infiltration and worsened pulmonary inflammation, whereas treatment with the AnxA1 mimetic peptide Ac2-26 reduces leukocyte accumulation and improves disease parameters in SARS-CoV-2 infection [169]. Importantly, in all these different viruses (DENV, CHIKV, and SARS-CoV-2), AnxA1 and its mimetic peptide were able to avoid excessive inflammation without altering the capacity of the host to deal with viral replication. Within this therapeutic rationale, pharmacological agents that restrain neutrophil recruitment to primary sites of infection and/or promote their timely apoptosis may hold significant potential for the treatment of viral diseases. We and others have demonstrated that promoting neutrophil apoptosis enhances bacterial clearance and reduces immunopathology [223, 224], a principle that likely extends to viral infections – particularly respiratory viruses that rely strongly on neutrophil-driven immunopathogenesis. Limiting neutrophil migration is especially relevant, as excessive accumulation of activated neutrophils contributes to tissue destruction without necessarily improving pathogen clearance. This effect is classically associated with anti-inflammatory therapies. However, pro-resolving mediators can also restrain neutrophil recruitment while extending their actions beyond migration blockade, promoting neutrophil apoptosis and potentially modulating their functional phenotype. This distinction underscores a fundamental difference between anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving mechanisms. Based on the literature and, importantly, on findings from our group, we argue that there is no strictly proportional relationship between the intensity of the inflammatory response and the host’s ability to control infection. Instead, an exaggerated inflammatory milieu, especially regarding neutrophil activity, often delays resolution and disrupts the restoration of tissue homeostasis. Thus, resolution-directed therapies that recalibrate neutrophil activity and adequate death by apoptosis represent a promising avenue in viral immunopathology.

Conclusions

Overall, we conclude that the most consistent evidence regarding neutrophils in viral infections, and the aspect most clearly linked to the resolution of inflammation, relates to the delay of neutrophil apoptosis observed in infections caused by viruses such as DENV, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV. Beyond this documented phenomenon, the modulation of neutrophil phenotype by pro-resolving mediators, extending past the mere induction of apoptosis, remains poorly explored and deserves further investigation. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may provide critical insights into how resolution pathways shape antiviral immunity while limiting immunopathology.
