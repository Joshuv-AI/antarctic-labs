Using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to explore mutual-help group implementation challenges in college recovery programs
Authors: Noel Vest, Erin Major, Meredith B Brooks, Joo H Lee, Isabel Redman, Justin Bell, Connor Buchholz, Allie Silverman, Mark McGovern, John Kelly, Christine Timko, Michael D Stein, Christina Freibott, Alison Hamilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMutual-help groups (MHGs) are an effective resource in improving health for emerging adults in addiction recovery. Collegiate recovery programs (CRPs) have the infrastructure to support mutual help meeting implementation for this high-risk priority population for substance misuse. Despite their established role as an addiction recovery resource, the implementation of MHGs within CRPs is inconsistent and highly variable across institutions. To address this gap, we explored barriers and facilitators to planning and implementing MHGs within CRPs.MethodWe conducted qualitative interviews with 18 program director and college administrator participants from five CRPs across the United States in 2023. The guide focused on four domains of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research 2.0: Innovation, Inner Setting, Outer Setting, and Individuals. Thematic analysis was conducted using an iterative coding approach to identify key facilitators and barriers to MHG implementation.ResultsFour cross-cutting themes shaped MHG implementation within CRPs: (1) recovery-aligned leadership and facilitation, (2) institutional positioning and infrastructure, (3) sustainability and resourcing, and (4) inclusivity and pathway fit. Across these themes, leadership advocacy and lived-experience credibility facilitated implementation, while limited staffing capacity constrained delivery. MHGs were more accessible when embedded within visible campus infrastructure aligned with student success priorities. In addition, domain level facilitators included the credibility and perceived effectiveness of MHGs (Innovation), strong institutional support from high-level leaders (Individuals), donor engagement and established recovery spaces that fostered peer connections (Inner Setting), and alignment of CRPs with broader university missions related to student retention and well-being (Outer Setting). Barriers included concerns about the accessibility and inclusivity of traditional 12-Step models (Innovation), administrative misalignment with development offices (Inner Setting), and limited institutional awareness and funding instability (Outer Setting).ConclusionsImplementation strategies to enhance MHG adoption in CRPs include diversifying MHG offerings, integrating CRPs into university financial structures, strengthening external treatment and recovery housing partnerships, and fostering inclusive recovery environments. Addressing these barriers could enhance accessibility and sustainability of MHGs, ultimately improving recovery support for college students with substance use disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14904-5.Keywords: Collegiate recovery programs, Mutual-help groups, Implementation science, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), Addiction recovery, Higher education

Background

The high prevalence of substance use and the accompanying risks of alcohol and other drug use disorders among US college students represent a critical public health challenge [1–3]. Collegiate recovery programs (CRPs) can address these issues by creating recovery-friendly environments on campuses through peer support, alcohol/drug-free events, dedicated student drop-in centers, and on-campus mutual help groups (MHGs) [4]. Defined as college-based programs that offer a range of recovery-support services and safe environments for students in or seeking addiction recovery, CRPs have been shown to improve recovery outcomes, educational achievement, and academic opportunities among students engaged with them [4–6]. Currently, 176 CRPs serve undergraduate and graduate students at two- and four-year public and private institutions across 40 states [7].

Though each CRP operates uniquely, program development is informed by a set of best practices formulated in the Association for Recovery in Higher Education (ARHE) standards and recommendations [8]. A core component of these practices is the integration of MHGs, which serve as an evidence-based intervention for individuals with addiction [9, 10]. MHGs are voluntary, peer-based support organizations for individuals with substance use concerns that facilitate recovery through shared experience and peer-driven support (e.g., typical formats include 12-step and non-12-step groups such as SMART Recovery) [11]. MHG participation has been linked to increased abstinence rates, improved psychosocial functioning, and reductions in substance use severity [12]. Young and emerging adults in particular benefit from MHGs, as these groups provide critical peer support, reinforce recovery-related identity development, and facilitate engagement in a substance-free social network [13–15]. Despite their demonstrated efficacy and inclusion in CRP best-practice recommendations, little research has examined how organizational and institutional contexts shape the implementation of MHGs within collegiate recovery programs.

Colleges represent a unique context in which to conduct implementation research due to their differing models of leadership, school year calendar, student culture, and the diversity of students and staff [16]. Likewise, colleges themselves may be influenced by the local community, prominent alumni, the Department of Education, and funding at state and federal levels [17, 18]. Unfortunately, these contexts through which universities operate often do not work in harmony to support implementation of substance use disorder recovery programming [19, 20]. Despite growing interest in campus-based recovery supports, prior research has rarely applied a determinants framework aligned with socio-ecological models (student-, program-, institutional-, and community-level factors) to examine how multilevel factors influence MHG implementation in collegiate settings [18].

This study addresses the existing research gap by using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [21, 22] to examine the key determinants influencing the implementation of MHGs within CRPs. By analyzing factors at the program director (PD) and college administration (CA) levels, this research provides insights into the barriers and facilitators affecting the adoption of this evidence-based practice. By identifying determinants of mutual-help group implementation in collegiate settings, this study fills a health services gap in understanding how addiction recovery supports can be integrated into complex educational systems to improve access and continuity of care.

Method

Setting and participants

The principal investigator (NV) visited all participating campuses (September 2023–November 2023) and conducted in-person interviews at each site. The five CRPs were identified early during the development of a related grant application through existing professional relationships and strategic partnership building, with the goal of capturing variation in geography, funding structure, and institutional context. All individuals who were contacted and met eligibility criteria agreed to participate (100% participation among those approached).” Eligible participants were 18 years or older and currently or recently employed as a PD or CA at one of the five college campuses. Guided by the socio-ecological framework of CRPs [18], PDs and CAs were selected as participants because they occupy distinct but interconnected roles in shaping recovery supports at the campus level. As such, a PD was defined as an individual with primary responsibility for the day-to-day operation of a CRP, including student engagement, coordination of recovery services, facilitation or oversight of recovery programming (e.g., mutual-help groups), and routine program administration. CA was defined as an individual holding an institutional leadership or oversight role relevant to collegiate recovery programming, such as responsibility for budgeting, policy development, staffing decisions, or integration of the CRP within broader university structures (e.g., student affairs, health services, or academic administration), but not primarily responsible for day-to-day operations. CAs were all Deans of their respective departments.

Study design

The CFIR 2.0 is a refined, evidence-informed structure that systematically addresses multilevel determinants of implementation in complex settings like higher education [21, 22]. Provided in Table 1, the CFIR core domains—Innovation, Inner Setting, Outer Setting, and Individuals (with the Process domain excluded for this study)—provide a comprehensive lens to examine the intervention’s characteristics (e.g., MHG innovation source), the university’s internal context (e.g., resources and culture), external influences (e.g., funding and partnerships), and the roles of key stakeholders (e.g., administration and implementation leads). This framework’s adaptability to context and focus on practical application ensure its relevance for advancing both the science and practice of recovery programming in collegiate settings.

CFIR 2.0 provided an interview guide rubric to assess barriers and facilitators at multiple levels, with questions mapped onto CFIR 2.0 constructs (see Supplement 1 for the Interview Guide). For example, to assess the “Innovation” domain, participants were asked, “How do you perceive the effectiveness and relevance of MHGs in supporting students in recovery?” To explore the “Inner Setting,” questions probed institutional culture, such as, “What resources does your CRP have to support MHGs, and what barriers exist?”. Interviews were 30–40 min long and all participants were consented and received a $25 gift card at the conclusion of the interview. All of the interviews were conducted in a private office and digitally recorded.

Data analysis

We used the CFIR to inform the structure of the thematic and matrix analysis. The thematic analysis was used to identify key themes of barriers and facilitators within CFIR domains. We followed the thematic analysis with a matrix analysis to compare the themes across CRP sites. The thematic analysis was conducted using an iterative approach, integrating CFIR [22] constructs, previous research, and inductive coding [23, 24].

After thoroughly reviewing the transcripts, two researchers (NV, EM) conducted an initial round of open coding on the interview transcripts to identify themes and codes inductively, starting independently and then convening to compare and refine the codes. This process was repeated three times on a total of six transcripts before creating a formalized codebook, in which these emergent codes were categorized according to the domains and constructs of CFIR. We did not identify any relevant codes outside of the CFIR domains. The resulting codebook was refined through multiple iterations to improve clarity, consistency, and comprehensiveness. Four coders (NV, EM, IR, JL) applied the final codebook to all transcripts using ATLAS.ti Web [25] with a minimum of two coders reviewing each transcript. Coders took part in a training session where they independently coded a subset of transcripts, followed by group discussions to ensure consistency. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions. Memos and annotations documented coding decisions, emergent patterns, and codebook modifications, ensuring a rigorous thematic analysis. Consistent with an information power approach [26, 27], sampling focused on recruiting participants whose professional roles positioned them to provide rich, relevant insight into determinants of MHG implementation within CRPs. Data collection concluded once interviews no longer yielded substantively new insights relevant to the study’s analytic focus and CFIR-informed research questions.

Themes were synthesized within CFIR domains, with additional attention to cross-domain interactions (e.g., how institutional leadership support [Inner Setting] influenced stakeholder buy-in [Individuals]). Matrix analysis was used to compare barriers and facilitators across CRPs, identifying commonalities and site-specific variations [28]. The “Innovation” domain reflected perceptions of the research and effectiveness of MHGs (e.g., innovation source). The “Individuals” domain included perceptions and attitudes about MHGs or experiences with CRP resources at different stakeholder levels. For the “Inner setting” CFIR domain, we included factors related to MHGs, the CRP, and its relative structure within the school (e.g., CRP culture, available resources). The “Outer setting” encompassed broader campus and external factors (e.g., financing, the role of family and alumni, partnerships). The CFIR Process domain was intentionally excluded, as the study focused on contextual and stakeholder-level determinants shaping mutual-help group implementation rather than discrete implementation activities. Consistent with this focus, participants infrequently discussed formal implementation processes, and process-related elements were embedded within broader organizational and leadership factors captured by the other CFIR domains.

Ethical approval and consent

The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist [29] and received approval from the Boston Medical Center Institutional Review Board H-43,127 on November 4, 2022.

Overview of key overarching themes

Across sites, four overarching themes shaped MHG implementation within CRPs (Table 3): (1) recovery-aligned leadership and facilitation; (2) institutional positioning and infrastructure supporting MHGs; (3) sustainability and resourcing of MHG delivery; and (4) inclusivity, pathway fit, and student engagement. These themes incorporated barriers and facilitators described by PDs and CAs and intersected primarily across the Inner Setting, Outer Setting, and Individuals domains.

First, recovery-aligned leadership and facilitation were central to successful MHG implementation. MGH facilitators with lived experience in recovery or strong recovery literacy were viewed as more credible and effective in engaging students, navigating multiple recovery pathways, and sustaining meetings over time. In contrast, MHGs were more difficult to implement or sustain when facilitation capacity was limited, overly centralized, or constrained by competing student-facing and administrative responsibilities. Second, the institutional positioning of MHGs within CRPs shaped their visibility, accessibility, and legitimacy. MHGs embedded within dedicated recovery spaces, recovery housing, or well-integrated counseling and student success structures were described as safer and more accessible for students. Conversely, MHGs located in stigmatizing or low-visibility settings, or organizationally marginalized within larger campus systems, faced barriers to consistent participation and awareness. Third, sustainability and resourcing influenced whether MHGs could be offered consistently and responsively. Stable institutional support aligned development infrastructure, and reliable access to space and facilitation enabled continuity of MHG delivery. Barriers included limited fundraising capacity, misalignment with institutional advancement priorities, and reliance on off-campus or externally operated meetings, which introduced logistical challenges for students. Finally, inclusivity and pathway fit were critical to student engagement in MHGs. Programs offering multiple recovery pathways and identity-responsive meetings were perceived as more welcoming and accessible to diverse student populations. In contrast, dominant recovery norms, limited representation, and recovery framings misaligned with students’ identities or goals deterred participation in MHGs, particularly among students early in recovery or from marginalized backgrounds. Together, these themes demonstrate that MHG implementation within CRPs is shaped by overlapping institutional, cultural, and individual factors that cut across CFIR domains. This approach highlights shared factors shaping MHG availability and avoids separating influences that participants experienced together.

Overview of domain and construct level themes

Innovation: Perceived credibility and fit of MHGs

Participants consistently described MHGs as highly credible, evidence informed, and influential due to their foundational research and respected status, enhancing trust and legitimacy among students. The peer-based nature of MHGs fostered a sense of belonging and safety, making them a key support system for students. According to participant #8, CA:

…there are different pathways in recovery, but mutual help support groups seem to still be primary part of most people’s experiences in terms of building recovery….

Many also expressed the importance of inclusivity and adaptability of multiple recovery pathways. Participants noted that attendees were free to share their experiences without judgment, making the meetings more accessible to students who may not resonate with traditional recovery models.

Concerns were raised about whether modern college students genuinely resonate with the traditional 12-Step model, which has historically been a cornerstone of mutual-help recovery. They questioned whether today’s students find the structured, abstinence-based, and often spiritually oriented approach of programs like Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Narcotics Anonymous (NA) relevant or engaging. Another major concern was the perceived coercion or mandatory nature of MHG attendance if required as part of student agreements within CRPs. While these meetings were intended to enhance recovery and personal growth, concerns were raised about whether requiring attendance might be counterproductive, particularly if students do not connect with the specific recovery models or feel forced into participation rather than engaging voluntarily. Lastly, the logistical barriers associated with required attendance—such as meetings being held off-campus—further complicated the issue. According to participant #7, CA:

I have lots of questions about whether or not today’s college students respond to the 12-step model and is that a barrier? I dunno what the solution is, but I think that’s a big challenge with today’s college students.

Individuals: Leadership advocacy and staffing capacity

Across sites, participants described how both senior administrators and frontline implementation leads shaped the feasibility, credibility, and sustainability of MHG offerings. Rather than functioning as distinct or isolated roles, leadership influences were experienced as overlapping. High-level leaders—including deans, senior administrators, board members, and external stakeholders—were consistently described as facilitators of MHG implementation. Their advocacy within institutional decision-making structures enabled access to funding, staffing, and physical space and helped elevate MHGs as a legitimate and valued component of campus recovery support. Notably, many high-level leaders had personal or familial connections to recovery, which strengthened their commitment and engagement. According to participant #4, CA:

So this is a student who is from a completely different area of the United States that came to [School] because of [School CRP], had an amazing experience in the [School CRP] program, and now her family has consistently been engaged in [School] since that point in time, to the point where her mother is on our Board of Regents.

No participants identified high-level leadership as a barrier, underscoring the importance of leadership buy-in for MHG sustainability.

Implementation leads—those responsible for organizing, facilitating, or supporting MHGs—also played a critical role. Lived experience in recovery was widely viewed as enhancing credibility with students, donors, and campus partners and was described as particularly important for sustaining engagement over time. Implementation leads who employed recovery models perceived as accessible and student-centered (e.g., SMART Recovery) were seen as especially effective in engaging diverse students. However, participants also described capacity-related barriers among implementation leads. When leadership responsibilities were heavily concentrated in student-facing and relational work, limited time and support constrained strategic planning, fundraising, and program growth. Noted participant #5, CA:

…his [program director] strength is connecting with the students and I think running the seminars, keeping friends and family going, and that’s about half, that takes up about half of this time. And so being able to do the other and thinking about the other stuff, I think doesn’t actually happen.

This tension between relational engagement and administrative capacity was a recurring challenge shaping the consistency and scalability of MHG implementation within CRPs.

Inner setting: Infrastructure, culture, and mission alignment

Dedicated physical space emerged as a central facilitator of MHG implementation. Programs with designated recovery centers, drop-in rooms, or recovery housing described these spaces as essential for fostering community, safety, and consistent meeting attendance. Participants noted that the presence of a visible, identifiable recovery space enhanced legitimacy and strengthened peer engagement. Participant #11, PD explained:

But having a place to go to assemble, to be safe, to feel like you have support, I think is huge. And I really believe with all my heart, the turning point for our center here was the building of the building.

Integration within broader campus systems also supported MHG delivery. When CRPs were embedded within student success structures, counseling services, academic advising, or university leadership teams, MHGs were more visible and better supported administratively. Participants frequently linked MHG offerings to institutional priorities such as student retention, persistence, and overall well-being, reinforcing their perceived value within the university mission.

Inclusive campus culture was another facilitator. Programs that framed recovery within broader commitments to access, equity, and student support—particularly for first-generation students, students of color, and LGBTQ+ students—described greater institutional alignment and responsiveness to student needs. According to participant #5, CA:

Our students are actually able to say, I was seen and respected and cared about and support was here when I needed it. That’s the hallmark of a successful program.

This was seen as particularly important given that mainstream recovery spaces, including some 12-step groups, have not always been perceived as safe or welcoming for all identities [30].

Cultural barriers affected MHG engagement. Participants described tensions related to dominant recovery norms, including the perceived overreliance on 12-step models and limited racial or gender representation within meetings. In some contexts, conservative campus climates or shifting diversity-related policies created uncertainty about inclusivity and belonging. These cultural dynamics influenced who felt comfortable participating in MHGs and how recovery was perceived within the broader campus environment. According to participant #18, PD:

So [School] is very, when it comes to colleges and universities, is on the more conservative side of colleges and universities. That being said, it’s also in an area of the country that’s probably one of the most welcoming, inviting groups of people that I know. And so while they can be conservative in their ideals, they’re also can be welcoming to those that are a little different than them.

Together, these findings suggest that MHG implementation within CRPs depends not only on individual leadership but also on institutional infrastructure, visibility, and cultural alignment within the university setting.

Outer setting: Financing and external partnerships

Family and alumni engagement emerged as important facilitators of MHG implementation, particularly through financial support and advocacy. Parents of students who had benefited from CRPs and associated MHGs were frequently described as committed donors and informal ambassadors for recovery programming. Stated by participant #11, PD:

Number one, the best source of funding are parents whose children have gone through the center and been successful and changed their life.

Alumni with lived experience in recovery similarly reinforced the perceived value of MHGs and contributed to long-term financial stability. Participants emphasized that sustained philanthropic support was often driven by storytelling and relationship-building rather than formal fundraising requests.

Financial sustainability was further supported when institutions invested in dedicated advancement infrastructure. Programs with access to development staff, presidential support, or established endowments described greater continuity in MHG offerings, staffing, and space. According to participant #3, PD:

And that support from the president has also allowed for our Institutional Advancement office to dedicate specific staff to fundraising for the program. So we’ve raised a significant endowment, $10 million that funds every staff person here.

In some cases, according to PD participant #11, institutional funds were also used to supplement student scholarships, reducing financial barriers to participation in MHGs and related recovery programming.

Our biggest investment from the school was often times help with scholarships.

Strong external partnerships also facilitated MHG implementation. Connections with academic advising, housing, counseling, and social work services helped normalize recovery support across campus, while relationships with community-based treatment providers and mutual-help organizations supported referrals and continuity of care beyond the university setting. According to participant #15, PD:

…usually if there’s a new one, like a new sober living or a new treatment center, they hear about our program and want to come and meet us. And partly for referrals, they want to let us know that they exist and tell us about the program and things like that. And they want to support what we’re doing also.

Despite these facilitators, several external barriers constrained MHG implementation. Tensions with university development offices were frequently described, particularly when recovery programs were not prioritized relative to other institutional fundraising goals. In these cases, misalignment limited access to donor support and placed additional fundraising burdens on program leaders. As noted by participant #11, PD:

…he wanted to give a large sum to the center and rather than just come directly to me, he went to the development office because he’d given other gifts through the years … and they tried to talk him into giving it to athletics. After that, I chose to have nothing to do with them.

Participants also identified challenges related to family involvement. While families were often supportive, excessive parental oversight—particularly efforts to monitor students’ recovery activities—was viewed as conflicting with core principles of autonomy, confidentiality, and student-driven recovery that underlie MHG participation. According to participant #14, PD:

I would describe them more as helicopter parents. And what they’re trying to do is they’re trying to have some control over students while they’re here and they’re trying to get their kid to sign FERPA waivers so that they can keep tabs on what they’re doing and what they’re not doing as far as their recovery. And that’s not what this place is set up for.

Additional barriers included broader structural and geographic constraints. Some programs faced limited access to local addiction treatment specialists, reducing opportunities for external clinical partnerships. Recovery housing, while widely viewed as a facilitator of engagement, also required substantial oversight and conflict management, occasionally straining program capacity. Participant #14, a PD noted:

It [recovery housing] can be hard to manage. There can be drama for sure. We’ve had students become very unhappy living there.

Together, these findings highlight how MHG implementation within CRPs is shaped by external financial, relational, family, and service environments. External partnerships and financing can substantially strengthen MHG availability and stability, but misalignment, resource inequities, and competing expectations can equally undermine sustained implementation.

Discussion

Key findings

This study examined multilevel determinants influencing the implementation of MHGs within CRPs using CFIR as an organizing framework. Three overarching findings emerged from the synthesized results (see Table 3). First, visible leadership advocacy and stable financial stability were foundational determinants that enabled all other implementation activities. Programs with engaged senior administrators, supportive boards, and reliable funding were better positioned to secure space, staff, and institutional legitimacy for MHGs. Second, dedicated infrastructure and alignment with institutional missions related to student success, retention, and well-being normalized recovery as part of campus life and supported sustained student engagement. Third, though MHGs were broadly viewed as credible and effective, concerns about the fit of traditional 12-step models for some students underscored the importance of flexible, inclusive approaches that reflect diverse recovery pathways. Taken together, these findings suggest that successful MHG implementation within CRPs depends less on whether MHGs are offered and more on how well they are embedded within institutional structures, resourced over time, and adapted to the needs of contemporary college students.

Interpretation and significance

These findings extend prior research on peer-based recovery supports by demonstrating that the presence of MHGs alone is insufficient to ensure consistent or sustainable implementation in collegiate settings. Instead, MHGs function most effectively when embedded within a broader recovery ecosystem supported by leadership champions, institutional investment, and dedicated space. Leadership advocacy emerged as a particularly powerful determinant, influencing financing decisions, staffing capacity, and cultural acceptance of recovery programming. This finding aligns with implementation research highlighting the central role of senior leadership in legitimizing and sustaining evidence-based practices in complex organizational environments [31, 32]. The study also highlights the practical importance of purposeful infrastructure. Dedicated recovery spaces and housing did more than facilitate meeting logistics; they signaled institutional commitment, fostered peer connection, and reduced stigma. Similarly, alignment between CRPs and broader university missions reframed recovery support as a core component of student success rather than secondary accommodation. This reframing may be especially important in higher education contexts where competing priorities can marginalize recovery programming. At the same time, participants’ concerns about the cultural relevance of traditional 12-step models point to a critical tension in MHG implementation. While MHGs were widely viewed as foundational to recovery, a one-size-fits-all approach was perceived as insufficient for increasingly diverse student populations. Programs that diversified offerings and emphasized choice were better equipped to engage students and sustain participation, underscoring the need for adaptability within evidence-based recovery supports.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, it included five CRPs and reflects the perspectives of PDs and CAs only, which may limit generalizability to other institutional contexts or stakeholder groups. Second, student perspectives were not included, limiting conclusions about how identified determinants directly shape student engagement or recovery outcomes. Third, participants often discussed MHGs within the broader context of CRPs, making it difficult to fully disentangle determinants specific to MHG implementation from those shaping recovery programming more generally. Finally, only a subset of CFIR constructs was examined, and additional determinants not captured here may influence MHG implementation in other settings. Nonetheless, this study represents the first theory-informed, multi-site qualitative examination of determinants shaping MHG implementation within CRPs. Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable insights into how organizational, cultural, and financial contexts shape recovery supports in higher education.

Implications and next steps

For stakeholders in higher education, these findings suggest that colleges and universities seeking to strengthen MHG implementation should prioritize leadership engagement, integrate CRPs into core institutional financing structures and budgets, and invest in visible recovery infrastructure (substance free housing and student drop-in centers). Diversifying MHG offerings and supporting inclusive, student-centered approaches may further enhance accessibility and engagement. Future research should incorporate student perspectives, examine how identified determinants relate to engagement and recovery outcomes, and explore implementation strategies that address the determinants revealed in this study. Mixed methods designs linking organizational determinants to measurable student outcomes would be particularly valuable in advancing implementation science in collegiate recovery settings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that institutional culture, administrative engagement, and resource allocation critically shape the implementation of MHGs within CRPs. Strengthening recovery support in higher education will require universities to align leadership priorities with student needs, secure sustainable funding, and foster inclusive environments that normalize recovery as an integral part of campus well-being. Policymakers and practitioners can use these insights to design campus policies and funding mechanisms that integrate recovery supports into the broader continuum of student health services, promoting equity and long-term recovery outcomes.
