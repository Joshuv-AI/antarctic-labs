Throughout the 21st century, society encountered numerous crises that
demanded quick and widespread action. In the present work, we documented
researchers’ responses to 25 of these crises, analyzing 1,537,284
published responses from 3,262,152 researchers spanning 225 countries
and territories. An analysis of publication speed and citation patterns
yielded support for a proverbial claim: “to go fast, go alone; to go
far, go together”. Solo authors were quicker to publish responses to
societal crises – but their responses received less attention in
scholarly, news, and policy venues. Growth towards large-scale
collaboration was associated with increased impact; However, this team
size advantage was characterized by diminishing returns and
significantly varied across sampled crises. Furthermore, pluralistic
machine learning modeling highlights the importance of considering
additional context, such as government effectiveness and the expertise
of team leaders. Taken together, results facilitate evidence-based
conversations regarding how researchers optimize speed and impact in the
midst of societal crises.

Throughout history, human societies have encountered and navigated
crises that demanded quick, urgent, and widespread action. For example,
in the first quarter of the 21st century, society witnessed some of the
deadliest terrorist attacks in modern history, a global pandemic, and
the rapid release of AI technologies. For decades, researchers have
worked to document how people respond to these societal crises, helping
separate proverbial myths from reality. For example, contrary to common
belief, research suggests that people tend to respond to crises

not
with panic – but with order, solidarity, and
cooperation
1,2
.

Curiously, the actions of researchers themselves are often overlooked in
crisis response research – leaving an incomplete narrative. For
example, to understand society’s response to the September 11 terrorism
attacks, it is important to consider that a leading academic society
endorsed psychologists’ participation in detainee
interrogation
3
. To understand society’s response to
the COVID-19 pandemic
4
, it is important to consider
the record-setting speed in which researchers developed mRNA
vaccines
5
. Last, to understand how society responds to
the shock of AI technologies, we argue it will once again be important
to consider the role of researchers – such as the ultra-massive teams
that developed GPT-4
6
, DeepSeek V3
7
,
and other technologies at the center of an ongoing “AI
race”
8
. In the present work, we document researchers’
responses to 25 of these societal crises, analyzing a total of 1,537,284
published responses from 3,262,152 researchers spanning 225 countries
and territories (Figure 1).

Proverbial claims about collaboration suggests “to go fast, go alone;
to go far, go together”. This belief is also often echoed in the
scientific community. Proponents of team science often suggest that
collaboration is a way to “go further,” as it (a) facilitates role
specialization, (b) increases total effort dedicated to a problem, and
(c) creates conditions believed to facilitate recombinant growth, wisdom
of the crowd, and/or collective intelligence
9–15
.
These benefits, however, are often suggested to come at a cost that may
be particularly noteworthy during crisis: speed. Indeed, proponents of
team science also often emphasize the time needed to (a) assemble teams,
(b) negotiate responsibilities, (c) navigate different institutional
policies, (d) manage disagreements, and so on
16–19
.
Working alone is presumed to be quicker, with simpler coordination,
fewer bureaucratic constraints, and greater decision-making agility.

In the present work, we test proverbial claims at scale, examining
1,537,284 separate published responses from a total of 3,262,152
researchers. As methodological tests of speed, we borrowed from the
sociological notion of
focusing events
20
. More
specifically, we sampled societal crises that (a) entered mainstream
awareness relatively suddenly, and (b) received relatively novel names
(e.g., “Hurricane Katrina”; Figure 2, Panel A). This allowed us to
estimate response speed by comparing (a) the approximate date that most
people became aware of each crisis, to (b) the publication dates of
papers explicitly mentioning relevant keywords (retrieved via

OpenAlex
21
). To index the
impact
of
researchers’ published responses, we considered the number of citations
their work received in scholarly, news, and policy venues (tracked by

OpenAlex
21
and

Altmetric
22
). Last, to establish comparative
benchmarks, we compared team size to 12 other predictors often
considered at the individual (e.g., leader prestige), group (e.g.,
number of involved institutions), and societal level (e.g., political
stability). Here, we leveraged a pluralistic set of machine learning
models to derive robust estimates of the extent to which each variable
predicts the speed and impact of researchers’ responses to
crises
23
.

Expanding upon insights from seminal field studies
1,2
,
the present work provides a relatively comprehensive assessment of
researchers’ published responses to an extreme phenomenon: societal
crises. Theoretically, this tests potentially extreme boundary
conditions of a robust finding in research on team science: work from
larger teams often achieves more impact (e.g., in scholarly articles,
patents, and policy venues), often at the cost of other potentially
desirable outcomes (e.g., innovativeness)
24–29
. By
sampling a relatively large number of events, we further estimate the

reliability
of such relationships across a wide variety of crises
(e.g., the H1N1 pandemic, Brexit, and the announcement of an impending
“metaverse”). Taken together, our work not only lends itself to a more
complete narrative of how society responds to crisis, but also to
evidence-based conversations regarding how to optimize the speed and
impact of researchers’ responses to these crises.

Results

Similar to what was documented decades ago with the general population,
our results illustrate that researchers also often respond to crisis
with order, solidarity, and cooperation
1,2
. In total,
we uncovered 1,537,284 published responses to 25 societal crises –
representing the collective response of 3,262,152 researchers from 225
countries and territories (Figure 1).

In many instances, we found that researchers do “go alone”. Averaged
across crises, 49.1% of published responses were attributed to a single
author (
SD
= 22.0% across crises). The other 50.9% of the time,
researchers “go together,” with teams ranging from 2 to 100+
co-authors. Next, we describe the implications this decision has for the
speed and impact of their published responses.

Movement towards large-scale collaboration yields
diminishing returns in
impact

Past field studies have documented a sociological tendency for people to
mass organize in response to crisis
1
. Our results
illustrate a similar dynamic in science, not only in terms of the number
of teams but also their
size
. Of 1,537,284 total published
responses to crises, we observed 122,353 publications (8.0%) with ten
to over a hundred authors.

Observing a large number and range of big teams helps us better
understand their reliability and limits. Here, we once again replicate
patterns from classic field studies on crisis response: collaboration
growth yields diminishing returns
30
. More
specifically, links between the size and measured impact of papers were
best characterized as logarithmic, as opposed to linear (Δ
BIC
≥
6,639; Wilcoxon signed-rank
p
< .001) or quadratic
(Δ
BIC
≥ 5,203; Wilcoxon signed-rank
p
< .001).
In other words, growth in team size was associated with diminishing
returns in measured impact. Notably, however, the rate of diminishing
returns significantly varied across crises; Including crisis-specific
random slopes consistently improved model fit (all Δ
BIC
≥ 8,063;
likelihood-ratio tests, all χ²(1) ≥ 8,091,
p
< .001).
These results provide initial evidence of the importance of context:
teams of similar size respond more impactfully to some crises than
others.

Size in context: Integrating predictors of quick and
impactful crisis responses in
science

Not surprisingly, many claims about the role of collaboration – both
proverbial and scientific – fail to consider size in context. For
example, perhaps it is difficult for people to “go fast” in the face
of political dysfunction or “go far” with relatively inexperienced
leaders. To illustrate the utility of considering team size alongside 12
other predictors measured at the individual, group, and societal level,
we deployed a pluralistic set of machine learning architectures (e.g.,
XGBoost, neural nets, random forests). When trained to consider the full
set of variables – vs. team size alone – models were significantly
more accurate at predicting response speed (Δ
R²
= .38) and
mentions in scholarly (Δ
R²
= .39), news (Δ
R²
= .09), and
policy venues (Δ
R²
= .07) in a hold-out test dataset (all
Wilcoxon signed-rank
p
< .001).

Considering team size along a dozen other variables also allows us to
compare how useful they are for predicting speed and impact. For
example, despite attracting significant attention from lay people and
researchers alike, permutation variable importance scoring indicates
that team size is
not
the strongest sampled predictor of speed
and impact. Team size was the (a) 11th strongest sampled predictor of

Discussion

Arguably every human society has had to navigate crises, whether they be
natural, technological, social, or of some other nature. Across
generations, these cumulative experiences have helped inspire folk
beliefs about how humans respond to crises — some more accurate than
others. For instance, past research helped debunk beliefs that people
respond to crises with panic
1,2
. Instead, it seems
that people respond with a surprising amount of order, solidarity, and
cooperation. Our results strengthen such assertions in the sense that we
show ≈1.6 million instances where researchers organized and published
responses to societal crises, approximately half of which involved a

team
of co-authors.

More centrally, we evaluated a popular but largely untested folk belief
about collaboration: “to go fast, go alone; to go far, go together”.
Here, we found more support for conventional wisdom. When considering
publication dates and mentions across academic, news, and policy venues,
results suggest that researchers do indeed publish faster alone and more
impactfully together. However, results also point to oft-neglected
nuance: larger teams yield diminishing returns in impact. Furthermore,
context matters: team sizes that were impact-maximizing in some crises
(e.g., COVID-19) were less successful in others (e.g., responses to
September 11 terrorism attacks).

To further highlight the role of context, we considered the predictive
validity of 12 additional candidate variables. Results help illustrate
that a combination of individual-level, group-level,
and

society-level context is needed to understand effective crises
responses
24
. For example, the prestige of the
corresponding author and the number of co-authors were consistently
strong predictors of measured impact, as was the number of papers a team
ultimately cites. For speed, conversely, the strongest predictors were
expert-derived ratings of facets of government effectiveness. In other
words, while it is generally the case that researchers publish faster
alone and more impactfully together, maximizing the speed and impact of
researchers’ responses to crises will require considering context from
multiple angles.

In terms of limitations, we navigated conceptual challenges regarding
the operational definition of a crisis, a crisis response, and the
impact of those responses. We similarly wrestled with mechanistic
uncertainty — e.g., uncertainty regarding whether large teams produce
vs. flock to better ideas. Below, we elaborate on these challenges and
their implications for research on crises.

First, we encountered considerable differences in how societal crises
are defined. Scholars across different research traditions have
emphasized different characteristics, such as suddenness, urgency,
social disruption, and public perception
31–33
. We did
not seek to resolve these long-standing debates. Instead, we sought a
working definition that permitted relatively clean tests of response
speed. Ultimately, we borrowed most heavily from the notion of sudden,
attention-grabbing
focusing events
20
. Due to
their suddenness, these events allowed us to operationalize response
speed in a literal fashion: the number of days that passed since people
typically became aware of the development. However, in doing so, we
excluded a variety of societal crises, such as those that (a) developed
relatively gradually (e.g., climate change), (b) attracted less
attention (e.g., sand supply scarcity), and/or (c) have not received
historically unique names (e.g., recent waves of political
polarization). Crises such as these make it difficult to estimate
response speed, but nonetheless represent critical threats to modern day
societies. There is an urgent need to understand such developments more
thoroughly (e.g., via future analyses of self-reported timelines,
research repository commitment history, and/or grant activity reports).

We similarly faced difficulty defining what constitutes a crisis
response in science, as well as how to measure its impact. Here, our
operationalizations were largely shaped by data availability. We sought
large quantities of data because the phenomena we seek to understand are
rare; Societal crises represent an extreme disruption of norms, as do
the mass collaborations they often inspire. In the context of science,
we found the largest swaths of data in bibliometric repositories of
published scholarly papers, the researchers who authored them, and the
extent to which the work was subsequently discussed across multiple
communities. However, a drawback of this approach is that it misses
other ways that researchers respond to crises (e.g., via community
service) and seek to improve the world (e.g., increasing societal
well-being vs. increasing citation counts). There are obvious challenges
to documenting those types of responses and quantifying those broader
forms of impact. Nonetheless, doing so is essential in order to develop
a comprehensive understanding of how researchers contribute to responses
to societal crises.

A third set of limitations involves mechanistic uncertainty. Put simply,
we do not know
why
we observe correlations between the size,
speed, and impact of crisis responses in science. It is possible there
is a simple causal story, wherein simply increasing the size of one’s
team leads to higher-quality insights. However, an equally plausible
story is that the most promising ideas better attract sustained
attention from larger groups of researchers. Similarly, it’s possible
that large teams have stronger social capital instead of stronger ideas
— elevating the impact of otherwise non-superior research. Evaluating
the mechanisms underlying such links is a oft-discussed goal in research
on teams
27–29,34,35
, a goal we encourage future
researchers to address via methods that better facilitate causal
inference (e.g., field experiments, naturalistic experiments, etc.).

Until these limitations are better addressed, we caution against a
de-prioritization of solo responses or a fetishization of large-scale
responses. Effective crisis response likely requires a balance of solo
and large team effort from researchers, a mixture of traditionally and
non-traditionally tracked activities, and an eye towards outcomes rarely
measured or discussed in hiring and promotion.

To conclude, human societies have always had to navigate periods of calm
and crises — a continuously repeating cycle. In the present work, we
take stock of the role of researchers in the aftermath of societal
crises, weighing in on a proverbial claim about collaboration: “to go
fast, go alone; to go far, go together”. Across 1,537,284 published
responses from 3,262,152 researchers spanning 225 countries and
territories, we find strong support for this proverbial claim.
Researchers do indeed publish faster alone and more impactfully
together. However, growth towards large-scale collaboration yields
diminishing returns that vary across crises. Furthermore, a pluralistic
set of machine learning models converge on the importance of considering
additional context, such as government effectiveness or the expertise of
team leaders. In the future, we emphatically encourage stakeholders to
consider these insights – as well as the potential interconnectedness
of different crisis responses, different types of activity and impact,
and the more conceptually challenging crises we did not sample in the
present work. After all, if society encounters crises we cannot
ultimately overcome, there will arguably be no institution of science
left to document and understand such developments.

Materials and methods

Ethics

To examine researchers’ published responses to crisis events, we
interoperated secondary data on (1) research articles, authors, and
institutions (via
OpenAlex
21
), (2) tracked
mentions in scholarly, news, and policy documents (via
Altmetric
Explorer
22
), and (3) country-level governance
indicators (via WorldBank’s
Worldwide Governance
Indicators
36
). Because the analysis focused on
secondary data, the University of Florida Institutional Review Board
declared this work exempt (Protocol #ET00042723). Anthropic Claude (3.7
Sonnet - Fable 5) was used to generate focusing event suggestions, as
well as to review all analysis code for accuracy, accessibility, and
reproducibility issues.

Sampling crisis events

The sampling plan was designed to identify crises that provide
relatively clean methodological tests of response speed. To do so, we
borrowed from the sociological notion of sudden, attention-grabbing,

focusing events
20
. More specifically, we
identified societal crises that (a) entered mainstream awareness
relatively suddenly, and (b) received relatively novel names. This
allowed us to estimate response speed by simply comparing (a) the
approximate date that each crisis entered mainstream awareness, to (b)
the publication dates of papers explicitly mentioning relevant keywords
(retrieved via
OpenAlex
21
). For example, there
are virtually no mentions of the phrase “ChatGPT” in the research
literature before 2022. However, in the three-year aftermath of its
release, researchers have mentioned it in 111,646 research publications
(Figure 2, Panel A).

To identify candidate focusing events, we considered (a) events
frequently discussed in crisis response research (e.g., the Deepwater
Horizon oil spill), (b) recommendations from foundational large language
models (e.g. Claude 3.7 Sonnet), and (c) our own lived experiences. From
this list, we conducted initial bibliometric searches to ensure that the
crisis had relatively unique identifying keywords and large growth
around the approximate date of the focusing event. From this list, we
chose 25, seeking a
variety
of different societal crises (Figure
1), including ones often characterized as natural (e.g., Hurricane
Katrina), sociopolitical (e.g., #MeToo), technological (e.g., ChatGPT),
and economic realms (e.g., the 2008 Global Financial Crisis). This was
done to promote more generalizable conclusions about links between the
size, speed, and impact of researchers’ responses to crises.

Identifying published
responses

Researchers respond to societal crises in many ways, including advising
policymakers, providing media commentary, and engaging the public
directly. Here, we focus on one of their most thoroughly tracked
contributions: publications. For each sampled crisis, we first
identified keywords or phrases that researchers used to explicitly
signal relevance. Second, we specified the approximate date that each
sampled crisis entered mainstream awareness. Third, we used

OpenAlex
to pull papers containing the relevant keywords in their
title, abstract, or (if available) main text.

As an example of this workflow, consider the COVID-19 pandemic. This
crisis entered mainstream awareness near late December 2019, when
initial reports of the virus emerged from Wuhan, China. We sampled
papers that made an explicit connection to this crisis by using keywords
like “COVID-19”, “SARS-CoV-2”, and “novel coronavirus”. See

Table S3
for full list of keyword strings.

In total, we identified 1,610,388 papers containing keywords linked to
our 25 sampled crises within the three years before and after each
event. Consistent with the notion of focusing events, only 2.9% of this
keyword use occurred in the 3 years
preceding
the approximate
date that each crisis entered mainstream awareness (Figure 2, Panel A).
Our analysis focuses on the 97.1% of papers published in the 3 years

following
these events. After removing non-articles and records
without tracked authorship meta-data, this produced a final sample of
1,537,284 papers.

Operationalizing response speed and
impact

For each paper, we operationalized response speed as the number of days
between (a) their publication date and (b) the approximate date each
crisis entered mainstream awareness. For impact, we considered multiple
indicators: the number of times each paper was mentioned in scholarly
works (
N
= 27,898,094 mentions examined), the news (
N
=
1,462,597 mentions), and policy documents (
N
= 115,423 mentions).
Scholarly citation counts were pulled from
OpenAlex
article
meta-data
21
. News and policy document citation counts
were pulled from
Altmetric Explorer
22
. Because
a Digital Object Identifier is required to match a paper to an

Altmetric Explorer
record, analyses of response speed and
scholarly citations draw on all 1,537,284 papers, whereas analyses of
news and policy mentions draw on the 1,370,810 papers carrying one (see

Table S1
and
Table S2
for the sample sizes behind each
estimate). To reduce sensitivity to outliers, speed and impact indices
were converted to percentiles when fitting regression models. Because
crises vary in the overall amount of attention they receive, percentiles
were calculated separately for each event (i.e., standardized). (See

Figure S1
for similar results without this standardization.) For
ease of interpretation, percentiles are always reported in a positive
direction, wherein higher values indicate faster and more impactful
publication.

Predictors of quick and impactful crisis responses in
science

The primary analyses focused on evaluating proverbial claims about links
between the size, speed, and impact of researchers’ published responses
to crises. Team size was extracted from
OpenAlex
meta-data. For
most analyses, team size was treated as a continuous variable. However,
for ease-of-interpretation, we also report common (but
arbitrary
28,37
) categorizations: papers published by a
(a) solo author, (b) small teams (2 - 4 authors), (c) medium teams (5 -
9 authors), and (d) big teams (10+ authors).

The secondary analyses focused on considering team size alongside three
other sets of predictors. The first captures other characteristics of
teams and their work, such as the unique number of institutions and
countries involved, the primary discipline, and the volume of literature
cited. The second set of predictors focused on the person who leads the
work – more specifically the corresponding author’s past academic
success (
h
-index) and type of home institution (education,
healthcare, government, company, non-profit, archival). These predictors
were all extracted from OpenAlex meta-data
21
.

The third set of predictors considered the corresponding author’s
broader societal context. More specifically, we connected their
affiliation data to the World Bank’s Worldwide Governance Indicators,
matched by year of publication (up to last recorded year: 2022) and
country
36
. These indicators contain expert ratings of
six dimensions, with higher values indicating normatively better
governance: (1) voice and accountability (e.g., freedom of expression),
(2) political stability (e.g., absence of violent destabilization), (3)
government effectiveness (e.g., quality of public services), (4)
regulatory quality (e.g., ability to promote private sector
development), (5) rule of law (e.g., adherence to social rules), and (6)
control of corruption (e.g., non-privatization of public power). Because
the World Bank series is biennial before 2002, intervening years were
filled by within-country linear interpolation.

Analytic strategy

Primary analysis

The primary analysis focused on evaluating the extent to which links
between the size and effectiveness of crisis responses are linear,
logarithmic, or quadratic. Conceptually, this represents constant,
diminishing, and curvilinear returns, respectively. For example, rather
than leading to constant linear gains, it is possible that increases in
team size lead to diminishing returns. This would suggest that
researchers navigate a plateau, wherein they must make increasingly
larger investments to increase the speed and impact of their crisis
responses. Alternatively, increases in team size may exhibit a
curvilinear “Goldilocks zone”: increases in team size may lead to
improved performance up to a point, after which further growth becomes

detrimental
. Such relationships are captured via linear
(
\beta_{1}X_{i}
), logarithmic (
\beta_{1}ln(X_{i})
), and
quadratic regression (
\beta_{1}X_{i}+\beta_{2}X_{i}^{2}
),
respectively.

Linear, logarithmic, and quadratic relationships between team size and
each outcome were modeled separately using the lme4 mixed-effect
modeling package
38
(v. 2.0.1) in R (v. 4.5.3). To
accommodate the fact that papers published on similar crises are
non-independent, we included random intercepts for each sampled crisis:

\beta_{0}+u_{0,j}
, wherein
u_{0,j}∼N(0,𝜎_{0}^{2})
. To ensure
that relatively uncommon large team efforts (e.g., 20 co-authors)
received the same priority as relatively common small team efforts
(e.g., 2 co-authors) during model fitting, we applied inverse frequency
weights. (See
Figure S1
for similar patterns from raw data
plots.) When comparing fit, we favored a model (e.g., logarithmic over
linear) when two criteria were both met: (a) it reduced the Bayesian
Information Criterion by more than 5 (Δ
BIC
> 5), and
(b) a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that its
distribution of weighted absolute residuals was significantly lower
(using
p
< .05 threshold). Mann-Whitney comparisons of
team-size categories were two-sided. No adjustments were made for
multiple comparisons. Given the unusually large size of our sample, we
focus more on interpreting patterns than statistical significance.

To evaluate the extent to which the best-fitting relationship varied
across sampled crises, random slopes were added. For example, when a
logarithmic model best fit the data, random slopes would be included for
the logarithmic term:
(\beta_{1}+u_{1,j})ln(X_{ij})
, wherein

u_{1,j}∼N(0,𝜎_{1}^{2})
. The present work mostly focuses on describing
the results of these models. Nonetheless, for convenience, we also
report comparisons of team size categories; e.g., papers published by
small (2 - 4 authors) vs. medium teams (5 - 9 authors). In these
scenarios, we used Mann-Whitney tests to compare group distributions
(using
p
< .05 threshold).

Secondary analysis

A complete understanding of how researchers respond to crises may
ultimately require considering team size alongside other variables that
may confound or moderate its effects. To do so, we fit a pluralistic set
of machine learning algorithms containing a fuller set of potentially
relevant predictors. (For more information on predictor selection, see

Predictors of quick and impactful crisis responses in science
.)
To evaluate the utility of adding these additional predictors, we
compared models with vs. without their inclusion. Because different
modeling approaches can produce meaningful variation in researchers’
conclusions
39
, we used AutoGluon
1.5.0
40
to train a variety of different models for
each outcome: gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM, CatBoost, and
XGBoost), randomized tree ensembles (random forests and extremely
randomized trees), and neural networks. When fitting models, we sought
authoritative inferences by using best-quality AutoGluon presets, with
dynamic stacking disabled and no additional stacking levels. Each model
was given up to 60 hours to train.

Prior to model training, we divided papers by crisis and then randomly
assigned 80% into a training/validation set. The remaining 20% was
held out as test data. Crucially, dividing papers by crisis before
splitting data helped ensure that their statistical patterns were
considered during both training and testing.

To estimate the importance of each predictor, we used a model-agnostic
permutation method
23
. This methodology estimates the
importance of each predictor by randomly shuffling its values and then
re-testing the accuracy of each algorithm. When the algorithm relies on
the predictor, randomly shuffling its values diminishes performance. We
applied this permutation-based method to each predictor of each
top-fitting base model for each of the 4 outcomes. Importance was
estimated with five random shuffles per predictor on a random subsample
of 20,000 hold-out papers. To facilitate comparability across outcomes
and models, variable importance values were rescaled to a [0, 1]
range within each base model before being plotted in Figure 3.
