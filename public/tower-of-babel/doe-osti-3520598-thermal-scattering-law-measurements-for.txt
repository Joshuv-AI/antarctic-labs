 1. INELASTIC NEUTRON SCATTERING MEASUREMENTS
Thermal inelastic neutron scattering (INS) experiments were carried out to probe the phonon spectra of
two different enrichments of γ-LiAlO2 . We utilized two time-of-flight neutron spectrometers at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL): (a) the ARCS direct-geometry spectrometer [1, 6, 15] and (b) the VISION
indirect-geometry spectrometer [13].
For direct-geometry spectrometers, choppers select the incident neutron energy, and time-of-flight
methods—based on the known detector distance—measure the final neutron energy, allowing the energy
transfer to be determined. The ARCS instrument is optimized for experiments that require high neutron
flux over a wide Q range and offers a moderate energy resolution but extensive angular coverage. ARCS
directly measures S (Q, ω), the dynamic structure factor that describes the probability of scattering events
transferring momentum Q and energy ω. Owing to its large Q and angular coverage, ARCS is especially
well suited for deriving phonon spectra under the incoherent approximation. Using this framework, the
phonon spectra can be extracted from S (Q, ω) using the following relation [14]:

In indirect-geometry spectrometers, the incident neutron beam is “white”; that is, it covers a broad energy
range, up to the maximum set by a T0 and bandwidth chopper. After scattering from the sample, neutrons
enter crystal analyzers—pyrolytic graphite, in the case of VISION—that fix the final neutron energy by
Bragg reflection. The neutrons subsequently pass through a 20 K beryllium filter that removes higher-order
reflections from the graphite analyzers. This step often produces a sharp feature at around 11–12 meV
(the so-called “λ2 peak”) in VISION measurements. The time-of-flight method, along with known flight
path distances, enables the determination of the incident energy and thus energy transfer. With this setup,
instruments like VISION maintain an approximately constant fractional energy resolution (1–1.5%)
across the 2–1000 meV energy range. The analyzers are arranged in forward (low Q) and backward (high
Q) scattering geometries. A comparison of the Q–ω trajectories between direct- and indirect-geometry
spectrometers is shown in Figure 1.

 2. TASK 3: ARCS MEASUREMENTS
These measurements provide direct experimental insight into the lattice dynamics of natural LiAlO2 ,
which are essential for the accurate determination of phonon densities of states and the subsequent
generation of thermal neutron scattering laws used in nuclear data evaluations and reactor physics
applications.

2.1 EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
The temperature-dependent scattering function, S(Q,E), measurements of LiAlO2 (Natural Li and
100% 7 Li enrichment) samples were performed using the ARCS instrument at the Spallation Neutron
Source (SNS) at ORNL [1]. The samples were measured first at 295, and 300 K. The samples were
accommodated in a vanadium can.
Three incident neutron energies, Ei = 40, 90, and 150 meV, were used so that the combined data can
provide the entire phonon density of states (PDOS) with high resolution (small full widths at half
maximum [FWHM] in energy transfer) and can capture the details of the PDOS. The ARCS energy
resolution decreases as energy transfer increases. The raw data were corrected for detector efficiency using
a vanadium standard measurement. Scattering from an empty sample can was subtracted from the data
presented here. The corrected data were normalized by the integrated proton current. For the extraction
of a PDOS, the data were also corrected for multi-phonon and multiple scattering using the multi-phonon
code [10].

2.2 S(Q,E)

The relationship between S(Q,E) and S(Q,ω) is S (Q, E) = S (Q,ω)ℏ . Figure 2 presents the measured
S(Q,E) at 295 K for the natural LiAlO2 sample measured inside the vanadium can (Fig. 2a), the vanadium
can measured alone (Fig. 2b), and the difference between them (Fig. 2c), which isolates the scattering
signal originating from the natural LiAlO2 sample. The measured intensity represents INS arising
from interactions between the incident neutrons and lattice vibrations (phonons), where the scattering
process involves changes in both the neutron momentum transfer (Q) and the energy transfer (E). In
vanadium, phonon excitations are confined to relatively low energies, primarily below 40 meV. As a result,
vanadium exhibits strong scattering intensity within this energy range, as clearly shown in Figure 2b.
Above approximately 40 meV, the scattering intensity from the vanadium rapidly decreases, reflecting
the absence of higher-energy phonon modes. This well-characterized phonon spectrum makes vanadium
an ideal reference material for background subtraction in INS experiments. In contrast, the measured
S(Q,E) of natural LiAlO2 , obtained after subtracting the vanadium contribution (Fig. 2c), reveals a much
richer vibrational spectrum. Two pronounced low-energy phonon features are observed below 40 meV.
Moreover, two higher-energy phonon features are evident at energies around 100 meV; these features
correspond to optical phonon modes due to stronger interatomic bonding. Between approximately 40 and
90 meV, the scattering intensity from natural LiAlO2 exhibits a broad and continuous distribution, which is
characteristic of overlapping phonon branches. This intensity gradually decreases with increasing energy
and becomes negligible near ∼ 90 meV, where the PDOS diminishes and no significant vibrational modes
are present.
Figure 3 compares the measured S(Q,E) for natural LiAlO2 and 100% 7 Li-enriched LiAlO2 samples at
temperatures of 295 and 600 K. The measurements indicate that both the increase in temperature and
the change in lithium isotopic mass due to enrichment have a negligible effect on the observed S(Q,E)

A direct comparison between the natural and 7 Li-enriched samples further shows that isotopic substitution

The S(Q,E) maps of the 7 Li-enriched sample (second row of Fig. 3) appear systematically fainter than
those of the natural lithium sample. This difference is attributed to experimental factors rather than
intrinsic material properties; they most likely arise from differences in powder packing density and,
consequently, a reduced effective sample mass in the enriched measurement. Despite this intensity
reduction, all key scattering features observed in the natural LiAlO2 sample are clearly preserved in the
enriched sample.

2.3.2 PDOS Calculation
The PDOSs were calculated using a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell with 128 atoms. The inter-atomic force constants
were calculated using the first-principles projector augmented wave (PAW) method as implemented in the
Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [9, 8, 7]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA)

of the Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) [11] formalism was used for the exchange-correlation (XC)
functional. Three PDOSs were considered for comparison with the measured ones, as shown in Figure 6.
First, the optimized crystal structure was used to calculate the PDOS. However, the optimized structure
resulted in phonon softening (shift to the left) when compared with the measured PDOSs. Therefore, the
experimental lattice constants (LCs) were used in the calculations (a = 5.1685 and c = 6.2565 Å) [4]. The
experimental structure resulted in phonon hardening (shift to the right). We then scaled the experimental
lattice constants by a factor of 1.0038, and the calculated phonons show better agreement with measured
PDOSs, as shown in Figure 9. All phonon calculations were performed by using a regular 6 × 6 × 6
k-point mesh and an energy cutoff of 820 eV. The PHONOPY software [17, 16] was then used to extract
the force constants and to sample the PDOS using 50 × 50 × 50 q-points.

Figure 7(a) shows the calculated partial PDOSs for γ-LiAlO2 . Although lithium is the lightest element
in the compound, its vibrational modes are primarily confined to energies below ∼80 meV, with two
pronounced peaks centered around 30 meV and 60 meV. These features indicate that the lithium atoms
participate mainly in low-energy to intermediate-energy lattice vibrations. In contrast, both aluminum
and oxygen exhibit broad and continuous PDOS contributions extending up to approximately 90 meV,
which reflect their involvement in a wide range of vibrational modes. In addition, a distinct high-energy
peak is observed around 100 meV. A clear phonon energy gap is present near 95 meV, separating
the mid-energy vibrational modes from the high-energy optical phonons. Figure 7(b) compares two
neutron-weighted PDOS estimates: (i) the OCLIMAX result extracted from the calculated S (Q, E) and (ii)
a neutron-weighted PDOS constructed from the phonopy partial PDOSs using the weighting factors σi /Mi
(i = Li, Al, O), where σi and Mi are the total bound neutron scattering cross section and atomic mass of
element i, respectively. The two neutron-weighted PDOSs show very good agreement over the entire
energy range, which validates the consistency between the OCLIMAX analysis and the phonopy-based
PDOS calculation.

Figure 8 presents a comparison between the calculated dynamic structure factor S(Q,E), generated using
the OCLIMAX code, and the experimentally measured S(Q,E) for natural γ-LiAlO2 at 295 K with an
incident energy of Ei = 40 meV. The calculated and measured spectra show very good agreement across
the entire (Q, E) space, reproducing both the regions of high scattering intensity and the weaker features at

 3. TASK 2: VISION MEASUREMENTS
3.1 EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Temperature-dependent measurements of the scattering function, S (Q, ω), were recorded on LiAlO2
samples (100% 7 Li enrichment) using the VISION instrument. The samples were measured first at 298,
600, and 823 K using the same material as in the ARCS measurements. After initial review, we determined
that these measurements were impacted by sample-related and experimental limitations (reduced counting
statistics and degraded resolution relative to expectations); therefore, a repeat measurement with improved
sample geometry and mass was performed.
Additional measurements were performed at 15 K using new, larger samples in an aluminum flat-plate
cell sample holder with dimensions of 50 × 50 × 6 mm (6 mm thickness). These 15 K measurements were
performed for both natural lithium and 100% 7 Li-enriched LiAlO2 . For reference, the ARCS samples were
contained in cylindrical vanadium cans (10 mm diameter, 50 mm height).
Similar to the ARCS measurement, scattering from an empty container was subtracted from the data
presented here. The corrected data were normalized by the integrated proton current.

3.2 S (Q, ω) AT 15 K

As mentioned in the introduction section, using OCLIMAX (a digital twin of the VISION instrument),
it is possible to calculate S (Q, ω) in both the incoherent approximation and the coherent one-phonon
approximation. Figure 11 compares the measured S (Q, ω) of natural LiAlO2 with calculated S (Q, ω)
using OCLIMAX in the incoherent and coherent one-phonon approximations. The calculated spectra are
in very good agreement with the measured data in both cases, reproducing the overall spectral envelope
and the dominant phonon features. The coherent one-phonon approximation is appropriate for this material
because its constituent nuclei exhibit significant coherent bound scattering cross sections, and it shows
improved agreement at lower energies, where interference effects and the structure factor influence the
intensity and lineshape. In contrast, the incoherent approximation underestimates the low-energy inelastic
intensity in this case. Whether a coherent treatment is required in the final ENDF TSL evaluation will be
further assessed using constraints from thermal total cross section transmission measurements.

3.3 S (Q, ω) MEASUREMENTS AT 295 K TO 823 K
Figure 12 shows a comparison of the measured S (Q, ω) of 100% 7 Li-enriched LiAlO2 at different
temperatures. As mentioned earlier, the 298 K, 600 K, and 823 K measurements were performed using
a smaller sample than the one used for the 15 K measurement; therefore, the counting statistics and the
effective energy resolution appear to be impacted. In general, the 298 K spectrum is consistent with the
15 K measurement in terms of the overall spectral shape and the presence of the main phonon features.
The 600 K spectrum is also consistent with the 298 K spectrum. These findings support the conclusion
from ARCS measurements that temperature has no major effect on the phonon band positions within the
experimental resolution over this range, and that temperature-induced lattice changes are minor.
The 823 K measurement was collected for the shortest amount of time, primarily because the measurement
campaign encountered experimental issues and schedule constraints that required prioritization of the most
critical measurements. As a result, the 823 K dataset is best interpreted qualitatively; nevertheless, the
dominant phonon features remain visible and broadly consistent with the lower-temperature spectra.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
4.1 CONCLUSIONS
ARCS and VISION time-of-flight inelastic neutron scattering measurements were performed on γ-LiAlO2
with natural lithium and 100% 7 Li enrichment over a wide temperature range. The main conclusions are as
follows.
 • Successful ARCS measurements were completed at 295 K and 600 K using incident energies Ei =
 40, 90, and 150 meV, enabling extraction of the PDOS over the full vibrational energy range relevant
 for thermal scattering law (TSL) evaluations. This work provided high-quality measurements for
 validation of the calculated phonon spectra.
 • VISION measurements were performed at 15 K (new, larger samples) and at elevated temperatures
 (298 K, 600 K, and 823 K). The 15 K datasets provide the high-quality benchmark for validating
 calculated S (Q, ω) due to improved counting statistics and resolution.
 • The measured S (Q, E) maps and extracted PDOS show no statistically significant shift in the
 dominant phonon features between 295 K and 600 K within the experimental resolution, indicating
 that temperature-induced changes in the vibrational spectrum are small over this range.
 • Comparison between natural and 100% 7 Li-enriched samples indicates that lithium isotopic
 enrichment produces no discernible changes in the measured inelastic spectra within experimental
 uncertainty, consistent with the predominantly Al–O character of the high-energy optical modes.
 • First-principles lattice dynamics calculations (Phonopy+VASP) and OCLIMAX simulations
 reproduce the measured spectra well for both ARCS and VISION, supporting the validity of the
 current modeling workflow for producing TSL-relevant inputs.

4.2 FUTURE WORK
The remaining work is focused on reducing residual modeling uncertainties, determining the appropriate
scattering approximation (coherent vs. incoherent), and delivering an evaluated TSL. Planned activities
include:
 • Perform X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements and Rietveld refinement to obtain the most
 physically accurate crystal structure (lattice parameters, atomic positions, and site occupancies) to
 use as input for atomistic modeling and phonon calculations.
 • Complete and validate the final lattice-dynamics calculations across the required temperature grid
 for TSL production, including evaluation of the sensitivity of high-energy optical modes to lattice
 constants.
 • Use neutron transmission measurements to constrain the thermal total cross section and assess
 whether a coherent treatment is required for the final ENDF TSL evaluation. Transmission
 measurements have been proposed/accepted at SNS and will be performed using the recently
 developed capability for temperature-dependent total cross sections at VENUS [12].
 • Generate and validate the final TSL evaluations and associated documentation, including comparison
 against both inelastic spectra and total cross section constraints.
 • Deliver the final reports and agreed-upon deliverables on schedule.
