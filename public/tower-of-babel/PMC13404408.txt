Rising burden of Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone, 1990–2023: analysis of Global Burden of Disease 2023 estimates
Authors: Mohamed B Jalloh, Gibrilla Fadlu Deen, Omar Bah, Ishmaela Bah, Abdul Aziz Suma, Onome T Abiri, Melvina C Thompson, Edries Tejan, Fatima Jalloh, Foray Mohamed Foray, Monalisa M J Faulkner, Bamba Gaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia is an increasing cause of disability and mortality worldwide. Primary dementia surveillance data remain limited in many low-income settings, including Sierra Leone.ObjectiveTo describe trends in Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 modelled estimates for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone from 1990 to 2023, and to decompose the change in estimated deaths into demographic and age-specific mortality components.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of GBD 2023 estimates for the cause category ‘Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias’ in Sierra Leone. We analysed estimated counts and age-standardised rates for prevalence, deaths, years of life lost, years lived with disability and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) stratified by age and sex. Temporal trends were described with log-linear regression to estimate annual per cent change, and a three-component decomposition attributed changes in deaths to population growth, ageing and age-specific mortality.ResultsGCD 2023 estimated that dementia deaths in Sierra Leone increased from 188 in 1990 to 474 in 2023, an absolute increase of 286 estimated deaths (152% relative increase). Estimated DALYs increased from 3703 to 7440. The modelled age-standardised death rate rose 26.8% (16.3 to 20.7 per 100 000) and the age-standardised DALY rate 13.4% (293.7 to 333.2 per 100 000). Estimated prevalent cases increased from 5647 to 8887, while the modelled age-standardised prevalence rate declined from 430.7 to 404.0 per 100 000 population. The log-linear annual percentage change was +1.02% for age-standardised death rates, +0.60% for DALY rates and −0.18% for prevalence rates. Decomposition attributed 62% of the increase in estimated deaths to changes in age-specific mortality, 30% to population growth and 8% to population ageing.ConclusionsGBD 2023 estimates show a rising absolute burden of dementia in Sierra Leone, against a background of limited local surveillance and mortality ascertainment.Keywords: DEMENTIA, ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE

Introduction

Dementia is a major disability, dependence and mortality in older adults.1 In the global burden of disease (GBD) framework, ‘Alzheimer disease and other dementias’ includes Alzheimer disease, vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, mixed dementias and dementias in other diseases classified elsewhere.

Global estimates indicate that the number of people living with dementia will increase substantially over the coming decades.2 This increase is expected to be greatest in low-income and middle-income countries, where population ageing is occurring alongside major health-system constraints. The GBD 2019 forecasting analysis estimated 57.4 million people living with dementia in 2019 and projected nearly a tripling by 2050.2 The Lancet Commission has also emphasised life-course risk reduction, timely diagnosis, caregiver support and health-system planning.3 4

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), empirical dementia data remain sparse and heterogeneous. Community-based studies and systematic reviews show substantial uncertainty in dementia prevalence and risk-factor estimates.5–8 This uncertainty reflects variation in diagnostic tools, underdiagnosis, cultural interpretation of cognitive symptoms and limited specialist services. These issues are especially important in smaller countries where vital registration, dementia registries and longitudinal ageing cohorts are limited or absent.

Sierra Leone is undergoing rapid demographic and epidemiological transition. Its older population is growing, while cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors remain common.9–11 At the same time, primary dementia surveillance, specialist neurology and geriatric services, neuroimaging capacity and long-term care infrastructure are constrained. Country-specific estimates are, therefore, useful for planning, but they also carry important uncertainty.

GBD estimates are valuable in data-sparse settings because they use a standardised cause hierarchy, multiple data sources, covariates and internally consistent modelling. They are, however, modelled outputs rather than direct surveillance observations. In Sierra Leone, where dementia surveillance and mortality ascertainment are limited, GBD 2023 estimates should be interpreted as modelled burden estimates rather than direct measures of disease occurrence, mortality or service performance.

This study aimed to describe GBD 2023 modelled estimates of Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone from 1990 to 2023. We examined prevalence, deaths, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) by age and sex. We also decomposed the change in estimated deaths into contributions from population growth, population ageing and changes in age-specific mortality.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a secondary analysis of GBD 2023 estimates for ‘Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias’ for Sierra Leone from 1990 to 2023. GBD applies a standardised cause hierarchy, systematic data synthesis and Bayesian meta-regression (DisMod-MR 2.1) to produce internally consistent estimates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, YLLs, YLDs and DALYs across 204 countries and territories.12 13 Detailed descriptions of input data sources, modelling strategy and validation procedures for dementia in GBD 2023 are provided in the GBD 2023 methods publications and online metadata, and we relied on those standard estimates without modification.1 12 No additional local primary data sources were used in this analysis.

Case definition and classification

Dementia was defined according to the GBD cause ‘Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias’, which aggregates International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes corresponding to Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, mixed dementias and dementias in other diseases classified elsewhere.2 This approach follows WHO and Alzheimer’s Disease International guidance that treats dementia as an umbrella syndrome with multiple underlying aetiologies.14

Although GBD does not provide separate estimates for individual subtypes in Sierra Leone, we acknowledge that Alzheimer’s disease likely represents about 60%–70% of cases globally, with vascular and other degenerative dementias comprising most of the remainder.1 15 Consequently, our estimates should be interpreted as all-cause dementia burden rather than Alzheimer’s disease alone.

Measures and data extraction

For each year from 1990 to 2023, we extracted counts and age-standardised rates (per 100 000 population) for prevalence, deaths, YLLs, YLDs and DALYs, with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), stratified by sex and age group. Age-standardised rates used the GBD world standard population. We obtained estimates from the GBD 2023 Results Tool16 on 12 October 2025 using the following query parameters: location=‘Sierra Leone’; cause=‘Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias’; measure=‘prevalence’, ‘deaths’, ‘YLLs’, ‘YLDs’, ‘DALYs’; metric=‘number’ and ‘rate’; years=1990–2023; sex=‘male’, ‘female’, ‘both’.

To describe age patterns, we aggregated ages into 65–74 years and 75 years and older, given the concentration of dementia burden in older adults. We focused on age-standardised rather than crude rates to distinguish demographic effects (population growth and ageing) from changes in age-specific epidemiology. No further inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied beyond the standard GBD dementia case definition and modelling framework.

Trend analysis

We summarised temporal trends in age-standardised rates using log-linear regression models of the form log(rate_t)=α+β·year_t, where t indexes calendar year. Annual per cent change (APC) and 95% CIs were computed as (exp(β)−1) 100. Goodness of fit was assessed using R². We report APC for deaths, DALYs, YLLs, YLDs and prevalence. Because our primary interest was in smooth, long-term trends over three decades rather than short-term inflection points, we selected this parsimonious specification instead of join point or age–period–cohort models, which require additional assumptions and would have been difficult to interpret for a single small country. All analyses were implemented in R V.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Decomposition analysis

To assess the drivers of change in dementia deaths, we applied a Das Gupta three-component decomposition, partitioning the difference in deaths between 1990 and 2023 into contributions from population growth, population ageing (changes in the age distribution within older age groups) and changes in age-specific mortality rates.

We report 95% UIs for all GBD 2023 metrics, defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 draws from the posterior distribution generated by the GBD modelling framework. These intervals reflect uncertainty arising from sampling error in input data, stochastic variation in model estimation and model specification. We did not add further uncertainty from our secondary trend and decomposition analyses, which used the published GBD summary estimates.

Ethics and reporting

GBD 2023 summary estimates are publicly available and fully deidentified. This secondary analysis of aggregated data did not require additional ethical approval. We followed the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) recommendations for studies reporting modelled health estimates and documented data extraction and analytic procedures to support reproducibility. A completed GATHER checklist for this study is provided in the online supplemental material.

Results

Overall estimated mortality and DALY burden

GBD 2023 estimated 188 deaths from Alzheimer disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone in 1990 and 474 deaths in 2023. This corresponded to an absolute increase of 286 GBD-estimated deaths and a relative increase of 152% (table 1). The modelled age-standardised death rate increased from 16.3 to 20.7 deaths per 100 000 population, a 26.8% increase (table 1, figure 1). The log-linear APC for the GBD-estimated age-standardised death rate was +1.02% per year (95% CI 0.92 to 1.12; p<0.001; R2=0.93) (table 2).

GBD-estimated DALYs increased from 3703 in 1990 to 7440 in 2023, a 101% increase (table 1). The modelled age-standardised DALY rate increased from 293.7 to 333.2 DALYs per 100 000 population, a 13.4% increase (table 1, figure 1). GBD assigns deaths to Alzheimer disease and other dementias using cause-of-death modelling rather than case-level clinical adjudication. This analysis, therefore, cannot identify the immediate clinical causes of death among people with dementia in Sierra Leone.

Prevalence

The GBD-estimated number of people living with Alzheimer disease and other dementias increased from 5647 in 1990 to 8887 in 2023, a 57% increase (table 1). Over the same period, the modelled age-standardised prevalence rate declined from 430.7 to 404.0 prevalent cases per 100 000 population, a 6.2% reduction (table 1, figure 1). The APC for the modelled age-standardised prevalence rate was −0.18% per year (95% CI −0.21 to −0.16; p<0.001; R2=0.86) (table 2).

Age and sex patterns

In 2023, GBD 2023 estimated that 89% of dementia deaths (421 of 474) and 79% of DALYs occurred among adults aged 75 years or older (figures 2 and 3). The modelled death rate among adults aged 75 years or older was 457 deaths per 100 000 population, compared with 51 deaths per 100 000 among adults aged 65–74 years (figure 3).

Females accounted for 69% of GBD-estimated deaths and 67% of DALYs in 2023 (table 1). The female age-standardised death rate was 24.5 per 100 000 population, compared with 15.3 per 100 000 among males (table 1, figure 1). Similar sex differences were observed for modelled age-standardised DALY rates (figure 1).

Decomposition of mortality change

Between 1990 and 2023, the modelled number of deaths from Alzheimer disease and other dementias increased by 286, from 188 to 474. The decomposition attributed 178 additional estimated deaths (62% of the increase) to changes in age-specific dementia mortality rates, 85 additional deaths (30%) to population growth and 22 additional deaths (8%) to population ageing within older age groups (figure 4). These results are descriptive and do not identify causal drivers of mortality change.

Discussion

Our analysis of GBD 2023 modelled estimates suggests that the absolute burden of Alzheimer disease and other dementias increased in Sierra Leone between 1990 and 2023. Estimated deaths, prevalent cases and DALYs increased. Most estimated deaths and DALYs in 2023 occurred among adults aged 75 years or older. Females had higher modelled age-standardised death and DALY rates than males.

The modelled age-standardised prevalence rate declined slightly, a pattern that differs from broad global trends and requires cautious interpretation. The mortality decomposition suggested that the increase in GBD-estimated deaths was associated mainly with changes in age-specific mortality and population growth, with a smaller contribution from population ageing. The study did not use observed death registration, dementia registry or cohort data. The findings should, therefore, not be interpreted as direct measures of dementia service performance.

The global dementia burden is expected to rise substantially as populations age, with much of the future increase projected in low-income and middle-income countries. Previous GBD dementia analyses have shown large increases in the number of people living with dementia and in dementia-attributable burden, although age-standardised trends vary by location and measure.1 2 Sierra Leone’s GBD-estimated increase in absolute burden is consistent with these demographic pressures. Its slight decline in modelled age-standardised prevalence differs from broad global patterns.

Dementia epidemiology in SSA remains difficult to quantify.8 17 Population-based studies are relatively few,18 diagnostic instruments vary19 20 and underdiagnosis is common.8 17 Limited specialist neurology and geriatric capacity, constrained neuroimaging, fragmented long-term care, and incomplete death certification can affect both observed data and model inputs.21 22 Country-specific GBD estimates are, therefore, useful for planning but should be interpreted carefully, especially for small populations and cause-specific mortality estimates.

Competing mortality may also shape modelled prevalence patterns. In settings where older adults experience high mortality from infectious diseases, cardiovascular disease, stroke, trauma, malnutrition or other comorbid conditions, the duration of survival with dementia may be shorter. This can reduce prevalence even when absolute case numbers rise as the population grows and ages. The present analysis cannot determine whether this mechanism explains the Sierra Leone pattern, but it is a plausible consideration when interpreting modelled prevalence and mortality together.2

GBD cause-of-death models assign deaths to Alzheimer disease and other dementias through a standardised framework, but they do not capture patient-level terminal events. For example, infection, aspiration, malnutrition or stroke that commonly precipitate death in people with dementia. In Sierra Leone, incomplete death certification and under-diagnosis of dementia make it difficult to infer immediate clinical causes of death from GBD outputs alone.2 23

Several non-mutually exclusive explanations could produce the observed pattern. First, shorter postonset survival driven by rising cardiometabolic risks, including hypertension, diabetes, stroke would lower prevalence while increasing dementia-attributed mortality.24 25 Metabolic risks have been linked to rising dementia deaths globally.25 Second, changes in detection, coding or regional covariate borrowing within the GBD model could reclassify cognitive disorders or alter prevalence estimates without a true change in incidence. Third, limited primary surveillance in Sierra Leone means model estimates rely heavily on covariates and regional data, increasing uncertainty and the chance of spurious trends. Hence, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of declining dementia risk or improved dementia control in Sierra Leone. They are better viewed as a hypothesis-generating modelled pattern requiring validation through local epidemiological studies.

Sex differences in burden, higher estimated prevalence in females mirror global patterns and likely reflect a mix of biological susceptibility, longer female survival into older ages and gendered exposures such as educational gaps and lifetime risk profiles.2 15 26 Caregiving and social impacts are also relevant: in many African settings, caregivers face severe financial strain, stigma and scarce formal supports, amplifying the public-health consequences of any rise in dementia mortality.7 27

Finally, our findings align with wider concerns about inequities in neurological care in low and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa. Commentaries highlight that people with dementia often face delayed diagnosis, limited access to specialist evaluation and rehabilitation, and substantial geographic and financial barriers to care.28 29 In this context, the rising estimated dementia mortality in Sierra Leone is likely not only a reflection of underlying disease biology but also of systemic constraints in the health system.

Implications for policy and practice

These findings support using GBD estimates as planning inputs while Sierra Leone strengthens locally generated data. Priority actions include improving dementia surveillance, strengthening civil registration and vital statistics, improving cause-of-death certification and developing population-based ageing and cognitive health studies. These steps would help validate modelled estimates and provide a stronger basis for policy evaluation.

Health-system planning should also consider feasible integration of cognitive assessment into primary care, particularly for older adults with non-communicable diseases, stroke or functional decline.30 Training primary care workers in dementia recognition, improving referral pathways for complex cognitive disorders and supporting caregivers could help address unmet need. These are implications of the estimated burden, not conclusions about the effectiveness or failure of existing Sierra Leone policy.

Given the burden of stroke and cardiovascular risk factors in many African settings, vascular cognitive impairment, poststroke cognitive impairment and mixed dementia may be important contributors to cognitive decline.9 31 32 These phenotypes cannot be distinguished within the GBD cause category used in this analysis. Dementia planning should, therefore, be linked with non-communicable disease, stroke, mental health, healthy ageing, long-term care and caregiver-support strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a country-level profile of GBD 2023 estimates for Alzheimer disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone over more than three decades. The analysis considered multiple burden measures, age and sex patterns, age-standardised rates and a decomposition of mortality change, all of which are relevant for health-system planning.

However, important limitations must be acknowledged. All estimates were derived from GBD 2023 and should be interpreted as modelled estimates rather than observed surveillance data. Sierra Leone has limited primary dementia epidemiology and death registration, and constrained diagnostic infrastructure. GBD estimates may, therefore, depend partly on covariates, regional information sharing and model assumptions. The estimated deaths reported here could not be validated against national case-level mortality records.

The analysis cannot identify the immediate causes of death among people with dementia. It also cannot distinguish Alzheimer disease from vascular, mixed, Lewy body, frontotemporal or other dementia subtypes. The observed rate changes could reflect true changes in disease occurrence, survival, competing mortality, model structure or sparse local data. Underdiagnosis and misclassification may affect model inputs. Population denominators are also modelled. The declining age-standardised prevalence trend may, therefore, reflect model assumptions, covariate patterns, mortality assumptions, competing mortality or limited local data rather than a true decline in dementia risk. The decomposition analysis is descriptive and should not be interpreted causally. Finally, UIs should be considered when interpreting all GBD estimates, especially in data-sparse settings.

Conclusion

GBD 2023 modelled estimates suggest that the absolute burden of Alzheimer disease and other dementias in Sierra Leone increased substantially between 1990 and 2023. Estimated deaths increased while the modelled age-standardised prevalence rate declined slightly. Decomposition suggested that changes in age-specific mortality and population growth contributed to the increase in estimated deaths, but these results are descriptive rather than causal. The findings support stronger dementia surveillance, improved death certification, population-based ageing research and health-system planning for dementia care in Sierra Leone.
