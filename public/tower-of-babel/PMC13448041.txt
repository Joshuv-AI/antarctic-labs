Reconsidering Intraoperative Hip Positioning in Single-Position Lateral Lumbar Interbody Fusion – A Commentary on “Optimal Positioning for Single-Position Lateral Lumbar Interbody Fusion”
Authors: Jiayi Chen
Published: 2026

To the editor,

I read with great interest the recent article by Limthongkul et al. [1] investigating the influence of hip positioning on lumbar lordosis (LL) and spinopelvic parameters during right lateral decubitus positioning for single-position lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF). The authors should be commended for providing a systematic radiographic evaluation of how different hip configurations affect spinal alignment, an issue with clear relevance for intraoperative positioning strategies and surgical outcomes.

However, I have some concerns regarding this study. Paraspinal muscle relaxation induced by general anesthesia may play a critical role in the observed reduction of LL. Previous studies have demonstrated that paraspinal muscle integrity is closely associated with sagittal alignment, and the loss of muscle tone under anesthesia may shift spinal stability from an active to a passive system [2]. This transition can exacerbate the flattening of LL, particularly in the lateral decubitus position, which itself has been shown to reduce lordosis compared to the prone position. Under these conditions, the biomechanical sufficiency of the 30° femoral neck (30FN) position warrants further consideration. While hip positioning may partially modulate spinopelvic alignment, its capacity to compensate for anesthesia-induced muscle relaxation remains uncertain. Therefore, the effectiveness of the 30FN position in preserving LL in anesthetized patients may be limited, and additional strategies (e.g., table flexion or prone positioning) may be required to optimize sagittal alignment.

It should be noted that the proposed positioning strategy may not be universally applicable to all patient populations. In particular, modifications may be required for patients with high-grade spondylolisthesis, severe sagittal imbalance, or a history of previous lumbar fusion. In these cases, structural deformities or reduced spinal flexibility may limit the ability of positional adjustments alone to restore optimal spinopelvic alignment. For instance, patients with severe sagittal imbalance often require substantial correction of LL, which may not be achievable through hip positioning alone. Similarly, prior lumbar fusion may reduce segmental mobility, thereby diminishing the impact of intraoperative positioning on global alignment. Therefore, individualized positioning strategies, potentially combined with additional surgical techniques, should be considered to optimize outcomes in these complex patient groups.

Importantly, intraoperative positioning further compounds this effect. Prior studies on LLIF and lateral decubitus positioning have demonstrated that LL is typically reduced compared to prone positioning, even before considering variations in hip configuration [3,4]. Therefore, the differences observed between hip positions in the present study likely represent relatively small adjustments within an already reduced lordotic baseline. From this perspective, the 30FN position should not be interpreted as a strategy that preserves LL, but rather as one that provides only limited modulation under constrained biomechanical conditions.

Importantly, investigations specifically examining positional effects have reported that both lateral decubitus and supine/prone transitions can significantly alter sagittal alignment, independent of surgical manipulation. These findings suggest that intraoperative lordosis is influenced by a combination of anesthesiainduced muscle relaxation and positional biomechanics, rather than hip configuration alone [5,6].

Overall, this study provides important biomechanical insight into intraoperative alignment management during LLIF. By highlighting the role of moderate unilateral hip flexion in preserving sagittal balance, the findings offer a useful foundation for optimizing surgical positioning and warrant further investigation in clinical settings.
