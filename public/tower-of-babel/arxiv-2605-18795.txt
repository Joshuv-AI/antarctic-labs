HELLoRA: Hot Experts Layer-Level Low-Rank Adaptation for Mixture-of-Experts Models
Jia Wei, Zhonghao Zhang, Ping Chen, Qianyang Li, Yancheng, Pan, Shaoxun Wang, Ziyi Qiu, Longxiang Wang

Abstract
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) dominates parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large language models, yet most variants target dense architectures. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models scale parameters at near-constant per-token compute, and their sparse activation patterns create untapped opportunities for more efficient adaptation. We propose Hot-Experts Layer-level Low-Rank Adaptation (HELLoRA), which attaches LoRA modules only to the most frequently activated experts at each layer. This simple mechanism reduces trainable parameters and adapter-induced FLOPs while improving downstream performance, an effect we attribute to a form of structured regularization that preserves pretrained expert specialization. To stress-test HELLoRA under extreme parameter budgets, we further compose it with LoRI to form HELLoRI, which freezes the up-projection and sparsifies the down-projection. Across three MoE backbones, namely OlMoE-1B-7B, Mixtral-8 × \times 7B, and DeepSeekMoE, and three task families covering mathematical reasoning, code generation, and safety alignment, HELLoRA consistently outperforms PEFT baselines. Across three MoE backbones and three task families, HELLoRA achieves a stronger accuracy–efficiency trade-off than vanilla LoRA. Relative to LoRA, HELLoRA uses only 16.3%, 30.1%, and 23.2% of the trainable parameters on OlMoE, Mixtral-8 × \times 7B, and DeepSeekMoE, respectively, while matching or improving performance on all reported metrics. These results demonstrate that activation-aware adapter placement is an effective and practical route to scaling PEFT for MoE language models.



1
Introduction

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models expand parameter capacity while keeping per-token compute nearly constant
(
Shazeer et al. 2017
;
Yun et al. 2024
)
. They match or surpass dense models of comparable compute across diverse domains
(
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
. Despite this efficiency,
adapting
MoE models to downstream tasks remains costly: the full parameter set is large, and naive application of LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
attaches adapters to all experts, yielding high memory and throughput overhead.

A key property of MoE models is
sparse activation
, meaning that only a small subset of experts is activated for each token. Although load-balancing losses encourage more uniform expert utilization during pre-training
(
Shazeer et al. 2017
)
, expert activation often becomes highly skewed within each layer on a downstream task, as illustrated in Figure
1
. A small number of
hot
experts account for most token processing, whereas a long tail of
cold
experts is rarely activated. The identity of these hot experts also varies across layers and tasks
(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
.

(b)

(b)

This observation suggests a simple design principle, namely
not adapting cold experts
. Attaching LoRA to cold experts wastes parameters on weights that are rarely used for the target task. Worse, it may
hurt
performance by perturbing expert specialization learned during pre-training, thereby injecting gradient noise into parameters whose updates are driven by sparse and unrepresentative token subsets.

We instantiate this idea in
HELLoRA
(Hot Experts Layer-Level Low Rank Adaptation). HELLoRA performs a lightweight warm-up on a small subset of the target data to profile expert activation frequencies in each layer. Based on these statistics, it selects the top-
k
hot experts per layer and inserts LoRA adapters only into those experts. Standard LoRA is applied to attention and gating modules, while cold experts are kept frozen.

HELLoRA offers two complementary advantages. It improves efficiency by avoiding adapter computation for cold experts in both the forward and backward passes. This is in contrast to masking based methods such as LoRI
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, which freeze parameters but still retain forward pass overhead. It also improves regularization by preserving the pretrained specialization of cold experts, thereby reducing cross-task interference. This view is supported by the results in Table
4
, where even
random
expert selection slightly outperforms full LoRA. Activation-aware selection yields an additional gain.

To further reduce the parameter budget, we combine HELLoRA with LoRI
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
and obtain
HELLoRI
. This variant freezes the
\mathbf{A}
matrix and updates only 10% of the entries in
\mathbf{B}
within the hot expert adapters.

Contributions.
We summarize our main contributions as follows.

1.

We identify a
double sparsity
effect in MoE fine-tuning,
where sparse expert activation compounds with the low-rank structure
of LoRA updates, and propose HELLoRA, a layer-level hot-expert
adapter placement strategy that exploits this effect.

2.

We evaluate HELLoRA extensively across three MoE backbones
(OlMoE-1B-7B, Mixtral-8
\times
7B, DeepSeekMoE) and three task
families (math reasoning, code generation, safety alignment),
demonstrating consistent overall gains over both standard PEFT baselines
(LoRA, DoRA, LoRI) and MoE-specific adapter methods (LoRAMoE),
with thorough ablations on selection strategy, layer choice,
warm-up requirements, and hot-expert count.

3.

We show that HELLoRA simultaneously improves accuracy,
parameter efficiency, adapter FLOPs, and wall-clock throughput, a
combination that prior masking-based methods do not deliver.

2
Related Work

LoRA and its variants.

LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
injects trainable low-rank factors into frozen linear layers. Subsequent work refines the factorization along several directions. DoRA
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
decomposes weight updates into separate magnitude and direction components. PiSSA
(
Meng et al. 2024
)
initializes the low-rank factors from the principal singular vectors of the pretrained weight. LoRA-FA
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
freezes the up-projection
\mathbf{A}
with minimal accuracy loss, roughly halving the number of trainable parameters. LoRI
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
goes further by additionally sparsifying
\mathbf{B}
, achieving competitive accuracy with an order-of-magnitude fewer updated parameters. These methods all target dense architectures and do not exploit MoE-specific structure.

Mixture-of-Experts models.

Modern MoE LLMs such as Mixtral
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
, OlMoE
(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
, and DeepSeekMoE
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
employ top-
k
routing with auxiliary load-balancing losses to encourage uniform expert utilization during pre-training. However, when evaluated on specific downstream tasks, per-layer expert usage becomes strongly skewed
(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
, motivating task-aware adapter placement strategies.

PEFT for MoE models.

Several lines of work combine LoRA with MoE structure. Adapter-mixture methods such as LoRAMoE
(
Dou et al. 2024
)
and
AdaMoLE
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
introduce mixtures over LoRA experts, adding routing mechanisms for the adapters themselves. These methods were designed for dense backbones. In contrast, HELLoRA exploits the native expert structure of MoE backbones, as our experiments confirm. Dynamic rank-allocation methods
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
adjust the LoRA rank across experts during training to redistribute adaptation capacity. These approaches either add architectural complexity or require online rank adjustment. In contrast, HELLoRA operates at the placement level, deciding which experts receive adapters before training begins through a simple one-shot profiling step. This design makes HELLoRA orthogonal to both adapter-mixture and rank-allocation strategies, and it can in principle be composed with either family of methods.

3
Method

3.1
Preliminaries

LoRA.

Given a pretrained weight
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{out}}}
, LoRA parameterizes the update as
\Delta=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
with
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times r}
,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{out}}}
, and
r\ll\min(d_{\text{in}},d_{\text{out}})
:

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{W}+\Delta)=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{x}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}.

(1)

MoE load-balancing.

An MoE layer contains
N_{E}
experts
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{E}}
with top-
k
routing. The auxiliary load-balancing loss
(
Shazeer et al. 2017
)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}=N_{E}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{E}}f_{i}\cdot P_{i}

(2)

encourages uniform expert usage during pre-training. Here
f_{i}
is the token fraction assigned to expert
E_{i}
and
P_{i}
is the mean routing probability. Crucially, widely-used implementations (OlMoE, Mixtral) compute
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}
over concatenated router logits across all layers, yielding approximately balanced
global
usage but potentially imbalanced
per-layer
usage on specific tasks.

3.2
HELLoRA: Hot-Experts Layer-Level Adapter Placement

As shown in Figure
2
, HELLoRA consists of two stages: (1) a lightweight profiling stage to identify hot experts, and (2) standard LoRA fine-tuning with adapters placed only on hot experts.

Stage 1: Layer-level hot-expert profiling.

We run a short LoRA warm-up on a random
p\%
sample of the target training data (default
p=10
). During this warm-up, we log the activation count
c_{i}^{(\ell)}
of each expert
i
at each MoE layer
\ell
. We then sort experts by
c_{i}^{(\ell)}
within each layer and select the top-
k
as
hot experts
. The output is a per-layer hot-expert list
\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}=\{i:c_{i}^{(\ell)}\text{ is among the top-}k\}
. Algorithm
1
summarizes the full procedure.

Stage 2: Selective adapter attachment.

We attach LoRA modules to all attention projections, the gating network, and only the hot experts in
\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}
at each MoE layer
\ell
. Cold experts remain fully frozen. The base model is frozen throughout and only the adapter parameters are trained.

Why hot experts?

We provide two complementary perspectives on why HELLoRA improves over full LoRA.

Efficiency.
Removing adapters from cold experts eliminates their adapter forward and backward passes entirely. Unlike masking methods such as LoRI, which freeze parameters but retain them in the computational graph, HELLoRA reduces actual FLOPs and memory traffic. The adapter FLOPs scale with
|\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}|
rather than
N_{E}
.

Regularization.
Pre-trained MoE models embed task-relevant knowledge in expert specialization. Adapting cold experts that are rarely activated for the target task injects noisy gradients computed from sparse, unrepresentative token subsets. Freezing cold experts preserves their pretrained function, acting as a form of structured regularization. Empirically, even random expert selection, which also restricts scope, slightly improves over full LoRA, while activation-aware selection yields substantially larger gains (Section
4.4.1
).

3.3
HELLoRI: Extreme Parameter Budgets

To explore the lower bound of trainable parameters, we compose HELLoRA with LoRI
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. HELLoRI freezes
\mathbf{A}
(initialized randomly and fixed) and updates only the top 10% of
\mathbf{B}
’s entries by absolute value:

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{x}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\odot\mathbf{M}),

(3)

where
\mathbf{M}
is a binary mask selecting the top-10% entries. This yields
\sim
0.03% trainable parameters relative to full fine-tuning.

5
Analysis and Discussion

Why does adapting cold experts hurt?

Cold experts are by definition rarely activated for the target task.
When LoRA is attached to a cold expert, its adapter receives gradient
updates from a sparse and unrepresentative subset of tokens. These noisy
updates can perturb the expert’s pretrained specialization, which may be
valuable for other capabilities, without contributing meaningfully to the
target task. In effect, adapting cold experts introduces optimization noise
while diluting pretrained knowledge. The layer-level hot-expert selection
in HELLoRA avoids this by concentrating adaptation capacity where the
model already directs its compute.

Variation across backbones.
The accuracy advantage of HELLoRA
varies with the activation sparsity of the backbone, with the largest gain on OlMoE (3.12 points), moderate gain on Mixtral (0.55 points), and modest gain on DeepSeekMoE (0.39 points). DeepSeekMoE’s shared experts absorb the most dominant activation patterns and are adapted under both methods, leaving less headroom for selective placement. Even in this setting, HELLoRA matches LoRA’s accuracy at roughly four times fewer trainable parameters, indicating that hot-expert selection remains an effective parameter-efficiency strategy across architectures.

Connection to structured pruning.
HELLoRA can be viewed as
structured pruning of the adapter space, with hot experts as the
"winning tickets" of adaptation in the sense of the lottery ticket
hypothesis
(
Frankle & Carlin 2019
)
. The random selection baseline
in Table
4
supports this view.

Practical guidelines.
For standard MoE architectures, setting

k
equal to the per-step activation count is a reliable starting point.
Architectures with shared experts require a larger
k
(roughly twice
the routed count) because shared experts flatten the routed activation
distribution. We recommend a 10% warm-up fraction and adapters on both
attention and gating components.

6
Limitations

HELLoRA has two main limitations. The warm-up stage depends on the
representativeness of the sampled data. If the warm-up distribution
diverges from the full training distribution, hot-expert identification
may be inaccurate. Our experiments show high stability with 10% of the
data (Section
4.4
), but extreme distribution shift could
degrade selection quality. In addition, HELLoRA assumes that a small
subset of experts carries most of the task-relevant signal. For tasks
requiring broad expert coverage, restricting adaptation to
k
experts
per layer may underfit. Adaptive strategies that adjust
k
per layer
based on activation entropy are a promising direction for future work.

7
Broader Impact

By reducing the cost of adapting MoE language models, HELLoRA lowers the barrier to fine-tuning capable LLMs for specialized tasks, with positive implications for resource-constrained labs and organizations. However, fine-tuning for harmful applications, including the generation of unsafe, deceptive, or abusive content, also becomes cheaper and more
accessible. Practitioners should combine HELLoRA with appropriate safety measures such as content filtering and alignment verification when deploying adapted models. Our safety alignment experiments show that HELLoRA is compatible with safety fine-tuning, which may partially mitigate this risk.

8
Conclusion

We introduced HELLoRA, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for MoE
models that places LoRA adapters only on the most frequently activated
experts at each layer. This simple design simultaneously reduces trainable
parameters by roughly six times relative to LoRA, lowers adapter-induced
FLOPs by 33 to 39%, increases training throughput by approximately
1.9 times, and improves downstream accuracy, a combination that prior
methods do not achieve. Across three MoE backbones and three task families,
HELLoRA consistently outperforms LoRA, DoRA, and LoRI variants.
Composing HELLoRA with LoRI yields HELLoRI, which matches LoRA
accuracy while training only 0.7% of its trainable parameters. These
results establish activation-aware adapter placement as a practical and
effective principle for scaling PEFT to large MoE language models.

Interpretation.

We interpret these results as evidence of
a structural mismatch. On a dense backbone, LoRAMoE’s adapter-level
routing introduces MoE-style specialization that did not previously
exist. On an MoE backbone, this specialization is redundant with the
native router, and the two routing layers must co-adapt through training.
Increasing adapter rank amplifies the interference between the two
routers rather than resolving it, which leads to degraded accuracy at
higher capacity. HELLoRA avoids this issue by operating at the placement
level. It selects which native experts receive adapters and leaves the
backbone’s routing intact. This design aligns adaptation with the
backbone’s existing sparse structure rather than adding a competing
structure on top.
