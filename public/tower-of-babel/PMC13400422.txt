Cholesterol crystal formation and embolism in the central nervous system and their potential role in Alzheimer's disease☆
Authors: Ali Houmani, F.N.U. Parul, Moiz Saeed, Ryan Skowronek, Venkat Katkoori, Irina A. Pikuleva, Maria B. Grant, Julia V. Busik, Majid Yavari, George S. Abela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The brain and nervous tissues are very rich in cholesterol with local sterol biosynthesis being the primary source. The production of cholesterol in the brain may also lead to cholesterol crystal formation that in turn can cause inflammation which potentially can contribute to Alzheimer's disease. In this review we provide examples of cholesterol crystals (CCs) in human brains of Alzheimer patients. Moreover, cholesterol in the arterial circulation is also a major contributor to brain pathology. Specifically, CCs embolization released during plaque rupture may lead to reduced cognitive function, transient ischemic attacks, and ischemic strokes. Ischemic strokes can be caused by either thrombotic emboli from atrial fibrillation or CC emboli and platelets from ruptured plaques in the carotid arteries and aortic arch. CC emboli can cause blood flow obstruction, localized inflammation, and vasospasm of the local arterial vasculature, all contributing to ischemia and brain injury. Prevention and treatment of CC emboli with statins and aspirin has been found to be effective. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that both statins and aspirin can dissolve CCs. Thus, preventing formation and/or dissolving CCs could potentially be effective in reducing end organ injury induced by both CCs formation and emboli. Although further investigation with human studies remain lacking, recent studies have demonstrated prevention of thrombus formation induced by CCs may also provide another approach to inhibiting end-organ ischemic injury.

Introduction

The two major types of stroke are ischemic (87%) and hemorrhagic (13%) [1]. The link between stroke subtypes and cholesterol levels has been well documented, with high levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLc) being associated with ischemic stroke and low LDLc levels (<70 mg/dL) more often observed in hemorrhagic stroke [2]. Thus, LDLc seems to have a critical role in the process of stroke as is known for myocardial infarction. This relationship has been well illustrated in a variety of clinical trials whereby lowering of LDLc levels using statins as well as other agents has been effective not only in significantly lowering the incidence of myocardial infarctions but strokes as well [3]. However, these strokes are primarily ischemic in origin. The mechanism of ischemic stroke is related primarily to embolic events that lead to the obstruction of blood flow to end organ sites. This process is most often the result of atherosclerotic plaque rupture usually from carotid or vertebral arteries. However, in addition to cholesterol crystal emboli it is important to recognize that cholesterol crystals (CCs) can also form within the brain matrix based on the extensive amount of cholesterol produced in that environment.

Plaque rupture is typically associated with fissuring or disruption of the fibrous cap in atheromatous lesions characterized by a large lipid core, a thin fibrous cap, and inflammatory cell infiltration with loss of smooth muscle cell support [4]. Although inflammation is a critical part of plaque destabilization, it does not clearly explain the subsequent rupture events. However, more recent evidence suggests that the CC formation within the plaque's lipid pool leads to crystal growth with rapid volume expansion within the core [5]. The expansion of the plaque core can thin down the fibrous cap by stretching while the sharp-tipped edges of the CCs can puncture and rupture the fibrous cap. Moreover, the process of crystallization has been found to depend on the local physicochemical milieu of the plaque [5], [6].

Ex vivo studies have demonstrated that cholesterol crystallization is promoted by hydration of the cholesterol molecule to the monohydrate form, saturation of free cholesterol, an alkaline pH, and a reduction in ambient temperature even by 1 or 2 °C. Inflammation within the plaque plays an important role in destabilization. The formation of CCs triggers the innate immune system to view CCs as foreign bodies and hence a target for attack. In fact, CCs have been found to activate the pre-IL-1β via NLRP3 inflammasome [7]. Furthermore, the crystals were also found to induce TNF and MIP2 secretion as well as activation of the complement cascade as well [8]. This process can lead to autolysis and digestive processes within the plaque weakening the overall structure. The site of the plaque rupture with exposure of the underlying thromboplastin and collagenous elements attract platelet deposition and thrombus formation that can lead to arterial occlusion and tissue infarction [9].

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a common and progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline and neuropathological hallmarks including amyloid-β plaques and tau pathology. It is distinguished from vascular dementia by the latter's stepwise progression, burden of ischemic or hemorrhagic parenchymal damage, and prominent subcortical dysfunction. Although these mechanisms are central to AD, CCs formation triggering inflammation and causing microvascular injury may represent an additional contributory pathway to the disease process [10].

Cholesterol crystal emboli

Release of CCs into the circulation following plaque rupture initiates several pathophysiological effects [11] (Fig. 2). 1) The direct contact of sharp-tipped CCs with the endothelium in the circulation has been shown to scrape the endothelial cell lining leading to disruption and abnormal function provoking arterial vasospasm [11], [12]. In fact, during a stroke vasospasm can worsen the clinical outcome [13], [14]. 2) The other major effect seen with CC emboli is direct tissue injury by presence of CCs lodged in the matrix of the organ tissue. In a rat model injected CCs that were embolized to the brain via the carotid artery were found to develop local inflammation and reduced cognitive function [15]. Similarly, CC emboli into the thigh muscle of rabbits were found to trigger an inflammatory response as detected by PET scanning and tissue histology [16]. 3) A large bolus of CCs released during plaque rupture can cause arteriolar and capillary obstruction that can further aggravate tissue ischemia and anoxic injury [17], [18].Fig. 2Schematic of multiple steps of crystal related arterial injury: 1) Plaque rupture with tethered edges of torn fibrous cap. Hemorrhagic stroke

A hemorrhagic stroke associated with CC emboli may be a rare occurrence, as CC emboli typically cause ischemic strokes rather than bleeding; however, in certain situations where CC emboli significantly damage the blood vessel wall, it can lead to small, localized areas of microhemorrhages. CCs perforating microvasculature have been seen in diabetic retinopathy as well as within atherosclerotic plaques [5], [19], [20]. Furthermore, intracranial arteries are more fragile with one elastic lamina compared to similar medium size arteries such as the coronaries that have two elastic laminas while the internal mammary arteries have up to twelve elastic laminas [21]. Thus, intracranial arteries are more prone to perforation and hemorrhage compared to the systemic arteries. Perforation of arteries by CCs have been reported in the fibrous capsule of atherosclerotic plaques during myocardial infarction as well as shearing of the vasa vasorum within the plaque [5], [20]. The ability of CCs to perforate fibrous tissue has also been confirmed in basic biomechanical experiments [5], [22] and dissolving CCs have shown to leave behind open holes in the arterial wall [23] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Holes in arterial surface left by Dissolving Cholesterol Crystals by SEM. Cryptogenic stroke

It is estimated that about one third of ischemic strokes are cryptogenic, or a stroke of unknown cause. Most cryptogenic strokes are thought to be embolic [24] with atrial fibrillation being a common source for thromboembolism. However, another suspected source for cryptogenic stroke is a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque commonly from the aortic arch [24], [25]. Plaque rupture at that site can lead to release of CCs and platelets to the brain. CC emboli were first described by Hollenhorst who injected CCs into the carotid arteries of simian mammals and demonstrated CCs in the retinal arteries and their significance in cerebrovascular events [26], [27].

As mentioned, growing sharp tipped CCs forming within the plaque core can rupture the fibrous cap and then enter the bloodstream. This process in unstable carotid plaques can cause cerebrovascular events even with plaques resulting in less than 70% stenosis [28]. Such plaques are often associated with CCs, that embolize to the retinal artery branches and are visible by fundoscopy and retinal angiography (Fig. 4). Cholesterol homeostasis in the brain

The brain has the richest lipid content (primarily cholesterol) of all the organs in the human body. Lipids constitute a major component of oligodendrocytes which myelinate the axons of the brain [29]. Thus, CCs may form spontaneously within the brain matrix as in atheromatous plaques and this has been observed in brains of Alzheimer patients [30] (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Cholesterol is a crucial component of the brain, synthesized locally within glial cells and to a lesser extent in neurons. This de novo synthesis meets the high demands of different central nervous system (CNS) regions, each having distinct rates of production to fulfill their specific needs [32], [33], [34]. Cholesterol biosynthesis occurs in the central nervous system primarily via two pathways: the Bloch pathway in astrocytes, with desmosterol as the main precursor, and the Kandutsch-Russel pathway in neurons, using 7-dehydrocholesterol or lanosterol as intermediates [35]. Also, cholesterol synthesis in neurons is further activated by the brain-derived neurotrophic factor.

Cholesterol synthesis is tightly regulated by sterol-regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs), specifically SREBP2, which resides in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane. When cellular cholesterol levels drop, the SREBP-SCAP (SREBP cleavage-activating protein) complex relocates to the Golgi apparatus, enabling the activation of SREBP2 that triggers the transcription of genes involved in cholesterol synthesis, while also downregulating LRP1 (low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1) to increase cholesterol availability for cells [35], [36], [37], [38]. Furthermore, cholesterol destined for intracellular storage is esterified by ACAT1 (acyl-coenzyme A: cholesterol acyltransferase-1) in the endoplasmic reticulum, resulting in the formation of lipid droplets [39], [40], [41]. The turnover rate of cholesterol in the brain is notably slow, with a long half-life up to 5 years [42].

Due to the impermeability of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to cholesterol, the CNS relies on specialized mechanisms to manage cholesterol excretion to prevent accumulation. Key excretory pathways include the conversion of cholesterol into oxysterols like 24S-hydroxycholesterol and 27-hydroxycholesterol by the P450 enzymes. These oxysterols can cross the BBB and regulate cholesterol homeostasis through their interaction with Liver X Receptors (LXRs), which controls the synthesis of ApoE and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) that are involved in lipid transport [34], [38], [42], [43], [44], [45]. Additionally, ABC transporters play a crucial role in loading cholesterol onto ApoE for efflux, particularly in states of neurodegeneration and demyelination [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52].

The lipoprotein ApoE is integral to CNS cholesterol transport, supporting neuronal and glial cell membrane repair and synaptic maintenance. There are three alleles of the APOE gene (APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4) each influencing cholesterol metabolism differently. The APOE4 allele is notably linked to increased Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk, primarily due to its reduced ability to transport lipids, increased degradation, and the generation of neurotoxic fragments that impair synaptic function [49], [51]. ApoE-cholesterol complexes interact with LDL receptors (i.e. LRP1), facilitating cholesterol uptake for cellular processes, including membrane synthesis and synaptic remodeling [34], [49], [50].

Alterations in cholesterol transport and metabolism are associated with neurodegenerative disorders such as the loss of LRP1 in neurons disrupting brain lipid homeostasis and leading to significant neurodegenerative outcomes [47], [49]. This underscores the importance of efficient cholesterol handling in maintaining CNS function and preventing disease.

Potential role of CCs in Alzheimer's disease

One potential mechanism by which CCs may contribute to neurodegeneration is through embolization from proximal atherosclerotic sources into the cerebral circulation. Rapp et al. demonstrated that microemboli of CCs disrupt the BBB, induce local neuroinflammation, and lead to measurable cognitive impairment in an experimental model [15]. These findings suggest a potential mechanistic link between CC embolization and functional neurological decline. In humans, pathological and clinical evidence indicates that aortogenic embolization represents a substantial contributor to ischemic brain injury and likely serves as an underrecognized source of recurrent cerebral microvascular insults [18], [53]. Together, these observations support the concept that repeated or cumulative embolization of CCs may contribute not only to overt stroke, but also to chronic microvascular injury and progressive cognitive dysfunction, consistent with CCs observed traversing the BBB (Fig. 5A,B).

In addition to embolic delivery, CCs may also form locally within the brain parenchyma itself, driven by the uniquely high cholesterol content of the central nervous system and its dependence on tightly regulated intracellular trafficking pathways [32], [33]. The brain relies almost entirely on in situ cholesterol synthesis and recycling, with apolipoprotein E (ApoE) serving as the principal lipoprotein mediator of cholesterol transport between glial cells and neurons [34], [35]. In the setting of ApoE dysfunction—particularly in APOE4-associated impaired lipid transport—cholesterol efflux and redistribution become inefficient, promoting intracellular cholesterol accumulation and local supersaturation [45], [49]. Moreover, the amyloid precursor protein (APP) undergoes sequential cleavage by alpha- or beta- then gamma-secretase enzymes, which reside in lipid rafts with their activity becoming accelerated in the presence of cholesterol [54]. In the setting of APP mutations of familial early-onset AD, APP and beta-secretase are colocalized within endosome-lysosomes, resulting in a higher proportion of beta-amyloid [54]. Under such conditions, cholesterol can undergo phase transition into its crystalline form, analogous to processes observed in atherosclerotic plaques [5], [6]. These provide a biologically plausible explanation for the abundant locally formed cholesterol crystals observed in Alzheimer brain tissue (Fig. 5C and D) [30]. Other disorders of cholesterol trafficking and metabolism can lead to a similar accumulation of cholesterol and symptoms, such as with dysfunctional NPC1 or NPC2 in Niemann-Pick Disease (colloquially referred to as “Childhood Alzheimer disease”), in which there is cholesterol-dependent redistribution of APP into lipid rafts and enhanced activity of beta- and gamma-secretase enzymes [55], [56].

Taken together, these observations suggest a dual-mechanism model in which CCs may contribute to AD pathogenesis through both (1) vascular embolization from unstable atherosclerotic plaques, causing BBB disruption, microvascular obstruction, and cumulative ischemic and inflammatory injury, and (2) local intraparenchymal crystal formation driven by disordered CNS cholesterol homeostasis and ApoE-dependent lipid trafficking failure. In either case, CCs act as biologically active mediators of mechanical injury and inflammation, providing a plausible link between microvascular dysfunction, chronic neuroinflammation, and progressive neurodegeneration that complements classical amyloid- and tau-based mechanisms.

Although supersaturation thresholds for CCs in the brain remain unknown reports of cholesterol dysregulation have been shown to interrupt remyelination of the Schwann sheaths by cholesterol build up leading to CCs formation that triggers inflammation that impedes nerve impulses [57]. Furthermore, the involvement of CCs must be viewed in the context of current knowledge about AD's pathogenesis, which includes the deposition of amyloid-β, neurofibrillary tangles, and the role of lipids in influencing neuronal behavior. Instead of being a key driver, the role of CCs could be complementary by inducing vascular damage and inflammation.

Detection of unstable carotid plaques

Unstable carotid plaques can release CCs before symptoms may be detected. Use of transcranial Doppler (TCD) for embolic high-intensity transient signals (HITS) or a ping by Doppler suggests an unstable plaque with increased risk for stroke [28]. More recently, an innovative approach was proposed by Abela et al. using simultaneous TCD and carotid duplex to identify unstable carotid plaques when applying a gentle pressure by the ultrasound probe as is the standard use which in turn releases CCs. The crystals are then detected as HITS or a ping registered by TCD while scanning the middle cerebral artery [28]. Moreover, even a single crystal can trigger a detective ‘ping’ on the TCD. Thus, plaques that release CCs are more likely to rupture and send a large shower of CCs to the brain.

Treatment options and emerging therapies

Effective management of the CCs requires a multifaceted approach targeting cholesterol load, inflammation, and crystal-induced injury. Therapeutic strategies have primarily been investigated in the context of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, but emerging evidence supports their relevance in the neurodegenerative disorders such as AD.

Lipid Lowering therapies: Several therapeutic options are available to reduce LDL cholesterol. Statins remain the first-line agents, thereby limiting cholesterol supersaturation and CC formation within vascular plaques. In the CNS, statins exert their effects through multiple mechanisms, including direct inhibition of crystal uptake by macrophages and microglia, prevention of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent IL-1β release, and reduction of 24S-hydroxycholesterol levels by 21.4% suggesting a markedly reduced cholesterol production in the brain [58], [59], [60]. They also suppress crystal-induced neuroinflammation by downregulating proinflammatory mediators and limiting microglial activation. Additionally, statins have pleiotropic neuroprotective effects, such as enhancing nitric oxide bioavailability, improving cerebral blood flow, exerting antioxidant activity, preserving blood–brain barrier integrity, and modulating amyloid-beta production—together reducing metabolic conditions that favor CC formation [61], [62], [63], [63], [65], [66]. Specifically, in patients with diabetes the BBB becomes dysfunctional thus allowing for greater interaction with systemic conditions [67].

Anti-inflammatory Approaches: CCs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting IL-1β release and local inflammation [7]. In the CANTOS trial, IL-1β blockade with canakinumab significantly reduced cardiovascular events despite no change in lipid levels [65]. Although crystal-triggered inflammation is most likely in atherosclerotic plaques, other sources of inflammation may also be contributors via other pathways that may be relevant to neuroinflammatory conditions such as AD but that will require further investigation.

Cholesterol Crystal Dissolution and Efflux Enhancement: Emerging direct anti-crystal approaches seek to dissolve or mobilize cholesterol crystals themselves. Cyclodextrins, especially 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD), statins, aspirin and colchicine have all been shown to dissolve CCs and some activating liver X receptors (LXRs), enhancing cholesterol efflux via ABCA1 and ABCG1 transporters [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73], [74]. HDL is the only biological molecule that has been shown to dissolve CCs and has the ability to enhance reverse cholesterol transport ABCA1/ABCG1 that can prevent CC formation [74], [75].

Antiplatelet and Fibrinolytic therapies: In animal models of CCs embolism, fibrinolytic agents effectively prevented arterial occlusion and organ injury where administration of urokinase within 3 h of cholesterol crystal injection prevented arterial occlusions, glomerular filtration rate loss, and kidney infarction in mice [76]. Intravenous, Glu-plasminogen given 4 h after crystal embolism attenuated thrombotic angiopathy, acute kidney injury, and ischemic necrosis in a dose-dependent manner without causing bleeding complication [76]. Although these findings haven't been tested in human studies or in the context of vascular insults in CNS (i.e Stroke), they indicate a potential role of fibrinolytic therapy in the CNS related complications of CC embolization but additional studies are needed to further establish its role in clinical practice.

Future directions

While these therapeutic approaches demonstrate meaningful advances in the management of cholesterol crystallization and vascular disease, their application in the context of neurodegeneration—particularly AD—remains largely unexplored. Although lowering systemic cholesterol, modulating inflammation, and enhancing efflux pathways can all theoretically reduce the likelihood of CC accumulation in neural tissue, no strategy has yet been validated as effective in preventing or dissolving cholesterol crystals within the brain. Furthermore, previously reported literature has emphasized on the role of various cholesterol modifying strategies including ACAT inhibitors, Oxysterol- targeting strategies on modulating liver X receptor (LXR) pathways, APOE- directed interventions in modulating various lipids and proinflammatory pathways, and the subsequent beneficial impact of appropriately timing these interventions in presymptomatic to early AD phases [10], [77], [78], [79], [80], [81] However, none of these reported strategies are targeted specifically to cholesterol crystals and their impact on the pathogenesis. This represents a critical gap and the need to extend cholesterol crystallization research beyond vascular biology into neurodegenerative disease.

Limitations

Several limitations in this report can be considered: 1. The histopathological evidence of CCs in AD is preliminary that will require controlled studies; 2. The mechanisms described are derived from atherosclerosis and may not be directly applicable to the cerebral milieu; 3. CCs might represent a secondary phenomenon in AD. However, despite these limitations, the presence of CCs in the context of AD is an unexplored area that has potential to shed light on the role of cholesterol in the brain.

Conclusions

The role of cholesterol in the brain and CNS is complex and highly regulated by various metabolic pathways. However, alteration of cholesterol metabolism can lead to cholesterol saturation with CC formation that can disrupt communication between neurons leading to cognitive dysfunction. CCs can result in both mechanical injury to adjacent tissues including the arterial wall as well as triggering inflammation that can further exacerbate tissue injury. Two sources of CCs may be contributing, CC embolizing from arterial plaques to the brain and local formation of CCs within the brain matrix that can damage Schwann sheaths needed for neural communication. Although statins are known to stabilize atherosclerotic plaques in arteries their role in the CNS has yet to be elucidated. In conclusion, the role of cholesterol in the brain is vital for normal neuronal function yet its excess may lead to CC formation causing neural cell injury leading to clinical cognitive impairment.
