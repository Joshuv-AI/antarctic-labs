Predicting Scale-Up of Metal-Organic Framework Syntheses with Large Language Models
Peter Walther, Hongrui Sheng, Xinxin Liu, Bin Feng, Reid Coyle, Xinhua Yan, Kyle Smith, Harrison Kayal, Shyam Chand Pal, Zhiling Zheng

Abstract
Scalable synthesis remains the gate between MOF discovery and industrial deployment, as scale-up know-how is fragmented across disparate reports. We introduce ESU-MOF, a literature-mined dataset and a positive-unlabeled learning strategy that fine-tunes large language models to predict scalability potential with 91.4% accuracy, enabling rapid data-driven triage for industrial MOF discovery.

Results and Discussion

In our workflow, we combined LLMs with PU learning to construct a literature-scale workflow for scalable synthesis prediction, ultimately bridging the critical gap between laboratory discovery and industrial manufacturing. At the onset of this study, we reasoned that the literature already contains the ingredients for such a model, although they are scattered in forms that are difficult to unify. Many MOFs have been reported both as initial small-scale solvothermal crystallizations and, later, as gram-scale or kilogram-scale preparations under related or modified conditions. Conceptually, these linked reports constitute an apprenticeship archive of synthesis scale-up.

To this regard, we assembled two literature pools from Web of Science to reflect the asymmetric nature of scale-up evidence. A possible-positive pool, denoted P, was collected using the search keywords “scale-up”, “gram-scale”, “kilogram”, and “pilot” in title or abstracts, under “metal-organic framework” peer-reviewed literatures between 1995 and 2026, intentionally with broad scope so that relevant protocols would not be lost at the search stage; this search yielded 117 candidate paper groups. An unlabeled pool, denoted U, was collected using “metal-organic framework” and “synthesis” with the same year range, and was then restricted to solvothermal syntheses containing one principal metal source and one principal linker, following the method established in our earlier MOF text-mining study

For the PU correction, we adopted the classical perspective that the observed positive label in the literature is a biased sampling of true positives. Consequently, the raw probabilities output by the model may inherently underestimate a protocol’s true scalability, necessitating a mathematical calibration. Operationally, we obtained the model’s raw score as the token-level probability of emitting label P, denoted
q(x)
, derived from log probabilities of the actual output token for an input protocol
x
. In practice, we then estimated
c
, the mean of
q(x)
over validation positives
\mathrm{P_{s}}
in the validation gold set, and this factor was computed to be 0.837. This constant implies that while the labelled literature captures the majority of scalable protocols, approximately 16% of genuinely scalable MOFs remain undocumented and hidden within the unlabelled corpus. Because industrial screening is fundamentally a ranking problem, we also apply a calibration layer via Platt scaling
[
33
]
on the gold validation set to produce a final score whose threshold may be selected for a desired trade-off between missed opportunities and false leads, and the resulting validation threshold was determined to be 0.39.

Upon the model fine-tuning, the first evaluation was designed to test whether the model can distinguish explicit scale-up protocols from unlikely ones under controlled conditions. On the gold benchmark, which compares
\mathrm{P_{s}}
against N, the validation set contained 65 protocols and the test set 73 protocols. Using the raw observed-positive score alone, the balanced accuracy was 82.4%. After PU correction and calibration, balanced accuracy increased to 91.4%, with a F1 score of 93.2% and Matthews correlation coefficient
[
26
,
4
]
of 82.8%, while ROC-AUC reached 95.8% (Fig. 2 (a); Supporting Information, Section S4.1), which shed light on a key point that the remaining errors are limited and balanced rather than concentrated in one failure mode. Bootstrap analysis further supported the robustness of this performance, with the 95% confidence interval for balanced accuracy spanning 83.7 to 97.2% and that for F1 spanning 87.4 to 97.9%.

A more demanding and, in our view, more useful test asks whether the model can surface promising small-scale protocols from the large body of ordinary literature where scalability is not explicitly declared. For this reason, we evaluated deployment performance on
\mathrm{P_{a}}
versus U. The validation deployment set contained 441 protocols, whereas the held-out test deployment set contained 367 protocols composed of 43 ancillary positives and 324 unlabeled candidates. On this test, the final calibrated model achieved an ROC-AUC of 94.5%. Additionally, precision at the top of the ranking was strong, reaching 80.0% at 10. At the level of held-out papers, which more closely resembles how a researcher screens a family of related syntheses, the model achieved a top-3 hit rate of 88.9% across 9 test papers. It is envisioned that such a ranking mode is especially valuable for industrial triage, where one rarely needs a model to certify every protocol; rather, one needs it to move the most plausible candidates to the front of the queue.

We next asked whether fine-tuning truly adds chemical discrimination beyond that available in a general-purpose model (Fig. 3). On the gold test benchmark, the balanced accuracy of fine-tuned model (91.4%) is significantly higher than those of the base models at 78.5% and 77.6%, respectively. On the more practically relevant deployment test, fine-tuning widened the margin further as average precision rose to 65.8%, whereas the base model only reached 28.2 to 28.7%. Even at the paper level, where untuned models retained some within-document ranking ability, the fine-tuned model still led with a mean average precision of 72.2% and a top-1 hit rate of 77.8%, illustrating high utility for down-selection in an industrial discovery pipeline.

Notably, this predictive power also substantially eclipses traditional machine learning baselines (Supporting Information, Section S4.1), which struggle to capture the nuanced, contextual heuristics of reaction conditions without extensive manual feature engineering. Nevertheless, the untuned models did preserve partial ranking structure within some papers, which suggests that chemistry language models already encode fragments of synthetic plausibility, yet without the calibration needed to separate a merely feasible solvothermal recipe from one with credible scalability. Thus, it provided evidence that fine-tuning on a literature-derived ESU-MOF dataset containing experimental knowledge enables the LLM to organize synthesis context into a much sharper ranking of industrially relevant candidates.

Importantly, these results should be interpreted against the reality that scalability is not a single property of a framework but a property of a protocol, and hence the model is assessing procedural plausibility rather than intrinsic crystallographic “goodness.” As supported by our results, often, scalability is written into solvent choice, reaction severity, and operational simplicity. Water-based or benign solvent systems, moderate reaction temperatures, shorter durations, and the absence of complex modulators can correlate with practical scale-up routes
[
3
,
31
,
38
]
. Likewise, from engineering point of view solvent burden, long solvothermal times, and workup intensity can be barriers to sustainable manufacturing, which reinforces the idea that these fields act as weak yet learnable proxies for process viability
[
32
]
.

In this study, it can be demonstrated that such scalability intuition, expressed neither as a single heuristic nor as a hand-built descriptor set, can become a learned judgment grounded in literature-scale experimental data. To this regard, the present study also sheds light on a broader role for large language models in materials chemistry, wherein they do more than extract facts and instead help estimate whether a new material can move beyond discovery into practical accessibility. Nevertheless, it should also be acknowledged that a limitation of the present workflow is that the input precursors is limited to one in our dataset, therefore it is unable to predict the scalability of multivariate MOFs that contain more than one metals or linkers
[
17
]
. Besides, the set of confirmed successful scale-up protocols remains only in the hundreds, and thus the positive class, while informative, is still modest relative to the breadth of MOF chemistry. It is envisioned that, as more gram-scale, kilogram-scale, and pilot-oriented procedures appear in the literature, the ESU-MOF dataset should become more chemically diverse and more sharply calibrated across families of metals, linkers, and solvent systems.

3
Conclusion

In summary, we show that scalability prediction for MOFs can be formulated as a literature-grounded positive-unlabeled learning problem and addressed effectively with LLMs. By constructing the ESU-MOF dataset and training on small-scale synthesis context, we obtained a model that identifies scalable MOF candidates with strong gold-benchmark accuracy (91.4%) and, more importantly, high deployment ranking power. Conceptually, the study suggests that synthesis procedures contain latent information about future manufacturability well before industrial development begins. It is therefore conceivable that, when a new MOF is discovered, one may already estimate whether its path toward practical use is narrow or promising. Besides, for existing reported MOF protocols, this workflow can potentially identify overlooked ones that contain high scale-up potential and thus commercial interest. In this regard, LLM prediction of MOF scalability may become part of how the field decides which discoveries are truly ready to move from the laboratory toward commercial and industrial realization.

S2
Workflow Implementation Details

The workflow consists of seven sequential Steps.
In this section we provide details of each step with reference to the source code modules.
All data and codes are available at
https://github.com/zzhenglab/MOF-Scaleup/

S2.1
PDF Text Extraction

Full-text PDFs (main manuscripts and supporting information) are parsed into plain text using
pypdf
.
As most documents exceed the usable context length of the extraction LLM,
we apply a
keyword-guided truncation
scheme to retain passages in synthesis conditions and scale-up details.

The extractor uses a curated set of domain-specific keywords
(e.g., “solvothermal”, “autoclave”, “gram-scale”, “metal”, “linker”, “modulator”, “yield”, “scale-up”, “kilogram”)
as anchors to identify relevant regions.
The extraction procedure consists of the following steps:

1.

Scan all pages to locate keyword occurrences.

2.

Define context windows around each occurrence.

3.

Merge overlapping windows.

4.

Extract the merged text regions, subject to a configurable maximum character limit.

This ensures that synthesis procedure sections receive priority over unrelated content (e.g., characterisation data, discussion),
while respecting the token budget of the extraction LLM.
Main text and supporting information are processed independently and then combined before being passed to the extraction agent, enabling cross-referencing between dispersed procedural details.

Keyword selection planner (
PdfConfigPlanner
).

The keyword inventory and truncation parameters are stored in
pdf_config.json
.
To initialize this configuration, human experts provide a small set of high-priority seed terms.
These seeds are then expanded by a dedicated
PdfConfigPlanner
, by caling GPT-5.4 to propose additional synthesis-relevant keywords for PDF pre-selection.

The planner takes any existing keywords as input and is instructed to expand them to
MIN_FOCUS_KEYWORDS
distinct entries, spanning experimental section markers, reactor or vessel types, scale-up and quantity indicators, solvent and reaction medium descriptors, work-up terminology, and yield/characterisation language.

Numeric truncation parameters (e.g., window sizes, character limits) are treated as engineering constants.
The planner is invoked at runtime if
pdf_config.json
is missing or the keyword count falls below the required threshold, leading consistent coverage without manual intervention.

S2.2
Structured Extraction

An
ExtractionAgent
class invokes a large language model with schema-constrained output to extract structured synthesis records.
The agent currently supports two API providers:

•

OpenAI
: Uses the Responses API with structured output mode, where the output schema is enforced by the API itself.

•

Anthropic
: Uses the Messages API with JSON output parsing at the application level.

Default to OpenAI (GPT-5.4).

Retry-with-feedback mechanism.

When the LLM output fails Pydantic schema validation, the validation error is appended to the conversation as a user message
(e.g., “Your output had error: {error}. Fix and retry.”), and the model is prompted to self-correct.
This loop runs for up to 2 retries with increasing backoff
(
1.0\times(\text{attempt}+1)
seconds).
In our practice, the retry mechanism is triggered for approximately
8-10\%
of papers, and after retries, the overall extraction success rate exceeds
99\%
.

User prompt template.

Each extraction call receives a user prompt that includes:
(i) the file identifier (DOI or filename),
(ii) the full article text (truncated), and
(iii) the supporting information text (if available),
delimited by explicit start/end markers.
Full prompts see
S3
.

Session memory via a content-addressable extraction cache.

We introduce a content-addressable session memory,
ExtractionCache
(
mof_scaleup.cache
), to reduce redundant structured-extraction calls, one of the dominant cost in the agent loop.
Unlike the coarse
--skip-existing
shortcut, the cache keys each episode by its full reasoning context,

\langle\text{provider},\ \text{model},\ \text{prompt\_version},\ \text{file\_id},\ \text{sha256(main\_pdf}\|\text{si\_pdf)}\rangle,

with 8-byte length-prefixing before hashing to ensure collision-safe separation between manuscript and SI.
This enforces strict
episodic isolation
:
reuse occurs only when both input bytes and inference configuration match.
On recall, cached outputs are revalidated via
ArticleExtraction
.
model_validate
()
.
Any stale or schema-incompatible entry is evicted before falling back to a fresh LLM call.
Successful extractions are written back with full configuration metadata and a UTC timestamp, yielding a replayable audit trail.
The mechanism is opt-in via
--cache-dir <path>
and otherwise leaves the pipeline fully stateless.
When enabled, recall costs only a hash over raw PDF bytes, negligible relative to a single model invocation.
The plain-JSON storage design keeps this strategy of low external denpendency.
It enables straightforward extensions such as TTL policies, drift-aware invalidation, or long-horizon decision logs.

S2.3
Protocol Normalisation

Extracted records undergo three post-processing steps:

(a) Solvent canonicalisation.

A lookup table of most commonly known solvent name/abbreviation mappings is used to standardise solvent identifiers.
For example, “DMF”, “dimethylformamide”, and “N,N-DMF” are all mapped to the canonical form “
N,N
-dimethylformamide” with abbreviation “DMF”.
The lookup table uses normalised keys (lowercased, whitespace/punctuation removed, Greek letters transliterated) for robust matching.

Solvent table Planner
(
SolventAliasPlanner
).
The alias table is stored in
solvent_aliases
.
json
and maintained by a
SolventAliasPlanner
class that calls GPT-5.4 to enumerate alias-to-canonical mappings.
Each entry maps a sovent alias (full name, abbreviation, or spelling variant) to a two-element array
[canonical_full_name, canonical_abbreviation]
.
The planner augments the existing table to
MIN_ALIAS_ENTRIES
entries, covering common MOF solvents
(water, DMF, DEF, DMAc, EtOH, MeOH, DMSO, MeCN, THF, 1,4-dioxane, acetone, NMP, ldots),
halogenated and aromatic solvents, acid/amine modulators used as co-solvents, fluorinated solvents, and alternate IUPAC names or common misspellings.
Existing entries always take precedence, and the LLM fills gaps only.
The planner is invoked automatically at runtime if the alias file has fewer than
MIN_ALIAS_ENTRIES
.

The solvent system is compacted so that empty slots are removed and single-solvent systems receive a default ratio of 1.

(b) Stoichiometry computation.

If the metal-to-linker molar ratio is not explicitly reported by the authors,
it is computed from the extracted molar amounts (mmol, mol, or
\mu
mol) of the primary metal source and linker.
The ratio text is formatted in
M\!:\!L
notation (e.g., “1:2”).
The stoichiometry source is tracked as
explicit
,
computed
, or
not_reported
.

(c) Yield parsing.

If a yield percentage was reported as text (e.g., “85%”) but the numeric field was left empty by the extraction,
a regex-based parser extracts the numeric value.

(d) Scale evidence reconciliation.

If any synthesis record within a paper has a scale label of “gram”,
“multi-gram”, or “kilogram”, the article-level flag
gram_scale_reported
is set to “yes”.
Product masses
\geq 1.0
g also trigger this flag.

S2.7
Calibrated Scoring

Referring to
[
19
]
, the post-processing of fine-tuned model outputs is implemented in the
ScoringPipeline
class, which provides a fit-once / transform-many interface.

For each input protocol
x
, the pipeline performs three sequential operations.

1.

Extracts a raw score from the token-level log-probabilities of the two candidate output tokens (“P” and “U”), yielding a continuous confidence value in
[0,1]
.

2.

This score is corrected under the PU-learning framework to account for incomplete positive labelling in the literature-derived training set.

3.

The corrected score is optionally calibrated by Platt scaling on the validation gold split, and a decision threshold selected on the same split is used to obtain the final binary prediction.

In addition to the scalar score, the log-probability extraction module records diagnostic metadata, including the generated token sequence, top-
k
alternative tokens at the classification position, and warnings when the emitted label disagrees with the highest-probability token.
These records are used for debugging and sanity checking, but do not alter the final score.

Mathematical formal expressions of the raw score, PU correction, calibration procedure, and threshold selection are provided in Section
S5.5
.

Dual Evaluation Rationale

The PU framework motivates the two distinct evaluation benchmarks described in Section
S4
:

•

Gold benchmark (
\mathrm{P_{s}}
vs. N):

Tests the model’s ability to separate protocols with confirmed scale-up evidence from expert-curated negatives.
Because both labels are “clean” (no label noise), this benchmark directly measures classification accuracy.
The Platt scaling and threshold are fitted on this benchmark’s validation split.

•

Deployment benchmark (
\mathrm{P_{a}}
vs. U):

Simulates the realistic use case of screening the general literature for latent scalability potential.
Auxiliary positives (
\mathrm{P_{a}}
) serve as ground-truth positives, while the unlabeled pool (U) serves as the negative set.
Because U contains an unknown fraction of true positives,
the deployment benchmark is evaluated primarily through ranking metrics (AUROC, AP, Precision@
k
)
rather than threshold-dependent classification metrics,
since any true positive in U that the model correctly ranks highly would be penalised as a “false positive” under threshold-based metrics.

This dual-benchmark design allows us to separately assess the model’s discriminative power (gold) and its practical utility for literature-scale screening (deployment).

A random 10% of selected papers from both the scale-up and general synthesis corpora were manually verified

After up to 2 retries, the overall extraction success rate exceeded 99%.

S5
Positive-Unlabeled (PU) Learning Framework

S5.1
Motivation

Standard binary classification requires both positive and negative training labels.
In the MOF scale-up prediction task, however, the absence of scale-up evidence in a publication does not imply that the synthesis is fundamentally non-scalable , the authors may simply not have attempted or reported a larger batch.
This
label asymmetry
means that the unlabeled pool
\mathcal{U}
is a mixture of true negatives and latent positives (i.e., genuinely scalable protocols that lack published scale-up data).
Training a standard classifier on such data would systematically underestimate the scalable class and produce biased probability estimates.

Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning
[
11
]
addresses precisely this setting:
the learner has access to a set of verified positives and a larger set of unlabeled examples, but no confirmed negatives.
The framework provides a principled correction that recovers calibrated posterior probabilities from a model trained on
\{\text{P},\text{U}\}
labels alone.

S5.2
Problem Formulation

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
be the true (unknown) binary label,
where
f(x)=1
denotes a genuinely scalable protocol and
\mathcal{X}
is the space of synthesis protocol descriptors (metal, linker, solvent, conditions, etc.).
Let
s(x)\in\{0,1\}
denote whether the protocol was
selected
(i.e., reported with explicit scale-up evidence in the literature).
In the PU setting the learner observes:

•

A set of
labelled positives

\mathcal{P}=\{x:s(x)=1\}
, drawn from

p(x\mid f(x)=1,s(x)=1)
.
In our dataset,
\mathcal{P}
comprises both strong positives (
\mathrm{P_{s}}
, protocols with direct scale-up evidence)
and auxiliary positives (
\mathrm{P_{a}}
, small-batch preparations of MOFs later shown to be scalable).

•

A set of
unlabeled examples

\mathcal{U}=\{x:s(x)=0\}
, drawn from the marginal
p(x)
,
which contains an unknown fraction of latent positives alongside true negatives.

Training label mapping.

During training, both
\mathrm{P_{s}}
and
\mathrm{P_{a}}
are mapped to the positive label “P”,
while U examples retain the label “U”.
Crucially, the expert-curated negative set (N,
n=161
) is
entirely excluded
from training
and reserved for the gold evaluation benchmark (Section
S4
),
ensuring that the model is trained under the strict PU assumption with no access to confirmed negatives.
The resulting training set contains 2,508 examples (288
\mathrm{P_{s}}
+ 255
\mathrm{P_{a}}
+ 1,965 U).

S5.3
The SCAR Assumption

The key Selected Completely At Random (SCAR) assumption
[
11
]
states that, among truly scalable protocols,
the probability of being labelled (i.e., reported with scale-up evidence) is a constant
c
independent of the protocol features:

p(s=1\mid x,f=1)=c\in(0,1]

(S1)

This means that the labelled positives
\mathcal{P}
are an unbiased sample of all true positives ,
which ones get reported with scale-up data is random with respect to the synthesis conditions themselves.

Justification in the MOF context.

Whether a research group reports scale-up results is driven primarily by factors orthogonal to the synthesis conditions themselves ,
funding priorities, application targets, journal scope, and industrial collaborations ,
rather than by the specific metal/linker/solvent/temperature combination.
A protocol using Zr/BDC in DMF at 120 °C is not inherently more or less likely to
appear

in a scale-up paper than one using Cu/BTC in water at 80 °C,
even though both may be equally scalable.
While SCAR is an idealisation (publication bias may introduce mild deviations),
it provides a tractable and well-studied framework that is substantially more appropriate
than the naive assumption that all unlabeled protocols are negative.

PU posterior correction.

Under SCAR, the true positive posterior can be recovered from the observable quantity
p(s=1\mid x)
:

p(f=1\mid x)=\frac{p(s=1\mid x)}{c}

(S2)

Since a standard classifier trained on
\{\text{P},\text{U}\}
labels learns to approximate
p(s=1\mid x)
rather than
p(f=1\mid x)
,
dividing by
c
corrects for the fraction of true positives that remain hidden in the unlabeled pool.

S5.4
Estimation of the Label Frequency
\hat{c}

The constant
c
, the probability that a truly scalable protocol appears in the labelled positive set ,
must be estimated from data.
Following the classical estimator of Elkan and Noto,
[
11
]
we compute
\hat{c}
as the average raw model confidence
on a held-out set of known positives:

\hat{c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{P_{s}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}_{P_{s}}}q(x)

(S3)

where
q(x)
is the raw score from the fine-tuned model (Eq.
S4
) and
\mathcal{V}_{P_{s}}

is the validation gold set of strong positives (
n=32
).
This estimator is consistent under SCAR: on true positives the model should assign high confidence,
and the average confidence converges to
c
.
The computed value is clipped to
[10^{-6},\;0.999]
for numerical stability.

Our estimated value
\hat{c}=0.8370
admits a concrete interpretation:
the fine-tuned model assigns, on average, a raw P-probability of 83.7% to protocols with confirmed scale-up evidence.
This indicates that (i) the model has learned a strong signal for scalability,
and (ii) the labelled literature captures most but not all genuinely scalable protocols ,
approximately 16% of truly scalable MOF syntheses remain hidden as latent positives in the unlabeled pool.
This motivates the PU correction: without it, the model would systematically underestimate scalability probabilities
by a factor of
\sim
1.2
\times
.

S5.5
Three-Stage scalability Scoring Method

The fine-tuned model output is converted into a calibrated scalability score by a three-step PU-aware post-processing procedure.
Implementation details see
S2.7
.

Step 1: Raw score extraction.

For each input protocol
x
, the model generates a single classification token, either “P” or “U”. The raw score
q(x)
is computed from the token-level log-probabilities:

q(x)=\frac{\exp(\ell_{\mathrm{P}})}{\exp(\ell_{\mathrm{P}})+\exp(\ell_{\mathrm{U}})}

(S4)

where
\ell_{\mathrm{P}}
and
\ell_{\mathrm{U}}
denote the log-probabilities of the “P” and “U” tokens at the classification position. In implementation, this quantity is evaluated using a numerically stable two-class softmax by subtracting
m=\max(\ell_{\mathrm{P}},\ell_{\mathrm{U}})
before exponentiation. The resulting
q(x)\in[0,1]
is interpreted as the model estimate of
p(s=1\mid x)
, where
s=1
denotes assignment to the observed positive class.

Step 2: PU correction.

Under the classical PU-learning perspective,
[
11
]
the observed positive label is only assigned to a subset of truly positive examples. Let
c=p(s=1\mid y=1)
denote the positive-labelling frequency. We estimate this quantity from the validation
\mathrm{P_{s}}
examples as

\hat{c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{P_{s}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}_{P_{s}}}q(x),

(S5)

and obtain the PU-corrected score by

s_{\mathrm{PU}}(x)=\min\!\left(\frac{q(x)}{\hat{c}},\,1\right).

(S6)

In our experiments,
\hat{c}=0.8370
(clipped to
[10^{-6},\,0.999]
for numerical stability). This correction compensates for the dilution of the positive class by unlabeled-but-potentially-positive examples and inflates intermediate raw scores accordingly.

Step 3: Probability calibration and threshold selection.

A logistic regression model (scikit-learn
LogisticRegression
with L-BFGS solver) is fitted on the PU-corrected scores of the validation gold set (
\mathrm{P_{s}}
vs. N), producing the final calibrated score

s_{\mathrm{final}}(x)=\sigma\!\left(w\,s_{\mathrm{PU}}(x)+b\right),

(S7)

where
\sigma
is the logistic sigmoid and
w,b
are learned parameters.
[
33
]
Platt scaling is applied only when both classes are present and the validation set contains at least 10 examples; otherwise,
s_{\mathrm{PU}}(x)
is used directly as the final score. Before fitting, input scores are clipped to
[10^{-6},\,1-10^{-6}]
to avoid numerical instability near the boundaries.

The classification threshold is selected by exhaustive grid search over 401 equally spaced points in
[0,1]
, maximizing balanced accuracy on the validation gold split. The resulting threshold is
\tau=0.3875
. Its relatively low value reflects the task preference for recovering potentially scalable protocols at high recall, provided that false-positive rates remain acceptable.
