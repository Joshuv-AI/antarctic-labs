1. Introduction
Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH)

 1. Introduction

PSH context and the resurgence of new development

• PSH remains the dominant form of grid-scale energy storage in the United States, yet no new facility
  larger than 50 MW has been commissioned since 1995.
• Interest has resurged for PSH to provide long-duration energy storage, reliable capacity, and grid
  services (Koritarov et al. 2021).
• At the end of 2024, nearly 70 projects having 61 GW of potential new capacity were in the Federal
  Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licensing and permitting pipeline (Schmidt et al. 2025).
• A clear understanding of capital costs is important for decisions involving new PSH deployment, but
  costs are highly site-specific and difficult to estimate without detailed design work.

                                                                                     National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   4

 1. Introduction (continued)

The NLR model and this validation

• To help improve industry-wide transparency into PSH costs, NLR developed a component-level,
  bottom-up cost model (Cohen et al. 2023)—a public Excel workbook and web tool calibrated to
  industry data (EPRI 1990), intended for screening-level cost assessment.
• Initial validation described in Cohen et al. (2023) compared cost model outputs to cost estimates for
  the proposed Eagle Mountain PSH facility.
• This presentation expands on that initial validation using the December 2025 public cost model
  release (nlr-psh-cost-model-public-12122005) to compare modeled and reported costs for six
  proposed or permitted U.S. PSH projects: Goldendale (WA), Seminoe (WY), Gordon Butte (MT),
  Swan Lake (OR), White Pine (OR), and Lewis Ridge (KY)
• Important caveat: no projects in this set have been built. Cost comparisons are made using
  applicant FERC estimates—themselves preliminary—not final engineered costs.

2. Approach
Inputs, assumptions, and the AACE Class 5
standard

 2. Approach

Model inputs and dollar-year handling

• Inputs are assembled from public FERC filings, primarily Exhibit A submissions.
• Where applicant filings provided explicit values (reservoir dimensions, dam heights, conveyance
  lengths, head, etc.), those values were used directly. Where these values were not explicit,
  assumptions were made and documented. The complete set of per-project input data and
  assumptions is tabulated in the Appendix.
• All comparisons used the December 2025 public release of the NLR model.
• Costs are reported in the dollar year associated with each project application. Rather than escalating
  costs from each application to a common dollar year, cost model results are instead deflated to match
  application dollar years to avoid manipulations of application data in a way that is inconsistent with
  their original production.

                                                                                     National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   7

 2. Approach (continued)

Overrides, indirect costs, and project-specific treatments
• Applicant-reported tunnel and shaft lengths were entered directly into the model's override column
  (spreadsheet column O) where available. These are critical for meaningful comparisons in the water
  conductor category because default cost model assumptions do not account for detailed site-specific
  conveyance designs.
• Indirect cost factors remain at model defaults for all projects except Lewis Ridge: mobilization/demobilization
  5%, sales tax 6%, contingency 33%, engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) 28%, developer cost 3%,
  overhead and profit 7%.
 ◦ Lewis Ridge is calibrated to the applicant's reported structure (13% / 6% / 39% / 21% / 3% / 20%), the only project with this
 level of detail.
 ◦ Variation in applicant indirect cost reporting and methodology is a significant source of comparison difficulty.
• For Seminoe (an open-loop configuration): lower dam and reservoir costs are excluded because no new
  lower dam is constructed, but actual costs could include lower dam/reservoir modifications for PSH operation.
• For Gordon Butte: the reported ternary pump-turbine configuration is priced as variable speed, the closest
  available option, as the model has no ternary setting. This choice likely underestimates pump-turbine and
  power station costs.
The validation criterion is based on the AACE Class 5 estimate uncertainty range of -50% to +100%
(AACE International 2011). Engineering design, geotechnical studies, and other site-level evaluation are
assumed necessary for more accurate cost estimates.

4. Discussion
Project-by-project analysis

 4.1 Gordon Butte
Meagher County, MT | 400 MW – 4 units | closed loop |
  rockfill dams | surface powerhouse (uniquely)

• Applicant cost (2015$): $912M (GB Energy Park / Absaroka,
    October 2015). Model: $1,285M. Difference: +$373M (+41%).

• 2015 FERC indirect cost structure is inconsistent with
 developer's own subsequent disclosures—single $66.6M
 engineering line (~10% of direct), no separate breakdown for
 contingency, EPC, owner's costs, etc.

• Absaroka 2020 data to PNNL (Mongird et al. 2020): indirect
    costs = 24% of direct; total direct = $2,710/kW ($1,044/kW
 electromechanical + $1,666/kW civil).

•   For 400 MW, this implies ~$1,084M direct → ~$1,344M all-in
 (24% indirect). This would result in a difference of -$59M (-4%),
 very close to the cost model estimate.

• Plausible interpretation: The 41% gap might reflect
 incomplete indirect cost information in the 2015 FERC
 submission rather than model overestimation.

                    4.2 Swan Lake (1 of 2)
Klamath County, OR | ~400 MW – 3 units | ~1,720 ft head |
   closed loop | rockfill ring dams | surface penstock
 ~11,085 ft (longest in set)

• Applicant cost (2015$): $765M (EDF Renewable, October
    2015 / refiled March 2016). Model: $1,413M. Difference:
 +$648M (+85%)—largest percentage overestimate, but within

• EDF's 2015 Exhibit D was originally filed with all cost

• Estimate was produced at an early stage of development,
 before completion of the geotechnical and site investigations
 FERC was still requesting in March 2016.

• After Rye Development's 2017 acquisition, developer's public
 estimate rose to $2 billion—a 150% increase, occurring before
 the early-2020s construction inflation.

               4.2 Swan Lake (2 of 2)

Implications and the 2024 amendment
• The 2015 figure could reflect a conceptual-stage estimate that did not survive more rigorous
  site investigation. The model's $1,413M (2015$) is closer to the $2,000M estimate after Rye
  Development acquisition.
• April 2024 Non-Capacity Amendment of License (Swan Lake North Hydro 2024) included several design
  changes: replace 3 × 131.1 MW units with 2 × 182.4 MW; modify powerhouse location/size; reduce spillway
  sizes; lower bottom elevations of powerhouse, reservoirs, and water conveyance; bury previously surface-
  mounted penstocks.
• Substantive changes reflect the evolution from conceptual layout to a fully engineered design, which
  drove cost estimates upward.
• Kiewit Infrastructure is engaged as EPC contractor; Knight Piésold as lead designer—indicating
  serious pre-construction development with a mature (undisclosed) cost basis.
• Gap between $1,413M (model) and $2,000M (developer statement) may reflect developer-estimated
  overhead/contingency above standard EPC, financing costs, Tribal compensation, and fully loaded
  budget vs. construction cost basis.

 4.3 Goldendale
Klickitat County, WA | 1,200 MW – 4 units | ~2,360 ft head |
    closed loop | rockfill dams, underground powerhouse
 and conveyance

•   Applicant cost (2019$): $2,849M (FFP Project 101 / Rye / CIP,
    June 2020). Model: $3,035M. Difference: +$186M (+6.5%).

• Component-level: model reservoirs and dams cost is +$269M
 (+50%), with larger divergence in individual upper/lower dam
 costs.

• Largest single source of disagreement: power station
 equipment. Applicant ($931M) is ~63% above model ($571M).

• Goldendale's reported pump/motor cost is unusually high
 relative to similar projects. This likely reflects applicant-specific
 equipment pricing (project-specific procurement and custom
 hydraulic design at a head/unit size near the upper limit of
 commercially available single-stage Francis turbines) not
 captured in the cost curves.

• Both model and applicant inputs use variable-speed pump-
 turbines, so technology type alone does not explain the gap.

 4.4 White Pine
Grant County, OR | ~1,000 MW – 3 units | ~2,250 ft head |
   closed loop | rockfill ring dams | underground
 powerhouse and conveyance
• Applicant cost (2021$): $2,187M (White Pine Waterpower,
    February 2023 FLA). Model: $2,485M. Difference: +$298M
 (+13.6%)—well within AACE Class 5.
• Component-level agreement is reasonable across most direct
 cost categories.
• Largest direct overestimate: reservoirs and dams ($558M
 model vs. $313M applicant)—driven by upper and lower dam
 costs.
• Water conductor costs underestimated ($135M vs. $330M
 applicant)—may reflect indirect costs being included in
 applicant’s cost or site-specific conditions.
• Power station equipment underestimated ($437M vs.
 $644M)—same pattern as Goldendale.
• Indirect costs overestimated ($1,120M vs. $684M)—though
 ratio is smaller than Gordon Butte/Swan Lake. This could
 reflect differences in where indirect costs are allocated
 (component vs. overall).

 4.5 Seminoe
Carbon County, WY | ~972 MW – 3 units | ~1,165 ft head |
   open loop (only one in set) | existing Seminoe Reservoir
 as lower; new RCC upper dam

• Applicant cost (2022$): $2,622M (Black Canyon Hydro / rPlus
    Hydro, January 2023). Model: $2,914M. Difference: +$292M
 (+11.1%).

• Open-loop configuration required zeroing the lower dam cost
 line in the model (no lower dam constructed), so model
 estimates ignore any necessary modifications to the existing
 lower reservoir and dam.

• With that adjustment, model direct cost estimates are generally
 reasonable.

• Surge facilities are included by the model (~$58M) given long
 conveyance and high reservoir distance-to-head ratio, but this
 cost line is absent from applicant filing.

• Indirect costs are overestimated by approximately 9%.

 4.6 Lewis Ridge
Bell County, KY | 282 MW – 2 units | ~1,016 ft head |
   closed loop | former coal mining lands | earthfill dams,
 surface penstock, underground powerhouse

• Received $81M DOE grant (DOE 2024); public statements
    cited ~$1.3B total in 2024$. Applicant cost (2025$): $1,192M
 (Lewis Ridge / Rye, June 2025). Model: $1,204M. Difference:
    +$12M (+1.0%).

• Near-perfect agreement achieved through calibration of
 indirect cost percentages to applicant's reported structure
 (differs substantially from model defaults).

• Applicant indirect breakdown implies: ~13% mobilization,
 ~39% contingency, ~21% EPC, ~20% overhead and profit.

• With these calibrated factors, model direct cost calculations
 align very well with applicant across nearly all categories.

• Most encouraging direct-cost validation result; confirms
 model cost curves perform well for an earthfill, surface-
 penstock, underground-powerhouse configuration at this
 head and capacity range.

5. Summary
Observations
What the six-project validation reveals

 5. Summary Observations

Indirect costs are the dominant driver of cost overestimation by the model
• The NLR model consistently results in higher total costs relative to applicant figures, predominantly
  because of higher indirect costs.
• This outcome is consistent with the previous Eagle Mountain validation (Cohen et al. 2023).
• Default cost model indirect cost assumptions are calibrated to industry norms, while applicant FERC
  submissions vary in how they account for all indirect cost categories.
• NLR cost model is intentionally conservative.
The largest overestimates correlate with nonstandard applicant indirect cost structures
• Gordon Butte (+41%) and Swan Lake (+85%) both involve applicant indirect structures well below industry
  norms and well below model defaults.
• When external cost data (developer public statements, third-party analyses) are considered, the model's
  outputs are more consistent with the complete body of available evidence than with applicant FERC
  estimates alone.
                                                                                     National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   21

5. Summary Observations (continued)

Model is better aligned with developer estimates for direct costs than indirect costs
• Four of six projects show direct cost totals within ~20% of applicant.
• Largest direct cost variance sources include: power station equipment (model’s EPRI-derived curves appear to
  underestimate at upper end of head/capacity range) and water conductors (sensitive to length allocation
  among subcomponents).
• Conveyance lengths should be populated using the override column when applicant-reported lengths are
  available.
Lewis Ridge: the calibration test
• This is the only project where model and applicant values are essentially identical, and the only one where
  indirect cost factors were calibrated to applicant structure.
• This comparison confirms that direct cost calculations are functioning correctly; indirect cost treatment is the
  primary lever determining the gap across the validation set.
Overall: The NLR PSH cost model performs within AACE Class 5 for all six projects (five
within AACE Class 4 [-30% to +50%] and four within 15%), which is reasonable performance
for a publicly available, parametric cost model applied at a preliminary design stage.
