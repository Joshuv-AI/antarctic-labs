Volume Term Adaptivity for Discontinuous Galerkin Schemes Journal: ??
Daniel Doehring, Jesse Chan, Hendrik Ranocha, Michael Schlottke–Lakemper, Manuel Torrilhon, and Gregor Gassner

Abstract
We introduce the concept of volume term adaptivity for high–order discontinuous Galerkin (DG) schemes solving time–dependent partial differential equations. Termed v –adaptivity, we present a novel general approach that exchanges the discretization of the volume contribution of the DG scheme at every Runge–Kutta stage based on suitable indicators. Depending on whether robustness or efficiency is the main concern, different adaptation strategies can be chosen. Precisely, the weak form volume term discretization is used instead of the entropy-conserving flux–differencing volume integral whenever the former produces more entropy than the latter, resulting in an entropy–stable scheme. Conversely, if increasing the efficiency is the main objective, the weak form volume integral may be employed as long as it does not increase entropy beyond a certain threshold or cause instabilities. Thus, depending on the choice of the indicator, the v –adaptive DG scheme improves robustness, efficiency and approximation quality compared to schemes with a uniform volume term discretization. We thoroughly verify the accuracy, linear stability, and entropy–admissibility of the v –adaptive DG scheme before applying it to various compressible flow problems in two and three dimensions.

In general, the usage of the (in general) more robust entropy–conservative/entropy–stable Discontinuous Galerkin schemes comes at the cost of a more involved volume term discretization.
In particular, for the flux–differencing form, the volume term evaluation requires the computation of special two–point flux functions at pairs of the quadrature nodes of the element.

We seek to use the cheaper weak form volume term as often as possible, with the option to switch to the more expensive flux–differencing form only when necessary to retain stability of the simulation.

2.1
Weak Formulation of the 1D Discontinuous Galerkin Spectral Element Method

Consider the vector–valued conservation law in one spatial dimension

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)+\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.1)

which can be transformed from a physical element
\Omega_{i}=[x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},x_{i+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}]
to the reference element
\widehat{\Omega}\coloneqq[-1,1]
via the affine mapping

\xi(x)\coloneqq 2\frac{x-x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{x_{i+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}-1,\quad x\in\Omega_{i}

(2.2)

and inverse

x_{i}(\xi)\coloneqq x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\frac{x_{i+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{2}(\xi+1),\quad\xi\in\widehat{\Omega}

(2.3)

with the Jacobian of the transformation

J_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\partial x_{i}(\xi)}{\partial\xi}=\frac{x_{i+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{2}\>.

(2.4)

Thus, the conservation law in reference space reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)+\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)+J_{i}^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow J_{i}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)+\partial_{\xi}\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\>.

(2.5c)

We expand the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
in terms of Lagrange polynomials
\ell_{j}(\xi)
of degree
p
defined on the Gauss–Lobatto nodes
\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{p}\in\widehat{\Omega}
as

\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t)\ell_{j}(\xi)\>,

(2.6)

with time–dependent degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t)
which are defined for every element
\Omega_{i}
and node
j=0,\ldots,p
.
We insert this expansion into
Eq.

2.5c
and make the traditional Galerkin ansatz by multiplying with test functions identical to ansatz functions
\ell_{k}(\xi)
and integrating over the reference element
\widehat{\Omega}
to obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\left[J_{i}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)+\partial_{\xi}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi\>,\quad k=0,\ldots,p

(2.7a)

\displaystyle=J_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{\xi}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi\>,\quad k=0,\ldots,p\>.

(2.7b)

The first term in
Eq.

2.7b
can be simplified by inserting
Eq.

2.6

\displaystyle J_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=J_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t)\ell_{j}(\xi)\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi

(2.8a)

\displaystyle=J_{i}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\omega_{l}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t)\ell_{j}(\xi_{l})\ell_{k}(\xi_{l})

(2.8b)

with
\omega_{l}
denoting the quadrature weights of the Gauss–Lobatto quadrature rule.
Due to the collocation property of the Lagrange polynomials, i.e.,
\ell_{j}(\xi_{l})=\delta_{jk}
,
Eq.

2.8b
simplifies to

J_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi=J_{i}\omega_{k}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{i,k}(t)=J_{i}\omega_{k}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i,k}(t)

(2.9)

which is can be expressed in matrix notation with diagonal
mass matrix

\mathbf{M}_{ij}\coloneqq\delta_{ij}\omega_{i},\quad i,j=0,\ldots,p

(2.10)

as

J_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big(t,x_{i}(\xi)\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi=J_{i}\mathbf{M}\underline{\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{i}(t)

(2.11)

where we assemble the coefficients
\boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t)
at quadrature nodes
j
into

\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}(t)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{i,0}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{u}_{i,p}(t)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.12)

Next, we consider the second summand in
Eq.

2.7b
and apply integration by parts to obtain

\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{\xi}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\ell_{k}(\xi)d\xi=\left[\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)\right]_{-1}^{1}-\int_{-1}^{1}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\ell_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi.

(2.13)

The first term on the right–hand side of
Eq.

2.13
represents the interface or

of the Discontinuous Galerkin scheme which simplifies with
\ell_{k}(-1)=\delta_{k0}
and
\ell_{k}(1)=\delta_{kp}

\left[\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)\right]_{-1}^{1}=\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\big)\delta_{kp}-\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\big)\delta_{k0}.

(2.14)

Analogous to finite volume schemes, the physical flux evaluations at element interfaces with potentially discontinuous states
\boldsymbol{u}\left(t,x^{L}_{i\pm\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)\neq\boldsymbol{u}\left(t,x^{R}_{i\pm\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)

\mathbf{B}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&\cdots&0\\

\left[\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi))\big)\ell_{k}(\xi)\right]_{-1}^{1}=\mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{f}^{\star}|_{-1}\\

\vdots\\
0\\

(2.16)

Finally, we come to the

-\int_{-1}^{1}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\ell_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi=-\sum_{l=0}^{p}\omega_{l}\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x_{i}(\xi_{l}))\big)\ell_{k}^{\prime}(\xi_{l})\>.

(2.17)

With the

\mathbf{D}_{ij}\coloneqq\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\xi_{i}),\quad i,j=0,\ldots,p

and mass matrix from

\displaystyle\,J_{i}\mathbf{M}\underline{\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{i}(t)+\mathbf{B}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{\star}-\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{M}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\,J_{i}\underline{\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{i}(t)=-\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{\star}+\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{M}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}\>.

Flux–Differencing Formulation of the 1D Discontinuous Galerkin Spectral Element Method

A crucial observation for the construction of entropy–conservative/entropy–stable Discontinuous Galerkin schemes is that the mass, boundary, and derivative matrices fulfill the

3.1
Rigorous Indicator for more Robust Entropy–Stable Schemes

We now turn our attention to designing a numerical scheme that is globally entropy dissipative by combining the weak form and flux–differencing volume term discretizations.
In particular, we seek to construct a more stable adaptive scheme by using the weak form volume term if it introduces more decay in the mathematical entropy than the flux–differencing form, i.e., if

\Delta S_{i}\coloneqq\dot{S}_{i}|_{\text{WF}}-\dot{S}_{i}|_{\text{FD}}<0

(3.6)

for element
\Omega_{i}
.
This way, we obtain a globally entropy–stable scheme (assuming the surface terms dissipate entropy) as we only use the weak form volume term when it is more dissipative than the flux–differencing form.
A related approach for the entire semidiscretization, i.e., not only the volume integral, was already suggested in
15
where also an adaptive scheme based on local entropy production was presented.
In particular, the authors suggest equipping an entropy–conservative scheme with an entropy–diffusive
companion
scheme and blending them based on local entropy production.
In
15
WENO and other finite volume based schemes are suggested as companion schemes, with the former being employed in
48
.
Thus, in the terminology of
15
, the herein presented approach uses the weak form/central–flux flux–differencing volume integral as the diffusive
companion
scheme for the entropy–conserving flux–differencing volume integral.
Other closely related works are the a–posteriori subcell limiting Discontinuous Galerkin schemes developed in
44
,
128
,
43
which are based on the multi-dimensional optimal order detection paradigm
29
,
37
,
38
.
Adaptive or switching schemes, albeit not necessarily for entropy–stability, have also been studied in
60
,
111
,
126
, for instance.

The indicator as presented in
Eq.

3.6
is unfortunately very expensive, as the flux–differencing volume term is always computed, even if it is not used in the final update.
As pointed out in
73
,
25
, this can be avoided by replacing
\dot{S}_{i}|_{\text{FD}}
by

\dot{S}_{i}|_{\text{FD}}=\psi\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{i,p}\big)-\psi\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{i,0}\big)\>.

(3.7)

Employing this in the indicator equation
Eq.

3.6
thus gives

\Delta S_{i}\coloneqq\dot{S}_{i}|_{\text{WF}}-\Big(\psi\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{i,p}\big)-\psi\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{i,0}\big)\Big)<0\>.

(3.8)

This way, we only need to compute the entropy production of the weak form volume term and the surface integral of the entropy potential to evaluate the indicator.
The flux–differencing volume term is in this case only computed if entropy is indeed increasing.
We remark that in the case where for a majority of cells the weak form volume term may be used, this is not only a more robust scheme, but also more efficient scheme for polynomial degrees
p>2
as the flux–differencing volume term is only computed on a subset of elements.

The indicator
Eq.

3.8
is computed at every Runge–Kutta stage and element in the
v
–adaptive framework.
Note that this indicator is of
a–posteriori
nature, as the weak form volume term is computed in any case, but potentially discarded.
We present numerical results for this scheme showcasing increased robustness over the pure flux–differencing form in
Section

5
.

3.2
Heuristic Indicator for more Efficient Schemes

If efficiency is the main concern for a simulation, one can relax the indicator by introducing an upper bound
\sigma_{i}
for the tolerated entropy production of the weak form volume term.
In particular, the weak form volume term is used if

\dot{S}_{i,\text{WF}}<\sigma_{i}\>.

(3.9)

The weak form volume integral serves in this case as a predictor, and correction via the flux–differencing volume integral is only applied if deemed necessary by the indicator.

The difficulty lies now in finding a reasonable upper bound
\sigma_{i}
for the admissible entropy production of the weak form volume term.
Is this bound too large, the weak form volume integral is potentially applied too often, leading to instabilities.
On the other hand, if the bound is too small, the flux–differencing volume term computation is used most of the time, leading to ultimately higher computational cost than a pure flux–differencing scheme.
We emphasize here that
\sigma
applies to the entropy production in reference space, which eliminates issues with magnitude across elements of different sizes and enables better reusability of the parameter across different meshes.
We experimented with different fixed and dynamic bounds, the latter computed from the erronous total entropy production
\dot{S}>0
observed for a complete Runge–Kutta step.
In practice, the dynamic approach did not yield improvements over a fixed bound, and thus we present results only for the latter case in
Section

5
.
Similar to identifying a stable CFL number, we determined a stable
\sigma
by performing bisection starting with e.g.
\sigma_{\text{max}}=10^{-2}
and
\sigma_{\text{min}}=0.0
.
More sophisticated approaches are left for future work.

3.3
Shock–Capturing Indicator

In the case where the weak form volume term is combined with the stabilizing blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume we typically employ the a–priori troubled–cell indicator from
85
,
57
.
The indicator converts the nodal representation in Lagrange polynomials into a modal representation in terms of orthogonal Legendre polynomials via

\underline{\boldsymbol{m}}=\mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}

(3.10)

with
\mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
denoting the Vandermonde matrix of the Legendre polynomials evaluated at the interpolation nodes
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left\{\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{p}\right\}
.
Note that
\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{v_{j}\}
in
Eq.

3.10
is the nodal representation of an indicator quantity such as density
\rho
, pressure
p
, or the product of these two
\rho\cdot p
.
From the modal coefficients
\boldsymbol{m}
the energies

\mathcal{E}_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{p-n}m_{j}^{2},\quad n=0,1,2

(3.11)

are computed.
The influence of the higher modes, i.e., potential oscillations, is then measured by the fractions

\varepsilon_{0}\coloneqq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}-\mathcal{E}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}},\quad\varepsilon_{1}\coloneqq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{1}-\mathcal{E}_{2}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}}

(3.12)

from which the maximum
\varepsilon\coloneqq\max(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1})
is computed.
Then, the blending factor
\beta
is computed from the standard logistic function as

\beta\coloneqq\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\kappa\frac{\varepsilon-\mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{T}}\right)}

(3.13)

with hyperparameters

\kappa\coloneqq\log\left(\frac{1-10^{-4}}{10^{-4}}\right)\approx 9.21024,\quad\mathcal{T}\coloneqq 0.5\cdot 10^{1.8\cdot p^{0.25}}\>.

(3.14)

To avoid unnecessary blending in the case of a very small
\beta
, a threshold
\beta_{\text{min}}\geq 0
is supplied, below which the indicator value is clipped to zero.
Similarly, one can limit the maximum blending factor to
\beta_{\text{max}}\leq 1
to avoid excessive blending in the case of a very large
\beta
:

\beta=\begin{cases}0&\beta<\beta_{\text{min}}\\
\beta&\beta_{\text{min}}\leq\beta\leq\beta_{\text{max}}\\
\beta_{\text{max}}&\beta>\beta_{\text{max}}\end{cases}\>.

(3.15)

The indicator is computed at every Runge–Kutta stage and element in the blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume framework with indicator variable
\boldsymbol{v}
taken from the previous Runge–Kutta stage.
Thus, the blending is determined a–priori any volume term computations of the current stage.

3.3.1
Shock–Capturing via Blended DG–FV

The blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume scheme uses a convex combination of a high–order Discontinuous Galerkin discretization and a non–oscillatory first or second–order finite volume discretization in troubled cells identified by the shock indicator
\beta
.
The volume term update is then given by

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}|_{\text{DG--FV}}\coloneqq(1-\beta_{i})\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}|_{\text{FD}}+\beta_{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}|_{\text{FV}}\,,\quad\beta_{i}\in[0,\beta_{\text{max}}]\,,

(3.16)

where
\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}|_{\text{FV}}
is a standard finite–volume update on the subcells of the
[-1,1]^{d}
reference element.
The
blending parameter

\beta_{i}
determines the amount of dissipation introduced by the finite volume update.
For a
p
–degree Discontinuous Galerkin scheme,
(p+1)^{d}
subcells are used per element in
d
spatial dimensions.
The natural size of these subcells is given by the quadrature weights
\omega_{i}
of the Lobatto–Legendre basis
57
.
Thus, the first subcell is given by
[-1,-1+\omega_{1}]
, the second by
[-1+\omega_{1},-1+\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}]
, and so on.
The scheme
Eq.

3.16
is provably entropy stable and the main shock–capturing methodology used in
Trixi.jl

88
,
102
,
101
.

3.4
Combining Shock–Capturing and Entropy–Production Indicators

Due to the different nature (a–priori vs a–posteriori) of the shock–capturing and
v
–adaptive indicators, they can be readily combined to give a nested volume term computation strategy.
In particular, the shock–capturing indicator is computed first to decide whether any stabilization via blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume is required.
If not, the volume term for this element is computed with the weak form, and the
v
–adaptive indicator is evaluated to decide whether the flux–differencing volume term is required for this element to maintain e.g. entropy stability.

In practice, however, we have for the discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method (i.e., tensor–product elements) not found examples where this hybrid strategy is more efficient to maintain entropy–conservative/entropy–stable properties than using always the flux–differencing volume term in non–stabilized regions.
We believe that this hybrid strategy could be beneficial for non tensor–product elements where the cost of the flux–differencing volume term is much higher than the weak form volume term, but this is left for future work.

\displaystyle=20

6
Conclusions

We have presented a general framework for adaptively exchanging the volume term/integral discretization in every cell and Runge–Kutta stage of high–order Discontinuous Galerkin schemes.
Based on a–priori and a–posteriori indicators, the cheap weak form volume integral discretization can be employed in non–critical regions, while the expensive and robust volume term discretizations like flux–differencing and finite–volume subcell limiting are used in cells where stabilization is required.
Additionally, we have demonstrated that usage of entropy–dissipative weak form volume integrals increases the robustness of a discretization.
An important property of this framework is that it strictly preserves the order of accuracy of the scheme.
We also verified that the
v
–adaptive schemes maintain convergence to entropy solutions and can in practice remedy linear stability issues of entropy–conservative schemes.
Furthermore, we have shown that tolerating some limited entropy increase due to the weak form volume integral can still give high–quality results.

We applied the proposed framework to a variety of test cases governed by the compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations in two three space dimensions.
In every case, the adaptive volume term discretization resulted in computational savings while maintaining stability.
Precisely, we used both the a–posteriori entropy–production based indicator to combine weak form and flux–differencing integrals, as well as the a–priori shock–capturing indicator to combine the weak form and blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume scheme.
Depending on the test case, the computational cost of the volume term computation can be reduced by factors up to
3
.
In general, performance gains for the Navier–Stokes equations are less pronounced due to the fact that there are more gradient variables than conserved variables, where only the latter require the more expensive flux–differencing volume term discretization.
For the examples considered here, we showed that the volume integral cost can be reduced by about
25
to
50\%
.

Although we focused on the discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method with collocated, tensor–product operators on quadrilateral and hexahedral meshes, the proposed framework is not limited to this particular discretization and can be applied to more general Discontinuous Galerkin schemes on unstructured meshes as well.
In fact, the
v
–adaptive schemes are most beneficial for discretizations where the volume term/volume integral operators admit no tensor–product structure, e.g., for Discontinuous Galerkin schemes on simplices with non–collocated nodes.

Consequently, we will focus on constructing efficient
v
–adaptive schemes with shock–capturing on simplices in future work.
In particular, nested, i.e., combined weak form, flux–differencing, and blended Discontinuous Galerkin–finite volume schemes on triangles and tetrahedra will be of interest.
Furthermore, we will explore the adaptive combination of weak form, flux–differencing and invariant domain preserving
84
,
96

and subcell monolithic convex limiting schemes

55
,
95
.
Finally, it is interesting to explore adaptivity in quadrature rules for the involved volume integrals as well.
Potentially, through using e.g. Gauss–Legendre instead of Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre quadrature, even more cells could be treated with the weak form volume integral instead of the flux–differencing volume term discretization.
