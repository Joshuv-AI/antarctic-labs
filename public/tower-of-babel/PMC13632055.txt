Firearm use and cardiovascular risk factors in the United States
Authors: Anthony Mufarreh, Vivek Bhat, Asef Hoque, Oishi Roychoudhury, Sienna Ford, Ankur Kalra
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death in the United States (US). Beyond well-studied individual-level risk factors, social, environmental, and behavioral contributors to CVD are being increasingly examined [1].

Firearm ownership is common in the United States (US), with around 30% of adults owning a gun [2]. Firearm-related violence is a prominent source of both individual and community-level stress. Chronic stress, including that related to violence exposure, has been associated with greater cardiovascular risk, while acute catastrophes have been linked to increased occurrence of cardiovascular events [3]. However, data on broader health correlates of firearms, particularly their association with CVD, remain limited, despite the great prevalence of both firearm use and CVD.

We aimed to assess the association between self-reported firearm use and cardiovascular risk factors and mortality, and to examine socioeconomic differences in these associations among US adults.

Methods

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a nationally representative sample of non-institutionalized US civilian population using multistage probability sampling design, with oversampling of underrepresented groups, and is conducted under the oversight of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board.

We included study cycles 2011–2012, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018 based on availability of the audiometry component question: ‘This next question is about your use of firearms that you may have used for target shooting, hunting, for your job, or in military service. Have you ever used firearms for any reason?’

We extracted data from questionnaire, examination, and laboratory components. Medical conditions were self-reported, in response to question ‘doctor told you have [medical condition]’. Mortality status was obtained by merging and linking with NCHS Linked Mortality Files, which utilize death certificate records within the National Death Index [4]. NHANES survey weights were applied to generate nationally representative estimates, accounting for differential probabilities of selection, survey nonresponse, and differences between sample and target population distributions.

We performed weighted univariate descriptive analyses stratified by firearm use. Differences between firearm users and non-users were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. We constructed unadjusted Poisson regression models with firearm use as the independent variable. Crude models were run for each outcome. Following initial screening, statistically significant outcomes were included in adjusted multivariable Poisson regression models.

With limited literature to guide confounder selection [5], socioeconomic variables (sex, age, race, marital status, education, income) were included based on prior studies. Given its close intertwining with firearm use, military service was also included in models. We hypothesized that the relationship between firearm use and morbidity is mediated through physiological stress pathways. Baseline comorbidities were considered potential mediators rather than confounders and were not included in adjusted models. All analyses were performed using R (version 3.6.2) survey package.

Results

We identified a total of 17,712 participants, of whom 6946 were excluded for missing firearm data, resulting in a final sample of 10,766 participants that represented a weighted sample of 441 million (Fig. 1, Panel A). Firearm users represented 46.8% of the sample. They were more likely to be male (66.3%), younger (18–39 years, 38.6%), non-Hispanic White (79.1%), have completed some college (36.3%), and have household income <$100,000 (69.6%). Firearm users had statistically significant greater prevalence of lifetime smoking (50.7%vs. 35.1%), waist circumference (101 vs. 98 cm), triglyceride levels (127 vs. 118 mg/dL), and being overweight (37.5 vs. 34.4%). They had higher rates of chest pain (27.7 vs. 22.9%) and myocardial infarction (3.1 vs. 2.1%).

Mortality data were available for 1049 participants, of whom 435 had firearm data (weighted 13.8 million deaths). On crude Poisson regression, firearm use was not associated with all-cause (RR: 1.27, 95% CI: 0.86 −1.88) or cause-specific mortality, but was associated with a higher risk of myocardial infarction (RR:1.5, 95% CI:1.07–2.1), being overweight (RR:1.09, 95% CI:1.02–1.17), chest pain (RR:1.21, 95% CI:1.08–1.36), higher triglycerides (RR:1.08, 95% CI:1.03–1.14), and lifetime smoking (RR:1.44, 95% CI:1.35–1.54).

In adjusted models, firearm use remained significantly associated with overweight (RR:1.17, 95% CI:1.08–1.27), chronic bronchitis (RR:1.46, 95% CI:1.18–1.81), chest pain (RR:1.24, 95% CI:1.09–1.42), and lifetime smoking (RR:1.22, 95% CI:1.13–1.32) (Fig. 1, Panel B). Among firearm users, females had statistically significantly greater risk of being overweight and chronic bronchitis, and lower risk of myocardial infarction, triglycerides, and lifetime smoking, compared with males. Non-Hispanic Black firearm users had increased risk of being overweight (RR:1.13, 95% CI:1.05–1.22), with lower triglycerides and risk of chronic bronchitis, compared with non-Hispanic Whites. College graduate firearm users had lower risk of myocardial infarction (RR:0.48, 95% CI:0.27, 0.84) and chest pain (RR:0.73, 95% CI:0.54, 0.98). Firearm users with household incomes of $45,000 to 99,999 had lower risk of myocardial infarction, chronic bronchitis, chest pain, and lifetime smoking compared with those with household income <$45,000.

Discussion

The grave impact and substantial prevalence of firearm-related violence in the US have prompted calls from national professional societies to examine its broader health impact beyond trauma and accidents [3]. In our analysis, we found that self-reported firearm use was associated with greater risk of cardiometabolic risk factors, including being overweight, chronic bronchitis, chest pain, and lifetime smoking. Prior work has found that community-level firearm incidents were associated with greater risk of stress-related CVD mortality [2]. We did not, however, find an association with mortality, suggesting that while firearm use is associated with a distinct cardiovascular risk profile, it may not directly relate to CVD mortality at the individual level.

Firearm users in our sample had broadly consistent characteristics with prior reports, being predominantly male, non-Hispanic White, college-educated, and with household income of <$100,000 [6]. Among firearm users, we found variations in CVD risk factors across sociodemographic groups, with higher prevalence of selected risk factors among female and non-Hispanic Black individuals and among those with lower education levels and income. Consistent with our primary analysis, we did not find an association between firearm use and mortality within these subgroups. Our observed associations between firearm use and cardiometabolic risk factors are possibly influenced by these underlying behavioral and sociodemographic characteristics. In particular, firearm users had a higher risk of lifetime smoking, a known contributor to CVD, and consistent with prior work demonstrating association between firearm use and polysubstance use [6]. This may contribute to the greater observed risk of chronic bronchitis in firearm users. Taken together, these findings suggest that firearm use may reflect underlying behavioral and sociodemographic risk profiles, rather than being an independent causal factor for CVD.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, firearm use and health outcomes were self-reported, introducing potential misclassification. Second, firearm use did not distinguish between recreational, occupational, or military use, nor did it capture ownership or exposure to firearm-related violence. Third, mortality events were limited, reducing power to detect differences between groups. Finally, residual confounding, particularly by behavioral factors such as smoking, remains possible. Regardless, through our analysis, we provide valuable preliminary insights into the relationship between firearm use and CVD, with further stratification by sociodemographic characteristics. Prospective studies remain needed to elucidate the impact of firearm ownership, usage, and exposure on CVD in the US.
