Exploring the genetic correlation between inflammatory bowel disease and psychiatric disorders: insights from genome-wide association studies
Authors: Jie Zhang, Jing Shen, Lifeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and psychiatric disorders exhibit complex comorbid patterns, with anxiety and depressive disorders being significantly more prevalent in IBD patients compared to the general population. These comorbidities may differ between Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), suggesting heterogeneous but potentially shared genetic mechanisms mediated through the gut–brain axis (GBA).ObjectiveTo systematically investigate global and local genetic correlations between IBD and eight psychiatric disorders and to identify shared pathogenic mechanisms underlying these comorbidities.MethodsUsing publicly available large-scale GWAS summary statistics, including a meta-analysis of 486,601 individuals for IBD and large international consortia datasets for eight psychiatric disorders, we conducted global genetic correlation analysis to quantify overall genetic overlap, local genetic correlation analysis to identify region-specific associations, summary data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) to evaluate putative causal gene–trait relationships, and multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) to detect pleiotropic variants.ResultsSignificant global genetic correlations were observed between IBD and ADHD (rho = 4.79 × 10− 3, p = 3.07 × 10− 5), anxiety disorder (rho = 1.89 × 10− 2, p = 8.02 × 10− 11), ASD (rho = 6.65 × 10− 3, p = 7.24 × 10− 4), OCD (rho = 7.84 × 10− 3, p = 1.32 × 10− 3), and PTSD (rho = 2.19 × 10− 2, p = 1.16 × 10− 19). Local genetic correlation analysis identified 274 significant regions (Bonferroni-corrected), with loci 464 (chr3: 47.6–50.4 Mb) and 960 (chr6: 31.3–31.4 Mb) showing consistent cross-trait associations. MTAG identified 1,125 independent pleiotropic loci, while SMR and MAGMA integration prioritized 21 key pleiotropic genes, including NOD2, IL10, and AURKB. Enrichment analyses highlighted immune-related and neurobiological pathways, including JAK–STAT signaling, FoxO signaling, oxidative stress response, and GABAergic synapse pathways.ConclusionOur findings provide multi-layered genetic evidence supporting a structured and locus-specific shared genetic architecture between IBD and several psychiatric disorders. The identification of key genes and pathways, particularly NOD2 and immune–synaptic signaling cascades, offers potential molecular targets for understanding and managing comorbid disease within the GBA framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-026-02302-1.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Psychiatric disorders, Genetic correlation, Pleiotropy, Mendelian randomization, Gut–brain axis

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is a group of chronic inflammatory disorders of the digestive system, primarily including Crohn’s Disease (CD) and Ulcerative Colitis (UC). In recent years, the incidence and prevalence of IBD have been steadily increasing worldwide, especially in Western countries and in Asian countries that are gradually adopting Western lifestyles [1–3]. The exact etiology of IBD remains unclear, but it is widely believed to result from the interaction of genetic, immune, and environmental factors, leading to an abnormal immune response of the gut to microbial communities, thereby triggering chronic inflammation [4, 5].

In recent years, the concept of the gut-brain axis (GBA) has gained increasing attention as a bidirectional signaling pathway connecting the gut and the central nervous system [6, 7]. Interactions between the gut and brain involve not only neural pathways (e.g., the vagus nerve) but also multiple mechanisms, including the immune system, endocrine system, and gut microbiota. This axis plays a crucial role in emotional regulation, stress response, and cognitive function [8, 9].

Importantly, the GBA is increasingly conceptualized as an integrated neuroimmune-microbial network, in which intestinal inflammation, circulating cytokines, microbial metabolites (such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan derivatives), and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation collectively influence central nervous system function [10]. An increasing number of studies suggest that an imbalance or abnormality in the gut microbiota can impact brain function and structure, leading to various psychiatric and neurological disorders such as mood disorders, anxiety, and depression [11–13]. Beyond microbiota dysbiosis, chronic intestinal inflammation in IBD may disrupt blood–brain barrier integrity, enhance microglial activation, and alter neuroinflammatory signaling, thereby increasing susceptibility to psychiatric symptoms [14].

The prevalence of psychiatric disorders, such as anxiety and depression, is significantly higher in patients with IBD than in the general population, and this high comorbidity may reflect dysfunction within the GBA [15–17]. Recent large-scale meta-analyses have further demonstrated bidirectional risk relationships between IBD and anxiety or depressive disorders, suggesting that the association cannot be explained solely by psychological burden of chronic illness, but may reflect shared biological or genetic vulnerability [16]. By advancing research on the GBA—particularly into the genetic and molecular mechanisms linking IBD with psychiatric disorders—we aim to uncover potential connections and provide new perspectives and targets for clinical treatment.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified various genetic variations (i.e., single nucleotide variants [SNVs]) associated with gastrointestinal and psychiatric disorders [18–22]. Both IBD and major psychiatric disorders are highly polygenic traits, with susceptibility loci enriched in immune regulation, cytokine signaling, and synaptic biology, indicating that immune–neural interactions may be genetically embedded in disease architecture [23, 24]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (LDSC) has demonstrated genetic correlation between these two categories of diseases [21, 23]. However, it remains unclear whether the overall genetic correlation is attributable to a few loci or the entire genome. Global genetic correlation estimates reflect averaged genome-wide effects and may mask heterogeneous, region-specific genetic sharing. Emerging analytical frameworks emphasize that local genetic correlations can exist even when global correlation is modest or non-significant [25]. In fact, genetic overlap may exist even in the absence of genetic correlation [18].

Despite these advances, prior cross-trait analyses often rely on single-method approaches, limiting the ability to disentangle diffuse polygenic overlap from locus-specific pleiotropy or gene-level causal mechanisms. A more layered analytical strategy integrating global correlation, local correlation, pleiotropic locus detection, and gene-level functional validation is therefore warranted. However, whether the shared genetic liability between IBD and psychiatric disorders is driven primarily by genome-wide polygenicity, structured locus-specific effects, or distinct gene-level mechanisms remains largely unresolved. Addressing this gap is essential not only for clarifying the biological basis of comorbidity but also for identifying specific genetic targets that may have clinical relevance.

To address this, we sought to determine whether IBD and psychiatric disorders share a structured genetic architecture and to identify the specific loci, genes, and biological pathways driving this overlap. To achieve this objective, we adopted a layered analytical strategy integrating multiple complementary statistical genetics approaches.

In this study, we conducted a systematic analysis of the genetic correlations between IBD and eight psychiatric disorders using large-scale GWAS summary data. We examined shared genetic liability at multiple levels, including genome-wide global genetic correlation, region-specific local genetic correlation, SNV-level pleiotropy, gene-level causal inference, and biological pathway enrichment.

Within this framework, we first identified pleiotropic SNVs and independent loci through genome-wide pleiotropy analysis. We then applied summary data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) to prioritize candidate pleiotropic genes and assess putative gene–trait associations. Tissue-specific enrichment analysis was conducted to evaluate expression patterns of prioritized genes. In parallel, local variance analysis annotation (LAVA) was used to dissect locus-specific genetic correlations. Together, these complementary analyses enabled us to distinguish diffuse polygenic overlap from structured locus-specific and gene-level mechanisms, thereby addressing the previously unresolved gap in cross-trait genetic architecture between IBD and psychiatric disorders.

Methods

GWAS datasets

This study included GWAS summary datasets for eight psychiatric disorders: Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) [26, 27], Anxiety Disorder (ANX) [26, 28], Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) [29], Bipolar Disorder (BD) [26, 30], Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) [26, 31], Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) [26, 32], Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) [26, 33], and Schizophrenia (SCZ) [26, 34]. The GWAS for IBD was obtained from a larger-scale meta-analysis involving 486,601 individuals (53,400 cases and 433,201 controls) [21] (detailed information can be found in Supplementary Table 1).

All GWAS resources were approved by the relevant ethics committees, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the original studies. All summary statistics used in the present study were obtained from publicly available repositories or consortia websites in accordance with their respective data access policies. No individual-level data were accessed. Therefore, additional ethical approval was not required for this secondary analysis. The genome-wide multi-factor association analysis followed the guidelines of the “Strengthening the Reporting of Genetic Association Studies” (STREGA). In the genetic analysis, all analyses except for genetic correlation analysis excluded the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region (chromosome 6: 25–35 Mb). The MHC region is characterized by extremely high gene density, extensive long-range LD, structural variation, and complex haplotype architecture. These features can inflate association signals, complicate fine-mapping, and introduce bias in pleiotropy or locus-specific analyses. To minimize spurious associations driven by extended LD structure, we excluded this region in downstream analyses focusing on locus-level and gene-level inference.

All GWAS summary statistics used in this study were derived from previously published large-scale meta-analyses, in which rigorous individual-level quality control procedures had already been applied, including genotype imputation, removal of low-quality variants, and population stratification correction.

For the present secondary analyses, we performed standard summary-level quality control procedures. Variants with missing effect sizes, ambiguous strand alignment (A/T or C/G), or inconsistent allele annotation were excluded. We retained SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) between 0.01 and 0.99 and harmonized all datasets to the same genomic reference build (GRCh37/hg19).

These preprocessing steps ensured consistency across datasets and minimized potential bias due to allele misalignment or low-quality variants.

Global genetic correlation

In this study, we employed the Genetic Novelty Analysis (GNOVA) method to assess the genetic correlation between psychiatric disorders and IBD. GNOVA is an innovative analytical tool designed to identify potential shared genetic foundations by considering both the genetic similarities and differences between traits [35]. Unlike LDSC or HDL, GNOVA utilizes summary statistics from GWAS and incorporates multiple statistical models, allowing for a more comprehensive capture of the genetic links between traits. Methodologically, GNOVA directly estimates genetic covariance using a generalized method-of-moments framework, whereas LDSC relies on regression of test statistics on LD scores and HDL adopts a likelihood-based approach. This difference enables GNOVA to achieve improved statistical efficiency under highly polygenic architectures and to better account for sample overlap across large consortia datasets.

We selected GNOVA as the primary method for global genetic correlation because it provides improved statistical power under polygenic architectures and allows correction for sample overlap, which is particularly relevant when using large consortia-based GWAS datasets. Compared with traditional LDSC, GNOVA can more flexibly partition covariance components and thus better estimate genome-wide shared heritability.

Specifically, we first generated a genetic interaction matrix between genotype and phenotype using GNOVA, which helps identify potential pleiotropy and comorbidity effects. This genome-wide analysis provides an overall estimate of shared genetic architecture but does not specify whether the correlation is driven by particular genomic regions. Therefore, it serves as the first layer of our analytical framework. During the analysis, we performed genetic correlation analysis with sample overlap correction and set significance thresholds, applying Bonferroni correction to address multiple testing.

Local genetic correlation analysis

To explore the traits and their local genetic correlations, we employed the LAVA method. LAVA is a method used to identify local genetic correlations within genomic regions, which detects significant genetic regions by assessing local genetic correlations between traits [25].

While global genetic correlation reflects averaged genome-wide effects, it may obscure region-specific heterogeneity. Therefore, LAVA was applied to partition the genome into approximately independent LD blocks, as defined by previously established European reference panels (approximately 2,495 LD blocks genome-wide). These blocks represent quasi-independent genomic segments with limited long-range LD.

Within each LD block, LAVA first estimates local SNP-based heritability for each trait and then computes local genetic covariance between traits using summary statistics and LD information from a reference panel. Local genetic correlation (rg) is subsequently calculated as the standardized covariance divided by the square root of the product of local heritabilities.

Significance of local genetic correlation was evaluated using a Wald test framework implemented in LAVA, and Bonferroni correction was applied across all tested LD blocks to control for multiple comparisons.

This second layer of analysis allows us to determine whether shared genetic risk is concentrated in specific loci or distributed across multiple genomic regions, thereby distinguishing structured locus-specific pleiotropy from diffuse polygenic overlap.

Multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) for pleiotropic loci

MTAG was performed to identify pleiotropic loci shared across traits. Independent major pleiotropic loci across the genome were identified using PLINK1.9 (r² < 0.02 and a 500 kb window) to ensure that the identified loci were independent and widely distributed across the genome [36]. The r²< 0.02 threshold represents a stringent LD pruning criterion commonly used in GWAS to define independent association signals, while the 500 kb window approximates the typical extent of local LD in European populations, thereby reducing redundancy due to correlated variants.

We employed MTAG to increase statistical power for detecting cross-trait pleiotropic variants by jointly analyzing genetically correlated traits. Unlike simple meta-analysis, MTAG leverages the covariance structure between traits to improve SNP effect estimation for each individual phenotype.

Specifically, MTAG uses a generalized least-squares framework that incorporates the genetic covariance matrix across traits to re-estimate SNP effect sizes in a trait-specific manner. This approach allows each SNP to retain phenotype-specific effect estimates while borrowing information from genetically correlated traits, thereby improving statistical efficiency without assuming identical effect directions across traits.

The MTAG method allows the estimation of the effect of each SNP in a joint analysis to identify genetic variations shared across multiple traits. This step represents the third layer of our framework, moving from correlation estimation to the identification of specific pleiotropic SNVs contributing to shared risk.

Functional annotation of identified variants was performed using ANNOVAR [37] to gain detailed insights into their functional impacts, alongside the calculation of combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) and RegulomeDB scores. SNVs with a CADD score greater than 12.37 are considered potentially deleterious [38]. RegulomeDB scores SNPs, with lower scores indicating stronger regulatory potential [39].

SMR analysis of shared pleiotropic genes

To explore the correlation of gene loci across traits in a cross-trait meta-analysis, we applied SMR analysis. The primary goal of SMR analysis is to evaluate whether genetic variations at specific gene loci have a shared causal effect across multiple traits [40].

While MTAG identifies variant-level pleiotropy, it does not determine whether these variants exert their effects through gene expression regulation. Therefore, SMR was introduced as the fourth analytical layer to integrate GWAS and eQTL data and to assess whether trait-associated SNPs influence disease risk via gene expression changes.

Specifically, SMR uses the top cis-eQTL of a gene as an instrumental variable and tests the association between genetically predicted gene expression and the trait of interest. This framework allows us to evaluate whether variation in gene expression is potentially causally associated with disease risk. Unlike correlation-based analyses (e.g., GNOVA or LAVA), which estimate shared genetic architecture, SMR tests a mediation hypothesis at the gene level. We assessed significance using Bonferroni correction across all tested genes (P_bonferroni < 0.05), ensuring control for multiple testing.

To distinguish true pleiotropic or mediating effects from linkage-driven associations, we performed the HEIDI (Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments) test. The HEIDI test evaluates whether the observed association is consistent with a single shared causal variant affecting both gene expression and the trait. A significant HEIDI result (P < 0.01) suggests heterogeneity and indicates that the association may be driven by two distinct variants in LD rather than a shared causal variant. Conversely, a non-significant HEIDI result (P > 0.01) supports the hypothesis of a shared causal variant.

The P = 0.01 threshold for HEIDI was selected in accordance with the original SMR framework to balance sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing pleiotropy/mediation from linkage effects.

Thus, SMR allows us to move from statistical pleiotropy to putative functional mediation, providing stronger biological interpretability of shared loci.

Pleiotropic gene enrichment analysis

After identifying pleiotropic genes, enrichment analysis helps us understand their roles in biological pathways and functional categories. To further explore the biological significance of these pleiotropic genes, we performed pathway enrichment analysis using tools like FUMA (Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS) [41] and Gene Ontology (GO).

FUMA is a web-based platform that integrates GWAS results with functional annotation and gene-set enrichment analysis. We used the SNP2GENE module in FUMA to map independent significant SNPs to genes based on positional mapping (within 10 kb), LD-based mapping, and publicly available eQTL information.

Specifically, we annotated functional variations using LD information from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel for European populations (EUR panel). LD clumping and SNP annotation were performed using the European ancestry reference to ensure consistency with the predominantly European GWAS datasets analyzed in this study.

We defined independent significant SNPs as those with P < 5 × 10− 6 and LD r² < 0.6 within a 1 Mb window. Lead SNPs were further defined as independent variants with r²< 0.1. Genomic risk loci were defined by merging LD blocks of lead SNPs located within 250 kb of each other [18].

Mapped genes were subsequently subjected to gene-set enrichment analysis using pre-defined gene sets from the Gene Ontology (GO) database, including Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC) categories. Enrichment significance was assessed using a hypergeometric test framework implemented in FUMA, and multiple testing correction was applied using Bonferroni-adjusted P-values.

Such analyses not only enhance our biological understanding of pleiotropic genes but also provide valuable information for further functional studies and mechanistic exploration.

This final layer integrates genetic findings with biological interpretation, enabling the translation of statistical associations into pathway-level insights relevant to immune regulation, neuronal signaling, and GBA mechanisms.

Local genetic correlation analysis

To identify local genetic loci with significant genetic correlations that might be overlooked in the genome-wide analysis, we applied LAVA analysis to the 8 paired traits. This analysis revealed 274 regions (11.0%) out of 2495 in 8 psychiatric disorders with significant local genetic correlations with IBD (pval_bonferroni < 0.05) (201 independent regions) (Fig. 3A, B, and Supplementary Table 3).

These 274 significant regions were identified across all tested IBD–psychiatric disorder pairs, indicating that approximately one-tenth of the genome exhibits locus-specific shared genetic effects beyond the global correlation signal. The identification of 201 independent loci suggests that the shared genetic architecture is not confined to a small number of hotspots but is distributed across multiple quasi-independent genomic segments, highlighting structured sub-genomic pleiotropy.

Region 464 (chr3: 47588462–50387742) and 960 (chr6: 31320269–31427209) were simultaneously identified in 5 out of 5 trait pairs. Region 464 was negatively correlated in IBD-ADHD and IBD-MDD (rho < 0), and positively correlated in IBD-ANX, IBD-ASD, and IBD-SCZ (rho > 0). Region 953 (chr6: 28666365–29529755) is located in the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) region (chromosome 6: 25–35 Mb), which is typically excluded from genetic analysis due to its complex LD structure.

MTAG analysis of pleiotropic loci and ANNOVAR functional annotation

Using MTAG for cross-trait meta-analysis, we identified 1161 significant LD-independent SNV associations across 274 local genetic correlation regions (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Table 4). These signals met the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P_bonferroni < 0.05). Because certain SNVs were detected in more than one IBD–psychiatric disorder pair, the total number of unique genomic variants after removing cross-trait duplicates was 1125. This distinction reflects cross-disorder pleiotropy rather than linkage redundancy, as the 1161 associations were already LD-independent signals identified through prior clumping procedures.

The distribution of LD-independent associations was highly uneven across trait pairs. The majority were observed in IBD–MDD (n = 992), followed by IBD–SCZ (n = 89), IBD–OCD (n = 42), and IBD–ADHD (n = 12).

Among the 1125 unique SNVs, most were specific to a single psychiatric–IBD trait pair, whereas a subset of variants were shared across multiple disorders, indicating locus-level cross-trait pleiotropy. Notably, rs749910 and rs2076756 were detected in four trait pairs and both mapped to the NOD2 gene. Additionally, nine variants mapping to RPL23AP12 were identified in three trait pairs (Supplementary Table 4), further supporting recurrent locus-level overlap.

Functional annotation using ANNOVAR revealed that among the 1125 unique SNVs, 492 were intronic variants, 476 were intergenic variants, and 11 were exonic variants. Fifty-four variants were predicted to be potentially deleterious (CADD > 12.37).

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis demonstrated that mapped genes were significantly expressed in multiple brain regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, cortex, hippocampus, frontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, hypothalamus, and substantia nigra (Supplementary Table 5). Among the 1125 unique variants, 21 SNVs were detected in at least two psychiatric–IBD trait pairs, reflecting cross-disorder pleiotropy. A smaller subset (n = 11) was shared across three or more trait pairs. Detailed variant-level associations, including trait pairs and corresponding genomic regions, are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

SMR analysis of shared pleiotropic genes

Annotating GWAS variants to genes based solely on proximity may be overly simplistic, as it may overlook pleiotropic effects and fail to account for tissue-specific gene expression. To address this, we used SMR to identify pleiotropic genes in regions with significant genetic correlations. Through these methods, we identified 516 genes, primarily in Blood and Brain tissues, (P_bonferroni < 0.05, P_HEIDI > 0.01) (Fig. 5A, C, and Supplementary Table 7).

Among these, 203 genes were identified using blood-derived eQTL datasets, whereas 245 genes were identified using brain tissue eQTL datasets, reflecting both peripheral immune and central nervous system contributions to cross-trait genetic architecture. The remaining genes were detected in overlapping or additional tissue datasets as detailed in Supplementary Table 7.

By intersecting these genes with the 80 genes obtained through MAGMA analysis from SNPs in MTAG analysis (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 6), we identified 21 key genes (RERE, MIER1, IL10, ZNF445, ZKSCAN7, ZNF660, ZNF197, KLHDC8B, GPX1, DAG1, BSN, APEH, AMZ1, ANKK1, STK24, SNX20, VAMP2, AURKB, SLC12A5, EP300, ZC3H7B) (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Table 8). Specifically, MAGMA was used to prioritize genes based on aggregated SNP-level association signals from MTAG, whereas SMR identified genes whose expression levels were potentially causally associated with the traits. To increase robustness and biological plausibility, we defined key genes as those supported by both approaches—that is, genes showing significant gene-level association in MAGMA and satisfying SMR significance criteria (P_bonferroni < 0.05) with no evidence of heterogeneity in HEIDI testing (P_HEIDI > 0.01).

This intersection-based filtering strategy integrates variant-level association evidence with gene-expression–mediated effects, thereby prioritizing genes supported by both statistical aggregation and functional mediation signals.

These genes were significantly enriched in several biologically coherent pathways. In KEGG analysis, enrichment was observed in the Polycomb Repressive Complex and FoxO signaling pathway, as well as in Cell Cycle and JAK–STAT signaling pathways (Fig. 5E, F; Supplementary Table 9).

The Polycomb Repressive Complex is involved in epigenetic gene silencing and long-term transcriptional regulation, suggesting that chromatin-mediated control of inflammatory and neuronal gene expression may contribute to shared disease susceptibility. The FoxO signaling pathway plays a central role in oxidative stress response, apoptosis, and metabolic regulation, processes that are dysregulated in both chronic intestinal inflammation and neuropsychiatric disorders. The JAK–STAT signaling pathway is a key mediator of cytokine signaling and immune activation, directly linking inflammatory responses in IBD to neuroimmune mechanisms implicated in psychiatric conditions.

GOBP enrichment highlighted terms such as regeneration, response to inactivity, and cell death in response to oxidative stress, indicating involvement in tissue repair, stress adaptation, and apoptosis.

In GOCC category, enrichment in GABAergic synapse, synaptic vesicle membrane, and exocytic vesicle membrane suggests perturbations in neurotransmitter release and inhibitory synaptic signaling. These findings are consistent with evidence that GABAergic dysfunction contributes to anxiety, mood disorders, and schizophrenia, while inflammatory mediators may influence synaptic plasticity within the GBA framework.

In GOMF category, enriched terms included RNA polymerase II transcription factor binding, dystroglycan binding, and protein serine/threonine kinase activity, reflecting transcriptional regulation and intracellular signaling processes relevant to immune activation and neuronal communication.

Collectively, these enriched pathways converge on interconnected processes involving immune signaling, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and synaptic transmission, supporting a biologically integrated GBA mechanism underlying the shared genetic architecture between IBD and psychiatric disorders.

Discussion

The comorbidity between IBD and psychiatric disorders has long been recognized in epidemiological and clinical studies, yet the biological basis underlying this association remains incompletely understood. Increasing evidence suggests that immune dysregulation, chronic systemic inflammation, and gut microbiota alterations may contribute to both intestinal pathology and neuropsychiatric vulnerability [10]. Large-scale genetic studies have further demonstrated partial genetic overlap between immune-mediated diseases and psychiatric conditions, indicating that shared heritability may underlie these comorbid patterns [23]. However, prior investigations have largely relied on global genetic correlation approaches, leaving uncertainty regarding whether shared risk is driven by specific loci, functional genes, or distributed polygenic effects.

Against this background, our findings provide multi-layered genetic evidence supporting a shared genetic architecture between IBD and several psychiatric disorders. The observed positive global genetic correlations between IBD and ADHD, anxiety disorder, ASD, OCD, and PTSD are consistent with previous reports indicating immune–psychiatric genetic convergence [23, 42]. Importantly, however, our local genetic correlation analyses reveal that shared genetic risk is not uniformly distributed across the genome but is region-specific and biologically heterogeneous. The identification of both positive and negative correlations across distinct loci suggests that certain genomic regions may confer convergent susceptibility, whereas others may exert antagonistic or trait-specific effects. This regional heterogeneity extends prior genome-wide findings by demonstrating that the genetic relationship between IBD and psychiatric disorders is structured and locus-dependent rather than solely reflecting diffuse polygenic overlap [18].

Pleiotropic variants were widely distributed between psychiatric disorders and IBD, with certain traits standing out in specific genomic regions. For example, loci 464 (chr3: 47588462–50387742) and 960 (chr6: 31320269–31427209) were identified in five trait pairs. Locus 464 showed negative correlations (rho < 0) for IBD-ADHD and IBD-MDD and positive correlations (rho > 0) for IBD-ANX, IBD-ASD, and IBD-SCZ. Locus 960 showed positive correlations (rho > 0) for IBD-ANX, IBD-BD, IBD-MDD, IBD-PTSD, and IBD-SCZ. Additionally, loci 953 (chr6: 28666365–29529755), 967 (chr6: 32682214–32897998), and 2076 (chr15: 74458114–76401952) were detected in four trait pairs. The repeated identification of loci 464 and 960 across multiple trait pairs suggests that these regions may function as key genomic hubs underlying shared gut–brain genetic liability. Notably, locus 960 lies within the extended MHC region, one of the most consistently replicated loci in immune-mediated diseases and psychiatric disorders, particularly schizophrenia and mood disorders [42, 43]. The uniformly positive local correlations observed at locus 960 across several psychiatric phenotypes and IBD may therefore reflect convergent immune-related mechanisms, such as antigen presentation, inflammatory signaling, or microglial activation pathways that influence both intestinal inflammation and neuropsychiatric vulnerability. In contrast, locus 464 demonstrated both positive and negative local genetic correlations depending on the psychiatric phenotype, suggesting biologically heterogeneous or antagonistic pleiotropic effects. Such bidirectional effects have been observed in cross-trait genetic studies and may indicate that certain alleles confer risk for one disorder while being neutral or even protective for another [23]. Interestingly, this chromosomal region has also been reported as a recurrent local genetic correlation hotspot in large-scale cross-trait analyses of psychiatric and metabolic phenotypes, further supporting its pleiotropic nature [18]. The negative correlations observed for IBD-ADHD and IBD-MDD at this locus may reflect divergent regulatory influences on neurodevelopmental or stress-response pathways, whereas the positive correlations with anxiety, ASD, and schizophrenia suggest partially shared susceptibility mechanisms. Together, these findings indicate that the genetic relationship between IBD and psychiatric disorders is locus-dependent rather than uniformly concordant, with certain regions driving convergent risk and others contributing to disorder-specific divergence.

Through MTAG analysis and functional annotation using ANNOVAR, we found that rs749910 and rs2076756 were detected in four trait pairs (ASD, OCD, PTSD, and SCZ), and both mapped to the gene NOD2. The variants rs9977672, rs9808651, rs4817988, rs4817986, rs2836884, rs2836883, rs2836882, rs2836881, and rs2836878 were identified in three trait pairs (ASD, BD, and SCZ), all mapping to the gene RPL23AP12.

NOD2 (Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2) is a key innate immune receptor and a member of the NOD-like receptor (NLR) family [44, 45]. It is primarily expressed in macrophages, dendritic cells, and intestinal epithelial cells [45]. The main function of NOD2 is to recognize specific microbial molecular patterns and to regulate cellular processes such as inflammation and autophagy by activating downstream signaling pathways [45, 46]. NOD2 primarily recognizes peptidoglycan fragments in bacterial cell walls, specifically muramyl dipeptide (MDP) [47, 48]. Upon recognition of MDP, NOD2 undergoes a conformational change through its nucleotide-binding domain (NBD), activating its downstream signaling pathways [48]. Activation of NOD2 is commonly accompanied by activation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways. NOD2 binds to receptor-interacting protein kinase 2 (RIPK2), promoting the translocation of NF-κB transcription factors into the nucleus and subsequently inducing the expression of inflammation-related genes, including various cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [49]. NOD2 also participates in cellular autophagy. Upon detecting bacterial MDP signals, NOD2 promotes the formation of autophagosomes, aiding cells in clearing pathogens, which is crucial for maintaining the microenvironmental balance in the intestine and other barrier tissues [50].

Beyond its canonical immune role, accumulating evidence suggests that NOD2 may influence GBA signaling through modulation of microbiota composition, intestinal barrier integrity, and systemic inflammatory tone [51]. Dysfunctional NOD2 signaling has been shown to alter microbial community structure and increase mucosal permeability, thereby facilitating chronic low-grade inflammation that may affect central nervous system function via immune–neuroendocrine pathways. Importantly, inflammatory mediators downstream of NOD2 activation, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression, schizophrenia, and other stress-related disorders [49].

Current studies indicate that mutations in NOD2 are significantly associated with CD and other forms of IBD [48, 52]. Dysfunction in NOD2 can impair the defensive functions of the intestinal barrier, leading to gut microbiota imbalance and ultimately chronic inflammation [52]. Specific NOD2 gene mutations, such as Leu1007fs [48], R702W [53], and G908R [53], have been closely associated with susceptibility to IBD in patients. In a clinical study, Bryan P. Kline found that the rs2076756 variant is associated with CD [54], while Schieffer KM’s research linked it to dysbiosis in gut microbiota [55]. Additionally, Yuanhao Yang et al. discovered that the rs2076756 variant is associated with multiple sclerosis and IBD [56], and Kugathasan S. identified an association between rs2836878 and IBD [57]. These findings align with our study; however, there is currently no research on its effects on psychiatric disorders. Other studies suggest that NOD2 plays a role in the GBA, particularly in regulating gut microbiota and inflammatory responses [55]. Abnormalities in NOD2 may increase susceptibility to certain psychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety, by altering neuroimmune responses [14].

In the combined analysis of SMR and MAGMA, we found that key genes AURKB, EP300, and IL10 were significantly enriched in the Polycomb repressive complex and FoxO signaling pathways. These pathways are involved in long-term gene expression regulation, cell cycle control, and cell survival, playing a crucial role in cellular stress responses by modulating the repression and activation of specific genes. This is likely related to the downstream pathway activation induced by specific NOD2 locus variations such as rs749910 and rs2076756. Importantly, these enriched pathways converge on core biological processes linking immune regulation, epigenetic control, and cellular stress adaptation. For instance, IL10 is a central anti-inflammatory cytokine that modulates intestinal immune tolerance and has also been implicated in neuroinflammatory regulation and psychiatric vulnerability [58]. EP300 functions as a histone acetyltransferase and transcriptional coactivator, integrating inflammatory and stress-response signals at the epigenetic level, thereby influencing long-term gene expression programs in both immune and neural cells [59]. AURKB plays a role in cell-cycle progression and chromosomal stability, processes that are increasingly recognized as relevant to immune cell proliferation and neurodevelopmental regulation. The enrichment of Polycomb-related pathways further suggests involvement of epigenetic repression mechanisms that can produce sustained transcriptional changes in response to chronic inflammation. Such epigenetic remodeling has been proposed as a mechanism through which peripheral immune activation may induce long-term neurobiological alterations within the GBA [60, 61]. Similarly, the FoxO signaling pathway is a key regulator of oxidative stress response and apoptosis, linking inflammatory burden with cellular survival mechanisms in both intestinal epithelium and neural tissues.

Additionally, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway is a crucial signaling pathway in immune and inflammatory responses, often exhibiting abnormalities in inflammatory diseases and certain neuropsychiatric disorders. Dysregulation of these signaling pathways may affect processes such as cell survival, regeneration, and apoptosis, and may be either activated or inhibited in chronic inflammatory diseases like IBD and psychiatric disorders. Pathways such as the cell cycle and cell death in response to oxidative stress indicate how cells regulate proliferation and death when responding to external stimuli or endogenous damage. Chronic inflammatory states (such as IBD) and psychiatric disorders are both associated with increased oxidative stress and cell apoptosis. Regeneration and response to inactivity (cellular response to a quiescent state) may be related to tissue repair and adaptive regulation. In both IBD and psychiatric disorders, cellular damage and repair, as well as chronic stress responses, may be interconnected processes. Structures and pathways related to synapses, such as GABAergic synapses and synaptic vesicle membranes, are directly involved in neural signal transmission. Dysfunction of GABAergic synapses is commonly seen in many psychiatric disorders, such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia. The GBA has been extensively studied in IBD patients, where interactions between gut inflammation and neural signaling may affect central nervous system function through abnormal synaptic transmission. Taken together, these enriched pathways form an integrated biological framework in which immune signaling, epigenetic regulation, oxidative stress response, and synaptic function intersect. This integrative pattern directly aligns with the GBA model proposed in the Introduction and addresses a key knowledge gap: while previous studies have demonstrated global genetic correlation between immune and psychiatric disorders, the specific functional pathways mediating this overlap remained unclear. Our findings suggest that shared susceptibility may be transmitted through coordinated immune–epigenetic–neurobiological mechanisms rather than isolated inflammatory signals alone.

However, this study also has several limitations. First, our data primarily comes from individuals of European descent, so these results may not be applicable to other ethnicities. Specifically, population-specific LD patterns and allele frequency distributions may influence both global and local genetic correlation estimates. Certain loci—particularly within complex regions such as the MHC—exhibit substantial ancestry-dependent LD structure, which may alter the magnitude or even the direction of local genetic correlations in non-European populations. Therefore, the pleiotropic regions identified in this study may not fully replicate in populations with different genetic architectures. This limitation affects the generalizability of our findings and underscores the need for cross-ancestry replication analyses. Second, although we have employed various statistical methods to validate the reliability of the study findings, the mechanisms of pleiotropic effects still require further functional experiments for validation. Importantly, our inferences regarding shared biological pathways and gene-level effects are based on statistical association and integrative genomic modeling (e.g., SMR and pathway enrichment), which cannot definitively establish molecular causality. While SMR reduces the likelihood of confounding due to LD, it does not replace direct experimental validation. Without functional assays—such as gene expression perturbation studies or cellular immune–neural interaction models—the mechanistic interpretations proposed here remain hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. This may limit the robustness of pathway-level conclusions. Additionally, due to limitations in the data sources, we cannot exclude the potential impact of environmental factors on genetic correlation analyses. Gene–environment interactions, including stress exposure, microbiome composition, diet, and inflammatory burden, may modulate the penetrance of shared genetic risk. Because GWAS summary statistics do not capture environmental heterogeneity, the observed genetic overlap may represent only part of a broader multifactorial interaction framework. Therefore, future research should focus on the genetic associations between psychiatric disorders and IBD across different ethnic and environmental backgrounds, and combine functional studies to explore the specific mechanisms of these pleiotropic genes.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings through multi-level experimental and translational approaches. Specifically, functional assays such as CRISPR-mediated perturbation of NOD2 and other identified genes in intestinal epithelial cells or microglial models could clarify their causal roles in immune–neural signaling. Longitudinal cohort studies integrating microbiome profiling, inflammatory biomarkers, and psychiatric phenotyping would help determine how shared genetic variants influence GBA dynamics over time. In addition, cross-ancestry replication analyses and fine-mapping in diverse populations are necessary to assess the generalizability of locus-specific effects. Together, such approaches would provide mechanistic validation and improve the translational relevance of the pleiotropic genetic signals identified in this study.

Conclusions

This study provides multi-layered genomic evidence that the relationship between IBD and psychiatric disorders is not incidental but is partly rooted in shared and locus-dependent genetic architecture. Rather than reflecting diffuse polygenic overlap alone, our findings demonstrate that specific genomic regions—particularly immune-related loci and pleiotropic genes such as NOD2—may function as structured interfaces linking intestinal inflammation with neuropsychiatric vulnerability. The convergence of immune signaling, epigenetic regulation, oxidative stress response, and synaptic pathways suggests that gut–brain comorbidity may arise from coordinated biological networks rather than isolated inflammatory mechanisms. By integrating global and local genetic correlation with gene-level and pathway analyses, this study advances understanding of how immune dysregulation may translate into neuropsychiatric risk. These insights refine the conceptual framework of the GBA and provide a genomic basis for future translational research targeting shared immune–neural mechanisms.
