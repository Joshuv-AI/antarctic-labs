A case of maturity‐onset diabetes of the young with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant
Authors: Tomofumi Takayoshi, Yushi Hirota, Kenji Sugawara, Aki Sugano, Mika Ohta, Takehito Takeuchi, Akane Yamamoto, Wataru Satake, Yutaka Takaoka, Wataru Ogawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionMaturity‐onset diabetes of the young (MODY) accounts for at least 1%–5% of diabetes cases and is usually caused by single gene variants. Accurate diagnosis of MODY is important for effective management, especially in young individuals who are lean and lack islet autoantibodies. Only a few MODY cases associated with multiple gene variants have been reported. We here describe a rare case of MODY with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant.Materials and MethodsWe conducted an extensive clinical analysis and explored the complex genetic architecture of the case in order to provide insight into the etiology of MODY. The candidate variants were identified by comprehensive whole‐exome and Sanger sequencing, and their functional impact was assessed by analysis of their effects on the surface charge or structural stability of HNF1A and NEUROD1.ResultsThe proband, a 21‐year‐old woman with early‐onset diabetes, was found to harbor a pathogenic HNF1A p.Arg159Trp variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser variant of uncertain significance. The NEUROD1 variant was also present in her mother and sister, both of whom manifested impaired glucose tolerance, but was absent in her father and brother. Structural analyses suggested that the HNF1A variant alters the surface charge of the protein, whereas the NEUROD1 variant may affect protein structural stability.ConclusionWe have identified an unusual case of MODY with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant of uncertain significance, and structural analyses suggested that the NEUROD1 variant may affect protein structure.Keywords: HNF1A, Maturity‐onset diabetes of the young, NEUROD1

INTRODUCTION

Maturity‐onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an autosomal dominant form of diabetes that is usually caused by a single gene variant. Although MODY is relatively uncommon, with an incidence of about 1%–5% of diabetes cases 1 , 2 , epidemiological data suggest that there may be a number of undiagnosed cases among individuals diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes 2 . Individuals with a lean physique and a family history of early‐onset diabetes diagnosed before the age of 25 years have typically been considered potential MODY cases 3 . However, given that some cases of MODY arise from de novo variants 4 , 5 , 6 , it is now recommended that the likelihood of MODY be assessed in individuals under 35 years of age who test negative for islet‐associated autoantibodies and have no history of obesity, irrespective of their family medical history 7 .

Variants of NEUROD1 8 , 9 and HNF1A 10 have been found to give rise to MODY, with both of these genes contributing to the development and maintenance of the function of pancreatic beta cells. HNF1A is a transcription factor that is expressed predominantly in the liver, pancreas, and kidneys 11 . It orchestrates the transcription of genes integral to glucose and lipid metabolism, and thereby plays a pivotal role in insulin secretion. Pathogenic variants of this gene are associated with MODY3, a subtype of diabetes that is characterized by impaired insulin secretion and for which sulfonylureas have been found to be highly effective 12 .

NEUROD1 is a basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor that is essential for the development and proper function of pancreatic beta cells 13 , 14 . It plays a key role in pancreatic morphogenesis and regulates the transcription of genes important for glucose homeostasis, including those for insulin, sulfonylurea receptor (SUR1), and glucokinase (GCK) 15 . Pathogenic variants of NEUROD1 are linked to MODY6, which is also characterized by impaired insulin secretion 16 . Whereas cases of MODY6 reported elsewhere have generally been mild, case reports from Japan have documented instances of diabetic ketoacidosis, potentially due to the inherently reduced insulin secretory capacity apparent in the Japanese population 8 . The clinical presentation of NEUROD1‐related MODY6 appears to be variable even among Japanese. Some cases present initially with diabetic ketoacidosis but do not progress to an insulin‐dependent state, whereas others may eventually require insulin therapy 8 , 16 . This variability suggests that additional genetic or environmental factors may influence disease progression.

Cases of MODY associated with variants in two genes among those frequently responsible for the condition—including HNF1A, HNF4A, HNF1B, and GCK—have been reported 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 . For example, cases with variants in both GCK and HNF1A have been documented. In these and other reports, some individuals manifest clinical symptoms with a single variant, whereas others harboring both variants remain confined to the impaired fasting glucose range 17 . Furthermore, the advent of whole‐exome sequencing has uncovered more MODY cases with concurrent variants in multiple genes 24 . Nevertheless, the number of such documented cases remains low, underscoring the need for further investigation, particularly for studies that incorporate structural analysis of the variant proteins.

In the present study, we describe a case of MODY with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant of uncertain significance, and we performed an in silico analysis to explore their possible structural effects.

METHODS

Clinical diagnostic criteria

Diabetes and impaired glucose tolerance were defined according to the Japan Diabetes Society criteria 25 . The plasma glucose thresholds used in these definitions are consistent with the World Health Organization criteria 26 . Diabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL, a 2‐h plasma glucose level of ≥200 mg/dL during a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test, a casual plasma glucose level of ≥200 mg/dL, or an HbA1c level of ≥6.5%. Impaired glucose tolerance was defined as a 2‐h plasma glucose level of 140–199 mg/dL during a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test.

Gene sequencing analysis

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from individuals suspected of having MODY and her family members. Genomic DNA was isolated and subjected to whole‐exon sequencing. Library preparation for the whole‐exon sequencing was performed using the SureSelect XT Low Input Kit, the SureSelect Human All Exon V6 capture library, and the SureSelect XT Low Input Dual Index P5 Indexed Adapters (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). High‐throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 3,000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA), generating 100 bp paired‐end reads. Comprehensive analysis was conducted to identify pathogenic variants potentially implicated in early‐onset diabetes, including variants in the 14 established MODY‐associated genes: HNF4A, GCK, HNF1A, PDX1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, KLF11, CEL, PAX4, INS, BLK, ABCC8, KCNJ11, and APPL1. For variants with potential pathogenicity, confirmatory direct Sanger sequencing was performed to establish a definitive diagnosis. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the initiation of genetic testing.

In silico structural analysis of WT and variant forms of HNF1A and NEUROD1

The three‐dimensional (3D) structure of wild‐type (WT) human HNF1A was obtained from Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 1IC8 chain B). The structure of the R159W variant of HNF1A was generated with MOE software (Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, Quebec, Canada) and subsequently subjected to structural optimization by GROMACS as described previously 27 , with slight modifications. The water‐protein system was gradually heated from 0 to 310 K over 250 ps, after which a 20,000‐ps production run was performed with the NPT ensemble and a unit of 1 fs. The convergence based on the trajectory result was stable after 3,000 ps, and the quality of each 3D structure was ascertained with the PROCHECK program 28 . PROCHECK yielded 0% in the disallowed regions of the Ramachandran plot, and provided insight into the correctness of the modeled structure 29 . Electrostatic surface potential was analyzed with the Adaptive Poisson–Boltzmann Solver (APBS) program (http://www.poisson‐boltzmann.org/apbs) and then displayed with PyMOL software (PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, version 1.3, Schrödinger LLC).

Molecular simulation analysis for human NEUROD1 was performed as described previously 30 , with slight modifications. The 3D structure of human NEUROD1 (amino acids 101–159) was analyzed by MOE software and with the structure of the mouse E47‐NEUROD1 complex (PDB: 2QL2) as a template. In addition, the 3D structure of another segment of human NEUROD1 (amino acids 80–105) was analyzed separately with QUARK software. The two segments were then superimposed and connected through the homology modeling function of MOE software to generate an extended form of NEUROD1 (amino acids 80–159). The extended structure of human NEUROD1 and the structure of human E47 (PDB: 3U5V chain A) were aligned with the structure of the mouse E47‐NEUROD1 complex (PDB: 2QL2) to obtain a model of the human E47‐NEUROD1 complex. The WT and variant forms (p.Arg98Ser, p.Arg103Pro 9 , p.Glu110Lys 31 , p.Arg111Leu 32 , 33 , and p.Ser159Pro 34 ) were subjected to 20,000‐ps molecular dynamics simulation using GROMACS with the AMBER03 force field as described in the previous research 35 , 36 . Time‐specific data from the simulations were then overlaid to assess the structural effects of the amino acid substitutions. For each variant, the amplitude angles were analyzed and compared with those of the WT using Student's t‐test.

Ethical considerations

All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committees on human experimentation (institutional and national), and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and later versions. Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals before they were included in the report. This investigation was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine (approval no. B190036; approval date, 29 May 2019).

RESULTS

Case

The proband, a 21‐year‐old female, underwent routine health checkups during infancy and childhood, with no notable findings. At a school health checkup at 15 years of age, glucosuria was detected, prompting her to seek medical care. At the time of the present study, her hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level was 7.2%, and her height and body mass were 159.8 cm and 49.9 kg, respectively, with a body mass index (BMI) of 19.5 kg/m2. Her physical, physiological, and intellectual development were unremarkable, and no neurological abnormalities were noted. She had a fasting glycemia of 88 mg/dL and fasting serum C‐peptide concentration of 1.1 ng/mL, with a postprandial glycemia of 145 mg/dL and serum C‐peptide level of 4.5 ng/mL at 2 h. A 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) revealed an insulinogenic index of 0.38 (Figure 1), and her 24‐h urinary C‐peptide excretion was 21.1 μg/day. Tests for antibodies to glutamic acid decarboxylase, to insulin, or to islet antigen 2 as well as for islet cell antibodies were negative. Abdominal imaging by computed tomography and ultrasonography revealed no abnormal findings, including morphological abnormalities of the pancreas or liver.

Results of 75‐g oral glucose tolerance tests for the proband at 15 and 21 years of age and for her family members. Plasma glucose and immunoreactive insulin (IRI) levels during the OGTT are shown.

The proband's father was 63 years of age and had a height of 165 cm and body mass of 52 kg, with a BMI of 19.1 kg/m2. He had no notable medical history, with his HbA1c level being in the normal range and a 75‐g OGTT revealing normal glucose tolerance. Her mother, aged 52 years, had a height of 164 cm and body mass of 68 kg (BMI, 25.3 kg/m2), with no neurological abnormalities observed. Whereas she had not previously been diagnosed with any abnormalities of glucose metabolism, a 75‐g OGTT performed as part of the proband's workup revealed impaired glucose tolerance, with a glucose level at 2 h of 191 mg/dL (Figure 1). The proband's brother, aged 25 years, had a height of 171 cm and body mass of 55 kg, with a BMI of 18.8 kg/m2. He had not been diagnosed with any abnormalities in glucose metabolism, and a 75‐g OGTT revealed normal glucose tolerance (Figure 1). Finally, the proband's sister, aged 19 years, had a height of 160 cm and body mass of 50 kg (BMI, 19.5 kg/m2), with no neurological abnormalities observed. Although she had not previously been diagnosed with any abnormalities of glucose metabolism, a 75‐g OGTT revealed impaired glucose tolerance, with a glucose level at 2 h of 161 mg/dL (Figure 1).

Genetic analysis

Whole‐exon sequencing of the proband revealed variants that give rise to amino acid substitutions in HNF1A p.Arg159Trp (c.475C>T, NM_001406915.1) and NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser (c.292C>A, NM_002500.5), using the Human GRCh38 reference genome (UCSC Genome Browser; http://genome.ucsc.edu/) (Figures 2, 3, 4). The HNF1A p.Arg159Trp variant corresponds to rs765432081 and has a CADD score of 31, whereas the NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser variant has a CADD score of 29.5, as assessed using the UCSC Genome Browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu/). Notably, no genome‐wide association study (GWAS) signals have been reported for the NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser variant to date. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, HNF1A p.Arg159Trp was classified as pathogenic, whereas NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser met the PM2 and PP3 criteria and was therefore classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). In gnomAD v4.1.1, the NEUROD1 c.292C>A (p.Arg98Ser; GRCh38: 2‐181678569‐G‐T, rs764333074) variant had an allele frequency of 0.000006195 in the total population (10/1,614,206 alleles), with no homozygotes, indicating that this variant is very rare in the general population.

Segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing showed that the proband's mother and sister, but not the father and brother, harbored the NEUROD1 variant (Figure 4). The HNF1A variant was not shared by any other family member examined. The HNF1A variant has previously been identified in individuals with MODY 37 , whereas the NEUROD1 variant has not been established as a pathogenic MODY variant.

In silico simulation analysis

According to the reported 3D structure of human HNF1A (PDB: 1IC8), Arg159 is located within the DNA‐binding domain of the protein but appears not to directly contribute to DNA binding. In the p.Arg159Trp variant, the surface of the DNA‐binding region is raised, and the electrostatic potential has shifted to a charge close to neutral, contrasting with the positive charge observed for the WT protein (Figure 5).

The DNA‐binding region is outlined in yellow. In the p.Arg159Trp variant, the surface of the DNA‐binding region is raised, and the electrostatic potential is shifted toward a near‐neutral charge, contrasting with the positively charged surface of the WT protein. The arrows indicate Arg159 (R159) and Trp159 (W159). The box contains cartoon representations of the protein from corresponding angles. MM, molecular modeling.

Whereas the 3D structure of human NEUROD1 has not been reported, the structure of the mouse ortholog complexed with the transcription factor E47 has been elucidated 38 . Given the high amino acid sequence similarity between human and mouse NEUROD1, we used the structure of the mouse E47‐NEUROD1 complex to calculate the 3D structure of a segment of human NEUROD1 including amino acid 98. The structural differences and fluctuations of NEUROD1 complexed with E47 were compared between the WT and R98S variant proteins by molecular dynamics analysis (Figure S1). The structural convergence of the R98S variant was disrupted as a result of the amino acid substitution, suggesting that the variant gives rise to protein instability and a potential reduction in DNA‐binding affinity.

The Arg103Pro variant, as well as other previously reported variants including Glu110Lys, Arg111Leu, and Ser159Pro similarly demonstrated disrupted structural convergence, showing structural features consistent with those observed in previously reported NEUROD1 variants.

DISCUSSION

We here report a case of diabetes that developed at a relatively young age in the absence of obesity. Genomic testing revealed that the proband harbors variants that give rise to amino acid substitutions in HNF1A and NEUROD1, both of which are causative genes for MODY 1 . Whereas the variant of HNF1A has been previously identified in MODY cases, the NEUROD1 variant has not been established as a pathogenic MODY variant. Given the established pathogenicity of the HNF1A variant, the diabetic phenotype of the proband can be primarily explained by this variant. The facts that family members with the NEUROD1 variant manifested impaired glucose tolerance and that the amino acid substitution may influence the three‐dimensional structure and function of the encoded protein, although functional validation remains necessary, suggest that this rare variant may have modestly modified glucose metabolism rather than acting as an independent monogenic cause of MODY.

Variants of HNF1A are a relatively common cause of MODY, accounting for 13.9% of such cases in Japan 39 . Reports from other countries have indicated a higher frequency, with HNF1A pathogenic variants being responsible for 52% of diagnosed MODY cases in the United Kingdom 40 . In contrast, MODY associated with NEUROD1 variants (MODY6) is rare, with the first Japanese case documented in 2018 16 . The frequency of reported MODY6 in Japan remains low, underscoring the need to accumulate more cases for analysis. NEUROD1 variants show incomplete penetrance and give rise to a diverse clinical spectrum of conditions, ranging from classical MODY phenotypes to manifestations akin to type 2 diabetes 8 . The age of disease onset also spans a wide range, from adolescence to late adulthood. Most individuals with MODY6 are non–insulin dependent, although undiagnosed cases may develop persistent hyperglycemia and associated complications such as microangiopathy 8 . Prompt genetic diagnosis is key for customization of therapeutic strategies and to mitigate the risk of complications. It has been suggested that maternal inheritance may precipitate an earlier onset of diabetes in MODY6, likely as a result of fetal exposure to maternal hyperglycemia during pregnancy 41 . In Japan, MODY6 associated with NEUROD1 variants has been documented to emerge at a considerably younger age than elsewhere, typically between 10 and 14 years, with instances of ketoacidosis also having been reported 8 . This precocious onset has been hypothesized to stem from the intrinsically lower insulin secretory capacity characteristic of Japanese individuals.

The proband of the present study inherited a NEUROD1 variant maternally and also harbors an HNF1A variant, which together raise the possibility that the combination of the two variants influenced disease manifestation. In contrast, the proband's younger sister, who is not obese and is of a similarly young age, manifests impaired glucose tolerance despite possessing only the NEUROD1 variant. These observations suggest that the isolated influence of the NEUROD1 variant on glucose metabolism may be relatively minor. The diabetes of the proband can be primarily explained by the pathogenic HNF1A variant, although the coexisting NEUROD1 variant may have modified glucose metabolism. The impaired glucose tolerance observed in the younger sister may be associated with the NEUROD1 variant; however, its independent contribution remains uncertain.

From a clinical perspective, identification of the pathogenic HNF1A variant supports a molecular diagnosis of HNF1A‐related MODY and may inform treatment selection, including consideration of sulfonylureas and other therapeutic options, as well as genetic counseling and follow‐up of family members. The coexisting NEUROD1 p.Arg98Ser variant may be relevant to the impaired glucose tolerance observed in family members, but it should be interpreted cautiously as a variant of uncertain significance rather than as an established monogenic cause of MODY. Identification of both variants may therefore be useful for explaining the clinical heterogeneity within the family and for guiding long‐term monitoring of glucose metabolism.

MODY was originally attributed to single gene variants with an autosomal dominant inheritance. However, cases with concurrent variants in multiple MODY‐related genes—such as HNF1A and GCK 17 , 18 , HNF1A and HNF4A 19 , 20 , 21 , and HNF1A and HNF1B 22 , 23 —have been identified. Furthermore, with the advent of whole‐exome sequencing, cases with concurrent variants in NEUROD1 and PDX1 24 have also been revealed, suggesting that more cases involving variants in multiple genes may be discovered in the future. Meanwhile, no reports have examined in detail the extent to which individual genetic variants influence pathology. Reports documenting cases with concurrent variants of HNF1A and HNF4A have suggested, on the basis of clinical manifestations, that diabetes in such cases may be of greater severity. Nevertheless, the pathogenic potential of these variants has been characterized solely on the basis of higher probabilities inferred from PolyPHEN scores and SIFT analyses 21 . To our knowledge, there have been no detailed reports of MODY with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant.

In conclusion, we have identified a case of MODY with a pathogenic HNF1A variant and a coexisting NEUROD1 variant of uncertain significance. Whereas reports of MODY cases with concurrent variants in multiple genes are few in number, the widespread application of whole‐exome sequencing is likely to identify more such cases. Further investigation into how combinations of gene variants result in clinical manifestations will increase understanding of MODY pathophysiology and support the development of precision care for this condition.
