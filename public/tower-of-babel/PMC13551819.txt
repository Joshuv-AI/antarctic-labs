Long-term optical stability and surface gloss of CAD/CAM restorative materials under simulated mechanical aging
Authors: Tuğba Aycan Erkal, Mehmet Uğur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe long-term optical performance of CAD/CAM restorative materials is a critical determinant of clinical esthetics. Daily mechanical challenges, such as toothbrushing, may progressively alter surface characteristics; however, the long-term optical behavior of contemporary CAD/CAM materials with different compositions remains insufficiently clarified. Therefore, this study investigated the color stability and surface gloss of different CAD/CAM restorative materials under simulated long-term mechanical aging.MethodsForty-eight disc-shaped specimens (12 × 14 × 2 mm; n = 12 per group) were fabricated from four CAD/CAM restorative materials: feldspathic ceramic (Vita Mark II), zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Vita Suprinity), polymer-infiltrated ceramic (Vita Enamic), and resin-based composite (Brilliant Crios). Specimens were finished according to the manufacturers’ recommendations. Simulated mechanical aging was performed using a toothbrushing simulator under a 2 N load at 1.25 Hz, corresponding to 1, 3, and 5 years of clinical use. Color change was calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE₀₀), and surface gloss was measured with a glossmeter. Data were analyzed using one-way and repeated-measures ANOVA (α = 0.05).ResultsAll materials showed statistically significant changes in color and surface gloss after simulated mechanical aging (p < 0.001). Feldspathic ceramic exhibited the lowest ΔE₀₀ values (0.531 after 5 years) and the highest gloss retention (62.68 GU after 5 years), whereas polymer-infiltrated ceramic and resin-based CAD/CAM materials demonstrated greater color change and gloss reduction over time. Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate showed higher early optical changes followed by relative stabilization. Despite significant inter-material differences, all ΔE₀₀ values remained within clinically acceptable thresholds.ConclusionsThe long-term optical stability of CAD/CAM restorative materials is strongly influenced by material composition. Hybrid and resin-based CAD/CAM materials are more susceptible to brushing-induced surface deterioration and gloss reduction, underscoring the importance of appropriate material selection and surface maintenance for long-term esthetic performance.

Background

Restorative dentistry aims to restore function and achieve long-term esthetic harmony. With the widespread adoption of digital workflows, CAD/CAM materials have become integral to contemporary prosthetic dentistry due to their standardized production and predictable clinical performance [1, 2].

Glass ceramics are widely used due to their favorable optical properties, chemical stability, and wear resistance. However, their brittleness and potential to cause antagonist enamel wear remain clinical concerns [2]. In contrast, resin-based composites offer improved resilience and reparability but are more susceptible to surface degradation, discoloration, and gloss loss over time [3]. To overcome these limitations, hybrid CAD/CAM materials such as polymer-infiltrated ceramics and resin-ceramic composites have been developed. These materials aim to combine the stiffness and color stability of ceramics with the damage tolerance and machinability of resin-based systems, thereby expanding their clinical applications, particularly in minimally invasive restorations [4, 5].

Long-term esthetic success depends not only on intrinsic color and translucency but also on surface properties such as roughness and gloss. Surface alterations influence light reflection and scattering, leading to perceptible changes in color and brightness [6]. A strong relationship between surface roughness and gloss has been demonstrated, with increased roughness leading to reduced gloss and compromised optical performance [7].

In the oral environment, restorations are exposed to mechanical, thermal, and chemical challenges. Daily toothbrushing represents a major source of mechanical abrasion that may progressively affect surface texture and optical properties [8, 9]. Previous studies have shown that simulated toothbrushing influences color stability and surface gloss, primarily through abrasion-induced changes in surface roughness [10, 11]. Although previous studies have investigated the effects of toothbrushing on CAD/CAM materials, most have focused on single material categories, short-term protocols, or did not simultaneously evaluate both color and gloss changes under standardized long-term brushing conditions. Specifically, comparative data across ceramic, hybrid ceramic, and resin-based CAD/CAM materials tested within the same experimental protocol and extended to five-year equivalence remain limited. Furthermore, the relative susceptibility of these material categories to brushing-induced optical degradation has not been systematically quantified using current perceptibility and acceptability thresholds.

Therefore, this in vitro study evaluated the effect of simulated toothbrushing (1, 3, and 5 years) on the color stability (ΔE₀₀) and surface gloss of four CAD/CAM restorative materials. The null hypothesis was that material composition and brushing duration would not affect color change or gloss values.

Methods

Study design and materials

Four commercially available CAD/CAM restorative materials with different compositions were evaluated (Table 1): feldspathic ceramic (Vita Mark II; VM), zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Vita Suprinity; VS), polymer-infiltrated ceramic (Vita Enamic; VE), and resin-based composite (Brilliant Crios; BC). A total of 48 disc-shaped specimens were prepared (n = 12 per material). The sample size was determined based on previous in vitro studies with similar experimental designs and was confirmed by post hoc power analysis.

Specimen preparation and surface standardization

All specimens were prepared with standardized dimensions (12 × 14 × 2 ± 0.05 mm). Shade selection was standardized across materials. Specimens were sectioned under water cooling using a low-speed precision saw (Isomet 1000, Buehler, USA), and thickness was verified using a digital caliper.

Surfaces were sequentially polished using 600-, 800-, 1000-, and 1200-grit silicon carbide papers under water irrigation. Specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min. Final surface treatments were performed according to manufacturers’ recommendations, including glazing for ceramic materials and polishing protocols for hybrid and resin-based materials.

Color and gloss measurements

Baseline color coordinates (CIE L*, a*, b*) were measured using a spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade Advance 4.0, Vita Zahnfabrik, Germany). Surface gloss was measured using a glossmeter (GQC6) at a 60° geometry in accordance with ISO 2813. This geometry represents the standard measurement angle for most materials and provides reliable comparison across different gloss levels.

All measurements were performed at the center of each specimen against a neutral gray background under standardized conditions. Prior to measurements, both devices were calibrated according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Three consecutive measurements were recorded for each specimen, and mean values were calculated.

Color differences after brushing were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE₀₀), which provides improved perceptual uniformity compared with earlier color difference formulas.

Toothbrushing simulation

Specimens were embedded in cylindrical molds and positioned in a toothbrushing simulator (Esetron MF-100, MOD Dental, Turkey). Medium-hard toothbrushes were aligned parallel to specimen surfaces. A slurry of toothpaste (Signal White Now) and distilled water (1:1) was used.

Brushing was performed under a constant load of 2 N at a frequency of 1.25 Hz with linear strokes of 20 mm. The protocol simulated long-term clinical use corresponding to 1, 3, and 5 years (7,300; 21,900; and 36,500 strokes, respectively), based on an estimated 20 strokes per day. Toothbrushes were replaced periodically, and slurry was refreshed regularly to ensure consistent abrasion.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

The effects of material type and brushing duration on color change (ΔE₀₀) and surface gloss were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, including interaction effects between factors. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (η²p), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 considered indicative of small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1), confirming that the sample size (n = 12 per group) provided a statistical power greater than 0.80.

Color change (ΔE₀₀)

Statistically significant differences in ΔE₀₀ values were observed among the materials at all evaluation periods (T1–T0: p = 0.004; T3–T0 and T5–T0: p < 0.001). Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of material type on color change (F(3) = 309.29, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.955), indicating a large effect size.

After 1 year of simulated toothbrushing (T1), the highest color change was recorded for VS (1.893 ± 1.205), followed by VE (1.158 ± 0.852), VM (0.620 ± 0.310), and BC (0.530 ± 0.346). VS exhibited significantly higher ΔE₀₀ values than VM and BC, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed between VE and the other materials.

At the 3-year evaluation (T3), the lowest ΔE₀₀ values were observed for VM (0.858 ± 0.453) and VS (1.379 ± 0.839), while the highest color changes were recorded for VE (2.214 ± 0.760) and BC (2.236 ± 0.911).

After 5 years of simulated brushing (T5), VE (1.928 ± 0.745) and BC (1.682 ± 0.956) continued to exhibit the highest ΔE₀₀ values, whereas VM showed the lowest color change (0.531 ± 0.247).

Within-group analysis revealed statistically significant changes over time for VE and BC (p < 0.01), primarily due to higher ΔE₀₀ values at T3 and T5 compared with T1, whereas no significant differences were observed between T3 and T5. In contrast, VM and VS showed no statistically significant changes over time (p > 0.05).

Gloss

Baseline gloss values differed significantly among the tested materials (p < 0.001). Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant effect of material type on surface gloss (F(3) = 390.44, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.964), reflecting a large effect size. VM exhibited the highest initial gloss (94.34 ± 1.73), followed by VS (88.04 ± 1.19) and VE (85.43 ± 1.42), while BC showed the lowest baseline gloss value (73.84 ± 3.44).

After 1 year of simulated brushing, gloss values decreased in all groups, with the most pronounced reductions observed in VE (63.99 ± 2.57) and BC (63.44 ± 2.40).

At the 3-year evaluation, further reductions in gloss were observed, particularly for VE (43.27 ± 1.97) and BC (44.53 ± 3.24). In contrast, VM (72.68 ± 1.24) and VS (62.50 ± 1.78) maintained comparatively higher gloss values.

After 5 years of simulated toothbrushing, VM preserved the highest gloss value (62.68 ± 1.56), whereas BC exhibited the lowest gloss value (20.09 ± 2.48). At this time point, statistically significant differences among all materials were observed (p < 0.001).

Within-group analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in gloss over time for all materials (p < 0.05), with VE and BC showing the most pronounced reductions.

Discussion

This study evaluated how simulated toothbrushing affects the color stability (ΔE₀₀) and surface gloss of four CAD/CAM restorative materials differing in composition and microstructure over a five-year equivalent period. Both material type and brushing duration significantly influenced optical changes, leading to rejection of the null hypothesis. These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that brushing-induced surface degradation produces increased roughness and reduced gloss in a material-dependent manner [12–14]. The material-dependent pattern of optical degradation observed in this study reflects the distinct microstructural responses of each material category to mechanical abrasion, ranging from glaze surface wear in glass ceramics to organic matrix degradation in hybrid and resin-based materials.

Feldspathic ceramic (VM) exhibited the highest resistance to brushing-induced optical changes, maintaining low ΔE₀₀ values and high gloss throughout the aging periods. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting superior optical stability of glass-based ceramics compared with hybrid and resin-based materials [2, 15].

Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (VS) showed higher color change during the early brushing period, followed by stabilization. This pattern is attributed to initial glaze wear caused by mechanical abrasion [14, 15]. This early increase may be attributed to the rapid wear of the superficial glaze layer in VS, which initially protects the underlying ceramic. Once this layer is partially removed, the more durable lithium silicate matrix appears to resist further optical degradation, resulting in stabilization at later time points. The ΔE₀₀ value observed at T1 (1.893) exceeds the perceptibility threshold and approaches or slightly exceeds the acceptability threshold, indicating that early changes may be clinically noticeable.

In contrast, polymer-infiltrated ceramic (VE) and resin-based composite (BC) exhibited greater susceptibility to color change and gloss reduction, particularly over longer periods. This behavior is mainly related to matrix degradation, where preferential wear of the organic phase exposes filler particles and increases surface roughness [9, 16]. Although VE and BC showed statistically similar color changes at T5, the slightly lower ΔE₀₀ value observed in BC (1.682) compared to VE (1.928) may reflect differences in filler composition and matrix hardness, as resin-based composites often exhibit more uniform surface wear than polymer-infiltrated ceramics. Consistent with the within-group findings, the higher ΔE₀₀ values observed for VE and BC at T3 and T5 compared with T1 indicate that most changes occurred after the first year, with subsequent relative stabilization.

Although shade selection was standardized across all materials (A1/1M2-HT), intrinsic differences in material composition and translucency may have influenced the measured color values and should be considered as a potential confounding factor, as demonstrated by Lo Giudice et al. [17], who reported that spectrophotometric evaluations are sensitive to translucency variations. Differences in translucency affect the interaction of incident light with the material surface and underlying substrate, potentially altering spectrophotometric color coordinates independently of brushing-induced surface changes.

Increased surface roughness promotes diffuse light scattering and reduces specular reflection, leading to gloss loss and perceptible color changes. A strong negative correlation between surface roughness and gloss has been reported, confirming that rougher surfaces exhibit lower gloss values [7, 18].

Beyond statistical significance, the clinical relevance of color changes should be interpreted based on established perceptibility and acceptability thresholds. Previous studies have reported perceptibility and acceptability thresholds of approximately ΔE₀₀ = 0.8 and ΔE₀₀ = 1.8, respectively [6, 19]. Although most ΔE₀₀ values in the present study remained within or close to clinically acceptable limits, it is important to note that statistically significant differences do not necessarily translate into clinically perceptible changes. In particular, the VS group exceeded the perceptibility threshold at T1 (1.893), suggesting that early-stage changes in zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate may be detectable by clinicians or patients. However, the stabilization observed at later time points indicates that these early changes do not continue to accumulate. For VE and BC, while mean values remained near the acceptability threshold at T3 and T5, the combination of color change and progressive gloss reduction may cumulatively compromise the esthetic appearance of restorations in long-term clinical service, even when individual color difference values remain technically within acceptable limits.

Surface gloss is a key determinant of esthetic perception, as it directly governs the balance between specular and diffuse light reflection. As surface roughness increases through abrasive wear, specular reflection diminishes and diffuse scattering predominates, resulting in perceptible gloss loss and altered color appearance [7, 18]. The progressive and material-dependent gloss reductions observed across all groups reflect cumulative surface degradation over the simulated aging periods [8, 9, 20]. While VS and BC exhibited comparable gloss at early time points, VS demonstrated superior gloss retention after 5 years of simulated brushing. Notably, the divergence in gloss trajectories between glass ceramics (VM, VS) and hybrid/resin materials (VE, BC) accelerated beyond the first year, suggesting that the early protective effect of polished or glazed surfaces is overcome by continued abrasive exposure in more susceptible material categories.

Clinically, these findings support a material-specific approach to CAD/CAM restoration selection and maintenance. Feldspathic ceramics appear most suitable for long-term optical stability in esthetically demanding areas. Hybrid and resin-based CAD/CAM materials, while offering mechanical advantages such as reduced brittleness and intraoral repairability, demonstrate greater susceptibility to brushing-induced gloss loss and color change. For these materials, periodic professional repolishing and patient counseling regarding oral hygiene habits may be important strategies to maintain acceptable esthetic outcomes over time.

Several limitations should be considered. The in vitro design does not fully replicate the oral environment. The toothpaste used (RDA ≈ 100–120) represents a moderately aggressive protocol, whereas clinical RDA values range from approximately 30 to 200 [8, 9]. Regarding brushing cycle equivalence, other published models have employed different assumptions, ranging from twice-daily brushing at 60 s per session [18] to estimates based on stroke counts per minute [7, 14], limiting direct inter-study comparisons. The absence of surface roughness measurements (Ra/Sa) prevented direct mechanistic correlation between optical changes and surface topography. However, Dal Piva et al. [15] demonstrated that brushing-induced wear of CAD/CAM ceramics results in measurable increases in roughness alongside gloss reduction. Future studies incorporating surface characterization, toothpastes of varying RDA values, and multifactorial aging protocols are warranted.

Conclusions

Simulated toothbrushing significantly affected the color stability and surface gloss of CAD/CAM restorative materials in a material-dependent manner. Feldspathic ceramic showed the highest resistance, whereas hybrid and resin-based materials exhibited greater susceptibility to discoloration and gloss loss over time. Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate demonstrated early changes followed by stabilization.

While most color changes remained within or close to clinically acceptable thresholds, some early-stage values approached or slightly exceeded these limits. These findings highlight the importance of material selection and surface maintenance for long-term esthetic performance.
