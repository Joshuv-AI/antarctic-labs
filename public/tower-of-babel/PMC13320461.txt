CarakaBinary: A binary-labelled dataset from early Āyurvedic texts for the identification of compound words in classical Sanskrit
Authors: Shriganesh Devaru Bhat, Premjith B․
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Charaka-Binary, the presented domain-specific Ayurvedic textual dataset in this article, is intended to support natural language processing (NLP) tasks in the Sanskrit language, particularly in analyzing compound words. Charaka-Binary includes 31,179 instances comprising 23,224 non-compound words and 7,955 compound words in Devanāgarī script along with the relevant numerical labels, including “0” indicating non-compound words and “1” denoting compound words to support future computational tasks. The dataset provides a resource for extracting compound words from Sanskrit text. We utilized the publicly accessible ancient Ayurvedic literature, Carakasaṃhitā. Among the 7 chapters of Carakasaṃhitā, we employed the first chapter along with its 30 lessons and fourth chapters’ two lessons as the foundation for dataset collection. The first phase of the dataset collection was to convert 228 pages of Carakasaṃhitā into individual .png files suitable for text extraction, while the second phase included the use of the Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technique to extract the text, followed by proofreading. Then the sentence segmentation step was carried out by word-level labeling into compound and non-compound word categories. The preprocessing steps, including proofreading, segmentation, and labeling, are carried out with the help of domain experts. Caraka-Binary can be used in both computational tasks, which include training models to identify Sanskrit compound words, as well as non-computational purposes, which encompass classroom teaching on Sanskrit compound words, comprehending their types, and analyzing compound patterns.

Background

Ayurveda, a branch of Indian knowledge systems (IKS), includes texts pertaining to health and disease management that is documented in the Sanskrit language. In the 2019 World Health Organization report, it was noted that 93 member states reported the use of Ayurveda in their countries, based on 2012 statistics [1]. Many individuals involved in the preservation and dissemination of Ayurveda, either may be second language (L2) learners or unfamiliar with traditional approaches to learning Sanskrit grammar [2] and linguistic theory. In Ayurveda, the identification of compound words and a thorough understanding of their meanings are may help an accurate interpretation. Prior research in compound word analysis employed generic data instead of focusing on the Ayurvedic domain. Therefore, CarakaBinary provides the resource to differentiate compound and non-compound words in the Sanskrit language, specifically within the Ayurvedic domain, to support Sanskrit NLP tasks and to assist L2 learners and beginners.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The construction of the dataset included the following steps:

1. Source of the dataset

The dataset is sourced from Carakasaṃhitā [3], a Sanskrit Ayurvedic text that includes domain-specific terminologies and compound words suitable for a binary text classification task, in which we focused only on the 30 lessons of the initial chapter and 2 lessons of the fourth chapter.

2. Converting pdf into editable text

Due to the targeted material was in PDF format, we utilized the SanskritOCR [4] tool a web-based SanskritOCR engine1 to convert the PDF into editable text. A total of 228 pages were evaluated for the conversion process. During conversion, we also utilized “pdftoppm” from the “Poppler” library (version 25.11.0) [5] to transform the target PDF into high-quality .png files to facilitate the Optical Character Recognition (OCR) process, given that the SanskritOCR platform exclusively accepts images as input. The “pdftoppm” provided the conversion of pages into distinct .png files, ensuring perfect layout and script clarity. We executed the command “pdftoppm -png -r 300 Caraka.pdf caraka.png.” We converted the image format to .png to prevent loss during compression and set the resolution to 300 DPI to ensure premium quality for the OCR process. The “pdftoppm” generated pages as separate picture in .png format, utilizing the default grayscale color mode and an upright orientation. The output file names were uniform (caraka-1.png, caraka-2.png) to facilitate the input photos for the OCR process workflow. Fig. 1 illustrates the sample .png file extracted from “pdftoppm.”Fig. 1An example output produced by pdftoppm.Fig 1 dummy alt text

An example output produced by pdftoppm.

After turning pages of the target PDF into separate .png files, we commenced the transformation of each .png file into editable text using the OCR process. The resulting editable text contained extraneous content in addition to the primary text. The upper margin of the left page included the page number, book title, and abbreviation of 'adhyāya,' (lesson) while the right page displayed the chapter number, book title, and page number in a left-to-right orientation, as shown in Fig. 2. Footnote numbers in the main text indicate multiple variant readings in the primary text, accompanied by corresponding footnotes at the bottom margin.Fig. 2An example output generated by SanskritOCR when a .png file was provided as input.Fig 2 dummy alt text

An example output generated by SanskritOCR when a .png file was provided as input.

We deemed the elements in the top and bottom margins superfluous, as our attention was only directed towards the main text. Consequently, we commenced the removal of superfluous elements from the editable text and accurately matched the characteristics of each phrase sequentially to ensure their order corresponds with that of the .png file as shown in Fig. 3. During this phase, we permitted unnecessary elements, including full stops and verse numbers, to coexist with the primary text, which were subsequently removed in later phases. During this phase, the punctuation at the end of each verse prevented a merger of content with other verses, while the verse numbers facilitated the accurate representation of all verses in the editable text within the PDF or PNG file.Fig. 3An arrangement of phrases derived from SanskritOCR in accordance with the original order in the editable text.Fig 3 dummy alt text

An arrangement of phrases derived from SanskritOCR in accordance with the original order in the editable text.

The Sanskrit OCR engine produced very accurate editable text that closely matched the original source material. The output produced by the OCR engine was manually examined in the proofreading phase, where only the minor character-level errors were identified, primarily involving Anusvāra (a dot above a Devanāgarī character) and Visarga (two dots, one below the other) representations in the Devanāgarī script, yielding a CER of 3.58%.

3. Editing for accuracy

After aligning the phrases according to the serial number, we commenced the proofreading phase, which involved reading each verse to verify the sequence of the phrases, analyzing textual problems resulting from the OCR process, and rectifying them accordingly.

4. Segmenting phrases into individual words

For novices and second language learners, text segmentation helps in recognizing compound words. The segmentation of a text in Sanskrit involves three essential aspects. The preliminary stage of segmentation will utilize the whitespace-based segmentation procedure (WBSP). WBSP involves replacing each whitespace in the sentence with newlines. The second approach is segmentation according to phonetic principles, known as the euphonic-based segmentation procedure (EBSP). Euphonic rules are grammatical laws governing substitutions between two alphabets that display textual juxtaposition. Both approaches WBSP and EBSP enable segmentation as practical techniques, whereas the compound-based segmentation process (CBSP) functions as a cognitive principle in this work. It concerns the segmentation of nouns that are semantically unified within a compound word. CBSP should not be employed in the preparation of the dataset that facilitates the compound word and non-compound word categorization, as CBSP diminishes semantic information and disrupts the language relationship that underlies their semantic integration, and it leads to a single non-compound word category. Compound-based segmentation helps in excluding compound words during the segmentation process, thereby preserving semantic information.

Consequently, we incorporated sub-phases in the proofreading stage, each dedicated to identifying euphonically altered combinations and semantic combinations independently. Euphonically modified combinations were digitally emphasized for inclusion in the segmentation process, while semantic combinations were indicated for exclusion.

5. Establishing grammatically correct word boundaries

In Sanskrit, each word simultaneously conveys core and grammatical meaning, with word boundaries aiding in the determination of grammatical meaning. In summary, the case markers illustrate the grammatical role of the core meaning of the word within the sentence. Retaining grammatically improper terms may distort the learning phase of novices and L2 learners in the concept of pedagogy, while in the concept of computational linguistics, it may mislead the model's training phase. Consequently, we conducted a distinct phase in which we concentrated on the assignment of grammatically valid word boundaries for each sample.

6. Manual annotation

Throughout the manual labeling procedure, we examined each instance in the output from the preceding step to ensure grammatical and linguistic correctness and integrity. Each instance was labeled as either ``NC'' for non-compound words or ``C'' for compound words. The annotator for CarakaBinary studied Sanskrit grammar in a conventional context and holds advanced degrees.

Before the manual annotation of the Sanskrit terms, we commenced an examination of the ideas of compound words and their semantic categories with specialized characters in accordance with Pāṇinian perspectives. This process enacted the head bridge for categorizing the Sanskrit Ayurvedic terms into predefined classes. In Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī tradition, there are five coarse-grained semantic classes explained under two categories, which all are considered under the compound class during the annotation process. The compound class includes two major classes namely, Kevala (Uncommon) and Viśeṣa (Specail) where Viśeṣa encompasses avyayībhāva (adverbial), tatpuruṣa (determinative), bahuvrihi (exocentric), dvandva (coordinative), and karmadhāraya (oppositional determinative). 8. Computational evaluation of dataset imbalance

In order to examine the implications with respect to the computational standpoint for the class weight imbalance, additionally, we executed classification experiments employing Support Vector Classification (SVC) with MuRIL and XLM-R multilingual embedding models, including both balanced and imbalanced class-weight settings. The models’ consistent performance across all settings in terms of Macro F1 Score demonstrated that the dataset preserves meaningful discriminative linguistic patterns without being affected by imbalanced class weight. Limitations

CarakaBinary is limited for binary classification of compound and non-compound Sanskrit Ayurvedic terms. Apart from categorical and numerical labels, it does not include further linguistic representations, including part-of-speech tags and others. Despite the distinguished Sanskrit scholar's annotation and validation of the dataset for compound and non-compound classifications, human errors cannot be completely eliminated. The dataset also possesses the imbalanced nature and includes duplicate entries.

Anticipated Challenges •The dataset belongs to the Sanskrit language, which possesses morphological complexity and unclear word boundaries that may possibly lead to ambiguity in determining between compound and non-compound words.•The compound word often includes euphonic alterations at the junction of its components that might complicate the identification of those components.•The dataset, prepared for word-level analysis, lacks the syntactic features necessary to categorize specific terms as compound words.•The meaning of the compound word is influenced by variations in linguistic interpretation, as identical components might produce compound words that appear similar yet differ in meaning. Consequently, specific compound terms may induce ambiguity, thereby lead complexity in compound type identification. However, the compound and non-compound categorisation will not affect by this ambiguity.
