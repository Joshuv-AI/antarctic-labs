From bioinformatics to bedside: PPBP drives glioblastoma progression under hypoxia via CXCR2/PI3K/Akt signaling and is targetable by Oenothein B
Authors: Xianwen Cao, Yuansheng Fan, Jiaying Wang, Kun Wang, Xuhan Wang, Xiaodong Cao, Da Zong, Junfei Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is an aggressive brain cancer closely linked to hypoxic conditions and altered lactate metabolism. This study aims to investigate the prognostic factors of GBM by developing models that integrating hypoxia and lactate metabolism, and to leverage bioinformatics techniques to gain deeper insights in this process.MethodsPublicly available GBM datasets identified hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related prognostic genes. Bioinformatics analyses included differential expression, functional enrichment, consensus clustering, immune profiling, and prognostic risk modeling. PPBP expression was validated via ELISA, Western blot, and RT-qPCR. Functional effects of PPBP knockdown/overexpression were assessed using CCK-8, EdU, wound healing, and Transwell assays under normoxia/hypoxia. Molecular mechanisms were explored with ChIP, dual-luciferase reporter, Co-IP, and pathway inhibition assays. Xenograft models evaluated in vivo tumor growth and survival. Virtual screening and SPR identified PPBP-targeting compounds, validated pharmacologically in vitro and in vivo.ResultsIntegrated bioinformatics and experimental research identified 165 hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related genes in GBM. A seven-gene prognostic signature was established, with PPBP validated as a key hypoxia-induced oncogene. PPBP knockdown suppressed malignant phenotypes and glycolysis under hypoxia via CXCR2/PI3K/AKT signaling. HIF-1α transcriptionally activated PPBP through direct promoter binding. The natural compound Oenothein B targeted PPBP, inhibiting tumor growth and lactate metabolism in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionThis research develops a hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related gene signature to predict GBM prognosis, identifies PPBP as a key driver of hypoxic tumor progression via HIF-1α transcription and CXCR2/PI3K/AKT signaling, and shows that the natural compound Oenothein B targets PPBP to suppress glioma growth, offering a potential GBM therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04341-z.Keywords: Glioblastoma multiforme, Hypoxia, Lactate metabolism, PPBP, Oenothein B

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) stands as the most prevalent and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor among adults, characterized by swift proliferation, widespread infiltration, and a notable resistance to treatment. The conventional treatment approach involves surgical removal of the tumor, followed by radiation therapy and chemotherapy with temozolomide. However, the median duration of overall survival is still around 15 months, and the five-year survival probability is below 5% [1]. Current treatments for GBM are increasingly diversified, including approaches such as tumor-treating fields (TTFields), CAR-T therapy, and PDL1 monoclonal antibodies [2, 3], yet therapeutic outcomes remain unsatisfactory. Therefore, it is crucial to discover new biomarkers and therapeutic targets to enhance the accuracy of prognostic predictions and refine treatment approaches for patients with GBM.

Hypoxia is a hallmark of solid tumors, resulting from inadequate oxygen supply due to rapid tumor growth and abnormal vascularization [4]. It promotes angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and tumor invasion primarily through the activation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs)-mediated transcriptional programs [5]. Lactate metabolism is closely linked to hypoxia and represents a core aspect of tumor metabolic remodeling. Under hypoxic conditions, tumor cells transition from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect, leading to substantial lactate production [6]. Lactate isn’t just a byproduct of metabolism, it also acts as a signaling agent, lowering the pH of the tumor environment, dampening immune reactions, and fostering tumor growth [7]. In GBM, hypoxia and lactate metabolism are strongly associated with the maintenance of stemness, therapy resistance, and immunosuppression. For example, studies have shown that HIF-1α upregulates glycolytic enzymes and lactate transporters, enhancing lactate secretion and further activating HIF-1 signaling through a positive feedback loop [8]. High lactate levels in GBM are significantly correlated with poor patient prognosis and resistance to conventional therapies [9]. Given the central role of hypoxia and lactate metabolism in driving malignant progression and shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment, in-depth investigation into their biological functions and specific molecular mechanisms in GBM is of great importance for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Based on clinical information and transcriptomic data of GBM from public databases, combined with hypoxia- and lactate metabolism-related gene sets, this study constructed a robust prognostic risk model through bioinformatic analyses and identified a key gene, PPBP. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, we confirmed PPBP’s function in advancing hypoxia-induced GBM progression and elucidated its molecular mechanism. This mechanism involves HIF-1α-mediated transcriptional activation, as well as the modulation of glycolysis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via the CXCR2/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Furthermore, via virtual drug screening and experimental validation, we identified the natural compound Oenothein B as a highly effective agent targeting PPBP, significantly inhibiting tumor growth. This study not only provides a novel biomarker for GBM prognosis but also offers a potential candidate drug for therapeutic strategies targeting the hypoxia–lactate metabolic axis.

Materials and methods

Data source

Survival information clinicopathological features and mRNA expression data, were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). This dataset included brain tissue samples from 169 individuals with GBM and 5 normal controls. A total of 169 GBM samples were enrolled in our primary cohort. Among these samples, IDH status was available in 130 cases, including 126 IDH wild-type (WT) and 4 IDH mutant cases. Additionally, the scRNA-seq dataset (GSE162631) included 4 GBM samples and 4 control samples based on the GPL24676 platform. For the scRNA-seq dataset applied in this study, IDH mutation status was not reported in the original study. The mRNA expression data and corresponding clinicopathological characteristics for GBM were obtained from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database (http://www.cgga.org.cn/) encompassing brain tissue samples from 139 GBM patients, consisting of 98 IDH WT and 41 IDH mutant cases. In the Genecard database, the keywords “lactic” and “hypoxia” were used to filter genes with a relevance score greater than 1. After removing duplicates, 2,786 hypoxia-related genes (HRGs) and 1,242 lactic metabolism-related genes (LMRGs) were collected.

Identification and analysis of candidate genes

We performed differential expression analysis between GBM and controls applying DESeq2 package (v 1.34.0) [10] within TCGA-GBM dataset (|log2FC| > 0.5 and P < 0.05) to acquire differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Following this, the intersection of DEGs, HRGs, and LMRGs was taken to obtain candidate genes. Afterwards, enrichment analyses were conducted employing Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) to uncover the potential biological roles and pathways linked to the identified candidate genes via ClusterProfiler package (v 4.2) [11] (P < 0.05). Subsequent analyses involved uploading the candidate genes to STRING database (http://string-db.org) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (interaction scores > 0.4).

Identification and survival analysis of GBM subtypes

Based on the expression levels of candidate genes, consensus clustering analysis was executed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package (v 1.58.0) [12] to stratify GBM patients into various subtypes. Subsequently, the “survminer” package (v 0.4.9) [13] was employed to carry out Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis to assess overall survival (OS) of GBM patients across different subtypes.

Identification and analysis of hub genes

Differential expression analysis was performed between subtypes in the TCGA-GBM dataset using DESeq2 (v 1.34.0), identifying DEGs1 based on the criteria |log2FC| >2 and adj.P < 0.05. Additionally, We conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to uncover the biological functions and pathways linked to DEGs1 (P < 0.05). A PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org), and the top 40 hub genes were selected through the MCC algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape.

Construction and validation of the risk model

In an effort to construct a risk assessment model for patients with GBM, we conducted a comprehensive series of analyses. Initially, univariate Cox analysis was conducted on this data via survival package, and hub genes with a P < 0.1 were subsequently included in proportional hazards (PH) assumption test. We implemented least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression through “glmnet” package (v 4.1.4) [14] to select strongly correlated features, constructing the most optimal model at the minimized lambda value. Finally, the risk score for each GBM patient was computed utilizing the expression levels of prognostic genes alongside their respective risk coefficients. Patients were subsequently categorized into high and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. The risk score formula was risk score . We illustrated the distribution of risk scores and survival statuses among GBM patients across different risk groups. Furthermore, we used the Kaplan-Meier survival curve, generated with the survminer package, to highlight differences in OS between different risk groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed with “survivalROC” package (v 1.0.3) [15] to quantify area under the curve (AUC) values at 1, 2, and 5 year intervals, thereby appraising the prognostic efficacy of the risk model. The validity of the risk model was also corroborated using CGGA-GBM dataset.

Clinical characteristics analysis and construction of the nomogram

An association analysis was performed between age, karnofsky performance score, subtype, gender, and MGMT status with prognostic genes across different clinical subgroups in TCGA-GBM samples. The chi-square test was employed to assess the disparities in the proportions of high- and low-risk groups among GBM patient subtypes, according to various clinical characteristics. Following this, the connection between risk scores and clinical characteristics was examined by analyzing the variations in risk scores among these five clinical indicators within different clinical subgroups.

Next, univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to correlate risk score with clinical characteristics (age, karnofsky performance score, subtype, gender, and MGMT status) of GBM patients in TCGA-GBM dataset. Following this, variables that met PH assumption as determined by the univariate Cox analysis were further assessed using multivariate Cox regression to delineate independent prognosis factors. Ultimately, we utilized independent prognosis factors to create a nomogram for forecasting 1, 2, and 5 year survival probabilities of GBM patients with “rms” package (v 6.5-1.5.5) [15]. In addition, calibration curve and ROC curve were plotted to evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram.

Immune profiling and evaluation

We employed Cibersort (available at http://cibersort.stanford.edu/) to assess the proportions of 22 immune cell types, aiming to explore the immune cell composition among various subtypes and risk groups. Naive CD4 T cells, which exhibited zero infiltration levels across all samples, were excluded from the analysis. Next, the expression levels of 38 immune checkpoint molecules [16] were assessed within the GBM samples. In addition, the immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores were predicted to identify differences between subgroups using relevant predictive algorithms. Finally, to assess tumor immunogenicity and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), the immunophenoscore (IPS) score was calculated using the IOBR package. The immune score derived from the ESTIMATE algorithm is primarily used to macroscopically assess the infiltration abundance of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, the IPS is a comprehensive scoring system specially developed to predict clinical response to immunotherapy, and serves as a clinical decision-support tool for screening potential beneficiaries of ICI therapy.

Mutation analysis and drug sensitivity analysis

This study systematically analyzed somatic mutation data from tumor patients using the maftools package to investigate mutation patterns across different subtypes and two risk groups. The top 20 most frequently mutated genes were identified, and their mutation landscapes were illustrated through waterfall plots for each subtype, facilitating an intuitive comparison of mutational distributions. Additionally, drug sensitivity predictions were performed using the oncoPredict package, leveraging data from 198 chemotherapeutic and targeted agents. Wilcoxon test was employed to compare drug sensitivities across subtypes, followed by Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing.

Functional and annotation analysis

To investigate pathway differences among subtypes and two risk groups during disease progression, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package with the KEGG gene set from MSigDB (ccp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt). Pathways with |NES| > 1 and adj.P < 0.05 were considered significantly activated or suppressed. To delve deeper into the distinct KEGG pathways between various risk groups within TCGA-GBM dataset, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was performed utilizing “GSVA” package (v 1.40) [17]. This analysis utilized the background gene set “ccp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt” to calculate enrichment scores for different pathways. The “limma” package was employed to contrast the enrichment pathways between various groups, with pathways exhibiting a P < 0.05, |t| > 2 being selected as key differentially enriched pathways.

The scRNA-seq analysis

The comprehensive analysis of single-cell sequencing data from GSE162631 dataset was conducted via “Seurat” package (v 4.1.0) [18]. To begin, quality control (QC) measure was implemented to eliminate low-quality data. Genes identified in less than 200 cells were excluded, as well as cells that met any of these conditions: nFeature_RNA ≥ 6000, nCount_RNA ≥ 40,000, or mitochondrial gene content ≥ 10%. Thereafter, we standardized the data to identify 2,000 highly variable genes. The data underwent scaling with the ScaleData function, and principal components with statistical significance were identified using the JackStrawPlot function. Next, cells that had been dimensionally reduced were clustered using t-SNE (resolution = 0.6). Cell taxa were annotated according to marker genes in the published literature [18]. Additionally, pseudo-time analysis was carried out via Monocle package (v 20) [19], based on the Macrophage. The “CellChat” package (v 1.5.0) [20] was employed to facilitate the analysis of cell communication among the cell clusters.

Clinical samples

Six pairs of GBM tumor tissues and adjacent normal brain tissues were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Neurosurgery, Wuxi People’s Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University between 2021 and 2025. All tumor specimens were histopathologically diagnosed as GBM according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, and all cases were confirmed as IDH-wildtype. All tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after resection and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi People’s Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human glioma cell lines U87 and U251, along with the human embryonic kidney cell line 293 T, were sourced fromthe Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, Canada) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Canada) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 10% CO₂. To simulate the tumor hypoxic microenvironment, cells were placed in a tri-gas incubator (Panasonic, MCO-5 M) with 1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors for HIF1A overexpression and PPBP shRNA were constructed by GeneChem (China). The targeting sequences for human PPBP shRNA were as follows: shRNA-A: 5′-GCTTCTGTCATTGCTGCTGAC-3′; shRNA-B: 5′-GCGAAAGGCAAAGAGGAAAGT-3′. A scrambled shRNA served as the negative control (NC).

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, RR036A), 1 µg of RNA was converted into cDNA. qPCR was carried out with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) on a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 3 system. The reaction program included initial denaturation, 40 amplification cycles, and melt curve analysis. Gene expression levels were determined utilizing the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method with GAPDH as the reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secretion level of PPBP in cell culture supernatant was measured using a Human NAP-2 ELISA kit (Cusabio, CSB-E04562h) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo Fisher Multiskan FC microplate reader. Concentrations were normalized to total cellular protein.

Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity assay

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well. After adherence, cells were cultured under normoxia (21% O₂) or hypoxia (1% O₂) for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. We added CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, C0037) to each well, followed by a 2-hour incubation, and then measured the absorbance at 450 nm (Thermo Fisher Multiskan FC).

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine assay (EdU) assay

Cell proliferation was assessed utilizing an EdU kit (Beyotime, C0075L). Cells were treated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Click reaction solution and Hoechst 33,342. The EdU-positive rate was calculated under a fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Invitrogen EVOS).

Cell migration and invasion assay

Wound healing assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates until confluent. Wounds were created utilizing a sterile pipette tip. Following washing, cells were cultured under normoxia or hypoxia. Images were taken at 0 and 24 h (Olympus CKX53), and migration distance was quantified using Image J.

Transwell invasion assay

Transwell chambers (Corning, 8.0 μm) were coated with Matrigel (Corning) diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM. Cells were then resuspended in serum-free media and seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 20% FBS. After 24 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and then eluted with 33% glacial acetic acid. The eluate was quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

After hypoxia treatment, cells were resuspended at 5 × 10⁶ cells/mL and seeded in black-walled clear-bottom plates. The ECAR was determined utilizing a Fluorometric Assay Kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F069) and a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader.

L-Lactate measurement

L-Lactate levels were assessed with a Lactate Assay Kit (Abbkine, KTB1100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 545/605 nm.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime ST506) and protease inhibitor cocktail (CWBIO CW2200S). Protein concentration was measured with a BCA kit (Beyotime P0010). A total of 0.1–0.5 µg of protein per sample was separated by SDS-PAGE using a Bio-Rad electrophoresis system and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocked(Beyotime P0252), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Blots were visualized using an ECL kit (Millipore) on a Tanon 5200 system. β-tubulin or β-actin was utilized as loading controls, and a complete list of antibodies employed in the Western blot analysis can be found in Table S2.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

A ChIP assay was conducted using a kit (CST, #9003). Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and the DNA was fragmented into pieces ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs. Immunoprecipitation was performed with specific antibodies or IgG control. Precipitated DNA underwent analysis by qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

A segment of the PPBP promoter containing wild-type (5′-AAAAACCCAGCGTGTTGCCCA-3′) or mutant (5′-AAAAACCCATAAAATTGCCCA-3′) HRE was cloned into a firefly luciferase vector. Cells underwent co-transfected with the reporter and Renilla luciferase control vectors. Luciferase activity was assessed utilizing a dual-luciferase assay system (Servicebio G1701-100T).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed with NP-40 buffer (MCE HY-K1002). A total of 500 µg of protein was incubated with anti-PPBP antibody (see Table S2) or IgG control overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) were incubated for 4 h. After that, the beads were washed and boiled in SDS loading buffer. The eluted proteins were subsequently analyzed via Western blot using anti - CXCR2 and anti - CXCR1 antibodies.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR was performed on a Biacore X100 system. Recombinant Human PPBP (Novoprotein, C056) was immobilized on a CM5 chip. Serial concentrations of Oenothein B (MCE, HY-N7765, HY-N9536, HY-N6782) were injected over the chip surface. The analysis of binding kinetics was carried out using a 1:1 binding model.

Animal models

Four- to six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were anesthetized and intracranially injected with 2 × 10⁵ luciferase-expressing U87-luc-shPPBP or control cells in 4 µL PBS. For the drug treatment groups, mice were administered Oenothein B (100 mg/kg/d) by oral gavage, while the control group received an equal volume of normal saline. Following intraperitoneal administration of D - luciferin, tumor growth was tracked on a weekly basis with an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer). All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2025241) prior to experimentation. Rodent health endpoints were defined as the occurrence of any of the following: ≥20% body weight loss, tumor size exceeding 20 mm, or the manifestation of abnormal central neurological symptoms. Mice meeting any of these criteria were humanely euthanized in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals to ensure animal welfare.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse brain tissues were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections underwent deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin (Beyotime, C0105S) and eosin (Beyotime, C0105S), dehydrated, and mounted. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L microscope.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out using R software (v 4.2). Group differences were assessed via the Wilcoxon test. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t - test was applied, while two - way ANOVA was used for multiple - group comparisons. A P-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Identification of 165 candidate genes in GBM and functional analysis

A total of 9,577 DEGs were identified between GBM and control groups, with 5,072 genes upregulated and 4,505 genes downregulated (Fig. 1A). The top 20 upregulated and downregulated genes are illustrated in Fig. 1B. The overlap between these DEGs, HRGs, and LMRGs yielded 165 candidate genes (Fig. 1C). These candidate genes were significantly enriched in 1,800 biological process terms (e.g. response to oxygen levels, response to decreased oxygen levels), 108 cellular component terms (e.g. mitochondrial matrix, membrane raft), and 154 molecular function terms (e.g. growth factor activity, cytokine activity) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, these genes were significantly enriched in 128 KEGG pathways (e.g. PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, central carbon metabolism in cancer) (Fig. 1E).

GBM subtypes with distinct survival and gene expression profiles

The results from consensus clustering indicated that 169 GBM patients were divided into 2 subtypes (k = 2), designated as cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. 1F). Patients within cluster 2 demonstrated a higher survival rate than those in cluster 1 (P = 0.014) (Fig. 1G). A total of 116 DEGs1 were identified between cluster 1 and cluster 2, with 26 genes upregulated and 90 genes downregulated (Fig. 1H-I). Enrichment analysis showed that DEGs1 were notably enriched in 531 GO terms (myeloid leukocyte migration, collagen trimer, and cytokine activity) and 128 KEGG pathways (cytokine-cytokine receptorinteraction and IL − 17 signaling pathway) (Fig. 1J-K). GSEA analysis between the two clusters identified significant suppression of pathways in cluster 1 such as cytokine cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 1L).

Immune landscape and drug sensitivity differences between GBM subtypes

Immunological analysis uncovered notable disparities in the immune microenvironment across different subtypes. Through immune cell infiltration analysis, 11 types of immune cells were found to have significantly varying infiltration rates between cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. S1A-B). These cell types include memory B cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, and naive B cells etc. Furthermore, 24 immune checkpoints exhibited significant differential expression between the two subtypes; all except CD200 were expressed at higher levels in cluster 2 (Fig. S1C). Additionally, cluster 2 demonstrated higher scores in immune scoring, stromal scoring, and estimate scoring (Fig. S1D). Conversely, cluster 1 exhibited a higher IPS score (Fig. S1E). In terms of drug sensitivity analysis, 21 drugs showed significant differences between the two groups, including KU-55,933, Staurosporine, and PLX-4720 (Fig. S1F). These findings underscore the complexity and heterogeneity of the immune landscape in different subtypes and highlight potential therapeutic targets for tailored interventions.

Prognostic genes based risk model for GBM patient stratification

Based on DEGs1, the MCC algorithm was utilized to identify the top 40 genes for prognostic gene selection (Fig. 2A). Among the 40 DEGs1, a total of 31 genes were found through univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.1) (Fig. 2B). At the minimum lambda value, seven genes (CXCL5, LCN2, IL11, CXCL13, COL5A1, PPBP, and FGF17) were selected and subsequently designated as prognostic genes in this study (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). The risk model was established by utilizing the expression patterns of these seven genes along withOS data. In the TCGA-GBM dataset, the distribution of risk scores and survival status of GBM patients in the two risk groups was demonstrated in the Fig. 2D. Notably, as the risk score increased, a significant rise in mortality was observed. Consistent with expectations, high-risk patients exhibited significantly worse prognosis (P = 0.0062) (Fig. 2E). The ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC values for predicting 1-year, 2-year, and 2.5-year survival were 0.643, 0.659, and 0.63, respectively, indicating that the risk model possesses robust predictive power for assessing GBM patient outcomes (Fig. 2F). Validation using the CGGA dataset further confirmed results consistent with those observed in the TCGA-GBM dataset (Fig. 2G-I). These findings highlight the reliable prognostic value of our signature in survival stratification, and its potential application in individualized clinical management deserves further prospective validation.

Clinical and molecular factors inform GBM prognostic nomogram

A heatmap was generated based on clinical characteristics to compare the expression profiles of prognostic genes between two risk subgroups, with annotations for patient attributes such as age, karnofsky performance score, subtype, gender, and MGMT status (Fig. S2A). Notably, significant differences in risk scores were observed among subtypes in the two risk groups (Fig. S2B). Further chi-square tests revealed a significant difference in subtype proportions between the two risk groups (Fig. S2C). Specifically, the Mesenchymal subtype accounted for 61.2% of patients in the high-risk group, whereas the Classical subtype was more prevalent in the low-risk group, comprising 37.7% of patients. To evaluate the potential of clinical features as independent prognostic factors, risk scores and clinical characteristics were included in a univariate Cox regression analysis. The results demonstrated that age, subtype, MGMT status and riskscore were all significantly associated with GBM patient survival outcomes (P < 0.05) and satisfied the PH assumption (Fig. S2D). Subsequently, these independent factors were incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression analysis, which identified subtype and riskscore as independent prognostic factors for constructing a nomogram (Fig. S2E-F). Calibration curves and ROC curves further demonstrated that the nomogram exhibited high predictive accuracy for survival outcomes (Fig. S2G-H). These findings underscore the utility of this model in predicting GBM prognosis and guiding personalized treatment strategies.

Immune microenvironment and pathway differences in GBM risk groups

Further analysis was conducted to observe the differences in the immune microenvironment between two risk groups (Fig. S3A-B). It was found that the infiltration proportions of 11 immune cell types showed significant differences between the two risk groups. These cell types include naive B cells, memory B cells, and resting CD4 memory T cells etc.

To gain deeper insights into the pathway differences between high- and low-risk groups in disease progression, we executed GSEA and GSVA (Fig. S3C-D). GSEA revealed that pathways such as cytokine cytokine receptor interaction and hematopoietic cell lineage were significantly activated in the high-risk group. GSVA indicated that pathways like proteasome and selenoamino acid metabolism exhibited significant differences between the two risk groups.

Distinct mutation patterns in GBM subtypes and risk groups

Mutation analysis revealed the genomic mutation landscape in tumor patients. Mutation analysis based on GBM subtypes showed that the top three mutated genes in cluster 1 were TP53, EGFR, and TTN, while in cluster 2, the top three mutated genes were PTEN, TP53, and TTN. Notably, missense mutation was the predominant type in both clusters (Fig. S3E-F). Similarly, mutation analysis based on two risk groups demonstrated that the top three mutated genes in the low-risk group were TP53, EGFR, and PTEN, whereas in the high-risk group, they were PTEN, TP53, and TTN. Again, missense mutation was the most frequent type observed (Fig. S3G-H). These genomic aberrations activate the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, which markedly enhances hypoxic adaptation of tumor cells and drives subsequent remodeling of the downstream secretome [21]. In summary, these findings highlighted distinct mutation patterns across GBM subtypes and risk groups, with TP53, PTEN, and TTN emerging as recurrently mutated genes. The predominance of missense mutations suggests their potential role in driving tumorigenesis and disease progression.

Single cell analysis highlights macrophage differentiation in GBM

To explore the changes in prognostic gene expression at the single-cell level, a series of analyses were conducted based on the GSE162631 dataset. After QC, a total of 96,386 cells and 26,359 genes were obtained (Fig. S4A), and data were normalized to select the top 2,000 most variable genes (Fig. S4B). PCA was executed, and the first 30 principal components were used for unsupervised clustering (Fig. S4C). T-SNE clustering revealed 17 cell clusters, which were annotated into 6 cell types: neutrophils, vascular mural cells, microglia, macrophages, endothelial cells, and T cells (Fig. 3A-C). Among these cell types, macrophages were more abundant in the tumor core, and therefore, macrophages were selected for pseudotime analysis (Fig. 3D). The results showed that macrophages in the tumor core predominantly existed in the early stages of differentiation (Fig. 3E). Additionally, CXCL5 exhibited higher expression in the early stages of differentiation, while other prognostic genes showed lower expression throughout the differentiation process (Fig. 3F, Fig. S4D). Cell communication analysis revealed that the interactions and frequency of interactions between microglia and T cells were significantly enhanced in the GBM group (Fig. 3G-H). In summary, single-cell analysis revealed the critical role of macrophages in the tumor core of GBM, along with their association with CXCL5 expression during differentiation.

PPBP is highly expressed in clinical GBM samples

First, we validated the expression of seven prognostic genes in tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from six GBM patients. We found that PPBP mRNA levels were significantly upregulated, while the other six genes showed no statistically significant differences (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we queried The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database and found higher protein expression of PPBP in GBM patients (Fig. S5A). Therefore, PPBP was identified as a key gene co-regulated by hypoxia and lactate metabolism mediating GBM development. Although PPBP has been reported to be secreted by macrophages and promote malignant progression of glioma cells [22], our previous single-cell analysis revealed that PPBP exhibited very low expression throughout the differentiation process of macrophages infiltrating the tumor core. Thus, we used ELISA to detect PPBP secretion in U251 and U87 cells. ELISA experiments detected PPBP secretion in both glioma cell lines under hypoxic culture conditions (Fig. S5B).

PPBP promotes GBM malignant progression under hypoxic conditions

We then constructed PPBP-knockdown U87 and U251 cell lines using lentivirus (Fig. 4B), verified the knockdown efficiency, and used them for subsequent experiments. Under normoxic culture conditions, PPBP knockdown did not significantly inhibit the viability or proliferation of U87 and U251 cells(Fig. S5 C-D), nor did it markedly affect cell migration (Fig. S5 E). Therefore, we cultured the cells under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂). CCK-8 assays showed that PPBP knockdown significantly reduced the viability of U87 and U251 cells under hypoxia (Fig. 4C-D). EdU assays indicated that PPBP knockdown mediated a decrease in cell proliferation ability (Fig. 4E-F). Moreover, Wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated that PPBP knockdown inhibited the migration and invasion capabilities of glioma cells (Fig. 4G-H).Quantitative analysis of cell invasion, proliferation and migration abilities with statistical comparisons is shown in Fig. 4I-K.

Next, we investigated the effect of PPBP on glycolysis and lactate metabolism. Using an L-lactate level detection kit, we confirmed that PPBP knockdown reduced intracellular lactate levels in glioma cells (Fig. 5A). ECAR experiments showed that PPBP knockdown decreased the extracellular acidification rate (Fig. 5B). We established an in vivo model using orthotopic xenografts in nude mice and confirmed that PPBP knockdown inhibited glioma growth in vivo and improved mouse survival prognosis (Fig. 5C-E).

Given that PPBP knockdown significantly reduced the migration, invasion, glycolysis, and lactate metabolism of U87 and U251 cells, we examined the protein levels of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and lactate metabolism marker genes (Fig. 5F). Western blot analysis demonstrated that after PPBP knockdown, EMT marker proteins such as Vimentin, Snail, and Twist were significantly reduced, and lactate metabolism proteins LDHA and the lactate transporter MCT4 were also markedly inhibited. These effects could be rescued by the addition of exogenous PPBP (10 ng/ml) (Fig. 5F).

PPBP transcription is activated by HIF-1α

Our in vitro experiments confirmed that PPBP knockdown did not affect the proliferation or migration of glioma cell lines under normoxia but showed significant inhibition of tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion under 1% O₂ conditions. Therefore, we hypothesized that PPBP expression might be associated with hypoxic stimulation. Accordingly, we detected PPBP expression at different time points under sustained hypoxia (Fig. 5G). Western blot showed that under hypoxic stimulation, the expression levels of HIF1A and PPBP continuously increased, both peaking at 24 h, showing consistent trends. Thus, we speculated that PPBP transcription might be activated by HIF1A. First, prediction using the JASPAR database suggested the presence of typical Hypoxia Response Elements (HREs) in the PPBP promoter, potentially binding HIF1A and activating transcription (Fig. 6A). To test this, we used HIF-1 A overexpression lentivirus to elevate HIF-1 A levels, performed ChIP assay after 24 h of hypoxic stimulation (1% O₂), and observed binding of HIF1A to the HRE domain of PPBP in U87, U251, and 293 T cells (Fig. 6B). To further confirm HIF1A’s binding to the PPBP promoter, we designed a dual-luciferase reporter gene system, which showed that the wild-type PPBP promoter significantly activated firefly luciferase activity (Fig. 6C).

PPBP activates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

Studies by Yan J et al. indicated that FGL2-wired macrophages release PPBP to induce glioma cell stem-like transformation in vitro and promote stem-like functions and migration of glioma cells in vivo [22], however, the precise mechanism by which PPBP operates is still not fully understood. Therefore, using median PPBP expression, we identified DEGs from the TCGA database and generated volcano and heat maps (Fig. 6D-E). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that both upregulated and downregulated DEGs in the PPBP high-expression subgroup were predominantly enriched in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6F-G). Consequently, we examined key genes of the PI3K-AKT pathway. The results showed that under hypoxia, PPBP knockdown did not affect the total protein levels of mTOR, PI3K, or AKT but significantly reduced their phosphorylation levels. These changes could be rescued by adding exogenous PPBP (Fig. 6H), demonstrating that PPBP mediates GBM occurrence through the PI3K-AKT pathway, further leading to EMT activation and enhanced glycolysis.

PPBP exerts its effects by binding to the CXCR2 receptor

PPBP is known to have two receptors, CXCR1 and CXCR2, but the primary receptor in glioma cells has not been identified. Therefore, we used Co-IP to detect PPBP receptors. We cultured cells under hypoxia for 24 h to enhance PPBP expression and then performed Co-IP to identify binding proteins. The Co-IP results indicated that the primary receptor for PPBP in glioma was CXCR2; CXCR1 was not detected in our experiments (Fig. 7A-B). To further validate this, we used the CXCR2-specific antagonist SB225002 to block CXCR2 and examined its effect on the PI3K-AKT pathway under hypoxia. The results showed that blocking CXCR2 significantly inhibited PPBP-induced activation of the AKT-PI3K pathway, and adding exogenous PPBP could not restore this activation, confirming CXCR2 as the primary receptor for PPBP in glioma cells (Fig. 7C). We then assessed whether the PPBP-CXCR2-PI3K/AKT axis is the predominant mechanism underlying PPBP’s oncogenic functions. Under hypoxic conditions, SB225002 treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation of U87 and U251 cells, as demonstrated by CCK-8 and EdU assays (Fig. S6A-B). Furthermore, SB225002 markedly suppressed glioma cell migration, as shown by wound healing assays (Fig. S6C). Notably, the addition of exogenous PPBP failed to rescue these inhibitory effects, suggesting that PPBP exerts its pro-tumorigenic functions primarily through CXCR2-mediated signaling.

Oenothein B targets PPBP and inhibits GBM progression

CXCR2 is an important GPCR drug target extensively studied, but due to the diversity of its ligands and functional complexity, no drugs have been successfully approved for cancer treatment. Therefore, designing drugs targeting PPBP might offer new options for glioma therapy. We performed virtual screening following the workflow in Fig. 8A. Based on the protein structure of PPBP (PDB: 1NAP), we conducted primary screening using KarmaDock against over 3,000,000 small molecules from the ZINC lead compound library, NPASS natural product library, FDA-approved drug library, and MCE bioactive compound library [23]. The top ten hits were re-evaluated using AutoDock. Based on the dual evaluation results (Fig. 8B), we visually selected compounds: Oenothein B (HY-N7765), which ranked first in both evaluations; Cinnamtannin A2 (HY-N9536), which had relatively high scores and known anti-inflammatory effects; and notably, several antibiotics emerged from the screening, such as Polymyxin B Sulfate (HY-A0248), Oligomycin (HY-N6782), and Nosiheptide (HY-107486). Among these, we selected Oligomycin (HY-N6782) for further testing (Fig. 8C). We used surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to detect the binding affinity between PPBP and these ligands. The results showed that Oenothein B (HY-N7765) still exhibited the strongest binding ability, while no binding was detected for Oligomycin (HY-N6782) (Fig. 8D). Therefore, we identified Oenothein B as a potential drug targeting PPBP; specific 3D and 2D binding conformations are shown in Fig. 8E-F.

(B) Docking scores from two different docking programs (KarmaDock and AutoDock) for the top candidate compounds. (C) Chemical structures of the selected candidate compounds for further validation. (D) Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) sensorgrams showing the binding kinetics between recombinant PPBP protein and the candidate compounds. We next intervened with Oenothein B under hypoxic conditions. CCK-8 assays and EdU experiments demonstrated that adding 100 µM Oenothein B significantly inhibited the viability and proliferation of glioma cells, and these inhibitory effects were reversed by the addition of exogenous PPBP (Fig. 9A- B). Likewise, adding PPBP markedly alleviated the inhibition of migration and invasion caused by Oenothein B (Fig. 9C-D). Quantitative analysis of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities with statistical comparisons is shown in Fig. 9E-G. We further evaluated Oenothein B’s impactin vivo. Beginning on day 7 post-implantation, mice were administered Oenothein B orally at a dose of 100 mg/kg/day [24, 25]. The results indicated that Oenothein B treatment suppressed tumor development, improved prognosis, and caused no observed organ toxicity (Fig. 9H-J; Fig. S7). Subsequently, we assessed the protein expression of the PI3K-AKT pathway in Oenothein B-treated cells. Western blot revealed that Oenothein B produced effects similar to the CXCR2 antagonist SB225002: the inclusion of Oenothein B substantially decreased the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR, and markedly suppressed the activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway triggered by the addition of PPBP (Fig. 9K). We further examined the effect of Oenothein B on lactate metabolism and EMT under hypoxia. Oenothein B treatment significantly reduced intracellular L-lactate levels (Fig. 9L) and decreased ECAR (Fig. 9M), indicating suppression of glycolytic activity. Western blot analysis revealed that Oenothein B markedly inhibited the expression of EMT markers (Vimentin, Snail, Twist) and lactate metabolism-related proteins (LDHA, MCT4), and these effects were partially reversed by the addition of exogenous PPBP (Fig. 9N). These results demonstrate that Oenothein B attenuates lactate metabolism and EMT in glioma cells through PPBP targeting.

Discussion

GBM is still among the most aggressive and treatment-resistant brain tumors, with hypoxia and lactate metabolism playing pivotal roles in its malignant progression [26, 27]. Hypoxia drives metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation, which not only acidifies the tumor microenvironment but also promotes immune suppression and treatment resistance [28]. In our research, we combined bioinformatics methods and experimental techniques to identify a hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related gene signature for GBM prognosis, with PPBP emerging as a key oncogene. We further elucidated that PPBP is transcriptionally activated by HIF-1α under hypoxia and promotes GBM progression via the CXCR2/PI3K/AKT pathway. Importantly, we identified Oenothein B as a natural compound targeting PPBP, effectively suppressing tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo settings.

Based on seven hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related genes (CXCL5, LCN2, IL11, CXCL13, COL5A1, PPBP, and FGF17), we constructed a prognostic risk model that successfully divides GBM patients into different risk categories, showing notable differences in survival outcomes. Compared to existing models focusing primarily on invasion-related markers or imaging features [29, 30], our model incorporates a broader spectrum of biological processes such as immune regulation and metabolic reprogramming, demonstrating robust predictive performance across multiple datasets. Consistent with our findings, PPBP was also identified as a component in another six-gene prognostic model for GBM developed via multi-omics analysis of TCGA cohort [31]. Additionally, the developed nomogram deeply integrates the aforementioned molecular risk score with clinical parameters (age, Karnofsky Performance Score, gender, MGMT status) and molecular subtypes. This integration not only captures molecular heterogeneity but also retains key clinical context, greatly enhancing predictive accuracy and clinical translational value, and providing clinicians with a practical tool to assess patients’ survival probabilities and formulate personalized treatment plans.

PPBP, a member of the CXC chemokine family, plays a role in regulating numerous cellular activities in both physiological and pathological processes, such as promoting glycolytic metabolism, modulating cell mitosis, and mediating prostaglandin synthesis. These functions provide a potential mechanistic basis for its involvement in tumor progression [32, 33]. Recent investigations have established a strong correlation between PPBP and the onset and progression of malignant tumors. Specifically, in breast cancer, targeted inhibition of PPBP can significantly impair the proliferative activity and metastatic capacity of tumor cells [34]. In multiple myeloma, PPBP activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting the uncontrolled proliferation of tumor cells and the formation of osteolytic lesions [35]. In colorectal cancer, PPBP not only shows an expression correlation with LDH-A (a key molecule in lactate metabolism) but also enhances the chemoresistance of tumor cells by regulating the STAT1/PHGDH-mediated serine metabolism axis [36, 37].

In the field of glioma research, preliminary explorations into the role of PPBP have been conducted. One study found that inhibiting the CSF1R receptor on the surface of bone marrow cells can reduce PPBP expression, thereby suppressing angiogenesis, invasion, and proliferation of gliomas [38]. However, there is currently no clear conclusion regarding the association between PPBP and the hypoxic microenvironment. Existing studies have shown contradictory results: on one hand, in cervical cancer cells and primary rat astrocytes, hypoxia can significantly upregulate PPBP expression [39, 40]; on the other hand, in hepatocellular carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma cells, PPBP expression shows no obvious correlation with hypoxia [41, 42]. Different from these studies, our research, through clinical sample detection, single-cell RNA-seq analysis, and functional experiments, confirmed that PPBP in GBM has dual secretion sources—it can be not only paracrinely produced by tumor-associated macrophages but also autocrinally synthesized by GBM parenchymal tumor cells. Moreover, under hypoxic conditions, the expression level of PPBP in GBM cells is significantly increased. More importantly, through ChIP experiments and dual-luciferase reporter gene assays, we are the first to confirm that hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α, a core factor in hypoxia response, can directly bind to the HRE in the PPBP promoter region and initiate its transcription. This finding clarifies the molecular mechanism underlying hypoxia-regulated PPBP expression in GBM and fills the research gap in this field.

It is known that the functional exertion of PPBP depends on its binding to cell membrane surface receptors, and two receptors, CXCR1 and CXCR2, have been identified so far [43]. To clarify the functional receptor of PPBP in GBM, we detected a specific interaction between PPBP and CXCR2 through Co-IP experiments, while no binding between PPBP and CXCR1 was observed. Subsequent receptor blocking experiments demonstrated that administration of SB225002, a specific CXCR2 antagonist, significantly diminished the stimulatory effects of PPBP on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GBM cells, as well as obstructed its activation of the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These results indicate that CXCR2 is the main receptor through which PPBP exerts its pro-tumor effects in GBM, and PPBP can promote the EMT process of GBM cells under hypoxic conditions by activating the CXCR2/PI3K/AKT pathway. This mechanism aligns with earlier findings indicating that the CXCR2-driven signaling pathway controls the aggressive characteristics of tumors [44, 45]. It should be noted that CXCR2 has more than one ligand, and CXCL8 is also an important regulatory factor for it. At present, it remains unclear whether there is a receptor competition or functional cooperation relationship between PPBP and CXCL8 in GBM, which requires further research to confirm.

In terms of therapeutic target discovery and drug screening, we innovatively combined computational simulation with experimental validation. Through virtual screening (based on the PPBP protein structure with PDB ID: 1NAP) and SPR experiments, we confirmed that Oenothein B, a natural polyphenolic compound, can bind to PPBP with high affinity and effectively inhibit the activation of the PPBP downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

In vitro functional experiments showed that Oenothein B can markedly impede the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GBM cells under hypoxic conditions, while reversing their abnormal lactate metabolism. In vivo experiments utilizing nude mouse orthotopic xenograft models further confirmed that Oenothein B can significantly inhibit tumor growth, prolong the survival period of model mice, and no obvious organ toxicity was observed, demonstrating good safety and therapeutic potential.

The anti-tumor activity of Oenothein B has been verified in various cancer models: in colorectal cancer, Oenothein B can relieve immune checkpoint inhibition and activate anti-tumor immune responses by directly binding to PD-1/PD-L1 proteins [46]; in non-small cell lung cancer, it can inhibit tumor cell proliferation and metastasis by downregulating the activity of the PI3K/Akt and NF-κB signaling pathways [47]. This study is the first to discover that Oenothein B can exert its anti-tumor effects by targeting PPBP in GBM, and its inhibitory effect on the PI3K/AKT pathway is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies. This further confirms the development potential of this compound as a multi-target anti-tumor drug and also provides a new candidate drug for the metabolic targeted therapy of GBM.

However, this study has several limitations. First, our bioinformatics analysis and prognostic model construction relied on public databases with a limited number of GBM samples, and the model’s generalizability requires validation in multi-center prospective cohorts. Second, functional experiments were based on two GBM cell lines, which cannot represent all molecular subtypes; validation in subtype-specific cell lines or patient-derived organoids is warranted. Third, the nude mouse model lacks an intact immune system, precluding evaluation of the impact on the GBM immune microenvironment and potential synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, regarding the translational potential of Oenothein B, a potential paradox exists: while PPBP’s oncogenic functions were hypoxia-dependent in vitro, Oenothein B administered from day 7 post-implantation showed efficacy before a substantial hypoxic gradient would be expected. This may reflect early localized hypoxic microregions and the reported multi-target properties of Oenothein B beyond PPBP binding. Furthermore, whether patients in the high-risk group defined by our prognostic model are more responsive to Oenothein B remains to be determined, and clinical translation involves additional challenges including pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and blood-brain barrier penetration. Future studies incorporating direct hypoxia detection, patient-derived GBM models stratified by risk scores, and comprehensive pharmacokinetic evaluation will be essential to address these gaps.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes a robust hypoxia-lactate metabolism-related gene signature for prognostic stratification in GBM, highlighting PPBP as a key oncogene that drives tumor progression under hypoxic conditions. Through integrated bioinformatics and experimental validation, we demonstrate that PPBP is transcriptionally activated by HIF-1α and promotes malignant phenotypes via the CXCR2/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, enhancing glycolysis, EMT, and immune suppression. Furthermore, we identify the natural compound Oenothein B as a potent inhibitor of PPBP, effectively suppressing tumor growth, lactate metabolism and EMT. These findings not only provide a novel prognostic tool for GBM but also offer a promising therapeutic strategy targeting the hypoxia–lactate metabolic axis, bridging the gap between computational discovery and clinical application.
