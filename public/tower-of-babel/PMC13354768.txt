A 5mC-related gene signature predicts prognosis and therapy response in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Shaohua Xu, Xuedong Niu, Changlin Zhang, Qianyuan Li, Chunhua Luo, Jun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreasing evidence has highlighted the biological significance of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) DNA modification in regulating tumorigenesis and cancer progression. However, the potential roles of the 5mC modification in HCC are still unknown. A consensus clustering algorithm was performed to determine 5mC modification patterns and establish a 5mC-related gene signature in HCC. Weighted gene co‑expression network analysis (WGCNA) integrated with machine learning approaches (LASSO, RF, and SVM‑RFE) was applied to identify candidate biomarkers, and a 5mC risk score model was subsequently constructed. The concordance index (C‑index), decision curve analysis (DCA), and ROC curve were employed to evaluate the performance of the nomogram. Two distinct 5mC modification patterns were identified, with cluster B exhibiting worse prognosis and enrichment of cell cycle and metabolic pathways. Using integrated WGCNA and machine learning, we constructed a three-gene (DNMT1, SPATS2, MCM6) risk score model that effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different overall survival. The risk score demonstrated robust prognostic performance across independent cohorts and was an independent prognostic factor. High risk scores correlated with advanced stage, higher tumor mutational burden, distinct immune microenvironment features, and increased sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic and targeted agents. A nomogram incorporating the risk score and clinicopathological features showed acceptable predictive accuracy and clinical net benefit. Experimental validation confirmed upregulation of DNMT1 and SPATS2 in HCC tissues, and their knockdown suppressed HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Introduction

Primary liver cancer, predominantly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC, ~ 90% of cases), is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide1. Characterized by insidious onset and low early diagnosis rates, most patients present at advanced stages, resulting in a dismal 5-year overall survival rate of only 12–15%2. Due to the wide heterogeneity of risk factors and pathogenesis of HCC, established strategies for prediction and prognostication remain limited3. Therefore, identifying reliable molecular biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognostic prediction is urgently needed.

Accumulating evidence highlights epigenetic alterations, particularly DNA methylation, as critical drivers of cancer initiation and progression. DNA methylation—a common epigenetic modification in mammals—is regulated by specific methylation regulators4. Among these, 5-methylcytosine (5mC) modifications play key roles in diverse biological processes, including gene expression regulation, genomic imprinting, and transposon inhibition5. Global DNA methylation profiles depend on the cross-talk among three types of 5mC regulators: writers (e.g., DNMTs), erasers (e.g., TETs), and readers (e.g., MDBs)6,7. DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) mediate the transfer of methyl groups to DNA, whereas the removal of the 5mC mark can occur either passively via DNA replication or actively through the ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes8. Imbalances between 5mC and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) marks lead to transcriptional dysregulation of promoters and enhancers, thereby altering cell fate.

In HCC, the mRNA levels of DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B are significantly upregulated and associated with poorer overall survival9. TET family proteins convert 5mC to 5hmC in an α‑ketoglutarate‑ and Fe(II)‑dependent manner8. Global 5hmC levels are significantly decreased in HCC, and correlate with poor differentiation and worse survival10,11. Methyl‑binding domain protein 2 (MBD2) is significantly involved in repressing transcription from hypermethylated promoters such as GSTP1 in HCC cells, and is associated with CpG island hypermethylation‑mediated gene silencing12; In addition, a novel blood‑based panel of methylated DNA markers has been developed for early‑stage HCC detection13. Although these findings indicate that 5mC modifications impact fundamental biological processes in HCC, the roles of 5mC regulators in shaping tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity, immune phenotypes, and therapeutic responses remain largely unexplored. Furthermore, a systematic model integrating multiple 5mC regulators to predict clinical outcomes and treatment efficacy is still lacking.

Therefore, we profiled 21 5mC regulators in HCC, characterized their modification landscape, identified two distinct 5mC modification subtypes, and established a 5mC risk scoring system (EME) to predict prognosis and treatment response, linking these regulators to TME, immune, and clinicopathological features.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

RNA-sequencing data, clinical data, somatic mutation data, and CNV files of HCC were retrieved from the TCGA-LIHC and ICGC-LIRI-JP database. Raw counts were also converted to transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values and further subjected to log2 transformation (TPM + 1). A total of 21 5mC regulators were collected from previously published literature.

Unsupervised clustering of 5mC regulator genes

Consensus unsupervised clustering analysis was conducted to explore distinct 5mC modification patterns using the ConsensusClusterPlus package. The number and stability of clusters were determined by a consensus clustering algorithm with 1,000 iterations. Detailed procedures were as previously described14.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the “DESeq2” R package15. An adjusted P < 0.001 and |log2FoldChange| > 1 were considered to be statistically significant. Using the “clusterProfiler” R package, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG16 analyses for the DEGs to identify the associated pathways. A false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

GSEA was employed to evaluate whether a priori defined gene sets exhibited statistically significant, concordant differences between the two phenotypic groups17. Significantly enriched pathways were defined by |NES| > 1, p-value < 0.05, and q-value < 0.05. GSVA was applied to calculate per-sample enrichment scores for predefined signaling pathways18, thereby quantifying their activity variations across subtypes and assessing the associated biological implications. Differences in hallmark pathways with |t-value| > 2 and p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp)19.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using WGCNA to identify modules associated with 5mC clusters20. After filtering genes by median absolute deviation from TCGA-LIHC data, a scale-free adjacency matrix was generated with a soft-threshold power of β = 6. Co‑expression modules were defined via topological overlap and hierarchical clustering (minModuleSize = 40, mergeCutHeight = 0.3). Module eigengenes were correlated with clinicopathological traits, and the module most strongly linked to 5mC clusters was retained for downstream analysis.

Identification of optimal feature gene biomarkers

To identify optimal diagnostic biomarkers, three machine‑learning algorithms—LASSO regression, SVM‑RFE, and random forest (RF)—were applied to select feature genes with high discriminative power. LASSO regression (binomial family, α = 1) was performed using the “glmnet” R package. SVM‑RFE (e1071 package) was evaluated through 10‑fold cross‑validation, with feature importance averaged across iterations. In RF, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) method was used to rank genes and determine the combination yielding the lowest error rate. Genes common to all three algorithms were considered candidate diagnostic biomarkers. Detailed procedures and parameters were as previously described14.

Construction of the 5mC risk score model for HCC prognosis

To screen for feature genes that independently predict prognosis, univariate Cox regression analysis was carried out employing the survival R package. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression algorithm was implemented to minimize the risk of overfitting. A multivariate Cox regression model was then used to identify the candidate genes that were used to establish the prognostic 5mC risk score. The risk score was calculated using the formula: 5mC risk score = Σ (gene expression × risk coefficient). Patients were then stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on the median risk score. Survival and ROC analyses were subsequently performed to evaluate the prognostic performance of the risk model.

Immunological characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (TME)

To investigate the role of the 5mC risk score in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TME), we assessed its correlation with multiple immunological features. These included the activity of the cancer‑immunity cycle, the infiltration levels of tumor‑infiltrating immune cells (TIICs), and the expression of immunomodulators and inhibitory immune checkpoints21,22. The activities of the cancer‑immunity cycle were quantified using single‑sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), as previously described.

Mutation and drug-susceptibility analysis

To explore the somatic mutation profiles in the different risk-score groups, mutation annotation format (MAF) data from TCGA-LIHC cohort were analyzed using the “maftools” R package23. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) scores were calculated for each HCC patient and compared between risk score groups. Immunophenoscore (IPS) data for HCC were obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) and compared between the different 5mC risk score groups to predict immunotherapeutic sensitivity. Furthermore, the semi-inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of common targeted/chemotherapy agents were predicted using the “oncoPredict” R package24. Drug and target information was sourced from DrugBank (https://go.Drugbank.com/).

Nomogram construction and validation

A nomogram integrating the 5mC risk score and key clinical variables (age, gender, TNM stage) was constructed using the “rms” R package25. Its prognostic performance was comprehensively evaluated through time‑dependent ROC analysis (with AUCs calculated at 1, 3, and 5 years), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) at the same time points26. Additionally, the concordance index (C-index) was employed to evaluate the predictive performance and discriminatory ability of the nomogram.

RT-qPCR analysis

A total of 35 paired HCC and adjacent normal tissue samples were obtained from Yichang Central People’s Hospital, with approval from the institutional ethics committee and written informed consent from all patients. For each patient, tumor tissue and matched adjacent non‑tumor tissue (≥ 1 cm from tumor margin) were collected. The cohort included 26 males and 9 females. Age range: 38–76 years (median 58 years). Tumor stage distribution: AJCC stage I (n = 12), stage II (n = 14), stage III (n = 9).

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol and reverse‑transcribed into cDNA via HiScript II Q RT SuperMix, after which qPCR was run using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. The primer sequences used were as follows: DNMT1 (Forward: GAGGAGGGCTACCTGGCTAA; Reverse: GCTTAGCCTCTCCATCGGAC), SPATS2 (Forward: GCGTTGCTTGCTGAAATGGA; Reverse: GCTACATCGGGATCTGGTCG), and MCM6 (Forward: CCAGTTCACCCAGAGCTTGT; Reverse: GGCAGATGTTTGGCTGTGTG).

Assessment of cell proliferation and migration

Cell proliferation and migration were assessed using CCK-8 and Transwell assays, as previously described27. For proliferation, transfected HCC cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured to the indicated time points. CCK-8 solution (10 µL/well) was added, and after 2 h of incubation, optical density (OD) was measured. For Transwell assays, invasion (with Matrigel) and migration (without Matrigel) assays used Transwell chambers with serum-free medium in the upper chamber and 10% FBS medium in the lower chamber. After 24 h, cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged.

Statistical analysis

Data processing, analysis, and visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). Continuous variables were summarized by mean ± standard deviation, with differences between groups assessed via Student’s t‑test or the Mann–Whitney U test. A two‑sided P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless stated otherwise.

Results

Biological characteristics of 5mC regulators in HCC

The workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Compared with normal tissues, nearly all 5mC epigenetic regulators were significantly upregulated in HCC (Fig. 1B), indicating the critical role of epigenetic regulation in this malignancy. As shown in Fig. 1C, most 5mC epigenetic regulators exhibited frequent copy number amplifications or deletions, with the most prevalent deletions observed in genes including DNMT1, UHRF1, TET2, MBD1/2/3, and ZBTB3. Survival analysis further indicated that most of these regulators were significantly associated with HCC prognosis (Supplementary Figure S1), suggesting their potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Consensus clustering identified k = 2 as the optimal number of clusters, yielding well-separated subgroups with minimal crossover among HCC samples (Fig. 1D). Survival analysis demonstrated that patients in 5mC cluster B had a significantly poorer prognosis than those in cluster A (Fig. 1E). GO/KEGG enrichment of DEGs between clusters (|log2FC| > 1, P-adj < 0.001) revealed pathways related to the cell cycle (e.g., DNA replication, cellular senescence), metabolism (e.g., drug metabolism, cysteine/methionine metabolism), and cancer-related pathways (e.g., ECM-receptor interaction) (Fig. 1F and G). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further showed that signaling transduction and cell cycle pathways were predominantly activated in 5mC cluster B, whereas metabolic processes were highly activated in cluster A (Fig. 1H). Consistently, GSVA of hallmark gene sets confirmed strong enrichment of cell cycle and metabolic activities in cluster B relative to cluster A, including DNA replication, PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 1I). Collectively, these findings suggest that 5mC epigenetic modifications are closely associated with the regulation of the cell cycle and metabolism, potentially contributing to tumor progression.

Identification of feature genes and construction of a 5mC risk model

To identify gene co-expression modules associated with the 5mC cluster, we performed WGCNA on the TCGA-LIHC dataset using the top 5,000 genes with the highest median absolute deviation (MAD). A sample dendrogram of 344 cases with complete clinical information was generated using average linkage hierarchical clustering and Pearson’s correlation (Fig. 2A), and eight distinct modules were identified for further investigation (Fig. 2C). Scale‑free topology analysis determined that a soft‑thresholding power of β = 6 (yielding R² = 0.9) was optimal for network construction (Fig. 2B). Evaluation of module–trait relationships indicated that the blue (r = 0.75, P = 6e-69), turquoise (r = 0.64, P = 4e-43), and yellow (r = 0.45, P = 1e-19) modules were significantly correlated with the 5mC cluster (Fig. 2D). Among these, the blue module exhibited the strongest correlation with clinical traits and was therefore selected for further analysis. Within this module, gene significance (GS) for 5mC cluster correlated strongly with module membership (MM) (cor = 0.94, P = 1e-200), leading to the identification of 153 hub genes (Fig. 2E). To identify key feature genes from the 153 hub genes, three machine learning algorithms (SVM-RFE, RF and LASSO) were implemented. From among those genes, LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation identified 27 potential signature genes (Fig. 2F). The SVM-RFE algorithm with 10-fold cross-validation determined the top 55 reliable candidate genes based on accuracy and the lowest error rate (Fig. 2G). The RF model ranked genes by relative importance and retained 35 genes with a MeanDecreaseGini score greater than 1.3 (Fig. 2H).

Then, to obtain a robust set of 5mC cluster-related feature genes, we intersected the genes selected by the three algorithms, yielding 14 key candidates (Fig. 2I). Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that all 14 genes were significantly associated with patient prognosis (Fig. 2J). These genes were then subjected to LASSO Cox regression analysis. Based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance criterion, three optimal prognostic genes—DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6—were retained (Fig. 2K and L). Using these genes, a 5mC-related risk model was constructed based on RNA expression profiles as follows: Risk score = (− 0.561 × expression of DNMT1) + (0.616 × expression of SPATS2) + (0.494 × expression of MCM6). Finally, to evaluate the individual contribution of each gene, we performed a leave‑one‑out ablation experiment on the training set. As shown in Figs. 2M-O, the complete model achieved a 5‑year AUC of 0.668, whereas ablation of DNMT1, SPATS2, or MCM6 each resulted in a reduced AUC (maximum Δ = 0.043), indicating that all three genes provide non‑redundant prognostic information and that each contributes independently to risk stratification. These genes (DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6) are associated with the 5mC modification patterns, although further studies are needed to determine whether they are direct targets of 5mC regulation.”

Evaluation of the prognostic value of the risk scoring model

Following the construction of 5mC-related risk score model, we evaluated and validated its prognostic performance. In the TCGA training set, 184 HCC patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups (n = 92 each) based on the median risk score. As shown in Fig. 3A, patients with higher 5mC risk scores exhibited significantly shorter overall survival (OS). The risk model demonstrated robust prognostic accuracy, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.724, 0.701, and 0.668 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. 3B). This prognostic value was consistently validated in the TCGA internal validation set, yielding AUC values of 0.669, 0.627, and 0.534 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. 3C and D). External validation in an independent HCC cohort further confirmed the model’s generalizability (Fig. 3E and F). These findings indicate that the 5mC risk score serves as a robust prognostic panel for HCC patients.

A marked difference in survival status was observed between the risk groups, with a substantially higher mortality rate in the high-risk group (Fig. 3G). Moreover, 5mC risk score was positively associated with advanced AJCC stage (III–IV vs. I–II; Fig. 3H). Given that genomic mutations are key drivers of tumor malignancy, we further examined the mutational landscape. The high-risk subgroup displayed a higher overall mutation frequency, with TP53 and CTNNB1 being the most frequently altered genes in HCC (Fig. 3I and J). Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was also elevated in the high-risk group, and lower TMB was correlated with better OS (Fig. 3K). When patients were stratified by both TMB and 5mC risk, those with high TMB and high 5mC risk had the poorest prognosis (Fig. 3L). Collectively, these results establish the 5mC risk model as a promising prognostic indicator for HCC patients.

The risk score predicts immune phenotypes and therapeutic opportunities

We investigated the presence of immune heterogeneity in different 5mC risk score groups. As shown in Fig. 4A, the 5mC risk score showed a positive correlation with most immunotherapy-related pathways, including the cell cycle, DNA replication, and p53 signaling pathway. The activities of several components of the cancer immunity cycle were also significantly upregulated in the low 5mC risk group, including immune cell infiltration (Step 5) and trafficking of immune cells to tumors (Step 4, primarily those exerting antitumor immunity), such as CD4 T cell recruitment, NK cell recruitment, and Th2 cell recruitment (Fig. 4B). The activity of the cancer immunity cycle directly reflects the integrated functions of the chemokine system and other immunomodulators28,29. Additionally, several immunosuppressive oncogenic pathways (including radiotherapy‑predicted pathways and EGFR ligands) showed significantly higher enrichment scores in the high 5mC risk group (Fig. 4C). This observation implies that HCC patients with high 5mC risk scores could be responsive to radiotherapy. A heatmap of the correlation matrix demonstrated that the 5mC risk score was markedly positively correlated with immune activation process and DNA repair signatures (Fig. 4D).

The Immunophenoscore (IPS) is a well-recognized indicator of patient response to immunotherapy, and significant differences were observed between the two risk score groups, indicating differential responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) between the two 5mC risk groups (Fig. 4E). IC50 values revealed that HCC patients with high 5mC risk scores exhibited greater sensitivity to common chemotherapeutic drugs, including dactinomycin, irinotecan, mitoxantrone, nelarabine, and oxaliplatin (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, HCC patients with higher 5mC risk scores also showed increased sensitivity to several targeted drugs, such as ribociclib, entinostat, niraparib, selumetinib, and topotecan (Fig. 4G). These results demonstrate that the 5mC risk score may help identify effective antitumor agents and guide precision medicine therapies for HCC treatment.

Construction and evaluation of a nomogram

To determine whether the 5mC risk score model could serve as an independent prognostic factor in HCC, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, the hazard ratios (HRs) for the 5mC risk score were 1.471 (95%CI: 1.280–1.692; p < 0.001) in univariate analysis and 1.404 (95%CI: 1.206–1.635; p < 0.001) in multivariate analysis, indicating that 5mC risk score was an independent prognostic indicator. To enhance clinical applicability, a nomogram integrating the 5mC risk score with clinicopathological characteristics was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. As shown in Fig. 5C, the 5mC risk score and TNM stage were the dominant contributors to OS prediction. The predictive performance and accuracy of the nomogram were assessed using the concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, the C-index of the nomogram was 0.682 (95% CI: 0.564–0.781) (Fig. 5D). Calibration plots showed excellent concordance between predicted and actual 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year OS in the TCGA‑LIHC cohort (Fig. 5E). Time-dependent ROC analysis further confirmed the predictive capability of the nomogram, with AUC values of 0.741, 0.742, and 0.702 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. 5F-H). DCA revealed that the nomogram incorporating the 5mC risk model provided greater net benefit compared to other clinical traits in predicting OS at all three time points in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. 5I–K), suggesting robust prognostic accuracy and clinical utility.

Experimental validation of key gene expression and biological function

To verify the three risk genes, we obtained the expression data from HCC patients and cell lines. As shown in Figs. 6A-E, the transcript levels of DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6 were significantly elevated in HCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. Consistent with these findings, immunohistochemical data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) revealed markedly increased protein expression of DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6 in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of HCC tissues relative to normal liver controls (Fig. 6F). We next investigated the relationship between DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6 expression and clinical outcomes. Survival analysis indicated that patients with high expression of any of these three genes had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared to those with low expression based on TCGA-LIHC dataset (Fig. 6G). To further validate these findings, we examined DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6 expression in an independent cohort of 35 paired HCC tissues from Yichang using RT‑qPCR. The results confirmed significant upregulation of all three genes in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 6H), further supporting the conclusions of our study.

To explore the functional roles of DNMT1 and SPATS2 in HCC, we generated stable cell lines with knockdown of DNMT1 or SPATS2 for in vitro experiments. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed at the mRNA level (Fig. 6I). Functional assays revealed that silencing DNMT1 or SPATS2 significantly suppressed HCC cell migration and invasion, as demonstrated by Transwell assays (Fig. 6J). Additionally, knockdown of either gene markedly reduced cell proliferation, evidenced by decreased EdU-positive rates (Fig. 6K) and impaired growth in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 6L and M). These findings not only confirm the reliability of the identified risk gene signature but also highlight the oncogenic roles of DNMT1 and SPATS2 in hepatocellular carcinoma progression.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the landscape of 5mC DNA modification in HCC and constructed an epigenetic module eigengene (EME) risk score model based on 5mC-related genes. This model demonstrated robust predictive performance for patient survival, immune microenvironment features, and drug sensitivity across multiple cohorts, offering new insights for molecular subtyping and personalized therapy in HCC.

We first observed significant dysregulation of 21 5mC regulators in HCC, predominantly attributable to copy number amplification rather than high-frequency mutations. This suggests that aberrations in the 5mC regulatory network may represent a widespread upstream oncogenic driver in HCC, promoting tumor progression by modulating downstream target genes. Survival analysis demonstrated that most 5mC regulators were significantly associated with patient prognosis, laying a solid foundation for subsequent molecular subtyping and risk modeling based on these regulators. Using consensus clustering, we identified two 5mC modification subtypes (clusters A and B) with distinct prognoses and molecular features. Cluster B exhibited poorer survival and significant enrichment in proliferation-related pathways, including cell cycle progression, PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, and metabolic reprogramming. In contrast, cluster A showed enrichment in basic metabolic processes, aligning with its better prognosis. These findings reveal that 5mC modification contributes to HCC heterogeneity by coordinating key biological processes.

To translate this heterogeneity into a clinically applicable metric, we combined WGCNA with three machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE, and RF) to identify core genes—DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6—from the module most associated with 5mC subtypes. This multi-step, multi-algorithm screening strategy minimized overfitting risks and ensured the robustness and representativeness of the selected genes. The resulting risk score demonstrated meaningful prognostic performance across training, validation, and external cohorts, functioning independently of traditional clinicopathological parameters. Biologically, DNMT1 maintains DNA methylation and is a well-established oncogene30–32. Its inclusion in the model suggests potential crosstalk between DNA and RNA methylation regulatory networks in HCC, collectively driving tumor progression. MCM6, essential for DNA replication licensing, correlates with proliferation and poor prognosis33. SPATS2, though less studied, has been implicated as an oncogene in various cancers 34,35. Our study is the first to link SPATS2 with 5mC modification and confirm its oncogenic role in HCC; SPATS2 knockdown suppressed malignant phenotypes in vitro, identifying it as a potential therapeutic target. Consistent with this, the functional roles of these three genes—specifically in DNA replication and cell cycle control—mirror the pathways enriched in the high-risk 5mC subtype. This alignment reinforces the hypothesis that 5mC-driven dysregulation of these core processes is a key determinant of poor prognosis in HCC.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) serves as an indicator of tumor genomic instability, and high TMB theoretically implies more neoantigen production, potentially conferring greater sensitivity to immunotherapy36,37. Integration of risk score with TMB revealed that high TMB with high-risk scores correlated with poorest prognosis, despite theoretical immunogenicity advantages. This suggests that the malignant phenotype represented by high-risk scores (e.g., hyperproliferation, metabolic hyperactivity) may outweigh the potential immunogenicity advantages conferred by high TMB, leading to immune evasion and unfavorable outcomes. This also explains why high-risk group patients showed greater sensitivity to various chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., irinotecan, oxaliplatin) and targeted drugs (e.g., the cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitor ribociclib, the PARP inhibitor niraparib). Most of these drugs target DNA replication, the cell cycle, or DNA damage repair pathways—precisely matching the biological characteristics of high-risk group tumors. Moreover, high-risk group showed lower Immunophenoscore (IPS) predictions for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICB), suggesting limited benefit from immunotherapy alone. This observation aligns with the concept that “proliferative” tumors typically manifest as “immune-cold” tumors with poor responsiveness to immunotherapy38. Thus, the 5mC risk model may serve not only as a prognostic tool but also as a predictor of treatment response, guiding precision therapy in HCC.

To facilitate clinical application, we constructed a nomogram incorporating the risk score and clinicopathological parameters, with high accuracy in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival. Decision curve analysis confirmed its superior net benefit over traditional indicators, offering an intuitive, quantitative tool for individualized risk assessment.

Despite the comprehensive findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all analyses were based on retrospective data; although internally and externally validated, large-scale prospective studies are ultimately needed to confirm the clinical applicability of this risk model. Second, our model relies on transcript levels of 5mC regulators rather than direct 5mC measurement; future studies integrating 5mC sequencing with expression data are warranted. Moreover, whether DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6 are direct targets of 5mC modification remains unknown, representing an important direction for future epigenetic investigations. Third, functional validation of DNMT1 and SPATS2 was limited to in vitro experiments. Their in vivo oncogenic roles and specific molecular mechanisms—particularly whether SPATS2 acts as a direct 5mC target or participates in the 5mC regulatory network—require further exploration using animal models and mechanistic studies. Additionally, MCM6, though included in the model, was not functionally validated here, warranting future research. Fourth, immune cell infiltration and cancer-immunity cycle activities were inferred using ssGSEA, a computational method. Future studies employing flow cytometry, multiplex IHC, or single‑cell sequencing are needed to validate these immune landscape predictions. We also note that the Immunophenoscore (IPS) is a well‑established computational predictor of immunotherapy response derived from TCIA, but this remains a prediction requiring experimental confirmation. Finally, given the complexity of 5mC regulatory networks, our three-gene model, while robust, may not fully capture the entire landscape of 5mC modification. Integrating 5mC modification profiles with risk scores in future studies could provide deeper insights. Importantly, this study is primarily a prognostic modeling and bioinformatic investigation that generates hypotheses, and we call for future experimental and epigenetic validation.

In conclusion, this study systematically characterized 5mC DNA modification in HCC, identified distinct modification patterns, and constructed a risk score model based on DNMT1, SPATS2, and MCM6. This model accurately predicts prognosis, reflects immune and drug response features, and provides insights for molecular subtyping and precision therapy. Experimental validation further supports the oncogenic roles of DNMT1 and SPATS2, underscoring the significance of 5mC modification in HCC biology and paving the way for translational applications and future basic research.
