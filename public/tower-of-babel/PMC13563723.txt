Malaria Outbreak in Meinit Goldia District, Southwest Ethiopia Peoples Regional State, 2025: Risk factors and epidemiological insights from a case-control study
Authors: Nigatu Admasu Desta, Simon Fikadu, Gemechu Chemeda, Abraham Lomboro Dimore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a severe, life-threatening disease worldwide, with Sub-Saharan Africa bearing the greatest burden. In Ethiopia, recurrent outbreaks cause substantial morbidity and strain local health systems. During WHO Epidemiologic Week 13 of 2025, Meinit Goldia District experienced a sharp increase in cases with delayed investigation and response. This study aimed to verify the outbreak, identify associated risk factors, and inform targeted control measures.MethodsA community-based case–control study was conducted in Meinit Goldia District from 26 March to 30 June 2025. Descriptive analysis used the district linelist of malaria cases and deaths. A multistage cluster sampling technique with proportional allocation was employed. A total of 145 rapid diagnostic test (RDT) confirmed malaria cases and 145 community-based, RDT negative asymptomatic controls were included in the study. Both case and controls were identified through active house-to-house surveillance in malaria-affected kebeles. Data on socio-demographics, ITN utilization, environmental exposures, and malaria knowledge were collected via interviewer-administered questionnaires using Kobo Collect v2025.3.3. Analyses were performed in STATA v17. Variables with P < 0.25 in bivariable analysis entered multivariable logistic regression; significance was set at P < 0.05. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Firth’s correction was used for exposures with sparse data.ResultsReview of the line list from the malaria-affected kebeles identified 3,630 laboratory-confirmed malaria cases, yielding an attack rate (AR) of 274.7 cases per 1,000 population in the eight outbreak-affected villages. The highest attack rates were observed among males (288.2 per 1,000 population) and residents of Mehal Dashi village. At the district level, the overall AR was 71.3 cases per 1,000 population. In multivariable analysis, the following variables were independently associated with malaria: Never utilized ITNs (AOR = 4.882; 95% CI: 2.011–11.847), Living near broken glass bottles (AOR = 4.847; 95% CI: 1.692–13.889), Open deep well near household (AOR = 3.735; 95% CI: 1.273–10.956), Poor knowledge of malaria transmission, signs, symptoms, and prevention/control measures (AOR = 2.847; 95% CI: 1.502–5.397), Sometimes utilized ITNs (AOR = 2.617; 95% CI: 1.204–5.692), and Wearing long protective clothing at night (AOR = 0.123; 95% CI: 0.054–0.280).ConclusionsThe outbreak in Meinit Goldia District was associated with gaps in vector control, local breeding sites, and delayed response. Immediate priorities include targeted ITN distribution and larval source management in high-burden areas, regular IRS, culturally appropriate health education, strengthened early warning and rapid response capacity, and enhanced entomological surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13844-z.

Background

Malaria is a febrile illness caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1, 3]. It remains one of the most severe and life-threatening infectious diseases worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions [2, 3]. The disease causes substantial morbidity, mortality, and socioeconomic burden, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, especially children under five years of age and pregnant women. Globally, nearly half of the world’s population is at risk of malaria [3]. In Ethiopia, malaria transmission is seasonal and unstable, with peak transmission typically occurring between September and December following the major rainy season from June to August [4, 5].

According to the 2025 World Health Organization (WHO) malaria report, an estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 malaria-related deaths occurred across 80 malaria-endemic countries, representing an increase of approximately nine million cases compared with 2023 [3]. The WHO African Region continues to bear the highest malaria burden, accounting for approximately 95% of global malaria cases and deaths. Children under five years of age contributed to nearly 75% of all malaria-related deaths in the region [3]. Beyond its health consequences, malaria imposes substantial economic burdens on households, healthcare systems, and national economies. In some African countries, malaria has been reported to reduce annual economic growth by approximately 1.3% [6].

Malaria remains endemic in Ethiopia, with periodic outbreaks occurring in various parts of the country. Approximately 75% of Ethiopia’s landmass and 69% of its population are at risk of malaria, particularly in areas located below 2,000 m above sea level [4, 5]. Malaria continues to be a major public health challenge, with more than 80% of cases occurring among adults and children aged five years and above [5, 7]. Plasmodium falciparum (65%) and Plasmodium vivax (35%) are the predominant malaria parasite species in the country [5]. Anopheles arabiensis is the principal malaria vector in Ethiopia, widely distributed across diverse ecological settings and commonly breeding in small, temporary, sunlit water collections such as rain pools [5, 8].

Although Ethiopia achieved a substantial decline in malaria cases from approximately 3.8 million in 2010 to about 994,000 in 2019, the burden rebounded to nearly 1.6 million cases in 2022 [5, 28]. The highest malaria burden has been documented in Gambella, Benishangul-Gumuz, western Amhara, and Tigray regions, particularly in lowland areas below 1,500 m where transmission intensity is high [5]. Persistent challenges such as vector resistance to insecticides, antimalarial drug resistance, improper use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), weak surveillance systems, and inadequate implementation of vector control interventions continue to hinder malaria prevention and control efforts [5, 19, 20–25]. Furthermore, emerging threats such as Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP-2) gene deletion, which compromises rapid diagnostic test (RDT) sensitivity, and the spread of the invasive vector Anopheles stephensi across several parts of the country further complicate malaria control efforts [5, 26].

In response to the malaria burden, the WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria (2016–2030) provides a comprehensive framework for malaria control and elimination, aiming to reduce malaria morbidity and mortality by at least 90%, eliminate malaria in at least 35 countries, and prevent malaria resurgence in malaria-free countries by 2030 [3]. Similarly, the President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI) Strategy (2021–2026), End Malaria Faster, seeks to accelerate malaria prevention and elimination by strengthening malaria surveillance, expanding access to insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), improving case management, and supporting indoor residual spraying (IRS) in high-burden countries, including Ethiopia [5]. Proven interventions such as ITNs, IRS, prompt diagnosis, and effective treatment remain critical to reducing malaria-related morbidity and mortality, especially in high-transmission settings [8, 19, 20].

Ethiopia has also implemented the National Malaria Strategic Plan (NMSP) (2021–2025), which aims to reduce malaria cases and deaths by 50% compared with the 2020 baseline and ultimately eliminate malaria by 2030 [8]. The strategic plan prioritizes early diagnosis and treatment, surveillance strengthening, vector control interventions including ITNs and IRS, and prevention of malaria reintroduction in malaria-free areas [2, 8, 27].

Despite these national and global efforts, malaria continues to pose a significant public health challenge in Southwest Ethiopia. In 2025, a total of 2,147,631 suspected fever cases were tested in the Southwest Ethiopia People’s Region (SWEPRS), of which 1,258,464 were confirmed malaria cases, yielding a malaria positivity rate of 58.6%. Of these confirmed cases, 64.5% were attributed to Plasmodium falciparum [9]. During the same period, West Omo Zone reported 146,036 suspected fever cases, among which 96,050 malaria cases were confirmed, resulting in a positivity rate of 65.8%; approximately 73% of confirmed cases were caused by Plasmodium falciparum [9].

Within West Omo Zone, malaria remains one of the leading public health problems in Meinit Goldia District and was reported among the top ten causes of morbidity in health facilities in 2025 [10]. Beginning in March 2025, the district experienced an unusually high number of malaria cases despite no documented malaria outbreaks during the preceding five years. During WHO epidemiological week 13 of 2025, a total of 179 malaria cases were reported, substantially exceeding the five-year epidemic threshold (2020–2024) of 36 cases for the same epidemiological week.

Several factors have been associated with malaria outbreaks, including improper utilization of insecticide-treated nets, poor environmental sanitation, the presence of mosquito breeding sites near residential areas, inadequate indoor residual spraying, local climatic conditions, drought, conflict, and environmental modifications that favor vector proliferation [11–18]. Although approximately 64% of households in malaria-endemic areas owned at least one ITN, utilization remains suboptimal, with a substantial gap between ownership and proper use [1, 2, 15]. Limited resources, socioeconomic instability, and inadequate implementation of malaria control strategies further compromise effective prevention and response efforts [16].

Early detection and containment of malaria outbreaks are essential to interrupt transmission, reduce morbidity and mortality, and minimize economic losses, particularly in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, outbreak investigations are crucial for verifying the existence of outbreaks, identifying associated risk factors, and implementing timely evidence-based interventions to prevent recurrence. Therefore, this study aimed to verify the existence of the malaria outbreak, identify risk factors associated with malaria transmission, and support appropriate response interventions in Meinit Goldia District, West Omo Zone, Southwest Ethiopia People’s Regional State.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at Meinit Goldia district (Fig. 1) which is one of the 10 districts in West Omo Zone of Southwest Ethiopia Peoples Regional State. Its elevation is from 1478 to 2079 m above sea level and located at a distance of 30 km from Jemu, the capital of the West Omo Zone and 860 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. It is divided into 17 kebeles with an estimated total population of 50,898. The district has three health centers, one primary hospital, and 17 health posts. It experiences an average annual temperature and rainfall range of 17 to 29 °C and 970 to 1,400 mm, respectively. It has two agroecological zones: “Weyna Dega” (midland between 1000 m and 1500 m) and “Dega” (cool temperature highlands greater than 1500 m above sea level) [8].

Study design, period, and population

A mixed epidemiological study design was employed, comprising an initial descriptive outbreak investigation followed by an analytical unmatched community-based case–control study. The descriptive component included routine surveillance data review, epidemic threshold analysis, and laboratory-confirmed case investigation to verify the existence of a malaria outbreak in Meinit Goldia District.

The study was conducted from 26 March 2025 to 30 June 2025 in Meinit Goldia District, Southwest Ethiopia People Regional State. The source population comprised all individuals residing in the district during the study period, while the study population included all eligible individuals selected from the outbreak-affected kebeles.

Inclusion criteria

Cases

Cases were individuals who resided in the malaria-affected kebeles of Meinit Goldia District for at least two weeks prior to illness onset, presented with clinical signs and symptoms consistent with malaria (such as fever, chills, headache, or sweating) during the outbreak period, tested positive for malaria infection using RDT during the investigation, and provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Controls

Controls were selected from neighboring community residents within the same malaria-affected kebeles as the cases and were identified through active house-to-house surveillance during the outbreak investigation. Eligible controls were neighboring community members without signs or symptoms of malaria and with negative malaria RDT results who provided informed consent to participate in the study. Controls were selected regardless of their age, gender, educational status, and socio-economic status.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria:

Had received antimalarial treatment within four weeks prior to testing, due to the potential for reduced parasite detection and false-negative RDT results;Were critically ill, had impaired consciousness, or were otherwise unable to participate in the interview, and no reliable caregiver or proxy respondent was available;Had resided in the study kebeles for less than two weeks prior to illness onset or enrollment, to ensure adequate exposure assessment during the outbreak period.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using Epi Info™ version 7.2.5.0 by considering several epidemiologically relevant exposure variables identified from previous malaria outbreak investigations conducted in Ethiopia. Assumptions included a 95% confidence level, 80% power, a 1:1 case-control ratio, and proportions of exposure among controls and adjusted odds ratios obtained from published literature [11, 13, 29, 31, 32, 40]. Among the candidate variables assessed, the presence of mosquito breeding site (intermittent river within a 1 km vicinity of the household) yielded the largest sample size using an odds ratio of 4.28 and 70.3% exposure among controls [29]. The calculated sample size was 132. After considering a design effect of 2 and a 10% allowance for non-response, the final sample size became 290 participants (145 cases and 145 controls) (Table 1).

Sampling technique

A multistage cluster sampling technique was employed during the outbreak investigation. The sampling frame for the first stage consisted of all ten malaria-affected kebeles in Meinit Goldia district. Four kebeles (Bigelech, Tenkir, Duma, and Chat) were selected by simple random sampling. In the second stage, a list of all villages (Gotts) within each selected kebele was obtained, and two villages were randomly selected from each kebele, resulting in eight study villages (Gongul, Zuma, Wayecha, Yeba, Mehal Dashi, Kudu Shuntuwa, Baluit, and Zingira). The total sample size was then allocated proportionally to the population size of each selected village. Both case and controls were identified through active house-to-house surveillance in malaria-affected kebeles within the same villages and screened using the predefined eligibility criteria. Because participants were recruited within geographically defined clusters (villages/Gotts), a design effect of 2 was applied during sample size determination to account for potential intra-cluster correlation and the increased variance associated with cluster sampling in outbreak investigations. The lottery method was applied to select individual participants in the selected household (Fig. 2).

Study variables

The dependent variable for this study was malaria illness, categorized as Yes or No.

The independent variables included socio-demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, educational status, occupation, marital status, and family size. Potential malaria risk factors assessed were staying outdoors overnight, wearing long protective clothing at night, travel history, housing type, presence of mosquito breeding sites near the home, sleeping under an insecticide-treated net (ITN) the previous night, frequency of ITN utilization, and adequacy of ITN ownership. In addition, participants’ knowledge regarding malaria transmission, signs and symptoms, and prevention and control measures was assessed.

Operational definitions

Threshold

A critical number of malaria cases used to confirm the occurrence of a malaria epidemic and trigger appropriate control and response measures [13].

Malaria outbreak

An increase in malaria cases exceeding the epidemic threshold during a specific epidemiological week, compared with the second-highest number of malaria cases reported during the corresponding week over the previous five years [13].

Wearing protective clothing

The practice of wearing long-sleeved clothes and garments that cover the legs and arms during nighttime to reduce exposure to mosquito bites [29].

Stagnant water near home

The presence of standing or immobile water within a one-kilometer radius of the respondent’s household, which may serve as a breeding site for mosquitoes [29].

Adequate insecticide-treated nets (ITNs)

Availability of at least one insecticide-treated net for every two household members [30].

Mosquito breeding site

The presence of environmental conditions conducive to mosquito breeding around the dwelling, including stagnant water, breeding materials, and tick grass near the household [31].

Outdoor overnight stay

Individuals who remain outdoors for more than six hours during nighttime, thereby increasing exposure to mosquito bites [29].

Screened house

A household equipped with screened windows and closed eaves designed to prevent mosquito entry during nighttime [31].

Village

In the Ethiopian context, a village refers to the smallest rural community unit within the local administrative structure, commonly recognized as a got or sub-unit of a kebele, typically comprising approximately 100 households.

Good knowledge

A score of ≥80% (≥16 correct responses out of 20 questions) on the malaria knowledge assessment tool, consistent with standard Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) study thresholds used in malaria research [32].

Data collection tool and procedures

The descriptive data were collected using a standard line list prepared according to national PHEM guideline. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was prepared after reviewing related literatures [13, 29, 30, 33]. The tool gathers information on socio-demographic characteristics, clinical presentation (for cases), potential risk factors, and knowledge of malaria transmission, prevention, and control. The data collection was conducted by eight health extension workers, one field epidemiology resident, and three laboratory technologists using Kobo Collect data collection tool on Android mobile devices.

Measurement of knowledge

Knowledge of malaria transmission, signs and symptoms, and prevention methods was assessed using a structured questionnaire administered to study participants. The questionnaire included multiple items covering the cause of malaria, modes of transmission, major signs and symptoms, and key preventive measures such as insecticide-treated net use, environmental management, and early care-seeking. A total of 20 questions were included. Each correct response was awarded one point, while incorrect or “don’t know” responses received zero. The total knowledge score was calculated by summing the points across all items. Participants who scored at least 80% of the total were classified as having good knowledge, whereas those who scored below 80% were classified as having poor knowledge.

Laboratory methods

During the outbreak investigation, microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) were used in different operational contexts. Giemsa-stained blood film microscopy was the primary diagnostic method during the outbreak verification and early response phase. It was conducted at district health centers and the hospital laboratories by trained laboratory personnel and was used for routine diagnosis, case management, and confirmation of the outbreak.

In contrast, for the analytical case-control study and community-based active case finding, malaria status was determined using CareStart™ Malaria Pf/Pv (HRP2/pLDH) Ag Combo RDTs (Access Bio, USA) which is designed to diagnose malaria infections caused by Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax, particularly in resource limited settings [34]. It is highly effective in diagnosing malaria (sensitivity 93.2% and specificity 98.6%) [35]. These RDTs were used at the community level by health extension workers, laboratory technologists, and surveillance teams during household visits, where immediate field diagnosis was required and microscopy was not readily available.

Data quality management

To ensure data quality, data collectors received a half-day orientation on the purpose of study and selection of cases and controls. Standard precautions followed, from sample collection to laboratory examination during malaria diagnosis. Before analysis, data cleaning was done for completeness and consistency of the collected data. Data were collected under the supervision of the principal investigator and Zonal PHEM directorate.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were checked for completeness and consistency, coded and cleaned using Microsoft Excel 2019 format, then imported to STATA version 17 for analysis. The extent of the outbreak was explained with respect to person, place, and time. Characteristics of both cases and controls were described using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and mean (± SD) for continuous variables. Hosmer and Lemshow’s goodness of fit test was conducted to test the model (P = 0.751).

For variables with sparse contingency tables (≤ 5 exposed units in any cell), Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was employed. This method addresses bias and inflated standard errors resulting from small sample sizes in exposure categories while maintaining statistical validity and interpretability (Heinze & Schemper, 2002). Specifically, “broken glass bottles” (19 cases vs. 4 controls exposed) and “open deep well” (15 cases vs. 4 controls exposed) exposures presented with limited exposed controls, warranting this bias-reduction approach (Table S1: Supplementary Material).

Variables with P < 0.25 in bivariable logistic regression were included in multivariable analysis. In multivariable models, variables with P < 0.05, accompanied by adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), were considered significantly associated with malaria outbreak. For variables with sparse data, Firth’s penalized AOR and CI are reported as the primary estimates.

Verifying the existence of malaria outbreak

An early warning of increased malaria cases in Meinit Goldia district was reported by the West Omo Zone Health Department during WHO Week 13, 2025. In response, the Southwest Ethiopia People Regional Health Bureau and Southwest Ethiopia Public Health Institute Public Health Emergency Management (PHEM) Directorate deployed a RRT to investigate the alert and support control measures.

Data review and initial verification

The outbreak verification team first reviewed routine surveillance data, including weekly PHEM reports and inpatient and outpatient disease registries from health facilities. This process aimed to assess the validity of the reported increase in malaria cases and to exclude potential influences such as reporting bias, duplication, or changes in healthcare-seeking behavior. Cross-validation of multiple data sources was undertaken to ensure the reliability of the reported increase before confirming an outbreak signal.

Epidemic threshold analysis

A retrospective analysis of weekly malaria surveillance data from 2020 to 2024 was conducted at the district health office and participating health facilities to establish the epidemic threshold. The number of malaria cases reported in WHO Week 13, 2025, was then compared with this threshold. The analysis demonstrated that malaria cases in 2025 exceeded the established epidemic threshold, confirming an unusual increase in transmission. Notably, such a rise had not been observed in the district over the preceding five years.

Diagnostic approach and outbreak confirmation

During the outbreak investigation period (26 March 2025 to 30 June 2025), different diagnostic approaches were used depending on the setting. Giemsa-stained blood film microscopy was the primary diagnostic method at health facilities and was used for routine diagnosis, outbreak verification, and patient management. During this period, the Regional, Zonal, and District Rapid Response Teams, in collaboration with local health facilities, examined 6424 febrile individuals using microscopy. Of these, 3630 were confirmed malaria cases, yielding a test positivity rate of 56.5%. Among confirmed cases, 2247 (62.0%) were due to Plasmodium falciparum. The high-test positivity rate, together with the sustained increase in case counts above the epidemic threshold, confirmed the occurrence of a malaria outbreak in the district.

Description of the outbreak by person

During the investigation period, 3630 confirmed malaria cases were diagnosed and documented on the linelist. The overall attack rate (AR) for the district was 71.3 cases per 1,000 population among the total district population of 50,898. However, the AR in this study was 274.7 cases per 1,000 population among the total at-risk population of 13,213 in the eight outbreak-affected study villages (Table 3).

Description of the outbreak by place

The village with the highest malaria positivity rate was reported from Gongul (82.3%), followed by Yeba (74.9%), and Zuma (67.6%). In the same way, Mehal Dashi, Wayecha, and Zingira villages reported the highest attack rates per 1,000 populations, with rates of 474.3, 336.1, and 311.6, respectively (Table 4).

Analytical results

Factors associated with malaria outbreak

Variables with P < 0.25 in the bivariable logistic regression analysis were included in multivariable analysis. These candidate variables were age, education, staying outside overnight, wearing long protective clothing at night, availability of mosquito breeding sites around home, sleeping under ITN last night, frequency of ITN utilization, and knowledge on malaria transmission, signs and symptoms, and prevention measures.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, six factors were independently associated with malaria infection.

Individuals who never used ITNs had nearly five times higher odds of malaria compared to those who always used ITNs (AOR = 4.882; 95% CI: 2.011–11.847). Those who sometimes used ITNs also had significantly increased odds (AOR = 2.617; 95% CI: 1.204–5.692), indicating a dose-response relationship between ITN use consistency and malaria protection.

Living near broken glass bottles was associated with approximately 4.8-fold higher odds of malaria (AOR = 4.847; 95% CI: 1.692–13.889). Firth’s penalized logistic regression was applied for this variable due to sparse data in the exposed control group (n = 4). Similarly, presence of an open deep well near the household was associated with 3.7-fold higher odds of malaria (AOR = 3.735; 95% CI: 1.273–10.956), and Firth’s correction was used to obtain stable confidence intervals.

Individuals with poor knowledge of malaria transmission, signs, symptoms, and prevention/control measures had nearly three times higher odds of malaria compared to those with good knowledge (AOR = 2.847; 95% CI: 1.502–5.397). Conversely, wearing long protective clothing at night was strongly protective, associated with 87.7% lower odds of malaria (AOR = 0.123; 95% CI: 0.054–0.280) (Table 6).

Public health interventions

Malaria diagnosis and case management

During the investigation period, comprehensive case management was provided for all 3630 laboratory-confirmed malaria cases in accordance with the National Malaria Guideline [37].

Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE) activities

The district health office deployed multidisciplinary health professionals to affected kebeles to deliver targeted health education on malaria prevention and control measures. A total of 2245 community members received comprehensive information on malaria transmission, early warning signs, appropriate care-seeking behavior, and preventive practices including proper ITN use and environmental management. Communication strategies were culturally adapted to ensure community understanding and acceptance.

Environmental and vector control assessment

During this analytical case–control household level study, field assessment also identified 7 temporary and 11 permanent mosquito breeding sites within the community. Approximately 90 mL of Abate (temephos) larvicide was applied to stagnant water bodies containing larvae, treating an estimated 3597 m2 of breeding sites.

ITN ownership and utilization assessment showed that 74.1% of households owned at least one ITN, but only 28.3% reported consistent and proper use. Moreover, IRS had not been implemented in the three years preceding the outbreak because of persistent insecticide shortages, reducing vector-control coverage and increasing vulnerability to malaria transmission.

During the outbreak response, the Rapid Response Team, in collaboration with the Southwest Ethiopia Peoples Regional Health Bureau, the Southwest Ethiopia Peoples Regional Public Health Institute, and local health offices, implemented indoor residual spraying in 4500 housing units across affected kebeles and distributed 2,650 insecticide-treated nets to households in high-risk areas. Implementation of standardized case management, RCCE, and other vector control activities together resulted in a 93.4% reduction in malaria cases during the outbreak response, with cases declining from 2192 in Epidemiologic Week 21 to 145 in Week 26 of 2025.

Discussion

This investigation documented a malaria outbreak in Meinit Goldia District, marking the first malaria outbreak investigation conducted in the Southwest Ethiopia Peoples’ Regional State (SWEPRS). The outbreak was characterized by substantial community transmission influenced by both epidemiological and environmental factors. Two contributions distinguish this study from previous Ethiopian outbreak investigations. First, we identified specific anthropogenic breeding sites (broken glass bottles and open deep wells) as independently associated risk factors. This provides more evidence than the broad environmental categories commonly reported in earlier case-control studies and highlights practical targets for community-level vector control. Second, we quantified a four-week delay between outbreak detection (WHO Week 13) and initiation of investigation (WHO Week 17), a gap that coincided with a fourfold increase in weekly malaria incidence. While delays have been noted anecdotally in prior Ethiopian studies, this study measured the magnitude of the response gap using epidemiological data, demonstrating its direct role in outbreak amplification.

Over a four-month period (WHO weeks 13–26, 2025), confirmed malaria cases exceeded the epidemic threshold with high attack rate, substantially higher than documented outbreaks in other malaria-endemic regions of Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa. This study reported the overall AR of 71.3 cases per 1,000 population among the total population of the district, which was considerably higher than findings from previous studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia. For instance, the AR was 12.1 in Laelay Adyabo district, Northern Ethiopia [11], 33.1 in Abergelle district, Northern Ethiopia [31], 36.4 in Bolosso Sore district, Southern Ethiopia [12], 20.0 in Simada district, Northwest Ethiopia [13], 41.3 in Ameya district, Oromia [41], and 20.2 in Nensebo district, West Arsi Zone, Oromia [29]. Similarly, the AR observed in this study exceeded that reported in Zimbabwe (71.7) [42]. The higher attack rate observed in Meinit Goldia district indicates a more intense malaria outbreak and a greater burden of disease within the population. This elevated attack rate may be attributable to environmental conditions favorable for mosquito breeding, inadequate access to preventive measures such as insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), the absence of indoor residual spraying (IRS) over the preceding three years, delays in outbreak response, lack of emergency preparedness and response plans, variations in healthcare infrastructure, and a highly susceptible population.

This finding is in contrast with the AR reported in Bena Tsemay district (114.0), Southern Ethiopia [40]. This difference may be due to the distinct pastoralist and nomadic population dynamics in Bena Tsemay, which alter healthcare-seeking timelines and baseline immunity profiles, compared to the settled agricultural communities in Meinit Goldia.

During this outbreak, individuals aged greater than 15 years constituted the most affected age group, accounting for 74% (95% CI: 65–75.9) of cases. This finding is consistent with malaria outbreak investigations conducted in the Afar Region (63.6%) [30] and Bolosso Sore District, Ethiopia (72%) [12]. Similar findings have also been reported from Colombia [38] and non-endemic districts of India [39]. One possible explanation is that adults, particularly those engaged in outdoor activities such as agriculture or occupations requiring nighttime exposure, experience increased contact with mosquito vectors and consequently higher infection rates. Supporting this explanation, the majority of cases in the current study were farmers (32%), and 18% of affected participants reported frequently staying outdoors at night.

The majority of confirmed malaria cases were caused by Plasmodium falciparum (62%). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Bena Tsemay district, Southern Ethiopia (64.3%) [43], Waghemra Zone of the Amhara Region (66.1%) [33], and Argoba District of Northeast Ethiopia (96%) [44]. Furthermore, previous reports indicate that Plasmodium falciparum commonly accounts for approximately 60% of malaria cases during outbreaks in Ethiopia [45]. This is also supported by the national malaria parasite distribution pattern, which indicates that Plasmodium falciparum constitutes approximately 65% of malaria infections in Ethiopia [8]. Because Plasmodium falciparum is associated with more severe disease manifestations, its predominance has important implications for clinical management and timely treatment. The dominance of this species may be related to environmental conditions favoring Anopheles mosquitoes, parasite-specific biological characteristics, possible antimalarial drug resistance, and increased exposure resulting from agricultural and outdoor occupational activities.

In contrast, a malaria outbreak investigation conducted in Nensebo District, West Arsi Zone, Oromia, reported Plasmodium vivax as the dominant species (52.8%) [29]. This difference may be attributed to the highland ecology of Nensebo District, where Plasmodium vivax is thought to predominate because of its relapsing characteristics. Changing malaria transmission patterns influenced by population movement, agricultural practices, and environmental changes may also contribute to the increasing dominance of Plasmodium vivax in certain parts of Ethiopia [29, 47].

The observed four-week delay between threshold exceedance and initiation of investigation in this outbreak stands in sharp contrast to international standards. WHO guidance emphasizes that malaria surveillance systems should enable detection within one week of threshold exceedance, notification within 24–48 h, investigation within 72 h, and implementation of response measures ideally within 72 h and no later than one week [36]. In the present outbreak, this delay coincided with a fourfold increase in weekly incidence, underscoring how deviations from recommended timelines can amplify transmission. This finding is consistent with evidence reported from Binga District, Zimbabwe [46], where delayed responses were associated with higher outbreak peaks and prolonged transmission. Strengthening surveillance systems and ensuring timely notification and investigation are therefore critical to align practice with global standards and reduce the public health impact of malaria epidemics.

A unique finding of this investigation was the significant association between the presence of broken glass bottles around households and malaria infection. Individuals living near broken glass bottles had approximately 4.8 times higher odds of developing malaria compared with those without such exposure (AOR = 4.847; 95% CI: 1.692–13.889). Although previous outbreak investigations in Ethiopia have consistently demonstrated increased malaria risk among individuals residing near mosquito breeding sites (11,13,29,31,33,40,44,48), most studies categorized breeding habitats broadly and did not specifically identify discarded glass bottles as independent risk factors. Therefore, the current study provides additional evidence regarding the contribution of anthropogenic environmental waste to malaria transmission.

Broken glass bottles and similar discarded containers can accumulate rainwater and create small artificial aquatic habitats suitable for mosquito breeding. During the rainy season, these containers may retain water long enough to support mosquito larval development, thereby increasing vector abundance around residential areas [51]. The association observed in this study suggests that improper solid-waste disposal may contribute to malaria transmission by generating numerous localized breeding sites in close proximity to households. Similar observations from Uganda [49] and Zimbabwe [42] have highlighted the importance of environmental sanitation and elimination of water-holding containers in reducing vector proliferation. The finding underscores the need for community-based environmental management interventions, including regular clean-up campaigns, proper waste disposal practices, and health education activities aimed at eliminating artificial breeding habitats. Because broken glass bottles represent a preventable and easily modifiable risk factor, addressing this exposure could provide a cost-effective strategy for reducing malaria transmission at the community level.

Importantly, the strong association between discarded broken glass bottles and malaria risk may indicate a shift in local vector ecology. Broken glass bottles accumulate small pockets of rainwater, creating artificial container habitats. While the principal native vector in Ethiopia, Anopheles arabiensis, typically prefers temporary sunlit ground pools, the invasive vector Anopheles stephensi preferentially exploits artificial containers. Given the documented geographic expansion of Anopheles stephensi from eastern areas deeper into the country, our findings raise the critical hypothesis that this invasive species may be contributing to transmission dynamics in the region. Although the absence of entomologists in our investigation team prevented species-level confirmation during this emergency response, we recommend that future outbreak investigations in this region incorporate entomological surveys.

The presence of an open deep well near the household was significantly associated with malaria infection, with affected individuals having approximately 3.7 times higher odds of contracting malaria than those without such exposure (AOR = 3.735; 95% CI: 1.273–10.956). This finding is consistent with previous malaria outbreak investigations conducted in Ethiopia, including Waghemra Zone (AOR = 2.7) [33], Abergelle District of Tigray (AOR = 6.56) [31], Simada District of Northwest Ethiopia (AOR = 3.32) [13], Bena Tsemay District of Southern Ethiopia (AOR = 6.5) [40], Ziway Dugda District of Oromia (AOR = 2.63) [48], Laelay Adyabo District (AOR = 9.08) [11], Argoba District of Northeast Ethiopia (AOR = 13.3) [44], and Nensebo District of Oromia (AOR = 4.28) [29]. Similar associations have also been reported in Uganda (AOR = 6.6) [49] and Zimbabwe (AOR = 8.0) [42], highlighting the consistent role of water-holding environmental features in sustaining malaria transmission across different epidemiological settings.

Open deep wells may serve as stable and long-lasting breeding habitats for Anopheles mosquitoes, particularly following periods of heavy rainfall when water accumulates and persists for extended durations. Such habitats can provide suitable conditions for mosquito larval development, thereby increasing vector density in surrounding communities [50, 51]. Households located near these breeding sites are consequently exposed to higher mosquito biting rates and an elevated risk of malaria infection. The finding emphasizes the importance of environmental management interventions, including covering or properly maintaining wells, draining stagnant water, and incorporating larval source management into routine malaria control activities. These measures may be particularly important in areas with recurrent outbreaks and limited implementation of other vector-control strategies.

Two exposure variables (broken glass bottles and open deep wells) had limited numbers of exposed controls (n = 4 each), resulting in sparse contingency tables that could have inflated parameter estimates and confidence intervals under standard maximum likelihood estimation. To address this issue, Firth’s penalized likelihood method was applied. This bias-reduction technique is recommended for analyses involving sparse cell counts and has been shown to provide more reliable parameter estimates and confidence intervals without compromising statistical validity (Heinze & Schemper, 2002). Sensitivity analyses comparing Firth-adjusted and standard maximum likelihood estimates demonstrated consistent findings.

In addition, individuals who never utilized ITNs had 4.882 times higher odds of contracting malaria (AOR = 4.882; 95% CI: 2.011–11.847) compared with those who always used them. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Simada District (AOR = 5.85) [13] and Chipinge District, Zimbabwe (AOR = 2.2) [52]. This underscores the critical role of consistent ITN utilization in malaria prevention. Factors such as inadequate access to ITNs, improper utilization, and misconceptions regarding effectiveness may explain the elevated risk among non-users. In the present study, only 28.3% of participants reported proper and consistent utilization, leaving many individuals vulnerable to mosquito bites and malaria infection.

However, this finding differs from a study conducted in Bolosso Sore District, Wolayta Zone (AOR = 32.098) [12]. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in awareness regarding ITN use, malaria transmission intensity, study populations, and local epidemiological conditions. Similar observations from Northern Sudan indicate that environments conducive to mosquito breeding pose a substantial threat to individuals who do not use ITNs [53].

Participants who reported sometimes using ITNs had significantly higher odds of malaria infection than those who consistently used ITNs (AOR = 2.617; 95% CI: 1.204–5.692). This finding contrasts with studies conducted in Bolosso Sore District (AOR = 10.214) [12] and Bena Tsemay District (AOR = 8.7) [40], which reported higher odds among occasional users. The discrepancy may reflect differences in transmission intensity, vector behavior, ITN coverage and condition, and adherence to malaria prevention measures. Nevertheless, the findings reinforce the importance of promoting regular and correct ITN use during both routine malaria control activities and outbreak responses.

Understanding malaria plays a crucial role in preventing exposure and reducing disease burden. Studies from Abeshege District, Ethiopia, have emphasized the importance of health education and awareness programs in minimizing malaria risk [55]. In the present study, the odds of malaria were 2.847 times higher among individuals with poor knowledge of malaria transmission, signs and symptoms, and prevention measures (AOR = 2.847; 95% CI: 1.502–5.397) compared with those who demonstrated good knowledge. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Simada District, Ethiopia (AOR = 4.36) [13], Ziway Dugda District, Ethiopia (AOR = 3.71) [48], and Zimbabwe (AOR = 2.11) [52]. Knowledge enables individuals to adopt preventive measures and seek treatment promptly, thereby reducing malaria burden. Poor knowledge may result in inadequate protective practices, increased exposure to infected mosquitoes, and delayed healthcare-seeking behavior. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening community education and awareness campaigns.

However, the magnitude of association observed in this study was lower than that reported in Waghemra Zone (AOR = 9.7) [33] and South Gondar (AOR = 26.93) [32]. Differences in study populations, malaria epidemiology, healthcare infrastructure, intervention coverage, and access to information may explain these variations.

The odds of malaria infection among individuals who wore protective clothing at night were 87.7% lower than among those who did not (AOR = 0.123; 95% CI: 0.054–0.280). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Bena Tsemay District, Ethiopia (AOR = 0.041) [40], Laelay Adyabo District, Ethiopia (AOR = 0.270) [11], Siansundu District, Zimbabwe (AOR = 0.156) [46], and Mudzi District, Zimbabwe (AOR = 0.200) [42]. Protective clothing serves as an effective physical barrier that reduces direct contact between humans and mosquitoes. Clothing with sufficient thickness and texture can substantially reduce mosquito biting and subsequent malaria transmission [55]. However, the higher magnitude of this protective effect (87.7% lower odds) should be interpreted with appropriate caution due to potential healthy-user bias. Individuals who proactively wear long protective clothing at night may systematically differ from those who do not by clustering other unmeasured protective health habits such as maintaining earlier bedtimes, utilizing repellents, or minimizing nighttime outdoor exposures leading to residual behavioral confounding that may partially inflate the observed effect size.

The national malaria prevention and control strategy recommends annual IRS implementation and provision of at least one ITN for every two household members [8]. This investigation revealed that 74.14% of households possessed at least one ITN before the outbreak. However, IRS had not been conducted in the study area during the previous three years, consistent with findings from the Afar Region and Ankesha District of the Amhara Region [30, 54]. The inadequate implementation of malaria prevention and control measures may reflect gaps in adherence to national guidelines, shortages of ITNs and IRS chemicals, or weaknesses in distribution systems. Following the current outbreak investigation, the Regional Health Bureau, in collaboration with the Ethiopian Public Health Institute, initiated emergency request of insecticides for IRS in additional high-risk areas affected by supply chain disruptions.

Limitations of the study

This outbreak investigation, although providing important insights into malaria risk factors in the study area, has several methodological and operational limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the findings.

The case-control design using asymptomatic, RDT-negative community members as controls may have introduced selection bias and reduced exposure variability between cases and controls, potentially attenuating the observed associations. Although we mitigated this limitation by selecting controls from the same source population through active house-to-house surveillance and conducting recruitment independently of exposure status to ensure comparability with cases, residual bias cannot be completely excluded.

The CareStart rapid diagnostic test (RDT) used to classify cases and controls has a reported sensitivity of 93.2%, meaning approximately 6.8% of true malaria-positive individuals may have been misclassified as negative and enrolled as controls. This non-differential misclassification of the outcome likely biased effect estimates toward the null, suggesting that the true associations between identified risk factors and malaria infection may be stronger than those reported.

Despite calculating the sample size using multiple epidemiologically relevant exposure variables and selecting the largest estimate to ensure adequate statistical power, sparse observations in some exposure categories within the final multivariable model may have reduced the precision of certain effect estimates. Although Firth’s penalized likelihood method was employed to reduce small-sample bias, findings related to variables with limited exposure frequencies should be interpreted cautiously.

The absence of entomologists constrained our ability to characterize vector species and assess insecticide resistance patterns. This hindered comprehensive interpretation of local transmission dynamics. Additionally, although the 20-item questionnaire used to assess malaria knowledge was adapted from established peer-reviewed literature, it was not subjected to formal internal consistency testing (Cronbach’s alpha) prior to field deployment in this specific subpopulation. This operational limitation arose from the urgent public health emergency context in which the investigation was conducted and may have biased estimates of participants’ knowledge.

Conclusions

This investigation confirmed a malaria outbreak with sustained community transmission in Meinit Goldia District and identified multiple independent risk factors. The evidence highlights critical gaps in vector control specifically the absence of IRS for three consecutive years, delayed outbreak response, and localized anthropogenic breeding sites that intensified transmission. To reduce the burden and prevent recurrence, immediate, targeted interventions must be implemented alongside strengthened surveillance and community engagement.
