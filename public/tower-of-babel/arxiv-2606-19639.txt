Mean-Field Control with a Common Hidden State under Decentralized Observations Thanks: E. Bayraktar is partially supported by the National Science Foundation under grant DMS-2507940 and by the Susan M. Smith chair.
Erhan Bayraktar, Ali Devran Kara

Abstract
We study optimal control of a system with multiple decision makers who share a common hidden state and receive fully decentralized observations through identical channels. The dynamics of the hidden state and the cost incurred by the agents depend on the agents’ actions only through their empirical distribution. In the limit problem with infinitely many agents, the problem reduces to a single agent control problem where the agent affects the hidden state dynamics via the conditional law of the actions given the past values of the hidden state process. We formulate this problem as a deterministic measure valued control problem over the space of policies and provide a dynamic programming recursion. We first show that for the limiting problem randomization over the control actions is necessary for optimality. However, randomization over the selection of policies (i.e., mixture policies) is not required. We then show that the optimal symmetric policies designed for the infinite population problem are near optimal for the finite population problem. In particular, we establish convergence rates that decay with number of agents as 1 N \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} , and grow exponentially with the memory length used in the policy.

The goal of this paper is to develop a general framework for multi-agent mean-field team control problems where multiple agents aim to cooperatively control a common hidden state process under local partial observations of the state. Mean field approximations.

II
Mean-field Limit Problem

We define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}=\{\text{all stochastic kernels from }\mathds{Y}^{t}\times\mathds{U}^{t-1}\text{ to }\mathds{U}\}.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{t})_{t=0}^{T-1}
be a sequence of randomized control functions with
\gamma_{t}\in\Gamma_{t}
such that for each
t

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}:\mathds{Y}^{t}\times\mathds{U}^{t-1}\to{\mathcal{P}}(\mathds{U}).

For the given
\gamma=(\gamma_{t})_{t}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}|x_{t},\dots,x_{0})\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathds{U})

to be the conditional law of
U_{t}
given the past hidden states
x_{t},\dots,x_{0}
. In particular, for some test function
h
, and for a given hidden state path
x_{t},\dots,x_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{U}}h(u_{t})\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})(du_{t})

\displaystyle=\int h(u_{t})\prod_{k=0}^{t}\gamma_{k}(du_{k}|h_{k})O(dy_{k}|x_{k})

where
h_{k}=(y_{k},\dots,y_{0},u_{k-1},\dots,u_{0})
with
h_{0}=y_{0}
.

For a given sequence of control functions, the dynamics of hidden state is given by

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]}),w_{t})

which can be equivalently represented by a stochastic kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(dx_{t+1}|x_{t},\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})).

We say that
\gamma^{n}\in\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}
converges to
\gamma\in\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}
if

\displaystyle P^{n}(dx_{[0,t]},dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t]})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{n}(du_{t}|h_{t})O(dy_{t}|x_{t})\mathcal{T}(dx_{t}|x_{t-1},\mathcal{L}^{\gamma^{n}}(u_{t}|x_{[0,t]}))

\displaystyle\qquad\dots\gamma^{n}_{0}(du_{0}|y_{0})O(dy_{0}|x_{0})P_{0}(dx_{0}).

converges weakly to

\displaystyle P(dx_{[0,t]},dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t]})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}(du_{t}|h_{t})O(dy_{t}|x_{t})\mathcal{T}(dx_{t}|x_{t-1},\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}|x_{[0,t]}))

\displaystyle\qquad\dots\gamma_{0}^{n}(du_{0}|y_{0})O(dy_{0}|x_{0})P_{0}(dx_{0}).

for all
t=0,\dots,T-1
.

Note that it is not directly obvious that the set of all policies
\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}
is sufficient for optimality, and one may need to consider randomizations over this set via mixture policies for optimality.

Hence, we define the decision variable for the infinite population problem to be the set of all mixture policies, alternatively, we define the decision variable to a probability measure

\displaystyle\Pi\in{\mathcal{P}}\left(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}\right).

Under a mixture policy, i.e. under some
\Pi
, we write
\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})(du_{t})
to denote the law of
u_{t}
given the hidden state history
x_{[0,t]}
. In particular, for the given
\Pi
, and for some test function
h
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{U}}h(u_{t})\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})(du_{t})

\displaystyle=\int h(u_{t})\prod_{k=0}^{t}\gamma_{k}(du_{k}|h_{k})O(dy_{k}|x_{k})\Pi(d\gamma).

The dynamics are then written as

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]}),w_{t}),

the stage-wise cost function similarly is given by

\displaystyle c(x_{t},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})).

The objective is to minimize the following cost function

\displaystyle J^{\infty}(P_{0},\Pi)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}E_{\Pi}\left[c(X_{t},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(U_{t}\mid X_{[0,t]}))\right]

for some finite time horizon
T<\infty
where
X_{0}\sim P_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathds{X})
.

The optimal cost is defined by

\displaystyle J^{\infty,*}(P_{0}):=\inf_{\Pi}J^{\infty}(P_{0},\Pi).

(4)

II-A

Optimality of Pure Policies

We now show that the mixture policies can be replicated using sequence of policies without randomization over the policy spaces.
Under the mixture policy
\Pi
, the joint distribution of actions given observations is:

\nu(du_{[0,t]}\mid y_{[0,t]})=\int\prod_{k=0}^{t}\gamma_{k}(du_{k}\mid y_{[0,k]},u_{[0,k-1]})\;\Pi(d\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

Lemma 1

(Replication of Mixture Policies)
.

Let
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}\right)
be a mixture policy. Then there exists a sequence of policies

\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\bar{\gamma}_{0},\bar{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\gamma}_{T-1})\in\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}

such that for every state sequence
x_{[0,t]}
:

\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})=\mathcal{L}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]}).

That is, the conditional law of the action given the states under the mixture policy equals the conditional law under the policy sequence
\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

We only prove the result for two time steps, the rest follows identically by inductive arguments.

We can integrate out
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})
from the joint distribution of the actions
u_{0},\dots,u_{t}
under the mixture policy such that:

\displaystyle\nu(du_{0}\mid y_{[0,t]})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi}\gamma_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0})\underbrace{\left[\int\prod_{k=0}^{t}\gamma_{k}(du_{k}\mid y_{[0,k]},u_{[0,k-1]})\right]}_{=\,1}\Pi(d\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

Each
\gamma_{k}
is a probability kernel, so the bracketed integral equals 1. Therefore,

\nu(du_{0}\mid y_{[0,t]})=\int_{\Pi}\gamma_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0})\;\Pi(d\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

The right-hand side depends only on
y_{0}
, not on
y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}
. So, we define:

\bar{\gamma}_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0})\;:=\;\int_{\Pi}\gamma_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0})\;\Pi(d\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

We now construct
\bar{\gamma}_{1}
.
For each fixed
y_{[0,t]}
, the mixture joint
\nu(du_{[0,1]}\mid y_{[0,t]})
is a probability measure on
u_{0}\times u_{1}
, and its marginal on
u_{0}
by construction is
\bar{\gamma}_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0})
. Furthermore, using the causality of the policies, it clearly is independent of
y_{[2,t]}
, that is
\nu(du_{[0,1]}\mid y_{[0,t]})=\nu(du_{[0,1]}\mid y_{[0,1]})
. Since the spaces are Polish by assumption, we can apply the disintegration theorem: there exists a stochastic kernel
\nu(du_{1}\mid u_{0},y_{[0,1]})

such that

\nu(du_{[0,1]}\mid y_{[0,1]})=\nu(du_{1}\mid u_{0},y_{[0,1]})\;\bar{\gamma}_{0}(du_{0}\mid y_{0}).

Thus, we can define
\bar{\gamma}_{1}(du_{1}\mid y_{0},y_{1},u_{0})
to be this kernel.

By repeating the same argument, we can find
(\bar{\gamma}_{k})_{k=0}^{t}
such that

\displaystyle\nu(du_{[0,t]}\mid y_{[0,t]})=\prod_{k=0}^{t}\bar{\gamma}_{k}(du_{k}\mid y_{[0,k]},u_{[0,k-1]}).

(5)

Under the mixture policy
\Pi
, the conditional law of the actions given the states can be written by integrating the joint distribution on actions against the observation channels:

\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{[0,t]}\mid x_{[0,t]})=\int\nu(du_{[0,t]}\mid y_{[0,t]})\prod_{k=0}^{t}O(dy_{k}\mid x_{k}).

By (
5
),
\nu
can be written using
\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(u_{[0,t]}\mid x_{[0,t]})

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{k=0}^{t}\bar{\gamma}_{k}(du_{k}\mid y_{[0,k]},u_{[0,k-1]})\prod_{k=0}^{t}O(dy_{k}\mid x_{k}).

This is the conditional law under the profile
\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, and thus conclude the proof.
∎∎

II-B

Measure Valued Formulation and Dynamic Programming

The problem can be reformulated using the joint distributions of the system’s random variables. At time
t
, we define the measure-valued state

\displaystyle P_{t}:=\mathds{P}(dx_{[0,t]},dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{X}^{t+1}\times\mathds{Y}^{t+1}\times\mathds{U}^{t})

as the joint distribution of the hidden states, observations, and past actions up to time
t
. The control action is represented by a measure

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}:=\mathds{P}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t]})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{Y}^{t+1}\times\mathds{U}^{t+1}),

corresponding to the joint distribution of observations and actions, subject to the consistency constraint

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]}\in A)=P_{t}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]}\in A)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathds{Y}^{t+1}\times\mathds{U}^{t})
. That is,
\Theta_{t}
and
P_{t}
agree on the marginal distribution of the information variables
(y_{[0,t]},u_{[0,t-1]})
available to the agent at time
t
.

By disintegrating
\Theta_{t}
, we recover the agent’s control kernel
\gamma_{t}\in\Gamma_{t}
:

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t]})=\gamma_{t}(du_{t}|h_{t})\,P_{t}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]}),

(6)

where, as before,
h_{t}=(y_{t},\dots,y_{0},u_{t-1},\dots,u_{0})
.

We note that the conditional law
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})
, introduced in Section
II
, can be expressed directly in terms of
P_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}(u_{t}\mid x_{[0,t]})(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\int\gamma_{t}(\cdot\mid h_{t})\,P_{t}(dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]}\mid x_{[0,t]})

\displaystyle=:\Lambda(P_{t},\gamma_{t})(\cdot|x_{[0,t]})

(7)

That is, the conditional distribution of
u_{t}
given the state path is obtained by averaging the agent’s policy
\gamma_{t}
over the observations and past actions using the conditional distribution encoded in
P_{t}
.

The deterministic dynamics for the measure valued state are then given by

\displaystyle P_{t+1}

\displaystyle=F(P_{t},\gamma_{t})

\displaystyle:=O(dy_{t+1}|x_{t+1})\,\mathcal{T}(dx_{t+1}|x_{t},\Lambda(P_{t},\gamma_{t})(\cdot|x_{[0,t]}))

\displaystyle\quad\times\gamma_{t}(du_{t}|h_{t})\,P_{t}(dx_{[0,t]},dy_{[0,t]},du_{[0,t-1]}).

(8)

The controls up to time
t-1
, namely
\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{t-1}
, are implicitly embedded in
P_{t}
and can be recovered through disintegration as in (
6
), while
\gamma_{t}
enters explicitly through the control measure
\Theta_{t}
.

The measure-valued formulation above admits a dynamic programming recursion. At
t=T-1
, for any given
\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{T-2}\in\prod_{t=0}^{T-2}\Gamma_{t}
(which are encoded in
P_{T-1}
), we define

\displaystyle V^{*}_{T-1}(P_{T-1})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\gamma_{T-1}\in\Gamma_{T-1}}\mathds{E}_{P_{T-1}}\bigg[c\big(x_{T-1},\Lambda(P_{T-1},\gamma_{T-1})(\cdot|x_{[0,T-1]})\big)\bigg],

where the expectation is over
x_{[0,T-1]}
with distribution determined by the marginal of
P_{T-1}
.

For a general time step
t=T-2,\dots,0
, the value function is defined by

\displaystyle V^{*}_{t}(P_{t})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\gamma_{t}\in\Gamma_{t}}\bigg\{\mathds{E}_{P_{t}}\bigg[c\big(x_{t},\Lambda(P_{t},\gamma_{t})(\cdot|x_{[0,t]})\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+V^{*}_{t+1}\big(F(P_{t},\gamma_{t})\big)\bigg\},

(9)

where
F(P_{t},\gamma_{t})
is the dynamics defined in (
II-B
).
The optimal value of the problem is then

\displaystyle V^{*}_{0}(\bar{P}_{0}),

where
\bar{P}_{0}(dx_{0},dy_{0}):=O(dy_{0}|x_{0})\,P_{0}(dx_{0})
.

Remark II.1
.

The dynamic program presented above is not directly tractable, as it involves optimization over infinite-dimensional policy spaces. Nonetheless, the formulation provides a significant simplification compared to the finite-agent regime. In the finite population setting, symmetric policies are not necessarily optimal, and thus one must define the dynamic programming recursion over the joint policy spaces of all agents. Whereas the infinite population formulation reduces the problem to a search over a single policy space at each time stage of the recursion. Further simplifications may be possible by considering parametrized family of policies to reduce the dimension of the search space; we leave this direction for future work.

Assumption III.1
.

\mathds{U}\subset\mathds{R}
is compact.

\mathcal{T}(dx_{1}|x,\mu_{\bf u})
is Lipschitz in

under
W_{1}
such that

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(\mathcal{T}(dx_{1}|x,\mu_{\bf u})-\mathcal{T}(\cdot|x^{\prime},\mu_{\bf u^{\prime}}\right)|

for some
K_{f}<\infty
, where

is the first order Wasserstein distance.

is Lipschitz in

under total variation such that

\displaystyle\|O(dy|x)-O(dy|x^{\prime})\|_{TV}\leq K_{O}\|x-x^{\prime}\|

for some
K_{O}<\infty
, for all

.

iii

c
is Lipschitz in

such that

\displaystyle|c(x,\mu_{{\bf u}})-c(x^{\prime},\mu_{{\bf u^{\prime}}})|\leq K_{c}\left(|x-x^{\prime}|+W_{1}(\mu_{\bf u},\mu_{\bf u^{\prime}})\right)

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption

, the following folds:

\displaystyle\left|J^{N,*}(P_{0})-J^{\infty,*}(P_{0})\right|\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{M_{t}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle K_{c}\frac{1-K_{f}}{(1-K_{f})^{2}+K_{O}K_{f}}\left[\left(\frac{1-K_{f}+K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\right)^{t}-K_{f}^{t}\right].

\displaystyle\Pi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\gamma^{i}}(\cdot)

for all

denote the
\epsilon-

\Pi

, and we use the
\epsilon
-optimal policies
\{\gamma_{t}^{i}\}_{i}
for the finite population problem. Denoting these finite population team policy by

\displaystyle J^{\infty,*}(P_{0})-J^{N,*}(P_{0})<J^{\infty}(P_{0},\Pi)-J^{N}(P_{0},{\bf\gamma})+\epsilon

We let

denote the hidden state variable under the finite population dynamics driven by the finite population
\epsilon
-optimal policies

such that

\displaystyle x^{N}_{t+1}=f(x_{t}^{N},\mu^{N}_{u_{t}},w_{t})

Furthermore, we let

\displaystyle x^{\infty}_{t+1}=f(x_{t}^{\infty},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]}),w^{\prime}_{t}).

We couple the noise sequences

and

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\|x_{k}^{N}-x_{k}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[W_{1}\big(\mathcal{T}(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{N},\mu^{N}_{u_{k-1}}),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathcal{T}(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{\infty},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{k-1}|x^{\infty}_{[0,k-1]}))\big)\bigg]

for all
k
. In particular, under this particular coupling, using the continuity of the transition kernel for the hidden state we have that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\|x_{k}^{N}-x_{k}^{\infty}\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{f}\mathds{E}\left[\|x_{k-1}^{N}-x_{k-1}^{\infty}\|\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+K_{f}\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mu^{N}_{u_{k-1}},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{k-1}|x^{\infty}_{[0,k-1]})\right)\right].

(10)

Under this coupling of the noise sequences, we then write

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{E}\left[c(x_{t}^{N},\mu^{N}_{u_{t}})\right]-\mathds{E}\left[c(x_{t}^{\infty},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]}))\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\left|c(x_{t}^{N},\mu^{N}_{u_{t}})-c(x_{t}^{\infty},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]}))\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{c}\left(\mathds{E}[\|x_{t}^{N}-x_{t}^{\infty}\|]+\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mu_{u_{t}}^{N},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{c}\bigg(\mathds{E}[\|x_{t}^{N}-x_{t}^{\infty}\|]+\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mu_{u_{t}}^{N},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{N}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{N}_{[0,t]}),\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]\bigg).

We first note that given
x^{N}_{[0,t]}
, the local histories,
h_{t}^{i}
, of the agents are independent. Hence, we can use Lemma
2
to write

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mu_{u_{t}}^{N},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{N}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}.

Furthermore, using the total variation continuity of the observation channels, we can also write

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{N}_{[0,t]}),\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{O}\sum_{k=0}^{t}\mathds{E}\left[\|x_{k}^{N}-x_{k}^{\infty}\|\right].

Denoting by
m_{t}:=\mathds{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mu_{u_{t}}^{N},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]})\right)\right]
, one can derive

\displaystyle m_{t}\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-K_{f}^{t-j})m_{j}.

Noting that
m_{0}=0
, one can solve for an upper-bound on
m_{t}
such that

\displaystyle m_{t}\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{1-K_{f}+K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\right)^{t-1}.

Furthermore, using (
III
), we can derive

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\|x_{k}^{N}-x_{k}^{\infty}\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot\frac{K_{f}(1-K_{f})}{(1-K_{f})^{2}+K_{O}K_{f}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left[\left(\frac{1-K_{f}+K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\right)^{t-1}-K_{f}^{t-1}\right].

Combining the bounds, we can write

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{E}\left[c(x_{t}^{N},\mu^{N}_{u_{t}})\right]-\mathds{E}\left[c(x_{t}^{\infty},\mathcal{L}^{\Pi}(u_{t}|x^{\infty}_{[0,t]}))\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq K_{c}\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot\frac{1-K_{f}}{(1-K_{f})^{2}+K_{O}K_{f}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left[\left(\frac{1-K_{f}+K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\right)^{t}-K_{f}^{t}\right].

For
J^{N,*}(P_{0})<J^{\infty,*}(P_{0})
, we let agent
i
use
\gamma_{t}(du_{t}|h_{t}^{i})
at time
t
, where
(\gamma_{t})_{t}
is an
\epsilon-
optimal policy profile for the infinite population problem. We can then follow the identical steps as in the first case. The only difference is that we use Lemma
2
for a fixed policy
\gamma
that does not change with the agent identity. Since,
\epsilon
is arbitrary, letting
\epsilon\to 0
concludes the proof.
∎∎

Lemma 2
.

Given a fixed sequence of function
\gamma^{1},\dots,\gamma^{N}
where
\gamma^{i}:\mathds{S}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathds{U})
for some space
\mathds{S}
and where
\mathds{U}\subset\mathds{R}
is compact. Let
D:=diam(\mathds{U})
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sup_{\|h\|_{Lip}\leq 1}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int h(u)\gamma^{i}(du|X^{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int h(u)\gamma^{i}(du|x)\mu(dx)\bigg|\bigg]\leq C\frac{D}{\sqrt{N}}

for some constant
c<\infty
, for i.i.d.
X^{i}\sim\mu
.

Proof.

Since
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\gamma^{i}(\cdot|X^{i})
and
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\int\gamma^{i}(\cdot|x)\mu(dx)
are both probability measures, the constant part of
h
cancels out. Hence, we can restrict the functions to the following set

\mathcal{F}:=\{h\colon\mathds{U}\to\mathds{R}:\|h\|_{\mathop{\rm Lip}}\leq 1,\;h(u_{0})=0\}

for a fixed
u_{0}\in\mathds{U}
, so that
\|h\|_{\infty}\leq D
for all
h\in\mathcal{F}
where
D:=diam(\mathds{U})
.

Define the centered process

\displaystyle Z_{h}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(g_{i}^{h}(X^{i})-\mathds{E}[g_{i}^{h}(X^{i})]\bigr),

\displaystyle\text{where }g_{i}^{h}(x):=\int_{\mathds{U}}h(u)\,\gamma^{i}(du\,|\,x).

We must bound
\mathds{E}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}|Z_{h}|]
. We use Dudley’s theorem for sub-Gaussian processes (e.g.
[
36
, Theorem 8.1.3]
):

For any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in\mathds{S}
, we have that

|g_{i}^{h}(x)-g_{i}^{h^{\prime}}(x)|\leq\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. By Hoeffding’s lemma and independence, we have that

\mathds{E}\bigl[e^{\lambda(Z_{h}-Z_{h^{\prime}})}\bigr]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2N}\right).

Hence
(Z_{h})_{h\in\mathcal{F}}
is a sub-Gaussian process with respect to the metric

\sigma(h,h^{\prime}):=\frac{\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(11)

By Dudley’s theorem for sub-Gaussian processes (e.g.
[
36
, Theorem 8.1.3]
):

\mathds{E}\!\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}|Z_{h}|\right]\leq C\int_{0}^{\mathop{\rm diam}(\mathcal{F},\,\sigma)}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\,\epsilon,\,\sigma)}\;d\epsilon,

(12)

Note that using the Lipschitz bound of the functions we have that
\mathop{\rm diam}(\mathcal{F},\sigma)\leq 2D/\sqrt{N}
where
D=diam(\mathds{U})
. Furthermore, we also have that
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon,\sigma)=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon\sqrt{N},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.

By
[
35
, Theorem 2.7.1]
, for the function class
\mathcal{F}
we have that,

\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\,\epsilon,\,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\;\leq\;K\left(\frac{D}{\epsilon}\right)\qquad\text{for all }0<\epsilon\leq D,

where
K>0
is a constant. Using this on (
12
), we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\!\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}|Z_{h}|\right]\leq C\int_{0}^{2D/\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{K\!\left(\frac{D}{\epsilon\sqrt{N}}\right)}\;d\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{C\sqrt{KD}}{N^{1/4}}\int_{0}^{2D/\sqrt{N}}\epsilon^{-1/2}\,d\epsilon=\frac{2\sqrt{K}D}{\sqrt{N}}.

∎∎

Remark III.1
.

The result is stated for one dimensional action spaces. However, using the truncated version of the Dudley’s theorem for sub-Gaussian processes, one can derive bounds for
\mathds{U}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
which behaves as
C_{2}D\frac{\log\sqrt{1+N}}{N}
for
d=2
, and as
C_{3}DN^{-1/(d+3)}
for
d=3
using the same proof arguments.

Corollary III.1

(Near Optimality of Symmetric Policies)
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Let

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=({\gamma}_{0},{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,{\gamma}_{T-1})\in\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}

denote an
\epsilon
-optimal policy sequence for the infinite population problem. We let
J^{N}(P_{0},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
denote the accumulated cost if every agent follows the symmetric policy
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
using their local observation histories.
Under Assumption
III.1
, the following folds:
For any
P_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathds{X})
, we have that

\displaystyle J^{N}(P_{0},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})-J^{N,*}(P_{0})\leq 2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{M_{t}}{\sqrt{N}}+\epsilon

where

\displaystyle M_{t}:=K_{c}\cdot\frac{1-K_{f}}{(1-K_{f})^{2}+K_{O}K_{f}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left[\left(\frac{1-K_{f}+K_{O}K_{f}}{1-K_{f}}\right)^{t}-K_{f}^{t}\right].

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle|J^{N}(P_{0},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})-J^{N,*}(P_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|J^{N}(P_{0},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})-J^{\infty,*}(P_{0})|+|J^{\infty,*}(P_{0})-J^{N,*}(P_{0})|

The second term is bounded by Theorem
1
and the first term is bounded by the second part of the proof of the same theorem.
∎

IV
Conclusion

We studied a multi-agent mean-field control problem in which the agents share a common hidden state and control its dynamics through the empirical distribution of their actions. The problem is partially observed and decentralized; each agent observes the hidden state through its own symmetric observation channel. We showed that the infinite-population limit corresponds to a measure-valued deterministic control problem, and provided a dynamic programming recursion for this formulation. As the main contribution, we established that symmetric policies constructed for the infinite-population problem are near optimal for the finite-population problem, and derived a convergence rate for this approximation. As a future direction, we aim to develop computationally efficient methods for solving this problem by considering parametrized families of policies combined with policy gradient methods, and by developing finite-memory approximations, where policies depend only on a truncated history rather than the entire past, to reduce the dimensionality of the policy spaces.
