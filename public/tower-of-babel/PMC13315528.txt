Partial replacement of Soybean Meal with Cricket nymph (Acheta domesticus) and Moringa Oleifera leaf meal in broiler diets: Impacts on performance, carcass traits, and nutrient digestibility
Authors: NGA Mulyantini S․S․, Ni Nengah Suryani, Ni Putu Febri Suryatni, Ulrikus Romsen Lole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of partially replacing soybean meal (SBM) with a combination of cricket meal (CM; Acheta domesticus nymph meal) and Moringa oleifera leaf meal (MOLM) on growth performance, carcass characteristics, and apparent ileal nutrient digestibility in broiler chickens. A total of 240 one-day-old unsexed broilers were assigned to four dietary treatments with six replicate pens of ten birds each. Treatments consisted of a control diet (D0) and three diets in which 10% (D1), 20% (D2), or 30% (D3) of the SBM inclusion level was replaced by a cm–MOLM blend at a 3:1 ratio. Titanium dioxide was used as an indigestible marker to determine digestibility. Broilers fed D1 and D2 showed greater body weight gain (BWG) than those fed D0 and D3. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was not significantly affected (P = 0.089), although birds fed D3 had lower growth despite higher feed intake (FI). Carcass weight and thigh yield were higher in D1 and D2 compared with the control (P < 0.001). Apparent ileal digestibility of crude protein and organic matter decreased at the 30% substitution level (P < 0.001), whereas ether extract digestibility increased with higher inclusion of the blend. Relative weights of the gizzard and liver increased in D3. Serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-cholesterol decreased with increasing substitution levels (P < 0.001), while HDL-cholesterol increased. In conclusion, replacing up to 20% of SBM with a cm–MOLM blend improved growth performance, carcass traits, and lipid profiles without impairing nutrient digestibility. These findings suggest that a 20% substitution level can be effectively utilized as a sustainable alternative protein source to reduce reliance on SBM while enhancing lean meat yield and the lipid profile of broilers.Keywords: Broiler, Cricket meal, Growth performance, Moringa oleifera leaf meal, Nutrient digestibility

Introduction

The increasing cost and environmental concerns associated with soybean meal (SBM), the primary protein source in poultry diets, have intensified the search for sustainable alternative feed ingredients. Insects have emerged as promising candidates due to their high nutritional value, efficient feed conversion, and lower environmental footprint compared with conventional protein sources. Among them, cricket (Acheta domesticus) meal (CM) is particularly notable for its high crude protein content and favorable amino acid profile, which is comparable to or may exceed that of SBM (Makkar et al., 2014; Matin et al., 2021). However, the presence of chitin in insect exoskeletons may limit nutrient digestibility in monogastric animals, depending on endogenous chitinase activity and dietary inclusion level (Abenaim and Conti, 2025).

To enhance the utilization of alternative protein sources, interest has grown in combining insect meals with bioactive plant ingredients. Moringa oleifera leaf meal (MOLM) is rich in antioxidants, vitamins, minerals, and phytochemicals that may support metabolic health and improve growth performance in broilers (Peñalver et al., 2022). Previous studies have evaluated cm or MOLM individually in broiler diets. Cricket meal has been shown to partially replace SBM without compromising growth performance at moderate inclusion levels (Matin et al., 2021; Nieto et al., 2024), while MOLM supplementation has been associated with improved carcass traits and modulation of serum lipid profiles due to its hypocholesterolemic effects (El-Abbasy et al., 2025a).

Despite these findings, limited information is available on the combined use of cm and MOLM as a composite replacement for SBM in broiler diets. A combination of these ingredients may provide a complementary nutritional and functional profile, where bioactive compounds in Moringa could help mitigate potential digestive constraints associated with higher inclusion of insect-based proteins. Moreover, the effects of such a combination on apparent ileal nutrient digestibility and detailed physiological responses in broilers remain insufficiently documented.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of partially replacing SBM with a 3:1 combination of cm and MOLM on growth performance, carcass characteristics, apparent ileal nutrient digestibility, internal organ weights, and blood biochemical profiles of broiler chickens. It was hypothesized that moderate substitution levels would maintain growth efficiency while enhancing carcass quality and metabolic health.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Universitas Nusa Cendana (Approval No. 2023/UN/IAREC/011) and were conducted in accordance with the guide for the care and use of agricultural animals in research and teaching (FASS, 2020). All efforts were made to minimize animal stress and discomfort through appropriate housing, handling, and management practices throughout the experimental period.

Birds and housing

A total of 240 one-day-old straight-run broiler chicks (Cobb 500; PT Charoen Pokphand Indonesia, Jakarta, Indonesia) were used in this study. Upon arrival, birds were individually weighed (40.2 ± 2.15 g). All chicks were vaccinated against Newcastle disease at the hatchery prior to arrival at the experimental facility.

From day 1 to 14, chicks were reared in a brooder house on rice husk litter (5–7 cm depth). The brooding temperature was maintained at 32 ± 2°C during the first week and gradually reduced to approximately 25°C by day 14, with relative humidity maintained at around 70%. From day 15 onward, birds were transferred to wire-floored cages in an environmentally controlled house for the remainder of the 42-day experimental period.

Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. A lighting schedule of 15h light and 9h darkness was applied during the entire experimental period. Ambient temperature and relative humidity inside the experimental house were recorded daily using a digital thermo-hygrometer (model HTC-2, Xintest, Shenzhen, China). Birds were maintained under uniform environmental and sanitary conditions throughout the experiment.

Feeding program

From day 1 to 14 (starter phase), all birds received a common commercial starter diet in crumble form (2,900 kcal/kg metabolizable energy [ME] and 22% crude protein [CP]) to ensure uniform early growth prior to the introduction of experimental treatments. From day 15 to 35 (grower phase) and from day 36 to 42 (finisher phase), birds were fed the respective experimental diets formulated to contain approximately 3,000 and 3,100 kcal/kg ME and 20% and 19% CP, respectively. All diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements of broiler chickens as recommended by the National Research Council (NRC, 1994). Although Cobb 500 broilers possess high genetic potential, NRC (1994) recommendations were used as a standardized benchmark to facilitate comparison with previous studies involving alternative tropical feed ingredients and to ensure that no baseline nutrient deficiencies occurred during the experiment. Experimental design

At day 14, birds were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments in a completely randomized design. Each treatment consisted of six replicate pens with ten birds per pen. Experimental diets were fed from day 15 to 42, during which 0, 10, 20, and 30% of the SBM inclusion level was replaced by a combination of cm and MOLM at a fixed ratio of 3:1. Diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous within each feeding phase. To minimize potential effects of particle size on feed selection, all ingredients were finely ground using a hammer mill (Model HM200, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) to achieve a uniform particle size and a homogeneous diet.

Feed analysis

The experimental diets, MOLM, and cm were analyzed for dry matter (DM), crude ash (CA), crude fiber (CF), ether extract (EE), and crude protein (CP) according to AOAC (AOAC, 2007 methods 934.01, 942.05, 978.04, and 920.39). DM was determined by drying samples in a forced-air oven at 105°C for 24 h. Organic matter (OM) was calculated as the difference between DM and ash content after incineration in a muffle furnace at 550°C for 6 hours. CP, Nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl method, and CP was calculated as N x 6.25. EE was determined by the Soxhlet extraction method using petroleum ether as the solvent. A dry matter basis was used to express all of the data. The amino acids were assayed by ion-exchange column chromatography after hydrolysis for 23 h in HCl (6 mol/L). Origin and preparation of Moringa oleifera leaf meal (MOLM)

Fresh Moringa oleifera leaves were harvested locally, separated from the petioles, and shade-dried at ambient temperature for approximately 3 days until a constant weight was achieved. The dried leaves were then pulverized and ground into a fine powder (approximately 0.25 mm particle size) using a laboratory blender (model HR2115, Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The resulting Moringa oleifera leaf meal (MOLM) was stored in airtight containers prior to chemical analysis and incorporation into the experimental diets. Cricket meal (CM)

The cricket meal used in this study was obtained from a commercial insect producer (DNSH Petstore, Tangerang, Banten, Indonesia) that supplies dried crickets for animal feeding purposes. The product was derived from Acheta domesticus at the nymph stage prior to wing development. The insects were oven-dried and finely ground to produce a uniform meal before incorporation into the experimental diets.

Data collection

Growth performance data were recorded on a pen basis, with six replicate pens per dietary treatment and ten birds per pen. Body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for the periods of 1–14, 15–35, 36–42, and overall (1-42 days) according to standard poultry performance evaluation methods. Performance during the starter phase (1-14 days) was recorded to confirm baseline uniformity and was not influenced by dietary treatments, and therefore was excluded from statistical analysis of dietary treatment effects. Mortality was recorded as it occurred throughout the experimental period. BWG was calculated as the difference between final and initial body weight for each period. FI was determined as the difference between feed offered and feed residuals for each period. FCR was calculated as the ratio of FI to BWG.

Carcass traits, commercial cuts, and relative organ weights

At the end of the experiment (day 42), four birds per replicate were randomly selected for carcass and organ evaluation. Birds were individually weighed and humanely euthanized in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Exsanguination was performed immediately after euthanasia by severing the carotid arteries and jugular veins, and birds were allowed to bleed for 3–5 min. Carcasses were then scalded at 60°C for 30 s to facilitate feather removal. After defeathering and evisceration, hot carcass weight was recorded to calculate carcass yield.

Apparent Ileal nutrient digestibility

For the determination of ileal nutrient digestibility, four birds per replicate pen were randomly selected and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Ileal digesta were collected from the distal ileum, defined as the intestinal segment between Meckel’s diverticulum and approximately 2 cm anterior to the ileo-cecal junction (Lemme et al., 2004; Sasia et al., 2024). Digesta samples from birds within the same pen were pooled, immediately frozen at -20°C, and subsequently freeze-dried (lyophilized) prior to analysis.

The freeze-dried samples were ground through a 1-mm screen using a laboratory mill and analyzed for dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), and organic matter (OM) according to AOAC (2007). Titanium dioxide (TiO₂; analytical grade, ≥ 99% purity; Cat. No. 808, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was included at 0.3% as an indigestible marker (Morgan et al., 2014). Titanium dioxide concentrations in feed and digesta were determined spectrophotometrically following acid digestion.

Blood Parameters

At the end of the trial (day 42), four birds per replicate pen with body weights closest to the pen mean were selected for blood sampling after a 10–12 h feed withdrawal. Approximately 3 mL of blood was collected from the brachial vein using sterile tubes without anticoagulant. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to obtain serum. Serum samples were kept chilled during transport and delivered to a certified clinical laboratory for biochemical analysis. All samples were analyzed within 24 h of collection. Serum concentrations of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), total protein (TP), glucose (GLU), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were determined spectrophotometrically using an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (BS-240, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Shenzhen, China) with commercial diagnostic reagent kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SAS software (SAS, version 9.4., Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) under a completely randomized design. Pen served as the experimental unit for growth performance and ileal digestibility, whereas individual birds were considered the experimental unit for carcass traits, organ weights, and blood parameters. For ileal digestibility, digesta samples from birds within the same pen were pooled prior to analysis; therefore, pen was used as the unit of analysis.

Dietary treatment was included as a fixed effect in the model. When the treatment effect was significant, means were separated using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. Results are presented as least squares means with their pooled standard error of the mean (SEM).

In the grower phase (15–35 d), dietary treatment affected growth performance. Broilers fed diets in which SBM was partially replaced with cm and MOLM at 10% (D1) and 20% (D2) showed higher BWG and FI than the control group (P < 0.001). In contrast, birds fed the 30% substitution diet (D3) had lower BWG than the other treatments.

During the finisher phase (36–42 d), the negative effect of the highest substitution level became more evident. Birds fed D1 and D2 had the greatest BWG, whereas those fed D3 showed higher FI but poorer FCR compared with the other treatments (P < 0.01).

Overall performance from 1 to 42 d indicated that broilers fed D1 and D2 achieved higher BWG and FI than those fed the control and D3 diets (P < 0.001). Feed conversion ratio numerically increased with increasing substitution level, but the overall difference was not statistically detected (P = 0.089).

The improved growth performance observed in broilers fed diets containing 10% and 20% substitution of SBM with the cm and MOLM blend suggests that moderate inclusion levels provided a favourable balance of nutrients and functional compounds. The results for cm inclusion are consistent with Matin et al. (2021); Slimen et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2005) who demonstrated that cm provides a high-quality amino acid profile, particularly its richness in lysine and branched chain amino acids, which are essential for muscle protein accretion in fast-growing broilers. Previous studies (Bovera et al., 2015; Onsongo et al., 2018) have demonstrated comparable or improved growth performance when insect included at moderate levels. Additionally, MOLM contains bioactive compounds and micronutrients (Falowo et al., 2018; Peñalver et al., 2022) that may have further supported metabolic efficiency and antioxidant status, thereby improving nutrient utilization and growth response.

In contrast, the decline in body weight gain and feed efficiency observed at the 30% substitution level indicates that excessive inclusion of the cm–MOLM blend may have exceeded the birds’ capacity to efficiently utilize these alternative ingredients. This reduction in performance may be associated with the higher levels of structural components such as chitin from cricket meal and fiber fractions from moringa leaves. insect meals are characteristically rich in chitin, which is known to limit nutrient digestibility by encapsulating nutrients, increasing digesta bulk, and accelerating gastrointestinal passage rate (Lokman et al., 2019; Ndiritu et al., 2023). Similar reductions in growth performance at high inclusion levels of insect meals or leaf meals have been reported in broilers, where elevated indigestible fractions reduced nutrient availability and metabolizable energy utilization (Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025; Cullere et al., 2016).

The increased FI observed in birds fed the highest substitution diet (D3) during the finisher phase likely represents a compensatory response to reduced nutrient availability. Broilers tend to increase voluntary feed intake when dietary nutrient density or digestibility is compromised, attempting to meet their physiological nutrient requirements. However, this compensation was insufficient to offset the reduced efficiency of nutrient utilization, as reflected in the poorer feed conversion ratio at the 30% substitution level.

Overall, these findings indicate that partial replacement of SBM with a cm–MOLM blend can enhance growth performance when applied at moderate levels (10–20%), likely due to improved amino acid supply and supportive bioactive effects. However, higher substitution levels may introduce excessive indigestible components that limit nutrient utilization and growth efficiency. This highlights the importance of optimizing inclusion rates when incorporating alternative protein sources into broiler diets.

Differences were also observed in the proportions of thigh and breast muscles. Broilers fed D1 and D2 had higher thigh percentages than those fed D0 and D3 (P < 0.001). The breast percentage was highest in D1 (29.44%) and differed from that in D0 (28.23%) and D3 (27.89%) (P = 0.003). In contrast, birds fed D3 showed lower values for most carcass traits, similar to or below those of the control group.

The increase in carcass weight and yield in broilers fed D1 and D2 indicates that replacing SBM with up to 20% of the cm–MOLM blend supported greater lean tissue deposition. This enhancement can be attributed to improved nutrient utilization and enhanced protein deposition, particularly during the grower and finisher phases. Mustafa et al. (2025), and Slimen et al. (2023) reported that insect meals, such as those from Hermetia illucens or Acheta domesticus, possess an excellent amino acid profile that supports muscle accretion in meat-type poultry. The synergistic effect of MOLM, which is rich in vitamins and minerals, likely acted as a metabolic catalyst. Verma et al. (2009) observed that bioactive compounds in botanical supplements can improve protein synthesis efficiency, which in this study manifested as increased carcass mass.

The improved breast and thigh yields in the D1 and D2 were likely linked to enhanced availability of essential amino acids, particularly lysine and methionine, which are critical for breast muscle development in broiler chickens. The birds fed the CM-MOLM blend suggests that the 3:1 ratio of these alternative ingredients provided a more available or balanced amino acid profile compared to the SBM-based control. This aligns with the findings of Khan et al. (2017, 2021) who demonstrated that Moringa oleifera inclusion can improve the carcass quality of broilers by enhancing muscle protein accretion through its antioxidant properties, which protect cells from oxidative stress during rapid growth.

However, the reduction in carcass parameters at the 30% substitution level (D3) reflects the poorer growth performance and nutrient digestibility observed in that group. The higher inclusion of cm and MOLM may have increased the proportion of indigestible structural components in the diet, which could have limited the net energy and amino acids available for muscle deposition. Nkukwana et al. (2014) noted that excessive inclusion of leaf meals in poultry diets often results in decreased carcass yields due to the presence of antinutritional factors like saponins and tannins, which interfere with protein utilization. Similar reductions in carcass and cut yields have been reported in broilers fed diets containing high levels of leaf meals or insect meals (Sebola et al., 2015). Our results confirm that while the CM-MOLM blend is a potent growth enhancer at moderate levels, a 20% substitution represents the upper limit for maximizing meat yield in broilers. Excessive substitution negatively affects carcass weight and muscle yield, highlighting the importance of optimizing inclusion levels to maximize carcass quality and edible meat production.

Apparent crude protein (CP) digestibility was similar among birds fed D0, D1, and D2, but was lower in birds fed the 30% substitution diet (D3) (P < 0.001). In contrast, apparent ether extract (EE) digestibility increased as the substitution level increased, with the highest value observed in birds fed D3 (P < 0.001). Apparent organic matter (OM) digestibility followed a pattern similar to that of DM, with higher values in D0 and D1 than in D2 and D3 (P < 0.001).

The reduction in DM and OM digestibility at higher substitution levels may be associated with increased proportions of indigestible structural components in the cm–MOLM blend. Insect meals are known to contain chitin, while Moringa oleifera leaves contribute fiber fractions, both of which may reduce nutrient accessibility and digestive efficiency at elevated inclusion levels. A similar decline in nutrient digestibility was reported by Bovera et al. (2018), who observed reduced DM and OM digestibility when 25% of dietary protein was replaced with Hermetia illucens larvae meal in laying hens, suggesting that high levels of insect-derived structural components can impair nutrient utilization. However, this finding contrasts with the results of (Biasato et al., 2025), who reported increased or unaffected DM digestibility in broilers fed insect-based diets compared with a standard diet across feeding phases. The discrepancy between studies may be related to differences in insect species, processing methods, inclusion levels, or the presence of additional fibrous plant materials such as Moringa oleifera leaf meal in the present study. These factors can influence the physical structure of the diet and the extent to which nutrients are accessible to digestive enzymes.

A key outcome of this study is that crude protein (CP) digestibility was maintained up to a 20% substitution level (D2) but declined markedly at 30% (D3). This indicates that moderate replacement of SBM did not impair protein utilization, whereas higher substitution levels negatively affected CP digestibility. The reduction at the highest substitution level may be associated with increased proportions of indigestible structural components in the cm–MOLM blend, which can interfere with protease activity and amino acid absorption. A comparable decline in CP digestibility with increasing insect inclusion has been reported by (Biasato et al., 2025), who evaluated Hermetia illucens and Tenebrio molitor meals included at 5–10% of the total diet. In that study, CP digestibility decreased from 84.5% in the control group to 76.89% in insect-based diets. Although the insect inclusion levels in the present study were of a similar magnitude (up to 8.55% cm in the grower phase and 7.65% in the finisher phase), the digestibility coefficients observed here were lower (75.02% in D0 and 70.80% in D3). This difference may be attributed to variations in insect species (Acheta domesticus vs. Hermetia illucens and Tenebrio molitor), processing methods, and the additional presence of fibrous components from Moringa oleifera leaf meal, which together could have compounded the reduction in protein digestibility at higher substitution levels.

Interestingly, apparent ether extract (EE) digestibility increased significantly with higher inclusion levels of the cm–MOLM blend, reaching the highest value in the D3 group. This response contrasts with the negative trend observed for crude protein digestibility. The improvement in lipid digestibility may be associated with the lipid profile of cricket meal, which contains readily digestible fatty acids that can enhance intestinal fat absorption. Insect lipids are known to contain substantial proportions of unsaturated fatty acids and phospholipids, which are more efficiently emulsified and absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract of broilers compared with lipids in conventional SBM-based diets. Enhanced EE digestibility with insect-derived ingredients has also been reported by Kierończyk et al. (2018), who observed improved fat utilization when insect lipids replaced conventional fat sources in broiler diets.

In contrast, Moringa oleifera leaf meal appears to have a neutral effect on fat utilization. Nkukwana et al. (2014) reported that supplementation of M. oleifera leaf meal at levels up to 25 g/kg diet did not significantly affect EE digestibility or overall nutrient utilization efficiency in broilers. Therefore, the increase in EE digestibility observed in the present study is more likely attributable to the inclusion of cricket-derived lipids rather than the MOLM component of the diet.

Although the cm–MOLM blend enhanced fat digestibility, inclusion beyond 20% compromised crude protein and organic matter digestibility. This imbalance helps explain the poorer growth performance observed in the D3 group, where improved lipid utilization could not compensate for reduced protein availability for tissue deposition.

The serum biochemical parameters of broilers fed varying levels of the CM-MOLM blend are presented in Table 9. Serum total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) decreased as the substitution level increased, with the lowest values observed in birds fed the 30% substitution diet (D3). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) also declined progressively from 44.87 mg/dL in the control group to 34.31 mg/dL in D3 (P < 0.001). In contrast, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was higher in birds fed the 20% and 30% substitution diets than in those fed the control and 10% substitution diets (P < 0.001).

Dietary substitution did not affect serum total protein (P = 0.869) or glucose concentration (P = 0.433). Total protein values remained stable across treatments (2.95–3.10 g/dL), and glucose concentrations varied slightly (199.87–203.16 mg/dL), remaining within the normal physiological range reported for broilers.

The changes in lipid-related parameters suggest that inclusion of the cm–MOLM blend influenced lipid metabolism. The reductions in TC, TG, and LDL-C together with the increase in HDL-C indicate an improved serum lipid profile, which may be associated with bioactive compounds present in cm and MOLM. Insect-derived meals, including cricket meal, are known to contain chitin, a structural polysaccharide that has been reported to inhibit intestinal lipid absorption and enhance bile acid excretion. Bovera et al. (2018); Hossain and Blair (2007) reported that chitinous derivatives in poultry diets can lower circulating LDL-C by interrupting the enterohepatic circulation. However, the effects of insect meals and Moringa oleifera on blood lipid parameters have been reported to vary among studies. Dabbou et al. (2018) reported that inclusion of defatted black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larva meal up to 15% did not significantly affect serum biochemical parameters in broilers. Similarly, (Husien et al., 2025) found that supplementation of Moringa oleifera at 0.4 g/kg diet had no significant effect on serum blood metabolites. In contrast, Mahfuz and Piao (2019) noted that M. oleifera has been used as a natural growth promoter and may exert hypocholesterolemic effects in chickens.

The variation among studies may be attributed to differences in insect species, fat content (full-fat vs. defatted meals), inclusion level, bird age, and the concentration of phytochemicals in Moringa leaves. These factors can modulate lipid digestion, bile acid metabolism, and hepatic lipid synthesis, ultimately influencing circulating lipid concentrations. Moringa's flavonoids also possess antioxidant properties that protect HDL-C from oxidative modification, which may explain the significantly higher HDL-C levels observed in the D2 and D3 groups.

The significant decrease in triglycerides, particularly in the D3 group, may be further linked to the high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in cricket meal. PUFA-rich insect meals can suppress hepatic lipogenesis, leading to lower concentrations of circulating triglycerides. Collectively, these results suggest that the CM-MOLM blend not only serves as a viable protein substitute but also acts as a functional feed additive that improves the systemic health of broilers by optimizing their lipid metabolism.

In contrast, total protein and glucose concentrations were not significantly affected by dietary treatments (P > 0.05). The stability of serum total protein indicates that protein metabolism and liver synthetic function were not compromised by partial replacement of SBM, even at the highest substitution level. Similarly, unchanged blood glucose concentrations suggest that carbohydrate metabolism and energy homeostasis remained stable across all dietary treatments. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which report that moderate inclusion of insect meals or Moringa leaf meal does not adversely affect basic metabolic indicators in broiler chickens (El-Abbasy et al., 2025b)

Overall, the blood biochemical profile results indicate that partial replacement of SBM with a combination of cm and MOLM favourably modulated lipid metabolism without negatively affecting protein or glucose metabolism. The observed hypolipidemic effects, characterized by reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-C, along with increased HDL-C, suggest potential health benefits associated with the inclusion of these alternative protein sources. The blood biochemical data reinforce that a 10–20% substitution of SBM with a CM-MOLM blend supports optimal metabolic health and protein utilization. These findings suggest that CM–MOLM inclusion may provide additional functional benefits beyond protein supply by improving lipid metabolism in broiler chickens.

Changes in digestive and metabolic organ weights may reflect physiological adaptation to differences in diet composition. The higher relative gizzard weight observed in birds fed D3 may be associated with greater mechanical activity required to process diets containing higher levels of structural components from insect and leaf ingredients. Both cm and MOLM contain higher proportions of structural carbohydrates and insoluble fiber which can stimulate gizzard development and muscular activity as the organ adapts to increased grinding requirements. Similar increases in gizzard weight have been reported in broilers fed high-fiber diets or diets containing leaf meals and insect-derived ingredients (Facey et al., 2023; Pietras et al., 2021). This finding is consistent with (Miya et al., 2019), who observed increased gizzard weights in broilers fed high levels of fibrous leaf meals as the organ works harder to reduce particle size.

Similarly, the higher relative weight of the liver observed in the D3 group may reflect enhanced metabolic activity related to nutrient detoxification, lipid metabolism, or processing of secondary plant compounds present in MOLM. The liver may have increased its activity to manage the metabolic processing of the high-quality lipids and chitin derivatives absorbed from the cm, as the liver is the central hub for lipid regulation in poultry (Zaefarian et al., 2019).While moderate levels of Moringa oleifera provide beneficial antioxidants, high inclusion levels (as in D3) introduce higher concentrations of anti-nutritional factors like saponins and tannins. (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022) noted that excessive intake of these compounds can lead to increased hepatic activity for detoxification, which often manifests as an increase in relative liver weight. Previous studies have reported increased liver weights in broilers fed diets containing high levels of phytogenic ingredients or alternative protein sources, which may be associated with adaptive metabolic responses rather than pathological changes (Biasato et al., 2018).

In contrast, the relative weights of the pancreas were not significantly affected by dietary treatments (P > 0.05), suggesting that pancreatic enzyme secretion capacity was not substantially altered by partial replacement of SBM. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the relative weights of the spleen and bursa of Fabricius among dietary treatments, indicating that immune organ development was not adversely affected by inclusion of cm and MOLM. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that moderate inclusion of insect meals or leaf meals does not impair immune organ development in broiler chickens (Ali et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025).

The organ weight data confirm that a 20% substitution level (D2) is the safety threshold for broilers. Exceeding this level (D3) triggers compensatory organ growth in the gizzard and liver, which likely redirects energy away from growth performance, explaining the lower body weight gain observed in the 30% substitution group. Overall, the results suggest that partial replacement of SBM with a combination of cm and MOLM induces adaptive changes in digestive organs, particularly the gizzard and liver, especially at higher substitution levels. However, the absence of significant effects on immune-related organs indicates that the dietary treatments did not negatively affect the physiological status or immune competence of the birds.

Conclusions

Partial replacement of soybean meal with a blend of Acheta domesticus nymph meal and Moringa oleifera leaf meal at a 3:1 ratio improved growth performance and carcass yield of broiler chickens when included at levels up to 20% of the original soybean meal content. This level of substitution did not adversely affect internal organ development or serum metabolic indicators and was associated with improved lipid profiles, as evidenced by reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-cholesterol, along with increased HDL-cholesterol.

In contrast, a 30% substitution level reduced growth performance and nutrient digestibility and was associated with increased relative weights of the liver and gizzard, likely reflecting physiological adaptation to higher levels of indigestible structural components from insect and leaf sources. These findings indicate that inclusion of the cm–MOLM blend should be limited to moderate levels to maintain optimal nutrient utilization and production performance in broiler chickens.
