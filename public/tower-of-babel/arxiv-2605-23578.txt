Safety, Liveness, and Fairness in Quantitative Argumentation Dialogues
Arunavo Ganguly, Julian Alfredo Mendez, Timotheus Kampik

Abstract
We introduce notions of safety , liveness , and fairness , as commonly used in temporal reasoning, to quantitative (bipolar) argumentation dialogues where repeated inferences are drawn from argumentation graphs with weighted nodes. Between inferences, these graphs undergo updates. Strong and weak safety capture that arguments’ (final) strengths remain above a specific threshold of justification and always reach the threshold eventually, respectively. Liveness requires that arguments’ strengths fluctuate across the threshold of justification. Fairness notions assess how safe arguments are spread within a sequence of argumentation graphs. We formally show how these notions are related, and discuss some analytical challenges with respect to providing general guarantees for our properties.

Quantitative (bipolar) argumentation is a formal argumentation variant in which inferences are drawn from graphs whose nodes (

Argumentation

QBAGs are of substantial interest to the formal argumentation community, notably because of their potential to complement machine learning approaches and help make them

Currently, QBAG applications are emerging that facilitate explainability and reliability of large language model-based inference

In such applications, argumentation can be viewed as a dynamic process during which QBAGs are manipulated, and repeated inferences are drawn.
Accordingly, it may be interesting to inquire about the dynamic properties of QBAG-based inference.

This paper sets out to systematise such inquiries by drawing inspiration from well-known notions of

However, we do not formally relate to these notions of “tracial control”, as we are interested in conceptually somewhat different questions, namely the dynamics of quantitative argument strengths.

Specifically, given a sequence of QBAGs (which we call a

Safety:

Do all of the topic arguments always stay above the threshold, or do they at least cross it

?
The former property, which we call

can, e.g., help us check whether obviously credible arguments always retain reasonable strengths in the dialogue, or if the dialogue, colloquially speaking, at times reaches “cuckoo-land” territory.
The latter property (
weak safety)
allows us to check whether an agent reaches an objective represented by the topic arguments’ final strength at the dialogue’s end.

Liveness:

Do all of the topic arguments “cross the threshold” in the course of the dialogue, i.e,, for each argument, is there a QBAG where its final strength is below the threshold, as well as a QBAG where its final strength is above the threshold?
Liveness helps us understand whether an intuitively “interesting” deliberation takes place with respect to the arguments: if the dialogue is not
live
with respect to the arguments, we do not observe any notable situations in which an eventually credible argument is substantially challenged, or where an eventually not credible argument appears to be credible.

Fairness:

Do all topic arguments get to have an equal shot at credibility? We present several drastic and gradual fairness notions, which are based on safety.

Preliminaries

Let
\langle\mathbb{I},\preceq\rangle
be a partial ordering.
Typically,
\mathbb{I}
will be a real interval along with the canonical ordering.
A
quantitative bipolar argumentation graph

G=\langle\mathit{Args},\tau,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Supp}\rangle

\mathit{Args}
is a set of arguments,
\mathit{Att}

\mathit{Supp}

assigns an

in
\mathbb{I}

Intuitively, an initial strength may represent

For some examples of QBAGs, see Figure
1
(Example

).

Unless stated otherwise, let

G=\langle\mathit{Args},\tau,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Supp}\rangle
be an arbitrary but fixed QBAG, with finite
\mathit{Args}
.
Given an argument
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}
, the attackers and supporters of
\mathsf{x}
are denoted by

\mathit{Att}_{G}(\mathsf{x}):=\{\mathsf{a}\in\mathit{Args}:(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{x})\in\mathit{Att}\}
and
\mathit{Supp}_{G}(\mathsf{x}):=\{\mathsf{a}\in\mathit{Args}:(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{x})\in\mathit{Supp}\}
,
respectively.
We drop the subscript
G
whenever it is clear from context.
Given two arguments
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in\mathit{Args}
, we say that
\mathsf{x}
reaches
\mathsf{y}
iff there is a path from
\mathsf{x}
to
\mathsf{y}
in the graph
\langle\mathit{Args},\mathit{Att}\cup\mathit{Supp}\rangle
.
An acyclic QBAG does not contain any loops, i.e. no argument
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}
can reach itself.
For any
\mathit{Args}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{Args}
, we define the restriction of
G
to
\mathit{Args}
as
G\downarrow_{\mathit{Args}^{\prime}}:=\langle\mathit{Args}^{\prime},\tau\cap(\mathit{Args}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{I}),\mathit{Att}\cap(\mathit{Args}^{\prime}\times\mathit{Args}^{\prime}),\mathit{Supp}\cap(\mathit{Args}^{\prime}\times\mathit{Args}^{\prime})\rangle
.
Finally, we say
G
is a sub-QBAG of
G^{\prime}=\langle\mathit{Args}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\mathit{Att}^{\prime},\mathit{Supp}^{\prime}\rangle
, denoted by
G^{\prime}\sqsubseteq G
, iff
\mathit{Args}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{Args}
,
\mathit{Att}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{Att}
,
\mathit{Supp}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{Supp}
and
\tau^{\prime}(\mathsf{x})=\tau(\mathsf{x})
, for every
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}^{\prime}
.
If
G\sqsubseteq G^{\prime}
but
G\not=G^{\prime}
, we say
G\sqsubset G^{\prime}
.

Our idea of dynamic argumentation is captured via QBAG chains.

Definition 1

(QBAG Chains)
.

A

{\cal G}=(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
.

Intuitively, chains are evolutions of a given QBAG along arbitrary dimensions, such as expansions, initial strength updates etc.
If a chain only encompasses expansions we call it a
QBAG expansion chain
; i.e., an
QBAG expansion chain

G_{i}\sqsubset G_{i+1}

Given a chain

G\prec_{\cal G}G^{\prime}
to denote that for some
1\leq i<n

G=G_{i}
and
G^{\prime}=G_{i+1}
.
When we write

, we mean that for some
G_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n

G_{i}=G
.
An expansion chain

(i)

(ii)

\mathit{Args}
.

Sometimes, an expansion chain

A

is a function
\sigma_{G}:\mathit{Args}\rightarrow\mathbb{I}\cup\{\perp\}
mapping the arguments in a QBAG
G

\mathbb{I}
, along with the symbol
\perp
for undefined
2

Modular semantics
are well-studied sub-class of gradual semantics, where the final strength is assigned by
aggregating
the final
strengths of its attackers and supporters, and then computing the
influence
on the
specific argument given the aggregation result and the argument’s initial strength.
More formally, an aggregation function has the form

Modular semantics first

In our examples we make use of

For the aggregation function

f(\mathit{Att}(\mathsf{x}),\mathit{Supp}(\mathsf{x}))=\prod_{\mathsf{b}\in\mathit{Att}(\mathsf{x})}(1-\sigma(\mathsf{b}))-\prod_{\mathsf{c}\in\mathit{Supp}(\mathsf{x})}(1-\sigma(\mathsf{c}))
and initial strength
\tau(\mathsf{a}),

DFQuAD is defined over acyclic graphs with a topological ordering as per the equation given below.

\displaystyle g(\tau(\mathsf{a}),f(\mathit{Att}(\mathsf{x})),\mathit{Supp}(\mathsf{x})):=\

\displaystyle\tau(\mathsf{a})-\tau(\mathsf{a})\times\max\{0,-f(\mathit{Att}(\mathsf{x}),\mathit{Supp}(\mathsf{x}))\}\hskip 3.99994pt+

\displaystyle(1-\tau(\mathsf{a}))\times\max\{0,f(\mathit{Att}(\mathsf{x}),\mathit{Supp}(\mathsf{x}))\}.

Assumption.

.
This is a problem for us, as we assume that final strengths are comparable.
However, for the sake of simplicity, we do not handle this edge case and the reader may assume that we focus on acyclic QBAGs, which is a common assumption in the literature

Safety, Liveness, and Fairness Properties

In this section, we formally introduce the SLF notions for QBAGs.
The notions are validated w.r.t. an initial QBAG
G
, a chain of updates
\cal G
, a semantics
\sigma
, and a threshold
t\in\mathbb{I}
.
We call
t
the
threshold of justification
.
Throughout this section, we fix a partial ordering
\mathbb{I}
, arbitrary QBAGs
G=\langle\mathit{Args},\tau,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Supp}\rangle
,

G^{\prime}=\langle\mathit{Args}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\mathit{Att}^{\prime},\mathit{Supp}^{\prime}\rangle
,

G^{\prime\prime}=\langle\mathit{Args}^{\prime\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime},\mathit{Att}^{\prime\prime},\mathit{Supp}^{\prime\prime}\rangle
, a QBAG chain
{\cal G}=(G_{1},\dots G_{n})
.
We also fix a set of topic arguments
T\subseteq\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq n}G_{i}
w.r.t. which we analyse the SLF notions.

Safety

Strongly safe
arguments exceed the threshold of justification against the repeated drawing of inferences during the expansion.

Definition 2

(Strong safety)
.

We say that a topic set
T
is safe w.r.t. a chain
\cal G,
a semantics
\sigma
, and
t\in\mathbb{I}
iff for every
G^{\prime}\in\cal G
, and
\mathsf{x}\in T
,
\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
.

In contrast, weak safety stipulates that arguments eventually exceed the threshold, thereby becoming justifiable at the end.

Definition 3

(Weak safety)
.

We say that a topic set
T
is weakly safe w.r.t. a chain
\cal G
, a semantics
\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
iff for every
\mathsf{x}\in T
,
\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
, where
G^{\prime}
is the end-point of
\cal G
.

We revisit our initial example to illustrate the safety notions.

Example 3

(Strong and weak safety)
.

In Figure
1
, the arguments
\mathsf{c}
and
\mathsf{b}
are strongly and weakly safe, respectively, w.r.t.
(G,G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime})
and the threshold
t=0.1
.
However, if the threshold is changed to
t=0.2
,
\mathsf{c}
is neither strongly safe nor weakly safe.
On the flip side,
\mathsf{d}
continues to be weakly safe.

It is immediate from the definition that strong safety implies its weak counterpart.

Theorem 1
.

If an argument
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq E
of a topic
E
is safe, then it is weakly safe.

Proof.

Let
T\subseteq\mathit{Args}
be safe w.r.t. a chain
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
, a semantics
\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
.
This means
\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
for each
\mathsf{x}\in T
, where
G^{\prime}\in\cal G
.
In particular,
\sigma_{G_{n}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
.
The previous statement is the condition for weak safety, as
G_{n}
is the end-point of the chain.
∎

In general, the converse of Theorem
1
does not hold, as seen in Example
3
.
However, under certain restrictions upon the expansion sets and the choice of semantics a partial converse can be achieved.
In the subsequent theorem, we propose a few of such cases.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose either of the following cases hold:

•

The chain
\cal G
has length
1
, or

•

\cal G
is a weak chain and
\sigma
is a modular semantics, or

•

Only arguments that cannot reach
\mathit{Args}
are added to the chain
\cal G
.

Then strong and weakly safe arguments coincide w.r.t.
G,\cal G,\sigma
.

Proof.

Assume
T
is weakly safe w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
, where the chain
\cal G
is denoted by
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
.
Now, we prove our claim by cases.

•

If
\cal G
has length
1
, then
{\cal G}=(G_{1})
, hence
G_{1}=G_{n}
.
Since the start and end-point of
\cal G
coincides, as per Definition
3
,
\sigma_{G_{1}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
, for every
\mathsf{x}\in T
.
Since
\cal G
is singleton,
\forall G^{\prime}\in\cal G
,
\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
, which is the condition for strong safety.

•

Weak expansions under any modular semantics
\sigma
do not alter the final strengths of
G
.
This is due to the strong directionality argument in
11
(Principle 4, Lemma 6.1).
So
\forall G^{\prime}\in\cal G
, and
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}
,
\sigma_{G}(\mathsf{x})=\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})
.
In particular, if any topic argument
\mathsf{y}
has
\sigma_{G_{n}}(\mathsf{y})\geq t
iff
\forall G^{\prime}\in{\cal G},\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{y})\geq t
.

•

Again as per
11
, any modular semantics
\sigma
satisfy strong directionality, i.e., arguments that cannot reach
T
, added during the updates, do not change the final strength of the topics.
Hence,
\forall G^{\prime}\in\cal G
, and
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}
,
\sigma_{G}(\mathsf{x})=\sigma_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{x})
.
Now, we follow the argument as given in the previous case.

∎

Liveness

Safe arguments might appear
monotone
or
unalive
due to their consistent levels of high credibility, as seen in Definitions
2
and
3
.
Our notion of liveness aims to capture the contrasting case of such observations.
The idea is formally introduced through fluctuations across the threshold-of-justification.
Intuitively, fluctuations quantify “flip-flopping” behaviour exhibited by an argument.

Definition 4
.

We say
\mathsf{x}
shows
k
-fluctuations w.r.t. a chain
{\cal G}=(G_{1},\dots,G_{n}),\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
iff there are
1\leq i_{0}\leq\dots\leq i_{k}\leq n
s.t. for each
1\leq j<n
,
either

\sigma_{G_{i_{j}}}(\mathsf{x})\leq t\leq\sigma_{G_{i_{j+1}}}(\mathsf{x})
, if possible or
\sigma_{G_{i_{j+1}}}(\mathsf{x})\leq t\leq\sigma_{G_{i_{j}}}(\mathsf{x})
, otherwise.

We say
\mathsf{x}
shows exactly
n
-fluctuations over a chain
\cal G
iff
\mathsf{x}
cannot show
n+1
-fluctuations.
Based upon this idea, we introduce the notion of liveness.

Definition 5

(Liveness)
.

We say topic set
T
is live w.r.t. a chain
\cal G
a semantics
\sigma
, and
a\in\mathbb{I}
iff each
\mathsf{x}\in T
shows at-least
1
-fluctuation w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and threshold
t
.

Again, we go back to our running example.

Example 4

(Liveness Example)
.

The chain
{\cal G}=(G,G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime})
has the topic set
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}\}
as live w.r.t. the threshold
0.2
.
On the other hand,
\{\mathsf{c}\}
shows no fluctuation w.r.t. the threshold
t=0.2
, thereby being strongly safe but unalive.

The latter part of the above observation can be generalised, i.e. a topic is not live iff it is strongly safe.
This observation provides an equivalent formulation of strong safety.

Theorem 3
.

A topic
T
is strongly safe iff it is not live w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and threshold
t\in\mathbb{I}
.

Proof.

Suppose
T
is strongly safe, then as per definition
\sigma_{G}(\mathsf{x})\geq t
,
\forall G\in{\cal G},\forall\mathsf{x}\in T
.
Now, the above condition is equivalent to saying
G
never goes below
t
in the chain,i.e. there are
0
fluctuations w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and
t
.
∎

We can guarantee that in certain cases no fluctuations are observed in the topic set.
These include cases of weak expansions, and evolutions that only append downstream arguments.
We formally observe it below as a corollary;
the proof is a straight-forward application of Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose either of the following cases hold:

•

The chain
\cal G
has length
1
, or

•

\cal G
is a weak chain and
\sigma
is a modular semantics, or

•

Only down-stream arguments are added to
\mathit{Args}
in the chain
\cal G
.

Then
\mathsf{x}\in\mathit{Args}
shows no fluctuations w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
.
\hfill\square

Fairness

We provide three notions towards binary classification of strength-fairness.
Intuitively, these notions condition the safety of all topic arguments on the existence
of safety in one topic argument in terms of a necessary condition.
Here, the existential safety notion (“one topic argument”) must be at
least as strong as the universal safety notion (“all topic arguments”).
Notice that given the argumentation-based setting, our fairness notions purposefully diverge conceptually from fairness notions in temporal reasoning, which tend to require executions
infinitely often
; in contrast, we are interested in notions reflecting fairness in finite resource distribution.

Definition 6

(Fairness)
.

We say a topic set
T
, w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t\in\mathbb{I}
is

•

Ideally Fair

iff some
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
is strongly safe implies
T
is strongly safe.

•

Lively Fair

iff some
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
is weakly safe implies
T
is weakly safe.

•

Cautiously Fair

iff some
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
is strongly safe implies
T
is weakly safe.

In our running example, sets of arguments satisfy different fairness notions.

Example 5

(Strength Fairness Example)
.

Refer again to Figure
1
, and consider
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
to be the topic set.
Example
3
states
\mathsf{c}
is strongly safe w.r.t.
(G,G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime})
and threshold
0.2
.
All the topic-arguments are above
0.2
at the end-point

, thereby making the set
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
cautiously and lively fair.
However, ideal fairness is violated by
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
as
\{\mathsf{c}\}
is strongly safe, whereas
\{\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
are weakly so.

The following connections are present between the notions of fairness.

Theorem 4
.

For any topic set
T
, the following holds w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t\in\mathbb{I}
.

•

Lively fairness implies cautious fairness.

•

Ideal fairness implies lively fairness, in the presence of a strongly safe argument.

Proof.

For the first case, assume
T
is lively fair w.r.t.
\cal G
,
\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}
.
If there is a strongly safe
\mathsf{x}\in T
then by Theorem

,
\mathsf{x}
is weakly safe, and consequently

is weakly safe due to live fairness.
This satisfies the constraint for being cautiously fair.

For the second case, suppose

is ideally fair with a strongly safe topic argument

, which implies
T
is strongly safe, and by Theorem
1
,
T
is weakly safe.
Now trivially, lively fairness is satisfied.
∎

Additionally, ideal fairness prohibits the liveness of topic arguments, thereby implying a “dull” behaviour of the dialogue with respect to the concerned arguments.

Theorem 5
.

If
T
is ideally fair then it is
not
live whenever there is a strongly safe
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
.

Proof.

Suppose
T
is ideally fair w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t
with a strongly safe
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
.
This implies every
\mathsf{x}\in T
satisfies
\sigma(\mathsf{x})\geq t
through the update chain
\cal G
, showing no fluctuations.
Hence,
T
is not live as per definition.
∎

Towards the gradual notions of fairness, we firstly draw on the Gini index
8
, which traditionally measures “inequalities” in the distribution of wealth, income, or consumption.
The idea is to analogously measure differences in the safety of topic arguments.
Consider the example below.

Example 6
.

In Figure
1
,
\mathsf{a}
and
\mathsf{b}
exceed the threshold
0.2
for the QBAGs
\{G,G^{\prime\prime}\}
, whereas
\mathsf{c}
exceeds the threshold for the whole chain
\{G,G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}\}
.
Thus the set
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
has the ordering
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})
when arranged by increasing number of threshold-exceeding QBAGs in the chain
(G,G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime})
.
Figure
2
plots the
safety curve

(solid line) using Equation
2
,
where
T=\mathit{Args}
is topic set,
t=0.2
,
{\cal G}=(G,G^{\prime},G)
, and
S(\mathsf{x})
returns the number of QBAGs within
\cal G
, for which
\mathsf{x}
exceeds the threshold
t=0.2
.
The
fairness line
(dotted line) for the topic set
\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
is a line joining
(0,0)
and
(3,7)
, as per Equation
1
.
Intuitively, the fairness line shows that the cumulative sum of threshold-exceeding arguments increase linearly w.r.t. the aforementioned ordering of the topics.

Fairness is now understood in terms of deviation of the safety curve from the fairness line.
The deviation, in turn, is measured in terms of area enclosed within their graphs, and normalised using a modified sigmoid function.
For this case, the Gini-based fairness score is
\approx
0.46212.

Figure 2
:
Plot of Fairness Line v/s
\zeta
.
The solid line is due to the safety-curve
\zeta
, and the fairness line is represented using dashes.
The area enclosed between the two curves measures the inequality in fluctuation.

Formally, given a chain
\cal G
, any argument
\mathsf{x}\in T
, and a threshold
t\in\mathbb{I}
, we define
S_{{\cal G},\sigma,t}(\mathsf{x})
to return the cardinality of the set

|\{G\in{\cal G}:\sigma(\mathsf{x})\geq t\}|,

i.e. the number of QBAGs within
\cal G
for which
\mathsf{x}
exceeds
t
.
We drop the subscripts whenever it is clear from context.
The
fairness line

f(x)
is the line joining
(0,0)
to
(|T|,\sum_{\mathsf{y}\in T}S(\mathsf{y}))
, which is defined piecewise

f(x)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\sum_{\mathsf{y}\in T}S(\mathsf{y})}{|T|}\right)\times x;&0\leq x\leq|T|,\\
0;&\text{$x>|T|,\,x<0$.}\end{cases}

(1)

The safety curve can be formalised as follows: suppose
\mathsf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{x}_{n}
are the arguments in
T
arranged in an increasing order, then
the fluctuation-curve is defined piece-wise:

\zeta_{{\cal G},T,\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}S_{{\cal G},\sigma,t}(\mathsf{x}_{j})\right)+\left(\frac{S_{{\cal G},\sigma,t}(\mathsf{x}_{i})}{\mathsf{x}_{i+1}-\mathsf{x}_{i}}\right)\times x;&i\leq x\leq i+1,\\
0;&\text{$x>|T|,\,x<0$.}\end{cases}

(2)

Based on these notions, we introduce Gini-based fairness.

Definition 7

(Gini-based fairness)
.

For the topic set
T
, define the un-normalised Gini-based fairness score using Equation
2
w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t\in\mathbb{I}
.:

{\cal F}^{u}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma):=\int_{0}^{|T|}|\zeta_{{\cal G},\sigma,t}(x)-f(x)|dx.

(3)

Now, Gini-based fluctuation fairness as

{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma):=\left(\frac{2}{{1+e^{-{\cal F}^{u}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)}}}-1\right).

Intuitively, the integral in Equation
3
measures the deviation of the safety curve from the fairness line in terms of area enclosed between them.
Hence, as inequality grows,
{\cal F}_{gini}
approaches 1,whereas the case of perfect equality encloses a non-positive area.
This is formally observed in the theorem below.

Theorem 6
.

Fairness of fluctuations is a positive function having its range in the unit interval
[0,1)
.
The value
0
represents perfect equality, i.e. when the safety curve coincides with the fairness line.

Proof.

Notice that, as
{\cal F}^{u}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)\geq 0
, it implies
0\leq{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)<1
.
In the case of
{\cal F}^{u}_{gini}(T,{\cal G},\sigma)=0
, i.e. when the fairness line and
\zeta
coincides,

\left(\frac{2}{{1+e^{-{\cal F}^{u}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)}}}\right)=1

and by definition,
{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=0
.
∎

We propose another gradual notion, inspired by Shannon entropy
18
.
To that end, we define a threshold-exceeding probability on the topic set
T
w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t\in\mathbb{I}
:

p_{{\cal G},T,\sigma}(\mathsf{x}):=\frac{\text{Total threshold exceeding QBAGs of }\mathsf{x}}{\text{Total fluctuations shown by the topic set }T}=\frac{S_{{\cal G},T,\sigma}(\mathsf{x})}{\sum_{\mathsf{y}\in T}S_{{\cal G},T,\sigma}(\mathsf{y})}.

Again, we drop the subscript whenever clear from context.
Notice that
p
is
undefined
in the absence of threshold exceeding behaviour.
Given a random threshold exceeding behaviour, we want to measure how
surprising
it is.
If the behaviour is from a topic with low number of fluctuation then it is highly surprising, and vice-versa.
Formally, we denote it as

I(p):=\log_{b}\left(\frac{1}{p(\mathsf{x})}\right)=-\log_{b}(p(\mathsf{x})),

where
b
is the least common multiple of the denominators of
p(\mathsf{x})
.
Now, Shannon-based fluctuation fairness is defined as the expectation of the surprise factor.

Definition 8

(Shannon-based fairness)
.

For the topic arguments
E
w.r.t. the chain
\cal G
, semantics
\sigma
and threshold
a\in\mathbb{I}
, Shannon-based fluctuation fairness is defined as:

\mathcal{F}_{shannon}({\cal G},T,\sigma):=\begin{cases}-\sum_{\mathsf{x}\in T}p(\mathsf{x})\log_{b}(p(\mathsf{x}));&\text{if $p$ is defined},\\
1;&\text{if $p$ is undefined}.\end{cases}

(4)

where
b
is the least common multiple of the denominators of
p(\mathsf{x})
.

Example 7
.

Let us consider the Shannon-based fairness for the topic set
T=\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}\}
in Figure
1
.
The probability of fluctuation is given by
p(\mathsf{a})=\frac{2}{7},p(\mathsf{b})=\frac{2}{7}
and
p(\mathsf{c})=\frac{3}{7}
.
It is easy to see that
b=7
and by definition,
{\cal F}_{shannon}\approx 0.55449.

The next result provides a case where Shannon-based fairness is maximised.

Theorem 7
.

If
\forall\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in T,S(\mathsf{x})=S(\mathsf{y})
w.r.t.
\cal G,\sigma
and
t\in\mathbb{I}

then
\mathcal{F}_{shannon}(E,{\cal G},\sigma)
is
1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\forall\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in T,S(\mathsf{x})=S(\mathsf{y})
, it immediately implies that
\forall\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y},p(\mathsf{x})=p(\mathsf{y})
.
The later now implies
p(\mathsf{x})=\frac{1}{m}
, where
m=|T|
, and consequently
b=m
.
Now the result is the given by the following chain of equality:

{\cal F}_{shannon}=-\sum_{\mathsf{x}\in T}p(\mathsf{x})\cdot log_{b}(p(\mathsf{x}))=-m\cdot\left(\frac{1}{m}\cdot log_{m}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)=1.

∎

Theorem
7
shows the interplay between binary and gradual notions of fairness.
If a topic
T
is ideally fair in the presence of some strongly safe
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
, then
{\cal F}_{shannon}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=1
.
Similarly, given the previous scenario,
{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=0
.

Theorem 8
.

If
T
is ideally fair w.r.t.
{\cal G},\sigma,t
, and some
\{\mathsf{x}\}\subseteq T
is strongly safe, then
{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=0
, and
{\cal F}_{shannon}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=1
.

Proof.

Notice that if the theorem’s antecedent holds, then
\forall\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in T
, we have
S(\mathsf{x})=S(\mathsf{y})
.
This means
p
is an uniform distribution, and by Theorem
7
,
{\cal F}_{shannon}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=1
.

On the other hand, the previous observation implies that
\zeta
is a linear function satisfying
\zeta(0)=0
, and
\zeta(|T|)=\sum_{\mathsf{x}\in T}S(x)
.
This in turn implies that
\zeta
coincides with the fairness line.
Finally by Theorem
6
,
{\cal F}_{gini}({\cal G},T,\sigma)=0
.
∎

4
Discussion and Conclusion

Our work contributes to the line of research on
argumentation dynamics
that, broadly speaking, studies argumentative inference where the representation of the exchange of arguments undergoes changes.
This naturally reflects the dynamic nature of dialectical reasoning.
Argumentation dynamics are frequently studied for different variants of computational argumentation—see
6
for a survey of works on abstract argumentation dynamics.
For the specific case of QBAGs, dynamics are often applied in order to facilitate some notion of
explainability
, notably by assessing the impact (a change to) one argument has on the final strength of another one
21
;
12
.
In these works, dynamics are thus essentially used as an analytical tool.
Conversely, our work introduces analytical tools for studying dynamics that occur in sequences of QBAGs, which we call
chains
or, intuitively,
dialogues
.
It may thus be most closely related to
10
, which studies which changes—also called
explanations
—made to a QBAG affect the relative strength of two topic arguments.
The current work could be extended to define explanations amounting to changes affecting safety, liveness, and fairness notions.

Beyond the above, our study of SLF notions for quantitative (bipolar) argumentation dialogues leaves the following two key questions for future work.

Providing SLF guarantees depending on argumentation semantics.

Our work analyses only very simple cases where some of our SLF notions can be guaranteed, notably cases based on weak expansions, where changes do not affect the topic arguments.
Indeed, we view the SLF notions as analysis tools that can be applied to
specific instances
of QBAG chains.
Let us argue that safety, liveness, and fairness is challenging for more general cases.
Figure
3
illustrates that even relatively simple scenarios may render it difficult to analytically show for general cases that SLF notions are satisfied or violated.
Consider the QBAG in Figure
3a
and topic arguments
\mathsf{a}
and
\mathsf{b}
.
The strength of
\mathsf{f}
is variable; consider a scenario where we have a chain of QBAGs that are all based on the one in the figure; however, the strength of
\mathsf{f}
increases (either due to an initial strength change or influence of additional supporters), e.g., from
0.1
to
0.5
and finally to
0.9
.
The effects of these changes on the final strengths of
\mathsf{a}
and
\mathsf{b}

Notice that intuitively, the effect of strength changes to

\mathsf{a}
and inversely for
\mathsf{b}

12

This makes it difficult to assess whether a topic set, even when consisting of a single argument is safe or live, and analogously, whether a topic set of several arguments is fair. In our example,

Defining SLF notions for partially ordered final strengths.

In this work, we have made the crucial assumption that our assessments of safety, liveness, and fairness pertain to topic arguments that are
comparable
.
However, this may not always be the case in QBAGs, as arguments may have
undefined
final strengths (practically, because a modular semantics may not converge for cyclic graphs).
Pragmatically, one may claim that undefined final strengths are an edge case whose consideration introduces overhead and thus confusion.
However, in some cases dealing with arguments of incomparable strengths can be useful, e.g., when moving from quantitative to labelling-based abstract argumentation where labels may form a lattice of argument strength (cf.
19
), as sketched in
10
.
