Surgical repair of steroidal and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug-associated full-thickness gastrointestinal ulcers in 10 cats
Authors: Juan Carlos Jimeno Sandoval, Constança Ribeiro da Cunha, Matteo Rossanese, Mariette Aurelie Pilot, Timothy Menghini, Erika Jennifer Amandine Villedieu, Robert James Quinn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to describe the clinical presentation, surgical management, complications and outcomes of cats with gastrointestinal (GI) ulcer perforation associated with recent administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids or both.MethodsA retrospective review of medical records from five UK referral hospitals was conducted between 2009 and 2024. Cats were included if they underwent surgical correction of GI ulceration within 14 days of NSAID and/or corticosteroid administration.ResultsA total of 10 cats met the inclusion criteria. The proximal duodenum was the most common site of perforation (n = 6/10), followed by the gastric body (n = 2/10). Surgical techniques varied and included primary repair (n = 5/10), Y-U pyloroplasty (n = 2/10), enterectomy (n = 2/10) and partial gastrectomy (n = 1/10). All cats survived the immediate postoperative period; however, one cat did not survive to discharge (median hospitalisation time 6 days [range 3–15]). Short-term complications were noted in three cats: one death (sepsis 9 days postoperatively) and three mild complications, with two of these complications occurring in the same cat. Long-term complications were noted in one cat (severe ulcerative duodenopathy and euthanased 16 days postoperatively). Long-term follow-up (median 928 days [range 9–1215]) revealed that 5/10 cats remained alive at study completion. Two deaths (n = 2/10) were attributed to GI ulcer-related complications.Conclusions and relevanceGI perforation secondary to recent NSAID or corticosteroid administration in cats carries a favourable prognosis after surgical correction. Despite the high-risk nature of GI perforation, the majority of cats survived to discharge.Keywords: Gastrointestinal, ulceration, perforation, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids

Introduction

Gastroduodenal ulcers are infrequently reported in cats. 1 They consist of mucosal defects that expose submucosa or deeper layers of the gastric and duodenal wall to gastric acid, most commonly affecting the stomach or proximal duodenum.1–3 These ulcers include lesions that vary from superficial epithelial erosions to full-thickness ulcers. 4 The aetiology is considered multifactorial, including ulcerogenic drug administration, inflammatory conditions, neoplastic processes affecting the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, systemic illnesses and ingestion of toxic or irritating substances.4–6 Neoplasia, particularly lymphoma, is most frequently identified as the primary underlying cause in feline cases.1,2,5,7–10 Although several experimental studies have demonstrated the ulcerogenic potential of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in feline models, clinical reports of NSAID-induced gastroduodenal ulceration in cats are limited.2,6,10–12 The true incidence of GI ulceration in cats receiving NSAIDs or corticosteroids remains unknown.

In dogs, NSAID administration is the most common predisposing factor for the development of ulceration. 13 Surgical repair of NSAID-associated full-thickness gastroduodenal perforations in dogs has been reported to have a 73% survival rate to discharge, with no ulcer-related deaths reported after discharge. 13

Dogs and cats with perforated GI ulcers are at significant risk of developing septic peritonitis, a condition associated with high mortality and necessitating prompt surgical intervention. The survival rate in cats with septic peritonitis is in the range of 46–69.9%.14–16

There is a limited body of literature describing the clinical findings, outcomes and prognosis associated with GI ulceration secondary to NSAIDs or corticosteroids in veterinary medicine, particularly in feline patients. The objective of this study was to describe the clinical presentation, surgical technique employed, postoperative complications and outcome after surgical treatment of GI ulcerations associated with steroids or NSAIDs in cats.

Our hypothesis was that primary debridement and closure of NSAID-associated or steroid-associated GI perforation would be associated with a good long-term prognosis.

Materials and methods

Medical records of cats with GI perforations suspected to be associated with NSAID or steroid administration, which presented to five referral hospitals in the UK between 2009 and 2024, were evaluated retrospectively. This retrospective descriptive study obtained ethical approval from the Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons ethics review panel (approval reference 2022-125-Sandoval2).

Cats were eligible for inclusion if they had a documented history of corticosteroid or NSAID administration within 14 days before undergoing surgical intervention for GI ulcer repair. Cats missing specific dosage information or the exact duration of drug administration were not excluded as long as the treatment occurred within the defined preoperative timeframe. Cats with gastroduodenal dehiscence from previous anastomotic, enterotomy or gastrotomy site failure, and cats with GI perforations from external trauma, tumours or foreign bodies were excluded from the study.

The decision to perform surgery was based on the presence of septic peritoneal fluid on cytology after abdominocentesis, pneumoperitoneum or suspicion of GI perforation on abdominal radiographs, ultrasound and/or CT.

Information obtained from the clinical records included signalment, clinical history, concurrent diseases, treatments received within the previous 2 weeks, clinical signs at presentation, duration of clinical signs, diagnostic findings, medical management before and after surgery, type of surgical procedure and gross findings at time of surgery, location and number of ulcers, histopathological examination, postoperative complications, duration of hospitalisation and outcomes. The final outcome was obtained from contemporaneous referral centre and primary care clinical records. When this was not available, owners were contacted using a standardised telephone script and questionnaire. Adverse events, complications and major reporting time frames were defined using the proposed terminology by Follette et al. 17 In addition, postoperative complications were further divided into transient, if they resolved, and permanent, if they persisted until death or last follow-up. Short-term complications were defined as those occurring during the first 14 days postoperatively, while long-term complications were those occurring after 15 days postoperatively. The complications’ severity was defined according to the Accordion Severity Classification of Postoperative Complications: Contracted Classification, 18 which is based on the Clavien–Dindo grading system. 19 Abridged details of the classification system are provided in Table S1 in the supplementary material.

All surgical procedures were performed either by a board-certified surgeon or by a European College of Veterinary Surgery resident under direct supervision. Histopathology samples were reviewed by a board-certified pathologist or by a European College of Veterinary Pathology resident under direct supervision.

Results

The medical records of 16 cats with full-thickness GI perforation that had received corticosteroids or NSAIDS within the preceding 2 weeks were identified and reviewed. Six cats were excluded because of underlying GI neoplasia (n = 3), incomplete medical records (n = 1), necroulcerative and lymphoplasmacytic duodenitis (n = 1) and foreign body removal (n = 1). A total of 10 cats met the inclusion criteria for this study. Four male (all neutered) and six female (all neutered) cats were identified. The median age was 7 years (range 4–14). The median body weight was 3.95 kg (range 2.3–7.25) and median body condition score was 4/9 (range 3–7). Breeds represented were domestic shorthair (n = 5), Maine Coon (n = 2), Persian (n = 2) and British Shorthair (n = 1).

Clinical signs reported at presentation included anorexia (n = 6/10), vomiting (n = 6/10), lethargy (n = 3/10), pale mucous membranes (n = 3/10), abdominal distention (n = 3/10), abdominal pain (n = 2/10), pyrexia (n = 2/10), melena (n = 1/10), diarrhoea (n = 1/10), hematemesis (n = 1/10) and tremors (n = 1/10).

All the cats included in the study had received either NSAIDs, corticosteroids or both. Five cats were treated with NSAIDs (meloxicam in all cases) for concurrent medical conditions, including cystitis (n = 2), soft tissue injury (n = 1), orthopaedic condition (n = 1) and undocumented indication (n = 1). Corticosteroids were prescribed in three cats for immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia (n = 1), chronic eosinophilic granulomas (n = 1) and chronic enteropathy (n = 1). Two cats received both NSAIDs and corticosteroids concomitantly before referral. The coadministration was intentional and lasted for 3 and 14 days, respectively. Indications for combined therapy included chronic pododermatitis (n = 1) and trauma after a road traffic accident (n = 1). No other medications were administered, aside from one cat that was prescribed gabapentin and fusidic acid along with concomitant used of NSAIDs and oral prednisolone because of chronic pododermatitis.

NSAID and steroid doses were specified and within the recommended therapeutic range in five cats. For the remaining five cases, doses were reported to be within the recommended therapeutic ranges, but the exact dosage was not specified.

The median age of cats receiving corticosteroids alone was 9 years (range 4–14), compared with 6 years (range 5–11) in those receiving NSAIDs alone and 7 years (range 4–9) in cats receiving both medications.

Haematological and complete biochemical analyses were performed in all patients. The most common abnormalities were as follows: regenerative anaemia (n = 5/10), which was mild in two cats, moderate in one cat and severe in two cats; lymphopenia with neutrophilia (n = 4/10), which was mild in two cats and moderate in two cats; leucocytosis with mild neutrophilia but no lymphocyte changes in one cat; and mild thrombocytopenia (n = 3/10). Complete biochemical analysis revealed the following: hypoalbuminaemia (n = 4/10), which was mild in three cats and severe in one cat; mild hypocalcaemia (n = 2/10); mild hyponatraemia (n = 2/10); mild hypokalaemia (n = 2/10); mild increased creatin kinase (n = 2/10); mild hypoglycaemia (n = 2/10); mild hyperglycaemia (n = 1/10); mild hyperbilirubinaemia (n = 1/10); mild elevation in urea without concurrent creatinine changes (n = 2/10); severe azotaemia (n = 1/10); and mild hyperlactataemia (n = 2/10).

All cats underwent diagnostic imaging, which included ultrasonography, radiography or CT, selected based on individual case requirements. Radiographs were performed in 3/10 cats. Abdominal radiographs were obtained in one cat, which was the only imaging modality performed, and revealed pneumoperitoneum (n = 1). Two cats had thoracic radiographs performed, which did not reveal abnormalities.

Abdominal ultrasound was performed in seven cats. The main abnormalities included gallbladder sediment (n = 5/7), free abdominal fluid (n = 4/7), lymphadenomegaly (n = 3/7), generalised loss of layering and mural thickening of the small intestine (n = 2/7), gastric dilation (n = 2/7), irregular gallbladder wall (n = 2/7), pneumoperitoneum (n = 1/7), common bile duct dilation (n = 1/7) and thickening of the gastric wall (n = 1/7). Ulceration was identified by ultrasonography in two cats. Four cats had changes suggestive of peritonitis, which was confirmed with abdominocentesis and cytological changes suggestive of septic peritonitis. Bacteria isolated from abdominocentesis included Escherichia coli (n = 1) and Pasteurella species (n = 1), which were sensitive to various antibiotics, including penicillin and fluoroquinolones.

Abdominal CT was performed in one cat, in which no other imaging modality was performed, and revealed moderate dilation of the common bile duct, moderate lymphadenopathy and a moderate volume of free abdominal fluid. Cytology of the free abdominal fluid was suggestive of septic peritonitis.

A definitive diagnosis of a full-thickness GI ulcer perforation was made at the time of surgery in all cats.

Location of the ulcer included the proximal duodenum (n = 6/10), gastric body (n = 2/10), pylorus (n = 1/10) and gastric fundus (n = 1/10). All the patients had only one full-thickness GI ulcer identified. Cats receiving prednisolone alone had ulcers identified in the gastric body (n = 1/3), gastric fundus (n = 1/3) and duodenum (n = 1/3); cats receiving meloxicam alone had ulcers identified in the duodenum (n = 4/5) and gastric body (n = 1/5); and cats receiving both prednisolone and meloxicam had ulcers identified in the pylorus (n = 1/2) and duodenum (n = 1/2).

Various medical interventions were instituted before and after diagnosis of the perforation, which included intravenous fluid therapy, analgesia, empirical antimicrobials, antimicrobials based on culture and sensitivity, GI cytoprotectants and antiemetics.

The surgical technique performed was based on the surgeon’s preference and findings at surgery. These comprised primary repair with debridement followed by augmentation with an omental patch (n = 5/10), Y-U pyloroplasty (n = 2/10), duodenal enterectomy with functional end-to-end stapled anastomosis (n = 1/10), duodenal enterectomy with hand-sewn anastomosis (n = 1/10) and partial gastrectomy with resection of the gastric ulcerated area using a linear gastrointestinal anastomosis stapler, which was then oversewn with polydioxanone (n = 1/10). Median surgery time was 105 mins (range 50–125).

Additional surgical procedures included feeding tube placement in all cats and abdominal active drains placement in two cats (Jackson Pratt type in one cat and not specified in the other cat). An oesophagostomy tube was placed in 7/10 cats, gastrostomy tube in 1/10, a nasogastric tube in 1/10 and a jejunostomy tube in 1/10 cats. Postoperative thoracic radiographs to check feeding tube positioning were obtained in 4/10 cats. The main abnormalities in these images included pleural effusion (n = 2), small volume pneumothorax (n = 1), and peritoneal effusion and gas consistent with the recent surgery (n = 1).

Histopathological examination of the ulcer was performed in 6/10 cats, which confirmed perforation due to ulceration with no evidence of neoplasia. One of the gastric ulcers was associated with severe, chronic, focally extensive fibrinosuppurative and necrotising gastric serositis with transmural granulation tissue. No other additional histopathological findings were reported in the remaining five cases.

Median follow-up was 928 days (range 9–1215). One cat did not survive to discharge. The median time to discharge was 6 days (range 3–15).

Anaesthesia-related adverse events occurred in four cats: hypothermia in two cats, concurrent hypothermia and hypotension in one cat, and hypotension alone in one cat. All events resolved during the procedure. Hypothermia was managed with active warming (n = 3). One cat with hypotension was treated with a single intravenous bolus of epinephrine (0.1 mg/kg), while information regarding the intervention for hypotension in the remaining cat was not available. No intraoperative surgical complications or adverse events were reported.

Short-term postoperative complications were observed in three cats, comprising one death and three mild complications, with two mild complications occurring in the same cat. The mild complications included swelling of the oesophagostomy tube site in one cat, vomiting that resolved with antiemetics and one superficial surgical site dehiscence that healed by second intention without requiring intervention. The fatal complication consisted of a cat that developed sepsis 9 days postoperatively resulting in death. The remaining 9/10 cats survived the short-term postoperative period.

Long-term complications were noted in 1/9 cats, which was a fatal complication. This cat presented 16 days postoperatively with lethargy and vomiting. Abdominal ultrasonography detected free abdominal fluid, compatible with neutrophilic inflammation, and the patient was euthanased because of severe segmental ulcerative duodenopathy.

At the end of the study period, five cats were alive. Three cats died of causes unrelated to GI ulceration. In total, two cats died from causes related to ulcer perforation at 9 and 16 days after surgery, respectively.

Discussion

The results of this retrospective descriptive study suggest that prognosis and outcome of surgically treated GI ulcers in cats associated with NSAIDs and/or steroids is favourable. Overall, 9/10 cats survived to discharge, with eight surviving long term.

The incidence of GI ulceration due to NSAIDs and steroids appears to be very low in cats based on the current study. The affected population was primarily middle-aged to older cats, consistent with the previous literature.5,6,8 This may reflect increased susceptibility in older cats due to age-related decline in mucosal defence mechanisms and the presence of comorbidities, or more likely, reflect the increased frequency of NSAID and steroid administration in older patients.

In human medicine, aside from underlying GI neoplasia, the primary risk factors for corticosteroid-induced gastric ulceration include concurrent NSAID use and advanced age. 9 In dogs, the combination of NSAIDs and corticosteroids appears to enhance the risk of gastric ulceration more than the administration of either NSAIDs or corticosteroids alone, although this synergistic effect is not fully understood. 2 In the present study, 2/10 cats had received both corticosteroids and NSAIDs before the identification of ulceration, which precluded meaningful statistical analysis to establish a correlation. It is generally accepted that ulceration is more likely when NSAIDs are used inappropriately; however, ulceration can still occur despite adherence to recommended dosing protocols. 6 In the present study, all NSAID and corticosteroid doses were administered according to standard guidelines, highlighting the need for continued vigilance even with appropriate use.

Unfortunately, owing to the low incidence of GI ulceration in cats caused by anti-inflammatory medication, case numbers were inadequate to perform any meaningful statistical analysis to identify risk factors associated with signalment.

Clinical signs associated with gastroduodenal ulceration in cats are often vague and non-specific, complicating early diagnosis. In our study, the clinical presentations were consistent with previous reports, with vomiting and anorexia being the most common signs. GI bleeding, when present, was typically subtle, with haematemesis and melaena infrequently observed, further supporting the notion that blood loss in feline GI ulcerations may often go undetected unless specifically investigated.1,6,20 Regenerative anaemia was the most common haematological abnormality in this study, likely due to chronic blood loss from ulceration or intra-abdominal bleeding. These findings emphasise the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for GI ulceration in cats receiving NSAIDs and corticosteroids, particularly when presenting with non-specific GI or systemic signs. Early diagnostic investigation, including imaging and laboratory testing, may aid in timely identification and management before life-threatening complications occur, such as perforation or peritonitis.

Readily available imaging techniques to detect GI ulceration include radiography and ultrasonography from which pneumoperitoneum, GI wall layering abnormalities or free abdominal fluid can be common findings; however, it is important to note that the presence of free fluid is not pathognomonic of perforation and that omental adhesions can mask perforations leading to minimal or absent peritoneal fluid despite severe pathology. Therefore, the imaging findings should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical signs and cytological evaluation of peritoneal fluid where available. The identification of intracellular bacteria in peritoneal samples, although not specific, can support a diagnosis of perforation and guide the decision towards surgical intervention.

Prompt surgical intervention should be considered when the index of suspicion for GI ulceration is high. Studies in humans have shown that delaying surgery beyond 12 h after the onset of clinical symptoms worsens the outcome in cases of ulcer perforation when surgery is indicated. 21 Surgery allows definitive diagnosis and repair, as well as allowing samples to be obtained for histopathological analysis to screen for neoplasia, which is a more frequent aetiology for ulceration. The authors recommend obtaining biopsies during surgical intervention whenever possible to facilitate accurate diagnosis and guide subsequent clinical management. In the present study, all ulcers were confirmed and identified visually during surgery; however, only six were sampled to rule out neoplasia. As multiple lesions are common in both neoplastic and non-neoplastic GI ulcers, a thorough examination of the GI tract with special attention to the stomach and the duodenum is essential during the surgical procedure. 1 Interestingly, a single full-thickness ulcer was identified in all cases in this study.

Comparing our study to what has been reported in dogs, nine (90%) cats survived to hospital discharge, compared with 8/11 (73%) dogs in a study for surgical repair of NSAIDs and/or corticosteroid-associated full-thickness GI ulcers. 13 Short-term postoperative complications were identified in 3/10 (30%) cats in the present study, with 1/10 (10%) resulting in death and two (20%) cats suffering from mild complications. In contrast, the canine study reported a higher overall rate of short-term complications, with 8/11 (72%) dogs affected. Among these, three (27%) dogs experienced death, including septic peritonitis, dehiscence and aspiration pneumonia, which led to euthanasia. Long-term complications were less frequently observed in cats (n = 1, 10%), while the canine study reported long-term complications in 2/11 (18%) dogs, which were all mild complications. In total, ulcer-related mortality over the entire study period was similar in both groups, affecting 20% of cats and 27% of dogs. 13 These findings must be interpreted with caution because of the small case numbers in both studies.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective, multicentric nature of the study, meaning investigations, operative techniques, perioperative management and follow-up protocols were not standardised. Nevertheless, this variability may better reflect the realities of routine clinical practice and the progression of diagnostic techniques and treatment over the last 15 years. An additional limitation was the absence of histopathological samples in four cats precluding definitive exclusion of an underlying neoplastic process as the aetiology of the ulcer, although the intraoperative findings and the clinical presentation strongly supported the diagnosis of medication-induced ulceration. The location of GI perforation has not been shown to influence prognosis in previous studies 5 ; the small case numbers included in this study precluded statistical analysis. Although only a small number of cases were included, cases were recruited from five large referral hospitals over a 15-year period, which highlights the rarity of this condition. Further investigations are warranted to determine whether specific ulcer locations correlate with clinical severity or outcomes in feline patients and if surgical technique affects outcome.

Conclusions

Clinical signs of NSAID- and/or steroid-associated GI perforations are often non-specific, making early diagnosis challenging. Surgical intervention provides definitive diagnosis and allows treatment resulting in favourable outcomes (8/10 achieved long-term survival). However, the risk of postoperative mortality and/or complications remains significant.
