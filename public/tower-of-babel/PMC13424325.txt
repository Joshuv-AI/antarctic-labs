Rapid and quantitative measurement of bacteriophage infectivity via fully automated droplet digital PCR
Authors: Yanfei Liu, Huiwei Zhao, Xinyue Cao, Shize Jiang, Jun Fu, Junjing Xue, Caiming Li, Zhangrun Xu, Ming Li, Wenbin Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical translation of phage therapy for multidrug-resistant infections is constrained by the lack of rapid, standardized therapeutic phage selection. Here, we introduce digital phage susceptibility testing (dPhaST), an automated droplet digital PCR workflow that quantifies phage-induced DNA release as a molecular signature of lysis. By targeting conserved 16S rRNA regions, dPhaST measures lytic activity across diverse bacterial pathogens within 3 h. Across 122 phage–host combinations involving 19 bacterial strains from six species, dPhaST shows 95.9% concordance with spot tests while resolving weak and heterogeneous lytic activities that are not readily distinguished phenotypically. It remains robust during the early infection window despite phage-encoded nuclease activity and tolerates phage cross-contamination better than spot tests. The method captures defense-mediated interactions involving CRISPR–Cas and Sir2-HerA systems. In this work, we show that automated digital quantification enables rapid and mechanistically informative profiling of early phage lytic efficacy across Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a critical threat to global public health. Currently, AMR infections cause over 1.2 million deaths annually, with deaths projected to rise to 10 million per year by 20501–3. This crisis overwhelms healthcare systems and imposes a massive global economic burden1. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria4, including members of the ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species)5–7, further exacerbate this risk by rendering common antibiotics ineffective. In response, bacteriophages have re-emerged as a promising therapeutic alternative4. In the past decade, phage therapy has transitioned from anecdotal case reports to controlled clinical trials, where it has shown promise in limiting resistance development and treating drug-resistant infections8. Notable applications include treating stubborn mycobacterial infections9,10, S. aureus endocarditis, and device-related P. aeruginosa cases4,11. In parallel, ongoing trials in Europe and the United States, backed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Transatlantic Taskforce on Antimicrobial Resistance (TATFAR), highlight the expanding role of phage therapy, bolstered by innovations such as machine-learning-based phage cocktail design and the development of anti-biofilm phage formulations12–14.

Despite these advances, the lack of fast, scalable, and standardized phage susceptibility testing (PST) is a major impediment to its daily use in clinics15–17. The gold-standard plaque assay relies on the observation of visible plaque formation on a bacterial lawn following overnight or longer incubation (18–48 h), which is laborious, difficult to automate, and lags behind time-critical treatment decision-making8. Although the spot test has higher throughput, it is inherently qualitative and highly prone to false positives due to trace phage cross-contamination. This problem becomes critical during the high-throughput screening of large phage libraries for personalized therapy, where false positives can lead to the selection of ineffective candidates and treatment failures. Notable developments to address these challenges include monitoring phage-induced lysis using ATP bioluminescence or fluorescent DNA dyes18,19, high-resolution phage adsorption imaging20, and microfluidic SlipChip devices21. Among these, ATP-based assays provide a particularly attractive strategy because they enable rapid detection of phage-induced lysis through a simple biochemical readout19. However, significant limitations persist, as these methods can be influenced by background signals from bacterial autolysis, depend on transient biochemical states, and may require specialized workflows, instrumentation, or assay optimization, limiting their robustness and broader clinical deployment16,22. Consequently, developing a rapid, accessible, and quantitative method that directly measures phage-induced lysis is needed to support same-day personalized treatment and advance the widespread clinical adoption of phage therapy.

Here, we show digital phage susceptibility testing (dPhaST), an automated droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) workflow for high-throughput absolute quantification of phage lytic activity in standard microplates within 3 h. Rather than monitoring bacterial growth or indirect metabolic signals, the assay directly measures the release of host 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes into the culture supernatant as a quantitative molecular signature of phage-induced lysis. We integrated this assay with our fully automated OsciDrop system23,24 and used universal primers targeting the conserved region of the 16S rRNA to ensure standardized, species-independent detection of bacterial pathogens. Our results demonstrate that dPhaST achieves >95% concordance with the spot test while offering superior reproducibility and inherent robustness against low-level cross-contamination. By delivering quantitative results and revealing infection outcomes obscured by traditional plaque assays, dPhaST effectively redefines the assessment of phage susceptibility in complex clinical contexts.

Results

Design of the dPhaST workflow

The selection of lytic phages for personalized phage therapy in the clinic is currently limited by a lack of rapid, scalable, and standardized assays to screen phage-mediated bacterial lysis. The conventional “gold-standard” agar overlay plaque assay remains the most widely used method for measuring phage lytic activity or infectivity, yet it is poorly compatible with a modern diagnostic laboratory centered on automation, standardization, and rapid turnaround. The workflow is labor-intensive: host bacteria must be cultivated to a defined optical density (OD600), mixed with soft agar, and poured as uniform overlays. These plates then require a short setting period for the soft-agar overlay to solidify, followed by an incubation of 18–48 h to visualize plaques. Although the spot test improves throughput, it remains largely qualitative, offering limited resolution for ranking lytic potency. These limitations are particularly consequential in personalized phage therapy, where inaccurate prioritization may lead to ineffective candidate selection22.

To overcome such operational barriers, we developed the dPhaST workflow, which shifts the paradigm from growth-dependent observation to quantitative molecular measurement (Fig. 1). Unlike the open-plate method, dPhaST utilizes a closed, liquid-phase format optimized for automation on standard microplates. In this workflow, MDR isolates and phage preparations are co-incubated in standard 96-well plates for a brief infection window (30–60 min). A short centrifugation step then pellets the intact cells. The resulting supernatant, containing bacterial genomic DNA released upon lysis, is analyzed using the automated, chip-free OsciDrop ddPCR system24. The ddPCR platform integrates precise liquid handling, pipette-based droplet printing, in situ thermocycling, and multicolor fluorescence imaging to automatically partition samples into nanoliter droplets for absolute quantification. Using universal primers and TaqMan probes targeting conserved regions of 16S rRNA, the assay converts the biological endpoint of bacterial lysis into a standardized, species-agnostic molecular signal. A universal digital PCR assay resolves dynamic phage lysis

We designed a universal ddPCR assay using a degenerate primer–probe set targeting conserved regions of 16S rRNA (Fig. 2a). This approach enables species-agnostic measurement of phage-induced bacterial lysis, yielding a standardized readout applicable across diverse pathogens. In validation tests using Escherichia coli (E. coli) as a representative host, the assay delivered clear droplet separation (Fig. 2b) and consistent fluorescence distributions throughout serial dilutions (Supplementary Fig. 1a) with good linearity between dilution factor and measured 16S rRNA gene copy numbers (R2 = 0.99, Fig. 2c), enabling robust absolute quantification with no requirement for optimizing assays specific to each clinical isolate. Notably, we observed this linear dynamic performance across all tested reference strains, with correlations ranging from R2 = 0.93–0.99 (Supplementary Fig. 1b–h). Furthermore, control measurements of phage stocks across a range of titers confirmed that residual host-derived DNA in purified phage preparations remained low at commonly used working concentrations and was substantially lower than the DNA release signals observed during productive infection (Supplementary Fig. 2). Using this validated platform, we next sought to define an appropriate multiplicity of infection (MOI) for dPhaST. Infection of E. coli MG1655 with phage λ cI857 at 37 °C showed that an MOI of 1 generated the maximal detectable DNA release after 1 h of shaking, whereas both lower (0.1) and higher (10 and 100) MOIs yielded reduced signals (Fig. 2d). A similar MOI-dependent pattern was observed across additional pathogens, including A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, E. faecalis, and P. aeruginosa (Supplementary Fig. 3). In most cases, the maximal signal was observed at MOI = 1, except for S. aureus, where the signal peaked at MOI = 5. These results support the use of a low-to-moderate MOI range for standardized early lysis detection across diverse bacterial species. We speculate that high MOIs (10 and 100) may rapidly suppress net bacterial growth and, in some settings, induce non-productive killing effects, whereas an MOI near 1 preserves a broader dynamic range for detecting early lysis-associated DNA release. In this respect, dPhaST differs from conventional plaque- or spot-based assays, in which higher phage input can sometimes generate more visible lysis zones through lysis from without and thereby complicate interpretation of productive infectivity22.

Time-course measurements further highlighted the assay’s ability to resolve distinct lysis dynamics. For instance, the model phage λ cI857 triggered rapid DNA release, with a strong signal already evident by 30 min and a plateau reached by 60 min (Fig. 2e). Conversely, pEcN-W26 exhibited a more gradual onset, showing a continuous increase in signal over 90 min (Fig. 2f). Phage p25005 displayed the slowest progression, with significant DNA release appearing only after 1 h and accumulation continuing up to 3 h (Fig. 2g). These quantitative profiles clearly demonstrate that ddPCR can provide critical information on lytic kinetics that is often missed by traditional endpoint plaque assays. Based on these dynamics, and to maintain a rapid screening workflow, we standardized the bacterial–phage co-incubation period to 1 h for subsequent ddPCR analysis.

Because some phages encode nucleases that degrade the bacterial genome to provide building blocks for their own DNA replication, we asked whether such viral nuclease-induced genome degradation has evident effects on dPhaST readouts. We selected the well-studied T4 for this evaluation, as this phage encodes an Endo II endonuclease that plays a critical role in initiating host genome degradation—a process that subsequently involves multiple nucleases25–27. In our recent study, we constructed an Endo II-deficient mutant, designated EM4, and confirmed that this mutant is incapable of degrading the host genome28. Notably, dPhaST did not reveal detectable differences in the DNA signals released from E. coli cells infected with wild-type T4 versus EM4 at various time points across the infection process (0–75 min post-infection; Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that viral nuclease-induced degradation of the bacterial genome may be partial and have little influence on dPhaST readouts.

To confirm that our approach is clinically relevant, we assessed whether our assay accurately quantifies 16S rRNA copies from a range of clinical isolates. We observed consistent linear correlations between serial dilution factors and measured copy numbers across multiple clinical MDR isolates, including E. coli, A. baumannii, and K. pneumoniae (Spearman correlation r = 0.97–0.98, p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these data indicate that our universal ddPCR assay represents a rapid, sensitive, and widely applicable tool to monitor phage-mediated bacterial lysis and can be used to identify the optimal infectivity parameters and differentially classify various lytic patterns.

Benchmarking dPhaST against phenotypic assays

We next benchmarked the dPhaST workflow against three conventional phage infectivity assays: the gold-standard plaque assay, the spot test, and OD600 reduction kinetics. We used a diverse panel of phages targeting E. coli MG1655 for this comparison (Fig. 3). While every method successfully identified the highly potent phage λ cI857, significant performance gaps appeared when characterizing phages with moderate or weak lytic activity. The plaque assay confirmed λ cI857 as the top candidate, yielding large and transparent plaques. However, the method had limited ability to resolve differences among the moderately lytic phages (p240069, p25005, pEcN-W26, and pEcN-W22). These phages produced similar plaques that were difficult to distinguish by morphology alone. Furthermore, at lower titers, these plaques appeared as random, indistinct spots (Supplementary Fig. 6a), making manual enumeration subjective and prone to error. Similarly, growth curves based on OD600 provided limited resolution. While strong lysis of λ cI857 was easy to observe, the kinetics for the moderately lytic phages were clustered together and indistinguishable from each other. This lack of resolution stems from the inherent limitations of turbidity measurements: OD600 readouts are inaccurate at low cell densities (<0.1) and very sensitive to the host’s metabolic status and culture medium, requiring prolonged incubation (Supplementary Fig. 6b), which limits screening efficiency and may prevent discrimination of the most effective phage in a library.

In contrast, dPhaST provided absolute, high-resolution quantification of the lytic activity in 3 h. The assay detected early DNA release in all five lytic phages, with λ cI857 showing a ~30-fold increase in signal. Importantly, dPhaST revealed small but significant differences in the behavior of moderately lytic phages (e.g., distinguishing pEcN-W26 from pEcN-W22) that could not be captured using EOP or OD measurements. A composite heatmap integrating these datasets confirms that although dPhaST is correlated with the EOP trend, its dynamic range is much larger (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 7). Because the assay converts the number of lytic events into absolute copy numbers instead of an arbitrary visual phenotype, dPhaST circumvents both the resolution limit of plaque assays and the low sensitivity of turbidimetry, making it an attractive method to screen phage libraries quickly and accurately.

Robustness of dPhaST against phage cross-contamination

High-throughput phage screening is susceptible to low-level cross-contamination, a practical issue that can produce misleading positive readouts in conventional phenotypic assays29. This problem is particularly challenging to address in spot tests because if more than one phage species is present in a sample, a positive clearing phenotype will be generated as long as one phage species can infect the host16. To evaluate whether dPhaST is more robust to this practical challenge, we established a non-permissive infection model using E. coli MG1655 and the non-infecting phage PhiX174. To simulate contamination events, we spiked this system with varying proportions (0.1–20%) of the infecting phages pEcN-W26 or p25005 and quantified DNA release after a 1-h co-incubation.

For phage pEcN-W26 contamination in PhiX174, dPhaST quantification remained stable and statistically indistinguishable from the negative control at contamination ratios ≤ 1% (P > 0.05, Fig. 4a). Significant signal elevation was only observed when contamination reached ≥ 5% (P < 0.0001). Conversely, parallel spot tests produced visible clearing zones at contamination levels as low as 0.1%, indicating that conventional spot-based phenotypic readouts are highly susceptible to trace cross-contamination. This intrinsic robustness stems from the fundamental difference in detection kinetics. Conventional spot tests require overnight incubation, giving even a single contaminating virion time to replicate exponentially and form a visible lysis zone. Conversely, dPhaST captures an absolute measure of genomic DNA release within a tight 1-h window. This short duration prevents the cascading amplification of low-abundance contaminants, effectively creating a “tolerance threshold” that filters out trace background noise while maintaining sensitivity for true infections. Notably, this threshold was inversely related to lytic potency: the more active phage pEcN-W26 triggered a signal at 5% contamination, while the less potent p25005 required 10% contamination to exceed baseline. This distinction reinforces our findings of pEcN-W26’s superior lytic activity (Fig. 3). Together, these results confirm that dPhaST offers a contamination-resistant platform that significantly outperforms traditional methods for high-throughput library screening.

Clinical phage selection by dPhaST

To validate the utility of dPhaST for phage selection, we profiled 122 phage–host combinations spanning six bacterial species and 33 phages. This panel comprised clinical MDR isolates together with reference host strains, including E. coli MG1655 and DSM 13127, resulting in 19 host strains in total. As outlined in the workflow schematic (Fig. 5a), phage libraries were screened against these bacterial strains, and the resulting dPhaST measurements were compared with conventional spot tests to enable prioritization of candidate therapeutic phages. We first defined a quantitative threshold for susceptibility using ROC curve analysis, with the spot test as the reference standard (Fig. 5b). We identified a dPhaST fold-change cutoff of 1.17 as the optimal threshold for distinguishing sensitive from resistant interactions, yielding an AUC of 0.961. Comparison of the two methods using a consistency matrix revealed a high overall concordance of 95.90% using this threshold (Fig. 5c). Negative concordance was 98.53%, as fold changes ≤ 1.17 consistently aligned with the absence of lysis zone. Conversely, the positive concordance was 92.59%; this slight divergence reflects the more stringent quantitative threshold used by dPhaST, which correctly filters out interactions that may produce faint, ambiguous clearing in spot tests but lack the significant genomic DNA release indicative of productive lysis30. Because dPhaST classification is based on fold change calculated within each strain relative to its corresponding uninfected control, interspecies variation in genomic 16S rRNA operon copy number affects only the absolute baseline signal and does not influence susceptibility determination. Thus, the ROC-derived cutoff reflects strain-normalized DNA release rather than cross-species absolute quantification.

Applying this validated threshold across clinical panels revealed that dPhaST resolves a continuous spectrum of lytic potency that is largely obscured by the discrete nature of spot tests (Fig. 5d–i). While spot tests generally categorize strains as “positive” or “negative” based on lysis-zone size, dPhaST revealed a broad, dynamic spectrum of susceptibility. For example, in profiling eight phages against five E. coli strains, we observed fold changes ranging from ~40 (indicative of highly potent lysis) to 1.17 (near the susceptibility threshold; Fig. 5d, Supplementary Figs. 8–11). Similarly, the interaction matrix for K. pneumoniae displayed a broad dynamic range, spanning highly productive interactions (>20-fold) to weak or negative outcomes (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Figs. 12–15). In A. baumannii, dPhaST distinguished moderate lytic candidates from weaker interactions that would be difficult to prioritize confidently on the basis of lysis-zone appearance alone (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Figs. 16–19).

Importantly, the expanded validation further demonstrated that this quantitative behavior is not restricted to the Gram-negative panels. In S. aureus, dPhaST differentiated relatively weak and strong lytic interactions within a narrow but still resolvable activity range (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Figs. 20, 21). In E. faecalis, the method similarly distinguished productive from weak or negative phage responses across the tested strain–phage pairs (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Figs. 22, 23). In P. aeruginosa, a clinically important Gram-negative and biofilm-forming pathogen often associated with difficult-to-treat infections, dPhaST identified marked differences in lytic efficacy among phages that would otherwise be interpreted more qualitatively by spot testing (Fig. 5i, Supplementary Figs. 24, 25). Together, these additional datasets extend the validation to both Gram-positive and Gram-negative clinical isolates and strengthen support for the broader applicability of dPhaST across diverse clinically relevant pathogens.

Crucially, dPhaST provided meaningful stratification within the “positive” category defined by spot tests. Phages producing comparable lysis-zone sizes on MDR isolates often exhibited substantially different DNA release profiles, indicating distinct levels of lytic efficacy. This added resolution is vital for prioritizing highly lytic phages over those with only moderate efficacy, which is important when constructing a treatment cocktail. Collectively, these results show that while dPhaST replicates the binary outcomes of conventional screening, its quantitative resolution enables more precise ranking and selection of optimal therapeutic phages.

dPhaST assessment of anti-phage defense systems

Bacterial defense against phage infection generally falls into two distinct categories: individual-level immunity, where the cell targets viral components to ensure its survival, and population-level immunity, often termed abortive infection (Abi)31–35, where infected cells undergo programmed death to limit viral propagation and protect the surrounding population (Fig. 6a). To evaluate the utility of dPhaST in characterizing these diverse mechanisms, we selected E. coli BW25113 engineered with two representative defense systems: the Sir2-HerA system36,37, a recently identified Abi mechanism, and the CRISPR–Cas system38–42. For the Sir2-HerA system, the wild-type operon profoundly suppressed bacterial lysis. DNA release remained at basal levels across all tested MOIs for phages T4 and T6, providing readouts indistinguishable from those of uninfected controls (Fig. 6b, c). Conversely, the Sir2-HerA mutant strain (containing a start codon mutation in sir2) displayed a steep, MOI-dependent surge in DNA release, indicating unrestricted lysis. These profiles are consistent with plaque assays, which displayed clear lysis zones for the mutant but complete inhibition for the wild type.

Furthermore, dPhaST revealed critical divergences in defense dynamics that the plaque assay obscures. When the E. coli BW25113 strain carrying the CRISPR–Cas system was challenged with T3 and T7, no lysis zone was observed in the plaque assay for either phage. However, dPhaST revealed distinct quantitative behaviors at MOI = 1: while T7 induced near-baseline DNA release, phage T3 triggered a significant rise in host DNA release despite the absence of plaque formation (Fig. 6d, e). This discrepancy suggests that while the CRISPR system successfully prevented lysis-zone formation in both cases, the defense against T3 involved significantly more host lysis than that against T7. Hence, dPhaST offers a unique capacity to quantify the robustness of immune barriers under specific viral loads, providing mechanistic insights into phage-host arms races that are unattainable with binary phenotypic assays.

Discussion

Phage therapy represents a pivotal frontier against the escalating crisis of MDR infections. However, its transition from the laboratory to the clinic is partially stalled by a lack of rapid, high-throughput, and precise methods to evaluate lytic activity. Current infectivity assays suffer from significant drawbacks: growth-based phenotypic assays, such as plaque assays and growth curves, are inherently slow and labor-intensive. By contrast, biochemical readouts such as ATP release offer a rapid and convenient means to detect phage-induced lysis19, but because they depend on transient metabolic signals, they may be more sensitive to inaccurate assay timing and varying physiological states. dPhaST complements these approaches by measuring host DNA release as a stable molecular endpoint, thereby enabling absolute quantification and more standardized comparison across phage–host pairs.

To address these critical diagnostic gaps, we developed dPhaST to precisely measure the kinetics and magnitude of host cell lysis by quantifying the absolute amount of host genomic DNA released. dPhaST captures the essential stages of phage infection and lysis, offering distinct advantages over traditional phenotypic methods: 1) faster turnaround and automation readiness, utilizing standard 96-well plates and pipetting interfaces to enable a fully automated, hands-free workflow from co-culture to digital quantification using the OsciDrop ddPCR system24—a chip-free platform developed by our group that utilizes standard pipette tips and liquid handling for seamless automation; 2) high sensitivity and robustness, reliably detecting low-frequency infections while exhibiting intrinsic tolerance to phage cross-contamination; and 3) standardized quantification, ensuring the reproducibility required for high-throughput screening. Furthermore, by targeting conserved regions of the 16S rRNA gene, the platform provides a universal, species-independent readout applicable to a broad range of pathogens, including representative ESKAPE pathogens, without requiring host-specific optimization.

Beyond diagnostics, dPhaST bridges clinical screening with mechanistic insights by shifting the evaluation focus from long-term lysis-zone development to immediate lytic efficacy. While conventional phenotypic assays primarily measure bacterial inhibition or phage amplification over extended incubation periods, dPhaST measures the rate of phage-mediated lysis in a clinically relevant short time window. In this sense, dPhaST is intended as an early-response assay, whereas longer-term outcomes such as regrowth or heteroresistant outgrowth should be assessed using complementary approaches when relevant. This capacity is particularly vital in critical-care settings that require rapid intervention, such as intraoperative phage application during debridement for osteomyelitis or arthritis, where efficacy depends on effective bacterial lysis within a limited timeframe (e.g., 40–60 min) rather than multi-cycle propagation43,44. Furthermore, by directly quantifying infection-associated molecular consequences instead of downstream phage proliferation, dPhaST reveals how host defense systems reshape, rather than simply abolish, infection outcomes, offering a functional view central to the ongoing molecular arms race45. Ultimately, the high-throughput, time-resolved nature of dPhaST establishes a robust framework for phage bioprospecting and personalized phage screening, advancing the scalability and precision of phage therapy.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The bacterial strains and clinical isolates used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 1. The ATCC reference strains were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection, the CGMCC reference strains were purchased from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center at the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IMCAS, Beijing, China), and E. coli DSM 13127 was obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ). E. coli MG1655 and E. coli DSM 13127 were used as reference strains. E. coli BW25113 strains carrying the Sir2-HerA or CRISPR–Cas system, along with the corresponding mutant strains, were constructed as previously reported. Reference strains, including Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, S. aureus ATCC 29213, K. pneumoniae ATCC 10031, A. baumannii ATCC 19606, P. aeruginosa PAO1, Enterobacter cloacae CGMCC 1.2022, Klebsiella aerogenes CGMCC 1.10716, E. coli MG1655, and E. coli DSM 13127, were used for assay validation, optimization, or benchmarking, as indicated in Supplementary Data 3.

Clinical isolates used for phage susceptibility profiling included E. coli strains Ec9-1, 25005, and EC64, kindly provided by Guangxin Luan’s laboratory at the Non-coding RNA and Drug Discovery Key Laboratory of Sichuan Province, Chengdu Medical College; K. pneumoniae strains Kpn04, Kpn05, Kpn08, and Kpn09, provided by Jie Feng’s laboratory at IMCAS; A. baumannii strains Aba03, Aba33, Aba60, and Aba111, provided by Yun Li’s laboratory at the Institute of Clinical Pharmacology, Peking University First Hospital; S. aureus strains 21014 and R5886, provided by Jingmin Gu’s laboratory at Jilin University (Changchun, China); E. faecalis strains 23002 and 23003, provided by CreatiPhage Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); and P. aeruginosa strains SPA01 and SPA02, provided by Ming Li’s laboratory at IMCAS.

All bacterial strains were cultured at 37 °C in lysogeny broth (LB) medium. Solid plates were prepared using LB medium supplemented with 1.5% agar, while liquid cultures were grown with agitation at 180 rpm. All bacterial stocks were stored at −80 °C in LB medium supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol. For experimental use, single colonies were inoculated into 5 mL of LB broth and cultured overnight (16–18 h).

Phage propagation and titration

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:50 by adding 100 μL of culture to 5 mL of fresh LB medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C with agitation at 180 rpm for 2–3 h until the cultures reached the mid-exponential growth phase (OD600 ≈ 0.4–0.6). For phage propagation, 50 μL of phage stock was inoculated into the mid-exponential-phase bacterial cultures and incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 180 rpm for 6 h to facilitate phage-mediated lysis. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 3743 g for 5 min, and the supernatants were filtered through 0.22-μm pore-size membranes. The filtrates were stored at 4 °C until further use. The phage libraries used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2. An overview of the bacterial strains, phages, and their use across the different experimental modules is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Phage titers, expressed as plaque-forming units per milliliter, PFU/mL, were determined using the double-layer agar assay. Briefly, phage stocks were serially diluted 10-fold in LB broth, mixed with mid-exponential-phase host bacteria, and combined with molten top agar (0.5% agar). The mixtures were overlaid onto solidified LB agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Plaques were enumerated the following day to calculate phage titers.

Performance of the universal ddPCR assay

Genomic DNA was extracted from 1 mL of bacterial cultures in the logarithmic growth phase using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Biomed, Beijing, China). For clinical multidrug-resistant isolates (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, S. aureus, E. faecalis, and P. aeruginosa), species identity was verified by PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene using universal primers (27 F/1492 R), followed by Sanger sequencing.

For the universal bacterial detection assay, the optimal concentrations were determined to be 0.5–1.0 μM for the degenerate 16S-targeting primers (16S_856F: 5’-AAACTCAAAGGAATTGRCGG-3’; 16S_1031R: 5’-RWGGGTYKCGCTMGTT-3’) and 0.25 μM for the hydrolysis probe (16S_991P: 5’-HEX-YACGAGCTGWCGRC-MGB-3’), which were designed to ensure broad taxonomic coverage. PCR primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and TaqMan probes were obtained from Bioligo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 2× Universal dPCR Mastermix for DNA (with UDG) was sourced from Maccura Biotechnology (Chengdu, China).

To evaluate the linearity and dynamic range of the universal ddPCR assay across a broad panel of reference bacterial strains, genomic DNA samples from E. faecalis, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, E. cloacae, E. coli, and K. aerogenes were subjected to a 10-fold serial dilution series. The stock DNA samples (10 µL) were mixed with 90 µL of sterile, nuclease-free water to generate dilution series, and the diluted samples were subsequently quantified using ddPCR.

Automated digital phage susceptibility testing

We executed the dPhaST workflow using the OsciDrop ddPCR system, a fully automated, chip-free platform that consolidates droplet generation, thermal cycling, and detection into a seamless “sample-in, answer-out” process. Bacterial isolates derived from patients were grown to the logarithmic phase and then co-incubated with phage libraries in 2-mL round-bottom 96-deep-well plates (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 1 h. Afterward, we centrifuged the samples briefly (5–10 min at 2272 g) to pellet intact cells, collecting the supernatant for subsequent analysis.

The optimized ddPCR reaction mixture, comprising the supernatant, primers, probes, and Mastermix, was loaded into 8-tube strips and placed on the system deck. The OsciDrop system then performed the automated quantification process: transferring samples via positive-pressure oil displacement, generating droplets to partition DNA into 20,000 1-nL droplets, performing in situ thermal cycling, capturing multicolor fluorescence images, and executing intelligent image analysis. The automated ddPCR system processes 24 samples in under 2 h, requiring less than 5 min of hands-on time, ultimately delivering absolute and highly sensitive quantification of phage-mediated lysis.

Phage cross-contamination tolerance

To test the assay’s resilience against phage cross-contamination, we used E. coli MG1655 as the host strain and bacteriophage PhiX174 as a nonpermissive phage that does not infect MG1655. The MG1655-permissive phages pEcN-W26 and p25005 were used as contaminating phages. Reaction mixtures with a final volume of 200 µL were prepared by mixing 100 µL of mid-logarithmic-phase E. coli MG1655 culture (OD600 = 0.1; ~1 × 107 CFU/mL) with 100 µL of phage suspension containing PhiX174 (1 × 107 PFU/mL; MOI = 1) and the indicated proportion of pEcN-W26 or p25005. To simulate phage cross-contamination, the contaminating phage was included at final ratios of 0.1%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% relative to the PhiX174 titer, along with a 0% control in which the contaminating phage was omitted. After incubating at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 1 h, intact cells were removed by centrifugation at 2272 g for 5 min, and 2.5 µL of the clarified supernatant was analyzed by ddPCR. To visually assess contamination-induced apparent positive lysis, parallel spot tests were performed on bacterial lawns under the same phage-contamination conditions.

Phage-mediated lysis kinetics

Freshly subcultured bacterial suspensions were adjusted to an initial OD600 of 0.1, and phage infections were started by adding a 100-µL aliquot of phage suspension (107 PFU/mL) to achieve an MOI of 1. Assays were performed in 96-well plates with a final working volume of 200 µL/well, using three technical replicates per group and a phage-free bacterial control. The OD600 was monitored using an EnSpire Multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT). The plates were incubated at 37 °C, and prior to each measurement, cultures were shaken at 180 rpm for 30 s. Data were acquired immediately after inoculation and then at 30-min intervals for a total of 5 h.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) from at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). For dPhaST, fold change was calculated as the ddPCR-measured 16S rRNA gene copy number (copies/µL) in each phage-treated sample divided by that of its paired uninfected control. Linear regression related dilution factor to measured copy number in the universal ddPCR assay, and Spearman correlation assessed monotonic relationships where indicated. Group comparisons used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for single-factor designs (e.g., MOI-dependent DNA release) and two-way ANOVA for time-course or two-factor experiments, each followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for comparisons against a control. To classify phage susceptibility, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed with the spot test as the reference; the area under the curve (AUC) was reported, and the fold-change cutoff was defined by maximizing Youden’s index. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software).
