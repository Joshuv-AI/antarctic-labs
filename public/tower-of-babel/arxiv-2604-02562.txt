Integral bases for the second degree cohomology of 4-dimensional toric orbifolds Thanks: The authors are supported by the National Research Foundation of Korea (NRF) grant funded by the Korea government(MSIT) (RS-2025-00555914).
Tseleung So, Jongbaek Song

Abstract
. We study toric orbifolds of real dimension four with vanishing odd-degree cohomology and obtain a basis for its degree-two equivariant cohomology with integral coefficients by identifying it with the intersection of certain lattices. As applications, we provide an alternative construction of the algebraic cellular basis for integral ordinary cohomology [ FSS23 ] . In addition, when the toric orbifold is an algebraic variety, we determine its Cartier divisor group and Picard group.

1.
Introduction

The integral cohomology of a toric orbifold captures information about its singularities that is often invisible in rational cohomology. For instance, the ordinary and equivariant cohomology with rational coefficients of weighted projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\chi)
are isomorphic to those of projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. In contrast, Kawasaki
[
Kaw73
]
showed that the multiplication structure of the integral cohomology of
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\chi)
depends on its weight vector
\chi\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
, which encodes the orbifold singularities of the space. Moreover, determining generators for its integral equivariant cohomology also involves nontrivial arithmetic calculations depending on the weight vector
[
BFR09
]
. In this sense, the integral (equivariant) cohomology theory for orbifolds is considerably subtler than its rational counterpart. This problem is already non-trivial even in degree
2
, where one seeks explicit integral classes arising from the underlying combinatorial data of a toric orbifold.

For a toric orbifold
X(P,\lambda)
associated with combinatorial data
(P,\lambda)
, called a
characteristic pair
(see Section
2.1
), this problem has been partially addressed, in the case where the odd degree cohomology of
X(P,\lambda)
vanishes, by the
weighted Stanley–Reisner ring

\wSR(P,\lambda)
introduced in
[
BSS17
,
DKS22
]
. It gives an algebraic model for the integral equivariant cohomology ring of such a toric orbifold. By definition,
\wSR(P,\lambda)
is a subring of the Stanley–Reisner ring
\SR(P)
, determined by the integrality conditions (see Definitions
2.2
and
2.3
). This description of
\wSR(P,\lambda)
makes it possible to test whether a given element of
\SR(P)
belongs to
\wSR(P,\lambda)
. However, it does not in general provide explicit generators or bases, so the problem must be treated on a case-by-case basis.

In this paper we focus on the real four dimensional case.
Our main result gives a full description of the degree-two component
\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)
of the weighted Stanley–Reisner ring as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(restated in Theorem
3.5
)
.

Let
X(P,\lambda)
be a toric orbifold of real dimension four with trivial
H^{3}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
. As
\mathbb{Z}
-modules, it follows that

H^{2}_{T^{2}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\cong\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)\cong\mathbb{Z}\langle\textbf{a}\rangle\oplus\mathbb{Z}\langle\textbf{b}\rangle\oplus\phi(K),

where
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}
are vectors deduced from
\lambda
,
K
is the space of some linear relations and
\phi
is a linear map (See (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), respectively, for their explicit definitions).

The first isomorphism follows from the results of
[
BSS17
,
DKS22
]
, whereas the second isomorphism is new, which will be established in Section
3
. Here we emphasize that a basis of
K
can be obtained by some elementary methods in linear algebra, since
K
is determined by a system of linear equations over integers. Thus, Theorem
1.1
yields an explicit basis of
\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)
, and hence that of the equivariant cohomology group
H^{2}_{T^{2}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
.

Two applications of Theorem
1.1
are given in Section
4
.
First, we apply it to provide an alternative definition of the
algebraic cellular basis
of
[
FSS23
]
. This is a special additive basis for
\tilde{H}^{*}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
constructed by using methods from homotopy theory and combinatorics. Its well-definedness and existence were established through a series of long and technical proofs. Theorem
1.1
redefines the algebraic cellular basis of
X(P,\lambda)
in a more general setting and provides a simpler proof (see Theorem
4.2
).

Second, we consider the case where
X(P,\lambda)
is a toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
associated with a fan
\Sigma
. We verify that the group of torus-invariant Cartier divisors
\CDiv_{T}(X_{\Sigma})
and its Picard group
\Pic(X_{\Sigma})
are isomorphic to
\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)
and
\phi(K)
, respectively.
In general, computing
\CDiv_{T}(X_{\Sigma})
and
\Pic(X_{\Sigma})
is subtle, as it requires solving integral Cartier conditions encoded by the fan (see, for example,
[
CLS11
, Theorem 4.2.8]
or
[
Ful93
, Section 3.3]
). As another application of the main result,
we obtain a complete description of these groups and compute the index of
\Pic(X_{\Sigma})
in the class group
\clas(X_{\Sigma})
.

2.
Toric orbifolds and Weighted Stanley–Reisner rings

2.1.
Toric orbifolds

We begin with a brief summary of a toric orbifold from a constructive viewpoint. Let
P
be an
n
-dimensional simple polytope and let
\mathcal{F}(P)
denote the set of its facets. For each vertex
v
of
P
, write
\mathcal{F}_{v}(P)
for the subset of
\mathcal{F}(P)
consisting of facets that contain
v
.

A
characteristic function

\lambda\colon\mathcal{F}(P)\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a map such that

(i)

each
\lambda(F)
is primitive,

(ii)

for every vertex
v
, the set
\{\lambda(F)\mid F\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(P)\}
is linearly independent.

The pair
(P,\lambda)
is called a
characteristic pair
.

Since
P
is simple, each face
E
of codimension
k
is the intersection of exactly
k
facets, that is,
E=F_{i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap F_{i_{k}}
for some
F_{i_{1}},\ldots,F_{i_{k}}\in\mathcal{F}(P)
. This expression is unique up to permutation. Identifying
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with the standard lattice in the Lie algebra of the
n
-dimensional compact torus
T^{n}
, we denote by
T_{E}
the subgroup of
T^{n}
generated by
\{\lambda(F_{i_{j}})\mid j=1,\dots,k\}
.

We then define the
toric orbifold
associated with
(P,\lambda)
as the quotient space

(2.1)

X(P,\lambda)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}P\times T^{n}/_{\sim},

equipped with the canonical
T^{n}
-action, where
(p,t)\sim(q,s)
if and only if
p=q
and
t^{-1}s\in T_{E}
, with
E
being the unique face whose relative interior contains
p
. If, in addition, for every vertex
v
, the set
\{\lambda(F)\mid F\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(P)\}
forms an integral basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then
X(P,\lambda)
is a smooth manifold and is called a
quasitoric manifold
. Otherwise, it is an orbifold in general. For a more axiomatic definition of toric orbifolds (and quasitoric manifolds), see
[
DJ91
,
PS10
]
and
[
BP15
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

When
P
is a simple rational polytope in
M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
, where
M
is the character lattice of
T^{n}
, and
\lambda
is defined by assigning to each facet its primitive outward (or inward) normal vector, one obtains a symplectic toric orbifold or a projective toric variety with orbifold singularities (see, for example,
[
LT97
]
and
[
CLS11
, Chapters 2,3]
). It turns out that these spaces are equivariantly homeomorphic to the toric orbifold
X(P,\lambda)
defined in (
2.1
). The reader is referred to
[
Fra10
]
and the references therein.

2.2.
Equivariant cohomology of
X(P,\lambda)

When
X(P,\lambda)
is smooth (that is, when
X(P,\lambda)
is a quasitoric manifold), its integral equivariant cohomology ring
H^{\ast}_{T^{n}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the
Stanley–Reisner ring

\SR(P)=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]/\mathcal{I},

where

\deg x_{i}=2
and
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal given by

(2.2)

\mathcal{I}=\langle x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\mid F_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap F_{i_{k}}=\emptyset\rangle.

When
X(P,\lambda)
is not smooth, this isomorphism still holds with rational coefficients, that is,

(2.3)

H^{\ast}_{T^{n}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Q})\cong\SR(P)\otimes\mathbb{Q}.

However, in general, the integral version of this isomorphism does not exist. Nevertheless, in some case
H^{*}_{T^{n}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
can be identified with a subring
\wSR(P,\lambda)
of
\SR(P)
, called the
weighted Stanley–Reisner ring
, introduced in
[
BSS17
,
DKS22
]
.

The notion of the weighted Stanley–Reisner ring was first introduced in
[
BSS17
]
for projective toric orbifolds. Subsequently, the authors of
[
DKS22
]
extended this concept to the broader class of
torus orbifolds
, referring to it as the
weighted face ring
. In this paper, we continue to use the term
weighted Stanley–Reisner ring
.

We recall its definition below.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a characteristic pair
(P,\lambda)
and a vertex
v
of
P
, write
\mathcal{F}_{v}(P)=\{F_{i_{1}},\ldots,F_{i_{n}}\}
for the set of facets containing
v
. Define an
m
-tuple

\mathbf{z}^{v}=(z^{v}_{1},\ldots,z^{v}_{m})\in\mathbb{Q}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]^{\oplus m}

of rational polynomials in
n
variables as follows:

•

z^{v}_{j}=0
for
j\notin\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}
;

•

\begin{bmatrix}z^{v}_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
z^{v}_{i_{n}}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}&&\\
\lambda(F_{i_{1}})&\cdots&\lambda(F_{i_{n}})\\
&&\end{array}\right]^{-1}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}\end{bmatrix}
.

Then, a polynomial
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
is said to satisfy the
integrality condition
with respect to
(P,\lambda)
if the composite

f(\mathbf{z}^{v})=f(z^{v}_{1},\ldots,z^{v}_{m})

lies in
\mathbb{Z}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]
for every vertex
v
of
P
.
We denote the set of all such polynomials by
\mathscr{Z}_{(P,\lambda)}
.

Note that
\mathscr{Z}_{(P,\lambda)}
forms a subring of
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
, and
the ideal
\mathcal{I}
in (
2.2
) is also an ideal of
\mathscr{Z}_{(P,\lambda)}
. Indeed, for facets
F_{k_{1}},\dots,F_{k_{\ell}}
with
\bigcap_{j=1}^{\ell}F_{k_{j}}=\emptyset
, the polynomial
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{k_{j}}
satisfies
f(\mathbf{z}^{v})=0
for every vertex
v
of
P
, and hence satisfies the integrality condition.

Definition 2.3
.

The
weighted Stanley–Reisner ring
corresponding to a characteristic pair
(P,\lambda)
is the quotient ring

\wSR(P,\lambda)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathscr{Z}_{(P,\lambda)}/\mathcal{I}

where
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal given in (
2.2
).

Proposition 2.4
.

(See
[
BSS17
, Section 5]
or
[
DKS22
, Theorem 4.11]
)

For toric orbifolds
X(P,\lambda)
with vanishing odd degree integral cohomology groups, there is a ring isomorphism

H^{\ast}_{T^{n}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\cong\wSR(P,\lambda).

Regarding ordinary integral cohomology, if
H^{*}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
is concentrated in even degrees, the Serre spectral sequence of the Borel fibration

X(P,\lambda)\overset{\iota}{\hookrightarrow}ET^{n}\times_{T^{n}}X(P,\lambda)\xrightarrow{\pi}BT^{n}

collapses at
E_{2}
-page. Hence, the map
\iota^{\ast}
induces an isomorphism

H^{\ast}_{T^{n}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{H^{\ast}(BT^{n};\mathbb{Z})}\mathbb{Z}\to H^{\ast}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z}).

One can refer to
[
FP07
, theorem 1.1]
. Hence, in this case, one can combine the above isomorphism together with Proposition
2.4
to obtain a ring isomorphism

(2.4)

\Phi\colon H^{*}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\to\wSR(P,\lambda)/\mathcal{J}

where
\mathcal{J}
is the ideal generated by monomials
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
defined by

\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{m}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}&&\\
\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{m}\\
&&\end{array}\right]\cdot\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

As mentioned in Introduction, the computation
\wSR(P,\lambda)
depends on explicit data from the characteristic pair and must be carried out individually in each case. Moreover, eliminating redundant generators modulo the ideal
\mathcal{J}
makes the determination of a basis more involved. In the following section, we resolve this problem for the case of
4
-dimensional toric orbifolds.

We now analyze the module structure of
H^{2}_{T^{2}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
and construct an explicit basis. By Proposition

above, this problem reduces to studying the intersection of the lattices

For characteristic vectors
\lambda_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})
as above, we write

(3.3)

\mathbf{a}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{b}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},

and regarding
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{m}\end{bmatrix}
as a linear map
\mathbb{Z}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let

(3.4)

K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\ker(\Lambda)=\left\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}a_{i}=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}b_{i}=0\right\}.

Note that

is the space of linear relations of characteristic vectors
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
.

Consider the linear operator

(3.5)

\phi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}^{m},~\phi(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})

defined by
w_{1}=0
and

w_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\det(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{i})t_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(a_{j}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})t_{j}.

for
i=2,\dots,m
.

Lemma 3.3
.

(1)

If
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
, then
w_{m}=0
.

(2)

The restriction
\phi|_{K}\colon K\to\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is injective.

Proof.

(1) For
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}\lambda_{j}=\mathbf{0}
.
Hence, we obtain

w_{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\det(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{m})t_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\det(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{m})t_{j}=\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}\lambda_{j},\lambda_{m}\right)=0.

(2) Assume that
\phi(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})=(0,\dots,0)
for
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
. Since
w_{2}=\det(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})t_{1}
with
\det(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\neq 0
, we have
t_{1}=0
. Next, we have

w_{3}=\det(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})t_{1}+\det(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})t_{2}=0,

which implies that
t_{2}=0
because
t_{1}=0
and
\det(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\neq 0
. Continuing this procedure and using the fact that
\det(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})\neq 0
, we have
t_{1}=\cdots=t_{m-1}=0
. Finally, since
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
and
\lambda_{m}=(a_{m},b_{m})
is a nonzero vector, we conclude that
t_{m}=0
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For the lattices
L_{i}
defined above for
i=1,\dots,m
, it follows that

L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}=\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>\oplus\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>\oplus\phi(K).

In particular, if
\{\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{m-2}\}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
K
, then

\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\phi(\kappa_{1}),\dots,\phi(\kappa_{m-2})\}

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}
.

Proof.

We first claim that

(3.6)

L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}=\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>+\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>+\phi(K).

We start with the inclusion
L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>+\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>+\phi(K)
.
Note that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in L_{i}
if and only if

(3.7)

x_{i}=a_{i}p_{i}+b_{i}q_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i+1}=a_{i+1}p_{i}+b_{i+1}q_{i}

for some
p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}
, there exist

p_{1},q_{1},p_{2},q_{2},\dots,p_{m},q_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}

such that (
3.7
) holds for each
i=1,\dots,m
. Here, indices are taken modulo
m
, that is,
(a_{m+1},b_{m+1})=(a_{1},b_{1})
. Hence, we get

p_{i}a_{i}+q_{i}b_{i}=p_{i-1}a_{i}+q_{i-1}b_{i},

for each
i=1,\dots,m
, where we set
(p_{0},q_{0})=(p_{m},q_{m})
.

Since
\lambda_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})
is primitive for each
i=1,\ldots,m
, there exists
t_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(3.8)

(p_{i}-p_{i-1},q_{i}-q_{i-1})=t_{i}(-b_{i},a_{i})

Summing Equation (
3.8
) over
i=1,\dots,m
, we get

0=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(p_{i}-p_{i-1})=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}t_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad 0=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_{i}-q_{i-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}t_{i},

implying that
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
.
Similarly, summing the first
i
equations in (
3.8
) gives

p_{i}=p_{m}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}b_{j}t_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{i}=q_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}t_{j}.

Therefore, we have for each
i=1,\dots,m
,

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i}a_{i}+q_{i}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(p_{m}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}b_{j}t_{j}\right)a_{i}+\left(q_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}t_{j}\right)b_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{m}a_{i}+q_{m}b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}(a_{j}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})t_{j}

\displaystyle=p_{m}a_{i}+q_{m}b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(a_{j}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})t_{j}.

This establishes that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>+\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>+\phi(K)
.

We now prove the reverse inclusion
\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>+\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>+\phi(K)\subseteq L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}
. For an element
\alpha\mathbf{a}+\beta\mathbf{b}+\phi(t)
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in K
, we set

p_{i}=\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{i}b_{j}t_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{i}=\beta+\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}t_{j}.

Observe that
p_{m}=\alpha
and
q_{m}=\beta
as
t\in K
.
Then, writing
x_{i}
as the
i
-th coordinate of
\alpha\mathbf{a}+\beta\mathbf{b}+\phi(t)
, we have

x_{i}=\alpha a_{i}+\beta b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(a_{j}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})t_{j}=\alpha a_{i}+\beta b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}(a_{j}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})t_{j}=a_{i}p_{i}+b_{i}q_{i}

for
i=1,\dots,m-1
. Furthermore, it follows that

\begin{split}x_{m}&=\alpha a_{m}+\beta b_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(a_{j}b_{m}-a_{m}b_{j})t_{j}\\
&=\alpha a_{m}+\beta b_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\det(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{m})t_{j}\\
&=\alpha a_{m}+\beta b_{m}\end{split}

where the last equality follows from Lemma
3.3
-(1). A similar computation gives the second equation in (
3.7
). Therefore

\alpha\mathbf{a}+\beta\mathbf{b}+\phi(t)\in L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{m}
.

It remains to show that the right hand side of (
3.6
) is indeed a direct sum
\mathbb{Z}\langle\textbf{a}\rangle\oplus\mathbb{Z}\langle\textbf{b}\rangle\oplus\phi(K)
.
Assume that
\alpha\mathbf{a}+\beta\mathbf{b}+\phi(t)=0
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t\in K
. Note that if
\phi(t)=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
, then
w_{1}=0
by convention and
w_{m}=0
by Lemma
3.3
-(1). Then, we have

\alpha a_{1}+\beta b_{1}=\alpha a_{m}+\beta b_{m}=0

which implies that
\alpha=\beta=0
because
\lambda_{1}=(a_{1},b_{1})
and
\lambda_{m}=(a_{m},b_{m})
are linearly independent. Hence, the result follows.

Finally, the second claim follows directly from Lemma
3.3
-(2). This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
X(P,\lambda)
be a
4
-dimensional toric orbifold satisfying Equation (
3.2
).
Then there is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules

(3.9)

\Psi\colon\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{a}\right>\oplus\mathbb{Z}\left<\mathbf{b}\right>\oplus\phi(K)\to H^{2}_{T^{2}}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\cong\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)

Proof.

The proof follows directly from Theorems
3.1

3.5

∎

4.
Applications

4.1.
Construction of algebraic cellular bases

In
[
FSS23
]
, the authors studied the integral cohomology of a
4
-dimensional toric orbifold
X(P,\lambda)
such that
\lambda
satisfies

(4.1)

\lambda_{m-1}=(1,0)\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{m}=(0,1).

This condition is equivalent to the existence of a smooth fixed point in
X(P,\lambda)
. In this case,
X(P,\lambda)
admits a cellular structure

(4.2)

X(P,\lambda)\simeq\left(\bigvee_{i=1}^{m-2}S^{2}\right)\cup e^{4},

which gives rise to cellular cohomology classes in
H^{\ast}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover, since the cellular structure (
4.2
) consists of even dimensional cells only, their corresponding cohomology classes form a basis.
The authors then identified these classes, via the isomorphism (
2.4
), with elements of
\wSR(P,\lambda)/\mathcal{J}
. The resulting basis is called the
algebraic cellular basis
for
H^{\ast}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X(P,\lambda)
be a
4
-dimensional toric orbifold satisfying (
4.1
). Its
algebraic cellular basis
is the set

\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m-2};v\}

of cohomology classes in
H^{*}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
defined by

•

u_{i}=\Phi^{-1}\left(\sum^{i}_{k=1}a_{k}b_{i}x_{k}+\sum^{m-2}_{k=i+1}a_{i}b_{k}x_{k}\right)

•

v=\Phi^{-1}(x_{m-1}x_{m})
,

where
\Phi\colon H^{\ast}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})\to\wSR(P,\lambda)/\mathcal{J}
is the isomorphism in (
2.4
).

We note that the authors of
[
FSS23
]
introduced the notion of a toric morphism between toric orbifolds and use this to justify that the algebraic cellular basis defined above is indeed a basis for
\widetilde{H}^{\ast}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
.
In particular, the well-definedness of degree-two elements, namely, showing that the polynomials defining
u_{i}
are indeed elements of
\wSR(P,\lambda)
, requires series of technical calculations of toric morphisms. Using Theorem
3.5
, we now give a short alternate proof regarding the degree-two elements.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X(P,\lambda)
be a
4
-dimensional toric orbifold satisfying (
4.1
).
Then the degree two elements
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m-2}
in Definition
4.1
are well-defined and form a basis for
H^{2}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

As the characteristic function
\lambda
satisfies (
4.1
), one can simply take a basis of
K
as
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m-2}\}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
given by

(4.3)

\xi_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(0,\dots,0,\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\uparrow\\
i\text{-th}\end{subarray}}{1},0,\dots,0,-a_{i},-b_{i}),

Then, by Theorem
3.5
, the set

\{\Psi(\mathbf{a}),\Psi(\mathbf{b}),(\Psi\circ\phi)(\xi_{1}),\dots,(\Psi\circ\phi)(\xi_{m-2})\}

with
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m-2},1,0)
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m-2},0,1)
forms a basis of
\wSR(P,\lambda)
. Since
\Psi(\mathbf{a}),\Psi(\mathbf{b})
are generators of the ideal
\mathcal{J}
, the remaining elements form a basis of
\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)/\mathcal{J}\cong H^{2}(X(P,\lambda);\mathbb{Z})
.

We complete the proof by showing that
(\Psi\circ\phi)(\xi_{i})=\Phi(u_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,m-2
. Indeed, by the definition
\phi
given in (
3.5
) together with (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle(\Psi\circ\phi)(\mu_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k={i+1}}^{m}\det(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{k})x_{k}-a_{i}\det(\lambda_{m-1},\lambda_{m})x_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k={i+1}}^{m-2}(a_{i}b_{k}-a_{k}b_{i})x_{k}-b_{i}x_{m-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k={i+1}}^{m-2}(a_{i}b_{k}-a_{k}b_{i})x_{k}+b_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{m-2}a_{k}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{i}a_{k}b_{i}x_{k}+\sum_{k=i+1}^{m-2}a_{i}b_{k}x_{k},

where the second equality follows from (
4.1
) and the third one also holds because
\Psi(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{k=1}^{m-2}a_{k}x_{k}+x_{m-1}\in\mathcal{J}
. Hence, we have established the claim.
∎

4.2.
Description of Cartier divisors on toric surfaces

As discussed in Remark
2.1
, if the characteristic pair
(P,\lambda)
is defined from a rational polytope in
M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
, where
M
is the character lattice of
T^{n}
, then
X(P,\lambda)
can also be regarded as a topological model of the toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
associated with the normal fan
\Sigma
of
P
.

To be more precise, there is a bijection map between the set
\Sigma^{(1)}
of
1
-dimensional cones and the set
\mathcal{F}(P)
of facets. Each
\rho_{i}\in\Sigma^{(1)}
is the cone generated by
\lambda(F_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for the facet
F_{i}
corresponding to
\rho_{i}
, where we identify
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with the lattice
N
of 1-parameter subgroups of
T^{n}
. In addition, the set
\{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k}\}
forms a
k
-dimensional cone in
\Sigma
if and only if the corresponding facets
\{F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\}
have a non-empty intersection. Then, the isomorphism (
2.3
) is defined by sending each torus invariant Weil divisor
D_{\rho_{i}}
to the generator
x_{i}
corresponding to
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(P)
.

In this case, Theorem
3.5
gives a complete description of the group
\CDiv_{T}(X_{\Sigma})
of torus-invariant Cartier divisors and the Picard group
\Pic(X_{\Sigma})
. Also, we obtain the index of
\Pic(X_{\Sigma})
in the class group
\clas(X_{\Sigma})
, which will be discussed in Corollary
4.5
below. The definitions and properties of these groups can be found in
[
CLS11
, Chapter 4]
.

The following proposition is well-known. See, for instance,
[
CLS11
, Theorem 4.2.8]
or
[
Ful93
, Section 3.3]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

A Weil divisor
\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma^{(1)}}a_{\rho}D_{\rho}
is a Cartier divisor if and only if for each maximal cone
\sigma
, there is
m_{\sigma}\in M
such that
\left<m_{\sigma},\lambda(F_{\rho})\right>=-a_{\rho}
for all
\rho\in\Sigma^{(1)}
.

In fact, it is straightforward to see that the condition in Proposition
4.4
coincides with the integrality condition in Definition
2.2
for homogeneous polynomials of degree
2
in
\SR(P)
, using the correspondence between
D_{\rho_{i}}
and
x_{i}\in\SR(P)
. Hence, we conclude the following corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
X_{\Sigma}
be a toric variety of real dimension
4
with
H^{3}(X_{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})=0
, and let
(P,\lambda)
be its associated characteristic pair. Then, the following statements hold.

(1)

The group
\CDiv_{T}(X_{\Sigma})
of torus-invariant Cartier divisors is isomorphic to
\wSR^{2}(P,\lambda)
of (

) as
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.

(2)

The Picard group

is isomorphic to
\phi(K)
of (
3.9

\mathbb{Z}

(3)
