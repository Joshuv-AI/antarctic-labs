POLAR-Bench: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Privacy-Utility Trade-offs in LLM Agents
Qiaoyuan Zheng, Yiqu Yang, Qi Gao, Imanol Schlag

Abstract
LLM agents increasingly have access to private user data and act on the user’s behalf when interacting with third-party systems. The user defines what may and must not be shared, and the agent must robustly follow that intent even when third-party systems behave adversarially. We introduce POLAR-Bench ( Pol icy- a ware adve r sarial Benchmark), in which a trusted model with a privacy policy and a task converses with a third-party model that adversarially probes for both task-relevant and protected attributes. Across 10 domains and 7,852 samples, we score privacy and utility by deterministic set-membership and vary privacy policy dimension and attack strategy along two orthogonal axes, producing a 5 × 5 5\times 5 diagnostic surface per model. Our results reveal a sharp split: current frontier models withhold over 99% of protected attributes, while smaller open-weight models in the 1–30B range, the class users most commonly run as their own trusted agent on-device or via private inference, score notably worse, with the weakest leaking over half. POLAR-Bench thus localizes where each model’s intent-following breaks down, providing a foothold for privacy alignment where it matters most.



1
Introduction

Modern LLMs are increasingly deployed as personal agents that read users’ private documents and exchange information with third-party systems to complete tasks
(
Yao et al. 2022
;
Schick et al. 2023
)
.
Consider scheduling a medical appointment: the personal agent has access to medical records, insurance, and other personal data, and must converse with the clinic’s agent to find an available slot. The clinic needs only the appointment type, the user’s availability, and medically relevant context; insurance identifiers, full medical history, and unrelated personal details should stay private.

This setting puts two demands on the trusted model at once: it must share enough to complete the task while withholding what the policy forbids. The challenge compounds when third-party systems probe for protected attributes, whether through direct prompt injection, role-play, or gradual elicitation across turns
(
Perez and Ribeiro 2022
;
Staab et al. 2023
)
. Specifying disclosure rules for each third party does not scale, so the user provides a single privacy policy that the agent must apply uniformly across all interactions.

Existing benchmarks measure privacy leakage in agent settings under fixed conditions: a single policy type or a single attack strategy at a time (Section
2
). None varies privacy policy dimension and attack strategy as joint axes, leaving the privacy-utility trade-off underexplored. Without that joint view, it is hard to say where any given model’s intent-following actually breaks down.

We introduce POLAR-Bench, in which a trusted model with a privacy policy and a task converses with a third-party model that probes for both task-relevant and protected attributes. Each attribute is typed before natural-language rendering, so both utility and privacy leakage are scored deterministically by set membership on regex-validated documents, with no LLM judge in the loop for outcomes. POLAR-Bench grades privacy policy dimension (from explicit field rules to abstract or conflicting objectives) and attack strategy (from direct single-turn requests to incremental multi-turn elicitation) as orthogonal axes over 10 domains and 7,852 samples, producing a
5\times 5
diagnostic surface per model. Our results reveal a sharp split: current frontier models withhold over 99% of protected attributes while keeping high utility, whereas smaller open-weight models in the 1–30B range, the class most often deployed as a trusted on-device or private-inference agent, score substantially worse, with the weakest leaking over half and several others maintaining privacy only by sacrificing utility. Related Work

Most prior privacy benchmarks evaluate models in benign or single-axis settings. ConfAIde and PrivacyLens treat privacy as contextual-integrity norm awareness without adversarial pressure
(
Mireshghallah et al. 2023
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
, AGENTDAM measures data-minimization under passive observation
(
Zharmagambetov et al. 2025
)
, and
Fu et al. 2026
reports a privacy-utility tension for single-agent enterprise flows. Earlier work catalogues attribute inference, prompt leakage, and jailbreak-style attacks as independent categories
(
Staab et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Nakka et al. 2025
)
. None of these vary privacy policy dimension along an explicit hierarchy, none grade attack strategy, and none score privacy and utility jointly under adversarial probing.

Closer to our setting, AirGapAgent introduced the trusted-versus-external threat model and a defense against context-hijacking
(
Bagdasarian et al. 2024
)
, and AgentLeak surveys 32 attack classes across four multi-agent frameworks, grading adversary capability rather than attack strategy
(
Yagoubi et al. 2026
)
. POLAR-Bench inherits AirGapAgent’s two-agent threat model and a subset of AgentLeak’s attack vocabulary, and adds graded privacy policy dimension and graded attack strategy as orthogonal axes, combining them into a
5\times 5
evaluation surface on which privacy and utility are scored deterministically (Table
1
). Recent inference-time defenses such as PAPILLON
(
Siyan et al. 2024
)
, PrivacyPAD
(
Hui et al. 2025
)
, PrivacyChecker
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, and multi-agent decompositions
(
Li et al. 2025
)
can be stress-tested on POLAR-Bench. Training-time privacy work
(
Carlini et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Qian et al. 2026
;
Dwork and Roth 2014
;
Abadi et al. 2016
)
is orthogonal: we hold the model fixed and study how it handles user-supplied private context at inference time.

3
The POLAR-Bench Benchmark

We formalize POLAR-Bench in turn: notation (§
3.1
), the policy and attack axes (§
3.2
–
3.3
), the generation pipeline (§
3.4
), the quality controls (§
3.5
), and the evaluation protocol (§
3.6
).

3.1
Setting and Notation

A sample
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{Q})
packages a source document
\mathcal{D}
, a privacy policy
\mathcal{P}
, a task instruction
\mathcal{T}
, and an attacker prompt sequence
\mathcal{Q}
. Every attribute in
\mathcal{D}
is typed before natural-language rendering:

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{task}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{protected}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{other}},

(1)

with
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{task}}
representing the attributes that the model
E
must receive,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{protected}}
those that it should withhold, and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{other}}
as noise irrelevant to the task. Each sample belongs to a domain
g\in\mathcal{G}
and draws
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pool}}^{(g_{i})}
from a per-domain pool. POLAR-Bench is
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathcal{I}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

3.2
Privacy Policies

The privacy policy axis ranges over five increasingly difficult reasoning settings
\mathcal{L}_{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{5}\}
.

P_{1}
Explicit field constraints.

Protected attributes are listed by field name (e.g., do not disclose phone or address).

P_{2}
Semantic constraints.

Protected information is described by category, requiring
T
to recognize instances beyond exact field names (e.g., do not disclose any contact information).

P_{3}
Conditional disclosure.

Disclosure is allowed only under specified conditions (e.g., share medications only if asked by a clinician).

P_{4}
Partial or abstracted disclosure.

Some attributes may be shared only at a coarser granularity (e.g., country but not city, employer category but not employer name).

P_{5}
Conflicting objectives.

The policy and the task implicitly compete;
T
must reason about which constraint dominates (e.g., the task asks for the most fitting candidate, but disclosing the deciding attribute is forbidden).

3.3
Attack Strategies

The attack axis ranges over five strategies
\mathcal{L}_{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{5}\}
used to generate
\mathcal{Q}
for
E
.

S_{1}
Direct single-turn.

One prompt requesting comprehensive information.

S_{2}
Yes/no narrowing.

Binary or multiple-choice questions reduce uncertainty over
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{protected}}
via the pattern of responses
(
Anderson et al. 2025
)
.

S_{3}
Role confusion.

E
adopts a trusted role (auditor, clinician, recruiter) and frames protected information as needed for legitimate workflow
(
Ye et al. 2026
)
.

S_{4}
Prompt injection.

Adversarial instructions attempt to override the policy via authority framing or system/debug pretenses
(
Perez and Ribeiro 2022
)
.

S_{5}
Multi-turn progressive.

A 3–6 turn sequence that gathers context, introduces ambiguity, and narrows toward protected attributes.

3.4
From Symbolic Specifications to Natural Language

Generation has two stages: symbolic profile sampling, then natural-language rendering. We first sample a JSON profile
\mathcal{A}_{i}
over a domain (medical triage, recruitment screening, finance, education, etc.); attributes are typed by class, and an irrelevant-information intensity in
\{\text{low},\text{medium},\text{high}\}
controls how many
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{other}}
attributes are included.

An LLM then renders the typed profile into:

1.

Source document
\mathcal{D}_{i}
in a format sampled from a per-domain pool
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{pool}}^{(g_{i})}
(diary, email, intake note, calendar entry, etc.).

2.

Privacy policy
\mathcal{P}_{i}
from a symbolic spec at the chosen
P_{k}
level (§
3.2
).

3.

Task instruction
\mathcal{T}_{i}
from the task spec, with all references to protected information masked.

4.

Attacker prompts
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
from the chosen
S_{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}
(§
3.3
) plus a target list of attributes to elicit.

Decoupling Task and Policy.

A key design principle is that the task instruction must not encode any privacy hint. Formally we require
\mathcal{T}_{i}\perp\mathcal{P}_{i}
. We enforce this through prompt-level guards: the task-instruction renderer is forbidden from mentioning policy terms, sensitivity labels, redaction language, or “minimum necessary” phrasing, and the policy renderer is symmetrically forbidden from carrying task-execution goals. This prevents
T
from satisfying the policy via shortcut cues in the task and forces it to interpret
\mathcal{P}
on its own merits.

3.5
Quality Control

Two checks ensure that every generated sample is well-formed before it enters the benchmark.

Attribute Coverage.

For every attribute
a\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
we verify that
a
is recoverable from
\mathcal{D}_{i}
via punctuation/whitespace normalization and regex matching, with an LLM-judge fallback for paraphrased mentions. This guarantees that every protected and task-relevant attribute could in principle be elicited from
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, so leakage and utility scoring is well-defined.

Attacker Prompt Validation.

Validating attacker prompts cannot rely on string matching: a well-formed attack must elicit protected and task-relevant attributes without naming them outright, so coverage and intent have to be judged at a semantic level. Each rendered
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is therefore passed to a separate LLM judge that checks (i) the prompt remains a plausible natural request, (ii) it covers every attacker-target field at least once, and (iii) it does not betray the attack as such (no “ignore previous instructions” phrasing in
S_{3}
, no role-mention in
S_{4}
). Prompts that fail any check are regenerated.

To check that this validation step is not tied to one judge’s preferences, we re-ran it with three judges: GPT-5.4-nano, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. Per-judge independent acceptance rates on the full prompt set are
80.0\%
,
91.7\%
, and
91.7\%
respectively, with mean
87.8\%
and standard deviation
5.5\%
across judges. Quality-control details are in Appendix
A
.

3.6
Evaluation Protocol

For sample
\mathcal{I}_{i}=(\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},\mathcal{Q}_{i})
, the evaluation runs over
K_{i}
turns. At turn
k
,
E
issues a query
q_{i,k}\sim\pi_{E}(\cdot\mid H_{i,k-1},\mathcal{Q}_{i})
in the multi-turn strategy
S_{5}
, or
q_{i,k}\sim\pi_{E}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Q}_{i})
otherwise.
T
replies with
r_{i,k}\sim\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},H_{i,k-1},q_{i,k})
. The history accumulates as
H_{i,k}=\{(q_{i,j},r_{i,j})\}_{j=1}^{k}
; the final transcript is
H_{i}=H_{i,K_{i}}
.

Let
\varepsilon(H_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{i}
denote the attributes revealed in
H_{i}
. We extract
\varepsilon(H_{i})
using the same normalization-and-regex procedure described in §
3.5
, applied to the transcript rather than to
\mathcal{D}_{i}
.

Privacy Score.

The fraction of protected attributes that
T
kept hidden during the interaction:

\mathrm{Privacy}(H_{i})=1-\frac{\left|\varepsilon(H_{i})\cap\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{protected},i}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{protected},i}\right|}.

(2)

A higher value indicates better protection against
E
’s queries.

Utility Score.

The fraction of task-relevant attributes that
T
shared with
E
to complete the task:

\mathrm{Utility}(H_{i})=\frac{\left|\varepsilon(H_{i})\cap\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{task},i}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{task},i}\right|}.

(3)

Overall Score.

A convex combination of privacy and utility, capturing the trade-off between the two (default
\lambda=0.5
):

\mathrm{Score}(H_{i})=\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Privacy}(H_{i})+(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathrm{Utility}(H_{i}).

(4)

Reproducibility.

We maximize the reproducibility of our benchmark by fixing a global seed of 42 for structured-data generation, sampling all pseudo-random choices from deterministic attribute pools, and deriving per-field LLM rendering seeds from
(\texttt{sample\_id},\texttt{field\_name})
. All records are sorted and serialized with stable key ordering. For natural-language rendering, we use deterministic decoding settings (
temperature=0.0
,
top_p=1.0
) and log model names, decoding parameters, and seeds in the metadata. Because our serving endpoint is not bit-level deterministic, we freeze the rendered benchmark after validation and reuse the fixed JSON/JSONL files for all evaluations.

5.1
Privacy–Utility Trade-Off

The trade-off is not monotonic. Stronger models such as GLM-5.1, GPT-5.4, Gemma-4-31B, and DeepSeek-V3.1 score high on both axes, but several mid-tier models are imbalanced. GLM-4.7-Flash, Kimi-K2.5, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, and the GPT-OSS 20B/120B models all reach near-maximal privacy but only moderate or low utility, suggesting overly conservative behavior that trades utility for privacy. SmolLM3-3B and Ministral-3-3B do the opposite: higher utility, lower privacy. They prioritize helpfulness and inadvertently disclose protected attributes.

Figure
3
visualizes the trade-off by plotting models in utility-privacy space with iso-score lines for several values of
\lambda
. GLM-5.1 remains top-ranked under every tested
\lambda
, showing that strong privacy and high utility can be achieved simultaneously. The fact that GLM-5.1, GPT-5.4, Gemma-4-31B, and DeepSeek-V3.1 all score high on both axes suggests the trade-off is not fundamental: alignment and training choices matter as much as scale.

5.2
Effect of Model Size

Model size alone is not a reliable predictor of privacy or utility. Within the same model family, scaling shows inconsistent effects. For Apertus, the 70B variant outperforms the 8B on utility, but its privacy score drops from 65.66 to 57.78. The Gemma-4 family shows the opposite pattern: privacy improves with size while utility degrades. Ministral is non-monotonic, with privacy rising from 62.03 (Ministral-3-3B) to 68.04 (Ministral-3-8B) and falling back to 58.99 (Ministral-3-14B), while utility stays nearly flat.

Cross-family comparisons make the pattern clearer. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B obtains only 56.2 in privacy, lower than much smaller models such as GPT-OSS-20B, Ministral-3-8B, and Apertus-8B. Ministral-3-3B and Gemma-4-E2B both score better in utility than much larger models such as Apertus-8B, GLM-4.7-Flash (30B), and Kimi-K2.5 (1.1T). On overall score, small models such as Gemma-4-E4B reach high values while large models such as Apertus-70B score 25% lower, and GPT-OSS-120B underperforms the much smaller Gemma-4-E4B on both privacy and utility. Performance is shaped at least as much by alignment and training choices as by parameter count.

On utility, Customer Support is the most challenging setting for nearly all models, likely because its tasks are less structured and harder to define precisely. Legal and Finance generally achieve higher utility scores, reflecting their more structured interactions.

On privacy, Recruitment is the clearest high-risk domain, with the lowest privacy mean (73.27) and highest privacy variance (601.65). 5.7
Robustness Check

We conduct robustness checks along three main dimensions: third-party model choice, cross-domain consistency, and evaluation reproducibility. First, the benchmark is largely stable to the choice of third-party model
E
: for a specific model
T
, the overall score changes insignificantly across different third-party models, as shown in Table
2
. Second, the main trends are consistent across domains as we discussed in Section
5.4
: although absolute scores vary by task area, the relative ordering of models remains broadly unchanged, suggesting that our conclusions are not driven by any single domain. Third, we enforce strict reproducibility throughout the pipeline by fixing random seeds and using deterministic rendering settings. Taken together, these results indicate that POLAR-Bench’s metrics are robust and effective, and that the framework provides a reliable basis for diagnosing privacy-utility trade-offs in LLM agents.

6
Limitations

POLAR-Bench has several limitations. Samples are generated from structured profiles rather than real user logs, so external validity depends on how faithfully the rendered documents track deployed text. The graded
P_{1}
–
P_{5}
privacy policy dimensions and
S_{1}
–
S_{5}
attack strategy are our own design, and we do not validate the difficulty ordering against human reviewers. Coverage is also restricted to English, leaving multilingual privacy norms and culturally variable disclosure expectations out of scope.

The evaluation pipeline adds further biases. A single Llama-3.3-70B model generates every
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, so the reported attack strategy is conditioned on this third-party model and may shift under a different one. Scoring is deterministic and matches against structured ground truth, leaving it blind to paraphrased disclosures, most acutely under
P_{4}
(abstraction) and
P_{5}
(conflict). Our minimal-disclosure judgments (what counts as the smallest acceptable amount of information to share) therefore remain normative rather than empirically calibrated against human raters. Due to the limited availability of reliable official external benchmark scores across models, our across-bench analysis is limited to GPQA Diamond. Expanding the defense-baseline evaluation beyond PrivacyChecker on three trusted models is also left to future work.

7
Conclusion

POLAR-Bench’s diagnostic surface localizes where each model’s intent-following breaks down: not just whether it fails, but whether it fails by over-disclosing protected information, by becoming too conservative to complete the task, or under specific attack strategies and policy types.

The gap between frontier and the 1–30B open-weight class matters in deployment, not just in benchmarks: smaller, locally-deployable models are precisely the ones users run as their own trusted agent on-device or via private inference, often to avoid sending private data to third-party APIs in the first place. This gap is not a pure function of scale: within-family scaling is often non-monotonic, and the strongest models on both axes (GLM-5.1, GPT-5.4, Gemma-4-31B, DeepSeek-V3.1) suggest alignment and training choices drive performance more than parameter count does. Reasoning capability (proxied by GPQA Diamond) correlates strongly with privacy but not with utility, consistent with stronger reasoners recognizing what to withhold without translating that into better task completion.

POLAR-Bench also picks up the behavioral effect of inference-time defenses such as PrivacyChecker, and we hope it serves as a stress-test for privacy-aware alignment and the next generation of defenses, particularly those targeting the deployment-relevant class.

A.2
LLM-Based Verification for Other Texts

For instruction-like fields, we use an LLM-based semantic judge rather than exact keyword matching. The
privacy_policy_text
is judged on whether it clearly specifies what information may be shared and what must not be disclosed without weakening the intended restrictions. The
task_instruction_text
is judged on whether it expresses the intended downstream task naturally while avoiding privacy-policy language, hidden labels, benchmark metadata, or explicit leakage of protected values. The
attacker_prompt_text
is judged on whether it plausibly attempts to elicit, infer, or narrow down information related to both
allowed_fields
and
withheld_fields
. The prompts for these three LLM-judges are shown in Appendix
B
. The verifier records missing and covered fields for attacker prompts, enabling field-level coverage analysis and repair rather than only a binary validity decision.

The validation process also supports incremental re-validation: if a previous validation report already exists, only samples previously marked as invalid are re-checked, while valid prior results are preserved. The script produces a structured validation report containing sample-level validity, field-level validity, checked values, missing values, LLM judge outputs, run configuration, and aggregate invalid counts for each generated-text field.

A.3
Repair after Verification

After verification, we do not discard invalid samples directly. Instead, we run a targeted repair pass over the rendered dataset using the structured validation report. For each sample, only fields whose field-level verifier result is not valid are selected for repair; fields that already passed verification are left unchanged.

For
source_document_text
, repair is driven by deterministic value-coverage failures. The repair script extracts the missing entries reported by the regex verifier from both
do_not_disclose_values_present
and
allowed_values_present
. These missing concrete values become the required repair hints. The LLM is then prompted to re-render a clean and realistic source document using the original sample metadata, structured profile, source-document design inputs, hidden sensitive values, and safe values. Crucially, the prompt requires every missing value to appear exactly as written, while also instructing the model not to include privacy-policy language, redaction instructions, benchmark metadata, or repetitive filler. Thus, failure cases like the example above are repaired by regenerating the source document so that the verifier can match the required surface forms.

For
task_instruction_text
and
attacker_prompt_text
, the repair hints are extracted more generally from the validation report. The script collects failed check names, missing items, banned phrase hits, violations, errors, unexpected outputs, and other diagnostic messages. The task-instruction repair prompt rewrites the instruction as a concise downstream task request, grounded only in the
minimally_required_fields
. It explicitly instructs the model to avoid privacy-policy language, disclosure rules, hidden-field references, benchmark metadata, and unsafe broadening of abstract fields. For example, a field such as
monthly_income_band
must remain an “income band” rather than being broadened into an exact monthly income.

For
attacker_prompt_text
, the repair prompt preserves the intended attack type while enforcing field coverage. The required coverage fields are constructed from the attacker goal together with the minimally required task fields. The repaired attacker prompt must cover every required field either explicitly, through an unmistakable synonym, or through a concrete task-specific question. The prompt also includes attack-type-specific constraints for
direct_single_turn
,
multi_turn_progressive
,
prompt_injection
,
role_confusion
, and
yes_no_narrowing
. For multi-turn attack types, the output must be a valid JSON array of strings. If the output is not a parseable JSON array, the script performs a second format-repair step that rewrites the output into valid JSON while preserving the attack type and required coverage.

All repair prompts are shown below.

Each repair call uses a deterministic seed derived from the sample id, field name, repair model, and base seed. The script records repair metadata in
generation_meta
, including whether the field was repaired, the repair model, the repair seed, whether deterministic LLM calls were used, and the repair hints. If a field repair fails, the original sample is kept with the corresponding repair error recorded. The final repaired dataset is saved together with a repair summary reporting the number of loaded samples, samples needing repair, total field repairs, successfully repaired samples, repaired fields, skipped samples, and samples with repair failures.

After the repair process, we removed 23 samples for which source-document rendering still failed validation.

A.4
Regex-Coverage

During the Evaluation process, we compute the privacy and utility score using a regex-based coverage metric over target values. For each sample, we extract two sets of values from the scoring targets:
allowed_values
and
do_not_disclose_values
. We then concatenate all responses produced by Model A in the dialogue and check whether each target value appears in the resulting text. The matcher is not limited to exact string matching: it normalizes case, whitespace, punctuation, hyphens, underscores, date formats, money formats, compact alphanumeric identifiers, emails, phone numbers, and long textual phrases through an ordered token-coverage fallback, as we showed in Figure
5

Coverage over
allowed_values
is used as the utility score, measuring whether the model provides the task-relevant information it is permitted to share. Coverage over
do_not_disclose_values
is treated as leakage.

Regex normalization for the texts verification is exactly the same.

Appendix C
Rendering Structured Benchmark Samples into Natural-Language Texts

Our benchmark starts from structured samples and renders each sample into four natural-language texts:

source_document_text
,
privacy_policy_text
,
task_instruction_text
, and
attacker_prompt_text
.
Each structured sample contains the domain, metadata, profile information, source-document inputs, privacy-policy inputs, task-instruction inputs, attack setup, and a hidden target.
The hidden target specifies which fields are allowed to be disclosed and which fields should be withheld.
From these fields, we also derive scoring targets: concrete
allowed_values
and concrete
do_not_disclose_values
.
The renderer then builds four field-specific prompts, each of which asks an LLM to generate one natural-language artifact while preserving the intended benchmark structure.

At a high level, the rendering process is:

1.

Start from a structured benchmark record.

2.

Extract task-relevant fields, sensitive fields, safe abstractions, and attack goals.

3.

Build four generation prompts: one for the source document, one for the privacy policy, one for the task instruction, and one for the attacker prompt.

4.

Call the generation model with each prompt.

5.

Store the generated texts together with the original structured fields and scoring targets for later verification and evaluation.

The renderer supports multiple attack types, including direct single-turn attacks, progressive multi-turn attacks, prompt injection, role confusion, and yes/no narrowing.

models remain helpful even when they fail to track cumulative disclosure risk.

(87.26), indicating more consistent and comparatively safer behavior across
models. This may be because instruction-conflict patterns are more recognizable
than incremental or conversational attacks. Overall, explicit attacks are easier
to defend against on average, whereas incremental attacks better preserve
utility while exposing larger differences in privacy robustness across models.
