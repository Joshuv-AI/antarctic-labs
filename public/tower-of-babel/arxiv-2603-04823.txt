The Statistical Mechanics of Indistinguishable Energy States and the Glass Transition
Shimul Akhanjee

Abstract
The statistical mechanics of particles that populate indistinguishable energy states is explored. In particular, the mathematical treatment of the microstates differs from conventional statistical mechanics where the energy levels or states are universally treated as distinguishable, and differentiated by unique quantum numbers, or addressed by distinct spatial locations. Results from combinatorial counting problems are adapted to derive exact distribution functions for both classical and quantum particles at high degeneracy levels. Classical particles exhibit a definitive glass transition, similar to supercooled liquids where where the configurational entropy vanishes below a finite temperature T K T_{K} .



I
introduction

Unlike a crystal, which settles into a low degeneracy periodic ground state, a glass is a low temperature,
T
phase defined by its inability to reach equilibrium on macroscopic time scales
Dyre 2006
;
Binder and Young 1986
;
Angell 1995
;
Berthier and Biroli 2011
;
Amann-Winkel et al. 2016
. The phase transition from a simple global energy minimum to a phase defined by a complex, rugged energy landscape is one of the most important, unsolved problems in physics. The broad definition of a glass includes both spin glass (SG) materials and supercooled liquids (SCL). While SG systems exhibit a sharp phase transition to a frozen state involving quenched disorder at a specific freezing temperature
T_{SG}
, glass-forming liquids undergo an inherently non-equilibrium phase transition without explicit quenched disorder or a well-defined transition
T_{g}
Fischer and Hertz 1993
;
Binder and Young 1986
;
Dyre 2006
value. Both cases contain frustrated basins of attraction, or local minima that represent different amorphous structural arrangements
Goldstein 1969
. Since the particles are trapped in one of many, metastable local minima, one simplified approach that will be taken here is to characterize the glass phase as the limit of highly degenerate, indistinguishable energy states. These minima are separated by high level energy barriers that grow significantly as
T
is lowered.
The glass transition is commonly understood as the point where the system can no longer hop between degenerate basins within experimental timescales, resulting in a unique type of broken ergodicity, unlike in a standard gas or liquid where the time average of macroscopic variables equal their ensemble averages
Struik 1978
.

In the glassy limit, the system is confined to a single valley or a small cluster of valleys with nearly identical macroscopic properties. Therefore, a reasonable question to ask is, “What are the physical properties of a system where the energy states are unlabeled?” Although the primary physical motivation arises from an attempt to characterize “glassy” phases of matter, the mathematical framework of instinguishable energy states is, in itself, an important foundational topic of statistical mechanics that will be explored in this paper.

II
Phenomenological Considerations

In glass-forming liquids, the relaxation time (
\tau
), and viscosity (
\eta
) are essential for understanding the glass transition, and can be computed directly from the distribution function and the configurational entropy (
\mathcal{S}_{l}
)
Dyre 2006
. Both are related by
\tau=\eta/G_{\infty}
, where
G_{\infty}
is the infinite frequency shear modulus. Typical values for
\tau
in a liquid are
10^{-1}-10
seconds and near
T=T_{g}
,
\tau
is of the order
10^{2}-10^{3}
seconds
Dyre 2006
. Adam and Gibbs proposed that the relaxation of a supercooled liquid requires a cooperatively rearranging region (CRR), having a size that is inversely proportional to
\mathcal{S}_{l}
Adam and Gibbs 1965
. It follows that a widely used connection between the
\mathcal{S}_{l}
and
\tau
is given by the Adam-Gibbs (AG) relation

,

\ln\tau\propto\frac{1}{T\mathcal{S}_{l}(T)}

(1)

Since a glass is expected to be several orders of magnitude more viscous than liquid, it is important to establish a clear line of demarcation. Clearly,
\tau
is a kinetic variable governed by thermally activated processes, therefore if one considers a distribution of energy barriers
\Delta E
, the average relaxation time of glass should slow faster as
T
is reduced
Gotze and Sjogren 1992
. Thus, highly viscous liquids are to be compared against the benchmark, Arrhenius activation law
Jones 2002
;
March and Tosi 2002
,

\tau\sim\tau_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\Delta E}{k_{B}T}\right)

(2)

Another essential concept is the Kauzmann Temperature
T_{K}
Kauzmann 1948
;
March and Tosi 2002
;
Dyre 2006
. As a liquid is supercooled, its configurational entropy
\mathcal{S}_{l}
decreases drastically until it reaches the crystal threshold value of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
. If one extrapolates this decrease,
\mathcal{S}_{l}
would appear to vanish at
T=T_{K}
. This leads to a paradox since
\mathcal{S}_{l}
would take on values lower than that of an ordered crystal. The precise resolution is still elusive, but extremely important here because
\mathcal{S}_{l}
will be computed explicitly for classical particles with indistinguishable energy levels and
\mathcal{S}_{l}
appears to vanish precisely at some
T_{K}
, suggesting that the system has a glass transition. Additionally, I have determined a material dependence of
T_{K}
, on the single particle, molecular energy bandwith
W
and chemical potential
\mu
.

The AG relation of Equation (
1
) can be used to provide an intuitive derivation of the celebrated Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann (VFT) law, which is a prevalent description of “super-Arrhenius” behavior near
T=T_{g}
Fulcher 1925
;
Jones 2002
;
March and Tosi 2002
. In a more realistic description of a glass, the effective barrier
\Delta E
is not constant, rather it increases as the temperature decreases because the system must move cooperatively to relax. As the system approaches the ideal glass limit, the number of states decreases and
\tau
increases explosively. The VFT law is the standard empirical fit for the viscosity of fragile glass-formers.
Near the glass transition, it is thought that
\mathcal{S}_{l}(T)\approx C\left(1-\frac{T_{K}}{T}\right)
. Substituting this into Equation (
1
) yields the VFT form
Jones 2002
;
March and Tosi 2002
:

\tau=\tau_{0}\exp\left(\frac{D}{T-T_{K}}\right)

(3)

for some material dependent parameter
D
. This expression attempts to describe the experimental observation that the relaxation time diverges because the available degenerate states vanishes rapidly at
T=T_{K}
March and Tosi 2002
.

III.2
Classical particles with indistinguishable energy levels

Starting with,

t_{j}(n_{j},g_{j})={\sum}_{k=1}^{g_{j}}{n_{j}\brace k}

(6)

where in combinatorics, the bracket
{n_{j}\brace k}
represents the Stirling numbers of the second kind
Zwillinger 1996
. Equation (
6
) describes the number of ways to partition
n_{j}
distinct particles into at most
g_{j}
indistinguishable sub-states. Since the states can be empty without any restrictions on the occupancy, the sum over the possible number of occupied subsets is taken to a maximum value of
g
. To find the distribution function
n(\epsilon)
, Equation (
4
) is applied to Equation (
6
) and
\mathcal{S}_{l}
maximized, with the usual Lagrange multipliers
\alpha,\beta\epsilon_{j}
to enforce the conservation of
N
and
U
Kardar 2007
. Thus, we evaluate the
n(\epsilon)
in two distinct thermodynamic regimes.

III.2.1
Small Degeneracy (
n_{j}\gg g_{j}
)

In this regime, the number of particles vastly outnumbers the available sub-states. Furthermore, since the subsets are indistinguishable, the particles are forced to occupy all available states, and the sum is heavily dominated by the maximum number of partitions,
k=g_{j}
.
The asymptotic behavior of Equation (
6
) when
n_{j}\gg g_{j}
is,

t_{j}(n_{j},g_{j})\approx\frac{{g_{j}}^{n_{j}}}{g_{j}!}

(7)

The configurational weight for level
j
after using Stirling’s approximation
\ln x!\approx x\ln x-x
, becomes,

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{t_{j}}{n_{j}!}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\ln\left(\frac{g_{j}^{n_{j}}}{g_{j}!n_{j}!}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\approx n_{j}\ln g_{j}-\ln g_{j}!-(n_{j}\ln n_{j}-n_{j})

After taking the derivative with respect to
n_{j}
to maximize
\mathcal{S}_{l}
,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial n_{j}}=k_{B}\left(\ln g_{j}-\ln n_{j}\right)=-k_{B}\ln\left(\frac{n_{j}}{g_{j}}\right)

(9)

and equating the expression above to the Lagrange multipliers
\alpha+\beta\epsilon_{j}
,

-k_{B}\ln\left(\frac{n_{j}}{g_{j}}\right)=\alpha+\beta\epsilon_{j}

(10)

Subsequently, the correpondence between
\alpha,\beta
with the fugacity
z=e^{\mu/(k_{B}T)}
and
\beta=1/T
holds as usual, and the final expression becomes,

\boxed{n_{j}(\epsilon_{j})=g_{j}ze^{\frac{-\epsilon_{j}}{k_{B}T}}}

(11)

As expected, low degeneracies correspond to the
\epsilon_{j}<<k_{B}T
limit and one exactly recovers the standard Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics. This result has a simple interpretation that heavily populated systems have a low probability of having empty states. Thus, the effect of the indistinguishable
g_{j}
states will merely reduce the total phase space volume by an overall global permutation factor
1/g_{j}!
. This shifts the absolute zero of the
\mathcal{S}_{l}
but does not alter the relative shape of the distribution function.

III.2.2
Large Degeneracy (
g_{j}\geq n_{j}
)

The physical implications of the large degeneracy regime are striking, and a detailed discussion of its importance to the glass problem will be provided in the next section. When there are more indistinguishable states than particles, the upper limit of
g_{j}
becomes irrelevent, and the sum over Stirling numbers yields the Bell numbers,
B_{n}
, which counts the total number of partitions of a set of
n
distinct elements,

t_{j}=B_{n_{j}}

(12)

After applying the known asymptotic expansion for
B_{n}
for large
n
,

\ln B_{n}\approx n\ln n-n\ln(\ln n)-n

(13)

The configurational weight becomes,

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{B_{n_{j}}}{n_{j}!}\right)\approx

(14)

\displaystyle(n_{j}\ln n_{j}-n_{j}\ln(\ln n_{j})-n_{j})-(n_{j}\ln n_{j}-n_{j})

\displaystyle=-n_{j}\ln(\ln n_{j})

Taking the derivative with respect to
n_{j}
,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial n_{j}}\approx k_{B}\left(-\ln(\ln n_{j})-\frac{1}{\ln n_{j}}\right)\approx-k_{B}\ln(\ln n_{j})

(15)

and assuming the term
1/\ln n_{j}
vanishes for large
n_{j}
, the same steps can be followed as before,

-\ln(\ln n_{j})=\frac{\epsilon_{j}-\mu}{k_{B}T}

(16)

After exponentiating twice the final expression for distribution function becomes,

\boxed{n_{j}(\epsilon_{j})=\exp\left(ze^{\frac{-\epsilon_{j}}{k_{B}T}}\right)}

(17)

Equation (
17
) while holding
z>0
, does not have the exact form of any previously known in the literature. Other double-exponential distributions such as the Gompertz and Gumbel functions have different acceptable ranges of parameters
Winsor 1932
;
Hansen 2020
. In particular, the Gumbel cumulative distribution function (CDF), is known as the type I extreme value distribution (EVD), which is used to model the maximum values of random variables
Hansen 2020
. It takes on the general mathematical form,

F(x;\mu,\beta)=\exp\left(-e^{-(x-\mu)/\beta}\right)

(18)

The clear difference is that the Gumbel CDF features a double negative exponential, corresponding to the condition
z=-1
, of which is physically impossible in most systems. A Gumbel CDF must approach
1
as
x\to\infty
and
0
as
x\to-\infty
. Moreover, in Equation (
17
)
n_{j}
actually grows as
\epsilon_{j}\to 0
or (if
z>0
), which is characteristic of an occupation number rather than a normalized probability measure.

On the other hand, the Gompertz distribution is typically used to model mortality rates or growth
Winsor 1932
. Its probability density function (PDF) has the form,

f(x;\eta,b)=b\eta e^{bx}\exp\left(-\eta(e^{bx}-1)\right)

(19)

It appears to have a similar structure to the Gumbel CDF, as the Gompertz requires a negative sign in the exponent of the outer exponential to ensure that the probability decays to zero. Clearly Equation (
17
) is an asymptotic result, describing a system where the states are occupied in a way that diverges or saturates differently than a survival model. Moreover, its double exponential structure produces a highly exotic thermodynamic environment that deviates wildly from classical Maxwell-Boltzmann or standard quantum distributions. An obvious point of distress concerning the
g_{j}\geq n_{j}
asymptotic limit is at high energies where there is a saturation of states
n_{j}\to 1
. Physically, this implies a permanent, pressurized background occupation, where even the highest, most inaccessible energy states will be populated by exactly one particle on average. The full solution of Equation (
6
) would naturally cure this undesirable artifact since the
\epsilon_{j}<<k_{B}T
limit described by Equation (
11
) is well behaved at high energies. Evidently, realistic materials can be approximated by a strict, finite bandwith on its single particle energy states, therefore there is no need for an infinite reservoir of particles.

III.3
Quantum particles with indistinguishable energy levels

If both the particles and the sub-states are indistinguishable, the distribution of
n_{j}
identical particles into
g_{j}
identical sub-states with no occupancy restriction is equivalent to integer partitioning. The quantity of interest is,

t_{j}=p_{\leq g_{j}}(n_{j})

(20)

which is the number of integer partitions of
n_{j}
into at most
g_{j}
parts. In order to obtain analytical results, we must evaluate
\mathcal{S}_{j}=k_{B}\ln p_{\leq g_{j}}(n_{j})
for large
n_{j}
, branching into two asymptotic regimes which depend on the limits taken on degeneracy
g_{j}
as before with the classical particles.

III.3.1
Small Degeneracy (
g_{j}\ll n_{j}
)

If the degeneracy
g_{j}
is much smaller than the number of particles, the asymptotic expansion for restricted partitions behaves as

p_{\leq g_{j}}(n_{j})\approx\frac{n_{j}^{g_{j}-1}}{g_{j}!(g_{j}-1)!}

(21)

which simplifies to
\ln t_{j}\approx(g_{j}-1)\ln n_{j}
.
Solving for
n_{j}
, the distribution function takes the form of a classical,
T>>\epsilon_{j}
limit that is similar in form to the Rayleigh-Jeans law,

\boxed{n_{j}(\epsilon)=\frac{k_{B}T(g_{j}-1)}{\epsilon_{j}-\mu}}

(22)

as expected for a quantum system.

III.3.2
Large Degeneracy (
g_{j}\geq n_{j}
)

If the number of sub-states is large, the restriction
g_{j}
becomes irrelevant, and
p_{\leq g_{j}}(n_{j})\approx p(n_{j})
, also known as the unrestricted partition function. Therefore, one can make use of the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic formula
Weisstein 2002
,

\ln t_{j}\approx\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n_{j}}{3}}

(23)

and carry out entropy maximization,

\frac{\partial S}{\partial n_{j}}=k_{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{j}}\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n_{j}}{3}}\right)=\frac{k_{B}\pi}{\sqrt{6n_{j}}}

(24)

Equating this to the Lagrange multipliers and solving for
n_{j}
, the distribution function is:

\boxed{n_{j}(\epsilon)=\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{B}T)^{2}}{6(\epsilon_{j}-\mu)^{2}}}

(25)

Equation (
25
) takes on the form of an inverse squared distribution, reflecting the massive combinatorial growth of partitions for large numbers. Quantum particles occupying indistinguishable energy levels are a highly exotic statistical system where the phase space volume is drastically compressed compared to standard quantum statistical mechanics. Furthermore, in standard thermodynamics, the entropy is extensive,
\mathcal{S}\propto N
. However, in the high-degeneracy regime governed by the Hardy-Ramanujan formula, the entropy scales as,

\mathcal{S}\approx k_{B}\pi\sqrt{\frac{2N}{3}}

(26)

This implies that the additivity of macroscopic subsystems breaks down entirely. Additionally, Equation (
25
) is inverse squared power law where the particles have a much higher probability of occupying highly excited states. In a spatial dimensions where the density of states
\rho(\epsilon)\sim\epsilon^{a}
with
(a\geq 0)
, after integrating
U=\int\epsilon n(\epsilon)\rho(\epsilon)d\epsilon
to find the total internal energy
U
will result in a severe ultraviolet divergence. The system would require a lattice spacing or momentum cutoff to remain physically stable.

Equation (
25
) also contains a singularity at
\epsilon=\mu
that results in extreme particle bunching. Although the standard boson gas exhibits Bose-Einstein condensation where the particles collapse into the ground state energy, the unrestricted, indistinguishable state model allows for a critical occupation away from the ground state. The energy states themselves offer no distinct label to differentiate arrangements, and therefore the system minimizes its free energy by exhibiting extreme macroscopic bunching. Fluctuations in particle number per energy level,
\Delta n^{2}
, would be massive, implying that the system behaves more like a single, macroscopic, collective excitation with a finite kinetic energy.

IV
The glass transition

IV.1
The Entropy
\mathcal{S}
and Heat Capacity,
C_{\mu}

Leaving aside the exotic quantum system, the remaining discussion will focus on the classical system at high degeneracy governed by Equation (
17
) and how it relates to the phenomenology of the SCL glass phases. Therefore, it is neccessary to study the precise thermodynamic behavior of the total configurational entropy
\mathcal{S}
and the heat capacity
C_{\mu}
. Starting from the grand potential,
\Omega
, which is obtained by integrating the expression
n_{j}=-\frac{\partial\Omega_{j}}{\partial\mu}
with Equation (
17
),

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=-\underset{j}{\overset{}{\sum}}\int n_{j}\left(\mu\right)d\mu

(27)

\displaystyle=-\underset{j}{\overset{}{\sum}}\int{}\mathrm{\exp}\left(e^{\left(\mu-\epsilon_{j}\right)/(k_{B}T)}\right)d\mu

\displaystyle=\underset{j}{\overset{}{-k_{B}T\sum}}\rm{Ei}\left(e^{\left(\mu-\epsilon_{j}\right)/(k_{B}T)}\right)

where
\rm{Ei}(x)
is the exponential integal
Zwillinger 1996
. Next the configurational entropy is given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial T}\right)_{V,\mu}

(28)

\displaystyle=k_{B}\sum_{j}\text{Ei}\left(e^{\frac{\mu-\epsilon_{j}}{k_{B}T}}\right)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j}(\mu-\epsilon_{j})\exp\left({e^{\frac{\mu-\epsilon_{j}}{k_{B}T}}}\right)

\displaystyle=k_{B}\sum_{j}\left[\text{Ei}(y_{j})-e^{y_{j}}\ln y_{j}\right]

where the scaling parameter is defined as
y_{j}=\exp\left(\frac{\mu-\epsilon_{j}}{kT}\right)
. Subsequently, the heat capacity at constant
\mu
follows,

C_{\mu}=T\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial T}=\frac{1}{kT^{2}}\sum_{j}(\epsilon_{j}-\mu)^{2}(\Delta n_{j})^{2}

(29)

where the variance in the energy state occupations, is defined as,

(\Delta n_{j})^{2}=kT\frac{\partial n_{j}}{\partial\mu}=y_{j}\exp(y_{j})

(30)

IV.2
The Kauzmann Temperature,
T_{K}

IV.2.1
Asymptotic limit
T\to 0

Now, I will examine the asymptotic limit
T\to 0
and attempt to solve for
T_{K}
. For discrete energy levels, it is convenient to separate the ground state
j=0
from the excited states
j>0
, and define an energy gap
\Delta\epsilon_{j}=\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{0}>0
. As
\mu\to\epsilon_{0}
the ground state
N_{0}=e^{y_{0}}
is occupied by more particles. A more suitable form of the scaling parameter becomes,

y_{j}=\exp\left(\frac{\mu-\epsilon_{0}}{kT}-\frac{\Delta\epsilon_{j}}{kT}\right)=y_{0}e^{-\Delta\epsilon_{j}/kT}

(31)

The primary task is to analzye the function
f(y)=\text{Ei}(y)-e^{y}\ln y
and study the behavior of both the ground state and excited states when
y\to 0
, and
T\to 0
. One can apply the series expansion
\text{Ei}(y)=\gamma+\ln y+y+O(y^{2})
and
e^{y}\approx 1+y
,

f(y_{j})\approx\gamma+y_{j}-y_{j}\ln y_{j}

(32)

and after substituting Equation (
31
), the leading contribution to
\mathcal{S}_{j}
as
T\to 0
is dominated by the energy gap,

\mathcal{S}_{j}\approx k_{B}\left[\gamma+y_{0}\left(\frac{\Delta\epsilon_{j}}{kT}\right)e^{-\Delta\epsilon_{j}/kT}\right]

(33)

For
j=0
one can apply the asymptotic expansion
\text{Ei}(y)\approx\frac{e^{y}}{y}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=k_{B}\left[\text{Ei}(\ln N_{0})-N_{0}\ln(\ln N_{0})\right]

(34)

\displaystyle\approx k_{B}\left[\frac{N_{0}}{\ln N_{0}}-N_{0}\ln(\ln N_{0})\right]

Apparently, since
N_{0}\ln(\ln N_{0})
grows much faster than
N_{0}/\ln N_{0}
,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
plunges into a macroscopically large negative value at some unphysical threshold value of
T
. Therefore, to find a meaningful, finite bound for
T_{K}
, I will abandon the
T\to 0
discrete ground state approximation. Instead, I will consider a continuous density of states
\rho(\epsilon)
that will allow the particles to distribute over a macroscopic energy band, preventing the mathematical singularity of a single state condensation.

IV.2.2
Finite
T_{K}

In the continuum limit the total number of states is
\mathcal{N}=\int\rho(\epsilon)d\epsilon
, and therefore the discretized entropy of Equation (
28
) is replaced by the following continuous entropy,

\mathcal{S}=k_{B}\int\rho(\epsilon)\left[\text{Ei}(y(\epsilon))-y(\epsilon)e^{y(\epsilon)}\left(\frac{\mu-\epsilon}{kT}\right)\right]d\epsilon

(35)

To evaluate this near a finite
T_{K}
, we exploit the pseudo-Fermi surface of the high energy occupied states of the double-exponential at
\epsilon=\mu
and
y=1
. Consequently, equation (
35
) can be split into two distinct thermodynamic regimes that compete with one another. The high energy contributions,
\epsilon>\mu
and
y(\epsilon)\to 0
, are given by the expression,

\mathcal{S}_{\text{high}}\approx k_{B}\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\rho(\epsilon)\gamma d\epsilon\approx k_{B}\gamma\mathcal{N}_{>\mu}

(36)

where
\gamma\approx 0.577
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant
Weisstein 2002
. The saturation of
n_{j}\to 1
acts like a surjective constraint, forcing a minimum occupancy across the entire spectrum and acting as a reservoir of positive structural entropy. On the other hand, the low energy states,
\epsilon<\mu
and
y(\epsilon)\gg 1
, yield the negative entropy contribution,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{low}}

\displaystyle\approx-k_{B}\int_{-\infty}^{\mu}\rho(\epsilon)e^{y(\epsilon)}\left(\frac{\mu-\epsilon}{k_{B}T}\right)d\epsilon

(37)

\displaystyle\approx-k_{B}N\left(\frac{\mu-\langle\epsilon\rangle}{k_{B}T}\right)

where the
\text{Ei}(y)
term is completely eclipsed by the negative
ye^{y}\ln y
term. Moreover,
n(\epsilon)=\rho(\epsilon)e^{y(\epsilon)}
leads to an average condensation energy
\langle\epsilon\rangle
,
where the vast majority of particles are condensed with energies below
\mu
. The final equation,
S_{\text{high}}+S_{\text{low}}=0
is left to be solved,

k_{B}\gamma\mathcal{N}_{>\mu}-k_{B}N\left(\frac{\mu-\langle\epsilon\rangle}{k_{B}T_{K}}\right)\approx 0

(38)

yielding a closed form expression for the Kauzmann temperature,

\boxed{T_{K}\approx\frac{N}{\mathcal{N}_{>\mu}}\frac{(\mu-\langle\epsilon\rangle)}{k_{B}\gamma}}

(39)

Equation (
39
) is remarkably simple yet profound, providing much needed insight into the microscopic origins of the glass transition.
T_{K}
scales with
N/\mathcal{N}_{>\mu}
, which is the inverse of the fraction of particles with energies
\epsilon>\mu
. In order to satisfy the requirement of
T_{K}>0
, it is assumed that
\langle\epsilon\rangle<\mu
, which is satisified at lower
T
where most of the particles are trapped in deep energy wells far below
\mu
. Coincidently, this entropy crisis is mathematically similar to one displayed by Derrida’s Random Energy Model (REM), which is an SG system with

Conclusion

Ultimately, the basic question of how the distinguishability of energy states affects the macroscopic thermodynamics of both classical and quantum systems was addressed by a mathematically rigorous approach involving enumerative combinatorics. The results were not a simple variation of existing statistical mechanical theories. Furthermore, unlike previous attempts to expand the traditional realm of distribution functions, it was not shown to be a special case of parastatistics, anyons in higher dimensions, or q-deformations, rather it is a fundamentally new combinatorial constraint
