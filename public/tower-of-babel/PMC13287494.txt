GPRF-HPNet Physics-guided texture-aware fusion for real-world underwater image enhancement
Authors: Jian Xu, Miss Laiha Mat Kiah, Rafidah Md Noor, Lei Xu, Chengjun Xu, Yanyong Wu, Lip Yee Por
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Underwater images suffer from wavelength dependent attenuation and multiple scattering, which often lead to severe color casts, veiling effects, and reduced contrast. These degradations weaken the stability of key vision modules such as feature detection, feature matching, edge extraction, and object recognition, and ultimately compromise applications including visual navigation, structural inspection, and environmental monitoring for underwater robots. To improve global color consistency while preserving local texture details, we propose a Physics-Guided Texture-Aware Fusion for Real-World Underwater Image Enhancement (GPRF-HPNet). In the preprocessing stage, a YCbCr domain attenuation map is exploited to guide color correction, followed by entropy driven dual histogram global contrast enhancement. The resulting intermediate images are further combined through gradient weighted wavelet fusion, which retains structural information and fine scale details. High frequency Gabor texture maps at four orientations, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0^\circ\:,45^\circ\:,90^\circ\:$$\end{document} and 135°, are then constructed as an explicit detail prior. These maps feed a texture branch that runs in paralle with a base branch focusing on structure and color. A parallel residual fusion unit performs joint feature extraction on the two branches, learns adaptive weights, and produces fused feature representations, after which a lightweight decoder reconstructs the enhanced image. Extensive experiments on the Color-Check7, Test-C60 and UCCS datasets demonstrate that the proposed method achieves consistent gains on six metrics, including UIQM and UCIQE and delivers more reliable performance in downstream tasks such as geometric rotation estimation and edge detection, while demonstrating strong generalization across diverse underwater scenes.

Introduction

The world’s oceans occupy close to 71% of the planet’s surface and are closely linked to human activities and well-being1. Underwater imaging has become a core enabling technology for the exploitation and stewardship of marine resources, allowing humans to perceive and explore the underwater environment more intuitively2. It has important application value in autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) navigation3, marine biodiversity monitoring4, underwater archaeology, and pipeline inspection5. However, the selective attenuation and multiple scattering of light in water cause short wavelengths (e.g., blue) to penetrate farther, while long wavelengths (e.g., red) are rapidly absorbed. In addition, scattering by suspended particulates and microorganisms further degrades image contrast and visibility, ultimately leading to severe color casts, haze, and detail loss in underwater images. These effects directly impair the performance of downstream tasks such as object recognition, obstacle avoidance, and 3D reconstruction6–8.

Existing underwater image enhancement techniques are commonly grouped into three families9: non-physical based models, physical based models, and deep learning based models. Non-physical approaches improve contrast and details by directly manipulating pixel distributions, typically via histogram equalization, Retinex, or local stretching. For instance, Zhang et al.10 proposed a Retinex inspired color correction with detail preservation, which mitigates color casts, blur, and low contrast caused by scattering and shows strong performance in both qualitative and quantitative evaluations across diverse scenarios. Ancuti et al.11 developed a single-image enhancement algorithm based on multi-scale fusion and adaptive weight mapping for underwater imaging, mitigating color casts, low contrast, and detail blurring caused by medium scattering and absorption. Their method yields significant improvements in dark-region exposure, global contrast, and edge sharpness, and further enhances the accuracy of image segmentation and keypoint matching. Fu et al.8 proposed a global-local dual branch network for underwater image enhancement that simultaneously compensates global color casts and local contrast degradation, thereby addressing color distortion and detail blurring caused by absorption and scattering, and achieving notable color correction and contrast improvement within a lightweight architecture.

Physical model-based methods estimate scene radiance and background light using the atmospheric scattering model or water absorption scattering models to restore color and visibility. For instance, Peng et al.5 proposed an underwater image enhancement algorithm based on a U-shaped Transformer to improve the recovery of uneven attenuation across color channels and spatial regions, overcoming inconsistencies in channel and region specific processing seen in traditional approaches and achieving more than a 2 dB PSNR gain on multiple benchmark datasets, with qualitative results superior to state-of-the-art methods. Zhang et al.12 introduced an enhancement algorithm grounded in channel discrepancy compensation and adaptive contrast stretching for underwater images, tackling color bias and insufficient contrast due to scattering and obtaining at least a 5% average improvement across standard benchmark metrics. Song et al.13 proposed an underwater enhancement algorithm that leverages a manually annotated background light database together with an improved dark channel prior to jointly estimate background light and multi-channel transmission, restoring image color and contrast while addressing inaccurate background light estimation and high computational complexity in prior methods. Their approach delivers higher background light estimation accuracy, markedly reduced runtime, and overall enhancement performance superior to existing techniques. Chiang and Chen14 presented the WCID method, which builds on dark channel dehazing while incorporating depth estimation and artificial light compensation for underwater enhancement, addressing visibility reduction and color distortion arising from scattering, wavelength dependent attenuation, and artificial illumination, and yielding significant gains in contrast, color correction, and visibility restoration.

Despite substantial progress in contrast enhancement and color correction, the above methods often rely on prior models and complex parameter tuning, making it difficult to maintain stable performance across diverse underwater environments. Consequently, recent work has increasingly adopted end-to-end deep learning techniques, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and generative adversarial networks (GANs), to automatically learn the nonlinear mapping from degraded to enhanced images, thereby improving adaptability and robustness. For example, Chen et al.15 proposed a perception oriented enhancement algorithm that integrates a deep enhancement network with a detector aware feedback mechanism for underwater image pre-processing to support subsequent detection tasks, addressing the lack of interaction between enhancement and detection modules in traditional pipelines and achieving simultaneous gains in visual quality and detection performance. Wang et al.16 introduced UIE-Net, an end-to-end CNN framework for underwater image enhancement that overcomes failures in specific scenarios while jointly accomplishing color correction and dehazing. Yeh et al.17 presented an unsupervised joint training strategy based on CycleGAN for underwater enhancement networks, alleviating slow convergence and overfitting stemming from data scarcity and the absence of ground-truth supervision, and producing accelerated convergence with improved enhancement accuracy. Shen et al.18 proposed UDAformer, a dual attention transformer for underwater enhancement that addresses difficulties in simultaneously extracting channel and pixel level features under non-uniform degradation and color channel loss, delivering qualitative and quantitative performance improvements over state-of-the-art methods on both real and synthetic datasets.

Although the aforementioned approaches have achieved notable results in underwater image enhancement, several challenges remain:

Challenge 1. Most existing methods are grounded in a single perspective. Physical model-based approaches rely on optical attenuation and scattering priors, which ensure a degree of physical consistency but struggle to capture high level semantic information. Deep learning-based methods can automatically extract rich semantics but are susceptible to dataset bias, often lacking physical constraints. Direct concatenation or weighted fusion tends to introduce redundant or mutually exclusive features, weakening enhancement quality and compromising model stability. How to preserve physical priors while effectively mining deep semantics to achieve complementary strengths remains a key open challenge.

Challenge 2. Traditional attention mechanisms are still limited in multi feature cross fusion. The channel and spatial features extracted by different branches vary significantly in scale and distribution. A single attention module cannot simultaneously accommodate all critical multi source features, which easily introduces redundancy and substantially increases computational complexity.

Challenge 3. Many existing models exhibit limited enhancement effectiveness and generalization across diverse datasets, primarily due to pronounced differences in degradation mechanisms and illumination conditions between standard synthetic and real-world environments. For example, while many models achieve high enhancement performance on synthetic datasets (e.g., SOTS, PSNR ≈ 28.5 dB, SSIM ≈ 0.90), on real turbid underwater scenes (e.g., UIEB) the same models often drop to PSNR ≈ 26.2 dB and SSIM ≈ 0.82, with UCIQE and UIQM decreasing from approximately 0.55/2.40 to 0.47/2.00.

To address the above challenges, we propose the Physics-Guided Texture-Aware Fusion for Real-World Underwater Image Enhancement (GPRF-HPNet). In the preprocessing stage, the method integrates physical priors with multi scale features by performing attenuation-map-guided color correction in YCbCr space, entropy driven dual-histogram global contrast optimization, and weighted wavelet based local detail enhancement to produce a preprocessed image that balances structure and texture. In the backbone network, the preprocessed result and high-frequency Gabor texture maps are encoded in parallel, and a feature cross-attention mechanism is introduced to dynamically exploit the complementarity between the two streams, achieving deep fusion of global semantics and local details. Unlike existing methods that separately emphasize color correction, contrast enhancement, or deep feature learning, the key novelty of this work lies in a physics-guided texture-aware fusion paradigm. The proposed framework first uses physically inspired preprocessing to regularize the degraded underwater image into a more stable color-structure representation, and then introduces four-direction Gabor texture priors to explicitly describe directional high-frequency details. Instead of directly concatenating handcrafted priors and deep features, the proposed parallel residual fusion mechanism adaptively controls the interaction between the structure-color branch and the texture branch. This design provides a more interpretable way to integrate physical degradation cues, directional texture priors, and deep feature fusion, thereby improving color consistency, local texture restoration, and cross-scene generalization in real-world underwater environments.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

(1) We propose a Physics-Guided Texture-Aware Fusion for Real-World Underwater Image Enhancement (GPRF-HPNet). The proposed model effectively integrates physics-inspired priors with deep semantic representations. Specifically, we design a parallel dual-branch architecture, where the first branch is constructed upon traditional physical-model-based processing, and the second branch is built upon deep learning-based enhancement. The effective fusion of these two heterogeneous branches exploits their complementary feature characteristics, thereby strengthening the model’s feature learning capability.

(2) We propose an attention mechanism founded on cross-feature fusion, which fully integrates features from the physical model and deep learning branches, effectively suppresses irrelevant features, reduces redundancy, and focuses on salient cues, thereby further enhancing feature learning.

(3) Experiments on the real-world underwater datasets Color-Check7, Test-C60 and UCCS show that GPRF-HPNet achieves average improvements of 15% on the six quality metrics UIQM, UCIQE, AG, IE, VAR and MA, In addition, the application-level SIFT feature-point detection test shows that the proposed method produces richer and more stable local key points, validating its effectiveness, generalization capability, and potential value for downstream feature-based underwater visual perception tasks.

Related work

Non-physical model-based methods

Non-physical model-based methods directly adjust color balance, contrast, and brightness via pixel-level transformations or filtering without modeling underwater optical processes19. For example, Gao et al.20 proposed an adaptive retinal enhancement algorithm inspired by the morphology and function of fish retinas to simultaneously correct blur and non-uniform color casts in underwater images, addressing limitations of traditional methods in color correction and edge sharpening, and achieving markedly superior edge contrast and color fidelity in both qualitative and quantitative evaluations. Ma et al.21 introduced a Retinex variational model incorporating information-entropy smoothing and a non-uniform illumination prior for underwater image enhancement, overcoming inaccurate reflectance–illumination separation under complex, non-uniform lighting and delivering superior quantitative and subjective performance across diverse illumination conditions, although robustness under extreme turbidity, motion blur, and ultra-low-light scenarios still requires improvement. Li et al.22 proposed an underwater enhancement algorithm that combines adaptive color restoration with haze-line dehazing to jointly compensate color distortion and remove haze, addressing the difficulty of balancing color fidelity and visibility, and achieving qualitative and quantitative gains on public datasets such as ChinaMM, SQUID, and UIEB. Song et al.23 presented a frequency-domain-prior latent diffusion model (FD-LDM) for underwater enhancement, overcoming the limited learning and representational capacity of traditional spatial-domain methods that neglect frequency information, and yielding significant improvements across diverse color-cast scenarios. Yang et al.24 proposed an underwater enhancement method based on Golden Jackal Optimization that adaptively optimizes color compensation and contrast in RGB and Lab color spaces, addressing severe color casts and low contrast and attaining excellent color recovery and texture-detail enhancement across a wide range of degradations.

Physical model-based methods

Physical model-based methods simulate the attenuation and scattering of light in water and suspended particulates and employ atmospheric scattering or absorption scattering models to estimate ambient illumination and transmission, thereby enabling more accurate dehazing and color correction25. For instance, Wu et al.26 proposed an underwater enhancement algorithm grounded in white-degradation modeling with adaptive color restoration and histogram equalization, addressing color shifts and contrast attenuation induced by absorption and scattering and achieving an effective balance among computational efficiency, color recovery, and robustness across multiple sea areas. Dong et al.27 developed an enhancement approach integrating RGB and Lab color models to mitigate color casts and low contrast caused by scattering and absorption, producing natural and realistic color restoration with high visibility. Tao et al.28 introduced an algorithm based on a maximum red-channel attenuation prior and multi-scale detail fusion to compensate wavelength-dependent absorption and scattering, addressing shortcomings in color correction and detail recovery and yielding significant improvements in color fidelity, global contrast, and local detail on multiple benchmark datasets. Zhu et al.29 proposed a wavelet-fusion method that combines color compensation, an improved dark channel prior, and unsharp masking (USM) for underwater enhancement, overcoming color casts and visibility loss due to absorption and markedly improving image visibility, texture details, and edge information on public datasets. Liang et al.30 presented an attenuation-map-guided color correction and multi-scale gradient-domain detail enhancement algorithm for single-image underwater restoration, addressing color shift and detail loss caused by absorption and scattering and achieving superior color fidelity and detail recovery under varying illumination conditions.

Deep learning model-based methods

Deep learning has been widely applied to underwater image enhancement owing to its capacity for autonomous feature learning31. Cong et al.32 proposed PUGAN, a physics-guided generative adversarial network for underwater enhancement that addresses the challenge of jointly ensuring perceptual aesthetics and physical consistency, achieving notable gains in PSNR and SSIM on multiple benchmark datasets, nevertheless, insufficient attention to degraded key regions and non-adaptive fusion remain issues. Liang et al.33 introduced NPT-UL, a framework based on non-physical transformations and unsupervised learning, which alleviates poor visual perception and over-enhancement in traditional algorithms and delivers substantial improvements on no-reference metrics such as UCIQE, UIQM, CFD, and BC, while limitations persist regarding physical consistency with real degradation mechanisms and the fidelity of structural details. Li et al.34 proposed CFD-Net, a contrastive feature disentanglement method with physical priors for underwater enhancement, addressing the domain gap between synthetic and real data, through multi-stream decomposition and cross-domain contrastive losses, it achieves precise separation of background, transmission, and ambient-light components and surpasses existing techniques on multiple quantitative and qualitative metrics, though challenges remain in recovering weak details and adapting to extremely turbid scenes. Liu et al.35 presented Rep-UWNet, a lightweight model employing structural re-parameterization and a multi-scale hybrid convolutional attention module to reduce parameter counts and accelerate inference, cutting parameters from 2.7 M to 0.45 M (≈ 83% reduction) while outperforming state-of-the-art methods in both objective and subjective quality; however, limitations persist in extremely turbid or low-light conditions and in generalization to more complex underwater scenarios. Huang et al.36 proposed a multi-scale cascaded Transformer with Adaptive Group Attention for underwater enhancement, addressing the large model size and deployment difficulty of conventional Transformers, achieving enhancement performance comparable to advanced methods across multiple public datasets with substantial model compression, yet still exhibiting performance degradation in extreme aquatic environments and requiring further improvement in real-time inference speed.

Recently, underwater image enhancement has further evolved toward Transformer-based, Mamba-based, diffusion-based, and frequency-domain learning frameworks. These methods improve global contextual modeling, long-range dependency representation, and frequency-aware feature extraction, which are beneficial for restoring visibility and structural details in degraded underwater scenes. However, several limitations remain. First, Transformer- and Mamba-based methods usually require careful architectural design to balance global modeling capacity and computational cost. Second, diffusion-based enhancement methods may provide strong generative capability but often involve iterative sampling, which can increase inference burden. Third, purely data-driven global modeling may still be insufficient to explicitly compensate wavelength-dependent attenuation, scattering, and directional texture degradation in real underwater environments. In contrast, our GPRF-HPNet combines physics-inspired hybrid preprocessing with four-direction Gabor texture priors and parallel residual fusion, aiming to achieve a more interpretable balance among color correction, contrast enhancement, and local texture restoration.

In addition, recent studies in remote sensing vision, such as SCAFNet37 for remote sensing image change detection and ORSI salient object detection38 via progressive interaction and saliency-guided enhancement, also demonstrate the effectiveness of semantic compensation, adaptive fusion, progressive interaction, and saliency-guided enhancement in complex visual scenes. Although these works are not designed for underwater image enhancement and therefore are not included as direct quantitative baselines, their concepts of adaptive feature fusion and progressive enhancement are conceptually relevant to our motivation of integrating structure-color cues with directional texture priors.

Method

In this section, Fig. 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the Gabor-Based Parallel Residual Fusion Network with Hybrid Preprocessing for Underwater Image Enhancement (GPRF-HPNet). First, the raw underwater image is processed by the designed preprocessing module to obtain a preprocessed image that concurrently carries global and local contrast, color-correction, and multi-scale structural information, and a set of multi-orientation (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) high-frequency texture feature maps. Subsequently, these preprocessing results are used as complementary inputs and are fed in parallel into four cascaded reinforced fusion units. At each stage, the base branch (global structure + color) and the texture branch (high-frequency details) are separately encoded, fusion weights are generated, and a proportionally weighted aggregation is performed, thereby realizing multi-step, progressive interaction between macro- and micro-level information. Finally, the output of Parallel Residual Fusion Unit 4 (PRFU4) is combined via element-wise multiplication and a global residual connection to produce the final enhanced image. During end-to-end training, the network jointly employs Charbonnier loss, structural similarity (SSIM) loss, and perceptual loss to ensure a coordinated enhancement of global color, structure, and multi-directional high-frequency information. Our GPRF-HPNet both preserves color and contrast optimization under physical priors and fully integrates multi-scale, high-frequency visual details, effectively addressing the difficulty of traditional single-module designs in simultaneously maintaining global consistency and local texture39.

Data preprocessing

Motivation

As shown in Fig. 2, the motivation of this subsection is to generate a preprocessed image that removes color casts while accommodating both global and local contrast and preserving high-frequency details, thereby providing rich feature inputs for subsequent deep networks. Targeting the color shifts, insufficient contrast, and texture loss caused by spectrally selective attenuation and multiple scattering in underwater images, we first perform attenuation-map–guided adaptive correction for different channels in the YCbCr space to rectify bluish, greenish, or yellowish casts. Subsequently, we apply in parallel an entropy-driven dual-histogram stretching strategy40 and a block-mean–driven enhancement strategy in the CIELab space10, which together expand the dynamic range while preserving fine textures. Finally, through multi-scale wavelet decomposition combined with average-gradient weighting, we reconstruct and fuse the low and high-frequency subbands to suppress artifacts and faithfully convey texture information. The preprocessed image obtained by the above pipeline exhibits significant improvements over the original in color balance, contrast, and detail richness, thereby laying the foundation for feature extraction and end-to-end deep learning fusion in the subsequent Parallel Reinforcement Fusion Unit (PRFU) module. Each preprocessing component plays a distinct and complementary role. The YCbCr attenuation-map-guided color correction mainly addresses wavelength-dependent color deviation by compensating chrominance channels under luminance guidance, thereby providing a stable color basis. The entropy-driven dual-histogram enhancement improves global visibility and contrast while reducing the risk of information loss caused by conventional histogram equalization. The integral-image-based local contrast enhancement strengthens local edges and weak textures with low computational overhead. Finally, the gradient-weighted wavelet fusion adaptively integrates global contrast, local structure, and high-frequency details in a multi-scale manner. Therefore, the hybrid preprocessing module is not an independent visual enhancement trick, but a physically inspired feature preparation stage that reduces the difficulty of subsequent network learning under limited real-world underwater data.

Entropy-driven global contrast enhancement

After the above color correction, the overall image contrast remains relatively low. Although traditional histogram equalization44 can expand the grayscale dynamic range, it merges multiple grayscale/color levels and thereby reduces image information entropy, which in turn leads to the loss of fine textures and details. Weighted wavelet-based perceptual fusion

Following the preprocessing of the global correction image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{GE}$$\end{document} and the local enhancement image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{LE}$$\end{document}, we adopt a strategy that combines wavelet decomposition with gradient-aware weighting47, adaptively fusing multi-scale low-frequency structures and high-frequency textures to serve as inputs to the Parallel Reinforcement Fusion Unit (PRFU). Specifically, each image undergoes an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l$$\end{document}-level first-order wavelet decomposition, from which the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l$$\end{document}-th-level low-frequency approximation coefficients are extracted:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{W}_{\phi\:}^{\left(l\right)}\left(m,n\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\sum\:_{x,y}I\left(x,y\right){\phi\:}_{m,n}\left(x,y\right),\end{array}$$\end{document}

and the high-frequency detail coefficients in three directions (vertical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:V$$\end{document}, horizontal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H$$\end{document}, and diagonal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D$$\end{document}) are given by:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{W}_{{\psi\:}_{k}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(m,n\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\sum\:_{x,y}I\left(x,y\right){\psi\:}_{k,m,n}\left(x,y\right),\:k\in\:\left\{V,H,D\right\}.\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:I\left(x,y\right)$$\end{document} denotes the grayscale value at pixel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(x,y\right)$$\end{document} of the preprocessed image (color-corrected with global and local contrast enhancement). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}_{m,n}\left(x,y\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{k,m,n}\left(x,y\right)$$\end{document} denote the scale function and directional detail wavelet functions, respectively, in two-dimensional wavelet decomposition. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m,n$$\end{document} are the spatial indices of the coefficient maps at level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l$$\end{document} along the horizontal and vertical directions. On this basis, we evaluate the richness of details in each reconstructed detail subband via the mean gradient:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{G}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}=\frac{1}{\left(M-1\right)\left(N-1\right)}\sum\:_{i,j}\sqrt{{\left({\partial\:}_{x}{J}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}\left(i,j\right)\right)}^{2}+{\left({\partial\:}_{y}{J}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}\left(i,j\right)\right)}^{2}},\end{array}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}\left(i,j\right)$$\end{document} denotes the intensity at pixel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(i,j\right)$$\end{document} of the single-channel detail image reconstructed from the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l$$\end{document}-th-level high-frequency coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{{\psi\:}_{k}}^{\left(l\right)}$$\end{document} via inverse wavelet transform. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\partial\:}_{x}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\partial\:}_{y}$$\end{document} are finite-difference approximations along the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively. To grant greater influence to the most detail-rich subband, the weighting coefficients are obtained as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{\lambda\:}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}=1+\frac{{G}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}}{{G}_{V}^{\left(l\right)}+{G}_{H}^{\left(l\right)}+{G}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}},\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:G}_{V}^{\left(l\right)},{G}_{H}^{\left(l\right)}$$\end{document},and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}$$\end{document} denote the average gradient energies of the level-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l$$\end{document} subbands in the vertical (V), horizontal (H), and diagonal (D) directions, respectively, they measure the abundance of details in each direction and are all strictly positive, thereby ensuring that the second term in (21) is positive and consequently \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}^{\left(l\right)}>1$$\end{document}. This property avoids suppressing any subband while preferentially amplifying the direction with the most salient differences. Finally, during fusion and reconstruction, each high-frequency coefficient map is multiplied by its corresponding weight and combined with the low-frequency coefficients for inverse wavelet transform and up sampling, the weighted wavelet-fused image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{FE}$$\end{document} is thus obtained:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{I}_{FE}=\sum\:_{l=1}^{J}{U}_{{2}^{l-1}}\left({W}_{\phi\:}^{\left(l\right)},{\lambda\:}_{V}^{\left(l\right)}{W}_{{\psi\:}_{V}}^{\left(l\right)},{\lambda\:}_{H}^{\left(l\right)}{W}_{{\psi\:}_{H}}^{\left(l\right)},{\lambda\:}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}{W}_{{\psi\:}_{D}}^{\left(l\right)}\right),\end{array}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{w}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\hspace{0.25em}{U}_{{2}^{l-1}}\mathrm{\:denotes\:the\:reconstruction\:plus\:up\:sampling\:operator\:at\:the\:corresponding\:scale.}$$\end{document}

As shown in Fig. 3, the architecture comprises two parallel branches. The first branch consists of a preprocessing adapter, ResNet18, and the CBAM module, and is used to extract globally perceptive color and structural features. The second branch consists of an enhancement adapter, ResNet18 as the backbone network, and the CBAM module, and is used to extract high-frequency detail and texture features. Subsequently, a fusion module generates a control matrix to perform weighted fusion of the features from the two branches, yielding the fused representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({I}_{1},{I}_{2}\right)$$\end{document} that simultaneously accounts for macroscopic structure and local details, thereby achieving the final underwater image enhancement. To make the implementation of PRFU clearer, we further summarize its processing flow as follows. Given a preprocessed image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{1}$$\end{document}and a high-frequency texture map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{2}$$\end{document}, the two inputs are first mapped by the preprocessing adapter and enhancement adapter, respectively, to make their channel dimensions compatible with the subsequent ResNet18 encoder. The two encoded features are then recalibrated by CBAM to enhance informative channel and spatial responses. Afterwards, the structure-color branch and texture branch separately generate normalized attention maps through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:1$$\end{document}convolution and Sigmoid activation. These attention maps are added element-wise to obtain the fusion-intensity control matrix, which adaptively controls the contribution of global structure and local texture information. Finally, the control matrix is multiplied element-wise with the baseline-branch feature to generate a 16-channel fused feature map for the subsequent G-PRFU module.

For ease of exposition, we take \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{1}={I}_{FE}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{2}$$\end{document} as an example to introduce PRFU. This module mainly comprises two parts: baseline feature extraction and attention-coefficient generation (Sect. 3.2.1–3.2.2), and texture feature extraction and fusion-intensity control (Sect. 3.2.3) The details are as follows.

Fused feature generation

Finally, the baseline-branch output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{b}$$\end{document} and the fusion-weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{M}_{f}$$\end{document} are multiplied element-wise across the channel and spatial dimensions to obtain the final fused feature map:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}O\left({I}_{1},{I}_{2}\right)={I}_{b}\hspace{0.25em}\otimes\:\hspace{0.25em}{M}_{f}\hspace{0.25em}\in\:\hspace{0.25em}{\mathbb{R}}^{16\times\:h\times\:w},\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\otimes\:$$\end{document} denotes element-wise multiplication. The resulting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({I}_{1},{I}_{2}\right)$$\end{document} preserves the color and structural information of the globally preprocessed image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{1}$$\end{document} while incorporating the fine details extracted from the high-frequency texture map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{2}$$\end{document}.

Overall, PRFU consists of two parallel branches. The first branch takes the globally preprocessed image as input, and the second branch takes the high-frequency texture map as input, both are encoded by ResNet18 + CBAM to extract the corresponding global structural features and local detail features. Subsequently, the features from the two branches undergo channel compression and attention-weight generation, and information fusion is realized through weighted addition and element-wise multiplication. Under a lightweight design, this process adaptively balances macroscopic consistency and microscopic details, and finally outputs a 16-channel fused feature map, providing a superior input for the subsequent parallel residual fusion network.

Fusion and feature extraction

G-PRFU processes the above five inputs sequentially through multi-stage cascaded residual convolution blocks (RE₁–RE₄) and parallel PRFU₁–PRFU₄ fusion operations, and finally outputs a 16-channel fused feature map:38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{F}_{\mathrm{fusion}}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{16\times\:h\times\:w},\:h=\frac{M}{32},w=\frac{N}{32}.\end{array}$$\end{document}

Channel expansion

To enhance the representational capacity for multi-directional textures, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{fusion}}$$\end{document} is fed into a Conv₁×₁ layer, expanding the number of channels from 16 to 32:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{F}^{32}={\phi\:}_{1\times\:1}^{\left(32\right)}\left({F}_{\mathrm{fusion}}\right)\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{32\times\:h\times\:w}.\end{array}$$\end{document}

CBAM attention

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{32}$$\end{document} is fed into the CBAM module48, where channel attention and spatial attention are applied sequentially to obtain attention-weighted features:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{F}_{\mathrm{att}}=\mathrm{C}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{M}\left({F}^{32}\right)\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{32\times\:h\times\:w}.\end{array}$$\end{document}

Enhanced output

Finally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{att}}$$\end{document} is passed through a multi-layer up sampling convolutional decoder with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:3\times\:3$$\end{document} kernels and stride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:=2$$\end{document} to recover the original resolution, yielding the final enhanced image:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}}=UP\left(\begin{array}{c}{F}_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{t}}\end{array}\right)\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times\:N\times\:3},\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{UP}\left(\cdot\:\right)$$\end{document} denotes the upsampling decoder network.

This architecture preserves multi-directional high-frequency details while ensuring global color and structural consistency, thereby providing rich feature support for downstream detail enhancement.

Composite loss

Charbonnier loss

The Charbonnier loss is a robust penalty for measuring pixel-level reconstruction error, it preserves the characteristics of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{1}$$\end{document} norm while remaining insensitive to outliers and maintaining smooth gradients. The Charbonnier loss is defined as:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\mathrm{cha}}=\sqrt{\parallel\:{I}_{\mathrm{en}}-{I}_{\mathrm{gt}}{\parallel\:}_{2}^{2}+{\epsilon}^{2}},\end{array}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{\mathrm{en}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{\mathrm{gt}}$$\end{document} denote the enhanced result and the pseudo ground-truth image, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon\:={10}^{-3}$$\end{document} ensures numerical stability.

SSIM loss

The SSIM loss enforces structural and contrast consistency between the enhanced output and the target, thereby strengthening local structure similarity. The metric is computed as:43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}\mathrm{M}\left(x,y\right)=\frac{\left(2{\mu\:}_{x}{\mu\:}_{y}+{C}_{1}\right)\left(2{\sigma\:}_{xy}+{C}_{2}\right)}{\left({\mu\:}_{x}^{2}+{\mu\:}_{y}^{2}+{C}_{1}\right)\left({\sigma\:}_{x}^{2}+{\sigma\:}_{y}^{2}+{C}_{2}\right)},\end{array}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{x},{\mu\:}_{y}$$\end{document} denote the local means, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{x}^{2},{\sigma\:}_{y}^{2}$$\end{document} are the local variances and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{xy}$$\end{document} is the covariance within the window. The constants are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{1}={\left(0.01L\right)}^{2}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{2}={\left(0.03L\right)}^{2}$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L=1$$\end{document}. For the whole image, we define44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{m}}\left({I}_{\mathrm{g}\mathrm{t}},{I}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}}\right)=1-SSIM\left({I}_{\mathrm{g}\mathrm{t}},{I}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}}\right).\end{array}$$\end{document}.

Perceptual loss

The perceptual loss is used to enforce consistency between the enhanced result and the target in terms of high-level semantics and perceptual structure, thereby avoiding the sacrifice of naturalness and details when only minimizing pixel-wise errors. The formula is given by:45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}}={\parallel\phi\:\left({I}_{\mathrm{g}\mathrm{t}}\right)-\phi\:\left({I}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}}\right)\parallel}_{1}.\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:\left(\cdot\:\right)$$\end{document} denotes a frozen feature extractor.

To strike a balance among pixel reconstruction, structural similarity, and perceptual quality, we adopt the following composite loss:46\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\mathrm{total}}={\lambda\:}_{1}{L}_{\mathrm{cha}}+{\lambda\:}_{2}{L}_{\mathrm{ssim}}+{\lambda\:}_{3}{L}_{\mathrm{per}},\end{array}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{1},{\lambda\:}_{2},{\lambda\:}_{3}$$\end{document} are the weighting hyperparameters of the three loss terms, set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{1}=1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{2}=1.1$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{3}=11$$\end{document}. These values are the optimized settings obtained through extensive repeated experiments.

Experiments

Datasets

We use three public and challenging image datasets: Color-Check711, Test-C6049, and UCCS50 to verify the effectiveness and feasibility of the model.

Color-Check7: contains seven real underwater photographs with a standard color checker, captured by seven different cameras. This dataset provides quantifiable color Refe, which facilitate the evaluation of color restoration accuracy and inter-device consistency, while also allowing observation of an algorithm’s robustness to color casts under different imaging responses.

Test-C60: derived from the public UIEB dataset, comprising 60 difficult underwater images without paired references. The images cover typical degradations such as turbidity, scattering, and backlighting, and are mainly used to examine the effectiveness of enhancement algorithms in improving visibility and reconstructing contrast, as well as their cross-scene generalization. Non-reference metrics such as UIQM and UCIQE are commonly used for quantitative evaluation.

UCCS: consists of 300 real-scene images and is partitioned, according to the mean of the b channel in the CIELAB space, into three subsets, green, cyan-green, and blue to characterize different types of color casts, likewise, no paired references are provided. This dataset focuses on assessing color correction and color stability, enabling grouped comparisons and robustness analysis under different water-optical conditions.

On the Color-Check7 dataset we mainly evaluate color restoration performance, whereas on Test-C60 and UCCS we emphasize analysis of visual performance. Together, the three constitute a complementary evaluation framework covering key metrics such as color correction, visibility improvement, contrast enhancement, and robustness to color casts.

Subsequently, multi-directional Gabor-based detail priors and adaptive fusion of wavelet high-frequency sub-bands explicitly strengthen texture representation and edge structures, reducing the tendency of deep networks to blindly hallucinate high-frequency details. In the network stage, CBAM re-weights features along the channel and spatial dimensions, increasing the proportion of informative responses while suppressing irrelevant activations. As a result, the proposed method improves color naturalness, edge sharpness, and perceptual consistency simultaneously without introducing noticeable artifacts.

In addition to the original variants, including w/o Preprocessing Module, w/o Multi-directional Texture Branch, and w/o Loss function, we further add the w/o CBAM variant and four single-direction Gabor variants, namely 0° only, 45° only, 90° only, and 135° only. These additional settings are used to more specifically evaluate the effectiveness of CBAM attention and the contribution of different directional Gabor texture priors.

As shown in Fig. 8, removing the preprocessing module leaves residual bluish-green color casts and results in insufficient brightness and contrast recovery. The boundaries of the color chart and background tile textures also become less clear. The quantitative results in Table 5 further support this observation. Compared with the w/o Preprocessing Module variant, the complete model improves UIQM from 1.07 to 1.52, with a gain of 0.45. UCIQE increases from 30.75 to 32.20, with a gain of 1.45. AG increases from 17.82 to 18.12, with a gain of 0.30. VAR increases from 13.20 to 17.21, with a gain of 4.01, and MA increases from 8.42 to 8.73, with a gain of 0.31. These results indicate that the preprocessing module effectively improves color consistency, global contrast, and structural detail quality, providing more stable inputs for subsequent network fusion.

When the multi-directional texture branch is removed, the enhanced images can still maintain basic color correction, but local textures and edge sharpness are weakened. The color-chart boundaries, tile seams, and facial details tend to become smoother. Quantitatively, compared with the w/o Multi-directional Texture Branch variant, the complete model improves UIQM by 0.41, AG by 0.25, IE by 0.19, VAR by 3.91, and MA by 0.36. The improvements in VAR and MA are particularly evident, indicating that the multi-directional texture branch not only helps recover local high-frequency details, but also improves perceptual stability and visual naturalness.

When the composite loss function is removed, the model can still perform basic enhancement, but color stability, structural consistency, and perceptual quality are reduced. Compared with the w/o Loss function variant, the complete model improves UIQM by 0.38, UCIQE by 0.09, AG by 0.10, IE by 0.11, VAR by 3.94, and MA by 0.14. This demonstrates that the joint constraint of Charbonnier loss, SSIM loss, and perceptual loss helps maintain pixel-level reconstruction accuracy, structural consistency, and high-level perceptual quality, especially contributing to overall quality stability.

For the CBAM ablation, the w/o CBAM variant performs worse than the complete model across all metrics. Specifically, compared with w/o CBAM, the complete model improves UIQM by 0.43, UCIQE by 2.95, AG by 0.48, IE by 0.87, VAR by 3.95, and MA by 0.62. The improvements in UCIQE, IE, and MA are especially notable, suggesting that CBAM effectively enhances channel-spatial feature recalibration and guides the network to focus on informative regions related to color recovery, structure preservation, and texture enhancement. Without CBAM, irrelevant scattering responses, weak-texture backgrounds, and color-degraded regions are more likely to enter the fusion stage, resulting in reduced color stability and perceptual quality.

For the ablation study of different Gabor directions, single-direction Gabor texture maps can enhance edge and texture responses along specific orientations, but they are insufficient to cover the multi-orientation structures commonly observed in complex underwater scenes. Compared with the 0° only variant, the complete four-direction model improves UIQM, UCIQE, AG, IE, VAR, and MA by 0.09, 1.11, 0.02, 0.29, 4.24, and 0.56, respectively. The 0° only variant achieves an AG value of 18.10, which is close to 18.12 of the complete model, indicating that horizontal textures contribute to restoring color-chart boundaries and background structures in Color-Check7. However, its VAR and MA remain lower than those of the complete model, suggesting that a single direction is insufficient for overall stability and perceptual naturalness.

Compared with the 45° only variant, the complete model improves UIQM, UCIQE, AG, IE, VAR, and MA by 0.15, 0.11, 0.28, 0.09, 3.24, and 0.34, respectively. The 45° only variant shows relatively stable UCIQE and IE values, suggesting that oblique textures help preserve local structures and color transitions, but its overall high-frequency structure recovery remains inferior to that of the complete four-direction model.

Compared with the 90° only variant, the complete model achieves the most obvious improvements, with gains of 0.31, 3.57, 1.99, 1.77, 5.14, and 1.50 on UIQM, UCIQE, AG, IE, VAR, and MA, respectively. This indicates that relying only on a single vertical directional prior is insufficient to cover multi-directional edges, water ripples, and local texture structures in complex underwater degradation scenarios.

Compared with the 135° only variant, the complete model improves UIQM, UCIQE, AG, IE, VAR, and MA by 0.05, 1.10, 0.18, 0.17, 0.39, and 0.10, respectively. The 135° only variant performs relatively strongly in UIQM, VAR, and MA, suggesting that this direction contributes positively to oblique edge recovery and local structural stability. Nevertheless, the complete four-direction model still achieves the best results across all six metrics. This demonstrates that the Gabor texture maps at 0°, 45°, 90°, and 135° provide complementary directional priors, jointly improving edge preservation, local texture clarity, and visual naturalness.

Overall, the ablation results demonstrate that the preprocessing module, multi-directional texture branch, composite loss function, CBAM attention, and four-direction Gabor texture priors all contribute positively to the final enhancement performance. By integrating physics-inspired preprocessing, attention-guided fusion, and multi-directional high-frequency texture modeling, the complete GPRF-HPNet achieves the best results across all evaluation metrics.

Building on prior studies64–66 that explored application-level evaluation, this work further treats the enhanced images as inputs to a downstream feature-point detection task to assess the ability of different enhancement methods to preserve local structural information. Specifically, we employ the SIFT algorithm to detect local keypoints from different enhanced results, and the number of detected feature points is used to reflect the availability of stable local structures for subsequent feature matching, image registration, and visual localization.

As shown in Fig. 9, only 10 SIFT key points are detected from the raw input image, indicating that severe color deviation, low contrast, and texture degradation significantly weaken local feature responses. Several comparison methods can increase the number of detected feature points. For example, MSPE, MFCP, FDLDM, CRUHL, and PUGAN obtain 726, 959, 1048, 1222, and 1299 key points, respectively. However, some of these results may still suffer from over-enhancement, noise amplification, or local color distortion, which may affect the stability and usability of the detected features. In contrast, the proposed method detects 1418 SIFT keypoints, achieving the highest number among all compared methods. This indicates that GPRF-HPNet can more effectively preserve local textures, edges, and structural details while correcting color bias and improving contrast.

Overall, this application test demonstrates that the proposed method not only improves the visual quality of underwater images, but also provides richer local structural information for downstream feature-based underwater vision tasks, such as image matching, image registration, target tracking, and underwater robotic visual perception.

Conclusion

We propose GPRF-HPNet for real-world underwater image enhancement. Following the processing scheme of “hybrid preprocessing + parallel residual fusion”, the method first combines attenuation-map-guided color correction, information-entropy-driven dual-histogram enhancement, and CIELab local contrast enhancement in a collaborative manner, together with adaptive fusion between low-frequency and high-frequency components via weighted wavelet fusion, to generate a preprocessed image that is color-neutral, contrast-balanced, and rich in textures. Subsequently, multi-scale features are encoded in parallel within the backbone, and CBAM is employed to perform channel-spatial attention fusion. Finally, the entire network is optimized end-to-end under a composite loss of Charbonnier + SSIM + perceptual loss. Extensive experiments on three public datasets, Color-Check7, Test-C60, and UCCS, demonstrate that our method has certain advantages over existing methods. For example, on Color-Check7, our method achieves 17.21 on the VAR metric, improving by 3.86 over MSPE (13.35), indicating stronger comprehensive robustness under multiple distortions. On Test-C60, our method attains 9.92 on VAR, improving by 1.17 over MSPE (8.75), which further verifies stability under complex real-world degradations. On UCCS, our method obtains 28.85 on UCIQE, increasing by 0.54 over MSPE (28.31), reflecting stability in the comprehensive quality of color balance, saturation, and contrast.

Although the proposed method is overall superior to multiple representative methods, there remains room for further improvement. Under extreme water types and complex lighting conditions, the balance between global brightness stability and subjective naturalness can still be strengthened. Meanwhile, there is still potential for improvement in detail recovery in deep-water shadows and weak-texture regions.

Future work will focus on developing physically consistent learnable preprocessing and more robust learning paradigms, and improving cross-water-type generalization and real-sea stability through self-supervised learning, semi-supervised learning, contrastive learning, domain adaptation, and uncertainty estimation. Meanwhile, systematic validation and statistical significance analysis on paired or quasi-paired ground-truth data will be supplemented, so as to more comprehensively evaluate applicability and generalization capability.
