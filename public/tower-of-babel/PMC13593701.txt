Real-world analysis of fibrinolytics-associated bleeding and literature synthesis
Authors: Xia Hong, Qun Wei, Zi-Lian Wang, Yu-Zhen Wang, Liu-Cheng Li, Qiu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFibrinolytic agents are essential for acute thrombotic diseases, yet their significant bleeding risks pose a critical clinical challenge, underscoring the need to understand and quantify these risks to optimize outcomes and ensure patient safety. This study leveraged real-world data from the FDA Adverse Event database to analyze global bleeding events associated with fibrinolytic agents from 2004 to 2024. Using disproportionality analysis with multiple complementary statistical algorithms, we identified robust signals linking specific bleeding events to fibrinolytic therapies. The analysis revealed that the majority of bleeding events occurred within the first 30 days of therapy, with severe outcomes such as death, hospitalization, and life-threatening complications predominating. Notably, neurological indications, including ischemic stroke and cerebrovascular accident, exhibited the highest bleeding reporting signal, while significant variations in reporting trends and outcomes were observed across different indications and demographics. Procedural and intracranial bleeding emerged as critical concerns, with strong statistical signals highlighting their clinical relevance. The insights advance the understanding of fibrinolytic-associated bleeding signals and provide an evidence base to support individualized risk–benefit assessments and targeted interventions to enhance patient outcomes in thrombotic disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05633-x.Keywords: Fibrinolytic agents, Adverse event, Bleeding, Disproportionality analysis, Risk management

Introduction

Fibrinolytic agents play a critical role in the management of acute thrombotic diseases, including acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis (Brouwers et al. 2020; Knapen et al. 2024). These medications work by activating the fibrinolytic system to dissolve blood clots, thereby restoring blood flow and improving patient outcomes. However, bleeding risk in patients receiving fibrinolytic therapy is not determined by the thrombolytic agent alone. A wide range of patient-specific factors including comorbidities such as cancer, atrial fibrillation, and obesity, as well as concomitant medications like antiplatelets and direct oral anticoagulants, can independently shift the hemostatic balance toward bleeding (Fig. 1). Despite their therapeutic benefits such as pulmonary embolism, fibrinolytic agents are associated with more major and minor bleeding events, particularly hemorrhagic stroke (Zuo et al. 2021). Traditional thrombolytic therapies, such as alteplase, have been reported to carry a systemic bleeding risk of up to 15%, highlighting the urgent need for strategies to mitigate these adverse events (Huang et al. 2019).

While thrombolytic therapy reduced all-cause mortality, it was associated with an elevated risk of major bleeding and cerebral hemorrhage directly impacting patient prognosis (Fig. 1) (Chatterjee et al. 2014). It has shown that early identification of bleeding risk factors is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes and reducing complications (Liu et al. 2023). The management of bleeding risks associated with antithrombotic regimens necessitates a multidisciplinary approach involving clinicians, pharmacists, and nurses (Talasaz et al. 2024). Understanding and quantifying the bleeding risks is crucial for developing personalized treatment plans and ensuring patient safety. Real-world analyses provide a more accurate representation of thrombolytic agent performance in terms of safety and efficacy, as they account for the variability of real clinical settings rather than idealized trial conditions.

Although bleeding is a pharmacologically anticipated complication of fibrinolytic therapy, the precise magnitude, temporal pattern, and indication-specific distribution of bleeding signals in real-world practice remain inadequately characterized. Quantifying these signals across diverse patient populations and clinical contexts is essential for informing individualized risk–benefit assessments, particularly given that fibrinolysis remains one of the few first-line options for acute vascular occlusion. This study aims to comprehensively analyze global bleeding events associated with several fibrinolytic agents using the FDA Adverse Event (AE) Reporting System (FAERS) database, a publicly accessible, self-reported pharmacovigilance database that collects global AEs (Hong et al. 2026; Jin et al. 2025). This analysis is particularly important given the significant bleeding risks associated with fibrinolytic agents, which can vary widely depending on the specific drug used. Real-world data offers a unique opportunity to capture AEs in diverse patient populations, reflecting actual clinical practice rather than controlled trial conditions, and thereby complementing the evidence derived from randomized controlled trials.

Methods

Data sources and extraction

The data analyzed in this study were obtained from the FAERS, a publicly available database that collects self-reported AE reports from healthcare professionals, pharmaceutical companies, and patients worldwide. The dataset covers AE reports from the first quarter of 2004 to the third quarter of 2024. For this analysis, AE reports related to fibrinolytic medications were extracted using queries from the Standardized MedDRA Queries (SMQ) files. The fibrinolytic drugs included in the study are alteplase, reteplase, streptokinase, urokinase, anistreplase, tenecteplase, lanoteplase, monteplase, and pamiteplase. Each dataset file is organized into seven sections: patient demographics, drug administration details, drug information, AEs, patient outcomes, report sources, drug therapy dates, and indications for use or diagnoses.

Within the FAERS framework, the files are linked using unique identifiers such as PRIMARYID, enabling the integration of demographic, drug, and outcome data from different sections of each report. The Medex_UIMA_1.3.8 software was used to standardize drug terminology in the database. Data extraction focused on fibrinolytic medications listed as the primary suspect drug in the reports, excluding irrelevant or combination drugs. AE signals were categorized using the preferred terms (PT) from MedDRA version 25.1. To ensure data accuracy, a two-step deduplication process was applied. First, the data were normalized and cleaned to remove redundant entries. Second, duplicates were identified and removed based on the most recent FDA_DT when both CASEID and FDA_DT matched, ensuring that only the most recent version of each unique report was retained for analysis. This rigorous methodology ensures the reliability and validity of the extracted data for further analysis.

Disproportionality analysis

Pharmacovigilance within the FAERS database relies on disproportionality analysis to evaluate the link between a drug and an AE. This approach compares the observed frequency of a specific drug-AE pair against the expected frequency based on all other reports in the database; a higher observed-than-expected ratio suggests a potential safety signal that warrants further investigation. This technique is based on two main statistical approaches: frequency-based and Bayesian. Both approaches use a 2 × 2 contingency table to identify potential risk signals, but neither is flawless (Supplementary Table 1). The frequency-based method is sensitive but can be less specific and influenced by individual data points, while the Bayesian method is more specific and stable but generally less sensitive. To balance these trade-offs and reduce the likelihood of false-positive or false-negative findings, we used four algorithms in parallel, requiring a signal to be detected by all four before it was confirmed.

In our study, we used four algorithms to detect AE signals: the frequency-based Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR) and Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), and the Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN) and Empirical Bayes Geometric Mean (EBGM) (Supplementary Table 2). For a signal to be considered positive in the frequency-based methods, the lower limit of the 95% CI of ROR had to exceed 1 and the number of reports containing both the suspect drug and AE had to be at least 3. For PRR, a signal was positive if PRR was ≥ 2, χ2 was ≥ 4, and the number of reports was ≥ 3. For the Bayesian methods, a signal was positive if EBGM05 was > 2 and the lower limit of the 95% CI of the Information Component (IC025) was greater than 0 for BCPNN. A higher value for each metric indicated a stronger correlation between the drug and AE. Only signals detected by all four algorithms were confirmed.

Ethics and study approval

This study utilized data from the FAERS database, which is a publicly available and anonymized resource designed to support the FDA’s drug safety surveillance program and is accessible for research purposes. Given that the data are anonymized and intended for surveillance and research, Institutional Review Board approval and patient consent were not required.

Statistical analysis

For each AE report, we collected data on demographic characteristics (age, sex, and weight), administrative details (report origins and nations), the time span from the start of treatment to the emergence of the AE (time-to-onset), and patient outcomes. Time-to-onset was determined by counting the days between therapy initiation and AE appearance, using the available start and event dates from the reports. Only reports with complete date information for both therapy initiation and AE onset were included in this time-to-onset analysis. The data were then analyzed using R statistical software, version 4.2.3 (Vienna, Austria).

Real-world bleeding events associated with fibrinolytic drugs

Temporal trends in AE reporting for fibrinolytic drugs

The reports related to fibrinolytic drugs from 2004 to 2024 revealed notable temporal trends in both the number of reports and reporting ratios (Fig. 2A). The number of AE reports fluctuated between 26 and 159 annually, with a relatively stable reporting ratio ranging from 0.7 to 4% during 2004 to 2016. Notably, a slight increase in reporting ratio was observed in 2009 (2.4%) and 2012 (4%). A significant surge occurred, with the reporting ratio peaking at 21.4% in 2017 and the number of reports reaching 855, marking the highest values in the study period. It suggests heightened awareness or changes in reporting practices. The reporting ratio and number of reports stabilized, with the ratio ranging from 4.5 to 7.8% and reports averaging around 200–272 annually post-2017, indicating a return to consistent reporting patterns.

Bleeding risk by indication for fibrinolytic drug use

The analysis of AE reports related to fibrinolytic drug use revealed significant variations in bleeding risk across different indications (Fig. 2B). Ischemic stroke accounted for the highest number of reports (1357) with a reporting ratio of 34%, indicating a strong association between fibrinolytic use and bleeding risk among all reported indications. The second most reported indication, cerebrovascular accident, contributed 680 reports (reporting ratio: 17%), highlighting substantial bleeding risk in patients with cerebrovascular events. However, 309 reports (reporting ratio: 7.7%) were associated with unknown indications, suggesting potential off-label use or reporting discrepancies. Acute myocardial infarction contributed 251 reports with a reporting ratio of 6.3%, indicating bleeding risk in cardiac applications. Cerebral infarction accounted for 229 reports (reporting ratio: 5.7%), further emphasizing neurological indications as high-risk contexts. It is important to note that in the database, “ischemic stroke” and “cerebral infarction” are coded as separate preferred terms within the MedDRA terminology. However, from a clinical standpoint, both terms denote acute cerebral ischemia due to vessel occlusion and represent the same underlying indication for fibrinolytic therapy. Their separate reporting in Fig. 2B reflects database coding conventions and reporter vocabulary variation rather than true clinical heterogeneity. These findings underscored that neurological conditions such as ischemic stroke and cerebrovascular accident presented the highest bleeding risk. The notable proportion of reports with unknown indications warrants further investigation into prescribing practices and reporting accuracy.

Characteristics of bleeding event reports related to fibrinolytic drugs

The analysis of AE reports related to fibrinolytic drugs from the FAERS database revealed several key characteristics across different demographic and reporting categories (Table 1). A total of 1318 (33.0%) reports were associated with female patients, while 1632 (40.9%) were linked to male patients. Notably, 1044 (26.1%) reports had missing or unspecified sex data, indicating potential gaps in reporting completeness. Weight data were available for a minority of reports, with 73 (1.8%) patients weighing less than 50 kg, 89 (2.2%) weighing more than 100 kg, and 921 (23.1%) weighing between 50 and 100 kg. The majority of reports (72.9%) lacked specific weight information.

Significant AE signals for fibrinolytic drugs at the PT level

A total of 3994 cases (126 kinds) of target PT were detected, 52 of which had significant differences by ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and EBGM methods (Supplementary Table 3). The analysis of AE reports from the FAERS database identified several significant signals associated with fibrinolytic drug use. The top 20 AEs, ranked by ROR, revealed a strong association between specific bleeding events and fibrinolytic therapy (Table 2). Vascular access site hemorrhage exhibited the highest ROR (96.4; 95% CI: 42.37–219.36), indicating a significant risk at procedural sites. Hemorrhagic transformation stroke (ROR: 1119.25; 95% CI: 1003.25–1248.66) and hemorrhagic infarction (ROR: 286.52; 95% CI: 223.24–367.73) showed exceptionally high signal strengths, underscoring their critical clinical relevance. Basal ganglia hemorrhage (ROR: 81.53; 95% CI: 57.49–115.61) and intraventricular hemorrhage (ROR: 75.43; 95% CI: 61.54–92.46) highlighted substantial risks of intracranial bleeding. Cerebral hemorrhage (ROR: 60.83; 95% CI: 57.15–64.75) was the most frequently reported severe AE (1053 cases), emphasizing its prevalence in clinical practice. Puncture site hemorrhage (ROR: 70.93; 95% CI: 47.54–105.82) and catheter site hemorrhage (ROR: 36.08; 95% CI: 26.76–48.64) indicated significant procedural-related bleeding risks. These results underscore the need for vigilant monitoring and management of bleeding risks, particularly in procedural and intracranial contexts, to optimize the safety of fibrinolytic therapy.

The time-to-onset distribution of bleeding events following fibrinolytic therapy

The analysis of time-to-onset data for bleeding events associated with fibrinolytic drug use revealed distinct patterns in event occurrence (Fig. 3A). The majority of bleeding events were reported within the first 30 days of treatment, with 417 reports (reporting ratio: 97.4%). In contrast, events occurring between 31 and 60 days were rare, with only 8 reports (reporting ratio: 1.9%). Minimal events were reported beyond 60 days, with just 1 event in the 61–90-day interval and 2 events beyond 360 days. It highlighted that the risk of bleeding events was markedly higher in the early phase of fibrinolytic therapy, with the majority occurring within the first 30 days. Importantly, this time-to-onset metric reflects the interval from treatment initiation to AE occurrence, not the time from symptom onset to treatment initiation (“door-to-needle” time), which is a well-established predictor of both thrombolytic efficacy and bleeding complications but is not captured in the database. Consequently, our analysis cannot assess whether earlier treatment initiation within the therapeutic window is associated with a differential bleeding risk profile.

Outcomes following fibrinolytic therapy

The outcomes associated with fibrinolytic drug use revealed significant patient impact across several categories (Fig. 3B). Death was the most frequently reported outcome, accounting for 1609 cases (reporting ratio of 40.3%), underscoring the particularly serious bleeding complications. Other serious important medical events were documented in 1186 cases (reporting ratio: 29.7%), reflecting additional clinical burdens. Hospitalization was required in 571 cases (reporting ratio: 14.3%), while life-threatening events were reported in 400 cases (reporting ratio: 10%). Less frequent outcomes included disability (44 cases) and events requiring intervention (10 cases). Notably, 174 cases (reporting ratio: 4.4%) had missing outcome data. These findings emphasize the clinical severity and diversity of adverse outcomes associated with fibrinolytic therapy, with death and serious medical events predominating. The data underscore the need for rigorous risk management and monitoring to mitigate the potentially fatal consequences of such bleeding events.

Discussion

With accelerating population aging and evolving lifestyles, thromboembolic diseases have emerged as an increasingly critical global health challenge (China ECotDoGftPaToTDi 2018). The expanding scope of antithrombotic therapy and the growing number of antithrombotic agents have made drug-related bleeding a more prominent clinical concern (Chow et al. 2024). Inadequate understanding of antithrombotic treatment, insufficient patient management, and poor medication adherence may disrupt the delicate balance between thrombosis and bleeding, potentially leading to severe AEs such as bleeding complications or thrombotic recurrence (Chan and Eikelboom 2019).

Thrombolytic drugs, known as fibrinolytic drugs, include endogenous or exogenous fibrinogen activators, which dissolve the formed thrombus by activating the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrinolytic enzyme, and degrading fibrin in the thrombus (Fig. 4) (Horlocker et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2020). According to the time of drug discovery and drug characteristics, thrombolytic drugs are mainly divided into the first-generation thrombolytic drugs (such as urokinase and streptokinase which can cause systemic fibrinolytic activation and increase the risk of bleeding), the second-generation thrombolytic drugs such as alteplase and prourokinase that are mainly used for emergency thrombolytic therapy of acute ST segment elevation myocardial infarction, and the third-generation thrombolytic drugs (such as tenecteplase and reteplase) which have the advantages of long half-life, strong specificity, high solubility, and low intracranial hemorrhage rate (Wang et al. 2020). Thrombolytic therapy can be used as a standalone medication for arterial and venous diseases or as an adjunct to surgical treatment, but there are still concerns about possible adverse effects of thrombolytic therapy, such as major or minor bleeding (Lawrence and Goodman 1992; Hao et al. 2015). Established predictors of bleeding after thrombolytic therapy include prolonged prothrombin time, recent major surgery, low body weight, and diabetes (Stewart and Kline 2020).

Mechanism of fibrinolytic drugs in treating thrombosis

Bigdata Observatory Platform for Stroke of China showed that the incidence of intracranial bleeding after intravenous thrombolytic therapy for acute ischemic stroke was 3.2% (Tu and Wang 2021). The incidence of intracranial bleeding after intravenous thrombolytic therapy (3%) is similar to the findings of a review of randomized controlled trials in patients with acute ischemic stroke (Stewart and Kline 2020). A meta-analysis showed that thrombolytic drugs combined with heparin reduced mortality and recurrence of pulmonary embolism compared with heparin alone, and the length of hospital stay and quality of life were similar, but the incidence of bleeding events and stroke was higher in thrombolytic drugs users (Hao et al. 2015). A systematic analysis of 48 cases of acute superior mesenteric artery thromboembolism treated with thrombolytic therapy was performed, among which 4 cases had bleeding complication, and 43 cases were survived and successful by angiographic verification (Schoots et al. 2005). Thrombolytic therapy has clinical practice in both arterial and venous thrombosis, and its bleeding is still an important problem. In order to improve the efficacy and safety of thrombolytic therapy, it is promising to continue to develop new drugs or thrombolytic drug carriers such as nano-targeted agents (Zhong et al. 2020).

For patients with acute large-vessel occlusion, it is recommended to promptly perform intravenous thrombolysis bridged to mechanical thrombectomy following symptom onset, as early thrombolysis can significantly impact functional outcomes (Wang et al. 2025). Fibrinolytic therapy-related hemorrhage events, especially intracerebral hemorrhage complications, including intraventricular hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage, seriously affect the prognosis of patients (Tao et al. 2025). This study represents a significant advancement in understanding the bleeding risks associated with fibrinolytic agents by leveraging real-world data from the FAERS database. Unlike previous studies that often rely on controlled clinical trial settings, our analysis captures the diversity and complexity of clinical practice across a global population. This approach not only enhances the generalizability of our findings but also provides a more accurate representation of the safety profile of fibrinolytic therapies in real-world scenarios. The use of multiple statistical algorithms for disproportionality analysis further strengthens the robustness of our results, offering a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the relationship between fibrinolytic agents and bleeding events. These innovations hold substantial clinical value by informing personalized risk assessment and management strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes in thrombotic disease management (Fig. 5).

Strategies to reduce bleeding risk or cope bleeding caused by fibrinolytic drugs

Our analysis revealed several critical insights. First, the majority of bleeding events occurred within the first 30 days of fibrinolytic therapy, emphasizing the importance of close monitoring during the early treatment phase. Second, neurological indications, such as ischemic stroke and cerebrovascular accident, were associated with the highest bleeding risk, highlighting the need for heightened vigilance in these patient populations. Third, procedural and intracranial bleeding emerged as particularly concerning AEs, with strong statistical signals underscoring their clinical relevance. These findings have direct implications for clinical practice, suggesting that risk stratification and preventive measures should be prioritized in high-risk patients, particularly those with neurological conditions or undergoing invasive procedures. When assessing individual bleeding risk, clinicians should also carefully evaluate concomitant comorbidities and concurrent use of other hemostasis-altering medications, as these factors may substantially modulate the net bleeding risk associated with fibrinolytic therapy. In everyday clinical decision-making, the bleeding signals identified in this study, especially procedural and intracranial hemorrhage, should be explicitly weighed against the life-saving potential of fibrinolytic therapy in acute thrombotic emergencies. This risk–benefit calculus is paramount, as fibrinolysis remains one of the few first-line treatments for vascular occlusion, and tailored patient selection based on individual risk profiles is essential to optimize outcomes.

Previous studies on fibrinolytic agents have reported varying bleeding risks, often attributed to differences in study populations, methodologies, and settings. Clinical trials typically enroll highly selected patient cohorts, which may not fully reflect the heterogeneity of real-world patient populations (Bainey et al. 2025). In contrast, our study captures a broader spectrum of patients, including those with comorbidities and varying treatment contexts, providing a more comprehensive risk profile. Discrepancies in results may also stem from differences in AE reporting and ascertainment. While clinical trials rely on predefined outcome measures and systematic follow-up, real-world data like FAERS are subject to voluntary reporting, which can introduce biases. However, the strengths of our approach lie in its ability to identify rare and delayed AEs that might be overlooked in controlled trials, thereby complementing existing evidence.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the FAERS database is subject to underreporting and reporting biases, as AEs are voluntarily submitted and may not capture all cases. Second, the lack of detailed clinical information, such as laboratory values, comorbidities (including cancer, atrial fibrillation, and obesity), and concomitant medications (e.g., antiplatelets, anticoagulants), limits our ability to conduct more granular risk assessments or to adjust for these important confounders. Furthermore, the database does not routinely document whether fibrinolysis was used as a standalone approach or in combination with manual/mechanical reperfusion strategies such as thrombus aspiration, which could independently modulate bleeding risk and thus confound the signals attributed to the fibrinolytic agent alone. Similarly, information on the timing and sequence of combined reperfusion therapies is not consistently recorded, precluding an assessment of their incremental contribution to hemorrhagic complications. Consequently, the bleeding signals identified in this study should be interpreted as associations with fibrinolytic therapy in the context of real-world prescribing, rather than as drug-attributable risks independent of patient-specific and treatment-related factors. These missing variables may lead to either overestimation or underestimation of the true drug-associated bleeding risk, depending on the direction of confounding in individual cases. Furthermore, the database does not capture critical treatment-related parameters such as symptom-to-treatment time, practitioner experience, institutional procedure volume, or whether fibrinolysis was performed in centers with routine versus occasional use of these therapies, all of which could influence bleeding risk and confound the signals observed. Additionally, the separation of “ischemic stroke” and “cerebral infarction” as distinct indication categories in Fig. 2B reflects MedDRA coding conventions and reporter terminology preferences rather than clinically meaningful differences in the underlying pathology or treatment indication; both terms should be interpreted collectively as representing acute cerebral ischemic events requiring reperfusion therapy. These recurring data gaps underscore the urgent need for standardized reporting frameworks that systematically capture the core clinical, therapeutic, and system-level variables essential for robust pharmacovigilance and personalized risk assessment. Third, the observational nature of the data precludes establishing causality between fibrinolytic agents and bleeding events, although disproportionality analysis helps identify potential signals. Fourth, the study’s reliance on predefined algorithms for signal detection may overlook novel or less commonly reported AEs. Fifth, in acute ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic transformation is partly an evolution of disease and reperfusion injury, not exclusively a drug-induced harm. Sixth, due to the presence of missing demographic data, the comparisons based on this are only of an exploratory nature. Seventh, the conclusion that most bleeding signals were reported within 30 days may merely reflect the following situations: the acute implementation of thrombolytic therapy (interventions lasting from a few minutes to several hours), the absence of treatment start/end dates, default or alternative dates, reporting behavior (acute catastrophic events are more likely to be reported), and only some reports having available start/end dates. Eighth, we reported 40.3% of death outcomes. This may be not credible as a real-world fatality rate of fibrinolysis-related bleeding; it reflects FAERS severity/reporting bias (serious outcomes are preferentially reported). Lastly, the generalizability of our findings may be affected by regional differences in drug prescribing patterns and healthcare practices.

The data gaps identified in our analysis prompt us to propose a core catalog of data elements that, if systematically captured in spontaneous reporting systems such as FAERS, would substantially enhance the depth and clinical utility of future pharmacovigilance analyses. We recommend that AE reports for fibrinolytic therapy include, at a minimum, the following five domains of information. First, patient-specific factors include baseline comorbidities (e.g., cancer, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, obesity, hypertension, prior stroke, or bleeding history), concomitant medications (particularly antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and other drugs affecting hemostasis), and key laboratory values (e.g., platelet count, coagulation parameters, renal function) to enable adequate risk adjustment. Second, treatment-specific factors include precise time from symptom onset to treatment initiation, fibrinolytic agent and dose administered, and clear documentation of whether fibrinolysis was used as monotherapy or in combination with mechanical thrombectomy or manual aspiration. Third, provider- and system-level factors include practitioner specialty and experience level, institutional annual procedure volume for fibrinolysis and/or thrombectomy, and the clinical setting in which treatment was delivered (e.g., comprehensive stroke center vs. primary care center). Fourth, outcome characterization includes standardized grading of bleeding severity (e.g., the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis definition of major bleeding), alongside functional outcome measures such as the modified Rankin Scale at discharge or 90-day follow-up. Fifth, reporting completeness includes mandatory reporting of age, sex, weight, and treatment indication as non-negotiable fields, with clear guidance to reporters on coding conventions to reduce terminological ambiguity. Implementation of such a standardized data framework would not only improve the robustness and comparability of signal detection analyses but also move the field closer to the goal of targeted, patient-individualized risk–benefit assessment in thrombolytic therapy.

Based on our findings, we recommend a multidisciplinary approach to mitigate drug-induced AE, involving clinicians, pharmacists, and nurses to optimize patient care (Basaraba et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023; Owens et al. 2014; Li et al. 2026). Nurses serve as essential caregivers at the core of the multidisciplinary team, orchestrating a multifaceted array of responsibilities including close monitoring for hemorrhagic signs, accurate administration of fibrinolytic agents, patient education on bleeding recognition, and rehabilitation support that are critical to mitigating bleeding risks and optimizing outcomes in thrombolytic therapy (Lu and Zhu 2021; Tong et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2015). Personalized risk assessment should be conducted prior to initiating therapy, with particular attention to patients with neurological conditions or those undergoing invasive procedures. Close monitoring, especially during the first 30 days of treatment, is essential to detect and manage bleeding events promptly (Zuo et al. 2021). Additionally, educating healthcare providers and patients about the recognition and reporting of AEs can enhance the safety surveillance of fibrinolytic agents. Although our study does not directly compare bleeding risks between fibrinolytic therapy and manual or mechanical reperfusion techniques, this comparison is clinically relevant. Previous meta-analyses have suggested that adding intravenous thrombolysis to mechanical thrombectomy does not further increase the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage in patients with acute ischemic stroke (Reda et al. 2025). However, direct head-to-head risk estimates between fibrinolysis and manual aspiration specifically are scarce, and such comparisons are further complicated by differences in indication, baseline stroke severity, and concomitant antithrombotic use. We therefore acknowledge that our real-world findings on fibrinolysis-associated bleeding should not be extrapolated to infer the safety profile of mechanical reperfusion strategies, and we encourage future prospective studies designed to directly compare bleeding outcomes across these therapeutic approaches.

Geographic variation in reporting volume was evident in our dataset, with the USA contributing the largest proportion of reports (44.0%), followed by China (10.6%), Germany (4.3%), France (3.1%), and Japan (2.5%). However, these figures reflect reporting frequency rather than true incidence, and regional differences in reporting practices, healthcare systems, and pharmacovigilance infrastructure may substantially influence the observed distribution. Furthermore, the FAERS database does not contain information on individual practitioner expertise, center-specific case volume, or annual procedural experience with fibrinolysis and/or thrombus aspiration. It is plausible that high-volume centers with greater routine experience may achieve lower per-procedure bleeding rates through more rigorous patient selection, more expeditious treatment, and better management of complications, even if their absolute number of reported events is higher due to greater case throughput. Conversely, pooling data across all regions without stratifying by center expertise or volume could dilute such center-specific protective effects, potentially obscuring signals that would be evident in more granular analyses. We therefore caution against interpreting the aggregate reporting ratios as direct indicators of clinical risk without considering these unmeasured practice-level factors.

Future research should validate our findings in diverse populations and address limitations in data granularity through prospective studies with standardized outcome measures. Implementing core data elements such as treatment times, operator experience, center volume, comorbidities, and adjunctive therapies in pharmacovigilance registries would enable robust risk stratification and direct comparisons across reperfusion strategies. Such granular data are critical for disentangling the independent bleeding risk of fibrinolytics from other interventions and for assessing whether center volume or reporting practices influence observed outcomes. Collaborative efforts among regulators, database providers, and researchers to adopt these frameworks would facilitate personalized bleeding risk prediction and optimize clinical decision-making. Exploring novel biomarkers and imaging techniques to predict bleeding risk may also enhance risk stratification. In cases of late presentation of acute ischemic stroke, JX10 (formerly known as TMS-007), a novel thrombolytic agent belonging to the Stachybotrys microspora triprenyl phenol family, demonstrated a 0% incidence of symptomatic intracranial bleeding among its cohorts (Niizuma et al. 2024). This finding suggests that JX10 has the potential to expand the therapeutic window for treating acute ischemic stroke. Thus, the development of targeted interventions, such as novel therapies or improved drug delivery systems to mitigate bleeding complications, represents a promising avenue for enhancing the safety and efficacy of fibrinolytic therapy.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive real-world signal landscape of bleeding events associated with fibrinolytic agents, systematically quantifying risks that are pharmacologically anticipated but variably reported across clinical settings. By integrating these real-world signals with clinical expertise, the clinician can better discriminate patients according to individual risk–benefit profiles, thereby advancing the safe and effective use of fibrinolytic therapies in managing thrombotic diseases.
