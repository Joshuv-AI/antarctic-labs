Co-designing water sanitation and hygiene (WASH) messages for a social and behaviour change campaign in Kiribati
Authors: Tahir Turk, Betero Buatia, Berite Anterea, Rosemary Faletoese, Apenisa Vaniqi, Christina Reiher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo examine sanitation behaviours, barriers and motivators in South Tarawa, and to assess how formative research, message pretesting and co-design processes can generate culturally resonant Social and Behaviour Change (SBC) messages for WASH programming in Pacific Island contexts.Study designRapid Assessment and Response (RAR) mixed-methods study.MethodsData were collected through semi-structured interviews with 12 key informants and eight focus group discussions with community members (N = 73) across seven intervention villages. Co-design of message concepts and brand stimuli was integrated into focus groups, supported by quantitative ratings of message impact using a Smiley Face scale. Qualitative data were analysed using a hybrid Grounded Theory and Thematic Analysis approach, while quantitative differences across demographic groups were examined using ANOVA, t-tests, Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests.ResultsFour organising themes and 22 subthemes were identified. Key barriers included entrenched social norms supporting open defecation, overcrowding, limited and poorly maintained sanitation infrastructure, and safety concerns for women and vulnerable groups. Participants preferred empowering narratives delivered by trusted opinion leaders, while humorous brand concepts reduced stigma around sanitation topics. Quantitative ratings confirmed the Leaders narrative and Sit-Wipe-Flush brand as the strongest performers.ConclusionsEmbedding co-design within formative SBC research produced culturally grounded insights into sanitation behaviour change in South Tarawa. Findings highlight the need for phased message rollout aligned with infrastructure availability and demonstrate the value of participatory design approaches to enhance relevance, ownership and long-term effectiveness of WASH SBC interventions in Pacific Island and other LMIC settings.

Introduction

Diarrhoeal diseases linked to inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) remain a major global health burden, causing more than 1.4 million deaths annually [1]. Despite decades of investment, over one third of the world's population still lacks basic sanitation, and an estimated 3.5 billion people do not have access to safely managed services. Although progress has been made toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6, current trajectories indicate that universal access to safe water and sanitation by 2030 is unlikely to be achieved. Gordon et al. highlight the persistent health burden associated with unsafe WASH conditions, while recent UNICEF and WHO reports emphasise widening inequalities in access [[2], [3]].

Kiribati, a remote Pacific Island nation of 33 atolls and reef islands, faces particularly acute WASH challenges. According to the 2023 WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme, only 63% of households in Kiribati have access to basic drinking water services, and just 40% have access to basic sanitation, with a substantial proportion relying on unimproved or shared facilities. More than half of the population resides on South Tarawa, a narrow and densely populated atoll where rapid population growth, migration from outer islands, and limited land availability place severe pressure on natural resources and public infrastructure [4]. Population densities in Betio, Bairiki and Bikenibeu are among the highest in the Pacific, and the demand for sanitation services far exceeds existing capacity. These pressures have contributed to persistent open defecation practices, particularly in the ocean, and recurrent outbreaks of diarrhoeal disease. In 2010 alone, more than 10,000 cases of diarrhea and dysentery were reported in South Tarawa, with the highest burden in the most crowded settlements [4].

Successive donor-funded programs have sought to expand sanitation infrastructure and improve hygiene behaviours. The current South Tarawa Sanitation Project (STSP), funded by the World Bank, aims to transform sanitation practices, strengthen community engagement, and increase acceptance of new infrastructure [5]. However, global evidence shows that behaviour change interventions often achieve inconsistent or short-lived results. Systematic reviews highlight gaps in formative research, limited attention to cultural norms, and insufficient measurement of sustained behavioural adoption [6].

Social and Behaviour Change (SBC) approaches offer a pathway to address these gaps by integrating insights from communication, behavioural science, anthropology and systems thinking [7]. Yet there is limited documentation of SBC interventions that incorporate co-design, concept testing and participatory message development in Pacific Island contexts [8]. Understanding how communities perceive sanitation risks, interpret messages and respond to different communication strategies is essential for designing interventions that resonate with local values and constraints.

Co-design refers to participatory, iterative processes in which end-users collaborate with researchers and implementers to shape problem definitions, intervention concepts and implementation strategies. It is grounded in principles of shared inquiry, mutual learning and collective imagination, and spans a continuum from consultative feedback to deeper collaborative design cycles depending on context and resource constraints. In this study, co-design was operationalised through structured participatory refinement of narrative concepts and brand stimuli during focus group discussions, consistent with recognised approaches to co-design in public health and service design. (Steen, 2013; Trischler, Dietrich & Rundle-Thiele, 2019).

This study pilots a formative research approach that embeds co-design with program beneficiaries to identify culturally relevant messages, campaign brands and communication channels for the STSP. The objectives were to: (1) assess awareness, knowledge, attitudes and behaviours related to sanitation; and (2) identify message concepts, influencers and dissemination pathways most likely to support improved WASH practices. By foregrounding the perspectives of women, youth, the elderly and other vulnerable groups, the study aims to strengthen the behavioural impact of WASH SBC interventions in Kiribati and generate insights applicable to other low- and middle-income settings [9].

Methods

A literature review of regional and national WASH programs and SBC approaches informed the development of the study design and research instruments. A rapid assessment and response (RAR) mixed-methods approach was selected due to its effectiveness in identifying behavioural determinants and operational constraints in low-resource settings [[10], [11], [12]], including Pacific Island contexts [13,14]. The RAR combined qualitative and quantitative methods to capture both supply- and demand-side perspectives on sanitation practices in South Tarawa.

Stakeholder interviews

Semi structured interviews (SSIs) were conducted with 12 key informants from government ministries, international NGOs, local NGOs and national media organisations. SSIs allowed exploration of organisational priorities, perceived gaps in WASH programming and opportunities for strengthening SBC interventions. Interview guides were informed by the literature review and refined through consultations with WASH partners [15].

Focus group discussions

Eight focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with community members from the seven intervention villages. FGDs explored awareness, knowledge, attitudes and behaviours related to sanitation among groups of 8–12 participants. Recruitment occurred between 15 March and 22 April 2025. Inclusion criteria required participants to be residents of the intervention villages, aged 18 years or older, able to provide informed consent and willing to discuss sanitation practices openly. A 32-item COREQ checklist guided qualitative study design. A participant screener assessed gender, age, socioeconomic status, location, vulnerability and participants’ ability to speak openly. Informed consent was obtained from all participants [16]. Moderators were trained in qualitative facilitation techniques, including probing, managing dominant voices and minimising positivity bias [17]. A standardised discussion agenda covered sanitation concerns, perceived disease risks, barriers to improved practices, preferred message sources, communication channels and potential “nudges” to behavioural change [18].

Co-design and message pretesting

Co-design was integrated into FGDs to assess message concepts and brand stimuli. Participants reviewed three narrative concepts and three brand visual options (English and Gilbertese versions), presented in rotated order to minimise positional bias. Discussions explored message clarity, emotional impact, relevance and potential for community discussion. Participants identified the strongest and weakest concepts and suggested improvements.

To complement qualitative feedback, participants completed a self-administered rating form using a Smiley Face scale to assess ten proximal indicators of message impact, including clarity, personal relevance, novelty, motivational potential, trustworthiness and likelihood of sharing with others.

Creative message stimuli

Narrative concepts and brand visuals were developed in English and Gilbertese. Moderators explored participants’ immediate reactions, perceived clarity, emotional impact and key takeaways. Participants discussed cultural alignment, likelihood of sharing and suggestions for improvement. Data analysis

Audio recordings from SSIs and FGDs were transcribed from Gilbertese into English, with identifying information removed. Transcripts were coded using a hybrid Grounded Theory and Thematic Analysis approach [20,21]. A primary reviewer independently coded all transcripts and developed initial organising themes and subthemes. Two secondary reviewers examined the coding structure, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Findings were triangulated with the literature review to assess convergence and contextual nuances [22,23].

Quantitative data from validated message-rating forms [12,13] were analysed using ANOVA, t-tests, Chi-square tests and Fisher's exact tests to examine differences across age, gender and village location. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Version 30.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Office of Te Beretitenti, Republic of Kiribati (OB1/0231/12:2025) and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Results

Integrating co-design into the formative research process enabled participants to directly shape message concepts, brand visuals and dissemination strategies. This approach strengthened cultural resonance, improved message clarity and enhanced community ownership.

Structural barriers

Four overarching themes and 22 subthemes emerged (Fig. 2). A dominant finding was the impact of extreme population density on sanitation behaviours. Participants described overcrowding as a central barrier, noting that the narrow geography of the atoll and rapid population growth made it difficult to construct household toilets or extend sewerage networks. These pressures were linked to widespread reports of insufficient and poorly functioning sanitation infrastructure. Many communities described a chronic shortage of useable toilets, with some areas unable to support sewer or water lines due to low-lying terrain. Social and emotional dimensions

Safety concerns and stigma were frequently raised, particularly by women, girls, the elderly and people with disabilities. Participants described feeling unsafe when defecating at night and recounted experiences of shame or being chased away. These accounts highlighted the emotional and social burden associated with inadequate sanitation options.

Stakeholders also pointed to resource constraints, including limited funding, gaps in previous donor-supported programs and challenges in sustaining earlier WASH initiatives. Maintenance issues were widely reported, with broken or blocked toilets described as a persistent problem. Views on paying for sanitation services were mixed.

Opportunities for program improvement

Participants emphasised the need for better integration between infrastructure roll-out and SBC activities. Many saw value in building local capacity, training community champions and empowering residents to take a more active role in sanitation maintenance and behaviour change efforts.

Most participants understood the link between poor sanitation and disease, although misconceptions persisted. Cultural norms and intergenerational habits continued to influence open defecation practices. Communication channels

Younger participants preferred mobile-based communication and social media, while older participants relied more on radio, community announcements and interpersonal communication. Radio remained a widely trusted channel across all groups. School-based communication and community meetings were also valued.

Behavioural nudges

Participants viewed visual cues, reminders and community recognition as acceptable and motivating, particularly when linked to cleanliness, pride and family wellbeing. Discussion

This study demonstrates the value of embedding co-design within formative SBC research to inform the development of a WASH communication campaign in South Tarawa. Co-design in public health spans a continuum from consultative feedback to deeper collaborative design cycles, depending on context, resources and program maturity. In this study, co-design was operationalised as a participatory refinement process embedded within rapid formative SBC research and response (RAR), which is consistent with recognised approaches in service design and participatory public health (Steen, 2013; Trischler, Dietrich and Rundle-Thiele, 2019). While the creative concepts were initially developed by the project team, participants played an active role in reshaping narrative framing, providing feedback on visual brand elements, identifying culturally grounded motivators and barriers, and determining which concepts should be prioritised for rollout. This extends beyond conventional message pre-testing by enabling iterative adaptation driven by community insight, rather than simple validation of pre-existing materials. The co-design process therefore contributed to greater cultural resonance, stronger community ownership and clearer alignment between communication strategies and the lived realities of participants in South Tarawa.

By integrating qualitative enquiry, participatory message development and quantitative message-rating methods, the study generated culturally grounded insights into behavioural, social and structural determinants shaping sanitation practices.

A central finding was the profound influence of overcrowding and limited land availability on sanitation behaviours. These structural barriers have been documented in previous assessments [24,[3], [25], [26], [27]], yet the present study highlights how they continue to shape daily practices and reinforce reliance on open defecation, particularly among vulnerable groups. The emotional and social dimensions of these constraints—fear, shame and safety concerns—underscore the need for SBC strategies that acknowledge lived realities rather than assuming individual agency alone can drive behaviour change [28,29].

The findings also reveal gaps in the sustainability of past WASH initiatives. Participants described broken or blocked toilets, inconsistent maintenance and uncertainty about responsibilities for repairs. These concerns echo global evidence that infrastructure investments must be paired with clear communication, community engagement and long-term support systems.

Despite these challenges, the study identified opportunities to enhance sanitation behaviours. Participants demonstrated strong understanding of sanitation-related disease risks and interest in community-driven solutions. Message frames that build self-efficacy, highlight practical steps and avoid blame were viewed as effective.

The co-design process provided valuable insights for message development. Participants responded positively to narrative concepts using empowering, relatable storylines delivered by trusted opinion leaders. Humorous brand concepts helped reduce stigma, while messages acknowledging local norms were viewed as credible. Quantitative ratings reinforced these preferences.

Preferences for dissemination channels reflected generational differences, suggesting the need for a multi-channel approach. Behavioural nudges were widely accepted and align with global evidence on their effectiveness in reinforcing daily behaviours.

Overall, the findings underscore the importance of integrating co-design, participatory message testing and behavioural insights into WASH programming in Pacific Island contexts.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of the Rapid Assessment and Response (RAR) approach, which has now been applied across multiple Pacific and international settings and provides a pragmatic, facilitated framework for rapid data collection in low-resource environments. Recent iterations of the RAR approach increasingly incorporate message pre-testing to support more efficient SBC strategy planning and development, enabling early identification of persuasive narratives and potential barriers to message acceptance.

Another strength is the integration of co-design within the formative research process. Co-design allowed participants to directly shape creative concepts, campaign brand designs and dissemination strategies, generating more nuanced and culturally relevant solutions. This participatory approach strengthened the cultural resonance of the proposed SBC materials and enhanced community ownership of the intervention.

However, several limitations should be noted. Focus group discussions and semi-structured interviews may be influenced by moderator effects and group dynamics, particularly when discussing sensitive topics such as open defecation. Social desirability bias may have led to underreporting of certain behaviours. Grounded Theory analysis relies heavily on the interpretation of the primary coder, although the use of secondary reviewers improved reliability. Despite these limitations, triangulation with quantitative message-rating data and alignment with existing literature increase the credibility of the findings.

Recommendations

The study identifies several gaps, challenges, and opportunities that should guide the design and implementation of the South Tarawa Sanitation Project (STSP) SBC interventions over the three-year programming period:

Prioritize awareness, risk perception and community mobilisation in Year 1. Early SBC activities should focus on raising awareness of the STSP, strengthening understanding of sanitation-related disease risks and preparing communities for infrastructure roll-out.

Adopt the preferred narrative and brand identity across all campaign materials. The Leaders narrative and Sit-Wipe-Flush brand should be adopted, incorporating minor revisions suggested by participants.

Integrate population-level SBC with community-based advocacy and capacity building. Training local leaders and establishing village-level sanitation committees will strengthen ownership and sustainability.

Establish a robust Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) framework. Baseline and annual AKAB assessments should guide iterative refinement of SBC strategies.

Conclusions

This rapid assessment and response study demonstrates the value of formative research and co-design in shaping effective SBC strategies for WASH in resource-constrained settings. By engaging communities directly in message development and testing, the study identified critical social, cultural and structural factors influencing sanitation behaviours in South Tarawa. Aligning SBC activities with infrastructure availability, strengthening community ownership and tailoring messages to local norms will enhance the relevance and long-term impact of WASH interventions.
