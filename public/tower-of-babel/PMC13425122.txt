Association between composite dietary antioxidant index and pulmonary function in middle-aged and older adults: results from two population-based studies
Authors: Rui Qiang Li, Yu Hui Lin, Jun Tao Kan, Jiao Wang, Ya Li Jin, Wei Sen Zhang, Lin Xu, Emma Yun-Zhi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe decline in pulmonary function among older adults is a widespread issue, lacking specific dietary guidelines for optimising respiratory health.MethodsData from the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were analysed to assess the association of the Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) with key pulmonary function indicators in individuals aged 50 and older.ResultsThe study included 8640 participants from GBCS and 4029 from NHANES, with a mean age of 62.5 years. After adjusting for confounders, higher CDAI scores were significantly linked to increased forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and FEV1 percentage predicted (FEV1pp) in GBCS (β=0.004 L, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.008; β=0.193%, 95% CI 0.045% to 0.342%). Similar trends were observed in NHANES for FEV1pp (adjusted β=0.353%, 95% CI 0.059% to 0.646%). Subgroup analyses showed that women exhibited consistent positive associations between CDAI and both FEV1pp and forced vital capacity (FVC) percentage predicted (FVCpp) in both cohorts. In GBCS, stronger associations were found for participants over 65 years.ConclusionsHigher CDAI scores correlate with improved pulmonary function, particularly in women and older adults, underscoring the need for dietary recommendations that emphasise antioxidant-rich foods to enhance respiratory health and support healthy ageing.

Introduction

The ageing population faces an increased burden of pulmonary diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and lung cancer, which significantly impact health and quality of life and impose considerable economic burdens.1 2 Globally, chronic respiratory diseases affected approximately 569.2 million people and caused 4.2 million deaths in 2023, remaining the third leading cause of death worldwide.3 The absolute burden continues to grow due to population ageing and demographic changes, despite declines in age-standardised rates over recent decades.4 The decline in pulmonary function in older adults is particularly concerning, as it impairs respiratory capacity and triggers systemic inflammation, elevating the risk of cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction and stroke.5 6

In addressing the respiratory dysfunction related to ageing, the role of diet, particularly dietary antioxidants, has garnered growing interest.7 8 Oxidative stress is involved in the aetiology of numerous chronic diseases, and dietary antioxidants have been shown to mitigate these risks by neutralising reactive oxygen species (ROS) and bolstering the endogenous antioxidant defences.9 10 The Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) was proposed to evaluate the overall impact of antioxidants on health outcomes, encapsulating a spectrum of dietary antioxidants, including vitamins (A, C, E), minerals (selenium, zinc, manganese) and phytochemicals (carotenoids).11 The CDAI is calculated by summing the standardised intakes of these components, with a higher score reflecting a greater overall antioxidant capacity. Unlike single-nutrient approaches, the CDAI captures the synergistic effects of multiple antioxidants, offering a more comprehensive assessment of the diet’s ability to combat oxidative stress.10

Although the potential benefits of dietary antioxidants on respiratory health have been indicated, most studies have focused on the effects of individual antioxidants.12–14 Previous studies indicated that the combined effects of vitamin C, vitamin E and carotenoids were more effective in improving pulmonary function than single components.15 16 Moreover, intervention with compound nutrients significantly improved pulmonary function in patients with COPD, while supplementation with single vitamins showed no significant effect.17 However, these interventional studies were often short-term and might not fully capture the benefits of antioxidants from a regular diet.18–20 Observational studies may provide complementary evidence to support dietary guidelines for the general population.

Despite these potential benefits, significant research gaps remain in the current literature. Most observational studies have focused on individual nutrients rather than the overall dietary antioxidant capacity. Specifically, the association between the CDAI and pulmonary function has not been adequately explored in large-scale populations, particularly among older adults who are most vulnerable to age-related pulmonary decline. Additionally, there is a lack of cross-cultural evidence to determine whether these associations are consistent across populations with diverse ethnic backgrounds and dietary patterns. Therefore, this study aims to examine the associations of the consumption of dietary antioxidants, measured using CDAI, with various pulmonary function indicators including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FEV1 percentage predicted (FEV1pp), forced vital capacity (FVC), FVC percentage predicted (FVCpp) and FEV1/FVC ratio, taking advantage of data obtained from two well-organised large-scale studies: the cross-sectional National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted in the USA and the prospective Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS) conducted in China.

Materials and methods

Study samples

The GBCS is a collaborative project involving the Guangzhou Twelfth People’s Hospital, the University of Hong Kong and the University of Birmingham, UK.21 Participants were recruited from the Guangzhou Health and Happiness Association for the Respectable Elders, a community organisation with branches in all ten districts of Guangzhou. The study targets Guangzhou residents aged 50 and above, with a nominal monthly fee of 4 RMB (approximately 50 US cents). Baseline information was collected through face-to-face computer-assisted interviews conducted by trained nurses from Guangzhou Twelfth People’s Hospital. Data collection includes demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, family and personal medical history and anthropometric measurements following a standardised protocol. The inclusion criteria were participants aged 50 years or older who had completed the baseline survey. Exclusion criteria included: (1) missing data on lung function parameters; (2) missing nutrient data; (3) lack of essential baseline status information and (4) self-reported history of asthma. After applying these criteria, a total of 8640 participants were included in the final analysis (online supplemental figure 1A).

The NHANES is a recurring survey overseen by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in the USA, with the primary objective of evaluating the health and nutritional status of the American populace.22 This survey furnishes nationally representative data encompassing a range of domains, including but not limited to health status, dietary patterns, nutritional intake, lifestyle choices and environmental influences, and its research methodology has received approval from the Research Ethics Review Board of the NCHS. In the current study, data from the years 2007–2008, 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 were used. To ensure comparability, participants aged 50 years or older were included. Exclusion criteria were: (1) missing lung function data; (2) missing nutrient data; (3) missing baseline status data and (4) a history of asthma. In total, 4029 participants were included in the analysis (online supplemental figure 1B).

Assessment of CDAI

The CDAI was assessed in the GBCS and the NHANES using validated methodologies to estimate the overall antioxidant potential of diets.11 23 24 In the GBCS, the CDAI was determined by summing the antioxidant scores of various foods consumed. The calculation of CDAI involved standardising the intake of manganese, selenium, zinc and vitamins A, C and E. This was achieved by subtracting the gender-specific mean intake and dividing by the gender-specific SD, followed by summing these standardised intakes.25 26 Dietary intake information, representing the participants’ habitual dietary intake over the past 7 days, was obtained through a validated Food Frequency Questionnaire.27 Using data from the Chinese Food Composition Tables, the nutrient content of food items was quantified by multiplying the consumption of each item by its standard portion size of 100 g. The nutrients quantified include energy (kcal), vitamin A (μg RE), vitamin C (mg), vitamin E (mg), zinc (mg), selenium (μg) and manganese (mg).

In the NHANES, the CDAI was calculated based on the intake of zinc, selenium, carotenoids and vitamins A, C and E. Each of these dietary antioxidants was standardised by subtracting the gender-specific mean and dividing by the gender-specific SD, and the CDAI was then derived by summing these standardised intakes.26 Dietary information was collected using 24-hour dietary recalls, which capture the actual intake during the 24-hour period immediately prior to the interview. If two recalls were conducted, data from the first recall was used for analysis. Participants provided detailed descriptions of all food items, beverages and water consumed within the previous day, including types, amounts, timings and sources. The nutritional analysis was performed using the food composition database maintained by the US Department of Agriculture. The initial use of different antioxidant components in calculating the CDAI was due to the inherent differences in dietary data between the GBCS and NHANES datasets. To optimise data utilisation, our primary analysis included all CDAI-related antioxidant components documented in each dataset.

Potential confounders

We selected covariates theoretically related to CDAI and pulmonary function parameters but do not lie on the causal pathway connecting the two (ie, potential confounding factors). In the GBCS, these covariates included age (<65 years, ≥65 years), sex (men, women), education (primary or below, middle school, college or above), occupation (manual, non-manual, others), personal annual income, CNY/year (<10 000, 10 000–14 999, ≥15 000, Don’t know), body mass index (BMI) (<18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, 25.0–27.4 kg/m2, ≥27.5 kg/m2), energy, smoking status (never, former, current), physical activity (inactive, moderate, active), self-reported diabetes (no, yes), self-reported hypertension (no, yes) and self-rated health (poor, good). In the NHANES, the covariates were age, sex, education, race, energy, poverty income ratio (PIR), BMI (<18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, 25.0–30.0 kg/m2, ≥30.0 kg/m2), cotinine, physical activity, self-rated health (no, yes), self-reported diabetes (no, yes) and self-reported hypertension (no, yes). Physical activity was evaluated using a physical activity questionnaire, which included questions such as ‘Have you done any vigorous-intensity activities in the past 30 days?’ and ‘Have you done any moderate-intensity activities in the past 30 days?’

Study outcomes

In the GBCS, spirometry procedures were conducted following established protocols.28 Specifically, at least three manoeuvres were performed without bronchodilators to obtain optimal measurements of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FVC and the FEV1/FVC ratio. The predicted values for FEV1 and FVC were calculated based on norms for the Chinese population.29

In the NHANES, spirometry assessments were conducted using Ohio 822/827 dry-rolling volume seal spirometers prior to bronchodilator administration, adhering to the guidelines of the American Thoracic Society.30 Measurements included FEV1 and FVC, with only manoeuvres meeting or exceeding the American Thoracic Society quality grade C (indicating at least partial adherence to standards) being included in the analysis. Original measurements were converted to percentage-predicted values using the Hankinson equations.31

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of the study population were described using frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and mean±SD and median (IQR) for continuous variables in both the GBCS and NHANES. The CDAI was treated as a continuous variable in the linear regression models. A series of generalised linear regression models were constructed to estimate the β coefficients and 95% CIs between CDAI and CDAI components and different pulmonary function-related outcomes (including FEV1, FEV1pp, FVC, FVCpp and FEV1/FVC). These models were adjusted for age, sex, education, race, energy, PIR, BMI, cotinine levels, physical activity, self-rated health and self-reported hypertension in the NHANES; and for age, sex, education, occupation, energy, personal annual income, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, self-rated health, self-reported diabetes and self-reported hypertension in the GBCS. As per the NHANES analysis guidelines, all analyses were conducted with the integration of sample dietary weights.32 To further visualise the relationship between CDAI and each pulmonary function indicator, restricted cubic spline (RCS) was also performed. Furthermore, we examined potential age and sex-related interactions in the association between CDAI and pulmonary function indicators.

Furthermore, we employed the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) machine learning algorithm model to predict the relative importance of CDAI and its components on pulmonary function parameters. The XGBoost model was used to analyse the contribution of each variable to pulmonary function parameters using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values. SHAP, rooted in cooperative game theory, provides a unified and mathematically consistent framework for model interpretability. Notably, SHAP values address the bias of traditional importance metrics towards continuous variables and offer both global and local interpretability. Additionally, using the penalty spline method, an XGBoost was constructed with a fully adjusted smooth curve to explore the potential association of the CDAI and CDAI components with pulmonary function parameters.33 In this study, the dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%). All variable selection and model training were performed solely on the training set. The test set was held out for final evaluation to assess the model’s generalisation performance. Variable selection in the XGBoost model was guided by their importance determined through Variance Inflation Factor and Relief-F analysis. The model parameters were set to a maximum of 50 iterations, a tree depth of 10 and a learning rate (eta) of 0.1. Cross-validation was employed in each boosting iteration to prevent overfitting. All data analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.2 with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study included 8640 participants from the GBCS and 4029 participants from the NHANES, with mean ages (SD) of 62.5 (6.8) years and 62.5 (8.1) years, respectively. Compared with NHANES, the GBCS cohort had a lower proportion of participants aged 65 years and above (37.9% vs 39.1%), men (27.4% vs 50.2%) and participants with a college education or higher (9.2% vs 52.1%). Conversely, the GBCS participants had a higher prevalence of normal weight (62.6% vs 22.1%), being physically active (42.1% vs 12.5%), self-reported good health status (84.2% vs 76.8%) and absence of hypertension (70.5% vs 50.9%). The CDAI showed statistically significant differences across various participant characteristics, including sex, age, race, education level, occupation, household income, smoking status, physical activity, self-reported health status and self-reported hypertension status (P ranging from <0.001 to 0.049) (table 1).

Association between CDAI and pulmonary function parameters

After adjusting for age, sex, education, occupation, energy, personal annual income, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, self-rated health and self-reported hypertension, the CDAI was significantly associated with increased FEV1 (0.004 L, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.008) and FEV1pp (0.193%, 95% CI 0.045% to 0.342%) (both p<0.05) in the GBCS. After similar adjustment, a significant positive association was observed between CDAI and FEV1pp (0.353%, 95% CI 0.059% to 0.646%) in the NHANES (table 2). No significant interactions were found between CDAI and education, occupation, personal annual income, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, self-rated health, self-reported diabetes or self-reported hypertension regarding pulmonary function parameters (P for interaction ranging from 0.067 to 0.752), except for age and sex (P for interaction <0.05) in the GBCS. Stronger associations of the CDAI score with higher FEV1 (0.005 L, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.009), FEV1pp (0.246%, 95% CI 0.071% to 0.421%), FVC (0.006 L, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.010), FVCpp (0.193%, 95% CI 0.034% to 0.353%) were found in GBCS women. A similar positive association was observed in participants aged ≥65 years, with FEV1 (β=0.008 L, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.014) and FVC (β=0.009 L, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.016). Likewise, stronger association in women from the NHANES regarding the associations of CDAI with FEV1pp (0.687%, 95% CI 0.215% to 1.160%) and FVCpp (0.355%, 95% CI 0.031% to 0.678%) were identified (table 3). Furthermore, significant positive associations between CDAI and all five pulmonary function parameters in both GBCS and NHANES were observed (figure 1).

Association of antioxidants with pulmonary function parameters

In the GBCS, selenium levels were significantly associated with increased FEV1 (0.054 L, 95% CI 0.023 to 0.085), FEV1pp (1.754%, 0.358% to 3.151%) and FVCpp (1.462%, 0.199% to 2.726%). Zinc levels were negatively associated with FEV1 (−0.099 L, 95% CI −0.175 to −0.022) and FEV1pp (−4.895%, −8.346% to −1.444%) (Supplementary Table 1). In the NHANES, vitamin E was positively associated with pulmonary function indicators, including the FEV1 (0.052 L, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.101), FEV1pp (1.784%, 0.203% to 3.366%), FVC (0.114 L, 95% CI 0.059 to 0.170) and FVCpp (1.706%, 0.490% to 2.922%) (online supplemental table 2).

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the main results. First, zinc showed a U-shaped association with pulmonary function indicators (online supplemental figure 2) in the GBCS, with a nadir at the level of 12.81 mg/day. Second, the partial dependence plot of the XGBoost model, assuming other features are fixed at their average levels, indicated positive associations between CDAI and pulmonary function indicators (online supplemental figure 3). Third, the CDAI scores, which were calculated using two 24-hour dietary recalls and included dietary supplements, showed consistent results: higher CDAI was significantly associated with increased FEV1pp (β=0.342%, 95% CI 0.078% to 0.605%) in the NHANES (online supplemental table 3). Fourth, we found consistent results with the primary analysis showing significant positive associations of harmonised CDAI with FEV1 and FEV1pp in GBCS, and with FEV1pp in NHANES, further supporting the robustness of our findings (online supplemental table 4). Fifth, we recalculated the CDAI excluding zinc and observed highly consistent results. The revised CDAI maintained significant positive associations with FEV1, FEV1pp, FVC and FVCpp in the GBCS, and with FEV1pp and FVCpp in the NHANES (online supplemental table 5).

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that a higher CDAI was positively associated with improved pulmonary function parameters in both the GBCS and the NHANES. The observed associations persisted after adjusting for a comprehensive set of potential confounders, including demographic, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors. Notably, the relationship between CDAI and pulmonary function was more pronounced among women and individuals over 65 years of age. These findings suggest that dietary antioxidants play a significant role in maintaining pulmonary health, potentially offering a protective effect against age-related decline in pulmonary function.

As a comprehensive indicator of antioxidants intake through diet,34 higher CDAI was associated with delayed ageing,23 reduced risk of hyperlipidaemia,35 decreased incidence of chronic kidney disease,36 improved cardiovascular health and lowered risk of stroke.36 In this study, we observed a dose-response association between CDAI and better pulmonary function indicators. The exact aetiology and pathogenesis of declining pulmonary function remain unclear, but oxidative stress might play a crucial role.37 Oxidative stress disrupts the balance between antioxidants and prooxidants, leading to tissue and organ damage.38 Dietary intake of antioxidants helps regulate plasma redox status, protecting the body from reactive oxygen and nitrogen species.39 Exogenous antioxidants can prevent inflammation and oxidative stress in patients with lung disease by regulating inflammatory factors such as alkaline phosphatase and C reactive protein,40 thereby slowing the decline in pulmonary function and reducing the risk of lung disease.41 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated significant protective effects of vitamin C supplementation against asthma and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction.42–44 However, recent studies indicated that high-dose vitamin B6 and B12 supplementation was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer in men, particularly among smokers.45 46 Furthermore, for well-nourished elderly individuals, daily intake of physiological doses of multivitamin and mineral supplements or 200 mg of vitamin E did not reduce the incidence or severity of acute respiratory infections; in fact, vitamin E was shown to exacerbate disease severity.47 These findings underscore the complex impact of various vitamins and micronutrients on health, highlighting the necessity for cautious use of high-dose supplements.48

Our study results indicated that the potential protective effect of CDAI on pulmonary function was more pronounced in women and those aged 65 years and older. Considering the cyclic changes in female hormone levels and the hormonal regulation of the inflammatory process, this may be due to hormonal changes across sexes and ages. Oestrogen is known for its anti-inflammatory effects, which may enhance the anti-inflammatory effects of dietary antioxidants.49 In older adults, as age increases, physiological functions including pulmonary function naturally decline. A higher CDAI score indicates lower levels of inflammation and better antioxidant capacity, which may help slow the ageing process and reduce the risks of lung diseases.50 Our results indicate that, in older age groups, where inflammation and oxidative stress due to physiological ageing are more pronounced, maintaining a higher CDAI score may offer greater benefits.

A study showed that higher serum levels of antioxidant vitamins (vitamin A, β-cryptoxanthin, vitamin C and vitamin E), selenium, standardised calcium, chloride and iron were independently associated with higher FEV1 levels.51 However, evidence on the effects of zinc on pulmonary function remains limited and controversial. For example, some studies showed a significant association between higher dietary zinc intake and a lower prevalence of pulmonary function disorders,52 while other studies indicated that increased dietary zinc intake reduced the risks of pulmonary function disorders.15 53 54 Interestingly, our study found a U-shape relationship between dietary zinc and FEV1 levels, indicating a potential threshold for dietary zinc intakes, after which the inverse associations with pulmonary function may be observed. One possible explanation is that dietary zinc has both antioxidant and pro-oxidative properties, which can elicit oxidative stress when levels exceed the physiological range.55

Our study had several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this study for the first time reported the association between CDAI and pulmonary function parameters. Moreover, we rigorously examined two extensive datasets, GBCS and NHANES and showed consistent results. The association between CDAI and pulmonary function parameters was further explored through the application of different methods (ie, generalised linear regression, RCS analysis and an XGBoost model). Additionally, given the cohort nature of the GBCS, future studies could leverage its longitudinal data to evaluate the long-term effects of CDAI on pulmonary function. Such analyses would help elucidate whether sustained high dietary antioxidant intake confers long-term protective effects on pulmonary function trajectories. However, some limitations warrant consideration. First, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal associations between CDAI and pulmonary function parameters cannot be established. Second, the CDAI was determined using varying combinations of nutrients in the NHANES and GBCS datasets, potentially introducing bias when evaluating the dietary antioxidant capacity in diverse study populations.11 26 However, our sensitivity analysis using a harmonised CDAI showed consistent results with our primary analysis. This consistency suggests that the dataset-specific discrepancy in CDAI components did not affect the main conclusions. Additionally, variations in the calculation formulas for FEV1pp and FVCpp between Chinese and American populations may limit the comparability of the results.

In conclusion, our analysis of the GBCS and NHANES databases found significant positive associations of CDAI with FEV1 and FEV1pp, with a stronger association in women and those aged 65 and older. The findings underscore the potential of dietary antioxidants to mitigate age-related declines in pulmonary function and reduce the risk of lung diseases. Further interventional studies are needed to establish causality and to explore the optimal combinations and dosages of dietary antioxidants for maintaining pulmonary function.
