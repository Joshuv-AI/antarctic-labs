Characterization and comparative analysis of the complete chloroplast genomes of twelve Allium species from Kazakhstan
Authors: Moldir Yermagambetova, Shyryn Almerekova, Alan Ibraimov, Bektemir Osmonali, Yerlan Turuspekov, Saule Abugalieva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Allium L. represents one of the largest and taxonomically complex groups of monocots, with Central Asia recognized as a major center of its diversity. Despite the high species richness of Allium in Kazakhstan, genomic data for many native taxa remain limited. In this study, we sequenced, assembled, and analyzed the complete chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species from Kazakhstan. All chloroplast genomes exhibited a conserved quadripartite structure, with genome sizes ranging from 152,029 to 153,521 bp and a uniform gene content of 137 genes, including 88 protein-coding genes, 38 tRNAs, 8 rRNAs, and 3 pseudogenes. Comparative analyses revealed high structural conservation, with most sequence divergence concentrated in intergenic regions. Several highly variable regions, including ycf1, matK, rpoC2, and ycf2, were identified as potential molecular markers. Phylogenetic analyses based on chloroplast genome sequences using Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian approaches recovered three major chloroplast genome-based lineages within Allium, largely consistent with previous phylogenomic studies. Divergence-time analyses suggested that major chloroplast lineage diversification events within the genus occurred during the early Eocene (ca. 47.97 Mya). Overall, this study expands the currently available chloroplast genomic resources for Allium from Kazakhstan, provides insights into chloroplast genome evolution and chloroplast genome-based relationships, and establishes a valuable foundation for future phylogenetic, taxonomic, and evolutionary studies of this diverse genus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58942-9.Keywords: Allium, Kazakhstan, Chloroplast genome, Protein-coding genes, Molecular markers, Phylogenetic analyses, Divergence timeSubject terms: Evolution, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

The genus Allium L. (Amaryllidaceae, subfamily Allioideae) is one of the largest monocotyledonous genera, comprising more than 900-1,000 species distributed predominantly across the Northern Hemisphere1–5. According to the most widely accepted classification, based primarily on nuclear ribosomal ITS (nrITS) sequences, Allium is divided into three major evolutionary lineages comprising 15 subgenera and approximately 72 Sects1,5. Based on recent molecular and morphological studies, an additional subgenus has been proposed within the third evolutionary lineage6. According to floristic treatments and regional taxonomic revisions, more than 120 species of Allium occur in Kazakhstan7–9, many of which are endemic or subendemic to the Tian Shan mountain system10,11. Consequently, Kazakhstan represents a key center of wild onion diversity in Central Asia.

The genus Allium has been the focus of extensive molecular research aimed at clarifying its phylogeny, classification, and evolutionary origin1,12–16. With the development of molecular methods, plastid markers such as matK, rbcL, rps16, trnD-trnT, trnL-trnF, trnQ-rps16, trnH-psbA, rbcL-atpB, and rpl32-trnL have been introduced to enhance resolution and improve species delimitation15,17–19. Additionally, morphological studies, particularly of seed and floral traits, have provided valuable taxonomic information for Allium20,21.

In Kazakhstan, molecular studies of Allium have predominantly relied on nrITS and plastid markers, such as matK11,22. Abugalieva et al.22 analyzed Allium species from Kazakhstan using ITS and matK sequences and revealed the relationship between morphology-based classification and molecular phylogenetic patterns. More recently, Vesselova and coauthors11 applied ITS analysis to species from the western Kyrgyz Alatau and reported similar discrepancies between molecular and morphological groupings. These regional studies highlight the limitations of analyses based on a small number of loci and emphasize the need for genome-scale data for further phylogenetic studies. Similar incongruences between traditional sectional delimitation and molecular results were reported in broader Central Asian studies23,24. However, analyses based on single or a few loci often lack sufficient phylogenetic signals to resolve complex evolutionary relationships in rapidly diversified groups14,25.

The chloroplast genome has become a powerful tool in plant phylogenetics due to its conserved quadripartite structure, relatively small effective population size, predominantly uniparental inheritance, and low recombination rate26,27. Complete chloroplast genome sequencing provides substantially more phylogenetically informative characters than individual loci and significantly improves taxonomic resolution28,29. Chloroplast genome-based studies on Allium have rapidly increased with comparative and phylogenomic analyses being conducted at sectional, subgeneric, and genus-wide levels3,24,25,30–33. Comparative chloroplast genome analyses have also identified several highly variable regions, including ycf1, ndhF, rpoC2, matK, and ndhD, which might be promising candidates for DNA barcoding marker development within the genus24,31,33. Dated phylogeny-based genus-wide chloroplast analyses suggested that Allium originated in the late Eocene (~ 42 Mya) and diversified into three principal evolutionary lineages from the early Eocene to the middle Miocene3,34.

Most available chloroplast genomes represent East Asian or economically important cultivated taxa30,31, whereas many wild species from Kazakhstan have not yet been investigated at the level of the complete chloroplast genome. Central Asia, including Kazakhstan, is also recognized as one of the main centers of Allium species diversity22,35. Therefore, species distributed across the steppe, desert, and mountain ecosystems of Kazakhstan may provide important insights into the genus’s ecological adaptation and lineage diversification.

In the present study, we sequenced and analyzed the complete chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species from Kazakhstan: A. atrosanguineum Schrenk, A. cretaceum N.Friesen & Seregin, A. inconspicuum Vved., A. inderiense Fisch. ex Bunge, A. lineare L., A. montanostepposum N.Friesen & Seregin, A. nutans L., A. sabulosum Steven ex Bunge, A. subscabrum (Regel) R.M.Fritsch, A. tianschanicum Rupr., A. korolkowii Regel, and A. turkestanicum Regel. This study aimed to: (1) characterize and compare the structure, gene content, and sequence variation of chloroplast genomes; (2) identify highly variable regions suitable as DNA barcodes for phylogenetic studies and evaluate the potential of simple sequence repeats as molecular markers for assessing population diversity; (3) reconstruct chloroplast genome-based phylogenetic relationships among Allium species; and (4) analyze divergence times among Allium species. By expanding chloroplast sampling in Kazakhstan, this study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of Allium evolution and systematics in this understudied region.

Materials and methods

Plant material and DNA extraction

Fresh leaf material from 12 species of the genus Allium (Allium atrosanguineum, A. cretaceum, A. inconspicuum, A. inderiense, A. lineare, A. montanostepposum, A. nutans, A. sabulosum, A. subscabrum, A. tianschanicum, A. korolkowii, and A. turkestanicum) (Fig. 1) was collected in various regions of Kazakhstan. Collecting was conducted under permits issued by the Committee of Forestry and Wildlife of the Ministry of Ecology, Geology, and Natural Resources of the Republic of Kazakhstan. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the herbarium of the Institute of Plant Biology and Biotechnology (Table S1). The specimens were formally identified by Bektemir Osmonali, PhD.

Detailed information on sampling locations is presented in Table 1. Collected leaves were immediately dried using silica gel and subsequently employed for genomic DNA isolation. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the dried leaf tissues following the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol described by Doyle and Doyle36. DNA quality and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Chloroplast genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

DNA samples that successfully passed quality control were used for library construction with the TruSeq Nano DNA Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). High-throughput sequencing of chloroplast genomes was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The quality of raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC v0.11.7 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc, accessed on 02 February 2025). Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with Trimmomatic v0.3837. Sequencing quality was high across all samples, with post-filter Q30 scores ranging from 91.4% to 97.1% and Q20 scores ranging from 97.6% to 99.6%. Chloroplast genome coverage was 100% for all samples, and the average mapping depth ranged from 168× to 652×. De novo assembly of chloroplast genomes was conducted using NOVOPlasty38, followed by genome annotation with GeSeq39. Circular gene maps of the annotated chloroplast genomes were generated using the online tool Chloroplot40. The newly obtained chloroplast genomes were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PZ015912 (A. atrosanguineum), PZ015913 (A. cretaceum), PZ015914 (A. inconspicuum), PZ015915 (A. inderiense), PZ015916 (A. lineare), PZ015917 (A. montanostepposum), PZ015918 (A. nutans), PZ015919 (A. sabulosum), PZ015920 (A. subscabrum), PZ015921 (A. tianschanicum), PZ015922 (A. turkestanicum), and PZ015923 (A. korolkowii). The raw sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession numbers SAMN57508487–SAMN57508498 (Table S1), and all datasets are associated with the BioProject ID PRJNA1458471.

Comparative chloroplast genome analysis

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genomes among the studied Allium species was performed using mVISTA41 in Shuffle-LAGAN mode42. Complete chloroplast genome sequences of the Allium species were aligned using Geneious Prime® 2025.0.3 (https://www.geneious.com, accessed on 10 February 2025). Nucleotide diversity (Pi) across the aligned sequences was calculated using DnaSP v643 with a sliding-window approach. The analysis was conducted with a window size of 600 bp and a step size of 200 bp.

Simple sequence repeats analysis

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the chloroplast genomes of the 12 Allium species were identified using the MISA software44. The minimum repeat thresholds were set at 8 for mononucleotide SSRs, 4 for di- and trinucleotide SSRs, and 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide SSRs.

Codon analysis and Ka/Ks ratio analysis

For codon usage analysis, protein-coding genes were extracted from the chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species. A total of 54 shared protein-coding sequences (CDS) were selected (Table S2) after filtering out overlapping genes and sequences shorter than 300 bp, following the criteria described by Wright45. Only non-redundant CDS containing a valid ATG start codon and exceeding 300 base pairs were retained for further analysis. Subsequently, codon usage bias indices, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values46 were calculated for each sequence using CodonW version 1.4.247. Pairwise nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates in protein-coding genes were estimated using the Yang-Nielsen (YN) method implemented in KaKs Calculator 2.048. A total of 79 shared CDSs were included in the analysis (Table S2). Genes containing premature stop codons, ambiguous alignments, substantial missing data, or insufficient homologous sequence length were excluded to ensure reliable estimation of substitution rates.

Phylogenetic analysis and divergence time estimation

The phylogenetic dataset comprised 12 newly sequenced chloroplast genomes of Allium species from Kazakhstan, together with 39 complete chloroplast genome sequences retrieved from NCBI GenBank. Two species, Nothoscordum bivalve and Tulbaghia violacea, were included as outgroup taxa. Phylogenetic analyses were performed based on concatenated protein-coding gene sequences. A total of 53 chloroplast genome sequences (51 Allium and 2 outgroup taxa) were used to reconstruct phylogenetic trees and infer the phylogenetic position of the studied taxa within the genus Allium (Table S1). Sequence alignment was performed in Geneious Prime® 2025.0.3 (https://www.geneious.com; accessed on 12 February 2025) using the MAFFT algorithm49. Of the 79 protein-coding genes retained after initial filtering, 77 shared CDSs (Table S2) in 53 chloroplast genomes were used for phylogenetic reconstruction and divergence-time analyses. Two genes were excluded due to alignment ambiguity and missing data, which could have affected tree reconstruction and divergence-time analyses. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) approaches. The ML analysis was performed in IQ-TREE 350 under the GTR + F+I+R3 substitution model with 100,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support. Bayesian inference was performed using MrBayes v3.251. The best partitioning scheme and substitution model were selected using PartitionFinder v2.1.1, using models=mrbayes; search=greedy, branchlengths=linked; model_selection=aicc. MrBayes v3.2.7 was run with two simultaneous runs, each using 10 million MCMC generations, sampling every 1,000, 4 chains (3 heated, 1 cold), and a burn-in of 0.25. Convergence was ensured by ensuring that the average standard deviation of split frequencies was less than 0.01, potential scale reduction factors (PSRFs) were close to 1, and minimum effective sample sizes (ESSs) were greater than 200 for all estimated parameters. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited in FigTree52. We time-calibrated the chloroplast genome-based phylogeny of Allium using MCMCtree in PAML v4.953 based on 77 shared CDS. No reliable fossils are available for Allium; therefore, we decided to enhance our sampling by incorporating species from Asparagales to calibrate using fossils from related clades. Because MCMCtree requires a precomputed tree topology, we used IQTREE 350 to reconstruct it with the MFP+MERGE options -alrt 1000 -B 1000. The root was constrained using a secondary calibration point for the stem age of Orchidaceae, 102–120 Ma54. The crown age of Astelia was constrained by 23.2 Ma55. The crown age of Hemerocallidoidea was 38-47.8 Ma56. The stem age of Yucca ranged from 14.5 to 16.3 Ma57. All calibrations were set with soft bounds of 0.025 probability at both sides. The analysis was run treating the sequence as a single partition. The rough substitution rate (rgene gamma) was set to G (2, 46.54), corresponding to 0.042976 substitutions per 10^8 years, as calculated by BASEML. The rate variance was defined as G (1, 10). An independent-rates log-normal relaxed-clock model (clock = 2) was applied, and the substitution model was GTR + GAMMA (model = 7, alpha = 0.5, ncatG = 5). The MCMC analysis was run for a total of 11,000,000 generations, with the first 1,000,000 generations discarded as burn-in. Samples were collected every 100 generations, yielding 100,000 samples for posterior estimation. Convergence was assessed using Tracer v1.7.258, and all ESS values exceeded 200.The resulting chronogram was visualized using chiplot59. Subgenus and section names for Allium species followed those of previously published studies1,24,34.

Results

General features of the 12 Allium chloroplast genomes

Complete chloroplast genomes were obtained for 12 Allium species collected across different regions of Kazakhstan. All chloroplast genomes exhibited the typical quadripartite chloroplast structure, comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeats (IRs) (Fig. 2).

Different colours indicate functional gene categories. LSC, large single-copy region; SSC, small single-copy region; IR, inverted repeat region.

The complete chloroplast genomes of the 12 studied Allium species ranged in size from 152,029 bp (A. inderiense) to 153,521 bp (A. atrosanguineum), with a total variation of approximately 1.5 kb (Table 2). This narrow size range indicates a high level of structural conservation among the analyzed taxa. All chloroplast genomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, consisting of an LSC region, an SSC region, and a pair of IRs.

The LSC region varied from 81,201 bp (A. inconspicuum) to 83,039 bp (A. subscabrum), the SSC region from 17,259 bp (A. subscabrum) to 18,059 bp (A. atrosanguineum), and the IR regions from 26,370 bp (A. turkestanicum) to 26,507 bp (A. atrosanguineum and A. inconspicuum). Gene content and functional categories

The chloroplast genomes of the 12 Allium species exhibited a highly conserved gene composition, comprising genes involved in self-replication, photosynthesis, and other chloroplast functions (Table 3). Each chloroplast genome encoded 137 genes, including 88 protein-coding genes, 38 tRNA genes, 8 rRNA genes, and 3 pseudogenes (infA and two copies of ycf15). Functionally, these genes were categorized into three groups: 76 genes related to self-replication, 47 genes associated with photosynthesis, and 14 genes assigned to other functions, including pseudogenes (Table 3).

A total of 22 genes were duplicated, primarily located in the inverted repeat (IR) regions. These included ten protein-coding genes, including pseudogene (rpl2, rpl23, rps7, rps12, rps19, ycf1, ycf2, ndhB, ycf15, and ycf68), eight tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnH-GUG, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnR-ACG, trnN-GUU, and trnV-GAC), and all rRNA genes (rrn16, rrn23, rrn4.5, and rrn5).

In addition, 15 genes contained introns, including 12 genes with a single intron (e.g., atpF, rpl2, rps16, ndhA, and rpoC1) and 3 genes with two introns (rps12, clpP, and ycf3). This intron organization is consistent with that reported for typical angiosperm chloroplast genomes (Table 3).

Comparative and nucleotide diversity analysis

Comparative alignment using mVISTA (Shuffle‑LAGAN) revealed strong overall structural conservation among the 12 Allium chloroplast genomes (Fig. 3). Coding regions exhibited higher sequence conservation than non-coding regions. Most sequence divergence was concentrated in intergenic regions. The comparison also showed that the IR regions were more conserved than the LSC and SSC regions. The overall gene order and genome structure were highly conserved, indicating a stable organization of the chloroplast genome among the examined Allium species.

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species using the mVISTA. Gray arrows above the alignment indicate gene orientation and position. Blue regions represent exons, light blue regions represent untranslated regions (UTRs), and red regions indicate conserved non-coding sequences (CNS). The vertical scale represents sequence identity ranging from 50% to 100%.

Nucleotide diversity (Pi) was assessed using two approaches: (1) based on CDS and (2) across the whole complete chloroplast genome (Fig. 4). For the CDS dataset, the overall nucleotide diversity was relatively low, ranging from 0 to 0.02689 with a mean Pi value of 0.0109. The relatively highest Pi value was observed in the matK (Pi = 0.0268) region, followed by rpoC2 (0.0243), ycf1-ndhF (0.0239), accD (0.0216), ccsA-ndhD (0.0214), rpoA (0.0192), and rpoC1 (0.01869) gene regions (Fig. 4a). In contrast, analysis of the complete chloroplast genome revealed relatively higher overall nucleotide diversity, ranging from 0 to 0.05394 with a mean Pi value of 0.0149 (Fig. 4b). The most variable region was ycf2 (Pi = 0.0539), followed by rps19 (0.0491), rpoA (0.0446), and rps16-trnQ(UUG) (0.0413), ycf1 (0.0382), trnT(GGU)-psbD (0.0373), and rps7-trnV(GAC) (0.0359). The distribution of nucleotide diversity across the chloroplast genome showed regional variation, with higher Pi values observed in the LSC and SSC regions, while the IR regions exhibited comparatively lower variability.

Simple sequence repeats identification

A total of 2,886 chloroplast SSRs were identified across the chloroplast genomes of twelve Allium species (Table S3; Fig. 5). The number of SSRs per chloroplast genome ranged from 230 in A. inderiense and A. inconspicuum to 253 in A. korolkowii (Fig. 5). Mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant type, accounting for 2,103 SSRs (72.9%), and were predominantly composed of A/T motifs, indicating a strong AT bias typical of chloroplast genomes. The number of mononucleotide repeats varied among species, ranging from 164 in A. subscabrum to 182 in A. korolkowii, A. cretaceum, and A. nutans. Dinucleotide repeats represented the second most common class, with 603 occurrences (20.9%), varying from 47 in A. lineare and A. cretaceum to 55 in A. korolkowii. Trinucleotide repeats were rare, with only 27 occurrences (1–5 per species). Among longer repeat types, tetranucleotide repeats accounted for 122 SSRs, with the highest numbers observed in A. cretaceum (14) and A. subscabrum (12). Pentanucleotide repeats were also infrequent (28 SSRs, 1–5 per species), whereas hexanucleotide repeats were the rarest type, with only three occurrences detected exclusively in A. korolkowii, A. inconspicuum, and A. nutans (Table S3).

Codon usage bias and relative synonymous codon usage analysis

A total of 54 CDS longer than 300 bp were selected for codon usage analysis. These concatenated genes ranged from 59,356 to 64,084 bp across the 12 Allium species and encoded 25,196 (A. turkestanicum) to 26,751 (A. subscabrum) synonymous codons. Among the amino acids, leucine (Leu), serine (Ser), and arginine (Arg), which are encoded by six synonymous codons, exhibited the highest variability in codon usage (Fig. 6; Table S4). The RSCU analysis showed that 29 codons had RSCU values greater than 1, indicating preferential usage, whereas methionine (Met, AUG) and tryptophan (Trp, UGG) exhibited no codon bias (RSCU = 1). Most preferred codons ended with A or U at the third codon position, while codons ending with G or C generally showed lower RSCU values. Among amino acids, isoleucine (Ile) was the most abundant, with a mean value of 2,324 codons, followed by glycine (Gly, 1,788), arginine (Arg, 1,584), and phenylalanine (Phe, 1,542). Leucine (Leu) was also highly represented, with a combined average of 2736 codons, while serine (Ser) showed a total mean value of 2087 codons.

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of 20 amino acids and stop codons in 12 Allium chloroplast genomes.

Moderately abundant amino acids included alanine (Ala, 1,393), valine (Val, 1,408), glutamic acid (Glu, 1,388), threonine (Thr, 1342), and asparagine (Asn, 1,260). Lower frequencies were observed for histidine (His, 661), glutamine (Gln, 925), and proline (Pro, 1,081), while cysteine (Cys, 312) and tryptophan (Trp, 467) were the least abundant amino acids. The amino acid composition was similar across all analyzed species, with only minor variations in codon counts (Table S4).

Ka/Ks-based selective pressure analysis

A total of 79 shared CDSs from 12 Allium species were used to estimate synonymous (Ks), nonsynonymous (Ka), and Ka/Ks ratios (Fig. 7; Table S5). The distribution of these values revealed that both Ks and Ka remained relatively low and stable across most genes, whereas Ka/Ks ratios exhibited variation, indicating heterogeneous selective pressures among genes. Most genes showed Ka/Ks values below 1, suggesting that purifying selection predominates across most protein-coding genes. However, several genes exhibited relatively high Ka/Ks ratios, suggesting either relaxed selective constraints or accelerated sequence evolution relative to other chloroplast genes. In particular, rps2 showed the highest Ka/Ks value (1.621395), followed by rpl23 (1.213490). Additionally, elevated Ka/Ks ratios were observed in rps16 (0.791096), ycf2 (0.781532), rpl22 (0.619725), ycf1 (0.599382), ndhC (0.525761), matK (0.520555), and rpoA (0.502692) (Fig. 7; Table S5). The LSC region exhibited the greatest variability in Ka/Ks values, including the highest value observed in rps2. The IR regions showed comparatively moderate variation, although genes such as rpl23 and ycf2 displayed elevated ratios. The SSC region also demonstrated variability, with genes such as ycf1 contributing to higher Ka/Ks values. Overall, most chloroplast protein-coding genes exhibited Ka/Ks ratios below 1, whereas elevated Ka/Ks values were observed in a small subset of genes, including rps2, rpl23, rps16, ycf1, and ycf2 (Fig. 7; Table S5).

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic relationships among Allium species were reconstructed from 77 CDS across complete chloroplast genomes using both ML and BI approaches (Fig. 8). The two methods produced highly congruent topologies with strong statistical support for most nodes, demonstrating strong support for the inferred chloroplast genome-based relationships. The analysis clearly distinguished the genus Allium from the outgroup taxa (Tulbaghia violacea and Nothoscordum bivalve) and recovered three major chloroplast genome-based lineages within the genus.

Clade I represents an early‑diverging, well‑supported basal clade composed mainly of species from subgenus Amerallium (sections Molium, Kingdonia, and Bromatorrhiza) and subgenus Microscordum. This group includes A. moly, A. kingdonii, A. hookeri, and A. monanthum, representing a taxonomically diverse assemblage of early‑branching Allium species. Clade II comprises taxa primarily assigned to subgenera Melanocrommyum, Porphyroprason, Vvedenskya, and Anguinum. Distinct, well‑supported subclades within this lineage correspond to sections Acmopetala, Kaloprason, Melanocrommyum, Longibidentata, and Decipientia. Among the species analyzed in this study, A. subscabrum was placed within this lineage, clustering together with other representatives of subgenus Melanocrommyum. Clade III, the largest and most heterogeneous group, includes species from multiple subgenera, such as Cepa, Allium, Rhizirideum, Polyprason, Reticulatobulbosa, and Butomissa. Numerous well‑resolved subclades correspond to traditional sectional groups such as Schoenoprasum, Cepa, Anuloprason, Campanulata, and Rhizirideum. The newly sequenced Kazakhstani species are distributed across multiple subclades within Lineage III (Fig. 8).

Divergence time estimation

Divergence time analysis based on 77 shared CDS revealed the temporal framework of chloroplast lineage evolution within Allium. The chloroplast genome-based divergence-time analysis suggests that Allium originated approximately 47.97 Mya during the early Eocene (Fig. 9). The major chloroplast genome-based lineages of Allium began to diversify approximately 31.15 Mya, in the early Oligocene. The initial separation of Lineage I is estimated at approximately 31.15 Mya (Oligocene). Subsequently, Lineages II and III diverged around 28.29 Mya, suggesting substantial diversification of chloroplast lineages during the Paleogene. Further diversification of chloroplast lineages mainly occurred from the Oligocene to the Miocene. In particular, Lineage III underwent extensive diversification during the Miocene, with multiple divergence events estimated to have occurred < 15 Mya among taxa of the subgenera Cepa, Rhizirideum, Allium, Reticulatobulbosa, and Polyprason. Lineage II also diversified during the Miocene, forming well-defined clades corresponding to Melanocrommyum and related subgenera. Lineage I exhibits comparatively earlier divergence but still shows internal diversification events primarily in the Miocene.

Discussion

The complete chloroplast genomes of the 12 Allium species from Kazakhstan analyzed in this study exhibit a remarkable degree of structural conservation across genome size, gene order, and GC content. Genome sizes ranged from 152,029 to 153,521 bp, with GC content around 37%, and all genomes contained 137 genes, including 88 protein-coding, 38 tRNA, 8 rRNA, and 3 pseudogenes (Table 2). These values were nearly identical to those reported for other Allium chloroplast genomes from Central and East Asia23,25,60. This high overall uniformity reinforces that Allium chloroplast genomes are among the most structurally stable within Amaryllidaceae33,61. The consistent pseudogenization of infA (translation initiation factor 1) and ycf15 (putative protein RF15) detected here supports previous evidence that pseudogene formation is a common and often evolutionarily conserved event in Allium chloroplast genomes24,62–64. Pseudogenization in chloroplast genomes may be associated with structural rearrangements, mutation accumulation, or lineage-specific evolutionary pressures, including adaptation to diverse ecological conditions or environmental changes26,64–66.

Comparative alignment of nucleotide sequences of 12 Allium chloroplast genomes revealed a highly conserved genomic structure, with most variation confined to intergenic regions rather than coding sequences (Fig. 3). This overall conservation was also supported by mVISTA analysis, which is consistent with previous studies reporting high synteny and limited variation in gene order across Allium chloroplast genomes33,34. Sequence variability was mainly confined to the LSC and SSC, whereas the IR regions showed strong conservation, consistent with previous results for many angiosperm taxa67–70.

Sliding-window analysis of nucleotide diversity identified relatively highly variable regions in both the CDS dataset and the complete chloroplast genome sequences (Fig. 4; Table 4). In particular, the ycf2, rps19, rpoA, rps16-trnQ(UUG), ycf1, trnT(GGU)-psbD, rps7-trnV(GAC), matK, rpoC2, ycf1-ndhF, accD, ccsA-ndhD, and rpoC1 gene regions exhibited relatively elevated nucleotide diversity. These results are consistent with previous studies on members of the Amaryllidaceae family30,33,34,71. Most sequence variations were localized in the LSC and SSC regions, whereas the IR regions remained highly conserved, likely owing to the stabilizing effect of sequence duplication72. Therefore, these relatively hypervariable loci represent promising molecular markers for further phylogenetic and DNA-barcoding studies in Allium.

Microsatellites, or SSRs, are widely used as informative molecular markers due to their high polymorphism, abundance, and reproducibility, representing valuable tools in population genetic analyses63,73–76. In the present study, 2,886 chloroplast SSRs were identified across the chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species (Fig. 5). The SSR profiles showed a pronounced bias toward A/T-rich mononucleotide repeats, which accounted for more than 70% of all detected SSRs, consistent with previous reports on monocot chloroplast genomes77–79. SSR distribution patterns were highly similar across all examined species, with mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeats predominating, whereas longer repeat motifs were relatively uncommon, as observed in many other plant taxa80–82. These findings indicate a conserved microsatellite composition across Allium chloroplast genomes and highlight the potential of chloroplast SSRs as molecular markers for assessing genetic diversity within and among Allium wild populations.

Codon usage preference is an important feature of biological evolution, influenced by multiple factors that affect the function of the genetic code, including genome size, mutational bias, genetic drift, natural selection, gene expression, and protein structure83. The codon usage patterns observed in the 12 Allium chloroplast genome nucleotide sequences (Fig. 6) are consistent with previous reports for this genus and other angiosperm chloroplast genomes, showing a strong preference for A/U-ending codons, which reflects the AT-rich nature of plastid DNA32,84–86. Such codon bias, including elevated RSCU values for A/U-ending codons, has been widely documented in Allium chloroplast genomes33,86. The lack of bias in methionine and tryptophan is expected because they are encoded by single codons, whereas the greater variability in leucine, serine, and arginine reflects the influence of synonymous codon multiplicity. Additionally, the codon usage bias in Allium is largely driven by mutational pressure toward AT enrichment, with only minor variation among closely related species, indicating strong evolutionary conservation87.

Selective‑pressure analysis revealed that most protein‑coding genes are under purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1), highlighting the functional stability of chloroplast genes (Fig. 7). Nevertheless, a small subset of genes, including rps2, rpl23, ycf1, and ycf2, exhibited elevated Ka/Ks ratios relative to the genomic background. Such patterns may reflect relaxed selective constraints or lineage-specific evolutionary dynamics. However, elevated pairwise Ka/Ks values alone are insufficient to infer positive selection. Pairwise Ka/Ks estimates can be affected by low synonymous substitution rates, alignment uncertainty, short coding sequences, or other methodological factors, particularly when based on a limited number of taxa88,89. Similar patterns of elevated Ka/Ks ratios in rps2 and ycf2 genes have also been reported in section Daghestanica31, section Bromatorrhiza33 of genus Allium, and several other plant chloroplast genomes90–92. These genes are involved in chloroplast ribosomal function (rps2) and plastome maintenance (ycf1, ycf2), and their relatively accelerated evolution may reflect differences in functional constraints among chloroplast genes. Therefore, the observed elevated Ka/Ks values should be regarded as indicative of potential variation in evolutionary rates rather than conclusive evidence of adaptive evolution.

Recent advances in sequencing technologies have enabled the generation of complete chloroplast genome sequences, which are now widely applied in phylogenetic studies of numerous plant taxa93–95 and are increasingly recognized as effective super-barcodes96,97. In the present study, we sequenced the complete chloroplast genomes of 12 Allium species from Kazakhstan and used these data to infer phylogenetic relationships using ML and BI methods (Fig. 8). The resulting phylogenetic trees resolved three major chloroplast genome-based lineages within Allium with strong statistical support. In both analyses, Allium species were clearly separated from the outgroup taxa, confirming the genus’s monophyly. In contrast, the subgenera Rhizirideum, Reticulatobulbosa, Allium, Polyprason, and Cepa within lineage III were recovered as paraphyletic. This pattern is consistent with previous studies based on nrITS14,98, chloroplast regions99, and complete chloroplast genome data64, which also questioned the monophyly of these subgenera. However, the phylogenetic results are based on a limited number of species and solely on chloroplast genome data, which may not fully capture the complex evolutionary processes shaping species relationships within the genus. Therefore, future studies should include broader taxon sampling and incorporate nuclear genomic data to clarify phylogenetic relationships within the genus. Nevertheless, the chloroplast genome sequences generated in this study represent a valuable contribution and may provide important information for future genome-scale phylogenetic studies of Allium.

Divergence time reconstruction (Fig. 9) based on chloroplast genome data indicated that the stem lineage of Allium originated in the early Eocene, approximately 47.97 Mya. Previous studies have reported broadly comparable estimates, including approximately 45.79 Mya based on nrITS98, 49.79 Mya based on complete chloroplast genomes3,34, 41.93 Mya3, and 47.7 Mya61 based on CDS of chloroplast genome, 52.2 Mya based on nrITS and three chloroplast markers100, and approximately 52 Mya based on Angiosperms353 target-enrichment probe set101. Although these estimates are generally consistent, the observed differences among studies may reflect differences in taxon sampling, molecular datasets, and the calibration points used for divergence time estimation16. The extensive diversification of chloroplast lineages observed within Lineage III appears to have occurred primarily during the Miocene, coinciding with major geological and climatic changes in Central Asia3,102. During this period, the continued uplift of the Tian Shan, Pamir, and Altai mountain systems, together with increasing regional aridification, likely enhanced habitat heterogeneity and promoted geographic isolation, ecological differentiation, and subsequent lineage diversification103. Overall, chloroplast genome-based analyses in this study support an early Paleogene origin of the major chloroplast genome-based lineages of Allium3,61, followed by extensive diversification during the Miocene. Notably, Lineage III experienced particularly intensive species diversification during this period, suggesting that the expansion of steppe and montane habitats associated with tectonic uplift and progressive aridification may have played an important role in its radiation and evolutionary success104–106.

Kazakhstan occupies a key position within the Central Asian floristic region and is one of the major centers of plant species diversity107,108, including for the genus Allium35. The country’s environmental heterogeneity, encompassing mountain systems, steppes, semi-deserts, and deserts7,8,11,22, may have contributed to the diversification and persistence of distinct Allium lineages. In addition, Kazakhstani Allium populations constitute a valuable reservoir of genetic resources for the genus. Despite the recognized significance of this region for Allium diversity and distribution, chloroplast genomic resources and biogeographical studies of Kazakhstani Allium species remain relatively limited.

The chloroplast genome sequences generated in this study represent an important addition to the currently available genomic resources for Allium. By making these data publicly available, we provide a valuable resource for future studies addressing phylogenetic relationships, taxonomic questions, germplasm diversity, and the chloroplast genome evolution within the genus, particularly for Central Asian taxa that remain underrepresented in genomic databases.

Although chloroplast genomes provide valuable phylogenetic information, chloroplast-based phylogenies may not fully reflect species relationships because of hybridization, introgression, and incomplete lineage sorting109,110. Therefore, the relationships inferred in this study should be interpreted as chloroplast genome-based relationships, and future studies integrating broader taxonomic sampling and nuclear genomic data are needed to better resolve species relationships and evolutionary patterns within Allium.

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of complete chloroplast genomes from 12 Allium species from Kazakhstan, substantially expanding genomic resources for Central Asian wild onions. All chloroplast genomes showed highly conserved structures, gene content, and GC composition, while several highly variable regions (ycf1, ycf2, matK, rpoC2, and accD) were identified as promising molecular markers. The identified SSRs further provide useful resources for future studies of genetic diversity, population structure, and phylogeography. Phylogenetic analyses based on chloroplast genomes recovered three major chloroplast genome-based lineages within Allium, largely consistent with previous chloroplast-based studies. Divergence-time analyses suggest an early Eocene origin, followed by extensive diversification of chloroplast lineages during the Miocene. Overall, the genomic resources generated in this study contribute to our understanding of chloroplast genome evolution in Allium. These provide a valuable foundation for future phylogenetic, taxonomic, conservation, and germplasm-related studies. Nevertheless, broader taxonomic sampling and integration of nuclear genomic data will be required to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of species relationships and chloroplast genome evolution within this complex genus.
