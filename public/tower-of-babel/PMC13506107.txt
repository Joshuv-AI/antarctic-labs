Posterior C1–C2 screw-rod salvage for Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability after failed foramen magnum decompression
Authors: Shengli Guo, Luokai Huangfu, Guangyu Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Chiari I malformation secondary to atlantoaxial instability requires reduction and stabilization. When initial foramen magnum decompression (FMD) is performed without recognition of this instability, subsequent salvage surgery is technically demanding and carries substantial risk.Objective:The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of posterior C1–C2 screw-rod salvage surgery in patients with Chiari I malformation and atlantoaxial instability following failed FMD.Materials and Methods:We retrospectively analyzed 21 consecutive patients with Chiari I malformation and atlantoaxial instability who underwent posterior C1–C2 screw-rod salvage surgery after failed FMD. Atlantoaxial instability was defined by dynamic imaging showing an atlantodental interval (ADI) >3 mm on flexion or vertical odontoid protrusion above Wackenheim’s line (WL). Clinical status was assessed with the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score. Radiological outcomes were evaluated by ADI, odontoid tip–WL distance, and cervicomedullary angle (CMA) on computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging.Results:The cohort comprised 14 males and 7 females (mean age: 39.33 ± 8.2 years). Screws were successfully placed in all C1 lateral masses and in 40 of 42 C2 pedicles; in the remaining two cases, C2 inferior articular facet screws were used. Postoperative CT showed complete horizontal reduction (ADI <3 mm) in 90.5% and satisfactory vertical reduction in 85.7%. The mean JOA score improved from 9.8 preoperatively to 14.1 at 6-month follow-up (P < 0.01). The mean CMA increased from 129.1° ± 7.8° to 148.8° ± 4.5° (P < 0.01). Solid bony fusion was achieved in all patients with follow-up imaging; no implant failure or neurological deterioration occurred.Conclusion:Posterior C1–C2 screw-rod salvage surgery is a safe and effective strategy for Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability after failed FMD. This posterior-only technique provides simultaneous reduction, decompression, and stabilization, yielding significant clinical improvement and reliable fusion even in the previously operated, scarred craniocervical field.

INTRODUCTION

Chiari I malformation is characterized by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, resulting in progressive compression of the medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord. This compression often leads to secondary syringomyelia and disabling neurological deficits.[1] While foramen magnum decompression (FMD), with or without duraplasty, represents the standard treatment for Chiari I malformation without atlantoaxial instability,[2] this approach is contraindicated when Chiari I malformation is secondary to atlantoaxial instability. In such cases, due to the inherent atlantoaxial instability, FMD is contraindicated as it typically exacerbates symptoms and may even lead to sudden death.

For patients with Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability, the current mainstream treatment is posterior reduction and stabilization. This procedure reduces the odontoid process, alleviating compression of the medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord, and achieves atlantoaxial fixation. Long-term follow-up demonstrates gradual regression of the tonsillar herniation. However, clinicians may occasionally focus predominantly on the Chiari malformation evident on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), potentially overlooking concomitant atlantoaxial instability. This oversight can lead to inappropriate selection of FMD, which may exacerbate symptoms in these patients. For these patients, subsequent posterior reduction and fusion is considerably more complex and associated with higher risks. The initial surgery creates a compromised surgical field, characterized by the loss of key bony landmarks and significant scar tissue formation that distorts normal anatomy. This markedly increases the risks of infection, dural tear, and vascular injury.

This study presents the outcomes of a consecutive series in which a posterior atlantoaxial reduction and fusion was employed as a salvage treatment for patients with Chiari I malformation and atlantoaxial instability who had previously undergone failed FMD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This retrospective study enrolled consecutive patients who underwent posterior C1–C2 screw-rod reduction and fixation at our hospital between January 2019 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria comprised a diagnosis of Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability, coupled with a history of inadequate FMD at other hospitals. Atlantoaxial instability was defined by dynamic X-ray imaging showing an atlantodental interval (ADI) >3 mm on flexion or vertical odontoid protrusion above Wackenheim’s line (WL). All operations were carried out by the same experienced attending neurosurgeon. Clinical data, including age, sex, diagnosis, presenting symptoms, and surgical outcomes, were recorded and analyzed.

Preoperative evaluation

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative assessment including dynamic cervical radiographs (flexion–extension views), thin-slice computed tomography (CT) (0.625–1.0 mm), and MRI. The ADI was measured to quantify horizontal C1–C2 dislocation, while vertical dislocation was evaluated using the odontoid tip–WL distance. The cervicomedullary angle (CMA), measured on MRI, was used to assess the degree of compression at the medulla oblongata and upper cervical spinal cord. Neurological status was assessed by the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score.

Operative technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position. The head was secured in a neutral position using a Sugita head holder. A midline skin incision was made, and the previously decompressed suboccipital bone, the lamina of C2, and the superior–inferior margins of the C2 pedicle were readily exposed. The lateral articulation between C1 and C2 was exposed along the superior articular facet of C2 to facilitate identification of the C1 lateral masses. The articular capsule was opened, and the cartilage endplate was removed to restore joint mobility. Partial drilling of the infraoccipital margin was performed when necessary to improve access for C1 lateral mass screw placement.

Bilateral C2 screws and C1 lateral mass screws (diameter: 3.5 mm; length: 18–24 mm; Libeier Bio-engineering Institute Co., Ltd) were inserted according to the principles originally described by Goel and Laheri[3] and later modified for reduction by us.[4] Throughout the procedure, the vertebral arteries were carefully identified and protected. After placement of the four screws, titanium rods were connected and gradually tightened, functioning as a lever to anteriorly displace C1 and achieve sagittal reduction. Distraction between the C1 and C2 screws was then applied to inferiorly translate C2 and restore vertical alignment. Cancellous bone graft harvested from the posterior iliac crest was packed into the distracted C1–C2 joint spaces bilaterally, with additional morselized bone graft placed over the decorticated surfaces of the C1 lateral masses and C2 spinous process. The wound was closed in layers, and a hard cervical collar was applied postoperatively for 3 months.

Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, including both motor and sensory evoked potentials, was employed in all cases.

Postoperative evaluation and follow-up

Postoperative CT and MRI were performed 1 week after surgery. ADI and the odontoid tip–WL distance were measured on CT again to assess the extent of reduction. ADI <3 mm and the odontoid tip–WL distance <0 mm were considered complete reduction. CMA was measured on MRI to evaluate the degree of decompression at the medulla oblongata and upper cervical spinal cord. Six months after surgery, follow-up CT and MRI were repeated to evaluate whether reduction had been maintained and the internal fixation remained stable. Neurologic function was evaluated by the JOA scores again. Bony fusion was defined as either the formation of a continuous bone bridge between the C1 lateral mass and the C2 lamina, or evident osseous union across the C1–C2 lateral articulations.

Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests were used to compare preoperative and postoperative values for the JOA scores, odontoid tip-to-WL distance, ADI, and CMA. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 19.0.1, IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Twenty-one consecutive patients with Chiari I malformation and atlantoaxial instability underwent C1–C2 screw-rod reduction and fixation at our institution. The cohort constituted 14 males and 7 females with a mean age of 39.33 ± 8.2 years (range: 21–53). All patients had a history of inadequate FMD performed elsewhere, which was supplemented by occipitocervical fixation in three cases. Following the initial FMD, 16 patients experienced only transient improvement before eventual symptom aggravation, while 2 reported no improvement, and 3 had immediate aggravation. The most common presenting symptom was unsteady gait (20 patients, 95.24%). Progressive extremity weakness and extremity paresthesia occurred in 18 (85.71%). Screw placement was successful in all C1 lateral masses (42/42, 100%) and 40 of 42 C2 pedicles (95.2%). In the remaining two cases, C2 inferior articular facet screws were placed due to inadequate pedicle size. No intraoperative neurovascular injury or postoperative neurological deterioration was observed. Intraoperative dural tear occurred in one patient. It was managed with a graft of autologous muscle fascia, followed by postoperative lumbar cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, which led to successful healing.

Postoperative three-dimensional (3D) CT, which was performed 1 week after surgery, demonstrated complete horizontal reduction (ADI <3 mm) in 19 of 21 patients (90.5%) and satisfactory vertical reduction (normalized odontoid tip-to-WL distance) in 18 patients (85.7%).

Twenty of the 21 enrolled patients (95.2%) were available for follow-up, with a mean duration of 12.25 months (range: 6–24). One patient was lost to follow-up. Paired t-test demonstrated a significant increase in the mean JOA score from 9.8 preoperatively to 14.1 at the 6-month follow-up assessment (t = 18.650, P < 0.01). At the final follow-up, 3D CT confirmed a maintained reduction without evidence of redisplacement and successful bony fusion across the C1–C2 segment in all patients. MRI showed significant decompression of the medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord, which was quantitatively reflected in the marked improvement of the CMA. The mean CMA increased significantly from 129.1° ± 7.8° preoperatively to 148.8° ± 4.5° postoperatively (t = 9.80, P < 0.01) [Table 2].

Illustrative patients

Patient 7

A 28-year-old male presented with weakness of all four limbs. MRI revealed atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) and Chiari malformation [Figure 1a]. He underwent FMD and occipitocervical fixation at a local hospital, which resulted in symptom relief. Postoperative MRI showed improvement in cerebellar tonsillar herniation [Figure 1b] compared to the preoperative condition. However, 1 year postoperatively, his symptoms gradually worsened, leading him to seek treatment at our hospital. Neurological examination revealed grade III muscle strength in the right limb and grade IV muscle strength in the left limb. CT showed occipitalization, AAD, basilar invagination (BI), and C2–C3 fusion [Figure 1c]. MRI indicated cerebellar tonsillar herniation, medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord compression, and spinal cord swelling [Figure 1d]. On July 9, 2019, he underwent atlantoaxial reduction and internal fixation at our hospital. Postoperative CT confirmed satisfactory reduction of the odontoid process [Figure 1e]. Follow-up MRI showed relief of medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord compression [Figure 1f]. Follow-up CT demonstrated bony fusion within the bilateral atlantoaxial lateral joints [Figure 1g], along with satisfactory positioning of the atlas lateral mass screw and axis pedicle screw [Figure 1h].

Patient 18

A 21-year-old male presented with unsteady gait, neck pain, and weakness of four limbs. MRI revealed Chiari I malformation and AAD [Figure 2a]. He underwent FMD at a local hospital, but his symptoms failed to resolve and gradually worsened. Six years later, the patient presented to our hospital. Neurological examination demonstrated an unsteady gait, with muscle strength graded IV in the left limb and right lower limb and III in the right upper limb. CT scans showed occipitalization, AAD, and BI [Figure 2b and c]. 3D CT imaging revealed a bone defect from the previous FMD [Figure 2d], which had disrupted the normal anatomical landmarks and increased the difficulty of the revision surgery. MRI demonstrated progression of the Chiari I malformation and the presence of syringomyelia [Figure 2e]. On December 22, 2023, the patient underwent posterior atlantoaxial reduction and fixation at our hospital. Postoperative CT confirmed complete reduction of the odontoid process [Figure 2f] with satisfactory positioning of the C1 lateral mass screw and C2 pedicle screw [Figure 2g]. Follow-up MRI showed significant improvement in cerebellar tonsillar herniation and complete resolution of the syringomyelia [Figure 2h].

DISCUSSION

Chiari I malformation represents a complex neurological disorder that frequently coexists with atlantoaxial instability and BI.[5] The management of Chiari I malformation should be individualized.[6] Studies of CSF flow dynamics at the foramen magnum demonstrate the efficacy of FMD in patients with pure Chiari I malformation, resulting in both radiographic and neurological improvement.[7] However, when the condition is secondary to atlantoaxial instability, surgical strategy requires a fundamental shift. Dynamic cervical imaging in various planes (flexion–extension, rotation, and lateral bending) via X-ray or CT is instrumental in detecting atlantoaxial instability.[8] In this study, all patients fulfilled our strict dynamic instability criteria (ADI >3 mm on flexion and/or vertical migration >0 mm above WL), which had been overlooked at the time of the initial FMD.

The concept of central or axial atlantoaxial instability, as articulated by Goel,[9] is relevant to the pathomechanism underlying our series. In a recent dynamic imaging study, Goel et al.[8] demonstrated that in patients with Chiari formation and normal ADI (Type 2/3 central or AAD), occult instability can be unmasked by facet malalignment exceeding 5 mm on dynamic rotation or lateral tilt CT. Our cohort differs from that population in that all patients already exhibited clearly abnormal ADI (>3 mm) and/or vertical migration on conventional dynamic flexion–extension imaging, corresponding to Type 1 atlantoaxial facet instability in Goel’s classification – a pattern characterized by anterior displacement of the atlas facet, increased ADI, and neural compression opposite the odontoid tip.[510] Despite the difference in the primary diagnostic modality, the unifying pathoanatomical theme is chronic atlantoaxial instability leading to secondary tonsillar herniation, BI, and syringomyelia. Our surgical strategy of posterior distraction-based reduction directly addresses both the horizontal and vertical components of this instability, thereby restoring normal craniocervical alignment and decompressing the cervicomedullary junction.

For patients with Chiari I malformation and atlantoaxial instability, relieving ventral compression at the cervicomedullary junction as well as reestablishing stability is paramount for neurological improvement. Posterior reduction and fixation is the primary surgical approach, primarily encompassing occipitocervical and atlantoaxial techniques. In addition, the transoral approach for atlantoaxial reduction and fixation has also demonstrated favorable outcomes.[1011] These procedures work by alleviating spinal canal stenosis at the foramen magnum, thereby reestablishing CSF circulation across the craniovertebral junction.[12] Our data clearly demonstrate that the neurological improvement is attributable to both immediate anatomical reduction of the odontoid (evidenced by the significantly increased CMA) and subsequent stabilization, which restores physiological CSF dynamics and prevents ongoing microtrauma. The contribution of stabilization alone, without full reduction, would be insufficient to explain the rapid neurological recovery observed.

Posterior C1–C2 fixation was first reported by Goel and Laheri[3] and Goel[13] and Harms and Melcher,[14] and we modified this technique for not only fixation but also reduction of congenital AAD. Our early studies[41516] indicated that this modified C1–C2 screw and rod system was a promising technique for the treatment of AAD. The present results show that it is also effective for revision surgeries for Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability after failed FMD. This technique offers several distinct advantages. First, after complete opening and release of the atlantoaxial articulation, both vertical and horizontal reduction can be achieved through a single posterior approach. Distraction was performed for vertical reduction; horizontal reduction was achieved by fastening the screws and rods between C1 and C2, which acted as a lever system and pushed C2 forward. Second, in patients with prior failed internal fixation, removal of the previous construct can be accomplished simultaneously with the revision surgery. Third, the bone grafting method does not require iliac crest cortical bone – cancellous bone harvested from the C2 spinous process can be packed into the distracted joint space, and it is straightforward and effective.

We deliberately emphasize the posterior-only nature of this salvage procedure. In complex craniovertebral junction pathology, some strategies involve staged operations, such as anterior transoral odontoidectomy or traction followed by posterior fixation. Our series demonstrates that even after failed FMD, a single posterior approach can achieve definitive reduction and stabilization without the need for anterior procedures or multistage interventions, thereby reducing cumulative surgical risk.

Although posterior reduction and fixation can achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes in most patients with Chiari I malformation and AAD, those with persistent symptoms require salvage procedures. FMD is indicated for patients with residual posterior fossa volume insufficiency,[17] while a transoral or endoscopic endonasal odontoidectomy is the appropriate intervention when significant ventral compression remains.[1819] Additional radiological parameters, such as the clivo-axial angle and C1–C2 facet inclination, may further help identify axial instability and guide treatment decisions,[520] and they deserve prospective evaluation. In particular, for patients in whom ADI is borderline or normal but clinical suspicion remains high, the dynamic rotation and lateral tilt CT protocol recently described by Goel et al.[8] may reveal occult instability that would otherwise escape detection.

The take-home message of our study is that for Chiari I malformation patients who have failed FMD, the underlying dynamic instability must be rigorously investigated. Once confirmed, a single posterior C1–C2 reduction and fusion offers a safe and highly effective salvage option, achieving both immediate neural decompression and longterm stabilization, with the added benefit of avoiding anterior approaches or extension to the occiput.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and relatively small sample size from a single center. Future multicenter prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to validate our findings.

CONCLUSION

Posterior C1–C2 screw-rod revision surgery is a safe and effective salvage strategy for managing Chiari I malformation with atlantoaxial instability after failed FMD. This posterior-only approach provides solid reduction, immediate neural decompression, and lasting stabilization, leading to significant clinical improvement, successful fusion, and even syringomyelia resolution. The technique represents a viable, practice-changing rescue option for this challenging patient population.
