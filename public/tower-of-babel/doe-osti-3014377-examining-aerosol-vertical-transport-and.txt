1.0 Executive Summary

Atmospheric aerosols affect the global energy budget by scattering and absorbing sunlight (direct effects) and by
changing the microphysical structure, lifetime, and coverage of clouds (indirect effects). Globally, the free
troposphere is a major source of nucleation- and Aitken-mode aerosols due to the enhanced new particle
formation rates at high altitudes. Recent studies have shown deep convective systems are capable of transporting
these small aerosols from the free troposphere to the boundary layer by strong convective downdrafts and weaker
downward motions in the stratiform regions. These vertically transported aerosols can grow into cloud
condensation nuclei (CCN) and play a significant role in the global climate. During the deep convective
processes, existing accumulation-mode aerosols that act as coagulation sinks of smaller particles are also removed
by wet scavenging. Compared to the vertical transport of these particles by entrainment mixing, which is slower
but more prevalent, the deep convective downdraft processes may be more rapid and efficient in the vertical
transport of aerosols. However, most of the current climate models do not include this mechanism as a source of
CCN, mainly because the frequency of deep convective events varies significantly with geographic location and
thus their contributions to CCN are unpredictable.

We target this critical gap in understanding the vertical transport and removal of aerosols by deep convections.
We proposed to analyze a multi-year, multi-site measurement record available from the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) ARM program, including the observations from the 2014/15 Observations and Modeling of the
Green Ocean Amazon (GoAmazon) field campaign, the 2017/18 Aerosol and Cloud Experiments in the Eastern
North Atlantic (ACE-ENA) field campaign, the 2018/19 Cloud, Aerosol, and Complex Terrain Interactions
(CACTI) field campaign, the 2021/22 Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) field
campaign, and the long-term measurements collected at the Southern Great Plains (SGP) atmospheric
observatory, where deep convective clouds were frequently observed. This project is aimed at the following three
objectives: (1) Gaining a detailed and quantitative understanding of the aerosols transported by a convective
downdraft and their evolution in the atmosphere; (2) Examining the wet scavenging mechanisms and efficiencies
of aerosols at altitudes of deep convective systems based on ground and aircraft measurements; (3) Evaluating the
contribution of deep convective systems to CCN as both a source and a sink of atmospheric aerosols and its
seasonal variabilities.

To achieve the research objectives, we performed the following tasks: (1) Identify deep convective events using
ARM meteorological and cloud radar data to analyze the frequency and intensity of such events at study
locations; (2) Study the correlation between the deep convective downdraft intensity and the properties of aerosols
transported from the free troposphere; (3) Derive the size-dependent wet scavenging efficiency of aerosols using
data collected from the DOE field campaigns; (4) Estimate the overall and seasonal contributions of deep
convective events to boundary layer aerosols; and (5) Synthesize long-term measurements of aerosol physical and
chemical properties to better understand the climate impacts of aerosols associated with deep convective events.

2.0 Background

The interaction between aerosols, clouds, and precipitation is a major source of uncertainty that limits our ability
to understand pre-industrial climates and predict future climates.1 Aerosols contribute to cloud condensation
nuclei (CCN), influencing the formation of clouds and the occurrence of convective events. At the same time,
aerosols are removed through scavenging by cloud droplets and washout by precipitation during convective
events. Understanding the relationship between aerosols and convective events is essential to studying the
aerosol-cloud interaction. Among different convective systems, deep convection is a highly efficient mechanism
for the vertical transport of air from near Earth's surface (0 to 2 km) to the upper troposphere (6 to 12 km).2-4
Typical convective storms have spatial scales of tens of kilometers,5 making their local influence in the upper
troposphere extremely strong. Downdrafts balance the rapid upward flow within the convective storms, and the

 Page 1 of 21

much slower descending flow occurs over a larger spatial scale. These convective downdrafts can exchange
aerosols between the free troposphere and the boundary layer.

These features suggest that deep convection may act as an essential source and sink of atmospheric aerosols and
CCN. However, there are relatively few observational studies focusing on a budget of atmospheric aerosols
associated with deep convection systems. Limited studies were conducted during the NASA’s Deep Convective
Clouds and Chemistry Project (DC3) field campaign and Studies of Emissions, Atmospheric Composition, Clouds
and Climate Coupling by Regional Surveys Mission (SEAC4RS) field campaign. These studies, together with a
series of recent publications pointed out the significance of the relationship between boundary layer aerosols and
deep convections.6-7 In this project, we plan to investigate the vertical transport and removal of aerosols
during deep convective events.

 Deep convective events as a source of aerosols and CCN in the atmosphere
Model simulations show that nearly half of the global CCN in the atmospheric boundary layer may be formed
through new particle formation.8-10 New particle formation is a two-stage process consisting of the formation of
thermodynamically stable clusters from trace gas molecules followed by the growth of these clusters to a
detectable size of around 3 nm.11-12 Due to the high coagulation rate of clusters smaller than 3 nm within the
preexisting aerosol population, for new particle formation to occur, these clusters need to grow fast enough to
escape removal by coagulation. New particle formation typically requires a high actinic flux for photochemical
reactions to take place,13 low temperature to stabilize the newly formed particles,14 low surface area
concentration to reduce the loss of nucleating vapors and nascent particles,12, 15 and availability of precursors for
particle nucleation.16

The transport of aerosols in the free troposphere down to the boundary layer is commonly believed to be a result
of descending free troposphere air and entrainment at the interface between the boundary layer and free
troposphere20-24 (Fig. 1). In both mid-latitude and tropical regions, clouds play a role in scavenging larger
activated aerosol through precipitation and thereby reducing aerosol surface area. In doing so, they pump reactive
gases and water vapor aloft into a cooler environment with enhanced actinic flux.3, 25 These processes result in
enhanced production of sulfuric acid gas that can result in the rapid homogeneous nucleation of new particles that
continue to grow and evolve through heterogeneous processes. In the mid-latitudes, this process occurs at
altitudes between 2 to 3.5 km such that active subsidence could bring newly formed particles close to the
inversion where they could be entrained into the boundary layer before they are assimilated into the Aitken mode.
In the tropics, the deeper convection resulted in particle formation at generally higher altitudes above 4 km. At
both latitudes, long aging, transport, and evolution time are needed, typically on a timescale of days,20-21 before
the moderate subsidence rates for the region bring the aerosols near to the boundary layer to be entrained. This
results in a more stable and well-aged monomodal aerosol above the tropical boundary layer that can be entrained
and sustain the relatively stable and larger Aitken mode in these regions.

Recent aircraft- and ground-based observations from the Department of Energy (DOE) Green Ocean Amazon
(GoAmazon) field campaign, however, witnessed a new pathway for the vertical transport of aerosols. The
transport of free tropospheric aerosols over the rainforest of continental Amazonia often occurs through rapid,
sporadic downdrafts associated with precipitation, especially deep convections, instead of the slow and
continuous entrainment.7 This study shows that high concentrations of nucleation- and Aitken-mode aerosols with
diameters of less than 50 nm are transported from the free troposphere into the boundary layer during
precipitation events by strong convective downdrafts and weaker downward motions in the stratiform regions.

Compared to the continuous entrainment mixing of free tropospheric aerosols, the vertical transport of free
tropospheric aerosols by deep convective events is more sporadic and depends on the meteorological condition of
the studied location. Despite its random feature, this vertical transport mechanism may serve as an essential
process for sustaining the CCN in the boundary layer for the following reasons. (1) The downdrafts during deep
convection may transport tropospheric aerosols more rapidly.7 Wind profilers retrieved that the convective
downward motion during deep convective events has a speed up to ~10 m s−1 at mid-levels,7 which is orders of
magnitude higher than the entrainment rate of around 1 cm s-1.27 Such rapid transport limits the free tropospheric
aerosols being scavenged by existing aerosols and cloud droplets in the boundary layer. (2) Free tropospheric
aerosols entrained to the boundary layer will encounter cloud layers that actively scavenge these aerosols;
whereas aerosols vertically transported by precipitation-driven downdrafts are less likely to encounter cloud
layers. (3) The precipitation associated with deep convection can remove a large number of existing aerosols in
the boundary layer by wet scavenging, leading to a longer lifetime of aerosols vertically transported from the free
troposphere.

An estimate of particle number flux using radar wind profiler measurements and rainfall statistics over the
Amazon during the wet season shows that the influx of particles due to the downward transport during
precipitation events is at least 3.1×1011 m-2 day-1,7 when ignoring the downward transport in the trailing
stratiform. The influx of particles due to entrainment mixing can be calculated by multiplying the difference of
aerosol concentrations between the free troposphere and the boundary layer with the entrainment rate and is 2.6
×1011 m-2 day-1 at maximum because the loss by cloud scavenging was not considered. The similar magnitude of
particle number fluxes due to these two free troposphere-boundary layer exchange mechanisms prove that the
vertical transport of free tropospheric aerosols by deep convection needs to be scrutinized as a potential or
a dominant source of aerosols in the boundary layer.

  Deep convective events as a sink of aerosols and CCN in the atmosphere
Wet scavenging is by far the most efficient atmospheric aerosol sink, and the detailed mechanism of this process
involves microphysical interactions between aerosols and hydrometeors. Therefore, deep convection is also
recognized as a sink of aerosols. Wet scavenging of aerosols, which is also known as precipitation scavenging or
wet removal, is traditionally classified as rainout (also termed in-cloud scavenging) and washout (also termed
below-cloud scavenging).28 Below-cloud scavenging involves aerosol particles being collected by falling
precipitation particles through Brownian diffusion, directional interception, inertial impaction, thermophoresis,
diffusiophoresis, and electrophoresis mechanisms.29 It is important for the removal of coarse (Dp > 2 μm) and very
fine particles (Dp < 10 nm) from the boundary layer, but is relatively inefficient for particles in the Aitken and
accumulation mode size range.30 The primary mechanism for in-cloud scavenging is the activation of aerosol
particles (nucleation scavenging), followed by the collection/conversion of cloud droplets (and the cloud-borne

 Page 4 of 21

aerosol) by/to precipitation particles. In-cloud scavenging is generally the predominant wet removal mechanism
for accumulation mode aerosol.31-32

Several modeling approaches are available to calculate the below-cloud scavenging and in-cloud scavenging of
aerosols.30, 32-33 The below-cloud scavenging rate can be calculated based on the concept of efficiency of the
collision between an aerosol particle and a raindrop, which includes the mechanisms of Brownian diffusion,
interception, and impaction. The modeling results show that for a given aerosol size, the below-cloud scavenging
coefficient varies about two orders of magnitude for a variation of rainfall rate between 0.01 and 100 mm hr−1. For
a given rainfall rate, the below-cloud scavenging coefficient varies significantly with aerosol diameter. For low
and moderate rainfall rates (0.1 – 10 mm hr−1), the below-cloud scavenging is negligible for particles in the range
of 100 nm to 1 μm in comparison with in-cloud scavenging. But for very intense precipitation, for example,
during the deep convective events, the in-cloud scavenging for particles in the entire size range becomes
significant. The in-cloud scavenging is further divided to the scavenging of interstitial particles by droplets and
the in-cloud coalescence scavenging of cloud droplets, where a series of parameters are used to characterize the
scavenging rates.34-35 These methods were used by the PI to study the seasonal variations and key controlling
processes of marine boundary layer aerosols in the eastern North Atlantic.36

The mechanisms of aerosol wet scavenging during deep convection were studied in detail by the NASA DC3 field
campaign, which aimed to determine the impact of deep, midlatitude continental convective clouds on
tropospheric composition and chemistry. DC3 observations indicate that over 80% of the sulfate, nitrate, and
organic aerosol mass was removed during transport to the upper regions of the convection.37 Another study found
that more than half of the coarse-mode dust mass concentrations are transported to convective outflow regions,
suggesting deep convection may be an important mechanism for transporting coarse-mode dust to the upper
troposphere, where these dust particles serve as ice nuclei for cirrus cloud formation and are therefore important
for cloud radiation and climate effects.38

In these studies, the aerosol removal efficiencies were calculated using single or multiple tracer gases (such as
CO, acetone, benzene, and ethane) which are not affected by precipitation removal. The commonly used two-
component approach estimates the efficiency of trace gas scavenging by assuming that the mixing ratio of an
insoluble trace gas in the fresh convective outflow linearly correlates with the mixing ratios in the boundary layer
and upper troposphere. The wet scavenging efficiency can be calculated based on the mixing ratio of the tracer
gas.39 For example, if the boundary layer, free troposphere, and in-cloud CO mixing ratios are known, the
fractions of the boundary layer and free troposphere air in the cloud can be estimated by linear interpolation.
Using this mixing ratio and the aerosol number concentrations in the boundary layer and free troposphere, one can
calculate the theoretical aerosol number concentration without in-cloud scavenging. Thus, from this calculated
value and the in-cloud aerosol number concentrations measured onboard the aircraft, a scavenging loss of aerosol
particles can be estimated.40-41 A new passive-tracer-based transport analysis framework is developed to
characterize convective transport using vertical profiles of several passive trace gases.37 The study shows that
scavenging efficiencies for aerosol number concentrations are high for larger particles (84% for 150 nm – 2.5 μm
diameter) but are lower for smaller particles (64% for 30 – 150 nm). Compared to the observation-based analysis,
the standard model strongly underestimates aerosol scavenging efficiencies by 32% and 41% in absolute
differences for submicron mass and number. Adding a new treatment of secondary activation improves simulated
aerosol scavenging significantly, producing wet scavenging efficiencies that are only 7% and 8% lower than
observed efficiencies.37, 42 With this enhanced model, the wet scavenging efficiencies and particle number
fluxes due to deep convective clouds can be better estimated.

Recent DOE field campaigns offer a unique opportunity to study the vertical transport and removal of aerosols
during deep convective events in different climate regions. The GoAmazon 2014/15 field campaign and the
CACTI 2018/19 field campaign collected extensive ground- and aircraft-based aerosol, trace gas, cloud, and
meteorological data during deep convective events.4, 6-7, 43-45 Long-term observations at the DOE Atmospheric
Radiation Measurement (ARM) Southern Great Plains (SGP) atmospheric observatory offer high-quality data
regarding the evolution of aerosols and meteorological parameters of deep convective events.46 These three
datasets were collected from a wide range of geographical locations including the tropics, the southern
hemisphere mid-latitudes, and the northern hemisphere mid-latitudes. The deep convections over the Amazon
forest are dominated by isolated convections, whereas the those over the SGP site tend to be composed of well-
developed squall lines.54 The deep convections with different types and intensities, together with different aerosol
properties in the free troposphere over these locations lead to different boundary layer aerosol properties. These
data can be synergized to examine key mechanisms of aerosol transport and removal during deep convections.

Understanding the role of deep convective systems in the budget of aerosols can help us reduce uncertainties in
predicting the climate under different aerosol circumstances, especially during pre-industrial conditions, where
there is no impact from anthropogenic emissions. The accurate quantification of this baseline condition will also
help simulate future climate more accurately. Under polluted conditions, deep convective systems become a net
sink of particles in the boundary layer when the boundary layer particle concentration exceeds that in the free
troposphere. This study will form a mathematical understanding of the impact of deep convective systems on the
budget of aerosols in the boundary layer, which can then be further integrated into climate models.

3.0 Project Objectives

Recent studies show that deep convection may serve as an essential source of aerosol particles in the boundary
layer through the convective downdraft of the free tropospheric air. In the meantime, deep convection is an
efficient sink of atmospheric aerosols through wet scavenging. Such combined mechanisms add uncertainties to
our understanding of aerosol-cloud interactions and need to be studied in detail. Given the evidence that deep
convection systems can impact the aerosols in the boundary layer, we need to address the key question: How
does deep convection influence the budget of atmospheric aerosols and CCN in the boundary layer?

In this work, we target the critical gap in understanding the vertical transport and removal of aerosols by
deep convections. We propose to achive this by analyzing ambient data collected from the GoAmazon 2014/15
campaign, the CACTI 2018/19 campaign, and the long term measurement activities being conducted at the SGP
site, where deep conventions were often observed. This project is aimed at the following three objectives (O),
which can be further expanded to several scientific questions (Q):

O1: Gaining a detailed and quantitative understanding of the aerosols transported by a convective
 downdraft and their evolution in the atmosphere. We will answer the following questions:
 • Q1: What are the frequencies and downdraft intensities of deep convective events at the three studied
 locations, and what are their major differences in the features of the deep convection?
 • Q2: How does downdraft intensity correlate with the size and concentration of the aerosols transported
 vertically by the convective downdrafts?
 • Q3: How to differentiate between aerosols transported by deep convective events and those formed by
 new particle formation in the boundary layer?

O2: Examining the wet scavenging mechanisms and efficiencies of aerosols at altitudes of deep convective
 systems based on aircraft measurements. Questions to be addressed include:
 • Q1: What is the wet scavenging efficiency of aerosols as a function of size during deep convective
 events?
 • Q2: How do the results obtained from the two-layer model differ from the multiple passive tracer
 analysis approach in calculating the aerosol wet scavenging efficiency?

O3: Evaluating the contribution of deep convective systems to CCN as both a source and a sink of
 atmospheric aerosols and its seasonal variabilities. We will answer the following questions:
 • Q1: What are the fluxes of aerosols in different size modes through vertical transport and wet
 scavenging associated with deep convective events?
 • Q2: How do fluxes through aerosol vertical transport and wet scavenging vary seasonally and
 geographically during deep convective events?
 • Q3: How likely will the vertically transported aerosols grow into CCN-related sizes?
 • Q4: What is the significance of the deep convection as a source and sink of boundary layer aerosols and
 CCN?

The proposal is responsive to the FOA as it addresses the influence of clouds or precipitation on aerosol
microphysical and chemical properties through vertical transport, and/or wet removal, a specific subtopic
of the FOA. The proposed activities squarely fit the mission and vision of the DOE Office of Biological and
Environmental Research, Atmospheric System Research program. Current knowledge is not sufficient to provide
accurate quantification of the source and sink of boundary layer aerosols during deep convection events. Existing
ground and airborne measurements characterizing aerosols during deep convections have not focused on the flux
of aerosols associated with the events. A comprehensive picture describing the vertical transport and removal of
aerosols during deep convective events is lacking. This information is critical for understanding and improving

the representation of the boundary aerosol budget. Data from this project can be used to improve representations
of deep convective systems in climate and earth system models, therefore reducing the uncertainties in climate
and earth system models toward the development of sustainable solutions for the Nation’s energy and
environmental challenges.

 intensity of such events at study locations

 transported from the free troposphere.

Task V. Synthesize long-term measurements of aerosol properties, both physical and chemical, to better
 understand the climate impacts of aerosols associated with deep convective events.

from North America residing above the marine boundary layer. In contrast, at SGP and HOU, CO generally

Although not statistically significant, the negative correlations between ΔθE and ΔN< 40 nm across all sites indicate
enhanced ultrafine concentrations during stronger convection, consistent with transport of particles formed aloft,
where NPF is favored. At ENA, total particle number often remained stable after DC, implying that downward
transport of ultrafine particles can offset wet deposition or dilution losses of larger particles. Such a replenishing
effect, also reported over the Amazon, highlights the role of DC in sustaining aerosol budgets in clean
environments. At SGP, elevated N< 40 nm were frequently observed before DCs impacted the site, primarily due to
accumulation mode particles extending below the mobility diameter of 40 nm. However, many of these events
exhibited the appearance of new modes of particles with sizes below 40 nm, sometimes within minutes, and other
times within an hour after DC overpass. The combination of high pre-convective background levels and
variability in post-convective particle size distributions likely contributed to large event-to-event scatter in
ΔN<40 nm, thereby weakening the correlation with ΔθE (Fig. 10d). At HOU, ultrafine particle enhancements also
extended into larger size bins (40–100 nm). This broader response is possibly influenced by the complex emission
landscape of Houston, where substantial local sources contribute to elevated background ultrafine particle
concentrations, making it difficult to distinguish particles originating from local emissions and those transported
from the free troposphere. Collectively, these findings indicate that while DC consistently promotes vertical
mixing, the detectability and magnitude of its aerosol and trace-gas impacts depend on the interplay between
convective strength, background composition, and local emissions.

We further used long-term RWP measurements at SGP to quantify the impact of DC events on vertical air mass
transport. ARM-derived vertical air velocity (w) data for 2019 were used to estimate aerosol descent timescales
during DC. For non-convective conditions, entrainment velocity was derived from Vr measurements from the
Doppler Lidar. The mean w near 1.3 km AGL (for 30-minute core convective period) was observed to be 1.3 ±
0.41 m s⁻¹. The entrainment velocity for non-convective conditions was 0.06 ± 0.24 m s⁻¹, consistent with
previous estimates on the order of cm s⁻¹. Assuming a boundary layer depth of ~1 km, these values correspond to
vertical transport timescales of ~10 minutes during DC, compared to >5 hours under non-convective conditions
via entrainment. Although less frequent, DC can transport as much free troposphere air to the boundary layer
within minutes as steady entrainment would in several hours, making DC an episodic yet highly efficient pathway
for rapid vertical transport.

With these values, we estimated cumulative mass flux for 2019–2023 at the SGP, where multi-year Vr data and w
are available. Assuming the mean w of 1.3 m s⁻¹ at 1.3 km AGL, a 30-minute core convective period per event,
and 66 identified cases, the cumulative mass of air transported from the free troposphere to the boundary layer
was ~1.9 × 105 kg m-2. In comparison, with continuous entrainment (non-convective mixing) over 5 years at an
entrainment velocity of 0.06 m s⁻¹ will transport ~1.1 × 107 kg m-2 of free troposphere air to the boundary layer.
Thus, DC events account for ~2% of the total mass transported per unit area. However, this estimate is
conservative, as many convective events persist for durations >4 hours (i.e., including trailing and periphery
stratiform regions as in Fig. 4, and reported previously by Wang et al., 2016), which could potentially increase the
contribution of DC to ~13%. Restricting to the SGP warm months (April–September), the contribution of DC
increases to ~4% (for 30-min core) to ~29% (for 4-h duration including trailing and stratiform phases),
underscoring the disproportionately large role of DC during these times. While entrainment dominates in
persistence, a single DC event can transport an air mass equivalent to that of entrainment over several days of
background mixing, highlighting the critical role of DC in redistributing trace gases and aerosols at SGP.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations

In conclusion, our analysis so far has provided insights into the complexities of aerosol dynamics and their impact
on atmospheric processes. One of the significant findings is the observation of vertical transport of aerosols
during deep convective events. The number of aerosols, as well as trace gases, transported through convective
downdraft are associated with the intensity of the deep convections. These vertically transported aerosols can
influence the CCN and cloud properties. The comparison of aerosol fluxes between the boundary layer and the
free troposphere through deep convection and entrainment has revealed the critical role of deep convective events
in aerosol transport. This understanding is crucial for developing more accurate atmospheric models and
improving our predictions of cloud and climate properties.

Overall, this project underscores the importance of detailed aerosol study in understanding atmospheric processes
during deep convective events. The insights gained from the DOE ARM field campaigns are not only important
for the scientific community but also have practical implications in modeling the Earth’s climate. Future case
studies regarding the evolution and transport of these aerosols, building upon our current findings, will continue
to enrich our understanding of deep convection and aerosol processes.

6.0 Lessons Learned

In the project, we met the following challenges, and we hope to share these lessons with the research community:
 1. Identification of the source of aerosols during deep convection events: Although many of the deep
 convection events bring aerosols with relatively smaller sizes, and deep convection generates convective
 downward motion that could transport these aerosols, they may not be necessarily associated with the
 convective downward transport. For example, at the SGP, deep convection events are often generated by

                                                    Page 15 of 21

 frontal passage, and the smaller particles may be transported horizontally behind the front. Under this
 situation, we will require more analysis to identify the source of these smaller particles. One option is to
 use the back trajectory analysis to find the associated air mass containing these smaller particles. One can
 also refer to the trace gas composition, such as carbon monoxide and ozone, to infer the potential source
 of the air mass.
 2. More research flights comparing the aerosol properties prior to and after deep convection are needed: To
 generate a thorough understanding regarding the impact of deep convection on aerosol and CCN
 properties, we will require more direct measurement of aerosols near cloud level. During the HI-SCALE
 campaign, there are a limited number of flights associated with deep convections, mainly because deep
 convection is not a major goal of the campaign. During the CACTI campaign, although there are more
 research flights associated with deep convections, the instrument for measuring aerosol properties,
 especially for smaller aerosols, has a limited time resolution and is often impacted by cloud droplet
 shattering.

Overall, this project generated new and exciting knowledge on aerosol and cloud interactions associated with deep
convective events. It has been a pleasure for our group to collaborate with experts at national laboratories and
universities and interact with them at the Joint ARM User Facility and ASR PI Meetings. We look forward to
working on future ARM/ASR projects.
