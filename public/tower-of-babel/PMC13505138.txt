MPGR-Net: multi-scale patch graph reasoning for weakly supervised pathological image segmentation
Authors: Xu Zhang, Huaiju Ge, Yanling Wang, Chongqi Wei, Yang Liu, Xinbin Zhang, Cheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Weakly supervised pathological image segmentation aims to localize disease regions using coarse annotations (e.g., image-level labels), which significantly reduces annotation cost but introduces challenges such as imprecise localization and noisy predictions. In this paper, we propose multi-scale patch graph reasoning network (MPGR-Net), a novel framework that integrates multi-scale graph reasoning into transformer-based architectures to address these limitations. Specifically, we first employ a Vision Transformer encoder to extract global-aware patch representations. To explicitly model structured spatial dependencies that are often overlooked by standard self-attention, we introduce a multi-scale patch graph reasoning module, where patch tokens are treated as graph nodes and connected via predefined spatial adjacency at multiple scales. This design enables effective aggregation of local and long-range contextual information while maintaining computational efficiency. Furthermore, we incorporate a lightweight cross-attention mechanism to enhance global-to-local feature interaction through channel-wise modulation. Extensive experiments on pathological image segmentation benchmarks demonstrate that MPGR-Net consistently outperforms existing weakly supervised methods, achieving superior segmentation accuracy and robustness. Ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of the proposed graph reasoning module.

Introduction

Pathological image segmentation plays a crucial role in computer-aided diagnosis and clinical decision-making, as it enables precise localization of disease regions in high-resolution histopathology images [1, 2]. However, obtaining pixel-level annotations for pathological images is extremely labor-intensive, time-consuming, and requires expert knowledge. To alleviate this burden, weakly supervised segmentation, which relies on coarse annotations such as image-level labels, has attracted increasing attention in recent years.

Despite its practical advantages, weakly supervised pathological image segmentation remains a challenging problem [3, 4]. The absence of fine-grained supervision often leads to incomplete object localization, noisy activation maps, and poor boundary delineation. Existing approaches typically rely on class activation maps (CAMs) or transformer-based attention mechanisms to infer spatial regions of interest [5–7]. However, these methods suffer from two major limitations. First, although Vision Transformers (ViTs) are effective in modeling long-range dependencies, their self-attention mechanism captures spatial relationships in an implicit manner, lacking explicit structural constraints. Second, most methods treat patch tokens independently or rely solely on attention weights, which may fail to enforce spatial consistency across neighboring regions.

To address these limitations, we propose MPGR-Net, a novel framework that integrates multi-scale patch graph reasoning into transformer-based segmentation for weakly supervised pathological images. The key idea is to explicitly model spatial dependencies among patch tokens using structured graph representations, thereby enhancing both local coherence and global contextual understanding.

Specifically, we first employ a Vision Transformer encoder to extract patch-level representations with global receptive fields. To overcome the limitations of implicit spatial modeling in self-attention, we construct a set of graphs over patch tokens, where each node corresponds to a patch and edges are defined based on spatial proximity at multiple scales. We then introduce a multi-scale graph reasoning module, which aggregates neighborhood information across different connectivity levels through graph neural network. This design enables the model to capture both fine-grained local structures and long-range spatial interactions in a unified framework. In addition, we incorporate a lightweight cross-attention-based global-to-local interaction module, where the global classification token is used to modulate patch features via channel-wise gating. This mechanism enhances the alignment between global semantics and local representations, leading to more accurate localization of pathological regions.

Extensive experiments on pathological image segmentation benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed MPGR-Net consistently outperforms existing weakly supervised methods. In particular, our approach achieves superior performance in terms of segmentation accuracy, robustness, and localization quality.

Related work

Weakly supervised pathological image segmentation

Weakly supervised pathological image segmentation has attracted increasing attention due to the high cost of dense pixel-level annotations in histopathology images. Early studies primarily rely on image-level labels to train segmentation models under weak supervision. A representative paradigm is Class Activation Mapping (CAM), which localizes discriminative regions by leveraging classification networks. However, CAM-based methods often suffer from incomplete activation, tending to highlight only the most discriminative parts of lesions while ignoring their full spatial extent [6, 8].

To address this limitation, numerous methods have been proposed to refine CAMs using additional constraints or learning strategies. Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) is widely adopted in weakly supervised medical image segmentation, where each image is treated as a bag of instances, and predictions are aggregated at the bag level [9, 10]. Although MIL improves robustness, it fails to explicitly model spatial dependencies among instances, leading to suboptimal segmentation boundaries.

To further improve localization quality, pseudo-label refinement methods have been introduced. These approaches iteratively refine coarse predictions using self-training or region growing strategies [2, 11]. In parallel, consistency learning and self-training frameworks have been explored to enhance robustness under weak supervision [12].

Recently, transformer-based architectures have been introduced into weakly supervised segmentation due to their strong global modeling capability. Methods such as TransCAM leverage transformer attention to refine CAM outputs, improving spatial coverage [5]. Hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures further enhance feature representation by combining local convolutional features with global attention mechanisms [13, 14]. Despite these advances, most existing methods still rely on implicit attention mechanisms and lack explicit spatial structural modeling, which limits their performance in complex pathological structures.

In contrast, our method introduces explicit multi-scale graph reasoning over patch tokens, enabling structured spatial modeling beyond attention-based approaches.

Graph-based representation learning

Graph-based representation learning provides a powerful framework for modeling non-Euclidean structured data. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) extend deep learning to graph-structured inputs by performing message passing between connected nodes [15, 16]. In vision tasks, images can be represented as graphs where nodes correspond to pixels, superpixels, or patch embeddings, and edges encode spatial or semantic relationships.

Early works apply graph learning to image segmentation by constructing superpixel graphs and performing graph neural network to refine region consistency [17, 18]. These methods demonstrate the effectiveness of explicit structure modeling but are limited by fixed graph construction and shallow architectures. With the rise of deep learning, graph-based methods have been integrated with CNN features to improve visual reasoning. For instance, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have been used for scene understanding and semantic segmentation by modeling contextual dependencies among regions [17, 19]. However, these approaches often rely on static or manually designed graph structures, limiting their adaptability. Recent studies have further explored hierarchical graph reasoning and local-to-global topology modeling for robust representation learning. For example, HIGDA [20] constructs hierarchical graph structures to capture local-to-global topological relationships for semi-supervised domain adaptation, while prototype-driven graph convolution methods enhance feature alignment through graph-based prototype propagation [21]. These works demonstrate the effectiveness of structured graph reasoning in modeling complex semantic dependencies, which further motivates our multi-scale patch graph reasoning framework.

In medical imaging, graph-based methods have been explored to model anatomical relationships and tissue interactions [22, 23]. Despite their effectiveness, most existing approaches operate on a single-scale graph and fail to capture hierarchical spatial dependencies. More recently, efforts have been made to combine transformers with graph neural networks. Some studies incorporate attention mechanisms into MIL frameworks to model instance relationships [24]. Others attempt to unify Transformer or CNN backbones with graph reasoning modules for improved representation learning [16]. However, these methods still lack explicit multi-scale graph construction and efficient integration with patch-based transformer tokens.

To address these limitations, we propose a multi-scale patch graph reasoning framework that explicitly constructs graphs at different spatial scales, enabling comprehensive modeling of both local and global dependencies in pathological images.

Global-to-local interaction

Although Vision Transformers are capable of modeling long-range dependencies via self-attention, the interaction between the global semantic token and local patch tokens remains implicit. To explicitly inject global contextual information into local representations, we introduce a Global-to-Local Interaction (GTLI) module.

The classification token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{\mathrm{cls}}$$\end{document} encodes high-level semantic information of the entire image, while patch tokens \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_p$$\end{document} primarily capture localized patterns. However, standard transformer layers do not explicitly enforce global-to-local modulation. This may limit the model’s ability to refine ambiguous or weakly activated regions.

To address this issue, we design a lightweight cross-attention mechanism that enables the global token to selectively enhance patch features through channel-wise gating.

Multi-scale patch graph reasoning

While transformer-based self-attention captures global dependencies, it lacks explicit modeling of structured spatial relationships. In dense prediction tasks such as medical image segmentation, spatial continuity and neighborhood consistency are crucial. To address this limitation, we propose a Multi-scale Patch Graph Reasoning (MPGR) module that explicitly models interactions among patch tokens via graph neural network over multiple spatial scales.

Decoder

After multi-scale graph reasoning, the refined patch tokens encode rich contextual and structural information. To generate dense segmentation maps, we adopt a mask transformer decoder, which models the interactions between patch tokens and class embeddings to produce segmentation masks in a learnable and global manner.

In addition, we introduce a set of learnable class embeddings:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times C},$$\end{document}

where K is the number of semantic classes.

Token-Class Interaction. The mask transformer models the interaction between patch tokens and class embeddings via attention:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Z} = \mathrm{Attention}(\textbf{E}, \textbf{X}^{*}),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times C}$$\end{document} encodes class-specific responses aggregated from patch tokens.

Alternatively, the interaction can be interpreted as computing the similarity between patch tokens and class embeddings:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p = \textbf{E} \cdot (\textbf{X}^{*})^{\top},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}$$\end{document} represents patch-level mask logits.

Patch-level Mask Prediction. The predicted masks are reshaped into spatial maps: 26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H_p \times W_p}.$$\end{document}

This formulation enables each class embedding to attend to relevant patch tokens, producing class-specific segmentation masks.

Upsampling to Pixel Space. The patch-level masks are upsampled to the original image resolution via bilinear interpolation:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M} = \mathrm{Interp}(\textbf{M}_p, H, W),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H \times W}$$\end{document} is the final segmentation output.

Training objective

To obtain image-level predictions under weak supervision, we apply the Normalized Global Weighted Pooling (NGWP) operator on the patch-level mask logits produced by the decoder. Given the coarse prediction map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H_p \times W_p}$$\end{document}, we first reshape it into:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}, \quad N = H_p \times W_p.$$\end{document}

The corresponding probability map is computed via softmax along the class dimension:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P} = \textrm{Softmax}(\textbf{M}_p),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}$$\end{document}.

The final image-level prediction is defined as:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y} = \textbf{y}_{\textrm{ngwp}} + \beta \, \textbf{y}_{\textrm{focal}},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\textrm{ngwp}}$$\end{document} denotes the NGWP aggregation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\textrm{focal}}$$\end{document} represents the focal regularization term.

Normalized Global Weighted Pooling. The NGWP operator performs confidence-aware aggregation over spatial locations:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\textrm{ngwp}} =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \textbf{M}_p^{(i)} \odot \textbf{P}^{(i)}}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \textbf{P}^{(i)}},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_p^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P}^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$$\end{document} denote the logits and probabilities at spatial position i, and ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication.

Focal Regularization. To encourage sparse and discriminative activations, a focal-style regularization term is introduced:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\textrm{focal}} =(1 - \bar{\textbf{P}})^3 \odot \log(\lambda + \bar{\textbf{P}}),$$\end{document}

where 33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\textbf{P}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \textbf{P}^{(i)}$$\end{document}

is the spatial mean probability per class, and λ is a small constant for numerical stability.

Loss Function. We apply a binary cross-entropy loss on the sigmoid-activated outputs: 34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{BCE}(\sigma(\textbf{y}), \textbf{y}_{\mathrm{gt}}),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\mathrm{gt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$$\end{document} denotes image-level labels.

Experiments

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed MPGR-Net on two publicly available weakly supervised histopathology segmentation benchmarks. We first introduce the datasets and implementation details, followed by comparisons with state-of-the-art methods and quantitative evaluation metrics.

Datasets

We evaluate our method on two representative histopathology datasets covering lung and breast cancer tissue segmentation under weak supervision.

LUAD-HistoSeg. This dataset consists of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) histopathology patches with image-level annotations [2]. It includes four tissue categories: Tumor Epithelial (TE), Tumor-Associated Stroma (TAS), Lymphocyte (LYM), and Necrosis (NEC). In total, 17,285 image patches of size 224 × 224 are provided, among which 16,678 are used for training with only image-level labels, while 300 validation and 307 test samples are annotated with pixel-level labels for evaluation.

BCSS-WSSS. The BCSS-WSSS dataset is derived from H&E-stained breast cancer whole-slide images [2], containing four classes: Tumor (TUM), Stroma (STR), Lymphocytic infiltrate (LYM), and Necrosis (NEC). The dataset includes 31,826 patches of size 224 × 224, split into 23,422 training images, 3,418 validation images, and 4,986 test images, where pixel-level annotations are available for validation and testing.

Implementation details

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090Ti GPU. The input images are resized to 224 × 224 and divided into patches of size P=16. We adopt a Vision Transformer backbone (ViT-S/16) with an embedding dimension of D=384 and 12 transformer layers. The proposed multi-scale graph reasoning module is stacked with L=8 layers and uses multiple spatial connectivities (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{4, 8, 12, 16\}$$\end{document}). A mask transformer decoder is employed to generate patch-level segmentation masks. The model is optimized using the SGD optimizer with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-2}$$\end{document}. The batch size is set to 32, and the model is trained for 10 epochs.

Evaluation metrics

We adopt the widely used Intersection over Union (IoU) and mean IoU (mIoU) to evaluate segmentation performance.

For each class c, the IoU is defined as:35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{IoU}_c = \frac{|\hat{M}_c \cap M_c|}{|\hat{M}_c \cup M_c|},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{M}_c$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_c$$\end{document} denote the predicted and ground-truth pixel sets, respectively.

The mean IoU is computed as:36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{mIoU} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \textrm{IoU}_c,$$\end{document}

where C is the number of classes.

By integrating hierarchical patch interactions, MPGR-Net alleviates the limitations of conventional CAM-based methods, such as fragmented activation and inaccurate boundaries. Discussion

We further analyze the behavior of MPGR-Net by jointly considering the quantitative results in Table 3 and the qualitative visualizations in Figs. 2 and 3, with a focus on the intrinsic differences between LUAD-HistoSeg and BCSS-WSSS.

LUAD-HistoSeg exhibits relatively clear tissue organization with well-defined structural patterns, such as glandular or duct-like boundaries. As shown in Table 3, the best performance is achieved with a moderate connectivity configuration (e.g., (4, 8, 12, 16)), suggesting that capturing local and mid-range spatial dependencies is sufficient for modeling such structured patterns. This observation is consistent with the visualization in Fig. 2, where MPGR produces smooth and coherent activation maps with well-preserved boundaries. The model effectively enhances region consistency while avoiding excessive diffusion across distinct tissue regions.

In contrast, BCSS-WSSS presents more diffuse and heterogeneous structures, where discriminative cues are often localized at the cellular level, such as nuclei distribution and subtle texture variations. As reflected in Table 3, configurations with larger connectivity ranges (e.g., including 20 and 24 neighbors) yield better performance. This indicates that broader receptive fields are beneficial for aggregating dispersed contextual information. The visualization in Fig. 3 further supports this observation: MPGR generates more globally consistent activation patterns and reduces noisy responses compared to the baseline methods, enabling better localization in structurally complex regions.

These results highlight that the effectiveness of multi-scale graph reasoning is closely related to the structural characteristics of the dataset. The preferred graph receptive field size is closely related to the morphological characteristics of the dataset. LUAD-HistoSeg exhibits relatively regular and spatially continuous tissue structures, where moderate connectivity is sufficient for capturing local contextual dependencies while preserving boundaries. In contrast, BCSS-WSSS contains more heterogeneous and diffuse tissue distributions with higher intra-class variability, making broader receptive fields more beneficial for aggregating long-range contextual information.

Overall, MPGR-Net demonstrates strong adaptability across different pathological scenarios by flexibly integrating multi-scale spatial relationships, allowing it to balance local detail preservation and global context modeling.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed MPGR-Net, a novel multi-scale patch graph reasoning framework for weakly supervised histopathological image segmentation. Unlike conventional methods that rely heavily on class activation maps, our approach explicitly models spatial dependencies among patch tokens through a multi-scale graph structure, enabling more effective contextual aggregation and structural reasoning.Specifically, we introduced a multi-scale graph construction strategy that captures spatial relationships at different receptive fields, along with a deep graph reasoning module to progressively refine token representations.

Extensive experiments on LUAD-HistoSeg and BCSS-WSSS demonstrate that MPGR-Net consistently outperforms existing weakly supervised segmentation methods. Both quantitative results and qualitative visualizations verify that the proposed method produces more accurate, coherent, and structurally consistent segmentation maps. In addition, comprehensive ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of each component, including connectivity design, graph depth, and attention configuration.

Moreover, our analysis reveals that the proposed multi-scale graph reasoning mechanism exhibits strong adaptability across datasets with different structural characteristics, effectively balancing local detail preservation and global context modeling.

In future work, we plan to explore more adaptive graph construction strategies, integrate domain-specific priors such as histological knowledge, and extend the framework to large-scale whole-slide image analysis and other weakly supervised medical imaging tasks.
