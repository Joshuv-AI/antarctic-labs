Knowledge, self-reported confidence and past experiences of polish parents in rescue manoeuvres for first aid management of paediatric choking: a cross-sectional pilot study in Poland
Authors: Jakub R. Bieliński, Dariusz Timler, Klaudiusz Nadolny, Filip Jaskiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Choking is a life-threatening paediatric emergency. Despite its importance, public knowledge of first aid remains limited, and data specifically concerning parents are scarce. This study aimed to assess Polish parents’ knowledge of paediatric choking first aid and identify factors associated with performance. A cross-sectional online survey was conducted between December 2024 and February 2025. The questionnaire included sociodemographic characteristics and scenario-based open-ended questions assessing the management of foreign body airway obstruction. A total of 156 parents participated. The median score for rescue manoeuvres was 4/12, while the median score for cardiac arrest management was 2/3, indicating generally limited knowledge. In univariate analyses, higher self-reported confidence was associated with better performance, whereas prior training showed a borderline association. In multivariate analysis, previous practical training was the only significant predictor of performance. This exploratory study revealed a discrepancy between parents’ self-reported confidence and their actual knowledge of paediatric choking management. Previous practical training may be associated with improved performance; however, the findings should be interpreted cautiously due to limited statistical power. The use of open-ended scenarios enabled a more realistic assessment of functional knowledge. Further large-scale studies are needed to support the development of targeted, skills-based educational interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54899-x.

Introduction

Paediatric basic life support (PBLS) encompasses first aid skills required to manage children experiencing critical, life-threatening emergencies, such as sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) or foreign body airway obstruction (FBAO). The basic idea is grounded in the principle that the taught rescue manoeuvres constitute essential skills that should be common knowledge possessed by every member of society1. However, scientific reports indicate that first aid competence remains suboptimal in the general population, with particularly substantial knowledge gaps persisting in the management of paediatric FBAO. Only approximately 40% of adults demonstrate adequate knowledge of fundamental rescue manoeuvres and a sufficient understanding of choking management algorithms2–4. Nevertheless, specific populations of adults who spend the most time with children and are present during high-risk asphyxia activities, such as eating and playing, have been overlooked in research.

FBAO represents one of the leading causes of accidental paediatric mortality worldwide5. In developed countries, around 300 to 600 fatal accidents are reported annually; however, the burden is significantly greater in developing countries, where mortality rates are estimated to be twice as high6,7. Despite growing awareness of the issue, there has been a steady rise in the incidence of paediatric choking over an extended period8. Furthermore, recent research indicates that nearly half of paediatric choking fatalities occur prior to any medical rescue interventions, indicating either a failure to recognise and manage choking appropriately or considerable delays in initiating appropriate rescue manoeuvres9,10. Complete airway obstruction will result in respiratory failure and cardiac arrest11. However, even in cases of partial airway obstruction, life-threatening complications such as airway oedema and inflammation can arise from the delayed extraction of foreign objects12. First aid provides an opportunity to prevent clinical deterioration potentially leading to critical or fatal outcomes.

Aims

The limited knowledge seen across the general population, combined with scant parental data, and the critical importance of FBAO, underscores the acute need for further investigations. Therefore, this study primarily aimed to assess knowledge on first aid for paediatric FBAO among Polish parents.

The secondary aim was to identify factors potentially influencing participants’ responses, especially prior training, number of children, past real-life choking experience, and self-reported confidence.

Methods

Study settings

The study was designed as a cross-sectional, descriptive, and exploratory survey-based study. Ethical approval was granted by the Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Lodz (resolution number RNN/238/24/KE dated December 10th, 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study and assured of confidentiality. It was also explained that completion and submission of the questionnaire constituted consent to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Data collection was performed from 15 December 2024 to 18 February 2025. The reporting of this study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines (Supplementary File S1).

Data source

Data were acquired via an online survey questionnaire (Supplementary File S2) created using Microsoft Forms software (version 16.89.1 (24091630), Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States of America) and distributed through various Facebook groups discussing parenting topics (Meta Platforms, Incorporated., Menlo Park, California, United States of America). The questionnaire consisted of two sections:

Section I included sociodemographic items and self-reported indicators such as prior first aid training and experience, as well as self-reported confidence in performing choking first aid.Section II contained 5 open-ended items based on paediatric FBAO scenarios, designed to assess the accuracy of intended actions for conscious and unconscious infants and children.

Cognitive interviews with 18 target-population parents were used to validate content and face validity. Participants were asked to complete a pilot questionnaire and provide verbal feedback on the clarity, relevance, and interpretation of each item. Based on this feedback, minor revisions were made to improve the tool. Cognitive interviewing is a widely accepted and well-documented approach in health and social sciences for optimizing question clarity and identifying sources of bias13–16.

Participants

Participants were recruited via parenting Facebook groups. Eligibility was open to self-identified parents or caregivers; participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated. Willingness to participate in the study and a completed questionnaire were inclusion criteria.

Sample size estimation followed two principles. For estimating the population mean FBAO knowledge score (mean = 3.67, SD = 2.48), 95 participants were required to achieve 95% confidence with a ± 0.5 margin of error17,18. For exploratory regression analysis with up to 9 predictors, a minimum of 120–135 observations was considered appropriate based on established methodological recommendations19,20. The final sample size (n = 156) met both criteria.

Statistical analysis

Responses to open-ended questions were scored using a four-level, a priori-developed rubric based on the 2025 European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines1. Each response was classified as:

Four scenario questions (two infant, two child) were individually scored, summing to a maximum of 12 points (“Sum of Manoeuvres’ Score”). The fifth question addressing the management of unconscious choking was scored separately (0–3, “Paediatric Cardiac Arrest Score”). This item was based on a general algorithm for paediatric cases, without distinguishing between specific intervention techniques (Supplementary File S3).

Initial coding was performed by the first author using a predefined rubric, which was developed based on current paediatric choking management guidelines. Although formal inter-rater reliability metrics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) were not calculated, scoring was based on a structured rubric derived from established clinical guidelines. A subset comprising 32 (20.5%) of responses was independently reviewed by a second author, with an agreement rate of approximately 93% prior to consensus discussion. Given the low level of interpretative ambiguity in the scoring criteria, this approach was considered sufficient for the exploratory nature of the study. This framework-based consensus approach is consistent with accepted practices in qualitative and mixed-methods research involving structured open-ended items in healthcare settings21,22.

PQStat v1.8.4 was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Due to non-normal outcome distributions, both parametric and non-parametric methods were applied.

Univariate and multivariate linear regressions were used to explore associations between predictor variables and performance scores. Additional robustness checks using non-parametric methods confirmed the key trends.

Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences in knowledge and performance scores (rescue manoeuvres and cardiac arrest management) between independent groups defined by binary predictors (e.g., gender, prior training, and experience).

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was employed to assess associations between self-rated confidence (ordinal scale: 1–5) and outcome scores.

All tests were two-tailed. Effect sizes for group comparisons were reported as rank biserial correlation (r).

It is important to emphasize that the primary objective of this study was not to establish definitive causal relationships, but to explore patterns and generate hypotheses for future research. In this context, the chosen methodology, including open-ended responses and exploratory statistical analysis, was considered appropriate to capture the complexity of real-world decision-making in first-aid scenarios, even at the expense of certain psychometric standards typically required in confirmatory studies.

Results

A total of 156 questionnaires were received and included in the analysis. Mothers accounted for the majority of participants (80.6%). The mean age of the study group was 40 years (Me = 38; IQR34–43. Thirty respondents (19%) had secondary education, while 126 (81%) reported higher education, including 19 (12%) parents with medical backgrounds. A total of 46 (29%) respondents lived in rural areas or in small towns (< 100,000 inhabitants), whereas 110 (71%) lived in medium-sized towns or large cities (> 100,000 inhabitants). Among the surveyed individuals, 70 (45%), 71 (45%), and 15 (10%) parents reported having 1, 2, and 3 children, respectively. In the univariate linear regression model for the Sum of Manoeuvres Score, none of the sociodemographic or experiential variables showed a statistically significant effect, with the exception of self-reported confidence (b = 0.4482; 95% CI: [0.0201–0.8763]; p = 0.0403), which was positively associated with performance (Supplementary File S4). A borderline non-significant result was also observed for the variable “time since practical training > 3 years” (p = 0.0561).

However, none of the predictors retained statistical significance in the multivariate linear regression model (Supplementary File S5). Notably, the effect of self-reported confidence was no longer significant following adjustment for the remaining variables (p = 0.2540).

In the univariate linear regression models for the Paediatric Cardiac Arrest Score, none of the factors reached statistical significance (Supplementary File S6). A small, non-significant effect was observed for prior practical training (p = 0.0649).

In the multivariate linear regression model adjusted for covariates, prior practical training emerged as the sole statistically significant predictor of performance (p = 0.0068), while no significant effects were observed for the remaining factors (Supplementary File S7).

Post hoc statistical power analysis showed that the achieved power for most individual variables was low (Supplementary File S8). This may suggest a risk of type II error (i.e. failure to detect an existing effect). For self-reported confidence, the observed power was 0.370, suggesting a possible relationship that requires further investigation in a larger sample.

Additionally, post hoc power analysis for both multivariate regression models (rescue manoeuvres and cardiac arrest scores) demonstrated an achieved power of only 0.052 for the detection of a medium effect size (f² = 0.10) with nine predictors and 156 cases. This confirmed that the study was underpowered to detect small-to-moderate effects in these models and supported the exploratory nature of the analysis.

Additional aspects, previously identified in our research as relevant, were also raised in the questionnaire23,24. The use of anti-choking suction devices (ACS) and the necessity of medical follow-up following the administration of rescue manoeuvres. Only 31 (20%) respondents were aware of the existence of such devices, while the remaining 125 (80%) reported no such knowledge. Furthermore, none of the participants owned or had previously used an ACS device prior to completing the questionnaire. It should be noted that these devices are not currently recommended by either ERC or AHA guidelines, and their inclusion in this study was exploratory, intended to assess public awareness rather than to evaluate their clinical use. A total of 123 (79%) parents correctly identified medical follow-up, defined as the necessity to consult a medical specialist for clinical examination and diagnostic imaging, as obligatory following a severe choking episode, and performing one of the rescue manoeuvres, such as: back blows and chest or abdominal thrusts. Conversely, 33 participants (21%) indicated that they would not seek medical follow-up for their child following an FBAO episode.

Discussion

This study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation aimed at assessing parental knowledge of paediatric choking management using a more realistic assessment approach than traditional survey formats. Rather than relying on recognition-based multiple-choice questions, we applied open-ended, scenario-based items to better reflect real-life decision-making during emergencies.

Our findings indicate a consistently low level of knowledge despite relatively high self-reported confidence and frequent prior exposure to paediatric choking events. This discrepancy is particularly important from a public health perspective, as it may reflect overestimation of one’s readiness to act in real-life emergencies. Considering the life-threatening nature of paediatric choking, where irreversible and progressing hypoxia can develop within minutes, these findings are clinically relevant and raise concerns regarding parental preparedness to provide effective first aid in FBAO situations23–26.

Given the exploratory design and limited statistical power of this study, the observed associations, particularly those on the role of hands-on training, should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they provide a valuable basis for future confirmatory research and targeted educational interventions.

Although nearly half of respondents reported previous hands-on training and prior experience managing a choking child, their training performance scores remained poor. Most notably, 50% of respondents scored 0 for the second manoeuvre in both infant and child scenarios, highlighting substantial gaps in knowledge of the correct sequence of rescue actions in paediatric choking management.

In their study in Poland, Wanot et al. found that 64.5% of parents declared confidence in their first aid knowledge, yet only 37.6% had received any formal training27. It should be noted that only 7.3% of the study group had real-life experience witnessing a child choking, which is much fewer than in our study. Wanot et al. reported that, in addition to the low prevalence of first aid training, respondents were inadequately prepared by healthcare education to manage FBAO events, indicating a systemic deficiency spanning both commercial training courses and the public healthcare system in Poland.

In a Polish study by Karyś et al., only 24% of responding mothers felt confident in first aid administration for choking children despite 74% having participated in first aid courses in the past28. Similar to this study, the level of knowledge was unsatisfactory and highly concerning. Only 20% of respondents knew the CPR guidelines and despite knowledge of legal obligation to deliver first aid by bystanders, only 24% of mothers would initiate first aid in real-life scenarios. Only half of respondents correctly identified the first rescue manoeuvre, while only 4% pointed to the correct second rescue manoeuvre. Despite awareness of the legal obligation to provide first aid as a bystander, only 24% of mothers declared that they would initiate first aid in a real-life emergency. The authors highlighted the importance of participation in first aid training courses and their effect on knowledge and practical skills, noting that women who attended first aid training courses were most likely to select correct answers. In our multivariate analysis, prior hands-on training was identified as a significant predictor of higher scores in this section of the test (p < 0.05). This finding highlights the importance of practical training in consolidating knowledge of CPR, which is consistent with previous reports on the role of the practical component in enhancing the effectiveness of CPR education.

Habeeb and Alarfaj assessed the level of awareness among Saudi parents regarding choking and other common paediatric emergencies requiring first aid29. Of the 300 respondents, only 6% demonstrated a level of knowledge that would be considered competent. These findings are very concerning as the respondents frequently opted for items corresponding to ineffective and even potentially harmful actions. Some of the most popular responses included lifting a child by the feet, instructing deep breaths, administering water orally, or blind oropharyngeal finger sweeping during a choking episode. The responses were selected by 64,6%, 62.6%, 55.1%, and 50% of participants, respectively. Similar misconceptions were noted in drowning scenarios, where 76% and 65% of respondents incorrectly opted for abdominal thrusts and back blows, respectively29. Comparably low levels of knowledge and awareness were also reported by Almutairi and Alharbi, who, in a sample of 385 parents, found adequate knowledge of paediatric choking management in only 36.9% of respondents30. Similarly, Bin Laswad et al. observed in a large cohort of 1,087 parents that although 23% of respondents had previously witnessed FB aspiration in a child, many still failed to provide correct answers, especially in infant-specific scenarios31.

The above research closely mirrors our findings, as highlighted by an acceptable knowledge threshold of 10 out of 15 points in the questionnaire. Notable gaps in problem understanding and knowledge can be observed even among experienced and highly self-confident parents, as only 8% of the study group reached or exceeded this score. These results demonstrate not only critically poor knowledge, but also that previous exposure to first aid content (e.g., lectures, handouts) and real-life FBAO witnessing, even when correlated with high self-confidence, do not necessarily ensure an acceptable level of knowledge, competence, or performance. This finding advocates for broader use of qualitative assessment tools, particularly for training evaluation and research.

Unlike other studies on this topic, this study did not use multiple-choice items to assess knowledge levels27,30. The original open-ended questionnaire, though time-consuming, better reflected true knowledge levels by avoiding forced-choice responses, allowing free expression of ideas, logical thinking, and long-term memory retrieval. This is further supported by multiple shallow or absent answers, particularly in the infant scenario-based knowledge questions.

Gaps in education and training design

The limited available literature highlights the insufficient attention devoted to FBAO. Therefore, first-aid education on choking may receive less interest than, for example, CPR. However, FBAO remains one of the most common preventable causes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in children. A properly trained parent may be the only person capable of providing effective assistance before decompensation occurs. Recognizing the signs of FBAO should merely precede the ability to correctly perform age-appropriate rescue manoeuvres25,26.

Unfortunately, incorrect techniques pose serious risks; reports have linked misapplied abdominal thrusts and Heimlich manoeuvre to severe complications, including aortic dissection, mesenteric trauma, oesophageal rupture, and cardiac injury23,32–37. Even chest thrusts and back blows, though generally safer, can cause injury if performed incorrectly. Thus, merely knowing manoeuvre names is insufficient; correct sequencing, technique, and anatomical adaptation are essential.

This emphasizes the importance of hands-on practical training. Omitting the practical components of rescue manoeuvres or aspects related to providing first aid to children, along with child-specific differences from adult first aid, should be avoided. Training participants should have access to paediatric mannequins. According to the literature, hybrid or blended learning, combining high- and low-fidelity simulations with virtual reality and mobile applications, increases participant engagement, resulting in better learning outcomes and longer knowledge retention38,39. Repeated exposure to interactive technologies with scientific formats also significantly improves knowledge acquisition40–42. However, as pointed out by Bakke-Wiborg et al., there is limited availability worldwide of choking-specific tools for laypersons43.

Nearly half of respondents who reported taking first aid courses in the past did not recertify within three years. These findings highlight the need for regular knowledge and skills refreshment. Literature shows that a substantial decline in first aid knowledge occurs within the first months after completing a training course44. As with lifeguard training, mandatory periodic recertification (e.g., every 2–3 years) should become standard practice for parents and caregivers, especially those caring for young children.

In our univariate regression analyses, none of the investigated demographic or experiential factors significantly predicted performance scores. While prior practical training was associated with borderline effects (p = 0.06) in both cardiac arrest and rescue manoeuvres scores, these remained non-significant. However, in the multivariate linear regression model, prior hands-on training emerged as a significant independent predictor of performance in the cardiac arrest scenario (b = 0.80; p = 0.0068). These findings underscore the crucial role of hands-on, skill-based education and practical training. Hands-on and skill-based education are essential for developing effective first-aid competencies30,38,39.

Many of the investigated factors considered potentially associated with parental knowledge showed no statistical significance. These factors included prior real-life experience with choking incidents, healthcare educational background, having more than one child, and drawing on multiple information sources. Some parents reported having previously employed techniques that are not recommended due to proven ineffectiveness or potential to cause harm, such as blind oropharyngeal finger sweeps or lifting the child by the legs in a head-down position. Similar observations were also reported by other researchers29,31,45. Previous experience involving actions inconsistent with current guidelines may have reinforced incorrect practices and misconceptions. Back blows, chest and abdominal thrusts remain the internationally recommended rescue manoeuvres for FBAO; however, each of these techniques carries certain risks when performed incorrectly. Previous reports documented severe complications arising from misapplied abdominal thrusts, including aortic dissection, oesophageal rupture, and cardiac injury34–37. Despite these risks, our findings, together with previous reports, suggest that instructional gaps persist, and inappropriate actions alongside incorrect use of theoretically proper manoeuvres are still common.

In order to address this, first aid training curricula should emphasize correct techniques, include realistic simulation-based practice, and promote routine periodic recertification. Furthermore, training programmes should also explicitly address contraindicated techniques, such as blind finger sweeps, forceful water intake, or child inversion manoeuvres. Acquisition of psychomotor competence should constitute an important training objective, ideally assessed via structured observational evaluation.

Self-reported confidence, willingness, and psychological factors

The present study revealed a significant relationship between self-reported confidence and knowledge scores in the univariate analysis (b = 0.45; p = 0.04). This was not confirmed in the multivariate analysis, likely due to underpowering. However, the observed directionality is noteworthy as it is consistent with prior evidence showing that self-reported confidence is an important predictor for both willingness to intervene and quality of emergency performance46. This research indicates that many laypersons, despite possessing theoretical knowledge, may still hesitate to intervene in real-life emergencies due to anxiety or uncertainty stemming from low self-confidence.

First aid training courses should therefore also address willingness to perform rescue manoeuvres. Expanding training beyond traditional knowledge-based curricula may improve both engagement and performance. Training programmes should also address positive motivation in participants by reducing fear of harming the victim, concerns about legal liability, and negative self-assessment of one’s own competencies. A practical approach to acquiring theoretical knowledge through realistic scenario-based practice also emerges as particularly valuable tool.

Researchers should recognize psychological factors, particularly self-reported confidence, as a critical and independent aspect of first-aid performance, and further evaluate their impact on the ability to implement theoretical knowledge in educational and real-life emergency settings.

Conclusions

The aim of this study was hypothesis generation, not causal inference or population generalisation. This exploratory study suggests that Polish parents have limited theoretical knowledge regarding first aid for paediatric choking and CPR in the context of FBAO. Respondents had relatively high self-reported confidence and frequent prior exposure to paediatric choking incidents; nevertheless, the accuracy and completeness of their knowledge were generally poor.

Prior practical training emerged as a significant predictor of higher knowledge scores in multivariate analysis; however, other investigated factors, including gender, place of residence, medical education, number of children, training recency and source, or having multiple knowledge sources, did not demonstrate statistically significant associations with knowledge outcomes. These findings may suggest that the presence of potentially relevant demographic characteristics or informal experience may not be sufficient to increase the level of knowledge, and therefore the preparedness to provide first aid.

Our findings underscore the importance of well-structured hands-on first aid training and recertification programmes for paediatric choking management, highlighting the need to improve the current level of parental knowledge regarding FBAO. Nevertheless, further research is needed, as this study has some limitations, including a limited sample size and restricted generalizability. However, the methodology employed demonstrated its value in the pilot phase and could be applied in further research with larger populations to verify the hypotheses formulated in this study and support the development of first aid educational programmes for caregivers. These findings should therefore be interpreted in light of the exploratory design and are intended to guide future confirmatory studies rather than provide definitive conclusions.
