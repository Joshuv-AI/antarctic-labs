Sharp bounds between the saturation number and the harmonic index
Chakshu Gupta

Abstract
The saturation number μ ∗ ​ ( G ) \mu^{*}(G) of a graph G G is the minimum cardinality of a maximal matching, and H ⁡ ( G ) H(G) is its harmonic index. TxGraffiti conjectured in 2023 that μ ∗ ​ ( G ) ≤ H ⁡ ( G ) \mu^{*}(G)\leq H(G) for every nontrivial connected graph G G , and Bıyıkoğlu refuted this by showing that the ratio μ ∗ ​ ( G ) / H ​ ( G ) \mu^{*}(G)/H(G) can be made arbitrarily large. Restricting to trees bounds the ratio sharply. Every nontrivial tree T T satisfies μ ∗ ​ ( T ) < 3 2 ​ H ​ ( T ) \mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T) , with the constant 3 / 2 3/2 best possible. A complementary bound H ⁡ ( G ) < 4 ​ μ ∗ ​ ( G ) H(G)<4\mu^{*}(G) holds for every graph with an edge, so on a nontrivial tree the saturation number is pinned to 1 4 ​ H ​ ( T ) < μ ∗ ​ ( T ) < 3 2 ​ H ​ ( T ) \tfrac{1}{4}H(T)<\mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T) , both constants best possible. The friendship graph F 4 F_{4} is a smallest counterexample to the conjecture, on nine vertices, and the smallest tree counterexample is the subdivided star on eleven vertices. For each positive integer m m a family of graphs with m m hubs has ratio approaching m + 1 m+1 , while the conjecture holds whenever all vertices have equal degree. Both invariants arise in applications, the harmonic index as a molecular descriptor and the saturation number as a measure of adsorption inefficiency, and the bounds estimate the latter, which is NP-hard to compute, by the former, which is computable in linear time.

Theorem 2

(Tree bound)
.

Every nontrivial tree

\mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T)
, and the

Proof.

Since every maximal matching is a matching,

for every nontrivial tree

|V(T)|

Base case.
If

with
k\geq 1

k

3H(T)-2\nu(T)=(4k-2)/(k+1)>0

Inductive step.
Otherwise
T
has a path on four vertices.
Fix a longest one,

and

is a leaf,

v_{0}
. Let

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{t}

v_{0}
is among them,

, and

is adjacent to both

and

is an integer at least
2

Each

The leaf

Each summand of

\displaystyle\frac{2}{D-1+d(z_{j})}-\frac{2}{D+d(z_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(D-1+d(z_{j}))(D+d(z_{j}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{D(D+1)}.

Summing the

It remains to bound the right-hand side. The values

For

\frac{2}{D+2}-\frac{2(D-1)}{D(D+1)}=\frac{4}{D(D+1)(D+2)}>0,

\displaystyle 3H(T)-2\nu(T)

\displaystyle=\bigl(3H(T^{-})-2\nu(T^{-})\bigr)+3\bigl(H(T)-H(T^{-})\bigr)-2\bigl(\nu(T)-\nu(T^{-})\bigr)

and then dropping the restriction in favour of all
edges at each vertex,

H(G)=\sum_{v\in S}\ \sum_{\varphi(xy)=v}\frac{2}{d(x)+d(y)}\ \leq\ \sum_{v\in S}\ \sum_{u\sim v}\frac{2}{d(u)+d(v)}.

For a fixed
v
, every neighbour
u
has
d(u)\geq 1
, so

2/(d(u)+d(v))\leq 2/(d(v)+1)
, and summing over the
d(v)
neighbours of
v
,

\sum_{u\sim v}\frac{2}{d(u)+d(v)}\ \leq\ \frac{2\,d(v)}{d(v)+1}\ <\ 2.

Hence
H(G)<\sum_{v\in S}2=2|S|=4\mu^{*}(G)
.

For best-possibility, let
D_{k}
be the balanced double star: two adjacent
centres, each joined to
k
leaves, on
n=2k+2
vertices. Its central edge
forms a maximal matching, every other edge being a pendant edge at a centre, so

\mu^{*}(D_{k})=1
. Each centre has degree
k+1
, so the
2k
pendant edges
each contribute
2/(k+2)
and the central edge contributes
1/(k+1)
, giving

H(D_{k})=\frac{4k}{k+2}+\frac{1}{k+1}\longrightarrow 4\qquad(k\to\infty).

Thus
H(D_{k})/\mu^{*}(D_{k})\to 4
, so no smaller constant suffices.
∎

Combined with the tree bound
\mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T)
of
Theorem
2
, this pins the saturation number of a nontrivial
tree between two harmonic multiples,

\tfrac{1}{4}H(T)<\mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T),

both constants best possible: the subdivided stars approach the upper one and
the double stars the lower.

4
Regular graphs

Every counterexample above has vertices of unequal degrees. The friendship
graph
F_{k}
has hub degree
2k
and rim degree
2
, the windmill
G_{m,k}

has hub degree
2k
and blade degree
m+1
, and the subdivided star
S_{k}

has degrees
k
,
2
, and
1
. When all degrees are equal, the conjecture
holds.

Theorem 6

(Regular graphs)
.

If
G
is a connected graph in which every vertex has degree
r

(
r\geq 1
), then
H(G)=n/2
, and consequently

\mu^{*}(G)\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\leq H(G)
.

Proof.

Every edge
\{u,v\}
has
d(u)+d(v)=2r
, so each edge contributes

2/(2r)=1/r
to the harmonic index. The graph has
nr/2
edges, so

H(G)=(nr/2)/r=n/2
. Since every matching has at most

\lfloor n/2\rfloor
edges,
\mu^{*}(G)\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\leq n/2=H(G)
.
∎

Equality
\mu^{*}(G)=H(G)=n/2
forces
n
even and every maximal
matching to be perfect. Such graphs are called randomly matchable.
Sumner
[
Sum79
]
proved that the only connected randomly
matchable graphs are the complete graph
K_{2t}
and the balanced complete
bipartite graph
K_{t,t}
. An exhaustive verification on all 54 connected
regular graphs with at most nine vertices confirms that
H=n/2
holds
exactly, that the conjecture holds for each, and that equality

\mu^{*}=n/2
occurs precisely for
K_{2},K_{4},C_{4}\cong K_{2,2},K_{6},K_{3,3},K_{8},K_{4,4}
. These graphs are all of the form
K_{2t}
or

K_{t,t}
, consistent with Sumner’s theorem.

5
Graphs of maximum degree four

An exhaustive search finds no counterexample to
\mu^{*}\leq H
of maximum
degree at most four on up to eleven vertices, in contrast to the smallest
counterexamples without that restriction, which appear already on nine
vertices (Section
3
). This suggests the inequality holds throughout the
class of
subquartic
graphs, those of maximum degree at most four.

Conjecture 1

(Subquartic graphs)
.

Every connected graph of maximum degree at most four satisfies

\mu^{*}(G)\leq H(G)
.

Among the graphs of maximum degree at most four on at most nine vertices,
equality
\mu^{*}=H
holds for exactly six: the complete graphs
K_{2}
and

K_{4}
, the four-cycle
C_{4}
, the complete bipartite graphs
K_{3,3}
and

K_{4,4}
, and the subdivided star
S_{4}
. The first five are regular and of the
form
K_{2t}
or
K_{t,t}
(Theorem
6
), while the subdivided
star
S_{4}
is the only non-regular extremal graph. A proof of
Conjecture
1
must therefore meet equality both at the
regular graphs of Theorem
6
and at the subdivided star
S_{4}
.

6
Discussion

Both invariants, the saturation number and the harmonic index, carry meaning
outside graph theory. The
harmonic index arose as a molecular descriptor and is used in chemical graph
theory to relate the structure of a compound to its
properties
[
Faj87
,
Zho12
]
. The saturation
number has a separate, physical reading. A maximal matching models a jammed
state of diatomic molecules adsorbed onto a substrate, each molecule occupying
a pair of adjacent sites, and the saturation number is the size of the smallest
such state, a measure of the worst-case inefficiency of
adsorption
[
AK17
]
. The inequality
\mu^{*}\leq H
and the
bounds proved above thus connect two quantities of independent applied origin.

The two quantities differ sharply in computational cost. The harmonic index is
computed in linear time, by summing one term over each edge. The saturation
number, equal to the independent domination number of the line graph
(Section
1
), is NP-hard to compute, and remains so on planar
graphs of maximum degree three
[
AK17
]
. The bounds
established above therefore estimate an NP-hard quantity by a linear-time one.
The universal inequality
H(G)<4\mu^{*}(G)
yields the lower bound

\mu^{*}(G)>\tfrac{1}{4}H(G)
for every graph. On trees, where the saturation
number is itself computable in polynomial time, the two-sided bound

\tfrac{1}{4}H(T)<\mu^{*}(T)<\tfrac{3}{2}H(T)
confines it to within a constant
factor of the harmonic index, both constants best possible. The generalized
windmills show that the upper bound does not extend to all graphs,
since a single high-degree hub drives
\mu^{*}/H
above any constant.

These bounds are structural, not algorithmic, and do not by themselves improve
adsorption models or domination algorithms. Their interest lies in that a
linear-time degree quantity brackets an NP-hard matching invariant, sharply on
trees and from below on every graph, and that the bracket fails outside the tree
class in a controlled way, through vertices of high degree.

7
Conclusion

TxGraffiti’s conjecture is false, but its failure is structured. A connected graph
violates
\mu^{*}\leq H
through the edges that a maximal matching is forced
to use, and the size of the violation tracks the harmonic weight of
those edges. When the forcing edges have high-degree endpoints, as in the
generalized windmill, their weight vanishes and
\mu^{*}/H
grows without
bound, so no degree-independent correction
\mu^{*}\leq c\,H
can hold
(Corollary
1
). The reverse inequality needs no such
correction: each saturated vertex absorbs harmonic weight below
2
, so

H(G)<4\mu^{*}(G)
for every graph with an edge (Theorem
5
), and the
harmonic index bounds the saturation number from below universally. When
every degree is equal the bound holds outright
(Theorem
6
), and on trees, where the forcing edges end in
leaves, it holds up to the constant
3/2

(Theorem
2
), which is best possible. An upper bound valid
for every connected graph must therefore temper the harmonic index by a
measure of degree disparity. Identifying the right measure remains open.
