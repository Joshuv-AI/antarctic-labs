Specific absorption rate of uniaxial single-domain nanomagnets: stochastic spin dynamics versus linear response theory
H. Kachkachi

Abstract
We compute the specific absorption rate of a uniaxial single-domain nanomagnet driven by an alternating magnetic field by two methods: i) direct numerical integration of the stochastic (Langevin) Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation (the LLL approach), and ii) linear response theory (LRT) based on the Debye susceptibility with the Néel relaxation time τ N \tau_{\mathrm{N}} . We first analytically show that both methods are equivalent for small magnetic field amplitude, and then compute their deviation Λ ≡ SAR LLL / SAR LRT − 1 \Lambda\equiv\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LLL}}/\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LRT}}-1 as a function of the magnetic field amplitude for two temperatures chosen on opposite sides of the Debye resonance. One of the main results is that the sign and magnitude of Λ \Lambda are governed by the dimensionless product ω ​ τ N \omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}} , in addition to the linearity parameter ξ = μ s ​ B 0 / k B ​ T \xi=\mu_{s}B_{0}/k_{B}T for the easy-axis geometry considered here. Indeed, below resonance ( ω ​ τ N < 1 \omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}<1 ), linear response theory overestimates the specific absorption rate, whereas above resonance ( ω ​ τ N > 1 \omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}>1 , the regime typical of blocked nanoparticles) linear response theory can underestimate the specific absorption rate by up to ∼ 70 % \sim 70\% at ξ ∼ 2 \xi\sim 2 . We expect this work to provide a quantitative guidance for the use of linear response theory in magnetic hyperthermia and related nanoscale heat-transport problems, and to serve as a single-particle benchmark for extensions to many-spin and interacting systems.



I
Introduction

Nowadays, heating of magnetic nanoparticles by an alternating magnetic
field (AMF)—magnetic hyperthermia (MH)—has become a serious
cancer-therapy modality sustained by an increasing attention
Pankhurst et al. 2003
;
Dutz and Hergt 2014
;
Hergt et al. 2006
;
Zhang and Lu 2024
;
Baldea et al. 2025
;
Bai et al. 2024
.
More broadly, it is a rich playground for studying heat generation and
transport at the nanoscale
Déjardin and Kachkachi 2022
;
J.-L. Déjardin, H.
Kachkachi 2024
;
Iglesias et al. 2021
;
Ruta et al. 2024
;
Kachkachi 2026
.
In this context, the figure of merit is provided by the specific loss
power (SLP), or more often by the specific absorption rate (SAR),

i.e.
, the power dissipated per unit mass of magnetic material.

In practice, the SAR is very often estimated within the
linear
response theory
(LRT), through the compact expression
\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LRT}}=\mu_{0}\omega\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)H_{0}^{2}/(2\rho)
,
where
H_{0}
is the field amplitude,
\omega=2\pi f
the angular frequency,
and
\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
the imaginary part of the complex susceptibility
Rosensweig 2002
;
Hergt et al. 2006
;
Déjardin et al. 2017
;
Déjardin and Kachkachi 2022
.
The advantage of this approach is that, combining the Debye relaxation
model and the Néel–Brown expression for the relaxation time
Néel 1949
;
Brown 1963
,
it gives a closed-form prediction that requires no (heavy) numerical
simulations. The obvious question that arises then is when LRT is
quantitatively valid. In general, in the literature, the answer is when the linearity condition
\xi\equiv\mu_{s}\mu_{0}H_{0}/k_{B}T\ll 1

is satisfied
Carrey, Mehdaoui, and Respaud 2011
;
Andreu and Natividad 2013
. However, we know that
this criterion is necessary but not sufficient. By way of example,
direct comparisons between stochastic LLG simulations and LRT for
iron-oxide assemblies
Usov, Rytov, and Bautin 2021
, and between linear and nonlinear
AC-susceptibility methods
Yamaminami et al. 2021
, demonstrate that
the sign and magnitude of LRT errors depend on other parameters, in
addition to
\xi
.

An alternative, exact in the macrospin approximation, numerical approach
proceeds by integrating the stochastic Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert
equation (LLGE) directly. In the sequel, this will be referred to
as the Landau–Lifshitz–Langevin (LLL) method. Then, the SAR may
be estimated as the time-averaged hysteresis-loop area
Lacroix et al. 2009
;
Carrey, Mehdaoui, and Respaud 2011
;
Martinez-Boubeta et al. 2013
;
Mehdaoui et al. 2013
.
LLL makes no linearity assumption and is one of the appropriate benchmarks
for LRT. Time-quantified Monte Carlo (TQMC) simulations, in which
a Monte Carlo step corresponds to a physical time unit derived from
the attempt frequency
O. Chubykalo, U. Nowak, R. Smirnov-Rueda, M.A.
Wongsam, R.W. Chantrell and J.M. Gonzalez 2003
;
U. Nowak, R. W. Chantrell, and E. C.
Kennedy 2000
;
Ledue, Vernay, and Kachkachi 2026
, can play the same role and
is particularly suited to multi-spin systems.

In the present work, we consider the simplest system of a single uniaxial nanomagnet, which is the building block of a nanoscale heating system. Then, we study the validity of LRT with respect not only to
\xi
alone, but also the dimensionless product
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
.
Indeed, the Debye susceptibility
\chi^{\prime\prime}
peaks at the
resonance temperature

T_{\mathrm{peak}}
where
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}(T_{\mathrm{peak}})=1
, and the sign of the leading nonlinear correction
\Lambda
changes near this point (more precisely at
\eta=\sqrt{3}
, as shown by the perturbative analysis in Sec.
II.5
and Figs.
3
and
4
), leading to qualitatively different, and even opposite, deviations of LRT on either side of the peak. We emphasize that this sign change has direct practical consequences for MH.
By way of illustration, we use a cobalt nanomagnet as the model system whose large anisotropy constant places
T_{\mathrm{peak}}
in a cryogenic
but accessible range, thus allowing both sides of the resonance to be explored. However, the formalism presented here is material-independent; in Sec.
III.3
we translate the analytical estimates to Fe
3
O
4
ferrofluids at physiological temperature.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
II
, we present the model and the two SAR methods (LLL and LRT), establish their analytical equivalence in the linear regime, and derive the perturbative expression for the leading nonlinear deviation
\Lambda
together with its physical interpretation.
In Section
III
, we discuss the numerical results: the Debye resonance and SAR versus temperature, the amplitude and frequency sweeps characterising
\Lambda(\xi,\eta)
on both sides of the resonance, and an analytical estimate for Fe
3
O
4
ferrofluids at physiological temperature.
Section
IV
summarises our conclusions.

II
Model and methods

II.1
Stochastic equation of motion

We consider a single uniaxial nanomagnet of volume
V
, anisotropy constant
K_{2}
, and saturation moment
\mu_{s}=M_{s}V
. The AC field
\bm{H}_{\mathrm{AC}}(t)=H_{0}\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}
is applied in the direction of the easy axis.
The unit magnetisation vector
\bm{m}
obeys the LLG equation in Landau–Lifshitz form,

\frac{d\bm{m}}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}}\bigl[\bm{m}\times\bm{h}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\alpha\,\bm{m}\times(\bm{m}\times\bm{h}_{\mathrm{eff}})\bigr],

(1)

where
\tau=t/t_{\mathrm{s}}
with
t_{\mathrm{s}}=(\alpha\gamma H_{\!a})^{-1}
,
H_{\!a}=2K_{2}V/\mu_{s}
is the anisotropy field, and
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\bm{h}_{\mathrm{an}}+\bm{h}_{\mathrm{AC}}+\bm{h}_{\mathrm{th}}
is the dimensionless effective field
1
1

1

In each contribution to the effective field, we use the notation
h\equiv H/H_{a}
, and
B_{0}=\mu_{0}\,H_{0}
.
. In the LLL approach, thermal fluctuations enter as a Gaussian white-noise field
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{th}}
, the Langevin field, with variance
D=\alpha^{2}/(1+\alpha^{2})\sigma
per unit time step
Brown 1979
;
García-Palacios and Lázaro 1998
, where
\sigma=K_{2}V/k_{B}T
is the reduced energy barrier. The Landau–Lifshitz equation (without the
1+\alpha^{2}
gyromagnetic renormalisation) is recovered in the limit
\alpha\to 0

García-Palacios and Lázaro 1998
;
J. L. Garcia-Palacios 2007
;
Déjardin, Kachkachi, and Martinez 2012
. We work at fixed
\alpha=1
throughout and accordingly use the intermediate-to-high damping approximation for the relaxation rate.

We introduce the dimensionless parameters

\sigma=\frac{K_{2}V}{k_{B}T},\quad h_{0}=\frac{B_{0}}{\mu_{0}H_{\!a}},\quad\xi=\frac{\mu_{s}B_{0}}{k_{B}T}=2\sigma h_{0},

(2)

and
\eta=\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
where
\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
is the zero-field Néel relaxation time (Sec.
II.3
).

II.2
SAR from the loop-area using LLL

The energy absorbed per AC cycle equals the hysteresis-loop area.
Projecting onto the field direction, we get

\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LLL}}=\frac{f\,\mu_{s}\,H_{\!a}}{V\,\rho}\,\left\langle\left|\omega\!\int_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{per}}}m_{z}(\tau)\sin(\omega\tau)\,d\tau\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{traj}},

(3)

where the average is over
N_{\mathrm{traj}}
independent stochastic trajectories, each integrated for
N_{\mathrm{cyc}}
stationary cycles after a transient of
N_{\mathrm{tr}}
cycles. The signal-to-noise ratio scales as
h_{0}\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{traj}}N_{\mathrm{cyc}}}
, which leads to the constraint on sampling:
N_{\mathrm{traj}}N_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gtrsim(10/h_{0,\min})^{2}
.
The variance is reduced by antithetic-variate sampling
Hammersley and Morton 1956
;
Owen 2008
(pairs of
\pm
noise trajectories) and all pair-loops are parallelised with OpenMP. In our runs we use
N_{\mathrm{traj}}=2000-3000
,
N_{\mathrm{cyc}}=50
.
The stochastic LLGE (
1
) is integrated numerically using a fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) scheme in which a single realisation of the thermal field
\bm{h}_{\mathrm{th}}
, a Gaussian vector with zero mean and variance
\langle h_{\mathrm{th},i}(\tau)\,h_{\mathrm{th},j}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta_{ij}\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
, is drawn once per step and held constant through all four sub-stages.
By holding the noise frozen within each step, we make the local increment consistent with the Stratonovich prescription, which is the physically correct interpretation for the magnetic Langevin equation
J. L. Garcia-Palacios 2007
.
The dimensionless time step is set adaptively to
\Delta\tau=T_{\mathrm{per}}/N_{\mathrm{step}}
with
N_{\mathrm{step}}=1000
steps per AC period (capped at
\Delta\tau_{\max}=t_{\mathrm{s}}
to stay within the stability region of the deterministic LLG). We note that step-size convergence was verified by doubling
N_{\mathrm{step}}
to 2000, and we have observed that the SAR changed by less than
0.5\%
across all field amplitudes and temperatures reported here.

II.3
SAR from linear response theory (LRT)

Within LRT
Kubo, Toda, and Hashitsume 1991
;
Rosensweig 2002
;
Déjardin et al. 2017
;
Déjardin and Kachkachi 2022
,

\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LRT}}=\frac{\mu_{0}\,\omega\,\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)\,H_{0}^{2}}{2\rho},\qquad\chi^{\prime\prime}=\chi_{0}\,\frac{\eta}{1+\eta^{2}},

(4)

with
\chi_{0}\equiv\mu_{0}\mu_{s}^{2}\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle_{\sigma}/(k_{B}TV)
being the static susceptibility, where the Boltzmann average at reduced anisotropy
\sigma
is given by

\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle_{\sigma}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}u^{2}\,e^{\sigma u^{2}}\,du}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\sigma u^{2}}\,du}

(5)

with limits:
\sigma\to 0\Rightarrow\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle\to\tfrac{1}{3}
(isotropic
Langevin);
\sigma\gg 1\Rightarrow\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle\to 1-1/(2\sigma)
(blocked).

Regarding, the relaxation time appearing in
\eta=\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
, in practice, the Aharoni-Néel-Brown expression is only valid for
h\leq 0.4
and
\sigma\geq 2
[see Ref.
Coffey et al. 1995
]. Cregg, Crothers and Wickstead
(
P.J. Cregg, D.S.F. Crothers, and A.W.
Wickstead 1994
)
proposed an improved expression that is valid in the whole range of
\sigma
:

\tau_{\mathrm{N}}=(1+\alpha^{2})\tau_{s}\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{1+\sigma}+2^{-\sigma}\right)^{-1}\left(e^{\sigma}-1\right).

(6)

As mentioned earlier, the prefactor
(1+\alpha^{2})
reflects the choice of dynamics (LLGE); for
\alpha\to 0
(Landau–Lifshitz limit) it reduces to unity and
t_{\mathrm{s}}\to(\gamma H_{\!a})^{-1}
.

II.4
Consistency of LRT and LLL

The magnetization of a system in the direction of the probing magnetic field
H
can be written as

m=\chi_{1}H+\chi_{3}H^{3}+\chi_{5}H^{5}+\ldots=\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi_{2n+1}H^{2n+1}\left(t\right).

Then, under the excitation
\mathbf{H}=h_{0}\,e^{i\omega t}\mathbf{e}_{h}
, we obtain the nonlinear magnetic response (
m_{n}=m_{n}^{\prime}-im_{n}^{\prime\prime}
)
Bitoh et al. 1995
;
Bitoh et al. 1996
:

\displaystyle m\left(t\right)=\Re\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}m_{2n+1}e^{i\left(2n+1\right)\omega t}\right]

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Bigg[m_{2n+1}^{\prime}\cos\left[\left(2n+1\right)\omega t\right]+m_{2n+1}^{\prime\prime}\sin\left[\left(2n+1\right)\omega t\right]\Bigg]

where

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}^{\prime}h_{0}+\frac{3}{4}\chi_{3}^{\prime}h_{0}^{3}+\ldots,m_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\chi_{1}^{\prime\prime}h_{0}+\frac{3}{4}\chi_{3}^{\prime\prime}h_{0}^{3}+\ldots,

\displaystyle m_{3}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\chi_{3}^{\prime}h_{0}^{3}+\frac{5}{16}\chi_{5}^{\prime}h_{0}^{5}+\ldots,m_{3}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{4}\chi_{3}^{\prime\prime}h_{0}^{3}+\frac{5}{16}\chi_{5}^{\prime\prime}h_{0}^{5}+\ldots.

Next, using expansion (
7
), in the limit
h_{0}\to 0
, the sine integral in Eq. (
3
) converges to
I=h_{0}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{1}\pi/\omega
, where
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is the (linear) out-of-phase susceptibility. Converting to SI via
\mu_{s}=M_{s}V
and
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{SI}}=\chi^{\prime\prime}_{1}\,\mu_{s}H_{\!a}V/(\mu_{0}M_{s}H_{0}^{2}V^{2})
one recovers Eq. (
4
) exactly
Coffey et al. 1998
.

This proves that
\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LLL}}
and
\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LRT}}
are consistent methods: any discrepancy at finite
h_{0}
, for instance, marks a nonlinear effect.

II.5
Deviation of LLL from LRT: perturbative expansion

To quantify the comparison between the two approaches, we introduce the relative deviation

\Lambda(\xi)\equiv\frac{\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LLL}}}{\mathrm{SAR}_{\mathrm{LRT}}}-1

(8)

In the next section, we discuss the behavior of
\Lambda
as obtained by the LLL approach and compare it to the approximate analytical expression we derive below from LRT.

Such an analytical expression can be obtained with the help of the perturbative approach used in Ref.
F. Vernay, Z. Sabsabi, H.
Kachkachi 2014
,
i.e.
,
\chi\simeq\chi^{(1)}+3H_{0}^{2}\,\chi^{(3)}
.
Then, using the fact that
\mathrm{SAR}\propto\Im[\chi]
, we infer the first correction to the LRT (in our notation):
\Lambda=3\xi^{2}\,\Im[\chi^{(3)}(\omega)]/\Im[\chi^{(1)}(\omega)]
.

Then, following Refs.
García-Palacios and Garanin 2004
;
Déjardin, Vernay, and Kachkachi 2020
, the longitudinal first-order and third-order susceptibilities for a uniaxial nanomagnet read (
\eta=\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
)

\chi^{(1)}(\omega)=\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(1)}}{(1+i\eta)},\quad\chi^{(3)}(\omega)=\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(3)}\,\frac{1-i\eta/2}{(1+i\eta)(1+3i\eta)},

(9)

where the static equilibrium susceptibilities,
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(1)}
and
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(3)}
, for the longitudinal geometry, can be obtained from Eq. (8) of Ref.
F. Vernay, Z. Sabsabi, H.
Kachkachi 2014
(upon setting there
\zeta=0
,
x=0
):

\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(1)}=1-\frac{1}{\sigma},\qquad\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(3)}=\frac{1}{3}\!\left(-1+\frac{2}{\sigma}\right).

(10)

This leads to the following analytical expression for
\Lambda

\Lambda(\sigma,\xi,\eta)\simeq-\xi^{2}\,F(\sigma,\eta),\quad F(\sigma,\eta)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\sigma-2}{\sigma-1}\,\frac{3-\eta^{2}}{1+9\eta^{2}}.

(11)

In the two limiting regimes this gives

\Lambda\simeq\begin{cases}-\dfrac{9}{2}\,\dfrac{\sigma-2}{\sigma-1}\,\xi^{2},&\eta\ll 1\quad\text{(fast regime)},\\[11.99998pt]
+\dfrac{\sigma-2}{6(\sigma-1)}\,\xi^{2},&\eta\gg 1\quad\text{(blocked regime)}.\end{cases}

(12)

where the sign of
\Lambda
in the blocked regime is positive for
\sigma>2
, consistent with LLL exceeding LRT, see Eq. (
8
).

The Debye susceptibility
\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\chi_{0}\,\eta/(1+\eta^{2})
is maximised at
\eta=\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}=1
and decreases on both sides. Then, as is well known, an applied field modifies the
effective
barrier height and thereby the
effective
relaxation time
\tau_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(h_{0})
.
For a uniaxial nanomagnet with the field along the easy axis, the barrier is lowered by the Zeeman energy:
\Delta E_{\pm}\propto\sigma(1\mp h_{0})^{2}
. Therefore
\tau_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(h_{0})<\tau_{\mathrm{N}}(0)
,
i.e.
, the field always moves
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
downward relative to its zero-field value. Consequently, we observe the following behavior [illustrated in Fig.
1
],

•

If
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}(0)<1
(fast regime,
T>T_{\mathrm{peak}}
), moving further below resonance reduces
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{eff}}
relative to
the LRT prediction, leading to
\Lambda<0
.

•

If
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}(0)>1
(blocked regime,
T<T_{\mathrm{peak}}
): the field
drives
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}
toward resonance, increasing
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{eff}}

above the LRT prediction and thereby
\Lambda>0
.

This effective-
\tau
picture locates the sign change of
\Lambda
at
\eta=1
. The perturbative formula Eq. (
11
) refines this: the exact threshold is
\eta=\sqrt{3}
, as seen directly in Fig.
4
.

Results

For a quantitative discussion of the results, we use the set of physical parameters associated with a cobalt nanomagnet

Dupuis, P. Mélinon, and A. Pérez 2001

IV
Conclusion

We have computed and compared the SAR of a uniaxial single-domain nanomagnet as rendered by linear response theory and direct stochastic spin-dynamics (LLL). We have checked that the two approaches are consistent and agree analytically in the linear regime.
Outside the linear regime, we have seen that the product
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}
is as important as
\xi
for assessing the validity of linear response theory. In particular, the perturbative formula Eq. (
11
) shows that
\Lambda
changes sign at
\eta=\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}=\sqrt{3}
, which is distinct from the Debye resonance at
\eta=1
; both thresholds lie close to
T_{\mathrm{peak}}
, but they are not the same condition. This distinction is made explicit in Fig.
4
.

For superparamagnetic nanomagnets (Fe
3
O
4
,
\gamma
-Fe
2
O
3
) used in MH, the relevant regime is the fast one (
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}\ll 1
at physiological temperature), where LRT
overestimates
the SAR (Sec.
III.3
). This is consistent with numerical evidence that LRT overestimates the SAR for small, Néel-dominated particles at high fields
Usov, Rytov, and Bautin 2021
. Therefore, protocols based on LRT predictions systematically under-deliver heat, a bias that grows with AC amplitude and with decreasing frequency.
Furthermore, we would like to emphasize that the interplay between Néel and Brownian relaxation, relevant for nanomagnets in a viscous liquid where both mechanisms contribute, introduces additional nonlinear corrections whose sign and magnitude differ from the purely Néel case. Accordingly, a direct comparison of LRT with the full nonlinear AC susceptibility in that setting has been carried out experimentally by Yamaminami
et al.

Yamaminami et al. 2021
. Then, extending the present LLL framework to include rotational degrees of freedom would provide a natural theoretical complement to that work.

On the other hand, the blocked regime (
\omega\tau_{\mathrm{N}}\gg 1
), which is accessible to high-anisotropy or large nanomagnets, exhibits the opposite situation. More precisely, LRT underestimates the SAR by up to
\sim 70\%
(
\Lambda\approx 0.7
at
\xi\approx 2
), as demonstrated for the cobalt model system.

In both cases, the stochastic LLL simulation (or TQMC
O. Chubykalo, U. Nowak, R. Smirnov-Rueda, M.A.
Wongsam, R.W. Chantrell and J.M. Gonzalez 2003
;
U. Nowak, R. W. Chantrell, and E. C.
Kennedy 2000
;
Ledue, Vernay, and Kachkachi 2026
) provides a more reliable quantitative tool beyond the strict linear regime.

The single-particle picture developed here serves as a controlled benchmark for extensions to interacting many-spin assemblies, where
the same LRT-versus-exact comparison can be carried out including dipolar and exchange interactions
Ruta, Chantrell, and Hovorka 2015
;
Déjardin et al. 2017
;
Déjardin, Vernay, and Kachkachi 2020
;
Usov, Rytov, and Bautin 2021
.
