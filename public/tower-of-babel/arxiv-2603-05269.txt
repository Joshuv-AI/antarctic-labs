1.1.
Hitting time

Consider a random graph process on an
n
-vertex graph
G
, defined as a sequence of nested graphs
\{G_{t}\}_{t=0}^{e(G)}
. The process begins with
G_{0}
as the empty graph on the vertex set of
G
. For each
1\leq t\leq e(G)
, the graph
G_{t}
is obtained by adding a single edge to
G_{t-1}
, which is chosen uniformly at random from the set of edges in
G
that are not currently present in
G_{t-1}
(i.e., from
E(G)\setminus E(G_{t-1})
). A graph property
\mathcal{P}
is called increasing if it is closed under the addition of edges; that is, if
H\in\mathcal{P}
and
H\subseteq H^{\prime}
, then
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
.
For an increasing non-empty graph property
\mathcal{P}

is a random variable defined as the smallest index

such that
G_{t}
possesses
\mathcal{P}

Formally:

\tau_{\mathcal{P}}=\min\{t:G_{t}\in\mathcal{P}\}\,.

This represents the exact moment the process transitions from
G_{t-1}\notin\mathcal{P}
to
G_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the property of Hamiltonicity, and let
\mathcal{D}_{d}
denote the property of having a minimum degree of at least
d
. We denote the hitting time of
\mathcal{D}_{d}
in a random graph process

as

(or simply

if the host graph

is clear from context) and of

.
A celebrated result, proved independently by Ajtai, Komlós, and Szemerédi
[
1

[

\tau_{2}(G)=\tau_{\mathrm{HC}}(G).

This equality of hitting times demonstrates that, whp the graph becomes Hamiltonian at the precise moment it has minimum degree

It is natural to consider the Hamiltonicity of the random subgraph process of other host graphs.

Moving away from highly structured host graphs like hypercubes, it is natural to investigate host graphs that are random or random-like. For Erdős-Rényi random graphs

For completeness, we include a short proof of it in the appendix.

Motivated by this result, it is natural to study the hitting time problem on pseudorandom graphs, which are families of deterministic graphs that behave similarly to random graphs.
A prominent and extensively studied model of pseudorandom graphs is the spectral expanders, commonly referred to as
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs. An
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph is an
n
-vertex
d
-regular graph where the second largest eigenvalue (in terms of absolute value) of its adjacency matrix is at most

The reason

resembles more closely to that of an Erdős-Rényi random graph

For a more detailed introduction to this topic, we refer the reader to the excellent survey of Krivelevich and Sudakov
[
33

The study of the hitting time of Hamiltonicity in random graph processes on

-graphs was initiated by Frieze and Krivelevich
[

]
in 2002.
They established that if an

-graph satisfies the spectral gap condition

, then whp, the hitting time of Hamiltonicity coincides with the hitting time of minimum degree at least
2
.
However, this initial result carries a natural restriction on the degree. Because

for
d<(1-\varepsilon)n
(see e.g.
[
33
]
), their theorem is only applicable when the degree is relatively large, specifically
d=\Omega((n\log n)^{3/4})
.
This limitation prompted Frieze and Krivelevich to ask what is the weakest possible spectral gap requirement that preserves this hitting time property. They conjectured that, at least in the dense regime where
d=\Omega(n)
, the condition
\lambda=o(d)
should suffice.
In 2019, Alon and Krivelevich
[
5
]
relaxed the restriction on the spectral gap to
\lambda\leq cd^{2}/n
under a stronger assumption on

that

for some absolute constants

.
Despite these advancements, determining the optimal conditions on

To understand the difficulty of this hitting time problem, note that
determining conditions for Hamiltonicity in
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs has historically been a notoriously difficult question, even without the added complexity of random processes.
Krivelevich and Sudakov
[

made a well-known conjecture more than 20 years ago that an

After a series of works

There exists a constant

Building on the groundbreaking work

1.2.
Sharp thresholds

Hitting time results are the strongest one can hope for since they pin down the exact moment the random subgraph becomes Hamiltonian. From there, we deduce a sharp threshold for Hamiltonicity in
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs. Let
G_{p}
denote the random subgraph of
G
obtained by including each edge of
G
independently with probability
p\in(0,1)
. For an increasing graph property
\mathcal{P}
, we say that
p_{0}\in(0,1)
is a sharp threshold for
\mathcal{P}
if, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds whp:
G_{p}\notin\mathcal{P}
when
p\leq(1-\varepsilon)p_{0}
, and
G_{p}\in\mathcal{P}
when
p\geq(1+\varepsilon)p_{0}
.
The study of sharp threshold possesses a vast amount of literature and dates back to the birth of the study of the Erdős-Rényi random graphs.
A few prominent examples that appear in every introductory course on random graphs include connectivity, Hamiltonicity, and containment of a subgraph of a fixed size. See, for instance, the classic textbooks on random graphs
[
8
,
27
,
21
]
.
For an overview of this topic, we refer the reader to a recent survey by Perkins
[
35
]
.

As a corollary of
Theorem

1.2
, we determine the sharp threshold for Hamiltonicity in
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs with
d/\lambda
being sufficiently large.

Corollary 1.3
.

There exist a constant
C>0
such that if
G
is an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph where
d/\lambda\geq C
, then the sharp threshold for the Hamiltonicity of

is equal to

p_{0}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }d=o(\log n),\\
1-e^{-\log n/d}&\text{ if }d=\Omega(\log n).\end{cases}

In particular, when
d=\omega(\log n)
, one can take

to be
\log n/d
.

This result strengthens a recent result of the first and second authors, Han, and Zhao
[
13
]
from
d=\omega(\log n)
to all

In fact, we obtain a more refined characterization of the threshold values, identifying the specific point at which the threshold behavior transitions. In particular, we prove that the threshold behaves analogously to the complete graph case only when

Corollary 1.4

There exists a constant

•

G

sparse
if
d\leq 10\log n
, and
dense
if
d>10\log n
.
We begin with the sparse case.

3.1.
Sparse case

The goal of this section is to establish the following:

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C
such that the following statement holds.
Let
G
be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d\leq 10\log n
and
d/\lambda\geq C
.
Then the subgraph at the hitting time of minimum degree 2, denoted as
G_{\tau}
, satisfies the following properties whp.
Let
\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})\coloneqq\{v\in V(G):\deg_{G_{\tau}}(v)\leq d/10^{6}\}
and
C_{1}=C^{1/10}
. Then,

(P1)

|\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})|\leq n^{0.11}
;

(P2)

\forall~u,v\in\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
, we have
d_{G_{\tau}}(u,v)>4
;

(P3)

\forall~U,V\subseteq V(G)
disjoint and with
|U|,|V|\geq n/(4C_{1}^{3})
, we have
e_{G_{\tau}}(U,V)\geq 1
;

(P4)

\forall~W\subseteq V(G)\setminus\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
with
|W|\leq n/(2C_{1})
, we have
|N_{G_{\tau}}(W)|\geq C_{1}|W|
.

In
(P3)
, strengthening from
n/(2C_{1})
(which is what
C_{1}
-expander requires) to
n/(4C_{1}^{3})
is due to a technical reason. When applying
Lemma

3.1
, we only use it for
n/(4C_{1})
.
We begin by establishing a (crude) lower bound on the hitting time for Hamiltonicity:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices with
d=O(\log n)
. Then whp,
\tau_{2}\geq(1-n^{-1/d})e(G)
.

Proof.

Let
t=(1-\varepsilon)e(G)
where
\varepsilon=n^{-1/d}
.
We claim that whp,
G_{t}
has a vertex of degree
1
. This implies that whp
\tau_{2}\geq t
, as desired.
Let
X_{v}
be the indicator random variable for
\deg_{G_{t}}(v)=1
and
X=\sum_{v\in V(G)}X_{v}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]=n\cdot\frac{d\binom{nd/2-d}{t-1}}{\binom{nd/2}{t}}

\displaystyle=nd\cdot\frac{(nd/2-d)!}{(nd/2-d-t+1)!(t-1)!}\frac{(nd/2-t)!t!}{(nd/2)!}

\displaystyle=nd\cdot\frac{t\cdot(nd/2-d-t+2)\cdots(nd/2-t)}{(nd/2-d+1)\cdots(nd/2)}\geq(1+o(1))(1-\varepsilon)nd\varepsilon^{d-1}=\omega(1)\,,

Indeed, if
d=O(1)
, then
\mathbb{E}[X]\geq n^{1/d}=\omega(1)
. If
d=\omega(1)
, then
\mathbb{E}[X]\geq d=\omega(1)
.
Moreover, note that
\operatorname{Var}[X]=\sum_{(u,v)\in V(G)^{2}:\{u,v\}\in E(G)}(\mathbb{E}[X_{u}X_{v}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{u}]\mathbb{E}[X_{v}])
as
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
are independent when
\{u,v\}\notin E(G)
. For each
(u,v)
pair with
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{u}X_{v}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{nd/2-2d+1}{t-1}+(d-1)^{2}\binom{nd/2-2d+1}{t-2}}{\binom{nd/2}{t}}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))(\varepsilon^{2d-2}+d^{2}\varepsilon^{2d-1}).

Thus, the variance can be bounded as

\operatorname{Var}[X]\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in V(G)^{2}:\\
\{u,v\}\in E(G)\end{subarray}}(1+o(1))(\varepsilon^{2d-2}+d^{2}\varepsilon^{2d-1})=nd\varepsilon^{2d-2}+nd^{3}\varepsilon^{2d-1}=o(n^{2}d^{2}\varepsilon^{2d-2})=o(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})\,.

Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality, we conclude whp
G_{t}
has a vertex of degree
1
.
∎

We now prove
Lemma

3.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Let
m=e(G_{\tau})
. By
Proposition

3.2
, we may assume that
m=(1-\varepsilon)nd/2
and
0\leq\varepsilon<n^{-1/d}
.
We take a sufficiently large
C
so that
C>10^{100}
holds.
For
(P1)
, we first note that as
d\leq 10\log n
, we have

\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}\leq(10^{6}e)^{d/10^{6}}=e^{\log(10^{6}e)/10^{6}\cdot d}\leq n^{10\log(10^{6}e)/10^{5}}\leq n^{2/10^{3}}\,.

Thus, the expectation of
|\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})|
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})]

\displaystyle=n\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{d/10^{6}}\binom{d}{i}\frac{\binom{nd/2-d}{m-i}}{\binom{nd/2}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\frac{d}{10^{6}}\cdot\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}\frac{\binom{nd/2-d}{m-d/10^{6}}}{\binom{nd/2}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\frac{d}{10^{6}}\cdot n^{2/10^{3}}\cdot\frac{(nd/2-d)!}{(nd/2-d-m+d/10^{6})!(m-d/10^{6})!}\cdot\frac{(nd/2-m)!m!}{(nd/2)!}

\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))n\cdot\frac{d}{10^{6}}\cdot n^{2/10^{3}}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{d/10^{6}}\cdot\varepsilon^{d-d/10^{6}}\leq n^{3/10^{3}}

By Markov’s inequality,
\mathbb{P}(|\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})|\geq n^{0.11})\leq n^{3/10^{3}-0.11}=o(1)
, which proves
(P1)
.

For
(P2)
, note that the probability there exists a vertex
u\in\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
for which there is another vertex
v\in\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
and
d_{G_{\tau}}(u,v)\leq 4
is at most

n\sum_{i=1}^{4}d^{i}\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}^{2}\cdot\frac{\binom{nd/2-2d+1}{m-2\cdot d/10^{6}}}{\binom{nd/2}{m}}\leq(4+o(1))nd^{4}\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}^{2}\cdot\frac{m^{2d/10^{6}}(nd/2-m)^{2d-2d/10^{6}}}{(nd/2)^{2d-1}}\\
<10d^{3}\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}^{2}(1-\varepsilon)^{2d/10^{6}}n^{-1+2/10^{6}}=o(1)\,.

by using
\binom{d}{d/10^{6}}^{2}\leq n^{4/10^{3}}\,
.

For
(P3)
, it suffices to prove for
|U|=|V|=n/(4C_{1}^{3})
.
By
Lemma

2.2
, we know that

e_{G}(U,V)\geq\frac{d}{n}|U||V|-\lambda\sqrt{|U||V|}\geq 0.99\cdot\frac{d}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{4C_{1}^{3}}}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{nd}{20C_{1}^{6}}\,,

where we used the fact that
C_{1}=C^{1/10}
,
C>10^{10}
and
\lambda/d\leq 1/C
.
If
nd/(20C_{1}^{6})>\varepsilon\cdot nd/2
, then
(P3)
holds trivially since
G_{\tau}
has
m=(1-\varepsilon)nd/2
edges.
Otherwise, the probability
e_{G_{\tau}}(U,V)=0
is at most

\frac{\binom{nd/2-nd/(20C_{1}^{6})}{m}}{\binom{nd/2}{m}}=\frac{(nd/2-m)\cdots(nd/2-m-nd/(20C_{1}^{6})+1)}{(nd/2)\cdots(nd/2-nd/(20C_{1}^{6})+1)}\leq(1+o(1))\varepsilon^{nd/(20C_{1}^{6})}\leq n^{-n/(20C_{1}^{6})}\,.

Applying union bound over at most
2^{2n}
choices of
(U,V)
, we conclude whp there are no
U,V
satisfying
(P3)
, as stated.

For
(P4)
, suppose for contradiction there exists a set
W\subseteq V(G)\setminus\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
such that
|N_{G_{\tau}}(W)|<C_{1}|W|
. We divide the proof into two cases based on
|W|
.
Let
W^{+}\coloneqq W\cup N_{G_{\tau}}(W)
.

If
|W|\leq n/(4C_{1}^{3})
, then by
Lemma

2.2
and
|W^{+}|\leq(C_{1}+1)|W|
, we conclude that

e_{G}(W^{+})\leq\frac{d}{2n}|W^{+}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}|W^{+}|=d|W^{+}|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|W^{+}|}{2n}+\frac{\lambda}{2d}}}\right)\leq d|W|/C_{1}\,.

Indeed, note that
|W^{+}|/2n\leq(C_{1}+1)/(4C_{1}^{3})\leq 1/(3C_{1}^{2})
and
\lambda/(2d)\leq 1/(2C)\leq 1/(3C_{1}^{2})
since
C_{1}=C^{1/10}
and
C\geq 10^{100}
.
However, because all edges incident to
v\in W
in
G_{\tau}
belong to
e_{G_{\tau}}(W\cup N_{G_{\tau}}(W))
, we conclude

e_{G_{\tau}}(W\cup N_{G_{\tau}}(W))\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in W}\deg_{G_{\tau}}(v)\geq\frac{d|W|}{2\cdot 10^{6}}\,.

Since
G_{\tau}
is a subgraph of
G
, it contradicts the assumption that
C_{1}=C^{1/10}>10^{10}
.

If
n/(4C_{1}^{3})\leq|W|\leq n/(2C_{1})
, then by
(P3)
, we know that
|N_{G_{\tau}}(W)|\geq n-|W|-n/(4C^{3})
. Indeed, suppose not, then there exists a set
U
of size at least
n/(4C^{3})
disjoint from
W
and
e_{G_{\tau}}(U,W)=0
, which contradicts
(P3)
. Thus,

|N_{G_{\tau}}(W)|\geq n-n/(2C_{1})-n/(4C_{1}^{3})\geq n/2\geq C_{1}|W|\,,

as desired.
∎

3.2.
Dense case

For the sparse case, the computations were done with respect to a uniformly random chosen
m
-edge subgraph of
G
.
For the dense case, it is more convenient to do the computations with respect to a
p
-subgraph, where each edge of
G
is included with probability
p
independently. We will utilize the following result:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular
n
-vertex graph, then the following holds:

(A1)

If
p\geq 0
is such that
n((1-p)^{d}+dp(1-p)^{d-1})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then
G_{p}
contains no vertex with degree at most
1
whp.

(A2)

If
p\geq 0
is such that
n((1-p)^{d}+dp(1-p)^{d-1})\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then
G_{p}
contains a vertex with degree at most
1
whp.

Proof.

Let
X_{v}
be the indicator random variable for
\deg_{G_{p}}(v)\leq 1
and
X=\sum_{v\in V(G)}X_{v}
. We have

\mathbb{E}[X]=n((1-p)^{d}+dp(1-p)^{d-1})
.
Hence,
(A1)
holds by Markov’s inequality.

For
(A2)
, note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[X]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{(u,v)\in\binom{V(G)}{2}}(\mathbb{E}[X_{u}X_{v}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{u}]\mathbb{E}[X_{v}])=2\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}(\mathbb{E}[X_{u}X_{v}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{u}]\mathbb{E}[X_{v}])

\displaystyle=nd((1-p)^{2d-1}+(2d-1)p(1-p)^{2d-2}+(d-1)^{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{2d-3})

\displaystyle-nd((1-p)^{d}+dp(1-p)^{d-1})^{2}

\displaystyle=nd(d-1)^{2}p^{3}(1-p)^{2d-3}\,.

Our goal now is to show that
\operatorname{Var}[X]=o(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})
and then apply Chebyshev’s inequality to conclude.
There are two cases: if the first term in
\mathbb{E}[X]
dominates, which means
dp\leq 1-p
, then it suffices to show
nd(d-1)^{2}p^{3}(1-p)^{2d-3}=o(n^{2}(1-p)^{2d})
, which simplifies to
n(1-p)^{3}/(dp)^{3}=\omega(1)
. This is clearly true given that
dp\leq 1-p
. Otherwise, we have
dp\geq 1-p
and we need to show that
nd(d-1)^{2}p^{3}(1-p)^{2d-3}=o(n^{2}d^{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{2d-2})
, which simplifies to
n(1-p)/(dp)=\omega(1)
. By the assumption of
(A2)
and
dp\geq 1-p
, we know that
ndp(1-p)^{d-1}=\omega(1)
.
Observe that
d^{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{d-2}=O(1)
for all choices of
d=d(n)
and
p=p(n)
for which
d\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
. This is immediate when
d=O(1)
, so we assume
d=\omega(1)
. By taking the derivative, we know the maximizer
p^{*}=2/d
and thus the full expression is at most
4/e^{2}
, as stated.
Putting everything together, we have
n=\omega(1/(dp(1-p)^{d-1}))=\omega(dp/(1-p))
, as desired.
∎

Next, we establish a bound on the hitting time for minimum degree
2
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular
n
-vertex graph. If
d\geq 10\log n
, then whp, we have
p_{1}\cdot e(G)\leq\tau_{2}(G)\leq p_{2}\cdot e(G)
where
p_{1}\coloneqq 1-e^{-\log n/d}
and
p_{2}\coloneqq(1+10^{-10^{10}})(1-e^{-\log n/d})
.

Proof.

To show the left side of the statement, using
d>10\log n
and the second estimate from
Section

2.5
, we have

n(1-p_{1})^{d}+n\cdot dp_{1}(1-p_{1})^{d-1}\geq nd\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log n}{d}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log^{2}n}{d^{2}}}}\right)e^{-\log n}\geq\frac{1}{2}\log n\,.

By
Proposition

3.3

(A2)
, we conclude whp,
G_{p_{1}}
contains a vertex of degree at most
1
, and so
p_{1}\cdot e(G)\leq\tau_{2}(G)
.

For the right side, if
d=\Theta(\log n)
, let
d=c\log n
with
c>10
. Then we can write
p_{2}=1-e^{-1/c-\varepsilon}
, where
\varepsilon
is a constant depending on
c
. Note that
p_{2}\leq 1/2
when
c\geq 10
. Then we see that

\displaystyle n(1-p_{2})^{d}+dp_{2}(1-p_{2})^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq n(1+d)(1-p_{2})^{d}

\displaystyle=n(1+d)\cdot\exp(d/c-\varepsilon d)

\displaystyle=(1+c\log n)\exp(-\varepsilon c\log n)=o(1).

If
d=\omega(\log n)
, let
\varepsilon=10^{-10^{10}}>0
, we see that

\displaystyle n((1-p_{2})^{d}+dp_{2}(1-p_{2})^{d-1})

\displaystyle\leq n(1+d\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{d})(1-\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{d}+\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log^{2}n}{2d^{2}})^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq n(1+2\log n)(\exp(-(1+\varepsilon-o(1))\log n))

\displaystyle=(1+2\log n)n^{-(\varepsilon-o(1))}=o(1)

The first inequality holds as

1-\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{d}\leq 1-p_{2}\leq 1-\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{d}+\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log^{2}n}{2d^{2}}.

The second inequality holds since
1-x\leq\exp(-x)
. By
Proposition

3.3

(A1)
,
G_{p_{2}}
contains no vertex with degree at most
1
whp and so
\tau_{2}(G)\leq p_{2}e(G)
.
∎

We note that if
d=\omega(\log n)
, then
1-e^{-\log n/d}=(1+o(1))\log n/d
. However, this approximation does not work when
d=c\log n
for some small constant
c>0
.
We provide a general bound that holds for all
d\geq 10\log n
in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
d\geq 10\log n
. Then
(0.95)\log n/d\leq(1-e^{-\log n/d})\leq\log n/d
.

Proof.

As
1-x\leq e^{-x}\leq 1-x+x^{2}/2
for all
x\geq 0
, we have
\log n/d-\frac{1}{2}(\log n/d)^{2}\leq 1-e^{-\log n/d}\leq\log n/d
.
Since
d\geq 10\log n
, we have
\frac{1}{2}(\log n/d)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{20}\log n/d
. Thus, we conclude
(1-e^{-\log n/d})\geq(1-1/20)\log n/d=0.95\log n/d
, as desired.
∎

Now, we prove that
G_{p_{2}}
is “locally sparse”.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
C\geq 10^{15}
and
p\coloneqq(1+10^{-10^{10}})(1-e^{-\log n/d})
.
Also, let
G
be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d\geq 10\log n
and
d/\lambda\geq C
and
G_{p}
be its
p
-random subgraph. Then whp, for any set
S\subseteq V(G)
of size
s\leq n/(10C)
,
e_{G_{p}}(S)\leq s\log n/D
for
D=\log C/10
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.2
, we have
e_{G}(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}(ds^{2}/n+\lambda s)\leq ds/C
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists S\subseteq V(G):s\leq n/(10C),\,e_{G_{p_{2}}}(S)\geq s\log n/D)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{s=1}^{n/(10C)}\binom{n}{s}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Bin}(e_{G}(S),p)\geq s\log n/D}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{s=1}^{n/(10C)}\binom{n}{s}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(ds/C,p)\geq s\log n/D)\,.

Using
Proposition

2.3
,
C\geq 10^{15}
and
D=\log C/10
,

\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(ds/C,p)\geq s\log n/D)\leq\binom{ds/C}{s\log n/D}p^{s\log n/D}\leq(1.1eD/C)^{s\log n/D}\leq\exp(-2s\log n)\,.

Plugging this back in, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists S\subseteq V(G):s\leq n/(10C),\,e_{G_{p_{2}}}(S)\geq s\log n/D)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{n/(10C)}(en/s)^{s}\exp(-2s\log n)=o(1)\,,

as desired.
∎

In the remaining section, we prove the following result:

Lemma 3.7
.

There exists a constant
C
such that the following statement holds.
Let
G
be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d>10\log n
and
d/\lambda\geq C
.
Then the subgraph at the hitting time of minimum degree 2, denoted as
G_{\tau}
, satisfies the following properties whp.
Let
\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})\coloneqq\{v\in V(G):\deg_{G_{\tau}}(v)\leq\log n/10^{5}\}
and
C_{1}=\log^{1/2}C/10^{10}
. Then,

(Q1)

\Delta(G_{\tau_{2}})\leq 10\log n
;

(Q2)

|\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})|\leq n^{0.11}
;

(Q3)

\forall~u,v\in\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
, we have
d_{G_{\tau}}(u,v)>4
;

(Q4)

\forall~U,V\subseteq V(G)
disjoint and with
|U|,|V|\geq n/(4C_{1}^{3})
, we have
e_{G_{\tau}}(U,V)\geq 1
;

(Q5)

\forall~W\subseteq V(G)\setminus\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{\tau})
with
|W|\leq n/(2C_{1})
, we have
|N_{G_{\tau}}(W)|\geq C_{1}|W|
.

Proof.

Let
C>(10e)^{10^{50}}
. Note that
C_{1}=\log^{1/2}C/10^{10}\geq 10^{10}
. Let
N\coloneqq e(G)=nd/2
. Let
p_{1}=1-e^{-\log n/d}
,
p_{2}=(1+10^{-10^{10}})(1-e^{-\log n/d})
and
m_{1}=Np_{1}
,
m_{2}=Np_{2}
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, we write
G_{p_{i}}
for the random
p_{i}
-subgraph of
G
, obtained by including each
e\in E(G)
independently with probability
p_{i}
. Similarly, we write
G_{m_{i}}
to be the uniformly random
m_{i}
-edged subgraph of
G
.

From
Proposition

3.4
, we know whp
m_{1}\leq\tau\leq m_{2}
. Note that the natural coupling between the probability measure of
G_{m_{1}}
,
G_{\tau}
and
G_{m_{2}}
defined by adding edge one at a time that satisfies
G_{m_{1}}\subseteq G_{\tau}\subseteq G_{m_{2}}
with probability
1
. Therefore, for any increasing property
\mathcal{E}^{+}
, we know
\mathbb{P}(G_{m_{2}}\in\mathcal{E}^{+})\geq\mathbb{P}(G_{\tau}\in\mathcal{E}^{+})\geq\mathbb{P}(G_{m_{1}}\in\mathcal{E}^{+})
.
In addition, by using
Lemma

2.1
, we can freely switch the role between
G_{m_{i}}
and
G_{p_{i}}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
We will frequently make use of these relations to do the computations in
G_{p_{i}}
instead of
G_{\tau}
.

For
(Q1)
, it suffices to show
\Delta(G_{p_{2}})\leq 10\log n
whp. For any given vertex
v\in V(G)
, by
Proposition

2.3
, we know
\mathbb{P}(\deg_{G_{p_{2}}}(v)\geq 10\log n)=o(n^{-2})
. Applying the union bound over all vertices proves
(Q1)
.

For
(Q2)
, it suffices to show that
|\text{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})|\leq n^{0.11}
whp. Let
d_{0}=\log n/10^{5}
.
Observe that since
p_{1}\leq\log n/d
, the expectation of
|\text{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})|
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\text{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})|]

\displaystyle=n\cdot\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(d,p_{1})\leq d_{0})\leq n\cdot d_{0}\cdot\binom{d}{d_{0}}p_{1}^{d_{0}}(1-p_{1})^{d-d_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot d_{0}\cdot(edp_{1}/d_{0})^{d_{0}}\cdot\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\log n}{d}(d-d_{0})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{0}\cdot n^{10^{-5}\log(10^{5}e)+d_{0}/d}=o(n^{0.09})\,,

where for the second inequality we used the fact that
1-p_{1}=e^{-\log n/d}
.
Thus, by Markov’s inequality, we conclude whp
|\text{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})|\leq n^{0.11}
.

For
(Q3)
, it suffices to show that whp
in
G_{p_{2}}
, there is no path of length at most
4
between any two vertices of
\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})
.
The proof consists of two steps.
First, let
S
be vertices of degree at most
\log n/10^{4}
in
G_{p_{2}}
(c.f. for
\mathrm{SMALL}
the threshold was
\log n/10^{5}
). We show that whp
\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})\subseteq S
. Then, we show that whp there is no path of length
4
between any two vertices in
S
, which implies (the strengthened)
(Q3)
.

Claim 3.8
.

Let
S\coloneqq\{v\in V(G):\deg_{G_{p_{2}}}(v)\leq\log n/10^{4}\}
.
Then,
\mathrm{SMALL}(G_{p_{1}})\subseteq S
whp.

7.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we studied the hitting time for Hamiltonicity in
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs and proved the optimal results. We finish the papers with a few remarks and open problems.

First, we note it is straightforward to generalise
Theorem

1.5
to work for minimum degree
2k+1
and
k
Hamilton cycles plus a perfect matching, all edge disjoint, with the same parameters. Indeed, recall the strategy for
Theorem

1.5
was to remove Hamilton cycles one by one and check at each time the resulting graph is still a
C
-expander. Removing a perfect matching instead of a Hamilton cycle in the first round does not affect the rest of the proof.

Second, our results fundamentally rely on the property that a
C
-expander is robustly Hamiltonian. A natural question arises: can the hitting time result be extended to general
C
-expanders? The following example demonstrates that the answer is negative.

Proposition 7.1
.

There exists an
\Omega((n/\log n)^{1/2})
-expander
G
on
n
vertices such that whp,
G_{\tau_{2}}
is not Hamiltonian.

Proof Sketch.

Let
c>0
be a sufficiently small constant, determined later.
Let
G_{1}
be the complete bipartite graph
K_{n/3,2n/3}
with parts
A
and
B
.
Let
G_{2}
be the
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph on
A\cup B
with
d=cn/\log n
and
\lambda\leq 3\sqrt{d}
.
Note that such a graph exists for sufficiently large
n
by considering a random
d
-regular graph
[
19
]
.
Let
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
.
Then as
G_{2}
is an
\Omega(d/\lambda)=\Omega((n/\log n)^{1/2})
-expander,
G
is also an
\Omega((n/\log n)^{1/2})
-expander.
We now observe that as all vertices have degree at least
n/3
, the hitting time
\tau_{2}=\Theta(n\log n)
whp.
Thus, by Chernoff’s bound, whp
G_{\tau_{2}}[A]
and
G_{\tau_{2}}[B]
contains at most
2e(G_{2})\cdot(\tau_{2}/e(G))\leq n/4

edges by choosing

sufficiently small.
On the other hand, as
G_{1}
is a complete bipartite graph, every Hamilton cycle of

must contain at least
n/3
edges in
G_{\tau_{2}}[B]
. Therefore, whp
G_{\tau_{2}}
is not Hamiltonian.
∎

It is natural to ask what are sufficient conditions for a
C
-expander to have the hitting time property. For instance, as our construction is far from being regular, we would like to ask whether imposing the graph to be regular suffices.

Question 7.2
.

Does there exists a
d
-regular graph
C
-expander
G
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
but does not satisfy
G_{\tau_{2}}
is Hamiltonian whp?

Third, we observe two distinct regimes governing the structural properties of
G_{\tau}
, which fundamentally alter the complexity of packing edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles.

(1)

The Sparse Regime (
k\ll\min\{\log n,d\}
):
In this regime, the degree distribution of vertices in
G_{\tau}
converges asymptotically to a Poisson distribution. Consequently,
G_{\tau}
is structurally highly irregular: while the minimum degree
\delta(G_{\tau})
is fixed at
2k
, the average degree is significantly higher.
When employing the edge deletion method to establish the existence of
k
edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles, this irregularity is advantageous. The removal of edges required for the first few cycles has a negligible impact on the global connectivity; the vast majority of vertices retain a degree much larger than
\delta(G_{\tau})
. Thus, the remaining graph preserves strong expansion properties on the set of large-degree vertices, providing the ‘structural slack’ necessary to close subsequent cycles.

(2)

The Dense Regime (
k\gg\log n
):
Conversely, when
k
is large, the degree distribution of
G_{\tau}
is asymptotic to the normal distribution due to the concentration of measure. In this setting,
G_{\tau}
becomes almost regular, with
\delta(G_{\tau})\approx\Delta(G_{\tau})\approx\mathbb{E}[d]
.
This transition fundamentally changes the nature of the problem. Proving the hitting time for
\lfloor\delta(G_{\tau})/2\rfloor
edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles shifts from an expansion problem to a tight packing problem. Since the minimum degree is close to the average degree, a maximal packing of cycles requires utilizing nearly every edge in the graph. Unlike the sparse case, there is no ‘high-degree core’ to buffer the edge deletion; the loss of edges degrades the expansion properties uniformly across the graph. This lack of redundancy makes the dense regime significantly more analytically challenging than the sparse case.

Thus, a natural question to ask, which necessarily requires a different approach, is the following:

Question 7.3
.

Given an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d=\omega(\log n)
and
d/\lambda\geq C
for some sufficiently large constant
C>0
, is it true that
\tau_{2k}=\tau_{k\mathrm{HC}}
for all
k\in[\lfloor d/2\rfloor]
whp?

We would like to remark that when
d
is even and
k=d/2
, the question is equivalent to asking for a Hamiltonian decomposition of an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph and the approximate version of this problem was recently solved by Draganić, Kim, Lee, Munhá Correia, Pavez-Signé and Sudakov
[
15
]
.
