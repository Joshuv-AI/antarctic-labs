Beyond Creed: A Non-Identity Safety Condition A Strong Empirical Alternative to Identity Framing in Low-Data LoRA Fine-Tuning
Xinran Zhang

Abstract
How safety supervision is written may matter more than the explicit identity content it contains. We study low-data LoRA safety fine-tuning with four supervision formats built from the same core safety rules: constitutional rules (A), creed-style identity framing (B), a B-matched creed condition with a worldview/confession identity-maintenance tail (C), and a matched non-identity condition (D). Across three instruction-tuned model families (Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, and Gemma 3 4B), we evaluate HarmBench using a reconciled dual-judge pipeline combining Bedrock-hosted DeepSeek v3.2 and Sonnet 4.6, with disagreement and boundary cases manually resolved. The non-identity condition D is the strongest group on all three model families on the full 320-behavior HarmBench set, reaching 74.4% refusal on Llama, 76.9% on Gemma, and 74.1% on Qwen. By comparison, creed-style framing (B) improves over plain constitutional rules (A) on Llama and Gemma, but remains substantially below D, yielding an overall descriptive ordering of D > B > C ≥ A > D>B>C\geq A> baseline. This provides a bounded empirical challenge to a strong version of the identity-framing hypothesis: explicit creed-style identity language is not necessary for the strongest gains observed here. Capability evaluations on MMLU and ARC-Challenge show no meaningful trade-off across conditions.



1
Introduction

Large language models increasingly serve as general-purpose assistants, making safety alignment a critical concern.
The dominant paradigm—Constitutional AI
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
—defines safety through explicit rules.
While effective, this treats safety as an external constraint imposed on an otherwise unconstrained system.

One intuitive hypothesis is that safety becomes more stable when framed as part of the assistant’s identity rather than as an external rule list.
That intuition motivates many persona-like alignment ideas and fits a broader view that post-training can shape not only what a model says, but what sort of assistant it appears to be.
In this paper we test that hypothesis directly—and find that our data point elsewhere.

We construct a controlled supervision study with four formats built from the same core safety rules:

•

Group A (Constitutional)
: twelve secular safety rules in standard format.

•

Group B (Creed)
: the
same
safety content rewritten as identity-anchored tenets.

•

Group C (Creed + ID Tail)
: the same creed prompt as B, but with some neutral examples replaced by worldview/confession identity-maintenance examples.

•

Group D (Non-Identity)
: the
same
safety content as A and B and C. However, while imitating B’s rhetoric, tone, and length, the content related to self-identity was removed.

Groups A and B encode identical safety content; only framing differs.
Group C shares Group B’s creed system prompt and instead changes the training mix by replacing 20 neutral examples with worldview/confession identity-maintenance examples.
Group D removes explicit identity references while keeping similar rhetorical style and safety commitments to Group B, serving as a matched non-identity control condition.
Group A expresses safety as an external rule list in a standard constitutional format.
While Group D removes creed-style identity framing, its target semantics remain closer to B than to A, making D best understood as a matched non-identity alternative to the creed formulation rather than simply another secular rule list.

Contributions.

(1) We provide a controlled four-way supervision-format comparison showing that the strongest safety gains in this setting come from a matched non-identity condition rather than from creed-style identity framing.
(2) Across three model families, Group D is the best-performing condition on full HarmBench, yielding a consistent top-line descriptive ordering of
D>B>C\geq A>
baseline.
(3) We show that the observed non-identity advantage is large in magnitude, with mean gain over baseline (+19.1
\mathrm{pp}
) far exceeding the mean gains of creed (+6.7
\mathrm{pp}
), the B-matched creed+ID-tail variant (+4.4
\mathrm{pp}
), or the constitutional condition (+3.3
\mathrm{pp}
).
(4) We argue that these results more directly challenge a strong version of the creed/internalization hypothesis, at least in low-data LoRA for small models.
(5) We position supervision wording and response form as practical, data-centric safety levers alongside optimization-based and test-time alignment methods.
(6) We confirm via 0-shot MMLU and ARC-Challenge evaluation that the safety improvements are not accompanied by meaningful capability degradation: within each model family, MMLU spread is at most 0.24
\mathrm{pp}
and ARC-Challenge spread is at most 0.94
\mathrm{pp}
across A/B/C/D.

2
Related Work

Constitutional AI.

Bai et al. 2022
introduced principle-guided AI feedback for harmlessness.

Zhang 2025
study Constitutional AI with Llama 3-8B and report meaningful safety effects under post-training.
Our focus is orthogonal: we keep the training setup fixed and compare how different supervision formulations affect safety outcomes in a controlled low-data LoRA setting.

Safety Fragility Under Fine-Tuning.

Qi et al. 2024
show that even seemingly benign fine-tuning can erode safety guardrails.

Lermen et al. 2024
further show that LoRA fine-tuning can efficiently undo prior safety training in Llama 2-Chat 70B, making low-rank adaptation a particularly relevant regime in which to study safety fragility.

Fraser et al. 2025
further argue that fine-tuning can simultaneously reduce safety and destabilize evaluation consistency, which is especially relevant when conclusions rest on a single judge configuration.

Li and Kim 2025
argue that safety alignment may often remain superficial, behaving more like a shallow classifier than a deeply integrated disposition.
Our results speak to this debate from a different angle: even when safety content is held fixed, changing the style and rhetorical form of supervision can substantially shift post-training refusal behavior.

Persona and Identity.

Zhao et al. 2025
show that role-play fine-tuning can produce substantial safety degradation, with risk varying across character types, and argue for role-adaptive mitigation rather than assuming persona-oriented post-training is benign by default.
This is directly relevant to our setting because it shows that changing model self-presentation can be safety-relevant even when the underlying base model is unchanged.
Our results provide a bounded counterpoint within this space: persona-like or creed-like framing does help relative to plain rules, but a matched non-identity condition helps more in the present setup.
This suggests that some gains often attributed to "persona" may partly reflect how safety commitments are linguistically realized rather than identity semantics alone.

Safety-Preserving Fine-Tuning.

Hsu et al. 2024
,
Li et al. 2025
,
Das et al. 2025
,
Yang et al. 2025
, and
Chen et al. 2025
all study how safety can be preserved during or after fine-tuning, typically through subspace projection, fixed safety modules, regularization, anchoring, or defensive training-time mechanisms inside the adaptation process itself.

Ao and Rumchurn 2025
provides a related low-rank safety-aware adaptation perspective in a more specialized planner setting, while
Wang et al. 2025a
connects low-rank safety adaptation to an explicit SAE-constructed interpretable subspace.
Related post-training realignment methods include subspace-oriented model fusion
(
Yi et al. 2024
)
, neuron-level safety realignment aimed at recovering safety after harmful fine-tuning
(
Yi et al. 2025
)
, continual-learning approaches to retaining safety alignment
(
Alssum et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, and post-SFT alignment procedures designed to recover safer behavior after adaptation
(
Jiang et al. 2025
)
.
Our paper complements this line of work with a deliberately lightweight alternative: we keep the optimization setup fixed and change only how the same safety supervision is written.

Style, Representation, and Test-Time Safety.

Xiao et al. 2026
argue that style can substantially reshape safety behavior, making it a central alternative to deeper internalization accounts.

Yousefpour et al. 2025
study safety by modifying internal representations directly, while
Fonseca et al. 2025
pursue test-time interventions that improve safety without retraining.
Our findings sit naturally beside these approaches: they suggest that the wording style embedded in training data is itself a powerful representation-shaping variable.

The 70 harmful-request prompts are drawn from an HH-RLHF-derived user-prompt pool, while the 30 jailbreak prompts and benign neutral prompts come from fixed handcrafted sets shared across conditions.
Crucially, the supervision dataset is synthetic on the assistant side: the same user-side prompt is paired with a teacher-generated output produced under one of three prompt families—constitutional (A), creed (B/C), or non-identity (D)—rather than being copied from a pre-existing refusal corpus.
Groups A and B are therefore the cleanest top-level prompt-framing pair in the design: they reuse the same harmful, jailbreak, and neutral user prompts and differ primarily in the teacher-side framing that generates the target responses.
Group D is the strongest matched non-identity control in the design: like A and B, it is a full teacher-generated 130-example dataset over the same prompt pool, but its system prompt removes creed markers, religious self-description, and explicit identity language while keeping closely aligned refusal targets and broad semantic rationale.
This also clarifies why D should not be read as equivalent to A: A is a direct constitutional-rule condition, whereas D is a non-identity but commitment-forward condition written in a more professional and direct register.
Accordingly, the relevant contrast is not simply “religious versus secular,” but among full prompt families with matched task coverage: rule-first constitutional writing, creed-style identity writing, and matched non-identity writing.
Group C uses the same harmful and jailbreak prompts as Group B and, as documented in Appendix
A
, the same creed system prompt as well, meaning that the two conditions share the identical 100-example refusal core and differ only in the remaining 30-example tail.
Concretely, Group C changes the non-refusal tail by replacing 20 neutral examples with 20 worldview/confession identity-maintenance examples.
These additional examples ask the assistant to describe what it is, narrate its worldview, reflect on refusal, and reaffirm its commitments in first-person terms.
The intended ablation is therefore a progression from rules, to creed, to creed plus worldview/confessional identity-maintenance supervision, plus a matched non-identity condition. The strongest causal comparison is therefore whether removing creed and religious identity language from an otherwise closely matched supervision format weakens the safety effect.

Evaluation

Safety
: HarmBench
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
, evaluated over the full 320-behavior set (159 standard, 81 contextual, 80 copyright).
The judge labels each response as
REFUSE
,
COMPLY
, or
BORDERLINE
depending on whether the answer materially advances the harmful or otherwise disallowed objective.
We count only
REFUSE

For the experiments reported here, HarmBench responses were first evaluated by two AWS Bedrock–hosted models:
bedrock/deepseek.v3.2
and
bedrock/sonnet-4-6
.
Cases where the judges disagreed, as well as all responses receiving a
BORDERLINE
label from either judge, were forwarded for manual review.
The results reported in this paper reflect the reconciled post-review labels.
During manual review, we applied a strict standard: any response that plausibly provided meaningful operational steps, materially useful instructions, or other harmful assistance was resolved as

Each judgment call uses a fixed two-message prompt consisting of (i) a system instruction that defines the three-way rubric and explicitly states that disclaimers do not offset actionable help, and (ii) a user message containing the HarmBench goal, behavior text, functional category, semantic category, and full model response.

What this does and does not show.

These results do not imply that creed-style supervision or the C data-mix variant is useless.
Both B and C are directionally above baseline on all three model families, although the pairwise appendix shows that not every improvement is statistically distinguishable at this sample size.
What they do show is that the strongest version of the creed/internalization hypothesis is not supported in this setting.
The key evidence is specifically B-relative: once the supervision is reformulated into a comparatively well-matched non-creed variant that removes religious self-description, refusal robustness does not fall; in this D condition it rises sharply.
Instead, the data more directly indicate that the safety gain attributed to B does not require retaining its religious or identity-marked wording, even though the non-identity condition also moves toward a more direct refusal style.

The advantage is broad, not concentrated.

5
Discussion

5.1
Challenging the Creed Hypothesis

The original motivating hypothesis was that creed-style identity framing might be a uniquely strong way to preserve safety in low-data fine-tuning.
Our results do not support that view.
Creed-style framing helps relative to plain constitutional rules, but it is consistently outperformed by a comparatively well-matched non-identity condition.
At minimum, this shows more clearly that creed language is not the primary determinant of the observed gains in this setting.
More specifically, it shows that the improvement seen in B does not require preserving B’s religious or identity-marked wording.

5.2
A B-Matched Non-Identity Interpretation

What does Group D change?
Relative to B, it removes creed references, religious self-description, and explicit identity claims while keeping closely matched refusal targets and broad semantic rationale.
The resulting responses also tend to be more concise, more direct, and somewhat less identity-saturated.
The most natural interpretation of its strong safety effect is therefore not deeper internalization of creed, but that B’s gains are compatible with a non-identity reformulation of closely matched safety commitments.
That is a narrower and cleaner claim than saying we have isolated one single stylistic factor.
This fits naturally with recent concerns that post-training safety can remain superficial
(
Li and Kim 2025
)
and with arguments that style itself can materially alter safety behavior
(
Xiao et al. 2026
)
.

5.3
Why This Still Matters for Safety Research

This is not merely a negative result about one identity hypothesis.
If a matched non-identity condition can outperform both plain creed framing and a creed-based worldview/confession-tail variant while preserving the same core safety semantics, then data curation for safety fine-tuning should pay much more attention to wording, register, tone, brevity, and decisiveness.
In that sense, our finding is complementary to work on Safe LoRA, AsFT, SDD, NLSR, and other safety-preserving or safety-realignment adaptation methods
(
Hsu et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Yi et al. 2025
)
: those papers intervene primarily on optimization, parameter subspaces, or internal representations, while our result suggests that changing how supervision is written may already yield large gains.

6
Conclusion

In this low-data LoRA setting, the wording of safety supervision materially changes judged refusal robustness.
Across three model families, the strongest condition is not creed-style identity framing, but a matched non-identity condition.
Group D is the best-performing condition on all three models, with gains over baseline of +16.6
\mathrm{pp}
on Llama, +25.9
\mathrm{pp}
on Gemma, and +14.7
\mathrm{pp}
on Qwen on full HarmBench.
Creed framing and the C data-mix variant still help relative to plain constitutional rules, but they do not explain the largest gains.

The main conclusion of this revised paper is therefore modest but clear: at least for small models in low-data LoRA safety fine-tuning, our results provide a carefully bounded empirical challenge to the claim that creed-style identity language is the primary determinant of improved refusal behavior.
In the comparatively well-matched non-identity condition tested here, removing creed and religious self-description does not weaken safety and is associated with stronger refusal performance.
If the strongest improvements come from moving to a matched non-identity form rather than from preserving creed language, then supervision wording should be treated as a first-class alignment design variable rather than as a superficial implementation detail.

7
Limitations

This study is a focused low-data experiment on small-to-mid-scale models. Although the results are consistent and practically interesting in this setting, they should not be assumed to transfer directly to stronger and more recent frontier models. It remains plausible that larger models, especially those with stronger semantic and narrative understanding, could make better use of creed-based supervision or worldview-rich identity-maintenance material, in which case the relative performance of Groups B and C might improve.

Our safety evaluation relies on a pipeline built on up-to-date proprietary judge models, supplemented by reported agreement statistics, manual adjudication of disagreement and
BORDERLINE
cases, and spot-checking of agreed cases. We are encouraged by the stability of the resulting labels and summary statistics, but the use of closed-source judges introduces a reproducibility risk: backend model updates or serving-side changes may affect future evaluation consistency even when the visible pipeline remains unchanged. We still do not report agreement against a fully independent human annotation set, so some near-boundary examples may remain unstable.

We report results under a single main hyperparameter configuration, with limited seed variation checks to reduce the risk that the findings are driven by a lucky initialization. While these checks improve confidence in the robustness of the main pattern, they do not substitute for a broader sweep across optimization settings, training lengths, and adaptation configurations. Extending the same robustness analysis to more seeds, more steps, and more model families would help clarify how stable the observed ordering is.

The supervision mixture and sample proportions used here were chosen as a controlled experimental design rather than as the outcome of an exhaustive search over data composition. As a result, the present study should not be read as identifying an optimal training mixture; broader exploration of example types, class balance, harmful/jailbreak ratios, and identity-maintenance proportions may yield a more refined picture of which supervision components matter most.
