Combined nocturnal sleep pattern and napping duration in relation to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease risk and prediction in type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Meng Zhu, Liang Yao, Lijuan Yang, Siyu Gao, Penghua Shi, Hong Chen, Tao Hong, Hailing Wang, Yuhua Wang, Xiang Hu, Qidong Zheng, Xuejiang Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the association of sleep modes with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsA total of 1900 patients with T2DM were enrolled between March 2017 and December 2024. Sleep behaviors were collected via questionnaires. Four sleep modes were defined by combining nocturnal sleep factors and nap: good nocturnal sleep pattern with short/long nap (GNSP-SN/LN) and poor nocturnal sleep pattern with short/long nap (PNSP-SN/LN). Cox regression analysis was used to examine the association between sleep and MASLD. Integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) quantified the added value of sleep modes to the Fatty Liver Index (FLI).ResultsOver an average follow-up of 3.23 years, 379 new-onset MASLD events were identified based on ultrasound criteria in this retrospective cohort study. Four nocturnal sleep factors and napping duration were positively related to the MASLD in T2DM patients (HR for adverse sleep behaviors ranged from 1.64 to 2.26). Compared to GNSP-SN, GNSP-LN (HR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.37–2.61), PNSP-SN (HR = 2.54, 95% CI: 1.91–3.37), and PNSP-LN (HR = 3.51, 95% CI: 2.53–4.87) were associated with higher MASLD risk. Napping more than 30 min can increase the MASLD risk (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.30–2.53, in GNSP-LN; HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.01–1.93, in PNSP-LN). Incorporating sleep modes into the FLI improved prediction, with an NRI of 0.21 and an IDI of 0.06.ConclusionsPoor nocturnal sleep was independently associated with a substantially higher risk of MASLD. Prolonged napping might increase the MASLD risk, regardless of the pattern of nocturnal sleep. Combining sleep modes with FLI provides exploratory evidence of improved discrimination for incident MASLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02206-z.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Nocturnal sleep pattern, Nap duration

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents the most prevalent liver disease worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 32.4% [1]. MASLD is a general term, including isolated hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, which can progress to fibrosis and cirrhosis [2]. It develops in an unhealthy metabolic environment, most notably overweight or obesity, and is associated with disturbances in glucose homeostasis and lipid processing [3]. MASLD is not only a liver disease, but also a component of a multifaceted, multi-organ disease driven by complex gene-environment interactions [4].

Sleep disturbances, including irregular sleep patterns and excessive napping, have emerged as potential modifiable risk factors for metabolic disorders. T2DM patients often experience insufficient sleep or prolonged sleep, both of which are related to suboptimal control of metabolic risk factors [5]. Moreover, pharmacological treatments of diabetes, such as insulin therapy, may disrupt the normal sleep patterns through diverse pathways [6]. The interplay between sleep and metabolism is complex. Many studies have elucidated the relationship between nocturnal sleep behavior and the MASLD risk [7–9]. Napping has also been hypothesized to play a role in the development and progression of MASLD. However, the previous epidemiological studies have reported conflicting results on the relationship between napping and MASLD [10–13]. In addition, at present, the relationship between sleep and MASLD has not been studied in the T2DM population. There has also been no research evaluating whether incorporating sleep traits improves the predictive performance of the Fatty Liver Index (FLI) model.

Sleep behavior is usually interrelated and may affect each other in a coordinated manner. Previous research has often explored the relationship between single sleep behaviors and MASLD, with little comprehensive evaluation of the impact of sleep factors. Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aims to explore the correlation of nocturnal sleep factors and nap duration with MASLD risk in the Chinese T2DM population, and to determine whether specific sleep modes can predict this risk.

Methods

Subjects

This study selected 8919 T2DM patients registered in the National Standardization Metabolic Management Center (MMC) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and the Health Community Group of Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital from March 2017 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) meet the diagnostic criteria for T2DM; (b) patients voluntarily received questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests; and (c) age between 18 and 85 years. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (a) type 1 diabetes mellitus and other special types of diabetes; (b) having a history of excessive drinking (≥ 210 g/w for men and ≥ 140 g/w for women); (c) MASLD and other liver diseases (such as chronic hepatitis B or C, autoimmune liver disease, cirrhosis, and liver cancer) at baseline. The event time for incident MASLD was defined as the time from baseline to the first ultrasound diagnosis of MASLD. Censoring time was defined as the time from baseline to the earliest of the following: (a) date of last available ultrasound examination for patients who did not develop MASLD during follow-up, (b) loss to follow-up, or (c) death from any cause, whichever occurred first. After the following inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1900 T2DM patients without MASLD at baseline were recruited in the cohort study. These patients were randomly divided into a 7/10 training set (Derivation Cohort) and a 3/10 validation set (Validation Cohort) (see Supplementary Fig. 1). The training set was used for model development, and the validation set was used to estimate the generalization of the model. This study had been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and the Health Community Group of Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all patients for inclusion in this study.

Clinical and laboratory evaluation

Anthropometric data such as age, gender, educational attainment, diabetes duration, drinking history, smoking history, height, weight, waist circumference (WC), and blood pressure (BP) were collected at the time of sample collection. Insulin resistance (IR) was estimated by the homeostatic model assessment-insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index: (fasting insulin [μU/mL]) × (fasting plasma glucose [FPG] [mmol/L])/22.5 [14]. Definition of variables

T2DM is diagnosed according to the American Diabetes Association standard [15]. Diabetes symptoms plus: (a) a random venous plasma glucose concentration ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; (b) a fasting plasma glucose concentration ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (c) a 2-h plasma glucose concentration ≥ 11.1 mmol/L 2 h after 75 g anhydrous glucose in an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). With no symptoms, diagnosis should not be based on a single glucose determination but requires confirmatory plasma venous determination. At least one additional glucose test result on another day with a value in the diabetic range is essential, either fasting, from a random sample, or from the two-hour post glucose load. If the fasting random values are not diagnostic, the two-hour value should be used. According to the 2024 EASL-EASD-EASO Clinical Practice Guidelines [16], MASLD is defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis in the absence of harmful alcohol intake, together with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (impaired fasting plasma glucose, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and low serum HDL-c levels). Abdominal ultrasound was performed at baseline and every 6 months during follow‑up to assess the presence of hepatic steatosis. Hepatic steatosis was diagnosed based on standard sonographic criteria, including homogeneous or heterogeneous increased echogenicity of the liver parenchyma compared with the renal cortex, together with features such as hepatomegaly and poor visualization of the intrahepatic vessel walls and diaphragm. All ultrasound examinations were performed and interpreted by two experienced sonographers. The sonographers were blinded to participants’ sleep variables and other exposure information at the time of image acquisition and interpretation. The two sonographers interpreted the scans jointly, reaching a consensus diagnosis for each examination. In cases of disagreement, a third senior sonographer was consulted. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg without using antihypertensive medication or BP < 140/90 mmHg but under antihypertension treatment [17]. Dyslipidemia was defined as TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L or HDL-c < 1.0 mmol/L or LDL-c ≥ 3.4 mmol/L or the patient was receiving lipid-lowering drugs [18]. BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2 was considered overweight. WC ≥ 90 cm in males and WC ≥ 85 cm in females were considered as central obesity.

Assessment of sleep behaviors

Sleep factors were collected through a self-reported sleep questionnaire (see Supplementary file). According to previous studies [7, 9, 19], bedtime was categorized into three groups: before 10:00 pm (early), between 10:00 and 10:59 pm (middle), and after 11:00 pm (late). The wake-up time was divided into two groups: before 6:00 am (early) and after 6:00 am (late). The nocturnal sleep duration was categorized into three groups: less than 7 h, between 7 and 8 h, and longer than 8 h. The nap duration was categorized into three groups: 0 h, 0–0.5 h, and more than 0.5 h. Sleep quality was self-reported and was divided into two groups: good and poor. Notably, sleep quality was assessed using a single binary item rather than a multi-dimensional validated scale such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). While the questionnaire also captured use of sleep medications, these data were not incorporated into the current analytical framework.

According to the recommendations of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the Sleep Research Society [20–22], the healthy sleep factors were defined as early bedtime (before 11:00 pm), early awakening (before 6:00 am), nocturnal sleep duration more than 7 h, short napping (napping ≤ 0.5 h), and good sleep quality. Based on the above recommendations, we examined whether these pre-specified healthy sleep factors were associated with the risk of MASLD in our cohort. The results showed that nocturnal sleep duration less than 7 h, wake-up after 6:00 am, bedtime after 11:00 pm, poor sleep quality, and napping more than 0.5 h are significantly associated with a high risk of MASLD (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Using these factors, the nocturnal sleep pattern was defined by the four nocturnal factors: bedtime, wake-up time, nocturnal sleep duration, and sleep quality. Participants with 3–4 of these four health factors were considered to have a good nocturnal sleep pattern (GNSP), while those with 0–2 health factors are considered to have poor nocturnal sleep pattern (PNSP). Nap duration was categorized as short nap (SN, nap duration ≤ 0.5 h) or long nap (LN, nap duration > 0.5 h). Finally, to capture the combined effect of nocturnal sleep patterns and napping, the two components were cross-tabulated to generate four sleep mode groups: GNSP-SN group, GNSP-LN group, PNSP-SN group, and PNSP-LN group.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software, version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and R-4.4.3. The continuous variable was expressed as the mean ± SD or median, with the interquartile range according to a normal or skewed distribution, respectively. The categorical variables are expressed as percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using a one-way analysis of variance analysis for normal continuous variables, a nonparametric rank-sum test for skewed continuous variables, and a chi-square test for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of sleep with incident MASLD in the cohort study. To develop a predictive model incorporating sleep modes for MASLD risk in patients with T2DM, we performed a stepwise Cox proportional hazards regression in the derivation cohort. All candidate variables, including demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, biochemical parameters, lifestyle factors, clinical conditions, and the four-category sleep mode, were considered for model selection. A stepwise selection procedure with bidirectional elimination was applied based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), with entry and retention significance levels set at 0.10 and 0.05, respectively. The final FLI-Sleep algorithm retained the core components of the original FLI along with the sleep mode variable. Model performance for predicting MASLD risk was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis in both derivation and validation cohorts. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for a fixed time horizon of 3 years, which approximates the median follow-up duration of the cohort (3.23 years), to ensure sufficient event accumulation and clinical relevance. Categorical net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were computed to quantify the incremental predictive value of the FLI-Sleep algorithm. The analysis was performed using predefined risk categories based on clinically relevant thresholds: low risk (< 10%), intermediate risk (10–20%), and high risk (> 20%) for 3-year incident MASLD.

Model 1: adjusted for age, gender. Model 2: adjusted for age, gender, diabetic duration, BMI, WC, hypertension, history of drinking and smoking. Model 3: adjusted for age, gender, diabetic duration, BMI, WC, hypertension, history of drinking and smoking, dyslipidemia, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR. We tested the proportional hazards assumption for all covariates included in the final models using Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violations were detected (all P > 0.05).

Nocturnal sleep patterns, napping, and new-onset MASLD

We examined whether these pre-specified healthy sleep factors were associated with the risk of MASLD in our cohort. The results showed that nocturnal sleep duration less than 7 h (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.46–2.49, p < 0.001), wake-up after 6:00 am (HR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.30–2.08, p < 0.001), bedtime after 11:00 pm (HR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.69–3.01, p < 0.001), poor sleep quality (HR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.51–2.37, p < 0.001) and nap duration more than 0.5 h (HR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.41–2.32, p < 0.001) are significantly associated with a high risk of MASLD (see Supplementary Fig. 2). The Cox regression analysis confirmed that the incidence of MASLD varied in different nocturnal sleep patterns and napping duration groups (see Supplementary Table 1). Compared with the GNSP group, people in the PNSP group have a higher risk of developing MASLD (HR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.86–2.95, p < 0.001). Compared with the SN group, participants who reported nap durations > 0.5 h had a greater risk of developing MASLD (HR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.35–2.12, p < 0.001).

To evaluate the impact of coexistent nocturnal sleep patterns and nap duration on MASLD risk, we grouped our population into four sleep mode groups. The Cox regression results confirmed that compared to the GNSP-SN group, the risk of MASLD increased with the increase of adverse sleep factors (see Table 2). The PNSP-LN group had the highest risk of MASLD (HR = 3.51, 95% CI: 2.53–4.87, p < 0.001). The intra-group comparison showed that regardless of whether in the GNSP group or PNSP group, patients who reported napping > 0.5 h had a higher MASLD risk compared to patients with short nap durations (see Supplementary Table 2). To clarify the impact of different sleep modes on the incidence risk of MASLD, Kaplan–Meier curve analysis was also conducted, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. With the extension of follow-up time, the cumulative incidence risk of MASLD in the four sleep modes increased, and the cumulative incidence rate of MASLD in the PNSP-LN group was significantly higher than that in the GNSP-SN group (Log-rank P < 0.001).

To distinguish the effects of nocturnal sleep duration and sleep timing, we conducted additional analysis restricted to participants with adequate nocturnal sleep duration (≥ 7 h). In this subgroup, participants were divided into four groups based on their bedtime and wake-up time. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, compared to the Early/Early group (reference group), the Late/Late group (bedtime ≥ 11:00 pm/wake-up > 6:00 am) remained associated with a significantly higher MASLD risk (HR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.74–3.48, p < 0.001). In contrast, the Late/Early group (bedtime ≥ 11:00 pm/wake-up ≤ 6:00 am) and the Early/Late group (bedtime < 11:00 pm/wake-up > 6:00 am) did not show statistically significant associations. A significant interaction was also observed between late bedtime and insufficient sleep duration (p for interaction < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

We stratified the research population based on age, gender, education, overweight and obesity, central obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. After multivariable adjustment, stratified analysis showed that nocturnal sleep patterns were significantly associated with MASLD risk in all subgroups, indicating that poor nocturnal sleep pattern increased the MASLD risk (see Fig. 1-a). In addition, there is a similar association with napping duration, where long napping brings higher MASLD risk (see Fig. 1-b). Figure 1c and d illustrated the associations between napping duration and MASLD incidence among (c) good nocturnal sleep pattern and (d) poor nocturnal sleep pattern. A significant interaction was observed between hypertension and napping durations (see Fig. 1b and c).

Furthermore, we examined whether nocturnal sleep patterns and nap duration interacted on an additive scale to influence MASLD risk. The analysis did not provide convincing evidence of a significant additive interaction between these two sleep behaviors (p = 0.179 for interaction).

Assessment of prediction models

The features of the derivation and validation cohorts were well matched, and the rates of MASLD in the derivation and validation cohorts were 19.5% and 21.1% (see Supplementary Table 4). In the derivation cohort, stepwise Cox regression identified sleep mode as a significant predictor of new-onset MASLD. Among all candidate variables, ln(TG), BMI, WC, and the four-category sleep mode were retained in the final model, leading to the following FLI-Sleep algorithm:

The FLI-Sleep algorithm improved the discrimination of MASLD risk compared to the original FLI model. In the derivation cohort, the FLI-Sleep algorithm yielded an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.71–0.78) for identifying MASLD risk, which was better than the performance of the FLI (AUC = 0.68 [95% CI: 0.65–0.71]; DeLong’s test: p < 0.001) (see Fig. 2). The AUC of the FLI-Sleep algorithm was also higher than the FLI in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.72 [95% CI: 0.67–0.77] vs. AUC = 0.68 [95% CI: 0.63–0.73], see Supplementary Fig. 4). The categorical NRI is 0.21 (95% CI: 0.15–0.27, p < 0.001), indicating a net improvement rate of 21% for correct reclassification, which is driven by more diseased individuals upgrading and non-diseased individuals downgrading. The IDI of 0.06 (95% CI: 0.05–0.08, p < 0.001) demonstrates that the discrimination slope has increased by 6%, with higher average predicted probabilities for diseased cases and lower ones for non-diseased controls. Collectively, these findings suggest that sleep modes may add incremental prognostic information to the FLI, though this requires confirmation in externally validated, well‑calibrated studies.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation to assess the impact of missing data. Pooled results from five imputed datasets were nearly identical to those from the complete-case analysis (see Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study assessed nocturnal sleep patterns, nap duration, and the MASLD risk in individuals with T2DM aged 18 to 85 years. Poor nocturnal sleep is an independent risk factor for MASLD in Chinese T2DM patients. Furthermore, napping exceeding 0.5 h was associated with a higher risk of MASLD, independent of nocturnal sleep patterns. Integrating these sleep behaviors into the FLI algorithm showed a modest but statistically significant improvement in discrimination and reclassification in our internal validation cohort.

Consistent with previous studies, we found that adverse nocturnal sleep traits and prolonged napping are each associated with higher MASLD risk [7, 23–25]. For example, Kim et al. reported short nocturnal sleep duration correlated with elevated NAFLD scores in Korean adults [26], and Yang et al. linked poor sleep quality to higher MASLD prevalence in middle-aged Chinese populations [9]. Notably, our research suggests that the PNSP-LN mode conferred the highest MASLD risk, emphasizing that the synergistic effect of poor nocturnal sleep and prolonged napping may exacerbate metabolic dysregulation in T2DM. Furthermore, supplementary analysis restricted to participants with adequate nocturnal sleep duration revealed that the delayed sleep timing still remained significantly associated with an increased MASLD risk (HR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.74–3.48). A significant interaction between late bedtime and insufficient nocturnal sleep duration was also observed (p for interaction < 0.001). These findings suggest that circadian rhythm disorder, rather than sleep deprivation, may play a leading role in promoting hepatic steatosis in T2DM patients. This aligns with emerging evidence that disruption of the central circadian clock can dysregulate hepatic lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory pathways, independently of sleep loss [27]. Therefore, interventions targeting sleep timing, such as advancing bedtime, may be particularly beneficial in this high-risk population. Our research focused more on T2DM, a population with higher metabolic risk and insufficient research on the relationship between sleep and MASLD, and defined a sleep mode that combines nocturnal and napping behaviors, which is more clinically relevant than studying a single sleep trait, and used longitudinal data to establish the temporal relationship between sleep and MASLD.

A key novel finding is that prolonged napping increases MASLD risk independently of nocturnal sleep patterns. Previous studies on napping and MASLD were contradictory. Peng et al. reported there was no association between 40-min naps and MASLD [12], while Hong et al. linked napping > 1 h to higher NAFLD risk [28]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study populations or failure to account for nocturnal sleep context [11, 12, 28]. Subgroup analyses indicated that prolonged napping elevates MASLD risk regardless of nocturnal sleep pattern, although the relative risk increase was modest in individuals with poor nocturnal sleep pattern. We hypothesize this attenuation reflects a “risk ceiling” effect: poor nocturnal sleep has already driven severe metabolic disturbance, so the incremental risk of prolonged napping may be overshadowed. This is supported by our population attributable fraction analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 5), which showed poor nocturnal sleep pattern to MASLD risk far outweighs the combined effects of prolonged napping, elevated BMI, and hypertension.

Poor sleep traits have been shown to affect many pathways leading to the development and progression of MASLD, including IR, sympathetic nervous system excitation, and systemic inflammation [29, 30]. Poor sleep can disrupt insulin signaling. Partial sleep deprivation can reduce insulin sensitivity by 16–32% in healthy adults [31] and increases endogenous glucose production [32]. These effects may worsen IR in T2DM and promote hepatic fat deposition. We observed that HOMA-IR was higher in patients in the PNSP group and LN group, indicating that IR is more common in this population (as shown in Table 1). Prolonged napping may further disrupt metabolism by altering the circadian rhythm of cortisol and growth hormone, thereby weakening tissue insulin response and enhancing ectopic fat accumulation [33–35]. Other studies have pointed out that poor nocturnal sleep patterns and excessive napping can synergistically disrupt the normal circadian rhythm and metabolic clock of the human body, as well as reduce the amplitude of temperature rhythms, promoting the occurrence and development of MASLD [36–39]. Additionally, sleep disturbances can activate the sympathetic nervous system, leading to increased fat deposition in liver cells and accelerating the development of MASLD [40–42]. Finally, sleep quality and duration are associated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1β, C-reactive protein, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which may lead to chronic liver inflammation and disease progression [43–45]. Our analysis confirms the PHR, a marker of systemic inflammation [46–48], acts as a mediator between sleep and MASLD, further supporting this inflammatory pathway (see Supplementary Fig. 6).

The calculation of FLI is based on simple biochemical indicators and measurement parameters, and is a simple and non-invasive assessment tool for quantifying the risk of fatty liver [49]. However, the FLI performs poorly in predicting new-onset MASLD [50]. In this exploratory analysis, we assessed whether incorporating sleep behaviors could capture additional prognostic information beyond standard metabolic indices. By adding sleep modes in FLI, our internal validation cohort showed a statistically significant improvement in discrimination and reclassification metrics. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies that combine sleep with clinical prediction tools. Based on sleep behavior, early screening and intervention strategies can be developed for high-risk populations to reduce or delay the progression of MASLD. A potential concern is the partial overlap between components of the FLI and the metabolic framework that defines our study population. Despite this overlap, the addition of sleep modes to FLI provided statistically significant improvements in NRI and IDI, indicating that the FLI‑Sleep model correctly reclassifies a meaningful proportion of individuals beyond what FLI alone can achieve. The stepwise Cox regression allowed metabolic and sleep variables to compete equally; only sleep factors that contributed independent predictive information remained in the final model. Thus, the incremental predictive value is clinically interesting because it suggests that easily ascertainable sleep behaviors carry additional risk information beyond standard metabolic indices. Nevertheless, the absolute AUC values indicate only modest discrimination, and these figures should not be overinterpreted as evidence of strong predictive accuracy.

Our study is a retrospective cohort study, which improves the credibility of the results. Foremost, previous studies have only focused on the impact of a single sleep factor on diseases, or combined sleep with other lifestyle factors in a general way, so they cannot comprehensively reflect the impact of sleep on MASLD. However, this study not only considers multiple sleep factors, but also combines nocturnal sleep with nap, presenting a more three-dimensional relationship between sleep and MASLD. Moreover, we incorporated sleep modes into the MASLD risk prediction model. However, there are still some limitations to our study. Firstly, this study diagnosed MASLD through abdominal ultrasound instead of the currently recognized gold standard liver biopsy, and the results may have some subjective differences. Furthermore, although the abdominal ultrasound examination was jointly conducted by two ultrasound physicians, we did not calculate formal inter-observer reliability statistics (such as the κ coefficient). This prevented us from quantitatively assessing the degree of consistency among evaluators, potentially affecting the standardized assessment of liver steatosis diagnosis to some extent. Future research should further adopt standardized ultrasound scoring systems or combine quantitative techniques such as Controlled Attenuation Parameter, and report reliability assessment indicators to improve the stability and reproducibility of diagnostic results. Secondly, this study collected sleep data through self-report, which may have a certain recall bias and affect the results. In addition, the nocturnal sleep quality assessment was brief and relied on only a single binary self-report item, rather than a validated full PSQI instrument. Such a simplified measurement may introduce non-differential misclassification, that is, random and non-biased measurement error distributed similarly across all study groups, which may weaken the true associations between sleep modes and MASLD risk. Furthermore, several important confounders, including specific sleep disorders, shift work, use of medications that affect sleep, and psychiatric comorbidities, were not available in our dataset and could not be adjusted for. These unmeasured factors may influence both sleep behaviors and MASLD risk. Therefore, in the future, the sleep questionnaire should be improved, and large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies should be conducted to confirm the results of this study. Third, several methodological limitations constrain the clinical applicability of the predictive model. The model was developed using stepwise variable selection and validated within a single dataset, without rigorous internal–external cross-validation. Due to the methodological complexity inherent in exploratory analyses of time-to-event outcomes, time-dependent calibration, decision curve analysis, and bootstrapping for optimism correction were not performed. Consequently, the present findings offer only preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence of an incremental prognostic contribution from sleep-related measures. The absence of these analytical procedures limits a comprehensive assessment of the clinical utility of the FLI-Sleep algorithm, and further validation studies are encouraged to address this gap. Additionally, the generalizability of the FLI-Sleep algorithm may be constrained by the characteristics of the study population. The majority of participants in this study had a normal BMI, which may not adequately reflect the broader population of individuals with T2DM, among whom overweight and obesity are more prevalent. Further validation in more diverse populations with a wider range of BMI is needed. To the best of our knowledge, no publicly available longitudinal dataset currently integrates both comprehensive sleep metrics and incident MASLD. Therefore, we could not perform external validation, and internal validation alone can only partially address concerns related to model overfitting. Thus, the definitive clinical utility of the FLI-Sleep algorithm will require evaluation in future multicenter prospective studies, particularly those involving populations with diverse ethnic backgrounds, healthcare settings, and lifestyle profiles.

Conclusion

Unhealthy nocturnal sleep patterns are correlated with the development of MASLD. Extended napping was independently associated with a higher risk of MASLD, irrespective of whether nocturnal sleep patterns were good or poor. The exploratory incorporation of sleep modes into the FLI showed incremental predictive signal for MASLD in people with T2DM. Although further evaluation is needed for clinical application, these findings emphasize the importance of considering sleep behavior as a potential auxiliary factor in metabolic risk assessment and warrant further prospective research.
