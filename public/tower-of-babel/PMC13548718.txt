Clinical nurses’ experiences with “Internet+Nursing Service” in a public tertiary hospital in Western China: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Yunling Long, Zhuoyuanyuan Chen, Yuling Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe “Internet+Nursing Service” (hereinafter referred to as INS) is an innovative model developed in China to extend professional nursing care to home settings, particularly for elderly and chronically ill patients. Despite its potential to enhance healthcare accessibility, little is known about the experiences of clinical nurses who deliver these services, especially in Western China.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the experiences of clinical nurses participating in INS in a public tertiary hospital in Western China.DesignA qualitative descriptive study was conducted.ParticipantsTwelve clinical nurses (all female; mean years of professional experience: 18) who had completed at least five INS assignments were included.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were used for data collection. Thematic analysis was applied to analyze the interview data.ResultsFour main themes emerged: (1) Service value, encompassing professional growth, patient convenience, and hospital reputation; (2) Practical barriers, including logistical support, safety risks, and procedural limitations; (3) Support needs, covering team collaboration and policy support; (4) Directions for improvement, focusing on process optimization, safety assurance, and publicity expansion.ConclusionINS plays a critical role in enhancing continuity of care for homebound patients, yet its sustainability in under-resourced settings is constrained by operational barriers, safety concerns, limited awareness, and lack of insurance reimbursement. Background

Against the backdrop of global healthcare challenges including an aging population, rising chronic disease burden, and uneven distribution of medical resources, the importance of continuity of care has become increasingly evident [1, 2]. The integration of information technology into healthcare has created opportunities for innovative nursing models [3]. Among these, INS has emerged as a new approach to extending professional nursing care into communities and homes via an “online appointment, offline service” model, particularly for discharged patients and those with limited mobility [4].

INS has been actively promoted in China as a national strategy to address these healthcare needs. Pilot initiatives were launched in 2019 in several developed coastal regions [5], with subsequent national health policies emphasizing the expansion of such integrated online-to-offline care models [6, 7]. While institutional frameworks and regional pilots—such as province-level digital platforms in Ningxia or nurse-led outreach models in Chongqing—have advanced service implementation in Western China, research remains disproportionately focused on policy analysis, model design, and quantitative surveys of nurse participation rates.

The current development of INS in Western China presents a complex landscape characterized by “regional model innovation coexisting with a lack of systematic research.” On one hand, regions represented by Ningxia [8] and Chongqing [9–11] have provided crucial institutional paradigms for service implementation through top-level design. Ningxia has laid the cornerstone for the digitalization of nursing services at the infrastructure and data interoperability levels by constructing a province-level integrated INS platform. Chongqing has innovated a regional promotion model led by nursing quality control centers, effectively integrating medical resources and bridging the “last mile” of service delivery to households. On the other hand, significant imbalances and gaps persist in research within this field. Existing literature predominantly focuses on policy descriptions, model construction, and cross-sectional surveys of nurses’ cognition and willingness to participate, while severely lacking in-depth qualitative research into the experiences of the core service providers—frontline nurses. Key issues, such as nurses’ genuine feelings during home-based service provision, concerns regarding occupational risks and safety, the alignment between workload and incentive mechanisms, and the specific challenges encountered in cross-cultural care contexts, remain nearly absent from academic discourse in the Western Chinese context. This neglect of research into the subjective experiences of nurses poses potential risks to the sustainability and humanistic dimensions of the current models. Consequently, the next phase of development for INS in Western China urgently needs to shift focus from emphasizing “platform construction and scale coverage” to a deeper concern for “service provider experiences and the labor process,” to ensure the healthy and sustainable evolution of this model.

The experiences and perceptions of nurses—as direct service providers—fundamentally shape both service quality and the long-term sustainability of INS [12]. In this study, we employ a descriptive qualitative approach to explore the experiences of clinical nurses participating in INS at a tertiary hospital in Western China. Through inductive thematic analysis, we identify four key dimensions: service value, practical barriers, support needs, and directions for improvement. In the discussion section, we draw on Social Exchange Theory as a sensitizing framework to help interpret these findings, particularly the ways in which nurses perceive rewards and costs associated with their participation. This interpretive lens offers one useful perspective for understanding the sustainability of INS, without claiming that the theory guided data collection or coding.

While qualitative studies on nurses’ experiences with INS have emerged from developed eastern provinces (e.g., Zhejiang, Shanghai), their findings—such as patient convenience, professional fulfillment, safety concerns, logistical burdens, and calls for policy support—reflect contexts with established reimbursement pilots, integrated digital platforms, and better logistical infrastructure. In contrast, Western China presents a fundamentally different landscape: no insurance coverage for INS, severe geographical constraints (e.g., mountainous terrain limiting service radius to ~ 10 km), a single tertiary hospital serving as the sole provider, and nurses bearing out-of-pocket travel costs with minimal institutional safety nets. Therefore, the distinct contribution of this study is not to replicate known themes but to reveal how the absence of systemic support amplifies known barriers into qualitatively different challenges—specifically, how the lack of reimbursement transforms cost from a minor deterrent into a primary exclusion factor; how geographic remoteness turns transportation inconvenience into financial and safety crises for nurses; and how the tension between tertiary hospital staffing shortages and expanding home services creates unsustainable workload models. By situating our findings within this under-resourced, pre-policy context, we provide empirical grounding for context-sensitive policy design in Western China and other similarly disadvantaged regions.

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study design was employed to gain an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences of clinical nurses providing INS.

Participants

Using a purposive sampling approach, clinical nurses who participated in the INS program at a Grade A tertiary hospital in Chongqing between February 2023 and December 2024 were selected as study participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a registered clinical nurse with a valid practicing certificate; (2) having at least 5 years of clinical nursing experience; (3) having provided INS to no fewer than 5 cases; (4) holding a professional title of Charge Nurse or above; (5) having engaged in the INS program for a minimum of 6 months; and (6) providing informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study.Exclusion criteria included: (1) nurses who were in training or advanced study programs; (2) those with a record of professional misconduct; and (3) individuals unwilling to participate in the research.

Sampling

The sample size was guided by the principle of information saturation [13]. Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively; saturation was considered achieved when three consecutive interviews yielded no new codes or themes. A total of 12 participants were enrolled, with sequential codes N1–N12. All were female and held a Bachelor’s degree in Nursing. Table 1 presents their demographic and professional characteristics. Because this study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, the findings may not fully represent nurses’ experiences in other hospitals or regions of Western China.

Moreover, this study purposively sampled clinical nurses who had already participated in INS and met the qualification requirements (≥ 5 years of experience, Charge Nurse or above). As such, the findings reflect the perspectives of relatively experienced, self-selected INS providers within a single tertiary hospital. They do not capture the views of nurses who declined INS participation, junior nurses, nurse managers, dispatch coordinators, or patients and families. Consequently, claims about broader systemic sustainability or policy direction are offered as interpretive insights grounded in nurses’ accounts, not as direct representations of those unstudied groups.

Research methods

This study employed a descriptive qualitative research approach, utilizing thematic analysis [14] as the core analytical tool. Data analysis was conducted following Braun & Clarke’s six-step framework for thematic analysis to explore the authentic work experiences of clinical nurses engaged in INS at a public Grade A tertiary hospital in Western China.

Setting

Beibei District is located in the northwest of Chongqing’s main urban area, serving as a hub for science, education, and culture, as well as an eco-friendly residential region. It has a permanent population of approximately 830,000, with an urbanization rate exceeding 85%, and an aging rate of 20.3% (higher than the municipal average). The population is relatively well-educated, with 32% holding a college degree or higher. The district is also well-equipped with medical resources, boasting 4.2 licensed physicians per 1,000 people [15].

This study was conducted at a Grade A tertiary general hospital in Beibei District. The hospital began preparations for the INS initiative in October 2022 and officially launched online order acceptance in February 2023. Services cover a wide range of areas, including clinical nursing, traditional Chinese medicine nursing, maternal and infant care, specialized nursing, rehabilitation nursing, and pediatric nursing. Specific services offered include venipuncture, indwelling catheter care, nasogastric tube care, blood glucose monitoring, PICC care, wound and stoma care, and infusion port maintenance, among others.

As of December 2024, 17 nurses had participated in the INS program, completing a total of 171 orders. This hospital is the only Grade A tertiary general hospital in the region and serves as the primary provider of “Internet+Nursing Services” in the area. Although the program was launched later compared to pilot hospitals in other cities, it has indicated a relatively high and growing number of orders, along with a comprehensive range of services. It is noteworthy that, currently, there are no specific policy supports or safeguard mechanisms for such services in Chongqing.

Development of the interview guide

Based on the research objectives and a review of the literature, a preliminary interview guide was drafted. The research team discussed and revised the guide, which was then reviewed by experts. Pre-interviews were conducted with two nurses who had provided INS on more than five occasions (these two nurses were not included in the final sample). The pre-interview results were used to further refine the guide to ensure it comprehensively captured nurses’ work experiences in the program.

During the pre-interviews, attention was paid to nurses’ descriptions of service processes, technology application, patient feedback, and personal perceptions. Common issues and key information were identified and served as focal points for the formal interviews. Additionally, the guide was adjusted based on shortcomings identified in the pre-interviews to enhance the relevance and effectiveness of the interviews. Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with the participants. Prior to each interview, the researcher contacted eligible nurses via telephone or WeChat to schedule a suitable time. The purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and guarantees of confidentiality were explained to each participant. They were informed that the interview would be audio-recorded and that written informed consent was required.

Interviews were conducted in a quiet meeting room free from interruptions. The interviewer began with neutral topics to help put participants at ease before gradually guiding the conversation according to the interview guide. A neutral stance was maintained throughout, and active listening techniques were employed to encourage detailed responses. Follow-up questions were used to elicit richer information. Non-verbal expressions, such as facial expressions and gestures, were also noted.

The order of questions was adjusted flexibly based on the flow of the conversation. All interviews were conducted in a relaxed and natural atmosphere, with each session lasting between 30 and 40 min.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [14]. The analysis was conducted collaboratively by three researchers (ZYY Chen, YL Long, YL Xie) to mitigate individual bias and enhance interpretive rigor. Notably, no pre-existing theoretical framework—including Social Exchange Theory—was used to guide coding or theme development; the analysis remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

Familiarization and coding

All transcripts were read repeatedly. Using an inductive, line-by-line approach, we generated initial codes that stayed close to participants’ words (e.g.,“logistics-transportation-burden”,“safety-concern-unfamiliar-environment”). Two researchers independently coded the first two transcripts, compared their codes, and resolved discrepancies through discussion. This consensus process produced a preliminary codebook with definitions and exemplars, which was then applied to the remaining transcripts using NVivo software to manage data organization.

Theme development

Codes were grouped into candidate themes, which were iteratively reviewed against the entire dataset. Themes were refined, split, or merged until a stable thematic structure emerged. The final four themes—Service Value, Practical Barriers, Support Needs, and Directions for Improvement—were defined and named.

Saturation assessment

Saturation was assessed continuously during coding and theme development. No new codes or themes appeared after the ninth interview, and three additional interviews (N10–N12) confirmed saturation, consistent with our pre-defined criterion [13].

Translation and reporting. All interviews were conducted and analyzed in Chinese. For reporting, illustrative quotes were translated into English by a bilingual researcher (YL Xie) and back-translated by another (YL Long) to verify accuracy. Trustworthiness and reflexibility

Credibility was enhanced through multiple methods. Researchers repeatedly read and analyzed interview transcripts to immerse themselves in the data.

Member checking was conducted by returning the full interview transcripts to each participant for verification. All 12 participants confirmed that the transcripts accurately reflected their experiences. No participant requested removal or correction of any statement. However, three participants (N4, N8, N11) added contextual details—for example, N8 elaborated on the emotional impact of a difficult catheterization procedure. These additions did not alter our identified themes but reinforced the centrality of safety concerns and procedural anxiety. Importantly, member checking did not lead to any revision of our interpretive themes; the core thematic structure remained stable after participant verification.

Peer debriefing was integral to the process. The three researchers (ZYY Chen, YL Long, YL Xie) met biweekly during analysis. Two specific instances materially influenced theme development. First, in an early meeting, two researchers had independently coded “patients refusing service due to cost” as a subtheme under “Practical Barriers,” while the third researcher had grouped it under “Policy Support Needs.” Through discussion, we recognized that this item functioned both as a barrier (descriptive) and as a justification for policy change (interpretive). We resolved this by placing the descriptive aspect under “Practical Barriers” and the policy implication under “Support Needs” – a decision that sharpened the distinction between what nurses reported and what they advocated. Second, during later meetings, we debated whether “team collaboration” and “policy support” should remain separate themes or be merged. After reviewing the data, we agreed that nurses discussed these as qualitatively different needs (operational vs. systemic), and keeping them separate better reflected participants’ accounts. These debriefing sessions directly shaped the final four-theme structure.

Dependability was ensured through maintenance of an audit trail. This included: the iteratively refined interview guide (three versions), anonymized raw transcripts (12 files), records of coding decisions and revisions to the codebook (two major revisions), minutes from six debriefing meetings, and successive versions of the thematic map (initial version with six candidate themes, refined version with four themes). This audit trail enables logical tracing of our analytical decisions.

Confirmability was strengthened by involving multiple researchers in data analysis to minimize individual bias. Findings are explicitly grounded in the data, as suggested by the inclusion of direct participant quotations under each theme and sub-theme in the Results section. To further ensure confirmability, we maintained a reflexive journal in which each researcher documented initial impressions and potential biases before coding began. For example, one researcher noted a prior assumption that “nurses would prioritize financial incentives,” which was not supported by the data (participants consistently emphasized patient gratitude and professional identity over monetary rewards). This awareness prevented that assumption from influencing coding.

Reflexivity was actively practiced throughout the study. The research team critically reflected on their positions and potential influences. All members have backgrounds in nursing and health services research and share an academic and professional interest in improving healthcare delivery models such as INS. We acknowledge that this prior interest may have shaped our focus on identifying both the benefits and challenges of the service. *To mitigate this, we consciously adopted an open and curious stance during interviews, predominantly using open-ended questions (e.g., “Can you tell me about a typical home visit?” rather than “What problems did you encounter?”). During data analysis, we used peer debriefing explicitly to question initial interpretations. One concrete example: an early draft of the results emphasized “patient convenience” as the primary value theme. During reflexivity discussion, we realized that this emphasis reflected our own positive bias toward INS as a policy innovation. Re-examining the data revealed that nurses equally emphasized professional growth and hospital reputation, leading us to restructure Theme 1 to include all three dimensions with balanced weight. This reflexivity-driven revision ensured that our findings remained closely aligned with participants’ accounts rather than our preconceptions.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beibei Hospital of Chongqing Medical University in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No.: 2025-Science (Ethics)-016). Written informed consent, including permission for audio recording, was obtained from each participant.

Results

Analysis of interviews with 12 clinical nurses participating in the “Internet+Nursing Services” program revealed four interrelated themes: service value, practical barriers, support needs, and directions for improvement.

Theme 1: Service value

Nurses consistently recognized the multiple values embedded in INS spanning patient-centered benefits, personal professional development, and organizational gains. These perceived values formed the motivational foundation for their continued participation.

Enhanced patient convenience and reduced healthcare burden.

Nurses reported that home-based services substantially alleviated the logistical and financial difficulties faced by patients with limited mobility, offering a humane and efficient alternative to hospital visits.

It is quite difficult to bring bedridden or mobility-impaired patients to the hospital—it requires considerable effort and expense. (N1, 15 years experience)

Every time I visit them at home, they are very grateful. They find it hard to move the patient to the hospital, especially going downstairs. This home service is very convenient for patients. (N4, 20 years experience)

It is very troublesome for vegetative patients to come to the hospital. INS offer them a convenient alternative. (N6,15years experience)

Professional growth and sense of achievement among nurses

Through direct patient interaction in home settings, nurses gained recognition and trust, which strengthened their professional identity and clinical confidence. Several also noted improvements in technical skills.

I have improved my ability to respond to emergencies. (N6, 15 years experience)

Patients and families are very happy with the home service. Their happiness gives me a strong sense of accomplishment. (N7, 21 years experience)

I feel proud to provide nursing care at home. Patients are very grateful for the help, and that makes me feel fulfilled. (N9, 20 years experience)

Especially with nasogastric tube insertion—since starting home visits, my technique has become more skilled and steady. (N8,13 years experience)

Enhanced hospital reputation and patient trust.

The program enabled nurses to deliver specialized, high-quality care beyond hospital walls, which not only improved patient satisfaction but also cultivated deep trust and loyalty toward the hospital.

I feel that overall patient satisfaction and positive feedback are very high. (N1, 15 years experience)

A patient who had been discharged from another hospital learned about our service and contacted me. After one visit, they trusted me completely and entrusted me with wound dressing and suture removal. (N4, 20years experience)

When we visit patients’ homes, they and their families are very welcoming—they even invited us to meals and showed great trust in us. (N12, 20 years experience)

However, this perceived value coexists with significant costs, creating a motivational foundation that is simultaneously strong and fragile.

Theme 2: Practical barriers

Despite the perceived value, nurses identified a range of systemic and operational barriers that undermined service efficiency, safety, and accessibility. These challenges were described as persistent and often structural.

Systemic barriers

Systemic obstacles were rooted in inadequate institutional support, policy gaps, and resource limitations. Nurses frequently cited difficulties in transportation, departmental staffing conflicts, and inconvenient access to medical supplies. Moreover, service radius restrictions and the absence of insurance reimbursement placed financial and geographical constraints on patients, limiting service uptake.

The company car is set to depart from the hospital, but nurses on rest days have to come from home to the hospital to take the car. They can’t set off directly from home, which is very time-consuming. For very remote areas, after taking a car there, it’s very hard to find a ride back. For example, last time I went to Jinyun Mountain, there was no transportation back. (N4, 20 years experience).

It’s hard to get a taxi in remote areas, so I have to drive myself, which increases my expenses. Sometimes I might be called in on my day off—I feel this affects my personal life. (N5, 15 years experience).

Personally, I find it very troublesome to get the service kit from the hospital every time. Nurses in our department who do home visits use a kit prepared by the department—it’s much more convenient. (N7, 21 years experience).

There are very few colleagues on duty in our ward. If I go out for a home visit, the ward will be short-staffed. (N8, 13 years experience).

When he placed the order, it seemed his home was too far away—outside our service radius. (N8, 13 years experience).

Currently, our hospital’s service radius is only about 10 kilometers. Some patients have these needs but live outside this range, so they can’t place orders. (N10, 11 years experience).

Many of our stoma patients don’t place orders online—they come directly to our ward. They feel it’s more expensive to order online. (N8, 13 years experience).

I wish the price could be a bit lower. Once we had a patient who was very suitable for INS, but after we mentioned the price, he hesitated and didn’t choose it. Every time, he brought the patient to the hospital. (N9, 20 years experience).

INS may become the norm in the future, but the cost is a bit high for patients, especially with high demand. Including material fees, it might be slightly expensive. (N10, 11 years experience).

Many patients only learned that our hospital offers INS after asking around repeatedly. (N3, 20 years experience).

They don’t know that our hospital can provide home services like indwelling gastric or urinary catheterization. I think promotion efforts need significant improvement—many people are simply unaware. (N8, 13 years experience).

One patient I visited last time said he hadn’t known our hospital offered home services—he only found out through another patient. (N12, 20 years experience).

Operational barriers

Operational challenges were primarily related to safety concerns and procedural shortcomings. Nurses described working alone in unfamiliar environments, encountering households with complex social dynamics, and facing anxious family members during technically difficult procedures. In addition, rigid qualification requirements, inconsistent supply management, and the 24-hour advance booking system hindered responsiveness and continuity of care.

I think safety is a very important consideration when I provide home visits alone. We enter an unfamiliar environment without knowing the specifics. (N1, 15 years experience).

For example, once I visited a family where the patient was an elderly woman, and her husband was kind, but all their children were drug users—long-haired, smoking, both men and women. It was terrifying, and I was worried about my safety. (N6, 15 years experience).

Once, when inserting a gastric tube, the guidewire couldn’t be removed. I was very anxious, and the family was also very worried. That visit took a long time—it took three attempts to successfully place the tube. I was concerned the family might become aggressive. (N8, 13 years experience).

Another issue is not knowing the patient’s background—could they have severe social habits? We have no idea about these aspects until we arrive. So, safety is a worry. (N10, 11 years experience).

For example, if a patient is used to a certain type of gastric or urinary catheter and finds it works well, but the nursing department doesn’t necessarily assign the same nurse every time, the materials brought may differ, and the patient finds it inconvenient. (N10, 11 years experience).

I encountered a situation where the same patient ordered both urinary and gastric tube services. On the platform, I only have qualifications for catheterization, while another nurse is qualified for gastric tube insertion. So, two nurses from different departments were sent—one for the gastric tube, one for the urinary catheter. It would be better if one nurse could have multiple qualifications and be sent alone. Sending two people for two procedures is a waste of manpower and time. (N9, 20 years experience).

In fact, every nurse is capable of performing these procedures. If a patient needs both a urinary catheter and a gastric tube, they could choose the same nurse for multiple services, which could also save costs. (N7, 21 years experience).

From the patient’s perspective, the 24-hour advance booking requirement is problematic. For example, if a urinary catheter is suddenly blocked and the patient’s bladder has been distended for hours, making them wait 24 hours for a home visit is a painful process. It also poses safety risks. I think the 24-hour requirement could be reduced to 8 hours, or there could be an expedited option based on the patient’s condition. (N1, 15 years experience).

Notably, nurses did not simply report barriers—they described how these barriers force them to accept personal risk and financial loss as routine aspects of the service.

Theme 3: Support needs

To address the barriers encountered, nurses articulated essential support needs at both the institutional and policy levels. These needs were framed as prerequisites for sustainable and safe service delivery.

Team collaboration

Nurses emphasized the importance of intra-departmental coordination and interdisciplinary collaboration. Support from head nurses in scheduling, the companionship of colleagues or trainees during visits, and access to specialist physicians were viewed as critical enablers.

I always try to go with a resident trainee. Having two people for home visits ensures safety and also helps the trainee learn about the service. (N1, 15 years experience).

We need support from doctors. Sometimes we bring a doctor along to examine a patient’s wound. (N9, 20 years experience).

When I need to go out during work hours, I have to ask the head nurse to adjust my schedule and arrange cover. Once, I encountered a patient with prostate hyperplasia during catheterization. It was difficult and I didn’t succeed. I communicated with a urology doctor, who recommended that the patient see a specialist at the hospital. (N12, 20 years experience).

Policy support

A recurring demand was the integration of INS into the health insurance reimbursement system. Nurses observed that out-of-pocket payment significantly deterred patients, particularly those requiring frequent or long-term care.

It would be great if our service fees could be covered by health insurance. (N9, 20 years experience).

For example, if it could be linked to medical insurance so patients don’t have to pay out-of-pocket, the service might be more readily accepted. Currently, outpatient services are reimbursable. (N11, 29 years experience).

Right now, home visits are completely self-paid, while outpatient care is partially reimbursed. I wonder if it’s possible in the future to link online orders with insurance to reduce the financial burden on patients. (N12, 20 years experience).

Support from the national government is needed. Including these costs in health insurance could alleviate financial pressure on the public. (N8, 13 years experience).

These support needs, while expressed as requests, also imply criticism of current institutional failures to provide basic safeguards.

Theme 4: Directions for improvement

Grounded in their frontline experiences, nurses proposed concrete strategies to improve the service model. These suggestions centered on process optimization, expanded outreach, safety enhancement, and cost reduction.

We could focus on promoting the service in welfare institutions and nursing homes—they have greater demand for home visits. (N1, 15 years experience).

Regular seminars should be held to share experiences and learn better ways to handle different situations. Better communication among staff is needed. (N3, 20 years experience).

The company car arrangement should be optimized to allow nurses to start from home. (N4, 20 years experience).

We could coordinate within the department to have an intern accompany and assist during visits. (N6, 15 years experience).

Personally, I find fetching the kit troublesome, so I prepared one in our department. The home visit procedure could be more detailed, and annual training should be organized. When suture removal is needed, we should bring a doctor. (N7, 21 years experience).

If the same patient orders both urinary and gastric tube services, only one nurse should be assigned. The cost for a home visit is quite high, and some patients cannot afford it. Insurance coverage would definitely reduce the cost burden. (N8, 13 years experience).

We should consider expanding the service range. Families with concerning social habits or history of drug use should be flagged in the system. The nursing department should clearly inform nurses and emphasize safety precautions. Bringing one or two interns, preferably male students, would be helpful. (N10, 11 years experience).

I feel a bit nervous going alone, so I always take an intern from the department. Whenever possible, I bring a male student with me. (N12, 20 years experience).

Importantly, most suggestions remain at the level of individual workarounds rather than systemic reforms, revealing a gap between frontline creativity and organizational accountability. While nurses‘ improvisations—bringing interns, preparing personal kits, using private vehicles—demonstrate remarkable adaptability and professional commitment, they also signal a troubling normalization: systemic gaps are being managed through individual sacrifice and unpaid labor rather than addressed through institutional responsibility.

This pattern reflects what we term responsibility transfer: the unspoken expectation that nurses should absorb the costs, risks, and coordination burdens of an under-resourced service model. When a nurse drives her own car to a remote mountain home without travel subsidy, she is not merely coping—she is substituting for missing institutional infrastructure. Distinguishing between legitimate professional discretion (e.g., clinical judgment) and inappropriate burden-shifting (e.g., bearing financial loss) is essential for designing sustainable INS. Without explicit organizational policies that assume responsibility for logistics, safety, and compensation, individual coping risks becoming a permanent substitute for systemic accountability.

Synthesizing the findings: tensions, trade-offs, and hidden contradictions.

Beyond listing nurses’ perceptions of value, barriers, support needs, and improvement ideas, a deeper interpretive reading reveals several underlying tensions that nurses navigate in their daily practice.

Tension 1: Professional commitment versus personal cost

Nurses consistently expressed strong intrinsic motivation—patient gratitude, skill development, professional identity—yet simultaneously reported bearing out‑of‑pocket travel expenses, safety fears, and schedule disruptions. Rather than withdrawing, they continued to participate, suggesting that professional obligation and perceived patient need override individual cost considerations in the short term. However, several nurses (e.g., N5, N8) hinted that this imbalance is not sustainable without institutional change. This tension reflects a quiet form of professional altruism that may mask structural exploitation if left unaddressed.

Tension 2: Policy enthusiasm versus on‑ground reality

National and regional policies promote INS as a scalable innovation, but nurses in this study described a system operating without insurance reimbursement, logistical support, or safety protocols. The implementation gap between policy rhetoric and frontline conditions creates what one nurse implicitly described as a “make‑do” culture: nurses improvise solutions (e.g., preparing their own kits, taking male interns for safety, using personal vehicles) to fill institutional voids. This gap transforms nurses from service providers into uncompensated system integrators, a role not formally recognized or rewarded.

Tension 3: Service value versus structural unsustainability

All nurses affirmed the value of INS for patients and themselves, yet they also identified features that undermine long‑term viability: 24‑hour advance booking that fails to meet urgent needs, rigid qualification rules that send two nurses for two procedures, and service radius limits that exclude many potential patients. These operational contradictions mean that even highly motivated nurses cannot deliver optimal care within existing rules. The result is a system that is valued but not viable—a critical insight for policymakers.

Tension 4: Individual coping versus collective solution—or when resilience becomes a trap

Nurses‘ proposed improvements—taking interns, coordinating with head nurses, bringing doctors, preparing personal kits, using private cars—are largely individual or small‑team workarounds rather than systemic reforms. These strategies are creative and pragmatic, yet they share a critical feature: they transfer the burden of system gaps onto individual nurses. Consider three examples from our data: (a) driving a personal vehicle to a remote area without reimbursement (N5, N4); (b) bringing male interns for safety because no formal security protocol exists (N10, N12); (c) preparing a personal kit because supply logistics are fragmented (N7). In each case, the nurse performs uncompensated labor (financial, emotional, organizational) that should be the responsibility of the institution.

We distinguish between two analytically separate concepts

Individual adaptive strategies—legitimate, temporary adjustments made by professionals within their discretion to meet patient needs (e.g., adjusting communication style, re‑ordering tasks). These are normal and often desirable.

Systemic responsibility transfer—when recurring gaps in infrastructure, policy, or resources are consistently absorbed by front-line workers through personal cost, without institutional remedy. This is not adaptation; it is hidden subsidization of the system.

Our data suggest that INS implementation in this under‑resourced setting has crossed from the former into the latter. Nurses have internalized the expectation that they should “make it work” even when root causes (no insurance coverage, no travel subsidy, no safety protocols) lie entirely outside their control. This internalization is reinforced by professional altruism (“patients need us”) and by the absence of formal grievance mechanisms. The result is a resilience trap: nurses’ very competence and commitment become the reason system deficits remain unaddressed. Hospital administrators and policymakers may perceive no urgent need for reform as long as nurses continue to fill the gaps—at their own expense.

Breaking this trap requires a deliberate policy shift: explicitly naming which responsibilities belong to the system and which to the individual. For example, safety protocols, travel subsidies, and insurance coverage are non‑negotiable system obligations. Nurses should not be required to compensate for their absence through personal risk or financial loss. Recognizing this boundary is not a critique of nurses’ creativity but a call for institutional accountability.

Together, these tensions illustrate that nurses’ accounts are not merely a list of positives and negatives, but a narrative of negotiated participation under structural disadvantage. Understanding these trade‑offs is essential for designing interventions that go beyond surface‑level fixes.

The four themes are not discrete but dynamically interwoven. The service value identified by nurses—patient convenience, professional fulfillment, and institutional reputation—provides the rationale for sustaining and scaling the program. However, this value can only be realized when practical barriers (systemic and operational) are adequately addressed. These barriers, in turn, give rise to specific support needs, including team collaboration and policy reform. The proposed directions for improvement reflect nurses’ pragmatic responses to these needs and barriers, forming a cyclical logic: recognizing value reveals the urgency of overcoming barriers; overcoming barriers requires targeted support; and implementing improvements reinforces and expands the value delivered. Together with the tensions articulated above, these themes illustrate a feedback loop essential for the iterative optimization of “Internet+Nursing Services.”

Discussion

This study explored clinical nurses’ perceptions of INS in a highly aging yet economically underdeveloped region of Western China. The four identified themes—service value, practical barriers, support needs, and improvement directions—are deeply interconnected. In this discussion, we draw on Social Exchange Theory solely as a post-hoc interpretive lens to help make sense of the patterns observed in nurses’ accounts. Social Exchange Theory conceptualizes human behavior as emerging from a subjective evaluation of rewards and costs within a relationship or role [12]. When applied retrospectively and heuristically, it offers one useful vocabulary for describing how nurses in our study narrated their continued participation despite significant personal burdens. We emphasize three boundaries of this theoretical use: (a) the theory did not guide data collection, coding, or theme development; (b) it is not used to generate normative recommendations directly—any policy or management suggestions presented in this discussion derive from nurses’ reported statements and our inductive analysis, not from the theory itself; and (c) we do not assume that nurses engage in explicit, rational, or self-interested cost-benefit calculations. Rather, the theory serves as a sensitizing device [14] that helps organize and interpret the recurring tension between perceived value and experienced hardship that participants spontaneously described.We emphasize that this theoretical framing is applied retrospectively for interpretive purposes and did not guide data collection or analysis.

Before discussing broader implications, we emphasize the scope of our findings. This study provides an in-depth account of how 12 experienced female nurses in one Western Chinese tertiary hospital perceive the rewards, barriers, and support needs of INS. While their narratives reveal important tensions and practical challenges, these data alone cannot support definitive conclusions about organizational readiness, policy effectiveness, or system-level sustainability across different hospitals, regions, or stakeholder groups (e.g., managers, dispatchers, patients). Where we discuss policy or organizational implications, we do so as reasoned interpretations from nurses’ perspectives—not as direct empirical findings—and we explicitly note where broader claims would require additional evidence.

Micro-level: Individual nurses‘ exchange calculus as interpreted through social exchange theory

We first examine how individual nurses narrated the rewards and costs of INS participation, as this micro-level perspective reveals the personal calculus underlying their continued engagement.Nurses’ accounts contained repeated references to what can be retrospectively framed as rewards and costs. Consistent with prior studies in developed cities [16–18], nurses derive significant intrinsic value from home-visit services.Empirically, participants reported intrinsic rewards such as patient gratitude (“their happiness gives me a strong sense of accomplishment”—N7), skill development (“my catheterization technique became more proficient”—N8), and professional identity (“I feel proud to provide nursing care at home”—N9). They also reported substantial personal costs: out‑of‑pocket travel expenses (“I have to drive myself, which increases my expenses”—N5), safety fears (“I think safety is a very important consideration when I provide home visits alone”—N1), and schedule disruptions (“I might be called in on my day off—this affects my personal life”—N5). These reported costs are disproportionately higher than those documented in studies from Eastern China, where logistical support and institutional safety nets are more developed [19–21].

Interpreted through a social exchange lens, this pattern can be seen as an imbalanced exchange from the nurses‘ perspective: the perceived rewards, while meaningful, are delivered at a consistently high personal cost. Social Exchange Theory would predict that when costs persistently exceed rewards without structural correction, voluntary participation may decline over time [12]. Importantly, this theoretical inference does not itself prescribe specific interventions. Instead, it draws attention to features that nurses themselves identified as unsustainable. Our recommendation—that hospitals consider travel subsidies, flexible scheduling, and safety safeguards—is grounded directly in participants’ explicit requests (e.g., “the company car arrangement should be optimized to allow nurses to start from home”—N4; “bringing one or two interns, preferably male students, would be helpful”—N10) and in the observed pattern of their accounts, not in a theoretical deduction from Social Exchange Theory.This recommendation aligns with broader evidence that perceived organizational support is a significant protective factor against nurses’ perceived stress [22, 23] and that work–family conflict—which our participants described in terms of scheduling disruptions and personal life interference affects some Chinese nurses [24].

Meso-level: Institutional conditions that enable or constrain exchange

Moving from individual perceptions to organizational conditions, we next analyze the institutional environment that enables or constrains equitable exchange for nurses. Empirically, nurses described several institutional gaps that increase their personal effort without commensurate organizational support. Inefficient qualification requirements, fragmented material supply, and insufficient multidisciplinary collaboration increase the effort nurses must expend to deliver care [10, 25]. These included: inefficient qualification rules that required two nurses for two procedures (“sending two people for two procedures is a waste of manpower and time”—N9); fragmented material supply (“I find it very troublesome to get the service kit from the hospital every time”—N7); and lack of staffing relief (“if I go out for a home visit, the ward will be short‑staffed”—N8). Nurses also articulated what they needed from the institution: head nurse scheduling adjustments, colleague accompaniment, and access to specialist physicians (Theme 3).

From the vantage point of Social Exchange Theory, these accounts suggest that the meso‑level institutional environment currently fails to provide reciprocal support for nurses‘ extra‑role efforts. In exchange terms, nurses are investing additional labor (travel, emotional safety work, coordination) without receiving equivalent organizational rewards (time, safety, financial compensation). Social Exchange Theory would imply that for sustainable participation, the institution must function as a fair exchange partner by reducing nurses’ personal costs. Again, however, this is a theoretical interpretation, not a direct finding. Our practical recommendation—that hospital leadership normalize home‑visit duties within routine workload calculations rather than treating them as extra tasks—is based on nurses‘ explicit statements about scheduling conflicts and staffing shortages, not on Social Exchange Theory itself. We note that our findings are consistent with broader evidence that perceived organizational support and nurse managers’ leadership are protective factors against nurse stress [22, 26].

Macro-level: Policy environment as the structural context of exchange

Finally, we consider the policy landscape as the macro-level structural context that shapes both patient access and nurses’opportunities for professional reward.Empirically, nurses repeatedly noted that the complete absence of health insurance coverage for INS created a financial barrier for patients, which in turn reduced service demand and, indirectly, nurses‘ opportunities to gain professional rewards. This contrasts sharply with pilot initiatives in Zhejiang Province [27, 28] and international models in Australia [29, 30] where home-based nursing services are partially or fully reimbursed through social or long-term care insurance. Several participants explicitly linked cost to patient refusal (“after we mentioned the price, he hesitated and didn’t choose it”—N9) and called for policy change (“it would be great if our service fees could be covered by health insurance”—N9; “including these costs in health insurance could alleviate financial pressure”—N8).

When interpreted through Social Exchange Theory, this macro‑level policy void can be understood as a structural exchange deficit. Patients face a high out‑of‑pocket cost with no third‑party subsidy; nurses face reduced demand and thus fewer opportunities to realize the professional rewards they value. In well‑developed INS settings (e.g., Ningbo, Hangzhou), partial insurance reimbursement functions as a macro‑level reward that lowers patient barriers and normalizes demand [27, 28]. The Social Exchange Theory interpretation helps explain why nurses repeatedly returned to the topic of insurance: without policy mediation, the individual exchange calculus remains tilted toward cost, threatening long‑term sustainability.

However, our proposal that INS be integrated into health insurance reimbursement systems is not a theoretical derivation from Social Exchange Theory. Rather, it is a policy recommendation directly grounded in nurses‘ repeated, unprompted statements about patient cost concerns and their observed effect on service uptake. Social Exchange Theory provides a coherent language for articulating why this recommendation addresses a structural imbalance, but it does not generate the recommendation. We present this proposal for consideration by policymakers, not as a finding of this study. We also acknowledge that testing the impact of insurance integration on nurse participation and patient access requires future health economics and implementation research beyond the scope of this qualitative investigation.

Clarification on theoretical interpretation

To avoid any misunderstanding of how Social Exchange Theory was used in this study, we reiterate: the theory served exclusively as a post‑hoc interpretive aid to help organize and make sense of patterns in nurses‘ narratives. It did not guide data collection (the interview guide was developed from literature and pre‑interviews, without reference to Social Exchange Theory), coding (all codes were inductively derived from participants’ words), or theme development (the four themes emerged from data, not from theoretical domains). The theory was applied retrospectively during the discussion phase to provide one possible vocabulary for describing the tension between rewards and costs that nurses spontaneously reported. Consequently, none of the study‘s conclusions or recommendations depend on the validity of Social Exchange Theory; they stand on the empirical data alone. The Social Exchange Theory interpretation is offered as an additional perspective, not as a foundational claim.

What is distinct about the Western China context?

The tensions identified above are not merely local idiosyncrasies; they reflect a specific configuration of resource scarcity that distinguishes our setting from the eastern Chinese contexts where most INS research has been conducted. Here we briefly synthesize four contextual differences, each of which amplifies a tension already noted in the Results.

First, absence of insurance reimbursement is not merely a policy gap but a fundamental exclusion mechanism (echoing Tension 2: policy enthusiasm vs. on‑ground reality). In eastern pilot cities, partial coverage makes out‑of‑pocket costs manageable [27, 28]; in our region, 100% self‑pay directly caused patients to forego services, and nurses repeatedly cited cost as the primary reason for low order volume.

Second, geographical and logistical scarcity transforms what eastern studies report as manageable inconvenience [19, 20] into personal financial and safety liabilities—nurses driving themselves to remote mountain areas with no return transport and facing service radius limits as low as 10 km (Tension 1: professional commitment vs. personal cost).

Third, safety risks are amplified by systemic isolation. Eastern studies often assume backup systems (e.g., GPS tracking, paired visits) [29]; here, nurses described entering high‑risk homes alone, performing difficult procedures without real‑time support, and managing fear individually (Tension 4: when resilience becomes a trap).

Fourth, institutional capacity constraints—chronic ward understaffing—turn a single home visit into a direct threat to inpatient coverage, a tension between inpatient and community nursing roles not foregrounded in previous qualitative literature.

In sum, these four differences do not merely add severity to known barriers; they transform the nature of INS implementation from an operational challenge into a structural one. Our findings therefore provide an empirical baseline for regions at an earlier stage of INS diffusion, highlighting which interventions (e.g., insurance integration, travel subsidies, safety protocols) are may be considered as essential prerequisites in similarly under‑resourced settings. However, because our data come from a single hospital and a purposive sample of experienced, motivated INS providers, these implications should be applied with caution. Readers should consider that the barriers and needs identified may be more pronounced—or qualitatively different—for nurses who chose not to participate in INS or who work in other organizational contexts.

Building directly on the fourth difference above—the routinization of individual workarounds in the absence of institutional support—we now examine the boundary between adaptive coping and system responsibility.

From individual coping to systemic accountability

The preceding analysis of safety and logistical gaps leads to a critical distinction: legitimate individual adaptation versus inappropriate system responsibility transfer.Individual adaptation includes clinical judgment in unfamiliar settings or flexible communication—normal professional discretion. Responsibility transfer occurs when recurring system gaps (e.g., no travel subsidy, safety protocols, or insurance) are consistently absorbed by nurses through personal cost, without institutional remedy. Examples from our data include driving private vehicles without reimbursement, bringing male interns for safety because no formal protocol exists, and preparing personal kits due to fragmented supplies.

This pattern signals a resilience trap: nurses’ commitment and ingenuity become the very reason system deficits remain unaddressed. Administrators may perceive no urgent need for reform as long as nurses continue to “make it work” at their own expense. Breaking this trap requires explicitly naming which responsibilities belong to the system—safety protocols, travel subsidies, insurance coverage—and holding institutions accountable for providing them. Sustainability should be evaluated not only by patient outcomes but by the proportion of system gaps closed through policy versus filled through individual sacrifice.

In summary, our discussion has moved across three analytical levels: micro (individual nurses’ reward‑cost calculus), meso (institutional support and staffing), and macro (policy environment). Across these levels, the Western China context amplifies known barriers into qualitatively distinct challenges that require context‑sensitive responses.

Implications for nursing education and leadership development

The findings also carry important implications for nursing education and leadership. Educational curricula should incorporate competencies specific to digital and home-based care, including risk assessment, inter-professional communication, and the use of mobile health technologies. Simulation-based training on managing unpredictable home environments and de-escalating family anxiety could better prepare nurses for the realities of INS. Furthermore, nursing leadership development must prioritize skills in resource negotiation, interdisciplinary team building, and policy advocacy. Head nurses and nursing directors in Western China, in particular, require support to articulate frontline needs to hospital administration and local government. Cultivating such leadership capital can transform individual coping strategies into collective organizational responses, shifting the exchange balance from cost-heavy to reward-sustainable.

Situating INS implementation within broader nursing workforce evidence

As detailed in the Discussion, the barriers and support needs identified in this study—safety fears, financial burden, scheduling disruptions, and fragmented assignments—are not unique to the INS context. Rather, they reflect long-standing structural conditions of nursing work in China. A growing body of evidence has shown that Chinese nurses consistently report high levels of work-related stress driven by cumulative factors including heavy clinical workloads, organizational demands, promotion pressure, and work–family conflict [24]. Crucially, recent longitudinal research by Cao et al. [24] demonstrates that nurse burnout remained stable before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating that long-term systemic stressors—not acute events—are the primary drivers of occupational distress. The systemic nature of nurse burnout in China has been further validated by a recent mixed-methods study analyzing over 10,000 social media posts, which developed a Systemic-Pressure Model demonstrating that burnout is a product of systemic healthcare system factors rather than individual adaptation failure [31].

This finding is essential for interpreting INS-related challenges: if burnout is fundamentally a product of persistent structural conditions (e.g., chronic understaffing, effort–reward imbalance, insufficient organizational support), then adding new duties such as INS without addressing those underlying conditions will merely exacerbate an already strained workforce.

What the INS context adds is a specific amplification mechanism. When nurses deliver INS without sufficient travel subsidies, safety protocols, or insurance reimbursement, the same underlying vulnerabilities (e.g., understaffing, lack of organizational support, effort–reward imbalance) become more visible and more damaging. For example, chronic ward understaffing turns a single home visit into a direct threat to inpatient coverage; absence of travel subsidies transforms an inconvenience into a personal financial liability. Thus, INS does not create new stressors in a vacuum—it activates and exacerbates pre-existing occupational strains.

This understanding shifts the focus from service-model tweaks to foundational workforce conditions. Sustainable INS requires adequate staffing to absorb home visits without compromising ward care, leadership that normalizes community nursing as routine practice, organizational policies that provide travel subsidies and safety safeguards, and national insurance coverage to reduce patient cost barriers. Without these foundational supports, INS risks becoming yet another layer of uncompensated responsibility on an already overstretched nursing workforce.In line with Cao et al. [24], we argue that interventions targeting INS sustainability must be evaluated not only for their direct effects on service delivery but also for their capacity to reduce, rather than add to, the long-term cumulative stress burden on nurses.

Conclusion

This study reveals that while INS generates substantial value for patients, nurses, and hospitals, their sustainability in under-resourced settings remains precarious due to operational barriers, safety risks, limited awareness, and absence of insurance reimbursement. Based on the experiences of nurses in this study, we propose three priority areas for consideration by hospital administrators and policymakers. These priorities are derived from nurses’ reported concerns and our interpretive analysis; they are presented as recommendations rather than direct findings. While further research is needed to assess generalizability, the following areas address the most salient concerns raised by frontline providers in this under-resourced setting:

Strengthen safety protocols and competency-based training.

Strengthen safety protocols and competency-based training.

Integrate services into health insurance reimbursement systems.

Integrate services into health insurance reimbursement systems.

Build interprofessional collaboration and digital health capacity.

Build interprofessional collaboration and digital health capacity.

In the specific under‑resourced setting of this study (a single tertiary hospital in Western China with no insurance reimbursement, severe geographical constraints, and lack of institutional safety nets), addressing these priorities appears essential for service viability in similar contexts. Nevertheless, our participants were exclusively experienced, self‑selected nurses who had already committed to INS; their perspectives may not represent those of less engaged nurses, junior staff, or nurses who declined participation. Therefore, these priorities should be interpreted as context‑bound and require validation in larger, more diverse samples across different INS implementation stages.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single center design restricts the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings, particularly those with different levels of digital maturity or organizational cultures. This study focused solely on the perspectives of frontline nurses, which limits our ability to draw conclusions at the organizational or policy level. The views of nurse managers, logistics coordinators, and patients—key actors in the INS ecosystem—were not captured. Second, despite our efforts to achieve maximum-variation purposive sampling, the sample lacked junior nurses (due to qualification requirements for home nursing services) and male nurses (because of the extreme scarcity of male nurses in this region and their absence from home nursing practice during the recruitment period). These contextual characteristics may have limited the diversity of perspectives captured.Furthermore, because our sample consisted exclusively of nurses who had already accepted INS assignments and met relatively high seniority criteria, we cannot infer the perspectives of nurses who declined participation, those with less experience, or those who left INS after trying it. This self-selection bias means our findings likely reflect the views of more engaged, resilient, or positively disposed nurses, potentially overrepresenting favorable attitudes and underrepresenting barriers that lead to non-participation or dropout. Claims about the sustainability of INS from a workforce perspective would therefore be strengthened by future research that includes these missing voices.

Similarly, our macro-level policy interpretations (e.g., regarding insurance reimbursement) are derived from nurs-es’ reports of patient cost concerns; direct policy analysis or patient cost-effect-iveness studies lie outside our dataset. Last but not least, data were collectedthrough self‑report measures, which are susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias.

Future research should address these limitations by first employing multi-stakeholder triangulation, including nurse managers, scheduling coordinators, and patients/caregivers, to provide a more holistic, system-level understanding of INS. Subsequent studies should also adopt multi-center designs across hospitals with varying characteristics in different regions of China. Such studies would allow for the quantification of the cost-effectiveness of specific safety interventions and the impact of insurance integration on nursing practice. Furthermore, comparative studies across regions with distinct socioeconomic, policy, and cultural backgrounds could illuminate how contextual factors shape the delicate balance between professional rewards for nurses and systemic costs for healthcare organizations. Finally, qualitative inquiries may provide deeper insights into the decision-making processes of nurses who decline to participate in internet nursing services, thereby informing targeted strategies to enhance engagement.
