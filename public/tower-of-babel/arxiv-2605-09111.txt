Introduction

For a number field
K
and a prime number
p
, we denote by
\mu_{p}(K),\lambda_{p}(K),\nu_{p}(K)
the Iwasawa invariants of the cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-extension
K_{\infty}
of
K
. A standard result by Ferrero-Washington
[
FW79
]
states that
\mu_{p}(K)=0
for any abelian number field
K
. Greenberg
[
Gre76
]
conjectured that
\lambda_{p}(K)
is also zero when
K
is a totally real number field. Most of the progress on this conjecture has focused on the case of real quadratic number fields and specifically for
p=2
.

A classical theorem of Iwasawa states that if
K/\mathbb{Q}
is a real quadratic extension and
p\nmid h_{K}
, then
\lambda_{p}(K)=0
. Whenever Iwasawa’s result applies, we call this a ”trivial case”.

In this paper, we focus on the cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-extension of real quadratic number fields.
Genus theory and the theorem of Rédei - Reichardt imply that for
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, the following cases are ”trivial” (in the sense that
2
does not split in
K
and the class number is odd):

D=\begin{cases}2,\\
p,\quad p\equiv 5\mod 8,\\
q,\quad q\equiv 3\mod 4,\\
pq,\quad p\equiv 3\mod 8,\quad q\equiv 7\mod 8\end{cases}

A lot of research has been devoted to the study of the ”non-trivial” cases, with various partial results. In this paper, we focus on the following case:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{pq})
, where

p\equiv 1\mod{8},\qquad q\equiv 9\mod{16},\qquad\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)=-1.

Assume

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)_{4}\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}\left(\frac{pq}{2}\right)_{4}=-1

and

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)_{4}=-1\quad\text{or}\quad\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=-1.

Then
\lambda_{2}(K)=0
.

The proof has two main parts. First, using a capitulation argument modeled on
Kumakawa
[
Kum21
]
, we show that it is enough to prove
q(K_{2})<4
, where
q(K_{2})

is the Hasse unit index of the biquadratic extension
K_{2}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}
, with
K_{2}
being the second layer of
K_{\infty}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{1}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
. Second, let
F_{1}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{(2+\sqrt{2})D}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}})
. We
compute the Hasse index by a square-class analysis of

E(K_{1})E(F_{1})E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})\subseteq E(K_{2}),

where
E(L)
denotes the units of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{L}
, and prove that
q(K_{2})\leq 2
.

Organization of the paper.

In Section 3, we recall known cases and place Theorem 1.1 among the
remaining cases for real quadratic fields. Section 4 reviews Greenberg’s
criterion and the ambiguous class groups used later. Section 5 collects the
genus theory, Rédei matrix, Hilbert symbol, and biquadratic class number
formulae needed in the proof. Section 6 treats an auxiliary case with

(p,q)\equiv(5,1)\mod 8
(See Proposition
\ref{th: case (5,1)})
. Finally, Section 7 proves Theorem 1.1: we first
analyze the fields
K_{1}
and
F_{1}
, then use a Kumakawa-type
capitulation argument to reduce the proof to bounding the Hasse unit index

q(K_{2})
, and conclude by proving
q(K_{2})\leq 2
.

4.2
Greenberg’s criterion

Theorem 4.2

(Greenberg)
.

Fix a prime
p
and assume
K
is a real quadratic number field such that there exists a unique prime
\mathfrak{p}
above p and that it’s totally ramified in the cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
- extension
K_{\infty}/K
. Then

B_{n}^{\prime}=1\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\Rightarrow\mu_{p}(K)=\lambda_{p}(K)=0.

Also, if
A_{0}
capitulates in
K_{\infty}
, then the same conclusion holds.

Proof.

This follows from
[
Gre76
, Theorem 1]
. We briefly sketch the argument here.

Let
i_{0,n}:A_{0}\to A_{n}
. Write
\mathfrak{p}
for the prime in
K
above
p
and
D_{n}
for the subgroup of
A_{n}
consisting of ideal classes which divide
p
.

Claim:

B_{n}^{\prime}=i_{0,n}(A_{0})D_{n}
.

Assuming the claim,
B_{n}^{\prime}=1
implies that
i_{0,n}(A_{0})=1
. Therefore, every ideal class in
A_{0}
becomes eventually principal. Hence,
[
Gre76
, Theorem 1]
implies that
\mu_{p}(K)=\lambda_{p}(K)=0
.

Proof of claim:
Since
\mathfrak{p}
is totally ramified in
K_{\infty}/K
, the primes of

K_{n}
above
\mathfrak{p}
are fixed by
\Gal(K_{n}/K)
. Hence,

D_{n}\subseteq B_{n}^{\prime}
. Also, the extension of any ideal class from

K
to
K_{n}
is strongly ambiguous, so

i_{0,n}(A_{0})D_{n}\subseteq B_{n}^{\prime}.

Conversely, let
[\mathfrak{c}]\in B_{n}^{\prime}
, and choose a representative

\mathfrak{c}
fixed by
\Gal(K_{n}/K)
. Split

\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is supported at the primes above

\mathfrak{p}
, and
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
is prime to
\mathfrak{p}
. Then

[\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{p}}]\in D_{n}
. Since
K_{n}/K
is unramified outside

\mathfrak{p}
, the
\Gal(K_{n}/K)
-invariant ideal
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
is the extension of
an ideal
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}
of
K
, say

\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{K_{n}}.

Thus

[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{n}]=[\mathfrak{c}]\,[\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{p}}]^{-1}\in A_{n}.

Taking norms to
K
, we obtain

N_{K_{n}/K}\bigl([\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{n}]\bigr)=[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]^{[K_{n}:K]}=[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]^{p^{n}}.

Since the left-hand side is the norm of a class in
A_{n}
, it lies in

A_{0}
. Hence

[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]^{p^{n}}\in A_{0}.

But if a class
x\in\Cl(K)
satisfies
x^{p^{n}}\in A_{0}
, then its
prime-to-
p
part must be trivial; therefore
x\in A_{0}
. Applying this to

x=[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]
, we get
[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}]\in A_{0}
. Consequently

[\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}]=[\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{n}]\in i_{0,n}(A_{0}),

and since
[\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{p}}]\in D_{n}
, we conclude that

[\mathfrak{c}]\in i_{0,n}(A_{0})D_{n}.

Therefore,

B_{n}^{\prime}=i_{0,n}(A_{0})D_{n}.

∎

Genus theory and Rédei Matrix

In this section, we recall the definition and some properties of the Rédei matrix associated to a real quadratic number field. We start with some important results on biquadratic extensions.

5.1
Biquadratic Extensions

We define a biquadratic extension as any extension of number fields
L/K
such that

\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

Most of the work on imaginary biquadratic extensions
L/\mathbb{Q}
was done by
Kuroda
[
Kur43
]
. For real biquadratic extensions, we have the following result
by Kubota, commonly known as the Kubota–Kuroda formula.

Theorem 5.1

(Kubota–Kuroda
[
Kub56
]
)
.

Let
L/\mathbb{Q}
be a totally real biquadratic extension, with unit group
E(L)

and
2
-class group
A(L)
. Let
L_{1},L_{2},
and
L_{3}
be the quadratic subfields of
L
.
Let
\epsilon_{i}
be the fundamental unit of
L_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
.
Let

q(L):=\bigl[E(L):\langle-1,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\rangle\bigr]

be the Hasse unit index of
L
. Then

|A(L)|=\frac{1}{4}\,q(L)\,|A(L_{1})|\,|A(L_{2})|\,|A(L_{3})|.

Further, a system
\Gamma
of fundamental units of
L
(F.S.U.) is one of the following
possibilities:

1.

\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\}
,

2.

\{\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\}
,

3.

\{\epsilon_{1},\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}},\epsilon_{3}\}
,

4.

\{\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\}
,

5.

\{\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}},\epsilon_{2},\sqrt{\epsilon_{3}}\}
,

6.

\{\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{3}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}}\}
,

7.

\{\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\}
.

Any unit
\epsilon_{i}
that appears under the square root sign is assumed to have
norm equal to
1
, except for the last case, where all the units can have the same
norm, either all
1
or all
-1
.

The theorem has been generalized to biquadratic extensions over arbitrary real number fields by Lemmermeyer
[
Lem94
]
. We get the following for a biquadratic extension over
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
L/\mathbf{Q}_{1}
be a biquadratic extension. Let
L_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
be the three quadratic subextensions of
L/\mathbf{Q}_{1}
and
h_{i}=h(L_{i})
. Then

h(L)=\frac{1}{8}q(L)\cdot h_{1}\cdot h_{2}\cdot h_{3},

where
q(L)=[E(L):E(L_{1})E(L_{2})E(L_{3})]
is the Hasse unit index.

Furthermore, if
L=F(\sqrt{d})
and
u\in F^{\times}
, then

u\in L^{2}\iff\begin{cases}u\in F^{2}\text{ or }\\
u\in dF^{2}\end{cases}

5.2
Genus Theory

We recall some standard results in classical genus theory:

Theorem 5.3

(Genus Theory)
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
be a real quadratic number field with discriminant
d_{K}
. Then

rk_{2}(Cl^{+}(K))=\omega(d_{K})-1,

where
\omega(d_{K})=\#\{p|d_{K}\}
and
Cl^{+}(K)
denotes the narrow class group. Also,

[Cl^{+}(K):Cl(K)]=\begin{cases}1\quad N(\epsilon_{D})=-1\\
2\quad N(\epsilon_{D})=1\end{cases}

Naturally, we need a criterion to determine the value of the norm of the fundamental unit. Scholz comes to the rescue:

Theorem 5.4

(Scholz,
[
Sch35
,
Kap72
]
)
.

Let
D=pq
with
p,q
odd primes and
p\equiv q\equiv 1\mod 4
.
Put
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, let
N=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}
be the norm,
let
h
(resp.
h^{+}
) be the (resp. narrow) class number of
K
,
and let
\epsilon_{D}
be a fundamental unit of
K
.

1.

If
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)=-1
, then

h\equiv h^{+}\equiv 2\mod 4,\qquad\text{and}\qquad N(\epsilon_{D})=-1.

2.

If
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)=1
, then:

(i)

If
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)_{4}=-\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)_{4}
, then
h^{+}\equiv 4\mod 8,\qquad 2h\equiv 4\mod 8,\qquad\text{and}\qquad N(\epsilon_{D})=1.

(ii)

If
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)_{4}=\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)_{4}=-1
, then

h\equiv h^{+}\equiv 4\mod 8,\qquad\text{and}\qquad N(\epsilon_{D})=-1.

(iii)

If
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)_{4}=\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)_{4}=1
, then

h^{+}\equiv 0\mod 8.

We therefore have a complete classification of the
2
-rank of the class group. For the
4
-rank, we use the Rédei matrix.

5.3

[

]

Let

(Kumakawa-type capitulation argument)
.

Let

be the cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-extension, and let

be the

-primary part of

be a prime of

, and

Let
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2}
be the primes of

above

\mathfrak{q}
. Let

be the maximal unramified abelian

2

Proof.

This is the class-field-theoretic argument used by Kumakawa in the proof of

[
Kum21
, Theorem 2.1.5]
; we recall the short argument for completeness.

Since

\Gal(L_{1}K_{2}/K_{2})\simeq\Gal(L_{1}/K_{1})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

Let

The image of

in

Thus, it suffices to prove

.

Since each

splits completely in

, class field

By assumption,

. Hence,

. Since each
\mathfrak{q}_{i}

Therefore

.

Now suppose that

. Since

, we have

The Artin map

. Since

. But

also has order

Thus,

The primes
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
and

are conjugate over

and

are conjugate under

By assumption

In both cases, the image of

7.4

Throughout this subsection put

\left(\tfrac{2}{p}\right)_{4}\left(\tfrac{2}{q}\right)_{4}\left(\tfrac{D}{2}\right)_{4}=-1

Let

.

Lemma 7.7
.

With the notation above,

q(K_{2})=[E(K_{2}):H]=\left|\frac{H\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}}{H^{2}}\right|=|S|.

Proof.

Since
K_{2}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}
is biquadratic with quadratic subfields

, for every

we have

u^{2}=\frac{N_{K_{2}/K_{1}}(u)\,N_{K_{2}/F_{1}}(u)\,N_{K_{2}/\mathbf{Q}_{2}}(u)}{N_{K_{2}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(u)}.

Notice that
E(K_{2})^{2}\subset H
and hence

\frac{H\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}}{H^{2}}=\frac{E(K_{2})^{2}}{H^{2}}.

Since
K_{2}
is totally real, we have

E(K_{2})[2]=\{\pm 1\}\subset H.

Therefore, the squaring map induces an isomorphism

E(K_{2})/H\simeq E(K_{2})^{2}/H^{2}.

Thus,

E(K_{2})/H\cong E(K_{2})^{2}/H^{2}=\frac{H\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}}{H^{2}}.

Taking cardinalities gives

q(K_{2})=[E(K_{2}):H]=\left|\frac{H\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}}{H^{2}}\right|=|S|.

E(\mathbf{Q}_{1})/E(\mathbf{Q}_{1})^{2}=\{1,-1,\epsilon_{2},-\epsilon_{2}\}.

Lemma 7.10
.

Under the standing assumptions of this subsection, one has

E(K_{1})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(K_{1})^{2},\qquad E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{2}.

Proof.

Since
K_{2}=K_{1}(\sqrt{\alpha})
, the quadratic square criterion gives, for
u\in K_{1}^{\times}
,

u\in K_{2}^{\times 2}\iff u\in K_{1}^{\times 2}\text{ or }u\in\alpha K_{1}^{\times 2}.

If
u\in E(K_{1})
, the second possibility is impossible:
\alpha
has odd valuation at the primes of
K_{1}
above
2
, whereas
u
is a unit. Hence

E(K_{1})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(K_{1})^{2}.

Similarly,
K_{2}=\mathbf{Q}_{2}(\sqrt{D})
. Thus, for

u\in K_{2}^{\times 2}\iff u\in\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{\times 2}\text{ or }u\in D\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{\times 2}.

If
u\in E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})
, the second possibility is impossible because
D=pq
has odd valuation at primes above
p
and
q
. Therefore,

E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{2}.

∎

Lemma 7.11
.

, we may write, modulo

Lemma 7.12

Assume

Lemma 7.14

(A norm obstruction)
.

Assume

\left(\tfrac{2}{p}\right)_{4}\left(\tfrac{2}{q}\right)_{4}\left(\tfrac{D}{2}\right)_{4}=-1.

Then

Lemma 7.16
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}_{2}=(\sqrt{2})
be the dyadic prime of
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
, and let
\mathfrak{l}
be the unique prime of
F_{1}
above
\mathfrak{P}_{2}

1.

E(F_{1})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(F_{1})^{2}

2.

\mathfrak{l}
is not principal.

3.

Neither
\sqrt{2}
nor
\alpha
is a norm from
F_{1}^{\times}
to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\times}
.

Moreover,

\sqrt{2}\in N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(F_{1}^{\times})\iff\left(\tfrac{2}{p}\right)_{4}=\left(\tfrac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=1,

and

\alpha\in N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(F_{1}^{\times})\iff\left(\tfrac{p}{2}\right)_{4}=\left(\tfrac{q}{2}\right)_{4}=1.

Consequently, since
q\equiv 9\mod{16}
,

E(F_{1})\cap K_{2}^{\times 2}=E(F_{1})^{2}\iff\left(\tfrac{2}{p}\right)_{4}=-1\quad\text{or}\quad\left(\tfrac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=-1.

Proof.

First note that
-1
is a norm from
F_{1}
to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
. Indeed, the computation of the
-1
column in Theorem
7.1
gives

(-1,\delta)_{v}=1

at every ramified finite place

of
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
, and the local norm condition is automatic at the remaining finite places and at the real places. Hence, the Hasse norm theorem gives

-1\in N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(F_{1}^{\times}).

Since
K_{2}=F_{1}(\sqrt{\alpha})
, the quadratic square criterion gives, for every
u\in F_{1}^{\times}
,

u\in K_{2}^{\times 2}\iff u\in F_{1}^{\times 2}\text{ or }u\in\alpha F_{1}^{\times 2}.

Thus, a nontrivial class in

and

Now
\alpha=\epsilon_{2}\sqrt{2}
and
\mathfrak{P}_{2}
ramifies in

\mathfrak{P}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{F_{1}}=\mathfrak{l}^{2},\quad(\alpha)=(\sqrt{2})\mathcal{O}_{F_{1}}=\mathfrak{l}^{2}.

is principal. This proves

Assume next that
\mathfrak{l}
is principal, say
\mathfrak{l}=(x)
. Then

(N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(x))=N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(\mathfrak{l})=\mathfrak{P}_{2}=(\sqrt{2}),

so

for some

. Since
E(\mathbf{Q}_{1})=\langle-1,\epsilon_{2}\rangle

-1
is a norm, and since every square in

is congruent modulo norms to either

or
\epsilon_{2}

\sqrt{2}
or

is a norm from
F_{1}
to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}

Conversely, suppose
\sqrt{2}
or
\alpha
is a norm from
F_{1}
to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
. Choose

with

N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(x)=\sqrt{2}\quad\text{or}\quad N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}(x)=\alpha.

Then

N_{F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}}((x)\mathfrak{l}^{-1})=1.

By Hilbert 90 for ideals, there is a fractional ideal
\mathfrak{a}
of
F_{1}
such that

where
\sigma
is the nontrivial element of
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{1}/\mathbf{Q}_{1})

Remark 7.2
.

We finish by comparing our argument with the work of Kumakawa
[
Kum21
]
.
In
[
Kum21
]
, the author studies the case

K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{pq}),\qquad p\equiv 3\mod 8,\qquad q\equiv 9\mod{16},\qquad\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)=-1,\qquad\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=1.

They prove the following result.

Theorem

(Kumakawa)
.

With the above assumptions, if
r_{4}(A_{2})=1
, then

|A_{n}|=|A_{2}|

for all
n\geq 2
. Therefore,
\lambda_{2}(K)=0
.

The proof in
[
Kum21
]
and the proof of Theorem
1.1

have the same general flavor. In both cases, the key point is to force the
capitulation of the class in
A_{0}
by analyzing the second layer
K_{2}
and
then applying Greenberg’s criterion.

There is, however, an important difference between the two arguments. In
Kumakawa’s setting, the necessary bound on the Hasse unit index is essentially
automatic. Equivalently, the analogue of the condition

q(K_{2})<4

does not require a separate computation. In our setting this was the main
extra difficulty: the reduction to capitulation only gives a useful conclusion
after proving that the Hasse unit index of the biquadratic extension

K_{2}/\mathbf{Q}_{1}
is small. This is why a substantial part of Section
7

is devoted to a square-class computation of

E(K_{1})E(F_{1})E(\mathbf{Q}_{2})\subseteq E(K_{2}),

which ultimately gives

q(K_{2})\leq 2.

A second difference is the form of the final hypothesis. Kumakawa assumes the
class-group condition

r_{4}(A_{2})=1.

This is already very close in spirit to our argument. The new feature of our result is that we replace
such a condition on
A_{2}
by explicit numerical conditions. Namely, the
quartic-symbol assumptions

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)_{4}\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}\left(\frac{pq}{2}\right)_{4}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)_{4}=-1\ \text{ or }\ \left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=-1

are used to prove the required bound on the Hasse unit index, and hence the
required smallness of
A_{2}
.

It seems reasonable to expect that an analogous refinement should be possible
in Kumakawa’s case. More precisely, one should be able to analyze the relevant
unit index and Rédei-type data in the family

p\equiv 3\mod 8,\qquad q\equiv 9\mod{16},\qquad\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)=-1,\qquad\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)_{4}=1,

and thereby obtain explicit quartic-symbol conditions which imply

r_{4}(A_{2})=1
. Such a criterion would turn Kumakawa’s result into a fully
explicit numerical criterion.
