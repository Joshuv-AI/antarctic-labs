What hip and spinopelvic characteristics are associated with ischiofemoral impingement?
Authors: Vincent J Leopold, Alberto Telias, Dyan V Flores, Jeroen Verhaegen, Zoe Kerhoulas, Andrew Speirs, Paul E Beaulé, George Grammatopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo determine the prevalence of MRI-confirmed ischiofemoral impingement (IFI) in young patients with nonarthritic hip pain, and to evaluate the associations of femoral, acetabular, pelvic, and spinopelvic parameters with IFI.MethodsWe included 250 patients with nonarthritic hip pain who underwent standardized imaging (MRI and CT, lateral standing and deep-seated spinopelvic radiographs). IFI was diagnosed by quadratus femoris oedema/atrophy. IFI-positive/negative hips were compared for morphological and spinopelvic parameters. Underlying pathologies (e.g. femoroacetabular impingement, dysplasia) were documented. Multivariate regression identified independent predictors of IFI and determinants of quadratus femoris space (QFS).ResultsIFI was identified in 14/250 patients (5.6%), all female. IFI cases had significantly reduced ischiofemoral space (24.8 mm (SD 6.7) vs 34.9 mm (SD 10.2); p < 0.001) and QFS (9.2 mm (SD 3.4) vs 20.7 mm (SD 7.0); p < 0.001). IFI cases demonstrated larger ischial angle (135.7° (SD 3.0°) vs 132.2° (SD 4.0°); p = 0.002), higher femoral version (16.5° (SD 10.3°) vs 10.4° (SD 12.3°); p = 0.031), and reduced femoral (34.2 mm (SD 5.6) vs 37.7 mm (SD 5.9); p = 0.016) and acetabular offset (29.4 mm (SD 3.5) vs 32.0 mm (SD 4.8); p = 0.036). In standing, IFI cases tended to have lower lumbar lordosis (LL) (55.31° (SD 7.95°) vs 59.91° (SD 10.50°); p = 0.077), higher pelvic tilt (15.68° (SD 4.73°) vs 12.39° (SD 6.20°); p = 0.047), and smaller mismatch between pelvic incidence and LL (–2.39° (SD 7.18°) vs –7.82° (SD 10.00°); p = 0.034). The change in LL across standing to deep-flexed sitting transition (ΔLL) was lower in IFI cases (50.31° (SD 10.89°) vs 56.89° (SD 10.64°); p = 0.020). Multivariate regression identified reduced QFS (odds ratio (OR) 1.92, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.89; p < 0.001) and greater LL (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.33; p = 0.047) as independent predictors of IFI. Size of QFS was associated positively with femoral offset (β = 0.35; p < 0.001) and mismatch between pelvic incidence and LL (β = 0.18; p = 0.028), and negatively with ischial angle (β = –0.34; p < 0.001), femoral version (β = –0.14; p = 0.016), and standing pelvic tilt (β = –0.16; p = 0.026).ConclusionIFI occurred in 5.6% of patients and was characterized by a distinct morphometric and sagittal alignment profile, including increased femoral version, reduced offset, greater ischial angle, reduced LL, increased pelvic tilt, and diminished spinal motion. These findings support a multifactorial model in which hip morphology and spinopelvic alignment jointly influence posterior hip clearance in IFI.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(6):792–801.Keywords: Ischiofemoral impingement, Spinopelvic interaction, Hip biomechanics, Femoral version, Pelvic tilt, Hip-spine syndrome, hips, MRI, quadratus femoris, oedema, femoroacetabular impingement, femoral version, hip pain, Spinopelvic parameters, Lumbar lordosis

Introduction

Ischiofemoral impingement (IFI) is an increasingly recognized cause of hip pain and dysfunction, resulting from mechanical compression between the lesser trochanter and the ischium.1-8 This narrowing of the ischiofemoral space (IFS) can lead to irritation and inflammation of adjacent soft tissues, particularly the quadratus femoris muscle, manifesting as pain and restricted mobility.1,3,4,7-10 Patients with IFI commonly experience deep, posterior hip pain that may radiate to the groin or thigh.6 Symptoms often worsen with hip flexion, extension, or internal rotation, suggesting a mechanical component to the condition. Some patients report a snapping or locking sensation, particularly during long-stride walking, likely due to dynamic impingement of the lesser trochanter against the ischium.4,6

The diagnosis of IFI primarily relies heavily on MRI, which allows visualization of the IFS and detection of soft-tissue changes such as quadratus femoris oedema or atrophy. Reduced IFS width and quadratus femoris muscle oedema are considered key MRI indicators of IFI.3,6,8,10,11 A 2015 meta-analysis by Singer et al7 established threshold values for diagnosing IFI on MRI, reporting that an IFS ≤ 15 mm and a quadratus femoris space (QFS) ≤ 10 mm are associated with sensitivities of 74.9% and 78.7% and specificities of 81% and 74.1%, respectively. However, these dimensional thresholds alone are not diagnostic; the presence of soft-tissue changes such as quadratus femoris oedema or atrophy remains essential for confirming IFI.

The aetiology of IFI is multifactorial, involving anatomical variations of the femur, pelvis, and acetabulum. Morphological characteristics, such as increased femoral version, coxa valga, or acetabular retroversion, may reduce the ischiofemoral distance. Sex-specific pelvic anatomy, particularly a wider intertuberous distance, more commonly observed in females, has also been linked to a narrower IFS, potentially increasing IFI risk.4,7,8,12,13 Beyond these structural variations, alterations in spinopelvic parameters have been hypothesized to influence the spatial relationship between the ischium and femur. Changes in pelvic tilt (PT), lumbar lordosis (LL), and pelvic incidence (PI) may affect hip biomechanics and modulate the dynamic ischiofemoral distance.14-19 However, the extent to which sagittal spinopelvic alignment contributes to the development of IFI remains insufficiently understood.14

The primary aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of MRI-confirmed IFI in a consecutive cohort of patients presenting with hip pain. In addition, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between femoral, acetabular, pelvic, and spinopelvic morphological (static and dynamic) parameters and the presence of IFI. We hypothesized that MRI-confirmed IFI would represent an uncommon finding among patients presenting with hip-related pain. Furthermore, we hypothesized that specific femoral, acetabular, and pelvic morphologies, as well as both static and dynamic spinopelvic parameters, are associated with the presence of IFI.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study used a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained database of nonarthritic patients presenting with hip pain at a high-volume, academic, tertiary-referral centre (The Ottawa Hospital, Ottawa, Canada). This study was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective design.

A total of 385 patients with nonarthritic hip pain were screened for inclusion. Patients were eligible if they presented with hip pain and had undergone comprehensive imaging, including hip or pelvic MRI, pelvic and femoral CT, and standardized standing and deep-seated radiological assessments of the spine and pelvis.

Exclusion criteria were applied to minimize confounding factors. Patients were excluded if they had a history of hip surgery, congenital hip deformities, severe osteoarthritis (Tönnis grade ≥ 2), or neuromuscular disorders affecting hip or pelvic mechanics. Additionally, patients without preoperative MRI or CT and those with poor-quality imaging that did not allow for accurate morphometric measurements were excluded from analysis. Study cohort

A total of 250 patients were included (mean age of 35.1 years (16 to 55; SD 8.6), and mean BMI of 27.3 kg/m² (13.9 to 40.0; SD 6.8). The cohort comprised 168 females (67.2%) and 82 males (32.8%). The most frequent diagnosis was femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) in 139 patients (55.6%), followed by hip dysplasia (n = 54; 21.6%), mixed-type morphology with both hip dysplasia and FAI features (n = 27; 10.8%), and isolated labral pathology (n = 22; 8.8%). One patient (0.4%) had sequelae of slipped capital femoral epiphysis, and in seven cases (2.8%) classification based on morphology was inconclusive. Patients were then organized into IFI and non-IFI groups by MRI findings by a fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologist (DVF). IFI classification was based solely on predefined MRI criteria and was independent of the primary clinical diagnosis assigned at presentation. The underlying hip pathology (e.g. FAI, dysplasia, mixed morphology) was documented separately. Thus, MRI-defined IFI was considered a radiological finding within the broader clinical context rather than an exclusive clinical diagnosis. The patient was considered MRI-positive for IFI if the following criteria were met: oedema of the quadratus femoris muscle defined as increased signal intensity on fat-suppressed, fluid-sensitive, or short TI inversion recovery sequences; and/or atrophy of the quadratus femoris muscle defined as volume loss and intramuscular fatty streaking on non-fat-suppressed T1-weighted or proton-density-weighted sequences.

Imaging assessment and measurements

All imaging studies were obtained using standardized protocols. Hip and spinopelvic parameters were measured using a combination of radiographs, CT, and MRI. Two observers (one orthopaedic hip preservation fellow (AT) and one musculoskeletal radiologist (DVF)) performed all measurements independently and were blinded to each other’s results.

Standardized anteroposterior pelvic radiographs were used to assess hip morphology. The following parameters were measured: lateral centre-edge angle, acetabular inclination angle, presence of crossover sign, and anterior and posterior wall indices.

All spinopelvic radiographs were obtained according to a standardized departmental protocol and included lateral radiographs of the lumbar spine, pelvis, and femur in both standing and deep-seated positions. For standing radiographs, patients stood upright with knees fully extended and feet positioned shoulder-width apart, maintaining a relaxed, natural posture while looking straight ahead. For the deep-seated radiograph, patients were seated with the femora parallel to the floor and the trunk leaning maximally forward. They were instructed to bend forward as much as possible to a position of comfort without abducting or rotating their thighs. LL was defined as the angle between the superior endplates of L1 and S1.20,21 PT was measured as the angle between the vertical and a line connecting the midpoint of the sacral endplate to the centre of the femoral heads.20,21 PI was measured as the angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral endplate and a line connecting its midpoint to the femoral head axis.20,21 Sacral slope (SS) was measured as the angle between the sacral endplate and a horizontal reference line.20,21 The PI–LL mismatch was calculated by subtracting LL from PI, representing the discrepancy between an individual’s PI and LL as an indicator of spinopelvic balance.20,21 The proximal femoral angle (PFA) was measured as the angle between the anterior cortex of the femoral shaft and the anterior pelvic plane, reflecting the degree of hip flexion.20,21

Dynamic postural changes were assessed by calculating the difference between standing and deep-flexed sitting values for each parameter. All Δ-values were defined as the value in standing minus the value in deep-flexed sitting (Δ = standing − sitting). Accordingly, positive Δ-values indicate a reduction of the respective parameter during flexion. The following parameters were analyzed: ΔLL, ΔSS, ΔPT, and ΔPFA. These Δ-values represent the dynamic mobility of the respective segment during postural transition.20-22 To quantify the relative contribution of hip versus lumbar motion during sagittal flexion, the hip user index was calculated as ΔPFA / (ΔLL + ΔPFA) × 100, with higher values indicating predominant use of the hip for sagittal adjustment and values ≥ 80% being defined as hip users.21

Pelvic and femoral morphometric measurements were obtained from standardized axial CT images with multiplanar reconstruction. Acetabular parameters included acetabular version and acetabular offset. Femoral parameters comprised the femoral neck-shaft angle, femoral version (measured using the Reikerås method),23 and femoral offset. Femoral offset (FO) is the length (mm) of the perpendicular line of the femoral axis at the tip of the greater trochanter to the femoral head centre. Acetabular offset (AO) is the distance (mm) from the femoral head centre to the ilioischial line, parallel to the pelvis. Neck–shaft angle (NSA) is the angle between the longitudinal axis of the femoral neck and the longitudinal axis of the femoral shaft.

In both groups, measurements of the ischiofemoral and quadratus femoris spaces were made. IFS is the smallest distance between the lateral cortex of the ischial tuberosity and medial cortex of the lesser trochanter, and QFS is the smallest space for passage of the quadratus femoris muscle delimited by the superolateral surface of the hamstring tendons and the posteromedial surface of the iliopsoas tendon or lesser trochanter.8 All measurements were performed on axial images through the lesser trochanter (Figure 3).

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics were described using frequencies, means, and SDs. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. Between-group comparisons of morphological and spinopelvic parameters were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous, non-normally distributed data, and Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. To account for known sex-related differences in pelvic morphology and spinopelvic parameters, all analyses were repeated in a female-only subcohort, as IFI was observed exclusively among females. To identify independent predictors of MRI-confirmed IFI, a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated for each predictor. In a secondary analysis, a linear regression model was constructed to evaluate which parameters independently correlated with QFS in the overall cohort. Interobserver reliability was assessed between observers using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs; two-way random, absolute agreement). ICC values ranged from 0.751 to 0.966. According to conventional thresholds, values between 0.61 and 0.80 indicated substantial agreement, and values > 0.81 indicated almost perfect agreement. Repeated measurements in 20% of cases showed similarly high reproducibility (ICC 0.720 to 0.990). A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power v. 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universtät, Germany) to assess whether the sample size was sufficient to detect a statistically significant difference in IFS between patients with and without MRI-confirmed IFI. Based on the observed means, the calculated effect size (Cohen’s d) was 1.056. With a significance level of α = 0.05, a sample size of 14 in the IFI group and 236 in the non-IFI group yielded a statistical power of 96.9%. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were managed using Excel v. 16.16.2 (Microsoft, USA) and analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows v. 30.0 (IBM, USA).

Results

Prevalence of IFI

Overall, 14 patients (5.6%), all of whom were female, showed MRI-based evidence of IFI. Patients with IFI had a mean age of 39.0 years (SD 9.5) and a mean BMI of 26.4 kg/m² (SD 3.4). In comparison, patients without IFI had a mean age of 34.9 years (SD 8.5) and a mean BMI of 27.4 kg/m² (SD 6.9). There were no significant differences in age (p = 0.113) or BMI (p = 0.990) between patients with and without IFI. Within this IFI group, five patients (35.7%) had hip dysplasia, four (28.6%) had FAI, three (21.4%) had mixed-type morphology, and two (14.3%) had isolated labral pathology. Morphological parameters associated with IFI

Patients with IFI demonstrated smaller ischiofemoral (24.8 mm (SD 6.7) vs 34.9 mm (SD 10.2); p < 0.001) and quadratus femoris (9.2 mm (SD 3.4) vs 20.7 mm (SD 7.0); p < 0.001) spaces than those without IFI. The ischial angle was significantly greater in the IFI group (135.7° (SD 3.0°) vs 132.3° (SD 4.0°); p = 0.002). Femoral (34.2 mm (SD 5.6) vs 37.7 mm (SD 5.9); p = 0.016) and acetabular (29.4 mm (SD 3.5) vs 32.0 mm (SD 4.8); p = 0.036) offsets were significantly smaller in IFI hips. Reikerås femoral version was higher in the IFI group (16.5° (SD 10.3°) vs 10.4° (SD 12.3°); p = 0.031). Acetabular morphology was largely comparable between groups, apart from a slightly higher posterior wall index in IFI patients (1.03 (SD 0.1) vs 0.93 (SD 0.2); p = 0.030). This effect did not persist in sex-specific analysis (p = 0.073). There were no significant differences in neck-shaft angle (p = 0.384), central acetabular version (p = 0.132), or cranial acetabular version (p = 0.833). When analysis was limited to females, IFI hips continued to show significantly smaller ischiofemoral and quadratus femoris spaces and a greater ischial angle as well as higher femoral version, confirming that these associations are independent of sex-related pelvic differences (Table II).

Spinopelvic parameters and IFI

IFI-positive hips showed reduced LL in standing position (55.3° (SD 7.9°) vs 59.9° (SD 10.5°); p = 0.077) and higher PT (15.7° (SD 4.7°) vs 12.4° (SD 6.2°); p = 0.047). The mismatch between PI and LL (PI-LL) was significantly smaller in the IFI group (–2.4° (SD 7.2°) vs –7.8° (SD 10.0°); p = 0.034). The distribution of PT and LL in relation to QFS is illustrated in Figure 5. Other spinopelvic parameters did not show differences. Dynamic analysis of postural change from standing to seated position revealed a significantly smaller reduction in LL in IFI patients (ΔLL: 50.3° (SD 10.9°) vs 56.9° (SD 10.6°); p = 0.020). Independent predictors of IFI

Multivariate logistic regression identified two independent predictors of IFI: reduced QFS (OR 1.92, 95% CI not shown; p < 0.001) and increased LL in standing position (OR 1.16; p = 0.047). The model demonstrated an excellent fit (Nagelkerke R² = 0.615), with a classification accuracy of 96.4% (99.5% for non-IFI, 41.7% for IFI cases). Discussion

In this cohort, MRI-based evidence of IFI was identified in approximately one in 20 patients (5.6%), representing an uncommon but clinically relevant finding. Notably, all affected patients were female, underscoring the importance of maintaining diagnostic awareness in women presenting with hip pain. IFI was observed in conjunction with various structural hip pathologies seen in a specialized clinic, including hip dysplasia, FAI, mixed-type morphology, and isolated labral pathology. Although the present study does not determine the specific contribution of IFI to patients’ symptoms or clarify the exact pain location, the coexistence of IFI with other morphological abnormalities underscores the need to consider it as a potential source of symptoms. In patients presenting with ill-defined or posterior hip pain, IFI should therefore be considered as a relevant (additional) differential diagnosis, regardless of the underlying primary pathology.25,26 Furthermore, this study identified distinct pelvic and femoral characteristics in IFI-positive hips. The reduced ischiofemoral and quadratus femoris spaces likely reflect underlying bony morphology, characterized by a greater ischial angle, lower femoral and acetabular offsets, and increased femoral version, which together narrow the posterior ischiofemoral interval. In addition, IFI patients showed increased PT and a smaller PI–LL mismatch in standing position, reflecting a flatter lumbar spine and a more posteriorly rotated pelvis. This combination effectively places the hip in relative hyperextension (for a given PI), thereby reducing posterior clearance and predisposing to ischiofemoral narrowing.27,28 In multivariate analysis, smaller QFS and greater LL in standing were independent predictors of IFI. Secondary regression showed that a smaller QFS was associated with lower femoral offset, higher femoral version, a steeper ischial angle, reduced PT, and a smaller PI–LL mismatch. As illustrated in Figure 5, QFS was closely linked to spinopelvic parameters, with higher PT and lower LL correlating with smaller QFS, emphasizing the functional interplay between spinal alignment and posterior hip clearance. These results support our hypothesis that both bone morphology and dynamic alignment of the pelvis and spine contribute to the development of IFI, underscoring their interdependence.

Our study found that femoral version was significantly higher in IFI patients compared with the control group, supporting the notion that increased anteversion may contribute to ischiofemoral narrowing by altering proximal femoral orientation and reducing the effective posterior offset of the lesser trochanter.1,9,29 Notably, femoral version was a significant negative predictor of QFS in our regression analysis, indicating its contribution to IFI. These findings are consistent with those of Heiman et al,12 who also identified IFI in a non-selected cohort of nonarthritic patients with hip-related symptoms in a relevant proportion of cases and identified increased femoral version as an independent risk factor for IFI. Moreover, our results align with Boschung et al,1 who observed a high prevalence of IFI in patients with markedly increased femoral version under specific dynamic conditions. The greater ischial angle observed in IFI patients supports the hypothesis that pelvic morphology contributes to IFS narrowing, consistent with prior studies showing that a wider intertuberous distance – commonly seen in females – is associated with reduced IFS.4,7,8 This is further reflected in our cohort, where all IFI-positive cases were female. In addition to static anatomical constraints, dynamic factors appear critical: Atkins et al9 demonstrated via in vivo fluoroscopy that IFS varies across the gait cycle, reaching its minimum during external rotation and hip extension, particularly during level and incline walking compared with standing. Their study also showed that females have consistently smaller IFS values under dynamic conditions, despite comparable joint kinematics, suggesting that sex-specific pelvic morphology may predispose posterior impingement risk. Importantly, they reported that static supine MRI tends to overestimate IFS compared with functional imaging, reinforcing the notion that static morphometrics may underestimate clinically relevant impingement.9

QFS represents a structural measure of posterior hip clearance and was not part of the diagnostic definition of IFI, which was based on MRI evidence of quadratus femoris oedema and/or atrophy. The association between reduced QFS and IFI therefore reflects a plausible local mechanical relationship rather than a circular definition. Notably, spinopelvic parameters were independently associated with QFS, suggesting that sagittal alignment may indirectly influence posterior clearance. Patients with IFI demonstrated a characteristic sagittal spinopelvic alignment marked by reduced LL and increased PT in standing, despite similar PI. This configuration reflects a disturbed sagittal balance characterized by a flatter lumbar spine and increased standing PT due to a posteriorly rotated pelvis, resulting in reduced posterior clearance. The PI-LL mismatch was significantly smaller in the IFI group, indicating that LL was lower than expected for the given PI, consistent with a relative ‘flat-back’ alignment. Moreover, dynamic assessment revealed a smaller reduction in LL (i.e. spinal flexion) during postural transition from standing to deep-flexed sitting, reflecting a stiffer lumbopelvic segment. These clinical findings align with the cadaveric study by Spencer-Gardner et al,30 which showed that PT directly affects IFS dimensions. A posterior tilt of 10° reduced the IFS to 24.3 mm, while an anterior tilt of 10° increased it to 37.2 mm, corresponding to a 53% increase across a 20° tilt range.30 Together with our in vivo data, this suggests that even modest posterior tilting, when combined with unfavorable femoral and pelvic morphology, can markedly narrow the ischiofemoral interval. The present findings further support the concept that reduced LL and increased PT in IFI patients likely reflect an altered sagittal alignment pattern of the lumbopelvic complex rather than purely compensatory mechanisms as previously suggested.14,31 Loss of lumbar curvature, elevated PT, and altered PI-LL balance are characteristic of degenerative sagittal malalignment and may initiate a vicious cycle in which progressive flat-back deformity and posterior pelvic rotation further reduce posterior clearance and aggravate IFI. The clinical implications of IFI and its associated morphological factors likely extend beyond the hip joint, reflecting a complex interplay within the posterior kinetic chain. Although the precise mechanisms remain poorly defined, reduced LL in IFI patients may arise from two distinct scenarios: flattening of the lumbar spine or a functional postural adaptation accompanying posterior pelvic rotation and hip extension.32,33 However, the concomitant finding of reduced ΔLL in our cohort argues against a purely compensatory mechanism and rather supports the presence of structural or degenerative stiffness within the lumbopelvic segment. These observations highlight that IFI should be considered within the broader context of spinopelvic mechanics, particularly in patients presenting with coexisting low back symptoms. From a clinical perspective, the primary contribution of this study lies in advancing the understanding of IFI within the broader hip-spine interaction model. While our findings do not directly mandate changes in surgical indications or treatment algorithms, they suggest that patients presenting with posterior or ill-defined hip pain may benefit from a more comprehensive evaluation of spinopelvic alignment in addition to local morphological assessment. Recognizing IFI as a condition influenced not only by focal structural narrowing but also by sagittal alignment may support more individualized diagnostic pathways and improve interpretation of imaging findings within the overall biomechanical context.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design relied on pre-existing imaging data, limiting the ability to control for potential confounders such as activity level, symptom duration, or prior treatments. These factors may have influenced both hip and spinal alignment and should be addressed in prospective studies. Second, although our analysis included a comprehensive quasistatic assessment of anatomical and radiological parameters, it did not account for dynamic changes in the IFS during functional activities such as walking or terminal hip extension. As previous studies have shown, static imaging may overestimate posterior clearance and fail to capture the positional narrowing that occurs during movement. Future studies should incorporate dynamic imaging and functional assessment to better reflect real-life joint mechanics and symptom-provoking conditions. Finally, clinical assessments including specific clinical findings and physical examination findings were not available in all cases, limiting insight into how structural imaging findings correlate with symptom severity and physical examination tests. Integrating clinical data with imaging results will be essential to understand the true impact of IFI on patient function and guide individualized treatment planning.

In conclusion, IFI as diagnosed by the presence of pain and quadratus femoris muscle oedema and/or atrophy on MRI was identified in a small but clinically relevant subset of young patients with hip pain, occurring exclusively in females and often in conjunction with other structural abnormalities such as dysplasia or FAI. The condition was associated with a distinct morphometric profile, including increased femoral version, larger ischial angle, and reduced femoral and acetabular offset. In addition, IFI patients exhibited a characteristic spinopelvic alignment with reduced LL, increased PT, and stiffer lumbar spines, suggesting a complex interaction between hip morphology and sagittal balance.

Take home message

- In young patients with non-arthritic hip pain, MRI-confirmed ischiofemoral impingement (IFI) was uncommon, but showed a distinct hip–spine phenotype characterized by reduced quadratus femoris space, increased standing pelvic tilt, lower lumbar lordosis, smaller pelvic incidence-lumbar lordosis mismatch, and reduced lumbar motion.

- Clinically, these findings suggest that posterior or ill-defined hip pain with suspected IFI should prompt assessment of sagittal spinopelvic alignment, as posterior hip clearance may be influenced not only by local femoral and pelvic morphology, but also by lumbopelvic stiffness and sagittal imbalance.
