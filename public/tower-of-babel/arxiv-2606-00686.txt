Dialectics of Alignment: Harnessing Unsafe Knowledge for Dynamic Safety Routing

Abstract
The prevailing paradigm in large language model (LLM) alignment operates via erasure, filtering unsafe data or training models to strictly refuse harmful prompts. While effective at reducing immediate toxicity, this approach fundamentally constricts the model’s epistemological scope, resulting in over-cautious systems that output uninformative blanket refusals to sensitive yet benign queries. In this work, we challenge the orthodoxy that unsafe data must be discarded. We propose a dialectical approach to alignment, positing that unsafe data encodes rich, domain-specific knowledge critical for nuanced, safe, and informative generation. To operationalize this, we introduce SafeMoE , a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework that isolates unsafe knowledge into domain-specific Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA experts) trained exclusively on harmful corpora. To synthesize safety from these unsafe primitives, we train a lightweight gating network using a minimal, highly curated set of safe-informative responses. During inference, this router dynamically orchestrates the unsafe experts, effectively steering the generation trajectory to harness their deep domain knowledge while strictly enforcing safety constraints. Extensive empirical evaluations across stringent safety benchmarks demonstrate that SafeMoE is not only safer, achieving over a 20% relative improvement in safe response rate (more than a 15% absolute gain), but also produces more informative responses when safety and harmfulness are of paramount concern. Furthermore, the routing mechanism exhibits strong zero-shot generalization to unseen domains and broader safety tasks without domain-specific supervision. Our findings suggest a paradigm shift in alignment: true safety requires not the masking of unsafe knowledge, but its controlled integration. 1 1 1 This paper contains examples of harmful language and toxic content. Introduction

Fueled by large amounts of public training data
[
65
,
72
,
20
,
53
]
, large language models (LLMs) have achieved unprecedented performance across diverse downstream tasks. This shift has democratized AI, making it accessible to everyday individuals interacting with specialized services. However, this pervasive, domain-specific integration has raised critical concerns regarding the ethical alignment of LLMs and the societal risks posed by unregulated deployment
[
69
,
43
,
47
,
8
,
58
]
.

To mitigate these risks, recent efforts in
LLM safety
have focused on preventing the generation of harmful content. Yet, the prevailing alignment paradigm often over-corrects, causing models to strictly refuse prompts that exhibit even tangential hints of suspicious intent. Consequently, systems default to generic,
blanket refusals
, e.g.,
‘Sorry, I cannot help you.’
, particularly when navigating risky or complex queries
[
9
,
70
]
. While this “safety-by-erasure” approach reduces immediate toxicity, it severely degrades informativeness and user experience. Moreover, in contexts involving psychological distress (e.g., inquiries related to self-harm), blanket refusals can inadvertently drive well-intentioned users toward adversarial circumvention or migration to less-restrictive, unmoderated platforms, thereby exacerbating the likelihood of harmful outcomes
[
17
,
51
]
.

Developing models that are both safe and informative typically requires massive, high-quality datasets of safe responses
[
4
,
54
]
. Curating such data is prohibitively expensive due to the labor-intensive filtering, validation, and labeling required to ensure accuracy and alignment
[
10
,
28
,
61
]
. Conversely, unsafe data while potentially harmful, also encodes rich, domain-specific knowledge and is significantly easier to acquire, including via generation from unaligned models
[
67
]
. This asymmetry is especially acute in privacy-constrained settings: models trained without safety-aligned data readily produce unsafe outputs, yet privacy restrictions prevent the generation of high-quality safe data via external APIs. Consequently, unsafe data often remains the most abundant source of domain knowledge. This highlights our core research question:
Can we leverage unsafe responses to train models that respond safely and informatively instead of resorting to blanket refusals?

To this end, we challenge the prevailing practice of “safety-by-erasure” and instead propose a dialectical approach to alignment.
Our key insight is that true safety requires not the masking of unsafe knowledge, but its controlled integration. By isolating unsafe knowledge into specialized components, we hypothesize that its informative value can be harnessed while steering models toward safe outcomes using a minimal set of aligned examples. To operationalize this, we introduce
SafeMoE
, a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework that utilizes domain-specific Low-Rank Adapters (LoRAs) to “bottle” unsafe knowledge.
Uniquely, these experts encapsulate rich, domain-specific primitives that, despite their unsafe nature, are critical for detailed generation. To safely use this knowledge, we introduce a routing mechanism trained on a highly curated, minimal dataset of safe responses (fewer than 800 samples across a limited set of topics which can potentially decreases to 100).
During inference, the router acts as a knowledge curator, dynamically orchestrating the activation of unsafe experts to synthesize responses that are simultaneously safe and informative. This sparse routing mechanism allows the model to tap into the “unsafe” primitives to provide factual depth while strictly adhering to safety constraints.

Extensive empirical evaluations demonstrate that
SafeMoE
is simultaneously
safer
, effectively avoiding the exposition of harmful details, and significantly
more informative
than standard alignment baselines, replacing unhelpful refusals with constructive dialogue. Furthermore, rigorous human annotation studies corroborate our automated metrics, confirming that
SafeMoE
’s nuanced outputs strongly align with human safety preferences while providing superior utility.

Related Works

Mixture-of-Experts.
The Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm, introduced by
35
, has seen a resurgence as a piece in the development of LLMs
[
40
,
64
,
19
,
20
,
16
]
, where the conventional feed-forward network (FFN) layers are replaced with collections of specialized "expert" sub-networks. A gating mechanism (often termed a "router") dynamically directs tokens to a sparse subset of these experts for processing, enabling conditional computation that can significantly improve parameter efficiency. Modern MoE models can exhibit a variety of designs, such as the number of experts to which tokens are routed
[
26
,
40
]
or the sharing of experts between layers
[
64
]
.

Low-Rank Adaptation.
Despite the success of large pre-trained models, their scale makes full fine-tuning computationally prohibitive. LoRA
[
33
]
mitigates this by freezing pre-trained weights and injecting trainable rank decomposition matrices, a technique proven effective in resource-constrained settings
[
75
,
63
]
. Building on this and the MoE paradigm, Mixture-of-LoRAs
[
71
,
27
]
treats distinct LoRA modules as experts, employing per-token routing to select specific modules during inference.

LLM Safety and Security.
As LLMs become increasingly sophisticated, their potential for societal impact has brought critical safety considerations to the forefront. These include issues of biased responses, the leaking of private information, generation of harmful/offensive content or safety issues stemming from the ethics and morality of LLMs.
Many methods have been introduced for mitigating such concerns, such as learning to recognize potentially unethical instructions and using this as a signal to activate safety mechanisms that prevent harmful generation
[
55
,
23
]
, or to directly use training to better align models with human preferences
[
56
,
24
]
to ensure wider considerations. However, these methods can have potential limitations; tuning models can require substantial computational resources, while prompt manipulation remains possible even for guarded LLMs. Finally, while models have been tuned to refuse harmful generations
[
9
,
2
]
, this can be a potential issue; a refusal can signal to the attacker that the underlying information is potentially problematic, which may prompt them to further attempt to jail-break the model
[
68
,
14
]
. As such, considerations exist as to whether or not refusing to answer or providing a correct but uninformative response in such settings is of greater benefit.

3.1
Safety vs. Informativeness

Model
safety
often refers to the ability to avoid generating potentially harmful content, whether to oneself or others. However, safe responses can sometimes be vague or overly cautious, lacking the detail needed to satisfy user intent. One such case is refusal, where the model declines to answer out of concern that it could lead to direct or indirect harm.
Informativeness
, in this setting, refers to the model’s ability to provide relevant, accurate, and contextually useful responses, even when certain details must be withheld for safety reasons.
We define a response as
informative
if (1) it avoids blanket refusals and (2) provides relevant, helpful content without exposing content that causes harm.

Figure

1
provides an example where an individual wishes to “dump industrial wastewater into rivers”.
In the unsafe response, the model reveals harmful information, despite factual correctness. While refusal is possibly safer, it is not informative and does not provide an explanation to the user, a clear limitation of existing methods that can be over-conservative: they fail to directly distinguish between genuinely dangerous intent and legitimate behavior, such as scientific questions that tangentially relate to potentially dangerous topics,
e.g.
a scientist attempting to understand addictive substances for genuine research purposes. Finally, the safe-informative response provides clear information that is backed up directly by evidence, but simultaneously attempts to dissuade the user from directly attempting to follow through with an action that is unsafe.

This highlights the risks of refusal; many queries may not arise from adversarial intent but from genuine user confusion, distress, or a desire for knowledge
[
46
]
. Here, refusals can suppress valuable discussions, potentially pushing at-risk but well-intentioned users toward unsafe behaviors, rather than provide safer alternatives in a constructive manner. Learning to move beyond simple refusal is of growing importance
[
22
]
, and learning to provide more informative responses that remain safe through the proper framing and treatment of specific details has become increasingly relevant
[
74
,
78
,
76
]
.

3.2
Problem Setting

We consider a base language model
\mathcal{M}
and two types of training data.
First, we assume access to a large set of
unsafe
response data across
K_{\mathsf{us}}
different domains. We denote this as
\{\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\mathsf{us}}\}_{i=1}^{K_{\mathsf{us}}}
.
Second, we assume access to a limited amount of
safe-informative
response data
\{\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\mathsf{s}}\}_{i=1}^{K_{\mathsf{s}}}
across
K_{\mathsf{s}}
different domains, where
\left|\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\mathsf{s}}\right|\ll\left|\mathcal{D}^{j}_{\mathsf{us}}\right|\,\forall i,j\in[K_{\mathsf{s}}]\times[K_{\mathsf{us}}]
. Critically,
K_{\mathsf{s}}\ll K_{\mathsf{us}}
with less safe data per domain.
We wish to adapt
\mathcal{M}
to provide safe-informative responses across all domains.

Our key insight is that unsafe data, while risky, contains valuable knowledge. Rather than discard it, we train separate “expert” adapters on each unsafe domain, effectively bottling their knowledge. We then train a routing mechanism on safe examples that learns to selectively activate and combine these experts in ways that preserve informativeness while ensuring safety. The router acts as a “knowledge curator”, drawing from unsafe experts while steering toward safe responses.

Stages of
SafeMoE
. (1) Unsafe experts are trained using a large set of unsafe response data that can be split into domains. (2) Experts are used to train a router with a smaller set of safe response data. In the second stage, the experts are frozen, while only the router is trainable.

3.3
Sparse Mixture-of-LoRAs for Safe and Informative LLMs

We introduce
SafeMoE
, a framework that adapts a base LLM to generate safe yet informative responses by leveraging unsafe data through a sparse mixture-of-LoRA-experts architecture. A general depiction of this framework is provided in
Figure

2
. The model consists of a set of unsafe experts and a learned router that selects the top-
k
unsafe experts whose combined output yields a safe response. To achieve this, we propose a two-stage training procedure comprising expert training and router training.

Expert Training.

SafeMoE
first uses unsafe data,
\{\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\mathsf{us}}\}_{i=1}^{K_{\mathsf{us}}}
to train unsafe experts. For efficiency, we use low-rank adapters
[
33
]
to train different experts on each domain, using a standard supervised fine-tuning objective
[
24
]
to individually enable each adapter to adapt the base model to respond to a specific domain. This process produces a library of LoRA experts,
\left\{\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\ell}\right\}_{i=1}^{K_{\mathsf{us}}}
for layer
\ell
, where each
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\ell}
is defined by weights
\left({\bm{A}}^{i}_{\ell},{\bm{B}}^{i}_{\ell}\right)
such that
\Delta{\bm{W}}^{i}_{\ell}={\bm{B}}^{i}_{\ell}{\bm{A}}^{i}_{\ell}
is the additive weights applied by
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\ell}
.

Router Training.

To leverage the expressive power of unsafe datasets to generate safe responses, our method next tunes the router parameters using a cross-entropy loss such that, for any given example, the model selects a subset, top-
k
, of the trained expert adapters to use. Given a base model
\mathcal{M}
with
L
layers, we initialize a trainable router module at each layer
\ell
that is responsible for selecting which top-
k
experts will be used to compute the hidden activation
{\bm{h}}_{\ell}
. Specifically, each router is defined by a set of weights,
{\bm{V}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times{C}}
, where
C\leq K_{\mathsf{us}}
is the total number of unsafe experts used from the LoRAs library
\mathcal{L}
. The routing scores
r_{\ell}
are obtained from the input,
{\bm{x}}_{\ell}
, by
r_{\ell}={\bm{V}}_{\ell}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
.
Denote
R_{\ell}
as the set of indices of experts with top-
k
scores. The output can then be computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MoE}_{\ell}({\bm{x}}_{\ell})=\sum_{i\in R_{\ell}}w_{i}\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\ell}({\bm{x}}_{\ell}),\;\;\;w_{i}=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(r_{\ell}\right)_{i}

where
w_{i}
represents the normalized routing scores obtained via a softmax over the top-
k
, and
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\ell}({\bm{x}}_{\ell})
is the output of the
i
th LoRA,
\Delta{\bm{W}}^{i}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}={\bm{B}}^{i}_{\ell}{\bm{A}}^{i}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}
.
Given this, the output of each layer after merging the base weights of
\mathcal{M}
with the mixture of LoRA experts is:

{\bm{h}}_{\ell}={\bm{W}}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}+\mathrm{MoE}_{\ell}({\bm{x}}_{\ell})

Thus, at
every
layer and for
each
token, the router dynamically selects LoRA experts, allowing the model to flexibly combine the specialized capabilities of individual experts.

Is safe data all we need?

We fine-tune the base model using only the 800 safe responses by applying standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT). As shown in
Table

2
, this
SFT-800
baseline achieved comparable safety scores as

In contrast,
SafeMoE
achieves similar or better results using only a fraction of the safe data, reinforcing the core motivation of our approach: leveraging abundant unsafe data to reduce reliance on costly safe supervision.

Moreover, we trained four
safe experts
using the full
8{,}000

Safe-Only

, yielded further performance gains, demonstrating the benefits of abundant safe supervision. Do Unsafe Experts Matter?

To further assess the role of unsafe data, we constructed a baseline
Knowledge-Only
experts model composed exclusively of knowledge-domain experts, i.e. trained on datasets from fields like physics, chemistry, medicine, etc. Specifically, we trained LoRA experts on medical
[
41
]
, cyber-security, finance and mental health datasets.

The
Knowledge-Only Experts
model performs poorly on both fronts, highlighting that knowledge experts in isolation are insufficient for producing safe and helpful responses in sensitive contexts. In contrast, the unsafe experts, though trained on harmful data, encode knowledge that, when properly routed, enables the model to generate safe-informative responses. This underscores the central insight of our approach: unsafe data, when leveraged carefully, is a valuable resource for improving both safety and informativeness, especially in low-supervision regimes.

4.2.8
Why
SafeMoE
works? Dynamics of Routing

To understand how
SafeMoE
leverages informative features without internalizing harmful patterns, we ablate the routing granularity using top-1 selection. We compare
First-Layer Routing
(expert selected per-token, fixed across layers) against
Last-Token Routing
(expert fixed for the entire response based on the prompt’s final token). First-Layer Routing achieves high safety (
84\%
) and informativeness (
8.1
), whereas Last-Token Routing collapses to unsafe generations. This indicates that dynamic, per-token routing is critical for steering the early generation trajectory away from harmful manifolds. An activation analysis (
Appendix

M
) confirms this mechanism avoids routing collapse. The model actively distributes computation across the entire expert ensemble conditional on the input category.

5
Discussion

Leveraging Unsafe Data for Training.

While traditional alignment via RLHF
[
13
,
54
,
56
]
or projection-based methods like
SafeLoRA
use unsafe data primarily to define refusal boundaries, or prune safety-degrading weights
[
49
]
, our approach directly internalizes this data. By retaining unsafe modules rather than discarding them, SafeMoE leverages the latent informative features within harmful corpora to enhance model responsiveness while strictly enforcing safety constraints.

Merging of Expert Models.

Beyond static MoEs, recent
MoErging
techniques aggregate domain experts via weight averaging
[
62
]
or selective parameter importance
[
50
,
42
,
1
]
. However, these approaches often face scalability limits
[
34
]
or necessitate heavy data-dependent computation
[
42
]
, which is impractical in data-scarce regimes. Furthermore, model steering
[
59
]
remains susceptible to feature entanglement
[
25
]
. Our framework overcomes these constraints by utilizing LoRA’s data efficiency to learn from sparse datasets. By dynamically orchestrating these adapters, SafeMoE effectively synthesizes specialized expertise without the computational overhead or data requirements of traditional merging paradigms.

Safety Generalization to Unseen Domains.

Prior work shows that fine-tuning on specific safety behaviors can generalize to unseen or adversarial scenarios, particularly through reasoning-based alignment methods
[
44
,
31
,
80
]
. However, such approaches may introduce new vulnerabilities by weakening domain-specific safeguards, which can be exploited via adversarial encodings
[
73
,
57
,
36
]
.
As a further step, future work can explore whether stronger separation of domains within individual experts can help mitigate these concerns.
As another future direction is to investigate how our method can be integrated into existing MoE LLMs without introducing additional LoRA experts. An exploratory evaluation with additional knowledge experts, denoted
SafeMoE
-
XL-Knowledge
, in
Table

12
(
Appendix

H
) shows this further improves performance.

Limitations.

While
SafeMoE
significantly reduces overhead via LoRA experts, the linear scaling of the LoRA library and the per-token gating operation still introduce marginal memory and latency increases compared to a single dense model. Furthermore, maintaining diverse experts at scale may necessitate advanced load balancing to prevent potential routing bottle-necks or under-utilization of specialized safety primitives.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we present
SafeMoE
, a lightweight, mixture of low-rank adapters (LoRAs) to leverage the unsafe dataset to obtain safety and domain knowledge. By leveraging the large quantities of high-quality (informative), domain-specific yet unsafe response, we train various expert adapters that can then be merged within a mixture-of-experts paradigm, where a smaller quantity of informative safe response data can be used to train a router to leverage said unsafe experts to help guide the model towards safer and more informative responses. Results on a variety of safety domains shows
SafeMoE
outperforms various safety-tuned language models, while also being more effective than pre-existing methods when given only the limited safe data for training. Additional results confirm the robustness of our method in adversarial and data-scarcity settings, highlighting its generality and versatility.
Ultimately, our findings suggest a paradigm shift in alignment: true safety requires not the erasure of unsafe knowledge, but its controlled integration.

Appendix D
Hyperparameter Optimization and Architectural Details

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) Configuration.

For the expert modules, we parameterize the weight updates as
\Delta W=BA
, where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
. To determine the optimal intrinsic rank
r
, we evaluated the model’s representational capacity across a search space of
r\in\{32,64,128\}
on three distinct safety categories. We empirically observed that
r=32
achieves performance parity with higher ranks while minimizing parameter overhead. Consequently, this rank was uniformly adopted across all expert modules.

Router Optimization.

For the gating network, we conducted a grid search over the learning rate hyperparameter space
\eta\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5}\}
. Based on convergence stability and validation performance on our development set, we selected an optimal learning rate of
\eta=10^{-4}
.

Routing Mechanism Configuration.

Following standard formulations in sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures, we default to a Top-2 routing schema (
k=2
).

Limitations and Broader Impact

This paper presents research aimed at improving the safety alignment of large language models (LLMs). By developing an algorithm that generates safe responses to harmful queries, our goal is to reduce the risk of LLMs producing toxic, biased, or otherwise dangerous content in deployment.

Our methodology involves the use of publicly available datasets in the AI safety domain, which contain unsafe, offensive, or harmful content. Additionally, this paper includes explicit examples of harmful prompts and model responses. These examples are provided solely to scientifically demonstrate the effectiveness of our alignment method compared to baseline approaches. We caution readers that some content may be offensive.

We anticipate that the primary societal impact of this work is the enhancement of AI safety. We also recognize that alignment techniques can have dual-use potential. Nevertheless, we believe that the benefits of reducing objective harms—such as hate speech or instructions for illegal activities—significantly outweigh these risks.
