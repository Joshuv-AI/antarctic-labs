Assessment of 21st century skills among health management students in Türkiye: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Sema Dalkılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTwenty-first-century skills—such as critical thinking, problem solving, leadership, digital literacy, and innovation—are among the core competencies required for future health managers to function effectively within the complex and technology-driven structure of modern healthcare systems. The purpose of this study was to determine the 21st-century skill levels of undergraduate Health Management students in Türkiye and to examine whether these skills significantly differ according to demographic (gender, age, year of study) and academic (academic achievement and accreditation status) variables.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional descriptive design. Data were collected via an online survey from 565 undergraduate Health Management students across Türkiye. The Multidimensional 21st Century Skills Scale, consisting of five subdimensions, was used as the data collection instrument. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and multiple regression analysis were performed to analyze the data. A significance level of p < .05 was adopted.ResultsThe overall 21st-century skill levels of the students were found to be above average. The highest mean scores were observed in the Career Consciousness and Information and Technology Literacy dimensions, whereas the lowest means were obtained in the Entrepreneurship and Innovation and Critical Thinking and Problem Solving subdimensions. Regarding gender, a significant difference was found only in the Career Consciousness dimension in favor of female students (p < .05). In general, increases in age and year of study were associated with higher 21st-century skill levels. Academic achievement produced significant differences particularly in Information and Technology Literacy and Critical Thinking skills. While no significant differences were found regarding the official accreditation status of universities, students’ perceived accreditation revealed significant differences in certain skill dimensions. Multiple regression analysis indicated that age and perceived accreditation were significant predictors of overall 21st-century skills.ConclusionsHealth Management students demonstrated strong profiles in career awareness and digital competencies, yet showed areas for development in critical thinking, innovation, and leadership. The findings suggest the importance of revising Health Management curricula in alignment with 21st-century skills, expanding experiential and project-based learning opportunities, and designing educational strategies that foster students’ innovation and entrepreneurship capacities.

Introduction

Contemporary health systems face a wide range of multidimensional challenges, including population aging, the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, rising demand for healthcare services, growing cost pressures, and the rapid advancement of medical technologies. These developments have made the planning, organization, and delivery of healthcare services increasingly complex, thereby making the effective and efficient management of healthcare organizations a critical necessity. In this context, the sustainability of health systems and the improvement of service quality largely depend on the managerial capacity and leadership skills of healthcare managers [21].

The literature emphasizes that healthcare managers must possess a range of leadership and managerial competencies in order to perform effectively. Systematic reviews indicate that key competency domains for healthcare managers include evidence-based decision-making, operations and resource management, knowledge of health systems and organizations, interpersonal communication and relationship management, leadership, change management, and professionalism [28].

In recent years, the digital transformation occurring within the healthcare sector has significantly altered the structure of health systems and the processes of service delivery. The integration of digital health technologies—such as electronic health records, big data analytics, telehealth applications, and artificial intelligence–supported decision systems—has made the planning and management of healthcare services even more complex [52]. This transformation requires healthcare managers not only to possess technical and managerial knowledge but also to develop advanced competencies such as data-driven decision-making, digital technology management, strategic thinking, innovation, and interdisciplinary collaboration [9, 19, 42]. Accordingly, professionals who will assume roles in health management must be equipped not only with traditional managerial knowledge but also with contemporary and multidimensional competencies.

The twenty-first century is characterized by rapidly evolving technological, social, and economic dynamics that require individuals—particularly those working in knowledge-based sectors—to possess not only technical knowledge but also a broad range of cognitive, social, and affective competencies. In this context, 21st-century skills such as critical thinking, problem solving, communication, collaboration, leadership, digital and information literacy, creativity, and entrepreneurship play a crucial role in enabling individuals to address complex problems, adapt to changing environments, and engage in lifelong learning [5]. These skills encompass cognitive, intrapersonal, and interpersonal competencies that allow individuals to effectively respond to rapidly changing social, technological, and economic conditions. A growing body of research indicates that such transferable skills are associated with positive outcomes in education, professional life, health, and civic participation. Therefore, the development of competencies such as critical thinking, communication, problem solving, and self-regulation has become one of the central goals of modern education systems [45]. In interdisciplinary and practice-oriented fields such as health management, these competencies are particularly critical for enabling graduates to assume innovative, collaborative, and leadership roles. Moreover, with the accelerating digital transformation in healthcare, strengthening the digital competencies of health managers has become essential for ensuring the sustainability of health systems, improving patient safety, and enhancing service quality [18].

Health management education should not be limited to the transfer of theoretical knowledge but should also aim to develop students’ higher-order thinking, decision-making, and practical application skills required in the twenty-first century. Research indicates that such competencies contribute not only to academic achievement but also to professional success, civic participation, and lifelong learning [45]. In the context of health management education, the development of these competencies enables graduates to lead within complex healthcare systems, generate innovative solutions, and collaborate effectively within multidisciplinary teams. Accordingly, the literature emphasizes that effective health managers require not only technical knowledge but also a broad set of cognitive, interpersonal, and managerial competencies. For example, Negandhi et al. [41] proposed an educational model aimed at strengthening the interdisciplinary leadership capacities of healthcare professionals and highlighted the importance of competencies such as self-management, strategic thinking, teamwork, and innovation. Similarly, Akaltan [1] emphasized that healthcare professionals should be equipped not only with clinical expertise but also with transferable professional competencies that support communication, critical thinking, and lifelong learning. Supporting this perspective, a bibliometric analysis conducted by Kaya [34] demonstrated that research on twenty-first-century skills has largely focused on themes such as creativity, problem solving, collaboration, and innovation, particularly within fields such as healthcare, engineering, and education. Taken together, these findings suggest that health management education should extend beyond the transfer of theoretical knowledge and prioritize the development of practical, innovative, and interdisciplinary competencies that enable professionals to adapt effectively to rapidly changing healthcare environments.

The rapid transformation of health systems has also necessitated a redefinition of the competencies expected from healthcare managers. In this context, competency-based approaches in health management education have gained increasing importance. For instance, the updated competency model developed for health management education by Enard et al. [15] identifies five core domains of competencies required for healthcare managers: leadership, critical thinking and analysis, management, culture and community engagement, and communication. The authors emphasize that competency models should be regularly updated in order for health management education programs to adapt to the evolving needs of the healthcare sector.

Accordingly, higher education institutions aim to equip future health managers not only with managerial knowledge but also with multidimensional competencies that enable them to adapt to the changing conditions of healthcare systems. In Türkiye, this transformation has been institutionalized through the National Core Education Program for Health Management (SAY-UÇEP), updated by the Turkish Higher Education Quality Council (THEQC). According to SAY-UÇEP, the competencies targeted in health management education are not limited to managerial knowledge and technical expertise; they also include learning skills such as collaboration, creativity, critical thinking, and problem solving, as well as literacy skills encompassing information, technology, finance, and health literacy, together with life and career skills.

Collaboration refers to the ability of individuals to communicate effectively with internal and external stakeholders and contribute to teamwork, while creativity involves generating new ideas and transforming them into value-producing solutions. Critical thinking and problem solving encompass the ability to analyze complex situations, systematically evaluate information sources, and make evidence-based decisions. Furthermore, information, technology, media, finance, and health literacy refer to individuals’ capacity to access, evaluate, and effectively utilize various information resources. Life and career skills include competencies such as flexibility and adaptability, innovation, self-management, and subjective well-being, which support individuals’ personal and professional development [12].

Competency-Based Education (CBE) represents a significant paradigmatic shift in higher education from time-based instructional models toward outcome- and performance-based approaches. Within this framework, students’ progression is determined not by the number of instructional hours or credit completion, but by their ability to demonstrate predetermined knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes through observable and measurable performance indicators [11, 39]. The CBE approach aims to structure educational programs around clearly defined learning outcomes and to evaluate whether students systematically acquire the competencies expected from them. Accordingly, establishing structural alignment between program outcomes, teaching methods, and assessment processes—referred to as constructive alignment—constitutes one of the fundamental principles of CBE. In this context, quality assurance mechanisms in higher education play a critical role in supporting the implementation of competency-based education.

Accreditation is defined as a systematic evaluation process conducted by independent organizations to assess whether educational programs comply with established standards and to ensure the quality of education. In higher education, accreditation systems are considered important mechanisms that not only provide quality assurance but also encourage the continuous improvement of educational programs [50]. With the growing adoption of competency-based education, accreditation systems are increasingly expected to consider the learning outcomes and competency levels achieved by students. In this regard, accreditation processes should evaluate not only the structural characteristics of educational programs but also the development of students’ knowledge, skills, and professional competencies [7]. Therefore, accreditation systems are regarded as key quality assurance mechanisms that contribute to the creation of learning environments that support students’ competency development. Consequently, accredited educational programs may provide more supportive learning environments for the development of contemporary professional skills.

The CBE literature conceptualizes competency development as a gradual and cumulative learning process. Throughout their education, students progressively achieve higher levels of mastery through structured learning experiences and feedback mechanisms [46]. Accordingly, certain individual and institutional factors representing students’ progress during their educational journey may be associated with competency development. In particular, variables such as academic achievement, class level, and institutional characteristics of educational programs are suggested to provide explanatory insights into students’ competency development [14].

Within this framework, the present study examines the 21st-century skills of health management students from the perspective of competency-based education and analyzes how these skills differ according to demographic and institutional variables.

In recent years, the importance of 21st-century skills in the context of higher education has attracted increasing attention, and numerous studies emphasize that universities should equip students with competencies such as critical thinking, collaboration, creativity, and digital literacy [4, 30, 44]. However, studies examining these skills within health education have largely focused on clinical professions such as medicine, nursing, midwifery, and physiotherapy [17, 33, 47]. Educational programs in these fields particularly emphasize the development of competencies such as critical thinking, communication, teamwork, problem solving, and leadership [1, 19].

Nevertheless, a review of the literature indicates that studies examining the contemporary skill levels of health management students—who will be responsible for the planning, organization, and management of health services—remain quite limited. Yet health management education aims to train professionals capable of managing complex processes within healthcare systems, including efficiency management, quality management, and human resource planning. Therefore, identifying the 21st-century skills of health management students and examining the factors influencing these skills constitute an important research area for the sustainability and effective management of health systems.

In this context, the present study aims to contribute to the literature as one of the first comprehensive nationwide studies examining the 21st-century skills of health management students in Türkiye. In addition, the study investigates whether students’ 21st-century skills differ according to demographic and academic characteristics and seeks to identify the factors influencing these skills.

Based on this purpose, the following hypotheses were developed:H1: The levels of 21st-century skills differ significantly according to demographic characteristics such as gender, age, and class level.H2: The levels of 21st-century skills differ significantly according to academic characteristics such as grade point average and the accreditation status of the university.H3: Demographic and institutional variables significantly predict the overall levels of 21st-century skills among health management students.

Method

Research design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional survey design to examine the 21st-century skill levels of undergraduate students enrolled in Health Management programs in Türkiye. The study aimed to determine students’ levels of 21st-century skills and to examine whether these skills differ significantly according to various demographic and academic characteristics.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee of Yozgat Bozok University (Decision No: 30/1, November 19, 2025). Participation in the study was voluntary, and digital informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The anonymity and confidentiality of the participants were ensured throughout the research process. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidelines.

Clinical trial number

Not applicable.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of undergraduate students enrolled in Health Management programs at public and foundation universities across Türkiye. According to national higher education data, Health Management programs are offered in approximately 78 universities in Türkiye. In this study, a non-probability convenience sampling method was employed. Data were collected through an online survey distributed via student groups, social media platforms, and departmental email lists. Participants were recruited from 24 different universities located in various regions of Türkiye, and the final sample consisted of 565 students.

To evaluate whether the obtained sample size was sufficient to represent the target population, a commonly used sample size determination approach in the literature was considered. According to the sample size table developed by Krejcie and Morgan [36], a minimum sample size of 384 participants is considered sufficient for large populations at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error.

In the present study, data were collected from 565 undergraduate students, which exceeds the recommended minimum sample size. Therefore, the obtained sample size was considered sufficient to provide adequate statistical power and to allow reliable interpretation of the research findings within the scope of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study required participants to be enrolled in an undergraduate Health Management program at a higher education institution in Türkiye, to be at least 18 years of age, to voluntarily agree to participate, and to provide informed consent. Only participants who fully completed the questionnaire and met these eligibility criteria were included in the analysis.

The exclusion criteria involved being enrolled in a program other than Health Management, failure to provide informed consent, incomplete or inconsistent survey responses, or detection of duplicate or invalid entries during data quality checks. All completed surveys met the eligibility criteria; therefore, data from all 565 participants were included in the final analysis.

Twenty-first century skills scale

The Multidimensional 21 st Century Skills Scale, developed by Çevik and Şentürk [10], was used in this study. The scale consists of five subdimensions: Information and Technology Literacy, Critical Thinking and Problem Solving, Entrepreneurship and Innovation, Social Responsibility and Leadership, Career Consciousness. The scale includes 41 items, rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Subscale scores were calculated as the arithmetic mean of the items belonging to each subdimension, and the overall 21st-century skills score was obtained by calculating the mean of all items included in the final measurement model.

Psychometric properties of the scale

To examine the psychometric properties of the scale within the present sample, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS software. Items with standardized factor loadings below 0.50 (Items 4, 10, 14, 15, and 35) were removed from the model following commonly accepted recommendations for structural equation modeling [24]. The final measurement model consisted of 36 items.

The CFA results indicated an acceptable level of model fit (CMIN/df = 3.60, CFI = 0.808, GFI = 0.835, RMSEA = 0.068). Although the CFI and GFI values were slightly below more conservative cutoff criteria, previous studies have suggested that values above 0.80 may still indicate acceptable fit in multidimensional measurement models [8, 23, 25].

The overall internal consistency coefficient of the scale was high (α = 0.89). All skewness and kurtosis values were within the acceptable range of ± 3, indicating approximate normality of the data distribution [29, 49].

Regarding mean scores, the highest mean was observed in Career Consciousness (M = 4.3, SD = 0.5), followed by Information and Technology Literacy (M = 4.1, SD = 0.4), while the lowest mean was obtained in Entrepreneurship and Innovation (M = 3.6, SD = 0.7).

As shown in Table 2, all fit indices were within acceptable ranges, confirming the construct validity of the measurement model.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected online via Google Forms between December 1 and December 20, 2025, on a voluntary basis. The anonymity and confidentiality of participants were ensured throughout the process. All completed surveys met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis.

To reach students from different universities, the survey link was distributed through departmental email lists, student groups, and social media platforms related to Health Management programs.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS software. First, descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were calculated. The assumption of normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values, and since all values were within acceptable limits, parametric tests were applied. For comparisons between two groups, independent samples t-tests were used, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for variables with more than two groups. Levene’s test was conducted to examine the homogeneity of variances, and depending on the results, Tukey or Games–Howell post-hoc tests were applied. Additionally, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify variables predicting students’ overall levels of 21st-century skills. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Overall, the findings indicate that the proposed hypotheses were partially supported. Discussion

An examination of the findings regarding the 21st-century skills of Health Management students reveals that, overall, they demonstrate an above-average level of competence, although there remain areas that warrant improvement. The highest mean score was observed in the Career Consciousness dimension, followed by Information and Technology Literacy. In contrast, the dimensions of Social Responsibility and Leadership, Entrepreneurship and Innovation, and Critical Thinking and Problem Solving showed relatively lower mean scores. This finding suggests that while students possess strong career awareness and digital competencies, they exhibit room for development in critical thinking, innovation, and leadership skills. Similarly, Karadaş et al. [31] reported that students demonstrated higher performance in practice-oriented skills, whereas their levels of critical and creative thinking were comparatively lower. Likewise, Aktaş [3] found that Career Consciousness was the highest-rated skill, while Entrepreneurship and Innovation were rated at a moderate level.

The consistently high levels of career consciousness observed in these studies may be related to the inclusion of the “Career Planning” course in many higher education curricula after 2020. Indeed, Dalkılıç et al. [13] demonstrated that participation in a career planning course had a significant and positive effect on students’ career decision-making competence. This finding indicates that curriculum-based initiatives designed to support career development contribute to strengthening students’ career awareness.

Conversely, Entrepreneurship and Innovation and Critical Thinking and Problem Solving emerged as the lowest-rated subdimensions, indicating areas that require further development. The relatively low level of entrepreneurship and innovation among health management students may be explained by their heightened anxiety regarding future employment opportunities [20], which may foster a preference toward public-sector positions rather than entrepreneurial initiatives. Additionally, the presence or absence of entrepreneurship and innovation courses in the curriculum may be a determining factor in the development of these skills. Supporting this, Baltacı and Deniz [6] found that entrepreneurship and innovation training provided to nursing students significantly improved their entrepreneurial tendencies and innovative thinking levels.

Entrepreneurship in the healthcare sector is not limited to generating new ideas; rather, it is a multidimensional process that involves identifying opportunities, introducing innovative solutions to the market, and developing sustainable business models. This process requires the simultaneous management of various factors such as financial sustainability, regulatory requirements, and health outcomes [22]. Therefore, this highlights the importance of practice-oriented and interdisciplinary educational approaches in developing the entrepreneurial skills of health management students.

The findings related to Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving skills also indicate a potential area for improvement. The fact that this dimension differed only by age and academic performance suggests that these skills may be associated with cognitive maturity and academic achievement. However, previous literature indicates that levels of critical thinking are also influenced by individual and socioeconomic factors. Kıraç and Kalaycı Kırlıoğlu [35] found that critical thinking levels among health management students significantly varied according to family income and year of study. Similarly, Uğurluoğlu et al. [51] demonstrated a significant positive relationship between self-esteem and critical thinking disposition. Accordingly, it can be inferred that individuals with strong self-respect, inner values, and self-confidence tend to exhibit more questioning and analytical approaches in decision-making processes.

The findings of the present study, together with evidence from the existing literature, indicate that other factors may also need to be considered in efforts to strengthen critical thinking skills among health management students, including psychosocial factors such as self-confidence, self-esteem, and self-efficacy. Therefore, incorporating not only cognitive but also affective and motivational learning outcomes into the curriculum could help future health managers develop the capacity to make more analytical, ethically grounded, and effective decisions.

The field of health management is not limited to business-oriented decision-making; due to the nature of healthcare services, managerial decisions often carry medical and ethical implications as well. For this reason, individuals who will take on managerial roles in healthcare institutions must possess the ability to make accurate, evidence-based, and analytical decisions that serve both institutional sustainability and public health [48]. In this context, fostering critical thinking dispositions among health management students is not merely an academic achievement but also a crucial requirement for ensuring the quality of future professional practice.

In comparisons based on gender, a significant difference was found only in the Career Consciousness subdimension, with female students scoring higher than their male counterparts. Karadaş et al. [31] reported that male students had higher total scores on the scale, whereas studies by Aktaş [3] and Karatepe [32] found results consistent with the present research, indicating that female students demonstrated greater career awareness than male students. Similarly, a study conducted among physiotherapy students suggested that gender differences in 21st-century skills may be associated with gender roles and socialization processes [47]. In addition, previous research indicates that female students may place greater emphasis on professional identity development and career planning processes, which may contribute to higher levels of career awareness [38].

When evaluated in terms of age and class level, significant differences were observed in Information and Technology Literacy, Entrepreneurship and Innovation, Career Consciousness, and the Overall Scale Score. Students aged 21 years and older scored higher across all these dimensions. Similarly, regarding class level, fourth-year students achieved the highest mean scores, whereas second-year students had the lowest. This result suggests that experience, cognitive maturity, and professional awareness gained through age and academic progression contribute positively to the development of 21st-century skills. Comparable findings were reported by Karadaş et al. [31], who found that upper-year students exhibited higher levels of these skills. In contrast, Karatepe [32] reported no significant differences by class level. From the perspective of competency-based education (CBME), students are expected to demonstrate competence in specific skills before progressing to higher levels of training [11]. Therefore, the gradual improvement of competencies such as critical thinking across the educational process may reflect the cumulative learning experiences gained during university education.

Regarding academic achievement, significant differences were found in Information and Technology Literacy and Critical Thinking and Problem Solving according to students’ grade point averages. Students with higher academic performance demonstrated higher levels of competence in these areas. This finding supports the notion that cognitive skills develop in parallel with academic achievement. Similarly, Engin and Korucuk [16] found statistically significant differences between students’ grade point averages and their levels of Information and Technology Literacy, Critical Thinking and Problem Solving, and Social Responsibility and Leadership. Students with stronger critical thinking abilities also tended to have higher academic performance [2].

Students were asked whether their program was accredited and were instructed to select one of three response options: “Yes,” “No,” or “I do not know.” According to the findings, approximately 28% of the participants were enrolled in accredited programs, whereas nearly 56% reported that they believed their program was accredited. This finding may indicate that some students were uncertain about the accreditation status of their programs or may have misunderstood the accreditation process.

No significant differences were found in students’ 21st-century skills based on the official accreditation status of their universities. However, students’ perceptions of whether their program was accredited revealed significant differences in certain skill dimensions. Specifically, significant differences were found in Entrepreneurship and Innovation, Social Responsibility and Leadership, Career Consciousness, and the overall scale score. These results indicate that students’ perceptual awareness of accreditation may play an important role in skill development, even when institutional accreditation status itself does not.

This finding is consistent with literature suggesting that accreditation exerts indirect influences on educational outcomes. Kumar et al. [37] reported that accreditation has meaningful and positive effects on quality, excellence, research, student achievement, and institutional reputation within higher education institutions. Similarly, Iqbal et al. [27] found that accreditation exerts an indirect yet strong impact on students’ motivation and academic performance.

In this context, the present findings indicate that accreditation may influence individual learning outcomes not only at the institutional level but also through the way students perceive their educational experiences. This suggests that within the competency-based education framework, not only the existence of institutional structures but also how students perceive the learning environment and quality assurance processes may influence the development of competencies [11, 45].

The results of the multiple regression analysis showed that the model was statistically significant, explaining approximately 4% of the variance in students’ overall 21st-century skills scores. According to the analysis, age and perceived accreditation were significant predictors of 21st-century skills. Increases in age were associated with higher overall skill levels, suggesting that cognitive maturity, self-management, and problem-solving experience gained over time contribute to skill development. Additionally, students’ perceptions of their university’s accreditation status significantly affected their 21st-century skills. This finding suggests that students’ perceptions of institutional quality serve as important cognitive–motivational factors that shape their learning motivation and performance.

Conversely, gender, class level, academic achievement, and official institutional accreditation status did not have a statistically significant effect on students’ skill levels.

Health management programs aim not only to equip students with the competencies required to meet the challenges of the healthcare sector but also to foster their sense of institutional commitment and belonging. Such institutional attachment can enhance students’ academic engagement, motivation, and performance, thereby enabling graduates to assume more effective roles within complex healthcare systems and contribute to organizational success. Naamati-Schneider and Alt [40] found that health management students with higher levels of emotional attachment to their universities also exhibited significantly greater acquisition of social skills. The authors emphasized that emotional commitment strengthens the educational environment and student satisfaction, and therefore, academic programs should be designed to foster not only intellectual but also psychosocial engagement.

The explanatory power of the regression model in this study was relatively low (R2 = 0.044); however, this is a commonly observed situation in social science and educational research. Complex phenomena such as human behavior, learning processes, and competency development emerge through the interaction of numerous individual, psychological, pedagogical, and environmental factors. Therefore, it is expected that only a limited portion of the variance in the dependent variable can be explained in such models. Indeed, methodological studies on empirical modeling in the social sciences indicate that low R2 values do not necessarily imply that a model is invalid; rather, models with low explanatory power can still be considered scientifically acceptable when the explanatory variables included in the model are statistically significant [43].

In this context, the findings of the present study suggest that the 21st-century skills of health management students have a multidimensional structure that cannot be explained solely by demographic and institutional variables. The development of students’ competencies is shaped by the interaction of various factors such as learning environments, pedagogical approaches, experiential learning opportunities, motivation, self-efficacy, and institutional learning culture. Previous research also indicates that 21st-century skills consist of multidimensional structures encompassing cognitive, intrapersonal, and interpersonal dimensions. Since these skills develop through interactions with numerous contextual and environmental factors, explaining the related outcomes using a limited number of variables may be challenging [45].

Therefore, future studies should incorporate pedagogical and psychosocial variables into the model in order to provide a more comprehensive explanation of the development of 21st-century skills in health management education.

Recommendations and limitations of the study

The findings of this study provide several recommendations for the improvement of health management education. First, the results indicate that although health management students possess certain 21st-century skills, higher-order competencies such as critical thinking, problem solving, entrepreneurship, and innovation require further support. Accordingly, it is recommended that health management programs incorporate more practice-oriented learning approaches aimed at developing students’ analytical thinking, creativity, and problem-solving skills. In particular, case analyses, project-based learning, field applications, and interdisciplinary group work may contribute to the development of these competencies.

In addition, including courses focused on entrepreneurship and innovation in the health management curriculum may help students adapt more effectively to changes within the healthcare sector. Collaborations with healthcare institutions and sector representatives may also enable students to integrate theoretical knowledge with practical experience and enhance their leadership skills. Furthermore, strengthening career planning and professional development courses may increase students’ career awareness and help them determine their professional orientations more consciously.

This study has several methodological limitations. First, the research employed a cross-sectional design, and the data were collected through a self-report method. Therefore, the relationships between variables cannot be interpreted causally, and participants’ tendency to provide socially desirable responses may have influenced the results. In addition, the study was limited to undergraduate health management students in Türkiye. This may restrict the generalizability of the findings to educational systems in other countries. Furthermore, a non-probability convenience sampling method was used in the study. This may limit the extent to which the sample fully represents all health management students and may partially reduce the generalizability of the findings.

Future research could conduct comparative studies including different universities or different countries. In addition, longitudinal studies may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how students’ 21st-century skills develop throughout the educational process. Moreover, qualitative or mixed-method research may offer deeper insights into how students acquire these competencies and the role of educational environments in this process.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that health management students in Türkiye generally demonstrate a positive profile in terms of 21st-century skills. However, the results also suggest that focusing solely on the acquisition of professional knowledge in health management education is not sufficient; higher-order competencies such as critical thinking, innovation, problem solving, and leadership should also be supported. In this context, it is important for health management programs to be structured in a way that promotes the development of the multidimensional competencies required by contemporary health systems. Such educational approaches may contribute to enabling future health managers to adapt more effectively to the rapidly changing and increasingly complex healthcare environment.
