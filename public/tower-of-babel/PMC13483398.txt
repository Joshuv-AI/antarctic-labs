Microbial communication in saline environments: quorum sensing and the future of anaerobic wastewater treatment
Authors: Yuxin Pan, Xue-Ting Wang, Yanxue Li, Wei Wang, Christian Sonne, Yen Wah Tong, Nanqi Ren, Yong Sik Ok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anaerobic treatment of industrial wastewater offers a sustainable and cost-effective approach to reducing environmental contamination while recovering biogas. High-salinity wastewater poses ecological risks and remains difficult to treat due to salt-induced sludge disintegration, volatile fatty acid accumulation, and reduced methane production. Quorum sensing is emerging as a key regulator of microbial activity and system stability under salt stress. This review summarizes salinity-stress inhibition mechanisms, evaluates quorum sensing-based mitigation strategies, and proposes a stage- and performance-based framework for quorum sensing application to advance resilient and efficient anaerobic treatment systems.

Challenges in anaerobic biological treatment of high-salinity wastewater

Rapid industrialization has significantly increased global wastewater generation, with estimates indicating a 51% rise in production from 2015 to 2050. This growth poses serious risks to human health, aquatic ecosystems, and socio-economic sustainability due to the discharge of untreated or poorly treated effluents [1]. In particular, industrial effluents from seafood processing, pickling, textile dyeing, and similar sources are marked by high salinity (over 1% w/v NaCl) [2], high organic loads (chemical oxygen demand (COD) levels between 5000 and 20,000 mg·L−1), and complex organic compositions [3, 4], making their treatment technically and economically challenging [5]. Anaerobic biological treatment, which leverages anaerobic microorganisms to degrade organic pollutants and generate methane in the absence of oxygen, is increasingly recognized as a sustainable and cost-effective solution for managing high-strength organic wastewater. However, the application of anaerobic systems to treat saline wastewater encounters significant challenges due to high salinity. Elevated salt levels can induce osmotic stress, reduce microbial metabolic activity, disrupt interactions among microorganisms, and alter microbial community structures. These changes can lead to decreased reactor performance and instability in the treatment process. To enhance the effectiveness of anaerobic treatment for high-salinity organic wastewater, it is essential to understand the impact of salinity on microbial processes and to develop effective strategies for improving salt tolerance among the microorganisms involved.

There are three main aspects of anaerobic biological wastewater treatment: granular sludge—a key functional component of anaerobic reactors, microbial physiology, and microbial communities [6–8]. Under high-salinity conditions, there is macro-level deterioration in each of these areas (Fig. 1). High salinity disrupts microbial energy metabolism, impairs microbial aggregate formation, and weakens the methanogens within microbial communities [2, 3, 6, 9]. These effects lead to reduced methane production, accumulation of volatile fatty acids (VFAs), and increased fluctuations in pH, ultimately causing system instability or even failure [5–8]. At salinity levels > 1–2% in food waste, methane production is significantly inhibited [9], whereas other studies on the co-digestion of high-salinity leachate and waste-activated sludge have shown that increasing influent salinity from 10 to 30 g/L leads to a significant increase in VFA concentrations, with substantial accumulation of acetate, propionate, and butyrate [7, 10]. Na⁺ concentrations exceeding a critical threshold of 5 g/L induces osmotic imbalance, cellular damage, and reduced metabolic activity in unacclimated microorganisms [10, 11]. Consequently, the size and structural integrity of granular sludge are compromised, hindering biomass accumulation and sustained microbial activity. This accelerates microbial biomass loss and ultimately reduces overall system stability [6, 12]. Quorum sensing (QS) is a key communication mechanism by which microorganisms secrete and transmit signaling molecules to sense population density and coordinate collective behaviors through the regulation of gene expression, playing an important role in microbial aggregation, biofilm formation, metabolic regulation, and community succession [14, 15]. Under salt stress, the expression of QS-related genes, particularly those associated with salt tolerance and operational stability, often shows a trend of upregulation [16]. Therefore, regulating microbial community behavior via QS may represent a novel strategy for alleviating salt stress and enhancing the operational stability of anaerobic biological treatment systems [17]. Although several reviews have summarized the roles of QS in anaerobic wastewater treatment processes and their corresponding regulatory strategies [18, 19], systematic analyses focusing on QS response characteristics, regulatory pathways, and engineering application potential in high-salinity anaerobic systems remain limited. Previous studies have shown that active QS behavior is ubiquitous in anaerobic biological treatment systems [16, 20, 21]; however, studies specifically addressing high-salinity stress remain relatively limited. In particular, the mechanisms by which QS mediates microbial aggregate stability, community reassembly, and metabolic network regulation to alleviate salinity-stress-induced inhibition in anaerobic biological treatment systems lack a clear theoretical framework and integrated understanding. In contrast to QS enhancement, quorum quenching (QQ) refers to the disruption of QS-mediated microbial communication through signal degradation, signal-receptor interference, or inhibition of signal transduction. In anaerobic biological treatment systems, QQ may serve as a complementary regulatory mechanism by suppressing undesirable or excessive QS-driven behaviors, thereby expanding the potential regulatory framework of microbial communication under high-salinity conditions.

Here we review the inhibitory mechanisms of high salinity in anaerobic biological treatment systems, including its impact on microbial physiology and energy metabolism, structural homeostasis of anaerobic granular sludge (AnGS), and microbial community structure and interactions. Furthermore, we address the QS regulation of microbial aggregation, community restructuring, and metabolic regulation in high-salinity anaerobic systems, and discuss the applicability and limitations of current QS-based regulatory strategies. We propose a comprehensive QS regulatory framework in relation to microbial community developmental stages and reactor operating conditions, which provides a novel theoretical perspective and practical approach for achieving more sustainable and efficient bioenergy recovery from high-salinity wastewater through QS-mediated regulation of anaerobic biological treatment systems.

Structural stability of anaerobic granular sludge under high-salinity conditions

AnGS is a dense microbial aggregate mainly composed of anaerobic functional microorganisms, such as hydrolytic bacteria, acidogens, acetogens, methanogens, extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), inorganic minerals, and organic particulates [22]. Owing to its compact structure and high microbial density, AnGS serves as the core functional component of anaerobic reactors and is essential for stable anaerobic wastewater treatment. Its compact granular structure enables efficient biomass retention, facilitates substrate transfer, and supports syntrophic interactions among functional microorganisms, thereby promoting organic matter degradation and methane production during wastewater treatment [23–25]. EPS are key components involved in AnGS formation and structural maintenance and are composed mainly of polysaccharides (PS) and proteins (PN). EPS can be classified into soluble EPS, loosely bound EPS (LB-EPS), and tightly bound EPS (TB-EPS) [18, 26–28]. These molecules crosslink to form a polymeric network containing pores and channels, providing structural support for AnGS formation [29].

To adapt to high-salinity environments, microorganisms maintain osmotic equilibrium through the over-secretion of EPS, which also helps preserve the structural integrity of granular sludge [9, 30]. Consequently, EPS content generally increases with increasing salinity [31], while the protein-to-polysaccharide (PN/PS) ratio may vary with salinity and has been reported to either increase or decrease under different conditions. Wang et al. reported that microorganisms in sequencing batch reactors operating at different NaCl concentrations secreted higher amounts of PN and PS as protective responses to salt stress, with TB-EPS playing a particularly important role in stabilizing AnGS [32]. However, EPS composition under salt stress remains inconclusive, as the relative proportions of PS and PN show contrasting trends across different studies. For example, Cai et al. observed that PN was the predominant component in anaerobic granules under high salinity conditions, with the PN/PS ratio increasing as salinity increased from 1.5 to 2.5% [33]. In contrast, another study found that the PN/PS ratio decreased with increasing salinity in a salinity shock experiment on the anaerobic treatment of fracturing wastewater [34]. These contrasting observations suggest that EPS composition is not regulated by salinity alone. Influent substrate may be an important determinant, as granules fed with partially acidified substrates generally contain substantially higher PN and PS contents than those grown on fully acidified substrates [35]. In addition, the temporal dynamics of EPS secretion should be considered. PS accumulation is generally more pronounced during the initial stage of salt stress, whereas PN enrichment becomes increasingly important for maintaining microbial viability and granule structural stability as granulation progresses [36]. Therefore, variation in PN/PS ratios is likely shaped by influent substrate characteristics, granulation stage, and the initial EPS matrix of the sludge, rather than by salinity alone. Flocculation experiments indicated that PN contributed approximately 6.8 times more to flocculation than PS, that increases in PN content within EPS were strongly associated with AnGS formation, and that PN levels were negatively correlated with sludge zeta potential [37]. Consequently, a high PN/PS ratio and low EPS zeta potential promote sludge granulation. The influence of salinity on the zeta potential of EPS is a critical factor governing microbial aggregation. Generally, a negative zeta potential signifies intensified electrostatic repulsion between cell surfaces, which poses a significant thermodynamic barrier to bacterial aggregation under environmental stress [38, 39]. Although salt stress typically stimulates the quantitative secretion of EPS, elevated salinity can simultaneously alter its qualitative physicochemical properties. Specifically, increasing salt concentration may lead to a more negative zeta potential and reduced surface hydrophobicity, thereby increasing the repulsive energy barrier [40]. This phenomenon suggests that the salinity-induced increase in negative surface charge may offset the adhesive benefits of enhanced EPS production, ultimately hindering the granulation process.

Salt stress may also indirectly affect sludge characteristics by altering EPS cross-linking conditions. With increasing salinity, TB-EPS content decreases while LB-EPS content increases, indicating that TB-EPS may be converted into LB-EPS under high-salinity conditions, thereby compromising AnGS stability [39, 41]. EPS contains numerous anionic functional groups, including carboxyl, hydroxyl, phosphoryl, and sulfhydryl groups, which form complexes with multivalent cations in solutions. Interactions between divalent cations such as Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ and specific EPS functional groups form critical bridges within the EPS matrix [42, 43]. These bridges help stabilize the EPS matrix structure within sludge flocs and play an important role in AnGS formation and stability [44]. However, ions such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻ can disrupt EPS cross-linking, leading to increased suspended sludge under high-salinity conditions [9]. EPS generally carries a net negative charge; therefore, excess Na⁺ competes with divalent cations (e.g., Ca2⁺ and Fe2⁺) for binding sites, weakening the bridging effects of multivalent cations and consequently reducing particle strength [45]. Moreover, EPS repels anions and acts as cation exchange membranes, suggesting that Cl⁻ contributes mainly through indirect physicochemical effects rather than direct occupation of bridging sites [46].

Microbial physiology and energy metabolic responses under high-salinity conditions

Under salt shock or prolonged exposure to high salinity, salinity stress may compromise the structural stability of AnGS by modifying the physicochemical properties of EPS, intensifying electrostatic repulsion among microbial cells, and attenuating multivalent-cation bridging within the EPS matrix. Consequently, the granular architecture becomes less compact and mechanically less robust, resulting in increased suspended sludge, enhanced biomass washout, and eventual AnGS disintegration. This process results in changes in cell morphology and metabolic activity. The microbial cytoplasmic membrane is semipermeable, allowing water molecules to cross via osmotic diffusion, whereas most inorganic ions and organic solutes are regulated by specific transport systems [31, 32, 47]. Under high-salinity conditions, elevated extracellular osmotic pressure drives water molecules outside cells, resulting in cellular dehydration and cytoplasmic shrinkage [6, 48]. At critical levels of external osmotic pressure, the cytoplasmic and intracellular water contents can decrease to approximately 20% and 50% of their original values, respectively. Consequently, cytoplasmic turgor pressure declines and cytoplasmic volume contracts, severely impairing microbial growth, motility, and metabolic activity [49, 50]. Cells mitigate damage from hyperosmotic stress through intracellular ion regulation and solute-based osmoregulation, thereby preventing excessive water loss [5, 51–53]. Microorganisms activate Na⁺/K⁺ antiporters to accumulate K⁺ and expel Na⁺, constituting a central mechanism for maintaining intracellular salinity. This process helps prevent protein inactivation and metabolic disruption caused by excessive Na⁺ accumulation [54, 55]. Simultaneously, cells undergo osmotic adjustment by actively synthesizing or importing compatible solutes such as glycine betaine, proline, and trehalose. These organic osmolytes prevent the aggregation of denatured proteins under high Na⁺ conditions, thereby preserving the functionality of proteins and other macromolecules [55–59]. However, adaptive responses require substantial consumption of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and proton motive force, and consequently, energy that would otherwise be allocated to growth and core metabolic functions is diverted to maintain osmotic homeostasis, resulting in reduced rates of substrate degradation and methane production.

High salinity disrupts microbial osmotic balance and elevates intracellular metal ion concentrations beyond tolerable thresholds, thereby reducing enzyme activity and weakening anaerobic biological treatment performance. Yin et al. reported decreased activities of acetate kinase (AK) and phosphotransferase in the β-oxidation pathways during butyrate oxidation, under high-salinity conditions (5.2%), directly limiting butyrate conversion efficiency. In parallel, the expression of key methanogenesis-related genes, such as mcrA and mtaA, was markedly suppressed, leading to reduced methane production [8]. High salinity negatively affects enzymatic activity and gene expression, disrupting the metabolic equilibrium between acidogenesis and methanogenesis. Several studies have demonstrated that such inhibition is not necessarily permanent, and certain metabolic activities, including β-oxidation and dehydrogenase activity, can be partially restored following long-term acclimation or targeted engineering interventions [17]. Furthermore, high-salinity conditions elevate intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, leading to lipid peroxidation of the cell membranes and DNA damage [60, 61]. These effects further exacerbate metabolic imbalances and compromise the stability of anaerobic biological treatment systems.

Microbial community structure and interactions under high salinity

High salinity typically adversely affects microbial growth; however, some microorganisms adapt to high-salinity environments through osmotic regulation mechanisms, leading to shifts in microbial community composition. Most previous studies indicate that salt stress has a limited impact on the acidogenesis phase of anaerobic biological treatment, and under high-salinity conditions, halotolerant bacteria primarily involved in hydrolysis and acid production processes are selectively enriched, including members of Chloroflexi, Thermotogota, Synergistota, Coprothermobacterota, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria, as well as halotolerant genera such as Thermovirga, Soehngenia, and Actinomyces [8, 39, 62, 63]. At a salinity of 20 g/L, VFA concentrations and CO₂-specific production rates remain comparable to those in the salt-free control, suggesting that microbial activity and metabolic function during acidogenesis were largely unaffected [64].

In contrast, methanogenesis is more sensitive to salinity stress in anaerobic biological treatment systems. Elevated salinity generally suppresses methanogenic activity and reduces methane production [34, 65]. Methanogen groups differ markedly in their sensitivity to salinity; in most cases, hydrogenotrophic methanogens exhibit higher salt tolerance than acetoclastic methanogens. Consequently, in anaerobic biological treatment systems inhibited by elevated Na⁺ concentrations, the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (HM) pathway becomes the dominant route for methane production [66]. Some studies have further suggested that both acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogens can acclimate to high-salinity environments and become dominant, depending on the substrate composition and operating conditions [7, 67].

High salinity exerts concentration-dependent effects on methanogenesis and direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET). DIET is a mechanism of direct electron transfer mediated by conductive pili and outer-membrane cytochrome c, with Geobacter species serving as key electroactive organisms [68]. Methanothrix can directly accept electrons from Geobacter to drive methane production [69]. Metatranscriptomic evidence further supports that Methanothrix species may grow faster when they receive some energy from DIET rather than solely from acetate [70]. In addition, genome-centric analysis identified Methanothrix harundinacea, a halotolerant subspecies with the ability to adapt to both low (5 g/L Na⁺) and high salinity conditions (20 g/L Na⁺) [71], indicating that some methanogens can persist under moderate salinity. Low-salinity conditions have consistently been reported to enhance methane production, with biomethane yields increasing by 9.69–35.29% at 3 g NaCl/L, suggesting that DIET-related syntrophic methanogenesis may still be maintained or even promoted under low salinity [7]. However, when salinity is ≥ 1%, the promotive effect shifts to inhibition, and methane production is almost completely inhibited at > 3% NaCl [63]. This inhibitory salinity level also markedly reduces the abundance of electroactive microorganisms capable of DIET with methanogens, such as Shewanella algae and Geobacter sulfurreducens. Consequently, the decline in methane production under salt stress arises not only from the reduced abundance and activity of salt-sensitive methanogens but also from DIET inhibition, which indirectly suppresses tightly coupled methanogenic processes. Potential regulatory roles of quorum sensing in microbial aggregation and granule stability

The performance of anaerobic biological treatment largely depends on the stability of functional microorganisms and their interactions, which are closely linked to biofilm formation. Extensive research has shown that signaling molecules can induce microbial aggregation, stimulate EPS secretion, and enhance AnGS stability in anaerobic biological treatment systems [15, 19, 72–74].

The addition of QS molecules, such as 3O-C6-HSL at a concentration of 2 µM in a bioreactor, can regulate EPS secretion, promote PS synthesis, and improve the treatment performance and structural stability of anaerobic granular sludge and related anaerobic microbial consortia [75, 76]. AHL-mediated QS may also regulate EPS composition under stress conditions. For example, C4-HSL has been reported to mitigate the inhibitory effects of aged polystyrene nanoplastics on sludge performance by increasing the PN/PS ratio of LB-EPS [77]. Moreover, QS regulates energy metabolism through ATP-associated genes, indicating that it can reshape the cellular energetic state, thereby providing a metabolic basis for coordinated community behaviors [78]. In addition, AHL-mediated QS is positively associated with genes involved in EPS synthesis and with genes involved in glycolysis or pentose phosphate pathway-related genes, suggesting that QS may indirectly facilitate EPS biosynthesis by modulating the energy or substrate supply [79]. Since EPS functions as a structural matrix mediating microbial adhesion, aggregation, and granulation, QS-driven EPS enhancement likely strengthens intercellular interactions, improves granule structural integrity, and promotes the formation of denser microbial aggregates. Consistently, enhanced QS signaling has been associated with larger granule diameters, higher EPS production, greater relative hydrophobicity, and faster recovery or growth of anaerobic granular sludge, thereby facilitating the transition from loose flocs to stable granular sludge structures [80, 81]. These findings suggest that QS may function as an important regulatory mechanism for mitigating sludge disintegration in anaerobic systems that treat high-salinity wastewater.

Potential regulatory roles of QS in metabolic processes

In anaerobic biological treatment systems, QS modulates metabolic processes by regulating the activity of key enzymes involved in hydrolysis, acidogenesis, and methanogenesis through signaling molecules, such as AHLs. Elevated concentrations of these molecules enhance hydrolysis and acidification, primarily by increasing the activity of enzymes associated with the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas (EMP) pathway, including 6-phosphofructokinase (EC 2.7.1.11) [82, 83]. QS can also elevate the activities of phosphoglycerate mutase (EC 5.4.2.11) and pyruvate dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.4.1) [21], suggesting that it upregulates the EMP pathway. Increased carbon flux through the EMP pathway not only accelerates the conversion of organic substrates into VFAs, such as acetate and propionate, but also generates additional ATP via substrate-level phosphorylation [84]. This additional ATP supports membrane-associated Na⁺/K⁺ transport systems, which are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis under high-salinity stress. Furthermore, QS may regulate the tricarboxylic acid cycle, thereby promoting the biosynthesis of amino acids, such as glutamate and proline [85]. This process likely mitigates the inhibitory effects of high osmotic pressure on microorganisms by facilitating the accumulation of compatible solutes. At the molecular level, recent evidence suggests that salinity stress can activate QS-related self-adaptive systems rather than merely increasing EPS secretion at the phenotypic level. During anaerobic digestion of food-waste under high salinity conditions, the aggregate relative abundance of genes related to QS increased by 22.2% and was accompanied by a 27.7% increase in total AHL concentration, indicating an active QS system under salt stress [16]. Similarly, in saline anammox granular sludge, two types of signal molecule synthesis genes, hdtS and luxI for AHL and luxS for autoinducer-2 (AI-2), were upregulated under salinity stress. Under adaptable salinity (< 15.73 g/L), hdtS and luxI were positively correlated with C6-HSL/C8-HSL secretion as well as genes involved in polysaccharide and amino-acid biosynthesis; however, at higher salinity (20–30 g/L), the reduction in QS-related genera weakened community interactions and salt resistance [73]. Moreover, AHLs may bind to corresponding receptor proteins to form transcriptional regulatory complexes that activate EPS-biosynthetic and antioxidant genes. Notably, different signaling molecules appear to target different functions in anaerobic systems. AHLs are the best-characterized signals and seem to act more directly on acidogenic stage, H2 production, and methanogenic activation [86, 87], whereas AI-2 and DSF appear to be associated with interspecies coordination and the restructuring of metabolite cross-feeding or electron-transfer networks that ultimately affect acetate utilization and methane recovery [88, 89].

The activities of enzymes involved in both acetoclastic methanogenesis (AM) and HM increase with increasing levels of signaling molecules [82, 90–93]. In the AM pathway, key enzymes, including AK (EC 2.7.2.1), phosphotransacetylase (EC 2.3.1.8), and acetyl-CoA synthetase (EC 6.2.1.1), exhibit enhanced activity in response to elevated signaling molecule production [21]. Notably, the expression levels of AK and phosphotransacetylase are increased by 10.6-fold and 20.7-fold, respectively, representing a more pronounced upregulation than that observed for acetyl-CoA synthetase [90]. In the HM pathway, the expression of the key enzyme methenyltetrahydromethanopterin cyclohydrolase (EC 3.5.4.27) is also upregulated. Moreover, exposure to 20 µM AHLs increases the expression of genes encoding methyl-coenzyme M reductase (EC 2.8.4.1) and tetrahydromethanopterin S-methyltransferase (EC 2.1.1.86), which are enzymes common to both methanogenic pathways, by 8–13% [82]. Simultaneously, QS-driven upregulation of the EPS biosynthesis pathway may generate additional electroactive substances, thereby enhancing extracellular electron transfer capacity.

Potential role of quorum sensing in regulating functional microbiome reconstruction

Signaling molecules play crucial roles in communication within microbial communities. They regulate bacterial and archaeal populations within a system by influencing the behavior of diverse microorganisms, thereby shaping the community structure and functional potential [94]. Liu et al. externally supplemented different AHLs during the initiation phase of a coupled sulfide autotrophic denitrification–anammox system and found that C6-HSL was more effective at increasing microbial diversity, whereas 3-oC6-HSL preferentially enhanced specific community functions. These findings demonstrate that signaling molecules significantly influence microbial community structure [95]. Numerous studies have shown that the addition of signaling molecules primarily increases the relative abundance of acidogenic bacteria, including Proteobacteria, Synergistota, and Firmicutes [96–98]. These findings suggest that QS enhancement selectively enriches microorganisms responsible for the initial stages of organic matter hydrolysis and acidification, thereby supplying more substrates for subsequent hydrogen and methane production. Signaling molecules also stimulate the hydrogen-producing phase; exogenous addition of AHLs increases the abundance and activity of key hydrogen-producing microorganisms, leading to higher hydrogen production rates and yields in dark fermentation systems [87].

QS is a widespread communication mechanism among bacteria; however, its direct influence on methanogenic archaea remains unclear. Archaea possess genes and proteins associated with signaling pathways, such as cyclic di-guanosine monophosphate (c-di-GMP)-related proteins identified in Methanocella arvoryzae MRE50 [99], suggesting that methanogenic Archaea may participate in specialized QS processes. QS enhancement increases methane production [100], potentially by promoting DIET. Exogenous supplementation with signaling molecules increases the abundance of QS-responsive key DIET bacteria [88], thereby enhancing the methane yield. Chen et al. reported that AHLs addition under sulfide stress promoted syntrophic interactions between acidogenic bacteria and Methanothrix, facilitating DIET and stabilizing system performance [82]. In addition, the influence of QS on DIET is closely related to its ability to reshape microbial interactions and community structure. QS enhancement can selectively enrich key syntrophic bacteria and methanogens, while strengthening their ecological associations and syntrophic cooperation [93, 101]. Moreover, QS enhancement has been reported to upregulate the DIET pathway [90]. Under QS-enhanced conditions coupled with conductive materials, it can also promote the synthesis of c-type cytochromes and humic acid-like substances, thereby improving the electron exchange capability of extracellular polymeric substances and facilitating DIET [102]. These findings suggest that QS-mediated functional microbiome reconstruction may support methane production not only through community reassembly, but also through the maintenance of efficient DIET-related syntrophic interactions under environmental stress [82, 101, 103]. In summary, QS can drive microbial community reassembly and enhance acidogenesis and methanogenesis. Under high-salinity conditions, QS-mediated regulation may maintain or reconstruct key functional microbial communities, offering a promising biological strategy for alleviating DIET suppression and mitigating declines in methane production in hypersaline environments.

This review focuses on the strategies and feasibility of regulating QS in high-salinity anaerobic treatment from four perspectives: the addition of exogenous signaling molecules, introduction of QS microorganisms, use of QS-active materials, and quorum quenching. Several studies have attempted to induce QS behavior in anaerobic biological treatment systems by adding exogenous signaling molecules, particularly AHLs. Supplementation with external AHLs selectively enriches key mutualistic bacteria, enhances DIET, and significantly increases methane production efficiency [101]. Under environmental stress, AHL supplementation also activates energy compensation and cellular defense mechanisms, thereby substantially improving the system’s capacity for organic matter removal [110]. Notably, signaling molecules differ in both function and duration of action during anaerobic treatment, particularly among AHLs with distinct molecular structures and acyl-chain lengths. C10-HSL is associated with sludge elasticity, whereas C14-HSL is related to aggregation capacity. In contrast, C4-HSL, C6-HSL, and 3OC6-HSL are primarily linked to sludge hydrophobicity [111]. Regarding the duration of action, DSF exhibits a stronger and longer-lasting enhancement effect, whereas C6-HSL acts more rapidly [76]. The appropriate timing of signal molecule addition is critical. Liu et al. investigated the effects of different AHL types (C4-HSL, C7-HSL, and C12-HSL) and the timing of their supplementation during the anaerobic treatment of agricultural waste. Their results indicated that the timing of AHL addition, rather than the AHL type, was critical for enhancing methane production [112]. Furthermore, AHLs do not affect the electron transport system of normal cells [95], suggesting that the application of signaling molecules during early-stage functional limitation or initial environmental stress may maximize their regulatory effects. However, signaling molecules are prone to degradation and adsorption, which hinders the maintenance of sufficiently high effective concentrations over extended periods. Consequently, this enhancement strategy faces challenges in practical applications.

Compared with the addition of exogenous signaling molecules, the inoculation of QS microorganisms that continuously produce signaling molecules is generally more sustainable and cost-effective. Under environmental stress, inoculation with QS bacteria such as Sphingomonas rubra and Serratia enhances QS activity [113], leading to significant increases in AHL concentrations, EPS production, and biofilm attachment strength. These results indicate that inoculation with QS bacteria produces macroscopic effects comparable to those of direct addition of the signaling molecule, including enhanced EPS secretion, sludge granulation, and stimulation of metabolic pathways such as DIET and methanogenesis. For example, although exogenous AHL addition (59–300 nmol/L) and AHL-producing bacterial inoculation (daily dosage of 0.5 abs OD600) differ in their delivery mechanisms, both strategies have been shown to significantly improve EPS production, adhesive strength, and metabolic capacity [114]. However, differences in signaling molecule concentration dynamics and duration of action result in distinct enhancement outcomes between these two QS regulation strategies. Sun et al. compared exogenous AHLs addition with inoculation of AHL-producing Acinetobacter strains and found that exogenous AHL supplementation maintained higher AHLs levels during the start-up phase, leading to more pronounced biofilm enhancement. In contrast, inoculation with AHL-producing bacteria exhibited greater advantages in promoting bacterial community diversity [114]. Several halotolerant QS strains have been identified, including Vibrio sp. LV-Q1, which significantly enhances the pollutant removal efficiency in reactors under 2–4% NaCl conditions. This strain promotes biofilm activity and attachment within reactors and increases the expression of genes in functional microorganisms [108]. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of obtaining QS strains that combine salt tolerance with effective signaling molecule production through salinity acclimation and QS functional screening. However, QS bacteria may compete with salt-tolerant methanogens and other key functional microorganisms for ecological niches, potentially negatively affecting the stability and performance of anaerobic treatment systems. Therefore, for practical applications, the screening and application of locally adapted QS microorganisms that possess both salt tolerance and competitive advantages under high-salinity conditions represent a more feasible and robust strategy.

Porous or conductive materials can promote signaling molecule enrichment and sustain microbial interactions. Incorporation of such materials typically upregulates the expression of QS-related genes, increases signaling molecule concentrations, facilitates EPS production, and establishes DIET pathways. For example, the addition of biochar enhanced AHLs secretion within anaerobic systems [107]. This initial enrichment of microorganisms is primarily induced by the abundant functional groups (e.g., carboxyl and hydroxyl groups) and porous structure of biochar, which facilitate selective microbial colonization on the material surface and increase local cell density, thereby favoring QS activation [102]. Therefore, porous materials can function as microbial carriers that promote the local enrichment of signal-producing microorganisms and enhance signaling molecules secretion. For conductive materials, He et al. reviewed the relationships between different types of conductive materials and QS signaling molecules in anaerobic digestion systems, highlighting their significant synergistic effects on the enhancement of anaerobic digestion [115]. In addition to stimulating QS, conductive materials, such as electroactive rare earth element-biochar [116], iron-modified biochar [21], and magnetite [109], can substantially increase the abundance of electroactive microorganisms, thereby enhancing DIET-related enzyme activity and improving the efficiency of extracellular electron transfer. This strategy is expected to exert a particularly strong regulatory effect on anaerobic biological treatment systems under salt stress. High-salinity environments inhibit DIET activity, suppress the function of methanogenic Archaea, and weaken the interactions between methanogens and other functional microorganisms. However, QS upregulation induced by porous or conductive materials promotes EPS restructuring, enriches electroconductive microorganisms, and restores key metabolic pathways [117, 118]. Consequently, the microbial community structure and interspecies interactions are optimized, leading to a significant enhancement in methane production.

Quorum quenching in high-salinity anaerobic treatment

Enhancing QS is a regulatory approach for managing anaerobic treatment systems; however, the controlled application of QQ under specific conditions can be used as an additional strategy. For example, QQ can mitigate membrane fouling in anaerobic membrane bioreactors or alleviate excessive VFA accumulation during the initial acidogenesis stage. The primary mechanism by which QQ reduces membrane fouling is the suppression of EPS production and restructuring of microbial communities to reduce competition from non-target microorganisms. However, the effects of QQ on methane production remain debatable. Some studies have reported that QQ increases methanogen abundance or activity [119, 120]. This effect may result from reduced competition from non-functional bacterial taxa, allowing more substrates and electrons to be directed toward methanogenesis, ultimately enhancing methane yield [121]. QQ may also stimulate acidogenic activity, leading to VFA accumulation during the early stages, which can subsequently support higher methane production in later phases [122]. Notably, most studies reporting neutral or negative impacts of QQ on methane production used AHL degradation-based quenching strategies [119, 123, 124]. Most AHL-degrading enzymes are AHL lactonases and AHL acylases; AHL acylases generate an amide intermediate during AHL hydrolysis, which can be further converted into ammonia, a compound that inhibits acetoclastic methanogens [125]. Furthermore, the inhibition of AI-2 signaling enhances acetate consumption by Methanosarcina sp., thereby increasing methane production [89], providing indirect support for this hypothesis. In high-salinity anaerobic digesters, strategically applying QQ to reduce competition from non-functional microorganisms may help prevent the diversion of substrates and electrons into non-methanogenic pathways, while preserving essential syntrophic interactions associated with methanogenesis. Overall, QS enhancement and QQ are not mutually exclusive strategies in high-salinity systems. Instead, the selection and timing of these strategies should be guided by the reactor operational stages and microbial community states to promote AnGS formation, maintain functional balance, and maximize methane production.

Stage-dependent QS differential regulation strategies

Notably, QS plays distinct regulatory roles at different stages of microbial community development, indicating that intervention strategies should be tailored to reactor operating conditions, as shown in Fig. 4. During the early stages of community establishment, QS primarily promotes biofilm formation and sludge flocculation. As the community matures, the regulatory role of QS shifts toward enhancing DIET mediated by conductive pili, thereby continuously optimizing methane production efficiency [126]. Exogenous signaling molecules act rapidly but lack long-term persistence; therefore, this strategy is particularly suitable during reactor start-up or the initial phase of salinity increase when rapid microbial aggregation and AnGS formation are required to mitigate salt stress. At this stage, inoculation with QS-active microorganisms should be considered. Although this approach exhibits a slower onset of action, it offers distinct advantages in enhancing microbial community diversity and prolonging the regulatory effects. Under stable operating conditions and with moderately elevated signaling molecule levels, incorporating conductive materials can further stimulate DIET, thereby enhancing methane yield. Research gaps and future perspectives

An increasing number of studies have indicated that QS has significant regulatory potential in the anaerobic treatment of high-salinity wastewater. However, the underlying mechanisms and engineering applications remain relatively unknown. Although current studies have confirmed that high-salinity environments can induce the expression of QS-related genes [16], a systematic explanation of the mechanisms by which QS mitigates the negative effects of osmotic stress on cellular metabolic networks, EPS structure, and DIET pathways is lacking. Furthermore, signaling molecules are inherently unstable, and qualitative research on the interference of high salinity with signaling molecule transmission remains scarce.

Moreover, the identities of microbial communities responsible for producing specific signaling molecules in anaerobic biological treatment systems remain unclear, with QS-related research on methanogenic microorganisms being particularly limited [127]. Although some studies have indicated that both acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogens harbor AHL-related regulatory genes [128], direct evidence for QS in methanogenic Archaea and its functional impact on their metabolic activity remains insufficient. Most studies have focused on the role of AHL-mediated QS in anaerobic wastewater treatment, whereas other signaling molecules, such as c-di-GMP and DSF, have received limited attention. Moreover, QS application in the anaerobic treatment of high-salinity wastewater remains largely unexplored.

As discussed above (Section "Potential regulatory roles of quorum sensing in microbial aggregation and granule stability", "Potential regulatory roles of QS in metabolic processes", and "Potential role of quorum sensing in regulating functional microbiome reconstruction"), QS enhancement stabilizes anaerobic systems and enhances methane yield by regulating EPS secretion, granular sludge formation, and key metabolic pathways. Therefore, this strategy has significant potential for alleviating salt inhibition and improving methane recovery in high-salinity wastewater treatment. However, current research on QS-enhanced stress mitigation has mainly focused on other adverse conditions, with relatively few studies on high-salinity conditions. Furthermore, most studies have relied on short-term batch experiments or laboratory-scale reactors, with limited validation in full-scale anaerobic wastewater treatment systems that treat high-salinity wastewater. In addition, the relatively high cost of exogenous signaling molecules may limit the economic feasibility of long-term reactor operation. Thus, compared with repeated dosing of purified signaling molecules, promoting endogenous QS activity, enriching QS-active microbial populations, or using low-cost QS-active materials may be more feasible for long-term application. Large-scale application will also require further evaluation of in situ signal generation, dosing stability, and the potential environmental risks associated with residual signaling molecules in the effluent.

From an application perspective, QS-based regulation should currently be considered a complementary strategy rather than a complete replacement for existing approaches, such as desalination, salinity dilution, stepwise microbial acclimation, addition of osmoprotective compounds or conductive materials, and optimization of reactor configuration. Compared with strategies that directly reduce salinity, QS-based regulation may be more suitable for systems in which dilution or desalination is economically or operationally impractical. Compared with microbial acclimation, QS regulation may actively modulate microbial communication, thereby more effectively guiding EPS secretion, microbial aggregation, maintenance of syntrophic interactions, and recovery of key metabolic functions. Nevertheless, its practical implementation will depend on whether stable QS regulation can be maintained under continuous-flow conditions and integrated with existing reactor operation strategies. Conclusions

High-salinity conditions pose major challenges to anaerobic biological treatment systems, including VFA accumulation, AnGS disintegration, and DIET inhibition, primarily due to disruptions in microbial energy metabolism and imbalanced community interactions. QS contributes to system stabilization by increasing the PN/PS ratio in EPS to maintain granule integrity, enhancing DIET, upregulating key enzymes involved in methanogenesis, and inducing microbial community restructuring toward stronger syntrophic interactions and a more balanced functional metabolism.

QS-based regulation can compensate for the reduced anaerobic activity caused by high salinity, indicating that QS is an important regulatory mechanism for maintaining the stability of anaerobic processes. The addition of exogenous signaling molecules, QS-active microorganisms, porous or conductive materials, and QQ application have been identified as promising regulatory strategies for anaerobic treatment of high-salinity wastewater. However, the underlying mechanisms and effective operational ranges of these strategies under high-salinity conditions require further investigation. Future studies should integrate long-term reactor experiments with multi-omics analyses to clarify QS-mediated regulatory mechanisms and define salinity- and stage-specific operational thresholds.

Additionally, considering the respective advantages and limitations of the four regulatory approaches, stage-based QS regulation (tailored to the developmental stage of the microbial community and the operational status of the reactor) is proposed as a more economical and controllable strategy for managing high-salinity anaerobic systems.
