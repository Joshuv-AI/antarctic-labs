Vibrio parahaemolyticusAHPND infection altered eicosanoid biosynthesis in the gastrointestinal tract of Litopenaeus vannamei
Authors: Wananit Wimuttisuk, Pisut Yotbuntueng, Pacharawan Deenarn, Punsa Tobwor, Surasak Jiemsup, Suganya Yongkiettrakul, Kamonluk Kittiwongpukdee, Siriwan Khidprasert, Chanadda Kasamechotchung, Kitlada Srichomthong, Phawit Putprasert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute Hepatopancreatic Necrosis Disease (AHPND) is one of the main culprits for massive shrimp mortality in the aquaculture industry. AHPND is caused by Vibrio parahaemolyticus (VPAHPND) containing plasmids for the PirA and PirB binary toxins. These toxins cause sloughing and cell death in the hepatopancreatic tubules as well as inflammation in the shrimp gastrointestinal tract. Nevertheless, changes in the pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling molecules have yet to be reported. Here, we examined changes in eicosanoid biosynthesis in the Litopenaeus vannamei gastrointestinal tract infected with VPAHPND isolate 5HP. At 24 h post-infection, cyclooxygenase enzyme levels increased in the hepatopancreases of VPAHPND-infected shrimp. The transcription levels of cytosolic phospholipase A2 were elevated in the intestines of VPAHPND-infected shrimp compared with control shrimp. Although the eicosanoid and PUFA profiles were differentially altered in shrimp hepatopancreas, stomach, and intestines, the levels of prostaglandin F2α were elevated, whereas the levels of 5-hydroxyeicosapentaenoic acid and 5-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid were suppressed in all three organs of VPAHPND-infected shrimp. These findings suggest that eicosanoid changes may serve as biomarkers for shrimp gastrointestinal health. Moreover, they indicate the conserved nature of the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway in the host-pathogen response to bacterial infection in crustaceans.

Introduction

Acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND) was first discovered in China in 2009 after mass mortality of Penaeus monodon and Litopenaeus vannamei [[1], [2], [3]]. Since then, the disease has spread throughout Southeast Asia [1,3]. AHPND is caused by a specific isolate of Vibrio parahaemolyticus (VPAHPND) that encodes Pir-like binary toxins, namely PirA and PirB [4,5]. VPAHPND infects and colonizes the shrimp stomach cuticle, where it secretes PirA/PirB toxins that damage hepatopancreatic cells [6,7]. During early infection, the number of fat storage cell vesicles and fat droplets in shrimp hepatopancreases decreases. As infection progresses, severe atrophy and sloughing of hepatopancreatic epithelial cells are observed [3]. Shrimp become lethargic and anorexic, resulting in 100% mortality in severely affected ponds [3]. Although inflammatory responses in shrimp hepatopancreases have been reported [3,8], changes in the associated inflammatory signaling molecules, such as eicosanoids, remain unexplored.

The production of eicosanoids begins with the extraction of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and arachidonic acid (ARA) from the phospholipid bilayer via cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) enzyme [9]. In the prostaglandin biosynthesis pathway, ARA is converted to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2) by the cyclooxygenase enzyme. PGH2 subsequently binds to various prostaglandin synthases to produce prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α). Additionally, PGD2 can undergo further non-enzymatic reactions to become 15-deoxy-Δ12,14-prostaglandin J2 (15d-PGJ2) [10]. Alternatively, ARA and EPA are converted by lipoxygenase enzymes to produce signaling molecules, such as hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acid (HPETE), leukotrienes, hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (HETE), and hydroxyeicosapentaenoic acid (HEPE) [11,12].

This study investigated changes in the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway in the gastrointestinal tract of shrimp infected with VPAHPND. Results showed the upregulation of the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway at the translation level, with metabolic changes varying according to the specific organ examined. These data highlight the conserved role of the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway in the crustacean immune response to bacterial infection.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Shrimp rearing and VPAHPND challenge was performed according to the guidelines and requirements from Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) as well as international and national legal and ethical requirements [13,14]. The VPAHPND challenge was authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Center for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology (BT-Animal 02/2565).

VPAHPND challenge

L. vannamei (2-3 g body weight) were acclimated in 10 x 300-L tanks (N = 80 each). The shrimp were fed with commercial feed twice daily at 3% body weight. Water temperature was maintained at 28 – 30°C, pH between 7.8 and 8.3, salinity levels at 20 ppt, total ammonia nitrogen <5 ppm, and nitrite <0.5 ppm. VPAHPND isolate 5HP was cultured in 1L of Tryptic soy broth (TSB) with 1.5% NaCl. The culture was added to five VPAHPND rearing tanks to achieve a 104 CFU/mL final concentration. One liter of TSB (1.5% NaCl) was added to the control tanks. Shrimp were challenged for 24 h. Shrimp hepatopancreas, stomach, and intestine were collected as previously described [15].

Verification of VPAHPND infection using the AP4 nested PCR

The VP infection was confirmed using nested PCR analysis of the AP4 gene (abbreviated from AHPND detection version 4) [16]. The primers for the first-step PCR reaction are AP4-F1 (5′ ATGAGTAACAATATAAAACATGAAAC) and AP4-R1 (5′ ACGATTTCGACGTTCCCCAA 3′). The nested PCR was performed using the products from the first-step PCR reaction as a template. The primers are AP4-F2 (5′ TTGAGAATACGGGACGTGGG 3′) and AP4-R2 (5′ GTTAGTCATGTGAGCACCTTC 3’). The annealing temperature and PCR conditions were performed as previously described [16].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Shrimp organs were homogenized separately in liquid N2 using a pestle and mortar. RNA extraction was performed using 40 mg of shrimp tissue per 1 ml Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA). Total RNA was dissolved in DEPC-treated water, and the contaminated genomic DNA was removed by treatment with DNase I (Promega, Wisconsin, USA) at 0.15 U/μg total RNA at 37°C for 30 min [15]. DNase-treated RNA was used as a template for the RevertAid™ First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The oligo (dT)18 was selected as a primer for the cDNA synthesis.

The qPCR analysis was performed using 500 ng of cDNA as a template and the SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix (Bio-Rad, California, USA). The fold change in the prostaglandin biosynthesis gene transcripts relative to elongation factor 1α (EF1α) was determined [17]. Primer sequences of the genes of interest and EF1α are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemical analysis of shrimp hepatopancreas

Fifty shrimp cephalothoraxes were fixed, embedded in paraffin block, and sectioned onto the Histogrip-coated glass slides (Invitrogen) [15,18]. The slides were incubated in the anti-cyclooxygenase antibody (Cat No. ab244261, Abcam, United Kingdom) diluted at a 1:50 (v/v) in PBS in a moist chamber at 37°C overnight. The slides were submerged in PBS and placed under constant agitation for 5 min. The PBS was removed, and the washing process was repeated twice. The slides were incubated in an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody diluted in PBS at a 1:500 (v/v) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, these slides were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. The peroxidase reaction was performed for 30 min using a peroxidase substrate kit (VECTOR NovaRED, California, USA). The slides were washed with distilled water and counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 s. The tissues were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and photographed [19]. The images were analyzed with the ImageJ program (LOCI, University of Wisconsin) to determine the immunoreactive signals in shrimp hepatopancreas [20].

Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS/MS) analysis

Shrimp organs were homogenized in liquid N2 and diluted in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (Invitrogen). The 500-μL aliquot of organ homogenates was mixed with 10 μL of 10% butylated hydroxytoluene (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) in ethanol (w/v) and adjusted to pH 4.0 by adding 5 μL of glacial acetic acid (Merck, New Jersey, USA). Deuterated eicosanoid standards, including EPA-d5, 5-HETE-d8, and PGE2-d4 (Cayman Chemicals, Michigan, USA), were added to the homogenates to determine percent recovery. The mixtures were subjected to ethyl acetate extraction as previously described [15]. The pooled organic phase was dried under speed vacuum and stored at −80°C.

The dried ethyl acetate extract was reconstituted in 100% HPLC-grade ethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 12-HETE-d8 (Cayman Chemical, Michigan, USA) was added to the extract as an internal standard for quantitative analysis. The extract was separated via liquid chromatography using the Waters Acclaim™ RSLC 120C18 (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.2 μm) column. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.01% (v/v) acetic acid in water. Mobile phase B consisted of 0.01% (v/v) acetic acid in acetonitrile. An Orbitrap Fusion™ Tribrid™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated in a negative ion mode using Xcalibur software (version 4.4.16.14). Additionally, the instrument calibration was performed using the Pierce™ FlexMix™ calibration solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The tandem mass spectrometry acquisition parameters and the scheduled parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) method were performed as previously described [21].

Statistical analysis

Significant differences between the means of the control and VPAHPND-infected groups were determined using the t-test (P < 0.05 for * and P < 0.01 for **).

Immunohistochemical analysis of COX in shrimp hepatopancreas

The cellular structure of shrimp hepatopancreases collected from the control and VPAHPND-infected groups was confirmed using hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 1D and G). Negative controls were performed on the control and VPAHPND-infected group to confirm the absence of COX immunoactivity signal (Fig. 1E and H). The L. vannamei COX enzyme was detected based on the red-brown precipitates, which localized in the apical compartment of B cells in the hepatopancreases of VPAHPND-infected shrimp and control shrimp (Fig. 1F and I, black arrows). Vesicles in B-cells were less clearly visible in the hepatopancreas of VPAHPND-infected samples compared to the control group. Moreover, COX immunoreactive signals from the VPAHPND-infected shrimp were higher than those of the control shrimp (Fig. 1J).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of eicosanoid biosynthesis genes

The qPCR analysis was performed on cDNA extracted from shrimp hepatopancreases, stomachs, and intestines to determine changes in the transcription levels of cPLA2, COX, and prostaglandin F synthase (PGFS). VPAHPND infection increased the transcription levels of cPLA2 in shrimp intestines (Fig. 1K). On the other hand, the transcription levels of COX and PGFS were comparable in all three organs of the control and VPAHPND-infected shrimp (Fig. 1L and M).

Changes in the eicosanoid profiles in the gastrointestinal tract of VPAHPND-infected shrimp

The UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis was performed on ethyl acetate extracts of shrimp hepatopancreases, stomachs, and intestines. Additionally, the UHPLC-HRMS/MS also detected the levels of three polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), including ARA, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and EPA.

Discussion

This study explores changes in the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway as part of the crustacean host-pathogen responses to VPAHPND infection in L. vannamei. The immunohistochemistry analysis demonstrated that VPAHPND infection increased the levels of COX transcripts in shrimp hepatopancreas. Additionally, the UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis revealed that VPAHPND infection differentially altered the levels of eicosanoids and PUFAs in shrimp hepatopancreas, stomach, and intestine.

During the initial phase of VPAHPND infection, the levels of VPAHPND presented in shrimp were correlated with the reduction in size of hepatopancreatic R and B cells [22]. As the VPAHPND infection became more severe, the atrophy in the shrimp hepatopancreas was observed due to extensive shedding and sloughing of hepatopancreatic tubular epithelial cells [22]. Because eicosanoids are early responders to bacterial and viral infections in mammals, our study examined changes that occurred at 24 hpi. The immunostaining signals revealed that COX was localized at the apical compartment of the B cells in both the control and VPAHPND-infected shrimp, suggesting that B cells are the production sites for prostaglandins in hepatopancreatic cells. Additionally, the increasing COX immunoreactive signals in the VPAHPND-infected hepatopancreas suggest the upregulation of eicosanoid biosynthesis. A separate study with the transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of L. vannamei hepatopancreas infected with VPAHPND uncovered the upregulation of phospholipase A2 (PLA2) and COX transcripts as well as increasing levels of ARA [23]. While the upregulation of COX transcripts matched the immunohistochemistry results from our study, the levels of ARA and cPLA2 transcripts differed. In fact, the absence of cPLA2 transcripts in hepatopancreas samples in this study may be responsible for the reduction of ARA levels in the hepatopancreases of VPAHPND-infected shrimp.

Recently, the UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis of L. vanmamei post-larvae led to the identification of 9 eicosanoids, including PGF2α, 15d-PGJ2, 5-HEPE, 8-HEPE, 12-HEPE, 18-HEPE, 8-HETE, 11-HETE, and 12-HETE [24]. Our study was able to identify four additional eicosanoids, namely 9-HEPE, 15-HEPE, 5-HETE, and 9-HETE, in the L. vannamei gastrointestinal tract, which indicates that the production of certain eicosanoids does not occur at the post-larvae stage. Moreover, HETE and HEPE, which were hydroxylated at the same carbon position, responded the same way to VPAHPND infection. We believe that their shared response likely depends on the availability of a specific lipoxygenase enzyme, which converts ARA into HETE and EPA into HEPE by placing the hydroxyl group at the same carbon position [25,26]. Nevertheless, the lipoxygenase genes have yet to be identified in crustaceans.

Although it was reported that the PirA and PirB binary toxin causes damage in shrimp hepatopancreas, several studies revealed that indicators for inflammation were also observed in the stomach and intestine of VPAHPND-infected shrimp [22,[27], [28], [29]]. For example, the L. vannamei with a severe VPAHPND infection displayed severe necrosis in the hepatopancreatic tubules, the detachment of epithelial cells, and hemocyte infiltrations in the shrimp stomachs and intestines [27]. The effects of VPAHPND infection in the shrimp gastrointestinal tract were also reported in the brine shrimp, in which PirA and PirB toxins resulted in necrosis and damaged epithelial cells as well as disrupting enterocytes in the hindgut and midgut regions [22]. Additionally, VPAHPND infection disrupted intestinal function by increasing intestinal permeability and reducing nutrient absorption in L. vannamei [28].

By establishing the eicosanoid biosynthesis pathway as part of the host-pathogen response in crustaceans, this study not only connects the previously existing pathway for the regulation of crustacean innate immunity but also emphasizes the conserved function of eicosanoids among mammals, insects, and crustaceans. Future studies to examine the impact of eicosanoids on the signal transduction pathway and innate immune response will offer a more in-depth perspective on the roles of the host-pathogen response in crustaceans.
