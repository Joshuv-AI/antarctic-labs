A reduced-order model for parametrized Optimal Transport problems
Elise Bonnet-Weill, Virginie Ehrlacher, Luca Nenna

Abstract
In this work, we aim at efficiently solving a parametrized family of optimal transport problems by using model order reduction methods. We propose a reduced-order model by adding to the primal (respectively dual) version of the high-fidelity model the additional constraint to live in a non negative sub cone (resp. in subspaces) of small dimension. The reduced-order model then reads as a linear program with a small number of degrees of freedom and constraints. We identify explicit conditions under which this reduced-order model has at least one solution. We propose two a posteriori error estimations that bounds the error between the optimal values of the high-fidelity problem and the reduced-order model. As one of these estimations requires the computation of non linear terms (with respect to the reduction of dimension), we use an Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) (see e.g. [ 33 ] or [ 2 ] ) to numerically efficiently compute this estimation. We apply the whole methodology on a simple 1D example and on a problem of color transfer between images, and compare its performances to Sinkhorn algorithm.

Introduction

The numerical simulation of complex physical, economic, or biological systems often requires the repeated solution of parametric problems under varying conditions — a task that rapidly becomes computationally prohibitive when state-of-the-art discretization methods are employed. In this context, Model Order Reduction (MOR) has emerged as a fundamental strategy to construct low-dimensional surrogate models that accurately reproduce the behavior of high-fidelity numerical simulations at a fraction of their computational cost. Among the various MOR strategies, Reduced Basis (RB) methods have established themselves as a mathematically rigorous and computationally efficient framework, particularly well-suited to parametric partial differential equations (PDEs). The aim of the present work is to propose a new reduced basis strategy to build reduced-order models to accelerate the resolution of parametrized Optimal Transport problems. We first give some introduction to reduced basis methods and numerical approaches for the resolution of optimal transport problems and then outline the main contributions of our paper.

Reduced Basis Methods.

The reduced basis methodology was firstly introduced in the pioneering works of
[
1
]
and
[
38
]
in the context of structural mechanics, and subsequently made mathematically rigorous through the contributions of
[
42
]
.
The central idea is to approximate the solution manifold — the set of all solutions to a parametric problem as the parameter varies over its admissible domain — by a low-dimensional affine subspace, the so-called reduced basis space, constructed from a carefully selected set of high-fidelity snapshot solutions.
The RB approximation is then sought in this surrogate space via a Galerkin or Petrov–Galerkin projection, yielding a system of dramatically reduced size that can be solved orders of magnitude faster than the original truth discretization. A theoretical foundation for the convergence of the greedy construction algorithm was later laid by
[
8
]
, and further refined by
[
6
]
. A cornerstone of the RB methodology is the availability of rigorous and efficiently computable a posteriori error estimators, which certify the accuracy of the reduced solution with respect to the high-fidelity reference and drive the greedy algorithm used for basis construction. Such estimators, typically based on dual norms of residuals, were developed for parabolic problems by
[
21
]
and for finite-volume evolution schemes by
[
22
]
.
These estimators enable an offline-online computational decomposition: the expensive pre-computations (basis generation, affine decomposition of operators) are performed once during an offline phase, while the online phase — consisting in solving the reduced system and evaluating the error bound for a new parameter instance — is performed at marginal cost, independent of the dimension of the high-fidelity space. The method has since been systematically exposed in textbook form by
[
24
]
and by
[
43
]
, and its general theory for affinely parametrized elliptic coercive PDEs is thoroughly surveyed in
[
45
]
. Extensions to non-affinely parametrized problems, such as those arising in geometry optimization, have been addressed through the Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) introduced by
[
2
]
, which approximates non-affine coefficient functions by a low-rank collateral expansion and permits the offline-online decomposition to be maintained. Non-affine and nonlinear parametric dependence was treated within the full RB framework by
[
20
]
. Beyond the classical greedy construction, the Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) provides an alternative, singular-value-decomposition-based approach to extracting dominant modes from a set of snapshots
[
50
,
23
]
.

Numerical Methods for Optimal Transport.

Optimal Transport (OT) is a classical mathematical theory, dating back to Monge
[
37
]
and Kantorovich
[
27
]
, that formalizes the problem of finding the most efficient way to redistribute one probability measure into another with respect to a given cost function. The monographs by Villani
[
49
,
48
]
and Santambrogio
[
46
]
, and the computational review by Peyré and Cuturi
[
40
]
, provide comprehensive accounts of the theory and its modern applications, which span machine learning, image processing, computational fluid mechanics, economics, and biology.

At the heart of the theory lies the Kantorovich dual problem, whose solutions — the Kantorovich potentials — are functions whose gradients (in the quadratic cost case) recover the optimal transport map through Brenier’s theorem. The regularity theory of these potentials is well-understood under smoothness assumptions on the source and target densities, tracing back to Caffarelli’s work on the Monge–Ampère equation and generalized to a large class of cost functions by
[
32
]
. Quantitative stability of optimal transport maps and their associated potentials with respect to perturbations of the input measures has been studied by
[
19
]
and, in a quantitative and linearized form amenable to data science applications, by Mérigot, Delalande and Chazal
[
34
]
. This stability theory is fundamental: it provides the mathematical basis for expecting the map sending a parametric family of measures to the corresponding Kantorovich potentials — to exhibit regularity in the parameter, a prerequisite for any successful model reduction strategy.

From a numerical point of view, the discrete formulation of OT leads to a linear programming (LP) problem — the classical transportation problem — whose computational complexity fast increases with the dimension of the problem. This has motivated the development of more scalable approaches. The entropic regularization of OT, introduced in its modern form in
[
11
,
18
,
5
]
, replaces the LP by a strictly convex optimization problem solvable via the Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix-scaling algorithm at a speed faster than LP-based solvers. The resulting Sinkhorn distances have become ubiquitous in machine learning, despite the bias introduced by regularization. De-biased versions, including the Sinkhorn divergences of
[
17
]
, restore (some) properties of a genuine distance while retaining computational efficiency.

Alternative numerical approaches include semi-discrete OT methods, which handle the case where one measure is continuous and the other discrete through Laguerre cell decompositions and Newton-type or quasi-Newton solvers, as developed by
[
36
]
and
[
31
]
; methods based on the dynamic Benamou–Brenier fluid mechanics formulation
[
4
]
, which reformulates OT as a convex optimization over flows and has led to efficient proximal splitting algorithms; and approaches grounded in the theory of semi-smooth Newton methods for the Monge–Ampère equation. A unified treatment of discretization methods and their algorithms, covering the assignment problem, entropic regularization, and semi-discrete OT, is provided in the handbook chapter by
[
35
]
. Sliced Wasserstein distances offer yet another computationally attractive alternative based on one-dimensional projections.

Parametric Optimal Transport and Model Reduction.

Despite this rich landscape of numerical methods, the problem of computing Kantorovich potentials repeatedly for varying parameter values — arising for instance in uncertainty quantification, shape optimization, multi-marginal OT, or Wasserstein barycenter computation — remains computationally intensive. Each parameter query requires solving an independent optimization problem of large scale, rendering parameter sweeps, sensitivity analyses, or real-time applications out of reach with standard solvers. The object of primary interest in this paper is therefore the parametric map which sends a parametric family of measures to the corresponding family of Kantorovich potentials. This is a fundamentally different perspective from the recent literature on MOR for transport-dominated PDEs
[
9
,
7
]
, which uses OT as a tool to improve reduced basis constructions for PDE solutions that are merely dominated by advection; here, the optimal transport problem itself is parametric, and the Kantorovich potential is the quantity of interest.

This shift in viewpoint introduces specific mathematical challenges. First, Kantorovich potentials are only defined up to an additive constant, so the solution map is multi-valued and must be normalized appropriately for a well-posed reduction problem. Second, the derivation of rigorous a posteriori error estimators for the Kantorovich potential — whose accuracy is typically measured in terms of the duality gap or the Wasserstein distance — requires adapting the residual-based methodology of classical RB methods to the OT setting. While the stability theory of Optimal Transport provides qualitative guidance, quantitative and computable error bounds for reduced approximations of Kantorovich potentials have not yet been established.

Recent efforts have begun to address the broader challenge of combining model reduction with optimal transport, forming a rich and rapidly growing literature. A pioneering contribution in this direction is
[
25
]
, who proposed to use solutions of Monge–Kantorovich mass transfer problems to extract advection modes — low-order representations of transport phenomena that are intrinsically elusive for standard POD-based methods. This seminal work demonstrated that OT provides a geometrically meaningful way to encode advection-dominated dynamics into reduced descriptions, and motivated a subsequent line of research on registration-based model reduction. This registration viewpoint is formalized in
[
47
]
into a general equation-independent framework for parameterized MOR, constructing parameter-dependent bijective mappings that align coherent solution features — shocks, shear layers — into a fixed reference configuration before applying linear compression. We refer the reader to extensions of this registration-based approach using OT in
[
26
,
10
]
.
Complementing these registration-based approaches, the theoretical groundwork for nonlinear model reduction in Wasserstein spaces was laid in
[
15
]
, using Wasserstein barycenters and tangent-space approximations for conservative PDEs. Several extensions were considered, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
7
,
14
,
12
,
39
]
. We highlight in particular the work
[
29
]
where the authors proposed a ROM framework integrating OT theory and neural-network methods via kernel POD with the Wasserstein distance.

However, all of these contributions — whether they use OT to construct registration mappings for PDE solutions, or address model reduction in Wasserstein spaces for PDE solutions — treat optimal transport as a computational tool for handling transport-dominated PDE fields. None of them addresses the problem of directly and efficiently computing parametric Kantorovich potentials themselves, which is the focus of the present work. The only work we are aware of in this direction is the contribution
[
30
]
where parametrized optimal transport problems were solved by means of neural networks and machine learning techniques.

Contributions of this work.

In this paper, we develop a novel reduced basis method for parametric optimal transport problems. More precisely, given a parametric family of optimal transport problems where the measures vary with a parameter, our framework constructs a low-dimensional approximation space for the associated family of Kantorovich potentials through an offline procedure (typically POD). To the best of our knowledge, the reduced basis approximation of parametric Kantorovich potentials, with certified a posteriori error estimation, has not yet been addressed in the literature. Our reduced-order model actually reads as a primal-dual approach with a reduced number of degrees of freedom. As a by-product, we also obtain reduced approximations of the parametric optimal transport plans. The key contributions are:

(i)

a well-posed formulation of the parametric Kantorovich dual suited to RB approximation;

(ii)

the derivation of computable and rigorous a posteriori error estimators enabling reliable online evaluation;

(iii)

an efficient offline-online decomposition that makes the online cost independent of the high-fidelity discretization dimension.

We demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the proposed approach on a range of parametric OT benchmarks, achieving speedups of several orders of magnitude, in particular for color transfer applications.

3
Reduced-order model for the discrete optimal transport problem and its dual

In this section, we introduce the reduced-order linear
programs that approximate the problems (

P
α

) and
(

D
α

). It consists in building a non-negative cone
(respectively two vector spaces) of smaller dimension by using
snapshots methods on the primal problem (

P
α

)
(respectively the dual (

D
α

)), and to rewrite the
problem where the minimization is performed on these cone or spaces.

We begin by explaining how these cone and spaces are defined. We
then build the reduced-order model, which reads as a linear program
with a small number of variables and constraints.
We then provide sufficient conditions under which the reduced-order model has a least one solution. Finally, we propose an
alternative method to build subspaces
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
of small
dimension.

3.1
Construction of the reduced bases

For the approximation of the primal solutions is selected in a reduced non-negative cone
\mathcal{W}_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{y}}
spanned by a family of vectors
\left\{\mathbf{w}_{r}\right\}_{1\leq r\leq R}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{y}}
for
R\ll\mathcal{N}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{y}
. Concerning the dual solutions we approximate them by looking at the elements of reduced subspaces
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}}
and
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{y}}
spanned by two families of vectors
\left\{\mathbf{u}_{n}\right\}_{1\leq n\leq N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}}
and
\left\{\mathbf{v}_{m}\right\}_{1\leq m\leq M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{y}}
for
N\ll\mathcal{N}_{x}
and
M\ll\mathcal{N}_{y}
.

The reduced variables will then be written as :

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbf{p}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{w}_{r}\;\text{with }\mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}^{R}_{+}\\
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{u}_{n}\;\text{with }\mathbf{a}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{b}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{v}_{m}\;\text{with }\mathbf{b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\\
\end{array}

(24)

In the following, we will denote by
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
the matrix whose columns are the vectors
\left\{\mathbf{w}_{r}\right\}_{1\leq r\leq R}
, and by
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
(respectively
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
) the matrix composed by
\left\{\mathbf{u}_{n}\right\}_{1\leq n\leq N}
(resp.
by
\left\{\mathbf{v}_{m}\right\}_{1\leq m\leq M}
).

Using these notations, the reduced solution may be rewritten as matrix-vector multiplication:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{a}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\end{array}

(25)

The Primal reduced space
\mathcal{W}_{+}

We admit here that the set of parameters
\mathcal{A}
is a convex set generated by a finite number of extreme points. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}\subset\mathcal{A}
be the set of extreme points of
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}
be a finite training set containing the set of extreme points, namely
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}\subset\mathcal{A}
.
Then
\mathcal{W}_{+}
is defined as the cone generated by the solutions to the problem (

P
α

) for all the parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}
, that is

\mathcal{W}_{+}:=\text{cone}_{+}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}},

(26)

and
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
is the matrix formed by stacking the transport plans
\left\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}}
columnwise.
We explain in Section
3.3
that the inclusion
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{P}
guarantees the non-emptiness of the feasible set.

Note that, as usual in basis reduction, it is possible to reduce the dimension of
\mathcal{W}_{+}
with a non-negative matrix factorization. In this case, the solutions at the extreme points
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}
must be added to
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
.

The Dual reduced spaces
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}

Let us assume that we are given a data set of dual solutions
\left\{(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\psi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{D}}
for a finite training subset
\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{D}\subset\mathcal{A}
.
The dual reduced space
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{V}
) is the space generated by
\left\{(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{D}}
and
\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{x}}
(resp. by
\left\{(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{train}}^{D}}
and
\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{y}}
). We explain in Section
3.3
why the
\mathbb{1}_{\bullet}
vectors are added. We propose later in Section
3.4
an alternative way of building
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, as the corresponding method requires further understanding of the reduced-order model.

3.2
The reduced-order model

We now explain how to use the reduced cones and bases in order to obtain a reduced-order model. In particular we proceed in two steps: (1) we build a semi-reduced-order model, where the ambient space of the high-fidelity primal (

P
α

) is reduced (2) we further reduce the dimension of the space variable of the dual of this semi-reduced-order model.

Notice that by inverting the two steps (we first reduce the dimension of the variables of the dual and then reduce the dimension of the variables of the associated primal) we obtain in the end the same fully reduced-order model, so only the semi-reduced-order models will differ. We explain how both of them are obtained in what follows.

Reduction on Primal-then-Dual

We propose here a semi-reduced-order model where the variables of the primal are searched in a non-negative cone of smaller dimension. It is obtained as rewriting (

P
α

) with the added constraint that the solutions should belong to the non-negative cone
\mathcal{W}_{+}
:

I_{R_{P}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\\
\mathbf{A}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\end{array}}\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\mathbf{c}\right\rangle.

(27)

If we use the notations introduced previously (
25
), this problem can be reformulated in the following form :

I_{R_{P}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{R}\\
\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\end{array}}\left\langle\mathbf{p},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}\right\rangle.

(
R_{P}P_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
)

The dual of this semi-reduced-order problem writes

{J_{R_{P}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathop{\sup}_{\begin{array}[]{c}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}+\mathcal{N}_{y}}\\

\boldsymbol{\psi}\end{pmatrix}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}\\
\end{array}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle.}

(
R_{P}D_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
)

We now add to this semi-reduced-order dual problem the constraint that the Kantorovitch potentials should belong to the subspaces
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
. We obtain the following reduced dual:

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lll}J_{R}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})&:=&\displaystyle\mathop{\sup}_{\begin{array}[]{c}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{A}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\end{pmatrix}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}\\
\end{array}}\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle+\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle\\

\mathbf{b}\end{pmatrix}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}\\

(28)

where

\widehat{\mathbf{S}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

The primal reduced problem then writes

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lll}I_{R}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})&:=&\displaystyle\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\

&=&\displaystyle\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{R}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{p}=\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\end{array}}\left\langle\mathbf{p},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}\right\rangle.\end{array}}

(30)

We can re-write this problem in the standard form of linear programming : let
\widehat{\mathbf{c}}:=\widehat{\textbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, and let
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\textbf{W}}
.

The reduced problem then writes

\boxed{I_{R}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{R}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\end{array}}\left\langle\mathbf{p},\widehat{\mathbf{c}}\right\rangle,}

(
RP_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
)

and its dual

\boxed{J_{R}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\mathop{\inf}_{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{b}\end{pmatrix}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{c}}\end{array}}\left\langle\mathbf{a},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathbf{b},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle,}

Lemma 3.7
.

The set
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=({\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{x}+K_{y}},\sum_{k=1}^{K_{x}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{x}_{k}=1,\sum_{l=1}^{K_{y}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{y}_{l}=1\right\}
has
K_{x}\times K_{y}
extreme points, defined by
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}=\left\{(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{l})\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
, where
\left\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}
and
\left\{\mathbf{f}_{k}\right\}_{1\leq l\leq K_{y}}
are the elementary basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{K_{x}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{K_{y}}
.

Proof.

We begin by showing the generative property of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}
. We then prove that the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}
are extreme points by noticing each point
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}
, can be expressed as one unique convex combination of points of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ext}}
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y})=\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}^{x}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}},\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l}^{y}\right\}_{1\leq l\leq K_{y}}\right)
be an element of
\mathcal{A}
. Let us show that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a convex combination of
\left\{\left(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{l}\right)\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
associated with the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}^{x}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l}^{y}\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
:

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{x}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l^{\prime}}^{y}(\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{f}_{l^{\prime}})=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}).

(38)

We first check that the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
form a convex combination. This arises from the fact that the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}
are themselves convex combinations, so for any
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq K_{x}
and
1\leq l^{\prime}\leq K_{y}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\geq 0
, and

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{x}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l^{\prime}}^{y}=1.

(39)

Now, notice that we can decompose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}
as sums of elementary vectors:
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{x}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}=\sum_{l=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}_{l}\mathbf{f}_{l}
.

Thus, for
1\leq k\leq K_{x}
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{x}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l^{\prime}}^{y}\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}}\right)_{k}&=\displaystyle{}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{x}\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}}\right)_{k}\\
&\displaystyle{}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}^{x}\mathbf{e}_{k})_{k}\\
&\displaystyle{}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{x}_{k},\end{array}

(40)

and for
1\leq l\leq K_{y}
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{x}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l^{\prime}}^{y}\mathbf{f}_{l^{\prime}}\right)_{l}&=\displaystyle{}\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l^{\prime}}^{y}\mathbf{f}_{l^{\prime}}\right)_{l}\\
&\displaystyle{}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l}^{y}\mathbf{f}_{l})_{l}\\
&\displaystyle{}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{y}_{l}\end{array}

(41)

This concludes the fact that
\left\{\left(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{l}\right)\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
is a generative set of
\mathcal{A}
.

Now, suppose that, for
1\leq k\leq K_{x}
ans
1\leq l\leq K_{y}
, the point
\displaystyle{}(\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{f}_{l^{\prime}})
is expressed as a convex combination of
\left\{\left(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{l}\right)\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
associated to the coefficients
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\}
. Then, in particular :

\mathbf{e}_{k}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K_{x}}\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\right)\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}},

(42)

By linear independence of the elementary vectors
\mathbf{e}_{k^{\prime}}

\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}=0,

(43)

and by non-negativity of the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we obtain that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=0}
if
k^{\prime}\neq k
.

By a similar computation, since the
\{\mathbf{f}_{l^{\prime}}\}_{1\leq l^{\prime}\leq K_{y}}
are independent, by non-negativity of the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, for every
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq K_{x}
,
\leq l^{\prime}\leq K_{y}

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}=0

Since the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
sum to one, this means that the only coefficient that can be non zero, namely

\left\{\left(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{l}\right)\right\}_{\footnotesize\begin{matrix}1\leq k\leq K_{x}\\
1\leq l\leq K_{y}\end{matrix}}
can be written as one unique convex combination of elements of this set.

). As before, only focus on the construction of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, because the construction of
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
is analogous.
To make the following method work, we place ourselves in that case where the generating family
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}
is a family of independent vectors (therefore of maximal rank
K_{x}
).
We first detail the construction in Algorithm

build this way satisfies the condition of Property

Algorithm 1

Gram-Schmidt process

1:

Inputs:

\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}

2:

Outputs:

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}:=\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}

3:

Initialisation:

4:

\mathbf{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

5:

for

k=2,\dots,K_{x}

do

6:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\sum_{z=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{z}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{z}

7:

end

for

Let us verify that the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
built this way satisfies the expected rank condition. This derives from direct consequences of the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalisation process.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}
built via Gram-Schmidt algorithm
1
applied to the family of independant vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq K_{x}}
. Then

1.

For every
1\leq k\neq k^{\prime}\leq K_{x}
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}

2.

For
1\leq k\leq K_{x}
,
\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k})

3.

For
2\leq k\leq K_{x}
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1})^{\perp}

4.

For
1\leq k\leq K_{x}
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\neq 0
.

Proof.

We prove this lemma recursively: for k=1,
\mathbf{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
, thus
\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1})=\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
and
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}>0
.

Let us now suppose the lemma true up to rank
k-1\geq 1
.
Let
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k-1
. Using item 1 up to rank
k-1
we have :

\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{k}=\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\sum_{z=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{z}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{z}=\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}=0,

(59)

which proves 1 up to rank
k
.

Now, by item 2 at rank
k-1
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}
can be seen as a vector combination of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
. Thus
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
, and again by item 2 on rank
k-1
,

\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1})=\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}),

(60)

thus,

\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k})\subset\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}).

(61)

For the converse inclusion, we know that

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}=\mathbf{u}_{k}+\sum_{z=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{z}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{z},

(62)

thus
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots\mathbf{u}_{k-1},\mathbf{u}_{k})
.
Again by item 2 applied on
k-1
we obtain

\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\subset\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}).

(63)

We deduce equality from the two inclusions, and item 2 is true at rank
k
.

Item 3 is a consequence of items 1 and 2 : since
\mathbf{u}_{k}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}}
for
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k-1
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1})^{\perp}
By item 2,
\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1})^{\perp}=\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1})^{\perp}
, thus
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1})^{\perp}
.

Finally, item 4 is a consequence of items 1 and 2 conjugated to the fact that, by hypothesis,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1}
. By independence of the family
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
, and by item 2,
\mathbf{u}_{k}\neq 0
. Indeed, otherwise we would have :

0=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\sum_{z=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{z}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{z},

(64)

thus
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1})=\mathrm{Im}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k-1})
, and that would contradict the independency hypothesis. Let us now write the scalar product and use item
1
:

\mathbf{u}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{T}\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}+\sum_{z=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{z}\|^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{z}\right)=\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}>0,

(65)

which gives item 4 at rank
k
.

∎

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
be the matrix build with Gram-Schmidt algorithm
1
on the family
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}}
. Then
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}|\dots|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\end{pmatrix}
is of rank
K_{x}
.

Proof.

Using the orthogonality conclusion 3 of the lemma
3.16
, we have :

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}|\dots|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\mathbf{u}_{K_{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{K_{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}&\dots&&\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\\
0&\mathbf{u}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&\dots&0&\mathbf{u}_{K_{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K_{x}}\end{pmatrix}.

(66)

By conclusion 4 in the lemma
3.16
, this upper triangular matrix has non zero diagonal and is thus of maximal rank
K_{x}
.
∎

Corollary 3.18

To prove Corollary

Lemma 3.19
.

Let

be positive integers and

\textbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}\times D_{3}}

\mathrm{ker}(\textbf{FM})=\left\{\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q},\textbf{M}\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{ker}(\textbf{F)}\right\}=\left\{\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q},\textbf{M}\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{ker}(\textbf{EF)}\right\}=\mathrm{ker}(\textbf{EFM}).

(67)

Therefore,
\mathrm{rank(\textbf{EFM})}=\mathrm{rank(\textbf{FM})}
.
∎

We now prove Corollary
3.18
.

Proof.

Since every column of
\mathrm{rank}((\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}})_{x})

(68)

From corollary
3.17
, the hypothesis of lemma
3.19
hold with
3.19

We thus focus on bounding the quantity

Let us first recall, that, by strong duality, the optimal values of the primal and dual of the high-fidelity model (resp. of the reduced-order model) are equal :

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{b}^{*}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{v}_{m}.\end{array}

(71)

Since this pair is optimal, the followig equality holds :

I_{R}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle+\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle.

\begin{array}[]{ll}I(\boldsymbol{\alpha})&\geq\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle+\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{c}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle.\\
\end{array}

Evaluation of the computational

We focus here on the computational cost of the term
\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{c}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle

\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\bar{c}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle

We begin by replacing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
with its expression in (

as a convex combination of

\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{y}}\alpha^{y}_{l}b_{m}\left\langle\mathbf{v}^{*}_{m},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{l}\right\rangle.

(77)

The expression
\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{c}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle
can be decomposed as follows:

\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{c}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right\rangle=\sum_{l=1}^{K_{y}}\alpha^{y}_{l}\left\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{c}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\boldsymbol{\nu}^{l}\right\rangle.

(78)

In general, the

\mathcal{N}_{y}\mathcal{N}_{x}

For a given

The reduction method that we proposed in Section
3

\mathcal{N}_{x}
and
\mathcal{N}_{y}

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}

⁢
R

P

Building the reduced-order model without computing
\mathbf{W}

We show here that there is no need to compute explicitly
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
to build the reduced order model (

⁢
R

P
α

).
The matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
has the form

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{R})\end{pmatrix},

(87)

where
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r})\}_{1\leq r\leq R}
are solutions of the high-fidelity problems
\{P_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}}\}_{1\leq r\leq R}
. In the reduced-order model (

⁢
R

P
α

),
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
appears in the cost in the form
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}
and in the constraints in the form
\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
. But both of these matrix can be reformulated. On one hand, we have

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})^{T}\mathbf{c}|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{R})^{T}\mathbf{c}\end{pmatrix}^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}I(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})|\cdots|I(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{R})\end{pmatrix}^{T},

(88)

Thus
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}\mathbf{c}
is obtained by stacking the optimal costs of the high-fidelity problems at
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}\}_{1\leq r\leq R}
.
On the other hand,

\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{R})\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{R})\end{pmatrix},

(89)

thus
\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
can even be obtained without solving any proble.

5.3
Application : Reduced-bases applied to color transfer

In this section, we present an application of the reduction method to a color transfer problem. The principle of color transfer is to modify an image so that its color histogram matches a target histogram (named color palette). To do so,
[
41
]
and
[
44
]
propose to match the histogram of the original image to the target palette with a map that minimizes the total transport cost between these two histograms.
In the present case, we propose a variation of this problem: given an image and
K_{y}
color histograms, we would like to transfer the color histogram of our image to color palettes built as convex combinations of the
K_{y}
histograms. We begin by introducing the transport problem solved to perform the color transfer. We then compare the results obtained with the reduced-order model to what is obtained solving the high-fidelity model.

Principle of color transfer

Given a source image
\mathrm{Im}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{x}}
its color histogram. Let
\{\mathrm{Target_{l}}\}_{1\leq l\leq K_{y}}
be target palettes and
\{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq K_{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{y}^{3}})^{K_{y}}
be their corresponding color histograms. Given a convex combination
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\sum_{1\leq l\leq K_{y}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{l}
of these color histograms, we would like to find a map
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{x}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{y}}
that transports
\boldsymbol{\mu}
onto
\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and that minimize the total transport cost :

\mathop{\min}_{\begin{array}[]{c}T:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{x}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{y}}\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i:T(i)=j}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})_{j},\text{ for }j=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}),1\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\leq\mathcal{N}_{y}\end{array}}\displaystyle\sum_{\small\begin{array}[]{c}i=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}),\\
1\leq i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\leq\mathcal{N}_{x}\end{array}}\mathbf{C}_{iT(i)},

(92)

where
\mathbf{C}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{x}\times\mathcal{N}^{3}_{y}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In practice, for
i=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
and
j=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
,
\{\mathbf{C}_{i,j}=|i_{1}-j_{1}|^{2}+|i_{2}-j_{2}|^{2}+|i_{3}-j_{3}|^{2}\}
.
As underlined in
[
40
]
, this discrete Monge problem is possibly not well defined. We relax this problem with the Kantorovitch formulation

\displaystyle\mathop{\min}_{\begin{array}[]{cc}&\boldsymbol{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{N}^{3}_{x}\times\mathcal{N}^{3}_{y}}\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{j}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}\text{ for }i=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}),1\leq i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\leq\mathcal{N}_{x},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\text{ for }j=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}),1\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\leq\mathcal{N}_{y}\\
\end{array}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\Pi},\mathbf{C}\right\rangle.

(93)

Problem (
93
) reads as a 3D and two-marginal optimal transport problem.
To solve it and build a reduced order model, we use the Sinkhorn method designed in
[
13
]
.
Once an optimal transport plan has been built, it is possible to obtain a map following the method described in Section
5.2
.
