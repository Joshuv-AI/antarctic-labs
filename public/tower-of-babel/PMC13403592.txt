Spinal cord stimulation for atypical anterior chest wall complex regional pain syndrome: illustrative case
Authors: Camron Chugg, Jennifer Bowden, Brian Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDComplex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) most commonly affects distal extremities; atypical truncal presentations may be underrecognized and can mimic cardiopulmonary pathology.OBSERVATIONSA 27-year-old male developed chronic, severe anterior chest wall pain characterized by marked tactile allodynia, episodic erythema, and environmental trigger sensitivity. An extensive multidisciplinary evaluation excluded cardiopulmonary, gastrointestinal, dermatological, infectious, and structural etiologies. The clinical phenotype met Budapest criteria for CRPS type I, including disproportionate pain, symptoms across multiple domains, and objective signs on examination, without an alternative diagnosis better explaining the presentation. Symptoms were refractory to multimodal therapy, prompting a spinal cord stimulation (SCS) trial followed by permanent implantation after significant trial benefit. The patient experienced sustained analgesia and functional improvement at 3, 12, and 24 months, with only minor stimulation-related limitations.LESSONSThis case demonstrates that CRPS may present in an atypical anterior chest wall distribution. Explicit Budapes criteria documentation and a reproducible exclusionary workup strengthen diagnostic confidence. SCS may provide durable relief for refractory atypical CRPS phenotypes. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26152

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a chronic neuropathic disorder characterized by sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor, and trophic abnormalities. Although most cases involve extremities, rare chest wall presentations have been described and may mimic acute coronary or pulmonary disease, leading to extensive evaluation and diagnostic delay.1

Anterior chest wall CRPS may involve sensitization of the intercostobrachial nerve resulting in referred hypersensitivity and pain in a distribution easily mistaken for cardiopulmonary pathology.2

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is well established for refractory CRPS; however, its role in chest wall–predominant CRPS remains poorly documented.3 This case highlights an atypical CRPS presentation successfully treated with neuromodulation.

Illustrative Case

A 27-year-old male presented with a 7-year history of severe anterior chest wall pain that began abruptly while he was traveling abroad. There was no clear inciting event identified. He did not recall any major trauma, infectious illness, or other memorable precipitant, although he did acknowledge the possibility of a minor event that went unnoticed at the time. He described the pain as a severe stabbing sensation with intermittent superimposed crushing pain. A patient-completed pain diagram demonstrated pain localization predominantly to the bilateral anterior chest wall with extension into the lateral thoracic/upper abdominal region. Before implantation, baseline pain was reported as 8/10, with flares up to 10/10. After implantation, baseline pain improved to 0–2/10, with intermittent flares typically rated 3–4/10 and worst pain up to 7/10 when stimulation programming was suboptimal (Fig. 1). Clinical Course and Evaluation

The patient underwent extensive multidisciplinary evaluation, including assessments by the cardiology, pulmonology, rheumatology, infectious disease, psychiatry, and physical medicine and rehabilitation departments, without diagnostic findings. He described pain reproducible with respiration, environmental changes (air pressure, pollutants, cold), and even light tactile stimulation. Examination revealed marked hyperemia over the anterior chest, prominent cutaneous ­hypersensitivity/allodynia, pain out of proportion to the stimulus, no musculoskeletal abnormalities, and no structural abnormalities on MRI to account for his symptoms (Fig. 2).

Multiple treatments, including narcotics, topical ketamine, gabapentin, antidepressants, steroid injection, physical therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and virtual reality therapy, provided only mild transient improvement. Although targeted regional nerve blocks can be considered in focal chest wall pain syndromes, they were not performed in this case prior to neuromodulation. These findings supported CRPS by exclusion. CRPS was diagnosed using the Budapest clinical criteria. The patient demonstrated 1) continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event, 2) at least one symptom in ≥ 3 of 4 categories (sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/edema, motor/trophic), and 3) at least one objective sign observed on examination in ≥ 2 categories.4 No alternative diagnosis better explained the presentation after a multidisciplinary evaluation and targeted testing. Collectively, the clinical phenotype and longitudinal course were consistent with CRPS type I involving an atypical distribution across the anterior chest wall.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the unusual chest wall distribution, a structured differential was performed:

Cardiopulmonary disease: repeatedly ruled out based on electrocardiographic, echocardiographic, stress test, pulmonary function test, and CT findings.

CRPS criteria most consistently matched the full symptom constellation. Because SCS modulates spinal dorsal column–dorsal horn network signaling involved in neuropathic pain processing, it can be conceptualized as a mechanism-based therapy for chronic neuropathic pain rather than a diagnosis-specific therapy.5,6 In practice, outcomes vary by pain phenotype and patient selection, and evidence-based consensus guidance emphasizes careful evaluation and appropriate trialing to optimize response and reduce avoidable therapy failure.7 Consistent with this, the strongest evidence base and guideline support for SCS exists for select refractory neuropathic pain conditions, including CRPS and painful diabetic neuropathy, after appropriate diagnostic workup and failure of conservative management.8,9

Trial of SCS and Permanent Implantation

A trial SCS was performed under mild sedation. Bilateral thoracic epidural leads were placed spanning T1–3 (Fig. 3), corresponding to the patient’s anterior chest wall pain distribution. During lead advancement, passage through the T4–8 region reproduced the patient’s characteristic pain, while positioning above T4 resulted in symptom relief. FIG. 3.Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs showing trial bilateral spinal cord stimulator lead placement spanning T1–3.

Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs showing trial bilateral spinal cord stimulator lead placement spanning T1–3.

During the trial, the patient reported 95% relief of his chest wall pain. He experienced no adverse effects. Given the significant improvement, he elected to proceed with permanent implantation.

Permanent bilateral leads were placed at the same T1–3 levels, with fluoroscopic confirmation (Fig. 4). Final programming was optimized to provide bilateral symptom coverage at the T2–3 level. Stimulation at the upper T2 region also produced coverage of a band-like upper abdominal pain distribution. Because a single subperception program did not adequately capture both the anterior chest wall and upper abdominal regions, the patient was discharged on a dual-field subperception program with broad-field shaping (200 Hz, 200-μsec pulse width) to cover both territories. The procedure was uncomplicated, and he was discharged with standard activity restrictions. Follow-Up and Outcomes

At 3 months, he reported near-complete resolution of his baseline anterior chest wall pain, with only occasional mild discomfort and no new neurological deficits. He noted a transient metallic taste at times but found it nonbothersome and continued to increase his physical activity without restriction.

By 12 months, the patient had returned fully to his previous activities, including outdoor winter recreation and broadsword practice, without limitations. He continued to experience near 100% relief of his baseline pain. He described intermittent, low-grade discomfort at the periphery of the stimulated sensory field, particularly during abrupt weather changes, and noted that he could sometimes sense when the device was “working hard” to modulate symptoms. He also reported a brief, occasional coppery taste but stated that it was not disruptive. Overall, he described a robust and sustained therapeutic effect with minimal side effects.

At 24 months, the patient continued to report excellent and durable relief, describing the device as working “wonderfully.” He experienced rare breakthrough episodes of low-intensity discomfort in situations that previously provoked severe pain, along with occasional brief “flickers” of pain at the edges of coverage. The metallic taste persisted intermittently but had become less noticeable over time. The implantable pulse generator (IPG) site caused mild discomfort during higher-intensity physical activity, but he felt this was minor compared to the overall benefit; he also expressed gratitude that the IPG was placed in the low back rather than the gluteal region, which he believed would have interfered with his physical activities. He stated that he was able to live what he considered a “rather normal” life and expressed significant gratitude for the sustained pain control provided by neuromodulation.

At a recent follow-up, the patient reported sustained overall improvement with intermittent symptom flares during extreme cold exposure, consistent with CRPS triggers.

Discussion

Observations

This case highlights that CRPS can present in an atypical distribution, including the anterior chest wall, and may mimic cardiopulmonary or gastrointestinal pathology. In patients with recurrent, severe chest wall pain accompanied by marked allodynia and vasomotor features (e.g., episodic erythema and temperature sensitivity), CRPS should be considered after a targeted evaluation excludes life-threatening or structural etiologies. Applying the Budapest clinical criteria helps ensure diagnostic rigor and prevents premature labeling when another diagnosis better explains the symptoms.

SCS has been successfully utilized for various non-CRPS chest wall pain conditions, providing useful context for understanding its applications in anterior chest wall CRPS. For refractory angina pectoris, SCS with lead placement at C5–T2 segments has demonstrated strong evidence for symptomatic benefits, improved quality of life, and enhanced exercise capacity, with stimulation typically delivered in three 1-hour sessions daily plus on-demand use to target myocardial afferent sympathetic fibers.10,11 In postthoracotomy pain syndrome, both conventional tonic stimulation and newer paresthesia-free modalities have proven effective, with optimal lead placement typically at T1–6 levels and best relief reported when stimulation contacts are positioned over T1 or T2 vertebrae.12,13 A multicenter case series of 10-kHz high-frequency SCS for chronic thoracic pain demonstrated an 89.5% response rate with sustained pain reduction at the 12-month follow-up, along with functional improvement and reduced medication use.12 For cancer-associated chest wall pain, including treatment-related ­neuropathic pain from thoracic procedures, SCS has shown efficacy in small case series, with one study of 14 lung cancer patients reporting > 50% pain reduction at 12 months postimplant following postsurgical pain.14 The present case of anterior chest wall CRPS shares neuropathic characteristics with postthoracotomy pain syndrome while occupying anatomical distribution that overlaps with cardiac pain referral patterns, suggesting that similar upper thoracic lead placement (T1–3) and either conventional or high-frequency stimulation paradigms may be appropriate for this rare presentation.

Lessons

For refractory CRPS, SCS can provide durable analgesia and meaningful functional restoration when conservative and interventional strategies fail. This case underscores the importance of 1) explicitly documenting Budapest criteria domains, including negative findings; 2) summarizing the exclusionary workup in a reproducible timeline; and 3) reporting neuromodulation details and longitudinal outcomes to guide clinicians managing atypical CRPS phenotypes. Key takeaways include considering CRPS in unexplained chest wall pain with prominent allodynia and vasomotor features, using a structured exclusionary workup, and reporting neuromodulation parameters and longitudinal outcomes to guide management of atypical phenotypes.
