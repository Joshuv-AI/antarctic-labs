The Quintic Wave Equation with Kelvin-Voigt Damping: Strichartz Estimates, Well-posedness and Global Stabilization Revisited
Marcelo M. Cavalcanti and Valéria N. Domingos Cavalcanti

Abstract
. This paper investigates the critical quintic wave equation in a 3D bounded domain subject to locally distributed Kelvin-Voigt damping. The study tackles two major mathematical challenges: the severe loss of derivatives induced by the localized thermo-viscous dissipation and the aggressive nature of the critical nonlinear term. First, we establish a robust well-posedness theory for arbitrarily large initial data. By shifting the analysis to the frequency space via a Littlewood-Paley decomposition , we employ Bernstein’s inequalities to lift the damping term into an L 2 L^{2} framework, allowing Strichartz estimates to be applied effectively. In the second part, we prove the uniform exponential stabilization of the energy. To overcome the reduction of the residual to the H − 2 H^{-2} level caused by the Kelvin-Voigt mechanism, we utilize the microlocal defect measure framework. The core of our stabilization proof relies on combining the critical Strichartz regularity L t 4 ​ L x 12 L^{4}_{t}L^{12}_{x} with a sharp Unique Continuation Property (UCP) to close the observability argument. Furthermore, we demonstrate that this microlocal mechanism is perfectly compatible with non-invasive damping geometries of arbitrarily small Lebesgue measure, successfully circumventing the geometric obstruction of trapped rays.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The phenomenon of energy concentration

The concentration phenomena associated with the "quintic wave" (generally described by the Nonlinear Quintic Schrödinger Equation - NLS or the Semilinear Quintonic Wave Equation) refer to situations where the energy or mass of the wave accumulates intensely in a very small region of space (a point or a small neighborhood), often leading to what is called a "blow-up" (singularity in finite time). From a physical point of view, this represents the collapse of a wave packet, while mathematically it corresponds to a concentration of mass (norm) or energy, where the solution profile loses compactness in Sobolev spaces. Mass/Energy Concentration: Mathematically, a sequence of solutions is said to concentrate if the mass (norm) or energy (norm) concentrates at a point when the concentrated mass is often a discrete quantity, equal to the norm of the "ground state". There is a threshold profile, namely, scattering threshold(the center-stable manifold), that separates solutions that scatter (go to zero) from those that concentrate and collapse (blow-up). Concentration is analyzed through scaling transformations (scaling invariant), where the solution is rescaled to maintain a constant energy norm while the wave packet size goes to zero.

The phenomenon of "collapse" or blow-up in quintic wave equations (and other critical nonlinear wave equations) can perfectly occur in bounded domains. In fact, the presence of edges (bounded domains) can facilitate blow-up compared to free space, as confinement prevents energy from dispersing to infinity, forcing it to concentrate. Here is the detailed analysis based on mathematical physics:

1. Collapse in Bounded Domains.

From a physical point of view, in a bounded domain, the initial energy "trapped" in the volume, combined with the non-linear focus (typical of the quintic wave), can overcome the linear mechanisms of dispersion. The wave "collapses" because the energy concentrates at a point (or region) in finite time, resulting in the singularity.

2. Mathematical View and Sobolev Spaces.

Mass/Energy Concentration: Mathematical collapse is the blow-up of the energy norm or norms in Sobolev spaces, such as:

•

As time approaches the collapse time, the solution ceases to be compact, meaning that the gradient of the solution becomes infinite, concentrating at one or more points.

•

Loss of Compactness: In bounded domains, immersion theorems (such as Rellich-Kondrachov) guarantee initial compactness, but the quintic nature of nonlinearity is "critical" in energy space. When the blow-up occurs, this compactness is violated, as the solution profile becomes singular (tends to a Dirac delta-type distribution in the limit).

3. Factors Affecting Collapse:

Boundary
: The shape of the domain and boundary conditions (e.g., Dirichlet at the edge) influence the rate of energy concentration, but collapse is an interior concentration phenomenon, i.e., it occurs where nonlinearity is strong.

Damping
: If the system includes damping terms (damped wave equations), it is possible for the system to converge to a global attractor, avoiding collapse, depending on the intensity of the damping.

Nonlinear Equations
: Collapse is characteristic of focusing equations (where nonlinearity attracts the wave), while in defocusing equations, the wave disperses.

In summary, the quintic wave in bounded domains frequently exhibits collapse (finite-time singularity) where the energy norm in Sobolev space explodes, a phenomenon that can be exacerbated by the inability of energy to escape the domain.

1.2.
The drawback of Galerkin’s method

Since we are in a bounded domain and with the spectral basis of the Laplacian, the weak existence and regularity of the approximate problem (via Galerkin) are practically "free" due to compactness and Aubin-Lions. The term
u^{5}\in L^{6/5}
is perfectly tamed by the Sobolev immersion
H_{0}^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{6}
.

The effective linear term in the interval
I
for the approximation
m
is:

A_{m}(I)=\|S(t-t_{0})(u_{m}(t_{0}),\partial_{t}u_{m}(t_{0}))\|_{L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}(I)}.

We have, for a fixed
m
, that, by the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral, we can shrink the interval
I
such that
A_{m}(I)\leq\epsilon_{0}
. Thus, we divide
[0,T]
into
N_{m}
subintervals.

The major problem is in the transition to the limit
m\rightarrow\infty
: For the final bound
\|u_{m}\|_{L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}([0,T])}\leq N\cdot C(E_{0})^{5}
not to explode when we take the supremum at
m
, the number of subintervals
N
must be independent of
m
. This means that we need a uniform
\delta>0
(independent of
m
) such that, if
|I|<\delta
, then
A_{m}(I)\leq\epsilon_{0}
for all
m
.

In the subcritical case (for example, cubic power in
3D
), we achieve this uniform
\delta
using subcritical Sobolev immersions interpolated with the energy
E_{0}
. Energy limits the Strichartz norm uniformly within small intervals (see, for instance,
[
8
]
).

In the critical case (quintic power in
3D
, norm
L^{5}L^{10}
), the Strichartz exponent "scales" exactly like the energy. The fact that the energy
E_{0}(u_{m})
is uniformly bounded does not prevent concentration. As the base
m
grows, the initial data
(u_{m}(0),\partial_{t}u_{m}(0))
can form increasingly finer "peaks" (energy concentration).

For a thin peak, the energy
H^{1}
remains the same (limited by
E_{0}
). But the norm
L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}
of the linear evolution concentrates in an increasingly smaller time interval around
t_{0}
. Therefore, to keep
A_{m}(I)\leq\epsilon_{0}
, the size of the interval
\delta_{m}
has to shrink to zero when
m\to\infty
. Consequently, the number of partitions
N_{m}\to\infty
, and the bound
N_{m}C(E_{0})^{5}
explode. It fails to extract a weak bound in
L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}
because the Galerkin bound is not uniform. This is precisely why the traditional Galerkin method fails to give uniqueness for the critical case, even in bounded domains.

To salvage this situation, we have two direct solutions:

Output 1: Small Data. If we assume that the initial energy
E_{0}
is small enough, the global Strichartz estimate gives us:

A_{m}([0,T])\leq CE_{0}\leq\epsilon_{0}

uniformly for all
m
. We don’t need to divide the interval.
N=1
. And the usual Bootstrap lemma closes the proof and we obtain the uniform bound
L^{5}L^{10}
in
m
. Then it’s just a matter of passing the limit and it’s over.

Output 2: If Large Data is needed (we have to change the Galerkin method to the Strichartz norm).
If we want uniqueness for large initial data, we prove the existence of the weak solution by Galerkin (as we already know it works). But uniqueness in
L^{5}L^{10}
does not come from estimating
u_{m}
. Instead, the existence of solutions in this critical space (which guarantees uniqueness) is generally achieved by constructing the local solution via the Picard Fixed-Point Theorem directly in the Strichartz space
C([0,\delta];H_{0}^{1})\cap L^{5}([0,\delta];L^{10})
. The fixed point gives us a local existence time
\delta
that depends on the profile of the initial data, and not just on
E_{0}
. (To extend this
\delta
to a global
T
for large data, then the heavy non-concentration arguments would come into play).

In summary: Weak existence proof using Galerkin and compactness works perfectly. But if the intention is to prove that the Galerkin limit is
L^{5}L^{10}
to guarantee uniqueness with large data, partitioning fails because
N
goes to infinity with
m
. If it’s for small data, partitioning is unnecessary and Bootstrap shines.

, although we did cite those references. Since our primary focus was on exponential decay, we did not mention this fact. Therefore, we would like to apologize to the readers for this oversight.

The article by Burq, Lebeau, and Planchon (in short BLP)
[
4
]
or Blair, Smith, and Sogge (BSS)
[
3
]
does not guarantee the uniform boundedness of Strichartz estimates for the Galerkin method approximations. Let’s explain exactly where the calculation stalls and why the literature avoids Galerkin at this specific step, going straight to the ’wound’ of harmonic analysis: The Spectral Projector Problem in
L^{p}
. In the classical Galerkin method, we project the equation onto a finite-dimensional space using the orthogonal projector
P_{m}
, which is an "abrupt" spectral cut on the first
m
eigenvectors of the Dirichlet Laplacian. To obtain a Strichartz estimate for the approximate solution
u_{m}
, you apply Duhamel’s formula to the projected equation:

\partial_{tt}u_{m}-\Delta u_{m}+P_{m}(u_{m}^{5})=0.

The problem lies in the non-linear term
P_{m}(u_{m}^{5})
. To close the estimate, we use the Strichartz bounds of the linear operator, which the BLP actually proves to hold in bounded domains. For this, we need the projector
P_{m}
to be uniformly bounded in the spatial
L^{p}
spaces required by the Strichartz duals (for example, something other than
p=2
).

This brings us to a classic and fundamental theorem of harmonic analysis (which goes back to the Fefferman ball multiplier problem
[
15
]
), and was exhaustively studied by Christopher Sogge and collaborators in domains/manifolds): The abrupt spectral projector
P_{m}
is not uniformly bounded on
L^{p}
for
p\neq 2
. The operator norm
\|P_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{p},L^{p})}
explodes when
m\to\infty
. Therefore, we cannot quote the projected nonlinearity independently of
m
. The constant of the Strichartz estimate from Galerkin’s method for
u_{m}
will depend on
m
and explode in the limit. The famous BLP paper (2008, in JAMS) proves the Strichartz estimates for the linear equation in domains with a boundary (overcoming wave reflection). But to deal with frequency cutoffs, they don’t use abrupt cutoffs like in Galerkin. They use pseudo-differential operators and smooth multipliers (Littlewood-Paley type truncations). The smooth cutoff function "damps" the oscillation and ensures the bound on
L^{p}
. Furthermore, to construct the solution to the critical nonlinear problem, BLP (as well as Shatah-Struwe in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) does not use the Galerkin approximation. They construct the local solution directly in the Strichartz space
L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}
using Picard’s Fixed Point Theorem, and then use energy non-concentration techniques (Morawetz type) to extend the solution globally.

Literature Overview

The stabilization of wave equations with localized dissipation has been
extensively studied within the geometric control framework initiated by
Bardos, Lebeau, and Rauch
[
2
]
, where sharp conditions linking
observability, control, and decay were established. Subsequent developments
clarified the microlocal propagation mechanisms governing energy concentration,
particularly near the boundary, through the analysis of generalized
bicharacteristics
[
5
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
21
]
.
Particularly those involving inhomogeneous media, variable
coefficients, or complex boundary configurations, the classical localized GCC fails due to
the existence of trapped rays. These are generalized bicharacteristics that oscillate indefinitely
without ever entering a standard localized control region, allowing high-frequency energy to
concentrate and consequently preventing uniform exponential decay.

In the presence of Kelvin–Voigt damping, however, the analytical structure
changes significantly. The damping operator introduces higher-order spatial
effects which interact nontrivially with geometric multipliers, frequently
requiring additional regularity assumptions. This phenomenon has been examined
in various stabilization settings, including localized and degenerate
dissipation mechanisms (see
[
1
]
),
[
24
]
.

From the dispersive viewpoint, Strichartz estimates play a decisive role in
handling critical nonlinearities. In domains with boundary, these estimates
require refined harmonic analysis tools, as developed by Blair, Smith, and
Sogge
[
3
]
, extending earlier Euclidean results of
Keel and Tao
[
19
]
. The interaction between boundary geometry and
dispersive smoothing was further explored by Burq, Lebeau, and Planchon

[
4
]
, providing a fundamental framework for energy-critical
wave equations.

Microlocal defect measures and H-measures provide the natural language for
describing high-frequency energy concentration in such problems. These tools,
introduced by Gérard
[
16
]
and Tartar
[
31
]
, have become
central in stabilization theory, particularly in configurations where compactness
arguments fail or where dissipation degenerates.

The present work combines these perspectives. By integrating harmonic analysis,
nonlinear energy methods, and microlocal propagation techniques, we obtain a
stability theory capable of handling Kelvin–Voigt mechanisms at the critical
level while preserving the geometric intuition underlying classical control
results.

1.4.
Contributions of the present article

The study of the critical wave equation with localized Kelvin-Voigt damping presents a notorious challenge: balancing the aggressive nature of the quintic nonlinearity in
3
spatial dimensions with the severe loss of derivatives induced by the viscous term
\text{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})
. In the first part of this paper, we explore the classical route. By relying on energy methods and seeking strong solutions, one can seemingly bypass the heavy machinery of dispersive estimates. However, this classical approach exacts a heavy toll: to control the critical nonlinearity and guarantee uniqueness, the initial data must be assumed to be strictly small, and the solutions are forced into the restrictive Shatah-Struwe class, requiring
u\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
.

To overcome this bottleneck and remove the small data restriction, the second part of our work shifts the paradigm from the physical domain to the frequency space, deploying the modern arsenal of Harmonic Analysis. The application of dispersive techniques to boundary value problems possesses a rich and profound history. The seminal works of Smith and Sogge
[
28
]
demonstrated how wave dispersion interacts with domains and rough boundaries. Subsequently, the celebrated contributions of the trio Burq, Lebeau, and Planchon
[
4
]
(BLP) solidified the use of spectral projectors and
Littlewood-Paley
theory tailored to Dirichlet boundary conditions, allowing Strichartz estimates to flourish outside of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, treating the singular damping terms via the Duhamel formulation crucially relies on the foundational machinery of Christ and Kiselev
[
10
]
, which elegantly translates homogeneous dispersive bounds into retarded estimates, allowing low-frequency perturbations to be seamlessly absorbed.

The core difficulty of the Kelvin-Voigt term lies in its
H^{-1}
regularity, which makes it inherently hostile to the classical Strichartz estimates on the right-hand side of the wave equation. Classical Strichartz estimates are calibrated for data and source terms with compatible scaling and regularity. The Kelvin-Voigt operator introduces a derivative loss which is incompatible with a direct application of the dispersive framework. The obstruction is therefore not merely dispersive, but functional-analytic in nature.

By introducing a Littlewood-Paley decomposition, we separate the wave into macroscopic (low-frequency) and microscopic (high-frequency) components. This dual-frequency mechanism is the central stabilizing principle of our nonlinear analysis:

•

The Low-Frequency Regime:
At low frequencies, the spectral projection
P_{\leq N}
combined with Bernstein’s inequalities converts spectral localization into effective derivative gains. In this regime, the projected damping term behaves as a bounded perturbation in
L^{2}
, thereby completely neutralizing the derivative loss and allowing the Strichartz estimates to perform flawlessly without destroying the regularity.

•

The High-Frequency Regime:
Conversely, at high frequencies, Bernstein’s inequality cannot be applied without yielding unfavorable positive powers of the frequency threshold
N
. To circumvent this severe derivative loss, we fundamentally shift our operator strategy by keeping the localized damping within the principal part of the wave operator. Applying the high-frequency projector
P_{>N}
generates a non-trivial commutator
[P_{>N},a(x)]
with the variable damping coefficient. By leveraging the theory of smooth spectral multipliers and Schur’s test, we rigorously demonstrate that this commutator acts as a smoothing operator of order
-1
. This remarkable cancellation completely absorbs the spatial derivative of the Kelvin-Voigt term without any asymptotic loss. With the damping safely incorporated on the left-hand side, the remaining high-frequency evolution is ultimately controlled by the asymptotic smallness of the initial energy tail (as
N\to\infty
) and a short-time perturbation argument, allowing us to seamlessly close the critical Strichartz estimates.

This spectral strategy yields our second and main result: a robust and complete local well-posedness theory. Through a delicate
bootstrap
argument—combining the smallness of the high-frequency tail with the smooth behavior of the low-frequency component over a short time—we completely bypass the need for small initial energy. Consequently, we establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions for arbitrarily large initial data, providing a definitive structural answer to the conflict between the critical nonlinear power and the Kelvin-Voigt dissipation.

Having established the global well-posedness framework, the third and final objective of this paper is to prove the uniform exponential stabilization of the system. The presence of the Kelvin-Voigt damping
\text{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})
restricted to a localized region
\omega
introduces severe obstacles for the asymptotic analysis. Unlike frictional damping, the viscous nature of our dissipation alters the regularity of the residual, reducing the convergence from the
H^{-1}
to the
H^{-2}
level. Consequently, classical compactness-uniqueness arguments fail to provide the necessary strong convergence to propagate the damping effects.

To bypass this obstacle, we employ the powerful framework of microlocal defect measures. While the loss of derivatives prevents direct propagation everywhere, the microlocal structure is perfectly preserved outside the damping region. The fundamental breakthrough in our stabilization proof is the application of the sharp Unique Continuation Property (UCP) for the wave equation with critical potentials, as established by Duyckaerts, Zhang, and Zuazua
[
14
]
. By exploiting the precise critical Strichartz regularity
u\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
inherited from our well-posedness theory, we can close the defect-measure contradiction argument directly. This completely eliminates the dependence on the truncation parameters without relying on artificial secondary contradictions, bridging the Harmonic Analysis results from the first part of the paper with the Control Theory goals of the second.

Finally, we demonstrate that our microlocal approach is entirely oblivious to volumetric constraints, relying solely on the dynamical interception of the generalized bicharacteristic flow. This allows our stabilization results to be seamlessly coupled with the highly complex, “non-invasive” damping geometries developed in
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In such configurations, the dissipative region can be constructed with an arbitrarily small Lebesgue measure, thus providing a definitive and elegant mathematical answer to the problem of trapped rays in inhomogeneous media.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 constructs global weak solutions via
C^{1}
truncation. Section 3 develops the local theory of strong solutions through Galerkin methods and higher-order energy estimates. Section 4 establishes uniqueness and weak-strong uniqueness principles. Section 5 introduces the Littlewood-Paley strategy and the frequency-space stabilization of the nonlinear estimates. Section 6 concludes with the microlocal analysis and the proof of uniform exponential decay.

), satisfying:

(10)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|u_{k}^{\prime}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla u_{k}^{\prime}|^{2}dxdt\leq C_{0}.

By the Aubin-Lions lemma, there exists a subsequence
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
weakly-
\ast
in
L^{\infty}H_{0}^{1}
and strongly a.e. in
\Omega\times(0,T)
.

The Nonlinearity:
By the 3D Sobolev embedding
H_{0}^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{6}
, the uniform bound (
10
) implies that
\{u_{k}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
.
Exploiting Lemma
2.1
(iii), we have:

(11)

\int_{\Omega}|f_{k}(u_{k}(t,x))|^{6/5}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}|u_{k}(t,x)|^{6}dx\leq C\|u_{k}(t)\|_{H_{0}^{1}}^{6}\leq C_{1},

where
C_{1}
is independent of
k
and
t
. Thus,
\{f_{k}(u_{k})\}
is uniformly bounded in the reflexive Banach space
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))
.

By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, we can extract a subsequence such that
f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup\chi
weakly-
\ast
in
L^{\infty}L^{6/5}
. Since
u_{k}\to u
a.e. and
f_{k}\to s^{5}
uniformly on compact sets (Lemma
2.1
(iv)), it is easy to see that
f_{k}(u_{k}(t,x))\to u(t,x)^{5}
a.e. in
\Omega\times(0,T)
.

By the standard Lions’ Lemma (Vitali’s convergence theorem for weakly convergent sequences), the weak limit coincides with the pointwise limit:

(12)

f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{5}\text{ weakly-$\ast$ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega)).

Passing to the limit
k\to\infty
in the weak formulation of (
3
) is now immediate, since test functions
\phi\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
exactly dualize the convergence of the nonlinearity. Thus,
u
is a global weak solution of the critical problem (
1
).

Theorem 2.2

(Existence of Global Weak Solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and assume that
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is a non-negative function. For any given initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and any
T>0
, there exists at least one global weak solution
u
to the critical Kelvin-Voigt damped wave equation (
1
).

Furthermore, the solution belongs to the class:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{a(x)}\nabla u_{t}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

and satisfies the following energy inequality for almost every
t\in(0,T)
:

(13)

E_{u}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla u_{t}(s,x)|^{2}\,dxds\leq E_{u}(0),

where the energy is defined as
E_{u}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\|u(t)\|_{6}^{6}
.

3.
Local Well-posedness of Strong Solutions via Galerkin Method and Small Data

In this section, we establish the local existence of strong solutions for the quintic wave equation with localized Kelvin-Voigt damping. Instead of relying on Strichartz estimates—which present severe microlocal obstructions when commuting with a continuous cut-off function over the localized parabolic damping—we explore the intrinsic degenerate parabolic structure of the system for regular initial data.Let us consider the standard Galerkin approximation for our problem. Let
(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be the spectral basis of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega
, and let
V_{m}=\text{span}\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{m}\}
. We look for approximate solutions of the form
u_{m}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{jm}(t)w_{j}(x)
solving the projected system:

(u_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t),w)_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla w)_{\Omega}+(a(x)\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}(t),\nabla w)_{\Omega}+(u_{m}^{5}(t),w)_{\Omega}=0,\quad\forall w\in V_{m},

with initial data
u_{m}(0)=P_{m}u_{0}\to u_{0}
in
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}
and
u_{m}^{\prime}(0)=P_{m}u_{1}\to u_{1}
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Before proceeding to the strong energy estimates, we recall that the standard weak dissipative energy:

E_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{m}^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\|u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{6}}^{6}

is non-increasing, satisfying
E_{m}(t)\leq E_{m}(0)\leq E_{0}
for all
t\geq 0
. In particular, this provides the uniform bound
\|\nabla u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2E_{0}}
, which will be crucial to control the nonlinear term later.

Defining the Strong Energy
.

Since
u_{m}\in V_{m}
and the basis is given by the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, we are allowed to take
w=-\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}\in V_{m}
as a test function in our approximate equation. This choice allows us to capture the regularizing effect of the Kelvin-Voigt damping. We define the strong energy functional as:

Y_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Substituting
w=-\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}
into the projected equation and integrating by parts over
\Omega
, we obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta u_{m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-\int_{\Omega}\text{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{m}^{\prime})(-\Delta u_{m}^{\prime})dx+\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{5}(-\Delta u_{m}^{\prime})dx=0.

Estimating the Linear Terms and the Damping Commutator
.

The term corresponding to the Kelvin-Voigt damping requires careful handling because the damping coefficient
a(x)
is localized and non-constant. Expanding the divergence, we have:

-\int_{\Omega}\text{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{m}^{\prime})(-\Delta u_{m}^{\prime})dx=\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla a\cdot\nabla u_{m}^{\prime})\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}dx.

The first term on the right-hand side provides the strong parabolic dissipation. The second term is a commutator of lower order. To absorb this commutator without losing derivatives, we strongly rely on the structural assumption on the damping function:

(14)

\displaystyle|\nabla a(x)|^{2}\leq C_{a}a(x).

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Young’s inequality taking (
14
) into account, we estimate:

\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla a\cdot\nabla u_{m}^{\prime})\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}dx\right|\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla a|}{\sqrt{a}}|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}|\left(\sqrt{a}|\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}|\right)dx

\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}|^{2}dx+\frac{C_{a}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}|^{2}dx.

Absorbing the dissipative term, the linear part of the equation yields the differential inequality:

Y_{m}^{\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}|^{2}dx\leq C_{a}Y_{m}(t)+\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{5}\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}dx.

Controlling the Quintic Nonlinearity via Gagliardo-Nirenberg

We now face the most delicate part of the proof: estimating the nonlinear source term uniformly. Integrating by parts once, we have:

\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{5}\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}dx=-5\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{4}\nabla u_{m}\cdot\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}dx.

To bound this term, we apply the generalized Hölder’s inequality with exponents
p_{1}=3
,
p_{2}=6
, and
p_{3}=2
:

\left|5\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{4}\nabla u_{m}\cdot\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}dx\right|\leq 5\|u_{m}^{4}\|_{L^{3}}\|\nabla u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}\|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}=5\|u_{m}\|_{L^{12}}^{4}\|\nabla u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}\|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}.

Let us analyze each term on the right-hand side in terms of the strong energy
Y_{m}(t)
: Clearly,
\|\nabla u_{m}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}Y_{m}(t)^{1/2}
.By the continuous Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,6}(\Omega)
in dimension 3, we have

\|\nabla u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}\leq C\|\Delta u_{m}\|_{L^{2}}\leq CY_{m}(t)^{1/2}

.

The core difficulty lies in controlling
\|u_{m}\|_{L^{12}}^{4}
efficiently to ensure a low polynomial growth for the resulting ODE. Instead of using the brute-force embedding
H^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
(which would yield a very high power and severely restrict the time of existence), we use the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality in
3D
. We interpolate
L^{12}
between
L^{6}
(which is uniformly bounded by the weak energy
E_{0}
) and
H^{2}
:

\|u_{m}\|_{L^{12}}\leq C\|\Delta u_{m}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/4}\|u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}^{3/4}.

Raising this inequality to the 4th power, and recalling that
\|u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}\leq C\|\nabla u_{m}\|_{L^{2}}\leq CE_{0}^{1/2}
, we obtain:

\|u_{m}\|_{L^{12}}^{4}\leq C\|\Delta u_{m}\|_{L^{2}}\|u_{m}\|_{L^{6}}^{3}\leq CY_{m}(t)^{1/2}E_{0}^{3/2}.

Combining all these estimates, the nonlinear term is bounded by:

\left|\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{5}\Delta u_{m}^{\prime}dx\right|\leq C\left(Y_{m}(t)^{1/2}E_{0}^{3/2}\right)\left(Y_{m}(t)^{1/2}\right)\left(Y_{m}(t)^{1/2}\right)=C_{1}E_{0}^{3/2}Y_{m}(t)^{3/2},

where
C_{1}>0
is a constant depending only on
\Omega
and the Sobolev embeddings.

The Bernoulli Differential Inequality and Local Existence
.

Putting the linear and nonlinear estimates together, we arrive at the following differential inequality for the strong energy:

Y_{m}^{\prime}(t)\leq C_{a}Y_{m}(t)+C_{1}E_{0}^{3/2}Y_{m}(t)^{3/2}.

This is a Bernoulli-type differential inequality. To solve it explicitly, we set
z(t)=Y_{m}(t)^{-1/2}
, which transforms the inequality into the linear form
z^{\prime}(t)\geq-\frac{C_{a}}{2}z(t)-\frac{C_{1}E_{0}^{3/2}}{2}
. Multiplying by the integrating factor
e^{C_{a}t/2}
and integrating from
0
to
t
, we can trace back to
Y_{m}(t)
to obtain:

Y_{m}(t)\leq\frac{Y_{m}(0)e^{C_{a}t}}{\left[1-\frac{C_{1}E_{0}^{3/2}}{C_{a}}\sqrt{Y_{m}(0)}\left(e^{C_{a}t/2}-1\right)\right]^{2}}.

Since
Y_{m}(0)
is bounded by the
H^{2}\times H^{1}
norm of the initial data, the denominator is strictly positive and bounded away from zero on some maximal interval
[0,T_{max})
, where
T_{max}>0
depends continuously on
Y_{m}(0)
,
E_{0}
, and the structural constant of the damping
C_{a}
. This uniform bound on
Y_{m}(t)
over
[0,T]
guarantees that the approximate solutions
u_{m}
remain bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}\cap H_{0}^{1})
and
u_{m}^{\prime}
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1})
. By standard compactness arguments (e.g., Aubin-Lions-Simon lemma), we can extract a subsequence that converges strongly, thus passing to the limit as
m\to\infty
and proving the local well-posedness of strong solutions.

Theorem 3.1

(Local Well-posedness of Strong Solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Assume the damping coefficient
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is non-negative and satisfies the structural condition
|\nabla a(x)|^{2}\leq C_{a}a(x)
for almost every
x\in\Omega
.

For any initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in\left(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, there exists a maximal time
T_{max}>0
, depending continuously on the initial weak energy
E_{0}
, the initial strong energy
Y(0)
, and the structural constant
C_{a}
, such that the critical wave equation admits a unique local strong solution
u
on
[0,T]
for any
T<T_{max}
.

Furthermore, the solution belongs to the strong regularity class:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{a(x)}\Delta u_{t}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

and its strong energy
Y_{u}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta u(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
satisfies the uniform bound:

(15)

Y_{u}(t)\leq\frac{Y_{u}(0)e^{C_{a}t}}{\left[1-\frac{C_{1}E_{0}^{3/2}}{C_{a}}\sqrt{Y_{u}(0)}\left(e^{C_{a}t/2}-1\right)\right]^{2}},\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{max}).

4.
Uniqueness and Weak-Strong Uniqueness Principles

In this section, we address the uniqueness of the solutions obtained previously. It is a well-known open problem whether weak solutions in the basic energy space
C([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
are unconditionally unique for the critical quintic wave equation in dimension 3. Because our Kelvin-Voigt damping
a(x)
is localized, the dynamics in the undamped region
\{x\in\Omega:a(x)=0\}
correspond exactly to the pure critical wave equation.

Therefore, without global Strichartz estimates, unconditional uniqueness is out of reach. Nevertheless, we can establish rigorous uniqueness within the class of strong solutions, as well as a Weak-Strong uniqueness principle relying on the auxiliary space
L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
.

4.1.
Uniqueness of Strong Solutions

We begin by showing that strong solutions, constructed via the Galerkin method for initial data in
H^{2}(\Omega)
, are uniquely determined.

Theorem 4.1

(Uniqueness of Strong Solutions)
.

Let
(u_{0},u_{1})\in(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Suppose
u
and
v
are two strong solutions to the problem (
1
) in the class
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
satisfying the same initial conditions. Then
u\equiv v
on
[0,T]
.

Proof.

Let
w=u-v
. Then
w
satisfies the initial value problem:

(16)

w_{tt}-\Delta w-\text{div}(a(x)\nabla w_{t})+u^{5}-v^{5}=0,\quad\text{with}\quad w(0)=0,\quad w_{t}(0)=0.

Testing the equation with
w_{t}
(which is a valid operation since
w_{t}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
), and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain the energy identity for the difference:

(17)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla w_{t}|^{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}(u^{5}-v^{5})w_{t}dx.

By the Mean Value Theorem, we can bound the nonlinear difference as follows:

|u^{5}-v^{5}|\leq 5\left(|u|^{4}+|v|^{4}\right)|w|.

Since
u
and
v
are strong solutions in dimension 3, the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
ensures that
u,v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
. Therefore, there exists a constant
M>0
, depending on the
L^{\infty}_{t}H^{2}_{x}
norms of
u
and
v
, such that:

|u(t,x)|^{4}+|v(t,x)|^{4}\leq M\quad\text{for a.e. }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega.

Substituting this back into the energy identity and dropping the non-negative dissipation term
\int a|\nabla w_{t}|^{2}dx
, we estimate the right-hand side using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 5M\int_{\Omega}|w||w_{t}|dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5M}{2}\left(\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

By the Poincaré inequality,
\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Thus, letting
E_{w}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we arrive at the linear differential inequality:

E_{w}^{\prime}(t)\leq CE_{w}(t),

where
C=5M\max(1,C_{P})
. Since
w(0)=w_{t}(0)=0
, we have
E_{w}(0)=0
. By Gronwall’s Lemma,
E_{w}(t)\equiv 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which concludes the proof that
u\equiv v
.
∎

4.2.
Uniqueness in the Strichartz Class

The true challenge arises when we relax the regularity of the solutions to the energy space. While unconditional uniqueness in the weak energy space
C([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
remains a notorious open problem for the critical quintic wave equation in 3D, we can establish rigorous uniqueness within the auxiliary Strichartz space
L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
. This is precisely the regularity class recovered by our well-posedness framework using the Littlewood-Paley frequency decomposition.

Theorem 4.2

(Uniqueness in the Strichartz Class)
.

Let
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
. Suppose
u
and
v
are two solutions to the critical problem (
1
) in the energy space
C([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
sharing the same initial data.
If, additionally, both solutions belong to the Strichartz class
u,v\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
, then
u\equiv v
on
[0,T]
.

Proof.

Let
w=u-v
. Since
u
and
v
share the same initial data,
w(0)=0
and
w_{t}(0)=0
. Because both
u
and
v
belong to
L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
, we can show that the nonlinear difference
u^{5}-v^{5}
is regular enough to be tested against
w_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Using the algebraic inequality
|u^{5}-v^{5}|\leq C(|u|^{4}+|v|^{4})|w|
, we apply the generalized Hölder’s inequality in space with exact exponents
p_{1}=3
and
p_{2}=6
:

\displaystyle\|u^{5}(t)-v^{5}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|(|u(t)|^{4}+|v(t)|^{4})|w(t)|\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|u(t)\|_{L^{12}(\Omega)}^{4}+\|v(t)\|_{L^{12}(\Omega)}^{4}\right)\|w(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}.

By the Sobolev embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
, we have
\|w(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\leq C_{S}\|\nabla w(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Since
u,v\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
, the function
h(t):=CC_{S}\left(\|u(t)\|_{L^{12}}^{4}+\|v(t)\|_{L^{12}}^{4}\right)
belongs to
L^{1}(0,T)
.
Thus,
u^{5}-v^{5}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, which rigorously justifies taking the
L^{2}
-inner product of the weak equation for
w
with
w_{t}
. This yields the energy identity:

(18)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla w_{t}|^{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}(u^{5}-v^{5})w_{t}dx.

Dropping the non-negative dissipation term and defining the energy of the difference as
E_{w}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we estimate the right-hand side using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle E_{w}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{5}-v^{5}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq h(t)\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq h(t)E_{w}(t).

Since
h\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
E_{w}(0)=0
, Grönwall’s Lemma implies
E_{w}(t)\equiv 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Consequently,
w\equiv 0
, meaning
u\equiv v
.
∎

5.
Littlewood-Paley Strategy

In the present section the Littlewood–Paley decomposition reveals an intrinsic stabilizing mechanism: the Kelvin–Voigt operator behaves as a spectral smoothing device. Low frequencies absorb derivative losses via Bernstein inequalities, while high frequencies remain dispersive.

5.1.
The Philosophy: Frequencies Instead of Space

The central challenge is the Kelvin-Voigt term:
\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})
. By having two spatial derivatives (
\nabla\cdot\nabla
), this term belongs to the dual space
H^{-1}
. The classical non-homogeneous Strichartz estimate for the wave equation does not accept terms in
H^{-1}
on the right-hand side.

The spatial flaw:
Attempting to isolate the damping with a spatial cutoff function
\psi(x)
generates geometric commutators and creates a “no man’s land” (
0<\psi<1
) where the quintic nonlinearity loses control of the Strichartz norm.

The spectral solution:
Instead of slicing the physical domain, we partition the
frequency
of the wave. To preserve the boundedness of the operators in
L^{p}
spaces and ensure the convolution kernels decay rapidly, we employ a
smooth
spectral projection. Let
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a smooth cutoff function such that
\chi(s)=1
for
|s|\leq 1
and
\chi(s)=0
for
|s|\geq 2
. We define the low-frequency projector
P_{\leq N}=\chi(\sqrt{-\Delta}/N)
and its high-frequency counterpart
P_{>N}=I-P_{\leq N}
. This smooth localization possesses the powerful property of satisfying
Bernstein’s Inequalities
. It effectively limits the cost of spatial derivatives, lifting the damping term from
H^{-1}
back to
L^{2}
, which is precisely the functional framework where the Strichartz Theorem operates perfectly.

5.2.
Step 1: The Regularized Problem and the Energy

To apply the
bootstrap
argument (a priori estimate) without the nonlinearity blowing up, we truncate the quintic function. Let
f_{k}(s)
be a globally Lipschitz function such that
f_{k}(s)=s^{5}
for
|s|\leq k
.

We consider the strong solution
u
(the index
k
is omitted) of the problem:

(19)

\displaystyle u_{tt}-\Delta u-\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})+f_{k}(u)=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(20)

\displaystyle u=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(21)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=u_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

Multiplying the PDE by
u_{t}
and integrating over space and time from
0
to
t
, we obtain the Global Energy Identity:

(22)

E(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla u_{t}(s,x)|^{2}\,dx\,ds=E(0)\leq C_{0},

where
E(0)
depends exclusively on the initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})
. This guarantees uniform bounds in
k
for
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
,
u_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, and for the thermo-viscous dissipation.

5.3.
Step 2: The Smooth Spectral Filter (
P_{\leq N}
)

We use the Spectral Theorem for the
-\Delta
operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions (see the Appendix of the present article). Let
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the eigenvalues and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions.

To ensure the resulting operators have rapidly decaying kernels and bounded commutators, we must employ a smooth spectral cutoff rather than a sharp truncation. For a frequency threshold
N>0
, we define the smooth Low-Frequency Projector via the functional calculus:

(23)

P_{\leq N}u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\chi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{N}\right)(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}\phi_{j}.

By construction, this operator commutes with both
\Delta
and
\partial_{t}
. Crucially, because
\chi
is smoothly supported on
[-2,2]
, the projector rigorously satisfies Bernstein’s Inequality:

(24)

\|\nabla(P_{\leq N}g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq CN\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where the constant
C
depends only on the choice of the profile
\chi
.

5.4.
Step 3: Low Frequencies and Strichartz

We define the macroscopic part of the wave as
w_{N}=P_{\leq N}u
. Applying the spectral projector
P_{\leq N}
to the truncated PDE (
19
), we obtain:

(25)

(w_{N})_{tt}-\Delta w_{N}=-P_{\leq N}(f_{k}(u))+P_{\leq N}\big(\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})\big).

To apply the Strichartz estimates to the low-frequency component
w_{N}
, we treat the entire right-hand side of (
25
) as a source term
F_{total}=F_{NL}+F_{KV}
, where
F_{NL}:=-P_{\leq N}(f_{k}(u))
and
F_{KV}:=P_{\leq N}\big(\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla u_{t})\big)
. Our goal is to estimate both terms in the dual Strichartz space
L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

To justify the interaction between the intrusive Kelvin-Voigt operator and the spectral projector, we exploit the self-adjointness of
P_{\leq N}
. For any test function
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we write:

(26)

\big\langle P_{\leq N}\operatorname{div}(a\nabla u_{t}),\phi\big\rangle=\big\langle\operatorname{div}(a\nabla u_{t}),P_{\leq N}\phi\big\rangle.

Integrating by parts in space yields:

(27)

\big\langle\operatorname{div}(a\nabla u_{t}),P_{\leq N}\phi\big\rangle=-\big\langle a\nabla u_{t},\nabla(P_{\leq N}\phi)\big\rangle.

At this point, we invoke the standard Bernstein inequality for spectral projectors associated with the Dirichlet Laplacian (see, for instance, Blair–Smith–Sogge
[
3
]
):

(28)

\|\nabla(P_{\leq N}\phi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq CN\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we deduce that the projector acts as a low-pass filter, recovering the spatial derivative at the cost of the frequency threshold
N
:

(29)

\|F_{KV}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq CN\|a\nabla u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{a}NE(t)^{1/2}.

Integrating over the time interval
[0,T]
and using the uniform bound from the first-order global energy
E(t)\leq E_{0}
, we obtain the targeted bound:

(30)

\|F_{KV}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C_{a}N\sqrt{T}E_{0}^{1/2}.

Remark 5.1
.

Due to the regularity of the truncated problem we constructed in Section 5, we are not applying the projector
P_{\leq N}
to the original weak solution. We are applying it to the strong solution
u_{k}
of the truncated problem! From Section 3,
u_{k}\in H^{2}\cap H_{0}^{1}
and
\partial_{t}u_{k}\in H_{0}^{1}
. Therefore,
\nabla u_{k,t}\in L^{2}
and, strictly speaking,
\operatorname{div}(a\nabla u_{k,t})
would be in
H^{-1}
. However, the low-frequency Littlewood-Paley projector (
P_{\leq N}
) has the wonderful property of being a regularizer. It cuts off the high frequencies, which means that even if you apply
P_{\leq N}
to a distribution in
H^{-1}
, the result falls across all
H^{s}
spaces, including
L^{2}
and
C^{\infty}
.

For the nonlinear term, we use the
L^{p}
-boundedness of the spectral projector. Since the truncated nonlinearity obeys
|f_{k}(s)|\leq|s|^{5}
uniformly in
k
, we have:

(31)

\|F_{NL}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f_{k}(u(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\||u(t)|^{5}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C\|u(t)\|_{L^{10}(\Omega)}^{5}.

Integrating in time from
0
to
T
yields exactly the critical Strichartz norm associated with the quintic wave equation:

(32)

\|F_{NL}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}^{5}.

Combining (
30
) and (
32
), the total source term is bounded in
L^{1}L^{2}
. Applying the standard Strichartz estimate to equation (
25
), we conclude that for the critical space
X_{T}:=L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))
, the low-frequency component satisfies:

(33)

\|w_{N}\|_{X_{T}}\leq C_{0}E_{0}^{1/2}+C_{1}\|u\|_{X_{T}}^{5}+C_{S}C_{a}N\sqrt{T}E_{0}^{1/2},

where
C_{0}
depends on the free wave propagation of the initial data,
C_{1}
is a universal constant, and
C_{S}
is the Strichartz constant.

5.5.
Step 4: High Frequencies and the Commutator Trick

Let
v_{N}=P_{>N}u=u-w_{N}
denote the microscopic fluctuation of the wave. To successfully tame the critical quintic term in our subsequent bootstrap argument, it is mandatory to ensure that the Strichartz norm
\|v_{N}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}
is strictly small. In dimension
n=3
, the standard Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{10}(\Omega)
fails, preventing a direct uniform bound solely from the energy space. To overcome this, we exploit the dispersive decay of the initial data.

Unlike the macroscopic regime (low frequencies), we cannot treat the Kelvin-Voigt operator as a bounded perturbation on the right-hand side of the free wave equation for
v_{N}
. Applying Bernstein’s inequality here would yield a positive power of the frequency threshold
N
, which diverges as
N\to\infty
. Conversely, keeping the term without Bernstein would require bounding
\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}
in
L^{2}
, which heavily depends on the truncation parameter
k
and would destroy the uniformity of the local existence time.

To completely circumvent this loss of derivatives, we keep the localized damping operator on the left-hand side. We define the damped wave operator
L
:

(34)

L:=\partial_{tt}-\Delta-\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla\partial_{t}).

When we apply the high-frequency spectral projector
P_{>N}
to the original PDE (
Lu=-f_{k}(u)
), the projector commutes perfectly with the constant-coefficient derivatives, but it does
not
commute perfectly with the variable multiplication by
a(x)
. We obtain the precise equation for
v_{N}
:

(35)

L(v_{N})=-P_{>N}(f_{k}(u))+\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t}),

where
\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})
is the commutator between the spectral projector and the damping coefficient:

(36)

\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})=\operatorname{div}\Big([P_{>N},a(x)]\nabla u_{t}\Big).

By the standard theory of pseudo-differential operators (see Appendix
B
for a detailed proof), the commutator of a zero-order multiplier (
P_{>N}
) with a smooth function
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is a smoothing operator of order
-1
. Consequently,
[P_{>N},a(x)]
maps
L^{2}(\Omega)
into
H^{1}(\Omega)
. The outer divergence operator then consumes exactly one spatial derivative, bringing the regularity gracefully back to
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Therefore, the commutator acts as a legitimate
L^{2}
-source bounded solely by the first-order energy, strictly independent of
k
:

(37)

\|\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{a}E_{0}^{1/2}.

With a perfectly bounded right-hand side, we decompose the high-frequency component into its linear damped evolution and a perturbation:
v_{N}=v_{N}^{lin}+v_{N}^{per}
.

The linear part
v_{N}^{lin}
is the solution to the homogeneous damped wave equation governed strictly by the high-frequency projection of the initial data:

(38)

\begin{cases}L(v_{N}^{lin})=0\quad\text{(Homogeneous damped wave)}\\
v_{N}^{lin}(0)=P_{>N}u_{0}\\
\partial_{t}v_{N}^{lin}(0)=P_{>N}u_{1}.\end{cases}

Since the localized dissipation only enhances the natural dispersive properties of the wave,
v_{N}^{lin}
obeys the standard Strichartz estimates. Applying the estimate, we obtain:

(39)

\|v_{N}^{lin}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}\leq C_{S}\left(\|\nabla(P_{>N}u_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|P_{>N}u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)=:\epsilon(N).

Since the initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})
belongs to the finite energy space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and is completely independent of the truncation
k
, the sequence of partial sums of its spectral decomposition converges. Consequently, the high-frequency tail of the energy must vanish:

(40)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\epsilon(N)=0.

For the perturbed part
v_{N}^{per}
, which solves
L(v_{N}^{per})=-P_{>N}(f_{k}(u))+\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})
with zero initial data, we apply the inhomogeneous Strichartz estimate for the damped operator
L
:

(41)

\|v_{N}^{per}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|P_{>N}(f_{k}(u))\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Because the commutator source is uniformly bounded in space, its time integration yields:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{N}(u_{t})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot T\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}\cdot T\cdot E_{0}^{1/2}.

Its contribution can therefore be made arbitrarily small by shrinking the time horizon
T
.

On the other hand, the nonlinear contribution is controlled inside the bootstrap regime:

(43)

\|P_{>N}(f_{k}(u))\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|f_{k}(u)\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}^{5}.

Thus, the requisite smallness needed to close the high-frequency argument is obtained by combining: (i) the
T
-smallness of the commutator term, and (ii) the bootstrap control of the critical norm of
u
on
(0,T)
. This yields:

(44)

\|v_{N}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}\leq\epsilon(N)+C_{a}\cdot T\cdot E_{0}^{1/2}+C_{1}\|u\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}^{5}.

This mechanism provides the essential smallness required to absorb the critical quintic term, completely circumventing the need to assume that the total initial energy
E_{0}
is small.

5.6.
Step 5: The Final Bootstrap Argument

To close the estimates and ensure that the nonlinearity does not blow up, we analyze the critical Strichartz norm of the solution. Let us denote this norm by
X(T):=\|u\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}
.

By the triangle inequality, the norm of the full solution is bounded by the sum of its low-frequency and high-frequency components:

(45)

X(T)\leq\|w_{N}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}+\|v_{N}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}.

Substituting the explicit estimates (
33
) and (
44
) into (
45
), we gather all the components to obtain:

(46)

X(T)\leq\underbrace{\|w_{N}^{lin}\|_{X_{T}}}_{\text{Low-freq linear}}+\underbrace{\epsilon(N)}_{\text{High-freq tail}}+\underbrace{C_{S}C_{a}N\sqrt{T}E_{0}^{1/2}}_{\text{Low-freq KV penalty}}+\underbrace{C_{a}\cdot T\cdot E_{0}^{1/2}}_{\text{High-freq KV commutator}}+\underbrace{C_{1}X(T)^{5}}_{\text{Nonlinearity}}.

At first glance, closing a bootstrap argument with equation (
46
) might seem problematic for arbitrarily large initial data. The reader might rightfully wonder: how can we guarantee that the independent terms on the right-hand side are strictly small when the initial energy
E_{0}
is large and the frequency cut-off
N
increases the penalty?

This is where the underlying structure of our decomposition shines. The success of the argument hinges on a strict, non-circular hierarchical choice of the parameters
N
(the frequency threshold) and
T
(the time horizon). We must proceed in the following precise order:

(1)

Fixing the Frequency
N
(Killing the Tail):
The term
\epsilon(N)
represents the Strichartz norm of the high-frequency linear evolution. Since the initial data has finite energy
E_{0}
, its spectral tail must vanish, meaning
\epsilon(N)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. We choose and
strictly fix
a sufficiently large
N_{0}
such that
\epsilon(N_{0})
is arbitrarily small.

(2)

Shrinking the Time
T
(Taming the Regularized Data and the Penalties):
With
N_{0}
now permanently fixed, we analyze the remaining terms as
T\to 0
:

•

The low-frequency Kelvin-Voigt penalty
C_{S}C_{a}N_{0}\sqrt{T}E_{0}^{1/2}
now solely depends on
\sqrt{T}
and explicitly vanishes as
T\to 0
.

•

The high-frequency commutator source
C_{a}\cdot T\cdot E_{0}^{1/2}
explicitly vanishes as
T\to 0
.

•

Most importantly, the low-frequency linear part
w_{N_{0}}^{lin}
is now a fixed, highly regular function (since
N_{0}
is fixed). By the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral, its norm over a shrinking time interval,
\|w_{N_{0}}^{lin}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))}
, must strongly converge to zero as
T\to 0
, regardless of how large the initial energy
E_{0}
originally was.

Once
N_{0}
is fixed and
T
is chosen sufficiently small, all the linear terms and penalties collectively collapse into a single, arbitrarily small constant
K>0
. The inequality (
46
) then gracefully reduces to the standard polynomial form:

(47)

X(T)\leq K+C_{1}X(T)^{5}.

To conclude that
X(T)
is bounded, we invoke a standard continuity argument (bootstrap principle). Consider the polynomial function
p(x)=x-C_{1}x^{5}
. For a sufficiently small constant
K
, the equation
x-C_{1}x^{5}=K
has two positive real roots, say
r_{1}<r_{2}
. This creates a "forbidden region"
(r_{1},r_{2})
where the algebraic inequality
x-C_{1}x^{5}\leq K
cannot be mathematically satisfied.

Since the mapping
t\mapsto X(t)=\|u\|_{L^{5}(0,t;L^{10}(\Omega))}
is continuous in time, and it originates at
X(0)=0
(which is strictly less than the first root
r_{1}
), the value of
X(t)
can never jump across the forbidden region to reach larger values. Therefore,
X(T)
must remain safely trapped in the bounded interval
[0,r_{1}]
for all times
t\in[0,T]
.

This establishes a uniform bound for the critical
L^{5}_{t}L^{10}_{x}
norm that depends only on the profile of the initial data and the structural constants of the problem, but is completely independent of the truncation parameter
k
. This uniform Strichartz bound is the fundamental key that allows us to safely pass to the limit as
k\to\infty
in the truncated nonlinearity. Consequently, we rigorously deduce the local existence of a finite energy weak solution that additionally belongs to the critical Strichartz space. The higher regularity required to classify it as a strong solution will be extracted in the subsequent analysis.

5.7.
Step 6: Passing to the Limit (
k\to\infty
)

Recall that the truncated nonlinearity
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
(
2
). In particular,
f_{k}
is odd,
C^{1}
, and satisfies the growth bounds

(48)

|f_{k}(s)|\leq C\,(|s|^{5}+k^{4}|s|),\qquad|f_{k}^{\prime}(s)|\leq C\,k^{4},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R},

with a constant
C
independent of
k
.

Let
u_{k}
be the weak solutions to the truncated problems on
(0,T)

constructed in the previous step. From the uniform energy estimate we have

(49)

u_{k}\ \text{bounded in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),\qquad(u_{k})_{t}\ \text{bounded in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

With the uniform bound established in the previous step, we can now pass to the limit in the truncated problem. The
bootstrap
argument ensures the existence of a time
T>0
and a constant
C>0
, both independent of
k
, such that the sequence of solutions
\{u_{k}\}
to the truncated problems (
19
) satisfies:

(50)

\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1})}+\|(u_{k})_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2})}+\|u_{k}\|_{L^{5}(0,T;L^{10})}\leq C.

In particular, by Sobolev embedding in dimension
3
,

(51)

\sup_{k}\,\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}\leq C(E_{0}).

By the Banach-Alaoglu Theorem, we can extract a subsequence, still denoted by
\{u_{k}\}
, and find a limit function
u
such that, as
k\to\infty
:

(52)

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{weak-*}\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

(53)

\displaystyle(u_{k})_{t}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u_{t}\quad\text{weak-*}\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Since
\{u_{k}\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
and its time derivative
\{(u_{k})_{t}\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, the Aubin-Lions Compactness Lemma implies that
\{u_{k}\}
converges strongly to
u
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Up to extracting a further subsequence, we obtain point-wise convergence almost everywhere:

(54)

u_{k}(t,x)\to u(t,x)\quad\text{a.e. in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

This point-wise convergence, combined with the definition of the truncation
f_{k}
, yields
f_{k}(u_{k})\to u^{5}
almost everywhere. To pass to the limit in the weak formulation, we must control the nonlinear term. From the critical Strichartz bound in (
50
), we know that
u_{k}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))
. Since
|f_{k}(s)|\leq|s|^{5}
, it follows that the sequence
\{f_{k}(u_{k})\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Applying standard weak convergence results (such as Lions’ Lemma), the almost everywhere convergence and the uniform integrability bound guarantee that:

(55)

f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{5}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

We now identify the limit of the nonlinear term.
Set

g_{k}:=f_{k}(u_{k}),\qquad g:=u^{5}.

We prove that, up to a subsequence,

(56)

f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{5}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))\quad\text{(hence also weakly in }L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\text{).}

Lemma 5.2

(Uniform integrability and weak compactness)
.

The family
\{f_{k}(u_{k})\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Using (
48
) and Hölder,

\|f_{k}(u_{k}(t))\|_{L^{6/5}}\leq C\big(\|u_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6}}^{5}+k^{4}\|u_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6/5}}\big).

Since
\Omega
is bounded,
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{6/5}}\leq C_{\Omega}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{6}}
, and by
(
51
) the first term is uniformly bounded.
For the second term, note that
f_{k}(s)=s^{5}
when
|s|\leq k
and

|f_{k}(s)|\leq C|s|^{5}
for all
s
(indeed, the truncation does not increase the magnitude),
hence

\|f_{k}(u_{k}(t))\|_{L^{6/5}}\leq C\|u_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6}}^{5}\leq C(E_{0}),

uniformly in
k
and
t
. This yields the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Identification of the limit)
.

Up to a subsequence,

f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{5},

weakly in

L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

We decompose

(57)

f_{k}(u_{k})-u^{5}=\big(f_{k}(u_{k})-u_{k}^{5}\big)+\big(u_{k}^{5}-u^{5}\big).

Step 1:
u_{k}^{5}\rightharpoonup u^{5}
.

By (
51
),
\{u_{k}^{5}\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))
.
Together with the a.e. convergence
u_{k}\to u
a.e., we have
u_{k}^{5}\to u^{5}
a.e.
Hence, by weak compactness and the standard a.e. identification principle
(Vitali/Dunford–Pettis, or Lions’ lemma), we obtain

(58)

u_{k}^{5}\rightharpoonup u^{5}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega)).

Step 2: the truncation error vanishes.

Since
f_{k}(s)=s^{5}
for
|s|\leq k
, we have

f_{k}(u_{k})-u_{k}^{5}=\big(f_{k}(u_{k})-u_{k}^{5}\big)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|u_{k}|>k\}}.

Moreover, by the explicit definition (
2
), there exists
C>0
such that

|f_{k}(s)-s^{5}|\leq C\,|s|^{5}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|s|>k\}}\qquad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore,

\|f_{k}(u_{k}(t))-u_{k}(t)^{5}\|_{L^{6/5}}^{6/5}\leq C\int_{\{|u_{k}(t)|>k\}}|u_{k}(t,x)|^{6}\,dx.

Using (
51
) and Chebyshev’s inequality,

\int_{\{|u_{k}(t)|>k\}}|u_{k}(t)|^{6}\,dx\leq\|u_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6}}^{6}\leq C(E_{0}),

and moreover the sets
\{|u_{k}(t)|>k\}
shrink as
k\to\infty
.
Hence for a.e.
t
,

\int_{\{|u_{k}(t)|>k\}}|u_{k}(t)|^{6}\,dx\longrightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

By dominated convergence in time (using the uniform
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{6}_{x}
bound),
we deduce

(59)

\|f_{k}(u_{k})-u_{k}^{5}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))}\longrightarrow 0.

Combining (
57
), (
58
), and (
59
),
we conclude that
f_{k}(u_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{5}
weakly in

L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega))
, proving (
56
).
∎

Finally, for any test function
\varphi\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
we have

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}f_{k}(u_{k})\,\varphi\,dx\,dt\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}u^{5}\,\varphi\,dx\,dt,

which is the desired convergence in the weak formulation.

Finally, the linear terms pass to the limit straightforwardly via weak convergence. The thermo-viscous damping term converges weakly since
\sqrt{a(x)}\nabla(u_{k})_{t}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Thus, the limit function
u
satisfies the original critical wave equation with Kelvin-Voigt damping in the sense of distributions on
(0,T)\times\Omega
. This completes the proof of local existence.

Theorem 5.4

(Local Well-Posedness for Arbitrary Data via Spectral Filtering)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
be a non-negative damping coefficient. For any arbitrarily large initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a time
T>0
(depending on the frequency profile of the initial data) such that the critical Kelvin-Voigt damped wave equation (
1
) admits a unique local solution
u
on
[0,T]
.

Furthermore, the solution satisfies the energy identity and belongs to the intersection of the fundamental energy space and the critical Strichartz classes:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in C([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{5}(0,T;L^{10}(\Omega))\cap L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{a(x)}\nabla u_{t}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Remark 5.5
.

The inclusion of the solution in the Strichartz space
L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
is a direct consequence of the admissible pairs in the Strichartz estimates applied during the bootstrap argument. Crucially, this is exactly the regularity required to trigger the Uniqueness Principle established in Theorem
4.2
, thus elevating the result from mere existence to full well-posedness for large data.

5.8.
Step 2: Uniform Higher Regularity Estimates via Strichartz

The primary objective of this section is to establish the existence of strong solutions for the critical quintic wave equation with Kelvin-Voigt damping, provided the initial data is sufficiently regular. This higher regularity is the cornerstone that will allow us, via a standard density argument, to obtain weak solutions for finite energy data later on.

The main result of this section is summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 5.6

(Global Strong Solutions for Regular Data)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Assume the damping coefficient
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
satisfies
a(x)\geq 0
and the geometric condition
|\nabla a|^{2}\leq ca(x)
for some constant
c>0
. For any regular initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the critical wave equation with Kelvin-Voigt damping admits a unique global strong solution
u
satisfying:

(60)

u\in C([0,T];H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

along with the critical Strichartz regularity
u\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
. Furthermore, the higher-order energy obeys the uniform a priori bound:

(61)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|\nabla\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}(E_{2}(0),E_{0}),

where
E_{2}(0)=\|\nabla u_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
,
E_{0}
is the standard first-order energy, and
\mathcal{M}
is a constant strictly independent of any truncation parameter.

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

To prove this theorem, we consider the regularized problem with a truncated nonlinearity
f_{k}(s)
.
For any fixed truncation parameter
k>0
, the nonlinearity
f_{k}(s)
is globally Lipschitz. Standard theory for semilinear wave equations ensures that, for smooth initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the regularized problem admits a unique strong solution
u_{k}\in C([0,T];H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
.

Our goal in this step is to establish an a priori bound for the higher-order energy of
u_{k}
that is
strictly independent
of the truncation parameter
k
. To achieve this, we will couple the higher-order energy identity with the uniform Strichartz bounds.

Let us define the second-order energy functional:

(62)

E_{2}(t):=\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-inner product of the exact PDE for
u_{k}
with the multiplier
-\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}
, and integrating by parts, we obtain the following identity:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}E_{2}(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}|^{2}\,dx

(63)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla a\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k})\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\,dx.

For the term involving the gradient of the damping coefficient, we employ the geometric assumption
|\nabla a|^{2}\leq ca(x)
. By writing
|\nabla a|=\frac{|\nabla a|}{\sqrt{a}}\sqrt{a}
and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, we deduce:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla a\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k})\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla a|}{\sqrt{a(x)}}|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}|\cdot\sqrt{a(x)}|\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}|\,dx

(64)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}|^{2}\,dx+\frac{c}{2}\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

The first term on the right-hand side is gracefully absorbed by the strong dissipation on the left-hand side of (
5.8
), while the second term is bounded by
\frac{c}{2}E_{2}(t)
.

The most delicate part is the uniform estimate for the nonlinear term. Since
|f_{k}^{\prime}(s)|\leq 5|s|^{4}
universally for all
k
, we can estimate the integral using the generalized Hölder inequality with the exact exponents
(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{2})
:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq 5\int_{\Omega}|u_{k}|^{4}|\nabla u_{k}||\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}|\,dx

(65)

\displaystyle\leq 5\|u_{k}\|_{12}^{4}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{6}\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\|_{2}.

In dimension
n=3
, the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,6}(\Omega)
ensures that
\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{6}\leq C_{sob}\|\Delta u_{k}\|_{2}
. Inserting this into (
5.8
) and using Young’s inequality, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq 5C_{sob}\|u_{k}\|_{12}^{4}\|\Delta u_{k}\|_{2}\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{k}(t)\|_{12}^{4}\left(\|\Delta u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=C\|u_{k}(t)\|_{12}^{4}E_{2}(t).

Combining all the estimates back into (
5.8
) and absorbing the fractional dissipation term, we arrive at the clean differential inequality:

(67)

\frac{d}{dt}E_{2}(t)+\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\Delta\partial_{t}u_{k}|^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(1+\|u_{k}(t)\|_{12}^{4}\right)E_{2}(t).

Applying Gronwall’s Lemma over the interval
[0,t]
yields:

(68)

E_{2}(t)\leq E_{2}(0)\exp\left(Ct+C\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{k}(s)\|_{12}^{4}\,ds\right).

Notice that the integral inside the exponential is exactly the fourth power of the Strichartz norm
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{4}(0,t;L^{12}(\Omega))}^{4}
. As established in our prior Strichartz analysis, by bounding the total source term in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, the Strichartz estimates simultaneously control all critical pairs. In particular, we are guaranteed that
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))}\leq C_{S}
, where
C_{S}
depends only on the initial energy
E_{0}
and is strictly independent of
k
.

Consequently, we obtain a uniform higher-order energy bound:

(69)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta u_{k}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}(E_{2}(0),E_{0}),

where
\mathcal{M}
is a constant independent of
k
. This uniform bound is the rigorous cornerstone that allows us to extract a strongly convergent subsequence as
k\to\infty
, effectively passing to the limit and proving the existence of strong solutions for the original critical quintic wave equation.
∎

6.
Stabilization Mechanism and Microlocal Structure

In this section we clarify the stabilization mechanism underlying the
Kelvin–Voigt model and its interaction with the microlocal defect
measure framework. The argument relies on the contradiction strategy
introduced in Lemma 3.1 of
[
9
]
, adapted here to
the truncated system. Since the demonstration is exactly the same in
[
9
]
, simply replacing the frictional dissipation term with the Kelvin-Voigt type dissipative term, we only give an idea of the proof by showing the behavior of the Kelvin-Voigt term.

6.1.
Contradiction setting

To prove the uniform exponential stabilization, we argue by contradiction. Suppose that the uniform observability inequality fails. Then, there exists a sequence of solutions
\{u_{k}^{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
(keeping the truncation parameter
k
fixed for the moment) such that their energies are not uniformly controlled by the dissipation. To analyze the regime where compactness is lost, we introduce the normalized sequence:

(70)

v_{k}^{m}(t,x)=\frac{u_{k}^{m}(t,x)}{\alpha_{m}},\quad\text{where }\alpha_{m}=\sqrt{E_{u_{k}^{m}}(0)}.

By construction, this new sequence satisfies
E_{v_{k}^{m}}(0)=1
for all
m
. Consequently,
\{v_{k}^{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in the energy space
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and converges weakly (up to a subsequence) to some limit function
v_{k}
.

While the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem provides strong convergence in lower-order spaces (such as
L^{2}_{t,x}
), the kinetic energy norm
\|\partial_{t}v_{k}^{m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
lacks compactness. To rigorously quantify this defect of compactness—specifically, the concentration of high-frequency oscillations—we employ the framework of Microlocal Defect Measures introduced by P. Gérard
[
16
]
and L. Tartar
[
31
]
.

By the structure theorem for bounded sequences in
L^{2}
, there exists a non-negative Radon measure
\mu
defined on the cosphere bundle
S^{*}(\Omega\times(0,T))
such that, for any classical pseudo-differential operator
A
of order zero with principal symbol
\sigma_{0}(A)
, we have:

(71)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\langle A(\partial_{t}v_{k}^{m}),\partial_{t}v_{k}^{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}=\langle\mu,\sigma_{0}(A)\rangle.

In particular, choosing
A
as the multiplication by a smooth test function
\phi(t,x)
, equation (
71
) reveals that
\mu
characterizes the weak-
\star
limit of the kinetic energy density:

(72)

|\partial_{t}v_{k}^{m}|^{2}\rightharpoonup\int_{S^{*}_{t,x}}d\mu\quad\text{in the sense of measures.}

Once that uniform stabilization fails, then there exist sequences
of solutions
(v_{k}^{m})
for the truncated problem and initial data
normalized such that

(73)

E_{v_{k}}^{m}(0)=1,\qquad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}a(x)|\nabla v_{k,t}^{m}|^{2}\,dxdt\longrightarrow 0,\penalty\ \hbox{as}\penalty\ m\rightarrow\infty\penalty\ (k\penalty\ \hbox{fixed}).

while

(74)

E_{k}^{m}(t)\geq c_{0}>0,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

The truncation introduces two parameters:

•

k
controlling the Galerkin/Littlewood–Paley truncation,

•

m
associated with the contradiction sequence.

Initially, the constants in Lemma 3.1 depend on
k
, but this dependence
is removed through a second contradiction argument.

6.2.
Loss of strong convergence due to Kelvin–Voigt damping

Passing to the limit
m\to\infty
, the key difficulty appears in the
Kelvin–Voigt term. Unlike frictional damping, we no longer recover
strong convergence of the residual in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

More precisely, from the equation we obtain only

(75)

\partial_{t}\Box v_{k}^{m}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega)),

where
\Box=\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta
denotes the wave operator.

This loss of one derivative is intrinsic to the Kelvin–Voigt mechanism.
Indeed, recalling the scaling argument

u_{\lambda}(t,x)=u(\lambda t,\lambda x),

we observe that

\Delta u_{t}\mapsto\lambda^{3}\Delta u_{t},

revealing the higher-order nature of the viscous term.

6.3.
Microlocal consequence

Estimate (
75
) implies only that the associated
microlocal defect measure
\mu
satisfies

(76)

\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\subset\mathrm{Char}(P),

where
P=\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta
is the principal wave operator.

At this level of regularity we cannot yet conclude propagation along
bicharacteristics. However, outside the damping region
\omega
,
the Kelvin–Voigt term vanishes and the equation reduces to the free
wave equation.

Therefore, on
\Omega\setminus\omega
, standard microlocal propagation
applies, yielding

(77)

\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\text{ is a union of bicharacteristics of }P,

and, consequently the support of
\mu
is transported along generalized bicharacteristics.

6.4.
Vanishing of the defect measure in the damping region

From (
73
), we recover

a(x)\nabla u_{k,t}^{m}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2},

which implies

(78)

\mu=0\quad\text{on }\omega.

Combining (
77
) and (
78
), we deduce:

If every bicharacteristic of
P
intersects
\omega
, then
\mu\equiv 0
.

This is precisely the Geometric Control Condition (GCC).

6.5.
Role of the CGC / GCC and Non-Invasive Geometries

Under the standard Geometric Control Condition (GCC), uniform stabilization follows immediately since the defect measure
\mu
is transported along the bicharacteristic flow and trivially annihilated upon entering the damping region
\omega
.

However, in many physical scenarios—particularly those involving inhomogeneous media, variable coefficients, or complex boundary configurations—the classical localized GCC fails due to the existence of trapped rays. These are generalized bicharacteristics that oscillate indefinitely without ever entering a standard localized control region, allowing high-frequency energy to concentrate and consequently preventing uniform exponential decay.

To overcome this fundamental geometric obstruction without trivially damping the entire domain, one can appeal to the highly ubiquitous, “non-invasive” damping geometries developed in the works of Cavalcanti
et al.

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. The core strategy in these papers relies on the intricate topological construction of a dissipative region
\omega
that acts as a pervasive geometrical net.

Specifically, it is possible to design an open set
\omega
satisfying two seemingly antagonistic properties:

•

Dynamical interception:
The region
\omega
is strategically distributed such that it intersects every single generalized bicharacteristic within a uniform finite time
T_{0}>0
.

•

Arbitrarily small volume:
The Lebesgue measure of the damping region can be made arbitrarily small, i.e.,
\operatorname{meas}(\omega)<\epsilon
for any given
\epsilon>0
.

From the microlocal perspective, this construction reveals a profound property of the defect measure framework: it is completely insensitive to volumetric and metric considerations. The measure
\mu
strictly encodes high-frequency concentration along the dynamical flow. As long as the damping set
\omega
dynamically intercepts all invariant sets of the flow, the relation
\mu=0
on
\omega
will systematically propagate along the bicharacteristics, ultimately forcing
\mu\equiv 0
everywhere in the domain.

Therefore, even when the underlying non-homogeneous metric strictly forbids stabilization via classical localized damping, the robust microlocal structure of the Kelvin-Voigt dissipation presented in this paper can be seamlessly coupled with these geometrically complex, arbitrarily small-measure damping sets to guarantee global exponential stabilization.

6.6.
Removal of the truncation parameter
k

The first contradiction argument yields estimates with constants
depending on
k
. A second contradiction eliminates this dependence. (see
[
9
]
)

Assuming failure of uniform bounds in
k
, we construct a renormalized
sequence. Repeating the defect-measure argument shows again that

\mu\equiv 0
, producing the final contradiction. Indeed,
in previous works dealing with subcritical or Lipschitz non-linearities (e.g.,
[
9
]
), the dependence on the truncation parameter
k
is typically removed through a second contradiction argument, assuming
C_{k}\to\infty
and applying the microlocal defect measure framework once again.

Here, however, we can bypass this second contradiction entirely by exploiting the sharp Unique Continuation Property (UCP) for the wave equation, taking full advantage of the critical Strichartz estimates obtained in Section 2.

Assume, by contradiction, that the uniform observability inequality fails for the original problem. By extracting a normalized contradiction sequence and passing to the weak limit as done previously, we obtain a limit function
v
satisfying:

(79)

\begin{cases}\Box v+\alpha^{4}v^{5}=0&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}v=0&\text{in }\omega\times(0,T).\end{cases}

Differentiating the equation with respect to time, the function
w=\partial_{t}v
solves the linearized equation:

(80)

\Box w+V(x,t)w=0,\quad\text{where }V(x,t)=5\alpha^{4}v^{4}.

This is the crucial step: thanks to the well-posedness framework established in Section 2, the limit solution
v
inherits the critical Strichartz regularity
v\in L^{4}(0,T;L^{12}(\Omega))
. Consequently, the potential
V(x,t)
belongs exactly to the critical space
L^{1}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))
.

According to the sharp unique continuation result by Duyckaerts, Zhang, and Zuazua
[
14
]
(Theorem 2.2), the observability over
\omega\times(0,T)
for a wave equation with a potential in this exact
L^{1}_{t}L^{3}_{x}
class implies that
w\equiv 0
in the whole domain
\Omega\times(0,T)
.

This immediately yields
v\equiv 0
. Since the limit solution vanishes identically, the equipartition of energy forces the initial normalized energy to go to zero, which contradicts the normalization assumption (
E_{v^{m}}(0)=1
). This single contradiction proves the uniform observability inequality directly for the critical problem, eliminating any dependence on the truncation parameter
k
.

Theorem 6.1

(Global Exponential Stabilization)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Assume that the localized Kelvin-Voigt damping coefficient
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is non-negative and strictly positive on an open subset
\omega\subset\Omega
. Suppose further that
\omega
satisfies the Geometric Control Condition (GCC), or is constructed as a non-invasive arbitrarily small-measure damping set that dynamically intercepts all generalized bicharacteristics of the wave operator.

Then, for any arbitrarily large initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, the unique global solution
u
of the critical Kelvin-Voigt damped wave equation (
1
) stabilizes exponentially. That is, there exist uniform constants
C\geq 1
and
\gamma>0
, independent of the initial data, such that the total energy of the system, defined by

(81)

E_{u}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\|u(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{6},

satisfies the decay estimate:

(82)

E_{u}(t)\leq CE_{u}(0)e^{-\gamma t},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

6.7.
Conclusion

The Kelvin–Voigt mechanism alters the regularity of the residual,
reducing convergence from
H^{-1}
to
H^{-2}
. This prevents direct
propagation everywhere but preserves the microlocal structure outside
the damping region.

The stabilization mechanism therefore remains entirely governed by:

•

Microlocal propagation in
\Omega\setminus\omega
,

•

Vanishing of
\mu
in
\omega
,

•

Geometric interception of bicharacteristics.

Remark 6.2

(Absence of dissipation on the illuminated boundary)
.

An important geometric configuration arises when the dissipative mechanism
is absent on the illuminated portion of the boundary.

In such situations, generalized bicharacteristics may undergo multiple
reflections without necessarily intersecting the damping region. The
analysis must therefore incorporate the microlocal structure of the
reflected flow.

The asymptotic behavior of high-frequency energy concentration for the
Dirichlet problem was investigated by Gérard and Leichtnam

[
17
]
. Their results describe the ergodic and propagation
properties of eigenfunctions, providing a rigorous framework for
understanding how energy distributes under repeated reflections at the
boundary.

From the stabilization viewpoint, the defect-measure argument remains
valid provided the reflected bicharacteristic flow does not admit trapped
trajectories avoiding the dissipative region. In particular, if the
underlying billiard dynamics prevents the formation of invariant
concentration sets, one still obtains
\mu\equiv 0
.

Therefore, the stabilization mechanism developed in this work is compatible
with configurations where dissipation is missing on part of the illuminated
boundary, under suitable dynamical conditions on the generalized
bicharacteristic flow.

Appendix A
Smooth Spectral Projectors and Bernstein Inequalities

In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the classical Littlewood-Paley decomposition relies heavily on the Fourier transform. For bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the natural substitute is the spectral decomposition associated with the Laplace operator. To ensure bounded commutators and rapid kernel decay, we must employ smooth spectral multipliers rather than sharp cutoffs. In this appendix, we define the smooth low and high-frequency spectral projectors via functional calculus and establish the corresponding Bernstein-type inequalities.

Let
A=-\Delta
with domain
D(A)=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
. Since
\Omega
is bounded,
A
is a strictly positive, self-adjoint operator with a compact inverse. By the Spectral Theorem, there exists a sequence of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that

(83)

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{j}\to\infty\quad\text{as }j\to\infty,

and a corresponding family of eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
which forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
and an orthogonal basis for
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

For any function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have the unique expansion
u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}\phi_{j}
. The fractional Sobolev spaces can be characterized via the spectral coefficients. In particular, for
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
:

(84)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=(Au,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}.

A.1.
Low-Frequency Projector and Bernstein’s Inequality

Let
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be an even, smooth bump function such that
0\leq\chi(s)\leq 1
for all
s
, with
\chi(s)=1
for
|s|\leq 1
and
\chi(s)=0
for
|s|\geq 2
. For a given frequency threshold
N>0
, we define the smooth low-frequency projector
P_{\leq N}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
by:

(85)

P_{\leq N}u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\chi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{N}\right)(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}\phi_{j}.

Because
\chi
is a bounded function,
P_{\leq N}
is a bounded, self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, it commutes with both
\Delta
and
\partial_{t}
.

The key feature of the low-frequency projector is that it smooths the function, allowing us to control higher-order derivatives at the cost of the parameter
N
.

Lemma A.1

(Bernstein Inequality for Low Frequencies)
.

For any
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
P_{\leq N}u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and we have:

(86)

\|\nabla(P_{\leq N}u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2N\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Using the spectral characterization (
84
) and the definition of
P_{\leq N}
, we obtain:

\|\nabla(P_{\leq N}u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\left|\chi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{N}\right)\right|^{2}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}.

Since the support of
\chi
is contained in
[-2,2]
, the terms in the sum are strictly zero whenever
\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}>2N
. For the non-zero terms, we have
\lambda_{j}\leq 4N^{2}
. Using the fact that
|\chi|\leq 1
, we bound the sum by:

\displaystyle\|\nabla(P_{\leq N}u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\leq 2N}4N^{2}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4N^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}=4N^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Taking the square root yields the desired inequality.
∎

Remark A.2

(Energy Splitting)
.

Because we are using smooth multipliers instead of sharp cutoffs,
P_{\leq N}
is not an idempotent projection (i.e.,
P_{\leq N}^{2}\neq P_{\leq N}
). However, since
0\leq\chi(s)\leq 1
, the projectors
P_{\leq N}
and
P_{>N}
are uniformly bounded operators in the energy space. In particular, the algebraic identity
\chi(s)+(1-\chi(s))=1
ensures
P_{\leq N}u+P_{>N}u=u
, guaranteeing that separating the macroscopic wave
w_{N}
from the microscopic fluctuation
v_{N}
does not artificially inflate the total bounds during the a priori estimates.

A.2.
High-Frequency Projector and Poincaré-type Inequality

The smooth high-frequency projector
P_{>N}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is defined as the complementary operator
P_{>N}=I-P_{\leq N}
, which translates to:

(87)

P_{>N}u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[1-\chi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{N}\right)\right](u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}\phi_{j}.

While low frequencies control derivative losses, high frequencies provide a gain in integrability when bounded in the energy space, acting as an enhanced Poincaré inequality.

Lemma A.3

(Reverse Bernstein Inequality for High Frequencies)
.

For any
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have:

(88)

\|P_{>N}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{N}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

By Plancherel’s theorem and the definition of
P_{>N}
, we write:

\|P_{>N}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|1-\chi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{N}\right)\right|^{2}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}.

By the definition of our cutoff function,
1-\chi(s)=0
for all
|s|\leq 1
. Therefore, the terms in the sum are strictly zero for
\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\leq N
. For the remaining terms where
\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}>N
, we have
\lambda_{j}>N^{2}
, which implies
1<\frac{\lambda_{j}}{N^{2}}
. Since
|1-\chi|\leq 1
, we can bound the sum by:

\displaystyle\|P_{>N}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}>N}1\cdot|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}>N}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{N^{2}}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|(u,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Taking the square root finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Rigorous Commutator Estimates for the Damping Term

The objective of this appendix is to provide a rigorous proof of the commutator bound (
37
) utilized in the high-frequency analysis. Specifically, we must estimate the
L^{2}
-norm of the divergence of the commutator between the high-frequency projector
P_{>N}
and the variable damping coefficient
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Let
f:=\nabla u_{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We aim to establish a uniform bound for:

\left\|\operatorname{div}\Big([P_{>N},a(x)]f\Big)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Step 1: Reduction to Low Frequencies.

Handling the high-frequency projector directly is cumbersome. We exploit the identity
P_{>N}=I-P_{\leq N}
. Since the identity operator perfectly commutes with pointwise multiplication by
a(x)
, we have:

[P_{>N},a(x)]=[I-P_{\leq N},a(x)]=-[P_{\leq N},a(x)].

Therefore, it suffices to estimate the divergence of the low-frequency commutator:
-\operatorname{div}\big([P_{\leq N},a(x)]f\big)
.

Step 2: Integral Representation and Kernel Bounds.

By the functional calculus of the Dirichlet Laplacian, the smooth low-frequency projector
P_{\leq N}=\chi(\sqrt{-\Delta}/N)
is an integral operator. Let
K_{N}(x,y)
be its integral kernel. We write the action of the commutator
[P_{\leq N},a]f
explicitly as:

\displaystyle\big([P_{\leq N},a]f\big)(x)

\displaystyle=P_{\leq N}(af)(x)-a(x)P_{\leq N}(f)(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K_{N}(x,y)a(y)f(y)\,dy-\int_{\Omega}K_{N}(x,y)a(x)f(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K_{N}(x,y)\Big(a(y)-a(x)\Big)f(y)\,dy.

Because
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is an even, rapidly decaying function, the standard spectral cluster estimates for bounded domains (see, e.g., Sogge or Taylor) guarantee that the kernel
K_{N}(x,y)
and its spatial derivatives satisfy the rapid decay bounds:

(89)

|\nabla_{x}K_{N}(x,y)|\leq C_{M}\frac{N^{4}}{(1+N|x-y|)^{M}},

for any integer
M>0
, where
C_{M}
is a constant independent of
N
.

Step 3: Applying the Divergence Operator.

Next, we apply the spatial gradient
\nabla_{x}
to the integral representation. By the product rule, the derivative acts on both the kernel
K_{N}
and the coefficient
a(x)
:

\nabla_{x}\big([P_{\leq N},a]f\big)(x)=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{x}K_{N}(x,y)\big(a(y)-a(x)\big)f(y)\,dy}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}-\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}K_{N}(x,y)\nabla a(x)f(y)\,dy}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}.

Step 4: Estimate for
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.

The second term can be rewritten simply as
-\nabla a(x)\cdot(P_{\leq N}f)(x)
. Since
\nabla a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and the smooth spectral projections are uniformly bounded operators on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we immediately obtain:

(90)

\|\mathcal{T}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|P_{\leq N}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

Step 5: Estimate for
\mathcal{T}_{1}
(The Cancellation Effect via Schur’s Test).

This term captures the core smoothing effect. Naively, the derivative
\nabla_{x}K_{N}
costs a factor of
N^{4}
(one
N
more than the volume factor
N^{3}
). However, we control the difference
\big(a(y)-a(x)\big)
using the Lipschitz continuity of
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
via the Mean Value Theorem:

|a(y)-a(x)|\leq\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}|x-y|.

Combining this with the kernel bound (
89
), we bound the modified kernel of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, which we denote by
\tilde{K}_{N}(x,y)
, as follows:

\displaystyle|\tilde{K}_{N}(x,y)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\nabla_{x}K_{N}(x,y)\Big|\cdot|a(y)-a(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{N^{4}|x-y|}{(1+N|x-y|)^{M}}

\displaystyle=C_{M}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot N^{3}\frac{N|x-y|}{(1+N|x-y|)^{M}}.

Let us choose
M>4
. Because
\frac{s}{(1+s)^{M}}
is bounded and integrable over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the integration of this modified kernel with respect to either
x
or
y
yields a uniform constant independent of
N
:

\sup_{x\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega}|\tilde{K}_{N}(x,y)|\,dy\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{y\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega}|\tilde{K}_{N}(x,y)|\,dx\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}.

By Schur’s Test for integral operators, this implies that the operator
\mathcal{T}_{1}
maps
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
boundedly:

(91)

\|\mathcal{T}_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

The diverging factor
N
was perfectly cancelled by the spatial difference
|x-y|
, absorbing the derivative without any asymptotic loss.

Conclusion.

Combining (
90
) and (
91
) via the triangle inequality, and recalling
f=\nabla u_{t}
, we conclude:

(92)

\left\|\operatorname{div}\Big([P_{>N},a(x)]\nabla u_{t}\Big)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{a}E_{0}^{1/2}.

This rigorously demonstrates that the divergence of the commutator is completely free of derivative loss, yielding a uniform bound independent of the frequency threshold
N
in the bounded domain setting.

Remark B.1

(Pseudodifferential Calculus and Sogge’s Spectral Multipliers)
.

The rigorous kernel bound and Schur’s test detailed above confirm the heuristic expected from classical pseudodifferential calculus. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the smooth projector
P_{\leq N}=\chi(\sqrt{-\Delta}/N)
acts as a Fourier multiplier with symbol
\chi(|\xi|/N)
. The commutator
[P_{\leq N},a(x)]
generates a principal symbol given by the Poisson bracket:

\sigma([P_{\leq N},a(x)])\sim\{\chi(|\xi|/N),a(x)\}=\nabla_{\xi}\left(\chi\left(\frac{|\xi|}{N}\right)\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}a(x)=\frac{1}{N}\nabla\chi\left(\frac{|\xi|}{N}\right)\cdot\nabla a(x).

This explicitly reveals that the commutator is a regularizing operator of order
-1
. The crucial
1/N
factor exactly cancels the spatial derivative coming from the divergence operator in the Kelvin-Voigt term, explaining the uniform
L^{2}
bound independent of
N
.

Furthermore, because
\chi
is a smooth multiplier, the celebrated spectral multiplier theorems for bounded domains by Sogge (see e.g.,
[
28
]
) guarantee that the projectors
P_{\leq N}
and
P_{>N}
are uniformly bounded operators on
L^{p}(\Omega)
for
1<p<\infty
. This deep fact ensures that separating the frequencies does not destroy the integrability required to close the critical Strichartz estimates in
L_{t}^{5}L_{x}^{10}(\Omega)
.
