Provable Quantum Advantage for Dynamical Phase Transition
Jue Xu, Xiao Yuan, Qi Zhao

Abstract
The universal scaling of critical behavior in phase transitions is a cornerstone of physics. Dynamical quantum phase transitions (DQPTs) are their nonequilibrium analogues: abrupt nonanalyticities that emerge as a quantum system evolves in time. Yet the hardness and cost of detecting this phenomenon remain largely unexplored. We prove that estimating DQPT to a certain precision is intractable even for quantum computers, whereas deciding a subsystem variant of DQPT is as hard as simulating generic quantum circuits, implying a provable exponential quantum advantage. Furthermore, to search for critical times of local DQPTs, we show a quadratically faster quantum algorithm that estimates observables of Hamiltonian dynamics at multiple time points with Heisenberg-limited precision and sublinear scaling in the number of time points. Moreover, through encoding classical evolution into quantum dynamics, our framework enables broader quantum speedups for detecting anomalous phenomena in classical systems.



Introduction
–
Characterizing quantum many-body dynamics far from equilibrium is a central challenge across physics, chemistry, and materials science.
In equilibrium, phase transitions arise as nonanalyticities of the free energy at critical points, separating distinct phases of matter.
The similar critical behaviors of phase transitions shared by vastly different physical systems are known as universality classes
Sachdev 2011
.
To extend this framework to nonequilibrium dynamics, where no free energy is defined and the state evolves unitarily, Heyl, Polkovnikov, and Kehrein
Heyl et al. 2013
;
Heyl 2018
introduced the dynamical quantum phase transition (DQPT).
The central observable is the Loschmidt echo,
the return probability of a quantum state to itself after a sudden quench
Gorin et al. 2006
, which plays the role of a dynamical partition function.
Its intensive logarithm, the rate function, develops sharp nonanalyticities at critical times in close analogy to a free-energy density.
These cusps obey scalings whose exponents depend only on an underlying universality class
Heyl 2015
.
DQPTs now provide an organizing principle for nonequilibrium quantum matter
Heyl 2018
, spanning broken-symmetry, long-range, topological, and driven systems
Heyl 2014
;
Van Damme et al. 2023
;
Zunkovic et al. 2018
;
Budich and Heyl 2016
;
Huang and Balatsky 2016
;
Lang et al. 2018
;
Zache et al. 2019
;
Hamazaki 2021

and linking to entanglement and information scrambling
De Nicola et al. 2021
;
Heyl et al. 2018
.

To bypass the barrier from its definition,

Beyond the critical time decision problem,

We construct a quantum gradient-estimation-based protocol

We also show that both the critical time and the universal scaling exponent are robust to Trotter error and noise,

We first formalize DQPTs.

\quantity{t_j}_{j=1}^{M}

Discussion
–
This work places DQPT detection alongside ground-state energy estimation as a physically motivated source of provable quantum advantage.
The lower bounds imply that our protocol is not merely optimal in quantum cases but also faster than classical approaches.
This work opens a new avenue for quantum algorithms in classical dynamical information processing.
Meanwhile, there remain several directions for future work.
Does

-

persist under average-case hardness for geometrically two-local, translation-invariant Hamiltonians
where experimental DQPTs actually live?
It is also of practical interest to reduce the cost of the search algorithm with prior knowledge.

Lemma A.2

First, we create a problem Hamiltonian

e^{-\textup{i}H_{U}\pi/2}\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathrm{\bf o}}=(-\textup{i})^{N}\ket{N}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\otimes U\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathrm{\bf o}}
, where the global phase
(-\textup{i})^{N}
is irrelevant for the projector and echo arguments below.

Proof.

The proof strategy is to introduce an analytically solvable auxiliary system
whose dynamics, when restricted to an invariant subspace, are spectrally
identical to the clock sector of
H_{U}
.
Consider
N
independent qubits with collective Pauli operator

\bar{X}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{(j)}
.
Since each qubit evolves independently via
e^{-\textup{i}Xt}=\cos t\,I-\textup{i}\sin t\,X
, the evolution from
\ket{0}^{\otimes N}
factorizes as

e^{-\textup{i}\bar{X}t}\ket{0}^{\otimes N}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\quantity(\cos t\,\ket{0}_j - \ii\sin t\,\ket{1}_j).

(28)

Hamming-weight decomposition.

Define the (normalized) Hamming-weight states

\ket{w_k}\coloneqq\binom{N}{k}^{-1/2}\sum_{\absolutevalue{x}=k}\ket{x}

for
k=0,\ldots,N
.
Expanding
Eq.

28
and collecting computational-basis states by Hamming weight, each weight-
k
string picks up the coefficient
(-\textup{i}\sin t)^{k}(\cos t)^{N-k}
.
Since there are
\binom{N}{k}
such strings,

e^{-\textup{i}\bar{X}t}\ket{0}^{\otimes N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-\textup{i}\sin t)^{k}(\cos t)^{N-k}\,\binom{N}{k}^{1/2}\,\ket{w_k}.

(29)

Tridiagonal structure of
\bar{X}
.

The operator
X^{(j)}
flips bit
j
.
For a string
\ket{x}
with
\absolutevalue{x}=k
, there are
k
positions where
x_{j}=1
(flipping to weight
k-1
) and
N-k
positions where
x_{j}=0
(flipping to weight
k+1
).
By a counting argument: each weight-
(k{-}1)
string
y
is produced from exactly
N-k+1
distinct pairs
(x,j)
with
\absolutevalue{x}=k
and
x_{j}=1
, since
y
has
N-k+1
zero-positions that can be set to 1 to yield a weight-
k
string.
Similarly, each weight-
(k{+}1)
string is produced from
k+1
pairs.
It follows that

\bar{X}\ket{w_k}=\sqrt{k(N{-}k{+}1)}\,\ket{w_{k-1}}+\sqrt{(k{+}1)(N{-}k)}\,\ket{w_{k+1}},

(30)

where the prefactors arise from the ratios of binomial coefficients:

\binom{N}{k}^{-1/2}(N{-}k{+}1)\binom{N}{k{-}1}^{1/2}=\sqrt{k(N{-}k{+}1)}
, and similarly for the raising term
Christandl et al. 2004
;
Berry et al. 2014
.

Spectral equivalence with
H_{U}
.

The payoff of the auxiliary construction is that
\bar{X}

decomposes into independent qubits (giving the closed-form
evolution in
Eq.

29
), yet its restriction
to the Hamming-weight subspace reproduces the clock dynamics
of
H_{U}
.
The relevant invariant subspace of
H_{U}
is the
history subspace

\mathcal{S}_{\psi_{0}}\coloneqq\operatorname{span}\{\ket{\alpha_k}\}_{k=0}^{N}

spanned by the history states

\ket{\alpha_k}\coloneqq\ket{k}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\otimes U_{k}\cdots U_{1}\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathrm{\bf o}}
,
on which the off-diagonal
U_{k},U_{k}^{\dagger}
blocks advance and rewind the
output register in lockstep with the clock:

H_{U}\ket{\alpha_k}=\sqrt{(k{+}1)(N{-}k)}\,\ket{\alpha_{k+1}}+\sqrt{k(N{-}k{+}1)}\,\ket{\alpha_{k-1}}.

(31)

Comparing
Eq.

31
with
Eq.

30
, the

(N{+}1)\times(N{+}1)
tridiagonal matrix of
H_{U}\big|_{\mathcal{S}_{\psi_{0}}}

in the basis
\{\ket{\alpha_k}\}
is identical to that of

\bar{X}\big|_{\{\ket{w_k}\}}
under the identification

\ket{w_k}\leftrightarrow\ket{\alpha_k}
.
Therefore the clock amplitudes under
H_{U}
are the same as those in
Eq.

29
: defining
c_{k}(t)\coloneqq(-\textup{i})^{k}\binom{N}{k}^{1/2}\sin^{k}(t)\,\cos^{N-k}(t)
, we obtain

\absolutevalue{c_k(t)}^{2}=\binom{N}{k}\sin^{2k}(t)\,\cos^{2(N-k)}(t)
,
establishing
Eq.

27
.

Gate application.

By the structure of
H_{U}
(
Eq.

25
), each clock transition
\ket{k{-}1}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\to\ket{k}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}
is accompanied by
U_{k}
acting on the output register.
Hence the full evolution is
e^{-\textup{i}H_{U}t}\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathrm{\bf o}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{k}(t)\,\ket{k}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\otimes U_{k}\cdots U_{1}\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathrm{\bf o}}
, establishing
Eq.

26
.
At
t=\pi/2
,
\sin(\pi/2)=1
and
\cos(\pi/2)=0
, so
\absolutevalue{c_k}^{2}=\delta_{k,N}
and the full circuit
U=U_{N}\cdots U_{1}
is applied with certainty up to global phases.
∎

The remaining analysis uses four standard estimates on the binomial distribution, collected here for reference.

Fact 2

(Binomial toolbox)
.

Write
\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p):=\binom{N}{j}p^{j}(1-p)^{N-j}
for the probability mass function (PMF) of the binomial distribution
\textup{{Bin}}(N,p)
,
write
\sigma^{2}:=Np(1-p)
,
and let
X\sim\textup{{Bin}}(N,p)
.

(i)

Tail derivative:
for
p=\sin^{2}t
,

\frac{d}{dt}\Pr[X\geq j_{0}]=\sin(2t)\cdot N\,\textup{{bin}}(j_{0}-1;\,N-1,\,p).

(45)

(ii)

Pointwise size:
if
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
then
\max_{j}\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)\leq C_{0}/\sigma
, and
\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)\geq c_{0}/\sigma
for every
j
with
|j-Np|\leq C
, with
c_{0}
depending only on
C
.

(iii)

Tail bound (Hoeffding):

\Pr[|X-Np|\geq s]\leq 2e^{-2s^{2}/N}
.

(iv)

Median:

|\Pr[X<m]-\tfrac{1}{2}|\leq C_{1}/\sigma
whenever
|m-Np|=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
.

Proof.

(i) Tail derivative.

Differentiating one PMF term in
p
and regrouping with the binomial identities
j\binom{N}{j}=N\binom{N-1}{j-1}
and
(N-j)\binom{N}{j}=N\binom{N-1}{j}
turns the derivative into a first difference of one-step-shorter PMFs,

\frac{d}{dp}\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)=N\bigl[\textup{{bin}}(j-1;N-1,p)-\textup{{bin}}(j;N-1,p)\bigr].

(46)

Summing this over the upper tail
j\geq j_{0}
telescopes,
leaving only the boundary term,
and the chain rule with
dp/dt=\sin(2t)
for
p=\sin^{2}t
converts the
p
-derivative into the time derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Pr[X\geq j_{0}]

\displaystyle=\sin(2t)\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}N\bigl[\textup{{bin}}(j-1;N-1,p)-\textup{{bin}}(j;N-1,p)\bigr]

(47)

\displaystyle=\sin(2t)\,N\,\textup{{bin}}(j_{0}-1;N-1,p),

which is
Eq.

45
:
the tail CDF moves in time at a rate set by the boundary PMF
\textup{{bin}}(j_{0}-1;N-1,p)
,
the population density crossing the cut
j_{0}
.

(ii) Pointwise size.

By the Stirling’s formula applied to
\binom{N}{j}
,
whenever
\sigma^{2}=Np(1-p)\geq 1
the PMF is controlled, uniformly in
j
, by a Gaussian envelope of width
\sigma
,

\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\,\exp\!\biggl(-\frac{(j-Np)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\biggr)\,\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

(48)

The exponential never exceeds
1
, so the envelope peaks at
1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma)
and
\max_{j}\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)\leq C_{0}/\sigma
for an absolute constant
C_{0}
.
On the bulk
|j-Np|\leq C
the exponent stays above
-C^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})\geq-C^{2}/2
(using
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
), so the envelope is bounded below by
\textup{{bin}}(j;N,p)\geq c_{0}/\sigma
with
c_{0}=\Theta(e^{-C^{2}/2})
depending only on
C
.
Thus every bulk site carries an
\Theta(1/\sigma)
share of the mass, and none carries more.

(iii) Tail bound (Hoeffding).

Write
X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
as a sum of independent
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)
,
each supported on
[0,1]
with
\mathbb{E}X=Np
,
the binomial’s defining representation.
Hoeffding’s inequality for bounded independent summands
(unit ranges, so
\sum_{i}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}=N
) gives the one-sided tail
\Pr[X-Np\geq s]\leq e^{-2s^{2}/N}
;
the same bound applies to the lower tail, and a union bound over the two doubles the constant:

\Pr\bigl[\,|X-Np|\geq s\,\bigr]\leq\Pr[X-Np\geq s]+\Pr[Np-X\geq s]\leq 2e^{-2s^{2}/N}.

(49)

This pins the clock to within
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N\log(1/\delta)})
of its mean except with probability
\delta
,
the concentration used to push the far wall’s tail below the probe split.

(iv) Median.

The median
\nu
of
\textup{{Bin}}(N,p)
satisfies
|\nu-Np|\leq 1
, so at the median
\Pr[X<\nu]\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Pr[X\leq\nu]=\Pr[X<\nu]+\textup{{bin}}(\nu;N,p)
, whence
|\Pr[X<\nu]-\tfrac{1}{2}|\leq\textup{{bin}}(\nu;N,p)\leq C_{0}/\sigma
by (ii).
Any cut
m
with
|m-Np|=\mathcal{O}(1)
lies
|m-\nu|=\mathcal{O}(1)
sites from the median, so sliding the threshold from
\nu
to
m
adds the mass of
\mathcal{O}(1)
integer sites, each at most
C_{0}/\sigma
:

\Bigl|\Pr[X<m]-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr|\leq\underbrace{\bigl|\Pr[X<m]-\Pr[X<\nu]\bigr|}_{\leq\,\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot C_{0}/\sigma}+\underbrace{\bigl|\Pr[X<\nu]-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|}_{\leq\,C_{0}/\sigma}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma}.

(50)

So a binomial is balanced to within
\mathcal{O}(1/\sigma)
about any near-mean threshold, the estimate behind the half-and-half probe split.
∎

We now present this coherent algorithm,

Lemma B.4

(Interleaved parameterized unitary construction)

\displaystyle:=\quantity(\prod_{j=1}^{M} U(t_{j-1}, t_j)\,e^{-2\ii x_j O_j})U(t_{M},t_{0})

Proof.

Theorem B.2

(Gradient-based estimation algorithm, rephrase
Huggins et al. 2022
;
Gilyén et al. 2019a
)
.

Let
\epsilon

be fixed constants, with
\epsilon\leq c

Let

and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

Suppose that

at

(denoted by

\absolutevalue{\partial_{\alpha}f(\vbx)}\leq c^{k}k^{(k/2)}

such that
\norm{\nabla f(\vbx) - \tilde{\vb{g}}}_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
This algorithm makes
\mathcal{O}(c\sqrt{M}\log(M/\delta)/\epsilon)
queries to a probability oracle for
f
.

The sublinear
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{M})
scaling rests on
quantum gradient estimation
: the parameterized unitary
Eq.

73
encodes the
M
target expectation values as the components of the gradient
\nabla f|_{\mathbf{0}}
of a single function, and the optimal gradient algorithm of Gilyén et al.
Gilyén et al. 2019a
—a refinement of Jordan’s original single-query gradient method

—reads out all
M
components together.
Preparing
M
index registers in uniform superposition and applying the probability oracle
F
controlled on the index (
Fig.

8
) probes
f
along all
M
directions in one coherent query; an inverse QFT then demultiplexes the accumulated phases into the individual partial derivatives.
Because the uniform superposition shares each query’s amplitude across the
M
directions, every component carries only
\sim 1/\sqrt{M}
of the signal, so amplitude amplification needs
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{M})
rounds to resolve it.
This
\sqrt{M}
factor is optimal: estimating
M
expectation values to additive error
\epsilon
through a black-box state-preparation oracle requires
\Omega(\sqrt{M}/\epsilon)
queries
Huggins et al. 2022
(
Lemma

C.3
).

The
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
precision, in place of the
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
standard quantum limit, is the Heisenberg scaling, and it comes from coherent access to the signal.
The Hadamard test encodes
f(\mathbf{x})
as a measurement probability, an amplitude squared (
Fig.

7
), which the gradient algorithm queries coherently so that the signal phase accumulates linearly in the number of oracle calls; classical sampling, by contrast, lowers the statistical error only as
1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{samples}}}
, giving
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})

The higher-order central-difference formulas of Gilyén et al.

are what secure this

scaling for the full gradient, a quadratic improvement in
\epsilon
over Jordan’s original method.
Realizing it requires coherent access to both

and
F^{\dagger}

Theorem B.3

(Non-adaptive snapshots estimation)
.

Given an

Proof.

By

and

,
the circuit

is a probability oracle for

satisfying the derivative growth condition with constant

Index registers and phase accumulation.

The gradient estimation algorithm

;
Huggins et al. 2022
introduces

The joint state

ranges over

.
The probability oracle

is then applied

in the index registers.
Near
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}

79

Linearization.

This linearization step accounts for the

, and amplitude amplification requires

rounds to extract the signal.

An inverse quantum Fourier transform on each

(

Query complexity.

Applying

By

The circuit uses

A guarantee stronger than the additive-error bound of

B.2.3
The adaptive version algorithm

The adaptive gradient estimation algorithm of Ref.
Wada et al. 2025
removes both overheads at once.
Determining each gradient component one binary digit at a time over
\lceil{\log_{2}(1/\epsilon)}\rceil
iterative rounds, with only
p=3
index qubits per coordinate, brings the space down to
\mathcal{O}(M)
ancilla qubits, while a geometric schedule of per-round failure probabilities yields the clean Heisenberg query count
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{M}\log M)
with no
\log(1/\eps)
factor (
Corollary

).
The algorithm maintains temporal estimates
\tilde{u}_{j}^{(q)}\in[-1,1]
(initialized to
\tilde{u}_{j}^{(0)}=0

\bra{\psi_0}O_{j}(t_{j})\ket{\psi_0}-\tilde{u}_{j}^{(q)}

\mathcal{O}(1/2^{q})
by induction, are amplified by a factor of

via
\mathcal{O}(2^{q}\sqrt{M})
queries to the probability oracle, bringing them back to
\mathcal{O}(1)
where the
3

C.2
Speedups: classical lower bounds

The previous subsection established the (nearly) optimality of our quantum algorithm for multi-time snapshot estimation.
A natural question is whether it brings a genuine speedup over

We answer affirmatively in the prepare-and-measure model (

Both bounds count the same resource, one call to the state-preparation oracle

per round.
The bound reflects two compounding costs: the

for the
M

We capture both with a multi-parameter estimation lemma (

) applied to a continuous near-
\tfrac{1}{2}

-local Hamiltonian.
Structurally, both the quantum and classical lower bounds couple the

C.2.1
Classical prepare-and-measure model and Bernoulli lower bound

Definition C.1

(Classical prepare-and-measure model)
.

A
classical prepare-and-measure
(PM) algorithm, with oracle access to a state-preparation unitary
U_{\psi}
(
U_{\psi}\ket{\vb{0}}=\ket{\psi_0}
) and to evolution under a Hamiltonian
H
, proceeds in rounds.
Each round
(i) prepares one copy of
\ket{\psi_0}
with a single call to
U_{\psi}
,
(ii) evolves it under
e^{-\textup{i}Ht}
for a time
t\geq 0
of the algorithm’s choosing, and
(iii) measures the fixed projector
P
, recording the one outcome bit.
The choice of
t
and the decision to halt may depend adaptively on previous outcomes and on internal randomness.
The cost is the number of rounds, equal to the number of
U_{\psi}
calls.

A PM round at time
t
thus returns a single sample from
\mathrm{Ber}\big(\bra{\psi_0}e^{\textup{i}Ht}\,P\,e^{-\textup{i}Ht}\ket{\psi_0}\big)
.
The PM class is a strict subclass of the coherent quantum algorithms of the previous subsection: it forbids coherence across rounds and restricts each round to one measurement of the fixed observable
P
, the operationally natural protocol for tracking
\langle P\rangle
along the dynamics.
This single-projector restriction is what the lower bound below exploits.

Lemma C.4

(Classical Bernoulli sample-complexity lower bound
Dagum et al. 2000
)
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and
\epsilon\in(0,p(1-p))
. Any algorithm that estimates the mean of a
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)
distribution to additive precision
\epsilon
with success probability at least
2/3
from classical i.i.d. samples requires
\Omega(p(1-p)/\epsilon^{2})
samples; for
p
bounded away from
\quantity{0,1}
, this is
\Omega(1/\epsilon^{2})
, matching the Chernoff–Hoeffding upper bound of
Lemma

B.1
.

C.2.2
Reduction from multi-parameter Bernoulli estimation

This scalar bound is a warm-up:
it fixes the per-coordinate (time point) cost but is not invoked directly below,
since a single PM round does not measure one snapshot in isolation.
The multi-time task compounds this scalar cost with that of resolving
M
distinct time points.
The obstruction is statistical and has two parts.
First, each projector measurement returns one bit of a
mixture
of the
M
snapshot values and cannot resolve them in parallel;
this is the source of the factor
M
.
Second, a
continuous
near-
\tfrac{1}{2}
encoding keeps every snapshot genuinely fractional, with competing hypotheses separated by only
\Theta(\epsilon)
, so each coordinate must be resolved to full precision
\epsilon
; this is the source of the
1/\epsilon^{2}
.
Estimating
M
such near-
\tfrac{1}{2}
coordinates through single-bit PM rounds is an
M
-parameter testing problem
.

Assouad’s lemma reduces it to
M
simultaneous two-point tests and lower-bounds the total error by their difficulty, measured by the total variation between half-cube mixtures, converting per-coordinate hardness into a clean
M\times(\text{per-coordinate})
bound
;
we use the following averaged form.

Lemma C.5

(Assouad’s lemma, averaged form
(
Tsybakov 2008
, Lemma 2.12)
)
.

Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{v}\}_{v\in\{-1,+1\}^{M}}
be probability measures on a common space,
and for
i\in[M]
let
\mathbb{P}_{\pm i}\coloneqq 2^{-(M-1)}\!\sum_{v:\,v_{i}=\pm 1}\mathbb{P}_{v}
denote the two half-cube mixtures.
Every estimator
\hat{v}
of
v
from a single draw under
\mathbb{P}_{v}
obeys,

2^{-M}\sum_{v}\mathbb{E}\,d_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{v},v)\;\geq\;\frac{M}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i}).

(105)

where
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{v},v)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}[\hat{v}_{i}\neq v_{i}]
is the Hamming distance and
\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)=\sup_{A}\absolutevalue{P(A) - Q(A)}
is the total variation distance (equivalently
\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega}\absolutevalue{P(\omega) - Q(\omega)}
for discrete outcomes).

Proof.

Each
\mathbb{P}_{v}
is a distribution on the same outcome space—in the application below, the space of query–outcome transcripts produced by the algorithm—so every total variation
\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})
compares two distributions over identical outcomes and is well defined.
Recovering
v
means deciding each of the
M
signs
v_{i}=\pm 1
,
and the Hamming distance
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{v},v)
counts the wrong decisions,
so the left-hand side of
Eq.

105
is the expected number of mistaken coordinates under a uniform prior over the hypercube.
The half-cube mixture
\mathbb{P}_{\pm i}
averages over all configurations of the other
M-1
coordinates,
isolating coordinate
i
and reducing the
M
-way estimation to
M
two-point tests, one per sign.
The left-hand side equals
\sum_{i=1}^{M}2^{-M}\sum_{v}\Pr(\hat v_i \neq v_i)
;
for each
i
, the inner average is
the error probability (under the uniform prior) of the test
\hat{v}_{i}
distinguishing
\mathbb{P}_{+i}
from
\mathbb{P}_{-i}
,
hence at least the Bayes error
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})\bigr)
.
Summing over
i
gives
Eq.

105
.
The bound quantifies the intuition:
one beats random guessing on coordinate
i
only insofar as its two mixtures are statistically distinguishable,
so the average risk falls below
M/2
by exactly
\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})
;
forcing the risk to zero requires every
\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})
close to
1
, a cost the query budget must pay coordinate by coordinate.
∎

Applying it to a hypercube of near-
\tfrac{1}{2}
instances yields the
M
-coordinate generalization of
Lemma

C.4
.
In the Hamiltonian reduction below,
the weight vector
\mathbf{a}
will be the clock weights
w_{q}(t)
(selected by the algorithm’s choice of evolution time
t
) and
\mathbf{p}
the projector marginals to be estimated,
while the offset
1-\norm{\vb{a}}_{1}
is the fixed,

\mathbf{p}
-independent leakage
\absolutevalue{c_0(t)}^{2}
.
We state the lemma abstractly for any such single-bit oracle.

Lemma C.6

(Classical lower bound for multi-parameter Bernoulli estimation)
.

Let
M
be a positive integer and
\alpha\in(0,1/8)
.
An unknown vector
\mathbf{p}\in[\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha,\,\tfrac{1}{2}+2\alpha]^{M}
is to be estimated from a
single-bit oracle

whose every query chooses a weight vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{M}
with
\norm{\vb{a}}_{1}\leq 1
and returns one bit
b\sim\mathrm{Ber}\big(1-\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\big)
,
equivalently
\mathrm{Ber}\big(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{p}+(1-\norm{\vb{a}}_{1})\big)
.
The choice of
\mathbf{a}
may depend adaptively on previous outcomes and on internal randomness.
Any algorithm that, for every such
\mathbf{p}
, outputs
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
with
\norm{\hat{\vb{p}} - \vb{p}}_{\infty}\leq\alpha
with probability at least
2/3
requires
\Omega(M/\alpha^{2})
queries.

Proof.

We apply Assouad’s lemma to a hypercube of hard instances.
It suffices to rule out algorithms that use at most
T
queries:
if such an algorithm halts earlier, pad its transcript with deterministic dummy queries
\mathbf{a}_{t}=\mathbf{0}
, which return the uninformative bit
b_{t}=1
and do not change its output,
so that every transcript has the fixed length
T
the divergence accounting below requires.
A dummy round has bias
1-\mathbf{0}^{\top}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})=1
under every hypothesis
\mathbf{p}
,
so it contributes
\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Ber}(1)\|\mathrm{Ber}(1))=0
to the divergence sum below; padding is thus free.
This also disposes of the boundary value
1
, reached only by such dummy rounds: there the two hypotheses coincide,
so the contribution vanishes rather than blowing up, while the informative rounds
\mathbf{a}_{t}\neq\mathbf{0}
stay off the boundary through the variance floor below.
For
v\in\{-1,+1\}^{M}
set
\mathbf{p}^{(v)}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}+2\alpha\,v\in[\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha,\,\tfrac{1}{2}+2\alpha]^{M}
.
The two hypotheses for each coordinate,
\tfrac{1}{2}\pm 2\alpha
, are
4\alpha
apart, so any
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
with
\norm{\hat{\vb{p}} - \vb{p}^{(v)}}_{\infty}\leq\alpha
has
\operatorname{sgn}(\hat{p}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2})=v_{i}
for all
i
;
an
\alpha
-accurate estimator thus recovers
v
exactly on success,
so
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{v},v)=0
on success and at most
M
always.
Therefore, success probability
\geq 2/3
forces the average Hamming risk

2^{-M}\sum_{v}\mathbb{E}\,d_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{v},v)\leq M\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}=M/3.

(106)

Let
\mathbb{P}_{v}
be the distribution of the transcript
(\mathbf{a}_{t},b_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
under
\mathbf{p}^{(v)}
, and let
v^{\oplus i}
flip coordinate
i
.
Write
s_{t}^{(v)}\coloneqq 1-\mathbf{a}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p}^{(v)})
for the bias of query
t
under hypothesis
v
,
and abbreviate the per-query, per-coordinate Kullback–Leibler divergence (relative entropy) by
D_{t,i}\coloneqq\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathrm{Ber}(s_{t}^{(v)})\,\|\,\mathrm{Ber}(s_{t}^{(v^{\oplus i})})\big)
, where
\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)\coloneqq\sum_{\omega}P(\omega)\log\tfrac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)}
.

Because each query may depend on past outcomes, the transcript distributions are not products;
still, the KL chain rule applies round by round.
The query policy is identical under
\mathbf{p}^{(v)}
and
\mathbf{p}^{(v^{\oplus i})}
(the algorithm never sees
v
),
so only the outcome bit of each round contributes, and for every
fixed
coordinate
i
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{v}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{v}}\!\left[D_{t,i}\right].

(107)

The quantity entering Assouad’s bound below is the
total
divergence
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}})
over all
M
coordinate flips, which we bound by first controlling the coordinate sum
\sum_{i=1}^{M}D_{t,i}
for one fixed query
t
and then summing over the
T
queries.
The two biases differ only through coordinate
i
, by
\absolutevalue{s_t^{(v)} - s_t^{(v^{\oplus i})}}=4\alpha\,a_{t,i}
.
The denominator of the Pinsker/KL bound below is the Bernoulli variance
s(1-s)
, which the window
\alpha<1/8
keeps bounded away from
0
.
For any
\mathbf{p}\in[\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha,\,\tfrac{1}{2}+2\alpha]^{M}
the bias
s=1-\mathbf{a}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})
satisfies
1-s=\mathbf{a}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\geq(\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha)\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}
and
s\geq\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{4}
, so that

s(1-s)\geq c_{1}\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1},\qquad c_{1}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{4}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}-2\alpha\big)=\Theta(1).

(108)

This holds for both
s_{t}^{(v)}
and
s_{t}^{(v^{\oplus i})}
, as
\mathbf{p}^{(v)},\mathbf{p}^{(v^{\oplus i})}
lie in the window.
Each Bernoulli relative entropy is bounded by the corresponding chi-square divergence,

\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Ber}(s)\|\mathrm{Ber}(s^{\prime}))\leq\chi^{2}(\mathrm{Ber}(s)\|\mathrm{Ber}(s^{\prime}))=(s-s^{\prime})^{2}/[s^{\prime}(1-s^{\prime})]

(
Tsybakov 2008
, Lemma 2.7)
,
the squared bias gap over the Bernoulli variance.
Inserting the bias gap
4\alpha\,a_{t,i}
and the variance floor
Eq.

108
(queries with
\mathbf{a}_{t}=\mathbf{0}
are deterministic and contribute nothing),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}D_{t,i}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\big(s_{t}^{(v)}-s_{t}^{(v^{\oplus i})}\big)^{2}}{s_{t}^{(v^{\oplus i})}\big(1-s_{t}^{(v^{\oplus i})}\big)}

(
\mathrm{KL}\leq\chi^{2}
)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{(4\alpha\,a_{t,i})^{2}}{c_{1}\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}}

(variance
\geq c_{1}\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}
)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{c_{1}}\,\frac{\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{2}^{2}}{\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}}\;\leq\;\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{c_{1}}.

(109)

where the last step uses
\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{2}^{2}\leq\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{\infty}\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}\leq\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{1}
and
\norm{\vb{a}_t}_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Summing the chain rule
Eq.

107
over the
M
coordinates, exchanging the finite

- and

-sums, and inserting the per-query bound
(which holds for every realization of the adaptive query
\mathbf{a}_{t}
, so its constant right-hand side

bounds the expectation

by monotonicity),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{v}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{v}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}D_{t,i}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{c_{1}}=\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{c_{1}}\,T,

(110)

valid for every
v
.

By
Lemma

C.5
, applied to the transcript distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{v}\}
with half-cube mixtures
\mathbb{P}_{\pm i}\coloneqq 2^{-(M-1)}\sum_{v:\,v_{i}=\pm 1}\mathbb{P}_{v}
, each averaging over the
2^{M-1}
hypotheses that fix the sign
v_{i}=\pm 1

(in contrast to the single-flip pair
\mathbb{P}_{v},\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}}
controlled in the divergence bound above),
the average Hamming risk is at least
\tfrac{M}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})
.
Convexity of total variation links the two in the first step below,
where each mixture-pair distance
\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})
is bounded by the averaged single-flip distance
\mathbb{E}_{v}\,\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{v},\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}})
.
Success forces this risk to be at most
M/3
, so the total-variation sum must be large.
We now upper-bound it in terms of the query budget
T
,
via convexity of total variation, Pinsker’s inequality
\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)}
, and Cauchy–Schwarz over the
M
coordinates:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{v}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{TV}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{v},\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}}\right)

(convexity of
\mathrm{TV}
)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{v}\sqrt{\tfrac{M}{2}\textstyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}})}

(Pinsker
+
Cauchy–Schwarz)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sqrt{8/c_{1}}\,\alpha\sqrt{MT}.

(
\textstyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{KL}\leq 16\alpha^{2}T/c_{1}
)

Here Pinsker is applied per coordinate and Cauchy–Schwarz collapses the
M
resulting square roots into a single factor
\sqrt{M}
.
This term-count
\sqrt{M}
is distinct from the
M
-independent budget bound
\sum_{i}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{v^{\oplus i}})\leq 16\alpha^{2}T/c_{1}
, so the
M
on the left of the final squeeze (from the Hamming risk) and the
\sqrt{M}
on the right (from Cauchy–Schwarz) have different origins and do not double-count.
Combining this total-variation bound with the success cap
M/3
on the average Hamming risk established above and the Assouad lower bound
Lemma

C.5
gives

\displaystyle\frac{M}{6}\;=\;\frac{M}{2}-\frac{M}{3}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{+i},\mathbb{P}_{-i})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8/c_{1}}\,\alpha\sqrt{MT}

(111)

\displaystyle\;\Longrightarrow\;T\geq\frac{c_{1}M}{72\,\alpha^{2}}=\Omega(M/\alpha^{2}).

(112)

∎

To turn this information-theoretic bound into a physical one, we exhibit a local Hamiltonian in which every prepare-and-measure round
is
the single-bit mixture oracle of
Lemma

C.6
: the Feynman–Kitaev clock, evolved for a time
t
, spreads the measured projector across the
M
output qubits with clock-weight coefficients
w_{q}(t)
, so each snapshot returns one Bernoulli bit whose bias is a weighted average of the
M
unknown projector marginals. Estimating all
M
snapshots to precision
\epsilon
therefore forces estimating these
M
marginals, and the
\Omega(M/\alpha^{2})
cost of
Lemma

C.6
becomes the claimed
\Omega(M/\epsilon^{2})
rounds.

Theorem C.2

(Classical
\Omega(M/\epsilon^{2})
lower bound for local
Problem B.2

(Hamiltonian snapshots estimation)
.
)
.

Let
M
be a positive integer and
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
for a sufficiently small absolute constant
\epsilon_{0}>0
.
There exist an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-local Hamiltonian
H
on

qubits, a single
1
-local projector

, and
M

Definition

C.1
) estimating the snapshot expectations
\bra{\psi_0}e^{\textup{i}Ht_{m}}\,P\,e^{-\textup{i}Ht_{m}}\ket{\psi_0}
at all

\epsilon

2/3

\Omega(M/\epsilon^{2})
rounds, in the worst case over the oracle-prepared initial state
\ket{\psi_0}
.

Proof.

We reuse the Feynman–Kitaev instance of
Theorem

C.1
:
the
\mathcal{O}(1)
-local Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{FK}}
on
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
qubits
(the same Feynman–Kitaev clock Hamiltonian, built from the cyclic-shift SWAP circuit
V=\Pi^{M}
of
N=M^{2}
nearest-neighbour gates), projector
P=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathrm{\bf o},1}
,
time points
\{t_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, clock weights
w_{q}(t)
,
and
M\times M
weight matrix
W_{mq}=w_{q}(t_{m})
with
\gamma\coloneqq\norm{W^{-1}}_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
the
\ell_{\infty}
error-amplification incurred when inverting

to recover the projector marginals (
Eq.

102
).
Set
\epsilon_{0}=1/(8\gamma)
,
so
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
ensures
\gamma\epsilon<1/8
, the admissible window of
Lemma

C.6
.
The power-of-two hypothesis of
Theorem

C.1
is not needed here: it stems from the Apeldoorn–Huggins reduction in that proof, whereas the Feynman–Kitaev construction and the diagonal dominance of
W
(

103
) hold for every positive integer
M
.
The statistical hardness resides entirely in the family of initial states
\{\ket{\psi'_v}\}
defined next, which carries that lemma’s near-
\tfrac{1}{2}
hypercube.

For
v\in\{-1,+1\}^{M}
,
let
\ket{\psi'_v}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}\ket{\phi_i}
be the product state on output qubits
2,\ldots,M+1
with marginals

,
and put
\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\bf c}}\otimes\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\bf o},1}\otimes\ket{\psi'_v}
;
the oracle
U_{\psi}
is built from
U_{\psi^{\prime}_{v}}
with
\mathcal{O}(1)
fixed-state preparations.
By the snapshot decomposition
Eq.

101
(with
p_{1}=1
),
a PM round that evolves to time
t
and measures the projector

returns one bit

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ber}\!\Big(\absolutevalue{c_0(t)}^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{q=0}^{M-1}w_{q}(t)\,p_{q+2}\Big)=\mathrm{Ber}\big(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{p}+(1-\norm{\vb{a}}_{1})\big),\qquad\mathbf{a}=\big(w_{q}(t)\big)_{q=0}^{M-1},

(113)

where
\mathbf{p}=(p_{2},\ldots,p_{M+1})\in[\tfrac{1}{2}-2\gamma\epsilon,\,\tfrac{1}{2}+2\gamma\epsilon]^{M}
and
\norm{\vb{a}}_{1}=\sum_{q}w_{q}(t)=1-\absolutevalue{c_0(t)}^{2}
.
Each PM round is therefore a single-bit-oracle query of
Lemma

C.6
for
\mathbf{p}
, the choice of evolution time

.
These realizable weights form a sub-family of the lemma’s admissible vectors, but its lower bound transfers verbatim:

\epsilon

(

By

∎

The classical lower bound counts the same resource as the quantum upper bound: calls to

, i.e. PM rounds.
Because the PM class is a strict subclass of the coherent quantum algorithms, the universal quantum bound
\Omega(\sqrt{M}/\epsilon)
of

C.1

In that regime the classical

On this single

-local instance, then, classical prepare-and-measure algorithms require

B.2

The quantum guarantee of

By Chebyshev’s inequality it delivers additive error at most
