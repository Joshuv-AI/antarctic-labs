<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., October 21, 2022</strong> - President John F. Kennedy made unilateral decisions to blockade Cuba and approve other military moves, but winning the support of European allies remained central to U.S. policy during the Cuban Missile Crisis, according to declassified records of briefings prepared for NATO members shortly before Kennedy announced the U.S. discovery of the Soviet missiles.</p>

<p>The first to learn of U.S. plans was British Ambassador David Ormsby-Gore, a close friend of the President’s. According to a declassified telegram found in the British archives, during lunch on October 21, Kennedy told Ormsby-Gore of the missiles and the two choices that he saw: a blockade or an air strike. Asked which option was best, Ormsby-Gore said a blockade, because an air strike would damage the U.S. politically. Kennedy said that was the choice he had made. He told the ambassador that his emphasis was on negotiating the missiles out of Cuba and that he did “not expect or intend that the present course of action” would lead to an invasion of Cuba.</p>de. He told the ambassador that his emphasis was on negotiating the missiles out of Cuba and that he did “not expect or intend that the present course of action” would lead to an invasion of Cuba.</p>

<p>Later that day, Kennedy alerted British Prime Minister Harold Macmillan to the discovery of the missiles and the dangers that he saw, but other NATO allies were not informed until the next day before President Kennedy’s televised speech. Kennedy and his advisers believed that sharing with allies the sensitive photographic intelligence that had informed U.S. decisions, even on short notice, would help win their support. Nevertheless, NATO allies resented the last-minute notification and even more so that they were not consulted about U.S. moves in advance. They supported U.S. actions, but some discontent lay beneath the surface.</p>

<p>&nbsp;</p>

<h3>Highlights</h3>

<p>This compilation consists of the available declassified U.S. records of the briefings given to NATO allies on October 22, 1962, the day of President Kennedy’s first public speech on the crisis. White House reports discuss the briefings of French President Charles De Gaulle, British Prime Minister Macmillan, West German Chancellor Konrad Adenauer, Canadian Prime Minister John Diefenbaker, and top NATO representatives. Many of the documents focus on the early communications with the British, the NATO ally with the closest relationship to the United States. One is the record of a lengthy Macmillan-Kennedy telephone conversation the evening of Kennedy’s speech on October 22,&nbsp; 1962. Others concern NATO reactions to U.S. policy, including unease about the late notice. Among the documents are oral histories by former Secretary of State Dean Acheson, who briefed De Gaulle and NATO, and CIA briefer Chester Cooper, who briefed top British officials.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p>oral histories by former Secretary of State Dean Acheson, who briefed De Gaulle and NATO, and CIA briefer Chester Cooper, who briefed top British officials.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p>

<p>Reports from Cooper and another CIA briefer, Sherman Kent, detail the meetings with De Gaulle and Macmillan, including the latter’s doubts about the U.S. blockade plan. During Cooper’s briefing for British Labour leaders, Hugh Gaitskell said Macmillan felt “hurt” by the lack of consultations. Belgian Foreign Minister Paul-Henry Spaak was “understanding” about why Washington had acted before consulting with allies, but “would frankly have preferred U.S. to inform NATO allies 24 hours in advance of our action,” especially because “nobody could have valid objection” to what the U.S. had done.</p>

<p>During Adenauer's briefing by the CIA's R. Jack Smith, he showed his familiarity with the Agency's use of cover names when he asked: “Are you sure your name is Smith? Perhaps you have two names.”</p>

<p>The briefing of Canadian Prime Minister John Diefenbaker was more difficult than the others. The Diefenbaker-Kennedy relationship was already bad, and the Prime Minister showed an attitude of “skepticism bordering on antagonism.” But the U.S. record of the meeting indicates that, as the briefing proceeded, Diefenbaker took on a “more considered, friendly, and cooperative manner.”</p>

<p>The decision by the U.S. Embassy in London to release intelligence photos of Soviet missile deployments to the British press irritated the White House and led the Embassy to send a telegram explaining that it took the extraordinary step in an effort to win over the skeptical British media.</p>ployments to the British press irritated the White House and led the Embassy to send a telegram explaining that it took the extraordinary step in an effort to win over the skeptical British media.</p>

<p>One British intelligence official who was in Washington when the crisis broke out was Joint Intelligence Bureau director Sir Kenneth Strong, who summarized his experience in a memorandum for Macmillan. From unnamed U.S. officials, Strong learned about the missile deployments in Cuba and a U.S. plan for a preemptive bomber strike against Soviet ICBMs if Moscow moved against West Berlin<span style="font-size:11.0pt"><span style="line-height:107%"><span style="font-family:&quot;Calibri&quot;,sans-serif">—</span></span></span>a worst-case scenario that Kennedy said would be “the final failure” of decision-makers at the White House and the Kremlin.</p>

<p>&nbsp;</p>

<h3>Context: Briefing Allies</h3>

<p>President Kennedy and his ExComm advisers believed that the importance of the U.S.-West European security relationship meant that U.S. policy had to be carefully calibrated to avoid jeopardizing NATO. The high value attached to alliance unity meant that Kennedy and his advisers agreed early during the crisis that NATO allies had to receive advance notice of the missile deployment and the U.S. decision for a blockade.</p>to alliance unity meant that Kennedy and his advisers agreed early during the crisis that NATO allies had to receive advance notice of the missile deployment and the U.S. decision for a blockade.</p>

<p>A week before Kennedy made his blockade announcement, while the possibility of an air strike was under debate, he declared that the U.S. had to <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d18">“alert,”</a> even “consult,” NATO allies on whatever decision was made. In general, Kennedy believed that allies such as De Gaulle and Macmillan had a “right to some warning,” implicitly because of the danger of the situation. Secretary of State Dean Rusk thought along the same lines: The allies would be nervous about being exposed to “all these great dangers without … without the slightest consultation, or warning, or preparation.” A few days later, Rusk observed that “an unannounced, unconsulted, quick action on our part” would lead to disunity in NATO that “the Soviets could … capitalize upon very strongly.”<a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2">[2]</a></p>

<p>Consultations were mentioned in the early days, but Kennedy ruled them out by observing that the British would “just object to the idea of a military response.” That raised the general problem of consulting with allies during a crisis: Kennedy wanted to avoid the prolonged debates that heightened the risk of leaks to Moscow or that could delay action until after the missiles in Cuba became operational. Yet, as averse as Kennedy was to consultation, he believed that the British had to be told, at least “the night before.”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p>issiles in Cuba became operational. Yet, as averse as Kennedy was to consultation, he believed that the British had to be told, at least “the night before.”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p>

<p>Kennedy and advisers like Ambassador Charles <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d23">Bohlen</a> took it for granted that any decisions made would have to be justifiable. The Allies had little interest in Cuba but worried about threats to West Berlin and to European security. Thus, if war broke out, how could the U.S. explain to allies why it had opted for an air strike or an invasion instead of less violent means or negotiations? On October 18, during an ExComm meeting, Kennedy displayed objectivity about the perceptions of others by observing that U.S. allies see Cuba “as a fixation of the United States and not a serious military threat.” They “think that … we’re slightly … demented on [Cuba],” so if the U.S. invaded or launched an air strike, “no matter how good our films are ... a lot of people would regard this as a <em>mad </em>act by the United States.”<a href="#_edn4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a></p>

<p>After Kennedy decided on the blockade and its date, plans for advance warning to NATO allies and others went ahead. On October 21, Under Secretary of State George <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d38">Ball</a> announced the plan (which was already underway): Ambassador Walter Dowling would meet with Adenauer; Ambassador David K.E. Bruce would speak with Macmillan; and Dean Acheson would brief De Gaulle and the North Atlantic Council (the new U.S. Ambassador to France, Charles E. Bohlen, was in transit, by ocean liner, on the Atlantic). Not mentioned was Diefenbaker, who would also be briefed. “Present at [the] briefings would be technical experts from CIA who could answer questions concerning the photographic intelligence which reveals the missile sites.”</p>er, who would also be briefed. “Present at [the] briefings would be technical experts from CIA who could answer questions concerning the photographic intelligence which reveals the missile sites.”</p>

<p>According to the memoirs of CIA briefer Cooper, late in the afternoon of October 20, Acheson, Dowling (who was then in Washington), Cooper, and two other CIA officials—Board of National Estimates chair Sherman <a href="https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/books-monographs/sherman-kent-and-the-board-of-national-estimates-collected-essays-2/">Kent</a> and Deputy Director of Current Intelligence R. Jack Smith—gathered at the State Department, where they received instructions on their assignments. Flying on a presidential aircraft, the group left the next morning, arriving at midnight at the British air force base at Greenham Common. Ambassador Bruce (armed with a handgun for security) met the arriving group and drove Cooper to the Embassy in London, while Acheson, Kent, Dowling, and Smith flew on to Paris and to Bonn. To brief the Canadian prime minister, former ambassador Livingston Merchant and CIA officer William Tidwell flew to Ottawa. <p>The British would receive the earliest warning, although that was not part of the State Department plan. The next day, on October 21, President Kennedy had a secret lunchtime meeting with his close friend, British Ambassador <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2021/07/05/jfk-cuban-missile-crisis-friend-497878">Ormsby-Gore</a>, to whom he gave a detailed briefing. <p>The meeting with heads of state in France, the United Kingdom, and West Germany, along with NATO officials, accomplished their basic purpose. Adenauer, De Gaulle, and Macmillan expressed support for U.S. plans and were fascinated by the photographic intelligence. The briefing to NATO received a generally positive reception. The briefing for Diefenbaker was more difficult because he was initially critical and argumentative. But the Canadian prime minister eventually came around, and the Canadian government proved highly supportive.&nbsp;Cooper's expedited release of selected photographs, which soon appeared in the British and French media, helped clinch the case before the European public.</p>overnment proved highly supportive.&nbsp;Cooper's expedited release of selected photographs, which soon appeared in the British and French media, helped clinch the case before the European public.</p>

<p>Cooper and other official observers quickly discerned underlying dissatisfaction among NATO partners. While the legitimacy of the U.S. response was not challenged, its implications for European security, should the situation escalate, troubled the allies, who believed that they should have been consulted in advance or at least been given earlier notice of U.S. decisions. British Labour leaders told Cooper that Macmillan was upset that Kennedy had not consulted him earlier, although the two would have significant exchanges as the crisis developed. Belgian Foreign Minister Paul-Henri Spaak told the U.S. ambassador that NATO should have been given 24 hours’ notice, while De Gaulle was said to have felt some “pique” about the lack of consultation. An intelligence survey indicated that those concerns were shared in NATO Europe, with “some Italians” being “extremely annoyed.” Kennedy’s decision to negotiate a secret swap of Jupiter missiles in Turkey for Soviet missiles in Cuba might have been controversial in NATO circles if it had become known, but the deal remained secret for years.</p>n to negotiate a secret swap of Jupiter missiles in Turkey for Soviet missiles in Cuba might have been controversial in NATO circles if it had become known, but the deal remained secret for years.</p>

<p>NATO allies did not make a fuss about crisis consultations, because that could have strained trans-Atlantic connections and allowed the Soviets to exploit the situation, which Rusk had feared. Thus, European reliance on the U.S. for security gave Washington freedom of action, although Kennedy’s recognition of the importance of allied opinion influenced the choices that he made. Some have argued that Kennedy’s avoidance of consultations in the early stages of the crisis had a “poisonous effect” on U.S.-European relations. If so, the poison did not kill the alliance but may have contributed to its weakening. While De Gaulle was already abandoning his proposal for a formal US-UK-French tripartite system of policy coordination, the Cuban Missile Crisis likely strengthened his determination to chart France’s own course outside of the NATO military structure.<a href="#_edn6" name="_ednref6">[6]</a></p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Soon after President Kennedy delivered his televised speech, he had a lengthy telephone conversation with Macmillan, which partly amounted to a further briefing on U.S. policy. After Kennedy stated the policy dilemma presented by Soviet action, Macmillan took a tough position, asking whether the U.S. was going to invade Cuba or “is this going to just drag on?” Kennedy explained that he was taking actions that could prevent early “escalation to war” with the blockade focusing on “offensive weapons of war.” Asked by Macmillan what he thought could happen, Kennedy replied that negotiations over the missiles “should be the object of our policy,” although the U.S. was preparing for an invasion and recognized the possibility of a Berlin crisis.</p>

<p>Informing Macmillan that he had just written to Khrushchev about what the U.S. was going to do, Kennedy emphasized that “our action is moderated by the realisation that we could move very quickly into a World War over this or to a nuclear war or to lose Berlin.” Particularly worrisome to Macmillan was that if the U.S. was forced into a “conference” with Moscow as a “last resort,” Khrushchev would have “all the cards.” When Macmillan suggested that the threat of an invasion was “more cards in our hand,” Kennedy observed that if that course appeared to be “wise,” the U.S. could implement the plan at the end of the week. Macmillan asked that Kennedy stay in touch with him and Ormsby-Gore.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Ambassador Ormsby-Gore follows up his earlier message on President Kennedy’s thinking with additional details which “he had previously hesitated to report because in some instances they were so frank.” Among them are that Kennedy had no intention to invade Cuba, that the crisis “will be resolved through negotiation and discussion,” and that one possibility would be the “reciprocal closing of military bases” involving the U.S. missile base in Turkey, which were “more or less worthless.” Thus, Kennedy foreshadowed the secret deal negotiated a week later. Ormsby-Gore writes that he had previously “hesitated to report these remarks because in some instances they were so frank that he would not repeat them to any member of his Administration except his brother Bobby.” Kennedy made some remarks on the Berlin situation “which I could not possibly put on the record,” according to Ormsby-Gore, but which Macmillan would find reassuring. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>During an ExComm meeting President Kennedy had expressed irritation about the British media getting the photos before they were released in the U.S.<a href="#_edn13" name="_ednref13">[13]</a> NSC staffer Michael Forrestal phoned Chester Cooper and sent the Embassy a <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/20813-09">message</a>&nbsp;to convey White House annoyance. This message, probably written by Cooper, gives a truncated explanation of the Embassy’s actions. Concerned by the skeptical British press reaction to Kennedy’s speech, Cooper and the U.S. Embassy sought and obtained the permission of CIA Deputy Director for Intelligence Ray Cline to display the photos in a televised press conference. <p>There was more to the story than what is described in this message, however, which may have elided several developments. Having received no reply, Cooper released the sanitized photos to the press. In the middle of the night, Ray Cline called and complained, and later Cooper received angry phone calls from the White House and the Pentagon. As noted, the release of the photos had a positive impact on British opinion, and Cooper heard little more about his initiative.<a href="#_edn14" name="_ednref14">[14]</a></p>

<p>Probably the most significant point of Strong’s briefing to Macmillan was his understanding that, if the Soviets moved against Berlin as a reaction to the blockade, the Americans “were prepared to go it alone either without consulting their allies or irrespective of what their allies said.” The U.S. government was prepared to “escalate into the nuclear” through a preemptive bomber strike against Soviet ICBM bases. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Sherman Kent, as Chair of the CIA’s Board of National Estimates, had wrongly estimated that the Soviets would not ship missiles to Cuba, but his senior role at the Agency made him a natural choice for providing intelligence briefings to both De Gaulle and the NATO Council. Soon after his mission, Kent prepared this detailed account of the briefings, which also included presentations to senior French intelligence officials and U.S. officials. According to Kent, De Gaulle paid close attention to the explanation of the photographs, using reading glasses to look at them closely.</p>

As Kent found out the next day, the U.S. and British press had received access to photos, and, by the morning of October 24, a few French newspapers were publishing them as well. <p>Kent also made efforts to win over skeptical journalists. Andre Fontaine, a lead columnist for <em>Le Monde</em>, wrote that “the American intelligence services sometimes make mistakes.” Kent, under the “pseudo of a Department of Defense civilian temporarily in Paris,” had a chance to give Fountain a private briefing. By then the latter needed less convincing because of Fidel Castro’s public complaints about U.S. violations of Cuban air space: “This is good enough for me to believe that you have been overflying Cuba and photographing from the air.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>One briefing that did not go smoothly was that of Canadian Prime Minister John Diefenbaker, but that was no surprise given the personal and political tensions in the Kennedy-Diefenbaker relationship. A few hours before Kennedy’s speech, Diefenbaker met with recently retired U.S. Ambassador Livingston Merchant, CIA officer William Tidwell (who had worked in the Miami station) and Station Chief Rolfe Kingsley. At Diefenbaker’s insistence, Merchant read aloud the text of Kennedy’s speech, with the Prime Minister interrupting to ask why the matter had not gone to the U.N. Security Council first. Merchant replied that “time was of the essence as the photographic evidence would show.” Reacting critically, Diefenbaker was worried that the U.S. position was “provocative” and that a blockade was “dangerous and a threat to allies of the United States.” During the intelligence briefing, he “scrutinized” the photos and asked questions about them, also asking about the earliest “ground report” of the missiles, which led to explanation of “various refugee and agents’ reports.” As Diefenbaker received the briefing, Merchant described his attitude as changing from “skepticism bordering on antagonism” to a “more considered, friendly, and cooperative manner.” The next day, the Canadian Air Force raised alert levels of air defense forces in response to a U.S. request, and, after a gaffe in his parliamentary remarks, Diefenbaker became vocally supportive of U.S. policy.<a href="#_edn17" name="_ednref17">[17]</a></p>air defense forces in response to a U.S. request, and, after a gaffe in his parliamentary remarks, Diefenbaker became vocally supportive of U.S. policy.<a href="#_edn17" name="_ednref17">[17]</a></p>

              <div class="field__item"><p>Excerpt from the <a href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78t03194a000300010013-8">issue</a> on CIA FOIA website</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Kent’s account, prepared ten years later, tells the story of the briefings to NATO allies in large part through the reports that he and Cooper prepared. While there are several other versions of his article online, this appears to be the most complete.</p>

<p>Kent closes his account with an extended discussion on the “Credibility of Photographic Evidence,” noting that the photo intelligence analysts had excellent reproductions, that, when used with the latest technology, including multi-power stereoscopic viewers, made it possible to look at images in three dimensions and measure the objects in feet and fractions of feet. The work experience of the analysts enabled them to determine exactly what they were seeing and to provide answers to those senior officials who wondered whether those really were Soviet ballistic missiles. None of that could be conveyed by the “appallingly deficient copies” that appeared in newspapers. Nevertheless, from the perspective of top officials, the “credibility” of the photographs had been established, and they supported release of the information to the public. Referring to the influence of the photo interpreters and a few top officials, Kent concluded that “never have so many taken so much on the say-so of so few.”</p>

              <div class="field__item"><h2 class="block-title">“Slightly Demented”</h2>

<div style=" padding:8px; margin:0px; left;  background-color: #F1F3F4">
<p>During an ExComm meeting on October 18, President Kennedy displayed notable objectivity about how allies perceived U.S. policy, remarking that they saw the U.S. government as having a “slightly demented” fixation with Cuba. Kennedy made this observation after Secretary of Defense McNamara raised the possibility of invading Cuba and launching airstrikes against the missile sites on the assumption that the Soviets did not yet have operational nuclear weapons there.</p>f Defense McNamara raised the possibility of invading Cuba and launching airstrikes against the missile sites on the assumption that the Soviets did not yet have operational nuclear weapons there.</p>

<p>The direction of the discussion changed when President Kennedy made the point that the Soviets would not use nuclear weapons in Cuba except in a general war situation. McNamara then raised the possibility of unsanctioned use of the weapons in Cuba and the broader problem of the significance of the Soviet deployments.</p>
</div>

<p>&nbsp;</p>

<p><em><strong>Kennedy</strong>: … I would think you’d have to go on the assumption that [the Soviets are] not gonna permit nuclear weapons to be used against the United States from Cuba unless they’re gonna be using them from everyplace.</em></p>

<p><em><strong>McNamara:</strong> Well they could … I’m not sure they can stop it. This is why I emphasized the point I did. I don’t believe the Soviets would authorize their use against the U.S., but they might nonetheless be used. And, therefore, I underline this assumption, that all of these cases [for attacks] are premised on the assumption there are no operational nuclear weapons there. If there’s any possibility of that I would strongly recommend that these plans be modified substantially.</em></p>tacks] are premised on the assumption there are no operational nuclear weapons there. If there’s any possibility of that I would strongly recommend that these plans be modified substantially.</em></p>

<p><em>Now I would go back just one second. I evaded the question Secretary Rusk asked me, and I evaded it because I wanted this information discussed first. The question he asked me was: How does—in effect—how does the introduction of these weapons to Cuba change the military equation, the military position of the U.S. versus the U.S.S.R.?</em></p>

<p><em>And, speaking strictly in military terms, really in terms of weapons, it doesn’t change it at all, in my personal opinion. My personal views are not shared by the Chiefs. They are not shared by many others in the department. However, I feel very strongly on this point and I think I could argue a case, a strong case, in defense of my position.</em></p>

<p><em>This doesn’t really have any bearing on the issue, in my opinion, because it’s not a military problem that we’re facing. It’s a political problem. It’s a problem of holding the alliance together. It’s a problem of … conditioning Khrushchev for our future moves, the problem of dealing with our domestic public, all requires action that, in my opinion, the shift in the military balance does not require.</em></p>

<p><em><strong>Kennedy</strong>: On holding the alliance. Which is going to strain the alliance more: This attack by us on Cuba, which most allies regard as a fixation of the United States and not a serious military threat? I mean you’d have to ... an awful lot of conditioning would have to go in before they would accept, support our action against Cuba, because they think that we’re … we’re slightly demen … demented on this subject.</em></p>.. an awful lot of conditioning would have to go in before they would accept, support our action against Cuba, because they think that we’re … we’re slightly demen … demented on this subject.</em></p>

<p><em>So there isn’t any doubt that whatever action we take against Cuba, no matter how good our films are, are going to cause [problems] in Latin America. ... [A] lot of people would regard this as a mad act by the United States, which is due to a loss of nerve because they will argue that, taken at its worst, the presence of these missiles really doesn’t change [the nuclear balance of power].</em></p>
