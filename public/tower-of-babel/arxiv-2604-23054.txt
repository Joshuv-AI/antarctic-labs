DeepImagine: Learning Biomedical Reasoning via Successive Counterfactual Imagining
Youze Zheng, Jianyou Wang, Yuhan Chen, Matthew Feng, Longtian Bao, Hanyuan Zhang, Maxim Khan, Aditya K. Sehgal, Christopher D. Rosin, Umber Dube, Ramamohan Paturi

Abstract
Predicting the outcomes of prospective clinical trials remains a major challenge for large language models (LLMs). Prior work has shown that both traditional correlational predictors, such as random forests and logistic regression, and strong commercial LLMs achieve limited performance on this task. In this paper, we propose DeepImagine , a framework for teaching LLMs biomedical reasoning through successive counterfactual imagining. The central idea is to approximate hidden causal mechanisms of clinical trials by training models to infer how observed trial results would change under controlled perturbations of experimental conditions, such as dosage, outcome measures, study arms, geography, and other trial attributes. To support this objective, we construct both natural and approximate counterfactual pairs from real clinical trials with reported outcomes. For settings where strict counterfactual supervision is available, such as paired outcome measures or dose-ranging study arms within the same trial, we train models with supervised fine-tuning. For broader settings where only approximate counterfactual pairs can be retrieved, we optimize models with reinforcement learning using verifiable rewards based on downstream benchmark correctness. We further augment training with synthetic reasoning traces that provide causally plausible explanations for local counterfactual transitions. Using this pipeline, we train language models under 10B parameters, including Qwen3.5-9B, and evaluate them on clinical trial outcome prediction. We aim to show that DeepImagine consistently improves over untuned language models and traditional correlational baselines. Finally, we aim to show that the learned reasoning trajectories provide interpretable signals about how models represent trial-level mechanisms, suggesting a practical path toward more mechanistic and scientifically useful biomedical language models.



1
Introduction

Clinical trial outcomes are influenced by a wide range of factors. Some of these are relatively observable, including enrollment size, trial phase, location, and sponsor characteristics. Larger enrollment can increase statistical power, making it easier to detect statistically significant effects. Phase III trials often face a higher threshold for success, as they represent one of the final stages before a drug can receive FDA approval. Trial location may also matter, since studies conducted in developed countries may benefit from stronger procedural adherence and tighter control over participants than those conducted in developing countries. Sponsor characteristics can further shape outcomes, as larger companies often have greater resources, more experienced research teams, and more mature development pipelines than small or mid-sized firms.

Despite the importance of these factors, it remains difficult to formulate an exact equation that specifies how they interact and jointly determine the outcome of a clinical trial. In addition, many non-clinical external factors may also affect trial results, although such factors are outside the scope of this work. Still, we hypothesize that clinical trial outcomes are ultimately governed by underlying laws and mechanisms, possibly with some degree of randomness, which means that there should exist a hidden set of causal laws and mechanisms that determines how these factors jointly produce observed results. If such laws and mechanisms could be identified, they could enable highly accurate prediction of trial outcomes. In this paper, we refer to this latent set of governing laws and mechanisms as the hidden causal model of clinical trial outcomes.

The central insight of this paper is that LLMs can improve prediction of a prospective clinical trial when the target trial can be linked to many highly similar historical trials with reported outcomes, and when the model is allowed to condition on those prior results. Although the success of a previous trial does not guarantee the success of a new one, we find that trials associated with a larger fraction of successful predecessors tend to have a higher probability of success. Conventional correlational methods would typically encode this signal as a numerical feature, such as the proportion of related prior trials that succeeded. In contrast, we hypothesize that an LLM can use these prior trials more mechanistically, by reasoning through a sequence of counterfactual imaginations. Specifically, beginning with a historical trial whose outcome is known, we change one experimental condition at a time to match the target trial, keeping all remaining conditions fixed, and ask the model to infer how the known outcome would change under that perturbation. By repeating this process over all differing factors, the model effectively traverses a chain of counterfactual states from the historical trial to the target trial, and the final imagined outcome becomes the prediction. See Figure
1
for an example of successive counterfactual imagination.

In this paper, we propose a training method that enables LLMs to gradually internalize these governing mechanisms. Our approach trains models on coupled experiments, where all experimental conditions are kept fixed except for a single factor. For instance, we provide a sequence of experiments with identical patient demographics, intervention drugs, locations, study designs, and outcome measures, while varying only the dosage from 10 mg daily to 100 mg daily. Importantly, each experiment in this sequence is drawn from a real clinical study with reported results. As a result, although the experiments function as counterfactual variants of one another for training purposes, they are all grounded in real-world outcomes. We train the model via supervised fine-tuning using these data.

For many variables, corresponding counterfactuals are unavailable. In this work, we use the term counterfactual to refer to a pair of clinical trial experiments in which only one condition differs while all other conditions are held fixed. In practice, this type of strict counterfactual is rarely available, because it would require a real clinical trial to have been conducted under an alternative version of the same experimental setup, with every aspect unchanged except for the single variable of interest. Consequently, although such counterfactual settings can be easily imagined, their true outcomes are usually unobservable. To address this limitation, we develop an information retrieval pipeline that links clinical trials with highly similar experimental settings, enabling the construction of approximate counterfactual pairs grounded in real reported outcomes. Empirically, we find that these pairings often differ in only a small number of variables. For example, a company may conduct a successful Phase II trial and subsequently launch a Phase III trial for the same drug. Although both trials involve the same intervention, they may differ in success criteria, such as outcome measures, and the Phase III trial may enroll a larger and more heterogeneous patient population. Using such pairings, we train the model to discover its own reasoning traces, rewarding it solely for its ability to predict the outcome of one trial from the other to a meaningful degree.

Taken together, these ideas suggest a different role for language models in clinical trial prediction: rather than acting purely as correlational predictors, they can be trained to approximate the latent causal mechanisms that govern how trial outcomes change under controlled perturbations of experimental conditions. Building on this view, we introduce DeepImagine, a framework that combines natural and approximate counterfactual trial pairings, synthetic causal reasoning traces, and reinforcement learning over verifiable outcomes. Through this combination, we aim to teach LLMs to perform successive counterfactual imagination as a practical approximation to biomedical causal reasoning. Empirically, we show that this approach improves prospective clinical trial outcome prediction over both traditional correlational baselines and untuned language models, while also producing reasoning traces that can be analyzed to recover interpretable structured causal models.

Preliminary

3.1
Counterfactual Imagining in Causal Inference

In causal inference, counterfactual reasoning asks what would have happened to an outcome had some aspect of the world been different, while all other causally relevant mechanisms remained governed by the same underlying system. In the structural causal model (SCM) framework of Pearl, a causal model is typically written as

M=(U,V,F,P(U)),

where
U
denotes exogenous variables,
V
denotes endogenous variables,
F
is a collection of structural assignments, and
P(U)
specifies the distribution over background factors. An intervention
do(X=x)
replaces the structural equation for
X
with the assignment
X=x
, thereby defining a modified submodel in which the downstream consequences of that intervention can be evaluated. Counterfactual quantities are then written as expressions such as
Y_{x}
, denoting the value that
Y
would attain under the intervention
do(X=x)
, and more generally as conditional probabilities of the form

P(Y_{x}=y\mid e),

where
e
denotes factual evidence about the realized world.

A standard account of counterfactual evaluation in Pearl’s framework proceeds in three steps: abduction, action, and prediction. In the abduction step, the model updates its beliefs about latent background variables using the observed evidence
e
. In the action step, it modifies the model by imposing the hypothetical intervention
do(X=x)
. In the prediction step, it computes the resulting outcome in the modified model. Thus, counterfactual inference is not merely prediction under a new input; rather, it is prediction for the same underlying unit after minimally altering the relevant causal mechanism specified by the antecedent. This distinction is important because counterfactuals inherit information from the factual world through abduction before the hypothetical change is applied.

Motivated by this tradition, we use the term counterfactual imagining to denote a model-side approximation to counterfactual inference: starting from an observed clinical trial result, the model imagines how that result would change if one trial condition were perturbed while all other relevant conditions were held fixed. Formally, if
x^{(0)}
denotes a source trial configuration with observed result
R^{(0)}
, and
x^{(T)}
denotes a target configuration, then a successive counterfactual imagination procedure constructs a path

x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(T)},

where each consecutive pair differs in exactly one experimental condition. The model then generates a corresponding sequence

R^{(1)},R^{(2)},\dots,R^{(T)},

with each
R^{(t)}
interpreted as the imagined result obtained after applying a local perturbation from
x^{(t-1)}
to
x^{(t)}
. In this sense, our formulation can be viewed as a sequential approximation to a complex counterfactual query: instead of directly estimating the target result from scratch, the model traverses a chain of local “what-if” transformations from the observed source trial to the unobserved target trial.

Our use of counterfactual imagining is therefore inspired by, but not identical to, classical SCM-based counterfactual inference. In the ideal SCM formulation, a counterfactual such as

P(R_{x^{\prime}}\mid e)

is defined with respect to a fully specified causal model and evaluated through abduction, action, and prediction. In our setting, the true causal law is unknown and only partial supervision is available. We therefore train a language model
P_{\theta}
to approximate this process through successive local transformations, using observed source results as factual evidence and trial-pair differences as approximate interventions. The goal is not to claim access to the full SCM of clinical trials, but rather to induce a practically useful approximation to counterfactual reasoning that preserves the central Pearlian intuition: infer what would happen under a hypothetical change by anchoring the computation in what is already known about the factual case.

3.2
Clinical Trial Terminology

We first give a quick introduction to terminology used in clinical trial outcome prediction, following the convention of CT Open.

Definition

(Intervention/Treatment)
.

For clinical trials, an intervention, or treatment, refers to the therapeutic regimen being evaluated. This may consist of a single drug, a combination of drugs, a medical procedure, or a combination of drugs and procedures.

Example:
A trial evaluating pembrolizumab alone, or pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy, would treat these regimens as interventions.

Definition

(Study, Treatment, and Comparator Arms)
.

A study arm is a group of participants assigned to a particular condition within a clinical trial. A treatment arm receives the intervention being tested, whereas a comparator arm serves as the control condition, such as a placebo, standard of care, or another treatment.

Example:
In a two-arm trial, one group may receive a new cancer drug, while the comparator arm receives standard chemotherapy.

Definition

(Outcome Measure and Endpoint)
.

An outcome measure, also called an endpoint, is a pre-specified quantity used to evaluate the effect of an intervention in a clinical trial. It defines what will be measured and is part of the trial design, rather than the observed trial result.

Example:
The 5-year overall survival rate is an outcome measure.

Definition

(Result and Outcome)
.

A result, or outcome, is the observed value of an outcome measure after the trial has been conducted. It describes what actually happened in the trial.

Example:
“85% of patients survived for at least 5 years after treatment” is a result, whereas “5-year overall survival rate” is the corresponding outcome measure.

We formally define a unit of results, given a clinical trial
C_{i_{1}}
, one of its proposed outcome measure
O_{j_{1}}
, and a study arm (i.e. a group that receives a specific kind of intervention)
A_{k_{1}}
, the reported results is defined as
R_{C_{i_{1}},O_{j_{1}},A_{k_{1}}}
. Intuitively speaking, our goal is to transform
R_{C_{i_{1}},O_{j_{1}},A_{k_{1}}}
to
R_{C_{i_{2}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k_{2}}}
via our trained language models’ successive counterfactual imaginations. Note,
R_{C_{i_{2}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k_{2}}}
represents the results for another clinical trial
C_{i_{2}}
, its proposed outcome measure
O_{j_{2}}
, and its study arm
A_{k_{2}}
.

4
Methodology

Let

x^{(0)}=(C_{i_{1}},O_{j_{1}},A_{k_{1}}),\qquad x^{(T)}=(C_{i_{2}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k_{2}}),

and let

R^{(0)}=R_{C_{i_{1}},O_{j_{1}},A_{k_{1}}},\qquad R^{(T)}=R_{C_{i_{2}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k_{2}}}.

We define a counterfactual path

x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(T)},

where each consecutive pair
x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}
differs in exactly one experimental condition, with all other conditions held fixed.

True data-generating law.

We assume that there exists a universally valid data-generating law
P
governing clinical trial outcomes. Under this law, the result at step
t
is fully determined by the current experimental condition
x^{(t)}
. Formally, we assume

P\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid x^{(t)},R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)}\right)=P\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid x^{(t)}\right).

That is, once the full current trial configuration
x^{(t)}
is specified, the previous result
R^{(t-1)}
and previous condition
x^{(t-1)}
should not provide additional information about
R^{(t)}
. This assumption reflects the view that trial outcomes are governed by the current experimental setup itself, rather than by previously observed outcomes.

Why successive counterfactual imagination is needed.

Although the true law depends only on
x^{(t)}
, directly learning

P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid x^{(t)}\right)

may be difficult for a finite-capacity language model, since it requires approximating the full mapping from a complete clinical trial specification to its result in a single step. Our key hypothesis is that the local counterfactual transition

P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right)

is easier to learn and provides a better practical approximation to the true law. Intuitively, when
x^{(t-1)}
and
x^{(t)}
differ in only one experimental condition, predicting how the result changes from
R^{(t-1)}
to
R^{(t)}
is a simpler problem than predicting
R^{(t)}
directly from
x^{(t)}
.

Successive counterfactual imagination.

We therefore model prediction as a sequence of local counterfactual transitions. The successive counterfactual imagination process induced by the trained language model
P_{\theta}
is defined recursively as

R^{(t)}\sim P_{\theta}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right),\qquad t=1,\dots,T.

In other words, at each step the model takes the previous known or imagined result
R^{(t-1)}
, applies a one-condition perturbation from
x^{(t-1)}
to
x^{(t)}
, and predicts the resulting counterfactual outcome
R^{(t)}
.

In principle, the conditional distribution of the final target result is obtained by marginalizing over all possible intermediate counterfactual outcomes:

P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(T)}\mid R^{(0)},x^{(0)},\dots,x^{(T)}\right)=\sum_{R^{(1)},\dots,R^{(T-1)}}\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right).

However, exact marginalization is generally intractable because the number of possible intermediate trajectories grows exponentially with the number of counterfactual steps.

Dominant-path approximation.

We therefore adopt a dominant-path approximation. Specifically, if the trained language model induces a sharply peaked conditional distribution at each step, so that there exists a sequence of intermediate results

R^{*(1)},R^{*(2)},\dots,R^{*(T-1)}

whose stepwise conditional probabilities dominate those of alternative trajectories, then the full marginal is well approximated by this single path:

P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(T)}\mid R^{(0)},x^{(0)},\dots,x^{(T)}\right)\approx\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{*(t)}\mid R^{*(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right).

Equivalently, the dominant intermediate trajectory may be defined as the maximum a posteriori counterfactual path

(R^{*(1)},\dots,R^{*(T-1)})=\arg\max_{R^{(1)},\dots,R^{(T-1)}}\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta}\!\left(R^{(t)}\mid R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right).

Under this approximation, successive counterfactual imagination reduces prediction to iteratively following the most likely local counterfactual transition from the source trial to the target trial.

Interpretation.

Importantly, our formulation does not claim that the true law itself is sequential. Rather, the sequential decomposition is an approximation strategy for the learned model. The true data-generating law
P
is assumed to depend only on the current experimental condition
x^{(t)}
, whereas the learned model
P_{\theta}
is trained to approximate this law through a sequence of simpler local counterfactual updates.

4.1
Discovering Natural Counterfactuals

In practice, a clinical trial often includes multiple outcome measures and multiple study arms. On average, a trial contains roughly two primary outcome measures and eight to ten secondary outcome measures, reflecting the fact that clinical studies are typically designed to evaluate several aspects of an intervention rather than a single effect. Controlled trials contain at least two arms: a comparator arm, often placebo or standard of care, and an intervention arm. In many cases, however, a trial includes more than two arms. Beyond the baseline comparator arm, there may be multiple treatment arms, each corresponding to a different intervention regimen. For example, one arm may administer 10 mg of a drug per day, another 20 mg per day, and a third 30 mg per day. This type of design is commonly referred to as a dose-ranging or dosing study, in which investigators seek to determine whether treatment effects become more pronounced as dosage increases.

We observe that, for a clinical trial
C_{1}
containing at least two outcome measures
O_{j_{1}}
and
O_{j_{2}}
, the units

(C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}),\qquad(C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k})

are both defined for every study arm
A_{k}
included in the trial. Their corresponding results,

R_{C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}},\qquad R_{C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k}},

are likewise both observed. Thus, multiple outcome measures within the same trial-arm pair naturally give rise to multiple co-reported result units.

Therefore, these co-reported trial outcomes can be used to supervise the fine-tuning of a language model
P_{\theta}
. In particular, for a fixed clinical trial
C_{1}
and study arm
A_{k}
, we train the model to predict the reported result associated with one outcome measure from the reported result associated with another outcome measure:

P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k}}\mid R_{C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}},(C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}),(C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k})\right).

Intuitively, this objective encourages the model to learn the relationship between different outcome measures reported for the same trial-arm pair.

Given a training set
\mathcal{D}
of such tuples, the supervised fine-tuning objective is the standard cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{(C_{1},O_{j_{1}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k})\in\mathcal{D}}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k}}\mid R_{C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}},(C_{1},O_{j_{1}},A_{k}),(C_{1},O_{j_{2}},A_{k})\right).

Similarly, for dose-ranging studies, the same clinical trial
C_{1}
and the same outcome measure
O_{j}
are often evaluated across multiple study arms corresponding to different dosage levels. In this setting, the trial-level context and evaluation criterion remain fixed, while the intervention specified by the study arm changes. As a result, pairs of units

(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}),\qquad(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}})

coexist within the same trial for different study arms
A_{k_{1}}
and
A_{k_{2}}
, together with their corresponding reported results

R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}},\qquad R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}}}.

These paired observations provide another natural source of supervision for fine-tuning a language model
P_{\theta}
. In particular, we train the model to predict the reported result under one study arm from the reported result under another study arm within the same trial and outcome measure:

P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}}}\mid R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}},(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}),(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}})\right).

Intuitively, this objective encourages the model to learn how changes in intervention arms, such as dosage differences, affect the observed result while holding the trial and outcome measure fixed.

Given a training set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{arm}}
of such tuples, the corresponding supervised fine-tuning objective is the standard cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{arm}}(\theta)=-\sum_{(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}},A_{k_{2}})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{arm}}}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}}}\mid R_{C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}},(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{1}}),(C_{1},O_{j},A_{k_{2}})\right).

Note,
A_{K_{1}},A_{K_{2}}
must be using primarily the same drug.

4.2
Approximating Other Counterfactuals

For all remaining variables, we absorb them into the clinical trial symbol
C
. These variables include, among others, the drug’s biological mechanism of action, geography (i.e., the countries in which participating hospitals administer the intervention), and patient eligibility criteria, whose inclusion and exclusion rules jointly define the health profile of the enrolled population. In general, natural counterfactuals for these variables are difficult to obtain. Nevertheless, a few important exceptions do arise in practice. First, the baseline comparator is sometimes not a placebo but an established drug. In such cases, the comparator and the investigational drug often share certain similarities while still differing in clinically meaningful ways. These pairings may therefore encourage the model to internally represent how trial results change when the intervention drug changes while other aspects of the trial remain similar. Second, a clinical trial conducted in a single country may later motivate a follow-up trial with an otherwise similar design but expanded to multiple countries or conducted globally. Such pairings provide a natural counterfactual setting for studying how changes in geography affect trial outcomes.

Outside these special cases, natural counterfactuals rarely exist for most trial variables. We therefore construct approximate counterfactual pairs using a combination of LLM-based information retrieval and FDA drug monographs. For each retrieved pair of trials,
C_{i_{1}}
and
C_{i_{2}}
, the corresponding trial attributes are highly similar overall, but several variables may differ simultaneously. In this setting, it is no longer feasible to construct supervision signals for a full sequence of one-variable perturbations, since the intermediate counterfactual outcomes are not observed in reality. Nevertheless, we retain the same hypothesis as in the supervised setting: successive counterfactual imagination remains the easier inductive path for the model. Rather than predicting the target result
R^{(T)}
directly from
x^{(T)}
, the model begins from the known source result
R^{(0)}
and generates a latent sequence of local counterfactual transitions that gradually transforms
x^{(0)}
into
x^{(T)}
.

Because these intermediate counterfactual states are unobserved, we can no longer optimize the model with direct stepwise supervision. Instead, we optimize
P_{\theta}
with reinforcement learning using only a verifiable terminal reward. Concretely, given the source result
R^{(0)}
and a counterfactual path

x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(T)},

we define the prompt

q=\bigl(R^{(0)},x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(T)}\bigr).

For each prompt
q
, we sample
G
candidate counterfactual trajectories

\tau_{i}=\bigl(R_{i}^{(1)},R_{i}^{(2)},\dots,R_{i}^{(T)}\bigr)\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q),\qquad i=1,\dots,G,

where each
\tau_{i}
is a complete successive counterfactual imagination trajectory from the source trial to the target trial. Consistent with our formulation in the previous section, the probability of each sampled trajectory factorizes as

P_{\theta}(\tau_{i}\mid q)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{i}^{(t)}\mid R_{i}^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right),

with
R_{i}^{(0)}=R^{(0)}
. The final element
R_{i}^{(T)}
is the model’s predicted result for the target unit
(C_{i_{2}},O_{j_{2}},A_{k_{2}})
.

Since only the terminal prediction is verifiable, we assign reward at the end of the trajectory. Let
y^{\star}
denote the gold benchmark answer associated with the target trial. When the benchmark is expressed as a multiple-choice label, we define a deterministic verifier
V
that maps the generated target result
R_{i}^{(T)}
to an answer label, and set

r_{i}=\mathcal{R}\!\left(R_{i}^{(T)},y^{\star}\right)=\mathbf{1}\{V(R_{i}^{(T)})=y^{\star}\}.

More generally,
\mathcal{R}
may be any deterministic reward function that verifies whether the predicted terminal result preserves the correct answer to the benchmark question.

Within each group of
G
sampled trajectories, we compute a normalized relative advantage

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\mu_{r}}{\sigma_{r}+\epsilon},

where

\mu_{r}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}r_{i},\qquad\sigma_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(r_{i}-\mu_{r})^{2}}.

Let

\rho_{i}(\theta)=\frac{P_{\theta}(\tau_{i}\mid q)}{P_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\tau_{i}\mid q)}=\frac{\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta}\!\left(R_{i}^{(t)}\mid R_{i}^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right)}{\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\!\left(R_{i}^{(t)}\mid R_{i}^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)}\right)}

denote the trajectory likelihood ratio between the current policy and the sampling policy. We then optimize the model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\Big(\rho_{i}(\theta)A_{i},\,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i}(\theta),1-\eta,1+\eta)A_{i}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle+\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)\,\|\,P_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid q)\right)\right].

Here,
\eta
is the clipping parameter and
P_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a reference policy, typically the supervised fine-tuned checkpoint or base model, used to prevent reward hacking and preserve fluency. The first term increases the probability of high-reward counterfactual trajectories relative to lower-reward trajectories sampled for the same prompt, while the KL penalty regularizes the updated policy toward the reference model.

The model is never directly supervised on the intermediate states
R^{(1)},\dots,R^{(T-1)}
; instead, these states remain latent. Reinforcement learning encourages
P_{\theta}
to discover intermediate counterfactual trajectories whose local transitions are plausible enough that the final imagined target result
R^{(T)}
yields the correct verifiable answer. In this sense, GRPO optimizes the same sequential counterfactual mechanism introduced earlier, but under terminal supervision rather than stepwise supervision.

4.3
Synthetic Reasoning for Supervised Fine-tuning

Empirically, during each turn in our successive counterfactual imagination, we add a synthetic reasoning trace that gives a plausibly causal explanation of why changing
x^{(t-1)}
into
x^{(t)}
would cause
R^{(t-1)}
to change into
R^{(t)}
, this reasoning is produced by a powerful GPT5.4 that has full access to both
x^{(t-1)}
,
x^{(t)}
and
R^{(t-1)}
,
R^{(t)}
. This reasoning is validated by our biomedical experts as being plausible. While the true data generating law remains hidden, we would have no way to actually verifying the correctness of such synthetic reasoning traces. In our experiments, we have found our language model fine-tuned on such synthetic reasoning traces would produce considerable improvements in terms of accurately predicting a single step of counterfactual imagining, as well as GPT5.4 can do, without going through GRPO. We denote the synthetic reasoning trace as
W=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
where each
w_{i}
is a token. Thus the formulation is turned into.

P_{\theta}(W,R^{(t)}\mid R^{(t-1)},x^{(t-1)},x^{(t)})

4.4
Discovering Approximate Counterfactuals

We elaborate our method to find pairs of clinical trial units that are highly similar at the attribute level. A clinical trial unit is represented as
\{C,O,A\}
, where
C
denotes the collection of
n
trial-level variables,

C=\{V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{n}\}.

To preserve fine-grained similarity signals, we embed each variable separately rather than collapsing the entire unit into a single representation. Concretely, we use the NV-Embed-v2 text embedding model to map each variable, as well as
O
and
A
, into dense vectors, yielding

\{\vec{V}_{1},\vec{V}_{2},\dots,\vec{V}_{n},\vec{O},\vec{A}\}.

Now suppose we have
N
clinical trial units,

\Big\{\{C_{1},O_{1},A_{1}\},\{C_{2},O_{2},A_{2}\},\dots,\{C_{N},O_{N},A_{N}\}\Big\}.

After embedding, these units are represented as

\Big\{\{\vec{V}_{1,1},\vec{V}_{2,1},\dots,\vec{V}_{n,1},\vec{O}_{1},\vec{A}_{1}\},\{\vec{V}_{1,2},\vec{V}_{2,2},\dots,\vec{V}_{n,2},\vec{O}_{2},\vec{A}_{2}\},\dots,\{\vec{V}_{1,N},\vec{V}_{2,N},\dots,\vec{V}_{n,N},\vec{O}_{N},\vec{A}_{N}\}\Big\}.

Let

E^{(Z)}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{Z}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{Z}_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\vec{Z}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{Z}}

denote the embedding matrix whose
i
-th row is the embedding of variable
Z
for the
i
-th clinical trial unit. We then compute the pairwise cosine similarity matrix

S^{(Z)}_{\mathrm{upper}}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\vec{Z}_{1},\vec{Z}_{1})&\cos(\vec{Z}_{1},\vec{Z}_{2})&\cdots&\cos(\vec{Z}_{1},\vec{Z}_{N})\\
0&\cos(\vec{Z}_{2},\vec{Z}_{2})&\cdots&\cos(\vec{Z}_{2},\vec{Z}_{N})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cos(\vec{Z}_{N},\vec{Z}_{N})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

where the
(i,j)
-th entry is

S^{(Z)}_{ij}=\cos(\vec{Z}_{i},\vec{Z}_{j})=\frac{\langle\vec{Z}_{i},\vec{Z}_{j}\rangle}{\|\vec{Z}_{i}\|\,\|\vec{Z}_{j}\|}.

We use
\delta=0.8
as an initial similarity threshold and retain a candidate pair
(Z_{i},Z_{j})
only when

\cos(\vec{Z}_{i},\vec{Z}_{j})\geq\delta,\qquad i<j.

For each retained pair, we then use an LLM, GPT-5.4-mini, to determine whether
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
are semantically aligned, e.g., whether they measure the same underlying quantity despite minor differences in wording. If the pair is judged sufficiently similar, we connect the corresponding clinical trial units,

\{C_{i},O_{i},A_{i}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{C_{j},O_{j},A_{j}\},

with an edge. We repeat this procedure for every variable-specific cosine similarity matrix and incrementally construct a graph over clinical trial units. Finally, we define two units to be an
M
-approximate counterfactual pair if there exist at least
M
such edges between them.

Evaluation Dataset Construction

We evaluate our approach by its downstream utility for clinical trial outcome prediction. From the training split of
28

To further assess the quality of the two counterfactual perturbation independently, we construct two dedicated evaluation datasets from this sample:

Outcome-measure perturbation set.

For each (trial, outcome measure) pair in the evaluation sample, we identify the most similar alternative outcome measure within the same trial, ranked by cosine similarity of the embeddings from
13
. A evaluated model is provided with the reported result of the nearest outcome measure and asked to predict the target outcome measure’s result under the same study arm.

Study-arm perturbation set.

Each evaluation question is associated with two study arms reported under a common outcome measure. We fix one of these arms and pair it with a different study arm drawn from the same trial that was measured under the same outcome. A evaluated model is provided with the paired arm’s reported result and asked to predict the fixed arm’s result.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced DeepImagine, a framework for teaching language models biomedical reasoning through successive counterfactual imagining. Our central premise is that, although the true causal laws governing clinical trial outcomes remain hidden, highly similar historical trials with reported results can provide useful anchors for approximating how outcomes change under controlled perturbations of trial conditions. By combining natural counterfactual supervision, approximate counterfactual retrieval, synthetic reasoning traces, and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, we train language models to predict prospective clinical trial outcomes through sequences of local counterfactual transitions rather than one-shot correlational prediction. Empirically, this approach improves performance over conventional correlational baselines and untuned language models, while also yielding interpretable reasoning traces that can be analyzed to recover structured causal hypotheses. More broadly, our results suggest that counterfactual imagination may provide a practical path toward more mechanistic and scientifically useful reasoning in biomedical language models.
