Multi-objective optimization of EDM machining of Waspaloy employing composite electrode
Authors: Putta Priyanka, V Jayakumar, P K Giridharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrical Discharge Machining (EDM) has emerged as an effective technique for machining difficult-to-cut superalloys such as Waspaloy, which are widely used in aerospace applications due to their superior mechanical strength and thermal stability but exhibit poor machinability under conventional processes. The present study focuses on enhancing EDM performance through the combined use of copper–graphite (Cu–Gr) composite electrodes and eco-friendly dielectric media, along with multi-objective optimization. Composite electrodes with graphite content 5 wt% were fabricated, and machining experiments were conducted under different process parameters, including discharge current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, and inter-electrode gap. Machining performance was evaluated in terms of material removal rate (MRR), tool wear rate (TWR), and surface roughness (Ra), while surface integrity was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). A TOPSIS-based multi-objective optimization approach was employed to determine the optimal machining conditions. The results indicated that discharge current and pulse-on time significantly influence MRR, with a maximum MRR of approximately 0.43 mm3/min achieved at 32 A and 30 µs. The minimum TWR of about 0.06 mm3/min was also observed at 8 A, indicating optimal discharge stability. Surface roughness was minimized to 1.8 μm using the CuGr5 electrode, while eco-friendly sunflower oil dielectric produced superior surface integrity with Ra as low as 1.013 μm and reduced recast layer thickness. Sensitivity analysis revealed non-linear parameter behavior with distinct optimal regions, and uncertainty analysis confirmed high experimental reliability with minimal variability. Multi-objective optimization identified the optimal condition with a performance index of 0.6213. The study demonstrates that the integration of composite electrode, bio-based dielectrics, and optimization techniques significantly enhances machining efficiency, surface quality, and sustainability in EDM of Waspaloy.Keywords: Electrical discharge machining, Waspaloy, Copper–graphite composite electrode, Eco-friendly dielectric fluids, Multi-objective optimization, TOPSIS, Surface integritySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys and titanium alloys are widely used in aerospace and high-temperature engineering due to their superior mechanical strength, thermal stability, and corrosiveness. In spite of these properties, they are poorly machinable with high cutting temperatures, work-hardening properties, and chemical activity, which leads to low thermal conductivity, a high work-hardening tendency, and poor surface quality. As a result, cutting tool optimization and cutting cooling or lubrication processes are now needed to enhance the efficiency, life, and sustainability of machining1. Surface integrity in nickel-based superalloys was a determining factor in both the machining performance and fatigue life, directly affecting functional reliability. It has already been established in the previous studies that machining parameters, tool materials, and the processing environments have a significant influence on the surface topography, residual stresses, strain hardening, and subsurface microstructural transformations. These surface and subsurface properties play a very important role in determining the component life in aerospace applications2. It has been demonstrated that machining-related defects and stress residue condition increase the initiation and propagation of fatigue cracks during cyclic loading situations3.

In-depth research on nickel-based superalloys has underlined the effects of alloy composition and manufacturing pathways on the creep resistance and fatigue strength as well as the fracture behavior and pointed out the emerging technologies in the area of enhancing performance4. Excess heat generation and prevailing wear processes like abrasion, adhesion, and chemical degradation make tool wear a significant constraint in machining, and thus better tool materials and coatings are required5. The improved cooling and lubrication methods, such as minimal quantity lubrication and nanofluid-aided machining, have shown significant cutting force reduction, with a better surface quality6. Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) has developed to become a viable alternative non-conventional machining method for machining hard-to-cut alloys like nickel-based superalloys and titanium alloys. Dielectric fluid was important in spark stability, debris elimination, and wear and surface integrity of the tool. Nevertheless, traditional hydrocarbon-based dielectrics produce dangerous emissions during thermal degradation, and this was something that would be of concern to the environment and to occupational health7. Consequently, environmentally friendly dielectric fluids have become a growing concern, and research has established that these types of fluids play a greater role in the rate of material removal, wear of the tool, and surface roughness8.

Recent studies have shown that biodegradable vegetable oil-based dielectrics, especially with the addition of nanoparticles, have a great impact on improving machining efficiency, surface quality, energy consumption, and environmental impact9. Further results on nickel-based superalloys in sustainable EDM showed that vegetable oil-based dielectric has better spark stability and surface integrity than the traditional oils10. Biodegradable dielectrics mixed in powder form have been demonstrated to improve the rate of material removal and decrease tool wear and specific energy consumption when compared to the situation of having graphite electrodes11. The surface cracking in titanium alloys and the machining performance were also verified to have reduced with the use of bio-dielectrics in comparative analysis12. The performance of EDM depends on the electrode material choice that has a significant impact on electrical and thermal conductivity. Research had found copper-tungsten electrodes to be reported to have lower wear and high-quality surfaces13. It has also been demonstrated that process optimization and electrode design can have a great influence on the electrochemical performance of an energy storage system and support the significance of fabrication strategies together with material choice14. The multi-objective optimization of EDM established the fact that the electrode material and dielectric selection formed a strong influence on the material removal rate, tool wear, and surface roughness15. It has already been found that electrode-electrolyte contacts, wettability, and defect engineering play a crucial role in controlling the electrochemical performance and surface reaction15,16. Additional studies on precision electrochemical micromachining demonstrated a determining effect of electrolyte composition and additives on machining precision and surface finish17.

The multi-objective methods of decision-making have been extensively applied in ranking the complex alternatives where there was a vexation of multiple criteria. Hybrid AHPTPSIS schemes have been associated with high potential to arrange the alternatives in a systematic manner through the combination of experimental data on performance with weighted decision criteria to recognize the best solution in the context of energy, manufacturing, and sustainability-driven solutions18. Optimization using TOPSIS has also been effectively applied in problems in engineering design with opposing objectives, where two-stage optimization plans allowed effective reduction of the design variables and effective ranking of optimal designs19. TOPSIS has been effective in machining, especially in multi-objective optimization involving conflicting measures of performance. Research using TOPSIS with the Taguchi method has been able to transform a variety of machining responses into a single decision index that allows simultaneous optimization of surface roughness and tool wear and systematic identification of optimal process parameters20. Outside manufacturing, TOPSIS-based decision-making models have also been used in the optimization of complex system-level problems, such as resource allocation problems where cost, energy consumption, and execution time need to be balanced, which once again illustrates the strength and applicability of TOPSIS in a wide variety of application contexts21.

The need to promote productivity and sustainability in EDM has recently been mentioned in terms of the measurement of energy consumption and the electrode degradation. most existing studies are primarily focused on cryogenic electrodes, hybrid nanofluid dielectrics, or AI-based predictive modeling22. The experimental studies that involved real-time energy measurement proved that machining parameters, especially discharge current, have a pre-eminent effect on machining time, electrode wear, and specific energy usage. It was also demonstrated that increased discharge current decreased the machining time and energy consumption per unit material removed, which was why optimization of parameters was the key to the energy-saving and sustainable EDM operations23. Applied ANN-based modeling to study the effects of cryogenic electrode treatment and nano-powder mixed dielectrics on wear behavior and dimensional accuracy during superalloy machining22,24. Unusual methods like hybrid nanofluid based dielectrics and systematic optimization methods have also been used to bring sustainable improvement in the performance of EDM22. Research on EDM of Ti-6Al-4 V alloy had reported that the rate of material removal, tool wear, and surface quality were greatly enhanced by optimized process parameters and powers of the alloy and enhanced as well by the concentration of powders used. The combination of Taguchi design and TOPSIS optimization was productive in finding the best machining conditions, which can promote eco-friendly and competitive EDM22. Optimization of EDM parameters that multi-objectively optimized the parameters of EDM among Ti-6Al-4 V further indicated the importance of tool electrode material as used in determining the machining performance25. Experimental work using Taguchi-based techniques and state-of-the-art optimization algorithms has shown that graphite electrodes tend to have better material removal rates and lesser tool wear rates, but brass electrodes have better surface finish. Surface analysis using semiconductor electrodes showed enhanced surface integrity at optimal combinations of electrode and parameter26. EDM performance strongly depends on process parameter optimization and surface integrity evaluation27, recent studies have optimized WEDM parameters for advanced alloys such as AlBe3, investigations are largely confined to conventional dielectric environments28. The role of alternative dielectric media and electrode-dielectric interactions remains insufficiently explored, particularly for sustainable EDM applications. the combined application of composite electrodes, eco-friendly dielectric media, SEM-based surface integrity validation, and a two-step TOPSIS-based multi-objective optimization framework remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study introduces a novel integrated multi-objective approach that simultaneously enhances machining performance, surface quality, and sustainability in EDM of Waspaloy.

Experimental procedure

Workpiece material preparation

To determine the relationship between temperature and mechanical performance in nickel superalloy, Waspaloy, which was often used in aerospace and high temperature structural design, was selected as the workpiece material of the present research, as it was of high mechanical strength, oxidation and thermal stability of high temperature. Even though they are advantageous as far as the in-service conditions are concerned, they provide machinability to be considerably less in the case of conventional cutting operation, and therefore, the Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) would be worth considering to remove materials. Waspaloy was a potential material and has chemical composition and thermo-mechanical properties that the material was a representative material that can be used in testing excellent EDM techniques using composite electrodes and other dielectric media.

Workpiece material was prepared into a cylindrical specimen of size ϕ12mm and length of 10 mm to conduct experiments on die-sinking EDM. All specimens were mechanically grounded before machining under successive grades of silicon carbide abrasive papers to remove irregularities on the surface and make initial surface conditions the same in all the trials. Such pre-treatment was required to minimize the effect of surface imperfection and stresses left in parts on machining and measurement of the outcome.

The samples underwent mechanical cleaning, followed by an ultrasonic cleaning in acetone during a set time interval in order to clean the surfaces of the samples and remove contaminants and adhesive material and oxide coats. The samples were dried by blowing on air at ambient temperature and attached to the table of EDM machine. This was a common preparation procedure that ensured repeatability of initial conditions of all experiments and enhancing reliability of subsequent measurements on the rate at which materials were removed, tool wear rate, and surface integrity.

Fabrication of composite tool electrodes

Copper-graphite (Cu-GR) composite tool electrodes were manufactured with the view of investigating the influence of the electrode mix on the EDM performance in machining Waspaloy. Electrolytic copper of high purity was selected as the matrix material since it has good electrical and thermal conductivity, and graphite powder was selected as the reinforcement phase since it has high thermal stability, lubricating properties, and can also resist erosion in high discharge conditions. Graphite electrodes with 5 wt. percent is prepared and labeled as CuGr5.

The stir casting method has been used to produce composite electrodes, which offer favorable dispersion of the reinforcement particles in the metallic part. Copper was melted in an induction furnace in a graphite crucible and superheated to aid the melting process. Preheating of graphite powder of average size was also done prior to addition to eliminate moisture content and enhance wettability. The previously prepared graphite was introduced gradually to the copper melt that was kept stirring to bits to achieve a uniform relationship of the reinforcement to the molten copper. It was done by reducing oxidation and entrapment of gases in a protective environment, and then the composite melt was poured into preheated steel molds to solidify.

The cast composite solidified rods were allowed to cool at ambient temperature and machined into the size to perform EDM experiments. To achieve a homogeneous distribution of the graphite particles in the copper matrix and to ascertain interfacial integrity, the microstructural characterization of the electrodes fabricated was performed with the assistance of scanning electron microscopy. The printed Cu-Gr composite electrodes were homogeneous with regard to their structures, thus meaning that they can be used in high-temperature and hard-to-cut materials EDM processes. EDM setup and machining conditions

The electrical discharge machining was proposed to be carried out with the involvement of a die-sinking EDM machine, the pulsed direct current (DC) power supply of which was used in the proposed experiment. The machining system was set up in such a way as to generate controlled discharges of an electrical nature between a conductive tool electrode and a Waspaloy workpiece that were both immersed in a flowing dielectric medium. The tool electrode was fixed on the tool holder in an erect position and the workpiece was firmly fixed on the machine table in such a way that it could remain in place and the spark gap could be maintained constant in the process of machining.

The flow of the dielectric fluid was maintained through an external position, pumping system to ensure that debris was removed effectively in the inter-electrode gap to ensure that continuous discharge conditions were achieved. It was emptied carefully to lower the risks of arcing, short-circuiting, and uncharacteristic operation, particularly in higher discharge energy situations such as during machining. The machine servo system automatically regulated the interelectrode gap to have the same volume of sparking during all experimental run times.

Measurement of machining performance

Waspaloy Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) was evaluated up to the extent of Material Removal Rate (MRR), Tool Wear Rate (TWR), and surface roughness (Ra). These are some of the important parameters of machining efficiencies, tool life, and surface integrity as selected response parameters. All of them were measured using calibrated instruments to ensure the achievement of both accuracy and repeatability.

Calibration of material removal rate was done by weighing the workpiece at the start and end points of the trial of each machining experiment using a high-precision electronic balance. The weighing was then repeated by weighing the specimens when thoroughly cleaned and dry to eliminate the influence of any attached debris, dielectric residues, and so forth. The material removal that was obtained was then calculated as a loss of weight in volumetric form using the density of Waspaloy, and then MRR was determined by dividing the volume removed by machining time.

The wear rate of the tool was also determined similarly, with the weight of the tool electrode being taken during the machining period. To be able to have consistent measurements, electrodes were dried and scrubbed and then weighted. A portable surface profilometer was used accordingly in the ISO standards in seeing the surface roughness of the machined specimens. In order to minimize the impact of measurement uncertainty, as well as to achieve representative surface characterization, numerous measurements were performed of different points of each machined surface, and the average value of Ra was reported.

Surface integrity analysis

To establish the influence of tool composition, i.e., electrode composition of tools and dielectric media on the surface morphology and thermal injury, the surface integrity of the EDM-machined Waspaloy specimens was tested. The evaluation of post-machining surfaces was reduced to establishing the characteristics of the surface, such as discharge craters, recast layer formation, microcracks, and sub solidified debris, which are useful in establishing the suitability of machined components for high-temperature and fatigue-sensitive components.

The machined surfaces have been examined under the Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) at different magnifications. Ultrasonic washing in ethanol was carried out in advance to remove the traces of dielectric fluid and adhered debris in all the specimens. The processed samples were fixed on conductive stubs and sputter-coated with a thin conductive layer to ensure that the samples do not generate charging effects on the surface when in contact with the electron beam. SEM images were captured with high vacuum conditions and with appropriate accelerating voltages in order to make sure that the global surface morphology and localized microstructural features were both examined.

Low-magnification micrographs were used to observe crater distribution, expulsion of molten material, and surface uniformity, and higher magnifications could be used to study recast layer characteristics, micro voids, and thermal microcracks. The machining parameters, electrode makeup, and type of dielectric were correlated to understand the discharge behavior and the thermal issues behind the observed surface features. The test served as a supplement to the quantitative machining performance data to provide an insight into the processes governing the surface formation of EDM of Waspaloy by using this qualitative surface integrity test. Results and discussions

Hydrocarbon oil and kerosene were selected as conventional dielectrics for their stable spark behaviour, while sunflower and groundnut oils were chosen as biodegradable, eco-friendly alternatives, and used motor oil to assess waste utilization and impurity effects. Lower viscosity fluids improve debris flushing and MRR, whereas higher viscosity fluids may cause unstable discharges. Higher dielectric strength and thermal conductivity enhance spark control and heat dissipation, improving surface quality during machining of Waspaloy. Hydrocarbon oils reduce tool wear via carbon deposition, while impurities in used oil may lead to unstable machining.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the variation in Material Removal Rate (MRR) with respect to different machining parameters is interpreted based on the mean response values obtained from the main effects plot. For discharge current, the mean MRR values increase from 0.15 mm3/min at 8 A to 0.25 mm3/min at 16 A, followed by a further rise to 0.31 mm3/min at 24 A. A peak MRR of 0.43 mm3/min is observed at 32 A, indicating that higher discharge current significantly enhances material removal due to increased spark energy and intensified thermal erosion. At 40 A, the MRR decreases to 0.28 mm3/min, suggesting that excessive current leads to unstable discharges, plasma channel expansion lowers energy density, while increased re-solidification of molten material forms a thicker recast layer, further diminishing net material removal, and inefficient energy utilization, thereby reducing machining efficiency.

With respect to inter-electrode gap, the MRR initially increases from 0.25 mm3/min at 1 mm to 0.29 mm3/min at 2 mm, and further to 0.31 mm3/min at 3 mm, indicating improved spark stability and effective debris flushing. The maximum MRR of 0.38 mm3/min is achieved at 4 mm, attributed to optimal spark distribution and enhanced discharge energy. However, at 5 mm, the MRR decreases to 0.30 mm3/min, due to excessive gap distance causing reduced spark intensity and energy dissipation.

For pulse-on time (Ton), the MRR rises from 0.20 mm3/min at 10 µs to 0.28 mm3/min at 20 µs, reaching a peak value of 0.42 mm3/min at 30 µs. This trend indicates that increasing pulse duration allows more energy transfer per spark, resulting in higher material removal. Beyond this, MRR decreases to 0.35 mm3/min at 40 µs and sharply drops to 0.17 mm3/min at 50 µs, suggesting that prolonged discharge duration leads to plasma instability, excessive melting, and inefficient flushing of molten material.

In the case of pulse-off time (Toff), the MRR remains constant at 0.29 mm3/min for 2 µs and 4 µs, indicating limited influence due to insufficient cooling time. At 6 µs, MRR decreases to 0.23 mm3/min, likely due to reduced spark frequency. However, at 8 µs, MRR increases to 0.33 mm3/min, reflecting improved dielectric recovery and stable spark generation. At 10 µs, MRR slightly decreases to 0.28 mm3/min, indicating that excessive idle time reduces overall machining efficiency30.

Regarding voltage, the MRR remains stable at 0.29 mm3/min for 25 V and 50 V, then increases to a maximum of 0.37 mm3/min at 75 V, demonstrating that moderate voltage enhances discharge energy and material removal. However, further increases to 100 V (0.30 mm3/min) and 125 V (0.19 mm3/min) result in reduced MRR due to excessive spark gap expansion and unstable discharge conditions.

ANOVA is an effective statistical tool to determine the significance and contribution of EDM process parameters, as demonstrated in EDM study31.

The ANOVA results presented in Table 4 reveal that pulse-on time (Ton) is the most influential parameter on MRR, contributing about 26%, confirming its dominant role in controlling discharge energy. Dielectric fluid follows with 24%, highlighting the significant effect of its properties on machining performance, which supports the study’s novelty. Current contributes around 19%, indicating its importance in spark intensity. Gap distance and pulse-off time (Toff) have moderate effects related to spark stability and debris removal, while voltage exhibits the least influence under the selected conditions.

As shown in Fig. 3, the variation in tool wear rate (TWR) with respect to different machining parameters is analyzed based on the mean response values. For dielectric fluid, the TWR values exhibit minor variation, with 0.13 mm3/min for Hydrocarbon oil, decreased to 0.07 mm3/min for Kerosene, increasing to 0.11 mm3/min for Sunflower oil, and stabilizing around 0.08–0.09 mm3/min for Used Motor Oil and Groundnut Oil. The relatively higher TWR observed for dielectric Hydrocarbon oil can be attributed to its lower cooling efficiency and reduced insulating strength, which promotes intensified tool erosion. In contrast, the lower TWR values for other fluids indicate improved spark stability and better heat dissipation characteristics25.

Main effects of EDM parameters on tool wear rate.

With respect to discharge current, the TWR increases from 0.05 mm3/min at 8 A to 0.15 mm3/min at 32 A and slightly dropped to 0.1 mm3/min at 40 A. This trend suggests that moderate current levels enhance machining efficiency by stabilizing the discharge channel and reducing tool erosion. However, at very low current, mild spark intensity and minimal thermal stress on the electrode leads to longer tool life, while at higher current, excessive thermal loading again contributes to tool degradation. For the inter-electrode gap, TWR decreases from 0.90 mm3/min at 1 mm to a peak of 0.6 mm3/min at 2 mm, then increases to 0.15 mm3/min at 3 mm, 0.125 mm3/min at 4 mm, and drops to 0.1 mm3/min at 5 mm. The higher wear at increased gap distances is associated with unstable discharge concentration and localized overheating of the tool electrode. As the gap moderate at 2 mm, improved flushing efficiency and uniform spark distribution reduce tool wear significantly.

In the case of pulse-on time (Ton), the TWR increases from 0.70 mm3/min at 10 µs to 0.1 mm3/min at 20 µs, peaks to 0.15 mm3/min at 30 µs, again reduces to 0.12 mm3/min at 40 µs, and finally decreases to 0.05 mm3/min at 50 µs. This fluctuating behavior indicates that both insufficient and excessive pulse duration can increase tool wear. Moderate pulse durations provide optimal energy distribution, minimizing excessive tool erosion32.

For pulse-off time (Toff), the TWR remains stable at 0.1 mm3/min for 2 µs and 4 µs, decreases to 0.080 mm3/min at 6 µs and increases to 0.125 mm3/min at 8 µs, and slightly decreases to 0.1 mm3/min at 10 µs. The reduction in TWR at higher pulse-off time (Toff) can be attributed to improved dielectric deionization and more effective debris flushing between successive discharges. Longer off-times provide sufficient time for plasma channel collapse and cooling of the electrode surface, thereby reducing abnormal arcing and concentrated thermal loading on the tool electrode. As a result, electrode erosion decreases and comparatively lower TWR values are obtained at higher Toff conditions33.

Regarding voltage, the TWR remains constant at 0.1 mm3/min for 25 V and 50 V, sharply increases to 0.14 mm3/min at 75 V, and then decreases to 0.10 mm3/min at 100 V and 0.05 mm3/min at 125 V. The peak TWR at 75 V indicates that higher discharge energy significantly accelerates tool erosion due to intense spark impact and thermal stress. Beyond this level, the reduction in TWR suggests energy dispersion and reduced effective spark intensity34.

Dielectric fluid contributes 24%, highlighting its role in cooling and debris removal, supporting the importance of bio-dielectrics. Current accounts for 20%, as higher discharge energy increases tool wear. Gap distance (15%) has a moderate effect through spark stability, while pulse-off time (8%) contributes to cooling between discharges. Voltage shows the least influence (5%) under the selected conditions.

As shown in Fig. 4, A lower discharge current of 8 A, the mean surface roughness was 1.6 μm, indicating smoother surface generation due to low discharge energy and reduced crater size32. Under this condition, limited melting and shallow material erosion resulted in finer surface features. As the current increased to 16 A, the mean Ra rose to around 2.6 μm, reflecting an increase in discharge energy that produced deeper craters and greater molten material expulsion. A further decrease in current to 24 A resulted in a slight drop in Ra to 2.2 μm, due to intensified thermal loading and irregular crater overlap. Intermediate–high current conditions produced relatively lower Ra due to improved spark stability and uniform crater formation under optimized dielectric flushing conditions. At the highest current level of 40 A, the surface roughness increased significantly to about 3.5 μm, which can be attributed to violent spark discharges, excessive melting, and the formation of larger craters and recast layers, leading to deteriorated surface quality35. Bio-based dielectrics altered plasma channel characteristics because of their different viscosity and thermal conductivity, leading to modified erosion behavior compared with conventional hydrocarbon dielectrics. The graphite phase in the CuGr5 electrode enhanced thermal dissipation and reduced localized overheating, contributing to improved surface integrity and controlled tool wear.

Main effects of EDM parameters on surface roughness (Ra).

For pulse-on time (Ton), a mean Ra of 1.8 μm was observed at 10 µs, indicating moderate energy input per discharge. Increasing Ton to 20 µs resulted in a peak Ra of about 3.5 μm, as prolonged discharge duration allowed excessive heat accumulation, causing unstable molten pools and rough surface texture. Further decreasing Ton to 30 µs reduced Ra to 2.8 μm, suggesting improved melting uniformity and partial stabilization of the discharge process. At 40 µs, Ra further decreased to around 2.5 μm, indicating that longer pulse duration promoted smoother material removal through controlled melting and improved debris flushing, thereby reducing surface irregularities.

With respect to pulse-off time (Toff), a mean Ra of 2 μm was recorded at 2 µs, improved dielectric deionization enhanced spark stability and uniform erosion. Increasing Toff to 4 µs increased Ra to around 3.8 μm, which can be attributed to optimal energy concentration during each discharge, leading to larger crater formation. The decreases in Toff to 6 µs resulted in a reduction in Ra to 2.8 μm, as. At 8 µs and 10 µs Ra remained nearly constant at about 2.5 and 2.0 μm respectively indicating a balance between spark frequency and discharge stability.

For the inter-electrode gap, the mean surface roughness decreased from 3.9 μm at 1 mm to 1.5 μm at 2 mm, due to decreased discharge voltage and spark intensity. At a gap of 3 mm, Ra marginally increased to around 2.3 μm, suggesting improved flushing efficiency and reduced secondary discharges. At 4 mm, Ra increased again to 2.8 μm, as larger gap distances promoted higher discharge energy and irregular spark dispersion, resulting in rougher surface morphology36.

Gap distance (13%) influences spark stability, and pulse-off time (Toff) (10%) provides moderate impact via cooling and debris removal. Voltage has the least effect (7%) under the given conditions.

Surface topography

SEM analysis was mainly applied for morphological identification of crater, microcrack, and recast layers37. In this qualitative assessment, there is an efficient correlation between surface integrity and machining variables, including the effect of the dielectric fluid38,39.

Figure 5(a) shows the surface topography of Waspaloy machined in Hydro carbon Oil dielectric of the SEM micrograph at 100× magnification. Its surface is homogeneously covered with discharge craters, globular solid droplets, and localized deposition patches. The crater morphology is relatively flat and relatively smooth, implying a controlled distribution of the energy of sparks during machining. There is a decrease in microcrack formation, indicating a lower concentration of thermal stress because of enhanced dielectric cooling and flushing efficiency. The existence of fine globules has been explained by the fact that the molten material is quickly melted and solidified in the course of the discharge of sparks40.

The morphology of the recast layer at 2000× magnification, as shown in Fig. 5(b), is a thin and discontinuous resolidified layer on the machined surface. The recast layer is seen in the form of irregular globular and fragmented deposits created as a result of the rapid quenching of the molten material, which was not fully expelled out of the discharge zone. Micro-voids, small surface undulations, can be observed, which means local shrinkage of solidification and partial entrapment of the debris41. Nevertheless, the thickness of the layer seems to be comparatively low, which indicates a good dielectric recovery and flushing effect. Subsequently, better interfacial bonding and a reduction in surface damage are deduced, which substantiates high surface integrity for those under the Hydro carbon Oil dielectric conditions42.

The Fig. 6(a) reveals a relatively uniform surface characterized by fine micro-craters and scattered resolidified patches. The craters are formed due to localized melting and vaporization of Waspaloy under high-energy spark discharges. Compared to conventional hydrocarbon dielectrics, the crater distribution appears finer and more homogeneous, indicating stable plasma channel formation and controlled energy dispersion. Minor surface patches correspond to redeposited molten material that was not completely flushed away during machining43. Fine microcracks are observed across certain regions, which are attributed to thermal stresses generated during rapid heating and quenching cycles. However, the overall surface texture appears comparatively smoother (Ra ≈ 1.013 μm), suggesting improved surface integrity44.

The oxygenated molecular structure of sunflower oil suppresses excessive carbon deposition during dielectric decomposition, thereby reducing secondary discharges and minimizing thermal damage. As a result, the recast layer thickness appears controlled, with reduced crack density and improved interfacial bonding45. These observations confirm that sunflower oil promotes stable discharge behavior, enhanced cooling efficiency, and superior surface integrity during EDM machining of Waspaloy.

The presence of degraded hydrocarbons and metallic contaminants in used motor oil destabilizes spark behavior, resulting in uneven energy distribution and inefficient debris flushing. Reduced porosity, shallow craters of discharge, and a relatively homogeneous recast layer in SEM images are linked to lower Ra values that are related to the reduced wear of the tool. It means that there are steady erosion conditions and effective thermal distribution in the tool–workpiece contact region, which leads to little degradation of electrodes during the machining. The rate of wear on the tool was also increasing progressively as the surface roughness increases and reflects observed features on the surface of the sediment, including craters, micro-voids, and irregular re-solidified debris. Such features of the surface are signs of unstable erosion behavior, which was associated with excessive localized melting and adhesion of debris that exacerbates electrode wear. The trend observed supports the idea that tool wear behavior has a close relationship with surface integrity that was determined through SEM analysis, proving the trends of machining performance that were determined by the experimental study48.

Figure 9(b) also illustrates the definite negative dependence between the surface roughness and rate of the material removal regarding the surface characteristics derived through SEM. Sensitivity analysis

A comprehensive sensitivity analysis was carried out to examine the influence of major EDM process parameters on the overall performance index (Pi), which integrates material removal rate, tool wear rate, and surface roughness into a unified metric. The parameters considered include discharge current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, inter-electrode gap distance, tool electrode material, and dielectric medium. The combined graphical representation enables simultaneous evaluation of these factors, revealing that machining performance is governed by strongly non-linear behavior with distinct optimal regions. Such behavior highlights the critical role of controlled spark energy, efficient dielectric recovery, and stable discharge conditions in achieving balanced machining performance50,51.

The analysis further provides a holistic understanding of the interplay between process, material, and environmental parameters in EDM of Waspaloy. Energy-related parameters, such as discharge current and pulse-on time, primarily dictate material erosion efficiency, whereas pulse-off time and gap distance influence spark stability and debris evacuation. In addition, the selection of composite electrode material and eco-friendly dielectric medium significantly affects thermal distribution and surface integrity. The observed trends collectively validate the necessity of multi-objective optimization, as optimal performance is achieved only through a synergistic combination of parameters rather than isolated tuning of individual factors.

The Fig. 10 illustrates the variation of the overall performance index (Pi) with respect to changes in major EDM process parameters, including discharge current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, inter-electrode gap, electrode composition, and dielectric medium. A non-linear response is observed for all parameters, indicating that machining performance is governed by complex interactions rather than linear relationships. The presence of peak regions in multiple curves suggests that optimal parameter settings exist within intermediate levels, beyond which performance deteriorates due to instability in spark generation and inefficient energy utilization.

Sensitivity analysis of EDM process parameters on overall performance index.

An increasing trend in the performance index is observed with discharge current up to a moderate level, after which a decline occurs. This behavior is attributed to the balance between spark energy and discharge stability. At lower current levels, insufficient energy leads to inefficient material removal, while excessive current results in plasma channel expansion, unstable discharges, and increased tool degradation. A similar trend is evident in the case of pulse-on time, where moderate durations enhance energy transfer per spark, whereas prolonged durations lead to excessive melting and poor debris flushing52.

The influence of pulse-off time and inter-electrode gap demonstrates the importance of spark recovery and flushing efficiency. Short pulse-off times restrict dielectric recovery, leading to unstable sparking, whereas excessively long durations reduce machining efficiency due to decreased spark frequency. Likewise, an optimal inter-electrode gap ensures uniform spark distribution and effective debris removal, while too small or too large gaps result in localized overheating or energy dissipation. These observations confirm that controlled discharge conditions are essential for achieving improved machining performance.

Variations in electrode material and dielectric medium further highlight their significant role in determining surface integrity and overall efficiency. Composite electrodes with moderate graphite content exhibit improved thermal stability and uniform spark distribution, leading to higher performance index values. Similarly, eco-friendly dielectric media demonstrate superior cooling and flushing characteristics, resulting in more stable machining conditions.

An uncertainty analysis was conducted to assess the reliability and robustness of the experimental measurements and the derived performance index (Pi) in the EDM machining of Waspaloy. The primary sources of uncertainty were identified as measurement errors in material removal rate MRR 2–5% due to variations in discharge energy and material re-solidification behavior. TWR 3–7% as tool wear is highly sensitive to unstable sparking and electrode material inconsistencies. Surface Roughness (Ra) 2–6% depending on instrument resolution and localized surface irregularities53,54. along with variations arising from machine stability, dielectric fluctuations, and electrode wear behavior. Instrumental limitations such as precision of the weighing balance, profilometer resolution, and environmental factors during experimentation were also considered as contributing factors. These uncertainties were assumed to be random in nature and were minimized through repeated trials and consistent experimental conditions.

In the TOPSIS framework, D⁺ represents the Euclidean distance from the ideal best solution, and D⁻ represents the distance from the ideal worst solution.
