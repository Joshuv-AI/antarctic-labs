Neuron-specific expression of transmembrane protein 130 (TMEM130)
Authors: Tomomichi Kayahara, Masahiko Itani, Hiroki Kurita, Tomohiro Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransmembrane protein 130 (TMEM130) was identified as significantly downregulated in rupture-prone intracranial aneurysm lesions in a rat model. This study aimed to characterize TMEM130 tissue distribution and physiological function in rats. RNA sequencing was conducted on rupture-prone intracranial aneurysm lesions and control arterial tissues to identify differentially expressed genes. Tmem130 mRNA spatial expression patterns were examined by in situ hybridization across multiple rat organs. A Tmem130-deficient rat line was generated using the CRISPR–Cas9 system to evaluate potential functional effects. Histological analyses and open-field behavioral tests were performed to assess neuronal morphology and locomotor or anxiety-like behavior, respectively. In situ hybridization revealed that Tmem130 expression was restricted to the brain and adrenal medulla, with widespread localization in neurons throughout cortical and subcortical regions. Here, TMEM130 was detected only in transcriptional level due to the absence of TMEM130-specific antibody. Tmem130−/− rats were viable and showed no gross histological abnormalities in brain structures or significant differences in locomotor or anxiety-like behavior compared with wild-type littermates. No phenotypic abnormalities were observed under standard laboratory conditions. TMEM130 is specifically and broadly expressed in neurons; however, the molecule may not be essential for basic neuronal structure or behavior in rats, although subtle, developmental, stress-dependent, or disease-relevant functions were not evaluated. Although its precise physiological role remains unclear, TMEM130 may represent a novel neuron-associated transmembrane protein, warranting further investigation into its molecular function in the central nervous system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59183-6.Keywords: Transmembrane protein 130, Neuron, Brain, Adrenal gland, Intracranial aneurysmSubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The rupture of intracranial aneurysm is the primary cause of subarachnoid hemorrhage, a disease with a poor prognosis1,2. Subarachnoid hemorrhage still causes a devastating outcome despite the modern advances in medicine; thus, the elucidation of the pathogenesis of intracranial aneurysm is mandatory to develop a novel therapeutic strategy. Recent studies have revealed that the increase of hemodynamic stress followed by macrophage-dependent chronic inflammation in arterial walls regulates the initiation and enlargement of intracranial aneurysms3. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the rupture remain to be elucidated. We have, therefore, performed comprehensive RNA-sequencing analysis using rupture-prone intracranial aneurysm lesions in a rat model4,5 to identify the factors contributing to the rupture of intracranial aneurysm6. Through the analyses, transmembrane protein 130 (TMEM130) was identified as a significantly down-regulated gene in rupture-prone lesions compared with control arterial walls. Although previous studies have explored TMEM130, its biological roles remain unclear7–13. A series of subsequent experiments in the present study revealed TMEM130 as a neuron-specific transcript with widespread distribution.

Methods

Study approval of animal experiments

All experiments involving animals, including their care and use, were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institute of Health. The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (Approval Numbers #19036, #20003, #20004, #21004, #21015, #22012, and #22041) and Jikei University School of Medicine (Approval Number #2023-004). In addition, all experiments involving recombinant DNA were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (Approval No. #2019-14, #20 − 07, #21 − 07, and #22 − 07). The present manuscript adheres to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for reporting animal experiments.

The details of the anesthesia and the euthanasia for rats

The induction of general anesthesia in rats was achieved by the inhalation of Isoflurane (induction; 5.0%, maintenance; 1.5–2.0%, #IYESC-0001, Pfizer Inc., New York, NY). In the euthanasia, rats were deeply anesthetized by the inhalation of Isoflurane (5.0%, #IYESC-0001, Pfizer Inc.), and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Each tissue was then harvested and subjected to the following examinations.

Induction of rupture-prone intracranial aneurysms in rats

Ten-week-old female Sprague-Dawley rats were maintained on a light/dark cycle of 12 h/12 h and had free access to chow and water. To induce rupture-prone intracranial aneurysms, rats were subjected to a bilateral ovariectomy, a ligation of the left carotid artery, the right external carotid artery, and the right pterygopalatine artery, and systemic hypertension was achieved by the combination of a high salt diet and the ligation of the left renal artery under general anesthesia. Immediately after the surgery, rats were fed chow containing 8% sodium chloride and 0.12% 3-aminopropionitrile (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan), an irreversible inhibitor of Lysyl Oxidase catalyzing the cross-linking of collagen and elastin. The rats that died within one week after surgical manipulations were excluded from the analyses. The animals were deeply anesthetized, and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, at 56th post-surgery. The circle of Willis was then stripped from the brain surface and a rupture-prone lesion induced at an anterior communicating artery or a posterior communicating artery was dissected5,6.

RNA sequencing analysis

We reanalyzed the comprehensive RNA sequencing data, deposited as GSE161044 in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/ from our laboratory6. The re-analysis was performed using DESeq2, and genes with 0.5 counts per million or more in at least one library were subjected to the re-analysis. In addition, not normalized data but read counts data were used for re-analysis. Then, we detected Tmem130 as a significantly under-expressed gene in rupture-prone aneurysms.

Generation of the Tmem130-deficient rat line

The Tmem130-deficient rat line was established using the CRISPR-Cas9 system with two guide RNAs (5’-GACCATCAGTAGTAAGACTA-3’ and 5’-GATTTCCAGGTACTCGGACG-3’). This resulted in the deletion of the genomic region encoding exons 2 and 8 (Fig. 1A).

Establishment of the Tmem130-deficient rat line. (A) Schematic presentation of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated deletion of Tmem130, showing the sequence and position of the two guide RNAs (gRNA) used; (B) Genotyping results for littermates of a heterozygous mating pair. Arrows (Tmem130-/-) and arrowheads (Tmem130+/+) indicate offspring subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining shown in Fig. 6. b.p., base pair; CDS, coding sequence; gRNA, guide RNA; N/C, negative control reaction without template.

Genotyping of offspring was performed using genomic DNA extracted from the tail tissue. DNA was isolated by lysis with buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 1 mg/mL Proteinase K (#70663, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). PCR was conducted using KOD Plus DNA polymerase (#KMM-101, Toyobo CO., LTD., Osaka, Japan) with the following primers: forward 5’-CCAGAGCTTCATTCACTCTAGGCAAGC-3’ and reverse 5’-GGGCAGATGACGACAGCTGGAC-3’ (mutant allele; 389 b.p.), and forward 5’-CCAGAGCTTCATTCACTCTAGGCAAGC-3’ and reverse 5’-CAGAGATGCTCACCTTTAATGGGGAGAACC-3’ (wild-type allele; 564 b.p.). The amplified fragments were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1B).

The established rat line was deposited in the National BioResource Project (http://www.anim.med.kyoto-u.ac.jp/nbr/default.aspx) under the designation SD-Tmem130em1Taoki.

In situ hybridization and hematoxylin-eosin staining

Five- to seven-week-old female Sprague-Dawley rats including Tmem130-/- and Tmem130+/+ rats were subjected to in situ hybridization or hematoxylin-eosin staining. For in situ hybridization, 5-µm-thick paraffin sections were prepared from various rat organs. The RNAscope Target Probe – Rn-Tmem130-C1 (#1305701-C1, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA), targeting nucleotides 309–1344 of rat Tmem130 mRNA (NM_001170399.1), and the RNAscope Target Probe – Rn-Rbfox3-C2 (#436351-C2, Advanced Cell Diagnostics), targeting nucleotides 16–1116 of rat Rbfox3 mRNA (NM_001134498.2), were used along with the RNAscope 2-plex Negative Control Probe (#320751, Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Hybridization was performed using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Duplex Reagent Kit (#322430, Advanced Cell Diagnostics), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were mounted using the VectaMount Permanent Mounting Medium (#H-5000, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Routine hematoxylin-eosin staining was performed on 5-µm-thick paraffin sections of rat brain tissue. Images were acquired on a BZ-X700 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) in brightfield mode.

Open-field test

An open-field test was conducted as one of behavioral test batteries to evaluate the spontaneous locomotor activity of 5-week-old female Tmem130+/+ and Tmem130-/- rats. Each rat was placed in an open-field chamber, and behavior was recorded over a 30-minute period. Subsequently, the time spent in the central area, defined as the central 50% of the chamber, was measured manually by visual inspection. Conversely, cognition, learning, social behavior, and stress responses of those rats were not examined in the present study.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In open-field test, the sample size was calculated using G*Power Software version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-University Düsseldorf in Germany). Effect size was calculated based on the mean time spent in the central area and standard deviation derived from the results of the test. Thereafter, the sample size, which was additionally calculated based on the results of the experiments, was n = 9 for Tmem130+/+ and n = 7 for Tmem130-/- (effect size: 1.35, significance level: 0.05, power: 0.8).

Results

Identification of Tmem130

Tmem130 was identified as a significantly under-expressed gene (false discovery rate < 0.05, fold change > 2) in intracranial aneurysm lesions with a high risk of rupture compared with remaining circle of Willis, based on comprehensive RNA sequencing data (Fig. 2A and B) (deposited as GSE161044 in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/)6.

Identification of Tmem130 through RNA sequencing analysis. (A) Rupture-prone aneurysms and the remaining circle of Willis were harvested from a rat model and subjected to comprehensive RNA sequencing analysis; (B) A list of under-expressed genes in rupture-prone aneurysms, compared to the remaining circle of Willis, with log2 fold-changes > 3.

Neuron-specific Tmem130 expression

The expression pattern of Tmem130 in rat tissues was examined using in situ hybridization. Positive hybridization signals specific to Tmem130 were observed exclusively in the brain and adrenal medulla (Figs. 3A, 4 and 5). Higher magnification images confirmed that the signals were broadly distributed among neurons throughout various brain regions, including the 2nd to 6th layers of the cerebral cortex, thalamus, striatum, and hippocampus, although not restricted to any specific nucleus or anatomical area (Fig. 3B). In addition, co-staining of Tmem130 and NeuN/Rbfox3 was observed in the cerebral cortex (Fig. 4). Conversely, the signals for Tmem130 were not detected in other tissues, including the aorta, femoral muscle, heart, kidney, liver, lung, spleen, and stomach (Fig. 5). Here, the signals were non-specifically observed including stroma in lung and spleen. Control hybridization using a probe targeting a bacterial gene did not produce any detectable signal, supporting the specificity of Tmem130. On the other hand, the specific antibody targeting TMEM130, whose signal was observed in Tmem130+/+ but not in Tmem130-/- tissues, was not available. Therefore, TMEM130 was detected only in transcriptional level in the present study.

Influence of Tmem130 deficiency in the central nervous system

To determine the functional role of TMEM130, a Tmem130-deficient rat line was established using CRISPR-Cas9 (Fig. 1A). Histological examination of various brain tissues harvested from Tmem130+/+ (n = 2) or Tmem130-/- (n = 2) rats, including the cerebral cortex, thalamus, striatum, hippocampus, cerebellum, and rhombencephalon, harvested from the offspring indicated in Fig. 1B, revealed no detectable differences in neuronal morphology distribution or overall tissue structure between Tmem130+/+ and Tmem130-/- rats (Fig. 6).

Histological analysis of Tmem130-deficient rat brains. Hematoxylin-eosin staining images of brain tissues, including the cerebral cortex, thalamus, striatum, hippocampus, cerebellum, and rhombencephalon, harvested from Tmem130+/+or Tmem130-/- rats. Magnified images corresponding to the left panel are shown on the right. Scale bar: 100 μm.

Behavior of Tmem130+/+ or Tmem130-/- rats

Spontaneous locomotor activity of Tmem130+/+ (n = 5) or Tmem130-/- (n = 4) rats was examined using the open-field test (Fig. 7A). No significant differences were detected in the time spent in the central area between the two genotypes (Fig. 7B), suggesting that Tmem130 deficiency may not affect anxiety-like behavior or locomotor activity. In addition, Tmem130-/- rats have exhibited no apparent difference in their growth and no apparent phenotype suggesting disorders in motor or cognitive functions, compared to their littermates that were born in a Mendelian pattern of inheritance.

Behavioral analysis in the open-field test. (A) Image of the open-field chamber used for behavioral testing. The central area was defined as the middle 50% area of the total field; (B) Percentage of time spent in the central area. Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that Tmem130 is specifically expressed in neurons, as revealed by in situ hybridization. Co-staining of Tmem130 with NeuN/Rbfox3 supported Tmem130 expression in neuron. However, no apparent differences in gross histology and locomotor or anxiety-like behavior were observed between Tmem130+/+ and Tmem130-/- rats, suggesting that the influence of Tmem130 deficiency on the central nervous system could not be clarified through the methods used in this investigation. Here, Tmem130 expression was not observed in the aorta, although Tmem130 was identified from RNA sequencing data using rupture-prone aneurysm lesions. The RNA sequencing findings, therefore, might reflect neuronal contamination, perivascular innervation, or secondary neurovascular signaling, rather than direct vascular involvement. Moreover, whole tissue of lung or spleen, including cells and stroma, was diffusely stained in in situ hybridization. Therefore, the signals in those tissues were considered to be non-specific ones. Previous studies on Tmem130 have been limited, with only seven available publications7–13. However, the function of TMEM130 remains largely unknown.

TMEM130 was initially identified by Vincent et al. as a novel gene encoding a transmembrane protein containing a polycystic kidney domain on chromosome 7q2210. In that study, TMEM130 was identified through bioinformatic genomic analysis of a patient with autism and intellectual disability who carried a balanced translocation involving chromosomes 6 and 7.

Chrystal et al. identified TMEM130 as the most ancient paralog in the premelanosome protein (PMEL) and glycoprotein non-metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB) families7. PMEL and GPNMB have been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, and GPNMB is highly expressed in the nervous system, supporting the notion that TMEM130 is associated with the nervous system and may have functional relevance in neural tissues.

Several studies by a Chinese research group have reported a link between the transcriptional expression of TMEM130 and cell migration ability in several malignant tumors. TMEM130 mRNA expression is downregulated via hypermethylation of the TMEM130 promoter in breast cancer cell lines8, nasopharyngeal carcinoma cell lines9, and gastric cancer cell lines13. Moreover, overexpression of TMEM130 suppresses cell migration in these cancer cell lines8,9,13. In contrast, Zhang et al. reported that alternative splicing of TMEM130 was upregulated in colorectal cancer tissues, suggesting a potentially complex role in tumor biology12.

The function of TMEM130 in the central nervous system (CNS) has not been elucidated in previous studies or in the present work. In contrast, the potential role pf TMEM family members in CNS including neurological diseases have been documented in previous studies as in the following. The expression of TMEM16A, TMEM45A, TMEM140, TMEM168, and TMEM176A has been detected in human glioma tissues or glioma cell lines using western blot analysis and quantitative PCR. Knockdown of these TMEM genes inhibited cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, while promoting apoptotic cell death or cell cycle arrest in human glioma cells in vitro14–18, suggesting the exacerbating role of TMEM family members in glioma. The role of TMEM family members in anxiety, depression or aggression has also been indicated. Genetic deletion of TMEM16B in mice failed to avoid the center of the open field and stayed in open-arm for a longer time in the elevated plus maze compared to wild-type littermates, indicating the reduction in anxiety-like behavior19. Overexpression of TMEM168 facilitated anxiety-like behavior in mice20. TMEM176A expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal/frontal cortex was significantly lower in depression-sensitive Flinders Sensitive rats compared to control line21, suggesting the role of this TMEM member to depression. Mice deficient in TMEM16B that expresses in the lateral septum demonstrated increased aggression evidence by total attack time to intruder mice in behavior assay22. The pathological role of TMEM family members in diseases affecting CNS has been indicated in genetic analyses or analyses using specimens from affected site of human cases like TMEM240 in spinocerebellar ataxia 2123, TMEM5 in cobblestone lissencephaly24, and TMEM106B in frontotemporal lobar degeneration25.

Our findings, consistent with previous studies, indicate that TMEM130 expression has only been identified at the transcriptional level, and the corresponding protein has not yet been detected. In addition, only female rats were subjected to the experiments. Therefore, sex-specific effects could be limitations in the present study. Moreover, there were no growth or phenotype differences between Tmem130+/+ and Tmem130-/- rats under standard laboratory conditions. However, this was not definitive evidence regarding the function of TMEM130. On the other hand, a limited number of rats were subjected to the histological analysis. Therefore, based solely on the result of the histological examination in this study, it cannot be conducted that there is no structural difference between Tmem130+/+ and Tmem130-/- rats. Furthermore, the number of rats was insufficient compared to the calculated sample size in open-field test, suggesting that a significant difference might be observed if additional rats were subjected to the experiment. Further investigations are necessary to determine the distribution of the TMEM130 protein and to elucidate its physiological and pathological roles, particularly within the nervous system and in the context of neurological diseases.
