 ABSTRACT

Friction Stir Welding (FSW) of irradiated and unirradiated SS304 stainless steel in a hot cell at ORNL
revealed surface-breaking cracks that extended into the joint interior, indicating incomplete bonding.
Measurements of actual plunge depth and motor current showed that mechanical compliance, likely in the
motion table, caused deflection that reduced tool penetration. Position control based solely on motor
encoder feedback, without force or torque monitoring, further contributed to discrepancies between
commanded and actual plunge depth. These findings underscore the need for increased machine stiffness,
direct plunge depth measurement, and force feedback to ensure weld integrity in hot cell FSW operations.

 v

 1. BACKGROUND

Friction Stir Welding (FSW) experiments performed on SS304 stainless steel in ORNL’s welding hot cell
revealed surface-breaking cracks (Figure 1a) in some weld samples. Each weld sample consisted of an
irradiated SS304 section in the middle, positioned between unirradiated SS304 tabs on either side. Cross-
sectional analysis confirmed that these flaws extended into the interior of the joint (Figure 1b), indicating
that the lack of bonding was not merely superficial but compromised the integrity of the entire weld. Such
defects primarily occurred in the latter portion of the weld length and extended into the unirradiated
SS304 run-off tab at the end of the welds. This internal lack of fusion adversely affected weld quality and
raised concerns about the mechanical performance of the joints under service conditions. Key process
parameters—such as low tool rotation speed, excessive traverse speed, or insufficient plunge depth—are
known causes of such cracks due to inadequate material plasticization and incomplete bonding near the
surface. However, a comprehensive investigation revealed that when the same welding parameters were
applied using a different FSW machine outside the hot cell, these defects did not occur. This suggests that
the root cause may be related to the specific hardware or operational constraints of the hot cell FSW
machine rather than the welding parameters alone. Additional investigations were conducted to examine
potential hardware-related issues within the hot cell welding setup.

 2. FSW MACHINE & EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Figure 2 below shows the FSW machine in the hot cell, consisting of a rotating tool with controlled Z-
axis plunge for tool insertion and depth control, and a motion table providing X-axis travel for the
welding process. The machine’s Z and X motions are position-controlled exclusively via the motor’s
built-in encoder feedback. Consequently, the actual plunge depth of the FSW tool into the material
surface during welding is uncertain. To measure the actual plunge depth, a dial gauge indicator was
attached to the welding head to monitor the relative distance to the material surface. Since force and
torque feedback were unavailable, the motor electric current of X-motion was recorded as an indicator of
resistance force during welding.

 3. RESULTS

A plunge depth setpoint of 0.33 in. below the surface of the SS304 sample was selected as part of the
process input parameters, after which the X-axis motion table began to travel. Figure 3 shows the actual
plunge depth measured during two welding trials. At approximately 20 s, the rotating tool reached its
target plunge depth, and the motion table began moving along the X-axis. Due to machine compliance,
the actual plunge depth at that moment was only 0.25 in. In fact, this discrepancy was expected and had
already been considered when selecting 0.33 in. as the setpoint. However, as the sample traveled along
the X-axis, the plunge depth did not remain constant. It gradually decreased, reaching its minimum value
after passing the midpoint of the sample (around 80 seconds in Figure 3). This behavior indicates
maximum deflection of the FSW system. Toward the end of the weld pass, the plunge depth increased
again, returning to approximately 0.25 in. The corresponding electric current of the X-axis motion table,
shown in Figure 4, followed a similar trend. It gradually decreased to a minimum during the latter portion
of the weld pass, indicating reduced resistance, and then returned to its initial value by the end. Figure 5
illustrates the machine deflection profile throughout the weld pass, with the greatest deflection occurring
in the latter portion. This increased deflection explains the formation of surface-breaking cracks, which

 4. CONCLUSION

The investigation demonstrated that surface-breaking cracks in FSW joints were strongly linked to
mechanical compliance of the hot cell welding machine, most likely the motion table. Position control
based solely on motor encoder feedback, without force or torque monitoring, led to significant
discrepancies between the commanded and actual plunge depth. Measured plunge depth data and current
readings revealed that deflection occurring in the latter portion of the weld pass reduced tool penetration,
creating conditions for incomplete bonding and internal flaws. These results suggest that improvements to
the FSW machine should focus on increasing structural stiffness, incorporating direct plunge depth
measurement, and integrating force or torque feedback to ensure consistent material plasticization and
