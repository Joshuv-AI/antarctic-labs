The WHO Classification of Genetic Tumour Syndromes: Considerations for Genetics

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe WHO Classification of Tumours is used widely by histopathologists and other disciplines as it provides an internationally agreed taxonomy of neoplastic disorders. Now published online and in print, it has often included genetic tumour syndromes (GTS) relevant to particular organs, as suggested by the editorial board. This highlighted the lack of a systematic classification of GTS, providing international standards for their diagnosis and a basis for research. The need for this was established by a series of meetings, leading to the adoption of a hierarchical classification of GTS based on the cellular mechanism affected, the molecular pathway involved, the clinical syndrome and the gene defects present. The major cellular mechanisms affected were identified as: growth factor receptors and related pathways; oxidative stress response and metabolism; cell cycle and apoptosis; DNA repair and genomic stability; telomere maintenance; epigenetic drivers and chromatin; RNA regulation and protein regulation. Assembly of the classification emphasised current gaps in knowledge, together with the need for further research. The new classification highlights the relationships between some genetic syndromes and provides an internationally agreed framework for the incorporation of new information as it becomes available.

Introduction

The diagnosis of patients and their families with genetic tumour predisposition syndromes has assumed increasing clinical importance, as it germline findings now directly influence surveillance, therapeutic decision‐making and risk assessment across multiple organ systems. The World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumours, provides the internationally accepted framework for tumour taxonomy, underpinning diagnostic practice worldwide and evolving through successive evidence‐based editions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] on a 5‐to‐6‐year cycle between editions, now completing the fifth edition WHO Classification of Tumours (WCT5) [2, 3, 4, 5].

Despite this progress, genetic tumour syndromes (GTS) have historically been incorporated in a fragmented manner, typically as appendices to WHO organ‐specific classifications. This approach obscures biological relationships, limits cross‐disciplinary integration given that many disorders impact multiple tumour types and fails to reflect the mechanistic basis of tumour predisposition. At the same time, gene‐centric catalogues, while valuable, do not adequately capture the shared pathways and cellular processes through which diverse variants converge to drive neoplasia.

To address this gap, we developed a hierarchical classification of GTS based on four linked levels: cellular mechanism, molecular pathway, clinical syndrome and genetic change (Figure 1). This structure reflects the biological architecture of tumour predisposition, allowing syndromes to be grouped by shared mechanisms while preserving clinical and genetic specificity. The resulting framework provides a simplified but extensible ontology that supports both diagnostic application and the systematic incorporation of emerging knowledge.

An example of the hierarchical classification for GTS for RAD51‐related cancer predisposition showing the cellular mechanism, pathway, syndrome and genes involved.

This new (WHO) Classification of GTS, the last volume of the fifth edition, was developed through multidisciplinary collaboration, drawing on expertise in pathology, clinical genetics, oncology and molecular biology, and informed by bibliometric analysis and international consensus discussions. It represents a first step towards a unified, mechanism‐based taxonomy of GTS within the WHO system.

Terminology

After some discussion we agreed that the name, ‘genetic tumour syndrome’ as a short form for genetic tumour predisposition syndrome was the best description of these disorders. The collective term ‘genetic’ was preferred over ‘familial’ or ‘hereditary’, so that the classification could encompass those syndromes which often have a post‐zygotic causation. There was considerable debate over the use of the term ‘germline’ or ‘constitutional’, with the decision going to the former term as translation of ‘constitutional’ can have problems in countries where it can be confused with consanguinity. As ‘germline’ is also more commonly used within the published literature, this was therefore preferred. Table 1 outlines common terms and definitions used throughout the volume.

There are few issues around the accurate and standardised description of genes and DNA sequence variance, which has been expertly curated by the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) [6, 7] and the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) for many years [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, there are several instances where colloquial shorthand is often used in medical practice rather than the more accurate (and often longer) correct scientific notation. The classification uses the scientific terms, and includes the colloquial terms where these will benefit understanding. One example is in the reporting of sequence variants in the histone H3.3 genes [12], in which the numbering based on immunohistochemistry (the more frequent use in practice) and on the DNA sequence differs, leading to possible confusion.

Syndromes, by contrast, are given names according to their published names and common usage. We have listed these by chromosomal location and gene in Table 2. There is a move towards a dyadic disease nomenclature including the gene symbol for GTS, but this is by no means ubiquitous (Table 2) [13]. The syndrome we call hereditary gastric and breast cancer syndrome (HGBCS) in the classification is an autosomal dominant GTS with inactivating constitutional CDH1 or CTNNA1 sequence variants, which has also been called hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (HDGC) syndrome; hereditary lobular breast cancer (HLBC); diffuse gastric and lobular breast cancer syndrome; diffuse gastric cancer syndrome/lobular breast cancer syndrome (DGCS/LBCS); CDH1‐related tumour susceptibility syndrome or cadherin‐related tumour susceptibility syndrome. This is largely historical, but a clear cause of confusion. In line with our practice in naming tumours, we have listed alternative terminology as ‘acceptable’, or ‘not recommended’, which we hope will go some way to solving these issues.

Classification

The hierarchical classification for GTS is based on four linked levels: the cellular mechanism (known as the category), the molecular pathway (family), the clinical syndrome and the gene(s) affected with a text description of the most common disease associated changes (Figure 1). The pathway‐based approach adopted addresses the imperfect relationship between genes and tumour predisposition syndromes and emphasises that disruption of specific biological processes underlies neoplasia, with certain pathways or nodes within them recurrently associated with tumour development. Eight broad cellular mechanisms define the highest level categories: growth factor receptors and related pathways; oxidative stress response and metabolism; cell cycle and apoptosis pathways; DNA repair and genomic stability; telomere maintenance; epigenetic drivers and chromatin; RNA regulation and protein regulation.

This can be best illustrated by a representative example.

Within the category of DNA repair and genomic stability, homologous recombination defines one of the pathways responsible for repair of double‐strand DNA breaks. Pathogenic germline variants in genes including BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51C and RAD51D are grouped within this pathway on the basis of shared biological function. Clinically, these variants define a spectrum of related tumour predisposition syndromes, most prominently hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, with increased risk across multiple tumour types. Although individual genes differ in penetrance and tumour spectrum, their classification within a common pathway reflects shared mechanism and supports convergent clinical strategies, including targeted surveillance and sensitivity to poly ADP‐ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibition.

This hierarchical structure unifies multiple genes within a single mechanistic framework while preserving gene‐level specificity. It accommodates overlapping phenotypes and enables incorporation of newly identified genes without redefining the classification.

This first edition of the classification of GTS has some limitations that precluded the inclusion of all possible syndromes (Table 2). In some cases, this was because they were extremely rare, while others were not yet sufficiently documented for inclusion. Those syndromes associated with haematolymphoid neoplasms caused considerable discussion. In the end, we opted to include the more common examples and a special introduction to haematolymphoid genetic disorders which will set the scene for future developments. Indeed, it is expected that the classification will evolve (and expand) with future editions.

Of those GTS included in this edition, the largest categories were growth factor receptors and their related pathways (38 syndromes), and DNA repair and genomic stability (23 syndromes), while only one protein regulation syndrome was noted—BAP1‐related tumour predisposition syndrome. The classification emphasises certain points, such as the importance of alteration of growth factor receptors and their pathways in predisposition syndromes, while growth factors themselves are not represented. Equally, all DNA repair pathways are represented, with the common syndromes associated with homologous recombination (e.g., BRCA1 and BRCA2) and mismatch repair defects covered in depth.

Pathways interact, and the effects of individual gene products on downstream signalling can be complex, producing distinct clinical syndromes despite a shared gene context. For example, variants in APC may result in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) or gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of stomach (GAPPS) disorders, reflecting differences in the nature and location of the underlying alteration with GAPPS associated with specific promoter variants whereas FAP resulting from loss of function variants throughout the coding region. Conversely, variants within different genes may converge on a common tumour type, as seen in schwannomatosis associated with NF2, SMARCB1 and LZTR1. These relationships illustrate the complexity of genotype–phenotype associations and underscore the value of a pathway‐based framework for organising this diversity.

Conclusion

The WHO Classification of GTS establishes a unified framework that aligns tumour predisposition with its underlying biological mechanisms. By organising syndromes across linked levels of mechanism, pathway, phenotype and gene, this classification enables integration across disciplines and provides a structure within which new discoveries can be systematically incorporated.

The framework also exposes areas of incomplete knowledge, highlighting pathways and syndromes that remain under‐characterised and directing attention towards priorities for future investigation. In doing so, it shifts the classification of tumour predisposition from a descriptive catalogue toward a biologically coherent system.

This is not a static taxonomy. It is an infrastructure. As genomic data expand and mechanistic understanding deepens, the value of classification will lie not only in naming disease, but in organising knowledge in a way that predicts, connects and evolves. A mechanism‐based approach is essential to that task, and future iterations will depend on its continued refinement. Classification, in this context, is not an endpoint. It is the foundation on which the next generation of discovery will be built.
