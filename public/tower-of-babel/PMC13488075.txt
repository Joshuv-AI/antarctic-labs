Apolipoprotein B100 predicts cardiovascular and limb events in type 2 diabetes patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia
Authors: Federico Biscetti, Maria Margherita Rando, Maria Anna Nicolazzi, Flavia Angelini, Roberto Iezzi, Luis H. Eraso, Paul J. Dimuzio, Dario Pitocco, Massimo Massetti, Antonio Gasbarrini, Andrea Flex
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) undergoing lower extremity revascularization (LER) face high risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and major adverse limb events (MALE), despite guideline-directed lipid-lowering therapy. Apolipoprotein B100 (ApoB100), reflecting total atherogenic particle number, may identify residual risk beyond LDL-cholesterol (LDL-C). Therefore, we investigated whether baseline ApoB100 levels independently predict MACE and MALE beyond conventional risk factors in this high-risk population.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 167 T2DM patients with CLTI undergoing LER were followed for 12 months with visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-procedure. We measured baseline ApoB100 and assessed its ability to predict MACE, MALE, and composite endpoints, adjusting for clinical covariates.ResultsComposite events occurred in 49.1% of patients, MACE in 24%, and MALE in 35.3%. ApoB100 levels were significantly higher in event groups (composite: 62.1 vs 38.0 mg/dL, p < 0.01; MACE: 66.1 vs 44.4 mg/dL, p < 0.01; MALE: 61.0 vs 42.4 mg/dL, p < 0.01). Multivariable analyses confirmed ApoB100 as an independent predictor (composite OR 1.14 per mg/dL, 95% CI 1.08–1.20, p < 0.01; MACE OR 1.10, p < 0.01; MALE OR 1.05, p < 0.01). ROC analysis demonstrated excellent predictive accuracy for ApoB100 (AUC 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.91), with optimal ROC-derived cut-off of 56.6 mg/dL. Adding baseline ApoB100 to conventional risk factors significantly improved model discrimination (AUC gains 0.08–0.15, all p < 0.01), while Kaplan–Meier curves by cut-off effectively stratified early events (log-rank p < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated baseline ApoB100 independently predicted MACE, MALE, and composite events post-LER in T2DM-CLTI patients, substantially improving clinical risk models. Integrating ApoB100 into post-LER management algorithms could refine individualized therapeutic strategies, especially in patients with residual atherogenic risk despite optimal LDL-C control.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03233-w.

Background

Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) frequently suffer from atherosclerotic complications [1, 2]. Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a common manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis and often remains clinically silent until it progresses to chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), necessitating urgent lower extremity revascularization (LER) [3]. At this stage, vascular involvement is usually diffuse and includes coronary and cerebrovascular beds alongside lower limb arteries,, which contributes to the high incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and major adverse limb events (MALE) observed after LER [1, 2, 4]. International guidelines recommend aggressive management of modifiable risk factors, including smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia, with particular emphasis on intensive lipid-lowering therapy [5]. In fact, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a crucial biomarker for cardiovascular prevention and remains the main target of lipid-lowering strategies [6]. However, despite improved therapeutic strategies and increasingly stringent therapeutic targets, many patients experience cardiovascular events after LER, a phenomenon referred to as residual risk [7, 8]. This persistent residual risk highlights a critical gap in our understanding of the biological upstream factors contributing to adverse cardiovascular outcomes after LER in this high-risk population, underscoring the need to identify measurable mechanisms underlying these events.

Apolipoprotein B100 (ApoB100) is a key glycoprotein synthesized by the liver and present on every very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and LDL particle [9]. Each VLDL and LDL particle contains only one ApoB100 molecule, so the plasma ApoB100 concentration reflects the total number of atherogenic particles, rather than the cholesterol content alone [10]. The ApoB100 molecule contains binding sites for the LDL receptor (LDL-R), crucial for the recognition and endocytosis of LDL by hepatocytes, and numerous binding sites for extracellular matrix proteoglycans that mediate the adhesion and retention of LDL-C in the vascular intima [9]. Once retained and internalized in the vascular wall, LDL-C undergoes oxidation, followed by macrophage uptake of oxidized LDL-C via scavenger receptors to form foam cells. This process triggers an inflammatory cascade, with the expression of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and growth factors that support the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaque [10]. Inflammation plays a significant role in diabetic patients with PAD by promoting pro-atherogenic and pro-thrombotic processes at both microvascular and macrovascular levels [8]. Additionally, apo(a) binds to ApoB100 to form lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], which carries cholesterol and oxidized phospholipids [9]. The apo(a) portion of Lp(a) is structurally similar to plasminogen, with potential prothrombotic and proatherogenic effects [11]. ApoB100 contributes to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaque as well as its subsequent destabilization through these interactions [12]. In T2DM, which significantly increases the risk of atherosclerosis and PAD, ApoB100 concentrations are frequently elevated, especially in patients with diabetic dyslipidemia [13]. In principle, this elevation suggests that ApoB100 may be an integrated marker of atherogenic particle burden and residual cardiovascular risk [9, 14].

Despite emerging data increasingly confirming the involvement of ApoB100 in the atherosclerotic complications of T2DM, there is still no strong evidence demonstrating an association between plasma ApoB100 levels and the incidence of cardiovascular events in patients with PAD. Most existing studies focus on the broader relationship between ApoB100 and cardiovascular risk, but few address this marker in the context of PAD specifically. The limited research available often examines heterogeneous patient populations or lacks adjustment for important confounding factors such as coexisting metabolic disturbances or specific PAD subtypes. Consequently, the potential predictive value of ApoB100 for post-revascularization outcomes in patients with both T2DM and advanced PAD remains largely uncharacterized. Specifically, there are no clear data on the possible relationship between ApoB100 levels and the development of complications after lower limb revascularization in diabetic patients with CLTI, underscoring a significant gap in the current literature and highlighting the need for focused investigation in this high-risk population. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether baseline ApoB100 levels are associated with vascular outcomes following LER in T2DM patients with CLTI and whether they may aid in risk stratification for these individuals.

Methods

Aim and study design

We conducted a prospective cohort study to evaluate the prognostic significance of ApoB100 for incident MACE and MALE following LER in diabetic patients with CLTI. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS, which complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Study population and clinical assessment

Between October 23, 2019, and March 24, 2023, a total of 183 consecutive T2DM patients admitted for CLTI to the Cardiovascular Internal Medicine Unit of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS, Roma, Italy, were initially screened for study participation. Of these, 12 patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 3 were excluded due to early failure of the revascularization procedure, as detailed below. The remaining 167 patients were prospectively followed for the entire study period.

The inclusion criteria included age > 18 years; diagnosis of T2DM of at least 1-year duration, according to current guidelines [15] and on active pharmacological treatment; diagnosis of PAD in accordance with Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS), European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS), and World Federation of Vascular Societies (WFVS) guidelines [16]; ankle–brachial index (ABI) < 0.90; and the presence of a lower extremity arterial stenosis > 50% documented by duplex ultrasound. In patients with ABI > 1.40, suggesting medial arterial calcification, the diagnosis of hemodynamically significant stenosis was confirmed by duplex ultrasound and CT angiography. Additional criteria included Rutherford stage 4 or 5 PAD, the presence of CLTI as previously defined [1], and a clear indication for lower extremity revascularization.

The exclusion criteria included pregnancy; presence of diabetic neuropathy as detailed below; estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; LER within the previous month; coronary revascularization within the previous 3 months; cerebrovascular events within the previous month; life expectancy < 12 months due to any condition; active malignancy; active autoimmune disease; Child–Pugh class B or C liver disease; monogenic familial dyslipidemia; known thrombophilia or any contraindication to antiplatelet or antithrombotic therapy; indication to full-dose oral anticoagulation (e.g. atrial fibrillation, prosthetic heart valve, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, antiphospholipid syndrome); and any contraindication to LER.

We additionally excluded individuals with neuropathic diabetic foot, and all screened patients underwent a diagnostic assessment. The protocol included quantitative assessment of vibration perception threshold using a biothesiometer to exclude a Neuropathy Disability Score > 5 and pathological nerve conduction velocity. Autonomic neuropathy was ruled out by cardiovascular reflex tests described by Ewing and Clarke [17]. In cases where osteomyelitis of the foot was clinically suspected, patients were referred for appropriate radiological investigations to rule out bone infection.

We collected the following patient data at baseline: age, diabetes duration, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, history of hypertension (defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg or current antihypertensive treatment), hypercholesterolemia (defined as total cholesterol > 200 mg/dL or ongoing lipid-lowering therapy), coronary artery disease (CAD; defined as prior myocardial infarction, angina, or coronary revascularization), cerebrovascular disease (CVD; defined as prior ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke), current pharmacological treatment (antidiabetic, lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, antiplatelet, antithrombotic), ABI, and Rutherford category. At admission, all patients were receiving single antiplatelet therapy, and after LER they were prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for one month. All patients who were not at LDL-C target (< 55 mg/dL, in accordance with ESC/EAS guidelines [18]) received statins, ezetimibe, or a combination of both.

Lower-limb endovascular revascularization and follow-up after the procedure

We performed revascularization by endovascular angioplasty with or without stent implantation, at the discretion of the operator [1, 2]. Regarding target lesion location, 32 patients (19.2%) had iliac disease, 102 (61.1%) had femoro-popliteal disease, and 61 (36.5%) had infra-popliteal disease. The procedure was considered technically successful when the final angiographic result demonstrated a residual stenosis < 30%. Procedural failure occurred in 3 patients, who were excluded from the study protocol. No complications were recorded according to the Society of Interventional Radiology definition [19].

We assessed the incidence of MACE, MALE, or both over a 12-month period after the index procedure, with clinical evaluations scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after LER. MACE was defined as the composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or myocardial infarction [20]. MALE was defined as the composite of acute limb ischemia, major amputation of the target limb, or limb-threatening ischemia requiring urgent revascularization [21].

Blood test and biochemical assays

On the day of LER, all participants underwent blood sampling after at least 8 h of overnight fasting. We measured HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, and creatinine. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated for each patient using the CKD-EPI equation [22]. ApoB100 levels were assessed using a commercial ELISA kit (CSB-E08100h, Cusabio, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each patient, fasting serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 8% and < 10%, respectively. The assay sensitivity was < 19.5 ng/mL, based on the mean ± 3 standard deviations of the 0 standard. Samples exceeding the assay range were diluted, and final concentrations were adjusted accordingly. All samples and standards were run in duplicate, and ApoB100 concentrations were calculated from a four-parameter logistic standard curve and expressed as mg/dL after correction for the dilution factor.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 19.0 for MacOS (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We calculated sample size based on a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and the inclusion of up to 10–12 predictors in multivariable models. Assuming an incidence of the composite endpoint (MACE or MALE) of about 45%, the power analysis revealed that a minimum of approximately 150 patients was adequate to detect a medium effect (Cohen’s f2 = 0.15), and the final cohort of 167 patients met this requirement. Final multivariable models included 8–10 variables after Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)-based stepwise selection, well within planned limits. We analyzed between-group comparisons (event vs no event) using two-sample t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables (Diabetes duration, BMI, ABI, HbA1c, FBG, Total cholesterol, LDL-C, Triglycerides, Creatinine, eGFR, ApoB100), and the Pearson chi-squared test for categorical variables. Furthermore, we used univariable logistic regression models to assess the association of each baseline variable with the composite endpoint. Variables showing a p value < 0.05 in univariable analyses and a variance inflation factor (VIF) < 5, together with clinically relevant covariates, were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors of the outcome. Potential interactions among glycemic (diabetes duration, insulin, FBG, HbA1c) and renal (creatinine, eGFR) covariates were tested via likelihood ratio tests (all p > 0.10) and excluded. Results of regression analyses are presented in tables as regression coefficients with standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and p values, while the corresponding odds ratios (ORs) or hazard ratios (HRs) are reported and interpreted in the Results section. Additionally, we used a Cox proportional hazards model for time-to-event analyses, with the proportional hazards assumption evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and global tests. Missing data were managed using a complete-case approach [23]. Missing data were sparse (e.g., ApoB100: 4/167 [2.4%]; LDL-C: 8/167 [4.8%]; HbA1c: 24/167 [14.4%]). Complete-case analysis was employed (n = 157–165 per model). Little's MCAR test confirmed missingness completely at random (χ2 = 348.6, df = 612, p = 1.00), justifying no imputation. For all multivariable models (both logistic regression for 12-month event occurrence and Cox proportional hazards for time-to-first-event), only patients with complete data for all covariates were included in each model. For Cox models, complete information on follow-up time and event status was also required. No imputation was performed. Cox models thus include fewer observations than logistic models due to censoring requirements.

To limit the risk of overfitting, stepwise backward variable selection based on the AIC was used. ApoB100 was retained in all models as a pre-specified variable of interest. This approach balanced statistical significance, clinical relevance, and model parsimony while prioritizing ApoB100. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) with Somers' D transformation. Model calibration was evaluated with Hosmer–Lemeshow test across deciles of predicted risk. To account for its skewed distribution, multivariable logistic regression was also performed using log-transformed ApoB100 as a covariate. The discriminative ability of baseline ApoB100 levels was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) reported. Internal validation of the model's discriminative performance was carried out by bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations, using the Somers' D transformation of the ROC area. The optimal cut-off value for ApoB100 was determined using the Youden index, to maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to depict time to events according to the optimal ApoB100 cut-off, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Two logistic regression models were built to assess the incremental predictive value of baseline ApoB100 levels for adverse outcomes. The baseline model included conventional cardiovascular risk factors: age, sex, BMI, duration of T2DM, smoking history, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, CAD, CVD, ABI, Rutherford category, FBG, HbA1c, LDL, and renal function. The extended model comprised all these covariates plus baseline ApoB100 entered as a continuous variable. Discrimination was quantified by the AUC, and AUCs were compared using the roccomp command in STATA, which applies DeLong’s test for two correlated ROC curves. Model calibration was examined with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, grouping patients into deciles of predicted risk.

Discussion

This prospective trial found that elevated ApoB100 levels were associated with a higher incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events following LER in diabetic patients with CLTI. Incorporating baseline ApoB100 levels into models with traditional cardiovascular risk factors significantly improved risk prediction during follow-up 12 months after revascularization.

These findings align with a growing body of evidence suggesting that ApoB100 more reliably indicates atherogenic burden than conventional lipid measures, such as LDL-C [10]. ApoB100 quantifies the total number of potentially atherogenic lipoproteins, including low-density and very-low-density lipoproteins, each containing one ApoB100 molecule [24]. Large-scale observational studies and cardiovascular outcome trials have consistently shown that ApoB100 predicts major adverse cardiovascular events more accurately than LDL-C, particularly in patients receiving statin therapy, where LDL-C levels are reduced but particle numbers remain elevated[25]. Furthermore, ApoB100 has been linked to PAD severity and progression [26]; however, limited research has evaluated its prognostic value after revascularization procedures in high-risk diabetic populations with CLTI.

The study sample exemplifies the high-risk clinical profile of T2DM patients with CLTI undergoing LER, characterized by advanced age, prolonged diabetes duration, and advanced PAD. The high prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors underscores the systemic atherosclerotic burden characteristic of this subgroup, with persistent atherogenic load despite intensive lipid-lowering therapy. Suboptimal glycemic control alongside widespread antiplatelet use delineates a cohort at extreme risk for cardiovascular and limb events, ideally suited to evaluate prognostic biomarkers like ApoB100 in a real-world setting of diabetic CLTI.

In patients with T2DM and CLTI undergoing LER, our findings fit within a wider line of research identifying several biomarkers as predictors of cardiovascular and limb outcomes in a patient population that, despite optimal medical therapy, still manifests a broad spectrum of complications [7]. In similar clinical setting, elevated Lp(a) levels, a lipoprotein exhibiting multiple adverse, prothrombotic, and inflammatory effects, were independently associated with MACE and MALE, significantly improving discrimination of base clinical models [23]. Similarly, low omentin-1 levels, an adipokine with vasoprotective and anti-inflammatory properties, were linked to higher MACE and MALE incidence post-LER, suggesting that adipose tissue dysfunction also substantially contributes to cardiovascular and peripheral vulnerability in this context [2]. Furthermore, elevated baseline levels of C-reactive protein and interleukin 1 were associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes after LER [27]. Compared to these predominantly inflammatory markers, ApoB100 provides a direct measure of global atherogenic particle burden, integrating lipid residual risk information in a population already featuring systemic inflammation and advanced vascular disease.

The high composite event rate over 12 months underscores the extreme vulnerability of this advanced diabetic CLTI cohort, where traditional factors effectively delineate adverse outcomes, consistent with prior PAD revascularization studies [23, 27]. The ApoB100’s robust association with cardiovascular events establishes its independence as a marker of residual atherothrombotic risk, surpassing LDL-C by directly quantifying atherogenic particle number. ApoB100 also demonstrated excellent discriminative ability on ROC analysis and produced clear separation of Kaplan–Meier event-free survival curves between high- and low-ApoB100 levels. Its addition to traditional clinical models substantially improved overall predictive performance, representing a clinically meaningful enhancement for post-LER risk assessment.

Examining outcomes separately by endpoint type further contextualizes these findings. ApoB100 demonstrated superior risk stratification over LDL-C, with significant AUC improvements, despite guideline-directed LDL-C optimization in our cohort where nearly half experienced events. For MACE, baseline disparities (longer diabetes, prevalent CAD/CVD, elevated creatinine) delineate high cardiovascular hazard, with ApoB100 markedly discriminative. Dichotomized at the ROC threshold, ApoB100 retained independence. Although Cox time-to-event modeling did not achieve formal significance for continuous ApoB100, likely due to lower event numbers, CVD and hyperglycemia reaffirmed established roles. Regarding MALE outcomes, advanced age, CVD, advanced PAD, and insulin therapy identified vulnerability, aligning with diabetic PAD progression dynamics. However, ApoB100 demonstrated potent univariable and multivariable discrimination in logistic and Cox models. This finding, though modestly smaller than for MACE/composite, confirms ApoB100’s clinically relevant contribution to limb-specific risk, implicating atherogenic particle load in restenosis, thrombosis, and revascularization failure pathogenesis.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie the strong association between elevated ApoB100 levels and adverse outcomes observed in our cohort. First, ApoB100 serves as the structural scaffold for all atherogenic lipoproteins, mediating their retention within the arterial intima through binding to extracellular matrix proteoglycans [28]. Once retained, these particles undergo oxidative modification, promoting foam cell formation, endothelial dysfunction, and a robust inflammatory cascade involving adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokines [29]. In T2DM patients with CLTI, this biological upstream process likely accelerates plaque progression, in-stent restenosis, and acute thrombotic occlusions both systemically and in the revascularized limb vasculature. Furthermore, ApoB100 is also the obligatory carrier protein for Lp(a), linking elevated ApoB particle number to prothrombotic and proinflammatory effects that exacerbate residual risk despite LDL-C optimization [10].

The relationship between ApoB100 and conventional lipid markers warrants specific consideration. Unexpectedly, we found a negative association between ApoB100 and LDL-C without significant group differences. This finding suggests that the number of atherogenic particles, as captured by ApoB100, may be a more informative marker than cholesterol content alone in intensively treated patients and could help explain residual risk potentially driven by more atherogenic LDL particles [30]. These findings underscore ApoB100’s incremental clinical utility over standard lipid markers. Unlike LDL-C, which measures cholesterol content, ApoB100 directly quantifies atherogenic particle number, including dense, cholesterol-depleted LDL particles common in statin-treated patients. This distinction could explain persistent residual risk despite LDL-C target achievement. While ApoB100 strongly predicted MACE in logistic models, it did not significantly predict MACE in Cox time-to-event analysis (despite directionally favorable HR), likely reflecting limited power from lower MACE incidence. This finding suggests that ApoB100 predominantly captures acute/subacute post-procedural atherothrombotic risk rather than long-term coronary progression.

These findings have important clinical implications for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in T2DM patients with CLTI undergoing LER. Despite guideline-directed lipid-lowering therapy achieving low LDL-C levels in our cohort, nearly half experienced composite events, underscoring substantial residual risk. ApoB100 measurement could identify high-risk subgroups warranting intensified surveillance, dual antiplatelet therapy prolongation, or escalation to proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, which reduce ApoB100 by 50–60% beyond statins [31]. Notably, the early divergence of the Kaplan–Meier curves suggests that ApoB100 may capture not only long-term residual risk, but also early vulnerability after lower extremity revascularization. In addition, emerging therapies targeting ApoB100 synthesis (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides) or Lp(a), an ApoB-bound particle, may further mitigate this risk, particularly given ApoB100’s role as Lp(a)’s carrier protein. Future studies should validate ApoB100 cut-offs prospectively and explore integration into dedicated CLTI risk scores for personalized prevention.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. All patients were white Caucasians of Italian origin recruited from a single Italian tertiary referral center with rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. This design enhances internal validity but limits generalizability to ethnically diverse populations, alternative revascularization strategies, and different healthcare settings. Primary or secondary care contexts often present patients with distinct comorbidity profiles and disease severities. Settings without universal national health services show different patterns of access to care and advanced lipid-lowering therapies. These settings may include more patients of lower socioeconomic status or with delayed referral compared with our population. The relatively small cohort size limits power for subgroup analyses or rare events, while the 12-month follow-up captures early but not necessarily long-term trajectories. Furthermore, single baseline ApoB100 measurement precludes dynamic assessments, and absence of serial lipid therapy data hinders evaluation of modifiable pathways. Notably, event groups showed similar LDL-C despite higher ApoB100, consistent with ApoB100 superiorly capturing residual risk from cholesterol-depleted, dense particles in treated patients. Lp(a) measurement, unavailable for all, may further explain this. Moreover, exclusion of severe renal dysfunction to minimize competing risks limits applicability to advanced renal disease, where ApoB100 prognostic utility warrants separate investigation. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., plaque morphology, genetic factors) may persist despite adjustments. Larger, multicenter trials with extended follow-up, and larger samples of non-diabetic PAD patients are needed to confirm, validate, and expand these findings and to assess generalizability across diverse clinical settings.

The strengths of this investigation lie in its prospective design, enrolling a homogeneous high-risk cohort of T2DM patients with advanced CLTI undergoing LER, with systematic 12-month follow-up and predefined hard clinical endpoints. Baseline ApoB100 was quantified via validated immunoassay alongside comprehensive phenotyping, enabling robust multivariable adjustment. Rigorous statistical modeling substantiates ApoB100’s incremental prognostic value.

Conclusions

This prospective study demonstrates that baseline ApoB100 serves as a strong and independent predictor of MACE, MALE, and composite adverse outcomes following LER in patients with T2DM and CLTI. ApoB100 enhances risk discrimination beyond conventional factors by quantifying atherogenic particle number in a population with persistent residual risk despite LDL-C optimization. These results support routine ApoB100 assessment to guide intensified preventive strategies and underscore the necessity for larger trials to validate its integration into clinical practice for precision medicine in diabetic peripheral vascular disease.
