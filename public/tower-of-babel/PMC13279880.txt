Anesthetic Management for an Adult With Fontan Palliation During CPAP Titration and Cardiac Catheterization: A Case Report
Authors: Amanda Redding, Cora Bisbee, Michel Sabbagh, Gabrielle Fisher, Stephanie Gaydos, John Rhodes, Marc Hassid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present the anesthetic management for a patient with single ventricle Fontan palliation undergoing simultaneous cardiac catheterization and CPAP titration, where the anesthetic ensured the pertinent goals of maintaining patient comfort, simulation of normal sleep, and preservation of physiologic conditions.

Introduction

As medical and surgical therapies advance, patients with congenital heart disease are aging into adulthood and developing comorbidities that have complex physiologic implications. For example, Fontan operations are conducted to provide palliation for patients with single ventricle physiology and other congenital heart defects that cannot support biventricular circulation. This is achieved by connection of the systemic venous drainage directly to the pulmonary artery (Figure 1). This subset of patients is susceptible to declining cardiac output as they age and is sensitive to increases in pulmonary vascular resistance.

Obstructive sleep apnea is a common adult comorbidity which can lead to hypertension and heart failure, having a potentially exaggerated impact on a patient with single ventricle physiology. Anesthetic management of patients presenting with Fontan palliation alongside acquired comorbidities should integrate these concerns. We describe our experience managing anesthesia for an adult with Fontan circulation presenting to the cardiac catheterization lab for hemodynamic evaluation during continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy titration, in which the anesthesia ensured the goals of optimal patient comfort, simulated normal sleep, and preserved physiologic conditions. To our knowledge, this is one of few reported cases of anesthetic management for this purpose.

Case History

A 35‐year‐old woman with a history of anxiety and complex congenital heart disease consisting of single ventricle physiology (double inlet left ventricle, double outlet right ventricle, transposition of the great arteries, and ventricular septal defect) and Fontan palliation presented for cardiac catheterization assessment and titration of CPAP therapy under anesthesia. Her palliative cardiac course included pulmonary artery banding at 2 months old, right modified Blalock‐Taussig shunt and shunt revision at 2 years old, and bidirectional Glenn anastomosis as well as lateral tunnel Fontan completion at 5 years old. She subsequently underwent a subaortic resection at 19 years old. She has been followed by our Adult Congenital Heart Disease Clinic for the past 9 years.

She recently was seen in a pulmonology clinic for excessive daytime sleepiness, nocturnal awakenings, and snoring. On clinical examination, she measured 168 cm with a weight of 84.1 kg and a BMI of 29.8 kg/m2. Her baseline oxygen saturation ranged from 90% to 93%. A diagnostic sleep study demonstrated mild sleep apnea with an apnea hypopnea index (AHI) of 12.8/h and a minimum oxygen saturation of 85%. Titration of CPAP was requested to be performed during cardiac catheterization while obtaining hemodynamic measurements to determine the safety of increasing levels of CPAP (Table 1).

Anesthesia was requested by the patient due to anxiety and by the proceduralist based on institutional preferences. The patient was brought to the cardiac catheterization lab after midazolam (2 mg) was administered for anxiolysis. Bolus doses of fentanyl (50 μg) and dexmedetomidine (52 μg) were administered in divided doses prior to the start of the procedure, and a dexmedetomidine infusion was started at 0.5 μg/kg/h and maintained at 0.3–0.5 μg/kg/h for the duration of the procedure. With the provided combination of multimodal anesthetics, the patient maintained anxiolysis with tolerance of the procedure and CPAP mask placement as well as simulated natural sleep with spontaneous respirations. She remained arousable throughout the procedure, without respiratory depression or obstruction, hemodynamic instability, or need for ventilatory support.

Access for the cardiac catheterization was obtained using a 7F Pinnacle sheath in the right femoral vein and 5F Pinnacle sheath in the right femoral artery. Measurements were obtained using a 5F Pigtail in both the vein and artery. After baseline hemodynamic measurements were obtained, CPAP was initiated at 5 cmH2O via a nasal mask and was increased by 2–3 cmH2O every 10 min, with measurement of Fontan pressure, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), and mixed venous oxygen saturation at each titration (Figure 2). During this trial, the CPAP pressure increases halted at a pressure of 16 cmH2O at which time the predefined hemodynamic thresholds were met. The patient's Fontan pressure increased from a baseline of 15 to 17 mmHg and her PCWP increased from a baseline of 11 to 13 mmHg (Table 1). The patient subsequently underwent a sleep study for CPAP titration with the knowledge that she could hemodynamically tolerate a maximum of 16 cmH2O. During the sleep study, a CPAP of 7 cmH2O was sufficient to eliminate apneas and desaturations. Further pulmonology follow‐up noted good CPAP compliance with a residual AHI of 0.5/h.

Conclusion and Results

Fontan completion is the last stage in the single ventricle palliation pathway. After this surgical stage, all systemic venous blood flows passively and directly into the pulmonary arteries, bypassing the heart. Cardiac output depends on maintaining low pulmonary vascular resistance [1].

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common comorbidity in adulthood that can be associated with development of hypertension, heart failure, and arrhythmias, and is commonly treated with CPAP. Patients with Fontan palliation are particularly susceptible to decreases in cardiac output due to increases in intrathoracic pressure from systemic venous blood flowing passively to the pulmonary arteries without the benefit of valves or a pumping ventricle. Initiating CPAP at levels causing too great an increase in intrathoracic pressure can cause detrimental decreases in cardiac output in the patient with Fontan palliation. For this reason, CPAP titration is typically limited to adult patients with stable Fontan physiology.

Dexmedetomidine is a centrally acting highly selective alpha‐2 agonist with minimal ventilatory effects. Capasso et al. [2] found that dexmedetomidine sedation caused a significantly lower incidence of upper airway obstruction as compared to propofol, while Hsu et al. [3] determined dexmedetomidine preserved the hypercapnic ventilatory response. Dexmedetomidine was chosen for our patient as the primary means for sedation due to preservation of ventilation, therefore avoiding hypoxia and hypercarbia which would increase pulmonary vascular resistance [3] and increase Fontan pressures. Furthermore, dexmedetomidine has the added benefit of closely simulating natural sleep [1, 2, 4], an important aspect of the CPAP titration.

Nanayakkara et al. [5] describe an assessment method for Fontan patients with OSA as well as the clinical scenario that prompted development of the hemodynamic assessment. This group used an assessment based on Fontan pressure measurements alongside echocardiographic findings. They observed a significant fall in Fontan pressures during obstructive episodes followed by increases thought to be related to transient rises in pulmonary vascular resistance. Interestingly, the individual who eventually became their first study patient developed heart failure requiring hospitalization after initiation of CPAP at 13 cmH2O with only echocardiographic assessment. Later, a more robust hemodynamic assessment established a CPAP maximum of 10 cmH2O for the patient.

In our case, the decision to perform a catheterization lab assessment came from the methodology described by Watson et al. [6]. We felt measuring Fontan pressure, PCWP, and mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) at increasing levels of CPAP would provide the best chance at detecting hemodynamic compromise. Patients with Fontan palliation are typically familiar with the cardiac catheterization lab, and in a subset of this patient population, local anesthetic without sedation suffices. However, many patients benefit from carefully administered anesthesia. This can be related to either patient anxiety, expectations, or routine institutional practices. It is preferred to choose an anesthetic regimen that has minimal effects on hemodynamics and allows for spontaneous ventilation in order to avoid decreases in venous return and increases in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) from positive pressure ventilation. When taking the CPAP assessment into account, it is essential to avoid over‐sedation to approximate the resting state and avoid gastric entrainment of air due to airway relaxation. Dexmedetomidine, an alpha 2 agonist, exhibits sedative and analgesic properties while allowing for maintenance of spontaneous ventilation [4].

Discussion

To our knowledge after performing a systematic literature search, this represents one of the few known reports of anesthesia management for CPAP titration and simultaneous heart catheterization in patients with Fontan circulation. In our case, the combination of midazolam, fentanyl, and dexmedetomidine provided an excellent balance of patient comfort and preservation of physiologic conditions allowing meaningful CPAP titration.
