Abstract
. We introduce modular inequalities for complements of plane curves, based on a Combinatorial Aomoto complex construction associated with the weak combinatorial type of a curve. We use this as a tool to investigate twisted Alexander polynomials, in particular to study the characteristic polynomial of the Milnor fiber associated to a projective plane curve, i.e. the classical Alexander polynomial. We give criteria for the non-triviality of the resonance in degree 1 1 , over a positive characteristic field, for curves with quasi fiber–type structure and we compute lower bounds for the multiplicities of some roots of twisted Alexander polynomials for this type of curves. We apply these results to theoretically compute the multiplicities of certain roots of twisted Alexander polynomials for fiber–type curves.

1.
Introduction

The Milnor fiber
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
of a complex projective curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
, defined by a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
, is the smooth affine hypersurface
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\mid f(x,y,z)=1\}
. The action of a
d
-th root of unity over
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
by
h(x,y,z)=(\xi x,\xi y,\xi z)
endows the homology
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})
with a
\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]
–module structure. Since
h_{*}
has finite order
d
, this is a semi-simple torsion module. Moreover, since
\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]
is a PID each of its torsion components is generated by a (Laurent) polynomial associated with
\phi_{k}
, where
\phi_{k}(t)
is a cyclotomic polynomial of order
k|d
. The product of all these polynomials

(1)

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\prod_{k|d}\phi_{k}^{b_{k}}(t)

is referred to as the
Alexander polynomial
of
\mathcal{C}
. The module
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})
controls the topology of a
d
-cyclic cover of the complement
\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
ramifying with maximal order at every irreducible component of
\mathcal{C}
, that is, associated with the epimorphism
\varepsilon:H_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}
defined as
\varepsilon(\mu_{i})=1
for any meridian
\mu_{i}
around an irreducible component
\mathcal{C}_{i}
of
\mathcal{C}
. If one is interested in cyclic
d
-covers of
\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
associated with other epimorphisms
\varepsilon:H_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}
defined as
\varepsilon(\mu_{i})=m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
it is useful to define the
twisted
Milnor fiber
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C},\bar{m})=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\mid F_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots F_{r}^{m_{r}}(x,y,z)=1\}
associated with the weights
\bar{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})
as long as
\gcd(\bar{m})=1
, which is the condition for
\varepsilon
to be an epimorphism.
The torsion of the
\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]
–module
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C},\bar{m});\mathbb{Q})

also defines the
twisted Alexander polynomial

(2)

\Delta_{(\mathcal{C},\bar{m})}(t)=\prod_{k|d}\phi_{k}^{b_{k,\bar{m}}}(t).

For complex projective line arrangements
\mathcal{L}
, Papadima–Suciu
[
20
]
provide
combinatorial
upper bounds for

b_{k}
in (
1
) for the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{\mathcal{L}}(t)
and
k
a prime power. In the theory of arrangements of hyperplanes, the
combinatorics
of an arrangement reverberates into the topological and algebraic properties of the arrangement and of its complement. Let us mention, for instance, the emblematic result by Brieskorn
[
3
]
and completed by Orlik–Solomon in
[
19
]
which states that the cohomology algebra of the complement of an arrangement of hyperplanes with integer coefficients is completely determined by the intersection lattice of the arrangement.

The purpose of this paper is to give estimates for the multiplicities
b_{k}
of the classical Alexander polynomial in (
1
) and
b_{k,\bar{m}}
for the twisted version in (
2
) in case
k=p^{s}
, for
p
prime.
In order to do that we use two different tools that offer respectively upper and lower bounds to these multiplicities
b_{k}
and
b_{k,\bar{m}}
.

Upper bounds are obtained via the
weak combinatorial type
of a curve
\mathcal{C}
, which can be seen as a generalization to curves of the classical concept of combinatorics of line arrangements, developed by the first author in
[
4
]
and jointly with Matei in
[
5
]
. The authors prove that the weak combinatorial type of a curve determines its cohomology ring.

The cohomology ring over a finite field
\Bbbk
produces a complex given by multiplication by a 1-form
\omega\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
denoted by

(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)

and called the
Aomoto Complex
. Note that the cohomology of these complexes produces a filtration of
H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
called the
resonance varieties
of
X
and defined as

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(X,\Bbbk):=\{\omega\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)\mid\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)\geq k\}.

On the other hand, given the weak combinatorial type of a curve we define in §
3
a graded algebra
A_{W,\Bbbk}^{*}
over a field
\Bbbk
. Multiplication by an element of order one
\omega\in A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}
also produces a family of complexes called
Combinatorial Aomoto Complex
and denoted by
(A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1},\cdot\curlywedge\omega)

whose cohomology produces a filtration of
A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}
called the
combinatorial resonance varieties
of
W
and defined as

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(W,\Bbbk):=\{\omega\in A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}\mid\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(A_{W,\Bbbk}^{*},\cdot\curlywedge\omega)\geq k\}.

Note that

\{0\}=\dots={\mathcal{R}}_{-1}(W,\Bbbk)={\mathcal{R}}_{0}(W,\Bbbk)\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}_{1}(W,\Bbbk)\subseteq\dots\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}_{r}(W,\Bbbk)={\mathcal{R}}_{r+1}(W,\Bbbk)=\dots=A^{1}_{W,\Bbbk},

where
r=\dim_{\Bbbk}A^{1}_{W,\Bbbk}
.
Both families of complexes are neither isomorphic nor quasi-isomorphic in general; however, one of our main results is Theorem
7
which states that there is a morphism of complexes that allows one to identify their resonance varieties, namely,

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(W,\Bbbk)\cong{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(X,\Bbbk).

Combining this with
[
20
, Theorem C]
we compute in §
4
, Theorem
11
, upper bounds on
b_{k}
for
k
a prime power, using combinatorial resonance varieties of the weak combinatorial type of the curve. We illustrate this result by computing bounds for the multiplicities of roots of the classical Alexander polynomials of order powers of primes, for a series of examples.

We also present in §
4.1
a triviality criterion for the cohomology in degree
1
of the Combinatorial Aomoto complex with coefficients in a finite field. This in turn enables us to formulate combinatorial conditions to ensure
b_{k}=0
, see Theorem
22
.
Section §
4.2
is devoted to the twisted counterpart of the previous results. In addition, in
§
4.3
we present some examples.

A tool for obtaining lower bounds for
b_{k}
, and more generally for
b_{k,\bar{m}}
, is presented in §
5
and applied to
(quasi) fiber-type curves
, that is, the class of curves that can be obtained as a union of fibers of a pencil. These lower bounds depend on certain properties of the pencil.
This class contains as a subclass the fiber–type curves, also known as pencil–type curves, in particular the Halpen pencils of index
k\geq 1
defined in
[
10
]
, and the projective line arrangements that admit combinatorial pencil/multinet structures as defined in
[
5
,
13
]
.

The lower bounds for
b_{k,\bar{m}}
in the (quasi) fiber-type curve case are given precisely by the number and multiplicity of the non-reduced fibers of the pencil
\mathcal{C}
is a union of, but also, and more surprisingly, by the extra multiple fibers that are not part of
\mathcal{C}
, see Theorem
37
. This generalizes several recent results, for instance
[
9
, Theorems 3.2 – 3.4]
.
It is worth noticing that this result applies not only to roots of unity of order prime powers as the results in section §
4
did.

In §
6
we present a series of applications to reprove known results by Dimca-Pokora-Sticlaru in
[
9
]
on Halphen curves and to provide new examples of calculations of
b_{k}
when it coincides with the upper and lower bounds obtained above.

Finally, in §
7

we revisit Yoshinaga’s counterexample to a conjecture by Papadima–Suciu
[
21
]
. The conjecture questioned the existence, in the class of arrangements of hyperplanes, of examples for which the modular inequalities are strict. We approach Yoshinaga’s example from a new perspective, i.e. viewed as a quasi fiber–type curve providing an alternative proof.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a reduced curve of degree
d
and let
\mathcal{C}:=\cup_{i=0}^{r}\mathcal{C}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\{F_{i}=0\}
, be the decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
into irreducible components.
Denote
\mathbb{P}^{2}:=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

2.1.
Torsion freeness of the homology of the complement of a projective curve

An initial condition of torsion freeness on the homology of the space
\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
is required in our context (
[
21
, Theorem C]
). This follows from a well-known result in the context of complements of hypersurfaces in the complex projective space (see for instance
[
8
, §4 Prop. 1.3]
).

Proposition 1
.

In the above notation, if
{\gcd}(\deg(F_{0}),\dots,\deg(F_{r}))=1
, then
H_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C};\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

2.2.
Milnor fiber and cohomology with local coefficients

For a reduced degree
d
curve
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
as before, we have a global Milnor fibration

\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{F_{0}\cdots F_{r}}\mathbb{C}^{*}.

Consider the associated
Milnor fiber

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}):=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\mid(F_{0}\cdots F_{r})(x)=1\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
There is an action on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by the multiplication by a primitive root of unity of degree
d
. Since
F_{i}
is homogeneous, this restricts to an action on
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
:

h:\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),

the so-called
geometric monodromy
. This in turn induces an action

h_{*}:H_{*}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H_{*}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})

on the homology groups of the Milnor fiber, the
algebraic monodromy
. This action defines a
\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]
-module structure on each homology group
H_{*}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})
, where
t
acts as the algebraic monodromy. Since
h^{d}=id
,
H_{*}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})
is a torsion module that is annihilated by
(t^{d}-1)=\Pi_{k|d}\phi_{k}
, where
\phi_{k}
is the
k
-th cyclotomic polynomial.

Computing the algebraic monodromy is a difficult and open problem, even for
*=1
, see e.g.
[
17
]
or
[
16
]
for results on the algebraic monodromy of the first rational homology module of the Milnor fiber for graphic arrangements or for complex reflection arrangements.

Let us recall the decomposition of
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})
with respect to the algebraic monodromy action:

H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C});\mathbb{Q})=\bigoplus_{k|d}(\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]/\phi_{k})^{b_{k}}.

We define the Alexander polynomial of the curve
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, by the formula

(3)

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\prod_{k|d}\phi_{k}^{b_{k}}.

The exponents
b_{k}
in the above formula are connected to the cohomology of the complement
\tilde{X}:=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
with coefficients in certain rank-one local systems. Let us briefly recall this connection.
Denote by
H^{1}(\tilde{X};\mathbb{C}_{\rho})
the cohomology with coefficients in the rank-one local system
\rho:\pi_{1}(\tilde{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
If
\rho(x)=\xi,\;\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
fixed, for all elements
x
of the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis, i.e., if
\rho
is constant on the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis, then the corresponding local system is called
equimonodromical
. In this case, we denote the local system
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
as
\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
by convention.
For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
, let
\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
be a primitive
k
-th root of unity.
Then it is known that
the multiplicity of
\xi_{k}
as a root of the Alexander polynomial, i.e. the exponents
b_{k}
from (
3
), satisfy the following equality

(4)

b_{k}={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}H^{1}(\tilde{X};\mathbb{C}_{\xi_{k}}).

For a generalization of the above constructions to non-reduced curves, see §
4.2
.

2.3.
Aomoto complexes

We need one more ingredient before we can introduce modular inequalities.
Let
X
be a finite type CW-complex and let
\Bbbk
be an arbitrary field. If
\omega\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
is such that
\omega^{2}=0
, one can define a cochain complex structure on the cohomology ring
H^{*}(X;\Bbbk)
with the differential defined by the multiplication by
\omega
:

(5)

H^{0}(X;\Bbbk)\overset{\cdot\wedge\omega}{\longrightarrow}H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)\overset{\cdot\wedge\omega}{\longrightarrow}H^{2}(X;\Bbbk)\overset{\cdot\wedge\omega}{\longrightarrow}\dots

This cochain complex is called the
Aomoto complex associated with
\omega
.

2.4.
Modular inequalities for curve complements

Generalizing a result by Cogolludo for rational curves from
[
4
]
, Cogolludo-Matei give in
[
5
]
a presentation of the cohomology module of the complement of an arbitrary reduced curve in the complex projective plane. Consider a projective curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}

\mathcal{C}_{0}

\mathcal{C}

We define

of
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
It follows by

is generated, as a vector space,
by the logarithmic

\sigma_{i}:=d\log\left({F_{i}}/{F_{0}^{d_{i}}}\right)

, where

is an irreducible polynomial defining the irreducible component

of

, as in
[
5

.

Assume there is a preferred set of generators for
\pi_{1}(X)
, then a homomorphism
\omega:\pi_{1}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is completely defined by a set of
r

\pi_{1}(X)
in
\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\pi_{1}(X),\mathbb{Z})\equiv\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(H_{1}(X),\mathbb{Z})\equiv H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})

with an element
\omega=\sum_{i}m_{i}\sigma_{i}
in

.

For a given complex number
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, one can define a rank-one local system
\rho_{\omega,\zeta}
of coefficients, i.e., an element of
\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\pi_{1}(X),\mathbb{C}^{*})

If
\zeta

is called a

local system.

By Proposition
1

is torsion free.
It follows that the modular inequalities stated by Papadima-Suciu in
[

hold for

We recall a version of their result stated in our particular context.
In order to do so, let us fix some notation.
Let
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for a prime
p
denote the finite field with
p

\pi_{1}(X)\overset{\omega}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Z}\to\Bbbk
denote the homomorphism defined by the classes of the integers
(m_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
in
\Bbbk
. Denote

\omega_{\Bbbk}=\sum_{i}m_{i}\sigma_{i}\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
.

Theorem 2
.

[
20
, Theorem C]

Let
X
be the affine complement of a projective curve
\mathcal{C}
,
p
a prime number,
\xi
a primitive root of the unity of order

, and let
\rho=\rho_{\omega,\xi}
. Then

(6)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{1}(X;\mathbb{C}_{\rho})\leq{\rm dim}_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega_{\Bbbk}).

Remark 3

If
X
is the affine complement of the curve
\mathcal{C}
and

3.
The Combinatorial Aomoto Complex

The purpose of this section is to present a complex that is quasi-isomorphic to the standard Aomoto complex,
and whose definition only depends on the weak combinatorial type of a curve.

3.1.
The weak combinatorial type of a projective curve

We will assume that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
is a plane projective curve, that is, the reduced structure of the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial in three variables. Now we define the

weak combinatorial type of
\mathcal{C}
as follows.
Let
S=\Sing(\mathcal{C})
be the collection of singular points of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbf{r}=\{1,...,r\}
the set of
subindices of the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}
.
For any
P\in S
, let
\Delta_{P}
be the collection of irreducible local branches of
\mathcal{C}
at
P
.
Consider the following map
\phi_{P}:\Delta_{P}\to\mathbf{r}
, where
\phi_{P}(\delta)=i
if
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is such that
\delta
is a branch in
(\mathcal{C}_{i},P)
. Define by
\textrm{SP}^{2}_{\phi_{P}}(\Delta_{P})
the symmetric
sum of
\Delta_{P}
outside of the
\phi_{P}
-diagonal, that is

\textrm{SP}^{2}_{\phi_{P}}(\Delta_{P}):=\{(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\in\Delta_{P}^{2}\mid\phi_{P}(\delta_{1})\neq\phi_{P}(\delta_{2})\}/\Sigma_{2}.

One has the map
\mu_{P}:\textrm{SP}^{2}_{\phi_{P}}(\Delta_{P})\to\mathbb{N}
, where
\mu_{P}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
is the intersection
number of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
at
P
.
Then the list
W_{\mathcal{C}}:=(\mathbf{r},S,\{\Delta_{P},\phi_{P},\mu_{P}\}_{P\in S})
is the
weak combinatorial type
of
\mathcal{C}
.
In
[
5
]
, the
combinatorial type
of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as
\tilde{W}_{\mathcal{C}}:=(W_{\mathcal{C}},\bar{d},\bar{g})
, where
W_{\mathcal{C}}
is the weak combinatorial type of
\mathcal{C}
,
\bar{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
is the list of degrees, and
\bar{g}
is the list of genera of the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}
. Note that, according to
[
5
]
,
\tilde{W}_{\mathcal{C}}
determines the ring structure
H^{*}(X;\mathbb{Z})
of the affine complement
X
of
\mathcal{C}
with respect to a line at infinity in general position.

3.2.
A graded algebra associated with a weak combinatorial type

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a plane curve and
W=(\mathbf{r},S,\{\Delta_{P},\phi_{P},\mu_{P}\}_{P\in S})
its weak combinatorial type,

\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
p
being prime.
For simplicity we will assume
\#\mathbf{r}>2
. In that case one can recover the degrees
d_{i}
of the components as follows. For every pair of elements
i,j\in\mathbf{r}
,
i\neq j
, define

d_{ij}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in S,\\
\phi_{P}(\delta_{i})=i,\\
\phi_{P}(\delta_{j})=j\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\delta_{j}).

Note that
d_{ij}=d_{ji}=\mathcal{C}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{j}
. Then one can recover
d_{i}
by considering any triple of different indices

(which exists by hypothesis) as

d_{i}:=\sqrt{\frac{d_{ij}d_{ik}}{d_{jk}}}.

We consider

(1)

A_{W}^{0}:=\Bbbk
,

(2)

A_{W}^{1}:=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbf{r}}\sigma_{i}\Bbbk
,

(3)

A_{W}^{2}:=A_{W}^{2,0}\oplus A_{W}^{2,\infty}
, where

A_{W}^{2,\infty}:=\langle\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}:i\in\mathbf{r}\rangle_{\Bbbk}/\langle\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}:p\nmid d_{i}\rangle_{\Bbbk},\quad A_{W}^{2,0}:=\bigoplus_{P\in S}\frac{A_{P}\wedge A_{P}}{I_{P}},\quad A_{P}:=\langle\psi_{P}^{\delta}:\delta\in\Delta_{P}\rangle_{\Bbbk}

and

(8)

I_{P}:=\{\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}+\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{3}}+\psi_{P}^{\delta_{3}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\}+\{\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}\mid\#\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P})=1\}\subset A_{P}\wedge A_{P}.

(4)

A_{W}^{i}=0
(
i\geq 3
).

Finally, we turn
A^{*}_{W}
into a graded algebra by means of the following product

(9)

\sigma_{i}\wedge\sigma_{j}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in S,\\
\delta_{1}\in\phi_{P}^{-1}(i),\\
\delta_{2}\in\phi_{P}^{-1}(j)\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\ \psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}+d_{j}\bar{\psi}_{\infty}^{i}-d_{i}\bar{\psi}_{\infty}^{j}.

Definition 4
.

Given an element of degree one, say
\omega\in A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}
, one can consider the complex

(A_{W,\Bbbk}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)
described by using the algebra structure as follows:

(10)

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}0&\to&A_{W}^{0}&\smash{\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\wedge\omega}}&A_{W}^{1}&\smash{\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\wedge\omega}}&A_{W}^{2}&\to&0\\
&&k&\mapsto&k\omega&&&&\\
&&&&\alpha&\mapsto&\alpha\wedge\omega.&&\end{array}

Such a complex
(A^{*}_{W,\Bbbk},\cdot\wedge\omega)
will be called the
Combinatorial Aomoto Complex of
W
with coefficients in
\Bbbk
. The reference to
\Bbbk
will be omitted if the field is clear from the context.

Remark 5
.

In order to describe a matrix of the homomorphism

A_{W}^{1}\ \smash{\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\wedge\omega}}\ A_{W}^{2}
we need a basis for

A_{W}^{2}
(note that
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{r}}
is a basis for the vector space
A_{W}^{1}
).
We will present a basis that will come in handy in the future.

•

For any point
P\in S
one can choose a local branch
\bar{\delta}\in\Delta_{P}

(resp. an element
\psi_{P}^{\bar{\delta}}\in A_{P}
), which will be referred to as the

preferred branch at
P
(resp.
preferred form at
P
).

•

Using this notation, we observe that the set

(11)

\{\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta}:=\psi_{P}^{\bar{\delta}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta}\}_{P\in S}

is a basis of the vector space
A^{2,0}_{W}
.

One has the relation

\psi_{P}^{\delta_{i}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{j}}=\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta_{j}}-\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta_{i}},

where
\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\bar{\delta}}=0
for the preferred branch
\bar{\delta}
by convention.

With respect to this basis, the product
A^{1}_{W}\wedge A^{1}_{W}
can be defined as

(12)

\sigma_{i}\wedge\sigma_{j}:=\sum_{P\in S}(\mu_{P}(\delta_{j},\mathcal{C}_{i})\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta_{j}}-\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta_{i}})+d_{j}\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}-d_{i}\bar{\psi}^{j}_{\infty},

extending by bi-linearity, where
\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})
denotes the multiplicity of the intersection of
the branch
\delta_{i}
and the curve
\mathcal{C}_{j}
at
P
, i.e.,

{\displaystyle{\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})=\sum_{\phi_{P}(\delta_{j})=j}\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\delta_{j})}}
.

As usual, one can consider the
cohomology jumping loci
of such a family of complexes
parametrized by
A_{W}^{1}
to obtain the family of resonance varieties associated with
A_{W,\Bbbk}^{*}
.

Definition 6
.

The subset of
A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(W,\Bbbk):=\{\omega\in A_{W,\Bbbk}^{1}\mid\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(A_{W,\Bbbk}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)\geq k\}

is called the
k
-th combinatorial resonance variety
of
W
.

Associated with the affine complement of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
X=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus(\mathcal{C}_{0}\cup\mathcal{C})=\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
and with
any
1
-cohomology form
\omega\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
, one can define the cohomology Aomoto complex with respect to
a field
\Bbbk
as
(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)
. The
k
-th resonance variety

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{C},\Bbbk)
of
\mathcal{C}
can be defined as the jumping loci of the family of cohomology Aomoto complexes
parametrized by

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{C},\Bbbk)=\{\omega\in H^{1}(X;\Bbbk)\mid\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)\geq k\}

Theorem 7
.

Under the above hypotheses,
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{C},\Bbbk)\cong{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk)
.

Proof.

Since the
k
-th resonance variety
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{C},\Bbbk)
can be computed as the first cohomology jumping loci of the Aomoto complex
(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)
of the affine complement
X
of
\mathcal{C}
, it is enough to show that the Aomoto complex
(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)
is 1-quasi-isomorphic to the Combinatorial Aomoto complex
(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\cdot\wedge\omega)
, that is,
there is a morphism of complexes producing
H^{k}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\cdot\wedge\omega)\cong H^{k}(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)
for
k\leq 1
.
In order to distinguish both products, we will denote by
\cdot\curlywedge\omega
the product in
A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}
as defined in (
12
) and by
\cdot\wedge\omega
the product in
H^{*}(X;\Bbbk)
.

For simplicity, let us denote
W=W_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Note that
H^{k}(X;\Bbbk)\cong A^{k}_{W}
as long as
k\neq 2
. Recall from
[
5
]
that
H^{2}(X;\Bbbk)
can be decomposed as

H^{2}(X;\Bbbk)=V_{0}\oplus V_{\infty}\oplus V_{g}\oplus\bar{V}_{g}
, where
V_{0}\cong A^{2,0}_{W}
.
This isomorphism is given by
\psi_{P}^{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}\mapsto\psi_{P}^{\delta}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta^{\prime}}
.
One can consider an intermediate graded ring
\tilde{A}^{*}
defined as

\tilde{A}^{k}=\begin{cases}V_{0}\oplus V_{\infty}\cong H^{2}(X;\Bbbk)/(V_{g}\oplus\bar{V}_{g})&\textrm{ if }k=2\\
A^{k}_{W}&\textrm{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\sigma_{i}\wedge\sigma_{j}
is inherited by the product in
H^{*}(X;\Bbbk)
. Note that
there is a projection morphism
H^{k}(X;\Bbbk)\to\tilde{A}^{k}
satisfying that

\sigma_{i}\wedge\sigma_{j}\in V_{0}\oplus V_{\infty}
, which implies

H^{1}(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)\cong H^{1}(\tilde{A}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)
,
and hence
the projection morphism
(H^{*}(X;\Bbbk),\cdot\wedge\omega)\to(\tilde{A}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)
is a 1-quasi-isomorphism.

Consider now the morphism
(A^{*}_{W},\cdot\curlywedge\omega)\to(\tilde{A}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)

given by the identity if
*\neq 2
and, for
*=2
, defined on the generators by

\psi_{P}^{\delta}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta^{\prime}}\mapsto\psi_{P}^{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}
,

\bar{\psi}_{\infty}^{i}\mapsto\sum_{k_{i}=2}^{d_{i}}\psi_{\infty}^{i,k_{i}}
.
Note that we can consider
A^{2}_{W}\subset\tilde{A}^{2}
via this correspondence.
We want to show that

(A^{*}_{W},\cdot\curlywedge\omega)\to(\tilde{A}^{*},\cdot\wedge\omega)
is a 1-quasi-isomorphism.
This is equivalent to proving that

\tilde{K}=\ker\tilde{A}^{1}\xrightarrow{\wedge\omega}\tilde{A}^{2}=\ker A_{W}^{1}\xrightarrow{\curlywedge\omega}A_{W}^{2}=K
.
By construction, it is clear that
\tilde{K}\subset K
. For the converse, assume

\alpha=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}\in\tilde{A}^{1}=A^{1}_{W}
,

\omega=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}\in\tilde{A}^{1}=A^{1}_{W}
. Note that

(13)

\alpha\wedge\omega=\sum_{P}\lambda_{\delta,P}\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}.

where the equality (
13
) is seen in
\tilde{A}^{2}
,
\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}:=\sum_{k_{i}=2}^{d_{i}}\psi^{i,k_{i}}_{\infty}
and
\bar{\psi}_{P}^{\delta}:=\psi_{P}^{\bar{\delta},\delta}
.
One needs to show that
\lambda_{i}=0
if
\alpha\in K
and
p\nmid d_{i}
.
Using definition (
12
), one obtains

(14)

\lambda_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}(\alpha_{k}\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{k})=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\sum_{k}d_{k}\beta_{k}&\sum_{k}d_{k}\alpha_{k}\end{array}\right|.

On the other hand, by (
13
) and (
12
), if
\phi_{P}(\delta)=i
, then one has

\lambda_{\delta,P}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})(\alpha_{k}\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{k}).

The condition
\alpha\in K
implies
\lambda_{\delta,P}=0
, i.e.

(15)

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\beta_{k}&\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\alpha_{k}\end{array}\right|=0.

Note that, Bezout’s theorem implies
\sum_{P\in S,\phi_{P}(\delta)=i}\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\beta_{k}=d_{i}\sum_{k}d_{k}\beta_{k}
and, likewise,
\sum_{P\in S,\phi_{P}(\delta)=i}\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\alpha_{k}=d_{i}\sum_{k}d_{k}\alpha_{k}
.
Hence, summing the determinants (
15
) over
(P\in S,\phi_{P}(\delta)=i)
we get

0=\sum_{P\in S,\phi_{P}(\delta)=i}\lambda_{\delta,P}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
d_{i}\sum_{k}d_{k}\beta_{k}&d_{i}\sum_{k}d_{k}\alpha_{k}\end{array}\right|=d_{i}\lambda_{i},

where the last equality follows by (
14
). Hence
d_{i}\lambda_{i}=0
, which implies
\lambda_{i}=0
, since we are assuming
p\nmid d_{i}
.
This ends the proof.
∎

The conditions described in (
15
) will be frequently used in the rest of the paper. We will often refer to them as
resonance conditions
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}
given by
\alpha=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i},\;\beta=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}
. Then
\alpha\curlywedge\beta=0
implies that the resonance conditions (
15
) hold for all
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
and all
\delta\in\Delta_{P}
. Moreover, the resonance conditions (
15
) are also sufficient in the statement above if
p\nmid d_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbf{r}
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the proof of Theorem
7
.
∎

Remark 9
.

Corollary
8
is an analog of
[
11
, Theorem 3.5]
, a result on matroids, and implicitly of
[

, Theorem 2.5]
, its version for realizable matroids, i.e. for hyperplane arrangements. The construction of the Combinatorial Aomoto complex described in

Corollary 10
.

If
P\in S
is such that
\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P})=\{i,j\}
and
\alpha=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k},\;\beta=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\sigma_{k}
with
\alpha\curlywedge\beta=0
, then either
\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{j}&\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|=0

or
\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})=\mu_{P}(\delta_{j},\mathcal{C}_{i})=0\in\Bbbk
for all branches
\delta_{i},\delta_{j}\in\Delta_{P}
, where
\phi_{P}(\delta_{i})=i
and
\phi_{P}(\delta_{j})=j
.

Proof.

Assume
\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})\in\Bbbk^{*}
for some
\delta_{i}\in\Delta_{P}
for which
\phi_{P}(\delta_{i})=i
. Then, the hypothesis

\alpha\curlywedge\beta=0
and (
15
) imply the following resonance condition

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})\beta_{j}&\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|=\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j})\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{j}&\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|=0,

which proves
\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{j}&\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|=0
.
∎

4.
Modular inequalities: upper bounds

Consider as above the affine complement
X
of a projective curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Consider also a rational character
\rho:\pi_{1}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
given by
\sigma_{i}\mapsto\xi^{m_{i}}_{k}
, where
\xi_{k}

is a primitive
k
-root of unity and
k
is a prime power
k=p^{s}
. Denote
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Consider
(A^{*}_{W,\Bbbk},\cdot\wedge\omega_{\rho})
the Combinatorial Aomoto complex associated with
W=W_{\mathcal{C}}
with
\Bbbk
coefficients, where
\omega_{\rho}:=\sum_{i}m_{i}\sigma_{i}\in A^{1}_{W,\Bbbk}
.
As an immediate consequence of Theorem
2
, Remark
3
, and Theorem
7
, one has the following result.

Theorem 11
.

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{1}(X;\mathbb{C}_{\rho})\leq\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(A^{*}_{W,\Bbbk},\cdot\wedge\omega_{\rho})
.

Remark 12
.

Note that Theorem
11
implies that the roots of the Alexander polynomial of an irreducible plane curve
\mathcal{C}
cannot be primitive roots of unity of order
p^{s}
,
p
prime number,
s\geq 1
. This recovers a classical result of Zariski from
[
24
]
, restated in modern terms and re-proved in
[
2
, Proposition 2.1]
.

4.1.
A criterion for triviality

Definition 13
.

Consider a projective curve
\mathcal{C}
and its weak combinatorics
W_{\mathcal{C}}
. Assume there is a point
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
such that
\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P})=\{i,j\}
and
\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\in\Bbbk^{*}
for some branch with
\phi_{P}(\delta)=i
. Such a point will be called
a
p
-transversal point of
\mathcal{C}
. Two components
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}
are called
p
-transversal at a point
if there is a
p
-transversal point
P\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\cap\mathcal{C}_{j}
of
\mathcal{C}
.

As an immediate consequence of Corollary
10
,
one has the following basic property of
p
-transversal points with respect to resonance varieties.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}
two
p
-transversal components at a point in
\mathcal{C}
.
Consider
\alpha,\beta\in A^{1}_{W,\Bbbk}
,
\alpha=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k}
, and

\beta=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\sigma_{k}
. If
\alpha\curlywedge\beta=0
, then

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{i}&\beta_{i}\\
\alpha_{j}&\beta_{j}\end{array}\right|=0\in\Bbbk.

In the special case of ordinary singularities, the resonance condition becomes simpler to describe. Recall that an ordinary singularity
P\in S
is given locally as the intersection of smooth branches that intersect pairwise transversally, that is,
\mu_{P}(\delta_{i},\delta_{j})=1
if
i\neq j
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{C}_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}_{i_{s}}\subset{\rm Sing}(\mathcal{C})
be an ordinary singularity such that
\#\Delta_{P}=s
and
\omega_{1},\beta\in A^{1}_{W,\Bbbk}
,
\omega_{1}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}
, and

\beta=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}
. If
\omega_{1}\curlywedge\beta=0
, then either

(16)

\beta_{i_{1}}=\dots=\beta_{i_{s}}\,\,\textrm{ if }s\in\Bbbk^{*},

or

(17)

\beta_{i_{1}}+\dots+\beta_{i_{s}}=0\in\Bbbk\textrm{ if }s=0\in\Bbbk.

Proof.

Since
\omega_{1}\curlywedge\beta=0
, the resonance condition (
15
) at
P
becomes

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i}&1\\
\beta_{i_{1}}+\dots+\beta_{i_{s}}&s\end{array}\right|=0
for all
i\in\{i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\}
.
∎

The purpose of the rest of this section is to describe a reduction method that can decide the triviality of

H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)
for a given
\alpha=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
.

One can use Lemma
14
repeatedly when the components of a curve are pairwise
p
-transversal at a point, in order
to calculate the cohomology of
(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)
.
For technical reasons, we sometimes need
\alpha=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}
to be non-coordinate, that is, such that
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in\mathbf{r}
. The following object will be useful to describe this method.

Definition 16
.

For a curve
\mathcal{C}
, one can define the
p
-transversality graph
as the non-oriented graph
\Gamma^{p}_{\mathcal{C}}=(V,E)
whose set of vertices
V=\mathbf{r}
and such that
e=\{i,j\}\in E
is an edge if
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}
are
p
-transversal at a point.

Proposition 17
.

If
\Gamma^{p}_{\mathcal{C}}
is complete, then

H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)=0
for any non-zero
\alpha\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
.

Moreover, if
\Gamma^{p}_{\mathcal{C}}
is connected, then

H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)=0
for any non-coordinate
\alpha\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
.

Proof.

Assume
\beta=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\sigma_{k}\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
such that
\beta\curlywedge\alpha=0
. Then, by Lemma
14

one has
\rk(\beta,\alpha)=1
and hence
\beta=\lambda\alpha
for some
\lambda\in\Bbbk
. The
moreover
part is also immediate since
in a field

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{i_{n-1}}&\beta_{i_{n-1}}\\
\alpha_{i_{n}}&\beta_{i_{n}}\end{array}\right|=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{i_{n}}&\beta_{i_{n}}\\
\alpha_{i_{n+1}}&\beta_{i_{n+1}}\end{array}\right|=0

implies

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{i_{n-1}}&\beta_{i_{n-1}}\\
\alpha_{i_{n+1}}&\beta_{i_{n+1}}\end{array}\right|=0.

∎

We will describe a recursive process to make this concept more general.
We will say the curve
\mathcal{C}
is
p
-transversal at
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
w.r.t.
\alpha
if
\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P})=\{i,j\}
and

\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\in\Bbbk^{*}
for some
\delta\in\Delta_{P}
such that
\phi_{P}(\delta)=i
.
One can start a recursive process as follows. Consider the quotient
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}/\sim
by identifying the previous components, say
[i]=[j]
and denote by
\pi:\mathbf{r}\to\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
the projection map. One can say
\mathcal{C}
is
p
-transversal at another point
Q\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
w.r.t.
(\alpha,\pi)
if
\pi(\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P}))=\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}\subset\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
and
\sum_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{Q}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\in\Bbbk^{*}

for some
\delta\in\Delta_{P}
such that
\pi(\phi_{P}(\delta))=i^{\prime}
. This process can be extended to any projection map.
The curve
\mathcal{C}
is called
completely
p
-reductive w.r.t.
\alpha
if one can apply this recursive method until the quotient
set
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
eventually contains only one element.

Example 18
.

Consider for instance that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
is the union of two curves
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
that intersect transversely.
Then
\mathcal{C}
is completely
p
-reductive for any
p
w.r.t. any non-coordinate
\alpha\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
. More generally, if the graph
\Gamma^{p}_{\mathcal{C}}
is connected, then
\mathcal{C}
is completely
p
-reductive w.r.t. any non-coordinate
\alpha\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a completely
p
-reductive curve w.r.t. a non-coordinate
\alpha\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
.
Then the cohomology jumping loci of its Combinatorial Aomoto complex
(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)
is trivial, i.e.,

H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk},\cdot\curlywedge\alpha)=0.

Proof.

Consider
\beta=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk}
such that
\beta\curlywedge\alpha=0
.
Let’s say the curve
\mathcal{C}
is
p
-transversal at
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
w.r.t.
\alpha
.
By Corollary
10
, this implies the existence of
\lambda\in\Bbbk
such that
(\beta_{i},\beta_{j})=\lambda(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
.
Assume the following inductive hypothesis: for any element
i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
there exists
\lambda\in\Bbbk
such that

\beta_{i}=\lambda\alpha_{i}
for all
\pi(i)=i^{\prime}
. One can prove the following. Assume
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the inductive hypothesis
w.r.t.
(\alpha,\pi)
and
\beta\curlywedge\alpha=0
. If
\mathcal{C}
is
p
-transversal at
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
w.r.t.
(\alpha,\pi)
and denote by
\pi^{\prime}

the resulting projection map, then
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the inductive hypothesis w.r.t.
(\alpha,\pi^{\prime})
. In order to prove this claim, note that by hypothesis

\pi(\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P}))=\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}\subset\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
and there exists
\delta\in\Delta_{P}
with
\phi_{P}(\delta)=i_{0}
,
\pi(i_{0})=i^{\prime}
such that
\sum_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{Q}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\in\Bbbk^{*}
. By condition (
15
) one has

(18)

\begin{array}[]{ll}0&=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i_{0}}&\alpha_{i_{0}}\\
\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\beta_{k}&\sum_{k}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{k})\alpha_{k}\end{array}\right|\\
\\
&=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{i_{0}}&\alpha_{i_{0}}\\
\sum\limits_{\pi(i)=i^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{i})\beta_{i}+\sum\limits_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\beta_{j}&\sum\limits_{\pi(i)=i^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{i})\alpha_{i}+\sum\limits_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|.\end{array}

By induction hypothesis
\beta_{i}=\lambda_{1}\alpha_{i}
(resp.
\beta_{j}=\lambda_{2}\alpha_{j}
) for any
i\in\mathbf{r}
such that
\pi(i)=i^{\prime}

(resp. for any
j\in\mathbf{r}
such that
\pi(j)=j^{\prime}
). Hence

(19)

\begin{array}[]{ll}0&=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{1}\alpha_{i_{0}}&\alpha_{i_{0}}\\
\lambda_{1}\sum\limits_{\pi(i)=i^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{i})\alpha_{i}+\lambda_{2}\sum\limits_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}&\sum\limits_{\pi(i)=i^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{i})\alpha_{i}+\sum\limits_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\end{array}\right|\\
\\
&=\alpha_{i_{0}}\sum\limits_{\pi(i)=i^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{i})\alpha_{i}\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{1}&1\\
\lambda_{1}&1\end{array}\right|+\alpha_{i_{0}}\sum\limits_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{P}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{1}&1\\
\lambda_{2}&1\end{array}\right|.\end{array}

Since
\alpha_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and
\sum_{\pi(j)=j^{\prime}}\mu_{Q}(\delta,\mathcal{C}_{j})\alpha_{j}\in\Bbbk^{*}
by hypothesis, this implies
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}

and hence
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the inductive hypothesis, that is,
\beta_{k}=\lambda_{1}\alpha_{k}
for all
\pi(k)=i^{\prime}
or
\pi(k)=j^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 20
.

Note that the technical condition in Proposition
19
about a non-coordinate
\alpha
can be avoided in practice by considering the subcurve of
\mathcal{C}
whose components correspond with the non-zero coefficients of
\alpha
. For instance, if
\alpha_{i}=0
for some

from

. Denote by

This reduction process can also be used if for instance if

,
and some

Proposition 21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a curve that is the union of

fibers of a pencil of curves. Then
\mathcal{C}
is not completely

-reductive for any

.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
be the decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
as union of members of a pencil. Notice that the base points of the pencil are never

4.2.
Twisted Milnor fibers and twisted Alexander polynomials

Consider
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
the decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
in irreducible components, where
\mathcal{C}_{i}

.

14

15

.

-reductive w.r.t.

p
-reductive w.r.t.

Remark 23

In

5.
Pencils and modular inequalities: lower bounds

5.1.
A definition of quasi fiber–type curves

Assume that a curve
\mathcal{C}
is such that its complement
\tilde{X}=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
admits a surjection

f:\tilde{X}\to S
onto a hyperbolic orbifold
S=\mathbb{P}^{1}_{(r,\bar{m})}
, that is,
\chi^{\orb}(S)<0
.

In that case there exists a partition
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
with
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{F_{i}=0\}
, some
m_{i,j}>0
for which
F_{i}=\prod_{j}F_{i,j}^{m_{i,j}}
(
F_{i,j}
irreducible), and
H_{i}
such that
G_{i}=F_{i}H^{m_{i}}_{i}
,
where
\bar{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})
,
d=\deg G_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, satisfying that
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
are aligned in the projective space of curves of degree
d
.

For convenience, we assume
m_{i}=0
if and only if
H_{i}
is constant. If, in addition, the pencil is
primitive
, that is, its generic member is irreducible, we will refer to this type of curve as
quasi fiber–type
.

If
\varphi
is the collection
(F_{i},m_{i,j},H_{i},\bar{m})
then we will simply refer to its quasi fiber–type decomposition as
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
. For simplicity, we will assume
\gcd(m_{i,j},j)=1
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
. The following results can easily be extended without this condition, but the statements become more involved.

We also say a quasi fiber–type curve has
index

k\geq 1
if the pencil containing
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
contains also a multiple member
G^{k}
outside
G_{i}
and
k|m_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.

Note that the existence of a quasi fiber–type curve of index
k
implies the existence of a surjection from the fundamental group of its complement
\tilde{X}
as follows

(22)

\pi_{1}(\tilde{X})\overset{f_{*}}{\mathbin{\twoheadrightarrow}}\pi^{{\rm orb}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\bar{m}^{\prime}}),

where
\bar{m}^{\prime}=(k,m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})
, see
[
1
]
for details.

Remark 27
.

A quasi fiber–type curve of index
k
for which
m_{i}=0
with
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
k>1
is also known as a

or

arrangement associated with a Halphen pencil of index
k
, see
[
10
, Section 2]
. A (quasi) fiber–type curve of index

is simply a (quasi) fiber–type curve.

Also note that a quasi fiber–type curve can have more than one index. For instance, if
F_{\lambda,\mu}:=\lambda F_{p}^{q}+\mu F_{q}^{p}
, where
F_{k}
, is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
in three variables, then
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{F_{\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}}=0\}
and
[\lambda_{i}:\mu_{i}]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{[0:1],[1:0]\}
with

are different points, is a quasi fiber–type curve of index
p
or
q
.

5.1.1.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
be a quasi fiber–type curve as above, we denote by

a meridian of the irreducible component
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}=\{F_{i,j}=0\}

Definition 28
.

Theorem 37

Assume

(2)

\tilde{X}
, where

\bar{\nu}:=\gcd(m_{i,j}\bar{\nu}_{i})

Corollary 38
