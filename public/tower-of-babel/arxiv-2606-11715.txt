Bracketing Relationships of Weighted Average Treatment Effects
Pengfei Tian, Fan Yang *, Peng Ding *

Abstract
Under the canonical setting of observational studies for causal inference, we show that the average treatment effect under the overlap weight, the weight that is proportional to the conditional variance of the treatment given the covariates, is bounded between the average treatment effects on the treated and control, under a monotonic relationship between the propensity score and the conditional average treatment effect. We further extend the result to weighted local average treatment effects, under the canonical setting with a binary instrumental variable and a binary treatment. We also extend the results to other weights. Based on the theory, we recommend the “CP-plot” of the estimated conditional average treatment effect against the estimated propensity score.

Introduction

Causal inference with observational studies is critical in many disciplines. Under the potential outcomes framework, the average treatment effect (ATE) can be identified under two assumptions. The first assumption is unconfoundedness that the treatment is independent of the potential outcomes given the observed covariates. The second assumption is the overlap assumption that the propensity score, defined as the conditional probability of the treatment given the observed covariates, is not degenerate
(
25
)
. While the majority of the literature focuses on the ATE, other weighted average treatment effects are also of scientific and policy relevance. For instance, the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) measures the effect on the treated population, the average treatment effect on the controls (ATC) measures the effect on the control population, and the average treatment effect under the overlap weight (ATO) measures the effect on the population weighted by the conditional variance of the treatment given the observed covariates
(
21
)
. ATO has attracted growing interest because of its robustness to extreme propensity scores
(
22
)
and its connection with various regression-based estimators
(
4
;
30
;
13
;
20
)
. Despite its popularity, the relationship between ATO and the more traditional ATT and ATC has not been characterized, in general. We fill this gap with a simple ordering result under a monotonicity assumption and develop several extensions.

First, we show that ATO lies between ATT and ATC whenever the conditional expectation of the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) given the propensity score is monotonic. This includes a recent result by
16
as a special case, which focuses on fixed-effects regression with a discrete covariate under a more stringent monotonicity assumption.

Second, we extend the bracketing result to the instrumental variable (IV) setting with a binary IV and a binary treatment
(
2
;
18
)
, known as the local average treatment effect (LATE) framework. Most of this literature focuses on the LATE, defined as the ATE among compliers, whose treatment status is changed by the IV. Recently,
9
introduced a general class of weighted LATEs. In particular, the overlap-weighted LATE has appeared frequently in the recent IV literature
(
7
, Section 4)
; see also
6
,
31
and
19
. We show that the overlap-weighted LATE is bracketed by the LATEs under the treated and control populations whenever the conditional expectation of the CATE among compliers given the propensity score of the IV is monotonic.

Third, we extend the results to a beta-weight family
(
24
)
indexed by two parameters, of which ATE, ATT, ATC, and ATO are special cases. We further obtain ordering results that go beyond beta weights, including matching weights and a general framework based on a one-dimensional ordering index.

Fourth, based on this theory, we recommend a practical diagnostic “CP–plot” of the estimated CATE against the estimated propensity score. We revisit several observational studies and an IV study.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows.
Section
2
studies propensity-score weighted causal estimands and establishes the bracketing relation between ATO, ATT, and ATC. It then discusses identification under ignorability and connects the overlap-weighted estimand to regression-based estimands. Section
3
studies propensity-score weighted local causal estimands in a binary IV setting and establishes the corresponding bracketing relation for the overlap-weighted local estimand. It then connects the overlap-weighted local estimand to the population two-stage least-squares (TSLS) coefficient. Section
4
provides empirical illustrations. Section
5
develops extensions to beta weights and other weighting schemes. Section
6
concludes with a discussion.

2
Bracketing and Regression Interpretation of the Overlap-Weighted Estimand

2.1
A bracketing result for the overlap-weighted estimand

We work in the standard potential-outcomes framework. For each unit,
Z\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether the unit receives treatment (
Z=1
) or control (
Z=0
). Let
Y(1)
denote the outcome that would be observed under treatment, and
Y(0)
the outcome that would be observed under control. The vector
X
collects observed pre-treatment covariates. The conditional average treatment effect (CATE) is defined as

\tau(X)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X\},

which summarizes the average treatment effect among units with the same covariate profile
X
. Population-level causal estimands are then obtained by averaging
\tau(X)
under different weighting schemes, with different weights corresponding to different target populations.

Let
e(X)=\mathbb{P}(Z=1\mid X)
denote the propensity score
(
25
)
.
Common estimands include ATE, ATT, and ATC:

\displaystyle\tau_{\textup{ATE}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}=\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\},

\displaystyle\tau_{\textup{ATT}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid Z=1\}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\tau(X)]}{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}},

\displaystyle\tau_{\textup{ATC}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid Z=0\}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\{1-e(X)\}\tau(X)]}{\mathbb{E}\{1-e(X)\}}.

ATE is always bracketed by ATT and ATC because it is a convex combination of the two:

\tau_{\textup{ATE}}=\mathbb{P}(Z=1)\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATT}}+\mathbb{P}(Z=0)\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATC}}=\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATT}}+\mathbb{E}\{1-e(X)\}\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATC}}.

21
proposed overlap weights, which assign weight proportional to the conditional variance of treatment given covariates,
\mathrm{var}(Z\mid X)=e(X)\{1-e(X)\}
. This leads to ATO,

\tau_{\textup{ATO}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\tau(X)]}{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}]}.

Unlike
e(X)
or
1-e(X)
, the overlap weight
e(X)\{1-e(X)\}
vanishes at both endpoints of
[0,1]
. Hence,
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
emphasizes covariate regions where both treatment arms are well represented and downweights regions with extreme propensity scores. This makes ATO a natural and stable target estimand for the overlap population, especially when treatment and control groups have limited overlap. Because ATO targets a different population from ATT and ATC, it is important to understand how these estimands are related. We pursue this comparison under a monotonicity condition on the conditional mean of the CATE given the propensity score.

Our theory relies on the following P-CATE function

\displaystyle g(e)=\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\mid e(X)=e\},

(1)

which equals the conditional mean of CATE given that the propensity score equals
e
. Because
\tau_{\textup{ATE}}
,
\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
,
\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
, and
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
are all averages of
\tau(X)
weighted by functions of
e(X)
, the P-CATE function
g
determines how these estimands relate. We impose the following monotonicity condition on
g
.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose that

(i)

g(e)
is a non-decreasing function, or

(ii)

g(e)
is a non-increasing function.

Assumption
1
holds whenever units with a higher (or lower) propensity to receive treatment also tend to benefit more from it. For example, doctors may be more likely to assign surgery to patients in worse condition, who may also be expected to benefit more from treatment. The assumption is weaker than requiring a pointwise monotone relationship between
\tau(X)
and
e(X)
, since it constrains only the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\mid e(X)\}
. The following example shows that Assumption
1
holds in a standard parametric model. We do not need to believe its parametric assumptions in practical data analysis, but use it to illustrate Assumption
1
.

Example 1
.

In a parametric model where
\tau(X)=X^{\top}\beta
and
e(X)=\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(X^{\top}\gamma)
for
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
, the conditional expectation formula for the jointly normal vector
(\beta^{\top}X,\gamma^{\top}X)^{\top}
gives

g(e)=\mathbb{E}[\beta^{\top}X\mid\gamma^{\top}X=\mathrm{logit}(e)]=\beta^{\top}\mu+(\beta^{\top}\Sigma\gamma)(\gamma^{\top}\Sigma\gamma)^{-1}\{\mathrm{logit}(e)-\gamma^{\top}\mu\}.

We can verify that
g(e)
is monotonic in
e
.

Monotonicity conditions of this type have appeared elsewhere as a condition for comparing causal quantities, including in bounds on fixed-effects estimands
(
16
)
, analyses of bias direction
(
28
)
, effect ordering in signed causal graphs
(
27
)
, and bias amplification by instrumental variables
(
12
)
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
e(X)\in(0,1)
. Under Assumption
1
(i), we have

\tau_{\textup{ATO}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATC}},\tau_{\textup{ATT}}]
.
Under Assumption
1
(ii), we have

\tau_{\textup{ATO}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATT}},\tau_{\textup{ATC}}].

Theorem
1
is a statement about causal estimands defined through potential outcomes, and does not rely on the ignorability assumption

(Y(1),Y(0))\perp\!\!\!\perp Z\mid X
. Under ignorability,
\tau(X)
admits the identification formula

\tau(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=1,X)-\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=0,X),

so
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
,
\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
, and
\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
are functionals of the observable data distribution, and the same bracketing relation holds for the corresponding statistical estimands.

We next consider a special case in which the conditional mean of the CATE given the propensity score is linear.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose that

\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\mid e(X)\}=a+be(X)

for some constants
a
and
b
.

Assumption
2
is a special case of Assumption
1
, with
b\geq 0
corresponding to (i) and
b\leq 0
corresponding to (ii). Under Assumption
2
,
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
is a convex combination of
\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume
e(X)\in(0,1)
and Assumption
2
. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{\textup{ATO}}=\lambda\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATT}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot\tau_{\textup{ATC}},

(2)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
. When
e(X)
is constant, we have
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}=\tau_{\textup{ATT}}=\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
; when
e(X)
is not constant, we have

\lambda=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}\mathbb{E}\{1-e(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)(1-e(X))\}\mathrm{var}\{e(X)\}}\mathbb{E}[\{1-e(X)\}\{e(X)-c^{*}\}^{2}]\in[0,1],

where

c^{*}=\arg\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\big[\{1-e(X)\}\{e(X)-c\}^{2}\big]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}]}{\mathbb{E}\{1-e(X)\}}.

The displayed formula for
\lambda
makes
\lambda\geq 0
immediate. To see
that
\lambda\leq 1
, interchange
e(X)
and
1-e(X)
. Then
1-\lambda

has the same form:

1-\lambda=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}\mathbb{E}\{1-e(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)(1-e(X))\}\mathrm{var}\{e(X)\}}\mathbb{E}\big[e(X)\{1-e(X)-c^{\dagger}\}^{2}\big],

where

c^{\dagger}={\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}]}/{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}}.

Hence
1-\lambda\geq 0
, and therefore
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Proposition
1
is related to, but distinct from, the result in
26
, which studies the coefficient of
Z
from the population least-squares regression of
Y
on an intercept,
Z
, and
X
. Under his conditions, the coefficient of
Z
also admits a convex-combination representation in
\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
, but with a mixing weight that depends primarily on the overall treated and control shares, where the smaller group receives more weight. Here, in contrast, the mixing weight
\lambda
depends on the full propensity-score distribution through

\mathbb{E}[\{1-e(X)\}\{e(X)-c^{*}\}^{2}],
so
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
depends not only on the proportion of treated, but also on how propensity score varies across the population. Two populations with the same treated share can therefore produce different values of
\lambda
if their propensity-score distributions differ.

2.2
A regression interpretation of the overlap-weighted estimand

The overlap-weighted estimand
\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
has been studied extensively
(
21
;
22
;
32
)
. To provide more insights for a general reader, we further review its close connection to linear regression. Define the population ordinary least squares (OLS) of
Y
on
(1,Z,X)
as

\displaystyle\left(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\right)=\arg\min_{b_{0},b_{1},b_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(Y-b_{0}-b_{1}Z-b_{2}^{\top}X\right)^{2}.

(3)

The following proposition makes this connection precise.

Proposition 2
.

Assume
Z\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(1),Y(0)\}\mid X
.

(i)

We have

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{w}(X)\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{w}(X)\}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\}Y(0)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{w}(X)\}},

(4)

where
\tilde{w}(X)=e(X)\{1-\tilde{e}(X)\}
and
\tilde{e}(X)=\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{1}^{\top}X
is the OLS projection of
Z
on
X
with

\left(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\right)=\arg\min_{c_{0},c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left(Z-c_{0}-c_{1}^{\top}X\right)^{2}.

(ii)

Furthermore, when
e(X)
is linear in
X
, we have

\beta_{1}=\tau_{\textup{ATO}}.

Part (i) of Proposition
2
appears in

29
. Part (ii) has been derived multiple times in
the literature; see, for example,
4
and

14
. More broadly, overlap-weighted estimands can arise from
several regression formulations; see
14
for a
discussion.

A typical case in which
e(X)
is linear in
X
is when
X
consists of a full set of indicators for the support of a discrete covariate; the linearity then holds automatically, and the second term on the right-hand side of (
4
) is zero. This observation links our result in Theorem
1
to the fixed-effects analysis of
16
, which arises as the discrete-
X
special case of Proposition
2
.

He considers a stratified design with
K
strata, where the propensity score is constant within each stratum. Let

S\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
denote stratum membership, let
n_{[k]}
denote the number of units in stratum
k
,
w_{[k]}=n_{[k]}/n
the population share, and
e_{[k]}=\mathbb{P}(Z=1\mid S=k)\in(0,1)
the fraction treated in stratum
k
. Define the stratum-specific ATE

\tau_{[k]}=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid S=k\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.

He studies the population OLS regression of
Y
on
\{Z,\mathbb{I}_{[1]},\ldots,\mathbb{I}_{[K]}\}
, where
\mathbb{I}_{[k]}
indicates stratum membership. The coefficient on
Z
defines the fixed-effects estimand

\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{[k]}e_{[k]}(1-e_{[k]})\tau_{[k]}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{[k]}e_{[k]}(1-e_{[k]})}.

In the discrete-strata setting, the function
g(e)=\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\mid e(X)=e\}

takes the form

g_{\mathrm{FE}}(e)=\frac{\sum_{k:e_{[k]}=e}w_{[k]}\tau_{[k]}}{\sum_{k:e_{[k]}=e}w_{[k]}},

that is, the average stratum-specific ATE among strata with
assignment propensity
e
, weighted by their population shares. By Proposition
2
,
\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}=\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
, and applying Theorem
1
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Assume
e_{[k]}\in(0,1)
for all
k
. If
g_{\mathrm{FE}}(e)
is
non-decreasing in
e
, then

\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATC}},\tau_{\textup{ATT}}].

If
g_{\mathrm{FE}}(e)
is non-increasing in
e
, then
\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATT}},\tau_{\textup{ATC}}].

For comparison, we recall Proposition 1 of
16
as a corollary of Corollary
1
, which uses a stronger monotonicity condition.

Corollary 2
(Proposition 1 of
16
).

If for all
k,k^{\prime}
,
e_{[k]}\geq e_{[k^{\prime}]}
if and only if
\tau_{[k]}\geq\tau_{[k^{\prime}]}
, then

\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATC}},\tau_{\textup{ATT}}]
. If for all
k,k^{\prime}
,
e_{[k]}\leq e_{[k^{\prime}]}
if and only if
\tau_{[k]}\geq\tau_{[k^{\prime}]}
, then
\tau_{\mathrm{FE}}\in[\tau_{\textup{ATT}},\tau_{\textup{ATC}}]
.

Corollary
1
relaxes the condition in Corollary
2
by allowing strata that share the same propensity score to have different treatment effects.

3
Extension to local average treatment effects

3.1
A bracketing result for the overlap-weighted LATE

We next develop the IV counterpart of the bracketing result in Section
2
. IVs provide a way to study causal effects when treatment receipt is not
ignorable given observed covariates, but an external source of variation shifts the
treatment take-up. Under the standard IV assumptions, this variation identifies
local causal effects for compliers, whose treatment status is affected by the
IV.
Consider a binary
IV
Z\in\{0,1\}
, a binary treatment
D\in\{0,1\}
, an outcome
Y
,
and pre-IV covariates
X
. For
z\in\{0,1\}
, let
D(z)
denote the
potential treatment under IV value
z
. For
z,d\in\{0,1\}
, let

Y(z,d)
denote the potential outcome under IV value
z
and treatment
value
d
. The observed treatment and outcome satisfy

D=ZD(1)+(1-Z)D(0),\qquad Y=ZY\{1,D(1)\}+(1-Z)Y\{0,D(0)\}.

We adopt the standard IV framework of
18
and

2
. The assumptions are stated conditionally on
the pre-IV covariates
X
.

Assumption 3
.

Suppose that:

(i)

Conditional IV exogeneity:

Z\perp\!\!\!\perp\{D(1),D(0),Y(1,1),Y(1,0),Y(0,1),Y(0,0)\}\mid X.

(ii)

Exclusion restriction:

Y(1,d)=Y(0,d),\qquad d=0,1.

(iii)

Relevance:

\mathbb{E}\{D(1)-D(0)\mid X\}>0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(iv)

Monotonicity:

D(1)\geq D(0).

Under the exclusion restriction in Assumption
3
(ii), let
Y(d)
denote the common value of
Y(1,d)

and
Y(0,d)
. The IV framework induces a natural target subpopulation, namely
the compliers, whose treatment status is affected by the IV. To define
this population formally, let

U=\bigl(D(1),D(0)\bigr)\in\{(1,1),(1,0),(0,0),(0,1)\},

corresponding respectively to always-takers
(\textup{a})
, compliers

(\textup{c})
, never-takers
(\textup{n})
, and defiers
(\textup{d})
.
Under Assumption
3
(iv), the defier stratum is absent. Define the CATE among compliers as

\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid U=\textup{c},X\}.

Let
e(X)=\mathbb{P}(Z=1\mid X)
denote the IV propensity score, and let

\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{c}\mid X)

denote the principal score for the complier stratum
(
11
)
. Under Assumption
3
(iii)(iv),

\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{E}\{D(1)-D(0)\mid X\}>0,
almost surely.
Thus the complier conditional effect and the conditional Wald ratio are well defined.
We then consider four local causal estimands, which are the complier analogs of ATE, ATT, ATC, and ATO and are special cases of weighted local average treatment
effects
(
9
)
:

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATE}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid U=\textup{c}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]},

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid Z=1,\,U=\textup{c}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]},

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid Z=0,\,U=\textup{c}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]},

and

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}.

Here
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATE}}
is the local average treatment effect (LATE) introduced by
2
, while

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}
are local analogs of ATT and ATC.
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATE}}
is always bracketed by
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}
because it is a convex combination of
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}
:

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATE}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\}}\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)]}{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\}}\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}.

The quantity
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}
is the overlap-weighted LATE.
To compare the relationships among local estimands
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}
,
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}
and
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}
, define the local P-CATE function

g^{\textup{c}}(e)=\mathbb{E}\{\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid U=\textup{c},e(X)=e\}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}}{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}},

which equals the CATE among compliers whose IV propensity score
equals
e
.
The four local estimands above are averages of
\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)
under the
complier-weighted distribution of
X
, with additional weights depending on
the IV propensity score
e(X)
. Therefore,
g^{\textup{c}}(e)
plays the
same role as
g(e)
in (
1
) in the observational study setting.
We impose the following monotonicity condition on
g^{\textup{c}}
.

Assumption 4
.

Suppose that

(i)

g^{\textup{c}}(e)
is a non-decreasing function, or

(ii)

g^{\textup{c}}(e)
is a non-increasing function.

Assumption
4
is the LATE counterpart of
Assumption
1
. It holds when compliers with a higher (or lower) IV propensity score tend to have larger treatment effects on average.
Assumption
4
is weaker than requiring
\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)
itself to be
pointwise monotone in
e(X)
, since it constrains only the conditional mean
under the complier distribution.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
e(X)\in(0,1)
. Under Assumption
4
(i), we have
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}\in\bigl[\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}},\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}\bigr].

Under Assumption
4
(ii), we have

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}\in\bigl[\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}},\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}\bigr].

As in the observational study setting, Theorem
2
is a statement about causal estimands. The quantities

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATE}}
,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATT}}
,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATC}}
, and

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}

are defined through potential outcomes and principal strata, so the bracketing result itself does not rely on Assumption
3
for identification. Under Assumption
3
, these local causal estimands admit identification formulas in terms of the observable-data distribution through

\pi^{c}(X)=\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid Z=1,X)-\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid Z=0,X)

and the conditional Wald formula

\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=1,X)-\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=0,X)}{\mathbb{E}(D\mid Z=1,X)-\mathbb{E}(D\mid Z=0,X)},

and the same ordering carries over to the corresponding statistical estimands.

3.2
A TSLS interpretation of the overlap-weighted LATE

Analogous to the results in Section
2.2
, the
overlap-weighted local estimand also has a regression interpretation through
population two-stage least squares (TSLS). Consider the population IV regression
of
Y
on
(1,D,X)
, using
Z
as the excluded IV for
D
and
treating
(1,X)
as included exogenous regressors. Equivalently, the first
stage projects
D
on
(1,Z,X)
, and the second stage projects
Y
on

(1,\widehat{D},X)
, where
\widehat{D}
is the population linear projection
of
D
on
(1,Z,X)
. Specifically, define

(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\arg\min_{h_{0},h_{1},h_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(D-h_{0}-h_{1}Z-h_{2}^{\top}X\right)^{2},

and let

\widehat{D}=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}Z+\theta_{2}^{\top}X
.
The population TSLS estimand
\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}
is the coefficient on

\widehat{D}
in the population linear projection of
Y
on

(1,\widehat{D},X)
. Let
\beta_{1}
denote the coefficient on
Z
from the
population OLS regression of
Y
on
(1,Z,X)
, as defined in (
3
).
With a single IV for a single endogenous treatment, this TSLS estimand is
numerically equivalent to the indirect least-squares estimand,

\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}={\beta_{1}}/{\theta_{1}}.

The following theorem clarifies when this regression estimand coincides with
the overlap-weighted LATE.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that
\theta_{1}\neq 0
.
Under Assumption
3
, the following holds:

(i)

We have

\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{w}(X)\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\mid U=\textup{c}]\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{c})+\mathbb{E}[\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\}Y(D(0))]}{\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{w}(X)\mid U=\textup{c}\}\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{c})+\mathbb{E}\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\mid U=\textup{a}\}\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{a})},

where
\tilde{w}(X)=e(X)\{1-\tilde{e}(X)\}
and
\tilde{e}(X)
is the linear projection of
Z
on
(1,X)
.

(ii)

Furthermore, when
e(X)
is linear in
X
, we have

\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]=\tau^{\textup{c}}_{\textup{ATO}}.

Theorem
3
interprets additive TSLS without treating the additive
linear specification as a correctly specified outcome model
(
3
;
5
)
.
Part (i) shows that, without linearity of the
IV propensity score, TSLS generally targets a ratio, which does not have a causal interpretation in general. The leading term is
a weighted average of complier treatment effects, and the additional terms come
from the gap between
e(X)
and its linear projection
\tilde{e}(X)
. Part (ii)
identifies a simple case in which this gap disappears. When
e(X)
is linear in

X
, we have
\tilde{e}(X)=e(X)
, and the population TSLS estimand reduces to the
overlap-weighted LATE.

\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid U=\textup{c},X\}.

Let

\tau_{Y}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=1,X)-\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Z=0,X),

Under Assumption

\tau_{D}(X)=\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)
and

\tau_{Y}(X)=\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)

\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)={\tau_{Y}(X)}/{\tau_{D}(X)}.

We estimate the IV propensity score
e(X)=\mathbb{P}(Z=1\mid X)
by a logistic

Y
separately among units with
Z=1
and

D

Z=1

Z=0

\widehat{\tau}_{D}(X)=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(D\mid Z=1,X)-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(D\mid Z=0,X)
.
The modeled conditional Wald estimate is then

\hat{\tau}^{\textup{c}}(X)={\widehat{\tau}_{Y}(X)}/{\widehat{\tau}_{D}(X)}.

The target monotonicity condition in Section
3

g^{\textup{c}}(e)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}}{\mathbb{E}\{\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{\tau_{D}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}}{\mathbb{E}\{\tau_{D}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}}.

\hat{e}(X)

\widehat{\tau}_{D}(X)

g^{\textup{c}}

5.1.1
Beta-weighted estimands

We now consider a more general family of weighting schemes proposed by

\displaystyle\beta_{u,v}(x)=e(x)^{u-1}\{1-e(x)\}^{v-1}.

\tau_{u,v}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\{\beta_{u,v}(X)\tau(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}\{\beta_{u,v}(X)\}}.

\tau_{2,2}=\tau_{\textup{ATO}},\qquad\tau_{2,1}=\tau_{\textup{ATT}},\qquad\tau_{1,2}=\tau_{\textup{ATC}},\qquad\tau_{1,1}=\tau_{\textup{ATE}}.

1
(ii), the quantity
\tau_{u,v}
is non-increasing in
u
and non-decreasing in
v
.

for continuous values of

and

5.2
General weights

5.2.1
A general ordering framework

The results extend to a broad class of weighted estimands of the form

\tau^{h}=\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\tau(X)\},

where
h
is a generic weighting function, possibly depending on
e(X)
. Given two weighting functions
h
and
k
, the following theorem gives a sufficient condition for comparing
\tau^{h}
and
\tau^{k}
.

Theorem 6
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\}=\mathbb{E}\{k(X)\}
. Suppose there exist a scalar function

\xi(X)
, a constant
C
, and a function
\ell(\cdot)
such that

h(X)-k(X)=\ell\{\xi(X)\},

and

\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\xi(X))\geq 0\}=\mathbf{1}\{\xi(X)\geq C\}.

Let

G(\delta)=\mathbb{E}\{\tau(X)\mid\xi(X)=\delta\}.

If
G(\delta)
is non-decreasing, then
\tau^{h}\geq\tau^{k}
. If

G(\delta)
is non-increasing, then
\tau^{h}\leq\tau^{k}
.

Theorem
6
generalizes
Theorem
1
. Taking
\xi(X)=e(X)
recovers the function

g
introduced in Section
2
, and the monotonicity condition on

G
reduces to Assumption
1
.
Consider first the comparison between ATT and ATO. Write their normalized weights as

h_{\mathrm{ATT}}(X)=\frac{e(X)}{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}},\qquad h_{\mathrm{ATO}}(X)=\frac{e(X)\{1-e(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}]}.

We have
\mathbb{E}\{h_{\mathrm{ATT}}(X)\}=\mathbb{E}\{h_{\mathrm{ATO}}(X)\}=1
, and conditional on
e(X)
,
h_{\mathrm{ATT}}(X)-h_{\mathrm{ATO}}(X)
is a constant. A direct calculation gives

\mathbf{1}\{h_{\mathrm{ATT}}(X)\geq h_{\mathrm{ATO}}(X)\}=\mathbf{1}\left\{e(X)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\{e^{2}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}\{e(X)\}}\right\}.

That is,
h_{\mathrm{ATT}}
puts relatively more weight on units with larger propensity scores,
whereas
h_{\mathrm{ATO}}
puts relatively more weight on units with smaller propensity
scores. If
G(e)
is increasing, then

\tau_{\textup{ATT}}\geq\tau_{\textup{ATO}}
. Similarly, comparing ATO with ATC, if

G(e)
is increasing,

\tau_{\textup{ATO}}\geq\tau_{\textup{ATC}}
.
Combining these two comparisons yields

\tau_{\textup{ATC}}\leq\tau_{\textup{ATO}}\leq\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
,
which is the same result as in Theorem
1
. When
G(e)
is decreasing,
the two inequalities reverse.

More generally, the same one-crossing argument applies beyond the overlap,
ATT, and ATC weights. Additional results for general weights, matching weights,
and local weighted estimands in the IV setting are collected in
Section
A
in Supplementary Materials.

6
Summary

This paper studies how monotonic relationships between treatment effects and propensity scores induce ordering relations among weighted causal estimands. In unconfounded observational studies, we show that the overlap-weighted estimand is bracketed by ATT and ATC. In the IV setting, we establish analogous results for weighted local average treatment effects, including the overlap-weighted LATE. We further extend these results to a general beta-weight family and to weighting schemes beyond beta weights.

The results suggest a practical diagnostic and reporting strategy. In
observational studies, analysts can plot an estimated conditional treatment
effect
\hat{\tau}(X)
against the estimated propensity score
\hat{e}(X)
;
in IV studies, the analogous plot uses a modeled conditional Wald estimate

\hat{\tau}^{\textup{c}}(X)
against the estimated IV propensity score

\hat{e}(X)
. Such plots should be interpreted as descriptive diagnostics
rather than formal tests of monotonicity. The most relevant pattern is the trend
over the region where the data are well supported, especially the central
overlap region, because overlap weights downweight observations with extreme
propensity scores.

When the smoothed relationship is approximately increasing over the relevant
region, the theory predicts

\tau_{\textup{ATC}}\leq\tau_{\textup{ATO}}\leq\tau_{\textup{ATT}}
, and analogously for the local IV estimands.
When the relationship is approximately decreasing, the ordering reverses. When
the relationship is visibly nonmonotone in the well-supported region, the
bracketing relation should be interpreted with caution rather than used as a
substantive conclusion. We therefore recommend reporting the effect–propensity
score diagnostic together with ATT, ATO, and ATC, or their local IV analogs, so
that the sensitivity of causal conclusions to the target population is made
explicit.

An analogous ordering result holds in the IV setting. For two nonnegative

\mathbb{E}[\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\}D(0)]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\}\mathbf{1}(U=\textup{a})\right]=\mathbb{E}[\{e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)\}\mid U=\textup{a}]\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{a}).

Substituting these identities into the display above yields part (i).

Step 2: The linear-
e(X)
case.

If
e(X)
is linear in
X
, then
\tilde{e}(X)=e(X)
almost surely, so the terms involving

e(X)-\tilde{e}(X)
vanish and
\tilde{w}(X)=e(X)\{1-e(X)\}
. Part (i) then reduces to

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\mid U=\textup{c}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\mid U=\textup{c}\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e(X)\{1-e(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\mid U=\textup{c}]}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\mid U=\textup{c}\right].

This proves the first statement in part (ii).

Step 3: An equivalent representation using
\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)
.

Rewrite the previous display as

\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\mathbf{1}(U=\textup{c})\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\mathbf{1}(U=\textup{c})\right]}.

Taking conditional expectations given
X
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\mathbf{1}(U=\textup{c})\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\mathbf{1}(U=\textup{c})\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X),

where
\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid U=\textup{c},X\}
.
Therefore,

\tau_{\mathrm{TSLS}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)\right]}\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\right],

which proves the second statement in part (ii).

B.9
Proof of Theorem
5

Proof.
.

Recall
\pi^{\textup{c}}(X)=\mathbb{P}(U=\textup{c}\mid X)
in Section
3.1
and
\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}[\cdot]
in (
S11
). Then

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v}(X)\}}.

Moreover,

g^{\textup{c}}(e)=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\mid e(X)=e\}=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid U=\textup{c},e(X)=e\}.

Thus the local beta-weighted estimands have the same form as the beta-weighted estimands in Theorem
4
, with the population distribution replaced by the complier distribution. We give the details for completeness.

Define the normalized beta weight under the complier distribution by

\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}(X)=\frac{\beta_{u,v}(X)}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v}(X)\}},\qquad\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}(X)\}=1.

Then

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}(X)\tau^{\textup{c}}(X)\}.

Since
\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}(X)
is a function of
e(X)
,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}(X)g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))\}.

We first prove monotonicity in
u
. Fix
v\geq 1
and take
u_{2}>u_{1}\geq 1
. Define

h(X)=\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u_{2},v}(X),\qquad k(X)=\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u_{1},v}(X).

Then
\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{h(X)\}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{k(X)\}=1
, and

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{2},v}-\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{1},v}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\!\left[\{h(X)-k(X)\}g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))\right].

Because
\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{h(X)-k(X)\}=0
, for any constant
C
,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{2},v}-\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{1},v}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\!\left[\{h(X)-k(X)\}\{g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))-g^{\textup{c}}(C)\}\right].

Moreover,

\frac{h(X)}{k(X)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u_{1},v}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u_{2},v}(X)\}}e(X)^{u_{2}-u_{1}},

which is non-decreasing in
e(X)
. Let

C=\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u_{2},v}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u_{1},v}(X)\}}\right]^{1/(u_{2}-u_{1})}.

Then

h(X)\geq k(X)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e(X)\geq C.

If
g^{\textup{c}}
is non-decreasing, then

\{h(X)-k(X)\}\{g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))-g^{\textup{c}}(C)\}\geq 0

almost surely under the complier distribution. Therefore,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{2},v}\geq\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u_{1},v}.

If
g^{\textup{c}}
is non-increasing, the same argument gives the reverse inequality. Hence
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}
is non-decreasing in
u
under Assumption
4
(i) and non-increasing in
u
under Assumption
4
(ii).

We next prove monotonicity in
v
. Fix
u\geq 1
and take
v_{2}>v_{1}\geq 1
. Define

h(X)=\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{2}}(X),\qquad k(X)=\omega^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{1}}(X).

Then

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{2}}-\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{1}}=\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\!\left[\{h(X)-k(X)\}\{g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))-g^{\textup{c}}(C^{\prime})\}\right]

for any constant
C^{\prime}
. Moreover,

\frac{h(X)}{k(X)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v_{1}}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v_{2}}(X)\}}\{1-e(X)\}^{v_{2}-v_{1}},

which is non-increasing in
e(X)
. Let

C^{\prime}=1-\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v_{2}}(X)\}}{\mathbb{E}^{\textup{c}}\{\beta_{u,v_{1}}(X)\}}\right]^{1/(v_{2}-v_{1})}.

Then

h(X)\geq k(X)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e(X)\leq C^{\prime}.

If
g^{\textup{c}}
is non-decreasing, then

\{h(X)-k(X)\}\{g^{\textup{c}}(e(X))-g^{\textup{c}}(C^{\prime})\}\leq 0

almost surely under the complier distribution. Therefore,

\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{2}}\leq\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v_{1}}.

If
g^{\textup{c}}
is non-increasing, the same argument gives the reverse inequality. Hence
\tau^{\textup{c}}_{u,v}
is non-increasing in
v
under Assumption
4
(i) and non-decreasing in
v
under Assumption
4
(ii).

Combining the two parts completes the proof.

B.10
Proof of Theorem
6

Proof.
.

Let
b(X)=h(X)-k(X)
. Since
\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\}=\mathbb{E}\{k(X)\}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\{b(X)\}=0
. Therefore,

\tau^{h}-\tau^{k}=\mathbb{E}[b(X)\tau(X)]=\mathbb{E}[b(X)\{\tau(X)-G(C)\}].

Because
b(X)=\ell\{\xi(X)\}
is a function of
\xi(X)
,

\mathbb{E}[b(X)\{\tau(X)-G(C)\}]=\mathbb{E}[b(X)\{G(\xi(X))-G(C)\}].

If
G
is non-decreasing, then
b(X)\geq 0
exactly when
\xi(X)\geq C
, so

b(X)\{G(\xi(X))-G(C)\}\geq 0
almost surely. Hence
\tau^{h}\geq\tau^{k}
.
The non-increasing case is analogous and gives the reverse inequality.
