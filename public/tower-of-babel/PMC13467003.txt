Bronchoscopic lavage for pulmonary aspiration: a case series
Authors: Tingting Wen, Bing Xiong, Chengjie Mei, Shengwen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary aspiration of gastric contents is a life-threatening complication during anesthesia. Although immediate interventions are recommended, the role of bronchoscopic lavage remains controversial due to theoretical concerns about disseminating contaminants and limited clinical evidence.Case DescriptionWe present four illustrative cases of high-risk patients who underwent bronchoscopic lavage following large-volume aspiration of gastric fluid, solid material, or bile. All patients showed immediate improvement in oxygenation and reduction in airway pressure after bronchoscopic lavage, with a total of 250–700 mL of normal saline instilled into the affected lobar bronchi. None developed severe complications such as aspiration pneumonia, severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), or death. Notably, one patient with pre-existing lung injury due to necrotizing pancreatitis also recovered favorably. In the two patients with available follow-up imaging, there was no radiographic evidence of bronchoscopic lavage -induced consolidation or diffused contamination.ConclusionsPrompt bronchoscopic lavage may be beneficial following large-volume aspiration, potentially through reducing inflammatory mediators and mechanically clearing particulate matter. Despite limitations inherent to its retrospective design and small sample size, this case series supports the safety and clinical utility of bronchoscopic lavage in this setting. Further large-scale studies are warranted to confirm these findings and establish standardized treatment protocols.Keywords: Pulmonary aspiration, bronchoscopic lavage, case series

Highlight box

Key findings

• This case series of four patients demonstrates that prompt bronchoscopic lavage following clinically significant pulmonary aspiration during anesthesia leads to immediate improvement in oxygenation and airway pressures. None of the patients developed aspiration pneumonia, severe acute respiratory distress syndrome, or death, including one with pre‑existing lung injury from necrotizing pancreatitis.

What is known and what is new?

• Current evidence on bronchoscopic lavage for pulmonary aspiration is limited to isolated case reports and animal studies, with concerns regarding potential dissemination of contaminants.

• This series provides the first aggregated experience of four cases, demonstrating safety and favorable outcomes even in high‑risk patients, and suggests that early intervention may be critical.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest a potential role for bronchoscopic lavage after large-volume aspiration. Prompt lavage may reduce inflammatory burden and mechanically clear particulate matter. Prospective studies with large sample sizes and standardized protocols are warranted to test this hypothesis.

Introduction

Pulmonary aspiration occurs in approximately 1 in 3,000 routine anesthetic procedures and accounts for up to 20% of all anesthesia-related deaths (1,2). Deep sedation and general anesthesia both increase the risk of aspiration due to depression or loss of laryngeal reflexes. The severity of lung injury has been shown to be directly related to the pH and volume of the aspirated material, with pH <2.5 and volume >20 mL in adults considered critical thresholds for severe aspiration (3). Aspiration pneumonitis—a chemical injury caused by inhaled gastric contents—predisposes patients to secondary bacterial aspiration pneumonia. Most severely, gastric aspiration can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which carries a high mortality rate (4,5).

When perioperative aspiration is recognized, immediate interventions—including positioning the patient left-lateral with head-down tilt, therapeutic bronchoscopy for suction, and supportive respiratory care—are indicated (6). The role of bronchoscopic lavage, however, remains controversial for several reasons. First, animal studies have shown that acidic aspirate is rapidly neutralized by tracheobronchial secretions within minutes (7), suggesting that saline lavage is unlikely to enhance acid neutralization. Second, saline alone in rats could cause collapse of the lung (8). Lastly, there is concern that bronchoscopic lavage may disperse gastric contents further into the lung, potentially worsening airway obstruction and leading to more widespread contamination (9). Despite these considerations, there is currently insufficient evidence to definitively support or refute the utility of bronchoscopic lavage in the management of pulmonary aspiration.

Here we describe four clinically significant pulmonary aspiration cases that occurred during the induction or maintenance phase of anesthesia, managed with bronchoscopic lavage at our institution. All patients achieved full pulmonary recovery without major complications from aspiration, suggesting potential utility in selected scenarios. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://acr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/acr-2026-0085/rc).

Case presentation

This retrospective case series describes four patients who experienced pulmonary aspiration during surgery at The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China. All procedures performed in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. IIT20251108A) and in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients or their legal guardians for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal. Inclusion criteria were patients who experienced pulmonary aspiration during deep sedation or general anesthesia between November 2022 and September 2025 from the patient pool of Dr. Wen and Dr. Song. Exclusion criteria were incomplete physiological or clinical data. No patients were excluded based on these criteria. Aspiration occurred during anesthesia induction in three patients and during the maintenance phase in one patient scheduled for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) under monitored anesthesia care. The aspirate consisted of gastric fluid and particulate matter (three cases) or bile (one case). In all instances, aspiration was immediately followed by oxygen desaturation [peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) nadir <75%] and an increase in airway pressure. Patient characteristics, treatment details, and outcomes

ASA, American Society of Anesthesiologists; CRP, C-reactive protein; ERCP, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography; ICU; intensive care unit; OR, operating room; PCT, procalcitonin; PEEP, positive end-expiratory pressure; POD, postoperative day; SpO2, peripheral oxygen saturation; WBC, white blood cell count.

Upon detection, bronchoscopy was initiated within 5–10 minutes after recognition of aspiration. A flexible bronchoscope (TIC-13; Zhejiang UE Medical Corp., Taizhou, China; 5.2 mm outer diameter, 2.2 mm working channel) was used with a suction pressure of approximately 120 mmHg. Bronchoscopic lavage was performed by instilling 50 mL aliquots of saline into affected segment bronchi, followed by immediate suction. The procedure was repeated until no visible aspirate remained in each segment, with total lavage volumes ranging from 250 to 700 mL. The lavage procedure lasted about 5–10 minutes. In all four patients, this resulted in an immediate improvement in SpO2 and a reduction in airway pressure, allowing the planned surgical procedure to continue.

Apart from bronchoscopic lavage, patients 2, 3, and 4 each received a single 40 mg intravenous dose of methylprednisolone after aspiration. Patient 2 received an additional 40 mg dose on postoperative day (POD) 0 in the intensive care unit (ICU). Additionally, three of the four patients received different postoperative nebulized bronchodilator regimens: patient 1 from POD 10 to 20, patient 2 from POD 2 to 33, and patient 4 from POD 0 to 3. Regarding ventilation, no recruitment maneuvers were performed after aspiration. Positive end-expiratory pressure was set at 3–5 cmH2O for patients 1 and 2 during mechanical ventilation in the ICU.

Postoperative outcomes varied between patients. Patients 1 and 2 were admitted to the ICU as planned. Patient 1, despite bilateral aspiration, showed only focal left-sided opacities on computed tomography (CT) at 6 and 48 hours. Patient 2 had more extensive bilateral infiltrates on chest X-ray at 8 hours (Figure 1). Both patients showed a decline in arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio that reached a nadir around 24 hours post-lavage (from 536 to 380 mmHg in patient 1, and from 311 to 212 mmHg in patient 2), followed by gradual improvement (Figure 2). Patient 1 was extubated on POD 9 with a PaO2/FiO2 of 400 mmHg, a delay primarily attributed to neurological status. Patient 2, who had pre-existing lung injury due to necrotizing pancreatitis, was extubated at 48 hours with a PaO2/FiO2 of 286 mmHg. Patients 3 and 4 were extubated immediately after surgery, observed in the post-anesthesia care unit, and subsequently transferred to the ward.

Radiological images of the two patients. Pre-operative radiological images revealed radiolucent lung fields in both patients (A,D,G). Although aspiration was bilateral, patient 1’s CT scans at 6 and 48 hours showed only minimal evidence of bilateral infiltration on scout views (B,C), with localized left lung opacities on axial images (E,F). Patient 2, however, had bilateral infiltrates on the 8-hour chest X-ray (H), yet without evidence of consolidation. CT, computed tomography.

Time course of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio. Both patient 1 and 2 exhibited an initial decline in PaO2/FiO2 ratio, reaching a nadir at approximately 24 hours, followed by gradual improvement. FiO2, fraction of inspired oxygen; PaO2, arterial oxygen partial pressure.

None of the four patients developed aspiration pneumonia during their hospitalization. The absence of pneumonia was supported by the following: no persistent fever (>38.5 ℃) or unexplained hypothermia; no purulent sputum; procalcitonin <2.0 µg/L when measured (patients 1 and 2); no new or progressive infiltrates consistent with pneumonia on follow-up imaging (patients 1 and 2); and no escalation of antibiotics specifically for suspected respiratory infection. Patients 1, 2, and 4 received routine postoperative antibiotics for the prevention and/or treatment of cerebral or abdominal infections. Bronchoscopic lavage cultures or cytology were not performed. Sputum cultures were sent for patient 1 on POD 5 and for patient 2 on POD 0. While the result for patient 1 was negative. Candida albicans was reported for patient 2. However, this was considered a false positive originating from oral mucus rather than a true infection, and the patient was not treated.

Discussion

The present study presented four cases of patients treated with bronchoscopic lavage after clinically significant aspiration. All patients showed immediate improvements in oxygenation and reduction of airway pressure after lavage. In all cases, patients did not develop major complications such as bacterial aspiration pneumonia, severe ARDS requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), or death, especially for one patient with previous pulmonary injury from necrotizing pancreatitis.

Although the use of bronchoscopic lavage following pulmonary aspiration remains controversial, there exists limited yet noteworthy clinical and experimental evidence to support its benefits. In our experience, bronchoscopic lavage proved beneficial for patients who had aspirated large volume gastric fluid, solid food, and bile. To date, reported use of bronchoscopic lavage in pulmonary aspiration remains limited to case reports, specific aspiration types, and animal studies. For example, Yasui et al. reported that bronchoscopic lavage was effective in two cases of particulate-related aspiration, although the volume of lavage fluid used was not documented (10). Bronchoscopic lavage has also demonstrated efficacy in cases of lipoid pneumonia and pulmonary exposure to chlorine powder (11,12). Animal models provide further supportive evidence: bronchoscopic lavage completely resolved polyethylene glycol solution aspiration in a dog (13) and massive lung lavage after sea water aspiration reduced lung tissue damage and attenuated inflammatory responses in dogs (14).

The potential mechanism by which bronchoscopic lavage exerts its beneficial effects may be explained as follows. First, animal studies have revealed a biphasic peak of pulmonary inflammation following aspiration (15). The initial peak, occurring within one hour, results from direct chemical injury, while the second peak arises from neutrophil and macrophage infiltration along with the release of inflammatory cytokines (16,17). Bronchoscopic lavage may help to reduce the levels of these pulmonary inflammatory mediators. Consistent with this, experiments in sea water-drowned dogs demonstrated that animals treated with bronchoscopic lavage exhibited reduced inflammatory infiltration compared with untreated controls (14), suggesting that bronchoscopic lavage reduces the inflammatory reaction. Furthermore, particulate matter within gastric aspirate can cause additional mechanical and chemical damage (18). Performing bronchoscopic lavage promptly after aspiration may not only shorten the contact time between particulate material and lung tissue but also facilitate the removal of these particles due to fluid dilution and mechanical clearance. Finally, in our case series, radiographic and CT imaging did not reveal saline lavage-induced consolidation or disseminated contamination in the two patients admitted to ICU. All four cases were considered instances of aseptic aspiration. However, there is continued skepticism regarding the use of bronchoscopic lavage in the context of bacterial aspiration.

In our case series, the clinical and radiological outcomes align with the proposed mechanistic benefits of bronchoscopic lavage. For patient 1, although aspiration occurred bilaterally, only localized left lung opacities were evident at 6 hours post-aspiration, with minimal infiltration noted on the right side. The left-sided opacities were considered likely related to incomplete bronchoscopic lavage clearance on that side. For patient 2, who underwent a bronchoscopic lavage with a total instillation of 700 mL, more severe pulmonary infiltration was evident 8 hours post-aspiration. Nonetheless, neither patient developed aspiration pneumonia—particularly notable in patient 2, who had pre-existing lung injury due to necrotizing pancreatitis. The absence of pronounced radiographic consolidation in these cases is remarkable, as porcine models and human case reports of pulmonary aspiration typically demonstrate pulmonary consolidation within 4 hours (19,20). Interestingly, Yasui et al. also reported initially severe radiographic findings that resolved following bronchoscopic lavage without atelectasis, accompanied by rapid clinical recovery (10).

Due to the small sample size of our study, several procedure-related risks related to bronchoscopic lavage could not be evaluated. Although we did not observe radiographic evidence of distal dissemination and atelectasis in the two patients with postoperative chest imaging, there remains a concern that instilling saline could distally disseminate aspirated material into smaller airways, especially when aspirated material is not completely removed by suction. Second, large-volume lavage may wash out pulmonary surfactant. This could explain why patients 1, 2, and 4 required nebulized bronchodilators in the days following aspiration, potentially due to airway hyperresponsiveness from surfactant depletion. Other procedure-related complications—including bleeding, pneumothorax, cardiovascular instability, transient hypoxemia—also warrant further investigation. This is particularly important when suction pressure is high or when multiple passes are required. Given these potential concerns, further studies with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols are needed to determine whether the benefits of bronchoscopic lavage outweigh its risks in patients with pulmonary aspiration.

This report has inherent limitations due to its retrospective case series design, small sample size, and absence of a control group. The cases were derived from a convenience sample of two physicians’ practices, which introduces selection bias. The aspirate volume was visually estimated at the bedside and therefore may be underestimated, particularly given the severity of desaturation and physiologic compromise observed in these patients. Additionally, we were unable to assess the pH of the aspirated material or obtain bronchoscopic lavage culture and cytology results. Furthermore, multiple interventions were performed concurrently—including early recognition, use of bronchodilators, antibiotics, and systemic corticosteroids, and general perioperative care—making it impossible to attribute the observed outcomes solely to bronchoscopic lavage. The observed clinical improvement likely reflects the combined effect of these interventions rather than lavage alone. The follow-up duration varied among patients (3 to 33 days). Given these limitations, no causal inferences can be drawn. Our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence of efficacy. Prospective, controlled studies with standardized protocols are needed to validate these preliminary observations.

Conclusions

In summary, our case series suggests that prompt bronchoscopic lavage following large-volume aspiration may contribute to favourable clinical outcomes, even in high-risk patients. These findings, although derived from a limited retrospective case series, should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. The mechanisms underlying its potential benefit warrant further investigation. Ultimately, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are required to definitively establish the role and optimal protocols for bronchoscopic lavage in the management of aspiration syndromes.
