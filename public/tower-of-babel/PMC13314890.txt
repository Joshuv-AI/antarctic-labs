Diagnostic accuracy of lung ultrasound for suspected pneumonia in ambulatory and primary care settings: A systematic review and Bayesian latent class meta-analysis
Authors: Shao-Min Huang, Yu-Ching Li, Cheuk-Kwan Sun, Fu-Sheng Chang
Published: 2026

Despite reported favorable diagnostic accuracy of lung ultrasound (LUS) for diagnosing pneumonia, most evidence came from emergency, inpatient, or intensive care settings rather than ambulatory or primary care populations and was based on the assumption of error-free reference standards.1 Therefore, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility of LUS for suspected pneumonia in ambulatory and primary care settings, while exploring the effect of imperfect reference standards.

With the protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261360739), we conducted a systematic review and diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy guideline.2 Studies evaluating LUS in patients with suspected pneumonia in ambulatory or primary care settings, using chest radiography (CXR), computed tomography (CT), or digital tomosynthesis (DT) as reference standards were retrieved from searching PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane from inception to April 5, 2026. Two reviewers independently screened study eligibility, extracted 2 × 2 diagnostic data, and assessed risk of bias using Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2).3 The conventional synthesis adopted a bivariate random-effects model in Meta-DiSc 2.0.4 Because CXR, CT, and DT cannot be assumed to be error-free reference standards, we also performed an exploratory Bayesian latent class meta-analysis (LCM) using MetaBayesDTA.5 Clinical utility was presented as Fagan nomograms.6 The pre-test probability was set at the median disease prevalence across the included studies, while the likelihood ratios were derived from Bayesian LCM.6

Seven studies comprising 708 patients were included. Four studies used CXR as the reference standard, two used CT, and one used DT. Five studies evaluated COVID-19 pneumonia and two evaluated non-COVID community-acquired pneumonia. The median disease prevalence was 57.1%. Although QUADAS-2 showed generally low applicability concerns, all studies had high or unclear risk of bias in at least one domain, mainly because of selective enrollment, unclear blinding, post hoc diagnostic thresholds, extended test intervals between LUS and the reference tests, or partial verification bias. In the conventional bivariate model, LUS showed a pooled sensitivity of 87.4% (95% CI, 82.7–91.0), specificity of 68.5% (95% CI, 53.4–80.5), positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 2.78 (95% CI, 1.79–4.32), and negative likelihood ratio (LR−) of 0.18 (95% CI, 0.13–0.27). The Spearman correlation between sensitivity and false-positive rate was 0.108, suggesting no clear threshold effect; the bivariate I2 was 31.2%. In the Bayesian latent class model, sensitivity remained similar at 84.1% (95% credible interval [CrI], 70.9–91.4), whereas specificity increased to 81.6% (95% CrI, 58.9–96.0). The corresponding LR+ and LR− were 4.55 (95% CrI, 1.91–21.27) and 0.20 (95% CrI, 0.10–0.42), respectively. Using the median prevalence as the pre-test probability, a positive LUS result increased the estimated post-test probability of pneumonia to 86%, whereas a negative result reduced it to 21%.

These findings suggest that LUS may meaningfully shift post-test diagnostic probability and guide timely decision-making in selected patients with suspected pneumonia. Conventional analyses may underestimate LUS specificity when imperfect imaging comparators are treated as error-free reference standards. Given the small evidence base and risk-of-bias concerns, the Bayesian latent-class estimates should be considered exploratory rather than definitive.

Ethical considerations

All analyses in the current study were based on published data without identifiable patient information.
