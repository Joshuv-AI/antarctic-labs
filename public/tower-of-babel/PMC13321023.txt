Comment on “Machine learning and regression–based models for prediction of postoperative atrial fibrillation following coronary artery bypass grafting: A systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Prashant Sharma, Awanit Kumar, Harsh Verma
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the systematic review and meta-analysis by Eini et al. [1]. evaluating machine learning and regression-based models for predicting postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF) following coronary artery bypass grafting.

Postoperative atrial fibrillation definition variability and its predictive consequences

The review identified heterogeneity in the definition of postoperative atrial fibrillation as a source of between-study variability; however, the clinical significance of this problem deserves sharper framing. The duration thresholds across the included studies ranged from 30 s to more than 5 min, and the ascertainment methods varied from continuous telemetry to intermittent electrocardiography. These are not equivalent strategies; continuous telemetry detects paroxysmal episodes that intermittent recordings systematically miss [2]. A model trained on continuously monitored cohorts and evaluated against intermittently monitored populations produces specificity values that reflect monitoring intensity rather than model discrimination. The pooled specificity of 0.83 must be read in this context, as its consistency across subgroups may obscure meaningful variation in what was actually measured [3].

Calibration and decision-curve evidence: the absent clinical bridge

The pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.84 describes rank-ordering ability, not clinical decision quality. No included study reported a decision curve analysis, and calibration was inconsistently documented. Without net benefit quantification at clinically relevant thresholds, the probability above which a surgeon would initiate prophylactic amiodarone, for instance, whether these models change perioperative management remains unanswered. A model with good discrimination and poor calibration can systematically misestimate the risk in patient subgroups where prophylactic decisions carry the greatest consequence [4].
