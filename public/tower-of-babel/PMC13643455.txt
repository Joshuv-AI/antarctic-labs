Externalizing Disorders and Substance Use Among Nigerian Adolescents: Prevalence, Comorbidity, and Dose-Response Association
Authors: Abiodun O. Adewuya, Kikelomo Ololade Wright, Fidelis Njokanma, Olabisi E. Oladipo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo estimate prevalence of externalizing disorders (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, oppositional defiant disorder, and conduct disorder) among Nigerian adolescents and examine associations with substance use disorder (SUD), alcohol use disorder (AUD), and associated factors.MethodA cross-sectional analysis of 9,437 adolescents aged 11 to 17 years from 47 Lagos State public secondary schools was conducted using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI-Kid) adapted for self-administration within its validated age range. Weighted multivariable logistic regression examined associations adjusting for demographic, family, and school factors.ResultsOverall, 15.1% of adolescents met criteria for at least 1 externalizing disorder, with male predominance (17.7% vs 12.4%; odds ratio 1.52, 95% CI 1.35-1.71). Comorbidity was common, with 43.7% meeting criteria for 2 or more diagnoses. A graded dose-response association was observed: each additional co-occurring diagnosis was associated with a 2.84-fold increase in SUD odds (95% CI 2.48-3.25). Adolescents meeting all 3 disorder criteria showed SUD prevalence of 18.8% (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 9.87, 95% CI 5.84-16.68) and AUD prevalence of 23.2% (AOR 6.73, 95% CI 3.91-11.59) compared with adolescents with no externalizing disorder. Class repetition (AOR 2.26, 95% CI 2.00-2.55), maternal drinking (AOR 1.69, 95% CI 1.15-2.48), and witnessing domestic violence (AOR 1.64, 95% CI 1.35-1.99) were most strongly associated with externalizing disorders.ConclusionExternalizing disorders are common among Nigerian adolescents and are strongly associated with substance use and functional impairment. The observed dose-response pattern is consistent with developmental cascade models; longitudinal research is needed to establish sequential disorder progression. School-based screening and integrated behavioral and academic interventions may reduce associated substance use burden.Diversity & Inclusion StatementWe worked to ensure sex and gender balance in the recruitment of human participants. Externalizing disorders, encompassing attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), and conduct disorder (CD), affect approximately 8% to 12% of adolescents globally and contribute substantially to the worldwide burden of disability-adjusted life years.1,2 Characterized by behavioral disinhibition and impulsivity, these conditions cause severe impairment across academic, social, and occupational domains.3,4 Research in high-income countries shows that externalizing disorders frequently co-occur and cluster in ways consistent with a developmental progression, whereby earlier behavioral conditions are associated with more severe presentations later in life and may be associated with increased risk of substance use disorders.3 Whether comparable patterns exist in sub-Saharan Africa, where community-based diagnostic research remains sparse and mental health infrastructure is severely limited, is largely unknown.5

African and Nigerian Context

The limited but growing epidemiological literature from sub-Saharan Africa suggests that externalizing disorders are neither rare nor uniformly understood within existing Western diagnostic frameworks. A continental systematic review estimated that mental health problems affect between 14% and 25% of adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa,5 whereas a dedicated meta-analysis of ADHD across Africa reported a pooled prevalence of 7.5% among children and adolescents, broadly consistent with global estimates but derived from a heterogeneous evidence base.6 Studies from Nigeria have relied predominantly on symptom screening tools rather than structured diagnostic interviews, limiting direct comparability with the international literature and leaving clinically meaningful questions about comorbidity patterns, functional impairment, and associated risk factors largely unaddressed.7 Without diagnostically rigorous prevalence data, evidence-based resource allocation, school-based screening design, and culturally adapted intervention remain constrained.

This evidential gap carries serious practical consequences. Nigeria’s mental health system is severely under-resourced, with fewer than 0.1 psychiatrists per 100,000 people,8 meaning that the vast majority of children with behavioral disorders receive no formal diagnosis or treatment.9 Establishing diagnostic-level estimates and identifying the demographic, family, and school factors most strongly associated with externalizing disorders is therefore a prerequisite for any serious policy response. Schools represent a particularly strategic platform in this context, offering structured access to the adolescent population and a potential vehicle for scaling identification and early intervention in a system with severe specialist workforce shortages.

Theoretical Background

Developmental cascade models propose that early behavioral difficulties may contribute to escalating psychosocial and behavioral problems over time.10 In this framework, children with ADHD symptoms often struggle to meet academic and social demands; these struggles may crystallize into ODD, and as maladaptive patterns intensify, some youth transition to CD, characterized by repeated violations of social norms.11 Two complementary theoretical accounts help explain the robust association between externalizing disorders and substance use observed in longitudinal research. First, the behavioral disinhibition model argues that conduct problems and substance misuse arise from a shared latent vulnerability, namely impulsivity and poor behavioral control, such that both represent different expressions of the same underlying trait.12 Second, the self-medication hypothesis suggests that academic underachievement and peer rejection, common among youth with ADHD and ODD, generate distress that may lead affected individuals toward substance use as a coping strategy.13 Although cascade models propose this sequential developmental progression, the present cross-sectional design permits examination of comorbidity patterns and graded associations only. Inferences about temporal ordering or the sequential unfolding of 1 disorder from another cannot be drawn from these data and will require prospective longitudinal investigation.

Several factors unique to Nigerian society may modulate these associations. Physical discipline is normative in homes and schools, with approximately two-thirds of adolescents reporting corporal punishment.14 Although harsh discipline is associated with externalizing behaviors internationally,15 its role in contexts where it is culturally sanctioned requires dedicated investigation. Nigeria’s extended family systems and polygamous structures differ markedly from Western nuclear family norms and could function as either protective or risk factors depending on the quality of parenting relationships and household resource allocation. Due to the severe shortage of mental health professionals, most children with behavioral disorders receive no intervention, potentially allowing difficulties to accumulate without support.8

Study Objectives

Using data from the Lagos Schools Emotional and Behavioral Health Survey (LSEBHS),16,17 this study provides to our knowledge the first diagnostically rigorous examination of externalizing disorder comorbidity and its association with substance use in a large representative Nigerian adolescent sample. We hypothesized that male participants would show higher rates of externalizing disorders with the largest sex difference for CD18,19; that comorbidity burden would be associated with graded increases in substance use risk20; and that externalizing disorders would be associated with substantial functional impairment, though magnitude may diverge from Western estimates given cultural and educational system differences.21

Method

Study Design, Setting, and Sample

This cross-sectional study used data from the LSEBHS,16,17 a large-scale epidemiological study of adolescent mental health conducted in Lagos State, the most populous state of Nigeria. A stratified multistage probability sampling method ensured a representative sample of the public secondary school population. The 5 administrative divisions of Lagos State served as primary sampling strata, within which 47 mixed-sex public senior secondary schools were selected using probability proportional to size sampling weighted by enrollment figures. Schools were further stratified by geographic classification as rural, semi-urban, or urban per National Population Commission criteria. Within each school, 2 intact classes were randomly selected from each of the 3 senior secondary levels (SSS1-SSS3).

From an initial pool of 10,202 invited students, 9,441 provided both parental consent and personal assent. Reasons for nonparticipation were parental refusal (4.2%, n = 429), student absence on the assessment day (2.1%, n = 214), and incomplete data precluding analysis (1.2%, n = 122). Four participants aged 18 years or older were subsequently excluded because the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI-Kid) is validated for ages 6 to 17 years only.21 Although the original sampling frame extended to age 21 due to grade repetition practices common in Nigerian secondary schools, the analytic sample was restricted to the validated age range to ensure methodological rigor. The final analytic sample comprised 9,437 adolescents aged 11 to 17 years (mean [SD] age = 15.61 [1.49] years), with balanced sex representation (50.4% female) and geographic distribution across rural (44.0%), semi-urban (29.9%), and urban (26.1%) areas. This study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Measures

Externalizing Disorder Diagnoses

ADHD, ODD, and CD were assessed using modules from the MINI-Kid, a structured diagnostic instrument aligned with DSM-IV criteria.22 The MINI-Kid has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity across diverse clinical and community populations.22 Modules were adapted for self-administration following procedures established in comparable school-based epidemiological studies.16,17 The ADHD module assessed inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms requiring onset before age 12 and functional impairment across settings. The ODD module required 4 or more of 8 specified symptoms sustained for 6 months or more. The CD module assessed 15 behaviors including aggression and property destruction, requiring at least 3 criteria in the past year. Participants were categorized into mutually exclusive groups: no disorder, single isolated diagnosis, and all comorbid combinations.

Substance Use Outcomes

Substance use disorder (SUD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) were assessed using their respective MINI-Kid modules, adapted for self-report. Both modules assessed criteria including tolerance, withdrawal, impaired control, and functional interference to determine past-year prevalence of SUD and AUD respectively, consistent with DSM-IV diagnostic thresholds.

Functional Impairment

Functional impairment was assessed across the following 3 domains using Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) items23:1.Academic functioning—having ever repeated a class due to academic failure; currently experiencing problems with schoolwork or teachers2.Social functioning—social isolation defined as having no close friends in school; peer relationship problems; bullying victimization in the past year3.Behavioral and disciplinary outcomes—school suspension in the past year; involvement in physical fights

Covariates and Associated Factors

Sociodemographic covariates included age, gender, family structure (both parents, mother only, father only, other), and polygamous family configuration. Polygamous family structure was included given its prevalence in this sample (19.2%) and its documented associations with differential parental supervision and resource allocation in West African contexts. Socioeconomic status (SES) was derived from parental occupation and household assets, classified as low, medium, or high using established Nigerian schemas. Additional covariates included geographic location, witnessing family violence, and frequent physical punishment from parents. Corporal punishment was operationalized as frequent physical punishment using GSHS frequency gradient items, selected a priori to capture exposure of sufficient regularity to plausibly relate to behavioral outcomes, consistent with dose-response literature on harsh discipline.15 Parental substance use was assessed through drinking behaviors of father and mother.

Procedures and Ethics

Data were collected in classrooms supervised by trained research assistants. Information sheets and consent forms were distributed for parental review before data collection days. Students completed questionnaires independently during 50- to 70-minute sessions, with seating arrangements and communication restrictions ensuring privacy. The study received approval from the Health Research and Ethics Committee of the host institution and the Lagos State Ministry of Education, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; no personally identifying information was recorded on the survey instruments at any stage. Students endorsing suicidal ideation or severe distress were informed through a statement at the end of the questionnaire that they could voluntarily and confidentially approach the designated research assistant or school counselor present in the room to receive information about available support services. This process was entirely self-initiated; no data linking individual responses to identities were created.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp) with data weighted to account for the complex sampling design and to reflect the population distribution. Weighted prevalence estimates and 95% CIs were calculated for all disorder categories and comorbid presentations. Sex differences were examined using χ2 tests with odds ratios (ORs) quantifying the strength of association. Multivariable logistic regression models examined associations between externalizing disorders and substance use outcomes, adjusting for age, gender, SES, family structure, and geographic location. To examine the dose-response relationship between comorbidity burden and substance use risk, a linear trend analysis tested whether substance use outcomes increased systematically with the number of co-occurring diagnoses (0, 1, 2, or 3). This approach tests additive comorbidity burden as a cross-sectional correlate of substance use risk; it does not model the sequential disorder progression posited by developmental cascade theory, which requires longitudinal data. Additional logistic regression models identified factors associated with any externalizing disorder. Reference categories were female gender; age younger than 16 years; medium or high SES; urban or semi-urban location; and no family adversity, bullying, or academic stress. Gender-stratified analyses and likelihood ratio tests examined whether associations differed between male and female participants.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The final analytic sample of 9,437 adolescents had a weighted mean (SD) age of 15.61 (1.49) years (range 11-17). Most adolescents (75.6%) resided with both biological parents, and a polygamous family structure was reported by 19.2%. Family adversity exposures were common: 63.1% reported corporal punishment in the past year, 8.0% had witnessed domestic violence, and 18.6% reported paternal alcohol use. Academic difficulties were prevalent, with 33.4% having repeated a class and 42.0% reporting current problems with schoolwork. Social isolation affected 4.5% of the sample, whereas 16.7% reported bullying victimization in the past year. Prevalence and Comorbidity Patterns

Overall, 15.1% (n = 1,427) of adolescents met criteria for at least 1 externalizing disorder (Table 1). ADHD was most prevalent (6.8%, n = 642), followed by ODD (5.9%, n = 557) and CD (2.4%, n = 227). Isolated presentations included ADHD only (4.1%), ODD only (3.0%), and CD only (2.1%). Comorbidity was common: among participants with any externalizing disorder, 43.7% met criteria for 2 or more diagnoses. The most frequent comorbid presentation was ADHD with ODD (2.6%, n = 241), followed by ADHD with CD (1.7%, n = 156) and ODD with CD (0.9%, n = 89). A small but clinically important subgroup (0.7%, n = 69) met criteria for all 3 disorders simultaneously, representing the highest comorbidity burden in the sample.

Sex differences were pronounced across all diagnostic categories. Male participants showed significantly higher rates of any externalizing disorder than female participants (17.7% vs 12.4%; OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.35-1.71). CD showed the strongest sex difference, with male participants nearly 3 times more likely to meet criteria (3.1% vs 1.1%; OR 2.89, 95% CI 2.15-3.89), followed by ADHD (8.1% vs 5.4%; OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.42-2.23) and ODD (6.8% vs 4.9%; OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.02-1.71). The sex gap widened substantially for comorbid presentations, with male participants 4 times more likely to meet criteria for all 3 disorders simultaneously (1.2% vs 0.3%; OR 4.15, 95% CI 2.51-6.86).

Association Between Comorbidity Burden and Substance Use

A clear dose-response association was observed between the number of co-occurring externalizing diagnoses and substance use outcomes (Table 2, Figure 1). Among adolescents with no externalizing disorder, the reference group for all comparisons, SUD prevalence was 1.6%, and AUD prevalence was 3.2%. Individual diagnoses were associated with elevated substance use rates: isolated ADHD was associated with SUD prevalence of 3.9% (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 2.10, 95% CI 1.52-2.89) and AUD prevalence of 6.4% (AOR 1.89, 95% CI 1.38-2.59) compared to participants with no externalizing disorder. Isolated ODD showed similar patterns (SUD: 4.2%, AOR 2.34, 95% CI 1.65-3.32), whereas isolated CD was associated with the strongest single-diagnosis relationship (SUD: 6.6%, AOR 3.40, 95% CI 2.45-4.72; AUD: 9.6%, AOR 2.84, 95% CI 1.98-4.08). Comorbidity substantially amplified substance use risk in a stepwise manner: adolescents with 2 co-occurring diagnoses showed SUD prevalence ranging from 7.5% (ADHD + ODD) to 12.4% (ODD + CD), whereas those meeting criteria for all 3 disorders showed SUD prevalence of 18.8% (AOR 9.87, 95% CI 5.84-16.68) and AUD prevalence of 23.2% (AOR 6.73, 95% CI 3.91-11.59) compared with 1.6% and 3.2%, respectively, among adolescents with no externalizing disorder. Gender-Stratified Patterns and Internalizing Comorbidity

Gender-stratified analyses revealed differential associations between disorder type and substance use. CD showed a particularly strong association with SUD among male participants (AOR 4.27, 95% CI 2.89-6.31) compared with female participants (AOR 2.36, 95% CI 1.21-4.61; interaction p = .042). Conversely, the association between ADHD and SUD showed a nonsignificant trend toward being stronger among female participants (AOR 2.84, 95% CI 1.62-4.98) than male participants (AOR 1.96, 95% CI 1.38-2.78; interaction p = .127). Substantial overlap with internalizing psychopathology was observed: among adolescents with any externalizing disorder, 46.8% also met criteria for an internalizing disorder compared with 20.3% without an externalizing disorder (OR 3.41, 95% CI 2.98-3.91, p < .001), with depression co-occurring in 23.7% vs 8.9% (OR 3.18, 95% CI 2.67-3.79, p < .001).

Discussion

Principal Findings

This large-scale, diagnostically rigorous study provides the first comprehensive examination of externalizing disorder comorbidity and its association with substance use among Nigerian adolescents attending school. A substantial minority (15.1%) met criteria for at least 1 externalizing disorder, with a pronounced male predominance across all diagnostic categories. The central finding is a graded dose-response association between comorbidity burden and substance use risk: adolescents meeting criteria for all 3 disorders simultaneously had nearly 10 times the odds of SUD compared with adolescents with no externalizing disorder, with substance use prevalence reaching 18.8% in this group with highest burden. Substantial functional impairment was documented across academic, social, and disciplinary domains, with class repetition emerging as the strongest correlate in the multivariable model (AOR 2.26), followed by maternal drinking (AOR 1.69) and witnessing domestic violence (AOR 1.64). These findings establish externalizing disorders as a major public health concern for Nigerian youth, with associations operating through patterns showing considerable consistency with high-income country research, while also revealing culturally specific features warranting dedicated investigation.

Cross-Cultural Convergence and Divergence

The 15.1% overall prevalence is broadly consistent with estimates from high-income countries, which typically report rates between 12% and 18%.24 ADHD prevalence (6.8%) in our study falls within the 5% to 8% range reported in recent meta-analyses.2,25,26 Several factors may account for this: cultural differences in how conduct problems manifest and are reported; reduced sensitivity of Western DSM criteria to behaviors such as defiance toward elders, which are primary concerns in Nigerian settings; or genuine population differences potentially associated with extended family structures and cultural values emphasizing respect and communal harmony.27,28 The CD prevalence of 2.4% is at the lower end of the 2% to 4% range reported internationally6 and closely approximates the 7.5% pooled African estimate.

Comorbidity patterns closely mirror patterns from population-based samples in high-income countries. The 71.4% rate of ODD co-occurrence among adolescents with ADHD is broadly consistent with longitudinal cohort findings documenting substantial overlap across different national contexts,29,30 suggesting that comorbidity patterns among externalizing disorders may generalize across markedly different socioeconomic and cultural settings. With respect to functional impairment, the rates observed, including class repetition affecting more than half of adolescents with externalizing disorders and suspension rates nearly 5 times higher than among unaffected peers, are broadly consistent in direction and approximate magnitude with patterns documented in school-based studies from high-income countries, though direct comparison is limited by measurement heterogeneity and educational system differences.

Mechanisms and Cultural Amplification Hypothesis

The association between ADHD and SUD (AOR 2.10) is consistent with the self-medication hypothesis, whereby substances may be used to manage inattention and restlessness, though directionality cannot be established here.31 The present findings are consistent with both theoretical pathways previously proposed to link externalizing disorders to substance use, though the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference about which pathway or pathways may be operative. The strong association between CD and SUD (AOR 3.40) is consistent with the behavioral disinhibition model, wherein shared traits of impulsivity and poor self-control may manifest as both conduct difficulties and substance misuse, such that co-occurrence represents different expressions of the same underlying vulnerability rather than one causing the other.32 The graded increase in substance use rates with each additional co-occurring diagnosis places adolescents with multiple externalizing disorders in a particularly high-risk group warranting prioritized intervention.

The Nigerian context may amplify these associations in important ways. The 63.1% prevalence of corporal punishment far exceeds rates typically reported in high-income countries.15 Although harsh discipline showed a modest independent association with externalizing disorders in the multivariable model (AOR 1.28), its population-level prevalence suggests its aggregate contribution may be considerable. Social learning theory would suggest that physical aggression modeled in the home may be internalized as a normative conflict resolution strategy,33 which may co-occur with conduct-related difficulties in school and peer contexts. The class repetition finding (AOR 2.26) highlights a potentially important intervention nexus. This association is likely bidirectional and mutually reinforcing: attention and behavioral difficulties may impair academic performance, generating frustration associated with oppositional behavior, whereas oppositional conduct may disrupt learning through school disengagement.34 The present data cannot establish which direction predominates, and the high co-occurrence with internalizing disorders (46.8%) further suggests that many Nigerian adolescents present with transdiagnostic vulnerabilities requiring comprehensive rather than disorder-specific assessment approaches.35

Rural Paradox and Health Equity Implications

Unexpectedly, externalizing disorders were more prevalent in rural (16.8%) than urban (13.6%) areas, diverging from patterns in high-income countries where urban poverty is more commonly associated with elevated behavioral problems.36 Selective urban migration may leave rural communities with higher concentrations of socioeconomically disadvantaged families; consistent with this, 41.2% of rural adolescents came from low SES backgrounds compared with 27.3% in urban areas. Additionally, scarcity of mental health services in rural settings may result in prolonged delays in addressing conditions, allowing difficulties to accumulate without support. Despite differences in prevalence, the dose-response association between comorbidity burden and substance use was consistent across geographic contexts (rural: AOR 2.67; urban: AOR 2.54), suggesting that this association generalizes across the rural-urban divide. These interpretations remain speculative given the cross-sectional design and warrant prospective investigation.

Schools represent critical sites for early identification of externalizing disorders in Nigeria. The graded dose-response association provides a rational basis for tiered prevention: universal social-emotional learning programs for all students; selective support for individuals meeting criteria for a single diagnosis; and intensive multicomponent intervention for the highest-burden subgroup, specifically the 3.3% meeting all 3 diagnostic criteria, who accounted for 14.6% of SUD cases in this sample. Interventions should simultaneously target behavior, academic functioning, and family context, as the strong association between class repetition and externalizing disorders suggests that academic support and remedial teaching may carry mental health benefits alongside their educational value. Family-focused programs offering alternatives to corporal punishment are warranted, and the stronger association for maternal drinking (AOR 1.69 vs AOR 1.41 for paternal drinking) suggests that maternal mental health and substance use warrant particular clinical attention. Given the severe mental health workforce shortage in Nigeria, task-shifting models are essential: training teachers to recognize behavioral difficulties, equipping school counselors to deliver evidence-based interventions,37 and integrating mental health screening into existing school health platforms represent pragmatic steps, with the existing school health emphasis of the Lagos State Ministry of Education providing a viable policy window for such integration.

This study’s strengths include its large representative sample (N = 9,437; participation rate 92.5%), use of standardized diagnostic assessment across a broad range of disorder categories, high response rate, and comprehensive functional impairment data across multiple domains, collectively addressing a critical gap in the Nigerian and broader African child psychiatry literature. However, several limitations warrant careful consideration. Most fundamentally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the direction of any association reported, including that between class repetition and externalizing disorders, cannot be established from these data, though theory and prior longitudinal research support the plausibility of bidirectional associations. Reliance on self-report may introduce response bias despite the anonymous administration procedures designed to mitigate this concern, and multi-informant assessment incorporating parent and teacher report would strengthen diagnostic validity. Although the MINI-Kid is a well-validated structured diagnostic instrument, it approximates, but does not replace, formal clinician-administered psychiatric diagnosis; other conditions not assessed, such as trauma-related disorders or additional neurodevelopmental presentations, may partly account for some observed behavioral patterns. Multiple statistical comparisons were conducted across several outcome domains; despite generally substantial and directionally consistent associations, the probability of type I error is elevated without formal correction, and secondary analyses should be interpreted with appropriate caution and replicated in independent samples. The school-based sampling frame excludes youth who do not attend school, who may have considerably higher rates of CD and substance use, meaning that prevalence estimates likely represent a conservative lower bound. The cultural validity of DSM constructs in the Nigerian context also warrants ongoing scrutiny.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies to establish temporal ordering, document developmental trajectories, and directly test whether the cascade sequence from ADHD through ODD to CD and subsequently to substance use operates in Nigerian adolescents. Intervention trials testing culturally adapted behavioral programs are urgently needed. Qualitative research exploring lived experiences, help-seeking barriers, and the role of protective factors such as religious involvement and extended family support would enrich interpretation of quantitative findings and inform culturally responsive intervention design.

Externalizing disorders affect a substantial proportion of Nigerian secondary school adolescents and are strongly associated with substance use and functional impairment across academic, social, and disciplinary domains. The graded dose-response association between comorbidity burden and substance use risk, whereby each additional co-occurring externalizing diagnosis was associated with substantially greater odds of SUD and AUD, mirrors patterns documented in populations of high-income countries, suggesting cross-cultural generalizability of this association. At the same time, the culturally specific correlates identified, including the high population-level prevalence of corporal punishment, the unexpected rural excess in disorder prevalence, and the predominance of family adversity indicators among the strongest associated factors, underscore that Nigerian adolescents experience these disorders within a distinct social and structural context that Western frameworks may not fully capture.

These findings make a strong case for integrating diagnostically informed mental health screening into Nigerian school health infrastructure and for developing culturally adapted, multicomponent interventions that simultaneously address behavioral, academic, and family-level risk factors. Longitudinal research is required to establish temporal ordering, clarify causal pathways, and directly test whether the developmental cascade sequence operates in this population. Building that evidence base is an urgent priority for child and adolescent psychiatry research in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa more broadly.
