Project Background:
The Development of Integrated Screening, Cultivar Optimization, and Verification Research
(DISCOVR) consortium, created in 2017, was designed to help close the gap between high-
impact laboratory R&D and the realization of a robust outdoor algae farming approach that meets
feedstock production targets, measured as biomass production cost and GHG emissions
reduction. Arizona State University’s (ASU) Arizona Center for Algae Technology and Innovation
(AzCATI) has been a member of DISCOVR since 2018 and has been the primary outdoor
cultivation partner generating year over year data for the State of Technology for algae biomass
productivity and for verifying improvements identified within the consortium and from other DOE
funded projects under a standardized, rigorous outdoor cultivation protocols in place since 2013.
DISCOVR has been led from the beginning by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) with
partners, Los Alamos National Laboratory, National Lab of the Rockies, Sandia National
Laboratories, Lawernce Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), Idaho National Laboratory,
Colorado School of Mines (CSM) and ASU. This final report covers the last 3 year AOP plan for
DISCOVR, specifically for the activities that ASU was directly responsible for and is NOT a full
report on the entirety of the DISCOVR activities by the project partners. Information on the overall
results of DISCOVR can be found in publications and presentations by the various partners and
applicable quarterly reports as submitted to DOE by the lead institution, PNNL.
Project Objectives:
The overall objective of DISCOVR was to demonstrate a comprehensive set of translatable cross-
cutting cultivation approaches, including cultivars and operations, that positively impact biomass
productivity, quality, culture stability and overall process carbon efficiency. In FY23-25, DISCOVR
had the following 4 project targets that were to be experimentally demonstrated:

 1. CO2 Utilization Efficiency (CUE). Improve carbon utilization efficiency (CUE) in outdoor
 cultivation system minimizing the loss of CO2 delivered while maintaining productivity.
 Project Target = 70%. The Farm Model assumes 90% of the carbon delivered to the ponds
 is utilized by the algae.
 2. Biomass Intrinsic Value. Greater control over biomass quality from algae cultivation,
 and maximizing the utilization and valorization of carbohydrate, protein and lipids, is critical
 to economical downstream conversion to fuels and products. The calculated intrinsic
 value, sum of the respective derived product streams in a multi-product biorefinery, needs
 to exceed the production costs to farm the biomass.
 3. Mean Time Before Failure (MTBF). Project Target >20 days. The Farm Model is based
 on pond resetting without additional intervention. Based on previous experience with
 marine strains, it is expected that pond crashes will develop, and that crop protection will
 become necessary. Proactive surveillance and microbiological analyses will accelerate
 the development of countermeasures. An increase in MTBF will result in an increase in
 annual biomass productivity.
 4. Annual Biomass Productivity. Through improvements identified during this 3-yr AOP
 cycle, demonstrate outdoors at AzCATI the ability to achieve an annual average
 productivity Project Target = 20 g/m2-day. The nth plant assumption for the Farm Model is
 25 g/m2-d.

TASK 2. IMPROVE CO2 UTILIZATION EFFICIENCY
Objective: Improve CO2 utilization efficiency (CUE) in outdoor cultivation system minimizing the
loss of CO2 delivered while maintaining productivity.
Rationale: Cost of CO2 remains a key driver in MBSP of algae biomass and thus achieving a high
CUE is a key parameter to controlling costs and the overall carbon footprint of algae cultivation.
Most TEA and LCA modelling for algae production assume CUEs of 80% or greater. However,
most current large-scale algae cultivation (>1-acre ponds) facilities have CUEs significantly below
that target, in some cases under 20%, though values as high as 74-90% have been reported at
scale (Weissman, et al, 1992). Our own research at AzCATI in small-scale raceways has shown
CUE ranging from 15-80% depending on cultivation conditions (Eustance, et al, 2020).
Approach: We propose a strategy to improve CUE through a combination of characterization of
existing top performing DISCOVR strains under varying media conditions favorable to higher CUE
potential (e.g., higher pH, Subtask 2.1 (PNNL)), media chemistry optimization (Subtask 2.2 (NLR))
and novel strain identification through prospecting higher pH/higher alkalinity natural
environments (Subtask 2.3, CSM). These will all be tightly coupled to actual outdoor
experimentation including extending scale to systems (to 100 m 2 ponds) that more closely mimic

effects in large-scale cultivation, in particular culture mixing and gas exchange, relative to indoor
systems or even small mini-pond outdoor systems (Subtask 2.4, ASU). Key to this approach will
be accurate measurements of CO2 delivered to the cultivation system thru the use of real-time
flow totalizers and off-line grab sample measurements (e.g., total organic carbon (TOC), dissolved
inorganic carbon (DIC), etc.)) using standardized methodology proven out in other ASU led DOE
projects and harmonized with NLR.
Outcome: Identification of one or more strategies to improve CUE to at least 70%.

Subtask 2.4 CUE Optimization in Large Outdoor Ponds (Everett Eustance AzCATI,
Subtask Lead)
Objective or Hypothesis: For optimizing CO2 transfer efficiency (CTE) and CUE, small-scale
equipment—such as mini-pond raceways (<1000 L) of several square meters—comes with
limitations for extrapolating to much larger, commercially-relevant scales, and this may contribute
to a misunderstanding of key performance indicators like CUE and productivity. Small scale
cultivation systems (<10 m2) are both very well mixed and have relatively outsized paddlewheel
surface area vs. pond surface area, which actually dominates a number of key parameters such
as evaporation and in/out gassing relative to full scale systems which can impact key metrics such
as CTE/CUE. Rationale: In order to establish translatability of improved CUE, DISCOVR should
test in systems that more readily approximate conditions expected at scale, in particular around
pond hydrodynamics and gas exchange. Approach: We will implement routine measurements of

CTE/CUE leveraging method development and sensor systems from other BETO projects at
NREL and ASU, in order to baseline current performance of DISCOVR strains in 4.2 m 2 raceways,
and track performance improvements when implementing and verifying outdoors key findings
from Subtasks 2.1, 2.2, and 3.3. We will utilize 100 m2 ponds with mixing profiles (i.e., aspect
ratios) and paddlewheel to surface area ratios that more closely resemble large scale ponds,
narrowing the scalability-gap of DISCOVR outdoor cultivation data. Translatability: All indoor

CUE Calculations:
Carbon utilization efficiency (CUE) was calculated based on the amount of C-CO 2 delivered, as
measured by the totalizers, and the estimated amount of C incorporated into the algal biomass.
The C content of the algal biomass was approximately 50% (information provided by AzCATI
using CHN analysis), and did not shift, as the cultures were kept in nutrient replete conditions
through frequent harvesting. To determine the total C consumed, the volumetric productivity (g-
biomass·L·d-1) was multiplied by the total volume of the raceway (820 L), and the carbon fraction
of the biomass (0.50 g-carbon·g-biomass-1). CUE was then calculated by dividing the change in
g-C contained in biomass due to growth by g-C delivered as CO2 and multiplying by 100 to

An additional experiment was performed to baseline pH effects on P. celeri (TG2). To date, we
had not compared two different pH values side by side under the exact same cultivation conditions
as we have with the other top performing DISCOVR strains, all of which consistently show a
significant drop in productivity as pH set point is increased from 7.0 (McGowen, J., Knoshaug, E.,
Laurens, L.M, Forrester, J. (2023) “Outdoor annual algae productivity improvements at the pre-
pilot scale through crop rotation and pond operational management strategies”, Algal Research,
102995). In order to confirm if, under the standard AzCATI SOT cultivation conditions TG2 would
show a similar drop of 25-30% in productivity when cultivating at pH 8 vs pH 7, we conducted a
side-by-side experiment that began right at the end of the quarter on June 28th, 2023 and ran thru
July 17th, 2023 (20 days). As TG2 is a major focus for DISCOVR, the results presented below
have importance overall for all other Task 2 subtasks, in particular when running indoor simulators
which, with the exception of the ePBR’s at LANL, do not show as dramatic a drop off in productivity
as that observed outdoors as AzCATI when comparing pH 7 and pH 8. While NLR had not directly
compared pH 7 to 8 indoors on the SAGE reactors, LANL has run T. striata at pH 7 and pH 8 in
ePBRS and demonstrated the same ~ 30% drop observed outdoors for when running a spring
AzCATI script. The lack of replication of the pH effect on productivity results indoors calls into
question the utility of screening for higher pH strains indoors and these results led to a re-
examination of the drivers for low productivity at higher pH values. The reason the LEAPS, and
CSM systems did not match the productivity drops observed at AzCATI was due to those systems
running at higher sparging rates as mixing was driven by sparging, and not solely mechanical,
leading to more O2 stripping and thus lower dissolved oxygen (DO) levels relative to what is
routinely observed outdoors or in ePBRs. Adjustments were made to the indoor systems to allow
for DO levels in range with that observed outdoors moving forward for testing under Task 1 or
Task 2 within DISCOVR for FY24 and 25.

Effect of pH setpoint and alkalinity on CUE at high dilution rates: The final 4.2 m2 CUE experiment
with Tetraselmis striata LANL1001 focused on the demonstrating continued improvement of CUE
during spring season (end of March, 2025) to provide additional data and confirmation for pH
setpoint (7 vs 7.5) when operating under two different alkalinity regimes improving CUE. SPW
9/11/13 were operated at pH 7.5 using ammonium bicarbonate and 30% daily harvesting
frequency, while SPW 10/12/14 were supplied with ammonium chloride and operated at pH 7.
Based on the time of the year (early spring), and the normal nutrient consumption rate, we were
able to utilize the alkalinity from salt water replacement to offset the acidification from ammonium
consumption. This allowed us to operate under two distinct conditions of ~60-80 mg CaCO 3/L vs
~220-250 mg-CaCO3/L. Subtask 2.4 Figure 18 shows the pH and AFDW over the course of the
experiment. In looking at the pH during the experiment, it is clearly noticeable that the overnight
rise disappears as the culture reaches lower alkalinity levels due to acidification from ammonium
assimilation. However, the change in alkalinity and the pH setpoint of 7 vs 7.5 showed minimal
affect on the culture density. When looking at the point-to-point biomass productivity values
(Subtask 2.4 Figure 19 – left graph), we see minimal difference between the two scenarios: 11.2
± 3.6 g/m2/d vs 11.3 ± 2.6 g/m2/d for ammonium bicarbonate (pH 7.5) vs ammonium chloride (pH
7). However, when evaluating the CO2 delivery for the two scenarios, there is a significant
difference. Subtask 2.4 Figure 19 (right graph) shows the average daily gas delivery
requirements for both conditions, which over the course of the experiment was 93 ± 4 L-CO 2/d for
ammonium bicarbonate compared to 75 ± 5 L-CO2/d for ammonium chloride. This represents a
20% decrease in CO2 demand and overall 25% increase in CUE, despite being at a lower pH
setpoint and again reinforces the need to balance alkalinity/nutrient source with pH setpoint
maximize CUE for a given cultivation condition.

Subtask 2.4 Summary and Key Findings: The overall summary of all results of cultivation trials
where CUE was tracked is shown in Subtask 2.4 Table 11. While we demonstrated and reported
on ability to hit >70% CUE at 4.2 m2 scale, we have struggled to consistently achieve >50-60%
at 100 m2 scale. This is primarily driven by reduced productivity at 100 m2 scale (though we can
consistently achieve productivity within 80% of 4.2 m 2 as long as we are matching harvest
frequency/pond dilution rates). This lower productivity, whether its due to unoptimized operations
(e.g., lower dilution rates than optimal due to limitations of downstream dewatering and media
recycling capacity) or more often pond contamination leading to reduced productivity, points to
the most important lesson from this cycle of DISCOVR - implementing improvements in achieving
higher CUE requires high productivity to be maintained and or a more dynamically responsive
CO2 system than simply single setpoint/on-off pH stat operation so as not to waste significant
amounts of CO2 simply maintaining system pH while underperforming cultures can’t utilize it
effectively. All the improvements that were demonstrated are about not wasting CO 2, in particular
not delivering CO2 when the cultures aren’t effective in its utilization. Aspects of designing and
implementing more responsive systems as well as increasing downstream dewatering capacity
are core to already proposed and selected projects (though not yet allowed to proceed by the
DOE – i.e., the RBRH projects including SMaRT led by ASU) as well as new proposals. We will
continue to adapt our systems to achieve higher CUE in at 100 m 2 as funding and technology
priorities of DOE allow.

Overall Project Results Task 5:
Outdoor Cultivation Trials (John McGowen, AzCATI, Subtask Lead)
Objective: AzCATI was to conduct rigorous, standardized, cultivation trials outdoors under real-
world conditions in support of Tasks 1-4 with delivery of final curated dataset for public
dissemination and as input to Task 6 (TEA). Rationale: Operating in a standardized, experimental,
real-world format, backed up by over 5 years of near continuous annual production data allows
for greater confidence in estimating what may be achievable at scale and identify additional gaps
in knowledge that need to be addressed. Approach: AzCATI will utilize an outdoor experimental
cultivation framework in use by DISCOVR since summer of 2018 and originally established in
2013 under the Algae Testbed Public Private Partnership (ATP3). Seasonal biomass productivity
data will be collected and reported using the current best performing seasonal strain and
operational conditions but with a greater emphasis and resources dedicated to verification of
strategies identified in other tasks, specifically Tasks 1, 2, 3, and 4. Translatability: The outdoor
testing at AzCATI represents the culmination of the DISCOVR pipeline and is meant to verify the
ability of core concepts for improving algae biomass productivity or other key factors identified as
translatable to a real-world environment. However, the scales at which the outdoor cultivation
trials to date have been conducted (i.e., at 4.2 m2) and the manner under which those trials were
conducted (e.g., freshwater make-up, no water re-use, no nutrient recycling, no accurate tracking
of CO2 for CUE) are gaps that to the extent possible in the next 3-yr cycle should be closed relative
to the operational assumptions of the NREL Farm Model. Outcomes: Seasonal and annual
productivities for top-performing DISCOVR strains and verification of operational conditions
identified thru Tasks 1-4 that close the gap with expected operations at scale with curated data
deposited for public use. A key aspect for this cycle will be the continued supply of samples to
partners within DISCOVR for biomass composition as well as outdoor-indoor biotic vs. abiotic
experimentation, characterization of whole pond culture samples (e.g., metabolomic, genomic
analysis) and pest and or novel strain isolations.

and harvest frequency, implementation of crop protection strategies. Strain selection, pH set point
and dilution rate remained the main factors for improving productivity along with effective crop

grouped by season to calculate a seasonal average and the 4 best seasonal averages for the

Select Examples of Task 5 Experimentation During FY23-25
P. celeri clonal isolate (TG2) versus P. celeri consortium (TG-Gulf): In looking at several years of
TG2 cultivation data, the DISCOVR team hypothesized that the high light, high temperature, high
dilution rate clonal isolate (TG2) from CSM may actually be less resilient under conditions where
temperature/light change dramatically as can be experienced under real-world diel conditions in
particular in shoulder season transitions into or out of summer. It was thought that taking a step
back to evaluate the P. celeri enrichment from the Gulf of Mexico that had been maintained by
CSM in liquid culture might offer more resilience having a diverse population of P. celeri could
perform better under rapidly changing environmental conditions, filling in gaps in performance

Some additional cultivation was attempted in the fall of 2025 as the DISCOVR project was
wrapping up. Ulva was again scaled up and launched into two 4.2m2 ponds. These ponds were
cultivated through the fall and winter past the end of the DISCOVR project using internal
ASU/AzCATI funding. More aggressive harvesting was utilized throughout the cultivation trial
which lasted over 7 months, terminating in May of 2026. The summary of the productivities
achieved are shown in Task 5 Table 4 relative to those obtained with microalgae during DISCOVR.
A significant improvement in cold weather performance was observed with relatively un-optimized
cultivation of Ulva. These early results, which validate indoor pond simulation work at PNNL,
indicate that Ulva could be cultivated with high productivity on-shore and is worth additional R&D
funding.
