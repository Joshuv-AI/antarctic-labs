Abstract
This paper addresses the fusion of a pair of spatially unregistered hyperspectral image (HSI) and multispectral image (MSI) covering roughly overlapping regions. HSIs offer high spectral but low spatial resolution, while MSIs provide the opposite. The goal is to integrate their complementary information to enhance both HSI spatial resolution and MSI spectral resolution. While hyperspectral-multispectral fusion (HMF) has been widely studied, the unregistered setting remains challenging. Many existing methods focus solely on MSI super-resolution, leaving HSI unchanged. Supervised deep learning approaches were proposed for HSI super-resolution, but rely on accurate training data, which is often unavailable. Moreover, theoretical analyses largely address the co-registered case, leaving unregistered HMF poorly understood. In this work, an unsupervised framework is proposed to simultaneously super-resolve both MSI and HSI. The method integrates coupled spectral unmixing for MSI super-resolution with latent-space adversarial learning for HSI super-resolution. Theoretical guarantees on the recoverability of the super-resolution MSI and HSI are established under reasonable generative models—providing, to our best knowledge, the first such insights for unregistered HMF. The approach is validated on semi-real and real HSI-MSI pairs across diverse conditions.



I
Introduction

Remote sensing leverages various imaging modalities, such as
hyperspectral images
(HSIs) and
multispectral images
(MSIs)
[
46
]
, to capture rich information in both spatial and spectral domains. However, due to hardware limitations, HSIs typically provide high spectral but low spatial resolution, whereas MSIs offer the opposite. To address this trade-off,
hyperspectral and multispectral image fusion
(HMF) techniques aim to generate
super-resolution spectral images
(SRIs) with high resolution in both domains by combining HSI and MSI pairs acquired over similar regions
[
46
]
.

HMF methods have been developed from diverse perspectives, ranging from early approaches such as component substitution and multiresolution analysis
[
4
,
1
]
, to more recent strategies based on coupled matrix and tensor decomposition
[
21
,
8
,
47
,
40
,
36
]
, and most recently, deep learning-based methods
[
49
,
29
,
51
,
33
]
. In particular, matrix and tensor factorization approaches
[
26
,
21
,
8
,
31
,
23
]
have established analytical frameworks with performance guarantees. These methods model the observed HSI and MSI as degraded versions of an underlying super-resolution image (SRI), and cast HMF as an inverse problem to recover the SRI. Properties of matrix and tensor factorizations are then exploited to analyze the recoverability of the SRI. However, the vast majority of existing methods assume that the HSI and MSI are spatially co-registered—i.e., the images cover exactly the same spatial region and the objects share a common underlying continuous coordinate system. In practice, this assumption can be difficult to satisfy, especially when spatial misalignment in the acquired raw images is non-negligible
[
33
,
53
]
.

Hyperspectral-multispectral fusion with spatially

) is a practically important and challenging problem (see Fig.

[

,

,

used classical models for coordinate registration and SRI recovery simultaneously, while more recent approaches leverage neural networks to compensate for both spatial misalignment and imaging degradations
[

,

,

.

Challenges.
Despite growing interest in unregistered HMF, several critical challenges remain open.

(i)
Limited Problem Scope.
Many existing works adopt narrow definitions of “unregistration” and limited fusion objectives. For example,
[
52
,
45
,
18
,
16
,
29
,
13
]
address HSI and MSI pairs that are misaligned in coordinate systems but still cover exactly the same spatial region—i.e., unregistration is limited to rigid transformations such as rotation and shift. Other works
[
6
,
33
,
20
]
allow partial spatial non-overlap between the HSI and MSI, but only super-resolve the MSI’s spectral information. That is, they estimate the super-resolution image (SRI) over the MSI region, while the corresponding high-resolution content over the HSI region remains unmodeled.

(ii)
Dependence on Training Data.
Several recent methods
[
24
,
25
]
apply deep learning to enhance the spatial resolution of the HSI. These methods in principle can super-resolve the HSI without the assistance of a corresponding MSI.
However, these approaches require large amounts of training data, which may be unavailable in practice.

(iii)
Lack of Theoretical Understanding.
Fundamental aspects of unregistered HMF remain largely unexplored. In particular, theoretical guarantees on recoverability of the underlying high-resolution images associated with the HSI and MSI have only been established for the co-registered setting
[
21
,
8
,
26
,
31
,
30
]
, and remain absent for the unregistered case.

Contributions.

This work develops an unsupervised framework for unregistered HMF with theoretical guarantees. The goal is to recover the high-resolution content over both the MSI-covered and HSI-covered regions. Our main contributions are:

\bullet

An Unsupervised Framework for Unregistered HMF.

We adopt the
linear mixture model
(LMM)
[
28
]
, assuming shared endmembers across the MSI and HSI regions. A coupled spectral unmixing criterion (via block-term tensor decomposition (BTD)) is used to recover the high-resolution image over the MSI region. To reconstruct the high-resolution image over the HSI region, we take a multimodal generative model learning perspective and introduce a collaborative adversarial learning loss that aligns the distribution of transformed HSI abundance patches with that of the MSI abundance patches, enhancing the spatial resolution of the HSI. The framework requires neither spatial co-registration nor training data.

\bullet

Recoverability Guarantees.

We provide the first theoretical recovery guarantees for unregistered HMF. We prove that: (i) The BTD-based coupled spectral unmixing stage can recover the high-resolution image over the MSI region under mild assumptions, such as spatial smoothness leading to low-rank abundance map. While coupled tensor models were previously applied to co-registered HMF
[
21
,
8
,
31
]
, our results show that it remains effective in the unregistered setting; and (ii) the proposed adversarial learning loss, under a reasonable multimodal patch generative model, enables recovery of the high-resolution image over the HSI region. To our knowledge, such guarantees have not been established in the unregistered setting.

The proposed method is validated on both semi-real and real datasets, and achieves strong performance under a variety of conditions.

A preliminary version of this work was accepted to ICASSP 2026
[
37
]
(submitted after the journal submission, with the Editor-in-Chief informed), where only pixel misalignment scenarios (without angular mismatches) were considered.
The present journal version substantially extends that work by providing detailed proofs, new theoretical results accounting for angular mismatches, and a robust recoverability theorem.
In addition, it includes more extensive simulations and real-data experiments.

II
Background and Problem Statement

Assume that there is a pair of HSI and MSI acquired over similar spatial regions, denoted by

\displaystyle\text{HSI}:~\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}\in\mathbb{R}^{I^{({\rm H})}\times J^{({\rm H})}\times K^{({\rm H})}}

\displaystyle\text{MSI}:~\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}\in\mathbb{R}^{I^{({\rm M})}\times J^{({\rm M})}\times K^{({\rm M})}},

respectively.
Here,
I^{({\rm H})}\times J^{({\rm H})}
and
K^{({\rm H})}
denote the spatial and the spectral dimensions of the HSI, respectively; that is,
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}(i,j,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{({\rm H})}}
denotes the
HSI pixel measured over
K^{({\rm H})}
bands at location
(i,j)
. Similar notations are used for
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
as well.
Note that when the HSI and MSI cover similar regions,
we normally have

I^{({\rm H})}J^{({\rm H})}\ll I^{({\rm M})}J^{({\rm M})},\quad K^{({\rm H})}\gg K^{({\rm M})}.

This is because the HSI has a low spatial resolution but a high spectral resolution, while the MSI has the opposite. Hence, to describe similar spatial regions, the MSI uses many more pixels.
In addition, HSI sensors often operates on hundreds of wavelengths (i.e.,
K^{({\rm H})}\geq 100
) yet MSI sensors have
K^{({\rm M})}
’s ranging from single-digit wavelengths to a couple of dozens.

II-B

Unregistered HMF

For unregistered
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
, we propose a degradation model as follows (see Fig.
3
):

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}\xRightarrow{\text{spectral degrad.}}\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})},\quad\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}\xRightarrow{\text{spatial degrad.}}\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})},

(2)

where
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
are two different super-resolution spectral images with roughly overlapped spatial regions.
Both of
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
have the MSI’s spatial resolution and the HSI’s spectral resolution.
However, these two images do not share the same underlying spatial coordinate system due to unregistration.
Spectral and spatial degradations are applied to
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
, respectively, to produce the observed
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
.
Under (
2
),
unregistered HMF aims at recovering
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
by integrating the information in
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
.
In other words, the goal is to super-resolve
the and spectral resolution of the MSI and
the spatial resolution of the HSI, respectively.

A natural approach to handling unregistered HMF is to first apply a co-registration algorithm to the HSI-MSI pair and then use existing co-registered HMF methods (see, e.g.,
[
52
,
45
]
). Traditional co-registration techniques typically seek transformations of the pixel coordinates to correct mismatches between two images
[
45
,
16
]
, though they often perform poorly when faced with relatively large spatial misalignments (e.g., mismatches more than several pixels
[
33
]
).
Learning-based one-stage approaches have also been proposed (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
51
,
33
,
45
]
), employing neural networks to capture key aspects of unregistered HMF, such as degradation and coordinate transformation functions. With the rapid advancement of deep learning, these methods sometimes show promising performance.

II-C

Challenges

Although some progress has been made, several critical challenges in unregistered HMF remain unresolved.

On the methodology side, there is no established method for simultaneously recovering both
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
. Most existing works aim solely to recover
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}
, without addressing the recovery of
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}
—see, e.g.,
[
6
,
33
,
20
]
. Some hyperspectral super-resolution methods (which are not fusion-based), such as
[
25
,
24
]

, attempt to recover
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
from
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
alone using training data synthesized through assumed spatial degradation models. However, these approaches rely heavily on the accuracy of the assumed degradation process to generate training data, which may not hold in practice. As a result, how to jointly recover
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}
from spatially misaligned and partially overlapping
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
remains an open problem.

On the theory side, unlike the co-registered HMF setting—where recoverability has been well studied
[
21
,
8
,
26
,
23
,
31
]
—existing work on unregistered HMF has largely focused on methodological innovations and empirical results. To the best of our knowledge, no theoretical framework has been developed to analyze the recoverability of either
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}
or
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}
in the unregistered setting. Even the aforementioned methods that focus on recovering
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}
(e.g.,
[
6
,
33
,
20
]
) do not provide theoretical guarantees.

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\circ{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}.

(3a)

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R^{\prime}}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\circ{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm M})},

r

Taking the HSI as an example,

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}]_{i,j}=1,\quad\sum_{r=1}^{R^{\prime}}[{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}]_{i,j}=1.

(4)

The LMM representations of the

III
MSR via Coupled Spectral Unmixing

We first deal with the problem of sharpening the spectral resolution of
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
to recover
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
—i.e., the MSR task.
Under (
3
), MSR boils down to
unmixing

\{\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
from the MSI and
\{\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
from the HSI, respectively. Then, the high-resolution abundance maps can be “re-assembled” with the high-resolution endmembers to recover
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}
via
\widehat{\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm M})}_{r}\circ\widehat{\bm{c}}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
,
where
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm M})}_{r}
and
\widehat{\bm{c}}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
are the estimated MSI abundance and HSI spectrum of material
r
, respectively.

III-A

Problem Formulation for MSR

Many spectral unmixing approaches can potentially serve our purpose (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
15
,
3
]
).
In this work, we propose to use a tensor-based unmixing framework, which works under the following relatively mild assumption:

Assumption 3

The abundance maps of the HSI and MSI are low-rank matrices; i.e.,

\displaystyle{\rm rank}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})})\leq L_{r}^{({\rm M})},~{\rm rank}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})})\leq L_{r}^{({\rm H})},~\forall r\in[R],

(7)

where
L_{r}^{({\rm H})}
and
L_{r}^{({\rm M})}
denote the rank upper bounds of the HSI and MSI abundance maps associated with material
r
, respectively.

Assumption
3
is often used in spectral image analysis (see, e.g.,
[
32
,
8
,
9
,
43
,
30
]
), which stems from the fact that the abundance maps exhibit spatial smoothness in many cases.
For MSR, we formulate the following criterion:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}}\left\|\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\circ\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

(8a)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\left\|\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\circ(\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})})\right\|_{\rm F}^{2},

\displaystyle{\rm subject~to:}~{\rm rank}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})})\leq L_{r}^{({\rm M})},~\forall r\in[R],

(8b)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~{\rm rank}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})})\leq L_{r}^{({\rm H})},~\forall r\in[R],

(8c)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\!\top\!},~~\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\!\top\!},

(8d)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})},\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\geq\bm{0},~\forall r\in[R].

(8e)

Note that the last two constraints are added according to the physical meaning of the factors; see (

III-B

MSR Recoverability

Denote
\{\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
as any optimal solution of (

Also represent the low-rank ground-truth abundances using factorization-based forms, i.e.,

We show the following:

Theorem 1

(MSR Recoverability)

Suppose that Assumptions

-
3

(\bm{A}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\bm{B}_{r}^{({\rm H})})

(\bm{A}_{r}^{({\rm M})},\bm{B}_{r}^{({\rm M})})

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\bm{S}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm H})},~\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}=\bm{S}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm M})},~\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\bm{c}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm H})},

almost surely.

The

A proof sketch is as follows.
Under the signal model in (

8

\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}

,
48
]
) in the supplementary material. Consequently,
\widehat{\underline{\bm{Y}}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm M})}
in (

The above result extends

and

to differ—a scenario that does not arise in the co-registered case considered in

The conditions in Theorem

favor cases where the number of materials and the rank of the abundance maps are moderate. The low rank condition in Assumption

[

,

,
30

IV
HSR via Abundance Adversarial Learning

The more challenging task is to enhance the spatial resolution of
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
, i.e., the HSR task.
Note that in the spectral unmixing stage, we identify
\{\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\}
such that
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\circ\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
. Under the LMM, the spatial information of the HSI is completely captured in
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
for
r=1,\ldots,R
. Therefore, the task amounts to enhancing the resolution of the abundance maps
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
for all
r
.

IV-A

Patch-based Distribution Matching

In this work, we aim to enhance the spatial resolution of patches from
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
so that the resulting resolution matches that of the corresponding MSI patches. We focus on patches because super-resolution is inherently a local spatial operation. To illustrate our idea, let
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]
and
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]
denote two patches extracted from the HSI and MSI, respectively, where
{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B^{({\rm H})}\times B^{({\rm H})}}
and
{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B^{({\rm M})}\times B^{({\rm M})}}
represent the pixel index sets of the patches centered at the coordinate
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is defined as follows:

Definition 1

The coordinate
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a global coordinate in the ground-truth continuous spatial domain, where the
w_{1}
- and
w_{2}
-axes are aligned with the north and east directions, respectively (cf. latitude and longitude in the geographic coordinate system).

Consider the case where these two patches correspond to the same underlying spatial region. In this case,
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]
can be interpreted as a high-resolution counterpart of
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]
.
We postulate the existence of an unknown nonlinear function
\bm{f}^{\star}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{B^{({\rm H})}\times B^{({\rm H})}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{B^{({\rm M})}\times B^{({\rm M})}}
that maps the low-resolution HSI patch to its high-resolution MSI counterpart:

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]=\bm{f}^{\star}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]\right),\quad\forall r\in[R].

(10)

Our objective then amounts to recovering
\bm{f}^{\star}
, whose existence will be discussed under a multimodal generative model shortly (see Lemma
1
).

In unregistered HMF, a core challenge lies in the fact that the correspondence between
{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}
and
{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}
is
unknown
.
A major reason is that the association of patches and the underlying coordinate
\bm{w}
is not available in the unregistered HMF case.
As a result, regression-based approaches for learning
\bm{f}^{\star}
in (
10
)—such as formulations of the form

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\star}\approx\arg\min_{\bm{f}}\sum_{\bm{w}}\|\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]-\bm{f}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}])\|^{2},

(11)

are not applicable.
To overcome this difficulty, an alternative is to find a function
\bm{f}
such that the
distributions
of
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]
and
\bm{f}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}])
are matched, rather than relying on paired patch matching as in (
11
).
Note that if one cannot distinguish the distributions
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]
and
\bm{f}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}])
, it means that they visually belong to the same feature space—the resolution of
\bm{f}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}])
matches that of
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}]
, and therefore (hopefully)
\bm{f}=\bm{f}^{\star}
.
In other words, assume that

\displaystyle{\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]\sim p_{r}^{({\rm H})},}~\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}]\sim p_{r}^{({\rm M})},

(12)

where
p_{r}^{({\rm H})}
and
p_{r}^{({\rm M})}
are joint distributions of the pixels of the HSI and MSI abundance patches of material
r
, respectively.
Our learning objective boils down to finding
\bm{f}
such that

\displaystyle[\bm{f}]_{\#}p_{r}^{({\rm H})}=p_{r}^{({\rm M})},~\forall r\in[R].

(13)

Distribution matching objective such as Eq. (
13
) can be realized using tools like the
generative adversarial networks
(GANs)
[
17
]
. Let
\widehat{\bm{f}}
be any solution of (
13
). Then,
one can estimate the super-resolved version of
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]
using

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{f}}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}]),~\forall\bm{w}

(14)

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}[{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\bm{f}}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{\bm{w}}])\circ\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})},~\forall{\cal H}.

(15)

Note that if
\widehat{\bm{f}}=\bm{f}^{\star}
, then we provably recover the super-resolved HSI patch by patch.

Remark 2

To implement the above idea, there are a couple of critical challenges to overcome.
First, how should one define (and subsequently sample) the patches
{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}
and
{\cal H}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}
to ensure the existence of a valid super-resolution signal
\bm{f}^{\star}
under reasonable generative models?
Second—and more critically—under what conditions does the method guarantee identifiability of
\bm{f}^{\star}
, and hence the recoverability of the high-resolution
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
?
The answers to both questions are far from obvious. In the following subsection, we develop implementation strategies based on a reasonable generative model, which offer insights into these fundamental issues.

IV-B

Angle Randomization and Recoverability

To realize the idea in (
13
), we hope to sample
\{{\cal M}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and
\{{\cal H}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that
there is an underlying one-to-one mapping between the HSI and MSI patches. This is necessary to ensure that there exists an
{\bm{f}}^{\star}
that maps any HSI patch uniquely to its target MSI representation.

To find such patch samples, some nuanced caveats exist: Notably, the
orientations
of coordinate systems of the MSI and HSI are in general unknown due to unregistration. If one simply samples square patches from both images, these patches may never correspond to the same underlying regions.
A remedy is as follows: Assume that the MSI and HSI patches are associated with
D\times D
m
2
areas in space.
One can randomize the rotation of the sampled patches, so that every possible rotation of a spatial
D\times D
m
2
window is covered in
\{{\cal M}_{\ell}\}
and
\{{\cal H}_{t}\}
.
However, as any
N\times
90 degree rotation (where

) of the
D\times D
m
2
square region covers the same spatial area, such replicates still lacks one-to-one correspondence.

To circumvent the above subtle but critical challenges, we define the following notions:

Definition 2

(Patch Boundary Indexing)

For any square patch, the boundaries are indexed by integers
\{1,2,3,4\}

Definition 3

(Patch Rotation Angle)

When the first boundary of a patch points to the (unknown) ground-truth north, we define the rotation angles of the patches as
\angle{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}=\angle{\cal H}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}=0

\angle{\cal M}^{\bm{w}}=\angle{\cal H}^{\bm{w}^{\prime}}=\theta

\theta

\bm{w}

(Patch Generative Model)

Under Assumption

Remark 3

Assumption

Fact 1

Assume that

[

We should remark that Eq. (

\bm{f}
to match
R

Assumption 5

(Sufficiently Diverse Abundances (SDA))

For the set of conditional distributions
\{p(\bm{z}_{r}|\bar{\theta})\}_{r=1}^{R}

\displaystyle\int_{\cal A}p(\bm{z}_{r^{\prime}}|\bar{\theta})d\bm{z}\neq\int_{\cal B}p(\bm{z}_{r^{\prime}}|\bar{\theta})d\bm{z},~\forall\bar{\theta}.

(20)

Simply speaking, as
\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}
and
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}

\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\bar{\theta})}]

\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w}^{\prime},\bar{\theta})}]

have different distributions—that is,
when there is no rotation mismatch, the abundance maps of different materials are sufficiently different.
Under this assumption, we show:

Theorem 2

(HSR Recoverability)

Assume that Eq. (
9

4
-
5

is continuous with respect to
\theta
.
In addition, assume that
\bm{f},\bm{d}_{r}
are universal function approximators.
Let
\widehat{\bm{f}}
denote any optimal solution of (
18
) when

.
Then,
we have
\widehat{\bm{f}}=\bm{f}^{\star}
and

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\bm{f}}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]\right)\circ\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})},

(21)

for any
\bm{w},\theta
.

Theorem
2
asserts that, as long as the abundance maps of the
R

\bm{f}^{\star}

Theorem

and

Assumption 6

(Relaxed

Define

(22)

Then, the relaxed

\displaystyle\max_{({\cal A},{\cal B})\in\mathcal{V}}\max\{{\rm dia(\mathcal{A})},{\rm dia(\mathcal{B})}\}\leq\eta

Note that (

Under this relaxed assumption, we show the following:

Theorem 3

(Robust HSR Recoverability)

Let all assumptions in Theorem

5
replaced by Assumption
6
.
In addition, assume that
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}
and
\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}
satisfy bi-Lipchitz conditions, particularly,

\displaystyle\|\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{z}_{1})-\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{z}_{2})\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{({\rm M})}\|\bm{z}_{1}-\bm{z}_{2}\|_{2},

(24a)

\displaystyle\|\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}(\bm{z}_{1})-\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}(\bm{z}_{2})\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha^{({\rm H})}\|\bm{z}_{1}-\bm{z}_{2}\|_{2},

(24b)

for all
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Then, we have

\|\widehat{\bm{f}}(\bm{h})-\bm{f}^{\star}(\bm{h})\|_{\rm F}\leq\frac{2(\alpha^{({\rm M})})^{2}}{\alpha^{({\rm H})}}\eta,

(25)

for
\forall\bm{h}\in{\rm dom}({\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}])
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\|\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]-\widehat{\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm H})}_{\rm SRI}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]\right\|_{\rm F}

(26)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{2(\alpha^{({\rm M})})^{2}}{\alpha^{({\rm H})}}\eta\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|\bm{c}_{r}^{({\rm H})}\|_{2},

holds for any
\bm{w}
and
\theta
.

Theorem
3
shows that both the estimation error of the super-resolution function and the reconstruction error of the super-resolved patches scale linearly with the degree of violation of the SDA assumption—as long as the functions
\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}
and
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}
preserve distances without collapsing or tearing. The proof is via integrating the the robustness analysis of diversified distribution matching
[
35
]
with the patch generative model in (
16
); see the supplementary material.

In the sequel, we refer to our overall method (consisting of the MSR and HSR stages) as
Factorized Representation for Enhanced Super-resolution using latent Component-adversarial Optimization
(
FRESCO
).

V
Implementation of FRESCO

In this section, we discuss the details of
FRESCO
implementation, i.e., practical reformulations and realizations of (
8
) and (
18
).

V-A

Practical Implementation of Problem (
8
)

To handle Problem (
8
), we use ideas from the co-registered HMF work
[
8
]
that also used a block-term tensor based formulation.
Specifically, we relax the hard constraints as regularization terms. The low-rank term is approximated by

{\cal L}^{\rm LR}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}({\rm tr}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})})^{\!\top\!}+\tau\bm{I})^{\frac{p}{2}}+{\rm tr}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})})^{\!\top\!}+\tau\bm{I})^{\frac{p}{2}}),

where
0<p\leq 1
which is a nonconvex Shattern-
p
function that is often used for low-rank promoting; also see
[
8
]
. The sum-to-one condition
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\!\top\!}
is promoted by the regularization

{\cal L}^{\rm sto}=\left\|\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}-\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\!\top\!}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\left\|\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}-\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\!\top\!}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}.

The same regularization terms are used for
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
.
Like in
[
8
]
, we also employ a nonconvex
\ell_{q}
function-based total variation regularization on each
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}

[
8
]
:

{\cal L}^{\rm TV}=\sum_{i=1}^{I^{({\rm M})}J^{({\rm M})}}\left([\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{r}]_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}+\left([\check{\bm{s}}_{r}]_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{q}{2}},

where
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{r}=(\bm{H}\otimes\bm{I})\bm{s}_{r}
,
\check{\bm{s}}_{r}=(\bm{I}\otimes\bm{H})\bm{s}_{r}
,
0<q\leq 1
,
\varepsilon>0
,
\bm{s}_{r}=\mathrm{{\rm vec}}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})})
,
\bm{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{J^{({\rm M})}\times J^{({\rm M})}}
, and

\bm{H}
is a circulant matrix whose first row is
[1,-1,0,\ldots,0]
.
The overall objective is as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm fitting}+\lambda^{\rm LR}{\cal L}^{\rm LR}+\lambda^{\rm sto}{\cal L}^{\rm sto}+\lambda^{\rm TV}{\cal L}^{\rm TV}

(27)

where we have
{\cal L}^{\rm fitting}=\eqref{eq:obj_MSR}
.

By our reformulation, Problem (
8
) is recast into a differentiable loss with only nonnegativity constraints. Hence, many off-the-shelf nonconvex optimization algorithms can be used to handle the problem. The formulation can be handled by the alternating projected gradient method proposed in
[
8
]
—although the algorithm was proposed in the context of co-registered HMF, the algorithmic framework can also be applied to tackle our coupled factorization problem.

V-B

Practical Realization of Problem (
18
)

As mentioned, the realization of (
18
) is based on sampling patches
{\cal M}_{\ell}
and
{\cal H}_{t}
with randomized centers and rotation angles. To make the learned
\bm{f}
bijective, we use a regularization as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm inv}(\bm{g},\bm{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bm{g}\left(\bm{f}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}_{t}]\right)\right)-\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}_{t}]\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left\|\bm{f}\left(\bm{g}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}_{\ell}]\right)\right)-\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}_{\ell}]\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right).

We observe that training GAN-based translation presents a challenging min-max optimization problem and divergence frequently happens. Particularly, when the updates explore disparate scales of
\bm{f}(\cdot)
in the beginning phase, the algorithm could fail to converge.
To fend against this effect, we propose a scaling regularization term, preventing
\bm{f}
and
\bm{g}
from changing the global intensity of the patches:

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm scale}

\displaystyle(\bm{g},\bm{f})=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mu\left(\bm{f}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}_{t}]\right)\right)-\mu\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}_{t}]\right)\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\mu\left(\bm{g}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}_{\ell}]\right)\right)-\mu\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}_{\ell}]\right)\right)^{2}\right),

where
\mu(\bm{X})
denotes the mean of
\bm{X}
. As will be shown in the ablation study (see Sec.
VI-B
), this regularization term assists stabilizing the numerical behavior.

Denote the loss in (
18
) as
{\cal L}^{\rm DM}(\bm{f},\{\bm{d}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R})
. Then, our overall loss for HSR is as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{f},\bm{g}}\max_{\{\bm{d}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}}{\cal L}^{\rm DM}

\displaystyle(\bm{f},\{\bm{d}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R})

(28)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{\rm inv}{\cal L}^{\rm inv}(\bm{g},\bm{f})+\lambda^{\rm scale}{\cal L}^{\rm scale}(\bm{g},\bm{f}),

where
\lambda^{\rm inv},\lambda^{\rm scale}\geq 0
are regularization parameters.
Here, both
\bm{f}
and
\bm{g}
are represented by neural networks.
The function
\bm{f}
is represented by an upscaling layer followed by a U-Net
[
34
]
, and the
\bm{g}
function is a downsampling neural network; see the detailed structure in the supplementary material.

V-C

Heuristic Remedy when
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
is Unavailable

Our development used the assumption that
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
is known.
When this information is not available, the formulation in (
8
) might not be able to align
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}
and
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
that associate with the same material.

As a remedy, we propose a heuristic to estimate
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
as follows

\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}}\sum_{k=1}^{K^{({\rm M})}}\left(\left(\mu\big(\underline{\widetilde{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm M})}(:,:,k)\big)-\mu\big(\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}(:,:,k)\big)\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad+\left.\left({\rm var}\big(\underline{\widetilde{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm M})}(:,:,k)\ \big)-{\rm var}\big(\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}(:,:,k)\big)\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle{\rm subject~to}\quad\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\geq\bm{0},\ \ \bm{P}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{\varOmega})=\bm{0},

(29)

where
\widetilde{\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm M})}(i,j,:)=\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}(i,j,:)
for all
(i,j)\in[I^{({\rm H})}]\times[J^{({\rm H})}]
,
and
{\rm var}(\bm{X})
denotes the variance of all elements of the input matrix
\bm{X}
.
In the above, the nonnegativity of
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
holds as spectral degradation is often understood as a weighted sum process
[
21
,
36
]
.
The index set
\bm{\varOmega}
represents the indices
(i,j)
where
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}(i,j)=0
, indicating that the
j
th band of
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
does not contribute to the
i
th band of
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
(as the MSI bands are considered local integrations of neighboring HSI bands
[
21
,
36
,
8
]
).

The idea of (
V-C
) is to treat
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}(i,j,k)
for all
k
as i.i.d. Gaussian variables.
Then, the ground-truth
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
should make the distributions of
\widetilde{\underline{\bm{Y}}}(i,j,k)
and
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}(i,j,k)
for any
k
identical—which is enforced by matching their means and variances.
This idea avoids using any spatial alignment information to estimate
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
, which is needed in existing methods, e.g., that in
[
36
]
.

We note that in our framework
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
is used to resolve the permutation ambiguity inherent in hyperspectral unmixing and to enforce a material-wise correspondence between the MSI and HSI abundance maps. However, recoverability in the MSR and HSR stages does not strictly depend on access to
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
, but only on whether such a correspondence can be established. In some cases, the abundance maps can be aligned without explicit knowledge of
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
, e.g., via visual inspection. In those cases,
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
is not required by the
FRESCO
pipeline.

V-D

Inference Strategy

The model
\widehat{\bm{f}}
is trained to super-resolve small patches. To convert the entire low-resolution abundance maps
\bm{S}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
for all
r\in[R]
into their high-resolution versions, we adopt a sliding-window strategy: overlapping patches are sequentially extracted from each
\bm{S}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
with a stride of
1
, where stride refers to the “step size” by which the window moves across the spatial domain of the image. Specifically, the sliding window moves across the image one pixel at a time—first moving left to right along each row, and then moving down to the next row—similar to how convolution operations scan an image.
Then, each patch is processed independently by
\widehat{\bm{f}}
. The resulting high-resolution patches are then placed back into their corresponding locations within the high-resolution abundance maps. In regions where outputs overlap, pixel values are averaged to mitigate boundary artifacts and ensure smooth transitions. After applying this patch-based super-resolution process to all
\bm{S}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
for
r\in[R]
, the high-resolution
\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\rm SRI}^{({\rm H})}
can be reconstructed via (
21
).

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed
FRESCO
, a new algorithmic framework to tackle the challenging problem of unregistered spectral image fusion for spatio-spectral super-resolution.
FRESCO
integrates coupled spectral unmixing (via coupled block-term tensor decomposition) and latent-space adversarial learning to jointly super-resolve both MSI and HSI in an unsupervised manner—without relying on training data or spatial co-registration. Importantly, we established the first theoretical guarantees for the recoverability of both super-resolved images under reasonable generative models in the unregistered spectral image fusion setting.
Extensive experiments on semi-real and real datasets demonstrate that
FRESCO
not only achieves state-of-the-art performance, but also offers a practical and theoretically grounded tool for unregistered hyperspectral-multispectral fusion.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof is similar to that under co-registered HMF settings
[
8
]
,
with some critical differences.
For example, the unregistered case allows the ranks of
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}
and
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
to be different.
Nonetheless, the key steps in
[
8
]
can be used in our case, leading to the desired conclusion of Theorem
1
.

A-A

Preliminaries

The proof will use properties of a tensor decomposition model, namely, the LL1 decomposition. The definition is as follows
[
7
]
:

Definition 4

(LL1 Decomposition)

A tensor
\underline{\bm{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J\times K}
admits a block-term decomposition (BTD) with multilinear rank-(
L_{r},L_{r},1
) terms if it can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}\circ\bm{c}_{r},~{\rm rank}(\bm{S}_{r})=L_{r},

(31)

for
r=1,\ldots,R
.

We will need to use the following lemma:

Lemma 2

[
7
]
For an LL1 tensor, denote
\bm{S}_{r}=\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\!\top\!}
where
\bm{A}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times L_{r}}
and
\bm{B}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times L_{r}}
. Assume that
L_{r}=L
and all
r\in[R]
are drawn from any joint absolutely continuous distribution. Then, the LL1 decomposition of
\underline{\bm{Y}}
is essentially unique almost surely, if
IJ\geq L^{2}R
and

\min\big(\lfloor\nicefrac{{I}}{{L}}\rfloor,R\big)+\min\big(\lfloor\nicefrac{{J}}{{L}}\rfloor,R\big)+\min\big(K,R\big)\geq 2R+2.

(32)

Here, “essential uniqueness” means that if
\underline{\bm{Y}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\big(\widehat{\bm{A}}_{r}(\widehat{\bm{B}}_{r}^{\!\top\!}\big))\circ\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}
, then we must have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}=\alpha_{r}\bm{S}_{\bm{\pi}(r)},~\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}=(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{r}}})\bm{c}_{\bm{\pi}(r)},

(33)

where
\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}=\widehat{\bm{A}}_{r}\widehat{\bm{B}}_{r}^{\!\top\!}
,
\alpha_{r}\neq 0
for all
r\in[R]
, and
\bm{\pi}
is a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,R\}
.
We will also use the following:

Lemma 3

[
21
]
Let
\widetilde{\bm{A}}=\bm{P}\bm{A}
, where
\bm{A}
is drawn from any absolutely continuous joint distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{I\times L}
and
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{I^{\prime}\times I}
has full row rank. Then,
\widetilde{\bm{A}}
follows an absolutely continuous joint distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{I^{\prime}\times L}
.

A-B

Proof of The Theorem

Note that that
\{\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})},\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
is an optimal solution to Problem (
8
). We will prove that the MSI and HSI decomposition parts share the same scaling and permutation ambiguities, and thus re-assembling
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm M})}_{r}
and
\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}
would correctly recover the desired super-resolution
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}
.

Lemma
2
indicates that the following equations hold

\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm M})}_{r}=\alpha_{r}\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)},~\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\widehat{\bm{c}}^{({\rm H})}_{r}=\alpha_{r}^{-1}\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)}

(34)

where
\alpha_{r}\neq 0
for all
r\in[R]
and
\bm{\pi}_{1}
denotes a permutation of
\{1,\cdots,R\}
resulted in the MSI decomposition (cf. Lemma
2
).

We first show that
\alpha_{r}=1
for all
r\in[R]
. Note that
\{\bm{A}_{r}^{({\rm M})},\bm{B}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
are drawn from continuous distribution, then the following matrix:

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}

\displaystyle=\big[{\rm{\rm vec}}(\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{1}),\cdots,{\rm{\rm vec}}(\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{R})\big]

\displaystyle=\big[{\rm vec}\big(\bm{A}^{({\rm M})}_{1}(\bm{B}^{({\rm M})}_{1})^{\!\top\!}\big),\cdots,{\rm vec}\big(\bm{A}^{({\rm M})}_{R}(\bm{B}^{({\rm M})}_{R})^{\!\top\!}\big)\big],

has full column rank almost surely under the condition
I^{({\rm M})}J^{({\rm M})}\geq(L^{({\rm M})})^{2}R\geq R
. Note that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm M})}_{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\alpha_{r}\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)}=\bm{1}_{I^{({\rm M})}\times J^{({\rm M})}}

(35)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\alpha_{r}-1)\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)}=\bm{0}_{I^{({\rm M})}\times J^{({\rm M})}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\alpha_{r}-1){\rm vec}\big(\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)}\big)=\bm{0}_{I^{({\rm M})}J^{({\rm M})}}

As
\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}
has full column rank indicates that
{\rm vec}\big(\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{r}\big)
are linearly independent for
r\in[R]
. Thus, the only solution to (
35
) is that
\alpha_{r}=1
for all
r\in[R]
.

Moreover, applying Lemma
2
to the
\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm H})}
, we have the following equations:

\widehat{\bm{S}}^{({\rm H})}_{r}=\beta_{r}\bm{S}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}_{2}(r)},~\widehat{\bm{c}}^{({\rm H})}_{r}=\beta_{r}^{-1}\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}_{2}(r)}

(36)

With the arguments one can obtain that
\beta_{1}=\cdots=\beta_{R}=1
.
Plugging (
36
) into (
34
), we have

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}_{2}(r)}=\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)}~,\forall r\in[R]

(37)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{C}(\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\bm{\Pi}_{2})=\bm{0}_{K^{({\rm M})}\times R}

(38)

where
\bm{C}=\big[\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{1},\cdots,\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{R}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{({\rm H})}\times R}
,
\bm{\Pi}_{1}=[\bm{e}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(1)},\cdots,\bm{e}_{\bm{\pi}_{1}(R)}]
and
\bm{\Pi}_{2}=[\bm{e}_{\bm{\pi}_{2}(1)},\cdots,\bm{e}_{\bm{\pi}_{2}(R)}]
, where
\bm{e}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th column of identity matrix
\bm{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
. Note that when
R=1
, then
\bm{\pi}_{1}(1)=\bm{\pi}_{2}(1)=1
holds trivially.

In what follows we claim that
\bm{\Pi}_{1}=\bm{\Pi}_{2}
still holds under the situation when
R\geq 2
. Since
\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{r}
is drawn from any absolutely continuous joint distribution for all
r\in[R]
and
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}
has full row rank, then Lemma
3
indicates that
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{C}
follows an absolutely continuous joint distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{K^{({\rm M})}\times R}
. It further indicates that Kruskal rank of
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{C}
is
\min(K^{({\rm M})},R)
with probability one.

Therefore, with the assumption
K^{({\rm M})}\geq 2
, we have
\min(K^{({\rm M})},R)\geq 2
, which means that any two columns of
\bm{P}^{({\rm M})}\bm{C}
are linearly independent. Since
\bm{\Pi}_{1}
and
\bm{\Pi}_{2}
are both permutation matrices, every column of
\big(\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big)
always has at most two non-zero elements. This means that the only solution to (
38
) is

\big[\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big]_{:,r}=\bm{0},\ \forall r\in[R],

which leads to
\bm{\pi}_{1}(r)=\bm{\pi}_{2}(r)=\bm{\pi}(r)
holds for all
r\in[R]
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\bm{S}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm H})},~\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}=\bm{S}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm M})},~\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\bm{c}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}^{({\rm H})},

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}^{({\rm M})}\circ\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}^{({\rm H})}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}\circ\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{\bm{\pi}(r)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}^{({\rm M})}_{r}\circ\bm{c}^{({\rm H})}_{r}=\underline{\bm{Y}}^{({\rm M})}_{\rm SRI}.

This completes the proof.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
1

We show that there exists an
\bm{f}^{\star}
that is a bijective and continuous function by construction.
Under the generative model in Assumption
4
, both
\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathrm{dom}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[\mathcal{H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}])
and
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathrm{dom}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[\mathcal{M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}])
are bijections, they are invertible between the domain and the range.

This means that that for any
\bm{z}_{r}
, we have
\bm{z}_{r}=(\bm{g}^{({\rm M})})^{-1}\bullet\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{z}_{r})
and
\bm{z}_{r}=(\bm{g}^{({\rm H})})^{-1}\bullet\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}(\bm{z}_{r})
. Then, one can construct
\bm{f}^{\star}=\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}\bullet(\bm{g}^{({\rm H})})^{-1}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\underbrace{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}\bullet(\bm{g}^{({\rm H})})^{-1}}_{\bm{f}^{\star}}\bullet\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}(\bm{z}_{r})=\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{z}_{r}),

(39)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\star}(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}])=\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}].

(40)

As
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}
and
\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}
are both continuous functions with continuous inverses, the composite function
\bm{f}^{\star}=\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}\bullet(\bm{g}^{({\rm H})})^{-1}
is guaranteed to be continuous as well. Similarly, one can also construct a continuous and bijective function

\bm{q}^{\star}=\bm{g}^{({\rm H})}\bullet(\bm{g}^{({\rm M})})^{-1}

that maps
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]
to its corresponding
\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]
. Therefore,
\bm{f}^{\star}
is a continuous bijective function,
which completes the proof.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
2

Step 1)
:
From Lemma
1
, we have established that

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\star}\left(\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]\right)=\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}],

(41)

for all the
\bm{w},\theta
.
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle[\bm{f}^{\star}]_{\#}p^{({\rm H})}_{r}=p^{({\rm M})}_{r},~\forall r\in[R],

(42)

\displaystyle\text{where~}\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm M})}[{\cal M}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]\sim p_{r}^{({\rm M})},~\bm{S}_{r}^{({\rm H})}[{\cal H}^{(\bm{w},\theta)}]\sim p_{r}^{({\rm H})}.

That is, point-wise correspondence implies distribution matching (but not the other way around).

Step 2)
: Now we show that as long as the SDA condition is satisfied, the collection of PDFs
\{p_{r}^{({\rm M})}\}_{r=1}^{R}
also satisfies a similar condition.

Note that
p(\bm{z}_{r})
is the marginal density obtained by averaging
p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)
over
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, i.e.,

p(\bm{z}_{r})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)d\theta,

where we have used the fact that
\theta
follows the uniform distribution between
[0,2\pi]
.

Consider two disjoint, open, and connected sets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
.
Assumption
5
indicates that their probability masses under the conditional density
p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)
are unequal for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, i.e.,

\int_{\mathcal{A}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z}\neq\int_{\mathcal{B}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z},\quad\forall\,\theta\in[0,2\pi].

(43)

Now we define

\bm{v}_{r}(\theta)\triangleq\int_{\mathcal{A}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z}-\int_{\mathcal{B}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z}.

(44)

Since
p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)
is continuous with respect to
\theta
, and the integrals are taken over fixed connected and open sets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{Z}
, it follows that
\bm{v}_{r}(\theta)
is also continuous on
[0,2\pi]
.
Moreover, (
43
) implies that
\bm{v}_{r}(\theta)\neq 0
for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. By the Intermediate Value Theorem,
\bm{v}_{r}(\theta)
is either strictly positive or strictly negative for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}p(\bm{z}_{r})\,d\bm{z}-\int_{\mathcal{B}}p(\bm{z}_{r})\,d\bm{z}

(45)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{\mathcal{A}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z}-\int_{\mathcal{B}}p(\bm{z}_{r}\mid\theta)\,d\bm{z}\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{v}_{r}(\theta)\,d\theta\neq 0.

Therefore, under the SDA, there is always an
({\cal A},{\cal B})
-dependent
r^{\prime}=r({\cal A},{\cal B})\in\{1,\ldots,R\}
, such that for any
({\cal A},{\cal B})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\cal A}p(\bm{z}_{r^{\prime}})d\bm{z}\neq\int_{\cal B}p(\bm{z}_{r^{\prime}})d\bm{z}.

(46)

As
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}
is bijective, we know that the following also holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal A})}p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})\,d\bm{m}\ \neq\ \int_{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal B})}p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})\,d\bm{m},

(47)

where we have used
\bm{m}
to represent the MSI patches for notation simplicity.
Here
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal A})
denotes the image of
{\cal A}
under
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}
, and
p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})
is the corresponding PDF of
\bm{m}=\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{z})
.
Indeed, by change of variables, we can write:

\int_{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal A})}p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})\,d\bm{m}=\int_{\cal A}p_{r^{\prime}}(\bm{z})\,d\bm{z},

and similarly for the integral over
\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal B})
,
leading to the conclusion in (
47
). That is, for any disjoint, connected, and open
{\cal A},{\cal B}\subseteq{\cal Z}
, there exists
r^{\prime}=r({\cal A},{\cal B})\in\{1,\ldots,R\}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal A})}p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})\,d\bm{m}\neq\int_{\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}({\cal B})}p_{r^{\prime}}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})\,d\bm{m}

(48)

Step 3)
:
We now show that solving (
18
) in the limit as
T,L\rightarrow\infty
recovers this ground-truth mapping.
To proceed, we first observe that any optimal solution
\widehat{\bm{f}}
to (
18
) satisfies

[\widehat{\bm{f}}]_{\#}p^{({\rm H})}_{r}=p^{({\rm M})}_{r},\quad\forall r\in[R],

(49)

as established in
[
17
,
35
]
.
Note that Lemma
1
guarantees the existence of a mapping
\bm{f}^{\star}
that satisfies (
17
). However, all the
\widehat{\bm{f}}=\bm{h}\bullet\bm{f}^{\star}
functions with

[\bm{h}]_{\#}p^{({\rm M})}_{r}=p^{({\rm M})}_{r},~\forall r\in[R]

(50)

satisfy (
49
) as well. Our goal is to show that there does not exist a non-identity continuous
\bm{h}
such that (
50
) holds. To this end, we follow the steps in
[
35
, Theorem 1]
, with slight modifications to accommodate our context. The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that there exists
\overline{\bm{m}}\in\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\mathcal{Z})
such that

\bm{h}(\overline{\bm{m}})\neq\overline{\bm{m}}.

(51)

Then, we can define an open set as follows:

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\overline{\bm{m}})\cap\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\mathcal{Z}),

(52)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\overline{\bm{m}})
defines a ball centered at
\overline{\bm{m}}
with a radius of
\epsilon
. The above means that
\bm{h}(\mathcal{D})\subseteq\bm{g}^{({\rm M})}(\mathcal{Z})
under (
50
). The continuity of
\bm{h}
also implies that

\bm{h}(\overline{\bm{m}})\in\bm{h}(\mathcal{D}).

(53)

One can always choose a small enough
\epsilon
such that

\mathcal{D}\cap\bm{h}(\mathcal{D})=\emptyset.

(54)

Note that
\mathcal{D}
is a connected set, which means that
\bm{h}(\mathcal{D})
is also connected. Thus, one can construct two disjoint, open, and connected sets satisfying

\int_{\mathcal{D}}p_{r}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})d\bm{m}=\int_{\bm{h}(\mathcal{D})}p_{r}^{({\rm M})}(\bm{m})d\bm{m},~\forall r\in[R].

(55)

Let

and
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}=(\bm{g}^{({\rm M})})^{-1}\left(\bm{h}(\mathcal{D})\right)
. The continuity of

indicates that

satisfying (

attaining (

) is

. Subsequently, one can recover

). This completes the proof.
