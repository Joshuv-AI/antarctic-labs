Spatial overhead reduction for 2D hypergraph product codes
Aarav Pabla, Yu-Xin Wang (王语馨), Yifan Hong

Abstract
The hypergraph product creates a quantum stabilizer code from two input classical linear codes; a paradigmatic example being the surface code as a hypergraph product of two classical repetition codes. Many properties of the hypergraph product code can be inherited from those of the classical codes such as the code dimension, minimum distance and certain fault-tolerant gadgets. We investigate ways to reduce the number of physical qubits in hypergraph product codes while maintaining some of their useful properties for fault tolerance. We show that the code dimension, canonical logical basis, and minimum distances of the hypergraph product code are preserved through this reduction. We also provide distance-preserving syndrome measurement schedules as well as examples of reduced hypergraph product codes with parameter improvements such as [ [ 610 , 64 , 6 ] ] → [ [ 441 , 64 , 6 ] ] [\![610,64,6]\!]\rightarrow[\![441,64,6]\!] and [ [ 1225 , 49 , 11 ] ] → [ [ 931 , 49 , 11 ] ] [\![1225,49,11]\!]\rightarrow[\![931,49,11]\!] . In memory simulations with circuit-level depolarizing noise, we observe that the reduced codes can have similar subthreshold performance as their unreduced versions, but using fewer physical qubits. Finally, we show how overhead reduction can be compatible with homomorphic measurement gadgets, fold-transversal gates and automorphisms, which extends the savings to logical computation.

Summary of main results

The main results of this work are summarized informally in the following statements. A technical assumption we require is that all logical qubits reside in a single HGP sector, which is satisfied when the input parity-check matrices have full rank. Our first result concerns the maximum qubit and check weights after overhead reduction.

3.6

The canonical logical Pauli basis

The preservation of the canonical logical Pauli basis will allow us to port over many of the fault-tolerant gadgets of HGP codes to their reduced versions. The next result concerns the minimum distances of the reduced HGP codes.

Theorem 1.3

(Preservation of code distance — Theorem

Theorem 1.4

(Distance-preserving syndrome extraction — Theorem

The reduced HGP codes possess a CNOT scheduling order for single-ancilla syndrome extraction such that the effective distance is the same as the code distance.

In all, the above results provide overhead savings when using a HGP code as a logical memory. 1.2
Related works

Shortly after the breakthroughs on asymptotically good LDPC codes, Leverrier and Zémor
63
discovered a link between their quantum Tanner codes
62
and the lifted product codes by Panteleev and Kalachev
81
, which also extends to the case of HGP codes and is the main motivator for our work. It was later pointed out by Breuckmann and Leverrier
64
that this general procedure encompasses the rotated surface code. However, their transformation is restricted to input LDPC codes with a bipartite Tanner graph structure, and we generalize their approach to arbitrary input LDPC codes.

Kovalev and Pryadko
59
previously proposed schemes to improve the rate of HGP codes by modifying the input codes but still retaining the physical HGP structure. In their symmetric square-matrix method, the logical qubits are split evenly between the bit-type and check-type sectors. In comparison, all of the logical qubits in our formulation reside solely in the bit-type sector. For their approach to apply generically, one needs to symmetrize a non-symmetric LDPC matrix via
H^{\mathsf{T}}H
, which can lead to a quadratic increase in the LDPC weights. In contrast, our approach can only double the LDPC weights. However, our reduction does not maintain the physical HGP structure and so certain HGP gadgets, while naturally compatible with their scheme, may not be compatible with our scheme.

Berthusen et al.
6
introduced a local concatenation scheme to lower the spacetime overhead of HGP codes in terms of circuit volume, which can be thought of as dynamical overhead reduction in the time direction. In comparison, our scheme lowers the static spatial overhead. It would be interesting to combine the two strategies to potentially achieve an even lower spacetime overhead.

3
Qubit reduction procedure

We motivate this section with a simple observation. Recall that we are only storing logical information in the bit-type logical sector. Since Proposition
2.1
tells us that the minimum weight of logical operators in the bit-type sector is a stabilizer-invariant, we can freely remove/puncture the check-type qubits without worrying about lowering the minimum distances of the HGP code. However, these check-type qubits were needed to ensure commutation between
X
-checks and
Z
-checks. Thus, we desire a procedure that can simultaneously remove check-type qubits and maintain stabilizer commutation. Ideally, such a procedure can also preserve the locality of the checks and hence the LDPC property of the code.

One naive approach for combining stabilizer checks might be to (
i
) take any check-type qubit that you want to remove, (
ii
) take linear combinations of the stabilizers connected to that qubit to remove all support off of it, (
iii
) discard that check-type qubit, and (
iv
) repeat for every check-type qubit, thereby serially removing the check-type qubits. However, in most cases, combining any two checks will increase the weight of the merged check on the bit-type qubits. If we combine any single check too many times, we risk losing the LDPC property of the code. This leads us to our first restriction for the procedure.

Restriction #1:
The reduction must be parallelizable where any given check is combined at most a constant number of times.

We will keep this constant to be 1 because in such a case, as we will see later, we can construct distance-preserving syndrome extraction circuits.

Our second restriction comes from noting the fact that this “parallel reduction” does not generically work when check-type qubits share stabilizers. The issue is that we can only safely remove a check-type qubit when we clean all
X
-checks or
Z
-checks off of it; otherwise we risk breaking stabilizer commutation. If we simultaneously try to clean a check-type qubit that shares, for instance, an
X
-check with another check-type qubit, we risk creating a new
X
-check with support on that second qubit. Removing this second qubit may cause the new
X
-check to fail to commute with its neighboring
Z
-checks. This obstruction leads to our second restriction for the procedure.

Restriction #2:
The procedure must work on a carefully–selected subset of
disjoint
stabilizers that do not share support on any qubits.

Specifically, the way we select this subset is as follows. If we partition the check-type qubits into groups that do not share any stabilizer support, we can combine stabilizers in those groups in parallel. At a high level, we choose this partition by coloring the check nodes of the classical input codes’ Tanner graphs such that two classical checks have the same color iff they do not share any bits in common. Such colorings can be found by coloring codes’ check-adjacency graphs, defined below. Finding a minimal coloring of a graph
G=(V,E)
is NP-hard, but greedy heuristics can be used to find a valid (not necessarily minimal) coloring in
\mathcal{O}(|V|+|E|)
time. In our case, fewer colors will equate to more check-type qubits removed.

Definition 3.1

(Check-adjacency graph)

Given a Tanner graph

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{C}=(C,E_{C})

The result is that if we can

-color the checks of

and
\chi_{2}
-color the checks of

or

(
Z
) stabilizers in one color group per row (column). Additionally, let us restrict ourselves to never combine

and

in the same color group. Doing so would be unoptimal since that would clearly equate to less check-type qubits removed.

Finally, we note that combining

(

denote the number of data qubits after the reduction. Define the matrices

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{X}

3.1.2
Cleaning stabilizers off check-type qubits

Now we discuss how the stabilizers are actually combined in each color group. For a given check-type qubit, let
\Delta
denote the number of
X
(or
Z
) checks that it participates in. Suppose we choose to combine
X
or
Z
checks connected to that check-type qubit. The relation between the new (combined) and old checks is represented by the linear transformation
w\in\mathbb{F}^{(\Delta-1)\times\Delta}_{2}
with nonzero entries

\displaystyle w=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&&&&\\
&1&1&&&\\
&&&\ddots&&\end{pmatrix}\,,

(22)

which is equivalent to the parity-check matrix of the 1D repetition code of length
\Delta
. In other words, for each check-type qubit that we want to remove, we have that the first new stabilizer is the combination of the first old stabilizer and the second old stabilizer, the second new stabilizer is the combination of the second old stabilizer and the third old stabilizer, etc. So for each putative check-type qubit, the number of stabilizers per check-type qubit decreases by one.

Because we only perform this reduction on disjoint checks, the entire reduction can be described by a matrix
W
which, upon reordering its columns, is of block-diagonal form with either
w
or
\mathds{1}
as the blocks. In other words, we have
W\sim w\oplus w\oplus\mathds{1}\oplus\cdots
.
Since each local matrix
w
has full (row) rank, so does the entire matrix
W
.

Claim 3.4

(LDPC weights of reduced HGP)
.

If the original HGP code was
(w_{q},w_{c})
-LDPC, then the reduced HGP code is
(\tilde{w}_{q},\tilde{w}_{c})
-LDPC with
\tilde{w}_{q}\leq 2w_{q}
and
\tilde{w}_{c}\leq 2(w_{c}-1)
.

Proof.

Let
\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm r}
and
\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm c}
denote the maximum row and column weight respectively of a binary matrix
A
. Since the entries are non-negative, we have
\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm r}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\infty}
and
\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm c}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{1}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{p}
denotes the vector-induced
p

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{p}
then implies that
\lvert{AB}\rvert_{\rm r}\leq\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm r}\lvert{B}\rvert_{\rm r}
and
\lvert{AB}\rvert_{\rm c}\leq\lvert{A}\rvert_{\rm c}\lvert{B}\rvert_{\rm c}
.

We first show that
\tilde{w}_{c}\leq 2(w_{c}-1)
. Since

is permutation-equivalent to a block-diagonal matrix with blocks

and

. It follows that

\tilde{w}_{c}=\lvert{\widetilde{H}_{X}}\rvert_{\rm r}=\lvert{W_{X}H_{X}V}\rvert_{\rm r}\leq 2\lvert{H_{X}V}\rvert_{\rm r}\leq 2(w_{c}-1)

{\widetilde{H}_{X}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\tilde{m}_{X}\times\tilde{n}}

\tilde{k}=k

\displaystyle=n-n_{\rm rem}+n_{\rm rem}-m_{X}-m_{Z}

\displaystyle=n-\operatorname{rank}H_{X}-\operatorname{rank}H_{Z}

(23)

4.1
Distance-preserving syndrome extraction

The error syndrome of a stabilizer code is the measurement outcomes of all its stabilizer checks. For a qLDPC code with low-weight stabilizers, it is customary to perform single-ancilla syndrome extraction: introduce an ancillary “syndrome” qubit for each stabilizer check, couple each syndrome qubit to its corresponding data qubits, and finally measure all syndrome qubits. For a stabilizer check with weight
w

w

d

If we think about repeated syndrome measurements in the context of a quantum memory, then potential faults in one round of syndrome extraction can lead to additional data errors in the next round. If we also consider errors on the syndrome qubits, then this radius of detection can decrease, since a single error on a syndrome qubit during the syndrome extraction circuit can propagate to
\lfloor w/2\rfloor

A (permutation) automorphism of a classical or quantum code is a relabeling of its data bits or qubits which preserves its codespace. 4.4
Code homomorphisms

In this section, we will show how HGP code homomorphisms can be adapted to our reduced HGP codes. Homomorphic gadgets can be used to selectively measure logical Pauli products within a single HGP block, dubbed grid-Pauli-product measurement (GPPM) gadgets
111
. In particular, we will show that both the data and ancilla code blocks can be reduced in an equivariant way that respects their homomorphic chain map.

The main feature that enables these homomorphic gadgets is the existence of a chain homomorphism
54
(Definition
4.5
) between the chain complex of the HGP data code
\mathcal{Q}
and a suitably modified HGP ancilla code
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, which enables a “homomorphic” (transversal) CNOT gate between corresponding logical qubits of the codes. For HGP codes, the ancilla code will be another HGP code with data qubits removed or added. Because this ancilla code keeps the same product structure as that of the data code, the homomorphic CNOT can be realized by applying physical CNOTs between corresponding pairs of physical qubits (and by doing nothing to physical qubits without a pair). In all, this enables us to do logical measurements.

We now review code homomorphisms, how homomorphic CNOTs arise from them, and how they appear in our HGP codes. We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for background on the relevant mathematical terminologies such as chain complexes and their relation to CSS codes. Recall that a quantum CSS code
\mathcal{Q}
is associated with a 3-term (co)chain complex

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}:\qquad S_{X}\xlongrightarrow{H^{\mathsf{T}}_{X}}Q\xlongrightarrow{H_{Z}}S_{Z}\,,

are the binary vector spaces of

4.4.1
Augmentation

Augmentation is a procedure where a new parity check is added to a linear code, thereby enlarging the parity-check matrix
H
by a single row. If this new check is linearly independent from the others, then the rank of
H
increases by 1, and so as a consequence the number of logical bits decreases by 1. Without loss of generality, recall from (

is
\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}_{k}&A^{\mathsf{T}}\end{pmatrix}
for some matrix

k

to respect this same bit ordering, where the
k

113

H=\begin{pmatrix}A&\mathds{1}_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}
. Clearly these satisfy
HG^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{0}
. By choosing the augmented checks to only act on the
k
informational bits, we can controllably alter the properties of the modified code to our desire.

As an example, suppose we have an
[n,k,d]
code
\mathcal{C}
whose generator matrix is in canonical form

with

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\mathbf{0}_{1\times(n-2)}\end{pmatrix}
to

to satisfy

for

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}A&\mathds{1}_{n-k}\\
\begin{matrix}1&1\end{matrix}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(46)

However, since we augmented on the informational bits, we can predict what this matrix looks like. First, note that only the first two logical bits (rows of
G

G
do not satisfy the augmented parity check, their linear combination over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
does (since the new parity check enforces that the first two bits of codewords must be the same). So we simply replace the first two rows of

. Since we simply took a linear combination of the rows of

In particular, any coloring of the check-type qubits prior to augmentation also remains a valid coloring after augmentation. If desired, one can additionally reduce the new check-type qubits to further lower the spatial overhead.

Puncturing is a procedure where bits are deleted from a linear code, thereby reducing the length of the code or number of columns in the parity-check and generator matrices. Similar to the case of augmentation, by puncturing only the informational bits, we maintain control over the modified code’s parameters. Suppose we have a

Lemma 4.8

(Puncturing informational bits does not alter
\operatorname{rank}H
)
.

\operatorname{rank}H^{\prime}=\operatorname{rank}H=n-k

Proof.

Suppose, on the contrary, that
\operatorname{rank}H^{\prime}<\operatorname{rank}H

\sum_{i}\mathbf{c}^{\prime}_{i}=0

\sum_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\neq 0

\hat{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\operatorname{rs}{H}
.

The support of the remaining codewords remains unchanged.

\displaystyle\tilde{N}^{(X)}_{\rm 2q}=2v^{2}\Delta(\Delta-1)+2(v-1)^{2}\Delta(\Delta-1)+(v-1)\Delta^{2}\,,

(66)

which results in a net difference of

\displaystyle N^{(X)}_{\rm 2q}-\tilde{N}^{(X)}_{\rm 2q}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\Big[(4-\Delta)(2v-1)^{2}-(\Delta-2)\Big]\,.

(67)

A similar calculation for the

\displaystyle N^{(Z)}_{\rm 2q}-\tilde{N}^{(Z)}_{\rm 2q}=\Delta\Big[1+2(4-\Delta)v(v-1)\Big]\,.

(68)

The total two-qubit gate savings is then given by summing (
67
) and (
68

\displaystyle N_{\rm 2q}-\tilde{N}_{\rm 2q}=\Delta(4-\Delta)(2v-1)^{2}\,,

(69)

which is positive when
\Delta\leq 3

\Delta=4
, and negative when
\Delta\geq 5

To assess the near-term prospects of our qubit reduction scheme in practice, we perform circuit-level

70

So far we have only compared the unreduced HGP code with its reduced version directly. Another comparison we can do is to (roughly) fix the number of data qubits and ask if there is a reduced HGP code with better performance than an unreduced code. In the left plot of Figure

7
Outlook

We introduced a general-purpose procedure to reduce the number of physical qubits in HGP codes while maintaining code parameters such as code dimension, logical operator basis and minimum distance. We also show compatibility with a variety of existing HGP fault-tolerant gadgets such as distance-preserving syndrome extraction, fold-transversal gates and code homomorphisms. We then demonstrate that carefully-structured input codes allow the most effective reductions both in terms of qubit overhead, degrees and practical performance.

There are several possible extensions of the qubit reduction that would promote its practicality for fault-tolerant quantum computation. On the numerical front, an improvement in decoding would directly lower the overhead for fault-tolerant quantum computation with these HGP codes by enabling the usage of smaller code blocks. There is room for significant improvements here since we only used phenomenological decoding graphs and a rudimentary Relay-BP configuration. On the theoretical front, the most immediate extension would be to generalize our procedure to higher-dimensional (
D>2
) HGP codes; i.e. products of more than 2 classical codes.
D>2
HGP codes can support single-shot QEC/initialization
84
as well as transversal
1
1

1

Here “transversal” is a bit looser from the usual sense and means a constant-depth circuit.
non-Clifford gates
14
;
43
;
66
;
53
;
114
;
100
, and so they have been of both theoretical and practical interest. Since the qubit reduction consists only of local modifications, we also expect it to preserve the confinement
84
and soundness
24
properties relevant for single-shot error correction and initialization, but leave formal verifications to future work.

There have been several recent works that introduce new HGP fault-tolerant gadgets: two based on code-switching
42
;
100
and two based on code surgery
112
;
27
. It would be of further practical relevance if our qubit reduction can be made compatible with a subset or all of these schemes. In
42
, addressable logical Clifford gates are performed in constant depth by code-switching between 2D and 3D HGP codes along different axes, interleaved with transversal Clifford gates. Extending the 2D qubit reduction to 3D would be the first step towards progress in this direction, then constructing chain maps between reduced 2D and 3D HGP codes to enable code-switching. In
100
, code-switching between structured 2D and 3D HGP codes enable logical CCZ gates in constant depth. To make qubit reduction compatible with this scheme would require finding a reduction schedule compatible with the algebraic structures that enable the transversal CCZ in the 3D code. In
112
and
27
, specialized HGP ancilla blocks are attached and detached to simultaneously measure extensive numbers of logical Paulis; in the first case different logical Paulis for high-rate surgery and in the second case stabilizer-equivalent logical Paulis for single-shot surgery. There are some constant-factor qubit savings when using these “coning” approaches compared to the homomorphic measurement scheme. Since the chain maps for coning are slightly different than for homomorphic measurements, the main challenge for showing reduction compatibility would be to prove distance-preservation for the merged data-ancilla code.

Recently, HGP codes have also been shown to achieve optimal maximum-likely-error (MLE) decoding under more realistic neutral-atom noise models that include atom loss, reaching the same decoding radius as that for erasures
68
. An extension to reduced HGP codes would boost their practicality for the neutral-atom platform.

Lastly, it would be interesting if any analytical guarantees for HGP qubit reduction can be extended to more exotic product constructions such as the lifted product
80
and balanced product
15
. These constructions allow for codes with even more efficient parameters than the HGP
65
;
10
;
22
. Advancing in this direction could include generalizing the Künneth theorem to tensor products of chain complexes based on group rings
113
, over which the lifted and (certain) balanced products are defined.

Chain complex formalism for classical and quantum codes

In this section, we cover the algebraic formalism of classical and quantum codes which gives a connection between the linear algebraic perspective of the codes to a pictorial perspective using Tanner graphs. This section will also give the background necessary for the results of Section

Definition A.1

(Chain complex)
.

A (based) chain complex over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a sequence of vector spaces
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
together with linear boundary maps

\partial_{i}:A_{i}\to A_{i-1}

such that for every
i
,

\displaystyle\partial_{i-1}\circ\partial_{i}=0\,.

(72)

Equivalently, a chain complex is written as

\displaystyle\cdots\xleftarrow{\partial_{0}}A_{0}\xleftarrow{\partial_{1}}A_{1}\xleftarrow{\partial_{2}}A_{2}\xleftarrow{\partial_{3}}\cdots\,.

(73)

The
i
th homology group is defined as
\mathcal{H}_{i}:=\ker\partial_{i}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{i+1}
.
The associated cochain complex on the dual spaces
\{A_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is defined by the coboundary maps

\delta^{i}:=\partial_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}:A_{i}^{*}\to A_{i+1}^{*}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\cdots\xrightarrow{\delta^{-2}}A_{-1}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{-1}}A_{0}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{0}}A_{1}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{1}}A_{2}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{2}}\cdots\,.

(74)

Since
\delta^{i}=\partial_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the cochain condition is equivalently

\delta^{i+1}\circ\delta^{i}=0

for every
i
. The
i
th cohomology group is defined as
\mathcal{H}^{i}:=\ker\delta^{i}/\operatorname{im}\delta^{i-1}
.

In this work, we focus on 2-term and 3-term chain complexes:

\displaystyle A_{0}\xleftarrow{\partial_{1}}A_{1}\,,

(75)

\displaystyle A_{0}\xleftarrow{\partial_{1}}A_{1}\xleftarrow{\partial_{2}}A_{2}\,,

(76)

with cochain complexes

\displaystyle A_{0}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{0}}A_{1}^{*}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle A_{0}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{0}}A_{1}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta^{1}}A_{2}^{*}\,.

(78)

Geometrically, when
A_{i}=\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{i}]
is generated by a basis of
i
-cells
X_{i}
, the map
\partial_{i}
sends a formal sum of
i
-cells to its boundary in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{i-1}]
. In particular, the 2-term case corresponds to edges and vertices, while the 3-term case corresponds to faces, edges, and vertices; see Figure
17
for an illustration.

Appendix D
Tanner codes

The Tanner construction
104
is a procedure to build a classical linear code from a regular base graph
G
and a local code
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
. Sipser and Spielman
94
utilized this approach to construct the first explicit (non-probabilistic) family of classical LDPC codes with asymptotically good parameters
[n,\mathrm{\Theta}(n),\mathrm{\Theta}(n)]
.

Definition D.1

(Tanner code
104
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
\Delta
-regular simple graph called the
base graph
, and let
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
be a linear code of length
\Delta
called the
local code
. The Tanner code
\mathcal{T}(G,\hat{\mathcal{C}})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\big(G,\hat{\mathcal{C}}\big)=\left\{c\in\mathbb{F}^{\lvert{E}\rvert}_{2}:\forall v\in V\,,\,c|_{E(v)}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}\right\}\,,

(130)

where
E(v)
denotes the edges incident to
v
.

In other words, given a base graph where each vertex has degree
\Delta
, physical bits are placed on the edges of
G
, and parity checks of the local code
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
are placed on the vertices. The local parity checks of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
enforce that all codewords of
\mathcal{T}(G,\hat{\mathcal{C}})
are codewords of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
when restricted to any vertex. When the local code is a single-parity-check code with
h=\mathbf{1}_{\Delta}
, then the Tanner code is also known as the cycle code on
G
. Definition
D.1
requires
G
to be a simple graph, but one can relax this definition to allow
G
to be a Tanner graph of another code. The Tanner construction can then be viewed as “upgrading” each parity check of some input code to a local code, typically done as a way to boost the code distance at the cost of a lower rate.

Theorem D.2

(Expander code
94
)
.

Let
G
be a simple
\Delta
-regular graph with second-largest eigenvalue
\lambda
, and let
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
be a linear code of length
\Delta
, rate
\hat{r}
and relative distance
\hat{\delta}
. Then the Tanner code
\mathcal{T}(G,\hat{\mathcal{C}})
has rate at least

\displaystyle r\geq 2\hat{r}-1

(131)

and relative distance at least

\displaystyle\delta\geq\left(\frac{\hat{\delta}-\lambda/\Delta}{1-\lambda/\Delta}\right)^{2}\,.

(132)

Note that the rate and relative distance are bounded away from zero when
\hat{r}>1/2
and
\hat{\delta}>\lambda/\Delta
. For certain spectral expander graph families
69
;
73
with an upper bound on
\lambda
, choosing appropriate local codes satisfying the above inequalities leads to family of asymptotically good LDPC codes by Theorem
D.2
.

Lemma D.3
.

If the classical input code is a Tanner code
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G},\hat{\mathcal{C}})
with local parity-check matrix
\hat{h}
and parameters
[\Delta,\hat{k},\hat{d}]
, then the number of remaining neighboring
X
-checks of a check-type qubit in the HGP code with itself obeys
\Delta^{\prime}\geq\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}=\Delta-\hat{k}
.

Proof.

Recall that for the quantum Tanner transformation, instead of a single check-type qubit, the local transformation acts on a set of
\hat{m}=\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}=\Delta-\hat{k}
local check-type qubits and takes the form
w=\hat{g}
, where
\hat{g}
is a generator matrix for
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
that satisfies
\hat{g}\hat{h}^{\mathsf{T}}=0
. Suppose, on the contrary, that we have deleted more than
\hat{k}
neighboring
X
-checks so that
\Delta^{\prime}<\hat{m}=\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}
. As a consequence, we now have more local check-type qubits than remaining local
X
-checks, which implies that
\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}^{\prime}<\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}
. However,
\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}^{\prime}<\operatorname{rank}\hat{h}
then implies that
\operatorname{rank}{H^{\prime}_{2}}<\operatorname{rank}{H_{2}}
, in contradiction with Lemma
4.8
.
∎
